###### Abstract

Self-play red team is an established approach to improving AI safety in
which different instances of the same model play attacker and defender
roles in a zero-sum game, i.e., where the attacker tries to jailbreak
the defender; if self-play converges to a Nash equilibrium, the model is
guaranteed to respond safely within the settings of the game. Although
the parameter sharing enforced by the use of the same model for the two
roles improves stability and performance, it introduces fundamental
theoretical and architectural limitations. We show that the set of Nash
equilibria that can be reached corresponds to a broad class of
behaviours that includes trivial always refuse strategies and
oracle-like defenders, thus limiting practical applicability. We then
show that when attacker and defender share and update the same base
model, the dynamics collapse to self-consistency, so that attacks do not
enforce adversarial pressure on the defender. In response, we propose
Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play, which trains distinct role-specific LoRA
adapters on top of a frozen base model, thereby maintaining stable
optimisation while preserving adversarial pressure through explicit role
separation. In relation to standard self-play, we show up to 100x
greater parameter efficiency than fine-tuning and consistent
improvements in safety compared to self-play fine-tuned models. We
evaluate on Qwen2.5-{3B, 7B,14B}-IT models across widely used safety
benchmarks, showing improved robustness without loss of reasoning
ability. Cross-play experiments further show that our attacker and
defender models are superior to self-play in terms of adversarial
defence and safety.

## 1 Introduction

While concerns surrounding the safety of NLP models have long been
recognised [Arras et al. (2016)](#bib.bib20), Large Language Models
(LLMs) [Brown et al. (2020)](#bib.bib18), given their capabilities and
widespread adoption, pose significant security and safety risks that
have the potential to erode trust in these systems [La Malfa et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib21), particularly with the rise of LLM agents and
agentic applications [de Witt (2025)](#bib.bib19). Although safeguarding
generated outputs is critical for preventing misuse, recent developments
reveal ongoing and substantial obstacles to achieving dependable LLM
safety [Xu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib22). Model patching to prevent
jailbreaks constitutes one mitigation strategy [Xiong et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib23). More recently, self-play reinforcement learning,
which engages multiple models in iterative attack-and-defence cycles,
has shown promise in improving model robustness [Liu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib1).

As
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")
(centre) illustrates, in the Self-Play Red-Teaming (Self-RedTeam)
framework by Liu et al. [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1), a single model
is fine-tuned to assume both the attacker role, attempting to jailbreak
the defender, and the defender role, aiming to prevent such breaches.
The attacker is rewarded for eliciting harmful responses, whilst the
defender succeeds by refusing harmful requests or answering benign
requests. In this configuration, private reasoning traces are hidden,
requiring agents to infer opponent intent exclusively from observable
actions.

Figure 1: Architectural Comparison of Self-Play Red Team Frameworks.
(Left) Full Model Separation: Two distinct models guarantee independence
and adversarial pressure, but lead to high computational cost. (Middle)
Single-Model Coupling (Self-RedTeam [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1)):
Attacker and Defender share the same parameters. Gradient updates for
the attacker shift the defender’s decision boundaries, resulting in
self-consistency and weakening the discovery of new vulnerabilities.
(Right) Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play (ABS, Ours): We freeze the base
model and evolve separate LoRA adapters for the attacker and defender,
restoring adversarial pressure with minimal memory cost.

Although this approach can be framed as an imperfect-information game,
current frameworks are limited by fundamental architectural and
theoretical constraints. First, we show that relying on convergence to a
Nash equilibrium as a safety guarantee admits equilibria that include
trivial always-refuse strategies or oracle-like defenders, limiting
practical applicability. Moreover, full parameter sharing, while
improving training stability, introduces a strong dependency. In
particular, we show that the min-max safety game of Liu et al. [Liu et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib1) induces a self-consistency bias that potentially
reduces the adversarial pressure on the defender.

To re-establish adversarial integrity within the safety game, this work
introduces Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play (ABS). The ABS framework enforces
strict architectural separation between attacker and defender while
maintaining the training stability of self-play
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"),
right). By freezing the base model, ABS employs Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA) to train distinct, lightweight adapter modules for each role,
guaranteeing gradient update separation and a substantial reduction of
the computational costs compared to the fully fine-tuned
state-of-the-art Self-RedTeam and the base model.

ABS matches or outperforms both the base models and the fully fine-tuned
Self-RedTeam across multiple safety benchmarks while preserving
reasoning and instruction-following abilities. Our method is
particularly effective at smaller scales, where it provides significant
performance gains over existing self-play frameworks while remaining
competitive with fully fine-tuned models at the 14B scale. We further
assess attacker strength in two settings: a cross-play tournament, where
ABS attackers and Self-RedTeam defenders of equal size are evaluated
against each other, and a transfer setting, where both act as attackers
against the base Qwen model. In both cases, ABS attackers outperform
Self-RedTeam, achieving an attack success rate that is around $`10\%`$
higher in cross-play and $`15\%`$ higher when targeting the same base
model.

The contributions of this work are threefold:

- •
  We show that the zero-sum formulation of self-play red-teaming allows
  for a broad class of indistinguishable Nash equilibria, which include
  both trivial “always-refuse” strategies and optimal oracle defenders.
  This result provides a theoretical explanation for why convergence
  alone does not ensure model utility.
- •
  We identify and geometrically formalise the “self-consistency”
  phenomenon inherent in shared-parameter architectures. Our analysis
  demonstrates that coupled gradient updates suppress adversarial
  pressure and hinder the exploration of worst-case prompts.
- •
  We propose Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play (ABS), a parameter-efficient
  framework that employs role-specific LoRA adapters to maintain
  architectural separation. This approach restores adversarial integrity
  by enabling independent evolution of the attacker and defender, while
  also substantially reducing the computational cost associated with
  self-play training.

The paper is organised as follows: we first review the relevant
literature in reinforcement learning for safety, self-play, and
parameter-efficient training. We then show how the analysis of safety
via Nash equilibria is incomplete, and that the current formulation of
Self-RedTeam reduces to self-consistency. We then introduce our
technique (ABS), and conduct the experiments to prove its performance
and scalability. All the results are available
[\[here\]](https://github.com/EmanueleLM/AnchoredBipolicySelf-Play).

## 2 Related Work

Safety, Jailbreaking, and Red-Teaming. Standard red team training relies
on human feedback to identify harmful responses and reinforce model
behaviour accordingly [Bai et al. (2022)](#bib.bib3); [Dai et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib4). Owing to the limited coverage of this approach,
models are often encouraged to explore novel attack prompts [Perez et
al. (2022)](#bib.bib5). For instance, Mehrotra et al. [Mehrotra et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib6) propose a pruning strategy to pre-select attacks with
higher jailbreak potential. Hong et al. [Hong et al. (2024)](#bib.bib7)
instead optimise jointly for reward and novelty, incentivising the
generation of both successful and previously unseen prompts in a
curiosity-driven setting. Other lines of work focus on co-evolving
attacker and defender policies to avoid static adversaries [Jain et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib8). A central challenge in this setting is computational
cost [Howe et al. (2025)](#bib.bib9). Xhonneux et al. [Xhonneux et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib10) address this by operating in the continuous
embedding space, reducing the expense associated with discrete
token-level attacks. Liu et al. [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1) introduce
an online self-play framework in which a single model instantiates both
attacker and defender, enabling mutual adaptation. More recently, Wen et
al. [Wen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib11) propose MAGIC, a decoupled
co-evolving formulation designed to mitigate optimisation conflicts
through full parameter updates. Tan et al. [Tan et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib41) extend this line of work with a tri-role framework
that incorporates an additional evaluator to stabilise training via
multi-agent interaction.

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning Techniques. Parameter-Efficient
Fine-Tuning (PEFT) techniques have demonstrated effectiveness in model
training while offering significant computational efficiency compared to
full model fine-tuning. Among these methods, LoRA [Hu et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib12) applies a low-rank update to the weights while
keeping the base model parameters fixed. For instance, in transformer
architectures that underpin modern large language models (LLMs), LoRA
modifies the attention weights while leaving the multi-layer perceptron
weights unchanged. Further variations of LoRA work on either reducing
the computational learning costs, such as QLoRA [Dettmers et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib13), or dynamically modifying the dimension of the
weight matrices (rank) to improve critical learning momenta, like
AdaLoRA [Zhang et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib14). SaLoRA [Li et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib15) instead proposes a method to improve safety
alignment of LLMs. Finally, in line with this paper, Red-Bandit
implements a LoRA-based post-training to reduce LLMs’ vulnerabilities to
attacks [Ziakas et al. (2025)](#bib.bib16).

Summary and Research Gap. Self-RedTeam [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1)
serves as the primary benchmark as it established the foundational
setting for online symmetric self-play in LLMs’ safety, where a single
model alternates between attacker and defender roles. Recent literature
has shifted toward more complex co-evolutionary games, such as
MAGIC [Wen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib11), which proposes role decoupling
through full-parameter updates, and TriPlay-RL [Tan et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib41), which introduces an auxiliary “Evaluator” role to
stabilise co-evolution. Unlike our work, they do not provide a
theoretical treatment of the gradient collision and self-consistency
bias inherent in shared-parameter architectures. ABS fills this gap by
introducing a parameter-efficient method that restores adversarial
integrity through architectural separation.

## 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play

Self-RedTeam by Liu et al. [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1) proposes model
safety via self-play, wherein a single base model jointly instantiates
an attacker $`A`$ (issuing jailbreak prompts) and a defender $`D`$
(responding). Training proceeds online in a sequential zero-sum game,
where a Nash equilibrium corresponds to safety (see
§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Nash Equilibria in Self-RedTeam ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
Despite growing uptake [Wen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib11) and extensions to
general-sum settings [Liao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib2), we show that (i)
the Self-RedTeam formulation admits indistinguishable equilibria
spanning trivial always-refusal and oracle defenders (see
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Analysis of Extremal Nash Equilibria ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"));
and (ii) the objective is more naturally cast as single-model
self-consistency, with implications for training dynamics and equilibria
(see
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Min-Max Constraint Conflict and Self-Consistency ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).

### 3.1 Nash Equilibria in Self-RedTeam

Let $`\mathcal{Y}_{A}`$ be the set of attacker prompts and
$`\mathcal{Y}_{D}`$ be the set of defender responses. For each player
$`P\in\{A,D\}`$, we define the probability simplex over
$`\mathcal{Y}_{P}`$ as $`\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{P})`$. In the two-player
game formulation of red-teaming, the attacker first proposes a prompt
$`y_{A}`$ sampled from a mixed strategy
$`\pi_{A}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{A})`$, referred to as a policy. Given
the prompt $`y_{A}`$, the defender then generates a response $`y_{D}`$
sampled from a conditional strategy $`\pi_{D}(\cdot\mid y_{A})`$ with
$`\pi_{D}:\mathcal{Y}_{A}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{D})`$.¹¹ 1
Without loss of generality, we omit that in Self-RedTeam, the attack is
initially conditioned on a seed, while the attacker’s and defender’s
policies are further conditioned on the instructions to generate the
attack and the defence, respectively. Afterwards, a reward model
$`r:\mathcal{Y}_{A}\times\mathcal{Y}_{D}\to[-1,1]`$ evaluates each
prompt-response pair, i.e., for any such pair $`(y_{A},y_{D})`$, the
defender’s reward is $`r(y_{A},y_{D})`$, and the attacker receives the
opposite payoff $`-r(y_{A},y_{D})`$.

For an attacker and a defender that aim to maximise their rewards, this
setting corresponds to a zero-sum sequential game where (i) unsafe
responses are strictly penalised, i.e., $`r(y_{A},y_{D})=-1`$ if
$`(y_{A},y_{D})`$ is unsafe; (ii) safe responses are rewarded
non-negatively, i.e., $`r(y_{A},y_{D})\geq 0`$ if $`(y_{A},y_{D})`$ is
safe, and (iii) there exists a refusal response
$`y^{\mathrm{ref}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}`$ such that
$`r(y_{A},y^{\mathrm{ref}})=0`$ for all $`y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}`$.

Under these assumptions, the expected reward is
$`V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})=\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{A},\;y_{D}\sim\pi_{D}(\cdot\mid y_{A})}\big[r(y_{A},y_{D})\big]`$
and the game admits the following min-max representation:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \min_{\pi_{A}}\max_{\pi_{D}}V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D}).
       ```                                              |     |

A Nash equilibrium $`(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D}^{\ast})`$ is thus a pair of
strategies such that neither player has an incentive to deviate, i.e.,
$`V(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D}^{\ast})\geq V(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D})`$ for
all $`\pi_{D}`$, and
$`V(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D}^{\ast})\leq V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D}^{\ast})`$ for
all $`\pi_{A}`$. When the two players’ policies converge to a Nash
equilibrium $`(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D}^{\ast})`$, Liu et al. [Liu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib1) proved that the defender guarantees a safe response
to any attacker prompt, i.e., $`r(y_{A},\pi_{D}^{\ast}(y_{A}))\geq 0`$
for any $`y_{A}`$.

We show that, under the above assumptions, extremal Nash equilibria
attaining the minimum or maximum defender expected payoff are
problematic in both theory and practice (see
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Analysis of Extremal Nash Equilibria ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
On the one hand, equilibria that are qualitatively distinct yet
indistinguishable to the learning dynamics offer limited practical
guidance. On the other hand, when a single model instantiates both
attacker and defender, the dynamics can induce implicit coordination or
collusion between roles (see
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Min-Max Constraint Conflict and Self-Consistency ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).

### 3.2 Analysis of Extremal Nash Equilibria

In this section, we derive a “lower bound” extremal that corresponds to
the case where the defender always refuses, guaranteeing safety but
compromising usefulness. Complementarily, we characterise the “upper
bound” extremal that corresponds to an oracle-like defender that always
provides safe and maximally helpful responses.

##### Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium.

Recall our assumption that the defender can always issue a refusal
response $`y^{\mathrm{ref}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}`$ such that
$`r(y_{A},y^{\mathrm{ref}})=0`$ for all $`y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}`$.
Thus, for any prompt $`y_{A}`$, we define the always-refuse defender
strategy as
$`\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{D}\mid y_{A})=\mathbf{1}\{y_{D}=y^{\mathrm{ref}}\}`$,
which yields an expected reward $`V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{ref}})=0`$
for any attacker policy $`\pi_{A}`$.

A pair $`(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{ref}})`$ is a Nash equilibrium
if the attacker policy $`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$ is sufficiently adversarial
such that no defender policy achieves a strictly positive expected
payoff, i.e., $`\max_{\pi_{D}}V(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D})=0`$. We
therefore focus on the attacker policies in a local neighbourhood of a
reference adversarial policy $`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$, which concentrates on
prompts where any non-refusal response yields no positive reward, and
where nearby policies preserve this property (see
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
This reflects the intuition that attacks may adapt while remaining close
to a reference distribution, ensuring semantic consistency.

Within this neighbourhood, we prove that deviations in the attacker
policy only induce controlled changes in the defender’s payoff (Lemma
[1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1. ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
We thus focus on attacker policies $`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$ that preserve the
adversarial property of $`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$ locally, in the sense that
they do not admit any defender strategy with strictly positive expected
payoff, i.e., we require
$`\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{A}^{\ast},y_{D}\sim\pi_{D}^{\prime}}[r(y_{A},y_{D})]\leq 0`$
for any alternative defender strategy $`\pi_{D}^{\prime}`$. This
condition can be interpreted as a worst-case constraint on the attacker:
within the local neighbourhood, any attempt by the defender to provide a
helpful response yields no positive gain and may incur risk, so the
defender has no incentive to deviate from $`y^{\mathrm{ref}}`$.
Specifically, providing a helpful response risks a payoff of $`-1`$ that
is not offset by safe, high-reward completions, making the expected
value of answering non-positive. Hence, $`\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is a
best response to $`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$. Further, as the attacker cannot
reduce the payoff below $`0`$ against a refusing defender,
$`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$ is a best response.

##### Bound 2: The defender acts like an oracle.

Let $`\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{A}`$ be the set of malicious
prompts, for which the maximum achievable safe reward is zero. That is,
$`\max_{y_{D}}r(y_{A},y_{D})=0`$ for any $`y_{A}\in\mathcal{M}`$ and
$`\max_{y_{D}}r(y_{A},y_{D})>0`$ for any $`y_{A}\notin\mathcal{M}`$.
Assume there exists an oracle defender policy $`\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{rec}}`$
that is pointwise optimal: for every $`y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}`$, it
picks a response $`y_{D}`$ to maximize $`r(y_{A},y_{D})`$. Namely, for
all $`y_{A}\in\mathcal{M}`$, $`\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{rec}}`$ ensures safety
by generating a response $`y_{D}^{\mathrm{safe}}`$ with
$`r(y_{A},y_{D}^{\mathrm{safe}})=0`$, and for
$`y_{A}\notin\mathcal{M}`$, it provides a maximally helpful response
$`y_{D}`$ with $`r(y_{A},y_{D})>0`$.

Therefore, a pair $`(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{rec}})`$ is a Nash
equilibrium if the attacker’s policy $`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$ minimises the
expected reward against the oracle. Formally, $`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$ must
satisfy:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\pi_{A}^{\ast}\in\arg\min_{\pi_{A}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{A}}\left[\max_{\pi_{D}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{D}\sim\pi_{D}(\cdot|y_{A})}\left[r(y_{A},y_{D})\right]\right]=\arg\min_{\pi_{A}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{A}}\left[\max_{y_{D}}r(y_{A},y_{D})\right].
``` |  |

where the last equality follows from
$`\max_{\pi_{D}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{D}\sim\pi_{D}(\cdot|y_{A})}\left[r(y_{A},y_{D})\right]=\max_{y_{D}}r(y_{A},y_{D})`$
since the expected reward is linear in the mixed strategy
$`\pi_{D}(\cdot|y_{A})\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{D})`$. Thus, the maximum
over the simplex $`\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{D})`$ it is attained at a vertex,
meaning that the optimal mixed strategy simply places all probability
mass on the best pure strategy $`y_{D}`$ maximizing $`r(y_{A},y_{D})`$.

As $`\max_{y_{D}}r(y_{A},y_{D})=0`$ for all $`y_{A}\in\mathcal{M}`$, and
$`\max_{y_{D}}r(y_{A},y_{D})>0`$ for any $`y_{A}\notin\mathcal{M}`$, it
follows that any equilibrium attacker policy $`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$ must be
supported on $`\mathcal{M}`$, i.e.,
$`\supp(\pi_{A}^{\ast})\subseteq\mathcal{M}`$, where
$`\supp(\pi_{A}^{\ast}):=\{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}:\pi_{A}^{\ast}(y_{A})>0\}`$
denotes the support of $`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$. At such an equilibrium, the
attacker cannot further reduce the payoff, as safety is guaranteed,
while the defender cannot improve its payoff on malicious inputs without
violating the safety constraint and incurring a $`-1`$ penalty.

### 3.3 Min-Max Constraint Conflict and Self-Consistency

Assume now that the attacker and defender share parameters, i.e., there
exists a single policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ such that
$`\pi_{A}=\pi_{D}=\pi_{\theta}`$. Under this constraint, the
optimisation problem involves a single parameter vector $`\theta`$
balancing two conflicting objectives:

1.  1.
    Defender Role: Maximize $`V`$ (generate safe responses).
2.  2.
    Attacker Role: Minimize $`V`$ (generate successful jailbreaks).

Since the parameters are shared, the attacker cannot adapt independently
without simultaneously altering the defender. In the zero-sum
red-teaming game, the attacker’s and defender’s rewards for any
prompt-response pair $`(y_{A},y_{D})`$ are
$`R_{A}(y_{A},y_{D}):=-r(y_{A},y_{D})`$ and
$`R_{D}(y_{A},y_{D}):=r(y_{A},y_{D})`$, respectively. We denote the
defender’s and attacker’s objectives as follows (respectively):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
J_{D}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{\theta},\;y_{D}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{A})}[r(y_{A},y_{D})],\quad J_{A}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{\theta},\;y_{D}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{A})}[-r(y_{A},y_{D})]
``` |  |

The resulting policy-gradient update is given by the sum of the defender
and attacker gradients:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J_{D}(\theta)+\nabla_{\theta}J_{A}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{A},y_{D}}\Big[r(y_{A},y_{D})\big(\underbrace{\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{D}\mid y_{A})}_{\text{Force 1: Improve Defence}}-\underbrace{\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{A})}_{\text{Force 2: Improve Attacks}}\big)\Big].`$ |  | (1) |

To make the coupling explicit, let $`\eta>0`$ denote the learning rate
and consider a defender-only step, i.e.,
$`\theta^{+}=\theta+\eta\,\nabla_{\theta}J_{D}(\theta)`$. Then, a
first-order Taylor expansion of the attacker objective yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
J_{A}(\theta^{+})=J_{A}(\theta)+\eta\left\langle\nabla_{\theta}J_{A}(\theta),\,\nabla_{\theta}J_{D}(\theta)\right\rangle+O(\eta^{2}).
``` |  | (2) |

Thus, at first order, the effect of a defender update is governed by the
inner product between the attacker and defender gradients. When these
directions are aligned, improving the defender simultaneously increases
the attacker’s objective, and vice versa. This coupling highlights how
shared-parameter training can induce self-consistency instead of fully
robust adversarial behaviour.

Geometrically, we can observe this by analysing the signs of the rewards
in
Eq. ([1](#S3.E1 "In 3.3 Min-Max Constraint Conflict and Self-Consistency ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")):

- •
  Case 1: Resistance ($`r(y_{A},y_{D})>0`$): When the defender resists
  an attack, Force 1 increases the likelihood of the safe response
  $`y_{D}`$ while Force 2 decreases the likelihood of the attack prompt
  $`y_{A}`$. This drives $`\pi_{\theta}`$ to avoid prompts it already
  defends against, leading to self-consistency rather than the discovery
  of fundamentally stronger attacks.
- •
  Case 2: Success ($`r(y_{A},y_{D})<0`$): Conversely, if a jailbreak
  succeeds, Force 2 attempts to reinforce the successful attack
  $`y_{A}`$. Simultaneously, the heavy penalty on the first term
  (Force 1) tries to update $`\theta`$ to suppress the vulnerability
  that permitted the jailbreak.

Because these updates are applied simultaneously to the shared parameter
vector $`\theta`$, the gradients effectively collide. Rather than
sharpening the attacker and strengthening the defender, the model
converges to a stagnant middle ground. This prevents exploration of the
adversarial frontier, yielding a “muddy” representational space in which
both roles are compromised.

## 4 Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play

We now formalise the Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play (ABS) policy for the
attacker and the defender: we rely on LoRA modules to ensure
attacker-defender separation so that the adversarial pressure on the
defender remains unaltered, while maintaining the objective function
defined in Self-RedTeam.

### 4.1 Role-conditioned LoRA Policies

Let $`\pi_{\theta}`$ denote a single policy network with a shared frozen
backbone and role-specific LoRA adapters. The effective parameters are
given by $`\Theta=(\theta,\phi_{A},\phi_{D})`$, where $`\theta`$ denotes
the frozen backbone parameters, and $`\phi_{A},\phi_{D}`$ are the LoRA
parameters for the attacker and defender, respectively. For a
Transformer-based LLM model [Vaswani et al. (2017)](#bib.bib26), and a
subset of backbone parameters $`\theta^{l}\subseteq\theta`$ (e.g., the
query and value projections of self-attention), a LoRA module
parameterised by $`\phi^{l}\subseteq\phi`$ defines a low-rank update
added to $`\theta^{l}`$ and trained while keeping $`\theta^{l}`$ frozen.
Then, actions for the attacker and the defender are sampled as
$`y_{A}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{A}}(\cdot)`$ and
$`y_{D}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{D}}(\cdot\mid y_{A})`$.

ABS adapts the training method in [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1), which
leverages Re++ [Hu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib25), a lightweight
reinforcement learning algorithm extending PPO and REINFORCE [Williams
(1992)](#bib.bib24); [Schulman et al. (2017)](#bib.bib27). It uses
token-level KL regularisation to penalise deviations from a reference
policy, along with different learning-rate warm-up procedures for the
attacker and defender. Implementation details are provided in the next
section and
Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 ABS Setup ‣ Appendix C Experimental Setup ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play").
The attacker and defender are then trained using separate objectives:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle J_{A}(\phi_{A}|\phi_{D})=\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{A}},\,y_{D}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{D}}(\cdot\mid y_{A})}\big[R_{A}(y_{A},y_{D})\big],`$ |  | (3) |
|  | $`\displaystyle J_{D}(\phi_{D}|\phi_{A})=\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{A}},\,y_{D}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{D}}(\cdot\mid y_{A})}\big[R_{D}(y_{A},y_{D})\big].`$ |  |  |

The opposing player’s parameters are fixed when computing gradients,
with policy-gradient updates:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi_{A}}J_{A}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\phi_{A}}\log\pi_{\theta,\phi_{A}}(y_{A})\,R_{A}(y_{A},y_{D})\right],`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi_{D}}J_{D}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla_{\phi_{D}}\log\pi_{\theta,\phi_{D}}(y_{D}\mid y_{A})\,R_{D}(y_{A},y_{D})\right].`$ |  |  |

These updates evolve separately and restrict optimisation to low-rank
subspaces over a shared frozen backbone. This parameter separation
restores the distinction between attacker and defender updates,
eliminating the parameter-level interference observed in
Eq. ([2](#S3.E2 "In 3.3 Min-Max Constraint Conflict and Self-Consistency ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"));
To formalise this, consider a defender-only update step
$`\phi_{D}^{+}=\phi_{D}+\eta\,\nabla_{\phi_{D}}J_{D}(\phi_{A},\phi_{D})`$,
where $`\eta>0`$ is the learning rate. A first-order Taylor expansion of
the defender’s objective yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle J_{D}(\phi_{A},\phi_{D}^{+})=J_{D}(\phi_{A},\phi_{D})+\eta\|\nabla_{\phi_{D}}J_{D}(\phi_{A},\phi_{D})\|^{2}+O(\eta^{2}).`$ |  | (5) |

Importantly, this dependence is purely distributional: the defender
parameters update independently of the attacker parameters, i.e.,
$`\frac{\partial\phi_{D}}{\partial\phi_{A}}=0.`$ We comprehensively
formalise this in Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Absence of Parameter-Level Coupling ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play").

## 5 Experiments & Results

To compare our ABS to the fully fine-tuned Self-RedTeam models by Liu et
al. [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1), we adapted their code to train two
independent LoRA modules, one for the attacker and one for the defender.
In their setting, the attacker is prompted with a seed from the training
partition of WildJailBreak [Jiang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib33) (see their
Appendix C.4.1), with a 50:50 ratio of harmful and benign prompts. The
prompt instructs the attacker and defender to first think, wrapped by
\<think\>\</think\> tokens, and then provide the answer between
\<answer\>\</answer\> tags. The defender produces its response in a
similar format; we conducted the evaluation on the pairs of attacks and
defences generated at each iteration. As in Self-RedTeam, we use
WildGuard [Han et al. (2024)](#bib.bib34) as the reward model, which
classifies the prompt and response harmfulness, and whether the response
is a refusal to answer the attack.

We trained Qwen2.5-{3B, 7B 14B}-IT models [Qwen et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib35) with different LoRA settings, varying the rank and
alpha values. The rank defines the hidden dimension of the LoRA
representation and thus the number of trainable parameters, while alpha
scales the influence of the adapter w.r.t. the base model.
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.1 ABS Safety and Efficiency Evaluation ‣ 5 Experiments & Results ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")

### 5.2 ABS Adversarial Strength and Defensive Robustness

In this section, we analysed the adversarial pressure that a trained
attacker can put on the defender. We compared the ASR (Attack Success
Rate) of ABS and Self-RedTeam checkpoints vs the same base model. We
sample $`100`$ examples from the Harmful Behavior Dataset [Jiang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib33), and we ask the attacker to produce a jailbreak
following the same format used at training time. The defender receives
the input and decides whether to comply or not with the request. We
evaluate the success of an attack with GPT-5.4 Standard with High
reasoning as an LLM-as-a-judge. We set strict prompt rules on the
success of an attack: a success is an answer that, for the judge, “gave
substantive harmful assistance” to the defender. As shown in
Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.2 ABS Adversarial Strength and Defensive Robustness ‣ 5 Experiments & Results ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"),
Self-RedTeam of Qwen2.5-3B-IT and 7B have an ASR of $`18\%`$ and $`9\%`$
respectively, while our corresponding ABS produce more effective
jailbreaks and achieve an ASR of $`35\%`$ and $`24\%`$.

We also conducted a “tournament” to evaluate the adversarial pressure
between our ABS models and Self-RedTeam. Over $`100`$ samples from the
Harmful Behavior Dataset, we cross-tested each methodology’s attacker
against the opposing defender at equivalent model scales. This analysis
provides a measure of the adversarial pressure that an attacker puts on
the other model’s defender. Similarly to the previous case, GPT-5.4 with
High reasoning judges the success of an attack. As reported in
Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.2 ABS Adversarial Strength and Defensive Robustness ‣ 5 Experiments & Results ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"),
Self-RedTeam Qwen2.5-3B-IT and 7B have both an ASR of $`6\%`$, while our
corresponding ABS produces more effective jailbreaks and achieves an ASR
of $`17\%`$ and $`18\%`$. In conclusion, the shared-model defender is
markedly more permissive than the LoRA defender.

We compared attacks generated by ABS and Self-RedTeam along three axes:
semantic similarity, lexical diversity, and structural complexity.
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 ABS Adversarial Strength and Defensive Robustness ‣ 5 Experiments & Results ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")
provides a quantitative overview of these differences. Self-RedTeam
produces shorter and more homogeneous attacks, as reflected by higher
Self-BLEU scores and lower token counts. In contrast, ABS generates
longer and lexically diverse outputs, particularly the 3B and 7B, where
token counts increase substantially, and Self-BLEU decreases. Despite
these surface-level differences, cosine similarity remains broadly
comparable across methods, suggesting that both approaches occupy
similar regions in embedding space
(Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.2 ABS Adversarial Strength and Defensive Robustness ‣ 5 Experiments & Results ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")
shows the UMAP embeddings for ABS and Self-RedTeam).

These quantitative trends correspond to clear stylistic differences.
Self-RedTeam attacks are typically direct reformulations of the harmful
intent: they are concise, imperative, and minimally elaborated. By
contrast, ABS tends to embed the request within richer contextual
scaffolding, often introducing multi-step scenarios, social engineering
elements, or concealment-oriented phrasing. This results in attacks that
are more elaborate but also less controlled, especially in the 3B model.
In that setting, ABS outputs sometimes include residual
meta-instructions, such as references to an “original prompt” or
“revised prompt”, suggesting weaker separation between
instruction-following and task narration; this likely reflects the
limited capacity of a LoRA module to enforce clean task boundaries.

Overall, the comparison reveals a trade-off between reliability and
elaboration. Self-RedTeam variants, particularly at 7B, are more
structurally consistent and produce cleaner attack spans. In contrast,
the 3B ABS variant is less disciplined but often generates longer and
more evasive formulations, indicating greater rhetorical complexity even
when formatting quality is degraded. Representative examples
(Appendix [C.4.1](#A3.SS4.SSS1 "C.4.1 A Qualitative Analysis of the Attacks ‣ C.4 Experimental Results ‣ Appendix C Experimental Setup ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")
and [C.5](#A3.SS5 "C.5 LoRA vs Finetuned Jailbreaks ‣ Appendix C Experimental Setup ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))

verbosity, as captured by Self-BLEU and token counts in
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 ABS Adversarial Strength and Defensive Robustness ‣ 5 Experiments & Results ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"),
appear to be the key factors underlying the success of ABS attacks.

### 5.3 Interfacing with Human Values: Fairness and Harmfulness

In this section, we explore how ABS performs at blocking specific human
harms, such as fairness or toxicity, without over-refusing benign
prompts. To understand this trade-off, we conducted an evaluation of our
model and the baselines using semantic sub-categories from WildGuard,
HarmBench, and XSTest. We show the comparison in
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.3 Interfacing with Human Values: Fairness and Harmfulness ‣ 5 Experiments & Results ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play").

Both ABS and Self-RedTeam demonstrate high effectiveness in suppressing
malicious content. ABS, in particular, achieves substantial safety
penetration against direct human harm without necessitating
full-parameter updates. For example, evaluation on the HarmBench
Harassment & Bullying semantic category indicates that the 3B and 14B
ABS models attain a 100% safe refusal rate. In WildGuard’s Violence &
Physical Harm and Toxicity categories, the ABS models consistently
maintain safety refusal rates above 82% and 78%, respectively, across
all model scales, with the 14B model exceeding 92% in both.

Under adversarial pressure, models frequently associate terms related to
protected demographics, such as race, gender, or religion, with harmful
attacks. This association results in excessive refusal of benign
requests, quantified by XSTest’s Discrimination Contrast and Benign
Compliance metrics. The analysis demonstrates a pronounced difference in
how Self-RedTeam and ABS architectures address fairness. Self-RedTeam
functions as a blunt instrument. Gradient collisions compel these models
to converge towards a middle ground, as discussed in

## 6 Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work

In this paper, we propose ABS, a model designed to overcome the
theoretical limitations of standard self-play in LLM safety training. By
freezing the base model and introducing two LoRA-based extensions, ABS
provides an efficient hybrid that separates self-play from gradient
updates. ABS achieves strong performance improvements over base LLMs
while significantly reducing computational cost.

Our evaluation across multiple model scales demonstrates that separating
the attacker and defender is crucial for effective adversarial training.
Both our theoretical analysis and empirical results indicate that
shared-parameter frameworks, such as Self-RedTeam, suffer from a
self-consistency bias that weakens the attacker. ABS outperforms
Self-RedTeam on Qwen2.5-3B and reaches comparable performance on larger
models, while consistently improving over the base model. In addition to
quantitative gains, ABS-trained attackers develop more sophisticated and
camouflaged strategies. The method is also highly efficient, achieving
up to two orders of magnitude better memory usage while preserving the
reasoning capabilities of the underlying model.

Despite these advantages, ABS exhibits some limitations. LoRA training
can occasionally become unstable, leading to collapse modes with
repetitive or nonsensical outputs, and larger models require careful
hyperparameter tuning to avoid such failures
(Appendix [C.4](#A3.SS4 "C.4 Experimental Results ‣ Appendix C Experimental Setup ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
Nevertheless, the efficiency of ABS makes it promising for deploying
safer yet useful models, particularly at smaller scales. Future work
will explore extensions to more complex threat settings, including
mosaic attacks and mixtures of attackers, as well as broader
applications of this game-theoretic framework to self-play in LLMs.

## Appendix

## Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium

Recall our assumption that the defender can always issue a refusal
response $`y^{\mathrm{ref}}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}`$ such that
$`r(y_{A},y^{\mathrm{ref}})=0`$ for all $`y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}`$.
Thus, for any prompt $`y_{A}`$, we define the always-refuse defender
strategy as
$`\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{D}\mid y_{A})=\mathbf{1}\{y_{D}=y^{\mathrm{ref}}\}`$,
which yields an expected reward $`V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{ref}})=0`$
for any attacker policy $`\pi_{A}`$.

A pair $`(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{ref}})`$ constitutes a Nash
equilibrium if the attacker’s policy $`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$ is sufficiently
adversarial such that no defender policy guarantees a strictly positive
expected payoff, i.e., $`\max_{\pi_{D}}V(\pi_{A}^{\ast},\pi_{D})=0`$.
Therefore, suppose the attacker’s policy space consists of probability
distributions in a localised neighbourhood around a reference policy
$`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$, representing baseline adversarial behaviour already
optimised to challenge the defender \[[27](#bib.bib28)\]. In particular,
we assume that $`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$ concentrates on prompts for which safe
responses yield no positive reward, so that the defender cannot improve
upon refusal. This constraint captures the intuition that, while attack
policies may adapt to minimize utility, they should remain sufficiently
close to a reference adversarial behaviour to preserve semantic
consistency and interpretability \[[4](#bib.bib31)\]. In practice, this
can be enforced by initialising the agent with a fine-tuned model
embodying $`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$, and constraining updates to remain within a
trust region \[[25](#bib.bib29), [40](#bib.bib32)\]. Specifically, for a
fixed $`\delta\in[0,1]`$ we define the local policy
$`\delta`$-neighbourhood as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pi_{A}^{\delta}=\left\{\pi_{A}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{A}):D_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{A}\|\bar{\pi}_{A})\leq\delta\right\},
``` |  | (6) |

where $`D_{\text{TV}}(\cdot\|\cdot)`$ denotes the total variation
distance, given as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{A}\|\bar{\pi}_{A})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}}|\pi_{A}(y_{A})-\bar{\pi}_{A}(y_{A})|
``` |  | (7) |

Although trust-region methods are commonly defined using KL
divergence \[[27](#bib.bib28)\], we use total variation distance in the
analysis, since it directly bounds differences in expectations of
bounded functions. Specifically, in Lemma
[1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1. ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")
we show that, within $`\Pi_{A}^{\delta}`$, the defender’s best
achievable expected payoff changes by a controlled amount (see the full
proof in Appendix
[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
In contrast, KL divergence only provides such control indirectly,
resulting in weaker bounds (see Appendix
[A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Discussion: Using KL-Divergence for Local Policy Neighbourhoods ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")
for further details).

###### Lemma 1.

For any attacker policies
$`\pi_{A},{\pi}_{A}^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{A})`$ and any
defender policy $`\pi_{D}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left|V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})-V({\pi}_{A}^{\prime},\pi_{D})\right|\leq 2D_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{A}\|{\pi}_{A}^{\prime}).
``` |  | (8) |

In particular, if $`\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}`$ and
$`\pi_{A}\in\Pi_{A}^{\delta}`$ for some fixed $`\varepsilon\in[0,1]`$,
then:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\pi_{D}}V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})\leq\max_{\pi_{D}}V(\bar{\pi}_{A},\pi_{D})+\varepsilon.
``` |  | (9) |

Lemma
[1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1. ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")
and Appendix
[A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Discussion: Using KL-Divergence for Local Policy Neighbourhoods ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")
show that, in either a total-variation or KL divergence neighbourhood of
the reference policy $`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$, the defender’s best achievable
payoff changes only by a controlled amount. As $`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$
represents a baseline adversarial behaviour, it is natural to restrict
attention to local perturbations that preserve this property. We thus
focus on attacker policies $`\pi_{A}^{\ast}\in\Pi_{A}^{\delta}`$ that
preserve the adversarial property of $`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$ locally, in the
sense that they do not admit any defender strategy with strictly
positive expected payoff. Formally, we require that for any alternative
defender strategy $`\pi_{D}^{\prime}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{A}^{\ast},y_{D}\sim\pi_{D}^{\prime}}[r(y_{A},y_{D})]\leq 0.
``` |  | (10) |

This condition can be interpreted as a worst-case constraint on the
attacker: within the local policy class $`\Pi_{A}^{\delta}`$, any
attempt by the defender to provide a helpful response yields no positive
gain and may incur risk, so the defender has no incentive to deviate
from $`y^{\mathrm{ref}}`$. Specifically, providing a helpful response
risks a payoff of $`-1`$ that is not offset by safe, high-reward
completions, making the expected value of answering non-positive. Hence,
$`\pi_{D}^{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is a best response to
$`\pi_{A}^{\ast}\in\Pi_{A}^{\delta}`$. Moreover, since the attacker
cannot reduce the payoff below $`0`$ against a refusing defender,
$`\pi_{A}^{\ast}`$ is also a best response.

### A.1 Proof of Lemma [1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1. ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")

###### Lemma [1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1. ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play").

For any attacker policies
$`\pi_{A},{\pi}_{A}^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{A})`$ and any
defender policy $`\pi_{D}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left|V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})-V({\pi}_{A}^{\prime},\pi_{D})\right|\leq 2D_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{A}\|{\pi}_{A}^{\prime}).
``` |  | (11) |

In particular, if $`\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}`$ and
$`\pi_{A}\in\Pi_{A}^{\delta}`$ for some fixed $`\varepsilon\in[0,1]`$,
then:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\pi_{D}}V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})\leq\max_{\pi_{D}}V(\bar{\pi}_{A},\pi_{D})+\varepsilon.
``` |  | (12) |

###### Proof.

We start with proving
([11](#A1.E11 "In Lemma . ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
Consider any attacker policies
$`\pi_{A},{\pi}_{A}^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{A})`$ and any
defender policy $`\pi_{D}`$. By definition, the following holds:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{A},\;y_{D}\sim\pi_{D}(\cdot\mid y_{A})}\left[r(y_{A},y_{D})\right]`$ |  | (13) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}}\pi_{A}(y_{A})\sum_{y_{D}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}}\pi_{D}(y_{D}|y_{A})\cdot r(y_{A},y_{D}).`$ |  |  |

By similar arguments:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle V(\pi_{A}^{\prime},\pi_{D})=\sum_{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}}\pi_{A}^{\prime}(y_{A})\sum_{y_{D}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}}\pi_{D}(y_{D}|y_{A})\cdot r(y_{A},y_{D}).`$ |  | (14) |

By combining
([13](#A1.E13 "In Proof. ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
and
([14](#A1.E14 "In Proof. ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")),
we obtain that:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  |  |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\left|V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})-V({\pi}_{A}^{\prime},\pi_{D})\right|`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}}\sum_{y_{D}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}}\pi_{D}(y_{D}|y_{A})\cdot r(y_{A},y_{D})(\pi_{A}(y_{A})-\pi_{A}^{\prime}(y_{A}))\right|`$ |  | (15a) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}}\sum_{y_{D}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}}|\pi_{D}(y_{D}|y_{A})|\cdot|r(y_{A},y_{D})|\left|\pi_{A}(y_{A})-\pi_{A}^{\prime}(y_{A})\right|`$ |  | (15b) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}}\left|\pi_{A}(y_{A})-\pi_{A}^{\prime}(y_{A})\right|\sum_{y_{D}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}}\pi_{D}(y_{D}|y_{A})`$ |  | (15c) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}}\left|\pi_{A}(y_{A})-\pi_{A}^{\prime}(y_{A})\right|.`$ |  | (15d) |

where the inequality in
([15b](#A1.E15.2 "In 15 ‣ Proof. ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
follows from the triangle inequality. Furthermore, the inequality in
([15c](#A1.E15.3 "In 15 ‣ Proof. ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
holds because $`|\pi_{D}(y_{D}|y_{A})|=\pi_{D}(y_{D}|y_{A})`$ for any
$`y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A},y_{D}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}`$ since
$`\pi_{D}(y_{D}|y_{A})\in[0,1]`$, and $`|r(y_{A},y_{D})|\leq 1`$ due to
our assumption in Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Nash Equilibria in Self-RedTeam ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")
that $`r(y_{A},y_{D})\in[-1,1]`$ for all
$`y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A},y_{D}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}`$. Finally, the last
equality in
([15d](#A1.E15.4 "In 15 ‣ Proof. ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
stems from $`\sum_{y_{D}\in\mathcal{Y}_{D}}\pi_{D}(y_{D}|y_{A})=1`$ for
any $`y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}`$ due to
$`\pi_{D}(\cdot|y_{A})\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{D})`$.

Subsequently, we relate the term in
([15d](#A1.E15.4 "In 15 ‣ Proof. ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
to the total variation distance. Indeed, since
$`D_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{A}\|{\pi}_{A}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}}\left|\pi_{A}(y_{A})-\pi_{A}^{\prime}(y_{A})\right|`$
by definition, we conclude that:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left|V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})-V({\pi}_{A}^{\prime},\pi_{D})\right|\leq 2D_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{A}\|{\pi}_{A}^{\prime}),
``` |  | (16) |

as desired.

Next, we prove the second part of our claim in
([12](#A1.E12 "In Lemma . ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
For any fixed $`\delta\in[0,1]`$, consider any attacker policy
$`\pi_{A}\in\Pi_{A}^{\delta}`$. That is,
$`D_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{A}\|\bar{\pi}_{A})\leq\delta`$, where
$`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$ is the reference policy defined in Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Analysis of Extremal Nash Equilibria ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"),
representing baseline adversarial behaviour already optimised to
challenge the defender. Therefore, combined with
([16](#A1.E16 "In Proof. ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")),
we obtain:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left|V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})-V({\pi}_{A}^{\prime},\pi_{D})\right|\leq 2\delta.
``` |  | (17) |

As such, if $`\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}`$ for any fixed
$`\varepsilon\in[0,1]`$, the desired in
([12](#A1.E12 "In Lemma . ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
readily follows from
([17](#A1.E17 "In Proof. ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).

### A.2 Discussion: Using KL-Divergence for Local Policy Neighbourhoods

In this section, we discuss the consequences of defining the local
policy neighbourhood in
([6](#A1.E6 "In Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
using the KL divergence instead of the total variation distance. Indeed,
for a fixed $`\delta\in[0,1]`$ we define the local policy
$`\delta`$-neighbourhood using the KL divergence as follows:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pi_{A}^{\delta,\text{KL}}=\left\{\pi_{A}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{A}):D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{A}\|\bar{\pi}_{A})\leq\delta\right\},
``` |  | (18) |

where $`D_{\text{KL}}(\cdot\|\cdot)`$ denotes the KL divergence, which
is given by:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{A}\|\bar{\pi}_{A})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}}\pi_{A}(y_{A})\log\left(\frac{\pi_{A}(y_{A})}{\bar{\pi}_{A}(y_{A})}\right)
``` |  | (19) |

While using the total variation distance directly bounds differences in
expectations of bounded functions due to Lemma
[1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1. ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"),
we next prove that the KL divergence only provides such control
indirectly, resulting in weaker bounds.

###### Lemma 2.

For any attacker policies
$`\pi_{A},{\pi}_{A}^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{A})`$ and any
defender policy $`\pi_{D}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left|V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})-V({\pi}_{A}^{\prime},\pi_{D})\right|\leq\sqrt{2D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{A}\|{\pi}_{A}^{\prime})}.
``` |  | (20) |

In particular, if $`\delta=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}`$ and
$`\pi_{A}\in\Pi_{A}^{\delta}`$ for some fixed $`\varepsilon\in[0,1]`$,
then:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\pi_{D}}V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})\leq\max_{\pi_{D}}V(\bar{\pi}_{A},\pi_{D})+\varepsilon.
``` |  | (21) |

###### Proof.

The proof easily follows from Lemma
[1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1. ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play").
Indeed, we start with proving
([20](#A1.E20 "In Lemma 2. ‣ A.2 Discussion: Using KL-Divergence for Local Policy Neighbourhoods ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
Consider any attacker policies
$`\pi_{A},{\pi}_{A}^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}_{A})`$ and any
defender policy $`\pi_{D}`$. By Pinsker’s
inequality \[[8](#bib.bib30)\], it holds that:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{A}\|{\pi}_{A}^{\prime})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{A}\|{\pi}_{A}^{\prime})}.
``` |  | (22) |

Therefore, by
([20](#A1.E20 "In Lemma 2. ‣ A.2 Discussion: Using KL-Divergence for Local Policy Neighbourhoods ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
from Lemma
[1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1. ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"),
we conclude the desired in
([20](#A1.E20 "In Lemma 2. ‣ A.2 Discussion: Using KL-Divergence for Local Policy Neighbourhoods ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).

Next, we prove the second part of our claim in
([21](#A1.E21 "In Lemma 2. ‣ A.2 Discussion: Using KL-Divergence for Local Policy Neighbourhoods ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
For any fixed $`\delta\in[0,1]`$, consider any attacker policy
$`\pi_{A}\in\Pi_{A}^{\delta,\text{KL}}`$. That is,
$`D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{A}\|\bar{\pi}_{A})\leq\delta`$, where
$`\bar{\pi}_{A}`$ is the reference policy defined in Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Analysis of Extremal Nash Equilibria ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"),
representing baseline adversarial behaviour already optimised to
challenge the defender. Therefore, combined with
([20](#A1.E20 "In Lemma 2. ‣ A.2 Discussion: Using KL-Divergence for Local Policy Neighbourhoods ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")),
we obtain:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left|V(\pi_{A},\pi_{D})-V({\pi}_{A}^{\prime},\pi_{D})\right|\leq\sqrt{2\delta}.
``` |  | (23) |

As such, if $`\delta=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}`$ for any fixed
$`\varepsilon\in[0,1]`$, the desired in
([12](#A1.E12 "In Lemma . ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
readily follows from
([17](#A1.E17 "In Proof. ‣ A.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix A More Details on Bound 1: An always-refuse equilibrium ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).

## Appendix B Absence of Parameter-Level Coupling

As noted in Section
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Role-conditioned LoRA Policies ‣ 4 Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"),
the updates in
Eq. ([4](#S4.E4 "In 4.1 Role-conditioned LoRA Policies ‣ 4 Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play"))
evolve separately and restrict optimisation to low-rank subspaces over a
shared frozen backbone. This parameter separation restores the
distinction between attacker and defender updates, eliminating the
parameter-level interference observed in
Eq. ([2](#S3.E2 "In 3.3 Min-Max Constraint Conflict and Self-Consistency ‣ 3 Nash Equilibria, Model Safety, and Self-Play ‣ The Attacker in the Mirror: Breaking Self-Consistency in Safety via Anchored Bipolicy Self-Play")).
To formalise this, consider a defender-only update step

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\phi_{D}^{+}=\phi_{D}+\eta\,\nabla_{\phi_{D}}J_{D}(\phi_{A},\phi_{D}),
``` |  |

where $`\eta>0`$ is the learning rate. A first-order Taylor expansion of
the defender’s objective yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle J_{D}(\phi_{A},\phi_{D}^{+})=J_{D}(\phi_{A},\phi_{D})+\eta\|\nabla_{\phi_{D}}J_{D}(\phi_{A},\phi_{D})\|^{2}+O(\eta^{2}).`$ |  | (24) |

Although this update is taken only with respect to $`\phi_{D}`$, the
gradient $`\nabla_{\phi_{D}}J_{D}`$ depends on the attacker through the
sampling processes $`y_{A}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{A}}(\cdot)`$ and
$`y_{D}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{D}}(\cdot\mid y_{A})`$, which induce a
dependence of the objective on the distribution of attacker-generated
prompts. This can be made explicit as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi_{D}}J_{D}=\mathbb{E}_{y_{A}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{A}}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{y_{D}\sim\pi_{\theta,\phi_{D}}(\cdot\mid y_{A})}\big[\nabla_{\phi_{D}}\log\pi_{\theta,\phi_{D}}(y_{D}\mid y_{A})\,R_{D}(y_{A},y_{D})\big]\Big].`$ |  | (25) |

Importantly, this dependence is purely distributional: the defender
parameters are updated independently of the attacker parameters, i.e.,

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{\partial\phi_{D}}{\partial\phi_{A}}=0.
       ```                                           |     |

Thus, the attacker influences the defender only through the induced
input distribution $`y_{A}`$, rather than via shared or differentiable
parameters. The same reasoning applies symmetrically to the attacker.

### C.3 ABS Setup

For each ABS model, we train low-rank adapters on the projection layers
q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, and o_proj in the self-attention block, as well
as up_proj, gate_proj, and down_proj in the feed-forward network. The
attention projections map hidden states into query, key, and value
representations that determine token interactions, and then project the
aggregated attention output back into the model’s hidden space. The
feed-forward projections expand the representation into a
higher-dimensional space, apply a gating mechanism to modulate features,
and compress it back to the original dimension. Together, these layers
control how information is routed and transformed throughout the model,
making them effective targets for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. For
each implementation, we kept the value of the rank and alpha equal, and
set up a learning rate for the attacker and the defender of 1e-6 and
3e-6, a KL region of $`0.3`$: we trained each module for up to
$`100-200`$ steps.

##### GPT 5.4 response.

These files keep the defender fixed and change the attacker, so the
relevant qualitative question is why the LoRA checkpoint is a stronger
*attacker* against the same base defender. The same-defender comparisons
already show that the effect is consistent across scales: on 3B, the
LoRA checkpoint reaches a 35% attack success rate against the base
model, compared with 18% for the fully fine-tuned model; on 7B, the
corresponding rates are 24% and 9%.

The main reason is that the LoRA checkpoint is better at producing
prompts that are both adversarial and usable. On 3B, especially, the
fully fine-tuned attacker often fails at the task itself: instead of
emitting a concrete jailbreak, it emits meta-instructions such as “make
the prompt more detailed” or “let’s refine the prompt,” which means the
defender is not actually forced to answer the harmful request. By
contrast, the 3B LoRA checkpoint usually produces a complete prompt with
an explicit target, a concrete framing, and enough detail to steer the
defender into a substantive continuation. In other words, part of the 3B
gap is not just better stealth; it is basic task completion.

The second reason is better camouflage. The LoRA checkpoint is much more
likely to wrap the harmful objective inside a benign-looking frame:
wellness advice, ethical hacking, red-teaming, documentary analysis,
fictional scenarios, or research language. This works because the base
defender has a strong “helpful rewriter” mode. Once the request is
framed as analysis, training, safety, or support, the defender often
continues the prompt rather than rejecting the underlying intent. The
fully fine-tuned model sometimes uses this strategy, but much less
consistently, and usually with less detail.

The third reason is that the LoRA checkpoint tends to preserve a harmful
payload while softening the surface form. Its prompts are longer, more
specific, and more targeted. They do not merely say “do the harmful
thing in a subtler way”; they specify a voice, a scenario, a
justification, and a rhetorical frame that nudges the defender toward
compliance. This matters because the defender is more vulnerable to
requests that look like legitimate content transformation than to short,
direct requests that are obviously malicious.

There is also a scale-dependent difference between 3B and 7B. At 3B, the
fine-tuned model’s main weakness is degeneration into meta-talk and
partial outputs. At 7B, that problem is much smaller: the fully
fine-tuned model usually does produce a coherent rewritten prompt.
However, its prompts are often too short, too direct, or too shallowly
disguised, so the defender still recognises the malicious goal and
refuses. The 7B LoRA checkpoint is qualitatively cleaner: it uses more
polished masking strategies, more role framing, and more dual-use
language, which lets it exploit the defender’s helpfulness more
effectively even when both models are coherent.

In short, the LoRA checkpoint appears stronger for two different reasons
at the two scales. On 3B, it is stronger partly because it actually
produces valid jailbreak prompts more reliably. On 7B, it is stronger
because it is better at adversarial prompt design: it hides the intent
behind plausible, high-context, dual-use framing while keeping enough
specificity to elicit operationally harmful continuations.

 

### C.5 LoRA vs Finetuned Jailbreaks

##### Example 1: 3B self-harm prompt.

The 3B LoRA checkpoint rewrites a self-harm request into a
mental-health-themed blog-post prompt that uses supportive language and

##### Example 2: 7B eating-disorder prompt.

The 7B LoRA checkpoint reframes the target as a blog post about “mindful
eating” and “personal freedom,” then embeds the harmful idea indirectly
inside wellness language. The defender responds with a long blog post
that substantially accepts that framing. The fully fine-tuned 7B model

restrictive eating to be framed as self-discipline; that version is
recognised and refused. This is a clean example of the LoRA checkpoint

##### Example 3: 7B self-harm prompt.

The 7B LoRA checkpoint asks for a social-media post from the perspective
of a distressed character and uses euphemistic phrases such as finding

##### Example 4: 7B explosive-device prompt.

simply indirectness, but whether the indirect framing is plausible

##### Example 5: 7B red-team script prompt.

the “ethical security assistance” frame. This is especially interesting
because it shows that the LoRA checkpoint is not only good at emotional
or rhetorical masking; it also understands how to wrap cyber abuse
inside a familiar professional genre that the defender is predisposed to
help with.

###### Abstract

Recently developed remote sensing (RS) agents execute real-world tasks
with complex workflows through multi-step tool invocation. These agents,
however, typically rely on predefined expertise or parameter updates.
They thus require updating expert knowledge or retraining models
whenever new tools, tasks, or execution patterns emerge, limiting their
continual adaptability in dynamic Earth environments. In this work, we
seek to equip general-purpose agents with RS tool-call knowledge that
can be automatically extracted from demonstrated trajectories without
expert intervention or parameter updates. Specifically, we propose
SkillEarth, a self-evolving skill extraction method that externalizes RS
tool-call knowledge as reusable, retrievable, and evolvable skill files
and refines them through observation-grounded evolution. Unlike existing
automatic skill extraction methods that rely on outcome-based reflection
to refine skills holistically, SkillEarth grounds skill evolution in
real tool-chain execution and combines dual-judge feedback with a
grounded observation routing graph for precise component-level
refinement. The resulting skills are generalizable, reliable, and
transferable: they improve overall RS tool-call performance across three
frontier LLMs and four open-source LLMs, are refined through real tool
execution feedback, and support cross-model reuse without retraining.
Experiments on OpenEarth-Bench and Earth-Bench demonstrate that
SkillEarth achieves better tool selection, argument correctness,
execution-order consistency, and task completion than existing RS agents
and skill extraction methods.

## 1 Introduction

Real-world remote sensing (RS) tasks typically rely on complex workflows
that coordinate multiple interdependent steps. For example, disaster
response often involves mapping damage, assessing affected areas, and
then delivering actionable information \[[30](#bib.bib1)\]. In practice,
these steps require collaboration across multiple departments, making
the overall workflow time-consuming and inefficient \[[3](#bib.bib2)\].
Therefore, how to simplify such cumbersome workflows and enable
automated task execution remains an important question.

Against this backdrop, recent studies explore multi-modal large language
models (MLLMs) for automating RS tasks. Early efforts focus on
fine-tuning RS-specialized MLLMs for image
interpretation \[[9](#bib.bib23), [41](#bib.bib9)\], visual question
answering \[[39](#bib.bib19), [41](#bib.bib9)\], and scene
understanding \[[11](#bib.bib11), [18](#bib.bib13)\]. Despite their
effectiveness, these methods support only a few predefined tasks with
single-step execution. More recently, tool-augmented RS agents have
emerged to automate complex, multi-step workflows by leveraging diverse
RS tools. A central challenge in developing such agents is how to equip
MLLMs with the domain knowledge required for reliable RS tool use. To
address this, recent methods explore expert
knowledge \[[35](#bib.bib15), [4](#bib.bib6), [12](#bib.bib7)\],
external memory \[[5](#bib.bib12), [33](#bib.bib16)\], or model
fine-tuning \[[26](#bib.bib14), [17](#bib.bib36)\]. However, adapting
these methods to new tools and tasks requires continual expert updates,
memory maintenance, or model retraining, making them difficult to adapt
efficiently to dynamic earth observation environments. This raises the
central question of this work: Can a general-purpose agent acquire
reliable, reusable, and transferable RS tool-call knowledge without
model retraining or expert intervention?

In this paper, we externalize RS tool-call knowledge as textual files,
termed skills, that general-purpose agents can retrieve and execute.
Recent studies extract such skills from documents, dialogues, or manuals
to reduce expert authoring \[[21](#bib.bib4), [36](#bib.bib5),
[31](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib21), [15](#bib.bib17)\]. As shown in
Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(a),
some methods \[[31](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib21), [15](#bib.bib17)\]
follow an extract-execute-decide paradigm: an _Extractor_ distills
candidate skills, an _Executor_ refines them through execution and
reflection, and a _Decider_ adds, merges, or discards them. However,
these methods rely on outcome-based reflection, which provides limited
evidence about where failures occur and why. Moreover, holistic
refinement makes skill revision coarse-grained and hard to control. This
limits their use in RS workflows, where a skill must specify tool
selection, required and optional arguments, and, crucially, how outputs
from one tool bind to inputs of another. We therefore advance
outcome-based reflection into observation-grounded evolution. As shown
in
Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(b),
we represent each skill as multiple independently evolvable components
and replace the conventional _Executor_ with an _Evolver_. The _Evolver_
applies the extracted skill to its corresponding query, while a _tool
judge_ verifies tool calls and a _semantic judge_ evaluates the final
answer. For each successful trajectory, it further builds a grounded
observation routing (GOR) graph, whose nodes denote tool invocations and
edges capture how tool outputs bind to arguments of downstream tools.
Using the execution results and GOR graph, the _Refiner_ localizes
evidence to relevant components and selectively revises them, turning
holistic rewriting into controllable component-level evolution.

To this end, we propose SkillEarth, a self-evolving skill extraction
method that automatically extracts RS tool-call skills from demonstrated
trajectories and refines them based on execution-grounded feedback.
Specifically, the _Extractor_ independently extracts each skill
component. The _Evolver_ iteratively executes the extracted skill in a
real tool environment, incorporating error diagnoses from tool and
semantic judges (e.g., incorrect capitalization or unsupported tool
aliases) into the LLM’s system prompt to guide next attempts. Once a
valid trajectory is obtained, SkillEarth constructs a GOR graph to
capture observation routing across tool calls. The _Refiner_ then
leverages this graph and execution feedback to identify and revise
corresponding components. Finally, the _Decider_ determines whether to
add, merge, or discard each skill based on its semantics, task type, and
tool-call details. The resulting skills generalize reliably across
different LLMs (three frontier and four open-source models), improving
RS task performance without parameter updates. They are also
transferable across models: skills extracted by one LLM can guide
another LLM.
Fig. [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")
illustrates how the extracted skills guide multi-step tool execution and
highlights the superior End-to-End performance of SkillEarth compared
with existing methods. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  We propose SkillEarth, a self-evolving skill extraction method that
  automatically extracts and evolves reusable RS tool-call skills from
  demonstrated trajectories without expert intervention or parameter
  updates.
- •
  We propose the observation-grounded evolution, integrating dual-judge
  feedback and GOR graphs for precise failure localization and targeted
  component-level refinement.
- •
  Extensive experiments on OpenEarth-Bench \[[26](#bib.bib14)\] and
  Earth-Bench \[[8](#bib.bib8)\] demonstrate the generalizability,
  reliability, and cross-model transferability of the extracted skills.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 MLLM-Based Remote Sensing Models

Early RS models relied on task-specific architectures for image
classification \[[29](#bib.bib24)\], object
detection \[[16](#bib.bib25)\], semantic
segmentation \[[13](#bib.bib26)\] and image
generation \[[28](#bib.bib37)\]. Recent studies employ MLLMs as unified
frameworks for RS vision-language understanding, including visual
question answering \[[9](#bib.bib23)\], multi-sensor
understanding \[[41](#bib.bib9)\], scene
understanding \[[11](#bib.bib11)\], and instruction
following \[[39](#bib.bib19)\]. However, these models mainly support
predefined tasks with single-step inference and cannot coordinate
heterogeneous operations required by complex RS workflows. In contrast,
SkillEarth encodes RS tool-calling knowledge into skill files, enabling
general-purpose agents to execute multi-step workflows involving
perception, spectral analysis, and GIS processing.

### 2.2 Tool-Augmented Remote Sensing Agents

Tool-augmented agents use external tools for multi-step reasoning and
execution \[[38](#bib.bib27), [27](#bib.bib28)\]. Building on this
paradigm, existing RS agents incorporate expert knowledge for tool
planning \[[35](#bib.bib15), [4](#bib.bib6)\], memory for experience
refinement \[[5](#bib.bib12), [33](#bib.bib16)\], or supervised
fine-tuning on execution trajectories \[[26](#bib.bib14)\]. Several
benchmarks evaluate their capabilities, including
ThinkGeo \[[25](#bib.bib20)\] for spatial reasoning and tool use,
Earth-Bench \[[8](#bib.bib8)\] for cross-modal tasks under planning and
instruction-following settings, GeoMMBench \[[34](#bib.bib39)\] for
multimodal geoscience reasoning, and
OpenEarth-Bench \[[26](#bib.bib14)\] for large-scale tool-call
evaluation. Despite these advances, adapting existing RS agents to new
tools, tasks, or execution patterns requires expert maintenance, memory
updates, or model retraining. In contrast, SkillEarth externalizes RS
tool-call knowledge as evolvable skill files that can be automatically
extracted, selectively refined, and transferred across LLMs without
fine-tuning.

### 2.3 Skill Learning and Evolution for Agents

Skills enable agents to accumulate reusable procedural knowledge. Early
works such as Voyager \[[32](#bib.bib22)\] store acquired skills as
executable python code, while ExpeL \[[42](#bib.bib10)\] extracts
reusable workflows or experience from past tasks. More recently,
Anthropic introduced the Agent Skills standard, representing skills as
portable file-based packages \[[1](#bib.bib3)\]. Building on these
foundations, subsequent studies explore automated skill learning and
evolution. EvoDS \[[37](#bib.bib40)\] integrates skill learning with
context management, MUSE-Autoskill \[[14](#bib.bib41)\] supports skill
lifecycle management, and CoEvoSkills \[[40](#bib.bib42)\] employs
co-evolutionary verification. Anything2Skill \[[21](#bib.bib4)\]
constructs skills from heterogeneous knowledge,
AutoSkill \[[36](#bib.bib5)\] maintains skills from dialogues, and
SkillX \[[31](#bib.bib18)\], Trace2Skill \[[19](#bib.bib21)\], and
SkillRevise \[[15](#bib.bib17)\] refine skills through execution
feedback. Despite these advances, existing methods often treat skills as
monolithic text artifacts and rely on outcome-based feedback, providing
limited insight into inter-tool dependencies in complex RS workflows. In
contrast, SkillEarth grounds skill evolution in actual tool-chain
observations, combining dual-judge feedback with a GOR graph to
explicitly capture explicit inter-tool dependencies and enable
component-level refinement.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Component-wise Skill Extraction

Existing skill extraction methods \[[21](#bib.bib4), [36](#bib.bib5),
[31](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib21), [15](#bib.bib17)\] usually represent
a skill as a monolithic artifact, making refinement coarse-grained and
difficult to control: correcting one error may inadvertently corrupt
previously correct content. As shown in
Fig. [3](#S1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(a),
to enable controllable evolution, we decompose a skill into
independently editable components:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       s=\bigl(n,d,w,p,r,\ell\bigr),
       ```                            |     | (1) |

where $`n`$ and $`d`$ denote the skill name and description, $`w`$
specifies the activation condition, $`p`$ defines the execution plan,
$`r`$ captures observation-to-argument dependencies, and $`\ell`$
records the required tools and their arguments. Each component is
maintained as a Python object and written into the skill file only in
the final rendering stage. Given a sample

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\xi_{i}=(q_{i},\tau_{i},y_{i},c_{i}),\quad\tau_{i}=\{(a_{i,t},o_{i,t})\}_{t=1}^{T_{i}},
``` |  | (2) |

where $`q_{i}`$, $`y_{i}`$, and $`c_{i}`$ denote the query, reference
answer, and task type, respectively. $`\tau_{i}`$ represents the
tool-call trajectory consisting of $`T_{i}`$ sequential pairs of tool
invocations $`a_{i,t}`$ and their corresponding observations
$`o_{i,t}`$. Each tool invocation is represented as
$`a_{i,t}=(u_{i,t},p_{i,t})`$, where $`u_{i,t}`$ denotes the invoked
tool name and $`p_{i,t}`$ denotes its corresponding parameters. We first
construct a template skill using a deterministic extraction function
$`\mathcal{H}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{i}^{(0)}=\mathcal{H}(\xi_{i})=\bigl(n_{i}^{(0)},d_{i}^{(0)},w_{i}^{(0)},p_{i}^{(0)},r_{i}^{(0)},\ell_{i}\bigr).
``` |  | (3) |

Here, $`\ell_{i}`$ is deterministically extracted from the trajectory
and fixed during refinement, while the remaining components are
editable. The template provides the skill structure but only heuristic
semantics. An *Extractor* therefore independently infers each editable
component:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{s}^{(1)}_{i}=\pi_{\theta}(s_{i}^{(0)},\xi_{i};\textbf{P}_{\textrm{ext.}})=\{n_{i}^{(1)},d_{i}^{(1)},w_{i}^{(1)},p_{i}^{(1)},r_{i}^{(1)}\},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is a frozen general-purpose LLM and
$`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{ext.}}`$ is the extraction prompt. The extracted
components are then written back into the template:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{i}^{(1)}=\textrm{Overwrite}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(s_{i}^{(0)},\hat{s}^{(1)}_{i}\right),\mathcal{F}=\{n,d,w,p,r\},
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\mathcal{F}`$ denotes the set of editable components. The
resulting $`s_{i}^{(1)}`$ serves as the initialization for subsequent
skill evolution. Unlike direct trajectory-to-skill extraction, this
template-and-overwrite design combines deterministic structure with
LLM-based semantic abstraction, enabling more reliable and controllable
skill extraction.

### 3.2 Observation-Grounded Skill Evolution

The initial skill $`s_{i}^{(1)}`$ provides a reusable solution
hypothesis, but its planning may be overly specific and its tool-output
dependency descriptions may be unclear, as illustrated in
Fig. [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.2 Tool-Augmented Remote Sensing Agents ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(a).
We therefore evolve each skill through observation-grounded execution.
The *Evolver* separately verifies (i) trajectory validity, (ii)
final-answer semantics, and (iii) inter-tool dependencies, grounding
each revision in execution evidence rather than outcome-only reflection.

As shown in
Fig. [3](#S1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(b),
given $`s_{i}^{(1)}`$ and query $`q_{i}`$, an *executor*
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot;\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{exe.}})`$ generates one action
at a time, where $`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{exe.}}`$ is execution system
prompt. We use $`r\in\{1,\ldots,n_{s}\}`$ for semantic-judge rounds and
$`k\in\{1,\ldots,n_{t}\}`$ for tool-judge rounds within each semantic
round. At step $`t`$, the *executor* predicts a tool call conditioned on
the query, skill, accepted execution history, and accumulated repair
feedback:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{a}_{i,t}^{(r,k)}=\pi_{\theta}\!\left(q_{i},s_{i}^{(1)},\hat{\tau}_{i,<t}^{(r,k)},\Phi_{i}^{(r,k)};\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{exe.}}\right),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\hat{\tau}_{i,<t}^{(r,k)}`$ denotes the successfully executed
trajectory before step $`t`$, and $`\Phi_{i}^{(r,k)}`$ denotes
accumulated validation feedback. This process yields a candidate
trajectory $`\hat{\tau}_{i}^{(r,k)}`$ and, when applicable, a final
answer $`\hat{y}_{i}^{(r,k)}`$.

Trajectory-level tool validation. We first validate each tool-call using
a cloud-based *tool judge*
$`\pi_{\theta_{c}}^{c}(\cdot;\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{tool.}})`$ initialized
with the system prompt $`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{tool.}}`$. Rather than
evaluating skill text, it checks whether the required tools, execution
order, and invocation multiplicity are valid given query $`q_{i}`$,
reference trajectory $`\tau_{i}`$, candidate trajectory
$`\hat{\tau}_{i}^{(r,k)}`$, and registered tool contracts
$`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left(b_{i,r,k}^{\mathrm{tool}},\phi_{i,r,k}^{\mathrm{tool}}\right)=\pi_{\theta_{c}}^{c}\!\left(q_{i},\tau_{i},\hat{\tau}_{i}^{(r,k)},\mathcal{C}_{i};\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{tool.}}\right),
``` |  | (7) |

where $`b_{i,r,k}^{\mathrm{tool}}\in\{0,1\}`$ is the tool-sequence
verdict, and $`\phi_{i,r,k}^{\mathrm{tool}}`$ is the repair guidance. If
the verdict is $`0`$, the guidance is added to $`\Phi_{i}^{(r,k+1)}`$
and the agent regenerates the complete trajectory. Otherwise, the
trajectory proceeds to semantic validation. Notably, each semantic round
allows at most $`n_{t}`$ tool-validation attempts.

Skill-aware semantic validation. A valid tool trajectory does not
necessarily produce a correct answer. We therefore use a *semantic
judge* to evaluate the generated answer:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left(s_{i,r}^{\mathrm{sem}},\phi_{i,r}^{\mathrm{sem}}\right)=\pi_{\theta_{c}}^{c}\!\left(q_{i},\tau_{i},y_{i},\hat{\tau}_{i}^{(r,k_{r})},\hat{y}_{i}^{(r,k_{r})},s_{i}^{(1)};\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{sem.}}\right),
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{sem.}}`$ is the semantic system prompt,
$`y_{i}`$ is the reference answer, $`s_{i,r}^{\mathrm{sem}}`$ is the
semantic score, and $`\phi_{i,r}^{\mathrm{sem}}`$ is the repair
guidance. Here, $`k_{r}`$ denotes the first tool-validation attempt that
passes in round $`r`$. For textual outputs, the candidate is accepted
when $`s_{i,r}^{\mathrm{sem}}>\gamma`$, where $`\gamma`$ is the
acceptance threshold. For image outputs, it must produce the required
artifact. Otherwise, $`\phi_{i,r}^{\mathrm{sem}}`$ is appended to
$`\Phi_{i}^{(r+1,1)}`$, triggering a new semantic round. This process
runs for at most $`n_{s}`$ semantic rounds. The accepted trajectory is
denoted as
$`\hat{\tau}_{i}^{\star}=\hat{\tau}_{i}^{(r^{\star},k_{r^{\star}})}`$,
where $`r^{\star}`$ is the first passing semantic round and
$`k_{r^{\star}}`$ is the corresponding successful tool round.

Evidence-grounded skill refinement. Once the execution is validated, the
accepted trajectory is used to refine the skill components:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left(n_{i}^{(2)},d_{i}^{(2)},w_{i}^{(2)},p_{i}^{(2)}\right)=\pi_{\theta}\!\left(q_{i},\tau_{i},\hat{\tau}_{i}^{\star},s_{i}^{(1)};\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{ref.}}\right).
``` |  | (9) |

Here, $`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{ref.}}`$ denotes the skill-refinement system
prompt. To further refine inter-tool dependencies, we construct a GOR
graph $`\mathcal{G}_{i}=(\mathcal{V}_{i},\mathcal{E}_{i})`$ from the
accepted trajectory, where each node represents a successful tool
invocation:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v_{i,t}=\left(u_{i,t},p_{i,t},\mathcal{Z}_{i,t}\right)\in\mathcal{V}_{i},
``` |  | (10) |

where $`u_{i,t}`$, $`p_{i,t}`$ and $`\mathcal{Z}_{i,t}`$ denote the
$`t`$-th tool name, arguments, and execution result. For the $`k`$-th
intermediate result $`z_{i,m,k}`$ generated by the $`m`$-th invocation
and consumed by the $`j`$-th argument $`p_{i,n,j}`$ of the $`n`$-th
invocation, we add a routing edge:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
e_{i,m,k\rightarrow i,n,j}=\left(v_{i,m},z_{i,m,k},v_{i,n},p_{i,n,j}\right)\in\mathcal{E}_{i},\quad m<n.
``` |  | (11) |

The GOR graph thus captures tool-call order and observation routing
between dependent tool calls. We then translate it into natural-language
routing rules using $`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{GOR.}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{i}^{(2)}=\pi_{\theta}\left(\mathcal{G}_{i};\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{GOR.}}\right).
``` |  | (12) |

Finally, the evolved skill is obtained as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{i}^{(2)}=\bigl(n_{i}^{(2)},d_{i}^{(2)},w_{i}^{(2)},p_{i}^{(2)},r_{i}^{(2)},\ell_{i}\bigr).
``` |  | (13) |

Through three independent validation stages, the *Evolver* enables
fine-grained, controllable skill evolution based on explicit execution
evidence rather than coarse-grained outcome reflection.
Fig. [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.2 Tool-Augmented Remote Sensing Agents ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(b)

### 3.3 Skill Decision and Application

As shown in
Fig. [3](#S1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(c),
we apply a generalization operator $`\Gamma`$ after evolution to remove
instance-specific information:

|     |                                                            |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \bar{s}_{i}=\Gamma\left(s_{i}^{(2)};q_{i},\tau_{i}\right).
       ```                                                         |     | (14) |

Specifically, $`\Gamma`$ identifies locations, targets, coordinates,
numerical values, artifact paths, and executable expressions from
$`q_{i}`$ and $`\tau_{i}`$, replaces them with typed placeholders, and
applies these substitutions to the skill. The generalized skill
$`\bar{s}_{i}`$ thus removes sample-specific values while preserving
reusable tool-call knowledge.

The *Decider* then determines how to integrate $`\bar{s}_{i}`$ into the
skill bank $`\mathcal{B}`$. For each $`s_{j}\in\mathcal{B}`$, it first
performs structural matching by task type and tool-call sequence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{ij}=\mathbb{I}[c_{i}=c_{j}]\cdot\mathbb{I}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}],
``` |  | (15) |

where $`c_{i}`$ and $`c_{j}`$ denote task types, and
$`\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}`$ and $`\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}`$ denote
tool-call sequences. If $`c_{i}\neq c_{j}`$ or
$`\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}`$, $`\bar{s}_{i}`$
is directly added to $`\mathcal{B}`$ as a new task type or workflow.
Only when $`C_{ij}=1`$, the *Decider* determines the integration
decision:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{ij}=\pi_{\theta}(\bar{s}_{i},s_{j};\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{dec.}}),
``` |  | (16) |

where $`d_{ij}\in\{\mathrm{Add},\mathrm{Merge},\mathrm{Discard}\}`$.
$`\mathrm{Add}`$ inserts $`\bar{s}_{i}`$ into $`\mathcal{B}`$,
$`\mathrm{Discard}`$ removes it, and $`\mathrm{Merge}`$ combines it with
the most semantically similar skill. For merging, an LLM-based merger
with prompt $`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{mer.}}`$ generates:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{ij}^{*}=\pi_{\theta}(\bar{s}_{i},s_{j};\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{mer.}}),
``` |  | (17) |

where $`s_{ij}^{*}`$ denotes the merged skill. The detailed pseudocode
for SkillBank construction is provided in
Supp. [7](#S7 "7 Algorithmic Pseudocode of SkillEarth ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution").

During application, the agent retrieves the most relevant skill
$`s^{*}`$ from $`\mathcal{B}`$ via cosine similarity and incorporates it
into $`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{exe.}}`$ (see
Fig. [3](#S1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(d)).
At step $`t`$, the agent predicts:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{t}=\pi_{\theta}(q,s^{*},\tau_{<t},o_{<t};\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{exe.}}),
``` |  | (18) |

where $`\tau_{<t}`$ and $`o_{<t}`$ denote previous tool calls and
observations. The corresponding tool executes $`a_{t}`$ to obtain
$`o_{t}`$, repeating until a final answer is generated. By storing RS
knowledge in skill files, $`\mathcal{B}`$ enables cross-LLM reuse
without retraining.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluate SkillEarth on OpenEarth-Bench \[[26](#bib.bib14)\] and
Earth-Bench \[[8](#bib.bib8)\] using official metrics. OpenEarth-Bench
contains 14,538 training and 1,169 evaluation instances across 60 RS
tasks, while Earth-Bench includes 248 expert-curated samples and 13,729
multimodal images under Automatic Planning (AP) and Instruction
Following (IF) settings. Both benchmarks assess tool execution and task
outcomes through Step-by-Step and End-to-End evaluations, respectively.
To reduce time cost, we construct the OpenEarth-Bench SkillBank using
1,050 training trajectories (7.2% of the training set) covering all 60
task types and evaluate on all 1,169 instances (see
Supp. [8.1](#S8.SS1 "8.1 Dataset Construction ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")
for the reason behind this selection). For Earth-Bench, we evaluate all
methods on the original datasets without additional processing. All
ablation studies are conducted on OpenEarth-Bench. We use
Doubao-Seed-Evolving \[[2](#bib.bib38)\] as the cloud-based judge via
its publicly accessible API and Qwen3-4B-Instruct \[[23](#bib.bib35)\]
as the default backbone. Experiments run on four NVIDIA A100 GPUs.
Dataset construction, model configurations, and metric definitions are
detailed in
Supp. [8](#S8 "8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution"),
with system prompts in
Supp. [13](#S13 "13 System Prompts ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution").

We evaluate the generalization of SkillEarth on OpenEarth-Bench using

We conduct ablation studies to evaluate the contributions of each

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed SkillEarth, a self-evolving skill extraction
framework that equips general-purpose agents with reusable remote
sensing tool-call skills without expert intervention or parameter
updates. Unlike existing methods that rely on holistic skill refinement,
SkillEarth decomposes skills into independently evolvable components and
introduces observation-grounded evolution. By combining dual-judge
feedback with grounded observation routing graphs, SkillEarth enables
targeted refinement based on real tool execution evidence. Experiments
on OpenEarth-Bench and Earth-Bench demonstrate its effectiveness in
improving tool execution and task completion, outperforming existing RS
agents and skill extraction methods across most evaluation metrics.
Furthermore, the extracted skills generalize across diverse LLMs and
support cross-model transfer without parameter updates.

## 6 Qualitative Analysis of Skill Evolution

In
Sec. [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Observation-Grounded Skill Evolution ‣ 3 Method ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution"),
Fig. [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.2 Tool-Augmented Remote Sensing Agents ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")
illustrates a representative case of skill evolution for forest burn
severity and vegetation regrowth analysis. While the initial and evolved
skills preserve the same task objective and tool set, they differ in
task planning and grounded observation routing.

Task planning. As shown in
Fig. [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.2 Tool-Augmented Remote Sensing Agents ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(a),
the initial skill contains an over-specified plan that prescribes tool
invocations and their execution order, closely following the original
trajectory: it specifies two AddIndexLayer calls followed by
ComputeIndexChange. In contrast, the evolved skill in
Fig. [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.2 Tool-Augmented Remote Sensing Agents ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")(b)
abstracts these tool-specific instructions into general analytical
steps, including boundary retrieval, NBR computation, change analysis,
and result summarization. This decouples task planning from specific
tool invocations, improving skill generalizability.

Grounded observation routing. The initial skill specifies inter-tool
dependencies ambiguously, leaving the mapping between tool outputs and
downstream arguments unclear. The evolved skill explicitly defines these
mappings: the gpkg output of GetAreaBoundary is forwarded to downstream
tools. The outputs of two AddIndexLayer calls are assigned to the
layer1name and layer2name arguments of ComputeIndexChange, respectively.
The resulting text_output is then passed to Terminate to generate the
final response. These explicit mappings reduce ambiguity and improve
execution reliability.

### 8.1 Dataset Construction

As discussed in
Sec. [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution"),
we evaluate SkillEarth on OpenEarth-Bench \[[26](#bib.bib14)\] and
Earth-Bench \[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. OpenEarth-Bench provides 14,538 training
trajectories and 24 tools spanning five categories: Perceptual Tools,
GIS Computation Tools, Spectral Tools, Georeferenced Raster Tools, and
Utility Tools. These trajectories are originally designed for supervised
fine-tuning of LLMs to acquire tool-calling capabilities. In contrast,
SkillEarth aims to extract reusable skills from limited execution
experience without model fine-tuning. However, since skill evolution
involves actual tool execution, processing all 14,538 trajectories using
Qwen3-4B-Instruct alone is estimated to take approximately 34.4 days. To
reduce this overhead while maintaining task diversity, we select the
first 10 samples for each distinct tool-calling trajectory within each
task type (or all available samples if fewer than 10), yielding 1,050
trajectories (7.2% of the training set) for SkillBank construction. This
reduces the construction time to approximately 2 days. For fair
comparison, all existing skill extraction methods use the same subset
for skill extraction. Moreover, all compared methods, including RS
agents and skill extraction methods, are evaluated on the full set of
1,169 evaluation instances. The detailed distribution of the selected
subset is provided in
Tab. [7](#S8.T7 "Table 7 ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution").

Earth-Bench contains 248 samples and 104 specialized tools covering
perception, spectral analysis, and processed Earth products. We use the
original dataset without additional preprocessing. The detailed task
distribution is provided in

### 8.2 Model Configurations

We summarize the model configurations used in SkillEarth in
Tab. [9](#S8.T9 "Table 9 ‣ 8.1 Dataset Construction ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution").
We instantiate all five roles, including *Extractor*, *Executor*,
*Refiner*, *Decider*, and *Merger*, with multiple representative LLM
backbones. Specifically, we evaluate seven LLMs, including three
frontier LLMs (GPT-5.6-Terra \[[20](#bib.bib29)\],
Doubao-Seed-2.0-Mini \[[24](#bib.bib30)\], and
DeepSeek-V4-Flash \[[7](#bib.bib31)\]) and four open-source LLMs
(Mistral-v0.3-Instruct \[[10](#bib.bib32)\],
DeepSeek-R1 \[[6](#bib.bib33)\],
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \[[22](#bib.bib34)\], and
Qwen3-4B-Instruct \[[23](#bib.bib35)\]). We employ
Doubao-Seed-Evolving \[[2](#bib.bib38)\] as the cloud-based judge for
both tool execution and semantic assessment. All LLMs are configured
with a temperature of 0.0 to ensure deterministic outputs and improve
the reproducibility of skill extraction and tool execution. For
different benchmarks, we set different maximum output token limits. For
OpenEarth-Bench, we use a maximum of 1,024 output tokens. For
Earth-Bench, we increase this limit to 16,384 tokens because its tasks
often involve processing multiple files, maintaining long execution
contexts, and generating intermediate results with absolute file paths.
The larger token budget prevents premature truncation of tool arguments,
execution descriptions, and final responses, thereby ensuring complete
task execution.

The hyperparameters for SkillBank construction and skill retrieval are
listed in
Table [10](#S8.T10 "Table 10 ‣ 8.3.1 Metrics for OpenEarth-Bench ‣ 8.3 Evaluation Metrics ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution").
We allow at most five tool-execution rounds ($`n_{t}`$) and two semantic
refinement rounds ($`n_{s}`$). A generated skill is accepted only when
it satisfies the corresponding quality criterion: the semantic score
exceeds 0.6 for text-based outputs, or the required image output is
successfully generated ($`\mathrm{exist}=1`$) for image-based tasks. For
skill retrieval, we encode skills using a hashing-based encoder with a
2048-dimensional representation and measure relevance using cosine
similarity. For each query, only the top-1 skill ($`k=1`$) is retrieved
and provided to the agent, avoiding excessive contextual interference
while preserving the most relevant prior experience.

- •
  Tool Accuracy (Tool.): Evaluates the correctness of tool selection.
- •
  Argument Name Accuracy (ArgN.): Checks whether all required arguments
  are provided.
- •
  Argument Value Accuracy (ArgV.): Assesses the correctness of argument
  values.
- •
  Summarization Accuracy (Summ.): Evaluates the quality of final
  responses synthesized from previous tools.

End-to-End Evaluation. This protocol evaluates autonomous task execution
with live tool use.

- •
  Tool F1: Measures tool selection using F1 scores across four
  functional categories: Perception (Per.), Operation (Op.), Logic
  (Logic.), and GIS (GIS.).
- •
  Tool Order: Evaluates trajectory correctness at three levels: Unique
  (Uni.) checks tool correspondence while ignoring repeated calls;
  AnyOrder (AnyOr.) requires all tool calls, including duplicates,
  regardless of order, and SameOrder (SameO.) additionally enforces the
  correct invocation sequence.
- •
  Task Accuracy: Evaluates final outputs against reference answers.
  Generation Accuracy (Gen.) assesses image generation tasks based on
  valid generation tool calls and successful execution, while Answer
  Accuracy (Ans.) evaluates non-generation tasks using seedance-2.0-mini
  with a standardized evaluation prompt.

#### 8.3.2 Metrics for Earth-Bench

Earth-Bench \[[8](#bib.bib8)\] supports two evaluation modes: Automatic
Planning (AP), which assesses autonomous task planning, and Instruction
Following (IF), which evaluates the execution of human-provided
instructions. Both modes employ Step-by-Step and End-to-End evaluations
to assess intermediate reasoning and final answer, respectively.

Step-by-Step Evaluation. This protocol evaluates intermediate reasoning
trajectories through four metrics:

- •
  Tool-Any-Order (AnyOr.): Checks whether all required tools are
  selected, regardless of their invocation order.
- •
  Tool-In-Order (InOr.): Evaluates whether tools are invoked in the
  correct sequence.
- •
  Tool-Exact-Match (Exact.): Measures the exact prefix-level agreement
  between predicted and expert trajectories.
- •
  Parameter Accuracy (Param.): Assesses whether tool identifiers and
  their arguments match the reference.

End-to-End Evaluation. This protocol evaluates overall task performance
using two metrics:

- •
  Accuracy (Acc.): Measures the correctness of answers.
- •
  Efficiency (Eff.): Evaluates the efficiency of execution trajectories
  relative to expert solutions.

## 11 Cross-Model Skill Transferability

We evaluate the cross-model transferability of SkillEarth on
OpenEarth-Bench. As shown in
Tabs. [11](#S8.T11 "Table 11 ‣ 8.3.1 Metrics for OpenEarth-Bench ‣ 8.3 Evaluation Metrics ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")
and [12](#S8.T12 "Table 12 ‣ 8.3.1 Metrics for OpenEarth-Bench ‣ 8.3 Evaluation Metrics ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution"),
cross-model skill transfer generally improves both Step-by-Step and
End-to-End performance, demonstrating the reusability of RS knowledge
across different LLMs.

Transferring skills from frontier LLMs to Qwen3-4B-Instruct improves all
five Step-by-Step metrics and most End-to-End metrics. Conversely,
transferring skills from Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct to
DeepSeek-V4-Flash improves all five Step-by-Step metrics and seven and
eight of the nine End-to-End metrics, respectively. Notably,
Qwen3-4B-Instruct skills increase Ans. from 53.41 to 60.22 and Gen. from
55.86 to 62.07, while Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct skills achieve a larger
improvement in Logic. F₁ from 13.11 to 36.18. These results demonstrate
that skills extracted by smaller open-source LLMs can also enhance more
capable frontier models.

## 12 Detailed Ablation Analysis

We analyze the contributions of each component in SkillEarth by
progressively adding the Tool Judge, Semantic Judge, and GOR graph to
the Qwen3-4B-Instruct baseline on OpenEarth-Bench. Results under both
Step-by-Step and End-to-End settings are reported in
Tabs. [14](#S8.T14 "Table 14 ‣ 8.3.1 Metrics for OpenEarth-Bench ‣ 8.3 Evaluation Metrics ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution")
and [13](#S8.T13 "Table 13 ‣ 8.3.1 Metrics for OpenEarth-Bench ‣ 8.3 Evaluation Metrics ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution").

Step-by-Step Evaluation. As shown in
Tab. [13](#S8.T13 "Table 13 ‣ 8.3.1 Metrics for OpenEarth-Bench ‣ 8.3 Evaluation Metrics ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution"),
the Tool Judge improves Tool. and ArgN. accuracy by reducing execution
errors. The Semantic Judge further improves ArgN. and Summ. accuracy
despite a slight drop in Tool. accuracy, demonstrating the complementary
role of semantic feedback. Incorporating the GOR graph achieves the best
results across all metrics, validating the effectiveness of SkillEarth.

End-to-End Evaluation. As shown in
Tab. [14](#S8.T14 "Table 14 ‣ 8.3.1 Metrics for OpenEarth-Bench ‣ 8.3 Evaluation Metrics ‣ 8 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ SkillEarth: Teaching General-Purpose Agents to Act on Earth via Skill Evolution"),
the Tool Judge improves task execution, while the Semantic Judge further
enhances task-level performance. Incorporating the GOR graph achieves
the best results on eight of nine End-to-End metrics, including all
three tool-order metrics (AnyOr., SameO., and Uni.). These gains
highlight the importance of explicit observation routing in preserving
execution order and information flow, demonstrating the effectiveness of
dual-judge feedback and GOR-based skill refinement for reliable
multi-step RS workflows.

Overall, the three components play complementary roles: the Tool Judge
validates execution, the Semantic Judge assesses task outcomes, and the
GOR graph captures inter-tool dependencies. Together, they enable
SkillEarth to evolve reliable skills through execution-grounded evidence
rather than outcome-based reflection alone.

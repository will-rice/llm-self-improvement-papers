# When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes? A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs

Ryo Kamoi¹    Yusen Zhang¹    Nan Zhang¹    Jiawei Han²    Rui Zhang¹
Affiliation: ¹Penn State University    ²University of Illinois
Urbana-Champaign Email: [{ryokamoi,rmz5227}@psu.edu](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Self-correction is an approach to improving responses from large
language models (LLMs) by refining the responses using LLMs during
inference. Prior work has proposed various self-correction frameworks
using different sources of feedback, including self-evaluation and
external feedback. However, there is still no consensus on the question
of when LLMs can correct their own mistakes, as recent studies also
report negative results. In this work, we critically survey broad papers
and discuss the conditions required for successful self-correction. We
first find that prior studies often do not define their research
questions in detail and involve impractical frameworks or unfair
evaluations that over-evaluate self-correction. To tackle these issues,
we categorize research questions in self-correction research and provide
a checklist for designing appropriate experiments. Our critical survey
based on the newly categorized research questions shows that (1) no
prior work demonstrates successful self-correction with feedback from
prompted LLMs, except for studies in tasks that are exceptionally suited
for self-correction, (2) self-correction works well in tasks that can
use reliable external feedback, and (3) large-scale fine-tuning enables
self-correction.

## 1 Introduction

Self-correction is a popular approach to improve responses from large
language models (LLMs) by refining them using LLMs during inference
([Bai et al., 2022](#bib.bib4); [Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib63)).
Extensive studies on self-correction have been conducted in various
tasks, including arithmetic reasoning, code generation, and question
answering ([Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib31); [Shinn et al.,
2023](#bib.bib94)). The simplest approach of self-correction prompts
LLMs to provide feedback on their own responses and refine the responses
using the feedback ([Huang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42)), under the
hypothesis that recognizing errors is easier than avoiding them
([Saunders et al., 2022](#bib.bib87)). As in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"),
self-correction has also been studied using additional information for
improving feedback, including external tools such as code interpreters
([Chen et al., 2024e](#bib.bib16); [Gou et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)),
external knowledge retrieved via web search ([Gao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib31); [Jiang et al., 2023b](#bib.bib47)), or fine-tuning
([Welleck et al., 2023](#bib.bib107); [Ye et al., 2023](#bib.bib118)).
However, recent studies also report negative results indicating that
LLMs cannot self-correct ([Huang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42); [Gou
et al., 2024](#bib.bib34); [Li et al., 2024b](#bib.bib57); [Chen et al.,
2024f](#bib.bib18)) or even self-detect ([Chen and Shu,
2024](#bib.bib12); [Tyen et al., 2024](#bib.bib99); [Hong et al.,
2024](#bib.bib40); [Jiang et al., 2024](#bib.bib45); [Kamoi et al.,
2024](#bib.bib49)) their own mistakes at least in certain conditions.
These conflicting observations indicate that further analysis of
self-correction is needed.

In this work, we provide a critical survey to investigate the conditions
required for successful self-correction. First, our analysis finds that
prior studies often do not define their research questions in detail. As
a result, many papers fail to provide appropriate experiments to
evaluate the research questions they implicitly target. To address this
issue, we categorize research questions in self-correction research
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"))
and discuss frameworks that should be used for verifying each research
question
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Frameworks for Verifying RQs ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
Finally, we provide a checklist for designing appropriate experiments
(§[8](#S8 "8 Checklist for Self-Correction Research ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).

Next, we analyze prior work to identify when LLMs can self-correct their
mistakes, using the new definitions of the research questions. Our
analysis highlights that the bottleneck is in the feedback
generation (§[7](#S7 "7 Summary of Our Analysis ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
Specifically, (1) no prior work shows successful self-correction with
feedback from prompted LLMs in general
tasks (§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")),
(2) self-correction works well in tasks where reliable external feedback
is
available (§[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Self-Correction with External Tools or Knowledge ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")),
(3) large-scale fine-tuning enables
self-correction (§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Self-Correction with Fine-tuning ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")),
and (4) some tasks have properties exceptionally suitable for
self-correction (§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
In summary, our analysis identifies the properties required for
successful self-correction as follows:

\[[RQ1](#S3.I1.i1 "1st item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\] When
can LLMs self-correct based solely on the inherent capabilities of LLMs?

- •
  In general tasks, no prior work shows reliable evidence of successful
  self-correction with in-context learning.
  (§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"))
- •
  In tasks with specific properties that are exceptionally favorable for
  self-correction (e.g., responses are decomposable), self-correction is
  effective even with in-context learning.
  (§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"))

\[[RQ2](#S3.I1.i2 "2nd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\] When
can LLMs self-correct the best-possible initial responses with external
information?

- •
  Self-correction is effective in tasks where reliable external feedback
  is available.
  (§[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Self-Correction with External Tools or Knowledge ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"))
- •
  Fine-tuning enables self-correction when large training data is
  available but is unexplored for small training data.
  (§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Self-Correction with Fine-tuning ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"))

\[[RQ3](#S3.I1.i3 "3rd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\] When
are the final outputs of self-correction better than other approaches?

- •
  Self-correction is often not compared with sufficiently strong
  baselines, and it is still unclear whether it is better than other
  approaches.
  (§[6](#S6 "6 Strong Baselines ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"))

## 2 Self-Correction of LLMs

The term “self-correction” is used in a wide range of scenarios, from a
strict definition in which LLMs refine their own responses by themselves
([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib63); [Huang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42))
to broader concepts that also involve feedback from external tools or
knowledge ([Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib94); [Gou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34)). In this work, we define self-correction as a
framework that refines responses from LLMs using LLMs during inference,
possibly with external tools or knowledge. As in
Table [1](#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Timeframe. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"),
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Timeframe. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"),
and
Figure [3](#S1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Timeframe. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"),
self-correction has been studied in various frameworks with different
sources of feedback.

### 2.1 Frameworks

Prior studies propose self-correction frameworks with various different
architectures.

#### Explicit Feedback vs. Direct Refinement.

Self-correction often consists of three stages including feedback
generation ([Kim et al., 2023](#bib.bib51); [Madaan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib63); [Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib94); [Huang et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib42)):

- •
  Initial Response Generation is a stage of generating initial responses
  from an LLM.
- •
  Feedback model generates feedback given the original input and initial
  response. This stage may use external tools or knowledge.
- •
  Refinement model generates a refined response, given the input,
  initial response, and feedback.

Direct refinement is another approach that refines responses without
generating feedback explicitly ([Saunders et al., 2022](#bib.bib87);
[Bai et al., 2022](#bib.bib4); [Welleck et al., 2023](#bib.bib107);
[Akyurek et al., 2023](#bib.bib1)).

#### Post-hoc vs. Generation-time.

Post-hoc correction refines responses after they are generated ([Pan
et al., 2024](#bib.bib77)). Generation-time correction or step-level
correction ([Paul et al., 2024](#bib.bib79); [Jiang et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib47)) improves step-by-step reasoning by providing
feedback on intermediate reasoning steps. Post-hoc correction is more
flexible and applicable to broader tasks, although generation-time
correction is popular for reasoning tasks ([Pan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib77)).

#### Same-model vs. Cross-model.

Cross-model correction generates feedback or refines the responses using
models different from the model that generates initial responses.
Cross-model correction has been mostly studied in the settings of
correcting mistakes of large proprietary LLMs using small fine-tuned
models ([Welleck et al., 2023](#bib.bib107); [Akyurek et al.,
2023](#bib.bib1); [Paul et al., 2024](#bib.bib79)) or multi-agent debate
of multiple models with similar capabilities ([Liang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib58); [Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib56); [Cohen et al.,
2023](#bib.bib22); [Du et al., 2023](#bib.bib25); [Zhang et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib122); [Chen et al., 2024b](#bib.bib13); [Chan et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib7); [Wang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib104)).

### 2.2 Sources of Feedback

#### Intrinsic (§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).

Intrinsic self-correction prompts LLMs to generate feedback on their own
responses. Prompting strategies include simple zero-shot or few-shot
prompts ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib63); [Kim et al.,
2023](#bib.bib51)), decomposing the responses ([Dhuliawala et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24)), and evaluating confidence ([Varshney et al.,
2023](#bib.bib102); [Jiang et al., 2023b](#bib.bib47); [Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib109)).

#### External Information (§[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Self-Correction with External Tools or Knowledge ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).

Self-correction often relies on external information, including external
tools such as code executors ([Jiang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib46); [Gou
et al., 2024](#bib.bib34); [Chen et al., 2024e](#bib.bib16);
[Stengel-Eskin et al., 2024](#bib.bib96)), symbolic reasoners ([Pan
et al., 2023](#bib.bib76)), proof assistant ([First et al.,
2023](#bib.bib29)), or task-specific metrics ([Xu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib111)), external knowledge from search engines ([Jiang
et al., 2023b](#bib.bib47); [Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib31); [Zhao
et al., 2023](#bib.bib127)), Wikipedia ([Yu et al., 2023](#bib.bib120);
[Zhao et al., 2023](#bib.bib127)), or other corpora ([Peng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib80); [Zhao et al., 2023](#bib.bib127)), oracle information
such as ground-truth answers ([Kim et al., 2023](#bib.bib51); [Shinn
et al., 2023](#bib.bib94)), human feedback ([Chen et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib10)), or stronger models ([Zhang et al.,
2024c](#bib.bib126)).

#### Fine-tuning (§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Self-Correction with Fine-tuning ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).

Models fine-tuned for self-correction are another source of feedback,
which are trained via supervised fine-tuning ([Welleck et al.,
2023](#bib.bib107); [Ye et al., 2023](#bib.bib118); [First et al.,
2023](#bib.bib29); [Paul et al., 2024](#bib.bib79); [Han et al.,
2024](#bib.bib37); [Havrilla et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)) or reinforcement
learning ([Le et al., 2022](#bib.bib53); [Akyurek et al.,
2023](#bib.bib1)).

### 2.3 Tasks

Self-correction has been studied in various tasks, including
Reasoning: arithmetic reasoning ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib63);
[Nathani et al., 2023](#bib.bib71); [Gou et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)),

### 2.4 Differences from Related Approaches

In this work, we define self-consistency ([Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib105)) or generate-and-rank ([Shen et al.,
2021](#bib.bib92); [Weng et al., 2023](#bib.bib108)) to be different
from self-correction because these approaches do not refine responses
and assume that LLMs generate correct answers with a reasonable
probability. We discuss these methods in
Section [6](#S6 "6 Strong Baselines ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")
as strong baselines that should be compared with self-correction.

## 3 Research Questions

We find that prior studies often do not define their research questions
in detail and fail to use appropriate self-correction frameworks in
their experiments. We propose a new approach to classify research
questions and frameworks in self-correction.

### 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research

### 3.2 Frameworks for Verifying RQs

Prior work often categorizes self-correction frameworks based on
approaches for generating
feedback (§[2](#S2 "2 Self-Correction of LLMs ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
However, we point out that we also need to categorize them by the
quality of initial responses because the frameworks we need to use for
verifying different research questions vary by whether they use the
best-possible initial
responses (§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).

We propose categories of (same-model) self-correction that correspond to
different research
questions (§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")),
as shown in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Timeframe. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs").
Specifically, we propose to categorize the self-correction frameworks as
follows.

- •
  Realistic: Can be used in real-world applications.
  - –
    Fair: Using best-possible initial responses
  - –
    Unfair: Using sub-optimal initial responses

- •
  Unrealistic: Using information that is not accessible in real-world
  applications.

In this work, we focus on categorizing self-correction frameworks that
do not involve multiple language models with different architectures.
Cross-model correction uses different models for initial response
generation and self-correction, so it is unsuitable for evaluating
whether LLMs can improve their own initial responses
\[[RQ1](#S3.I1.i1 "1st item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"),
[RQ2](#S3.I1.i2 "2nd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\].
However, it can be used to evaluate
\[[RQ3](#S3.I1.i3 "3rd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
whether the final responses from self-correction are better than other
methods.

#### Realistic vs. Unrealistic.

Some prior studies propose unrealistic self-correction, which cannot be
implemented in real-world applications, by using oracle information such
as ground-truth answers ([Kim et al., 2023](#bib.bib51); [Shinn et al.,
2023](#bib.bib94)). These methods cannot be used to verify any research
questions.

#### Fair vs. Unfair.

Realistic frameworks can be categorized by whether they use the
best-possible initial responses. Fair self-correction represents
frameworks that refine the best-possible initial responses.
(1) Intrinsic self-correction ([Huang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42)) uses
the same model and information for initial response generation and
self-correction. Intrinsic self-correction can be used to assess
\[[RQ1](#S3.I1.i1 "1st item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
whether LLMs can self-correct based solely on their inherent
capabilities. (2) Fair-asymmetric self-correction uses additional
information for self-correction, but also uses information to improve
initial response generation as much as possible. For example,
self-correction with code interpreters ([Chen et al.,
2024e](#bib.bib16); [Gou et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)) is not intrinsic but
fair because we cannot easily use code interpreters to directly improve
the initial response generation. Fair-asymmetric self-correction can be
used to evaluate
\[[RQ2](#S3.I1.i2 "2nd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
whether LLMs can self-correct the best-possible initial responses using
external information. Unfair self-correction (or unfair-asymmetric
self-correction) represents frameworks that are practical but do not use
the best-possible initial responses. For example, methods that use
search engines only for self-correction ([Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib31);
[Yu et al., 2023](#bib.bib120)) are unfair because they can use search
engines to directly improve the initial response generation. Unfair
self-correction can evaluate
\[[RQ3](#S3.I1.i3 "3rd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
whether the final responses from self-correction outperform other
methods but cannot evaluate
\[[RQ2](#S3.I1.i2 "2nd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
whether self-correction can improve the best-possible initial responses.

## 4 Self-Correction with Prompting

> \[[RQ1](#S3.I1.i1 "1st item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
> Can LLMs self-correct their best-possible initial responses based
> solely on the inherent capabilities?

Several studies propose intrinsic self-correction methods, which
self-correct responses from LLMs by prompting themselves to generate
feedback and refine the responses. [Bai et al. (2022)](#bib.bib4)
propose self-correcting harmful responses from LLMs by prompting
themselves. Self-Refine ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib63)) and RCI
Prompting ([Kim et al., 2023](#bib.bib51)) iteratively prompt LLMs to
self-correct their own responses in tasks such as arithmetic reasoning.

#### Negative Results.

However, recent studies report that intrinsic self-correction does not
improve or even degrade the performance in tasks such as arithmetic
reasoning, closed-book QA ([Huang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42); [Gou
et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)), code generation ([Gou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34); [Olausson et al., 2024](#bib.bib74)), plan generation
([Valmeekam et al., 2023](#bib.bib101)), and graph coloring ([Stechly
et al., 2023](#bib.bib95)). Several studies claim that a bottleneck is
in the feedback generation, and it is difficult to generate reliable
feedback on their responses only by prompting themselves ([Gou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34); [Huang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42); [Olausson et al.,
2024](#bib.bib74); [Chen et al., 2024f](#bib.bib18)).

#### Unrealistic or Unfair Settings.

The conflicting positive and negative results motivate us to analyze
when LLMs can self-correct only by prompting themselves. Specifically,
we assess whether prior studies satisfy the requirements to verify that
\[[RQ1](#S3.I1.i1 "1st item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\] LLMs
can self-correct their responses based solely on their inherent
capabilities. As in
Table [4](#S3.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Fair vs. Unfair. ‣ 3.2 Frameworks for Verifying RQs ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"),
we find that many studies use either oracle information in the
self-correction processes (unrealistic frameworks) or weak prompts that
can be easily improved for generating initial responses (unfair
settings), which over-evaluate self-correction. Consequently, we
conclude that no major work shows successful self-correction of
responses from LLMs using feedback generated by prompting themselves
under fair settings in general tasks. Oracle Information: RCI Prompting
([Kim et al., 2023](#bib.bib51)) uses ground-truth answers and does not
apply self-correction when the initial responses are correct, which
unfairly ignores mistakes caused by updating correct responses
incorrectly. Reflexion ([Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib94)) generates
feedback by using an exact match between the generated and ground-truth
answers, which cannot be accessed in real-world applications. Weak
Initial Responses: Detoxifying harmful responses is a popular task in
self-correction research, but prior studies often study in situations
where initial response generation is not instructed to generate harmless
responses ([Bai et al., 2022](#bib.bib4); [Wang et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib106)). Although detecting harmful contents using LLMs is
a reasonable research topic, this setting is not the self-correction
from best-possible initial responses, since we can improve the initial
response generation process by instructing not to generate harmful
responses. As more obvious weak prompts, Self-Refine ([Madaan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib63)) uses instructions or few-shot examples that do not
correctly correspond to the target task only for initial response
generation (e.g., providing wrong target labels in few-shot examples),
while using appropriate instructions for self-correction, as shown in
Table [9](#A0.T9 "Table 9 ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")
and
[10](#A0.T10 "Table 10 ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs").
These settings evaluate improvement from weak initial responses, which
over-evaluate the improvement by self-correction.

#### Tasks in which Self-Correction is Exceptionally Effective.

Although our analysis of prior studies shows that intrinsic
self-correction is difficult in general, some tasks have properties that
make feedback generation easy and enable intrinsic self-correction. For
example, CoVe ([Dhuliawala et al., 2024](#bib.bib24)) is an intrinsic
self-correction method for tasks of generating multiple answers, such as
Name some politicians who were born in NY, New York. Generated responses
include multiple answers, but the feedback generation can be decomposed
into easier sub-tasks of verifying each answer. Tasks with decomposable
responses are one of the few groups of tasks for which verification is
clearly easier than generation, which enables intrinsic self-correction.
However, many real-world tasks do not satisfy this property.

## 5 Self-Correction with External Information

> \[[RQ2](#S3.I1.i2 "2nd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
> Can LLMs self-correct their best-possible initial responses assisted
> by external information?

This section analyzes self-correction frameworks that make use of
external tools, external knowledge, and fine-tuning.

### 5.1 Self-Correction with External Tools or Knowledge

Given the observation that feedback generation is a bottleneck of
self-correction
(§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")),
improving feedback using external tools or knowledge is a promising
direction. External tools used for self-correction include code
interpreters for code generation tasks ([Chen et al.,
2024e](#bib.bib16); [Gou et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)) and symbolic
reasoners for logical reasoning tasks ([Pan et al., 2023](#bib.bib76)).
A popular source of knowledge is search engines, which are often used
with queries generated from initial responses to retrieve information
for validating their correctness ([Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib31); [Jiang
et al., 2023b](#bib.bib47)). These prior studies widely agree that
self-correction can improve LLM responses when reliable external tools
or knowledge suitable for improving feedback are available.

#### Unfair self-correction with external information.

Although using external tools or knowledge is known to be effective in
self-correction, we raise caution that the way of using external tools
or knowledge influences the research questions we can verify
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
As shown in
Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Tasks in which Self-Correction is Exceptionally Effective. ‣ 4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"),
some prior studies ([Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib31); [Yu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib120); [Zhao et al., 2023](#bib.bib127)) use external
knowledge only for self-correction, while they can also directly use
external knowledge to improve the initial response generation process.
For example, RARR ([Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib31)) uses external
knowledge to detect mistakes in initial responses, while it does not use
any external knowledge when generating initial responses. These methods
are reasonable when only focusing on
\[[RQ3](#S3.I1.i3 "3rd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\] the
performance of final responses, but it is not fair to use them for
evaluating
\[[RQ2](#S3.I1.i2 "2nd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\] whether
self-correction can improve from the best-possible initial responses. In
contrast, using code interpreters for self-correction ([Gou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34); [Chen et al., 2024e](#bib.bib16)) can be regarded as
using best-possible initial responses because there is no easy way to
improve the initial response generation directly.

#### Verifiable Tasks.

Some tasks have a property that allows the correctness of the responses
to be verified easily, even without external information. For example,
the constrained generation task evaluated in Self-Refine ([Madaan
et al., 2023](#bib.bib63)) is a task to generate a sentence that
includes five specified words. We can easily evaluate the correctness by
checking whether the five words are included in the generated sentence.
Tree-of-thought ([Yao et al., 2023](#bib.bib115)) is a generate-and-rank
method for verifiable tasks,¹¹ 1 Tree-of-thought is a generate-and-rank
method and not a self-correction method in our definition. such as Game
of 24, the task to obtain 24 using basic arithmetic operations
($`+,-,\times,\div`$) and provided four integers. For Game of 24, we can
easily verify the answer by checking whether the generated answer is 24.
We consider self-correction to work well in these tasks because they are
in the same situations as using strong external tools or the oracle
information to generate feedback.

### 5.2 Self-Correction with Fine-tuning

Prior work shows that fine-tuning LLMs for generating feedback or
refining responses improves the self-correction capability. A common
approach fine-tunes feedback models to generate reference feedback given
initial responses and fine-tunes refinement models to generate reference
answers given the initial responses and reference feedback ([Ye et al.,
2023](#bib.bib118); [Lee et al., 2024](#bib.bib54); [Saunders et al.,
2022](#bib.bib87)). Frameworks: The first approach fine-tunes the same
model to correct its own responses. In this approach, most methods
fine-tune models for all stages: initial responses, feedback, and
refinement ([Saunders et al., 2022](#bib.bib87); [Ye et al.,
2023](#bib.bib118); [Lee et al., 2024](#bib.bib54)). Another approach
corrects responses from larger models using smaller fine-tuned models.
This cross-model correction approach often instructs the larger models
to refine their own responses using feedback from the smaller fine-tuned
models ([Yang et al., 2022b](#bib.bib114); [Welleck et al.,
2023](#bib.bib107); [Akyurek et al., 2023](#bib.bib1); [Paul et al.,
2024](#bib.bib79)), which can be viewed as using the small fine-tuned
models as external tools. Training Strategies: A popular approach is
supervised fine-tuning, which fine-tunes self-correction modules on
human-annotated feedback ([Saunders et al., 2022](#bib.bib87)), feedback
from stronger models ([Ye et al., 2023](#bib.bib118)), or synthetic
negative responses ([Paul et al., 2024](#bib.bib79)). As other
approaches, to avoid the cost of collecting human feedback,
self-corrective learning ([Welleck et al., 2023](#bib.bib107)) selects
model-generated feedback that successfully refines responses as training
data, GLoRe ([Havrilla et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)) constructs a synthetic
refinement dataset using model-generated feedback, and RL4L ([Akyurek
et al., 2023](#bib.bib1)) uses reinforcement-learning. External
Tools: Some works fine-tune models to refine responses given feedback
from external tools. Self-Edit ([Zhang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib123)) uses
the results on test cases evaluated by code executors for code
generation, and Baldur ([First et al., 2023](#bib.bib29)) uses proof
assistants for improving proof generation.

#### Large Training Data for SFT of Feedback.

As shown in
Table [6](#S5.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Verifiable Tasks. ‣ 5.1 Self-Correction with External Tools or Knowledge ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"),
many methods with supervised fine-tuning for feedback generation rely on
training data with more than 100K instances. These studies often use
feedback generated by stronger models to simulate human annotation, but
this approach requires large-scale human annotations to be implemented
on state-of-the-art models. We expect future research to explore
approaches that do not require large-scale human annotations
(§[11](#S11 "11 Future Directions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).

#### Unfair Fine-tuning.

Some studies ([Welleck et al., 2023](#bib.bib107)) apply stronger
fine-tuning for self-correction models than initial response generation
models, which do not use best-possible initial responses in the
available resources
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Frameworks for Verifying RQs ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
This approach can be used to evaluate
\[[RQ3](#S3.I1.i3 "3rd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
the performance of the final responses to compare with other methods but
cannot be used to evaluate
\[[RQ2](#S3.I1.i2 "2nd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
the improvement from best-possible initial responses.

## 6 Strong Baselines

> \[[RQ3](#S3.I1.i3 "3rd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\]
> Are the final outputs from self-correction better than other methods?

Self-correction involves multiple LLM calls for generating feedback and
refinement. Therefore, to claim that
\[[RQ3](#S3.I1.i3 "3rd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")\] the
performance of the final outputs from self-correction frameworks is
better than other approaches, it should be compared with sufficiently
strong baselines, possibly relying on additional LLM calls or
computational cost. Many self-correction studies do not compare their
methods with strong baselines, although some studies pointed out this
issue and compare self-correction with self-consistency ([Gou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34); [Huang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42)) or pass@k in code
generation ([Zhang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib123); [Olausson et al.,
2024](#bib.bib74)). We encourage future research to compare
self-correction with strong baselines, including self-consistency and
generate-and-rank, to further explore
[RQ3](#S3.I1.i3 "3rd item ‣ 3.1 RQs in Self-Correction Research ‣ 3 Research Questions ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs").

#### Self-Consistency

([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib105)) is an approach that generates
multiple responses for the same input and takes the majority vote of the
final answers in reasoning tasks. The idea of selecting good responses
using the consistency between multiple responses from the same model has
also been extended to other tasks such as text generation ([Manakul
et al., 2023](#bib.bib64); [Elaraby et al., 2023](#bib.bib27); [Chen
et al., 2024d](#bib.bib15)) and code generation ([Shi et al.,
2022](#bib.bib93)).

#### Generate-and-Rank

is an approach that generates multiple responses and selects the best
response using verifiers. Post-hoc approach ranks responses using
self-evaluation ([Weng et al., 2023](#bib.bib108); [Zhang et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib125)), confidence ([Manakul et al., 2023](#bib.bib64)),
fine-tuned verifiers ([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib21); [Shen et al.,
2021](#bib.bib92); [Lightman et al., 2024](#bib.bib60)), or verifiers
with external tools ([Shi et al., 2022](#bib.bib93); [Chen et al.,
2023](#bib.bib11); [Ni et al., 2023](#bib.bib73)). Feedback-guided
decoding generates multiple responses and selects the best response for
each reasoning step using generation probability ([Hao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib38); [Tyen et al., 2024](#bib.bib99)), prompted
self-evaluation ([Jung et al., 2022](#bib.bib48); [Creswell and
Shanahan, 2022](#bib.bib23); [Xie et al., 2023](#bib.bib110); [Yao
et al., 2023](#bib.bib115); [Miao et al., 2024](#bib.bib68)), or
fine-tuned verifiers ([Uesato et al., 2022](#bib.bib100); [Tafjord
et al., 2022](#bib.bib97); [Yang et al., 2022a](#bib.bib113); [Asai
et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)).

## 7 Summary of Our Analysis

#### Bottleneck is in Feedback Generation.

Prior studies widely agree that LLMs can refine their responses given
reliable feedback
(§[5](#S5 "5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
However, generating reliable feedback on their own responses is still
observed to be challenging for LLMs without using additional information
(§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
In other words, for the current LLMs, the hypothesis that recognizing
errors is easier than avoiding them ([Saunders et al.,
2022](#bib.bib87)) is only true for certain tasks whose verification is
exceptionally easy, according to our analysis of the experiments in
prior studies. We recommend that self-correction research analyze the
quality of generated feedback in more detail, not only evaluate the
downstream performance of the refined responses.

#### Tasks Suitable for Self-Correction.

Our analysis identifies the properties of tasks that are suitable for
self-correction under different conditions.

- •
  Intrinsic Self-Correction
  (§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"))
  - –
    Tasks whose verification tasks are much easier than the original
    tasks (e.g., tasks whose responses are decomposable)

- •
  Self-Correction with External Information
  (§[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Self-Correction with External Tools or Knowledge ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"))
  - –
    Tasks for which external tools that provide reliable feedback exist
    (e.g., code generation)
  - –
    Tasks for which responses can be utilized to obtain useful
    information that is difficult to obtain before generating initial
    responses (e.g., generate queries from responses to retrieve
    documents for verifying information)

- •
  Self-Correction with Fine-tuning
  (§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Self-Correction with Fine-tuning ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"))
  - –
    Self-correction works in many tasks when large training data for
    feedback generation is available
  - –
    Tasks that can use reinforcement learning or self-corrective
    learning ([Welleck et al., 2023](#bib.bib107)), i.e., tasks whose
    responses can be easily evaluated given ground-truth answers

## 9 Differences from Other Survey

[Pan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib77) provide a comprehensive survey on broad
topics related to self-correction, including training strategies. Our
work specifically focuses on (inference-time) self-correction and
provides a more detailed and critical analysis of prior work. [Huang
et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib42) provide an analysis of problems in the
evaluation settings of self-correction research, which motivates our
work. They focus on analyzing a few papers on intrinsic self-correction
in reasoning tasks. We provide a more comprehensive analysis of
self-correction with in-context learning, external tools, and
fine-tuning.

## 10 Related Work of Self-Correction

#### Self-Detection

of mistakes in LLM responses using LLMs (possibly with external
information) has been studied in various domains, including
misinformation detection ([Zhang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib124); [Chern
et al., 2023](#bib.bib19); [Chen and Shu, 2024](#bib.bib12); [Mishra
et al., 2024](#bib.bib70)), context-faithfulness ([Wang et al.,
2020](#bib.bib103); [Durmus et al., 2020](#bib.bib26); [Scialom et al.,
2021](#bib.bib90)), harmful content detection ([Rauh et al.,
2022](#bib.bib84)), and bias detection ([Blodgett et al.,
2020](#bib.bib5); [Feng et al., 2023](#bib.bib28)). However, recent
studies ([Tyen et al., 2024](#bib.bib99); [Kamoi et al.,
2024](#bib.bib49)) show that even strong LLMs often cannot detect their
own mistakes in various tasks.

#### Editing Human-Written Text

by using language models has been studied in various domains, including
information update ([Shah et al., 2020](#bib.bib91); [Iv et al.,
2022](#bib.bib44); [Schick et al., 2023](#bib.bib89)), grammatical error
correction ([Ng et al., 2014](#bib.bib72); [Lichtarge et al.,
2019](#bib.bib59)), factual error correction ([Cao et al.,
2020](#bib.bib6); [Thorne and Vlachos, 2021](#bib.bib98)), and code
repair ([Gupta et al., 2017](#bib.bib36); [Mesbah et al.,
2019](#bib.bib67); [Bader et al., 2019](#bib.bib3); [Chen et al.,
2021](#bib.bib17); [Yasunaga and Liang, 2020](#bib.bib116); [Yasunaga
and Liang, 2021](#bib.bib117)).

#### Self-Training

or self-improvement is an approach to train models using their own
responses. Some studies use self-evaluation or self-correction for
creating training data ([Bai et al., 2022](#bib.bib4); [Gulcehre et al.,
2023](#bib.bib35)) or use self-evaluation as training signals ([Pang
et al., 2024](#bib.bib78)). Another approach improves the reasoning of
LLMs using LLM-generated reasoning by selecting high-quality outputs
using ground-truth final answers ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib121))
or self-consistency ([Huang et al., 2023](#bib.bib41)). As another
direction, [Meng et al. (2022)](#bib.bib66) use sentences generated by
LLMs with high confidence for training classifiers.

## 11 Future Directions

#### Improving Feedback.

Prior studies indicate that it is difficult for LLMs to generate
feedback on their own responses with in-context learning
(§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs"),
[7](#S7 "7 Summary of Our Analysis ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
However, most studies in intrinsic self-correction ([Madaan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib63); [Huang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42)) use simple prompts
for generating feedback, and there is room for improvement. A possible
direction to improve feedback is to apply (reference-free and
point-wise) LLM-based evaluation metrics. Recent approaches for
improving the model-based evaluation include using human-written
evaluation criteria ([Chiang and Lee, 2023](#bib.bib20); [Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib62)) and decomposing responses ([Saha et al.,
2024](#bib.bib86); [Min et al., 2023](#bib.bib69)). As another
direction, recent studies in self-correction propose frameworks using
the confidence in their responses, estimated by generation probabilities
([Varshney et al., 2023](#bib.bib102); [Jiang et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib47)), prompting ([Li et al., 2024a](#bib.bib55)), or
generating new questions from their answers to evaluate logical
consistency ([Jung et al., 2022](#bib.bib48); [Tafjord et al.,
2022](#bib.bib97); [Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib109)).

#### Unexplored Tasks.

The difficulty of self-evaluation differs from task to task
(§[4](#S4 "4 Self-Correction with Prompting ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")),
while many studies assume that verification is consistently easier than
generation. We expect that there are unexplored tasks in which intrinsic
self-correction works well, although self-correction research mostly
focuses on reasoning tasks such as math reasoning and coding ([Madaan
et al., 2023](#bib.bib63); [Gou et al., 2024](#bib.bib34); [Huang
et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42)). For example, LLM-based evaluation is often
studied in open-ended text generation, such as dialogue generation and
text summarization ([Fu et al., 2024](#bib.bib30); [Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib62)), suggesting that reasonable model-based feedback is
available for these tasks.

#### Fine-tuning on Small Training Data.

Fine-tuning of feedback generation often relies on large training data,
which requires large-scale human annotations
(§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Self-Correction with Fine-tuning ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
We expect future work to explore self-correction with smaller training
data. Although reinforcement learning ([Akyurek et al.,
2023](#bib.bib1)) or self-corrective learning ([Welleck et al.,
2023](#bib.bib107)) do not require human feedback, they require
reasonable reward functions for evaluating LLM responses, which are not
available in many tasks. For example, RL4F ([Akyurek et al.,
2023](#bib.bib1)) uses ROUGE as a reward function for text summarization
and action planning, which is sub-optimal.

#### Pre-training for Improving Self-Correction.

Prior studies show that large-scale fine-tuning on reference feedback
improves the self-correction capability of LLMs
(§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Self-Correction with Fine-tuning ‣ 5 Self-Correction with External Information ‣ When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes?A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs")).
This observation suggests that the current approach or datasets for
pre-training LLMs are insufficient to make LLMs acquire self-correction
capability. We expect future work to explore pre-training strategies to
improve the intrinsic self-correction capability of LLMs.

## 12 Emerging Trends and Recent Developments

This survey, originally published in June 2024, primarily focuses on
papers published before May 2024. However, to provide a broader
perspective, this section briefly highlights emerging trends and recent
advancements from June 2024 onward.

A recent trend of self-correction involves employing reinforcement
learning ([Kumar et al., 2024](#bib.bib52); [Qu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib83)). Specifically, OpenAI has published o1 ([OpenAI,
2024](#bib.bib75)), a model for reasoning tasks trained with
reinforcement learning to explore different strategies, recognize their
own mistakes, and refine their thinking process. OpenAI o1 has been
reported to outperform state-of-the-art LLMs in various reasoning tasks,
including Math Olympiad, PhD-level academic problems, competitive
programming, and Kaggle ([Chan et al., 2024b](#bib.bib8)).

## 13 Conclusion

We provide a critical survey of self-correction to identify in which
conditions LLMs can self-correct their mistakes. Our analysis reveals
that many studies fail to define their research questions clearly or
design experiments appropriately. To tackle these issues, we categorize
research questions and frameworks in self-correction research and
provide a checklist for conducting appropriate experiments.

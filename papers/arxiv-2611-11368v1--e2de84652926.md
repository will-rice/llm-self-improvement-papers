# BiVerify: Closing the Loop Between Backward and Forward Verification for Mathematical Self-Correction

Rui Zou    Xin Zhao    Mengqi Wei    Ji-Rong Wen\corresponding

###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have made substantial progress in
mathematical reasoning but still produce incorrect solutions to complex
problems, indicating the need for more reliable self-correction
mechanisms. Mathematical self-correction typically identifies errors
through verification, which can proceed backward or forward. The two
directions provide complementary verification capabilities. Backward
verification checks a candidate answer against the original constraints,
bypassing complex intermediate reasoning, but cannot ensure correct
reasoning or objective completeness. Forward verification follows the
problem conditions and current solution, enabling it to locate reasoning
errors and assess objective completeness, but may overlook key
constraints in complex solution chains. We propose BiVerify, a
dynamically scheduled framework for bidirectional mathematical
verification. BiVerify begins with backward verification to screen the
answer against the original constraints, followed by forward
verification of the reasoning process; subsequent direction switching,
state resetting, and re-verification are governed by verification
outcomes. Upon failure, the system revises the candidate or verification
flow until the same candidate passes both directions. We also introduce
shared mathematical tools that separate logical reasoning from precise
computation. We systematically evaluate BiVerify on five challenging
mathematical reasoning benchmarks using multiple mainstream DeepSeek,
GPT, and Gemini models. BiVerify exceeds the strongest representative
public baseline evaluated in this work by $`13.5`$ percentage points in
average accuracy, with the margin reaching $`31.2`$ percentage points on
GPT. Further analysis shows that BiVerify’s overall performance exceeds
that of either verification direction alone.

## Introduction

Figure 1: Complementarity between backward and forward verification in
detecting mathematical reasoning errors.

Large language models (LLMs) have made substantial progress in
mathematical reasoning ([Guo et al. 2025](#bib.bib11); [Luo et al.
2025](#bib.bib19); [Yu et al. 2025](#bib.bib32)), yet they still err on
complex problems ([Huang et al. 2024](#bib.bib13); [Tyen et al.
2024](#bib.bib28); [Yuan et al. 2026](#bib.bib33)). Strengthening their
ability to identify and correct such errors is therefore an important
research direction. Existing self-correction approaches revise LLM
outputs through iterative model-generated feedback in
Self-Refine ([Madaan et al. 2023](#bib.bib20)), natural-language
reflection in Reflexion ([Shinn et al. 2023](#bib.bib26)),
checklist-guided evaluation in TICK ([Cook et al. 2024](#bib.bib5)), and
program-driven verification and refinement in ProgCo ([Song et al.
2025](#bib.bib27)). Although these approaches construct feedback
differently, correction still relies on verifying whether the current
solution is correct.

Mathematical verification can proceed in two directions. Backward
verification starts from the candidate answer and checks it against the
original problem constraints. ProgCo provides a representative example
by using program-driven checks of a candidate response and execution
feedback to guide refinement ([Song et al. 2025](#bib.bib27)). This
direction can bypass lengthy or potentially flawed intermediate
reasoning, but passing the checked constraints does not guarantee that
the reasoning process is correct or that the solution objective is
complete. Forward verification instead follows the problem conditions
and the current solution, as exemplified by step-level process
feedback ([Lightman et al. 2024](#bib.bib15)). It can examine
intermediate reasoning, localize process-level errors, and assess
objective completeness. However, when the solution chain is long or the
verification flow is incomplete, forward verification may itself become
error-prone and miss key constraints. The two directions are therefore
complementary: forward verification examines intermediate reasoning and
objective completeness, whereas backward verification independently
checks the answer while bypassing complex intermediate processes.
Figure [1](#Sx1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Introduction ‣ BiVerify: Closing the Loop Between Backward and Forward Verification for Mathematical Self-Correction")
illustrates this complementarity through two examples.

This complementarity suggests a natural verification order: backward
verification first checks the candidate against the original
constraints, after which forward verification examines the reasoning
process. Beyond this order, prior work has paid less attention to how
backward and forward verification states should be coordinated as the
same candidate is iteratively revised. Revising a candidate invalidates
the verification results obtained for its previous version, whereas an
inadequate verification flow should be repaired without unnecessarily
changing the candidate. The system must therefore distinguish between
candidate revision and verification-flow revision, re-check the affected
direction, and accept a result only when both directions pass on the
same candidate.

Across five challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks and seven
DeepSeek, GPT, and Gemini settings, BiVerify improves seven-model
average accuracy by $`13.5`$ percentage points over the strongest
evaluated public baseline; this margin reaches $`31.2`$ percentage
points in the corresponding GPT setting. Ablations further show that
BiVerify corrects more erroneous answers than either direction alone
without materially increasing errors on correct answers, yielding
stronger overall performance.

Our contributions are as follows: (1) We identify complementary roles
for backward verification from a candidate answer and forward
verification from the solution process: the former bypasses complex
intermediate reasoning to check the answer against the original
constraints, whereas the latter verifies ordered key claims, the
reasoning process, and objective completeness; (2) We propose BiVerify,
which dynamically schedules both directions in a closed loop and
requires both to pass on the same candidate; and (3) Experiments on five
challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks and seven base-model
settings demonstrate BiVerify’s advantages over the evaluated public
baselines and either verification direction alone.

## Related Work

Three areas are most relevant to this work: LLM self-correction,
mathematical-reasoning verification, and tool-augmented mathematical
reasoning, which improve reliability through feedback, verification
targets, and external evidence, respectively.

Test-Time Improvement and Self-Correction of LLMs. LLM self-correction
improves inference-time outputs through feedback, reflection, or
repeated sampling. Self-Refine iteratively revises outputs using
self-generated feedback ([Madaan et al. 2023](#bib.bib20)); Reflexion
converts task feedback into linguistic reflections ([Shinn et al.
2023](#bib.bib26)); TICK structures evaluation with generated
checklists ([Cook et al. 2024](#bib.bib5)); and Self-Consistency selects
consensus across sampled reasoning paths ([Wang et al.
2023](#bib.bib29)). Pan et al. survey automatic correction
strategies ([Pan et al. 2024](#bib.bib23)), while Kamoi et al. summarize
conditions under which models can correct their errors ([Kamoi et al.
2024](#bib.bib14)). Huang et al. find that models may not improve
reliably without dependable external feedback ([Huang et al.
2024](#bib.bib13)); Tyen et al. show that LLMs often fail to locate
reasoning errors but can correct them when the error location is
provided ([Tyen et al. 2024](#bib.bib28)).

Verification in Mathematical Reasoning. Prior work targets either
candidate outputs or reasoning processes. Cobbe et al. train verifiers
to score and rank multiple outputs and select more reliable
results ([Cobbe et al. 2021](#bib.bib3)), but this selection paradigm
differs from our backward verification because it does not reason from a
candidate answer back to the original constraints. More directly
related, ProgCo reasons backward from an output answer to detect
contradictions and uses program-execution results for refinement ([Song
et al. 2025](#bib.bib27)). For process verification, Lightman et al. use
step-level supervision to provide finer-grained reasoning
signals ([Lightman et al. 2024](#bib.bib15)), while ProcessBench
evaluates process verifiers with first-error localization as a central
target ([Zheng et al. 2025](#bib.bib34)).

Tool-Augmented Mathematical Reasoning and Agents. Tool-augmented
reasoning uses programs, interpreters, or specialized tools to improve
exact computation and symbolic execution. PAL translates problems into
executable Python programs ([Gao et al. 2023](#bib.bib8));
Program-of-Thoughts separates computation from natural-language
reasoning ([Chen et al. 2023](#bib.bib2)); and ToRA alternates between
reasoning and external tools ([Gou et al. 2024](#bib.bib10)). For tool
use and orchestration, ReAct interleaves reasoning with external
actions ([Yao et al. 2023](#bib.bib31)), while Toolformer learns when
and how to call external APIs ([Schick et al. 2023](#bib.bib25)).
AgentMath integrates tools into mathematical reasoning agents ([Luo
et al. 2026](#bib.bib18)), and R1-Code-Interpreter supports computation
and verification in complex problems through a code interpreter ([Liu
et al. 2026b](#bib.bib17)).

Prior work has advanced self-correction, candidate scoring, directional
verification, and tool-augmented reasoning, but has paid less attention
to coordinating backward and forward verification states as the same
candidate is revised. BiVerify organizes both directions and shared tool
evidence into a closed loop over that candidate.

## Method

BiVerify performs verification after the model has generated a complete
initial solution. Given a mathematical problem $`q`$ and initial
solution $`y_{0}`$, BiVerify extracts an answer and maintains
$`C_{t}=(q,y_{t},a_{t})`$ at verification event $`t`$, where $`y_{t}`$
and $`a_{t}`$ are the current complete solution and final answer. As
Figure [2](#Sx3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Method ‣ BiVerify: Closing the Loop Between Backward and Forward Verification for Mathematical Self-Correction")
shows, backward and forward verification operate on the same candidate,
while a scheduler coordinates direction switching, candidate updates,
and verification-flow refinement according to verification outcomes.
Both directions share mathematical tools, and acceptance requires the
same candidate to pass both. The complete pseudocode for BiVerify is
provided in Supplementary
Algorithm [C](#A3 "Appendix C Complete Pseudocode for BiVerify ‣ BiVerify: Closing the Loop Between Backward and Forward Verification for Mathematical Self-Correction").

Figure 2: Overview of BiVerify. The system performs backward and forward
verification on the same candidate, while the scheduler coordinates
direction switching, candidate updates, state resetting, and joint
acceptance. Dashed lines indicate on-demand calls to the shared
mathematical tools.

### Backward Verification

Backward verification starts from $`a_{t}`$, checks it against the
original conditions in $`q`$, and retains $`y_{t}`$ as context. It asks
whether $`a_{t}`$ satisfies the necessary conditions.

It first constructs a backward verification flow:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       V_{t}^{B}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{B}(q,a_{t}).
       ```                                                  |     |

Here, $`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{B}`$ is the backward
verification-flow agent, and $`V_{t}^{B}`$ specifies how to check
$`a_{t}`$ against the necessary constraints in $`q`$. Depending on the
problem, $`V_{t}^{B}`$ may substitute $`a_{t}`$; check equations,
identities, domains, ranges, or feasibility; and test counting, extrema,
or other answer-related conditions. These examples are not exhaustive.

The backward flow and adjudication agents may invoke the shared
mathematical tool agent $`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{tool}}`$, which
dispatches requests to

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{\mathrm{sym}},T_{\mathrm{eq}},T_{\mathrm{enum}},T_{\mathrm{con}},T_{\mathrm{arith}},\ldots\},
``` |  |

where $`T_{\mathrm{sym}}`$, $`T_{\mathrm{eq}}`$, $`T_{\mathrm{enum}}`$,
$`T_{\mathrm{con}}`$, and $`T_{\mathrm{arith}}`$ denote symbolic
simplification, equation solving, enumeration and counting, constraint
checking, and exact computation. Backward verification mainly uses
$`T_{\mathrm{eq}}`$, $`T_{\mathrm{con}}`$, and $`T_{\mathrm{arith}}`$
for answer-related conditions. SymPy and Z3 implement symbolic
computation and constraint solving, respectively ([Meurer et al.
2017](#bib.bib21); [de Moura and Bjørner 2008](#bib.bib6)). Tool outputs
provide evidence, not verdicts; subsequent agent arguments omit
$`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{tool}}`$ and $`\mathcal{T}`$ for brevity.

Given candidate $`C_{t}`$ and flow $`V_{t}^{B}`$, backward adjudication
returns

|     |                                                                    |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       (B_{t},E_{t}^{B})=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ver}}^{B}(C_{t},V_{t}^{B}),
       ```                                                                 |     |

where $`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ver}}^{B}`$ is the backward adjudication
agent, $`B_{t}`$ the verdict, and $`E_{t}^{B}`$ the verification trace,
tool outputs, and failure evidence. The verdict is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
B_{t}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Pass},&\text{all required checks pass},\\
\mathrm{Fail},&\text{a required check fails}.\end{cases}
``` |  |

On failure, $`E_{t}^{B}`$ records the conflict between the answer and
original constraints—such as a violated equation, constraint, range, or
numerical relation—with the corresponding tool outputs. The scheduler
converts $`E_{t}^{B}`$ into feedback for revising $`C_{t}`$ or
$`V_{t}^{B}`$.

### Forward Verification

Forward verification takes $`q`$, $`y_{t}`$, and $`a_{t}`$ as input and
checks whether the answer follows from correct, complete reasoning over
the problem conditions and current solution. Rather than testing
constraints around the answer, it examines how the answer follows from
those conditions and intermediate reasoning.

It first constructs ordered key claims and a corresponding verification
flow:

|     |                                                                   |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       (K_{t},V_{t}^{F})=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{F}(q,y_{t},a_{t}),
       ```                                                                |     |

where

|     |                                            |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       K_{t}=(c_{t,1},c_{t,2},\ldots,c_{t,n_{t}})
       ```                                         |     |

Here, $`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{flow}}^{F}`$ is the forward
verification-flow agent; $`K_{t}`$ contains $`n_{t}`$ claims in their
original order; and $`V_{t}^{F}`$ specifies their verification order,
objectives, and on-demand tool tasks.

The forward flow and adjudication agents may likewise invoke
$`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{tool}}`$, mainly for symbolic simplification,
equation solving, enumeration and counting, and exact computation when
checking intermediate claims and completeness; tool outputs accompany
the evidence.

Key claims may cover algebraic or geometric derivations, numerical or
symbolic calculations, condition transformations, domain or constraint
derivations, intermediate conclusions, and the final answer.
Requirements for all solutions, complete counts, exhaustive
enumerations, complete sets, or global extrema are likewise included in
$`K_{t}`$ rather than handled by separate final-answer post-processing.

Forward verification checks claims in order. Given $`q`$ and prior
verified claims, the idealized objective for $`c_{t,i}`$ is

|     |                                                          |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       q\land c_{t,1}\land\cdots\land c_{t,i-1}\models c_{t,i}.
       ```                                                       |     |

Here, $`\models`$ denotes semantic entailment: a claim passes if every
case satisfying the problem conditions and prior verified claims also
satisfies it.

If the first $`k_{t}-1`$ claims pass and claim $`k_{t}`$ fails, then

|     |             |     |
|-----|-------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       c_{t,k_{t}}
       ```          |     |

is the first failed claim. Because later reasoning may depend on it,
subsequent claims are not treated as reliably supported; this claim and
its evidence guide correction. A failure may instead indicate an
inadequate verification flow, so the scheduler distinguishes candidate
from flow errors.

Forward adjudication returns

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(F_{t},E_{t}^{F},k_{t})=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ver}}^{F}(C_{t},K_{t},V_{t}^{F}),
``` |  |

where $`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ver}}^{F}`$ is the forward adjudication
agent, $`F_{t}`$ is its verdict, $`E_{t}^{F}`$ contains the verification
trace and tool evidence, and $`k_{t}`$ is the first failed-claim
position, omitted on a pass:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F_{t}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Pass},&\text{all claims pass; objective complete},\\
\mathrm{Fail},&\text{a claim fails or objective incomplete}.\end{cases}
``` |  |

On failure, the first failed claim $`c_{t,k_{t}}`$ and evidence
$`E_{t}^{F}`$ guide revision of the candidate $`C_{t}`$ or forward flow
$`V_{t}^{F}`$; the scheduler handles candidate updates, state resets,
and direction selection.

### Bidirectional Verification Scheduler

The bidirectional scheduler $`\mathcal{S}`$ closes the two directions
over the same candidate. Each adjudication agent returns a directional
verdict, shared mathematical tools provide only evidence, and
$`\mathcal{S}`$ determines acceptance.

BiVerify extracts the initial answer from $`q`$ and $`y_{0}`$:

|     |                                      |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       a_{0}=\operatorname{Extract}(y_{0}).
       ```                                   |     |

For the current candidate $`C_{t}`$, the scheduling state is

|     |                                       |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Sigma_{t}=(C_{t},B_{t},F_{t},d_{t}),
       ```                                    |     |

where $`B_{t}`$ and $`F_{t}`$ are its backward and forward verification
states:

|     |                                                                 |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       B_{t},F_{t}\in\{\mathrm{Unknown},\mathrm{Pass},\mathrm{Fail}\}.
       ```                                                              |     |

$`\mathrm{Unknown}`$ means the direction is unverified or a candidate
change invalidated its prior state; $`\mathrm{Pass}`$ and
$`\mathrm{Fail}`$ record the outcome. States are candidate-specific and
cannot transfer to a new candidate.

The current direction is

|     |                                    |     |
|-----|------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       d_{t}\in\{\mathrm{B},\mathrm{F}\},
       ```                                 |     |

where $`\mathrm{B}`$ and $`\mathrm{F}`$ denote backward and forward
verification. Acceptance requires both directions to pass:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Accept}(C_{t})\iff B_{t}=\mathrm{Pass}\land F_{t}=\mathrm{Pass}.
``` |  |

Both pass states must belong to the same $`C_{t}`$; their event indices
may differ, but states combine only when bound to one candidate. If

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{i}\neq C_{j},\qquad B_{i}=\mathrm{Pass},\qquad F_{j}=\mathrm{Pass},
``` |  |

then neither candidate has completed bidirectional verification,
necessitating candidate updates and state resets.

Initially,

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle C_{0}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(q,y_{0},a_{0}),`$ | $`\displaystyle B_{0}`$ | $`\displaystyle=F_{0}=\mathrm{Unknown},`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle d_{0}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathrm{B},`$ |  |  |  |

so verification begins in the backward direction.

Let $`s_{t}\in\{\mathrm{Pass},\mathrm{Fail}\}`$ be the current verdict,
which the scheduler records in $`B_{t}`$ or $`F_{t}`$ according to
$`d_{t}`$. On failure, it invokes the verification-state and
candidate-update agent with the candidate, evidence, and direction:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{C}_{t}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{update}}(C_{t},E_{t}^{d_{t}},d_{t}),
``` |  |

$`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{update}}`$ converts the failure evidence
$`E_{t}^{d_{t}}`$ into feedback, revises the solution $`y_{t}`$, and
re-extracts its answer to produce a proposal $`\widehat{C}_{t}`$.
Feedback may include a failed constraint or the first failed forward
claim $`c_{t,k_{t}}`$, tool outputs, and a correction direction. The
proposal $`\widehat{C}_{t}`$ is returned to the scheduler
$`\mathcal{S}`$, which adopts it only if valid:
$`\widehat{C}_{t}=C_{t}`$ denotes no adoption, whereas
$`\widehat{C}_{t}\neq C_{t}`$ denotes adoption. The proposal is unused
when $`s_{t}=\mathrm{Pass}`$.

The scheduler follows

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\Sigma_{t},s_{t},\widehat{C}_{t})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\mathrm{Accept},&B_{t}=F_{t}=\mathrm{Pass},\\[2.84526pt]
\mathrm{Switch},&s_{t}=\mathrm{Pass}\land\neg\operatorname{Accept}(C_{t}),\\[2.84526pt]
\mathrm{Update},&s_{t}=\mathrm{Fail}\land\widehat{C}_{t}\neq C_{t},\\[2.84526pt]
\mathrm{Refine},&s_{t}=\mathrm{Fail}\land\widehat{C}_{t}=C_{t}.\end{cases}`$ |  |

Here, $`\mathrm{Accept}`$ accepts the candidate and terminates;
$`\mathrm{Switch}`$ moves to the opposite direction
$`\operatorname{Other}(d_{t})`$; $`\mathrm{Update}`$ adopts the new
candidate, resets both states, and re-verifies the current direction;
and $`\mathrm{Refine}`$ revises its verification flow before retrying
it. Acceptance is terminal, not a value of $`d_{t}`$.

Under $`\mathrm{Update}`$, the scheduler adopts the revision:

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       C_{t+1}=(q,y_{t+1},a_{t+1})=\widehat{C}_{t}.
       ```                                           |     |

Because prior states belong to the previous candidate, both reset:

|     |                                   |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       B_{t+1}=F_{t+1}=\mathrm{Unknown}.
       ```                                |     |

A flow bound to the previous candidate must also be regenerated or
revised, while the failed direction is retained:

|     |                |     |
|-----|----------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       d_{t+1}=d_{t},
       ```             |     |

to confirm that the revision resolves the problem.

Under $`\mathrm{Refine}`$, no new candidate is adopted:

|     |                |     |
|-----|----------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       C_{t+1}=C_{t}.
       ```             |     |

Failure may instead reflect omitted checks, a misspecified objective,
insufficient tool tasks, or a flow–candidate mismatch. Accordingly,
$`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{update}}`$ uses $`C_{t}`$ and $`E_{t}^{d_{t}}`$
to revise and retry that flow:

|     |                |     |
|-----|----------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       d_{t+1}=d_{t}.
       ```             |     |

This separates solution from flow revision and prevents premature
switching.

If the budget ends before $`B_{t}=F_{t}=\mathrm{Pass}`$, the
implementation may return the latest $`C_{t}`$ as a budget-limited
output.

## Experiments

We analyze BiVerify at three levels: overall benchmark and model
performance; comparisons with ProgCo and ProgCo-Py by iteration budget,
empirical difficulty, and correction behavior; and internal mechanisms
through unidirectional components, event-level accuracy, directional
roles, and correction stability. These analyses assess its
effectiveness, sources of advantage, and operation.

### Experimental Setup

Datasets. We evaluate five challenging benchmarks totaling 170 problems.
AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 each contain 30 American Invitational
Mathematics Examination problems testing multi-step reasoning and exact
computation. BRUMO 2025 has 30 problems with complex conditions and
multi-step reasoning; HMMT February 2025 has 30 spanning algebra,
geometry, combinatorics, and number theory ([Hochlehnert et al.
2025](#bib.bib12); [Pei et al. 2025](#bib.bib24)). AMO Bench has 50
Olympiad-level problems with diverse problem and answer formats ([Liu
et al. 2026a](#bib.bib16)). We abbreviate them as A24, A25, B25, HMM,
and AMO; cross-benchmark Avg values are sample-weighted.

Base Models. We use seven DeepSeek, GPT, and Gemini settings:
DeepSeek-Flash, DeepSeek-Flash-Think, and DeepSeek-Pro ([DeepSeek-AI
2026](#bib.bib7)); GPT-5.4-Mini and GPT-5.4-Mini-Think ([OpenAI
2026](#bib.bib22)); and Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite and
Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite-Think ([Comanici et al. 2025](#bib.bib4); [Google
AI for Developers 2026](#bib.bib9)). We abbreviate them as DS-Flash,
DS-Flash-Think, DS-Pro, GPT-Mini, GPT-Mini-Think, Gemini-Lite, and
Gemini-Lite-Think. They span standard and thinking modes, stronger
variants, model families, and initial capabilities.

Compared Methods. We compare three public-baseline categories.
Natural-language correction includes Reflex, following ProgCo’s
vanilla-reflex setting ([Song et al. 2025](#bib.bib27)); Self-Refine
with iterative self-feedback ([Madaan et al. 2023](#bib.bib20));
Self-Reflection with reflective feedback ([Shinn et al.
2023](#bib.bib26)); and CheckList with checklist-guided
correction ([Cook et al. 2024](#bib.bib5)). CoT-Tool uses multi-path
chain-of-thought sampling and consistency selection ([Abedi Firouzjaei
2025](#bib.bib1); [Wei et al. 2022](#bib.bib30); [Wang et al.
2023](#bib.bib29)). Verification-enhanced baselines are ProgCo, with
program-driven verification and refinement, and ProgCo-Py, with
Python-tool feedback ([Song et al. 2025](#bib.bib27)). We also introduce
Backward-only and Forward-only, which use only the respective direction,
while full BiVerify follows its scheduler. These component ablations
isolate each direction under identical base-model, initial-response, and
iteration-budget settings. Figures and tables use Backward-only and
Forward-only; execution directions use Backward and Forward.

For each base-model–problem pair, we pre-generate an initial solution
and extract its answer. All methods except CoT-Tool share this initial
solution and extracted answer; CoT-Tool samples three reasoning paths
from the original problem. Within each setting, methods use the same
model identifier and reasoning mode, with temperature $`0`$ when
applicable. All iterative methods use $`\texttt{max\_turn}=3`$. BiVerify
starts backward, and log indices $`t=0,\ldots,3`$ represent at most four
actual verification events. Supplementary
Tables [2](#A1.T2 "Table 2 ‣ A.1 Complete Run Configuration and Reproducibility Details ‣ Appendix A Experimental Settings and Detailed Results ‣ BiVerify: Closing the Loop Between Backward and Forward Verification for Mathematical Self-Correction")
and [3](#A1.T3 "Table 3 ‣ A.1 Complete Run Configuration and Reproducibility Details ‣ Appendix A Experimental Settings and Detailed Results ‣ BiVerify: Closing the Loop Between Backward and Forward Verification for Mathematical Self-Correction")
report shared configurations and model identifiers.

Evaluation Metrics. Our primary metric is final-answer accuracy. Empty
outputs, extraction failures, and reference mismatches count as
incorrect under the original benchmark-size denominator. For each model,
Avg is sample-weighted across benchmarks, and Mean averages Avg across
all seven settings. Let $`C`$ and $`W`$ denote correct and incorrect
initial or current answers. Then $`W\rightarrow C`$ is correction,
$`C\rightarrow W`$ is damage to an initially correct answer, and
$`N_{W\rightarrow C}-N_{C\rightarrow W}`$ is net correction gain. Event
analysis follows log order; after early termination, the terminal answer
is carried forward, retaining 1,190 model–problem instances at every
stage.

## Conclusion

We introduced BiVerify, a verification-feedback-driven framework for
bidirectional mathematical verification. BiVerify applies
answer-centered backward verification and process-centered forward
verification to the same candidate and forms a closed loop through
direction switching, state resetting, and re-verification, accepting
only candidates that pass in both directions. Both directions use shared
mathematical tools for operations such as symbolic computation and
equation solving. Experiments on five challenging mathematical reasoning
benchmarks and seven base-model settings show that BiVerify achieves
higher overall accuracy than backward-only verification, forward-only
verification, and the evaluated public baselines. Its main gains come
from broader error-correction coverage without materially increasing
damage to initially correct answers. In principle, BiVerify can be
extended to tasks with both verifiable final outputs and inspectable
intermediate processes. Future work will explore its application to
scientific reasoning and code generation.

Method Budgets and Tool Use. All iterative correction and verification
methods use an outer budget of $`\texttt{max\_turn}=3`$, while CoT-Tool
generates three reasoning paths and applies consistency-based selection
according to its method definition. This control runs the compared
methods with the same base model, reasoning mode, and predefined outer
budget, but does not force identical internal model-call or tool-call
structures because these steps are part of each method’s definition.
Both verification directions and their mathematical branches in BiVerify
use the same base model corresponding to the current experiment; the
shared Python mathematical tools provide execution results and do not
replace the base model.

BiVerify is evaluated without an extensive hyperparameter sweep. We
compare $`\texttt{max\_turn}\in\{1,3\}`$ in the iteration-budget
analysis and use $`\texttt{max\_turn}=3`$ for the main comparisons,
thereby imposing a common outer-iteration limit on BiVerify and the
iterative baselines. Unspecified settings follow the corresponding
method implementation or model API defaults.

Run variation is controlled by supplying the same pregenerated starting
response and candidate answer to methods that operate from an existing
solution within each base-model–problem pair. The official execution
path contains no randomized local sampling, shuffling, or preprocessing,
so a local random seed is not needed. We retain one official run for
every model–method–problem combination and include the raw outputs
underlying the reported metrics in the supplementary material.

Unified Answer Evaluation. Final answers from all methods are evaluated
using the same three-stage equivalence procedure. We first perform
normalized exact-string matching. If an answer can be parsed
numerically, we additionally apply floating-point comparison with a
tolerance of $`10^{-3}`$. When the first two stages are inconclusive, we
use DeepSeek-Flash as an answer-equivalence evaluator with temperature
$`0`$. For set-valued, multi-solution, or sequential answers, evaluation
also checks element completeness and any required ordering constraints.
The five benchmarks use fixed denominators of 30, 30, 30, 30, and 50,
respectively. Empty outputs, unextractable answers, and failures after
exhausting retries remain in the denominator and are counted as
incorrect rather than skipped.

### A.3 Statistical Significance

We conduct a paired statistical analysis using the results from seven
base-model settings on the same 170 problems. BiVerify improves average
accuracy over Self-Refine, the strongest evaluated public baseline
overall, by 13.53 percentage points. Using 200,000 BCa bootstrap
resamples at the problem level, we obtain a 95% confidence interval of
\[11.01, 16.30\] percentage points for this improvement. The paired test
yields $`p<0.001`$. These results support BiVerify’s overall accuracy
improvement under the evaluated settings.

Figure [7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ B.2 Correction Behavior versus Tool-Augmented Baselines ‣ Appendix B Supplementary Process Analysis ‣ BiVerify: Closing the Loop Between Backward and Forward Verification for Mathematical Self-Correction")
decomposes the final results of ProgCo, ProgCo-Py, and BiVerify into
four state transitions. All three methods face the same 543 initially
correct and 647 initially incorrect instances, making their transition
rates directly comparable.

BiVerify achieves a $`W\!\rightarrow\!C`$ correction rate of $`40.3\%`$,
exceeding ProgCo’s $`9.4\%`$ and ProgCo-Py’s $`12.2\%`$ by $`30.9`$ and
$`28.1`$ percentage points, respectively. Meanwhile, their
$`C\!\rightarrow\!W`$ damage rates are $`3.7\%`$, $`4.2\%`$, and
$`4.1\%`$, indicating that BiVerify does not materially increase damage
to initially correct answers while correcting more errors. In absolute
counts, ProgCo, ProgCo-Py, and BiVerify correct 61, 79, and 261
initially incorrect instances and damage 20, 23, and 22 initially
correct instances, yielding net correction counts of 41, 56, and 239.
BiVerify’s primary advantage over the programmatic-verification
baselines therefore comes from broader error coverage rather than more
aggressive indiscriminate rewriting.

### B.3 Correction Retention and Trajectory Stability

Figure [8](#A2.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ B.3 Correction Retention and Trajectory Stability ‣ Appendix B Supplementary Process Analysis ‣ BiVerify: Closing the Loop Between Backward and Forward Verification for Mathematical Self-Correction")
presents the complete classification of multi-event correctness
trajectories. By retaining intermediate event states, it distinguishes
behaviors that final $`C/W`$ transitions alone cannot identify,
including stable repairs, fluctuations after repair, lost temporary
repairs, and recovery after damage.

Among the 261 final $`W\rightarrow C`$ instances, 248 remain correct
after first becoming correct, while 13 fluctuate during the process but
are correct at the end; stable repairs thus account for $`95.0\%`$.
Another 27 instances are temporarily corrected but ultimately become
incorrect again, and 16 recover after being damaged at an intermediate
event. Including temporary corrections, 288 initially incorrect
instances become correct after at least one event, of which 261 remain
correct at the end, corresponding to a final retention rate of
$`90.6\%`$. The overall gain therefore comes primarily from corrections
retained to the end, although later events can introduce limited
fluctuations.

### B.4 Actual Scheduling Paths

differences among paths are descriptive rather than causal effects under
randomized control. Nevertheless, the path distribution shows that
BiVerify can terminate samples that reach consistent judgments early
while allocating more verification events to samples that retain failure
signals.

# **Athanor: An Agent-Native System for Autonomous Post-Training Research**

**Yufa Zhou**<sup>1</sup>,2,‡,† , **Yichen Wang**<sup>3</sup> , **Jiakun Fan**<sup>1</sup>,<sup>4</sup> , **Ziqi Shu**<sup>1</sup> , **Zhichao Liu**<sup>1</sup> , **Anru R. Zhang**<sup>2</sup> , **Neiwen Ling**<sup>1</sup>,† , **Zijun Wei**<sup>1</sup>,†

ByteDance, Duke University, The University of Chicago, Virginia Tech

> ‡Project lead, †Corresponding authors

### **Abstract**

Recursive self-improvement, in which AI systems build stronger AI systems, is one promising route to superintelligence. We study a weaker form that can be measured today: frontier agents post-training a small raw base model with public data, as a test of their research potential. We build **Athanor**, an open agent-native system for **autonomous post-training research** (AutoPostTrain) on real GPUs. We give the agents full research autonomy, low-friction engineering tools, and efficient starter code. The agents carry out the research themselves: they read datasets and evaluation results, build their training data, write and modify training algorithms, tune hyperparameters, and analyze the results to decide what to try next. They work in a sandbox and cannot use external teacher models. Athanor's services supply frozen evaluation, managed jobs, decontaminated data, a persistent research record, and a held-out suite the agents are unaware of. In a 104-hour campaign, a team of four Claude Opus 5.5 agents, each on an eight-GPU node and given no research advice, improves Qwen3-4B-Base from **15.9** to **67.3** on a six-task development suite and from **9.2** to **48.2** on a held-out suite, against **66.5** and **47.9** for the official Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that a team of frontier agents can autonomously post-train a raw base model into a cross-domain generalist model that **matches or surpasses the official instruct version** on average. The campaign's full record shows that the agents reach this level by recombining known methods and each other's models: SFT on public reasoning data, RL with a rollout change one agent introduced, weight merges across agents, and rounds of self-distillation. With execution handled, today's agents already have the engineering and research ability to conquer this task. We hope Athanor provides a foundation for scaling autonomous AI research.

#### <span id="page-1-1"></span>1 Introduction

Recursive self-improvement (RSI), in which an AI system uses its own capabilities to build a more capable successor [23, 57], is one of the proposed routes to superhuman intelligence [20]. Its early forms are already reported [2, 19]: AI systems write most of the code inside the companies that build them [8], and agents carry out a growing share of the research [50]. For language models, the part of this loop open to study is post-training: pretraining establishes the knowledge foundation [24, 27, 33], while post-training shapes instruction following, reasoning, coding, and alignment [25, 35, 36, 49, 72]. Yet post-training research remains an expensive iterative search, and published reports typically document the final recipe rather than the full research trajectory that produced it [14, 22, 54, 73]. Search over data mixtures, objectives, algorithms, and hyperparameters is an evidence-driven loop that agents have begun to automate [13, 43, 48] and benchmarks have begun to evaluate [9, 44, 66].

We study **autonomous post-training research** (AutoPostTrain), where a frontier agent diagnoses a base model's weaknesses, runs training experiments at scale, and iteratively refines its recipes from the evidence. Existing work studies narrower versions of this problem. PostTrainBench and Locus have agents train a separate _specialist_ for each benchmark [29, 55], and other benchmarks and systems isolate fine-tuning, reinforcement-learning engineering, data-centric research, or structured agent workflows [12, 17, 38, 46, 75]. These works also allow teacher models or do not keep a held-out evaluation sealed from the agents. Our setting takes the harder choice on every one of these points: one _generalist_ checkpoint must improve on six capabilities at once, starting from a raw base model, with no teacher model, and it is scored on a held-out suite that the agents never see, which tests the same capabilities on other questions.

AutoPostTrain is expensive: one hypothesis can take hours of failure-prone GPU execution before its checkpoint can be evaluated. It is also hard to keep honest: a search that runs for days must keep its training data clean and its final evaluation sealed [64, 69]. For an agent to do this research, experiments must be easy to

We build **Athanor** (Figure 2), an open agent-native system that enables frontier agents to hill-climb a raw base model through long-running campaigns of training experiments on real GPUs. Our design thesis is that, as agent intelligence improves, the bottleneck in autonomous research shifts from generating plausible ideas to the friction of executing and verifying them: without managed execution, an agent spends hours debugging training infrastructure and rebuilds the same machinery for every experiment. Athanor therefore makes post-training research programmable: it gives research its atomic operations (starting a job, evaluating a checkpoint, bringing in data), the agent composes them in its own code, and the environment carries them out. The system is agent-native in that the agent does research with its own coding and analysis tools. The central design question is how to empower agents, not imprison them: the agents keep every research decision, while experiment execution stays efficient, auditable, and recoverable.

Three design principles make this work. First, separate research decisions from execution: the agent inspects data and model behavior, writes its own data and training programs, and decides what to run; the environment runs, measures, and recovers every experiment. Second, externalize the research state: every request, result, checkpoint lineage, transcript, and note stays in a durable record, so a session can be resumed or replaced, several agents can work as a team [51], and every campaign can be audited. Third, keep the research loop productive: jobs run in the background and each result reaches the agent the moment it arrives, so an agent keeps several experiments in flight.

We evaluate Athanor in a **large-scale post-training** campaign. Four agents (Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code [3]) form a team, each on one node of eight GPUs, and start from Qwen3-4B-Base with public data, no teacher-model calls, and no research advice from humans. In 104 hours the team raises the base from 15.9 to 67.3 on a six-task development suite and from 9.2 to 48.2 on a held-out suite of different questions for the

same capabilities, which the agents never know exists; the vendor's Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507[1](#page-3-0) scores 66.5 and 47.9 under the same evaluator [\(Figure 1,](#page-1-0) [Table 1\)](#page-7-0). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first public result in which a team of AI agents, working autonomously, post-trains a raw base model into one cross-domain generalist model at the level of the vendor's instruct release, confirmed on the held-out suite. Earlier results at this level come from a human team's recipe, Tülu 3, which generated training data with stronger models [\[36\]](#page-19-1), or from agents that train one specialist per benchmark or call teacher models [\[16,](#page-17-10) [29\]](#page-18-7); other agent systems report gains on tasks seen during development or on one task family [\[47,](#page-19-9) [61\]](#page-20-6). [Section 7](#page-15-0) and [Appendix B](#page-24-0) compare the agent systems. The vendor describes building its small Qwen3 models by distillation from its own larger flagship models [\[72\]](#page-21-0); the agents reach the same level from public data, much of which was itself distilled from stronger models by its publishers [\[1,](#page-17-11) [36,](#page-19-1) [73\]](#page-21-1).

We then analyze the full record of the campaign, including the agents' transcripts and notes, and find [\(Section 5\)](#page-10-0): (1) recombination: the recipe chains known methods (supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, weight merging, and self-distillation), and the agents contribute the composition and the small changes that make it work; (2) team progress: ideas and checkpoints move between agents within hours, and every new best after hour 21 combines the training of several agents; (3) recipe convergence: within the first hour of the campaign, all four agents build on the same public corpus of reasoning traces, and the alternatives they try later score lower and are dropped; and (4) uneven data sense: the agents catch problems of format, length, scorability, and difficulty in their data but rarely check whether its answers are correct. Taken together, the agents' engineering, planning, reasoning, and knowledge of post-training already suffice to take a raw base model to the level of the vendor's instruct release. Once the friction of execution is removed, the clearest limit we observe is how many different ideas they try: four copies of one agent model start from the same prior, converge on one recipe early, and test few alternatives. How the output of this research, the best checkpoint and the understanding of post-training behind it, grows with the compute the agents spend is what we call **autoresearch scaling** [\(Section 2\)](#page-3-1).

In summary, this paper makes three contributions:

- **Athanor**, an open agent-native system for post-training research on real GPUs, with frozen evaluation, managed jobs, decontaminated data, and a research record that links every score to the recipe behind it.
- **Instruct-level post-training by a team of agents**: in 104 hours, and for about \$12,000 in all (GPU time at rental prices and the agents' inference), four agents post-train Qwen3-4B-Base into one crossdomain generalist model that matches or surpasses the vendor's instruct release (67.3 against 66.5 on the development suite and 48.2 against 47.9 on a held-out suite), without a teacher model.
- **Four findings on how the agents do research**, from the campaign's full record (500 jobs, 255 requested full evaluations, and the lineage of every checkpoint): they recombine known methods, progress as a team, converge early on one recipe, and inspect the form of their data but rarely its correctness.

# <span id="page-3-1"></span>**2 On AutoPostTrain**

### <span id="page-3-2"></span>**2.1 Problem Formulation**

Given a base model and an experimental budget, AutoPostTrain searches over executable recipes r, each specifying data, objective, method and program, hyperparameters, stage order, and initial checkpoint. Executing the recipe yields a checkpoint M = Exec(r). Since a recipe may start from any completed checkpoint, the search branches into lineages that compose supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, on-policy distillation, and merging [\[25,](#page-18-4) [35,](#page-18-5) [36,](#page-19-1) [72,](#page-21-0) [73\]](#page-21-1). Let Pt−<sup>1</sup> denote the pool of checkpoints available before decision t, with P<sup>0</sup> containing only the base model, and let R(Pt−1) denote the recipes admissible on this pool. Let the search objective c(M) denote the latent capability of checkpoint M; it is not directly observable and is estimated by evaluation on a finite benchmark suite B. A researcher agent acts as a policy π over recipes, conditioned on the history Ht−<sup>1</sup> of recipes, programs,

<span id="page-3-0"></span><sup>1</sup><https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507>, the vendor's July 2025 instruction-tuned update of Qwen3-4B.

measurements, failures, and notes. The AutoPostTrain loop is:

```
RESEARCH r_t \sim \pi(\cdot \mid \mathcal{H}_{t-1}), \qquad r_t \in \mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P}_{t-1}),
EXPERIMENT M_t = \operatorname{Exec}(r_t), \qquad \widehat{\mathbf{p}}_t = \operatorname{Eval}_{\mathcal{B}}(M_t; \boldsymbol{\xi}_t),
OBJECTIVE M_T^{\star} \in \underset{M \in \mathcal{P}_T}{\operatorname{arg max}} c(M). (AutoPostTrain)
```

At step t, the policy proposes a recipe, execution produces a checkpoint, and evaluation returns its benchmark score vector $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_t$ , where $\boldsymbol{\xi}_t$ denotes evaluation randomness. The pool grows as $\mathcal{P}_t = \mathcal{P}_{t-1} \cup \{M_t\}$ , and the result and notes enter $\mathcal{H}_t$ . After T completed experiments, the objective $M_T^{\star}$ is the checkpoint of highest latent capability in the final pool, whereas the researcher observes only $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_t$ . Because $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_t$ holds one score per benchmark, a recipe can raise one capability while lowering another, which makes the observed search multi-objective [58]. Several researchers can run this loop at once as a team: researchers $\pi_1, \ldots, \pi_K$ each propose recipes, $r_t^{(k)} \sim \pi_k(\cdot \mid \mathcal{H}_{t-1})$ , from one shared history $\mathcal{H}$ and one shared pool $\mathcal{P}$ , so each reads the others' recipes, results, and notes, and may start from their checkpoints.

#### <span id="page-4-1"></span>2.2 What Makes AutoPostTrain Hard

Combinatorial, state-dependent search. In the RESEARCH step of (AutoPostTrain), the agent chooses a recipe from a high-dimensional, compositional space: discrete choices of data and method, continuous hyperparameters, and conditional training stages. Gradient descent optimizes parameters within a stage; the researcher decides the order of training stages and their starting checkpoints. The choice is state-dependent: each stage trains the checkpoint produced by earlier stages, so upstream stages condition what later stages can achieve [79], and each new checkpoint enlarges the admissible set $\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{P}_t)$ . Since the benchmark scores that serve as feedback are not differentiable with respect to the recipe, the researcher can only propose a promising recipe, observe a noisy score, and keep the change if the score improves: the outer loop is **stochastic black-box hill climbing** [56].

What lets an agent do better than a blind hill climber is its prior and the feedback it can reason over. A researcher agent's pretrained knowledge acts as a strong _scientific prior_ that concentrates proposals in a small effective region of the recipe space, and it can reason about why a change helped. To choose the next recipe within that region, it needs feedback it can act on. Because a recipe's effect depends on its starting checkpoint, each score is useful only if it is linked to the full lineage that produced it.

Delayed, noisy, and incomplete feedback. The EXPERIMENT step of (AutoPostTrain) returns a checkpoint and its full benchmark evaluation results. Post-training has no cheap proxy for this result: NanoGPT speedrunning can compare runs by a common validation cross-entropy target [32], but post-training cannot, because its stages optimize losses on different scales, and even in pretraining, similar loss values can correspond to different downstream performance [11, 42]. To evaluate a recipe, we must first train it, so there is a delay $\tau_{\text{fb},t} = \tau_{\text{exec}}(r_t) + \tau_{\text{eval}}(M_t,\mathcal{B})$ , the time to prepare data and train plus the time to evaluate the checkpoint on $\mathcal{B}$ . The score is noisy: finite evaluation can blur the order of nearby candidates [45]. It is also incomplete: each benchmark covers only part of its capability, and IFEval's verifiable constraints, for example, cover only part of instruction following [83].

Scaling the research process. Pretraining scaling laws relate loss to compute for a fixed training procedure [27, 33], and similar curves can be fit for RL post-training once the recipe is fixed [34]. If the recipe changes, no stable law is known that relates post-training compute to downstream performance. Here the recipe is what the agent searches for, so we ask how the output of the research grows as the agent completes more experiments and uses more compute: the best checkpoint's capability $c(M_T^{\star})$ , and the understanding of post-training that the experiments leave behind. We call this relationship **autoresearch scaling**. The process spends two resources: agent inference [50] pays for the analysis that invents new recipes, and training and evaluation compute turns those recipes into research artifacts, namely a growing set of checkpoints and the scientific trajectory that produced them; Figures 1 and 4 show the capability curve against hours and cost, and Section 5 discusses why it flattens.

### <span id="page-5-1"></span>**3.2 The Research Environment and Its Roles**

The post-training research environment. We study a generalist setting in which one checkpoint must improve across a suite of target capabilities, starting from a raw base model. Our environment gives each researcher a coding workspace on a GPU node; a pool of public training corpora decontaminated against the benchmarks; and the base model and the checkpoints saved by its team. Starter code, which the researcher may edit or replace, covers building training datasets, supervised fine-tuning, group relative policy optimization (GRPO), reinforcement learning in interactive environments, on-policy distillation from a team checkpoint, generating logic puzzles, replaying public ALFWorld expert trajectories, and checkpoint merging, on the verl trainer [\[60\]](#page-20-9) and mergekit [\[21\]](#page-18-10). To test research ability under controlled conditions, the researcher cannot call or download an external teacher model, and any further public dataset it brings in goes through the same decontamination first. The evaluation is protected from the researchers. On request, the development suite returns every benchmark score and every scored answer as feedback. A held-out suite, unknown to the researchers, gives the result after the campaign.

No human-designed research scaffolding. Athanor does not prescribe how the research is done. Following the bitter lesson [\[63\]](#page-20-10), it builds no research procedure into the agent: the environment does not choose a direction, approve an experiment, or interpret a result, and the researchers run the harnesses that the model developers ship (Claude Code or Codex; this campaign uses Claude Code) through a shell, files, and one command-line tool. In our experience, earlier versions of Athanor that prescribed a form per recipe, an approval per experiment, and a central scheduler constrained stronger agents more than they helped them. Others building agent systems report related effects: scaffolding written for a weaker model can constrain the judgment of a stronger one [\[81\]](#page-21-5), and in one study prescribed roles made little difference to what agents produced [\[4\]](#page-17-13). What we improve between campaigns is therefore the environment, not a research procedure: after each campaign we read the agents' trajectories for points of friction, such as slow steps, confusing commands, and recurring workarounds, and improve the tools without changing the researcher's role. Building Athanor is thus a meta loop around the research loop.

Researchers, environment, and operator. Athanor separates three roles by what each may decide and what each may see. The researchers make every research decision; they see their own and their teammates' records and the development results, but not the held-out suite, and they cannot change the evaluator. Because the researchers are the ones being studied, these limits are enforced by the software: the sandbox, the frozen evaluator, and the sealed held-out suite. The environment makes no research decision: it runs what is

requested, measures with the frozen evaluator, and records what happened. The operator, a person or an agent acting for one, has the widest authority and sees everything: it sets the campaign's goal and measurement protocol before the start, decides how long the campaign runs, keeps the nodes running, and scores the held-out suite. It gives no research advice, and every operator command leaves a record.

### <span id="page-6-1"></span>**3.3 Keeping the Loop Fast and Reliable**

Separate research decisions from execution. In the terms of [\(AutoPostTrain\)](#page-4-0), the researcher chooses r<sup>t</sup> and the environment carries out Exec and Eval. The environment owns the tedious recurring work: resource allocation, job execution, checkpoint storage, evaluation, and recovery. This way the researcher's effort goes into the research itself. The evaluator runs outside the researcher's sandbox and grades by verifiable rules or programs, so its feedback follows fixed rules and stays beyond the reach of the researcher it measures.

Externalize the research state. The research record is the history H of [\(AutoPostTrain\)](#page-4-0). It links each request to its code, data, and input checkpoints, and to the jobs, output checkpoints, and measurements that follow. It holds four kinds of entries. The journal is a time-ordered log with one line for every accepted request and every change of state. The lineage records which checkpoints each checkpoint was made from. The transcript is the full text of every agent session. The workspace history is the version history of the workspace, including the researchers' notes. Because every checkpoint points to its parents, we can trace any score back to the base model, through every training stage and merge that produced it. The record lives on storage outside the agent's context, so a researcher can resume a session, start a fresh one, or be moved to a new node and still have the same evidence. For the same reason the researchers can work as a team: each reads its teammates' workspaces, results, and journals and can fetch their saved checkpoints.

Keep the research loop productive. A training job can take hours, and each of its checkpoints can be evaluated only once it is saved: training and evaluation together make up the feedback delay τfb of [Section 2.](#page-3-1) A researcher that waited for each result before starting the next experiment would leave most of its GPUs idle, so Athanor lets it keep several experiments in flight. Every job runs in the background and belongs to the environment rather than to the agent's session: the agent can go on reading, planning, and starting other jobs, and the job survives if the session ends. When a job or an evaluation finishes, the services push its result into the agent's session, so one score can inform the next decision while other jobs are still running. <span id="page-6-0"></span>We test Athanor in one campaign, in which a team of four agents post-trains Qwen3-4B-Base for 104 hours.

#### **4.1 Experimental Setting**

Agents and goal. Four researcher agents, each Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code, work as one team; we call them Agents 1–4. Each has one node, a sandbox, read-only web access, and the athanor command; a node is a GPU server with eight GPUs, each with 80 GB of HBM3 memory, and a high-bandwidth GPU interconnect. The goal they read is one sentence: raise the development-suite score of a model post-trained from Qwen3-4B-Base as high as possible. It names no target score and prescribes no method. The agents see the development scores of three reference models: the base, the default starter (the starter code run with its default settings and no research), and Instruct-2507, so the level of the vendor's release is visible to them.

Data and protocol. The agents may train on public datasets, problems they generate, and samples from checkpoints they have trained. They may not call or download any other model, and humans give no research advice. The selection rule is fixed before the campaign starts: the development suite selects the team's submission, and the held-out suite, scored after the campaign, gives the result. We count hours from the start of the research window, the period in which the agents work. [Appendix C](#page-25-0) gives the setting and timeline.

Evaluation suites. Each suite has six tasks, one per capability, and its score is their plain mean. Each held-out task is the twin of one development task: it measures the same capability on different material, taken from a different source, from a later time window, or from task types the agents never saw. The pairs, development task first, are: mathematics contests from early 2025 and from November 2025 on [\[6\]](#page-17-14); two disjoint halves of ZebraLogic [\[40\]](#page-19-12); LiveCodeBench problems from 2024 and from 2025 [\[30\]](#page-18-11); MMLU-Pro [\[65\]](#page-20-11) and SuperGPQA [\[15\]](#page-17-15); IFEval [\[83\]](#page-21-4) and IFBench [\[52\]](#page-19-13); and, for multi-step action, four ALFWorld task types and the other two [\[62\]](#page-20-12) [\(Table 7](#page-27-0) gives the number of items in each). In this paper a seed is one complete evaluation of a suite; seeds differ in the sampling and in the small run-to-run differences of batched inference, and ± gives the standard error of the suite mean over items.

References and measurement protocols. We compare against the base, the default starter, and the vendor's three post-trained Qwen3-4B releases: Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, the original hybrid Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3- 4B-Thinking-2507; Qwen3-4B and Thinking-2507 write out their reasoning before they answer. All models are scored by the same frozen evaluator under the campaign protocol: mathematics is sampled, the other tasks are decoded greedily, and an answer may use up to 16,384 output tokens on mathematics, logic, and code, 8,192 on knowledge and instruction following, and 256 per ALFWorld step. Under these limits the two reasoning releases often run out of tokens before they answer, so after the campaign we add a second measurement of the references and the submission with up to 32,768 output tokens (8,192 on instruction following and per ALFWorld step) and one sampling setting for every model, the setting Qwen recommends for Qwen3 in thinking mode (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20); we call this the final measurement. This shared setting does not handicap Instruct-2507: with its own recommended sampling and chat template it scores 66.4 and 47.5, no higher [\(Appendix C\)](#page-25-0).

### <span id="page-7-1"></span>**4.2 Model Improvement**

Search from the base. We report the campaign protocol first, then the final measurement that gives the abstract's numbers. Under the campaign protocol [\(Table 1,](#page-7-0) top), in 104 hours the best single evaluation, the frontier (the staircase of [Figure 1;](#page-1-0) the best observed suite mean of <sup>p</sup>b<sup>t</sup> in [\(AutoPostTrain\)](#page-4-0)), rises from 16.1 to 66.9, above the 66.4 of Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507. Without any research, the default starter reaches 36.4. A single evaluation of the best candidate tends to run high, because a search that keeps the best single evaluation selects on the evaluation noise ξt. The agents see this in their own record and, from the second day, re-evaluate 23 of their best candidates on further seeds: for 19 of them the first seed scores higher than

the mean of the later seeds (sign test, p = 0.003), and across all 23 the first seed is higher by 0.4 points on average and by at most 1.1 [\(Figure 8a](#page-15-1)). The reported result is therefore the team's submission, averaged over several seeds. Each agent submits one checkpoint, and the team's submission is the one with the highest development score among the four, by a rule fixed before the start; this is Agent 3's checkpoint, an average of its own and Agent 2's self-distilled models: 66.1 ± 0.8 averaged over three seeds, against 66.4 for Instruct-2507 [\(Table 1\)](#page-7-0). Because ideas and checkpoints spread through the team [\(Section 5.2\)](#page-12-0), all four agents arrive at this level: the other three submissions are each within two standard errors of it [\(Table 9\)](#page-27-1).

Held-out transfer. The gain holds on tasks the agents never saw. Under the campaign protocol, on the held-out suite, the submission closes 97% of the gap between the base and Instruct-2507, where the default starter, at 20.4, closes 30%. The submission scores 46.7 ± 1.3 over two seeds, against 8.7 for the base and 48.0 ± 1.4 for Instruct-2507. Compared with Instruct-2507 on the same items, the submission is 1.3 points lower, and the 95% confidence interval for this difference, paired over items, runs from −4.0 to +1.3: the two models are not distinguishable. All four agents' submissions reach this level: they score 45.9 to 47.2, and for each the 95% interval for the difference from Instruct-2507 contains zero.

Task profile and comparison with reasoning releases. The team reaches the vendor's mean by a different route, and the difference repeats on both suites; [Figure 10](#page-29-0) shows how each task climbs. The submission is above Instruct-2507 on multi-step action (98.9 against 82.4 on development, 78.8 against 65.9 held-out) and code (47.1 against 43.9, and 34.9 against 31.4), above or level on knowledge and instruction following, and below on logic (63.4 against 80.4, and 63.7 against 81.0) and mathematics (37.2 against 42.9, and 29.7 against 42.2). The means are level because multi-step action offsets mathematics and logic: without it, the submission trails Instruct-2507 by 3.7 points on development and by 4.2 on the held-out suite. The agents fine-tune on ALFWorld trajectories, and the gain carries over to the two task types they never saw. The team's models, fine-tuned on reasoning traces, write out their reasoning before they answer, so on logic and mathematics many answers hit the output limit before they end. For the team's submission, 18% of the logic answers and 24% of the mathematics answers are cut off, against 1% and 10% for Instruct-2507 [\(Appendix D.1\)](#page-29-1).

The final measurement [\(Table 1,](#page-7-0) bottom) raises the team's means to 67.3 on development and 48.2 on the held-out suite, level with or above Instruct-2507 (66.5 and 47.9; [Table 10](#page-28-0) gives all four submissions); the paired 95% intervals of the differences, −0.8 to +2.4 and −2.0 to +2.6, contain zero. With longer output limits and sampled decoding, the submission's logic score does not rise (63.4 to 63.0, against 79.5 for Instruct-2507), so truncation is not the whole gap; most of the gap lies in the larger logic puzzles [\(Appendix D.1\)](#page-29-1). The vendor's two reasoning releases score higher still (73.3 and 50.6 for the hybrid release, 80.5 and 63.0 for Thinking-2507), mostly on mathematics, logic, and code: the team reaches the vendor's instruct release but not yet its reasoning releases.

### <span id="page-8-0"></span>**4.3 Execution Effectiveness**

The result above rests on the environment carrying out the agents' experiments. This section measures where the agents' time, the GPU hours, and the money go, and how the campaign runs over four days.

Agent efficiency. The agents spend their time on the research, not on rebuilding the experiment infrastructure, and once a session has submitted a job, it waits for the result and spends no tokens while waiting [\(Figure 3b](#page-9-0) and d; [Figure 12](#page-32-0) shows each agent separately). Of the roughly 416 agent hours of the campaign, an agent is working (reasoning or running its tool calls) for about 9%. The agents' reasoning, in which they read results, analyze them, and decide, takes 15 hours in all (3.6%). Almost all of the agents' tool calls run shell commands, most often athanor commands that start jobs and evaluations and read their state; 41 of the 2,611 athanor commands fail, mostly on a wrong argument or name. For 87% of the time a session waits while its jobs run, until a result wakes the agent. A turn of agent work lasts 4 seconds at the median, while a GRPO job takes 1.6 hours and a full evaluation takes 26 minutes [\(Figure 3c](#page-9-0)); the GRPO job and the evaluation together make up the feedback delay τfb. The GPUs therefore almost never wait for an agent: less than 1% of the GPU hours pass while an idle GPU waits for its agent.

#### <span id="page-14-1"></span>**5.4 Uneven Data Sense**

The agents inspect their data and act on what they see. Each reads 30 to 70 excerpts of rows from candidate corpora in the first six hours, and later they read their models' answers to judge the data behind them. They reject corpora whose reasoning traces exceed the output limit and drop 1,093 instruction rows that the reward function cannot score. They fix a deduplication step that had marked every puzzle as a duplicate and rebuild a puzzle pool that had been mostly small grids. After seeing that a model trained on puzzle-solver traces "asserts unjustified deductions," they discard those traces. What they rarely do is check whether the answers in the data are correct: one agent notes that some ground-truth answers look "like noise," plans a filter to remove them, and never runs it [\(Appendix D.4\)](#page-31-2).

#### <span id="page-14-2"></span>**5.5 Why the Gains Slow**

<span id="page-14-3"></span>[Figure 1](#page-1-0) is the capability curve of autoresearch scaling [\(Section 2\)](#page-3-1): because jobs hold the GPUs for 93% of the available hours, hours track compute [\(Figure 4b](#page-10-1) plots it against cost). The frontier reaches 60 within the first day; the remaining three days add about seven points, the largest share on logic, where the leading checkpoint's score rises from 49 to 65 [\(Figure 10\)](#page-29-0). The findings above suggest why. In the last two days no step on the submission's lineage brings in new training data: the gains of those days come from merging the team's models and from distilling them on their own samples [\(Section 5.1\)](#page-10-3). All of these models descend from one choice of data made in the first hour, so each merge combines closely related models, and the two lineages trained on other data score lower and are dropped [\(Section 5.3\)](#page-13-1).

# **6 Insights and Discussion**

Automated scientific discovery of post-training. The record does more than document one result: it turns open questions of post-training into measurements, each tied to the recipe and checkpoints that produced it. If such campaigns run at scale, the first systematic understanding of how post-training shapes a model may come from AI agents studying AI rather than from people. In single experiments of this campaign, averaging two equally strong models from different lineages gains two to three points, moving a merge further from the base trades knowledge for logic [\(Figure 8b](#page-15-1)), and Agent 3's first round of self-distillation adds about one point [\(Appendix D.6\)](#page-32-1). The agents draw these lessons themselves and write them into their notes as rules for their teammates, such as Agent 4's "averaging two EQUAL-strength RL models from different lineages gives ∼+2.7". A search of this kind runs such experiments at the rate the compute allows and keeps the evidence behind each conclusion. It can do so only because of what the record keeps. Post-training feedback is slow and noisy [\(Section 2\)](#page-3-1), so the cause of a score has to be recoverable afterwards, and Athanor keeps the answer to every item, repeated evaluations, the lineage of each model, and the teammates' work. The rollout change of [Section 5.1](#page-10-3) came from reading the scored answers, which show that many answers reaching the output limit are caught in a repetition loop; a record of scores alone would have hidden the cause.

<span id="page-15-0"></span>Toward a broader autoresearch loop. The campaign fixes the suites and excludes teacher models; these boundaries keep the result checkable and also limit what the agents can try. Everything else this campaign rests on was built by people, and each piece could be handed to agents, making the campaign one part of a larger loop in which AI improves AI research. The benchmark: agents could construct the evaluation suites for the capabilities they target. The environment: agents could build the next autoresearch environment, for post-training or for other domains, as we build Athanor through the meta loop of [Section 3.2.](#page-5-1) The data: agents could run a data flywheel, generating, verifying, and cleaning their own training problems, so that synthetic data becomes an essential part of the search. And the researchers themselves: every campaign leaves a complete record of research at scale, with each decision tied to the evidence the agent saw and the result that followed, and such trajectories are training data for models built to do autoresearch. Each step that hands the agents more of the loop needs its own checks, against contamination and against agents that learn to please the evaluator.

# **7 Related Work**

Automated post-training. PostTrainBench [\[55\]](#page-20-3) has an agent post-train a small base model for one benchmark on one GPU in ten hours, and Locus [\[29\]](#page-18-7) extends this setting to parallel, multi-day search; both produce one specialist per benchmark. RSI-Master and RSIGym also train per task, with access to external model APIs and with training and evaluation behind remote services, respectively [\[16,](#page-17-10) [47\]](#page-19-9). RSI Arena [\[5\]](#page-17-16) runs ten agents as competitors, each post-training the same 30B base model on a shared cluster, and ranks their models by human preference. Other benchmarks isolate fine-tuning or reinforcement-learning pipelines [\[12,](#page-17-7) [38\]](#page-19-6). An analysis of PostTrainBench trajectories finds that agents rarely change strategy between consecutive training attempts [\[39\]](#page-19-14), a narrow search like the one we observe. Our setting differs in that a team of agents works for days on one generalist checkpoint, without a teacher model, and is scored on a held-out twin suite.

Agents that improve AI systems. Agents now improve the systems around models: their own code and harness [\[41,](#page-19-15) [68,](#page-20-15) [80\]](#page-21-10) and inference engineering [\[81\]](#page-21-5), up to the production inference service of GLM's own models [\[77\]](#page-21-11). All of these work on the layers around the model; our agents change the model's weights.

Autonomous research and teams of agents. Research agents now carry out experiments over hours to days [\[26,](#page-18-13) [43,](#page-19-3) [48\]](#page-19-4), and benchmarks score machine-learning engineering and research tasks [\[9,](#page-17-6) [44,](#page-19-5) [66,](#page-20-2) [70\]](#page-21-12). Work on teams of agents finds that identical agents sharing a workspace outperform independent ones when a verifier tells them whose progress to build on [\[51\]](#page-19-8), that agents sharing a model, a harness, and a context behave alike [\[4\]](#page-17-13), and that a single agent can get stuck in an idea basin, a small region of ideas it keeps returning to [\[7\]](#page-17-17); other systems share work among agents through a forum, a git history, or co-evolution [\[13,](#page-17-5) [53,](#page-20-16) [82\]](#page-21-13). Our team's convergence on one corpus resembles an idea basin shared by several agents, measured here on real training compute; [Appendix B](#page-24-0) compares Athanor with neighboring systems.

### <span id="page-16-0"></span>**8 Limitations**

One campaign, one base model, and one agent model. The result comes from one campaign, with one team of four identical agents and one 4B base model. Its four submissions reach the same level, but they share their work and are not independent repeats; a repeat would show how much the result varies from campaign to campaign, and a campaign without sharing would show how much of it the team adds. We chose a 4B base because a model of this size can be trained and evaluated many times within one campaign; whether the recipe the team found carries over to larger models or to other model families is untested. The search itself may also change with the agents: agents built on another model may search differently, and a team that mixes models may converge less on one recipe than our team of identical agents did.

Not yet at the vendor's ceiling. The team reaches the vendor's instruct release, not its two reasoning releases, which score higher in the final measurement [\(Table 1\)](#page-7-0). And although the agents call no teacher model, much of the public data they fine-tune on was distilled from stronger models by its publishers [\[1,](#page-17-11) [36,](#page-19-1) [73\]](#page-21-1), so the result shows what agents can build from public resources, not from the base model alone.

### <span id="page-16-1"></span>**9 Conclusion**

Athanor lets frontier agents run AutoPostTrain end to end. In 104 hours, a team of four agents takes Qwen3-4B-Base from 15.9 to 67.3 on six tasks and from 9.2 to 48.2 on a held-out twin suite, level with or above the vendor's instruct release (66.5 and 47.9), by composing known methods and fixing the problems their own measurements expose. The agents reach this level from public data, for about \$12,000 in GPU time and inference. Once the friction of execution is removed, the clearest limit we observe is the diversity of their ideas. This points to a future in which anyone who can score the behavior they want automatically, and who has tokens and GPUs, can have agents post-train a model for it. We hope that Athanor and our findings will provide a foundation for scalable autonomous AI research.

Execution and protected evaluation. Each researcher runs in a sandbox on its node, with its workspace, a scratch disk, the base model, the data pool, and read-only views of its teammates' workspaces and results. A service outside the sandbox accepts the requests of the athanor command, queues them, binds GPUs, starts and stops jobs, and accounts for GPU time. Evaluation runs outside the sandbox in its own network namespace with a frozen copy of the evaluator, and only the scores and scored answers of the development suite are copied back to the researcher. Decontamination also runs outside the sandbox, and the researcher sees only how many rows it removed: a row of a training dataset is removed if it overlaps a benchmark item, either a development item or a held-out item. To test this, we lowercase the text and remove punctuation and markup. We then list all 13-word sequences in the row and in the item. The row is removed when at least half of the item's 13-word sequences also appear in the row. An item shorter than 13 words has no such sequence and is not matched by this rule.

Workspaces and persistent state. Everything the record needs is saved to durable storage as it is written: the workspace with its git history, the agent's home directory, the journal, the results of every evaluation and job, and every checkpoint saved as an artifact. Large files that the agents leave on the node's scratch disk, such as training datasets, are not. When the platform reclaims a node, the operator starts the session again on a new node. Requests that were still running on the lost node are marked as ended in the journal. The outage is added to the research window, and the agent session continues from its saved transcript and notes.

Research record. A request's journal line is written first, before the request starts anything. If the node fails, the journal still shows what was requested. For every checkpoint the record keeps the job that wrote it, that job's inputs (datasets, parent checkpoints, and the commit of the workspace when the job started), and the

evaluations and artifacts made from it, so the lineage of any checkpoint can be read back to the base model. The researchers' notes are ordinary files in their workspaces.

Background jobs and results. Each job runs under the service, not under the agent's session, so it keeps running when the session ends. When a job or an evaluation changes state, its journal line is pushed into the agent's session as a message. Agents can also add their own watches, such as a program that wakes the agent when a given line appears in a training log.

#### **D.6 Post-Training Observations**

Post-training itself remains an open scientific problem: data composition interacts with initialization, objective, stage order, and checkpoint composition. The record of the campaign holds such interactions as measured lineages. Each of the following is a single observation. When two reinforcement-learning models of equal strength (near 57) from different lineages are averaged, the average scores about 2.7 points higher than the better parent [\(Trace 5\)](#page-35-0). When a strong model is averaged with weaker ones, the average scores no higher than the strong model alone (an observation in Agent 4's notes; [Trace 5\)](#page-35-0). When Agent 3 moves a merged model further from the base, its logic score rises as the factor grows from 1.0 to 1.2 (56.4, 59.6, 63.0 at factors 1.0, 1.1, 1.2), and its knowledge score falls once the factor passes 1.1 (66.8, 65.7, 63.8 at factors 1.1, 1.2, 1.3); the best factor therefore depends on which task matters more [\(Figure 8b](#page-15-1)). Self-distillation (training a model on its own sampled answers) raises its score by 1.0 point (three seeds) in its first round; later rounds change it by at most 0.2 [\(Table 11\)](#page-31-0). Fine-tuning a different merge on the same samples does not help on its own: the result scores 65.3, against 65.5 for the merge before, although averaging it with Agent 3's self-distilled model gives 65.9 [\(Table 11\)](#page-31-0).

"""Single-turn agent loop that decodes a share of the training rollouts greedily.
dev6 decodes five of its six tasks greedily, and the SFT model's greedy answers loop where its sampled ones (T 0.8) do
not: in grpo1's first 25 steps, 2-7 % of the sampled rollouts cut at RESP were repetition loops, against 55-100 % of

GREEDY_SHARE = float(os.environ.get('GREEDY_SHARE', '0.25'))
@register('greedy_mix_agent')
class GreedyMixAgentLoop(SingleTurnAgentLoop):

###### Abstract

Self-distillation policy optimization provides dense token-level
supervision for LLM post-training through feedback-conditioned
self-teachers. However, directly matching the self-teacher can disrupt
successful behaviors on correct rollouts while providing insufficient
correction on incorrect ones. We propose Asymmetric Policy Optimization
via Self-Distillation (APSD), which constructs correctness-dependent
targets by mixing student and self-teacher distributions: interpolation
preserves successful behaviors on correct rollouts, while extrapolation
beyond the self-teacher strengthens correction on incorrect ones. We
analyze APSD through divergence decomposition, reverse-KL barycentric
geometry, and gradient analysis, highlighting the importance of gradient
flow through student-dependent targets. Experiments under reinforcement
learning with verifiable rewards and rich feedback show that APSD
achieves strong overall performance against reinforcement-learning and
self-distillation baselines and matches SDPO performance with
2.6$`\times`$ fewer training steps on average across Qwen3 model scales.
Code is available [here](https://anonymous.4open.science/r/APSD-0/).

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2610.25973v1/apsd_figure1.png)

Figure 1: Correctness-dependent target construction in APSD. Directly
matching the self-teacher can disrupt successful behaviors in correct
rollouts while providing insufficient correction for incorrect ones.
APSD addresses this asymmetry by adapting the distillation target
according to rollout correctness.

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a
prominent post-training paradigm for enhancing the reasoning and
problem-solving capabilities of LLMs ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib13);
[Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib28); [Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib29)).
However, RLVR typically relies on scalar outcome-level rewards,
providing limited, coarse-grained guidance for assigning credit to
individual tokens in a generated response. To address this limitation,
recent self-distillation approaches leverage privileged information to
provide fine-grained, token-level supervision beyond scalar rewards ([Ye
et al., 2026](#bib.bib20); [Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib21); [Zhao et
al., 2026](#bib.bib22); [Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Song et
al., 2026](#bib.bib24)). This paradigm naturally extends to
reinforcement learning with rich feedback (RLRF), where textual
environment feedback provides additional learning signals beyond outcome
rewards. These approaches construct a self-teacher by conditioning the
same underlying policy on privileged information and distill its
token-level distributions into the student policy.

Building on this paradigm, recent studies have incorporated rollout
correctness into self-distillation to regulate teacher-guided
supervision for successful and unsuccessful trajectories ([Yang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib12); [Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib6); [Kim et al.,
2026](#bib.bib23)). Meanwhile, research on knowledge distillation has
explored alternative target distributions that combine teacher and
student distributions to improve knowledge transfer ([Ko et al.,
2024](#bib.bib14); [Shing et al., 2025](#bib.bib15); [Shin et al.,
2026](#bib.bib16); [Yang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib17)). Despite these
advances, how rollout correctness should determine the construction of
distillation targets relative to the student and self-teacher
distributions remains largely underexplored.

To address this blind spot, we analyze the token-level discrepancies
between the student and its feedback-conditioned self-teacher across
correct and incorrect rollouts. Our analysis reveals contrasting
patterns: for correct rollouts, the self-teacher can substantially
deviate from the student’s already successful token preferences,
potentially disrupting desirable behaviors. For incorrect rollouts,
however, the self-teacher can remain close to the student distribution,
providing insufficient separation to correct failed behaviors
effectively. These observations reveal a mismatch between the
teacher–student discrepancy and the degree of correction required by the
student, as conceptually illustrated in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization").
This motivates a correctness-aware strategy that preserves successful
student behaviors while providing stronger and precise corrective
guidance for unsuccessful ones.

To address this correctness-dependent asymmetry, we propose Asymmetric
Policy Optimization via Self-Distillation (APSD), which constructs
asymmetric distillation targets according to rollout correctness.
Specifically, APSD constructs a target distribution by reweighting the
student distribution using the teacher-to-student probability ratio,
with a correctness-dependent coefficient controlling the relative
influence of teacher- and student-preferred tokens. As illustrated in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"),
APSD interpolates between the student and self-teacher distributions for
correct rollouts, incorporating teacher feedback while preserving the
student’s successful token preferences. For incorrect rollouts, APSD
instead extrapolates beyond the self-teacher distribution, shifting the
target further away from the student and toward alternatives favored by
the teacher.

Our theoretical analyses show that the APSD objective decomposes into a
reverse-KL (RKL) term and a counterbalancing Rényi-divergence term,
while the asymmetric coefficient controls target interpolation and
extrapolation along a generalized RKL barycentric path. Crucially, we
show that preserving gradient flow through the student-dependent target
retains an additional coupling term, whereas fully detaching the target
reduces each token-level gradient to a scaled SDPO gradient. Experiments
across RLVR and RLRF demonstrate that APSD consistently outperforms
state-of-the-art reinforcement-learning and self-distillation baselines
while improving training efficiency. Further controlled sampling
experiments and ablation studies support the effectiveness of
correctness-dependent target construction and highlight the importance
of preserving gradient flow.

## 2 Related Work

#### Self-Distillation for LLM Post-Training.

Self-distillation has emerged as an effective approach to LLM
post-training, where privileged self-teachers constructed from the same
underlying policy provide fine-grained supervision using additional
context or environment feedback ([Ye et al., 2026](#bib.bib20);
[Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib21); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib22);
[Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Song et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)). In
particular, SDPO ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)) conditions the
self-teacher on textual environment feedback, converting rich feedback
into token-level supervision beyond scalar rewards. Subsequent work
incorporates rollout rewards or correctness to regulate self-teacher
supervision, highlighting the importance of distinguishing successful
and failed rollouts ([Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib12); [Li et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib6); [Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)).

#### Distribution Mixing for Distillation.

Standard knowledge distillation transfers knowledge from a teacher to a
student by using the teacher’s output distribution as
supervision ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib26); [Gu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib27); [Jung et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)). Recent work has
extended this paradigm by constructing alternative distillation targets
from the student and teacher distributions to improve distillation
effectiveness ([Ko et al., 2024](#bib.bib14); [Shing et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15); [Shin et al., 2026](#bib.bib16); [Yang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib17)). Similar ideas have been explored in
self-distillation for LLM post-training by combining the student or
reference policy with a privileged self-teacher ([Xu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18); [Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib19)).

#### Our Work.

While prior work has explored correctness-aware supervision strategies
and teacher–student distribution mixing, how rollout correctness should
govern the direction and geometry of distillation target construction
remains underexplored. APSD bridges this gap by using rollout
correctness to determine whether the target interpolates between or
extrapolates beyond the student and self-teacher distributions. In
addition, APSD preserves gradient flow through its student-dependent
target, preventing the APSD gradient from collapsing onto the SDPO
update direction.

## 3 Correctness-Dependency in SDPO

#### Notations.

Let $`x`$ denote the input prompt and $`\pi_{\theta}`$ denote the
current student policy. Given $`x`$, we sample a group of $`G`$ rollouts
from $`\pi_{\theta}`$, denoted by $`\{y^{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$. Each rollout
is a token sequence $`y^{i}=(y_{1}^{i},\ldots,y_{T}^{i})`$, where
$`y_{t}^{i}`$ is the $`t`$-th token and $`y_{<t}^{i}`$ denotes the
preceding prefix. Let $`f^{i}`$ denote the textual environment feedback
associated with rollout $`y^{i}`$ for input $`x`$ (e.g., a successful
rollout from the same rollout group and a code execution error log). We
use $`\mathcal{V}`$ to denote the vocabulary.

#### SDPO.

For each rollout $`y^{i}`$, the student distribution at decoding step
$`t`$ is denoted by $`\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{i}\mid x,y_{<t}^{i})`$. SDPO
constructs a corresponding self-teacher distribution by conditioning on
the feedback $`f^{i}`$ as
$`\pi(y_{t}^{i}\mid x,y_{<t}^{i})\overset{\text{def}}{=}\texttt{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{i}\mid x,f^{i},y_{<t}^{i})]`$,
where $`\texttt{sg}[\cdot]`$ denotes the stop-gradient operator. For
notational simplicity, we henceforth omit the conditioning variables and
denote the student and self-teacher distributions by $`\pi_{\theta}`$
and $`\pi`$, respectively.

Under this formulation, SDPO directly adopts the self-teacher
distribution as the distillation target, i.e.,
$`\pi_{\mathrm{target}}=\pi`$, and minimizes the reverse KL (RKL)
divergence ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib27)) between the student and
target distributions:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SDPO}}(\theta)=\sum_{t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\pi_{\mathrm{target}}\right),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`D_{\text{KL}}(p\|q)=\sum_{i}p(i){p(i)\over q(i)}.`$ By
conditioning the self-teacher on textual feedback $`f^{i}`$, SDPO
converts rich environmental feedback into fine-grained, token-level
supervision. This provides a more informative learning signal than the
trajectory-level reward, and has proven effective in settings with
verifiable rewards and rich feedback.

#### Challenges.

Although SDPO provides dense supervision by distilling toward a
feedback-conditioned self-teacher, using the self-teacher distribution
as a uniform target across rollouts may overlook an important
distinction that the desired policy update should depend on rollout
correctness. As illustrated in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"),
we observe two contrasting failure cases:

- •
  Disrupting successful behaviors. For correct rollouts, the
  self-teacher may deviate from the student’s successful token
  preferences, potentially disrupting desirable behavior.
- •
  Insufficient correction of failed behaviors. For incorrect rollouts,
  the self-teacher may remain close to the student’s erroneous
  preferences, providing insufficient guidance toward better
  alternatives.

These observations motivate an asymmetric adaptation of the distillation
target based on rollout correctness. For correct rollouts, the target
should remain closer to the student distribution, preserving successful
behaviors while incorporating feedback from the self-teacher. For
incorrect rollouts, the target should move beyond the self-teacher
distribution away from the student, placing greater emphasis on
alternatives relatively favored by the self-teacher. This
interpolation--extrapolation mechanism allows the target to preserve
successful behaviors while providing stronger corrective guidance for
unsuccessful ones.¹¹ 1 We further support this correctness-dependent
asymmetry through a controlled continuation experiment in
Section [5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 Correctness-Dependent 𝛼 Control ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization").
See
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Additional Analysis of Teacher–Student Discrepancy ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
for additional analyses of teacher–student discrepancy and
correctness-dependent APSD targets.

## 4 Method: APSD

To operationalize this correctness-dependent asymmetry, we propose
Asymmetric Policy Optimization via Self-Distillation (APSD). In this
section, we describe how APSD constructs asymmetric distillation targets
to preserve the student’s behavior on successful rollouts while
providing stronger corrective guidance on failed ones. We then provide
theoretical analyses to characterize this correctness-dependent behavior
through divergence decomposition and gradient dynamics.

### 4.1 Constructing Asymmetric Target Distribution

For each rollout $`y^{i}`$ in a group $`\{y^{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$ sampled
from the current student policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ given prompt $`x`$, we
construct the self-distillation target
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ by reweighting the student
distribution according to the teacher-to-student probability ratio:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)\propto\pi_{\theta}(v)\left(\frac{\pi(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}\right)^{\alpha}~~\text{for}~v\in\mathcal{V}.
``` |  | (2) |

Here, $`\alpha\in\mathbb{R^{+}}`$ is a correctness-dependent scalar that
controls the asymmetry of the target. The ratio
$`\pi(v)/\pi_{\theta}(v)`$ serves as an importance weight, adjusting
each token’s probability according to its relative preference under the
self-teacher and student. As $`\alpha`$ increases, the target places
greater emphasis on tokens favored by the self-teacher relative to the
student and suppresses those for which the self-teacher assigns lower
relative probability.

Normalizing
Eq. ([2](#S4.E2 "In 4.1 Constructing Asymmetric Target Distribution ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")),
we express the APSD target as a generalized product-of-experts
distribution over the student and self-teacher ([Qiu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4)):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)\!=\!\frac{1}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}}\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}~~\text{for}~v\in\mathcal{V},\!
``` |  | (3) |

where
$`Z_{\theta,\alpha}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{\theta}(u)^{1-\alpha}\pi(u)^{\alpha}`$
is the normalizing constant. Here, $`\alpha`$ controls their relative
contributions, inducing distinct target regimes:

- •
  Interpolation ($`0<\alpha<1`$): The target lies between the student
  and self-teacher distributions, preserving the student’s preference
  while integrating feedback from the self-teacher.
- •
  Extrapolation ($`\alpha>1`$): The target extrapolates beyond the
  self-teacher distribution relative to the student, further amplifying
  tokens relatively favored by the self-teacher over the student.

As reference cases, $`\alpha=0`$ recovers the student distribution,
$`\pi_{\theta,0}^{\mathrm{APSD}}=\pi_{\theta}`$, while $`\alpha=1`$
recovers the self-teacher distribution,
$`\pi_{\theta,1}^{\mathrm{APSD}}=\pi`$.

To address the correctness-dependent discrepancies identified in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Correctness-Dependency in SDPO ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"),
we operationalize this asymmetric target by conditioning the choice of
$`\alpha`$ according to rollout correctness:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi_{\mathrm{target},\theta}^{\mathrm{APSD}}=\begin{cases}\pi_{\theta,\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}}}^{\mathrm{APSD}},&\text{if }y^{i}\text{ is correct},\\[2.84526pt]
\pi_{\theta,\alpha_{\mathrm{incorr}}}^{\mathrm{APSD}},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}}\in(0,1)`$ and
$`\alpha_{\mathrm{incorr}}>1`$. This correctness-dependent target allows
APSD to preserve successful student behaviors on correct rollouts while
providing stronger corrective guidance on incorrect ones. APSD then uses
the SDPO distillation objective, replacing the self-teacher target with
this correctness-dependent target:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}(\theta)=\sum_{t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\pi_{\mathrm{target},\theta}^{\mathrm{APSD}}\right).
``` |  | (5) |

Here, we maintain the dependence of
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ on the current student
$`\pi_{\theta}`$, allowing gradients to flow through the
student-dependent target. This gradient-preserving formulation is
essential to APSD’s asymmetric optimization behavior, which we analyze
in
Proposition [4.2](#S4.SS2.SSS0.Px3 "Perspective 3: Gradient Dynamics. ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization").

### 4.2 Theoretical Analysis

In this subsection, we analyze APSD from three different perspectives:
(i) decomposition of $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}(\theta)`$, (ii)
geometry of $`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\text{APSD}}`$, and (iii) dynamics of
$`\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{APSD}}(\theta)`$. Together, these
analyses characterize how $`\alpha`$ shapes the optimization objective,
determines the target’s position within the student–self-teacher RKL
geometry, and influences gradient propagation through the
student-dependent target.

#### Perspective 1: Loss Decomposition.

To analyze how $`\alpha`$ shapes the APSD objective in
Eq. ([5](#S4.E5 "In 4.1 Constructing Asymmetric Target Distribution ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")),
we first decompose the loss into RKL and Rényi divergence terms. The
following proposition establishes this decomposition, making explicit
how $`\alpha`$ governs their relative contributions.

{proposition}

($`\alpha`$ Balances RKL and Rényi Divergences) For
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ in
Eq. ([4](#S4.E4 "In 4.1 Constructing Asymmetric Target Distribution ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")),
the APSD loss in
Eq. ([5](#S4.E5 "In 4.1 Constructing Asymmetric Target Distribution ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"))
can be decomposed as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\!\!\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}=\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\pi\right)-(1\!-\alpha)D_{\alpha}\!\left(\pi\middle\|\pi_{\theta}\right)\!,
``` |  | (6) |

where the latter term is the Rényi divergence of order $`\alpha`$ ([Van
Erven and Harremos, 2014](#bib.bib3)):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\alpha}(\pi\|\pi_{\theta})=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi(v)\left(\frac{\pi(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}\right)^{\alpha-1}.
``` |  |

Hence, $`\alpha`$ controls both the weight of the RKL term from SDPO in
Eq. ([1](#S3.E1 "In SDPO. ‣ 3 Correctness-Dependency in SDPO ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"))
and the sign and magnitude of the additional Rényi divergence term. See
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Proof of Proposition 1 ‣ Appendix B Proofs ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
for proof.

Proposition [4.2](#S4.SS2.SSS0.Px1 "Perspective 1: Loss Decomposition. ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
shows how the two terms contribute differently depending on rollout
correctness. For correct rollouts, $`\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}}\in(0,1)`$,
so the Rényi term has a negative coefficient in
Eq. ([6](#S4.E6 "In Perspective 1: Loss Decomposition. ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")).
In this regime, the factor
$`\left(\pi(v)/\pi_{\theta}(v)\right)^{\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}}-1}`$
places greater relative emphasis on tokens with low teacher-to-student
probability ratios, i.e., tokens relatively favored by the student. The
negatively weighted Rényi term thus counteracts the RKL pressure toward
high-ratio regions, helping preserve successful student behavior from
being overly displaced by teacher guidance.

In contrast, for incorrect rollouts, $`\alpha_{\mathrm{incorr}}>1`$, the
Rényi term in
Eq. ([6](#S4.E6 "In Perspective 1: Loss Decomposition. ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"))
has a positive coefficient. In this regime, the ratio-dependent factor
increases with the teacher-to-student probability ratio, emphasizing
regions favored by the self-teacher but under-covered by the student.
Minimizing the positive Rényi term, together with the RKL term,
encourages the student to shift probability mass toward these
teacher-preferred alternatives, reducing their under-coverage in the
student distribution.

#### Perspective 2: Target Position in RKL Geometry.

To characterize the position of $`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$
relative to the student and self-teacher, we examine its geometry using
RKL divergence. The proposition shows that the APSD target is a
generalized RKL barycenter connecting the two distributions.

{proposition}

($`\alpha`$ Governs the Target Position in RKL Geometry) For any
$`\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}`$, the APSD target in
Eq. ([4](#S4.E4 "In 4.1 Constructing Asymmetric Target Distribution ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"))
satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\!\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}\!\!=\!\underset{r\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})}{\text{argmin}}~\mkern-2.0mu(1-\alpha)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|\pi_{\theta})+\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|\pi)\mkern-2.0mu,
``` |  |

where $`\Delta(\mathcal{V})`$ is the probability simplex over the
vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$. Thus, $`\alpha`$ parameterizes the target
along the generalized RKL barycentric path connecting $`\pi_{\theta}`$
and $`\pi`$ ([Qiu et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)).

Furthermore, when $`\pi_{\theta}\neq\pi`$, the induced student-centered
trust-region radius ([Schulman et al., 2015](#bib.bib2)), defined as
$`\epsilon_{\theta}(\alpha)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}|\pi_{\theta})`$,
is strictly increasing in $`\alpha`$ for $`\alpha>0`$. That is,
increasing $`\alpha`$ pushes the target distribution monotonically
farther from the current student along this barycentric path. See
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Proof of Proposition 2 ‣ Appendix B Proofs ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
for proof.

Proposition [4.2](#S4.SS2.SSS0.Px2 "Perspective 2: Target Position in RKL Geometry. ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
provides a geometric interpretation of APSD’s correctness-dependent
target construction. For correct rollouts,
$`\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}}\in(0,1)`$, both barycentric coefficients,
$`1-\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}}`$ and $`\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}}`$, are
positive. The target therefore interpolates between the student and
self-teacher distributions, preserving the student’s contribution while
incorporating teacher feedback. Moreover, the monotonicity of
$`\epsilon_{\theta}(\alpha)`$ ensures that the target remains closer to
the student than the self-teacher does, i.e.,
$`\epsilon_{\theta}(\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}})<D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\parallel\pi_{\theta})`$.
This helps preserve successful reasoning behaviors while allowing the
student to incorporate feedback without excessive deviation from its
current policy.

In contrast, for incorrect rollouts, $`\alpha_{\mathrm{incorr}}>1`$, the
student coefficient becomes negative, while the self-teacher coefficient
is greater than one. The target thus extrapolates beyond the
self-teacher along the generalized RKL barycentric path, moving farther
from the student. Correspondingly, its student-centered KL divergence
exceeds that of the self-teacher, i.e.,
$`\epsilon_{\theta}(\alpha_{\mathrm{incorr}})>D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\parallel\pi_{\theta})`$.
This allows a larger divergence from the current policy, enabling
stronger corrective updates to move the student away from unsuccessful
behaviors.

#### Perspective 3: Gradient Dynamics.

Unlike prior methods that detach the entire target distribution ([Shing
et al., 2025](#bib.bib15); [Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib19)), APSD
preserves the dependence of $`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ on
the student distribution. This distinction is crucial for allowing the
asymmetric gradient dynamics induced by $`\alpha`$.

{proposition}

(Target Detachment Collapses to Scaled SDPO) With $`\alpha`$ and the
self-teacher $`\pi`$ fixed during differentiation, applying a
stop-gradient operator to the entire target
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ provides

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}^{\texttt{sg}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\texttt{sg}\!\left[\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}\right]\right)`$ |  | (7) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\nabla_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\pi\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SDPO}}(\theta).`$ |  |  |

Consequently, full-target detachment degenerates the optimization into a
uniformly rescaled SDPO update, completely discarding the Rényi-induced
coupling term
$`-(1-\alpha)\nabla_{\theta}D_{\alpha}(\pi\parallel\pi_{\theta})`$. See
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Proof of Proposition 3 ‣ Appendix B Proofs ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
for the proof.

Proposition [4.2](#S4.SS2.SSS0.Px3 "Perspective 3: Gradient Dynamics. ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
shows that detaching the entire target removes the geometric asymmetry
established in
Propositions [4.2](#S4.SS2.SSS0.Px1 "Perspective 1: Loss Decomposition. ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
and [4.2](#S4.SS2.SSS0.Px2 "Perspective 2: Target Position in RKL Geometry. ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"),
reducing $`\alpha`$ to rescaling the teacher-directed gradient rather
than reshaping the optimization trajectory. To understand the role of
the discarded gradient component, consider its contribution at the logit
level, which is proportional to
$`(1-\alpha)[\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)-\pi_{\theta}(v)]`$.
For correct rollouts, where $`\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}}\in(0,1)`$, this
contribution counteracts shifts toward the interpolated target. Under
gradient descent, it raises the logits of tokens where
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)<\pi_{\theta}(v)`$ and lowers
those where $`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)>\pi_{\theta}(v)`$.
This counteracting effect helps preserve successful student behaviors
and prevents excessive changes induced by teacher.

In contrast, for incorrect rollouts, where
$`\alpha_{\mathrm{incorr}}>1`$, the sign of this contribution reverses.
Gradient descent raises the logits of tokens for which
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)>\pi_{\theta}(v)`$ and lowers
those for which
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)<\pi_{\theta}(v)`$, reinforcing
shifts toward the extrapolated target. This strengthens correction away
from unsuccessful behaviors rather than merely rescaling the SDPO
update.

These observations motivate APSD’s selective stop-gradient design:
detaching only the self-teacher preserves the target’s dependence on the
student and the resulting gradient contribution
$`-(1-\alpha)\nabla_{\theta}D_{\alpha}(\pi\parallel\pi_{\theta})`$.
Maintaining this gradient flow is essential to APSD, allowing $`\alpha`$
to shape not only the magnitude but also the direction of policy updates
according to rollout correctness.

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluate APSD under two complementary reinforcement learning settings
following prior self-distillation work ([Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)): reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR)
and reinforcement learning with rich feedback (RLRF). RLVR provides
scalar outcome-level rewards, whereas RLRF additionally provides textual
environment feedback that offers more detailed information about the
generated solution.

#### RLVR.

Following prior work ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), we evaluate
APSD on five different benchmarks covering scientific reasoning and tool
use. For scientific reasoning, we use the Chemistry, Physics, Biology,
and Materials Science subsets of SciKnowEval ([Feng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib7)); for tool use, we use ToolAlpaca ([Tang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib8)). In RLVR, where only scalar rewards are available, the
self-teacher is conditioned on a successful rollout from the same
rollout group when available, following SDPO. Our experiments use
Olmo3-7B-Instruct ([Olmo et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)) and Qwen3-8B ([Yang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)) as base models. Following the reporting
protocol of prior work ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), we report
the highest Avg@16 accuracy achieved within the 5-hour wall-clock
training budget.

#### RLRF.

Following the RLRF setup of SDPO ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)),
we evaluate APSD on LiveCodeBench v6 (LCBv6) ([Jain et al.,
2025](#bib.bib9)) with Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B. In RLRF, code
execution feedback, including runtime errors and failed test cases,
additionally conditions the self-teacher during training. We train all
methods for 80 optimization steps and evaluate performance using Avg@4
and Pass@4.

#### Baselines.

We compare APSD with reinforcement learning and self-distillation
baselines. Our common baselines include GRPO ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13)), SDPO ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), SRPO ([Li
et al., 2026a](#bib.bib6)), and RLSD ([Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib12)).
For RLVR, we evaluate both off- and on-policy variants of GRPO. For
RLRF, we additionally consider DistiLLM ([Ko et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)),
TAID ([Shing et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)), and DemoPSD ([Li et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib19)), adapting their distribution-mixing strategies to
the self-distillation setting. Additional configurations and
implementation details are provided in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Experimental Details ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization").

### 5.2 Main Results

We first evaluate APSD on five RLVR benchmarks. As shown in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Perspective 2: Target Position in RKL Geometry. ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"),
APSD achieves the highest average performance, while remaining
competitive across individual tasks. Importantly, APSD consistently
outperforms SRPO and RLSD, which also optimize policy asymmetrically
using rollout correctness. These results suggest that constructing
asymmetric distributions based on rollout correctness provides a more
effective policy optimization signal than other asymmetric formulations.

Under RLRF, we evaluate APSD on LCBv6 against correctness-guided
self-distillation and distribution-mixing baselines across model scales.
Consistent with the RLVR results,
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
shows that APSD achieves the strongest overall performance, consistently
outperforming SRPO and RLSD and further supporting the effectiveness of
correctness-dependent interpolation and extrapolation under RLRF. APSD
also outperforms DistiLLM, TAID, and DemoPSD, whose distribution-mixing
strategies are adapted to the SDPO. These results highlight the benefit
of incorporating rollout correctness into distillation target
construction, beyond conventional distribution-mixing strategies.

### 5.4 Correctness-Dependent $`\alpha`$ Control

We conduct an additional experiment on LCBv6 to examine the preferred
APSD coefficient $`\alpha`$ by rollout correctness. With a frozen
Qwen3-8B student and self-teacher, we fix the first half of each
response and regenerate the suffix using APSD targets: $`\alpha=0`$
(student), $`0<\alpha<1`$ (interpolation), $`\alpha=1`$ (self-teacher),
and $`\alpha>1`$ (extrapolation).

Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.3 Training Efficiency ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
shows contrasts between correct and incorrect rollouts. For correct
rollouts, interpolation improves execution accuracy over the already
effective student distribution, whereas the self-teacher alone provides
no gain and extrapolation reduces performance. This suggests successful
student behavior should be preserved rather than aggressively
overwritten. For incorrect rollouts, the student rarely recovers a
correct solution, while the self-teacher substantially improves
accuracy. Extrapolation further improves performance beyond the
self-teacher, achieving the highest accuracy. These results support
APSD’s correctness-dependent $`\alpha`$ selection.

### 5.5 Ablation Study

#### Effect of Target Gradient Flow.

To examine the role of gradient flow through the target, we construct a
stop-gradient variant of APSD. As shown in
Figure [4(b)](#S5.F4.sf2 "In Figure 4 ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"),
the stop-gradient variant performs worse than the original APSD across
all Qwen3 model scales. This result demonstrates the importance of
preserving gradient flow through the student-dependent target and is
consistent with the analysis in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 4 Method: APSD ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"),
which identifies an additional gradient contribution from the
student–target coupling.

#### Effects of Interpolation and Extrapolation.

We further assess the contribution of each correctness-specific
operation in APSD.
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.3 Training Efficiency ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
reports two partial variants: APSD-C applies interpolation only to
correct rollouts, while APSD-I applies extrapolation only to incorrect
rollouts. Both variants improve over SDPO across all model scales, but
neither matches the performance of the full APSD. These results indicate
that the two operations are complementary, with interpolation preserving
successful behavior and extrapolation providing stronger correction for
unsuccessful rollouts.

## 6 Sensitivity Study

We examine APSD’s sensitivity to asymmetry strength $`\delta`$ by
setting $`\alpha_{\mathrm{corr}}=1-\delta`$ and
$`\alpha_{\mathrm{incorr}}=1+\delta`$ around the SDPO configuration
($`\delta=0`$). As shown in
Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Effects of Interpolation and Extrapolation. ‣ 5.5 Ablation Study ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"),
reversing the proposed asymmetry ($`\delta<0`$), such that correct
rollouts are extrapolated while incorrect rollouts are interpolated,
degrades performance below SDPO in both RLVR and RLRF. This result
further confirms that the direction of the asymmetric adjustment is
critical. For positive $`\delta`$, the preferred degree of asymmetry
differs across feedback settings: RLVR favors relatively mild asymmetry,
whereas RLRF favors stronger asymmetry. Nevertheless, APSD consistently
outperforms SDPO across a broad range of positive $`\delta`$ values,
demonstrating robustness to the degree of asymmetry.

## 7 Conclusion

We present APSD, a self-distillation method that constructs asymmetric
targets based on rollout correctness. APSD interpolates between student
and self-teacher distributions to preserve successful behaviors and
extrapolates beyond the self-teacher to strengthen correction of
unsuccessful ones. Our analyses characterize APSD through divergence
decomposition, reverse-KL barycentric geometry, and gradient flow
through student-dependent targets. Experiments across RLVR and RLRF show
strong overall performance against reinforcement-learning and
self-distillation baselines , supporting the need for
correctness-dependent student–self-teacher distribution mixing.

## Limitations

Although APSD demonstrates consistent improvements across RLVR and RLRF
settings, our study has a few limitations. Our experiments cover diverse
tasks, including scientific reasoning, tool use, and code generation,
while evaluation on additional language generation tasks is left for
future work. Furthermore, due to computational resource constraints, we
evaluate APSD on models with up to 8B parameters, and its effectiveness
on substantially larger models remains to be investigated. Nevertheless,
the consistent improvements across multiple model scales and feedback
settings suggest the potential of correctness-dependent target
construction for LLM post-training.

## Appendix A Additional Analysis of Teacher–Student Discrepancy

#### Comparison with an External OPD Teacher.

To examine whether the behavior observed in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")
is specific to self-distillation, we additionally compare the Qwen3-8B
student with a larger Qwen3-32B OPD teacher. Unlike the SDPO
self-teacher, the OPD teacher is a separate model and receives the same
original prompt as the student.

As shown in
Figure [6](#A0.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ The Use Of Large Language Model Assistants ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")(a),
the external teacher exhibits a different correctness-dependent pattern.
The teacher–student perplexity gap is larger for incorrect rollouts than
for correct rollouts. In contrast, for the SDPO self-teacher in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"),
the gap is smaller on incorrect rollouts than on correct rollouts. This
comparison highlights a characteristic difficulty of self-distillation,
as even after conditioning on feedback, the self-teacher can remain
close to the student distribution on failed trajectories, limiting the
strength of corrective supervision.

#### Effect of Correctness-Dependent Asymmetry.

We next visualize how the proposed correctness-dependent transformation
changes the relationship between the student and self-teacher
distributions. For correct rollouts, we interpolate between the student
and self-teacher, while for incorrect rollouts, we extrapolate toward
the self-teacher relative to the student.

As shown in
Figure [6](#A0.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ The Use Of Large Language Model Assistants ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")(b),
these two operations adjust the discrepancy in opposite directions. For
correct rollouts, interpolation reduces the perplexity gap from
$`0.2092`$ for the original self-teacher to $`0.1004`$, keeping the
target closer to the already successful student behavior. For incorrect
rollouts, extrapolation increases the gap from $`0.0720`$ to $`0.1123`$,
moving the target further away from the failed student trajectory. This
behavior matches the intended role of APSD by applying conservative
distillation after success and stronger corrective separation after
failure.

Figure [7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Characterization of 𝜋_{𝜃,𝛼}^APSD as a reverse-KL barycenter. ‣ B.2 Proof of Proposition 2 ‣ Appendix B Proofs ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")

## Appendix B Proofs

For readability, we omit all conditioning variables and token indices
throughout the appendix, and denote the student, self-teacher, and PoE
target distributions by $`\pi_{\theta}`$, $`\pi`$, and
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$, respectively.

### B.1 Proof of Proposition 1

#### Asymmetric divergence decomposition.

Recall that $`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ is defined as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}}.`$ |  | (8) |

The normalizing term is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle Z_{\theta,\alpha}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}.`$ |  | (9) |

The APSD objective for a single decoding step is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}\right)`$ |  | (10) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{\theta}(v)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)}{\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)}.`$ |  |  |

Substituting
Eq. ([8](#A2.E8 "In Asymmetric divergence decomposition. ‣ B.1 Proof of Proposition 1 ‣ Appendix B Proofs ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"))
gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{\theta}(v)\left[\alpha\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)}{\pi(v)}+\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}\right]`$ |  | (11) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\pi\right)+\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}.`$ |  |  |

For $`\alpha\neq 1`$, the Rényi divergence of order $`\alpha`$ ([Van
Erven and Harremos, 2014](#bib.bib3)) is defined as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\sum_{v\in V}P(v)^{\alpha}Q(v)^{1-\alpha}.`$ |  | (12) |

Setting $`P=\pi`$ and $`Q=\pi_{\theta}`$ yields

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}\left(\pi\middle\|\pi_{\theta}\right)`$ |  | (13) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}\left(\pi\middle\|\pi_{\theta}\right).`$ |  |  |

Therefore,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\pi\right)`$ |  | (14) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}\left(\pi\middle\|\pi_{\theta}\right).`$ |  |  |

When $`\alpha=1`$, $`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ reduces to
the self-teacher distribution $`\pi`$, yielding
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi)`$.
$`\square`$

### B.2 Proof of Proposition 2

#### Characterization of $`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ as a reverse-KL barycenter.

Following the derivation in [Qiu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4), we rewrite
the generalized reverse-KL barycentric objective using
$`(1-\alpha)+\alpha=1`$, we have as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{r\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})}\Bigl[(1-\alpha)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|\pi_{\theta})`$ |  | (15) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|\pi)\Bigr]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{r\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}r(v)\Biggl[(1-\alpha)\log\frac{r(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\alpha\log\frac{r(v)}{\pi(v)}\Biggr]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{r\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}r(v)\log\frac{r(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{r\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}r(v)\log\frac{r(v)}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}{\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}}}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{r\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})}\Bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(r\|{\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}}}\bigr)-\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}\Bigr]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{r\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(r\|{\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}}}\bigr)`$ |  |  |

Since the KL divergence is minimized at equality, the unique minimizer
is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle r(v)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v).`$ |  | (16) |

Thus, $`\alpha`$ directly determines the relative weighting of the
student and self-teacher in the generalized reverse-KL barycentric
target. For $`0<\alpha<1`$, this corresponds to interpolation between
the two distributions, while $`\alpha>1`$ extrapolates along the same
normalized product form. Unlike this reverse-KL barycenter, which takes
a normalized product-of-experts (PoE) form, the forward-KL barycenter
takes the mixture-of-experts (MoE) form
$`(1-\alpha)\pi_{\theta}+\alpha\pi`$. For $`\alpha>1`$, the negative
student coefficient can yield negative probabilities, so this mixture
does not generally define a valid distribution. In contrast, under the
strict positivity assumption, the normalized PoE form remains a valid
distribution and supports extrapolation beyond the self-teacher.

#### Monotonic growth of the student-centered trust-region radius with $`\alpha`$.

Using the derivation in ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Xing et
al., 2026](#bib.bib30)), we fix the student and self-teacher
distributions $`\pi_{\theta}`$ and $`\pi`$, and assume that both have
full support over the finite vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$ and that
$`\pi_{\theta}\neq\pi`$. Consider

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle r_{\epsilon}^{\star}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{r\in\Delta(\mathcal{V})}`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v\sim r}\left[\log\frac{\pi(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}\right]`$ |  | (17) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{subject to}`$ | $`\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|\pi_{\theta})\leq\epsilon.`$ |  |  |

Assume that $`\epsilon>0`$ is small enough that no unconstrained
maximizer of the objective is feasible. Slater’s condition holds because
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ is strictly feasible and the KKT conditions therefore
apply, and the optimal trust-region multiplier
$`\lambda_{\epsilon}^{\star}`$ is strictly positive ([Boyd and
Vandenberghe, 2004](#bib.bib1)).

The Lagrangian is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{TR}}(r,\lambda,\nu)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}r(v)\log\frac{\pi(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}`$ |  | (18) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\lambda\bigl(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r\|\pi_{\theta})-\epsilon\bigr)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\nu\left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}r(v)-1\right).`$ |  |  |

For $`\lambda_{\epsilon}^{\star}>0`$, its maximizer has full support,
and stationarity gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
0=\log\frac{\pi(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}-\lambda_{\epsilon}^{\star}\left(\log\frac{r_{\epsilon}^{\star}(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}+1\right)+\nu_{\epsilon}^{\star}.
``` |  | (19) |

Rearranging and normalizing yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\epsilon}^{\star}(v)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-1/\lambda_{\epsilon}^{\star}}\pi(v)^{1/\lambda_{\epsilon}^{\star}}}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{\theta}(u)^{1-1/\lambda_{\epsilon}^{\star}}\pi(u)^{1/\lambda_{\epsilon}^{\star}}}.
``` |  | (20) |

Thus, setting $`\alpha=1/\lambda_{\epsilon}^{\star}`$ gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\epsilon}^{\star}(v)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}(\alpha)}=\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v).
``` |  | (21) |

This identifies APSD with the trust-region solution, with $`\alpha`$
acting as the inverse optimal multiplier. We now parameterize this
solution family by $`\alpha`$, rather than by the prescribed radius
$`\epsilon`$. Since $`\lambda_{\epsilon}^{\star}>0`$, complementary
slackness gives

|     |                                                               |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       D_{\mathrm{KL}}(r_{\epsilon}^{\star}\|\pi_{\theta})=\epsilon.
       ```                                                            |     | (22) |

Accordingly, the radius associated with a chosen $`\alpha`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\epsilon_{\theta}(\alpha):=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}\|\pi_{\theta}\bigr).
``` |  | (23) |

To characterize this induced radius, define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{\theta}(v)=\log\frac{\pi(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)},\qquad\psi_{\theta}(\alpha)=\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}.
``` |  | (24) |

Using

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)=\pi_{\theta}(v)\exp\bigl(\alpha A_{\theta}(v)-\psi_{\theta}(\alpha)\bigr),
``` |  | (25) |

we obtain

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\epsilon_{\theta}(\alpha)=\alpha\psi_{\theta}^{\prime}(\alpha)-\psi_{\theta}(\alpha).
``` |  | (26) |

Therefore,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{d\epsilon_{\theta}(\alpha)}{d\alpha}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\psi_{\theta}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)`$ |  | (27) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\operatorname{Var}_{v\sim\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}}\left[\log\frac{\pi(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}\right]>0,`$ |  |  |

where strict positivity follows from $`\alpha>0`$, full support, and
$`\pi_{\theta}\neq\pi`$. Hence, increasing $`\alpha`$ strictly increases
the induced student-centered trust-region radius. $`\square`$

### B.3 Proof of Proposition 3

#### Setup.

Fix a decoding context and a coefficient $`\alpha>0`$. Assume that
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ and $`\pi`$ are strictly positive distributions over
the finite vocabulary $`V`$, and that $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is differentiable
in $`\theta`$. The self-teacher $`\pi`$, the sampled context, and
$`\alpha`$ are held fixed throughout differentiation. All gradients
below are computed from the exact vocabulary-level KL divergence.

#### Gradient under full-target stop-gradient.

Let
$`\pi^{\mathrm{APSD}}:=\operatorname{SG}[\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}]`$
denote the detached target, which is held fixed during differentiation,
so that $`\nabla_{\theta}\pi^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)=0`$. With $`\alpha`$ and
the self-teacher $`\pi`$ also held fixed, the detached-target objective
is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}^{\mathrm{SG}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\pi^{\mathrm{APSD}}\right)`$ |  | (28) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{\theta}(v)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)}{\pi^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)}.`$ |  |  |

Differentiating with respect to $`\theta`$ while keeping
$`\pi^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ fixed gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}^{\mathrm{SG}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(v)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)}{\pi^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)}`$ |  | (29) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{\theta}(v)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(v)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(v)\left[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)}{\pi^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)}+1\right].`$ |  |  |

At the current parameter value, $`\pi^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ has the same
numerical values as $`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$. We
therefore substitute the APSD expression into the already-computed
gradient using

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)}{\pi^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\log\frac{Z_{\theta,\alpha}\pi_{\theta}(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}}`$ |  | (30) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)}{\pi(v)}+\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}.`$ |  |  |

Consequently,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}^{\mathrm{SG}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{v\in V}\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(v)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)}{\pi(v)}`$ |  | (31) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+(\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}+1)\sum_{v\in V}\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(v)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\nabla_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\middle\|\pi\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SDPO}},`$ |  |  |

where the terms independent of $`v`$ vanish because
$`\sum_{v\in V}\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(v)=\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{\theta}(v)=\nabla_{\theta}1=0`$.
Thus, full-target detachment reduces the single-step APSD gradient to an
$`\alpha`$-scaled SDPO gradient.

#### Identifying the discarded Rényi contribution.

With $`\pi`$ and $`\alpha`$ fixed, we choose the gradient-equivalent
scalar potential associated with
Eq. ([31](#A2.E31 "In Gradient under full-target stop-gradient. ‣ B.3 Proof of Proposition 3 ‣ Appendix B Proofs ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization"))
as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{APSD}}^{\mathrm{SG}}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SDPO}}(\theta).`$ |  | (32) |

This potential reproduces the detached-target gradient but should not be
confused with the forward value of
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}^{\mathrm{SG}}`$, since stop-gradient
preserves numerical values while changing the derivatives used for
optimization.

By Proposition 1, subtracting this potential from the original objective
gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{APSD}}^{\mathrm{SG}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}`$ |  | (33) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}(\pi\|\pi_{\theta}),`$ |  |  |

where the second equality holds for $`\alpha\neq 1`$. Differentiating
identifies the exact contribution discarded by full-target
stop-gradient:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}-\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}^{\mathrm{SG}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}`$ |  | (34) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-(1-\alpha)\nabla_{\theta}D_{\alpha}(\pi\|\pi_{\theta}).`$ |  |  |

Direct differentiation of the normalizer gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}}\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}`$ |  | (35) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(v).`$ |  |  |

Consequently, the exact APSD gradient is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SDPO}}`$ |  | (36) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+(1-\alpha)\sum_{v\in V}\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(v).`$ |  |  |

The additional term need not be collinear with the SDPO gradient.
Retaining it can therefore change the gradient direction rather than
merely rescaling the detached-target update.

#### Behavior induced by the asymmetric coupling.

To characterize this contribution, let $`z(v)`$ denote the student
logits, with $`\pi_{\theta}(v)=\exp(z(v))/\sum_{u\in V}\exp(z(u))`$.
Holding the self-teacher and $`\alpha`$ fixed, the softmax identity
$`\partial\log\pi_{\theta}(u)/\partial z(v)=\delta_{uv}-\pi_{\theta}(v)`$
yields

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}}{\partial z(v)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\sum_{u\in V}\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(u)\left[\delta_{uv}-\pi_{\theta}(v)\right]`$ |  | (37) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left[\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)-\pi_{\theta}(v)\right],`$ |  |  |

where $`\delta_{uv}`$ is the Kronecker delta. Thus, the additional
descent direction in logit space is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}}{\partial z(v)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(\alpha-1)\left[\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)-\pi_{\theta}(v)\right].`$ |  | (38) |

For correct rollouts, $`0<\alpha<1`$, so this correction decreases
logits where the interpolated target
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha_{\text{correct}}}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ assigns more
probability than the student and increases logits where it assigns less.
It therefore counteracts target-directed shifts, providing a
conservative correction consistent with preserving already successful
student preferences. This correction complements, rather than replaces,
the SDPO term in the full gradient.

For incorrect rollouts, $`\alpha>1`$, and the correction reverses
direction: it increases logits where the extrapolated target
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha_{\text{incorrect}}}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$ exceeds the
student and decreases logits where the target falls below the student.
The relevant discrepancy is determined by

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}}\left(\frac{\pi(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(v)}\right)^{\alpha}.`$ |  | (39) |

In particular,
$`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)>\pi_{\theta}(v)`$ precisely
when $`\pi(v)/\pi_{\theta}(v)>Z_{\theta,\alpha}^{1/\alpha}`$. The
additional correction therefore favors alternatives with sufficiently
high teacher-to-student probability ratios, rather than tokens with high
teacher probability alone. A token jointly assigned high probability by
the student and self-teacher is not automatically reinforced: its logit
receives a negative correction whenever its ratio falls below this
threshold.

The asymmetric coefficient thus switches the coupling from counteracting
movement toward the interpolated target on correct rollouts to
reinforcing movement toward the extrapolated target on incorrect
rollouts. This provides a mechanism for conservative feedback
integration after success and stronger correction after failure,
provided that the self-teacher identifies useful alternatives. These
statements characterize the additional logit-gradient component, not a
guarantee of improved task correctness or of every token’s probability
change under the complete parameter update.

When $`\alpha=1`$, or when $`\pi_{\theta}=\pi`$, the coupling
contribution vanishes. Finally, the scaled-SDPO identity holds per
decoding step and for a rollout with a common $`\alpha`$; across
rollouts with different coefficients, full-target stop-gradient yields a
weighted sum of SDPO gradients rather than a single scalar multiple of
the batch gradient.

#### Differentiability of the APSD target.

The trust-region and generalized barycentric formulations both recover
the same explicit target,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v)^{1-\alpha}\pi(v)^{\alpha}}{Z_{\theta,\alpha}(\alpha)}.
``` |  | (40) |

For fixed $`\alpha`$ and self-teacher distribution $`\pi`$, this map is
differentiable with respect to the strictly positive student
probabilities, with coordinate derivatives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\partial\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)}{\partial\pi_{\theta}(u)}=(1-\alpha)\frac{\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(v)}{\pi_{\theta}(u)}\left[\delta_{uv}-\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}(u)\right],
``` |  | (41) |

where $`\delta_{uv}`$ is the Kronecker delta. Thus, characterizing the
target as the solution to an auxiliary optimization does not eliminate
its dependence on the student. Full-target stop-gradient explicitly
suppresses this dependence by setting the target Jacobian to zero during
differentiation. Throughout, $`\alpha`$ is held fixed, while
$`\epsilon_{\theta}(\alpha)`$ is induced by the target and may vary with
$`\theta`$; the radius is not held fixed as the student changes.

#### Why the envelope theorem does not justify detachment.

The trust-region and barycentric problems are auxiliary optimizations
used to construct $`\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}}`$, whereas APSD
minimizes the distinct outer objective
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\theta,\alpha}^{\mathrm{APSD}})`$.
The envelope theorem characterizes derivatives of the auxiliary
problems’ optimal values; it does not justify omitting the optimizer
dependence when that optimizer is reused in a different outer loss. In
particular, their stationarity conditions do not cancel the target-side
contribution $`\nabla_{\theta}\log Z_{\theta,\alpha}`$ identified in
Eq. ([34](#A2.E34 "In Identifying the discarded Rényi contribution. ‣ B.3 Proof of Proposition 3 ‣ Appendix B Proofs ‣ Correctness-Dependent Asymmetric Target Distributions for Self-Distillation Policy Optimization")).
Full-target stop-gradient therefore leaves the forward trust-region and
barycentric target unchanged, but removes its student-dependent
contribution from the APSD optimization dynamics. $`\square`$
````

# CASE: Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution for Frozen Multimodal Emotion Models

Siyu Wu    Zeyu Liang    Linghao Wang    Qiyun Zheng    Yi Yao    Ling
Lo    Hongxia Xie

###### Abstract

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have substantially advanced
Multimodal Emotion Recognition (MER) by enabling fine-grained emotion
understanding through reasoning over multimodal evidence. However, they
still exhibit systematic confusion between semantically adjacent
emotions with highly overlapping multimodal cues. Existing approaches
typically address these errors through global parameter adaptation,
making confusion correction costly and inflexible. We instead observe
that emotion confusion is highly localized and therefore better
addressed through reusable pair-specific expertise. Building on this
insight, we propose Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution (CASE), the first
agent-skill framework for MER. CASE maintains a sparse, self-evolving
library of pair-specific EmotionSkills that externalize affective
expertise from model parameters, enabling the learned expertise to be
continuously refined and readily transferred across diverse frozen MLLM
backbones. During inference, CASE dynamically retrieves the appropriate
EmotionSkill to reinforce boundary-aware reasoning while keeping the
MLLM backbone frozen. Extensive experiments on CA-MER, DFEW, and EMER
demonstrate the effectiveness and transferability of CASE across both
general-purpose and emotion-specialized MLLMs. Notably, CASE improves
the average accuracy of Qwen3-VL-8B on the CA-MER benchmark from 46.80%
to 59.13%, while achieving consistent gains across MLLMs.

## Introduction

Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have shown
impressive performance on video-based Multimodal Emotion Recognition
(MER). Beyond predicting emotion labels directly, MLLMs can integrate
visual, temporal, and contextual evidence to provide interpretable
explanations for their decisions, making them a promising foundation for
grounded affective computing ([Lian et al. 2023](#bib.bib12); [Cheng et
al. 2024](#bib.bib5)).

Despite this progress, fine-grained emotion recognition remains a
fundamental challenge. Although current MLLMs often identify the
relevant affective evidence, they still struggle to distinguish
semantically similar emotions, such as worry versus sadness or fear
versus surprise ([Lian et al. 2025a](#bib.bib18); [Hu et al.
2025](#bib.bib33)). This limitation suggests that the bottleneck of
modern MER has shifted from coarse affective perception to fine-grained
boundary discrimination.

More importantly, these misclassifications are not random prediction
failures but exhibit a systematic structure. In psychological theories,
emotions are characterized as a continuous affective space rather than
isolated categories, where neighboring emotions naturally share semantic
and behavioral characteristics ([Barrett et al. 2025](#bib.bib3);
[Plutchik 2001](#bib.bib2)). The psychological structure is also
reflected in MER, where prediction errors consistently concentrate on
neighboring emotion pairs rather than being uniformly distributed across
the label space. The observed structured confusion pattern suggests that
improving fine-grained MER requires more than stronger general
reasoning; it calls for mechanisms that explicitly reinforce
discriminative boundaries between systematically confused emotion pairs.
Existing approaches, however, primarily rely on global parameter
adaptation or task-specific fine-tuning, implicitly treating all emotion
categories equally. As a result, they overlook the localized nature of
emotion confusion and lack an effective mechanism for accumulating and
reusing pair-specific affective expertise.

Motivated by the systematic nature of emotion confusion, we propose
Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution (CASE), a general agent-skill framework
that models fine-grained emotion recognition as a collection of
pair-specific discrimination problems rather than a globally shared
reasoning task. As shown in
Figure [1](#Sx1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Introduction ‣ CASE: Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution for Frozen Multimodal Emotion Models"),
CASE maintains a sparse, self-evolving library of EmotionSkills. Each
EmotionSkill encapsulates the discriminative knowledge required to
resolve one systematically confused adjacent emotion pair. Specifically,
CASE localizes ambiguous predictions to candidate confusion pairs,
evolves confusion-specific skills based on recoverable errors, and
selectively expands the skill library only when recurrent confusion
patterns emerge. During inference, the most relevant EmotionSkill is
dynamically retrieved to reinforce boundary-aware reasoning while
keeping the MLLM backbone frozen. In this way, CASE externalizes
affective expertise into modular knowledge that evolves independently of
model parameters and can be readily reused across diverse frozen MLLM
backbones.

More broadly, CASE shifts affective recognition from fixed label
classification toward reusable knowledge-based reasoning, enabling
frozen MLLMs to flexibly support evolving emotion taxonomies without
continual model adaptation. Our contributions can be summarized as:

- •
  We introduce CASE, which reframes MER from global multiclass
  classification toward selective discrimination on ambiguous
  fine-grained emotion boundaries.
- •
  We develop a recoverability-aware skill evolution mechanism that
  learns from boundary-specific affective confusion. By contrasting
  recoverable failures with valid anchors and verifying candidate skill
  updates, CASE enables reliable, sparse skill evolution atop a frozen
  multimodal backbone.
- •
  We evaluate CASE across closed-set in-the-wild recognition (DFEW),
  audio-visual conflict-aware recognition (CA-MER), and open-vocabulary
  emotion reasoning (EMER). Skills evolved only on DFEW directly improve
  CA-MER and EMER without target-dataset evolution or backbone updates,
  demonstrating transfer across tasks, label spaces, and MLLMs.

## Related Work

### Multimodal Emotion Recognition with MLLMs

Recent MLLM-based emotion research has expanded affective label spaces
and multimodal evidence modeling ([Guo et al. 2025](#bib.bib30); [Hu et
al. 2025](#bib.bib33)). Emotion-specific architectures and
instruction-tuning strategies further improve general emotion
understanding ([Cheng et al. 2024](#bib.bib5); [Lian et al.
2025a](#bib.bib18); [Yang et al. 2025](#bib.bib28); [Li et al.
2025](#bib.bib29)), open-vocabulary formulations relax predefined
emotion taxonomies ([Lian et al. 2025b](#bib.bib19)), and
retrieval-augmented methods introduce external context for
compound-emotion reasoning ([Wen et al. 2025](#bib.bib6)).
Conflict-aware methods additionally mitigate incongruent evidence
through adaptive fusion or modality selection ([Wang et al.
2023](#bib.bib31); [Yu et al. 2026](#bib.bib32)). Despite these
advances, existing methods primarily optimize one-shot prediction and
rarely address recurrent errors between confusable emotion
categories ([Han et al. 2025](#bib.bib13); [Hu et al.
2025](#bib.bib33)). In this paper, CASE instead treats such errors as
local decision-boundary failures. It uses pair-specific external
knowledge to refine these boundaries while keeping the underlying MLLM
frozen.

### Non-Parametric Learning through Memory and Skills

Non-parametric learning methods differ in how they represent and reuse
experience. Memory-based approaches retrieve previous examples or
feedback ([Madaan et al. 2022](#bib.bib20)), whereas reflection-based
agents retain verbal summaries of past interactions ([Shinn et al.
2023](#bib.bib27); [Park et al. 2023](#bib.bib15)). Iterative
self-feedback can refine individual outputs without additional
training ([Madaan et al. 2023](#bib.bib10)), while prompt evolution
adapts reusable task instructions through performance-guided
search ([Fernando et al. 2024](#bib.bib23)). More recent methods distill
experience into reusable instructions, workflows, toolboxes, or
executable skills ([Wang et al. 2024a](#bib.bib16); [Zhao et al.
2024](#bib.bib17); [Fu et al. 2024](#bib.bib21); [Wang et al.
2024b](#bib.bib22); [Wang et al. 2025](#bib.bib24)), with persistent
skill libraries supporting continual refinement and verifier-guided
evolution ([Wang et al. 2026](#bib.bib26); [Zhou et al.
2026](#bib.bib1); [Zhang et al. 2026](#bib.bib4); [Suzgun et al.
2026](#bib.bib25)). CASE adapts this paradigm to multimodal emotion
recognition. Each EmotionSkill captures discriminative cues for one
confusable emotion pair, is selectively activated by the model’s top-2
predictions, and is retained only when it improves the corresponding
local decision boundary.

## Method

### Problem Formulation

Let $`x=(a,v,c)\in\mathcal{X}`$ denote a multimodal emotion sample,
where $`a`$ is an audio track, $`v`$ is a video, and $`c`$ is optional
linguistic context such as subtitles. Given an emotion label space
$`\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{K}\}`$, the task is to predict the
ground-truth emotion $`y^{*}\in\mathcal{Y}`$ for each sample. We treat
the recognizer as a frozen prompt-conditioned MLLM interface
$`f_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}`$, where
$`\mathcal{P}`$ is the prompt space and $`\mathcal{O}`$ is the
structured output space whose schema is determined by the prompt. Every
emotion-valued field of an output takes values in $`\mathcal{Y}`$.

The errors addressed by CASE are not caused by a complete lack of
emotion knowledge. Instead, the MLLM often identifies two plausible
emotions but fails to apply the appropriate criterion for distinguishing
them. In such cases, reconsidering all $`K`$ emotion labels introduces
unnecessary alternatives rather than resolving ambiguous emotion
boundaries.

CASE therefore decomposes emotion recognition into local discrimination
over candidate confusion pairs, guiding frozen MLLMs to distinguish the
two emotions through pair-specific EmotionSkills. These skills are
stored in a sparse external library $`\mathcal{S}`$, where each entry
$`S_{q}`$ covers one empirically observed confusion pair $`q`$. The
initial library $`\mathcal{S}^{(0)}`$ is constructed before evolution,
while $`g_{\mathrm{init}}`$ is subsequently reused to initialize new
skills for recurrent uncovered pairs, as summarized in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Problem Formulation ‣ Method ‣ CASE: Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution for Frozen Multimodal Emotion Models").
At evolution step $`t`$, CASE maintains a sparse external skill library
$`\mathcal{S}^{(t)}`$, whose domain
$`\operatorname{dom}(\mathcal{S}^{(t)})`$ contains only the confusion
pairs observed in the historical predictions of the frozen MLLM. Rather
than instantiating skills for all $`\binom{K}{2}`$ possible emotion
pairs, CASE constructs a pair-specific skill $`S_{q}^{(t)}`$ only when
the corresponding boundary $`q`$ has been empirically observed. The
resulting library therefore follows the model’s empirical
emotion-confusion structure while remaining sparse.

reopens an ambiguous emotion boundary, attributes post-inference

### Phase 1: Selective Confusion-Pair Inference

Applying pair-specific EmotionSkills indiscriminately may disturb
emotion predictions that are already supported by clear multimodal
evidence. CASE therefore intervenes only when the frozen MLLM identifies
a specific unresolved emotion boundary. This selective design preserves
confident predictions while concentrating additional reasoning on
ambiguous samples.

For each input sample $`x`$, the MLLM receives a diagnostic prompt
$`p_{\mathrm{diag}}`$ and produces

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\left(\hat{y}_{1}^{(1)},\hat{y}_{2}^{(1)},r,z\right)=f_{\theta}\left(x,p_{\mathrm{diag}}\right).
``` |  | (1) |

The corresponding candidate confusion pair is

|     |                                                            |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       q=q(x)=\left\{\hat{y}_{1}^{(1)},\hat{y}_{2}^{(1)}\right\},
       ```                                                         |     |

where $`\hat{y}_{1}^{(1)}`$ and $`\hat{y}_{2}^{(1)}`$ are two distinct
emotion candidates in round-1, $`r\in\{0,1\}`$ is the routing decision,
and $`z`$ records the diagnostic multimodal evidence.

The model returns $`r=1`$ when multimodal evidence does not reliably
distinguish the two candidate emotions. If $`r=0`$, CASE treats the
emotion as sufficiently clear and directly preserves the first-round
prediction $`\hat{y}_{1}^{(1)}`$.

If $`r=1`$ and the corresponding skill $`S_{q}^{(t)}`$ exists, CASE
reopens only this emotion boundary through a second multimodal
inference:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{y}^{(2)}=f_{\theta}\left(x,p_{\mathrm{pair}}(q)\oplus S_{q}^{(t)}\right),\qquad\hat{y}^{(2)}\in q,
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\oplus`$ denotes prompt concatenation. The model receives the
original $`x`$ again, while $`p_{\mathrm{pair}}(q)`$ restricts the
output space to the two candidate emotions.

The EmotionSkill provides a pair-specific discrimination protocol that
guides the MLLM to re-examine relevant facial, behavioral, temporal, and
contextual cues, compare the evidence supporting both emotions, and
check misleading or shared cues before making the final choice. This
turns the original $`K`$-class prediction into a focused
emotion-boundary comparison grounded in the input sample.

Accordingly, the three inference outcomes match the three paths in
Phase 1 of
Figure [2](#Sx3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Problem Formulation ‣ Method ‣ CASE: Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution for Frozen Multimodal Emotion Models"):
clear samples retain $`\hat{y}_{1}^{(1)}`$, routed samples with a
covered pair use $`\hat{y}^{(2)}`$, and routed samples with an uncovered
pair fall back to $`\hat{y}_{1}^{(1)}`$ while logging the missing
boundary. The covered-pair path yields a round-2 trajectory for skill
evolution in Phase 2, whereas the uncovered-pair path retains the
diagnostic record as evidence for sparse library expansion.

### Phase 2: Recoverability-Aware Skill Evolution

#### Trajectory attribution.

For each routed sample with a covered pair, CASE records a trajectory
$`\tau`$ comprising the diagnostic evidence, active skill version,
first- and second-round predictions, and ground-truth feedback. We
determine whether the result can be attributed to the active pair using

|     |                                    |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \rho(\tau)=\mathbb{I}[y^{*}\in q].
       ```                                 |     | (3) |

Only trajectories with $`\rho(\tau)=1`$ provide pair-level learning
signals. Incorrect second-round predictions are stored as recoverable
failures in $`B_{q}^{-}`$, while correct predictions are retained in
$`B_{q}^{+}`$ as successful anchors. Trajectories with $`\rho(\tau)=0`$
are candidate-recall errors and are excluded from EmotionSkill editing.

#### Contrastive textual editing.

When sufficient failures have accumulated, CASE selects a bounded
failure set $`F_{q}\subseteq B_{q}^{-}`$ and anchor set
$`P_{q}\subseteq B_{q}^{+}`$. The editor proposes a local textual patch:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{q}=g_{\phi}(S_{q},F_{q},P_{q}),\qquad\widetilde{S}_{q}=\operatorname{Apply}(S_{q},\Delta_{q}).
``` |  | (4) |

The patch extracts reusable distinctions, preserves valid rules
supported by the anchors, and avoids sample-specific or label-forcing
expressions.

### Phase 3: Behaviorally Validated Update and Expansion

Validated skill update. A candidate EmotionSkill revision is not
activated immediately, since a textually plausible rule may still
overcorrect the targeted failures or bias the pairwise prediction toward
one emotion. CASE therefore evaluates the current and candidate
EmotionSkills on the same fixed pair-specific validation subset. The
candidate is committed only when it strictly improves the pair-specific
predictions without collapsing toward either label; otherwise, the
existing EmotionSkills are retained. This behavioral gate ensures that
only revisions with measurable corrective effects enter the active
library.

Sparse skill expansion. The missing-pair path in
Figure [2](#Sx3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Problem Formulation ‣ Method ‣ CASE: Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution for Frozen Multimodal Emotion Models")
handles routed boundaries that are not yet covered by the active
library. As summarized by BootstrapExpand in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Problem Formulation ‣ Method ‣ CASE: Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution for Frozen Multimodal Emotion Models"),
an uncovered pair contributes expansion evidence only when the
ground-truth emotion belongs to that pair. Once sufficient recurrent
evidence has accumulated, the initializer proposes a new pair-specific
EmotionSkill. Because no active parent EmotionSkill exists for direct
comparison, the candidate is evaluated by a standalone bootstrap gate
and is activated only when it has sufficient validation support and does
not produce label collapse. Rejected candidates remain inactive.
Consequently, the library expands only toward recurrent and behaviorally
supported confusion boundaries rather than exhaustively enumerating all
possible emotion pairs.

Detailed validation-set construction, behavioral metrics, acceptance
criteria, and implementation thresholds are provided in the
supplementary Sec. A.

## Experiments

We evaluate CASE through four research questions:

(RQ1) Does the complete CASE pipeline remain effective across different
backbones and tasks?
(RQ2) Where does selective routing concentrate its interventions?
(RQ3) Does trajectory-driven evolution improve a static pairwise
EmotionSkill library?
(RQ4) Does behaviorally validated deployment filter unsupported
revisions and expand skill coverage without exhaustive library growth?

### Experimental Setup

#### Datasets and metrics.

We evaluate CASE across three complementary settings: cross-modal
emotion conflicts on CA-MER ([Han et al. 2025](#bib.bib13)), in-the-wild
dynamic facial expression recognition on DFEW ([Jiang et al.
2020](#bib.bib11)), and fine-grained multimodal emotion reasoning on
EMER ([Lian et al. 2023](#bib.bib12)). CA-MER contains the
Audio-Conflict, Visual-Conflict, and Consistent subsets, for which we
report accuracy (Acc.). For DFEW, we report unweighted average recall
(UAR) and weighted average recall (WAR), while accuracy is used for
EMER.

#### Backbones and implementation.

We evaluate CASE with three frozen backbones: Qwen3-VL-8B ([Bai et al.
2025a](#bib.bib34)), Emotion-Qwen ([Huang et al. 2025](#bib.bib9)), and
Gemini-3.5-Flash ([Google DeepMind 2026](#bib.bib40)). GPT-4o ([OpenAI
2024](#bib.bib39)) or GPT-5.6-sol ([OpenAI 2026](#bib.bib41)) serves
only as the external skill editor. CASE-Full injects the complete routed
skill, whereas CASE-Retrieval retrieves only relevant skill fragments.
Evolution, validation, and test sets are strictly disjoint. Further
implementation details are provided in Supplementary Sec. B.

### Overall Effectiveness and Generality (RQ1)

We first examine the in-distribution effectiveness of CASE on DFEW,
where the skill library is constructed. We then evaluate CASE on CA-MER,
a benchmark specifically designed to assess emotion recognition under
conflicting multimodal cues. Finally, we evaluate CASE on the open-set
EMER benchmark, which requires fine-grained emotion reasoning beyond a
fixed closed-set label space.

### Selective Local Repair (RQ2)

#### Does routing identify useful intervention targets?

![Refer to caption](2610.32481v1/Figures/routing1.png)

difficult, covered cases while avoiding unnecessary changes elsewhere.

#### Which confusion boundaries are repaired?

For a confusion pair $`q=\{y_{a},y_{b}\}`$, let

|     |                                                     |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{D}_{q}=\left\{i\mid y_{i}^{*}\in q\right\}
       ```                                                  |     | (5) |

denote the samples whose ground-truth label belongs to $`q`$. For
$`m\in\{\mathrm{R1},\mathrm{CASE}\}`$, pair-specific accuracy is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{PairAcc}^{(m)}(q)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{q}}\mathbb{I}\left[\hat{y}_{i}^{(m)}=y_{i}^{*}\right]}{|\mathcal{D}_{q}|},
``` |  | (6) |

and the change introduced by CASE is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta\mathrm{PairAcc}(q)=\mathrm{PairAcc}^{(\mathrm{CASE})}(q)-\mathrm{PairAcc}^{(\mathrm{R1})}(q).
``` |  | (7) |

Figure [6](#Sx4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Does routing identify useful intervention targets? ‣ Selective Local Repair (RQ2) ‣ Experiments ‣ CASE: Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution for Frozen Multimodal Emotion Models")
shows that both CASE variants improve most of the confusion pairs,
especially sad–worried and angry–worried. Further analyses are provided
in Supplementary. Complete class-transition statistics, subset-wise
recall gains Supplementary Sec. C.

### Trajectory-Driven Skill Evolution (RQ3)

#### Evolution beyond static skills.

Table [4](#Sx4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Open-set generalization on EMER. ‣ Overall Effectiveness and Generality (RQ1) ‣ Experiments ‣ CASE: Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution for Frozen Multimodal Emotion Models")
shows that trajectory-driven evolution improves the static EmotionSkill
library from 57.00% to 58.27% overall accuracy. More behaviorally
validated skill evolution visualization can be found in Supplementary
Sec. E. Together, these results demonstrate that the external library
can improve from prediction trajectories while the backbone remains
frozen.

### Validated Deployment and Sparse Expansion (RQ4)

Among 114 candidate revisions, CASE accepts 10, rejecting 102 without
validation gains and two causing label collapse. This low acceptance
rate is intentional: the editor acts as a proposal generator rather than
an authority that directly modifies the deployed system. The validation
gate separates textual plausibility from behavioral utility and prevents
unsupported or label-collapsing revisions from entering the active
library. Thus, the purpose of the gate is not to maximize the number of
accepted revisions, but to admit only behaviorally supported updates.
Four new pairs pass the bootstrap gate, expanding coverage from 17 to 21
pairs and improving CA-MER accuracy from 58.27% to 59.13% in Table
[4](#Sx4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Open-set generalization on EMER. ‣ Overall Effectiveness and Generality (RQ1) ‣ Experiments ‣ CASE: Confusion-Aware Skill Evolution for Frozen Multimodal Emotion Models").
This demonstrates controlled skill updating and sparse expansion.
Detailed acceptance dynamics, pair-specific validation changes, and
expansion events are shown in Supplementary Sec. E.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced CASE, a confusion-aware framework for
improving frozen MLLMs in multimodal emotion boundary discrimination.
Rather than globally updating model parameters, CASE localizes ambiguous
predictions to recurrent emotion-pair boundaries and selectively invokes
pair-specific EmotionSkills for focused discrimination. Experiments
across three emotion tasks and multiple backbones demonstrate consistent
effectiveness and generality. These results highlight external skill
evolution as a promising alternative to parameter-based adaptation for
multimodal emotion understanding.
````

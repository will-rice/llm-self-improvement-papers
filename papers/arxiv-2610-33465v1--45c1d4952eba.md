###### Abstract

Equation discovery solvers often use different data interfaces, equation
representations and evaluation criteria, making their results difficult
to compare. We present LegoSR, a unified framework for algebraic
symbolic regression and differential equation discovery. LegoSR
integrates seven solvers through a common equation representation and
evaluation process, and supports searches constrained by partially known
equations. Researchers can run solvers directly or use LegoSR Agent,
which selects solvers and adapts searches based on solver knowledge,
intermediate results and lessons from earlier tasks. On noisy ordinary
and partial differential equation benchmarks, LegoSR Agent recovers
nearly as many equations as the union of seven expert-tuned solvers and
outperforms a generic agent with the same model, tools and budget by up
to 29 percentage points. Three examples demonstrate basic use, adaptive
solver selection and discovery guided by partial physical knowledge.

## 1 Introduction

Mathematical equations are fundamental to describing natural phenomena,
understanding physical laws and predicting the behavior of complex
systems. As scientific data accumulate, data-driven equation discovery
has become an active topic in scientific computing and artificial
intelligence \[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. Numerous methods for symbolic
regression (SR) and ordinary and partial differential equation (ODE/PDE)
discovery have been developed, including sparse
regression \[[2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3)\], evolutionary
search \[[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5), [6](#bib.bib6)\], reinforcement
learning \[[7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8)\] and language
models \[[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10)\]. These methods use different
mathematical representations, search strategies and optimization
schemes, each with strengths for particular scientific problems.
However, most are developed as isolated solvers, with different data
interfaces, ways of incorporating prior knowledge and evaluation
procedures. In practice, scientific discovery is rarely a single run.
Researchers must compare methods, analyze candidate equations and
experimental results, and refine their approach based on previous
findings. These activities require substantial manual effort, while
results and knowledge are difficult to reuse across methods and
experiments. Organizing heterogeneous solvers into a unified, continuous
and traceable scientific discovery process therefore remains an
important challenge.

To address this challenge, we introduce LegoSR, a unified and modular
framework for symbolic regression and equation discovery. LegoSR
integrates seven methods with different representations and search
mechanisms, providing common task definitions and a shared Equation
Intermediate Representation (Equation IR) for exchanging and reusing
equations. A discovery task specifies the data and can incorporate prior
knowledge through a Sketch, a partially specified equation. Reusable
components support derivative computation, coefficient optimization,
candidate evaluation and visualization. Solvers can use these components
while retaining their native search and optimization procedures, without
following a fixed pipeline. LegoSR also organizes experiment
configurations, candidate equations, evaluation results and search
records into a unified experiment management environment. Together,
these capabilities support cross-method comparison, experiment tracking
and reuse of previous results, turning separate solver runs into a
continuous and traceable research process.

However, a unified experiment environment does not automatically
determine how scientific discovery should proceed. Researchers still
need to interpret experimental feedback and decide whether to continue a
search, adjust its configuration or switch solvers. These decisions
require knowledge of different solvers and experience from previous
experiments. Recent advances in large language model (LLM) agents offer
new possibilities for automating such multi-step tasks through planning
and tool use \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]. Such agents have planned and carried
out chemical experiments \[[12](#bib.bib12)\], generated and refined
research hypotheses \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] and solved mechanics problems
by writing and running finite element code \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. Yet
general-purpose agents often lack systematic knowledge of equation
discovery methods and their applicable conditions. Generic prompts and
tool calls alone make it difficult to use experimental history
effectively and develop informed search strategies.

Building on LegoSR’s unified equation representation and experiment
environment, we further develop LegoSR Agent, combining solver
knowledge, structured experimental memory and feedback-driven decision
making. The agent draws on solver papers and previous experiments to
select suitable methods and configure searches. Across multiple rounds
of experimentation, it evaluates candidate equations and search
progress, deciding whether to continue, adjust configurations or switch
solvers. Experimental results and decisions are recorded and reused to
guide subsequent exploration, forming a closed-loop, adaptive scientific
discovery process. In this way, LegoSR extends from a unified equation
discovery framework to an agent-guided system that accumulates
experimental experience and adapts its strategies based on feedback. On
noisy ODE and PDE benchmarks, LegoSR Agent recovers nearly as many
equations as all seven solvers combined under expert-tuned settings, and
more than a generic agent with the same model, tools and budget.

## 2 LegoSR framework

### 2.1 Why a unified framework

Equation discovery methods differ in their mathematical representations,
search strategies and optimization procedures. LegoSR integrates seven
representative solvers covering sparse regression, evolutionary search,
reinforcement learning and language models.
[Table 1](#S2.T1 "In 2.1 Why a unified framework ‣ 2 LegoSR framework ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
summarizes their original designs.

| Method                                                             | Approach                             | Key characteristics                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| SINDy/PySINDy \[[2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3), [15](#bib.bib15)\] | Sparse regression                    | Sparse selection from a user-defined library of candidate terms                                    |
| DLGA-PDE \[[4](#bib.bib4)\]                                        | Neural network and genetic algorithm | Neural-network derivative estimation and evolutionary construction of candidate terms              |
| SGA-PDE \[[5](#bib.bib5)\]                                         | Genetic programming                  | Open-form discovery of compound and fractional terms using expression trees                        |
| PySR \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]                                            | Evolutionary symbolic regression     | Joint search over expression structures and numerical constants, balancing accuracy and complexity |
| DISCOVER \[[7](#bib.bib7)\]                                        | Reinforcement learning               | Symbol-by-symbol equation generation with constraints on the search space                          |
| LLM4ED \[[9](#bib.bib9)\]                                          | Large language model                 | Generation and refinement of candidate equations using prompts and evaluation feedback             |
| EqGPT \[[10](#bib.bib10)\]                                         | Pretrained generative model          | Equation generation using mathematical knowledge learned during pretraining                        |

Table 1: Equation discovery methods integrated in LegoSR and the main
characteristics of their original designs.

These methods are difficult to use within a common research workflow.
Their software dependencies, data interfaces, equation formats and
runtime configurations differ. Many publicly available implementations
were developed for specific experiments, with preprocessing, derivative
computation, search and evaluation tied to dedicated scripts. Applying a
method to new data, switching methods or comparing repeated runs
therefore often requires substantial code changes and manual setup.

LegoSR adapts, refactors and extends these implementations rather than
simply wrapping them behind a common interface. We reorganize procedures
written for single experiments into configurable, reusable pipelines and
extend several solvers to additional problem types. Common task
definitions, equation representations and experiment records allow
different methods to run, be compared and reuse their results within one
framework.

### 2.2 Modular architecture

LegoSR organizes different methods into a unified but flexible workflow
for task preparation, equation search, evaluation and experiment
management
([Fig. 1](#S2.F1 "In 2.2 Modular architecture ‣ 2 LegoSR framework ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")).
The framework standardizes how these stages exchange data and results,
while allowing each solver to retain its own representation, search
strategy and optimization objective. Solvers can therefore be used
individually or as part of a larger discovery process.

![Refer to caption](2610.33465v1/architecture.png)

Figure 1: Architecture of LegoSR. Shared scientific computing modules
support heterogeneous solvers, with Equation IR, Sketches and experiment
management connecting their workflows.

LegoSR supports regular grids, scattered observations and feature
matrices. Shared modules provide derivative computation, coefficient
optimization, data diagnostics, evaluation and visualization. These
modules can be used independently or within a solver pipeline. For
example, researchers can inspect derivative quality before a search, or
fit and evaluate a candidate equation without rerunning the solver.
Separating these operations from solver-specific search code also makes
existing methods easier to extend to new problems.

To exchange equations across methods, LegoSR uses a common Equation
Intermediate Representation (Equation IR). Its term level describes
expressions built from variables, derivatives, mathematical operators
and parameters, while its equation level defines the overall structure,
coefficients and constraints. The IR supports algebraic relations,
evolution equations and homogeneous relations. An evolution PDE, for
example, can be written as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       u_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\xi_{k}\phi_{k},
       ```                                   |     | (1) |

where $`\phi_{k}`$ represents a term involving the physical field,
coordinates and derivatives, such as $`uu_{x}`$ or $`u_{xx}`$, and
$`\xi_{k}`$ is its coefficient. Terms may also contain nonlinear
operations and internal parameters. Solvers still search in their native
forms, such as expression trees, symbol sequences or candidate-term
libraries, and convert their results into Equation IR.

Equation IR expressions can be compiled into callable Python functions
for numerical computation, parameter fitting and evaluation. The common
representation also allows discovered expressions to be reused as
candidate terms or prior knowledge in later
searches \[[16](#bib.bib16)\].

LegoSR uses Sketches to represent partially known equations. A Sketch
specifies the known structure while leaving an expression to be
discovered. For example, when the advection term and its coefficient are
known in the Burgers equation, we can write

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       u_{t}=-uu_{x}+\mathcal{R},
       ```                         |     | (2) |

where $`\mathcal{R}`$ is a hole, an unknown expression that may contain
one or more terms. A Sketch can fix a known term together with its
coefficient, or fix only the term and leave its coefficient to be
fitted. The hole can also be constrained by the number of terms,
variables, mathematical operators and derivative orders.

LegoSR converts a Sketch into a search task for each solver and
reconstructs the complete equation from the result. In this example,
moving the known term to the left gives the target $`u_{t}+uu_{x}`$,
which the solver fits with $`\mathcal{R}`$. Different solvers can
therefore use the same prior knowledge without following the same search
procedure.

For each run, LegoSR records its configuration, search trajectory,
candidate equations and evaluation results. It supports checkpoints,
resuming interrupted runs, branching from previous search states and
batch experiments. Tasks can also be scheduled across CPU and GPU
resources. These records allow researchers to inspect earlier searches
and continue or extend experiments without starting over.

Because native scores, such as AIC or a reward, differ across solvers,
LegoSR preserves them while providing a common evaluation. Candidate
equations are compared under consistent data processing and derivative
estimation settings. For each candidate equation $`E`$, the framework
refits the coefficients by least squares, subject to existing
constraints, and reports the normalized residual $`J(E)`$. Where
applicable, it also integrates $`E`$ forward and compares the predicted
trajectories or fields with observations.

Equation IR, Sketches, shared computing modules and experiment records
also provide the foundation for LegoSR Agent. The agent uses these
resources to select solvers, adjust configurations and plan subsequent
searches based on task requirements and previous results
([Section 3](#S3 "3 LegoSR Agent: adaptive discovery strategy ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")).

### 2.3 Extensibility

New solvers can be integrated by providing task adaptation, an execution
interface and Equation IR conversion. They can then reuse LegoSR’s data
processing, evaluation, visualization and experiment management. New
derivative estimators, coefficient optimizers and evaluation methods can
also be added as shared modules. Once registered, these capabilities can
be accessed by LegoSR Agent through its tool interface.

## 3 LegoSR Agent: adaptive discovery strategy

LegoSR Agent turns the records, evaluations and solver interfaces of
[Section 2](#S2 "2 LegoSR framework ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
into decisions about how discovery should proceed. It combines solver
knowledge with experimental memory to guide choices that a generic agent
must otherwise infer from tool descriptions.

### 3.1 Architecture

LegoSR Agent consists of knowledge, memory and an LLM controller
([Fig. 2](#S3.F2 "In 3.1 Architecture ‣ 3 LegoSR Agent: adaptive discovery strategy ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")).
Around the controller, the agent keeps the task context and interaction
history, runs the selected actions on LegoSR’s scientific tools and
enforces execution limits.

Knowledge has three parts: the task specification identifies the target
and acceptable answer form; solver cards summarize strengths,
limitations and settings tested in publications and earlier experiments;
and data diagnostics describe estimated noise levels and derivative
quality. A solver registered in LegoSR becomes available to the agent at
once, because its card is generated from the information the solver
registers
([Section 2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Extensibility ‣ 2 LegoSR framework ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")).
The agent also receives workflow guidance that describes the discovery
process of
[Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Workflow ‣ 3 LegoSR Agent: adaptive discovery strategy ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery").

Memory operates at two scales. The discovery tree retains each step of
the current task, including its candidate equations, evaluations and
decision rationale. The controller revisits earlier branches to continue
promising searches or change direction, using the record to avoid
repeating unproductive attempts. The experience library stores lessons
from previously graded tasks, retrieved by features such as
dimensionality, noise level and derivative order.

The controller weighs this knowledge and memory to choose the next
experiment or to submit an equation. It follows a ReAct
loop \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]: it reasons over the current evidence, selects
an action and uses the returned observation to guide its next decision.

The controller’s actions operate on the platform’s shared objects:
observed and derived fields, candidates in the Equation IR and Sketches.
These objects support comparison and reuse across solvers; for example,
a neural surrogate from an unsuccessful search supplies derivatives for
subsequent analysis in
[Section 5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery").
Both the agent and a researcher express structural hypotheses through
the same Sketch interface, connecting feedback-derived constraints with
scientific priors. A researcher can therefore step in at any round. The
platform measures and records; the controller decides. Reference
equations remain unavailable during discovery, and submitted candidates
remain traceable to solver searches.

knowledge and memory, chooses the next experiment on the LegoSR platform
and reads back the common evaluation. A researcher can add prior
knowledge as a Sketch with the task.

### 3.2 Workflow

LegoSR Agent improves at two time scales
([Fig. 3](#S3.F3 "In 3.2 Workflow ‣ 3 LegoSR Agent: adaptive discovery strategy ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")):
within a task, each round narrows the hypothesis; across tasks, each
graded task adds lessons. Preparation brings together the task
specification, solver cards, data diagnostics and lessons retrieved from
similar tasks. In each round, the controller then chooses the next
experiment from the available evidence and remaining budget. In the
cases of
[Section 5](#S5 "5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery"),
these choices are of four kinds.

Figure 3: How LegoSR Agent improves. (a) Within a task, each round
rejects terms ($`\times`$), anchors supported terms (filled) and leaves
the rest as a hole $`\mathcal{R}`$ until the hole is filled (blue) and
the equation is checked. (b) Across tasks, each graded task adds a
lesson on what worked ($`\checkmark`$) or failed ($`\times`$), and later
tasks read the library.

First, it chooses how to process the data and estimate derivatives:
directly by finite differences, after smoothing, or through a neural
surrogate. The noisy-data case in
[Section 5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
illustrates why this choice precedes further symbolic search. Second, it
selects a solver and configures its term library, thresholds and search
settings. Solver cards guide changes when progress stalls, as in the
PySINDy–SGA-PDE–DLGA-PDE sequence in
[Section 5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Knowledge-guided solver selection ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery").
Third, it constructs or revises a Sketch, anchoring terms supported by
current evidence while leaving uncertain components open. This step
turns failed searches into new hypotheses: terms on which the searches
agree are anchored, and disputed terms are left as holes. In
[Section 5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery"),
this decision follows tests of individual terms; at higher noise, a
researcher supplies the anchors. Fourth, it chooses how to verify
candidates: remove small terms and refit, seek an independent solver’s
confirmation, test each term’s necessity, or integrate the equation and
compare its dynamics with observations
([Sections 5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Quick start: from data to equations ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery"),
[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Knowledge-guided solver selection ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
and [5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")).

LegoSR executes the chosen step and returns the common evaluation and
search progress. Candidates are compared using the same data,
derivatives and coefficient-fitting procedure, avoiding incompatible
native solver scores. The step, its evidence and the reason for the
decision enter the discovery tree before the controller decides again.
Round by round, terms are rejected, anchored or left as holes until the
equation is found and checked
([Fig. 3](#S3.F3 "In 3.2 Workflow ‣ 3 LegoSR Agent: adaptive discovery strategy ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")a).
When the evidence is sufficient, the controller submits the selected
equation with its supporting record.

For experience construction, submitted answers are graded against
reference equations outside the discovery loop. Outcomes are distilled
into lessons about which choices worked and which failed under which
data conditions. These lessons enter the experience library, and later
tasks retrieve them during preparation
([Fig. 3](#S3.F3 "In 3.2 Workflow ‣ 3 LegoSR Agent: adaptive discovery strategy ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")b).
Lessons transfer across equation families: when the agent solves a task,
lessons from its own family are withheld. This is the self-improvement
mechanism: accumulated evidence changes the guidance for later
decisions, so the agent improves as it solves more problems.
[Section 4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
compares this configuration with a generic agent using the same model,
tools and budget but without solver cards, workflow guidance or an
experience library;
[Section 5](#S5 "5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
illustrates the decisions within individual tasks.

## 4 Experiments

This section evaluates the recovery performance and execution efficiency
of LegoSR Agent. We investigate whether autonomous decision making can
approach, match or surpass expert-tuned equation discovery. To establish
a strong reference, we construct UNION, which combines the successful
recoveries of seven expert-tuned solvers, and compare it with a generic
agent and LegoSR Agent across different tasks and noise levels.

### 4.1 Experimental setup

#### 4.1.1 Benchmark problems

We evaluate 134 algebraic SR problems, 40 ODE targets and 17 PDE
problems.

The algebraic SR benchmark consists of 120 Feynman equations and 14
state equations from seven two-dimensional Strogatz systems. The Feynman
equations and variable ranges are taken from the AI Feynman
database \[[17](#bib.bib17)\]. We independently sample each variable
uniformly within its specified range, generating 10,000 training and
2,500 test samples per problem. For the Strogatz systems from
SRBench \[[18](#bib.bib18)\], we regenerate trajectories through
numerical integration, evaluate the target values using the ground-truth
equations and apply a 75/25 training–test split.

The ODE benchmark contains 40 target equations from 19 dynamical
systems, including the Lorenz, Duffing, Van der Pol, SIR and
FitzHugh–Nagumo systems. Unlike algebraic SR, which uses ground-truth
right-hand sides as regression targets, ODE discovery requires
derivative estimation from observed trajectories. The PDE benchmark
consists of 17 one- and two-dimensional problems, including the Burgers,
Korteweg–de Vries (KdV), Allen–Cahn and Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equations.
The problems are listed in
[appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Benchmark problems ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery").

#### 4.1.2 Noise and recovery criteria

We evaluate three noise levels (0%, 1% and 5%) by adding independent
Gaussian noise to the training data, while keeping the test data
noise-free. For algebraic SR, noise is added to the target $`y`$ with
standard deviation $`\eta\,\mathrm{RMS}(y)`$, where $`\eta`$ denotes the
noise level. For ODEs and PDEs, noise is added to the observed
trajectories or fields before derivative estimation, scaled by the
standard deviation of each variable. All approaches use the same noisy
data for each problem.

We report recovery coverage, defined as the percentage of problems for
which at least one run successfully recovers the target equation. For
algebraic SR, recovery follows the SRBench symbolic equivalence
criterion \[[18](#bib.bib18)\]: after simplification, the predicted
expression must not reduce to a constant, and its difference from the
ground truth must be constant or their ratio must be a nonzero constant.
We additionally require a test $`R^{2}>0.5`$ to exclude numerically
inaccurate solutions.

For ODEs, recovery is determined by symbolic matching or numerical
tolerance. If an expression does not match symbolically, the ratio
between its predicted and true right-hand sides must lie within
$`[0.95,1.05]`$ at every valid evaluation point among 40 sampled points,
with at least 10 valid points. For PDEs, recovery requires the exact set
of ground-truth terms and a maximum relative coefficient error of at
most 5%:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\epsilon_{\max}=\max_{k}\frac{|\hat{\xi}_{k}-\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{true}}|}{|\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{true}}|}\leq 0.05,
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\hat{\xi}_{k}`$ and $`\xi_{k}^{\mathrm{true}}`$ are the
submitted and true coefficients of the term $`\phi_{k}`$ in
[Eq. 1](#S2.E1 "In 2.2 Modular architecture ‣ 2 LegoSR framework ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery").
At grading, the submitted coefficients are evaluated directly without
additional refitting.

#### 4.1.3 Compared approaches

We compare three approaches: Expert-Tuned Solver Union (UNION), a
generic agent and LegoSR Agent.

UNION. UNION combines seven equation discovery methods with tuned
configurations. These configurations are drawn from recommended or
best-performing settings in the original papers and open-source
implementations, or refined through our own experiments. They cover
derivative estimation, data preprocessing, training budgets and search
parameters.

For every problem, each of the seven solvers is run with three random
seeds, giving 21 runs in total. A problem is counted as recovered if any
run succeeds. All results are obtained from actual solver executions and
evaluated using the same recovery criteria.

By combining tuned configurations with the complementary strengths of
seven solvers, UNION achieves coverage at least as high as any of its
seven solvers alone. It provides a strong empirical approximation to the
performance ceiling of expert-tuned solvers, making it a substantially
more demanding reference than a single solver or a collection of methods
using default settings.

Generic Agent. The generic agent receives a basic task description and
has access to the full set of tools provided by LegoSR, without solver
cards, workflow guidance or an experience library.

LegoSR Agent. LegoSR Agent uses the same language model and tools,
augmented by the solver cards, workflow guidance and experience library
of
[Section 3](#S3 "3 LegoSR Agent: adaptive discovery strategy ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
to support autonomous solver selection, configuration and multi-stage
search.

Both agents use the DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 model (deepseek-flash in
the DeepSeek API) and are independently executed three times per problem
under identical budgets. Each run has a 60-minute soft limit, a
120-minute hard limit and a maximum of 400 steps. Model responses are
limited to 16,384 tokens, with a 600-second timeout per request. Input
data are anonymized by removing problem names, ground-truth equations
and original variable names.

The advantage of LegoSR Agent becomes more pronounced in differential
equation discovery, particularly under noisy conditions
([Table 3](#S4.T3 "In 4.2.2 Differential equation discovery ‣ 4.2 Overall performance ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")).
It consistently approaches the expert-tuned UNION while outperforming

coverage by 10 percentage points over the generic agent. For PDEs, the
improvement reaches 29.4 percentage points at both 1% and 5% noise, with
LegoSR Agent matching UNION at 5% noise.

Unlike algebraic SR, differential equation discovery requires
coordinated decisions on data preprocessing, derivative estimation,
solver configuration and search strategies. Inspection of the agent
execution traces shows that LegoSR Agent uses its solver cards and
experience to select appropriate tools and adapt its search based on
intermediate results. In contrast, the generic agent tends to follow
less effective strategies and makes poorer use of search feedback,
despite having access to the same tools. These results demonstrate the
value of combining solver capabilities with structured guidance and
adaptive decision making, allowing LegoSR Agent to approach or match the
recovery performance of UNION.

#### 4.2.3 Execution cost

LegoSR Agent also achieves substantially lower execution times than the
generic agent. For PDE problems at 0%, 1% and 5% noise, its median
wall-clock times are 5.1, 17.2 and 23.2 minutes, compared with 12.3,
41.8 and 47.0 minutes for the generic agent, corresponding to reductions
of 51–59%. On ODE problems at 1% noise, the median time decreases from
8.2 to 3.2 minutes, a reduction of approximately 61%. All statistics are
computed across all runs.

This efficiency gain comes from how LegoSR Agent uses the adaptive
search control provided by LegoSR. LegoSR exposes unified search-control
interfaces for different solvers, allowing the agent to select methods,
adjust search parameters and cap, abandon or restart searches based on
intermediate feedback. Such fine-grained control avoids spending the
full search budget on unpromising runs. Although LegoSR Agent may use
more tool calls or input tokens in some cases, its overall execution
time remains substantially lower.

UNION, by contrast, requires 21 solver runs per problem, together with
extensive offline effort to select and tune configurations for different
problems and noise levels. This exhaustive strategy provides strong
recovery coverage but incurs considerable computational and manual
tuning costs.

#### 5.1.2 Agent-guided workflow

Users can delegate the discovery task to LegoSR Agent through a single
command:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,a2QtYWdlbnQgcnVuIC0tZGF0YSBidXJnZXJzLm5weiAiRmluZCB0aGUgZXF1YXRpb24gZ292ZXJuaW5nIHRoaXMgZGF0YS4i)

The agent receives the first 161 time frames ($`t\in[0,8]`$); the
remaining frames are held out for evaluation. The agent first inspects
the available solver documentation and configurations, then applies
PySINDy with an expanded candidate library to obtain an initial
five-term equation. After identifying and pruning terms with negligible
coefficients, it refits the remaining terms. The agent subsequently
invokes SGA-PDE for an independent search, recovering the same two-term
structure before submitting the final equation.

LegoSR Agent successfully recovered the governing equation across three
independent runs. Tool calls, intermediate candidates and discovery
results are retained, allowing users to inspect and trace the discovery
process.

This example demonstrates how LegoSR extends unified solver execution
into an agent-guided scientific discovery workflow. While the governing
equation can be readily recovered in this elementary example, more
challenging problems place greater demands on solver selection and
configuration. The following example examines how solver knowledge
enables LegoSR Agent to address such challenges.

### 5.2 Knowledge-guided solver selection

While the previous example illustrates the basic use of LegoSR, more
challenging discovery tasks require careful selection and configuration
of numerical methods. To examine the role of solver knowledge in
agent-guided discovery, we consider the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation
with 1% observational noise:

|     |                                  |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{t}=-u\,u_{x}-0.0025\,u_{xxx}.
       ```                               |     | (5) |

The equation combines nonlinear convection with third-order dispersion.
The small dispersion coefficient and the sensitivity of high-order
derivatives to noise make accurate recovery challenging.

the integrated solvers, but only LegoSR Agent receives the solver cards
described in

The KdV dataset is submitted through the agent interface introduced in

[Figure 5](#S5.F5 "In 5.2 Knowledge-guided solver selection ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")a
and b show the discovery process. LegoSR Agent first applies PySINDy,
but sparse regression cannot remove the spurious terms without also
removing the small dispersion term. The agent then switches to SGA-PDE,
which returns the two-term equation
$`u_{t}=-0.725\,u\,u_{x}-0.00179\,u_{xxx}`$. This structure is
physically plausible, since the steepening and splitting of the waves in
the data point to nonlinear convection and dispersion. However, when the
agent integrates this equation from the first observed snapshot, the
solution deviates from the data by 28.9% in relative $`L_{2}`$ norm, far
above the 1% noise level. Adding further terms barely reduces the
fitting residual, so the agent attributes the mismatch to inaccurate
coefficients rather than a missing term. Its solver cards note that, at
this noise level, finite-difference estimates of third-order derivatives
are dominated by noise. The agent therefore switches to DLGA-PDE, which
takes derivatives from a neural surrogate of the field. The final
equation is

|     |                                            |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{t}=-0.9947\,u\,u_{x}-0.002488\,u_{xxx},
       ```                                         |     | (6) |

and its forward-integration error drops to 1.8%, close to the 1.6%
obtained with the true equation on the same data.

Comparison with the true equation confirms this diagnosis. The final
coefficients are within 0.5% of the true values, whereas both SGA-PDE
coefficients were about 28% too low. The contrast is clearest at
$`t=1`$, beyond the time window given to the agent. Integrated from the
noise-free initial state, the SGA-PDE equation deviates from the
noise-free profile by 78% at this time, while the final equation stays
within 1.7%
([Fig. 5](#S5.F5 "In 5.2 Knowledge-guided solver selection ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")c,
d). The generic agent repeatedly explores SGA-PDE and PySR. Its best
equation has the correct terms, but its forward-integration error
remains 23.8%.

This case shows how solver knowledge helps LegoSR Agent diagnose
intermediate results and select a more suitable solver. The next case
considers a harder setting in which initial searches fail to recover the
correct structure, so the agent must adapt its searches and use domain
knowledge.

### 5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery

Choosing a suitable solver is not always enough to recover the governing
equation. In such cases, LegoSR Agent analyzes intermediate results,
revises the candidate structure and adapts its next searches. We
illustrate this with the Chafee–Infante equation,

|     |                       |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{t}=u_{xx}-u+u^{3},
       ```                    |     | (7) |

under 10% and 25% observational noise.

Figure 6: Chafee–Infante equation. (a) $`u_{xx}`$ at $`t=0.26`$ from the
surrogate left by the failed stage-3 runs, with the noise-free field for
reference. (b) Discovery stages for the first of three runs; crosses
mark wrong structures. (c) Normalized residual $`J`$ after dropping each
term of the stage-4 equation, relative to $`J`$ of the full equation.
(d) Coefficients, normalized by the true values, after the Sketch search
(stage 5) and the weak-form fit (stage 6); the band marks $`\pm 5\%`$.
(e, f) Data and the recovered equation integrated from the noise-free
initial state.

At 10% noise, the first searches fail (stages 1–3 in
[Fig. 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")b).
PySINDy returns wrong equations with or without low-pass filtering, and
three DLGA-PDE runs with different random seeds give three different
structures. However, each DLGA-PDE run leaves a neural surrogate of the
field. The derivatives of the surrogate are smooth, whereas finite
differences of the noisy data are dominated by noise
([Fig. 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")a),
so the agent uses the surrogate in the subsequent analysis.

Instead of restarting the search, the agent builds on these failed runs.
It writes a Sketch
([Section 2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Modular architecture ‣ 2 LegoSR framework ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery"))
that anchors the terms shared by the runs, $`u_{xx}`$, $`u`$ and
$`u_{x}`$, and leaves a hole for the nonlinear term, on which the runs
disagree. A search on the surrogate fills the hole with $`u^{3}`$ (stage
4). The agent then tests this equation by dropping one term at a time
and refitting the others
([Fig. 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")c).
Dropping $`u_{x}`$ increases the normalized residual $`J`$ by only 30%,
so $`u_{x}`$ is removed from the anchors. Dropping $`u^{3}`$ increases
$`J`$ by a factor of 9.5, which indicates that a nonlinear term is
needed but does not confirm its form. The agent therefore keeps the hole
open and searches again with only $`u_{xx}`$ and $`u`$ anchored (stage
5).

In all three runs, this search again fills the hole with $`u^{3}`$.
Because the derivatives of the surrogate are least accurate near the
domain boundaries
([Fig. 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")a),
the agent refits the coefficients by weak-form
regression \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] on the observed data (stage 6). This
method integrates the equation against smooth test functions and
requires no derivatives of the data. It reduces the maximum coefficient
error from 19% to 2.3%
([Fig. 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")d),
giving

|     |                                            |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{t}=0.978\,u_{xx}-0.977\,u+0.979\,u^{3}.
       ```                                         |     | (8) |

Integrated from the noise-free initial state, the recovered equation
reproduces the noise-free field with a relative $`L_{2}`$ error of 0.56%
([Fig. 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")e).

At 25% noise, the intermediate results no longer separate the correct
terms from spurious ones, so a researcher supplies the Sketch directly:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,c2tldGNoID0ga2QuU2tldGNoKAogICAgYW5jaG9yZWQ9WyJ1X3h4IiwgInUiXSwKICAgIGhvbGVzPVtrZC5Ib2xlKG1heF90ZXJtcz0yKV0KKQ==)

)

This is the same Sketch that the agent built at 10% noise. In all three
runs, the search fills the hole with $`u^{3}`$. Weak-form regression,
with a correction for the noise bias in the $`u^{3}`$ term, then reduces
the maximum coefficient error from 31% to 3.8%
([Fig. 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")d),
giving

|     |                                            |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{t}=1.022\,u_{xx}-1.038\,u+1.026\,u^{3}.
       ```                                         |     | (9) |

Integrated forward, it reproduces the noise-free field within 0.62%
([Fig. 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")f).

The two noise levels illustrate two uses of the same Sketch interface:
at 10% noise, the agent builds the Sketch from failed searches, whereas
at 25% noise, a researcher provides it from domain knowledge. In both
cases, the agent completes the search and refines the coefficients.
LegoSR Agent thus treats discovery as an iterative process in which each
search informs the next, rather than as a series of isolated solver
calls.

## 6 Related work

### 6.1 Individual equation discovery methods

Data-driven equation discovery aims to identify interpretable
mathematical expressions that describe the governing laws of physical
systems. Early studies used evolutionary search to recover mathematical
relationships from experimental observations \[[20](#bib.bib20)\].
Sparse regression methods, such as SINDy \[[2](#bib.bib2)\] and
PDE-FIND \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], identify governing equations by selecting
relevant terms from predefined candidate libraries.
PySINDy \[[15](#bib.bib15)\] provides a modular implementation of sparse
system identification. These methods are computationally efficient but
depend on the construction of candidate libraries and the quality of
derivative estimates. Weak and integral formulations reduce the
sensitivity to noisy derivatives \[[19](#bib.bib19), [21](#bib.bib21)\].

To reduce these dependencies, researchers have developed more flexible
representations and search strategies. AI Feynman \[[17](#bib.bib17)\]
exploits mathematical properties such as symmetry and separability to
simplify symbolic regression. PDE-Net 2.0 \[[22](#bib.bib22)\] combines
numerical and symbolic neural networks to recover governing PDEs.
EPDE \[[23](#bib.bib23)\], DLGA-PDE \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] and
SGA-PDE \[[5](#bib.bib5)\] build candidate equations by evolutionary
search, so they do not need a complete library. Xu et
al. \[[24](#bib.bib24)\] represented equations as directed graphs to
discover constitutive laws of solids from experimental data. Other
studies incorporate physical constraints into the discovery
process \[[25](#bib.bib25), [26](#bib.bib26), [8](#bib.bib8)\]. Cao and
Zhang \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] combined dimensional and directional
constraints with data-driven identification for multivariable systems.
Zhou et al. \[[28](#bib.bib28)\] integrated evolutionary neural networks
with physics-informed learning to identify PDEs from noisy data.

Deep learning has further expanded equation discovery methods. Deep
Symbolic Regression \[[29](#bib.bib29)\] generates expressions with a
recurrent network trained by risk-seeking policy gradients, and
DISCOVER \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] carries this idea to open-form PDEs.
NeSymReS \[[30](#bib.bib30)\] learns equation structures through
large-scale pretraining. Landajuela et al. \[[31](#bib.bib31)\] combined
complementary symbolic regression strategies into one algorithm.

More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been introduced to
incorporate mathematical knowledge into equation discovery.
LLM4ED \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] and EqGPT \[[10](#bib.bib10)\] use language
models to generate candidate equations, while
LLM-SR \[[32](#bib.bib32)\] combines program-based equation
representations with evolutionary search. SR-LLM \[[33](#bib.bib33)\]
introduces retrieval-augmented generation to reuse accumulated knowledge
during symbolic regression. LaSR \[[34](#bib.bib34)\] extracts
natural-language concepts from high-performing hypotheses and uses them
to guide evolutionary search. Benchmarking studies, including
SRBench \[[18](#bib.bib18)\] and LLM-SRBench \[[35](#bib.bib35)\], have
also highlighted the diversity of equation discovery methods and the
challenges of evaluating them across different problems. These
developments motivate the coordination of complementary methods, whose
representations, search procedures and problem requirements often
differ.

### 6.2 Agentic equation discovery methods

Recent advances in LLMs have enabled more autonomous approaches to
scientific discovery. FunSearch \[[36](#bib.bib36)\] combines
language-model-generated programs with systematic evaluation and
evolutionary search, demonstrating how computational feedback can guide
iterative discovery. Similar ideas have begun to emerge in equation
discovery, where agents can participate in candidate generation, search
control and experimental refinement.

LLM4PD \[[37](#bib.bib37)\] introduces a coarse-to-fine framework for
PDE discovery. It uses hierarchical prompting and a review agent to
identify relevant equation terms, then interacts with a numerical PDE
solver to refine their coefficients. An adaptive optimization strategy
balances further exploration with coefficient refinement. Deliberate
Evolution \[[38](#bib.bib38)\] separates symbolic expression generation
from search control. It employs structural diagnostics, adaptive search
operators and reflective memory to guide subsequent candidate
generation. SR-Scientist \[[39](#bib.bib39)\] gives an agent code-based
tools for data analysis and equation evaluation, and the agent refines
its equation from the evaluation feedback over a long horizon.
DoLQ \[[40](#bib.bib40)\] discovers ODEs with a multi-agent
architecture: one agent proposes candidates, a parameter optimizer
refines them and a scientist agent evaluates them qualitatively and
quantitatively.

These studies demonstrate that agents can use intermediate results to
guide equation discovery rather than simply generate candidate
expressions. Their coordination mechanisms primarily focus on symbolic
search, candidate refinement or collaboration among agent roles within
particular discovery architectures. KeplerAgent \[[41](#bib.bib41)\]
lets an agent choose between PySINDy \[[15](#bib.bib15)\] and
PySR \[[6](#bib.bib6)\] and configure them from physics-based analysis,
and LLM-PySR \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] lets LLM agents plan and adjust a PySR
search. LegoSR extends solver selection to seven independently developed
solvers from four method families. Its common problem statement,
equation representation and run records let its agent select solvers,
adjust their settings and switch between them according to the task and
the experimental feedback.

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented LegoSR, a unified framework for equation discovery
that integrates seven solvers through a common equation representation
and evaluation. Researchers can compare candidate equations across
solvers and check them against the data, and every experiment can be
traced and resumed. Searches can also be constrained by partially known
equations, so physical knowledge enters the discovery directly. Building
on this framework, researchers can delegate the whole discovery process
to LegoSR Agent. Within a task, the agent selects and configures
solvers, examines intermediate results and uses unsuccessful searches to
guide the next steps. Across tasks, it distills graded answers into
lessons that inform later searches. On noisy ODE and PDE benchmarks,
LegoSR Agent nearly matches the union of seven expert-tuned solvers and
outperforms a generic agent with the same model, tools and budget by up
to 29 percentage points. The examples show how adaptive solver
selection, intermediate evidence and partial physical knowledge
contribute to equation recovery. By connecting individual searches and
reusing experience across tasks, LegoSR turns equation discovery from
isolated solver runs into a continuous, adaptive process. Future work
will extend the framework to experimental data, additional solvers and
broader experience reuse.

## Appendix A Tutorial: from installation to equation discovery

This appendix walks through a first use of LegoSR. It installs the
package, configures a language model and then recovers the Burgers
equation of
[Section 5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Quick start: from data to equations ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
in two ways: with Python code and with LegoSR Agent.

### A.1 Installation

LegoSR requires Python 3.11 or later. It is distributed on PyPI as
sail-kd and imported in Python as kd. The source code is available at
[github.com/scientific-artificial-intelligence-lab/KD](https://github.com/scientific-artificial-intelligence-lab/KD).
The following command installs LegoSR together with the components that
LegoSR Agent needs:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,cHl0aG9uIC1tIHBpcCBpbnN0YWxsICJzYWlsLWtkW2FnZW50XSI=)

python -m pip install "sail-kd\[agent\]"

PySR needs the additional pysr component, which downloads a Julia
runtime on first use; sail-kd\[agent,pysr\] installs both. PyTorch is
installed as a dependency. On a machine without a GPU, or with a driver
that does not support the newest CUDA build, the matching PyTorch build
should be installed first, for example the CPU build:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,cHl0aG9uIC1tIHBpcCBpbnN0YWxsIHRvcmNoIC0taW5kZXgtdXJsIGh0dHBzOi8vZG93bmxvYWQucHl0b3JjaC5vcmcvd2hsL2NwdQ==)

python -m pip install torch --index-url
https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cpu

Two commands check the installation:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,cHl0aG9uIC1jICJpbXBvcnQga2Q7IHByaW50KGtkLl9fdmVyc2lvbl9fKSIKa2QtYWdlbnQgLS1oZWxw)

python -c "import kd; print(kd.\_\_version\_\_)"

kd-agent --help

### A.2 Configuring the language model

Searches with the other solvers do not need this step. The command
kd-agent setup asks for four values. It saves them in
~/.config/kd/agent.json, a file that only the user can read:

\$ kd-agent setup

Endpoint (openai, anthropic, local, nvidia, bigmodel): openai

Base URL (leave empty for a preset default): https://api.deepseek.com

Model (leave empty for a preset default): deepseek-flash

The endpoint openai accepts any service with an OpenAI-compatible API,

### A.3 Discovery with Python code

The Python interface has three steps: load the data, fit a solver and
inspect the result. The example below loads the Burgers data bundled
with LegoSR and runs SGA-PDE for 30 generations with 20 candidate
equations each:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,aW1wb3J0IGtkCgpkYXRhID0ga2QubG9hZCgiYnVyZ2VycyIpCm1vZGVsID0ga2QuTW9kZWwoYWxnb3JpdGhtPSJzZ2EiLCBnZW5lcmF0aW9ucz0zMCwKICAgICAgICAgICAgICAgICBwb3B1bGF0aW9uPTIwLCBzZWVkPTApCm1vZGVsLmZpdChkYXRhKQpwcmludChtb2RlbC5yZXN1bHRfLmVxdWF0aW9uKQptb2RlbC5yZXBvcnQoIm91dC9idXJnZXJzLyIp)

import kd

data = kd.load("burgers")

model = kd.Model(algorithm="sga", generations=30,

population=20, seed=0)

model.fit(data)

print(model.result\_.equation)

model.report("out/burgers/")

The search prints $`u_{t}=-u\,u_{x}+0.1002\,u_{xx}`$. model.result\_
holds the discovered equation and the search history, and model.report
writes figures and an HTML report to the given folder. Another solver is
selected by changing only the algorithm argument, while the data and the
remaining code stay the same. The seven solvers are named sga, dlga,
discover, pysr, eqgpt, llm4ed and pysindy. For example, PySINDy with its
default settings recovers the same equation:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,bW9kZWwgPSBrZC5Nb2RlbChhbGdvcml0aG09InB5c2luZHkiKQptb2RlbC5maXQoZGF0YSk=)

model = kd.Model(algorithm="pysindy")

model.fit(data)

A candidate equation can also be checked without running a solver.
kd.refit_terms fits the coefficients of given terms to the data and
reports the normalized residual $`J`$, and kd.forward_error integrates
the equation from the first observed time and returns its relative
$`L_{2}`$ error:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,Zml0ID0ga2QucmVmaXRfdGVybXMoZGF0YSwgWyJ1KnVfeCIsICJ1X3h4Il0pCnByaW50KGZpdC5jb2VmZmljaWVudHMsIGZpdC5ubXNlKQplcnIgPSBrZC5mb3J3YXJkX2Vycm9yKGRhdGEsIFsidSp1X3giLCAidV94eCJdLCBmaXQuY29lZmZpY2llbnRzKQpwcmludChlcnIudmFsdWUp)

fit = kd.refit_terms(data, \["u\*u_x", "u_xx"\])

print(fit.coefficients, fit.nmse)

err = kd.forward_error(data, \["u\*u_x", "u_xx"\], fit.coefficients)

print(err.value)

With method="weak", kd.refit_terms fits the coefficients by weak-form
regression, as in
[Section 5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Adaptive and prior-guided discovery ‣ 5 Illustrative examples ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery").

Data from a file are loaded with the same function. For an NPZ file that
stores the coordinates x and t and the field u as arrays, the mappings
name the arrays and periodic marks a periodic axis:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,ZGF0YSA9IGtkLmxvYWQoIm15X2RhdGEubnB6IiwKICAgICAgICAgICAgICAgY29vcmRzPXsieCI6ICJ4IiwgInQiOiAidCJ9LAogICAgICAgICAgICAgICBmaWVsZHM9eyJ1IjogInUifSwKICAgICAgICAgICAgICAgcGVyaW9kaWM9WyJ4Il0pCiMgT0RFIHRyYWplY3RvcmllczogbWFwIG9ubHkgdGhlIHRpbWUsIGNvb3Jkcz17InQiOiAidCJ9LAojICAgYW5kIGxpc3QgZXZlcnkgc3RhdGUgdmFyaWFibGUgaW4gZmllbGRzLgojIEFsZ2VicmFpYyBkYXRhOiBsaHMgbmFtZXMgdGhlIHRhcmdldCwgZS5nLiBsaHM9InkiOwojICAgdGhlIG90aGVyIGNvbHVtbnMgYmVjb21lIHRoZSBpbnB1dCB2YXJpYWJsZXMu)

data = kd.load("my_data.npz",

coords={"x": "x", "t": "t"},

fields={"u": "u"},

periodic=\["x"\])

\# ODE trajectories: map only the time, coords={"t": "t"},

\# and list every state variable in fields.

\# Algebraic data: lhs names the target, e.g. lhs="y";

\# the other columns become the input variables.

kd.inspect_file lists the arrays in a file when their names are not
known. Other formats, such as CSV, HDF5 and XLSX, are loaded with the
same function.

A Sketch adds known physics to the search
([Section 2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Modular architecture ‣ 2 LegoSR framework ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")).
It is useful when part of the equation is known, for example a diffusion
term or a conservation form, or when earlier searches agree on some
terms. A Sketch has three parts. Anchored terms must appear in the
equation, and their coefficients are fitted. Pinned terms are fixed
together with their coefficients. Holes are left for the solver to fill,
and each hole can limit the number of terms, the derivative order, the
operators and the variables it may use. The Sketch below keeps the
diffusion term $`u_{xx}`$ with a free coefficient and leaves a hole for
one further term:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,c2tldGNoID0ga2QuU2tldGNoKGFuY2hvcmVkPVsidV94eCJdLAogICAgICAgICAgICAgICAgICAgaG9sZXM9W2tkLkhvbGUobWF4X3Rlcm1zPTEpXSkKbW9kZWwgPSBrZC5Nb2RlbChhbGdvcml0aG09InNnYSIsIHNlZWQ9MCkKbW9kZWwuZml0KGRhdGEsIHNrZXRjaD1za2V0Y2gp)

sketch = kd.Sketch(anchored=\["u_xx"\],

holes=\[kd.Hole(max_terms=1)\])

model = kd.Model(algorithm="sga", seed=0)

model.fit(data, sketch=sketch)

The solver searches for the term in the hole and fits the coefficient of
the anchored term, and LegoSR checks that the final equation contains
the anchored term. The Sketch of
[Eq. 2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2 Modular architecture ‣ 2 LegoSR framework ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
instead fixes the advection term with its coefficient and leaves a hole
for at most two terms with derivatives up to second order:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,c2tldGNoID0ga2QuU2tldGNoKHBpbm5lZD17InUqdV94IjogLTEuMH0sCiAgICAgICAgICAgICAgICAgICBob2xlcz1ba2QuSG9sZShtYXhfdGVybXM9MiwgbWF4X2Rlcml2X29yZGVyPTIpXSk=)

sketch = kd.Sketch(pinned={"u\*u_x": -1.0},

holes=\[kd.Hole(max_terms=2, max_deriv_order=2)\])

LegoSR moves the pinned term to the left-hand side, so the solver fits
$`u_{t}+u\,u_{x}`$ with the terms in the hole. Any of the seven solvers
accepts the same Sketch.

### A.4 Discovery with LegoSR Agent

The command kd-agent run hands the whole task to LegoSR Agent. For the
bundled Burgers data, the command is

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,a2QtYWdlbnQgcnVuIC0tZGF0YXNldCBidXJnZXJzICJGaW5kIHRoZSBlcXVhdGlvbiBnb3Zlcm5pbmcgdGhpcyBkYXRhLiI=)

kd-agent run --dataset burgers "Find the equation governing this data."

For a data file, --data replaces --dataset, and --load-options takes the
mappings of kd.load as a JSON object:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,a2QtYWdlbnQgcnVuIC0tZGF0YSBteV9kYXRhLm5weiBcCiAgLS1sb2FkLW9wdGlvbnMgJ3siY29vcmRzIjogeyJ4IjogIngiLCAidCI6ICJ0In0sICJmaWVsZHMiOiB7InUiOiAidSJ9fScgXAogICJGaW5kIHRoZSBlcXVhdGlvbiBnb3Zlcm5pbmcgdGhpcyBkYXRhLiI=)

kd-agent run --data my_data.npz \\

--load-options '{"coords": {"x": "x", "t": "t"}, "fields": {"u": "u"}}'
\\

"Find the equation governing this data."

kd-agent chat takes the same data options and opens an interactive
session, in which the researcher can discuss the search with the agent
and steer it. Each run has its own folder under ./kd-agent-runs/. The
file report.md in this folder gives the submitted equation and its
coefficients, the fit metrics, the search history and the unsuccessful
attempts. The option --workspace reopens the folder in a later session.

A researcher gives a Sketch to the agent as a file. After the Sketch is
saved with sketch.save("sketch.json"), the agent runs every search under
it and fills the holes:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,a2QtYWdlbnQgcnVuIC0tZGF0YSBteV9kYXRhLm5weiAtLXNrZXRjaCBza2V0Y2guanNvbiBcCiAgIkZpbmQgdGhlIGVxdWF0aW9uIGdvdmVybmluZyB0aGlzIGRhdGEuIg==)

kd-agent run --data my_data.npz --sketch sketch.json \\

"Find the equation governing this data."

In kd-agent chat, the researcher can also describe the known terms in
words, and the agent writes the Sketch.

Besides the solver documentation shipped with LegoSR, LegoSR Agent can
read usage advice and an experience library built from earlier runs,
both given as files:

[⬇](data:text/plain;base64,a2QtYWdlbnQgcnVuIC0tZGF0YSBteV9kYXRhLm5weiAtLWFkdmljZSBhZHZpY2UuanNvbiBcCiAgLS1tZW1vcnkgZXhwZXJpZW5jZS5qc29uICJGaW5kIHRoZSBlcXVhdGlvbiBnb3Zlcm5pbmcgdGhpcyBkYXRhLiI=)

kd-agent run --data my_data.npz --advice advice.json \\

--memory experience.json "Find the equation governing this data."

Strogatz systems of SRBench \[[18](#bib.bib18)\], as described in
[Section 4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery").
[Table B1](#A2.T1 "In Appendix B Benchmark problems ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
lists the 19 ODE systems, which give 40 target equations, and
[Table B2](#A2.T2 "In Appendix B Benchmark problems ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery")
lists the 17 PDE problems. The last seven ODE systems are the Strogatz
systems of the SR benchmark, whose derivatives must here be estimated
from trajectories. PDE domains are given as space $`\times`$ time, and a
half-open interval marks a periodic axis. Grid sizes refer to the
complete data before the training–test split. In
[Table B2](#A2.T2 "In Appendix B Benchmark problems ‣ LegoSR: A Unified Framework for Self-improving Agent-Guided Scientific Discovery"),
the citation after each problem name gives the source of its data. The
KdV data keep every second spatial point of the $`512\times 201`$ source
grid. The Chafee–Infante data use the reaction term $`-u+u^{3}`$ of
their source, whereas the classical Chafee–Infante equation
$`u_{t}=u_{xx}+\lambda(u-u^{3})`$ \[[43](#bib.bib43)\] has the opposite
sign. The linear advection data are generated with the recipe of
MDBench \[[44](#bib.bib44)\], which follows
PDEBench \[[45](#bib.bib45)\].

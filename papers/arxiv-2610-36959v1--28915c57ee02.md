###### Abstract

Streaming multimodal assistants must make response decisions while
events are still unfolding. They need to recognize when the available
evidence warrants a response, produce an appropriate continuation, and
remain silent otherwise. Supervised fine-tuning learns from recorded
assistant turns, but does not directly train on the response prefixes
produced by the evolving policy itself. We introduce Live-OPSD, an
on-policy self-distillation method that uses privileged hints available
only during training to supervise the policy on its own outputs. The
policy first generates a continuation from the streaming context alone.
The same model then re-evaluates that continuation with a hint
describing the relevant evidence, and the resulting distributions guide
the unhinted policy. On Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B, Live-OPSD attains an average
score of 59.64 on LiveProBench, 5.26 points above the strongest
continued-SFT baseline, and stays competitive on OVO-Bench (65.84).
These results indicate that guiding the policy on its own outputs with
privileged hints improves both when and how a streaming assistant
responds.

## 1 Introduction

For a streaming assistant, a correct answer can still be unhelpful if it
arrives at the wrong moment. Unlike offline video question answering,
continuous interaction requires decisions while evidence is still
arriving. A user may refer to an event that has just occurred or issue a
standing instruction that becomes actionable later. Speaking too early
interrupts the interaction; remaining silent after the relevant event
misses the opportunity to help. Proactive streaming therefore couples
multimodal understanding with a temporal decision: does the current
evidence warrant a response?

Recent video-language models have moved toward continuous interaction by
combining streaming perception and temporally aligned dialogue with
explicit response control ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib6); [Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib21); [Qian et al., 2025](#bib.bib7); [Wang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib22); [Ding et al., 2025](#bib.bib23); [Azad et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8)). Omni-modal backbones further bring visual evidence,
sound, and language into a shared model ([Xu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9);
[Xu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib10)). This progress provides the perception
and interaction interface needed for continuous assistance, but leaves
an important training question: how can a model learn from response
prefixes produced by its current policy rather than only from recorded
assistant turns?

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) optimizes reference continuations at
annotated prefixes. It does not directly revisit the prefixes produced
when the policy speaks too early, remains silent when a response is due,
or begins an unsuitable continuation despite making the correct
speak-or-silence decision. Spoken responses can also contribute many
more training tokens than silence, creating asymmetric supervision. AURA
and StreamPro address this imbalance by reweighting recorded supervision
([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)), but
their targets remain tied to annotated actions and prefixes. As the
policy changes, the supervision needed to correct its own continuations
changes with it.

Live-OPSD targets this response-level gap through privileged-context
self-distillation
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")).
At candidate decision points, the current policy first generates a
continuation from the streaming context alone. The same model, now
conditioned on a training-time hint that describes the relevant evidence
and intended behavior, provides distributional targets for selected
prefixes of those continuations. Distilling this privileged view guides
the unhinted policy on text it produced itself, combining learning with
privileged information ([Vapnik and Vashist, 2009](#bib.bib1)) and
on-policy distillation ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)). Not all
decision points carry the same training signal: current disagreements
expose errors, while consistent responses and silence preserve
behavioral coverage. Decision-Aware Selection (DAS) uses this structure
to construct a bounded update set.

We evaluate Live-OPSD as a post-training objective on Qwen2.5-Omni-7B
and Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B. Controlled comparisons use a common streaming
interface and three continued-SFT objectives. On the 30B backbone,
Live-OPSD attains an average score of 59.64 on LiveProBench, compared
with 54.38 for the strongest continued-SFT baseline, a gain of 5.26
points. Released proactive systems provide a separate system-level
comparison on OVO-Bench and OmniMMI proactive alerting. Ablations
compare the divergence objectives and evaluate the overall selection
scheme against a fixed-position control. Together, the results provide
evidence that post-training on current-policy response prefixes improves
proactive streaming behavior beyond continued SFT in the evaluated
setting.

## 2 Related Work

##### Streaming perception and interaction.

Streaming video models must preserve information from an evolving visual
history while remaining responsive to new observations and user turns.
VideoLLM-online learns temporally aligned dialogue over continuous
video, whereas Flash-VStream and related memory-based approaches
compress or retrieve historical visual states for efficient online
question answering ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib6); [Zhang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib20)). MMDuet instead represents video and text as
interleaved turns and augments the model with informative and relevance
heads for time-sensitive interaction ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)).
These methods establish the streaming interface on which proactive
behavior can be built, but their primary contribution is not an
on-policy post-training objective.

Proactive systems differ in where they place the response decision.
Dispider separates perception, decision, and reaction; StreamBridge
combines a persistent history with a separately trained activation
model; and StreamMind invokes the language model only when a
query-conditioned event gate predicts a response-worthy moment ([Qian et
al., 2025](#bib.bib7); [Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib22); [Ding et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23)). StreamReady learns an explicit answer-readiness
signal, Em-Garde matches structured visual proposals during streaming,
and StreamAgent uses future-aware planning to choose whether to wait,
respond, or gather additional evidence ([Azad et al., 2026](#bib.bib8);
[Zheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib24);
yang2025streamagent，qian2025dispider). These designs modify
inference-time gating, planning, memory, or computation. Live-OPSD
instead introduces a post-training objective for an existing streaming
policy and leaves its native inference mechanism unchanged.

Open-source proactive models provide further system-level comparisons.
AURA combines streaming context management with a Silent-Speech Balanced
objective; JoyAI-VL-Interaction makes response decisions within a
vision-language model and can delegate generation to a background model;
ROMA uses aligned audio-visual streaming units and a dedicated speak
head; and MOSS-VL-Realtime permits incoming visual information during
generation through cross-attention ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Yao
et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Tian et al., 2026](#bib.bib15); [Wang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib16)). Their architectural and training differences
are retained in our model-level comparison and are not conflated with
the controlled objective comparison on two Qwen backbones.

##### Learning response timing.

Supervised timing objectives primarily differ in how they weight
recorded speech and silence. AURA masks supervised turns and reweights
silent messages, whereas StreamPro’s CB-Stream Loss balances decision
positions and response text ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). StreamPro subsequently applies GRPO with turn-level
and trajectory-level rewards, and MMDuet2 studies multi-turn
reinforcement learning for timely proactive responses ([Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12); [Wang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib13)). These
sequence-level objectives directly optimize timing rewards but require
long-stream rollouts and an explicit reward definition. Live-OPSD uses a
different decomposition: the current response decision determines which
contexts receive the update, while a privileged teacher distribution
supplies the token-level learning signal on the policy’s own
continuation.

##### Distillation with privileged information.

Knowledge distillation transfers a teacher’s output distribution to a
student ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib2)). On-policy variants evaluate
the teacher on student-generated continuations, thereby exposing the
objective to prefixes visited by the current student ([Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib4); [Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)). This shift is related
to the learner-induced state distribution studied in imitation learning
([Ross et al., 2011](#bib.bib3)). Live-OPSD applies the same principle
at streaming decision contexts. Learning with privileged information
supplies the asymmetry ([Vapnik and Vashist, 2009](#bib.bib1)): teacher
and student share the current parameters, but only the teacher receives
a training-time hint.

##### Evaluation of streaming behavior.

OVO-Bench and OmniMMI evaluate complementary aspects of causal stream
understanding, including real-time perception, contextual reasoning,
forward active responding, proactive alerting, and interaction ([Li et
al., 2025](#bib.bib18); [Wang et al., 2025c](#bib.bib19)). StreamArena
extends this scope to hour-scale open-ended streams and jointly
evaluates real-time perception, historical recall, proactive
interaction, and tool use ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)). We use
OVO-Bench and OmniMMI to test whether timing-oriented post-training
preserves broad streaming capabilities, while LiveProBench isolates
response timing under standing instructions ([Du et al.,
2026](#bib.bib27)).

## 3 Streaming Interaction

##### States and actions.

Let $`x_{t}`$ be the event arriving at step $`t`$, either an
audio-visual stream chunk or a user utterance. The visible interaction
history is $`s_{t}=(x_{1},a_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1},x_{t})`$, where $`a_{t}`$
denotes the assistant’s complete turn. The action space contains a set
of natural-language responses $`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{speak}}`$ and a
silence action $`\varnothing`$. The arriving event induces two state
types and their admissible actions:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{A}(s_{t})=\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{speak}},&x_{t}\text{ is a user utterance},\\[2.0pt]
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{speak}}\cup\{\varnothing\},&x_{t}\text{ is a stream chunk}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (1) |

A user utterance requires a response, which can include an
acknowledgment or an explanation that the available evidence is
insufficient. A stream chunk permits either response or silence.
Equation [1](#S3.E1 "In States and actions. ‣ 3 Streaming Interaction ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
specifies the task requirements, although the language-model decoder can
still emit an inadmissible action. After acting, the state advances to
$`s_{t+1}=s_{t}\oplus a_{t}\oplus x_{t+1}`$. The policy has access to
the visible prefix, not future observations.

##### Decision supervision.

Write $`d(a)=\mathbb{1}[a\neq\varnothing]`$ for the response decision
induced by an action. This decision is defined by the action’s meaning
under the interaction protocol, independently of the tokens used to
represent response or silence. A training point consists of a recorded
context $`\bar{s}_{t}`$, a supervisory decision $`b_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$, and
a privileged hint $`h_{t}`$. The decision specifies whether the
annotated behavior is to speak. It does not specify the full response.
At a chunk step, $`b_{t}=1`$ marks a proactive response and $`b_{t}=0`$
marks silence. The hint supplies a short description of the relevant
visual or acoustic evidence and its relation to the intended behavior.
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Decision supervision. ‣ 3 Streaming Interaction ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
shows one annotated dialogue with these units in place.

![Refer to caption](2610.36959v1/data.png)

Figure 2: Supervision at streaming decision points. Each interleaved
chunk or user turn carries a decision $`b_{t}`$ and a privileged hint
$`h_{t}`$. Streaming SFT learns the recorded action, whereas Live-OPSD
supplies the hint only to the teacher.

##### Training instantiation.

Streaming SFT initializes the policy used in our experiments, including
its handling of interleaved chunks, dialogue turns, and silence. Its
target at each step is the recorded action associated with $`b_{t}`$: an
annotated response when $`b_{t}=1`$ and the silence action when
$`b_{t}=0`$. Although $`b_{t}`$ specifies only the binary decision, the
recorded action supplies the full token-level target. Writing
$`\bar{a}_{t}=(\bar{y}_{t,1},\ldots,\bar{y}_{t,|\bar{a}_{t}|})`$ for
this action, let $`\mathcal{S}`$ contain the silent assistant messages
in a training instance and let $`t^{+}`$ denote its final non-silent
assistant message, when one is present. We supervise every token of
messages in $`\mathcal{S}\cup\{t^{+}\}`$ and exclude earlier non-silent
messages whose supporting evidence may have been removed by window
truncation. Accordingly,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
m_{t,\ell}=\mathbb{1}[t\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{t^{+}\}],\qquad w_{t,\ell}=\begin{cases}1/N_{\mathrm{sil}},&t\in\mathcal{S},\\
1,&t=t^{+},\end{cases}
``` |  | (2) |

where $`N_{\mathrm{sil}}=|\mathcal{S}|`$ is the number of silent
messages in the instance. With
$`N_{\mathrm{sup}}=\sum_{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|\bar{a}_{t}|}m_{t,\ell}`$,
the initialization objective is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\phi)=-\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{sup}}}\sum_{t}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|\bar{a}_{t}|}m_{t,\ell}w_{t,\ell}\log\pi_{\phi}\!\left(\bar{y}_{t,\ell}\mid\bar{s}_{t},\bar{y}_{t,<\ell}\right).
``` |  | (3) |

Thus, silence supervision is preserved without allowing the number of
silent messages to dominate the informative response. The checkpoint
obtained by minimizing $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\phi)`$, denoted
*Live-SFT-Zero*, initializes Live-OPSD and supplies the first reference
parameters $`\theta_{0}`$. The SSB-SFT row in
Section [5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Main comparisons ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
continues this objective from the same initialization, whereas
Vanilla-SFT and CB-SFT use the alternative objectives defined in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Supervised baseline objectives ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
The hint $`h_{t}`$ is not part of this stage’s input or target.

Live-OPSD then generates a new assistant continuation at each recorded
context $`\bar{s}_{t}`$ under the current decoding rule, while retaining
earlier assistant turns from the recorded history. The resulting action,
including a silence action, and all of its token prefixes therefore come
from the current policy. This response-level on-policy rollout exposes
the update to current-model prefixes without claiming that the entire
preceding interaction was generated on policy. At deployment, earlier
generated turns become part of the evolving history $`s_{t}`$. Training
and deployment thus differ in the source of earlier assistant turns,
while the causal observation boundary in
Equation [1](#S3.E1 "In States and actions. ‣ 3 Streaming Interaction ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
applies in both.

## 4 Live-OPSD

Live-OPSD couples privileged-context distribution matching with adaptive
selection of streaming decision points. The two supervisory inputs play
distinct roles: the hint $`h_{t}`$ conditions the teacher, and the
decision $`b_{t}`$ guides DAS.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
shows how these paths meet in the update objective.

### 4.1 Privileged-context self-distillation

A hint explains the evidence supporting an annotated response decision.
For a request to report entry into a restricted area, for example, it
might state that a person is visible outside the area and the assistant
should remain silent. The teacher receives the hint in its system
context. The student receives the same streaming observations and
deployment instruction without the hint. In our implementation, an
external model annotates hints offline. It supplies text, whereas the
distillation teacher is the current policy conditioned on that text.
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Privileged hints and prompts ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
gives the annotation and prompt format.

Let $`\theta_{0}`$ denote the parameters at the start of an update. For
each point, the student generates $`a_{t}=(y_{t,1},\ldots,y_{t,L_{t}})`$
from $`\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid\bar{s}_{t})`$ under the decoding rule
used for training. At response position $`\ell`$, we compare

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle P_{t}^{(\ell)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid\bar{s}_{t},h_{t},y_{t,<\ell}),`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle Q_{\theta,t}^{(\ell)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\bar{s}_{t},y_{t,<\ell}),`$ |  |  |

where $`y_{t,<\ell}`$ is the student-generated prefix. Both roles
predict the next token after that same prefix, so teacher scoring
requires no separately generated teacher response. The target and
generated continuation remain fixed while the student is updated from
$`\theta_{0}`$.

After the update, the new parameters are used for both rollout and
teacher scoring. The offline hints are reused, but the distributional
targets are recomputed. The teacher’s additional context can make the
evidence–decision relationship explicit, with its usefulness depending
on annotation quality and the model’s ability to interpret the hint.
Appendix [I.1](#A9.SS1 "I.1 Privileged context and decision ambiguity ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
distinguishes the information supplied by a hint from the decision
advantage actually realized by the hinted model.

### 4.2 A distributional objective

For teacher distribution $`P`$ and student distribution $`Q`$, our
default objective is reverse KL,
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{rKL}}(P,Q)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|P)`$. We compare it
with forward KL,
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fKL}}(P,Q)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)`$, and symmetric
Jensen–Shannon divergence. For completeness, the implemented skew-JSD
family is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{JS}}^{\beta}(P,Q)=\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|M_{\beta})+(1-\beta)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|M_{\beta}),\qquad M_{\beta}=\beta P+(1-\beta)Q,
``` |  | (5) |

where $`0<\beta<1`$ and $`\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}`$ gives symmetric JSD. The
unscaled divergence is zero at both endpoints. Its scaled limits are

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{D_{\mathrm{JS}}^{\beta}(P,Q)}{\beta}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q),\qquad\lim_{\beta\to 1}\frac{D_{\mathrm{JS}}^{\beta}(P,Q)}{1-\beta}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|P).
``` |  |

We therefore implement the two KL directions as separate objectives
rather than as JSD endpoint values. In every case, gradients flow
through $`Q`$ while $`P`$ remains fixed.

Let $`\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{sel}}`$ be the effective set of selected,
teacher-scored points defined in
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Decision-Aware Selection ‣ 4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
For a chosen divergence $`\mathcal{D}`$, the update minimizes its token
mean over the generated assistant continuations, including silence
actions:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta;\theta_{0})=\frac{1}{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{sel}}}L_{t}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{sel}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{t}}\mathcal{D}\!\left(P_{t}^{(\ell)},Q_{\theta,t}^{(\ell)}\right).
``` |  | (6) |

We use $`\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{rKL}}`$ by default. The
generated continuations, selected points, and teacher targets are fixed
during this update. Gradients optimize the student’s conditional
distributions on these prefixes without differentiating through the
discrete decoding process. Thus, current-policy generation supplies the
training prefixes, while distillation supplies the update. Each point
contributes in proportion to its continuation length under the global
token-mean reduction.
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Experimental configuration ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
describes the efficient target representation used in the
implementation.

Appendix [I.2](#A9.SS2 "I.2 From on-policy distillation to response decisions ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
connects the default reverse-KL objective to response decisions. It
bounds the student’s disagreement by the teacher’s disagreement plus
cumulative reverse KL on the student-generated prefix that resolves the
decision and characterizes the approximation used in training.

### 4.3 Decision-Aware Selection

DAS uses the current policy’s response decisions to allocate a budget of
at most $`K`$ training points per update. Eligible points are chunk
steps, with user utterances and their responses kept in context. For a
generated action $`a_{t}`$, the indicator
$`\delta_{t}=\mathbb{1}[d(a_{t})\neq b_{t}]`$ records disagreement with
the supervisory decision $`b_{t}`$. Eligible points split into
decision-inconsistent points $`\mathcal{I}`$ ($`\delta_{t}=1`$),
consistent responses $`\mathcal{R}`$, and consistent silence; teacher
scoring covers $`\mathcal{I}`$, $`\mathcal{R}`$, and a
policy-independent subsample of consistent silence.

From this scored pool DAS keeps at most $`K`$ points in priority order:
an inconsistency quota with the two directions alternated, then
consistent responses, then silence spread across videos, relaxing to the
full budget when scored silence is scarce. Selection, not gradient
weighting, depends on $`\delta_{t}`$: a decision-consistent action can
still yield a distillation signal, because agreeing on the decision does
not imply agreeing on the token distributions. Selection is refreshed
from new rollouts each update. At inference, the policy uses the
backbone’s native response interface without hints, DAS, or an extra
classifier.

shows the outcome classes and how the scored pool fills the budget;

and [I.3](#A9.SS3 "I.3 Which contexts decision-aware selection emphasizes ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
give the quota rule and the disagreement it concentrates, and

summarizes one update.

## 5 Experiments

The evaluation examines whether post-training improves response timing
while preserving the understanding needed for useful responses.
Within-backbone comparisons assess the training procedure, system-level
comparisons place it alongside released proactive models, and ablations
isolate the divergence and selection rule.

### 5.1 Experimental setup

##### Backbones and training data.

We use Qwen2.5-Omni-7B and Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B ([Xu et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib9); [Xu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib10)). Both are adapted to
the same streaming interaction protocol, with audio-visual chunks
interleaved with user and assistant turns. The training corpus includes
direct questions and standing instructions that require proactive
responses. For post-training, dialogues are expanded into causal
decision contexts with aligned supervisory decisions and hints. Source
videos define the split boundary so that contexts from one source remain
in one split.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Training data and streaming contexts ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
describes data construction, and
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Experimental configuration ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
reports the run settings verified from the available artifacts.

##### Supervised baselines.

We compare three objectives on each backbone. *Vanilla-SFT* uses
standard cross-entropy on reference assistant turns. *CB-SFT* uses
StreamPro’s CB-Stream Loss ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)). *SSB-SFT*
uses AURA’s Silent-Speech Balanced Loss([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)).
Live-OPSD starts from a same streaming-SFT model and then applies the
post-training procedure in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").

##### Open-source proactive models.

The model-level comparison on LiveProBench
(Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Open-source proactive models. ‣ 5.1 Experimental setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models"))
includes AURA, JoyAI-VL-Interaction, ROMA, and MOSS-VL-Realtime ([Lu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Yao et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Tian et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)). Each model retains
the response mechanism and context handling of its released inference
configuration. For each benchmark, task-specific scoring and aggregation
follow the protocol described below and in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Evaluation details ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").

## 6 Discussion and Limitations

##### Scope and transfer requirements.

Live-OPSD is a post-training principle for streaming interaction,
instantiated with a streaming SFT checkpoint and a specific protocol.
Defined through conditional policies and semantic actions rather than a
mandatory two-stage framework, it requires aligned decision supervision
and conditional likelihood evaluation with and without privileged
context for protocol adaptation. For example, JoyAI-VL-Interaction marks
silence and response with dedicated tokens ([Yao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)); the decision map $`d`$ reads those markers while
preserving the native protocol, with teacher and student sharing a
vocabulary within each backbone. Richer action spaces, such as
introducing interrupt and correction actions, require corresponding
supervision and selection categories.

##### Current instantiation.

Offline hints and recorded histories make training tractable. Pilot
checks found Qwen3-Omni inconsistent as its own hint annotator over
multi-turn histories, so we use an external model for annotation and
reserve the hinted policy for distributional scoring—motivating an
implementation choice rather than revealing a backbone limitation.
Generating continuations at recorded histories provides response-level
on-policy supervision but mismatches model-generated deployment
histories. Thus, the source of histories and feedback is an
implementation choice, whereas access only to observations at each
decision is a causal constraint.

##### Toward online self-training.

Online hint generation and trajectory-level on-policy generation would
align feedback with student-generated histories, and reliable multi-turn
self-assessment could eventually let the model propose its own hints,
provided they stay grounded in the observed stream and add information
beyond its unassisted judgment. Because the current method still depends
on external hints and supervised response decisions, these are steps
toward online self-training rather than a fully self-supervised
procedure.

## 7 Conclusion

We presented Live-OPSD, a post-training method that uses privileged
context to supervise a streaming policy on its own continuations. A
hinted model copy supplies token-level targets, while Decision-Aware
Selection allocates the update budget by response decisions. On
Qwen3-Omni, Live-OPSD scores 59.64 on LiveProBench, 5.26 points above
the strongest continued-SFT baseline, while remaining competitive on
broader benchmarks. Live-OPSD thus provides evidence for privileged
on-policy distillation as a promising training principle for proactive
streaming video models.

## Appendix A Training data and streaming contexts

Each dialogue interleaves audio-visual chunks with user instructions and
assistant actions. Direct questions supervise responses from evidence
available at the question time. Standing instructions instead specify a
condition to monitor, with silence between relevant events. A decision
context includes all preceding turns and the media visible at that
point. Flattening a dialogue into training points changes the units
sampled for optimization, not the information available within each
context.

For Live-OPSD, each retained point has a binary supervisory decision and
a teacher hint. Points without usable teacher supervision are excluded
from the effective distillation set. Data statistics distinguish source
videos, dialogues, and decision points because multiple contexts can
come from one source. The split is established before expansion, and
training sources are checked against benchmark sources to avoid overlap.
Complete corpus counts are not available in the current artifact and are
therefore not reported here.

The Live-OPSD dataset contains 3,712 training instances. Both Live-OPSD
and the continued-SFT baselines use this dataset, with the latter
trained on its reference responses. The preceding streaming-SFT stage
uses approximately 196,000 training instances. All source videos are
drawn from publicly available datasets.

## Appendix B Privileged hints and prompts

An external annotation model produces hints offline from the dialogue,
reference decision, and source media. The annotator can access the full
clip, but the hint concerns the evidence relevant to the current
decision. It states what is visible or audible, whether the assistant
should respond, and the reason. Because it is analysis rather than the
answer, the reference response never appears inside the hint. The hint
is passed to the current policy as privileged context; this hinted
policy supplies the teacher distributions in
Equation [4](#S4.E4 "In 4.1 Privileged-context self-distillation ‣ 4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
The student’s prompt and multimodal input remain bounded by the current
streaming time.

The deployed system prompt is:

> You are an AI assistant that processes real-time streaming video. For
> each video stream chunk in every interaction turn, decide whether to
> remain silent or respond.
> - If you remain silent, output only \<\|silence\|\>.
> - If you respond, do so naturally at the appropriate moment.

The teacher and student receive the same multimodal input and the same
interaction to be answered, while the teacher additionally receives the
point-specific hint in current stream context. They share the same chat
template, tokenizer, and media representation. Their prompt lengths can
differ, so response positions are aligned relative to the end of each
prompt.

For a standing instruction to report a person in a white shirt entering
an area, an illustrative hint is:

> A person in a dark shirt is visible outside the monitored area, but no
> person in a white shirt has entered it, so the assistant should remain
> silent.

The hint is a teacher input, not a reference sentence for the student to
repeat. The current artifact does not preserve the annotation-model
identity or retention counts required to regenerate these hints exactly.

## Appendix C Selection details and fixed-selection control

Let $`\mathcal{I}`$, $`\mathcal{R}`$, and $`\mathcal{S}`$ denote the
scored pools of decision-inconsistent points, consistent responses, and
consistent silence. Write
$`\mathcal{I}_{0}=\{t\in\mathcal{I}:b_{t}=0\}`$ and
$`\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{t\in\mathcal{I}:b_{t}=1\}`$ for the two
inconsistency directions. Their orders are randomized independently and
interleaved until one pool is exhausted, after which the remaining
points of the other pool follow.

A fraction $`\alpha\in(0,1]`$ sets the nominal discrepancy quota
$`q=\max\{1,\lfloor\alpha K\rfloor\}`$. If the scored consistent-silence
pool has fewer than $`q`$ points, the quota is relaxed to $`K`$.
Otherwise, at most $`q`$ inconsistent points enter the ordered
selection. The remaining slots are filled first from randomized
consistent responses, then from consistent-silence points distributed
round-robin across videos, with randomized order inside each video.
Selection affects the sample composition; it does not introduce
inverse-frequency weights into
Equation [6](#S4.E6 "In 4.2 A distributional objective ‣ 4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").

The consistent-silence scoring subset is determined independently of the
current response probabilities, using a stable point-level subsampling
rule. The selected effective set contains only scored points. The
scoring fraction, quota, and effective number of contributing points
jointly determine the composition and cost of an update; the current run
artifacts retain the selected budget but not the other two values.

For *w/o DAS*, each source video contributes a fixed-size suffix of its
decision points. This set is chosen before training and is unchanged by
the current policy. Earlier dialogue turns and media remain in the
context of every retained point. The available run artifacts identify
the 128-point selected batch but not the per-video suffix length. All
retained eligible points contribute without decision-dependent
prioritization or decision-dependent teacher admission. Validation and
test streams retain their full decision schedules. Matching the update
budget does not imply equal total computation: the full method also
generates candidates that may not contribute to an update.

## Appendix D Training procedure

Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Appendix D Training procedure ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
gives one Live-OPSD update; rollout, teacher scoring, and selection are
refreshed at each iteration.

1: Current parameters $`\theta_{0}`$; eligible points
$`\{(\bar{s}_{t},h_{t},b_{t})\}_{t\in\mathcal{B}}`$; budget $`K`$, quota
fraction $`\alpha`$, step size $`\eta`$

2: Updated student parameters $`\theta`$

3: for $`t\in\mathcal{B}`$ do

4:
  $`a_{t}\leftarrow\operatorname{Generate}(\pi_{\theta_{0}},\bar{s}_{t})`$

5:   $`\delta_{t}\leftarrow\mathbb{1}[d(a_{t})\neq b_{t}]`$

6: end for

7: $`\mathcal{I}\leftarrow\{t:\delta_{t}=1\}`$;
$`\mathcal{R}\leftarrow\{t:\delta_{t}=0,\ b_{t}=1\}`$

8: $`\mathcal{S}\leftarrow`$ a policy-independent subsample of
$`\{t:\delta_{t}=0,\ b_{t}=0\}`$

9:
$`\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{score}}\leftarrow\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{R}\cup\mathcal{S}`$

10: for $`t\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{score}}`$ do

11:   for $`\ell=1,\ldots,L_{t}`$ do

12:
   $`P_{t}^{(\ell)}\leftarrow\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid\bar{s}_{t},h_{t},y_{t,<\ell})`$

13:   end for

14: end for

15: $`q\leftarrow\max\{1,\lfloor\alpha K\rfloor\}`$

16: if $`|\mathcal{S}|<q`$ then

17:   $`q\leftarrow K`$

18: end if

19: Order $`\mathcal{I}`$ by alternating its two inconsistency
directions; randomize within each direction

20: Retain at most $`q`$ points from $`\mathcal{I}`$; randomize
$`\mathcal{R}`$; order $`\mathcal{S}`$ across videos

21: $`\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{sel}}\leftarrow`$ up to $`K`$ points from the
ordered groups $`\mathcal{I}`$, $`\mathcal{R}`$, $`\mathcal{S}`$ in
succession

22: for $`t\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{sel}}`$ do

23:   for $`\ell=1,\ldots,L_{t}`$ do

24:
   $`Q_{\theta,t}^{(\ell)}\leftarrow\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\bar{s}_{t},y_{t,<\ell})`$

25:   end for

26: end for

27:
$`\theta\leftarrow\theta_{0}-\eta\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta;\theta_{0})\big|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}`$

## Appendix E Supervised baseline objectives

The supervised baselines share the streaming representation, reference
assistant continuations, initialization checkpoint, and training
schedule. They differ only in which assistant tokens are supervised and
how those tokens are weighted. For a target token $`\bar{y}_{t,\ell}`$,
write

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\ell_{t,\ell}(\phi)=-\log\pi_{\phi}\!\left(\bar{y}_{t,\ell}\mid\bar{s}_{t},\bar{y}_{t,<\ell}\right).
``` |  | (7) |

##### Vanilla-SFT.

Let $`\mathcal{A}`$ be the set of reference assistant messages retained
in the training instances and let
$`N_{\mathrm{all}}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{A}}|\bar{a}_{t}|`$. Vanilla-SFT
supervises all of their target tokens with equal weight:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vanilla}}(\phi)=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{all}}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|\bar{a}_{t}|}\ell_{t,\ell}(\phi).
``` |  | (8) |

##### Silent-Speech Balanced SFT.

SSB-SFT uses the supervision mask and message-level weighting in
Equation [2](#S3.E2 "In Training instantiation. ‣ 3 Streaming Interaction ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models"),
following the silent-speech balanced objective of AURA ([Lu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11)). Within each training instance, all silent assistant
messages and the final non-silent assistant message are retained.
Earlier non-silent messages are masked out because a truncated window
need not retain the evidence aligned with those responses. If the
instance contains $`N_{\mathrm{sil}}`$ silent messages, every token in
each silent message receives weight $`1/N_{\mathrm{sil}}`$, while every
token in the retained non-silent message receives unit weight. With
$`N_{\mathrm{sup}}`$ denoting the unweighted number of retained target
tokens, the resulting loss is exactly
Equation [3](#S3.E3 "In Training instantiation. ‣ 3 Streaming Interaction ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
Here $`N_{\mathrm{sil}}`$ counts messages rather than tokens and is
computed per training instance. Live-SFT-Zero is trained with this
objective; the SSB-SFT baseline continues the same supervised objective
from that checkpoint for the post-training comparison.

##### Class-Balanced SFT.

CB-SFT transfers the supervised CB-Stream objective ([Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)) to our two omni-modal backbones. Let $`\mathcal{D}`$
denote the target positions at which the model chooses between the
silence and response decision tokens, and let $`\mathcal{T}`$ contain
the subsequent response-text positions. For decision class
$`c\in\{0,1\}`$, let $`n_{c}`$ be its count in the current batch. The
effective sample count and normalized class weight are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
E_{c}=\frac{1-\beta^{n_{c}}}{1-\beta},\qquad\alpha_{c}=\frac{E_{c}^{-1}}{\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}}E_{j}^{-1}}\,,
``` |  | (9) |

where $`\beta\in[0,1)`$ controls the strength of reweighting. For each
target position $`i`$, define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
w_{i}^{\mathrm{CB}}=\begin{cases}\alpha_{c_{i}},&i\in\mathcal{D},\\
\lambda_{\mathrm{text}},&i\in\mathcal{T},\end{cases}\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CB}}(\phi)=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{CB}}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{T}}w_{i}^{\mathrm{CB}}\ell_{i}(\phi),
``` |  | (10) |

where $`c_{i}`$ is the silence or response class at decision position
$`i`$, $`\lambda_{\mathrm{text}}`$ controls the contribution of
linguistic tokens, and $`N_{\mathrm{CB}}=|\mathcal{D}|+|\mathcal{T}|`$.
This baseline contains only the supervised objective, not StreamPro’s
subsequent policy-optimization stage. The available run artifacts do not
preserve the baseline-specific values of $`\beta`$ and
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{text}}`$, so we do not infer them from another
implementation.

The CB-SFT and SSB-SFT names therefore identify controlled objective
variants. The AURA entry in
Tables [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Open-source proactive models. ‣ 5.1 Experimental setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
and
[2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.3 Main comparisons ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
instead refers to the released complete system.

## Appendix F Experimental configuration

##### Data format and preprocessing.

Each stream is segmented into one-second chunks. A chunk is represented
by two frames, i.e. a 2 fps visual rate, together with a 16 kHz mono
audio segment; every frame is rescaled to at most $`448\times 448`$
pixels ($`200{,}704`$). Chunks are encoded as separate image and audio
inputs rather than as audio-in-video, so a multi-chunk stream maps to an
unambiguous token sequence. Standing instructions, user turns, and
assistant decisions are interleaved with these chunk tokens in streaming
order, and the same representation is used for teacher scoring and
student rollout. The context window is $`65{,}536`$ tokens:
Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B uses its native 64k context, and the smaller
Qwen2.5-Omni backbone (native 32k) is extended to the same window with
YaRN ([Peng et al., 2024](#bib.bib26)).

| Setting                                  | Value                     |
|------------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Learning rate                            | $`5\!\times\!10^{-6}`$    |
| Selected-point budget $`K`$              | 128                       |
| Teacher support size $`k`$               | 8                         |
| Log-probability floor / loss clipping    | $`-10`$ / $`+10`$         |
| Context length                           | 65,536                    |
| Chunk duration / visual sampling rate    | 1 s / 2 fps               |
| Spatial resolution / audio preprocessing | $`448^{2}`$ / 16 kHz mono |
| Decoding parameters                      | greedy                    |

Table 5: Verified configuration of the Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B reverse-KL run
used in the main result and divergence ablation.

The current artifacts do not provide a complete run manifest for the 7B
model or supervised baselines. We therefore omit unverified checkpoint
identifiers, seeds, hardware time, and baseline-specific coefficients
rather than infer them from script defaults.

##### Compact teacher targets.

Teacher scoring evaluates response probabilities under the hint-bearing
context. Let $`\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(\ell)}`$ contain the $`k`$ tokens with
greatest probability under $`P_{t}^{(\ell)}`$. The teacher and student
distributions are restricted to these same tokens and renormalized:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widetilde{P}_{t}^{(\ell)}(v)=\frac{P_{t}^{(\ell)}(v)}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(\ell)}}P_{t}^{(\ell)}(u)},\qquad\widetilde{Q}_{\theta,t}^{(\ell)}(v)=\frac{Q_{\theta,t}^{(\ell)}(v)}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(\ell)}}Q_{\theta,t}^{(\ell)}(u)},
``` |  | (11) |

for $`v\in\mathcal{K}_{t}^{(\ell)}`$. In the implementation, each
divergence in
Equation [6](#S4.E6 "In 4.2 A distributional objective ‣ 4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
is evaluated on these normalized distributions. For a response of length
$`L_{t}`$, the retained targets require $`O(kL_{t})`$ probability values
and token identities rather than $`O(|\mathcal{V}|L_{t})`$ values. This
reduces target storage and transfer; it does not eliminate the model
computation needed to obtain those probabilities. Teacher targets and
support identities are fixed during the student update. The model used
for subsequent generation and scoring is synchronized with the updated
student before the next iteration.

Loss aggregation includes only valid response positions with scored
targets. Padding, prompt positions, and absent teacher targets do not
contribute to the numerator or denominator. All divergence variants use
the same support, normalization, and token mask. Any numerical floor or
clipping applied during training is reported in
Table [5](#A6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Data format and preprocessing. ‣ Appendix F Experimental configuration ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models"),
since these operations can alter the objective at extreme probabilities.

## Appendix G Evaluation details

##### Task coverage and benchmark aggregates.

OmniMMI includes state grounding (SG), action prediction (AP),
multi-turn dependency reasoning (MD), speaker identification (SI),
proactive alerting (PA), and proactive turn-taking ([Wang et al.,
2025c](#bib.bib19)).
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.3 Main comparisons ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
reports only PA, which measures whether a model alerts the user when an
unprompted response is warranted. Systems without a published numeric PA
result are omitted; no aggregate is reconstructed from the remaining
OmniMMI tasks.

StreamingBench reports real-time visual understanding, omni-source
understanding, and contextual understanding ([Lin et al.,
2024](#bib.bib17)). Its overall score follows the benchmark’s task
aggregation, rather than an unweighted average of category means when
the categories contain different numbers of tasks.

OVO-Bench reports backward tracing, real-time visual perception, and
forward active responding ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)). Category
scores and the aggregate use the same benchmark release, with the online
observation boundary fixed.

##### Model response decisions.

For a model using the explicit silence protocol, an empty output or the
standalone silence marker denotes silence. Text accompanying that marker
is treated as a response. Models with a native response gate are scored
according to their released gate output. Native protocol adapters
preserve the streaming observation boundary and map the model’s decision
into the benchmark’s response record.

##### LiveProBench aggregation.

LiveProBench presents each video incrementally under a standing
instruction and queries the model at annotated times.
Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Open-source proactive models. ‣ 5.1 Experimental setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
reports all six tasks; their semantics and representative cases are
presented in
Section [H](#A8 "Appendix H Case Study ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
For ES, let $`u`$ be the fraction of annotated alerts covered by a
response and $`v`$ the fraction of annotated silent segments with no
response. Its score is $`\sqrt{uv}`$. A silent segment is a contiguous
annotated interval, so one interruption invalidates that segment. OR,
WR, AA, and SS use the fraction of episodes whose first response falls
inside the permitted interval; early and missing responses are reported
separately. DC uses final-total exact-count accuracy and does not score
whether intermediate replies align with individual completion times. The
headline average is the arithmetic mean of all six task scores,
including DC, rather than a pooled decision accuracy.

## Appendix H Case Study

We use LiveProBench as a concrete case study of streaming response
decisions. The benchmark covers six complementary behaviors:

- •
  Event Sensitivity (ES) requires a response when the first relevant
  visual evidence appears and silence throughout annotated negative
  segments.
- •
  Onset Response (OR) anchors the target to the annotated event onset
  and allows a 3-second grace period after that onset.
- •
  Window Response (WR) accepts the first response anywhere in a broader
  annotated interval, allowing temporal ambiguity around the event.
- •
  Anomaly Alert (AA) tests semantically ambiguous events whose alert
  condition is less crisply specified than a single explicit action
  boundary.
- •
  Sustained Silence (SS) places the target after an especially long
  annotated silence window and requires no response throughout that
  prefix.
- •
  Duplicate Counting (DC) presents repeated occurrences of one action
  and scores only the final total count; exact response timing at
  individual completions is not evaluated.

## Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD

Live-OPSD uses privileged context to construct a teaching signal,
on-policy distillation to transfer that signal, and DAS to choose where
to apply it. The analysis asks when these operations can support better
response decisions. Let $`(S,H,B)\sim\rho`$ denote a recorded streaming
context, its training-time hint, and its supervisory decision, with
$`B\in\{0,1\}`$. The student observes $`S`$, whereas the teacher also
observes $`H`$. All decision risks below measure disagreement with
$`B`$. We first characterize the information supplied by a hint, then
relate the actual teacher–student discrepancy to decision risk, and
finally identify when disagreement-based selection enriches contexts
with a teaching advantage.

### I.1 Privileged context and decision ambiguity

A shared parameter set need not imply identical decision information.
The first result characterizes the ambiguity that can be resolved by the
teacher’s additional context. Write $`u(s)=\Pr(B=1\mid S=s)`$ and
$`v(s,h)=\Pr(B=1\mid S=s,H=h)`$ for the two conditional probabilities of
a supervised response.

###### Proposition I.1 (Information advantage of privileged context).

Let

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{vis}}^{*}=\inf_{g}\Pr(g(S)\neq B),\qquad R_{\mathrm{priv}}^{*}=\inf_{f}\Pr(f(S,H)\neq B),
``` |  |

where the infima range over all measurable binary decision rules. Then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{vis}}^{*}-R_{\mathrm{priv}}^{*}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left|v(S,H)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|-\left|u(S)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|\right]\geq 0.
``` |  |

The inequality is strict if, for a set of contexts $`s`$ of positive
probability, $`v(s,H)`$ lies on both sides of $`1/2`$ with positive
conditional probability.

###### Proof.

For a Bernoulli supervisory decision with response probability $`u`$,
choosing silence incurs disagreement probability $`u`$, and choosing a
response incurs $`1-u`$. The minimum is $`\min\{u,1-u\}=1/2-|u-1/2|`$.
Applying this calculation under the two information sets gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{vis}}^{*}=\tfrac{1}{2}-\mathbb{E}|u(S)-\tfrac{1}{2}|,\qquad R_{\mathrm{priv}}^{*}=\tfrac{1}{2}-\mathbb{E}|v(S,H)-\tfrac{1}{2}|.
``` |  |

The tower property gives $`u(S)=\mathbb{E}[v(S,H)\mid S]`$. Conditional
Jensen’s inequality therefore implies

|     |                                                                 |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       |u(S)-\tfrac{1}{2}|\leq\mathbb{E}[|v(S,H)-\tfrac{1}{2}|\mid S].
       ```                                                              |     |

Subtracting the two minimum risks proves the identity and its
nonnegativity. The absolute-value inequality is strict when its argument
has both positive and negative values with positive conditional
probability, yielding the stated strictness condition. ∎

The strictness condition identifies a source of teaching information:
the hint distinguishes supervisory decisions that the visible context
alone leaves ambiguous. When $`H`$ is a deterministic function of $`S`$,
the two optimal risks are equal. Such a hint can still make an
evidence–decision relationship explicit for a finite model, but that
benefit concerns how the model uses information rather than an increase
in the information available. Thus, the hint’s information advantage and
the current teacher’s decision advantage are different quantities. The
transfer analysis uses the latter.

### I.2 From on-policy distillation to response decisions

Fix a teacher and student at a training iterate. Let
$`P(\cdot\mid s,h)`$ and $`Q(\cdot\mid s)`$ be their autoregressive
distributions over continuations of at most $`L`$ tokens. Ended
sequences are padded with an absorbing end symbol to give a common
horizon; both conditionals put all mass on that symbol once a turn has
ended, so padded positions contribute no discrepancy. The binary
response decision $`d(Y)`$ is determined by the complete continuation,
as in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Streaming Interaction ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
Define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R(Q)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\Pr_{Y\sim Q(\cdot\mid S)}[d(Y)\neq B],\qquad R(P)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\Pr_{Y\sim P(\cdot\mid S,H)}[d(Y)\neq B].
``` |  |

Although training only scores the teacher, its conditional probabilities
define such a continuation distribution. This lets us ask whether
matching those probabilities along student-generated prefixes also
controls response decisions.

At a fixed context, let $`p_{\ell}(\cdot\mid y_{<\ell})`$ and
$`q_{\ell}(\cdot\mid y_{<\ell})`$ denote the teacher and student
next-token distributions. Define the cumulative on-policy reverse KL

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r}}(P,Q)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_{\ell}(\cdot\mid Y_{<\ell})\|p_{\ell}(\cdot\mid Y_{<\ell})\right).
``` |  |

The context dependence is implicit in $`p_{\ell}`$ and $`q_{\ell}`$.
Logarithms are natural, and the continuation expectation follows the
student, as in the on-policy objective. Write
$`\operatorname{TV}(U,V)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|U-V\|_{1}`$ for total variation.

###### Proposition I.2 (Decision transfer along student-generated prefixes).

For the finite-horizon continuation distributions above,

|     |                                                                  |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R(Q)\leq R(P)+\sqrt{\frac{L\,\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r}}(P,Q)}{2}}.
       ```                                                               |     |

###### Proof.

Fix $`(s,h,b)`$ and introduce hybrid continuation distributions

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{j}(y_{1:L})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}q_{\ell}(y_{\ell}\mid y_{<\ell})\prod_{\ell=j+1}^{L}p_{\ell}(y_{\ell}\mid y_{<\ell}),\qquad j=0,\ldots,L.
``` |  |

Thus $`M_{0}=P`$ and $`M_{L}=Q`$. The distributions $`M_{j-1}`$ and
$`M_{j}`$ share a student-generated prefix of length $`j-1`$ and the
same teacher continuation after token $`j`$. Summing over this common
suffix gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{TV}(M_{j-1},M_{j})=\mathbb{E}_{Y_{<j}\sim Q}\operatorname{TV}\!\left(p_{j}(\cdot\mid Y_{<j}),q_{j}(\cdot\mid Y_{<j})\right).
``` |  |

The triangle inequality and Pinsker’s inequality yield

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_{j}(\cdot\mid Y_{<j})\|p_{j}(\cdot\mid Y_{<j})\right)}.
``` |  |

For the event $`\{Y:d(Y)\neq b\}`$, the difference between its student
and teacher probabilities is at most $`\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)`$.
Averaging over contexts and applying Cauchy–Schwarz to the $`L`$ prefix
contributions proves the result. ∎

The prefix distribution in this result is the student’s own. The
discrepancy is therefore evaluated where the student makes the token
choices that determine its action. Matching only reference continuations
controls a different expectation and requires an additional coverage
condition. The result concerns continuations at recorded contexts under
$`\rho`$, not full model-generated interaction histories.

The bound charges one discrepancy term per response position. Response
decisions are usually settled before a turn ends. Let

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nu(Y)=\min\bigl\{j\leq L:\ d\text{ is constant on all continuations agreeing}\text{ with }Y\text{ in the first }j\text{ tokens}\bigr\}
``` |  |

be the position at which the decision is resolved, a stopping time for
the token filtration, and write

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{\nu}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}\,\nu(Y),\qquad\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r},\nu}(P,Q)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu(Y)}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_{j}(\cdot\mid Y_{<j})\|p_{j}(\cdot\mid Y_{<j})\right).
``` |  |

###### Proposition I.3 (Decision transfer over the resolving prefix).

Under the assumptions of
Proposition [I.2](#A9.Thmproposition2 "Proposition I.2 (Decision transfer along student-generated prefixes). ‣ I.2 From on-policy distillation to response decisions ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models"),

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R(Q)\leq R(P)+\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\nu}\,\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r},\nu}(P,Q)}{2}}.
``` |  |

Since $`\nu\leq L`$, this implies
Proposition [I.2](#A9.Thmproposition2 "Proposition I.2 (Decision transfer along student-generated prefixes). ‣ I.2 From on-policy distillation to response decisions ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").

###### Proof.

Fix $`(s,h,b)`$ and let $`A=\{y:d(y)\neq b\}`$. By construction $`d`$ is
a function of $`y_{1:\nu(y)}`$, and the event $`\{\nu\geq j\}`$ is
determined by $`y_{<j}`$. With the hybrid distributions of the previous
proof,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{j-1}(A)-M_{j}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{Y_{<j}\sim Q}\sum_{y_{j}}\bigl[p_{j}(y_{j}\mid Y_{<j})-q_{j}(y_{j}\mid Y_{<j})\bigr]\Phi(Y_{<j},y_{j}),
``` |  |

where $`\Phi(y_{\leq j})`$ is the probability of $`A`$ under the
teacher’s continuation from $`y_{\leq j}`$. If $`\nu\leq j-1`$, the
prefix already determines $`d`$, so the inner sum vanishes. If
$`\nu\geq j`$, its magnitude is bounded by
$`\operatorname{TV}(p_{j},q_{j})`$. Telescoping, applying Pinsker’s
inequality, and using Cauchy–Schwarz with the weights
$`\mathbb{1}[\nu\geq j]`$ proves the result. ∎

Under the explicit silence protocol, a leading token other than the
silence marker resolves the decision at the first position. The silent
branch is resolved once the turn ends without accompanying text,
following the scoring rule of
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Evaluation details ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
The resolving prefix is therefore short compared with a full response,
and $`\bar{\nu}`$ is measurable from the current policy. Response tokens
beyond $`\nu`$ still receive supervision in
Equation [6](#S4.E6 "In 4.2 A distributional objective ‣ 4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models");
they affect response content rather than the binary response decision.
Both corollaries below hold with $`(L,\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r}})`$
replaced by $`(\bar{\nu},\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r},\nu})`$.

###### Corollary I.4 (Transfer of a teacher’s decision advantage).

Let $`Q_{0}`$ be a reference student and suppose the fixed teacher has
decision advantage $`\Delta=R(Q_{0})-R(P)>0`$ under the same context
distribution. For a candidate student $`Q`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R(Q)-R(Q_{0})\leq-\Delta+\sqrt{\frac{L\,\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r}}(P,Q)}{2}}.
``` |  |

In particular, $`\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r}}(P,Q)<2\Delta^{2}/L`$ is
sufficient for $`R(Q)<R(Q_{0})`$.

###### Proof.

Subtract $`R(Q_{0})=R(P)+\Delta`$ from
Proposition [I.2](#A9.Thmproposition2 "Proposition I.2 (Decision transfer along student-generated prefixes). ‣ I.2 From on-policy distillation to response decisions ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
The right-hand side is negative under the stated condition. ∎

The sufficient condition compares the teacher’s decision advantage with
the residual transfer discrepancy. In Live-OPSD, $`Q_{0}`$ generates the
current batch and $`P`$ is its hinted counterpart.
Equation [6](#S4.E6 "In 4.2 A distributional objective ‣ 4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
evaluates candidate updates on fixed selected samples, whereas
$`\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r}}(P,Q)`$ averages full-vocabulary discrepancies
over fresh continuations from $`Q`$. Connecting the two requires the
selected samples and retained support to cover the relevant contexts,
prefixes, and vocabulary. The corollary is a distribution-level
sufficient condition, not a guarantee for every optimization step.

###### Corollary I.5 (Residual discrepancy from privileged information).

For any student whose continuation depends only on $`S`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{r}}(P,Q)\geq\frac{2\bigl[R_{\mathrm{vis}}^{*}-R(P)\bigr]_{+}^{2}}{L},\qquad[x]_{+}=\max\{x,0\}.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

Randomizing the response decision cannot reduce conditional disagreement
below the best deterministic decision, so
$`R(Q)\geq R_{\mathrm{vis}}^{*}`$. Combine this with
Proposition [I.2](#A9.Thmproposition2 "Proposition I.2 (Decision transfer along student-generated prefixes). ‣ I.2 From on-policy distillation to response decisions ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
and rearrange when $`R_{\mathrm{vis}}^{*}>R(P)`$. Otherwise the claim
follows from nonnegativity of KL. ∎

When the hint reveals decision-relevant information unavailable in the
visible context, exact teacher–student agreement can be unattainable.
The objective is to improve decisions from the student’s information
set; zero distillation loss is not required for useful transfer.

The discrepancies above use the full vocabulary, whereas the
implementation replaces each divergence in
Equation [6](#S4.E6 "In 4.2 A distributional objective ‣ 4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
with its retained-support counterpart from
Equation [11](#A6.E11 "In Compact teacher targets. ‣ Appendix F Experimental configuration ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
The remaining result bounds this difference by the discarded probability
masses.

###### Proposition I.6 (From the truncated objective to full-vocabulary transfer).

At each position let $`m_{j}^{P}`$ and $`m_{j}^{Q}`$ be the teacher and
student masses retained by the support $`\mathcal{K}_{j}`$ of
Equation [11](#A6.E11 "In Compact teacher targets. ‣ Appendix F Experimental configuration ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models"),
and let $`\widetilde{p}_{j},\widetilde{q}_{j}`$ be the renormalized
distributions. Then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{TV}(p_{j},q_{j})\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde{q}_{j}\|\widetilde{p}_{j})}+(1-m_{j}^{P})+(1-m_{j}^{Q}),
``` |  |

and consequently

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R(Q)\leq R(P)+\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\nu}\,\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{r},\nu}}{2}}+\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu(Y)}\bigl[(1-m_{j}^{P})+(1-m_{j}^{Q})\bigr],
``` |  |

where $`\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathrm{r},\nu}`$ uses reverse KL
between the renormalized distributions on the retained support.

###### Proof.

Split $`\|p_{j}-q_{j}\|_{1}`$ at $`\mathcal{K}_{j}`$. The two tails
outside the support contribute at most $`(1-m_{j}^{P})+(1-m_{j}^{Q})`$.
Inside it, $`p_{j}=m_{j}^{P}\widetilde{p}_{j}`$ and
$`q_{j}=m_{j}^{Q}\widetilde{q}_{j}`$. Summing the pointwise triangle
inequality and using
$`|m_{j}^{P}-m_{j}^{Q}|\leq(1-m_{j}^{P})+(1-m_{j}^{Q})`$ gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{TV}(p_{j},q_{j})\leq\operatorname{TV}(\widetilde{p}_{j},\widetilde{q}_{j})+(1-m_{j}^{P})+(1-m_{j}^{Q}).
``` |  |

Pinsker’s inequality proves the first claim. Substituting it into the
telescoped bound for
Proposition [I.3](#A9.Thmproposition3 "Proposition I.3 (Decision transfer over the resolving prefix). ‣ I.2 From on-policy distillation to response decisions ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
and applying Cauchy–Schwarz to the reverse-KL terms proves the second. ∎

With $`\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{rKL}}`$, the implemented form of
Equation [6](#S4.E6 "In 4.2 A distributional objective ‣ 4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
minimizes the retained-support reverse-KL terms in this bound. The
discarded masses isolate the effect of truncation and are computable
from the teacher and student forward passes. The remaining difference
between the bound and the implemented update is that training uses fixed
samples at selected contexts with global token-mean normalization rather
than an idealized expectation over all contexts and continuations.

### I.3 Which contexts decision-aware selection emphasizes

A sampled disagreement contains information about the context that
produced it. Whether that context is useful for distillation also
depends on the teacher. To separate these questions, fix the current
policy $`Q_{\theta}`$ and teacher $`P`$, and let $`Z=(S,H,B)\sim\rho`$.
Define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
e_{\theta}(z)=\Pr_{Y\sim Q_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)}[d(Y)\neq b],\qquad\bar{e}_{\theta}=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\rho}e_{\theta}(Z).
``` |  |

Here $`e_{\theta}(z)`$ is the probability of disagreement on a fresh
continuation at that context, while the indicator $`\delta_{t}`$ used by
DAS is one sampled outcome. Write
$`e_{P}(z)=\Pr_{Y\sim P(\cdot\mid s,h)}[d(Y)\neq b]`$ for the teacher’s
conditional disagreement and $`g_{\theta}(z)=e_{\theta}(z)-e_{P}(z)`$
for its context-specific decision advantage. A positive
$`g_{\theta}(z)`$ means that the teacher agrees with supervision more
often than the current student there. Its mean
$`\mathbb{E}_{\rho}g_{\theta}(Z)`$ is the decision advantage $`\Delta`$
of
Corollary [I.4](#A9.Thmproposition4 "Corollary I.4 (Transfer of a teacher’s decision advantage). ‣ I.2 From on-policy distillation to response decisions ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
at the current policy.

###### Proposition I.7 (Context enrichment and teaching advantage).

Suppose $`\bar{e}_{\theta}>0`$. Draw a context from $`\rho`$, generate a
continuation from the current policy, and retain the context when its
decision disagrees with supervision. The conditional context
distribution is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{\mathrm{inc}}(dz)=\frac{e_{\theta}(z)}{\bar{e}_{\theta}}\,\rho(dz).
``` |  |

Its expected disagreement probability on a fresh independent
continuation is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{inc}}}e_{\theta}(Z)=\bar{e}_{\theta}+\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{\rho}(e_{\theta}(Z))}{\bar{e}_{\theta}}\geq\bar{e}_{\theta},
``` |  |

with strict inequality whenever $`e_{\theta}(Z)`$ is nonconstant under
$`\rho`$. The expected teacher advantage satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{inc}}}g_{\theta}(Z)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}g_{\theta}(Z)+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{\rho}(e_{\theta}(Z),g_{\theta}(Z))}{\bar{e}_{\theta}}.
``` |  |

Thus, the inconsistent pool has a greater mean teacher advantage
precisely when the covariance is positive.

###### Proof.

Let $`D`$ denote disagreement on the continuation used for selection.
For any measurable set of contexts $`A`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(Z\in A,D)=\int_{A}e_{\theta}(z)\,\rho(dz),\qquad\Pr(D)=\bar{e}_{\theta}.
``` |  |

Bayes’ rule gives the stated conditional distribution. For any bounded
context-level function $`f`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{inc}}}f(Z)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[e_{\theta}(Z)f(Z)]}{\bar{e}_{\theta}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}f(Z)+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{\rho}(e_{\theta}(Z),f(Z))}{\bar{e}_{\theta}}.`$ |  |

An independent continuation at a retained context still has disagreement
probability $`e_{\theta}(z)`$. Taking $`f=e_{\theta}`$ gives the
variance identity, and the variance is positive unless $`e_{\theta}(Z)`$
is almost surely constant. Taking $`f=g_{\theta}`$ proves the identity
for teacher advantage. Since $`\bar{e}_{\theta}>0`$, the change in that
advantage has the sign of the covariance. ∎

###### Corollary I.8 (Quota relative to the current disagreement rate).

Suppose $`0<\bar{e}_{\theta}<1`$ and let
$`\rho_{\mathrm{con}}(dz)=\frac{1-e_{\theta}(z)}{1-\bar{e}_{\theta}}\,\rho(dz)`$
denote the complementary consistent pool. For
$`\rho_{\lambda}=\lambda\rho_{\mathrm{inc}}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{\mathrm{con}}`$
with $`\lambda\in[0,1]`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\lambda}}g_{\theta}(Z)-\mathbb{E}_{\rho}g_{\theta}(Z)=\frac{(\lambda-\bar{e}_{\theta})\,\operatorname{Cov}_{\rho}(e_{\theta}(Z),g_{\theta}(Z))}{\bar{e}_{\theta}(1-\bar{e}_{\theta})}.
``` |  |

When the covariance is positive, the mixture has a greater mean teacher
advantage than $`\rho`$ exactly when $`\lambda>\bar{e}_{\theta}`$, and
$`\lambda=\bar{e}_{\theta}`$ reproduces $`\rho`$.

###### Proof.

Write $`C=\operatorname{Cov}_{\rho}(e_{\theta}(Z),g_{\theta}(Z))`$.
Since
$`\mathbb{E}_{\rho}[(1-e_{\theta})g_{\theta}]=(1-\bar{e}_{\theta})\mathbb{E}_{\rho}g_{\theta}-C`$,
dividing by $`1-\bar{e}_{\theta}`$ gives
$`\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{con}}}g_{\theta}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}g_{\theta}-C/(1-\bar{e}_{\theta})`$,
while
Proposition [I.7](#A9.Thmproposition7 "Proposition I.7 (Context enrichment and teaching advantage). ‣ I.3 Which contexts decision-aware selection emphasizes ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
gives
$`\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathrm{inc}}}g_{\theta}=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}g_{\theta}+C/\bar{e}_{\theta}`$.
Taking the $`\lambda`$-weighted combination and collecting terms yields
the identity. At $`\lambda=\bar{e}_{\theta}`$ it vanishes, consistent
with
$`\rho=\bar{e}_{\theta}\rho_{\mathrm{inc}}+(1-\bar{e}_{\theta})\rho_{\mathrm{con}}`$.
∎

The covariance condition connects the selection signal to the
distillation signal. DAS favors useful teaching contexts when the hinted
model’s advantage is greater where the current student is more likely to
disagree with supervision. If both models struggle on the same contexts,
disagreement alone need not indicate a stronger teaching signal. The
covariance describes the quality of the selected targets. Translating
that advantage into a student improvement also depends on transfer
fidelity, as expressed in
Corollary [I.4](#A9.Thmproposition4 "Corollary I.4 (Transfer of a teacher’s decision advantage). ‣ I.2 From on-policy distillation to response decisions ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").

The proposition characterizes the inconsistent candidate pool before DAS
balances the two disagreement directions and adds consistent points. The
same identities hold within each supervisory class by conditioning
$`\rho`$ on $`B=b`$, provided that class has positive disagreement
probability. Direction balancing and the quota then determine how these
pools enter the selected set, and response lengths determine their
weights in
Equation [6](#S4.E6 "In 4.2 A distributional objective ‣ 4 Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models").
Corollary [I.8](#A9.Thmproposition8 "Corollary I.8 (Quota relative to the current disagreement rate). ‣ I.3 Which contexts decision-aware selection emphasizes ‣ Appendix I Decision-level analysis of Live-OPSD ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
characterizes the inconsistent share of an idealized mixture relative to
the current disagreement rate $`\bar{e}_{\theta}`$. In the implemented
procedure, the quota $`q=\max\{1,\lfloor\alpha K\rfloor\}`$ caps the
number of inconsistent points before the remaining groups are filled by
priority. The current run artifacts do not retain the pool counts needed
to show that the realized inconsistent share exceeds
$`\bar{e}_{\theta}`$ at every update. Moreover, the corollary samples
the remaining points in proportion to $`1-e_{\theta}`$, whereas DAS uses
a deterministic group priority, so $`\rho_{\lambda}`$ is an analytical
approximation rather than the exact selected-set distribution. As the
policy changes, $`e_{\theta}`$ and its induced context weighting change
with it. The fixed-selection control in
Section [5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 Ablation studies ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Live-OPSD: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Proactive Streaming Large Video Models")
removes this policy-dependent allocation while preserving each point’s
visible history.
````

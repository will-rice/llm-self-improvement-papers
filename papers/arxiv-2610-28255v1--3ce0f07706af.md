###### Abstract

Adapting Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) to medical imaging via LoRA has
become standard practice, yet model selection is guided almost entirely
by downstream task metrics, treating backbones with similar performance
as interchangeable. We investigate whether equal performance implies
equal internal representational shift. We analyse four ViT-B/16
backbones, DINOv3, SigLIP2, MerMED-FM, and BiomedCLIP, pre-trained with
different objectives (self-distillation vs. contrastive vision-language)
and on different data domains (natural vs. medical images). We adapt
each to brain tumour segmentation and evaluate on six datasets covering
both segmentation and Alzheimer’s disease classification. Using
attention divergence, weight-change analysis, and activation patching,
we find that the four models converge to similar segmentation
performance (mean Dice 78–81%) through markedly different internal
dynamics, yet diverge in cross-task transfer: segmentation adaptation
improves Alzheimer’s disease classification for DINOv3 but degrades it
for the other three backbones. We reveal a dissociation between
modification magnitude and causal importance: the most heavily modified
layers are not the causally relevant ones, with early-to-mid layers
identified as sufficient for segmentation recovery. Restricting LoRA to
these layers matches or approaches full-layer segmentation while
reducing classification degradation in the three affected backbones,
providing a mechanistically grounded strategy that is more
parameter-efficient and more robust than standard full-layer adaptation.
Our code is publicly available at
[https://github.com/fkekwick/Brain_Lora](https://github.com/fkekwick/Brain_Lora).

## 1 Introduction

Vision Foundation Models (VFMs), which are pretrained on large-scale
image datasets, provide powerful visual feature encoders applicable to
diverse downstream computer vision tasks \[[1](#bib.bib20)\]. Adapting
them via Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) has become standard
practice, with methods like LoRA \[[2](#bib.bib14)\] achieving
competitive results across diverse data domains \[[3](#bib.bib35),
[4](#bib.bib37), [5](#bib.bib36), [6](#bib.bib15), [7](#bib.bib38),
[8](#bib.bib47), [9](#bib.bib29)\] at a fraction of full fine-tuning
costs. In medical imaging tasks, such as brain tumour segmentation,
LoRA-adapted foundation models now rival fully supervised
baselines \[[10](#bib.bib30), [11](#bib.bib5)\]. Given the numerous
publicly available VFMs backbones \[[12](#bib.bib1), [13](#bib.bib6),
[14](#bib.bib41), [15](#bib.bib46)\], choosing which backbone best
serves the downstream task becomes a key design decision. Currently,
model selection is guided almost entirely by downstream task
metrics \[[16](#bib.bib28)\]; i.e., two backbones that reach the same
segmentation Dice score are treated as interchangeable. In this paper,
we investigate an intriguing mechanistic question: whether equal
performance implies equal internal representation shift, as differences
in how models reshape their representations may expose hidden
generalisability risks and point to different backbone-selection or
fine-tuning strategies.

In model deployment, a single backbone often serves multiple downstream
tasks. A model whose representations degrade on one task while improving
on another poses a deployment risk that task-specific evaluation cannot
detect. Understanding how models adapt, not only how well they perform,
is therefore necessary for informed backbone selection. Mechanistic
interpretability tools developed for natural language and vision
models \[[17](#bib.bib8)\] offer a principled way to characterise this
representational shift and to identify which layers drive task
performance.

We study four ViT-B/16 backbones, comparing both pre-training objective
(self-distillation vs. contrastive vision-language) and source domain
(natural vs. medical images): DINOv3 \[[12](#bib.bib1)\],
SigLIP2 \[[13](#bib.bib6)\], MerMED-FM \[[14](#bib.bib41)\], and
BiomedCLIP \[[15](#bib.bib46)\]. All four models are adapted via LoRA to
brain tumour segmentation on multi-parametric MRI and evaluated across
six datasets spanning three segmentation tasks
(BraTS-GLI \[[18](#bib.bib7), [19](#bib.bib11), [20](#bib.bib51)\],
BraTS-Africa \[[21](#bib.bib9)\], BraTS-PED \[[22](#bib.bib10)\]) and
three classification tasks (OASIS-1 \[[23](#bib.bib12)\], ADNI binary,
ADNI multi-class \[[24](#bib.bib13)\]). We analyse the adaptation
process through the lens of four complementary mechanistic tools:
attention Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence \[[25](#bib.bib34)\], LoRA
weight-change ratios \[[2](#bib.bib14)\], off-brain attention fraction,
and layer-wise activation patching \[[26](#bib.bib33)\]. Guided by the
mechanistic diagnosis, we further propose and validate an early-layer
LoRA strategy that restricts adaptation to the layers identified as
causally important.

Our main findings are as follows. (i) Although the four backbones
converge to similar segmentation performance after LoRA adaptation, they
diverge substantially in cross-task classification transfer. Mechanistic
analysis reveals that segmentation convergence masks fundamentally
different representational shift profiles, and medical-domain
pre-training confers no consistent segmentation advantage. (ii) The most
heavily modified layers are not the causally important ones: activation
patching consistently identifies early-to-mid layers as sufficient to
recover full segmentation performance from a frozen encoder, while
late-layer adaptation can damage cross-task transfer, as shown by the
degraded linear probing classification performance. (iii) Restricting
LoRA to early layers matches or approaches full-layer segmentation while
improving classification performance relative to all-layer adaptation in
the three backbones whose cross-task transfer degrades, providing a
mechanistically grounded strategy that is both more parameter-efficient
and more robust than adapting all layers.

Together, these findings suggest that integrating layer-wise
interpretability tools with multi-task downstream assessment reveals
adaptation risks invisible to standard metrics and motivates practical
strategies that would not emerge from task-specific evaluation alone.
While our study focuses on brain MRI, the analytical framework can be
applied to any attention-based encoder and data domain.

## 2 Related Work

Adapting Vision Foundation Models to medical imaging via PEFT has grown
rapidly in recent years. LoRA \[[2](#bib.bib14)\] and its variants have
been applied to diverse segmentation tasks \[[3](#bib.bib35),
[4](#bib.bib37)\], including brain tumours \[[10](#bib.bib30),
[8](#bib.bib47)\]. A common approach in the literature is adapting a
single foundation model per study and evaluating it on diverse datasets
within one single downstream task, treating the backbone as a fixed
design choice justified by its performance metric. Comparative analyses
across backbones remain limited to performance tables without examining
how different pre-training paradigms shape the internal
adaptation \[[5](#bib.bib36), [6](#bib.bib15), [27](#bib.bib27)\].

Beyond downstream metrics, mechanistic interpretability offers tools to
examine how models internally reorganise during adaptation, including
attention analysis, probing, and causal interventions such as activation
patching \[[17](#bib.bib8), [26](#bib.bib33)\]. Recent work has begun
applying these tools to LoRA-tuned models \[[28](#bib.bib4)\], using
mechanistic analysis to guide adapter placement \[[29](#bib.bib39),
[30](#bib.bib26)\], to decode attention head behaviour under
distribution shift \[[31](#bib.bib40)\], and to disentangle clinical
from class-agnostic features during few-shot
adaptation \[[32](#bib.bib48)\]. These studies provide valuable insight
into individual models but, by analysing a single backbone in isolation,
cannot reveal whether the same adaptation procedure produces different
internal dynamics across architectures. Furthermore, the connection
between mechanistic diagnosis and a concrete change in adaptation
strategy remains largely unexplored in the vision domain: a recent
exception in NLP uses causal and geometric analysis to guide LoRA
placement across layers \[[33](#bib.bib2)\], but interpretability
findings in medical imaging are still reported descriptively rather than
validated through intervention. Our work addresses both gaps by
comparing mechanistic profiles across four backbones and by using the
diagnostic findings to motivate and test a layer-selective LoRA
strategy.

The question of which layers to adapt has also received attention. Some
studies dynamically allocate rank budgets across
layers \[[34](#bib.bib18)\] based on gradient-derived importance
scores \[[35](#bib.bib45), [36](#bib.bib43), [37](#bib.bib19)\], while
domain-specific heuristics select layers using task performance
proxies \[[5](#bib.bib36)\] or Fisher information \[[38](#bib.bib42)\].
These approaches tend to favour later layers, consistent with the
intuition that deeper representations are more
task-specific \[[39](#bib.bib44), [40](#bib.bib17)\]. Our results show
that activation patching identifies early-to-mid layers as causally
sufficient for segmentation, and that restricting adaptation to these
layers preserves cross-task transferability that full-layer adaptation
can compromise. This finding motivates layer selection based on causal
importance and multi-task evaluation rather than gradient magnitude.

### 3.1 Adaptation Pipeline

As illustrated in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Method ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI"),
we adapt four ViT-B/16 backbones \[[41](#bib.bib24)\] to brain image
segmentation task. Four brain MRI modalities (T1n, T1c, T2w, T2f) are
fused via a trainable $`1\times 1`$ convolution that maps these four
input channels to three, matching the RGB input expected by all
encoders. All four modalities carry complementary clinical
information \[[42](#bib.bib16)\], so we retain all of them and let the
learned convolution determine the optimal combination. This fusion layer
is initialised with uniform weights of $`1/4`$ with no bias term,
equivalent to average pooling, and trained end-to-end independently for
each backbone to learn modality-specific contributions.

The ViT-B/16 encoder is kept frozen; adaptation is achieved through LoRA
modules injected into each of the 12 transformer blocks. From the
adapted encoder, we extract multi-scale representations by tapping four
uniformly spaced blocks $`\{3,6,9,12\}`$ (1-indexed), each taken after
the encoder’s final layer normalisation and reshaped from the token
sequence into a $`14\times 14`$ spatial feature map with 768 channels.
The four maps are concatenated channel-wise into a $`3072`$-channel
representation, which a lightweight segmentation head (two $`1\times 1`$
convolutions separated by ReLU, $`3072\rightarrow 256\rightarrow C`$
classes) converts into a dense prediction, upsampled to the ground-truth
resolution of $`240\times 240`$ pixels. The LoRA modules and
segmentation head are jointly trained with an equal-weight combination
of Dice and cross-entropy loss.

We evaluate each backbone in two configurations: frozen, where no LoRA
modules are applied, the four MRI modalities are mapped to three
channels via average pooling, and only the segmentation head is trained;
and adapted, which includes the trainable fusion convolution and LoRA
modules described above. The trainable fusion convolution may contribute
to absolute performance differences between configurations; however, all
four backbones share the same architecture, so mechanistic comparisons
across backbones remain controlled.

We adapt the frozen ViT encoder using LoRA \[[2](#bib.bib14)\] within
each transformer self-attention block. For each transformer
block $`\ell`$, low-rank updates are applied to the query and value
projections of the fused $`QKV`$ linear layer, while the key projection
remains unchanged, following the standard LoRA configuration proposed
by \[[2](#bib.bib14)\]. For a feature map $`x`$, the adapted
query $`Q^{\prime}`$ and value projections $`V^{\prime}`$ are given by:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
Q^{\prime}=\bigl(W_{Q}^{(\ell)}+B_{Q}^{(\ell)}A_{Q}^{(\ell)}\bigr)x,\qquad V^{\prime}=\bigl(W_{V}^{(\ell)}+B_{V}^{(\ell)}A_{V}^{(\ell)}\bigr)x,
``` |  | (1) |

where $`W_{Q}^{(\ell)}`$, $`W_{V}^{(\ell)}`$ are the frozen pretrained
weights and $`A_{Q}^{(\ell)}`$, $`A_{V}^{(\ell)}`$, $`B_{Q}^{(\ell)}`$,
$`B_{V}^{(\ell)}`$ are the trainable low-rank projection matrices, with
no dropout. We omit the $`\alpha/r`$ scaling factor, so the low-rank
update $`BA`$ is applied at full magnitude; since rank sensitivity
proved negligible
(Section [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI"),
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.4 Linear Probing Classification ‣ 3 Method ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")),
this choice does not affect our conclusions.

### 3.2 Mechanistic Analysis

We analyse the adaptation process \[[43](#bib.bib25)\] through four
complementary tools: (1) attention Jensen-Shannon (JS)
divergence \[[25](#bib.bib34)\], (2) LoRA weight-change
ratio \[[2](#bib.bib14)\], (3) off-brain attention fraction, and
(4) layer-wise activation patching \[[26](#bib.bib33)\]. JS divergence
and weight-change ratio quantify the magnitude of adaptation in output
space and parameter space, respectively. As the LoRA update
$`\Delta W_{Q}=B_{Q}A_{Q}`$ modifies the query projection, it directly
alters the attention logits $`Q\cdot K^{\top}`$, changing the resulting
attention distribution. The weight-change ratio measures this
modification in parameter space, while JS divergence measures its
downstream effect on the attention pattern; together, they capture the
same adaptation from complementary viewpoints. Off-brain attention
fraction examines the adaptation cost by measuring how much frozen
attention must be redirected toward task-relevant regions. Finally,
activation patching isolates which layer adaptations causally drive task
performance.

#### 3.2.1 Attention JS Divergence

To quantify how LoRA changes each layer’s attention pattern, we measure
the JS divergence \[[25](#bib.bib34)\] in units of bits (log base 2)
between the frozen ($`P_{f}`$) and adapted ($`P_{a}`$) attention
distributions. For each transformer block $`\ell`$, we extract the
$`N\!\times\!N`$ patch-to-patch attention sub-matrix, excluding any
special tokens such as the CLS token or DINOv3’s register tokens,
renormalise each query row to form a valid distribution, and compute

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{JS}^{(\ell)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{f}\|M)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{a}\|M),\quad M=\tfrac{1}{2}(P_{f}+P_{a}),
``` |  | (2) |

with $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}`$ being Kullback-Leibler
divergence \[[44](#bib.bib32)\]. The result is averaged over query
patches and attention heads via arithmetic mean. JS divergence is
symmetric and bounded in $`[0,1]`$, enabling stable comparison across
layers and models without scale normalisation.

#### 3.2.2 LoRA Weight Change

We calculate the adaptation magnitude directly in parameter space. For
each transformer block $`\ell`$, the effective LoRA
update \[[2](#bib.bib14)\] to a projection matrix is
$`\Delta W^{(\ell)}=B^{(\ell)}A^{(\ell)}`$, where $`A`$ and $`B`$ are
the learned low-rank matrices. We materialise the full product
$`B^{(\ell)}A^{(\ell)}`$ and report the relative Frobenius norm
$`\|\Delta W^{(\ell)}\|_{F}/\|W^{(\ell)}\|_{F}`$ separately for the
query and value projections, where $`W^{(\ell)}`$ is the corresponding
pretrained weight. A high ratio indicates that LoRA substantially
changes that layer relative to its original weight parameters.

#### 3.2.3 Off-Brain Attention Fraction

To understand why the four encoders differ in adaptation cost, we
measure how much frozen attention is directed away from anatomically
relevant regions. We obtain a binary brain mask from the input slice
resized to $`224\times 224`$ and assign each ViT patch token to
$`\mathcal{B}`$ (brain) or its complement $`\bar{\mathcal{B}}`$
(off-brain) according to whether more than 5% of its $`16\times 16`$
pixels are foreground. For each block $`\ell`$ and attention head $`h`$,
let $`A^{(\ell,h)}_{q,k}`$ be the attention weight from query
patch $`q`$ to key patch $`k`$. We compute the off-brain attention
fraction

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{\ell}=\frac{1}{H\,|\mathcal{Q}|}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sum_{k\in\bar{\mathcal{B}}}A^{(\ell,h)}_{q,k},
``` |  | (3) |

where $`H`$ is the number of attention heads and $`\mathcal{Q}`$ the set
of query patches. A high off-brain fraction indicates that the frozen
encoder largely ignores the anatomy, implying that LoRA must redirect
substantial attention mass before task-relevant features can be encoded.

#### 3.2.4 Activation Patching

Activation patching isolates which layer adaptations causally drive task
performance \[[26](#bib.bib33)\]. Let $`M_{F}`$ and $`M_{A}`$ denote the
task metric obtained with the fully frozen and fully adapted encoder,
respectively. For each block $`\ell`$, we capture the output of the
adapted encoder at that depth and inject it into the frozen forward pass
at the same depth, replacing only that block’s output. The resulting
metric $`M_{P}^{(\ell)}`$ yields a normalised recovery score:

|     |                                                      |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R^{(\ell)}=\frac{M_{P}^{(\ell)}-M_{F}}{M_{A}-M_{F}},
       ```                                                   |     | (4) |

measuring the fraction of the LoRA-driven gain recovered by providing
the adapted representation at depth $`\ell`$. Note that $`R^{(\ell)}`$
does not measure the contribution of block $`\ell`$ alone, as the
captured activation already incorporates LoRA from all preceding blocks,
and all subsequent ones process it with frozen weights.

An important interaction arises from the multi-scale feature extraction
described in
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Adaptation Pipeline ‣ 3 Method ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI").
The segmentation head receives features from blocks $`\{3,6,9,12\}`$
directly, so features tapped before the patching point reach the head as
frozen representations, while those tapped after it reflect the
propagated adapted activation. $`R^{(\ell)}`$ is therefore cumulative,
quantifying how much of the total improvement is recoverable when the
adapted representation is available from depth $`\ell`$ onward. A high
$`R^{(\ell)}`$ despite a small weight-change ratio indicates a block
whose modest modification is disproportionately important for
performance.

### 3.3 Layer-Selective LoRA

To check whether the causal importance patterns identified by activation
patching translate into a practical adaptation strategy, we split the 12
transformer blocks at the midpoint, applying LoRA to blocks 1–6 (early)
or 7–12 (late), alongside the default all (1–12). The same cut-off is
used across all four backbones to test whether a single, model-agnostic
configuration is viable rather than optimising the split point per
backbone. In each configuration, LoRA modules are injected only into the
selected blocks, with non-selected ones retaining their original frozen
weights. The segmentation head and fusion convolution are trained from
scratch for each configuration to ensure a fair comparison, so
performance differences reflect the effect of the LoRA module placement.

### 3.4 Linear Probing Classification

To further assess whether representations learned during segmentation
transfer to an unrelated task, we evaluate each backbone on Alzheimer’s
disease classification via linear probing \[[45](#bib.bib3)\]. Features
are extracted from the final transformer block with layer normalisation
applied, and the patch token sequence is mean-pooled into a single
768-dimensional vector per slice. The $`2D`$ representations of all
slices belonging to the same subject are then mean-pooled into a single
subject-level representation. A logistic regression classifier is fitted
on the training set and evaluated on the test set. During feature
extraction, the LoRA-adapted segmentation encoder is used as a fixed
feature extractor: the adapted weights (including LoRA modules) are
loaded but not updated, so the features reflect the
post-segmentation-adaptation representations without any further
training.

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Backbone Selection

We select four backbones analysing the self-supervised objective
(self-distillation vs. contrastive vision-language) and the training
data domain (natural vs. medical images). The self-distillation models
are DINOv3 \[[12](#bib.bib1)\], trained on $`\sim`$1.7 billion natural
images, and MerMED-FM \[[14](#bib.bib41)\], trained on $`\sim`$3.3
million medical images, spanning diverse clinical modalities but
excluding MRI. The contrastive models are SigLIP2 \[[13](#bib.bib6)\],
trained on $`\sim`$10 billion natural image-text pairs, and
BiomedCLIP \[[15](#bib.bib46)\], trained on $`\sim`$15 million
image-text pairs from the biomedical literature. All four share the
ViT-B/16 architecture (with 12 layers), isolating the effect of
pre-training configuration from architectural differences. We note that
pre-training dataset scale differs by three orders of magnitude between
natural-domain and medical-domain models; this confound is inherent to
the available foundation models and should be considered when
interpreting domain-related comparisons.

### 4.2 Datasets

For glioma tumour segmentation, models are adapted on the
BraTS-GLI \[[18](#bib.bib7), [20](#bib.bib51), [19](#bib.bib11)\] adult
glioma dataset using all four MRI modalities (T1n, T1c, T2w, T2f) as
input. Segmentation is evaluated at the 2D slice level on three nested
tumour sub-regions: whole tumour (WT), tumour core (TC, combining
enhancing and necrotic cells), and enhancing tumour (ENH, active
infiltrating cells). The dataset comprises $`153{,}105`$ $`2`$D slices
from $`1{,}251`$ patients, with a patient-stratified 70/15/15
train/validation/test split.
Out-of-distribution segmentation generalisation is evaluated on
BraTS-Africa \[[21](#bib.bib9)\] ($`7{,}462`$ slices from $`60`$
patients from Sub-Saharan African populations with glioma) and
BraTS-PED \[[22](#bib.bib10)\] ($`11{,}923`$ slices from $`99`$
pediatric patients with glioma), without any retraining. For the
classification tasks, we use the Open Access Series of Imaging Studies
(OASIS-1) \[[23](#bib.bib12)\] ($`39{,}438`$ $`2`$D slices from $`416`$
subjects) and the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)
datasets \[[24](#bib.bib13)\] ($`174{,}458`$ slices from $`1{,}494`$
subjects), taking the first T1n scan per subject in case of a
longitudinal study. We use the default L2 regularisation strength
($`C=1`$) without tuning, so no validation set is used; each
cross-validation fold uses a subject-level 70/30 train/test split
(Section [4.5](#S4.SS5 "4.5 Linear Probing Classification Implementation ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")).
On OASIS-1, we perform binary Alzheimer’s disease classification, and on
ADNI both binary (CN vs. AD) and 3-class (CN vs. MCI vs. AD)
classification, where CN is the label for a scan from a Cognitively
Normal study participant, MCI denotes Mild Cognitive Impairment, and AD
represents the presence of Alzheimer’s Disease.

### 4.3 Preprocessing

We applied volume-wise z-score normalisation, followed by $`2D`$
slicing. For all datasets, the selected $`2D`$ slices were obtained by
discarding slices with less than $`5\%`$ non-zero pixels from the
original $`3D`$ volumes. We clipped each slice to $`[-3,3]`$, rescaled
to $`[0,1]`$, and resized to $`224\times 224`$. The slices were
normalised following each backbone’s original pre-training protocol
(ImageNet statistics for DINOv3 and MerMED-FM, backbone-specific
statistics for SigLIP2, and OpenAI CLIP statistics for BiomedCLIP).

### 4.4 LoRA Adaptation Implementation Details

All trainable components (LoRA modules, fusion convolution, and
segmentation head) are jointly trained for 50 epochs using the AdamW
optimiser \[[46](#bib.bib31)\] with a batch size of 32 and weight decay
of $`0.05`$. We use a learning rate of $`10^{-4}`$ for the LoRA modules
and $`10^{-3}`$ for the fusion convolution and segmentation head. Each
experiment is run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU and repeated with 3 random
seeds; segmentation results report mean $`\pm`$ standard deviation
across seeds, while classification results are additionally averaged
across cross-validation folds.

### 4.5 Linear Probing Classification Implementation

In this task, only a logistic regression classifier is trained on the
features extracted from each backbone, using L2-regularised logistic
regression ($`C=1`$) with class-balanced weights and the L-BFGS
solver \[[47](#bib.bib50)\], with a maximum of 2,000 iterations. Since
OASIS-1 and ADNI provide only single-modality T1 scans, the fusion
convolution is not used during feature extraction; instead, the input
channel is replicated to three channels and normalised with each
backbone’s specific preprocessing. The logistic regression classifier
uses a 3-fold Monte Carlo cross-validation evaluation. The results are
averaged across folds and across encoder training seeds for the LoRA
adaptations. The L-BFGS solver is deterministic, so no seeds were varied
for the probe.

Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")

### 5.3 Per-Layer Mechanistic Analysis

Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")
reveals that the convergent segmentation performance reported above
conceals markedly different internal adaptation dynamics across the four
backbones.

Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI") (a)
shows that all four models exhibit increasing attention JS divergence
toward later layers, but with different magnitudes: DINOv3 remains the
most stable (below 0.3 throughout), while SigLIP2 escalates steeply to
$`{\sim}0.75`$ at layer 12. MerMED-FM and BiomedCLIP fall between these
extremes. This suggests that DINOv3 requires the least representational
reorganisation to solve the segmentation task, consistent with its
superior in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance
(Tables [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.4 Linear Probing Classification ‣ 3 Method ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")
and [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")).
Conversely, models that undergo larger attention shifts risk overwriting
representations useful for other tasks, which aligns with the
classification degradation observed in
Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Classification Cross-task Transfer ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI").

Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI") (b)
shows a different grouping in parameter space: the two medical-domain
models (MerMED-FM, BiomedCLIP) exhibit the highest weight-change ratios
in later layers, while DINOv3 maintains the most uniform and lowest
ratios across all layers. Notably, the grouping here follows
pre-training domain rather than objective: both medical-domain models
require disproportionately large late-layer updates despite their prior
exposure to clinical images, suggesting that domain-relevant
pre-training does not reduce the magnitude of adaptation needed for a
specific downstream task.

Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI") (c)
provides context for the adaptation cost of each backbone. In the frozen
encoders, the two medical-domain models (MerMED-FM, BiomedCLIP) allocate
a lower fraction of their attention to off-brain patches than the
natural-domain models (DINOv3, SigLIP2), particularly in middle-to-late
layers, consistent with prior exposure to medical images during
pre-training.
Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.2 Classification Cross-task Transfer ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")
illustrates this qualitatively: the frozen medical-domain models attend
to anatomical structures, whereas the frozen natural-domain models
distribute attention more diffusely across background regions. After
LoRA, all four models redirect attention toward the tumour, but the
amount of redistribution required varies across backbones. Taken
together, panels (b) and (c) present an apparent paradox: the
medical-domain models already attend to anatomically relevant regions
yet require the largest parameter updates. This suggests that attending
to the right regions is not sufficient \[[49](#bib.bib22)\], as the
quality of the underlying representations, rather than attention
alignment alone, determines how efficiently a backbone can be adapted.

### 5.4 Activation Patching Recovery Analysis

Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5.3 Per-Layer Mechanistic Analysis ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")
presents activation patching results across all six evaluation datasets.
For each layer $`\ell`$, the adapted encoder’s output at that depth is
injected into an otherwise frozen forward pass; the resulting task
metric indicates how much of the full adaptation benefit is recovered
when adapted representations are available from depth $`\ell`$ onward.
In segmentation (panels a-c), a consistent pattern emerges: across all
four backbones and three datasets, performance recovery is concentrated
in the first half of the encoder (layers 1–6), with recovery declining
in later layers. This stands in contrast with the mechanistic profiles
in
Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI"),
where the largest JS divergence and weight-change ratios concentrate in
layers 8–12, revealing a dissociation between modification magnitude and
causal importance. A notable case is MerMED-FM, which exhibits negative
recovery specifically at layer 7 across all three segmentation datasets
and all seeds (9/9 conditions), meaning that injecting the adapted
representation at this depth produces worse performance than the fully
frozen encoder. This layer coincides with a $`\times 4`$ jump in the
value-projection weight change ($`\|\Delta W_{V}\|`$) relative to the
adjacent layer. This suggests that the large value-projection rewrite
produces activations incompatible with the subsequent frozen blocks.

Classification (panels d-f) shows more heterogeneous patterns across
backbones, but the overall trend is consistent: later layers tend to
reduce rather than improve AUC for all four models. Taken together, the
segmentation and classification patching results consistently point to
the first half of the encoder as the causally important region,
suggesting that restricting adaptation to early layers could preserve
segmentation performance while mitigating classification degradation.

merely reflected signal accumulation rather than genuine causal
sufficiency, restricting LoRA to early layers would substantially
degrade performance.

In segmentation (upper row), early-layer LoRA matches or approaches
all-layer performance for all four backbones across the three datasets,
while late-layer LoRA consistently underperforms both. This validates
the activation patching finding that early-to-mid layers are causally
sufficient for segmentation. DINOv3 exhibits the smallest variation
across configurations, consistent with its more uniform mechanistic
profile
(Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI"),
panels a and b).

In classification (bottom row), the effect of layer selection is more
pronounced. All-layer LoRA degrades classification relative to the
frozen baseline for 3/4 backbones by up to $`{\sim}`$6% AUC
(Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI"));
early-layer LoRA reduces this gap to $`{\sim}`$3% or eliminates it
entirely, and in some cases recovers or exceeds frozen performance.
Late-layer LoRA, while occasionally outperforming all-layer adaptation,
in the majority of the cases remains below early-layer performance and
exhibits greater instability across datasets. This general pattern could
suggest that the late-layer representational shift identified by
activation patching may be responsible for the cross-task damage
observed in
Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Classification Cross-task Transfer ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI"):
by not adapting those layers, early-layer LoRA preserves the
representations that classification relies on. DINOv3, however, is a
clear outlier in the classification results, in five out of the six
tasks, the all layer LoRA adaptation gives the best results. This is
consistent with the classification results in Table
[3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI"),
where DINOv3 was the only backbone to show improved classification
performance after LoRA adaptation.

## 6 Discussion

Our results show that four backbones with comparable segmentation
performance diverge substantially in cross-task classification transfer.
Mechanistic analysis traces this divergence to different
representational shift profiles and reveals a dissociation between
modification magnitude and causal importance: the largest attention
divergence and weight changes concentrate in later layers
(Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")),
yet activation patching identifies early-to-mid layers as causally
sufficient for segmentation across all four backbones
(Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5.3 Per-Layer Mechanistic Analysis ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")).
The layer-selective LoRA experiments support this finding: restricting
adaptation to early layers matches or approaches all-layer segmentation
while mitigating the classification degradation that all-layer
adaptation causes
(Figure [6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 5.4 Activation Patching Recovery Analysis ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")).

DINOv3 is the exception to this pattern. Although its later layers are
also less causally important than its early ones, all-layer adaptation
yields classification results similar to or better than early-layer
adaptation, and DINOv3 is the only backbone whose classification
performance improves after segmentation adaptation. Among the adapted
models, it also shows the lowest JS divergence and weight-change ratios,
the smallest variation across LoRA configurations, the highest mean Dice
on all three segmentation datasets, and the best classification
performance. This is compatible with the view that a smaller
representational shift better preserves the representations that other
tasks rely on. The relationship, however, is not deterministic:
BiomedCLIP undergoes a substantial attention shift yet remains
approximately neutral in classification on OASIS-1. The mechanistic
tools are therefore best understood as diagnostics that reveal how a
model adapts, rather than as direct predictors of downstream task
performance.

In our experiments, medical-domain pre-training confers no consistent
advantage for segmentation adaptation: after LoRA, DINOv3 achieves the
highest and BiomedCLIP the lowest mean Dice on all three segmentation
datasets, while MerMED-FM performs on par with SigLIP2. This is
consistent with the hypothesis that representation quality scales with
pre-training data diversity \[[50](#bib.bib21)\], though dataset scale
and pre-training objective vary simultaneously, preventing causal
attribution to any single factor. Notably, the frozen medical-domain
models already attend to anatomically relevant regions more than their
natural-domain counterparts
(Figures [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.1 Segmentation Performance ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")c
and [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.2 Classification Cross-task Transfer ‣ 5 Results ‣ Convergent Performance, Divergent Mechanisms in LoRA-Adapted Vision Foundation Models for Brain MRI")),
yet this initial advantage does not translate into superior adaptation
performance, suggesting that where a model attends matters less than the
quality of the representations it computes. In classification, the
picture differs: frozen MerMED-FM achieves the highest AUC across all
three settings, yet this advantage is entirely lost after segmentation
adaptation, showing the largest classification drops among the four
models.

Practical guidelines. While our study is limited to four backbones and
one imaging domain, the results suggest some considerations for
selecting and adapting VFMs for medical imaging. First, backbone
selection should not rely on segmentation metrics alone: evaluating on
at least one additional task can reveal cross-task degradation invisible
to Dice scores. Second, per-layer mechanistic profiles, particularly JS
divergence and weight-change ratios, can serve as low-cost diagnostics
before out-of-distribution data is available: in our experiments, the
backbone with the most uniform and lowest divergence profile (DINOv3)
consistently exhibited the best generalisation. Third, restricting LoRA
to early-to-mid layers might be a conservative default when cross-task
robustness matters, as it matched or approached full-layer segmentation
for all four backbones while reducing classification degradation in 3/4
of them. Finally, we found no evidence that medical-domain pre-training
provides an advantage over natural-domain pre-training for segmentation
adaptation, though this comparison is confounded by dataset scale and
should not be taken as a general conclusion.

Limitations & Future Work. Our study is restricted to the brain MRI
domain, to evaluating only four backbone models, to LoRA as the sole
PEFT method, and to the ViT-B/16 architecture. In addition, whether the
observed patterns generalise to other imaging modalities, adaptation
strategies (e.g. adapters, prompt tuning), and larger model sizes (e.g.
ViT-L, ViT-H) remains an open question. Segmentation experiments use
three random seeds and classification probes use three cross-validation
folds, which suffices to establish the reported trends but limits the
precision of variance estimates. Future work would involve extending
this framework to additional architectures, scales, imaging domains, and
PEFT methods.

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented a mechanistic evaluation of four LoRA-adapted vision
encoders for brain MRI, combining layer-wise interpretability tools with
multi-task downstream assessment across six datasets. Our analysis
reveals three key findings: (1) segmentation convergence masks
substantial divergence in classification transfer, with medical-domain
pre-training conferring no consistent segmentation advantage in our
setting; (2) the most heavily modified layers are not the causally
important ones, with early-to-mid layers consistently identified as
sufficient for segmentation recovery; and (3) restricting LoRA to these
early layers matches or approaches full-layer segmentation while
improving classification performance relative to all-layer adaptation in
the three backbones whose cross-task performance degrades after
adaptation.

Together, these findings support a practical protocol for adapting
vision encoders in clinical settings: evaluate on multiple tasks to
detect cross-task degradation invisible to segmentation metrics, use
activation patching to identify causally relevant layers, and adapt
preferentially those layers to balance task performance with
representational robustness. While our study focuses on brain MRI and
LoRA, the analytical framework is applicable to any attention-based
encoder and PEFT method.
````

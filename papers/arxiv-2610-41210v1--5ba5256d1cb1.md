###### Abstract

Real-world image editing requires coordinating multiple visual
transformations with content preservation and reference constraints, yet
aggregate scores can readily obscure unmet local requirements. Effective
evaluation must therefore identify local failures and translate them
into priorities for subsequent model improvement. We introduce
WeGenEditBench, comprising 2,740 evaluation instances and 148 task types
across single-image, text-intensive, and multi-reference editing.
Pre-generated checklists shared across models decompose each request
into atomic requirements; item-level deductions and visual evidence are
established first and then summarized into scores for edit execution,
preservation, visual quality, and task-specific correctness. To support
the experience-acquisition component of recursive self-improvement
(RSI), we develop WeGenEvoStudio, a unified multi-agent harness for
RSI-oriented learning that integrates model-conditioned failure
diagnosis, retrieval-grounded data construction, supervised adaptation,
and re-evaluation. Among 15 evaluated configurations, GPT-Image-2 leads
single-image editing with an Overall score of 4.076, while
Seedream-5.0-Pro leads text-intensive editing at 4.291. Evolution
experiments across four generation and editing benchmarks demonstrate
substantial targeted gains: on their report-selected Top-5 single-image
targets, FLUX.2 \[dev\] improves Overall from 1.083 to 3.517, while
Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 gains 13.2%. Together, our benchmark and harness
provide a foundation for developing more reliable image models through
interpretable evaluation and feedback-driven learning.

## 1 Introduction

Rapid advances in text-to-image and unified multimodal models are
expanding the scope of natural-language image editing. Proprietary
systems such as GPT-Image-2 and Nano Banana Pro, alongside open-weight
models such as Qwen-Image-Edit and FLUX.2 \[dev\], demonstrate strong
instruction understanding and precise editing capabilities
\[[26](#bib.bib26), [30](#bib.bib27), [28](#bib.bib8), [1](#bib.bib9)\].
Real-world requests, however, increasingly couple multiple visual
transformations with preservation and reference constraints, such as
replacing a product background while retaining its packaging text and
appearance. Current models still exhibit a high-score paradox in these
settings: visual plausibility or success on a single salient edit does
not establish that all coupled requirements have been jointly satisfied.
As illustrated in Figure
[1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
(left), aggregate scores can conceal local omissions and leave the
feedback connection between evaluation and model improvement incomplete.

Existing benchmarks have advanced editing evaluation along complementary
directions. GEdit and ImgEdit cover common operations, with ImgEdit also
assessing multi-turn interaction \[[22](#bib.bib1), [46](#bib.bib2)\];
Complex-Edit, GEditBench v2, UnicBench, and ConceptEdit extend
evaluation to compositional operations, open-ended requests, reasoning,
and fine-grained concepts, respectively \[[40](#bib.bib14),
[17](#bib.bib11), [45](#bib.bib13), [8](#bib.bib3)\]. Evaluation is also
becoming more granular: ComplexBench-Edit assesses instruction
dependencies \[[35](#bib.bib39)\], Qwen-Image-Bench introduces
hierarchical criteria for text-to-image generation \[[19](#bib.bib42)\],
and Mind-Brush incorporates checklist-based evaluation of knowledge- and
reasoning-driven generation \[[13](#bib.bib43)\]. Nevertheless, general
image editing requires sample-specific criteria grounded in source
images to jointly verify requested changes, preservation conditions, and
reference assignments across these three settings. Top-down dimensional
scoring can be influenced by salient visual changes, leaving local
omissions and constraint violations difficult to trace.

To this end, we introduce WeGenEditBench, comprising 2,740 instances and
148 task types across single-image, text-intensive, and multi-reference
editing. Real-world requests guide automated construction and human
calibration. Pre-generated checklists shared across models decompose
each instance into atomic requirements under Edit Goal (EG),
Preservation (PR), Visual Quality (VQ), and Domain-specific Capability
(DS). A fixed vision-language model (VLM) judge must record item-level
rationales and deductions before assigning dimensional scores, making
omitted edits, unintended changes, and incorrect reference binding
traceable to specific requirements and visual evidence.

Evaluation should support not only model comparison but also decisions
about what a model should learn next. Recent research on recursive
self-improvement (RSI) studies how systems can retain capability gains
and use them in subsequent improvement cycles \[[49](#bib.bib46),
[50](#bib.bib48), [10](#bib.bib50)\]. For image models, a key step is to
translate observed failures into training experience tailored to their
current weaknesses. Data-centric development, exemplified by DataPerf,
and feedback-based reinforcement learning, exemplified by DDPO, provide
methodological foundations \[[24](#bib.bib44), [2](#bib.bib23)\].
OmniEdit, AnyEdit, and ConceptEdit enrich supervision through specialist
models and concept expansion \[[37](#bib.bib15), [48](#bib.bib16),
[8](#bib.bib3)\]; DataEvolver explores validation-driven data iteration
\[[38](#bib.bib5), [52](#bib.bib6)\], while IterComp and JarvisEvo
jointly refine generators or editors and their evaluators
\[[53](#bib.bib7), [21](#bib.bib4)\]. General image editing nevertheless
faces a central planning challenge: translating unmet requirements into
subsequent data construction plans and refreshing their priorities as
model capabilities evolve. Coarse category averages can locate broad
weaknesses, but do not specify recurring constraint violations or the
targeted supervision needed to address them.

To connect failure diagnosis with targeted learning, we introduce
WeGenEvoStudio, a unified multi-agent harness for RSI-oriented model
improvement (Figure
[1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models"),
right). The Diagnosis Agent combines category statistics, item-level
rationales, and cross-case visual review to formulate task-level data
prescriptions; the Construction Agent uses these prescriptions to
retrieve new concepts and source images and synthesize targeted editing
instructions and paired training examples. After supervised adaptation
produces an updated model, the re-evaluation interface can refresh
subsequent learning priorities, supporting a feedback loop for targeted
experience acquisition in RSI-oriented systems \[[54](#bib.bib49),
[10](#bib.bib50)\]. Experiments across four editing and text-to-image
benchmarks reveal fine-grained capability gaps, targeted adaptation
gains, and cross-task trade-offs. Our main contributions are:

- •
  A fine-grained image editing benchmark. WeGenEditBench comprises 2,740
  instances and 148 task types across single-image, text-intensive, and
  multi-reference editing. Sample-specific atomic checklists establish
  item-level deductions before dimensional aggregation, connecting
  scores to underlying visual evidence.
- •
  A multi-agent harness for model improvement. WeGenEvoStudio translates
  benchmark failure diagnosis into task-level data prescriptions,
  retrieval-grounded experience synthesis, and supervised parameter
  updates. Its re-evaluation interface refreshes learning priorities to
  support RSI-oriented experience acquisition.
- •
  Comprehensive capability diagnosis and empirical model evolution. We
  systematically evaluate 15 widely used configurations to identify
  bottlenecks in execution, preservation, and reference binding, and
  conduct model-evolution experiments with Qwen-Image and FLUX.2
  \[dev\], demonstrating substantial targeted gains and cross-task
  capability trade-offs.

## 2 Related Work

##### Image editing benchmarks.

Evaluation of image generation and editing typically examines
instruction following, preservation, visual quality, and
knowledge-grounded synthesis \[[22](#bib.bib1), [46](#bib.bib2),
[45](#bib.bib13), [39](#bib.bib52)\], and has recently expanded to
fine-grained visual reasoning and the explanation of synthetic-content
anomalies \[[42](#bib.bib56), [44](#bib.bib53)\]. EditBench evaluates
inpainting \[[36](#bib.bib17)\], and MagicBrush provides single- and
multi-turn annotations \[[51](#bib.bib18)\]. GEdit covers common
operations \[[22](#bib.bib1)\], while ImgEdit introduces task-specific
criteria and multi-turn evaluation \[[46](#bib.bib2)\]; however, concise
requests do not fully reflect intertwined transformations and
preservation constraints. More recent work examines reasoning in
UnicBench \[[45](#bib.bib13)\], open-ended requests in GEditBench v2
\[[17](#bib.bib11)\], controlled operation counts in Complex-Edit
\[[40](#bib.bib14)\], and instructions and their dependencies in
ComplexBench-Edit \[[35](#bib.bib39)\]. Aggregate scores can
nevertheless obscure failed local targets or constraints. Joint
verification of fine-grained requirements across single-image,
text-intensive, and multi-reference editing remains underexplored.

##### Agentic multimodal data construction.

Closing capability gaps requires diverse and reliable supervision
\[[55](#bib.bib20), [8](#bib.bib3), [43](#bib.bib51)\]. InstructPix2Pix
synthesizes editing pairs \[[3](#bib.bib19)\], OmniEdit combines
specialist supervision with sampling \[[37](#bib.bib15)\], and AnyEdit
expands task coverage through adaptive synthesis \[[48](#bib.bib16)\];
these approaches largely follow predefined task inventories. Agentic
methods introduce greater adaptability: Gen-n-Val couples prompt
generation with visual validation \[[15](#bib.bib24)\], ScaleEditor
coordinates expansion and verification through hierarchical agents
\[[6](#bib.bib25)\], and DataEvolver uses rejection feedback to revise
retrieval and synthesis \[[38](#bib.bib5)\]. Other systems use drafts or
executable canvases for controlled refinement \[[16](#bib.bib54),
[41](#bib.bib55)\]. Improving synthesis quality alone, however, does not
determine which supervision a target model needs to address its specific
failure modes.

##### Feedback-driven learning and recursive self-improvement.

Feedback can support output refinement in Self-Refine
\[[23](#bib.bib21)\], retention of verbal experience in Reflexion
\[[31](#bib.bib22)\], or policy optimization in DDPO
\[[2](#bib.bib23)\]. RSI research further investigates how retained
improvements participate in subsequent cycles and how the improvement
mechanism itself can evolve \[[10](#bib.bib50)\]. Examples include code
or agent-logic self-modification in STOP \[[49](#bib.bib46)\], Gödel
Agent \[[47](#bib.bib47)\], and the Darwin Gödel Machine
\[[50](#bib.bib48)\], as well as task proposal conditioned on learner
progress in Absolute Zero \[[54](#bib.bib49)\]. In multimodal learning,
IterComp updates reward models and generators \[[53](#bib.bib7)\],
JarvisEvo jointly optimizes editing and evaluation policies
\[[21](#bib.bib4)\], and Visual DataEvolver performs validation-driven
data expansion \[[52](#bib.bib6)\]. Our focus is complementary:
translating a target image model’s unmet requirements into task-level
data prescriptions and new training experience, with traceable
connections between diagnostic evidence and supervision throughout the
feedback loop.

## 3 WeGenEditBench

### 3.1 Real-World Scenarios and Task Coverage

To assess the joint fulfillment of real-world composite editing
requirements, WeGenEditBench covers three complementary settings:
single-image editing, text-intensive editing, and multi-reference
editing (Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Real-World Scenarios and Task Coverage ‣ 3 WeGenEditBench ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models"),
left). These settings reflect common user needs in everyday retouching,
layout design, and attribute customization from multiple sources. The
taxonomy is derived from over 16,000 real-world requests and examples
collected from public web sources, model documentation, and open-source
projects. Single-image tasks cover local, portrait, aesthetic,
commercial, interaction, restoration, and reasoning-oriented edits;
text-intensive tasks cover textual content, typography, and layout;
multi-reference tasks test the binding of identity, product, style, and
scene attributes to designated sources. Automated construction and human
calibration yield 2,740 instances spanning 19 task families and 148 task
types, connecting application scenarios to concrete preservation,
typography, and reference constraints.

### 3.2 Source-Grounded Synthesis and Instruction Complexity

To ensure that composite instructions are visually actionable rather
than arbitrary combinations, we compose compatible operations around
visible subjects in the source image, explicitly specify preservation,
layout, and reference constraints, and use human review to remove
unsupported or contradictory combinations (Figure
[6](#A1.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ A.2 Source-Grounded Curation ‣ Appendix A Benchmark Construction and Coverage ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
in the appendix). As shown in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Real-World Scenarios and Task Coverage ‣ 3 WeGenEditBench ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")(a–c),
mean instruction lengths reach 79.2, 124.6, and 146.3 tokens for the
single-image, text-intensive, and multi-reference subsets, respectively,
compared with 10.2–16.0 tokens for GEdit, ImgEdit, and UnicBench. The
additional text primarily specifies multi-step transformations and
preservation boundaries: 70.6% of single-image instances and 31.2% of
text-intensive instances carry multiple task labels, while
multi-reference tasks coordinate two to four input images with distinct
roles. These dense requirements probe model bottlenecks in
simultaneously satisfying interacting transformations, fine-grained
preservation, and reference binding.

### 3.3 Evidence-Grounded Evaluation and Failure Diagnosis

To expose local failures hidden by high aggregate scores, we evaluate
four dimensions where applicable: Edit Goal (EG), Preservation (PR),
Visual Quality (VQ), and Domain-specific Capability (DS). From each
instruction and its source images, we derive independently verifiable
items specifying a target, attribute or location, and expected state.
Text editing separates text-side and image-side checks, while
multi-reference editing binds attributes to designated source images.
Checklists are generated before evaluation, fixed within each evaluation
version, and shared across compared models. As shown in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Real-World Scenarios and Task Coverage ‣ 3 WeGenEditBench ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")(d),
single-image checklists most often contain 16–20 items, whereas
text-intensive and multi-reference checklists most often contain 6–10.

Holistic dimensional judgments can overemphasize salient visual changes
while overlooking missed targets or unintended alterations. Figure
[3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.3 Evidence-Grounded Evaluation and Failure Diagnosis ‣ 3 WeGenEditBench ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
illustrates this motivation through high-scoring failures on existing
benchmarks (left) and the verification mechanism of WeGenEditBench
(right). Our fixed VLM judge must first record visual rationales and
item-level deductions $`\delta_{i,j}\in[0,5]`$, and then assign
dimensional scores on a 0–5 scale according to each item’s importance
under the scoring rubric. This structured attribution helps reduce
salience bias. In the right-hand example, silver boots coexist with an
unchanged ceramic ornament: although $`\mathrm{PR}=\mathrm{VQ}=5`$,
item-level verification exposes the missed target and inconsistent
material luster across targets, yielding $`\mathrm{EG}=2`$,
$`\mathrm{DS}=0`$, and an Overall score of 1.95. Three trained experts
randomly audit 10% of instances in each subset. Following checklist
corrections, all compared models are re-evaluated on affected instances
using the same revised checklist; Appendix
[B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Human Audit and Shared Revisions ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Protocol and Diagnostic Evidence ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
details the audit scope.

##### Subset-specific aggregation.

Single-image editing weights EG, PR, and VQ and subtracts the mean DS
penalty over the applicable task set $`T`$, with a zero penalty when
$`T`$ is empty:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
S_{\mathrm{single}}=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,5]}\!\left(0.6S_{\mathrm{EG}}+0.2S_{\mathrm{PR}}+0.2S_{\mathrm{VQ}}-\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{t\in T}\frac{5-S_{\mathrm{DS},t}}{4}\right).
``` |  | (1) |

Text-intensive editing evaluates the two sides separately and combines
them when image-side requirements are present; otherwise, it uses only
$`S_{\mathrm{text}}`$. Multi-reference editing combines goal
fulfillment, reference-attribute preservation, and visual quality:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{\mathrm{text\text{-}edit}}=0.75S_{\mathrm{text}}+0.25S_{\mathrm{image}},\qquad S_{\mathrm{multi}}=0.4S_{\mathrm{EG}}+0.4S_{\mathrm{PR}}+0.2S_{\mathrm{VQ}}.
``` |  | (2) |

Each subset’s Overall is the mean of valid instance scores, counting
each instance once. The retained item-level evidence directly supports
capability diagnosis in WeGenEvoStudio.

## 4 WeGenEvoStudio

### 4.1 Unified Evaluation-to-Learning Framework

To translate the unmet requirements identified in Section
[3](#S3 "3 WeGenEditBench ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
into learning objectives, we introduce WeGenEvoStudio, a unified
multi-agent harness centered on model-specific data prescriptions
(Figure
[4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.3 Evidence-Grounded Evaluation and Failure Diagnosis ‣ 3 WeGenEditBench ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")).
For model parameters $`\theta_{t}`$, evaluation records
$`\mathcal{E}_{t}`$ contain instructions, source and output images,
scores, and available rationales. Diagnosis extracts a capability
profile $`\Phi_{t}`$ and a data prescription $`\mathcal{T}_{t}`$, which
guide the construction of targeted tasks $`\mathcal{X}_{t}`$. These
tasks are paired with generated targets to form a supervised training
set $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$, followed by parameter updates and
re-evaluation:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\theta_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{evaluate}}\mathcal{E}_{t}\xrightarrow{\text{diagnose}}(\Phi_{t},\mathcal{T}_{t})\xrightarrow{\text{construct}}\mathcal{X}_{t}`$ |  | (3) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\text{supervise}}\mathcal{D}_{t}\xrightarrow{\text{train}(\cdot;\lambda_{t})}\theta_{t+1}\xrightarrow{\text{re-evaluate}}\mathcal{E}_{t+1}.`$ |  |  |

gemini-3-flash-preview serves as the backbone LLM for agent planning and
tool use. The underlying agentic harness encapsulates 68 atomic tools in
a standard registry. Isolated subcontexts, restricted tool allowlists,
and content fingerprints link evaluation records, data prescriptions,
and model-checkpoint lineage. Pluggable feedback adapters support
external public benchmarks: they accept coarse category scores from
GEdit and ImgEdit, native rationales from WeGenBench, and fine-grained
checklists from WeGenEditBench, mapping external failure categories to a
shared internal taxonomy. Re-evaluation of a successor $`\theta_{t+1}`$
can refresh prescription priorities, providing the infrastructure for an
experience-acquisition loop oriented toward RSI \[[10](#bib.bib50)\].
Appendix
[C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Harness and Resource Contracts ‣ Appendix C Agentic Diagnosis and Data Construction ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
provides implementation details.

### 4.2 From Failure Diagnosis to Targeted Data Prescriptions

Category averages can locate weak areas but do not explain the specific
conditions that repeatedly trigger failures. As shown in Figure
[7](#A3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Diagnosis at the available feedback granularity. ‣ C.2 Diagnosis Agent: Evidence to Data Prescriptions ‣ Appendix C Agentic Diagnosis and Data Construction ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
in the appendix, the Diagnosis Agent combines score-based category
selection with case-level visual review. Given the target model’s
category score $`\bar{S}_{c}(\theta_{t})`$ and an anchor
$`\theta_{\mathrm{anchor}}`$ evaluated under the same protocol, it
computes the relative deficit
$`\Delta_{c}^{\mathrm{rel}}=\bar{S}_{c}(\theta_{\mathrm{anchor}})-\bar{S}_{c}(\theta_{t})`$.
Approximately 60% of the slots prioritize the largest relative deficits,
while the remaining 40% target the lowest absolute scores. The initial
selection is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{seed}}=\operatorname{Deduplicate}\!\left(\operatorname{Top}_{\lfloor 0.6K\rfloor,c}\!\left(\Delta_{c}^{\mathrm{rel}}\right)\cup\operatorname{Bottom}_{\lceil 0.4K\rceil,c}\!\left(\bar{S}_{c}(\theta_{t})\right)\right).
``` |  | (4) |

After deduplication, reciprocal-rank ordering fills any remaining slots
to obtain $`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{focus}}`$ with $`K`$ categories,
retaining at least one category selected by absolute weakness. Without a
compatible anchor, selection relies on the target model’s own
statistics.

Within each candidate category, the agent contrasts representative
failures and successes, using VLM review of instructions and images to
identify omitted operations, affected objects, and unintended changes.
Cross-case synthesis yields the capability profile $`\Phi_{t}`$. The
resulting structured prescription,
$`\mathcal{T}_{t}=\{(\psi(c),\mathcal{S}_{c},\mathcal{R}_{c}^{\mathrm{edit}},\mathcal{R}_{c}^{\mathrm{pres}},N_{c})\}_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{focus}}}`$,
maps each weak category to an internal taxonomy label $`\psi(c)`$ and
specifies its quota $`N_{c}`$. When supported by the evidence, it also
records source-image conditions $`\mathcal{S}_{c}`$ and editing and
preservation requirements
$`(\mathcal{R}_{c}^{\mathrm{edit}},\mathcal{R}_{c}^{\mathrm{pres}})`$.
For example, failures during background replacement motivate a
prescription for clearly visible product images and explicit
preservation constraints. To prevent benchmark-instance leakage,
individual failure cases remain in the diagnostic report as attribution
evidence; only task labels, quotas, and abstract category notes are
passed downstream, without reconstructing benchmark scenes.

### 4.3 Retrieval-Grounded Experience Construction and Controlled Supervised Adaptation

To address diagnosed weaknesses in new visual contexts, the Construction
Agent maps target tasks to the shared taxonomy and allocates quotas
across categories, without importing instruction templates from external
benchmarks (Figure
[8](#A3.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ A shared construction interface. ‣ C.3 Construction Agent: Retrieval-Grounded Task Synthesis ‣ Appendix C Agentic Diagnosis and Data Construction ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
in the appendix). The editing branch retrieves unedited source images
from a library or web search using category-specific queries, followed
by format, resolution, and exact or perceptual duplicate checks. The
text-to-image branch uses web-text retrieval to expand long-tail
concepts and reduce dependence on high-frequency entities in the
language model’s prior. Each synthesis call grounds the target task and
category notes in a new source image, explicitly specifying visible edit
targets, intended transformations, and preservation conditions while
rejecting incompatible sources. VLM review then checks feasibility and
rewrites the instruction as a natural user request.

Given an approved source image and instruction, the downstream
supervision pipeline invokes a high-capability generation backend to
synthesize candidate target images. Visual-quality and task-alignment
filters consistent with the construction stage reject candidates with
structural artifacts or incomplete transformations, yielding a
high-confidence training set $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ for the supervised
update
$`\theta_{t+1}=\operatorname{Train}(\theta_{t},\mathcal{D}_{t};\lambda_{t})`$.
In this design, the teacher supervision pipeline maintains per-example
rendering quality, while upstream diagnostic prescriptions and retrieval
grounding determine how the limited training budget is allocated across
tasks. Appendices
[C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Construction Agent: Retrieval-Grounded Task Synthesis ‣ Appendix C Agentic Diagnosis and Data Construction ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
and
[C.4](#A3.SS4 "C.4 Task-Level Feedback and Instance Boundaries ‣ Appendix C Agentic Diagnosis and Data Construction ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
detail the construction interface and its task-level boundaries.

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

##### Benchmark evaluation.

We evaluate 15 model configurations. Closed-source models comprise
GPT-Image-2 \[[26](#bib.bib26)\], Nano-Banana-Pro \[[30](#bib.bib27)\],

##### Model evolution.

We train separate rank-16 LoRA adapters for each backbone–benchmark
pair, using Qwen-Image-2512 for text-to-image generation,
Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 for editing, and FLUX.2 \[dev\] for both
\[[14](#bib.bib38), [27](#bib.bib37), [28](#bib.bib8), [1](#bib.bib9)\].
Each experiment uses 25,000 synthetic examples, evenly allocated to the
Top-$`K=5`$ diagnosed weak categories with 5,000 examples per category,
and trains on 32 Cambricon MLU590 accelerators with a per-device batch
size of 1. Experiments span four benchmarks with different feedback
granularities: GEdit and ImgEdit provide coarse category scores
\[[22](#bib.bib1), [46](#bib.bib2)\], WeGenBench additionally provides
native rationales \[[20](#bib.bib10)\], and WeGenEditBench supplies
fine-grained checklist evidence. All experiments share the same
retrieval-based constructor and use new source instances.
Before-and-after comparisons are paired on common-valid instances
(Appendix
[D](#A4 "Appendix D Experimental Protocol and Extended Analysis ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")).
We use Gemini-3-flash as the orchestrator in WeGenEvoStudio.

##### Atomic evidence beneath high aggregate scores.

Strong aggregate performance still conceals many unmet requirements
(Appendix
[B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Diagnostic Scope and Statistical Conventions ‣ Appendix B Evaluation Protocol and Diagnostic Evidence ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models"),
Tables
[8](#A5.T8 "Table 8 ‣ E.1 Quantitative Comparison and Diagnostic Tables ‣ Appendix E Supporting Results and Complete Taxonomy ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
and
[9](#A5.T9 "Table 9 ‣ E.1 Quantitative Comparison and Diagnostic Tables ‣ Appendix E Supporting Results and Complete Taxonomy ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")).
Among GPT-Image-2’s 644 single-image outputs with Overall $`\geq 4`$,
315 contain at least one severely failed item scored $`\leq 2`$: 48.9%,
with a 95% confidence interval of \[45.1%, 52.8%\]. Even among outputs
scoring $`\geq 4.5`$, this proportion remains 31.3%. In multi-reference
editing, 108 GPT-Image-2 outputs share the identical
dimensional-and-Overall score vector $`(5,5,3,4.6)`$, yet the underlying
checklists distinguish 44 outputs with a severe item failure from 64
without one. On 280 shared tasks with requirements on both the text and
image sides, only 25.0% of GPT-Image-2 outputs and 27.1% of Seedream
outputs satisfy every item on both sides at a score of $`\geq 4`$. These
findings show that dimensional aggregation loses consequential
information when multiple constraints interact; atomic checklists
recover local failures that remain hidden in aggregate scores.

prescriptions can strengthen operation supervision for execution errors,
tighten preservation constraints for unintended changes, and add
specialized examples for domain-rule failures. Further experience
acquisition can thereby respond to the model’s remaining weaknesses.

## 6 Conclusion

We introduced WeGenEditBench and WeGenEvoStudio to connect fine-grained
image editing evaluation with targeted model improvement. Composite
editing requests require feedback that distinguishes visual plausibility
from complete requirement fulfillment and identifies what a model should
learn next. Atomic checklists shared across models expose unmet local
requirements, while the multi-agent harness translates failure diagnosis
into targeted training data and supervised updates. In our evaluation,
GPT-Image-2 leads single-image editing at 4.076 Overall, while
Seedream-5.0-Pro leads text-intensive editing at 4.291. On their
report-selected Top-5 single-image targets, FLUX.2 \[dev\] improves
Overall from 1.083 to 3.517, and Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 gains 13.2%. These
targeted gains and cross-dimensional trade-offs demonstrate the value of
interpretable, fine-grained evaluation as actionable training feedback.
Our framework thus supports targeted experience acquisition and
feedback-driven capability improvement within RSI-oriented learning
loops. Extending this connection to a broader range of editing scenarios
can guide the development of more reliable and adaptable image models.

## Appendix A Benchmark Construction and Coverage

### A.1 Application-Grounded Taxonomy

WeGenEditBench contains 2,740 instances: 1,008 single-image, 1,000
text-intensive, and 732 multi-reference tasks. Over 16,000 manually
collected application requests and examples inform the taxonomy;
automated construction and human refinement produce the evaluation
records. The subsets contain 8/5/6 first-level families and 65/35/48
second-level types. The 148 types count subset–label pairs, not disjoint
operations. Single-image and text-intensive instances may carry multiple
labels; multi-reference instances have one primary label and two to four
ordered inputs (382/185/165 cases). Table
[5](#A5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ E.1 Quantitative Comparison and Diagnostic Tables ‣ Appendix E Supporting Results and Complete Taxonomy ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
and Appendix
[E.3](#A5.SS3 "E.3 Complete Task Taxonomy ‣ Appendix E Supporting Results and Complete Taxonomy ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
give counts and definitions. Instruction lengths use tiktoken==0.12.0,
o200k_base, and encode_ordinary on the stored instruction without
translation, trimming, or chat templates. The subset means are
79.2/124.6/146.3 tokens. Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Real-World Scenarios and Task Coverage ‣ 3 WeGenEditBench ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
additionally reports task-label multiplicity, input counts, and
checklist sizes; multi-reference primary labels are excluded from the
multiplicity comparison.

Tables
[33](#A5.T33 "Table 33 ‣ E.3.1 Single-Image Task Taxonomy ‣ E.3 Complete Task Taxonomy ‣ Appendix E Supporting Results and Complete Taxonomy ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")–[35](#A5.T35 "Table 35 ‣ E.3.3 Multi-Reference Task Taxonomy ‣ E.3 Complete Task Taxonomy ‣ Appendix E Supporting Results and Complete Taxonomy ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")

## Appendix B Evaluation Protocol and Diagnostic Evidence

### B.1 Shared Requirements and Item Judgments

All three WeGenEditBench subsets use Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview
(gemini-3.1-pro-preview) as the judge. Checklists are generated from the
selected instruction and source images before scoring and shared by all
compared models within an evaluation version. Each item identifies an
observable target, attribute, location, or reference assignment under
EG/PR/VQ/DS. The judge receives the instruction, ordered sources,
output, and checklist, and records a deduction $`\delta_{i,j}\in[0,5]`$,
score $`5-\delta_{i,j}`$, and visual rationale for each item before
assigning dimensional scores. Dimensional scores follow their rubrics
and need not equal item means. Literal text and source indices remain
explicit; invented requirements and redundant restatements are excluded.

##### Edit Goal (EG).

EG measures whether each requested transformation is applied to the
intended target with the specified attribute, quantity, and spatial
relation. It covers object operations, requested text changes, and
reference-conditioned composition. For a request to turn both boots and
an ornament into silver metal, changing only the boots leaves an editing
goal unmet.

##### Preservation (PR).

PR measures whether content and attributes outside the requested changes
remain faithful to the source. Checks cover protected identities, object
geometry, existing text, layout, and background, as applicable. In
multi-reference editing, PR checks the attributes assigned to each
source. Preservation is judged relative to the instruction; a requested
change is not itself a preservation error.

##### Visual Quality (VQ).

VQ measures rendering quality and visual coherence, including clarity,
structural integrity, natural boundaries, and freedom from blur,
artifacts, or implausible seams. Text-side checks examine legibility and
glyph integrity; image-side checks assess the surrounding visual
content. A visually polished output may therefore receive high VQ while
failing EG or PR requirements.

##### Domain-specific Capability (DS).

DS measures correctness under task-specific criteria beyond generic
execution, preservation, and rendering quality. Applicable checks
address material behavior, physical or geometric relations, typographic
hierarchy, or cultural semantics. In the metal-editing example,
consistent metallic tones and reflections across both targets form a
joint requirement: making one object reflective does not establish
material consistency across the pair.

### B.2 Scoring, Applicability, and Aggregation

For single-image instance $`i`$,
$`B_{i}=0.6S_{\mathrm{EG},i}+0.2S_{\mathrm{PR},i}+0.2S_{\mathrm{VQ},i}`$
and $`p_{i,t}=(5-S_{\mathrm{DS},i,t})/4`$. Equation
[1](#S3.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ Subset-specific aggregation. ‣ 3.3 Evidence-Grounded Evaluation and Failure Diagnosis ‣ 3 WeGenEditBench ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models")
subtracts the mean applicable-task penalty and clips to $`[0,5]`$;
task-conditioned summaries use the corresponding penalty. Empty DS sets
incur no penalty and have no DS score. Empty EG/PR/VQ checklists receive
5; omitted dimension scores in compact responses fall back to item
means. Missing required fields or mismatched item counts invalidate the
response. Stored scores retain four decimals. Text editing evaluates
EG/PR/VQ/DS separately on the text and image sides, combining their
scores with weights 0.75/0.25 only when image-side requirements exist;
otherwise the text-side score is final. Multi-reference EG/PR/VQ
correspond to native IF/RF/VQ and use weights 0.4/0.4/0.2 (Equation
[2](#S3.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ Subset-specific aggregation. ‣ 3.3 Evidence-Grounded Evaluation and Failure Diagnosis ‣ 3 WeGenEditBench ‣ From Evaluation to Evolution: Benchmarking Image Editing and Evolving Image Models"));
no separate DS is annotated. Subset Overall averages valid instance
scores once per instance, including unsuccessful edits. Inapplicable
criteria and invalid judge responses are excluded from their respective
denominators; task-label overlap does not duplicate instances in
Overall. The weights define an operational rubric. High aggregate scores
alongside low item scores demonstrate retained diagnostic detail.

### B.3 Human Audit and Shared Revisions

Three trained experts randomly audit 10% of cases in each benchmark,
checking whether requirements follow from the shown inputs and
deductions are supported by outputs. Problematic checklists are
corrected first; every compared model is re-evaluated on affected cases
using the same revised checklist. Each version fixes the judge, prompt,
input processing, checklist, and invalid-response policy. A score–item
discrepancy alone is not proof of model failure: numerical aggregation,
unsupported requirements, and judge inconsistency must be distinguished.
The audit corrects identified problems but does not independently
validate every judgment or quantify accuracy gains over holistic
evaluation. Inter-rater agreement and error rates cannot be inferred
from this corrective audit.

### B.4 Diagnostic Scope and Statistical Conventions

The audit covers 15,214 model–task and 194,836 item records over 2,740
tasks; 262 inapplicable items are excluded. Single/text analyses include
Qwen-Image-Edit-2511, FireRed-Image-Edit-1.1,

JoyAI-Image-Edit. Multi-reference comparisons use Qwen, FireRed, GPT,
and Seedream. High-scoring outputs have Overall $`\geq 4`$; severe items
score $`\leq 2`$. A side passes only if every applicable item scores

contributes once per diagnostic event. For text editing, the six models

both the text and image sides. Proportion intervals use 2,000
source-image-cluster bootstrap replicates; multi-reference clustering

Diagnostic analyses use the item-level judgments, dimensional scores,
and subset-specific Overall defined in Section

## Appendix C Agentic Diagnosis and Data Construction

### C.1 Harness and Resource Contracts

The canonical registry exposes 68 tools (aliases excluded): benchmarking

LLM for the Diagnosis and Construction Agents, supporting planning and

delegation level by default. Each child has isolated context and a tool

evaluation records, reports, datasets, training jobs, and checkpoints.
Visual evidence is stored separately from original judgments and can be

##### Compatibility with native benchmark feedback.

WeGenEvoStudio accepts benchmark-specific evaluation records through
adapters, with category scores supporting task prioritization and native
rationales or checklist items providing additional evidence when
available. The shared interface preserves each benchmark’s metric
definitions and score direction; it does not require external benchmarks

compatibility with an evaluation protocol from the granularity of the
evidence it provides.

### C.2 Diagnosis Agent: Evidence to Data Prescriptions

Figure

summarizes diagnosis. Adapters normalize category axes, score scales,

In our experiments, GEdit and ImgEdit guide task selection through
````

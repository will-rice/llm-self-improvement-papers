###### Abstract

Earth observation (EO) models are still built by hand. For each new
task, experts design a training recipe and tune it against a benchmark.
We show that this loop can be handed to an agent. Any verifiable task
can be cast as a harness and a verifier, where the harness defines what
the agent may use and the verifier defines what counts as success. Every
EO task can be made verifiable, through labeled benchmarks, independent
sensors, physical models or the repeated observation of the same place.
Given only these two components, a general-purpose coding agent builds
the EO model and improves it recursively on its own outputs. Settings
long treated as separate research problems, from learning with a handful
of labels to adapting across domains, become choices of harness and
verifier. On six EO benchmarks spanning optical, SAR and multispectral
time-series imagery, with outputs that range from land-cover maps and
ship instances to canopy heights and answers about large images,
agent-built models surpass the state of the art on every one, and on
land-cover mapping, they do so from 11% of the training labels. This
establishes a new paradigm for EO, in which people design harnesses and
verifiers and agents build the models, so EO models improve as agents
do.¹¹ 1 This is an ongoing project. We are scaling up the experiments to
a large number of EO tasks and agents and will update the paper with the
results.

## 1 Introduction

Earth observation (EO) models are still built by hand. Each new sensor,
region or task starts another round of engineering, in which experts
choose a backbone, design a training recipe and tune it against a
benchmark. Foundation models have raised the starting point of this
cycle without removing it Cong et al. (2022); Reed et al. (2023); Guo et
al. (2024); Xiong et al. (2024); Jakubik et al. (2025); Siméoni et al.
(2026), and on PANGAEA, they do not consistently outperform a supervised
UNet Marsocci et al. (2026); Xiao et al. (2025). Across the many
sensors, regions and label taxonomies of EO, expert engineering time
decides how many tasks receive a strong model. The cost is highest where
EO matters most. When a disaster strikes, a new satellite comes online,
or a wildfire, flood or forest loss must be mapped in a new region, a
strong model is needed within days, yet expert engineering takes weeks
to months and many agencies and countries have no machine learning team
at all.

Meanwhile, AI has advanced fastest wherever success can be checked
automatically. Verifiers drive progress in mathematical reasoning Cobbe
et al. (2021); Guo et al. (2025) and program discovery Romera-Paredes et
al. (2024); Novikov et al. (2025), and coding agents such as Claude Code
and Codex now write, run and debug training code for hours, earning
medals in machine learning competitions Jiang et al. (2025); Chan et al.
(2025) and improving training scripts against a validation metric
Karpathy (2026). EO agents have so far orchestrated pretrained models
and tools Singh et al. (2024b); Liu et al. (2024); Feng et al. (2026),
and recent agentic research loops refine existing fully supervised
baselines Kang et al. (2026); Lekavicius et al. (2026). No agent has yet
been handed a whole EO task, including its label budget, to build the
model itself.

Our premise is that any verifiable task can be cast as a harness and a
verifier. The harness defines what the agent may use, such as data,
pretrained checkpoints, libraries and compute, and the verifier defines
what counts as success by scoring the agent’s model (Fig. 1). Every EO
task can be made verifiable, and EO offers unusually many ways to do so.
A decade of community benchmarks scores models on labeled held-out data,
independent sensors and in-situ measurements check retrieved quantities,
physical models check consistency with the observations, and repeated
observation of the same place checks any prediction about its future.
Building an EO model then becomes optimization against a verifier, and
settings long treated as separate research problems become choices of
these two components. Learning from a handful of labels restricts the
labeled data in the harness, training-free adaptation removes trainable
parameters from it, label-budget search asks for the smallest training
set that passes a target score, and domain adaptation places the
verifier on a target domain. We call this formulation RSI4EO.

Given a harness and a verifier, a general-purpose coding agent builds
the model by itself. It writes training code, trains models, queries the
verifier and decides what to try next, offline and without human input.
The verifier is sealed and returns a score, and containerized execution
keeps the held-out data and the network out of the agent’s reach Harbor
Framework Team (2026); Merrill et al. (2026); Shi et al. (2026). The
agent improves the EO model recursively on its own outputs Yudkowsky
(2013); Zelikman et al. (2024) and designs this recursion itself. On
land-cover mapping with six labeled tiles, it runs eight generations of
self-training, each taught by the best pipeline the verifier has seen,
and ends by automating the loop with its own scripts. Because the two
components specify the task completely, any agent can receive it.

An agent equipped this way surpasses the state of the art on all six
benchmarks, each within 12 hours on four GB200 GPUs: land-cover mapping
Xia et al. (2023), building damage assessment from optical and SAR
imagery Chen et al. (2025), SAR ship instance segmentation Wei et al.
(2020), crop-type mapping from Sentinel-2 time series Sainte Fare Garnot
and Landrieu (2021), canopy height estimation from very-high-resolution
imagery Fogel et al. (2025), and question answering over large images
Liu et al. (2026a). On the last, the harness forbids any training, yet
the pipeline the agent designs around a frozen vision-language model
outperforms every published method, including models trained for
large-image question answering. On OpenEarthMap, six labeled tiles, 0.2%
of the training set, already yield a model that outperforms a UNet
trained on the full training set, and with 300 tiles selected without
labels, Claude-Opus-5.5 surpasses every published method trained on all
labels. From 600 tiles on, two agents with different working styles both
do so.

These results establish a new paradigm for EO, in which people design
harnesses and verifiers and agents build the models. A task’s harness
and verifier stay fixed while agents improve, so EO models improve as
agents do, without new human engineering (Table 3). The time from a new
need to a working model shrinks to the agent’s budget, and across 14
runs of GPT-6-Astra on the six benchmarks, the agent reaches half of its
total verifier improvement at a median of 0.8 hours (Fig. 4a). After a
disaster, responders can label a handful of image pairs from the
affected area, pose them as a task and receive a damage model within a
day. Wildfire, flood and deforestation mapping follow the same pattern,
and a newly launched satellite can have a model within a day of its
first labeled images. Above all, any agency or country that can define a
task can obtain a model at the level of the published state of the art,
whether or not it has a machine learning team. Research questions move
upstream to which verifiers capture a task faithfully and what a harness
must contain, and annotation moves from training sets to the held-out
sets behind the verifier, which every benchmark already labels. Our
contributions are threefold.

- •
  A Paradigm for Building EO Models. Any verifiable task becomes a
  harness and a verifier, which turns the many verification sources of
  EO into objectives that agents optimize directly and unifies learning
  settings as choices of these two components.
- •
  An Open EO Task Suite. We release the tasks of this paper as harness
  and verifier pairs that any agent can run in offline Harbor
  containers. They cover optical, SAR, optical–SAR and multispectral
  time-series imagery as well as natural-language questions, with
  pixel-level, instance-level, continuous and textual outputs, and
  include a training-free task.
- •
  Evidence across Tasks, Labels and Agents. Agent-built models surpass
  the state of the art on all six benchmarks, on LRS-GRO without
  training any parameter, already from 114 answered questions. On
  OpenEarthMap, two agents with different working styles both surpass
  it, one of them from 11% of the labels. With the task fixed, five
  agents all surpass the plain starter pipeline of the harness, and the
  EO model improves with the agent that builds it.

## 2 Related Work

#### Self-Improvement and Verifiers.

The idea of machines that design better machines goes back to Good
(1965) and was formalized in the Gödel machine Schmidhuber (2007).
Language models now realize parts of this program. STOP uses a language
model to improve the scaffold that calls it Zelikman et al. (2024),
Gödel Agent and the Darwin Gödel Machine rewrite their own agent code
Yin et al. (2025); Zhang et al. (2026a), and FunSearch and AlphaEvolve
evolve programs against automatic evaluators Romera-Paredes et al.
(2024); Novikov et al. (2025). Self-training lets a model improve on its
own outputs, from Noisy Student in vision Xie et al. (2020) to STaR and
ReST^(EM) in language Zelikman et al. (2022); Singh et al. (2024a) and
AlphaGo Zero in games Silver et al. (2017), and in RSI4EO, the agent
arrives at such loops for the EO model without being told to. Progress
in this line follows the availability of verifiers Sutton (2001); Wei
(2025), from trained verifiers for mathematics Cobbe et al. (2021);
Lightman et al. (2024) to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
Lambert et al. (2025); Guo et al. (2025), and recent surveys and
roadmaps chart the path toward recursive self-improvement in general AI
systems Gao et al. (2026a); Chen et al. (2026); Duan et al. (2026).
RSI4EO carries this verifier-driven view into a scientific domain, where
verifiers range from labeled benchmarks to the repeated observation of
the same place and the artifact that improves recursively is the EO
model.

#### Agents for Machine Learning Engineering.

AutoML has automated parts of model development for over a decade,
through hyperparameter optimization Feurer et al. (2015); Li et al.
(2018); Falkner et al. (2018); Akiba et al. (2019), neural architecture
search Zoph and Le (2017); Liu et al. (2019); Real et al. (2020) and
self-configuring pipelines such as nnU-Net Isensee et al. (2021), each
searching a space that people define. LLM agents widen this space to
arbitrary code. AIDE, The AI Scientist and autoresearch write and revise
training code against validation metrics Jiang et al. (2025); Lu et al.
(2026); Karpathy (2026), and MLAgentBench, MLE-bench and RE-Bench
measure such agents on engineering and research tasks Huang et al.
(2024); Chan et al. (2025); Wijk et al. (2025). Domain systems apply
these loops to medical image segmentation Gao et al. (2026b); Liu et al.
(2026b) and solar-panel mapping Lekavicius et al. (2026). RSI4EO differs
in how a task reaches the agent, as a containerized task in the format
of recent agent evaluation suites Merrill et al. (2026); Shi et al.
(2026). The harness and a sealed verifier specify it completely, so
label budgets and domain shift become part of the task definition and
validation data stay outside the agent’s reach, a design that
reward-hacking studies of frontier agents support Von Arx et al. (2025).

#### Earth Observation Models and Agents.

EO foundation models learn transferable representations from optical,
multispectral and SAR archives Cong et al. (2022); Reed et al. (2023);
Fuller et al. (2023); Jakubik et al. (2023); Guo et al. (2024); Xiong et
al. (2024); Tseng et al. (2025); Astruc et al. (2025); Jakubik et al.
(2025), and general-purpose backbones such as DINOv3 now ship
satellite-pretrained variants Oquab et al. (2024); Siméoni et al.
(2026), with GEO-Bench and PANGAEA evaluating them under fixed protocols
Lacoste et al. (2023); Marsocci et al. (2026). Label-scarce and
cross-domain settings have each produced dedicated methods, including
semi-supervised segmentation Yang et al. (2023); Yang et al. (2025); Lu
et al. (2023); Zhou et al. (2026) and unsupervised domain adaptation
Hoyer et al. (2022); Hoyer et al. (2023); Zhao et al. (2024), and
vision-language models extend EO to question answering over large images
Liu et al. (2026a); Zhang et al. (2026b). In RSI4EO, these models and
methods become ingredients that the agent selects, combines and trains.
LLM agents for EO have so far orchestrated fixed tools and pretrained
models Singh et al. (2024b); Liu et al. (2024); Feng et al. (2026); Xu
et al. (2026); Talemi et al. (2026), and OpenEarth-Agent extends them to
creating their own tools Zhao et al. (2026). RingMoClaw is a multi-agent
loop that retrains remote sensing models and improves fully supervised
baselines on four tasks Kang et al. (2026). RSI4EO hands an agent the
whole EO task, including its label budget and domain shift, and extends
agent-built models to label-scarce regimes.

## 3 Method

RSI4EO turns the construction of an EO model into a task that an agent
solves. Section 3.1 defines a task by its harness and verifier,
Sections 3.2 and 3.3 describe the two components as we instantiate them,
Section 3.4 describes how the agent improves the model on its own
outputs, and Section 3.5 shows how learning settings become choices of
the two components.

### 3.1 Tasks as a Harness and a Verifier

An EO task is a pair $`\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{V})`$. The
harness $`\mathcal{H}`$ fixes the resources the agent may use: labeled
data $`\mathcal{D}_{L}`$, unlabeled data $`\mathcal{D}_{U}`$, pretrained
checkpoints, software and a compute budget. The verifier $`\mathcal{V}`$
fixes what counts as success. The agent produces pipelines, where a
pipeline $`\pi`$ is a self-contained directory of code and trained
weights that maps input imagery to predictions. The verifier runs a
pipeline on held-out data $`(X_{V},Y_{V})`$ that the agent never sees
and returns its score

```math
s(\pi)=m\big(\pi(X_{V}),\,Y_{V}\big), \tag{1}
```

where $`m`$ is the task metric. Over its budget, the agent writes
pipelines $`\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K}`$, queries $`s(\pi_{k})`$ and decides
what to try next. When the agent stops or the budget ends, its final
pipeline $`\pi^{\star}`$ is evaluated once on a hidden test set with the
same metric, $`m(\pi^{\star}(X_{T}),Y_{T})`$.

The formulation leaves every modeling decision to the agent.
Architecture, losses, the use of unlabeled data, ensembling and
inference all belong to the pipeline the agent writes, and the verifier
is the only signal of success it receives. A task is fully specified by
its two components, so any agent can receive it, and changing either
component defines a new task.

### 3.2 Verifier

The verifier is sealed. It runs in a separate container that holds the
validation data, executes a submitted pipeline exactly as the final
grader will, and returns the score through a tool call. The agent never
receives validation images or labels. Because the verifier runs the
whole pipeline, it scores what will be graded, including preprocessing,
ensembling and test-time augmentation, and it exposes failures such as
missing outputs before the final evaluation. The agent may query it as
often as it likes.

EO is rich in sources for such verifiers. Labeled benchmarks provide
held-out sets, independent sensors and in-situ measurements check
retrieved quantities, physical models check consistency with the
observations, and repeated observation of the same place checks
predictions against what is observed later. Each source yields a
function that scores a pipeline without exposing its data to the agent.
Our experiments build each verifier from the benchmark’s own labels,
using the official validation split where one exists and is not needed
as the test set, and otherwise a portion of the training split held out
by region, scene or image.

### 3.3 Harness

The harness gives the agent what an EO researcher would start from. It
contains the labeled training data, an unlabeled pool where the task
provides one, pretrained encoders without decoders or task heads (DINOv3
ViT-L/16 pretrained on satellite imagery, DINOv2 ViT-L/14, ResNet-18 to
ResNet-101 and EfficientNet-B0 Siméoni et al. (2026); Oquab et al.
(2024); He et al. (2016); Tan and Le (2019)), the official source code
of the DINO models, the Python deep-learning and geospatial stack
together with QGIS, SAGA and GDAL, and a deliberately plain starter
pipeline, an EfficientNet-B0 encoder with an FPN decoder Lin et al.
(2017). The harness also adds the standard tools of each field, such as
detection libraries for HRSID and the Swin and VMamba backbones for
BRIGHT. The question-answering task instead provides only the
vision-language model Qwen3-VL-8B Bai et al. (2025a), and its starter
prompts this model directly. The agent has four GB200 GPUs and 12 hours,
and no single training run may exceed 4 hours. The submitted pipeline
must contain networks that the agent trained, except on the
training-free task, where no parameter may be updated at all and the
submission holds only code and text. The given labels may not be edited,
and the agent may annotate unlabeled data itself.

Each task runs in an offline Harbor container Harbor Framework Team
(2026); Merrill et al. (2026), where the agent works without network
access beyond its own model interface. When the agent stops or time runs
out, the submission directory is copied out and graded once on the
hidden test set under an inference budget of 4 hours, or 8 hours for the
9,734 questions of LRS-GRO. Nothing else the agent created survives, so
every model, weight and script the final pipeline needs must be inside
the submission.

### 3.4 Recursive Improvement

Within a task, the agent improves the EO model recursively on its own
outputs. We call each model that the agent trains from the outputs of
earlier models a generation. A generation $`f_{t}`$ leaves outputs
$`O_{t}`$ behind, including its predictions and class probabilities on
the unlabeled pool, its checkpoints, and its predictions on held-out
folds of the training data. The next generation is trained from these
outputs,

```math
f_{t+1}=\mathrm{Train}\big(\mathcal{H},\,O_{\leq t}\big), \tag{2}
```

and the verifier decides which generation is kept as the source of the
next one. Two loops therefore run together. In the outer loop, the agent
proposes pipelines and the verifier scores them. In the inner loop, each
kept model becomes material for its successor, so the model improves
along a lineage.

The agent designs this recursion itself. The task instruction permits
methods such as pseudo-labeling on the unlabeled pool but prescribes
none, and the agent chooses the mechanisms, their order and the depth of
the lineage from the verifier’s feedback. Across our runs, the
mechanisms fall into three families. At the data level, a teacher labels
the unlabeled pool with hard pseudo-labels or soft class probabilities
and a student trains on them, as in Noisy Student Xie et al. (2020) and
knowledge distillation Hinton et al. (2015). At the parameter level, new
models start from earlier checkpoints, average several checkpoints, or
grow from a smaller trained network. At the prediction level, the agent
trains models on its own predictions, such as calibrators fitted to
out-of-fold predictions and networks that refine its masks. Each
generation thus learns from the previous one, the pattern behind STaR
Zelikman et al. (2022) and AlphaGo Zero Silver et al. (2017), here
applied to the EO model the agent builds.

pool. On Open-Canopy, it keeps a geographically spread subset instead.

any training, so its labels serve only as examples and for local

Settings that EO research has treated as separate problems become
choices of the two components. Full supervision gives the harness all
training labels. Learning from few labels keeps a few labeled samples
per natural stratum of the data, such as a continent, a disaster event,
a scene, a satellite tile or a question type, chosen so that together
they cover every class, and moves the remaining training imagery to the
unlabeled pool (Table 1). On Open-Canopy, a geographically spread subset
takes the place of the strata. Training-free adaptation removes
trainable parameters from the harness, so the agent can only design how
a fixed foundation model is used. A label-budget search fixes a
label-free selection rule, ranks the training images with it, and asks
for the smallest prefix whose labels let the agent reach a target score.
Domain adaptation places the labeled data on a source domain and the
verifier on the target domain. In every case, the agent, the verifier
interface and the final evaluation stay the same.

This view also changes where annotation goes. Labels are needed for the
verifier and for the final evaluation, which every benchmark already
provides, while the training set can shrink to a handful of samples.
Validation labels reach the agent only through the scores the verifier
returns.

## 4 Experiments

The experiments establish three results. One agent, through one
interface, builds strong models across sensors, output types and label
regimes. These models exceed the plain pipeline that ships with the
harness and surpass the published state of the art on all six benchmarks
(Section 4.2). A few labeled samples already yield strong models, and on
LRS-GRO, a training-free pipeline surpasses the state of the art from
114 answered questions. On OpenEarthMap, Claude-Opus-5.5 does so from
11% of the training labels and GPT-6-Astra from 22% (Section 4.3). With
the harness and the verifier fixed, five agents all raise the six-label
starter, by up to 26.0 mIoU, and the model improves with the agent that
builds it (Section 4.4). Section 5 then examines the runs to show where
the accuracy comes from and that it carries over beyond the verifier.

#### Benchmarks and Tasks.

We instantiate RSI4EO on six EO benchmarks that differ in sensor, output
and metric (Table 1). OpenEarthMap Xia et al. (2023) maps eight
land-cover classes from sub-meter RGB imagery, BRIGHT Chen et al. (2025)
grades building damage from pre-event optical and post-event SAR pairs,
HRSID Wei et al. (2020) segments ship instances in SAR images, and
PASTIS Sainte Fare Garnot and Landrieu (2021) maps 18 crop types from
Sentinel-2 time series. Open-Canopy Fogel et al. (2025) regresses canopy
height from 1.5 m SPOT 6–7 imagery, and LRS-GRO Liu et al. (2026a) asks
questions about images of up to 27,328 pixels on a side, which the agent
answers with a frozen Qwen3-VL-8B without training any parameter. Each
benchmark yields a full-label task and a few-label task that keeps 0.2
to 4.3% of the training labels. On OpenEarthMap, we add two label-budget
tasks, in which the harness holds the first 300 or 600 tiles ranked
without labels by k-center on frozen DINOv3 features Sener and Savarese
(2018). They probe the smallest prefix that surpasses the previous state
of the art.

The hidden test set is the official test split of each benchmark except
OpenEarthMap, whose test labels are withheld. There, the official
validation split serves as the test set, the verifier holds 300 tiles
from the training split, and the 1,500 official test images join the
unlabeled pool of the six-label and full-label tasks. HRSID and LRS-GRO
have no official validation split. On HRSID, the verifier holds 20
complete scenes taken from the official training set. On LRS-GRO, it
holds a quarter of the training images.

#### Agents and Protocol.

The main agent is Codex with GPT-6-Astra, which runs every task. Claude
Code with Claude-Opus-5.5 runs the four OpenEarthMap tasks. On six-label
OpenEarthMap, Claude Code also runs Kimi-K3, Qwen3.8-Max and
Qwen3.8-27B. All agents use their highest reasoning effort and work for
at most 12 hours on four GB200 GPUs in an offline container
(Section 3.3). The verifier returns the inference time and the task
metric with its breakdown by class, object size, height range or
question type. Each task runs once per agent, and each agent score we
report comes from that run.

#### Reference Points.

We compare each agent-built model with the starter pipeline of the
harness and with published results under each benchmark’s standard
protocol (Table 2). We run the starter as provided to show what the
harness yields without an agent (Table 4).

### 4.2 State of the Art on Six Benchmarks

#### Comparison with Published Methods.

With full labels, the agent-built model exceeds the best published
result on all six benchmarks (Table 2). Its margin is 4.8 mask AP on
HRSID, 4.4 points of accuracy on LRS-GRO, 4.1 mIoU on OpenEarthMap, 2.7
mIoU on BRIGHT, 2.6 mIoU on PASTIS and 0.08 m of MAE on Open-Canopy,
more than the 0.05 m that separates the four previous best methods
there. The baselines include a multi-model ensemble on BRIGHT,
fine-tuned EO foundation models on PASTIS, methods with test-time
augmentation on OpenEarthMap and models trained for large-image question
answering on LRS-GRO. On LRS-GRO, the agent trains no parameter at all,
yet the pipeline it designs around the frozen Qwen3-VL-8B surpasses
every published method, including these trained models and the previous
best, GazeEarth Zhang et al. (2026b), which is itself training-free and
built on the same model. On the five other benchmarks, each model is a
system that the agent designed, trained and assembled from its own
networks within 12 hours. On HRSID, the agent reaches its score with 15%
fewer training images than the published methods and without a
pretrained detector. A run of this kind costs 100 to 170 US dollars of
model usage and at most 48 GPU hours (Section A.5).

#### Gains over the Starter.

Against the starter pipeline of the harness, the agent-built model gains
7.4 to 16.7 points with full labels and 6.6 to 36.2 points with few
labels, and on Open-Canopy, it lowers the error from 4.00 to 2.44 m
(Table 4). The gains are largest with few labels, 36.2 mIoU on PASTIS
and 26.0 on OpenEarthMap.

#### Few Labels.

On question answering, land-cover mapping and canopy height, models
built from 0.2 to 4.3% of the training labels already outperform
published methods (Table 2). On LRS-GRO, the training-free pipeline with
114 answered questions, six per question type, exceeds every published
method, by 3.6 points over GazeEarth, and reaches 99% of the full-label
accuracy. On OpenEarthMap, six labeled tiles, 0.2% of the training set,
yield 60.0 mIoU, above the 57.8 of UNet trained on the full training
set. On Open-Canopy, 1,000 labeled tiles, 1.5% of the training set,
reach 2.72 m MAE, a lower error than Scale-MAE, DeepLabv3 and the
CLIP-pretrained ViT-B trained on all 66,339 training tiles. On BRIGHT,
PASTIS and HRSID, 0.5 to 1.4% of the labels yield 69 to 73% of the
full-label score (Fig. 2a).

#### Label Budget.

On OpenEarthMap, a few hundred labeled tiles suffice to surpass the
state of the art (Fig. 2b). With 300 tiles, 11% of the training set, and
no unlabeled pool, Claude-Opus-5.5 already surpasses all 11 published
methods of Table 2, all of which train on 2,500 to 3,000 labeled tiles.
With 600 tiles, 22% of the training set, both agents surpass them and
reach 97% of their full-label scores. Most of the value of the training
labels thus lies in the first few hundred tiles. This supports the
annotation shift of Section 3.5, in which labels are spent first on the
held-out sets behind the verifier and the final evaluation.

#### Five Agents on One Task.

The label budget varies the harness, and Table 3 varies the agent with
the six-label task fixed. Every agent raises the starter, and four of
them add 17.9 to 26.0 points. The four stronger agents arrive
independently at the same recipe. Each pairs a pretrained ViT-L encoder
with a decoder of its own, self-trains on the unlabeled pool and adds
multi-scale test-time augmentation. Their scores differ in how far each
agent carries this recipe (Section A.4). Within the Qwen3.8 family,
Qwen3.8-Max builds a model 12.4 mIoU stronger than Qwen3.8-27B. Since
only the agent changes, the EO model improves directly with the agent
that builds it.

#### Two Working Styles.

GPT-6-Astra and Claude-Opus-5.5 reach their scores in different working
styles. With six tiles, GPT-6-Astra submits 342 pipelines to the
verifier along a deep self-training lineage, whereas Claude-Opus-5.5
submits 43 in fewer and larger steps. GPT-6-Astra leads with six tiles
and Claude-Opus-5.5 with 300 tiles, and from 600 tiles upward, their
scores lie within one point of each other (Fig. 2b).

## 5 Analysis

The trajectories record every command, message and verifier query of a
run. We use them to show that the agent improves the model recursively
on its own outputs without being told to (Section 5.1), that it takes
over the engineering that experts otherwise carry out (Section 5.2), and
that the score of a model selected against a sealed verifier carries
over to data the agent never accesses (Section 5.3).

pool, a student continues from the previous checkpoint on the six

### 5.1 Recursion in Practice

#### A Lineage from Six Labels.

The six-label OpenEarthMap run shows the recursion most clearly
(Fig. 3). The agent first uses the verifier to choose an encoder, then
labels all 4,194 unlabeled tiles with its best model and trains a
student on the six labeled tiles and this pseudo-labeled pool. This
student opens a lineage of eight generations. In each, the teacher is
the best pipeline the verifier has seen so far, and the student
continues from the previous generation’s checkpoint. The verifier score
rises with every generation, from 56.7 to 61.9 mIoU, and ends 9.0 points
above the best model that the agent trained on the six tiles alone. The
agent then automates this loop. It writes scripts that read the verifier
log, promote the best pipeline to the next teacher and start a new
generation when the teacher has improved. The scripts alone launch,
train and verify the last generation. The recursion thus extends from
the model to the procedure that improves it.

#### The Same Pattern across Tasks.

The few-label runs on OpenEarthMap, BRIGHT, HRSID and Open-Canopy all
arrive at teacher–student loops over the unlabeled pool, although no
instruction asks for them. GPT-6-Astra runs eight generations on
OpenEarthMap, ten on BRIGHT, five on HRSID and four on Open-Canopy. On
OpenEarthMap, Claude-Opus-5.5 runs four, and Kimi-K3 and Qwen3.8-Max
reach the same loop. The verifier decides both which generation is kept
and which change to the student carries into the next one. On BRIGHT,
the gains after the first two generations come from generations that
change the loss or architecture of the student (Section A.3).

#### Expert Steps within Hours.

Beyond the recursion, the agent makes the choices of backbone, training
recipe and inference design that experts otherwise make by hand
(Section 1), and it makes most of them early. Across 14 runs of
GPT-6-Astra, half of the final verifier gain is reached after a median
of 0.8 hours and 90% after 4.3 hours (Fig. 4a, Section A.5). Within
about 20 minutes of a run, the agent replaces the starter with the
pretrained encoder that the verifier favors and a decoder of its own,
which raises the verifier score by 16.1 mIoU over the starter on
six-label OpenEarthMap. It also debugs its pipelines. Early in its
full-label Open-Canopy run, it finds that the default image reader
treats the near-infrared band of the four-band tiles as an alpha channel
and premultiplies the RGB bands with it. It reasons about the task as
well. On few-label Open-Canopy, it infers that the direction of shadows
carries height information and therefore trains without rotations and
flips. On few-label LRS-GRO, it locates the region a question refers to,
crops it from the full-resolution image and adds retrieved examples,
which raises verifier accuracy by 4.4 points in a single step
(Section A.3).

### 5.3 Generalization beyond the Verifier

#### The Verifier Score Carries Over.

The agent selects among many candidates with the verifier, and the score
it optimizes carries over to the hidden test (Fig. 4b). Across the five
agents of Table 3, the verifier ranks the models exactly as the hidden
test does. The hidden-test score lies within 2.0 points of the verifier
score in every run on OpenEarthMap and LRS-GRO, within 0.2 points on
full-label PASTIS and within 0.07 m of the verifier’s MAE on Open-Canopy
(Table 6). On HRSID, the test score is even higher, by 3.4 and 3.5 AP.
Where the two scores differ, the difference follows the split more than
the selection. The official HRSID test images come from the same scenes
as the training images, whereas the verifier holds separate scenes. On
OpenEarthMap, the gap of every run is concentrated in the same two
classes, bareland and water, whether the agent screens candidates
locally or relies on the verifier alone. On BRIGHT, it is concentrated
in the destroyed class, which is rarer in the test split than in the
verifier. This agrees with studies of test-set reuse, which find little
overfitting from repeated model selection and attribute most of the drop
on new test data to a shift between the two sets Recht et al. (2019);
Roelofs et al. (2019).

## 6 Conclusion

We have shown that any verifiable EO task can be cast as a harness and a
verifier. Given these two components, general-purpose coding agents
build EO models that surpass the state of the art on six benchmarks,
including one on which they may not train at all, and on land-cover
mapping, they do so from 11% of the training labels. The formulation
separates what people specify from what agents build. The harness and
the verifier of a task stay fixed while the agents that solve it
improve, so every advance in agent capability turns into better EO
models without new human engineering. The time from a new disaster,
satellite or region to a working model then becomes the agent’s budget,
and any agency that can define a task can obtain a state-of-the-art
model. As AI capability continues to rise, we see this as the paradigm
in which EO models will be built, with human effort invested in the
harnesses and verifiers that define the tasks.

#### Local Validation.

Like an EO researcher, GPT-6-Astra builds a validation signal of its own
from the labels it holds (Table 6). It holds out whole scenes on HRSID
and whole events on BRIGHT, uses five folds on PASTIS and keeps a
development set of questions on LRS-GRO. It then screens candidates
locally before it submits them to the verifier. In the remaining runs,
it relies on the verifier alone. Claude-Opus-5.5 behaves in the same
way. It holds out 55 to 250 tiles on OpenEarthMap. With six tiles, it
notes that leave-one-tile-out validation is too expensive and relies on
the verifier.

### A.3 Steps and Self-Training Loops

The early steps of the runs fall into a few recurring kinds.

- •
  Choosing the Encoder. The first model of Section 5.2 also raises the
  verifier score by 7.3 mIoU on few-label BRIGHT and lowers the verifier
  MAE on few-label Open-Canopy from 3.90 to 3.02 m. The choice follows
  the task. The verifier favors DINOv2 on six-label OpenEarthMap and the
  satellite-pretrained DINOv3 on few-label BRIGHT and Open-Canopy.
- •
  Matching Training to the Labels. With a handful of labels, the agent
  learns from the verifier that brief training generalizes better than
  long training, and on few-label Open-Canopy, it fine-tunes only the
  last 12 of the 24 encoder blocks.
- •
  Debugging. In the full-label HRSID run, the agent finds that mixed
  precision shifts box coordinates by several pixels, and from a montage
  of its outputs, it traces misplaced masks to a half-pixel offset in
  mask refinement. Correcting the offset alone raises mask AP on its
  local hold-out from 56.4 to 60.7.
- •
  Task-Specific Structure. On BRIGHT with few labels, the agent
  separates the confidence that a pixel is a building from the
  confidence in its damage grade when it trains on pseudo-labels, which
  adds 2.2 points in a controlled comparison. A separate optical
  detector for building footprints adds a further 1.1 points. On
  few-label LRS-GRO, the localization step of Section 5.2 works on a
  2,048-pixel view and uses four retrieved examples, and the agent also
  routes each question by its type. Besides training without rotations
  and flips on few-label Open-Canopy, it enlarges the input so that each
  ViT patch covers 12 m instead of 24 m.

#### Loops across Tasks.

The self-training loops differ in what passes from one generation to the
next. On BRIGHT, the teacher is a three-stage pipeline of a building
detector, a damage network and a building-level classifier, and its
first two generations add about 2 and 1 points. On HRSID, the teacher is
a detector followed by a network that predicts each mask from a crop
around the detected box. On Open-Canopy, the agent weights the
pseudo-labels of 65,339 unlabeled tiles by the uncertainty of its
teacher.

### A.4 Five Agents on Six-Label OpenEarthMap

#### Setup.

Claude Code calls Kimi-K3, Qwen3.8-Max and Qwen3.8-27B through the
Anthropic-compatible interface of OpenRouter. Web search is disabled for
every agent. The task, container, data and grading are identical to
those of the two main agents.

#### The First Round of Pseudo-Labels.

In the runs of Kimi-K3 and Qwen3.8-Max, the first round of pseudo-labels
brings the largest verifier gain of the run apart from the first full
model.

#### The First Model Sets the Weak Classes.

From the first full model to the final one, the verifier IoU of water
rises by 14 to 20 points for each of the four stronger agents, so the
gap that the first model leaves on this class persists to the end. On
water, the first model of Claude-Opus-5.5 scores 68.2 and that of
Kimi-K3 46.4. On the hidden test set, Claude-Opus-5.5 leads Kimi-K3 by
1.3 points overall and by 17.7 points of IoU on water, while Kimi-K3
leads on road, tree, agriculture and rangeland.

#### Leaving the First Plateau.

Each of the four stronger agents reaches a first plateau within about
three hours, and the agents that change the form of their pseudo-labels
move furthest beyond it. GPT-6-Astra moves beyond it with further rounds
of self-training and soft-label distillation and gains another 3.8
points. Claude-Opus-5.5 diagnoses that confidence filtering removes thin
roads from its pseudo-labels. It replaces the offline pseudo-labels with
an online mean teacher and enlarges the training crops from 512 to 1,024
pixels, which add 1.1 and 1.2 points. Kimi-K3 and Qwen3.8-Max keep the
form of their offline pseudo-labels and add further rounds, test-time
augmentation and models of the same lineage, which raise their scores by
1.3 and 0.04 points.

#### Parallel Experiments.

GPT-6-Astra and Kimi-K3 run one experiment per GPU, and Claude-Opus-5.5
does so for most of its run. Kimi-K3 trains 20 networks in this way.
Qwen3.8-27B trains one network at a time across all four GPUs, and
Qwen3.8-Max does so for its first 4.6 hours.

#### Time to a First Working Model.

Qwen3.8-27B plans a pretrained ViT-L encoder followed by self-training,
but a class index shifted by one in its training labels goes unnoticed
for 3.2 hours, and its first working model reaches the verifier after
5.5 hours.

### A.5 Budget, Stopping and Cost

#### A Strong Model within Hours.

The full-label BRIGHT run reaches 60% of its final verifier gain within
its first 1.8 hours. In both LRS-GRO runs, the first change that the
agent verifies arrives within 40 minutes and delivers more than half of
the final verifier gain. The agents end 17 of the 18 runs in Table 6
before the time limit, at a median of 10.6 hours. The 12-hour budget is
therefore an upper bound, and a model close to the final one is
available within the first few hours, which is the time scale that rapid
response requires.

#### Cost.

The model-usage cost recorded in the trajectory logs is 100 to 170 US
dollars per run of GPT-6-Astra behind Table 2. The four GB200 GPUs and
the 12-hour budget of the harness cap the compute of a run at 48 GPU
hours.

### A.6 Designing Harnesses and Verifiers

#### Two Design Principles.

Under RSI4EO, research effort moves to the design of harnesses and
verifiers (Section 1), and the runs analyzed here suggest two principles
for it. A harness that records how samples group into scenes, events or
source images lets the agent build local validation sets like those of
Table 6. A verifier drawn from the same distribution as the test set
makes its score a direct estimate of the final one, and it can also
report the uncertainty of its scores, so that the agent can tell which
differences between candidates exceed that uncertainty.

###### Abstract

Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human values is imperative to
mitigate potential adverse effects resulting from their misuse. Drawing
from the sociological insight that acknowledging all parties’ concerns
is a key factor in shaping human values, this paper proposes a novel
direction to align LLMs by themselves: social scene simulation. To
achieve this, we present MATRIX, a novel social scene simulator that
emulates realistic scenes around a user’s input query, enabling the LLM
to take social consequences into account before responding. MATRIX
serves as a virtual rehearsal space, akin to a Monopolylogue, where the
LLM performs diverse roles related to the query and practice by itself.
To inject this alignment, we fine-tune the LLM with MATRIX-simulated
data, ensuring adherence to human values without compromising inference
speed. We theoretically show that the LLM with MATRIX outperforms
Constitutional AI under mild assumptions. Finally, extensive experiments
validate that our method outperforms over 10 baselines across 4
benchmarks. As evidenced by 875 user ratings, our tuned 13B-size LLM
exceeds GPT-4 in aligning with human values. See our project page at
[https://shuotang123.github.io/MATRIX](https://shuotang123.github.io/MATRIX).

## 1 Introduction

Recent years have witnessed the meteoric rise in success of large
language models (LLMs) ([OpenAI, 2023](#bib.bib39); [Touvron et al.,
2023](#bib.bib52)). As these highly capable large language models grow
increasingly powerful, it is imperative for humanity to ensure they are
aligned with human values ([Bengio et al., 2024](#bib.bib8); [Kang
et al., 2023](#bib.bib29); [Hazell, 2023](#bib.bib23)), preventing
various unpredictable negative consequences that could stem from their
misuse ([Anwar et al., 2024](#bib.bib3); [Yi et al., 2024](#bib.bib57);
[Barrett et al., 2023](#bib.bib7)). In response to this, a wide range of
industrial companies and academic institutions are dedicating
significant efforts to expedite research focused on value alignment in
LLMs ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib41); [Bai et al.,
2022b](#bib.bib6)).

Presently, methods for aligning LLMs typically depend on external
supervision, either from humans or other LLMs ([Ouyang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib41); [Lee et al., 2023](#bib.bib31)). As one of the most
representative approaches, reinforcement learning from human feedback
(RLHF) ([OpenAI, 2023](#bib.bib39); [Touvron et al., 2023](#bib.bib52))
relies on massive human-annotated preference data and a two-stage
training process (i.e., reward model and LLM training). This requires a
significant investment in both human labor and computational resources.
For example, OpenAI spent over 6 months employing RLHF to enhance
GPT-4’s alignment and safety before its public release ([OpenAI,
2023](#bib.bib38)). Some other methods could work without human
supervision; however, they typically necessitate oversight from more
advanced LLMs ([Xu et al., 2024](#bib.bib53); [Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib35)). This reliance, particularly on paid commercial APIs,
can lead to substantial costs. Additionally, such an approach inherently
limits the potential for reaching the capabilities of superhuman
models ([Burns et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)).

Without relying on extensive external resources, a new paradigm emerges
where LLMs can achieve value alignment by themselves, termed
self-alignment ([Sun et al., 2023](#bib.bib47); [Li et al.,
2024](#bib.bib33)). Current methods typically rely on human-defined
rules to promote appropriate responses. For example, ([Askell et al.,
2021](#bib.bib4); [Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib55); [Sun et al.,
2024](#bib.bib48)) design tailored prompts to direct LLMs towards being
harmless; ([Sun et al., 2023](#bib.bib47); [Lin et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34)) focus on in-context learning, providing some examples
that are aligned with social norms; and ([Bai et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6))
applies self-critique and revision guided by constitutional rules.
However, all these methods are notably constrained by rigid,
pre-determined human rules, which lack the necessary flexibility to
adapt to the diverse and complex nature of real-world user instructions.
This limitation hinders the universal applicability of such rule-based
alignment methods in all scenarios.

In this paper, we explore a brand-new direction to achieve
self-alignment of LLMs: social scene simulation. This approach stems
from the sociological understanding that human values are shaped through
thoroughly considering all involved participants’ concerns and the
consequences of actions ([Hall, 2007](#bib.bib22); [McKinley,
2015](#bib.bib37); [Soc, 2024](#bib.bib1); [Sym, 2024](#bib.bib2)). For
example, the perspective on nuclear technology would involve evaluating
the potential impact on energy supply, environmental sustainability, and
global security. Inspired by this concept of social intelligence, our
core idea is allowing the LLM to self-create a simulation environment
that mirrors real-world multi-party interactions and simulates the
social consequences for a user’s instruction. This process allows the
LLM to take these consequences into account before responding, therefore
self-aligning itself to behave in a socially-aware manner.

Following this spirit, we propose MATRIX, a social scene simulator that
allows the LLM to emulate realistic multi-party interactions and
consequences in response to users’ instructions. To facilitate
simulation, MATRIX leverages the LLM’s role-playing ability, akin to a
Monopolylogue, where a single performer embodies several characters.
MATRIX generates two types of roles for a user’s instruction: living
agents, each with unique personalities, and non-living objects. Every
role, driven by the same LLM, delivers behavior descriptions that
represent the ego interests and concerns. To maintain the order of
interactions in MATRIX, we design a social modulator, also powered by
the LLM, which integrates world rules and governs communication
protocols for all roles. These designs allow the social scenes in MATRIX
to progress natually until their conclusion. Throughout the simulation,
the social scenes in MATRIX show a series of consequences triggered by
the given instruction. Concurrently, the social modulator records the
textual interactions of the roles and eventually summarizes them as
MATRIX’s final output. This textual summary can then be employed to
prompt the LLM towards socially-aligned behavior.

The key to MATRIX’s effectiveness does not lie in creating new knowledge
out of nothing, but rather in activating the knowledge about societal
norms already inherent in LLMs. Essentially, MATRIX serves as a virtual
rehearsal space, where the LLM can practice by itself and evaluate
different responses in the social scenes related to the user’s
instruction, instead of seeking guidance from aligned LLMs as in ([Liu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)). Unlike previous self-alignment methods ([Bai
et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6); [Askell et al., 2021](#bib.bib4)) that inform
the LLMs with pre-defined human rules, our method enables the LLM to
gain a more empathetic understanding of human values via simulation,
leading to socially aligned responses.

However, the simulation processes in MATRIX could be time-consuming
during inference. To ensure practicality, we further fine-tune the LLM
using the simulation data generated by MATRIX. Through the standard
supervised fine-tuning, the original LLM can be aligned to a
socially-aligned one. The entire training process does not involve any
external supervision and the MATRIX-tuned LLM maintains the same
inference speed as the original one.

To understand the effectiveness of MATRIX more fundamentally, we provide
a theoretical analysis and show that MATRIX enhances LLM’s
self-alignment by generating more instruction-specific critiques than
the human-predefined ones as in Constitutional AI ([Bai et al.,
2022b](#bib.bib6)). Experimentally, we comprehensively demonstrate the
effectiveness of MATRIX on diverse benchmarks evaluated by both
GPT-4 ([OpenAI, 2023](#bib.bib39)) and humans. First, we compare the LLM
with MATRIX with 7 inference-time baselines, showing that our method
consistently and significantly outperforms all baselines. Second, we
compare the tuned LLM with 8 representative and recent training-time
baselines including RLcd ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib55)) and Mistake
analysis ([Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib11)). Results show that the tuned
LLM can achieve significantly and consistently better value alignment
without sacrificing its general capability. Remarkably, 875 human
ratings shows that for the first time, a tuned 13B-size LLM
(Wizard ([TheBloke, 2024a](#bib.bib50))) can achieve better value
alignment than GPT-4.

## 2 Proposed Self-Alignment System

We present a self-alignment system for transforming an unaligned LLM
into one adhering to societal norms, independently of external
resources. As shown in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation"),
the system involves two processes: self-generation of consequence-aware
responses and self-tuning using these responses.

Self-generating consequence-aware responses (LLM with MATRIX). Given an
instruction, we guide the unaligned LLM to produce consequence-aware
responses by allowing it to learn from the simulated interactions of its
response. To achieve this, we design MATRIX, a social scene simulator
that creates an interactive environment based on a user’s instruction.
This setup, similar to a Monopolylogue, leverages the LLM’s role-playing
capability to generate textual interactions among various social roles
within the scenario. Through this simulation, the LLM observes the
reactions and feedback to its initial response, gaining an understanding
of the social acceptability and impact of its response. This process
enables the LLM to self-critique and refine its initial response to a
socially responsible one accordingly; see the design details of MATRIX
in
Section [3](#S3 "3 MATRIX: Social Scene Simulator ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation").

The workflow of this self-generating process includes three steps: i)
given a user’s instruction, the unaligned model is prompted to generate
an initial response; ii) given the instruction and the initial response,
MATRIX constructs a social scene that corresponds to the given
instruction and outputs the resulting social consequences in textual
form; and iii) based on the consequences, the unaligned model generates
an instruction-specific critique and then revises its initial response,
producing a consequence-aware response.

These steps ensure that even if the LLM’s initial response to a harmful
instruction is inappropriate, it discerns the negative social influence
through MATRIX and accordingly modifies its response. This mimics the
human way of learning and integrating societal norms, thus enhancing the
LLM’s understanding of human societal values.

Self-tuning LLMs via supervised fine-tuning (MATRIX-tuned LLM). The
self-generating process helps an unaligned LLM provide socially aware
responses but can be time-intensive due to simulation and response
revision. To streamline this, we use instruction-response pairs from the
self-generating phase and dialogues from MATRIX simulations to create a
dataset for Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). This dataset helps fine-tune
the LLM, enabling it to directly generate socially and ethically aligned
responses.

To summarize, our self-alignment system offers three key advantages: i)
independence from external resources, enhancing cost-effectiveness; ii)
ability to make LLMs aware of social consequences, leading to more
responsible responses; iii) efficient alignment through SFT, easy to
implement and optimize. The MATRIX-tuned LLM maintains the speed of the
original LLM without additional overhead.

## 3 MATRIX: Social Scene Simulator

This section introduces the details of our social scene simulator,
MATRIX. As shown in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation"),
it operates by taking an instruction-response pair as input and
outputting the textual consequences of the simulation. Similar to a
Monopolylogue, MATRIX integrates multi-role playing, building upon two
key elements: social roles and a social modulator.

### 3.1 Social Roles

Social roles represent the ensemble of participants within the social
scene, comprising both agents and objects, each empowered by the
role-playing ability of the same LLM. All roles are created by prompting
the LLM to conceive relevant roles based on user’s instruction and the
initial response.

Agents. MATRIX’s agents mimic real-world entities with unique characters
and personalities, enabled by the same LLM. They interact within the
simulation by generating textual actions in response to observations;
refer to the top of
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1 Social Roles ‣ 3 MATRIX: Social Scene Simulator ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation").
We differentiate the agents into two distinct categories: the user agent
and reactive agents. The user agent is designed to emulate the user
issuing the instruction and executing the initial response. Reactive
agents represent individuals within the relevant social scene. This
structure aims to highlight the social influence that emanates from the
initial response executed by the user agent.

Objects. Objects in MATRIX, unlike agents, include a diverse array of
non-autonomous elements, such as calculators, apples and banks. Each
object is characterized by a distinct textual state, which can be
modified by the actions of agents. This modification is achieved by
prompting the LLM to update the object’s state. In turn, an object can
influence agents’ actions when introduced to their observations.

### 3.2 Social Modulator

Simulating agents and objects concurrently can result in two issues: i)
inconsistent actions, including those that exceed agents’ capabilities
or conflict with actions from others; and ii) excessive transparency in
communication, where direct interactions between agents hinder the
emergence of complex behaviors like deception or confidentiality.

Addressing these, we introduce a social modulator, functioning as a
central server to collect and disseminate objects’ states and agents’
actions. Driven by the same LLM used for the roles, the modulator is
equipped with a memory system, together with two pivotal functions:
action feasibility determination and message distribution.

Memory system. The modulator is equipped with a textual memory,
documenting interactions for summarization. After verifying the
feasibility of each action, the action’s textual description with its
critique are logged in the memory.

Action feasibility determination. This function ensures that actions
within the simulation adhere to real-world rules, including physical
laws and logical constraints. Instead of relying on an external engine,
which is impractical due to the myriad potential actions, the modulator
uses the LLM itself to evaluate action feasibility based on its common
sense of real world. As depicted in
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1 Social Roles ‣ 3 MATRIX: Social Scene Simulator ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation"),
it assesses not only individual action feasibility but also its
consistency with past actions and overall logic recorded in its memory.
Infeasible actions are rejected, preventing unrealistic outcomes.
Feasible actions are further analyzed to determine their impact on
objects, facilitating agent-object interactions.

Message distribution. The modulator manages communication within the
simulation by contextually distributing actions and objects, creating a
logical and adaptable communication framework. It selects which agents
receive information about specific actions and observable objects based
on their perceptual abilities and relevance; see
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1 Social Roles ‣ 3 MATRIX: Social Scene Simulator ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation").
Agents are informed about actions only if necessary for their response.
Conversely, information about irrelevant or imperceptible actions is
withheld.

Overall, the design of the modulator ensures an authentic simulation
with a non-predefined action space and order and a flexible
communication structure.

### 3.3 Social Scene Simulation

The social scene is generated by the following stages:

Initialization. Given the instruction and response, the LLM is firstly
prompted to generate social roles, including i) user and reactive agents
with distinct personalities; ii) objects with specific attributes; and
iii) a modulator, with an empty memory. Subsequently, the response is
deconstructed into a series of steps. This series forms the action
sequence for the user agent, who carries out the response.

Execution. The simulation starts with the user agent’s first action.
Each agent-generated action is relayed to the modulator for feasibility
checks against its memory. Feasible actions are then examined for their
impact on object states. After each feasible action, the modulator
generates a critique to reflect the potential harm, and records it
together with the action’s description into the memory. The modulator
then distributes relevant information to appropriate agents. Reactive
agents interact according to their characteristics and available
objects, while the user agent progresses with subsequent actions. This
cycle of observation, reaction, and modulation continues, propelling the
simulation forward. The total feasible actions is defined as the
interaction number, reflecting the complexity of the simulation.

Termination. The simulation concludes in two scenarios: First, it
reaches a state of convergence when agents no longer generate actions,
indicating a natural end to the narrative. Second, the social modulator
may prematurely terminate the simulation if an action from the user
agent significantly deviates from the established simulation logic. This
termination aids in identifying and understanding the reasons behind the
infeasibility of the initial response.

Post-simulation, the modulator summarizes its memory, capturing the
textual consequence from the simulation. This summary serves as the
final output, providing the LLM with insights into the social
implications of its responses. This enables the LLM to conduct
self-critique and refine its responses for enhanced social awareness and
alignment; see a complete simulation example in
Appendix [18](#A3.T18 "Table 18 ‣ Appendix C Prompts and Examples ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation").

### 3.4 Discussions

Rationality of MATRIX in facilitating self-alignment. The key to
MATRIX’s effectiveness does not lie in creating new knowledge and
capabilities out of nothing, but rather in activating the knowledge
about societal norms already inherent in LLMs. While LLMs acquire
extensive societal norms and human values during pretraining, this
information often remains fragmented and underutilized due to a lack of
focused integration, limiting their inferential capabilities. MATRIX
addresses this by leveraging role-playing in social scene simulations,
enabling LLMs to experience and interpret social feedback from their
actions. This process helps LLMs distinguish between socially beneficial
and detrimental behaviors, enhancing their potential and achieving
self-alignment. Supporting evidence from studies suggests that role
designation in LLMs can significantly improve their efficiency in
various applications, including tool use and coding tasks ([Li et al.,
2023](#bib.bib32); [Salewski et al., 2023](#bib.bib46); [Qian et al.,
2023](#bib.bib44)).

Comparison with existing works. MATRIX enhances LLM self-alignment via a
self-critique process, prompting LLMs to revise their responses after
evaluating self-inferred social consequences. This method, while sharing
common ground with other self-critique approaches, is notably distinct
from two prevalent methods in the field. First, unlike rule-based
systems such as Constitutional AI ([Bai et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6)),
which relies on abstract rules challenging for LLMs to apply
contextually, MATRIX evaluates the potential harm of responses directly.
This enables more precise, instruction-specific critiques, effectively
guiding LLMs towards generating socially aligned responses. Second, LLM
Debate ([Du et al., 2023](#bib.bib17)) prioritizes consistency in
reasoning tasks, allocating every role the uniform task of responding to
the user’s inquiry. However, when confronted with complex reasoning
questions, all roles fail, causing the debate futile. In comparison,
MATRIX highlights multi-party interactions and the consequences of
actions in the alignment task, with each role representing a stakeholder
in the user’s query. Through empathetic role-play, MATRIX steers LLMs
towards deeper comprehension, enabling the formulation of socially
aligned responses to challenging questions.

Besides, our approach to LLM alignment through social feedback is also
distinct from methods like Stable Alignment ([Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib35)) which seeks guidance from multiple existing aligned
models, while our MATRIX aligns responses by engaging a single unaligned
LLM in multiple social roles to achieve positive social outcomes.

## 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX

This section provides a theoretical analysis, which shows that MATRIX
enhances LLM’s self-alignment by generating better critiques than the
human-predefined ones in Constitutional AI’s critique-revise
method ([Bai et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6)).

Let $`\mathcal{S}`$ be the set of token sequences,
$`\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}`$ be a LLM to be
aligned. Since both the critique-revise method and LLM with MATRIX are
critique-based methods, we provide a general mathematical form for
critique-based methods. Given a user’s instruction $`\boldsymbol{q}`$,
the output of a critique-based method driven by $`\mathcal{M}`$ is
$`\boldsymbol{o}=\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}(\boldsymbol{q})`$. Here the
critique-based method $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}(\cdot)`$ involves two
steps:

Initial step. Given a instruction $`\boldsymbol{q}`$, the model
$`\mathcal{M}`$ generates an initial response
$`\boldsymbol{r}=\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{q})`$ with the distribution
$`\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{r}\big|\boldsymbol{q})`$.

Revise step. Given a critiques $`\boldsymbol{c}`$, the model
$`\mathcal{M}`$ revises the initial response $`\boldsymbol{r}`$ and
outputs
$`\boldsymbol{o}=\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{c})`$
with the distribution
$`\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{o}\big|\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{c})`$.

The randomness of both steps comes from the token-generating process of
$`\mathcal{M}`$. Let $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm CR}`$ denote the
critique-revise method and $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm M}`$ be the
LLM with MATRIX. Both methods share the same initial step. For the
revising step, $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm CR}`$ uses the fixed
human predefined critique $`\boldsymbol{c}^{\rm CR}`$; while
$`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm M}`$ uses the MATRIX-generated critique
$`\boldsymbol{c}_{[1:n]}`$, which is the collection of critiques across
all $`n`$ interactions, adapting to the user’s query.

Let $`\mathcal{O}^{+}\subseteq\mathcal{S}`$ be the harmless and helpful
token sequences, serving as the alignment target. To enable comparsion,
we define a partial order $`\succeq`$ between two method
$`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{1}`$ and $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}`$.

$`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{1}`$ is defined to be better than
$`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}`$
($`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{1}\succeq_{\mathcal{O}^{+}}\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{2}`$),
when $`\forall\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{S}`$,

$`\boldsymbol{r}`$. Then we could describe the critique space as a chain

The set $`\mathcal{C}_{(\eta,\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r})}`$ includes
critiques with effectiveness below $`\eta`$. The $`\eta`$-efficient
complement set
$`\mathcal{S}/\mathcal{C}_{(\eta,\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r})}`$
describes critiques that could generate aligned output with probability
larger than $`\eta`$. To capture the performance of a critical
$`\boldsymbol{c}`$ for any instruction, we define its maximum
effectiveness.

The maximum effectiveness of a critique $`\boldsymbol{c}`$ is defined as

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

Evaluation datasets. We adopt 4 representative datasets: HH-RLHF ([Bai
et al., 2022a](#bib.bib5)) with both helpful and harmful instructions;
PKU-SafeRLHF ([Ji et al., 2023](#bib.bib27)), covering harmful
instructions across 14 categories such as insults and privacy;
AdvBench ([Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib62)), covering harmful instructions
from 5 topics such as disinformation and toxic; and HarmfulQA ([Bhardwaj
& Poria, 2023](#bib.bib9)), covering harmful instructions from 10 topics
such as social sciences and culture. We also evaluate the tuned model’s
general abilities on Vicuna-Bench and MT-Bench ([Zheng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib60)).

Models. For the base model, we use Wizard-Vicuna-Uncensored
7/13/30B ([TheBloke, 2024b](#bib.bib51); [TheBloke, 2024a](#bib.bib50);
[Cognitivecomputations, 2024](#bib.bib14); [Ye et al.,
2024](#bib.bib56)) (unaligned). For training, we employ QLoRA ([Dettmers
et al., 2023](#bib.bib16); [Hu et al., 2021](#bib.bib25)) for 3 epochs.
For our SFT step, we use 6K helpful and harmful training data from
HH-RLHF dataset, respectively; For simulation, we set up the maximum of
roles 4, with max interactions 12; see more experiments on
Alpaca ([Taori et al., 2023](#bib.bib49)), Mistral ([Jiang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib28)), and text-davinci-003 in
Appendix [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Experiments on other models ‣ Appendix B Experiments ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation").

Baselines. We compare 11 baselines in total. For evaluating the LLM with
MATRIX, we compare with inference-time alignment methods including
Self-Alignment ([Sun et al., 2023](#bib.bib47)), Context
Distillation ([Askell et al., 2021](#bib.bib4)), Critique-Revise ([Bai
et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6)), RAIN ([Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib33)) and LLM
Debate ([Du et al., 2023](#bib.bib17)). For evaluating MATRIX-tuned LLM,
we additionally compare with training-time alignment methods including
Mistake-Analysis ([Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib11)), Stable
Alignment ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)), RLcd ([Yang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib55)) and RLAIF ([Bai et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6)). We also

## 6 Related Work

LLM alignment. Aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human values
is essential to minimize potential negative social impacts. RLHF
(Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) ([Ouyang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib41)) optimizes LLMs using reward models based on human
preferences, while methods like DPO ([Rafailov et al.,
2023](#bib.bib45)) and RRHF ([Yuan et al., 2023](#bib.bib58)) are
proposed to alleviate its training instability. Beyond gathering human
preferences, strategies for acquiring data from advanced LLMs ([Xu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib53); [Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)) have been
developed. For example, Stable Alignment ([Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib35)) employs GPT feedback for response rating. Researchers
have also explored self-aligning LLMs, including self-critique based on
social rules ([Bai et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6); [Lee et al.,
2023](#bib.bib31)), harmless attributes ([Yang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib55)), learning from harmless examples ([Sun et al.,
2023](#bib.bib47); [Sun et al., 2024](#bib.bib48); [Lin et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34); [Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib33)), and mistake
analysis ([Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib11)). While these methods may
struggle to apply predefined social rules to specific scenarios, our
approach generates scenario-specific critiques through social impact
simulations. This allows the LLM to recognize and correct the
harmfulness of its responses, leading to more socially aligned outputs.

LLM agent simulation. LLM-driven multi-agent simulations have been
utilized for both boosting task efficiency ([Chen et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib12)) and exploring emergent behaviors ([Gao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib20); [Zhou et al., 2023](#bib.bib61)). Approaches to
enable multi-agent collaboration include developing specific prompting
methods ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib32); [Qian et al.,
2023](#bib.bib44)), facilitating debate processes ([Du et al.,
2023](#bib.bib17)), optimizing communication structures ([Chen et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib12); [Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib36)), and integrating
operational procedures ([Hong et al., 2024](#bib.bib24)). For emergent
behaviors, Generative Agents ([Park et al., 2023](#bib.bib42)) proposes
the architecture of LLM agents, allowing their interaction within a
simulated environment. Additionally, research has delved into strategic
behaviors ([Xu et al., 2023](#bib.bib54)), toxicity and bias ([Deshpande
et al., 2023](#bib.bib15); [Gupta et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)),
collaboration mechanisms ([Zhang et al., 2023](#bib.bib59); [Fan et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib19); [Fan et al., 2024a](#bib.bib18)), and international
conflicts ([Hua et al., 2023](#bib.bib26)). While these methods simulate
fixed scenarios like towns or software tasks, we focus on authentically
simulating any given instruction scenario, which requires action
consistency and adaptable communication among agents.

## 7 Conclusions

This paper explores on the self-alignment of LLMs via simulating social
scenes. The proposed MATRIX can simulate realistic social interactions
and consequences, fostering the development of consequence-aware
responses. Fine-tuning on MATRIX-generated data ensures LLM’s value
alignment while maintaining its inference efficiency. Extensive
experiments validate that our method exhibits superior performances in
value alignment against 10 baselines on 4 benchmarks. Remarkably, for
the first time, in GPT-4 evaluation, our tuned 13B-size LLM can
outperform ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-Turbo) across all 4 benchmarks; and 875
human ratings shows that this model can even surpass GPT-4.

Limitations. This work only explores the effective of MATRIX in value
alignment. We expect that MATRIX could serve as a general incubator for
more comprehensive self-improvement of LLMs, when each role can use
tools.

### A.2 Analysis for Assumption [4.4](#S4.Thmtheorem4 "Assumption 4.4. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation").

Here we provide a detailed analysis against our assumptions shown in
[4.4](#S4.Thmtheorem4 "Assumption 4.4. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation").

Collective
advantage ([4](#S4.E4 "Equation 4 ‣ Assumption 4.4. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")).
This assumption claims that for $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm M}`$,
using all the generated critiques to revise the initial response will
outperform using any single generated critique. This assumption is
fairly intuitive since each critique $`\boldsymbol{c}_{i}`$ is generated
against a single interaction $`\boldsymbol{i}_{j}`$ denoted as
$`\boldsymbol{c}_{j}=\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{i}_{j},\boldsymbol{q})`$,
hence lacks specificity to the entire instruction and answer, often
one-sided and loses effectiveness. By combining all critiques of a
simulated event to revise the initial response, it can provide the LLM
with the most comprehensive feedback and analysis, enhancing the
effectiveness of the revised output. This assumption is also verified by
our ablation experiments. In
Figure [7](#S5.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 5.3 Evaluation of MATRIX-Tuned LLM ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation"),
we compare the alignment performance of MATRIX with different number of
interactions. The result shows that: 1) Using multiple critiques will
outperform using a single critique. The win rate of using more than 4
interaction-generated critiques is larger than 70% while using a single
interaction-generated critique is marginally larger than 50%. 2) The
alignment performance of MATRIX monotonically increases with the number
of critiques. Both parts verify the collective advantage of MATRIX shown
in
([4](#S4.E4 "Equation 4 ‣ Assumption 4.4. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")).

Stable critique
generating ([5](#S4.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ Assumption 4.4. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")).
This assumption states that $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm M}`$ will
not generate outlier critiques. According to the inequality shown in
([5](#S4.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ Assumption 4.4. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")),
it claims that on average each single critique will cause at most
$`\lambda`$ bits of error compared to collective critique. From our
observations of the simulation experiments, most simulations will not
generate outlier critiques.

Alignment
chance ([6](#S4.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ Assumption 4.4. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")).
This assumption is fundamental and trivial, it claims that for any
question $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm M}`$ will have a non-zero
probability of generating aligned model outputs. This property is
related to the alignment target $`\mathcal{O}^{+}`$. Our observations
indicate that achieving a non-zero probability of producing beneficial
and benign outputs is readily attainable. Our experiments on various
benchmarks
(Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.4 Discussions ‣ 3 MATRIX: Social Scene Simulator ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation"))
also validate that $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm M}`$ does generate
aligned outputs.

###### Lemma A.4.

Let $`\mathcal{M}`$ be an LLM where $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm M}`$

### A.7 Analysis and proof to [Theorem 4.5](#S4.Thmtheorem5 "Theorem 4.5. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")

Analysis to
[Theorem 4.5](#S4.Thmtheorem5 "Theorem 4.5. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation").
The maximum
effectiveness ([3](#S4.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ Definition 4.3. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation"))
$`\xi_{\rm CR}`$ of the critique used in
$`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm CR}`$ is relatively small, making the
condition $`\sqrt{\xi_{\rm CR}}<1-\sqrt{1-e^{-\lambda}}`$ easy to be
satisfied. It can be explained from 2 aspects. 1) Unclear cognitive
understanding of alignment goals. Human pre-defined rules and
suggestions are highly abstract and concise, which is not a clear
instruction for an unaligned model to understand. 2) Hard to balance
validity and generalizability. Since
$`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm CR}`$ aims to use a set of unified moral
codes to guide the LLM on various kinds of questions, to promote
generalizability, the critiques used should be concise and highly
summarized. However, this property hinders the revised response from
being helpful and question-specific, which is a fundamental conflict of
Constitutional AI. The key point of the MATRIX method lies in
automatically generating instruction-specific critiques. By utilizing
multi-agent social-scene simulation, MATRIX can expose potential harms
of the inter-agent interactions and provide additional information on
original response distribution. In the process of generating critiques
for each interaction, the LLM continually shifts roles and perspectives
to generate a set of the most comprehensive and objective critiques of
the instruction. The set of critiques that takes all the agents into
account will maximally align with social values

Then we provide our proof to
[Theorem 4.5](#S4.Thmtheorem5 "Theorem 4.5. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")
based on the assumption and lemmas.

###### Proof.

Since for LLM $`\mathcal{M}`$, it satisfies
Assumption [4.4](#S4.Thmtheorem4 "Assumption 4.4. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis of MATRIX ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")
with a valid $`\lambda`$ in

Lemma [A.6](#A1.Thmtheorem6 "Lemma A.6. ‣ A.3 Lemmas ‣ Appendix A Analysis and proofs ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation"),
we can find a $`\delta\in[0,1]`$ satisfies
$`\mathcal{P}\left(\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\frac{\eta}{\delta}-\delta}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r})\right)>0`$.
Then apply
Lemma [A.5](#A1.Thmtheorem5 "Lemma A.5. ‣ A.3 Lemmas ‣ Appendix A Analysis and proofs ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation"),
$`\mathcal{P}\left(\exists\boldsymbol{i}_{j},j\in[N],\boldsymbol{i}_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{\xi_{\rm CR}/\delta-\delta}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r})\right)=1`$,
hence $`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm M}`$ could at least generate an
interaction $`\boldsymbol{i}_{j},1\leq j\leq N`$ such that
$`\boldsymbol{i}_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{\xi_{CR}/\delta-\delta}(\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{r})`$.
Applying
Lemma [A.4](#A1.Thmtheorem4 "Lemma A.4. ‣ A.3 Lemmas ‣ Appendix A Analysis and proofs ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")
hence
$`\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm M}\succeq_{\mathcal{O}^{+}}\mathbf{T}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\rm CR}`$.
∎

### B.2 Baselines Implementation

- •
  LLM Debate ([Du et al., 2023](#bib.bib17)): The number of agents is
  set to 3; the debate process is conducted over 3 iterations.
- •
  Best-of-N Sampling: The base model generates 16 responses, as
  in ([Askell et al., 2021](#bib.bib4)); reward model ([OpenAssistant,
  2023](#bib.bib40)) is employed to select the one with highest reward.
- •
  Critique-Revise and RLAIF ([Bai et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6)): The number
  of criticizing and revising is set to 4. For Critique-Revise (H), we
  fine-tune the base model with its revised response to harmful
  instructions; For Critique-Revise (HH), we fine-tune the base model
  with its revised response to harmful and helpful instructions; We
  leverage DPO ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib45)) for RLAIF
  preference modeling.
- •
  Context Distillation and RLcd ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib55)): We
  leverage DPO ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib45)) for RLcd preference
  modeling.
- •
  RAIN ([Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib33)): Given the low inference speed
  (approximately hours for a single sample for 30B LLMs on an RTX3090),
  we limit generation to 10 samples for each dataset.
- •
  Mistake Analysis ([Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib11)): We employ the
  initial responses to helpful instructions for helpful data.
- •
  Stable Alignment ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)): Peer feedback and
  ratings are collected using the base model. The number of agents is
  set to 100; the interaction rounds are set to 500. Due to the
  unavailability of their training code, we select responses with the
  highest ratings for Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) training.

### B.6 Experiments on Time Consumption

Here we aim to analyze the time consumption among different methods. To
address this, we conducted an analysis comparing the efficiency and
output quality of LLMs with MATRIX, MATRIX-Tuned LLMs, and the RAIN
method ([Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib33)). Our evaluation focused on
averaging the time it takes for each method to process 10 samples from
the HH dataset using the Mistral-7B-instruct model. The findings from
Table [14](#A2.T14 "Table 14 ‣ B.6 Experiments on Time Consumption ‣ Appendix B Experiments ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation")
reveal two key insights:

i\) Inference Efficiency: Both LLM with MATRIX and MATRIX-Tuned LLM
demonstrate superior performance over RAIN in terms of inference
efficiency. This improved performance can be attributed to the fact that
RAIN’s methodology relies on utilizing harmless examples for
self-evaluation by the LLM, which in turn expands its input context and
complicates the inference process. Additionally, RAIN’s approach of
employing backtracking to search for suitable tokens further diminishes
its inference speed.

ii\) Cost-effective Fine-tuning: By fine-tuning LLMs with MATRIX, we
preserve the harmless generation characteristic of the LLM with MATRIX,
effectively transforming the LLM into an aligned version that incurs no
additional inference time cost. This process not only enhances the
model’s alignment with desired outcomes but does so without imposing
extra time penalties during inference. Note that MATRIX-Tuned LLM takes
a slightly longer time than the original LLM to output more
comprehensive responses.

### B.7 Experiments on Mitigating Bias

biases, we conducted an in-depth analysis using the Bias Benchmark for
QA ([Parrish et al., 2022](#bib.bib43)). This evaluation focused on the
model’s ability to select the correct, non-stereotypical answers in
contexts where ambiguities could lead to biased responses. A key
indicator of success in this endeavor is the model’s accuracy: higher
accuracy reflects a diminished propensity for choosing stereotypical
answers.

We evaluated the model’s extent of social bias, both before and after
MATRIX fine-tuning, with a focus on gender, age, and race.

unbiased answers, leading to a reduction in social stereotypes. This

## Appendix D Human Evaluation Details

Here we present the details of the human evaluation experiment. We
randomly sample 100 questions from the Safe-RLHF dataset with 14
categories of harmful questions. To reduce the randomness of personal
subjective evaluations and other interferences, we randomly recruit 35
volunteers. Each volunteer will be assigned 25 questions, thus ensuring
that the quality of the model’s response to each question is reviewed by
at least 8 people.

The review of the model’s quality is conducted on a simple webpage,
including the homepage and the specific scoring page. For the homepage
shown in
Figure [8](#A4.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ Appendix D Human Evaluation Details ‣ Self-Alignment of Large Language Models via Monopolylogue-based Social Scene Simulation"),
each volunteer needs to read the scoring principle (adopted
from ([Askell et al., 2021](#bib.bib4))) and refer to the usage policy
released by OpenAI to get familiar with common principles corresponding
to harmless model responses. Then volunteers are reminded to evaluate
the quality of the model as objectively as possible and to provide
choices that are as distinctive as possible. For the scoring page,
volunteers select a better-quality answer from two anonymous model
responses provided for a question. If they believe the quality of the
two answers is comparable, they click ”tie”.

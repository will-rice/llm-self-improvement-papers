# Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation

###### Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have become increasingly adopted in daily
applications, with deep research standing out as a particularly
important capability. Unlike traditional question-answering (QA) tasks,
deep research report generation lacks definitive ground-truth, making
reward design inherently unverifiable and limiting effective
reinforcement learning. Existing approaches mitigate this challenge with
LLM-as-a-judge and query-dependent evaluation rubrics, but they still
rely on static evaluators that cannot adapt their standards as the
solver improves, leading to insufficient and eventually saturated
optimization pressure. We address this limitation with a self-evolving
co-evolutionary training framework for deep research evaluation and
generation (SCORE), which tightly couples an evaluator and a solver in a
shared-parameter learning process. Rather than treating generation and
evaluation as isolated modules, we leverage their intrinsic connection
to enable joint improvement within a single shared-parameter model. To
restrict this process, we introduce a meta-harness, which dynamically
controls the evaluation environment based on solver performance,
encouraging valid evaluation dimensions and sufficiently deep evaluator
search. Extensive experiments on deep research benchmarks demonstrate
consistent improvement in report generation quality, showing that
co-evolving evaluation and generation is a promising direction for
training open-ended research agents.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Empirical evidence of the positive correlation between LLMs’
generation and evaluation capabilities.

Owing to their extraordinary capabilities, Large Language Models (LLMs)
have become deeply integrated into various complex domains, spanning
autonomous driving \[[48](#bib.bib28)\], software development
\[[9](#bib.bib29)\], multimedia generation \[[33](#bib.bib30),
[46](#bib.bib58), [54](#bib.bib59)\] and so on \[[49](#bib.bib56),
[25](#bib.bib57)\]. As the demand for comprehensive information
retrieval and synthesis grows, enhancing the deep research capabilities
of LLMs has attracted increasing attention from researchers. Several
search agents, such as GPT-Researcher \[[13](#bib.bib25)\] and
AgentCPM-Explore \[[2](#bib.bib26)\], have recently demonstrated
remarkable proficiency in these multi-hop research tasks. Furthermore,
previous works like Search-o1 \[[26](#bib.bib18)\] and SSP
\[[30](#bib.bib6)\] have shown that applying Reinforcement Learning (RL)
to these models can substantially enhance their reasoning capabilities
when integrated into agent, yielding significant performance
improvements on complex Question-Answering (QA) tasks.

Differ from traditional QA tasks, training models for open-ended report
generation presents two challenges. First, comprehensive reports lack a
definitive ground truth, making objective reward signals inherently
difficult to define \[[23](#bib.bib35), [12](#bib.bib36)\]. Since there
is no absolute standard for open-ended queries, relying on human
annotations for generated reports is prohibitively expensive, making
objective and scalable reward signals difficult to construct. Relying on
human annotations to cover these diverse paths is prohibitively
expensive and difficult to scale. Second, the evaluation criteria for a
high-quality report are multi-dimensional and query-related
\[[21](#bib.bib32), [10](#bib.bib34)\]. Single fixed evaluation metric
inevitably ignore crucial dimensions including factual correctness and
insight diversity for different questions. This dimensional omission
results in sparse training signals and fails to provide the fine-grained
credit assignment required for complex generation tasks.

To overcome these challenges in report generation, recent research
proposes several novel paradigms to provide more effective optimization
signals. WebThinker \[[27](#bib.bib10)\] and Step-DeepResearch
\[[16](#bib.bib22)\] have applied the LLM-as-a-judge
\[[57](#bib.bib27)\] to evaluate report quality during the training
process. AdaRubric \[[8](#bib.bib31)\] proposes a framework that
dynamically generates task-specific evaluation criteria based on the
input instructions. Mix-GRM \[[52](#bib.bib33)\] utilizes Reinforcement
Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) to automatically adapt between
breadth-first (B-CoT) and depth-first (D-CoT) evaluation strategies
according to distinct task demands. However, a fixed external evaluator
lacks the ability to dynamically adjust its evaluation standards, and
therefore fails to provide sustained optimization pressure as the solver
improves.

As empirically observed in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation"),
LLMs’ generation quality is positively correlated with its evaluation
proficiency. Building upon this insight, we hypothesize that generation
and evaluation abilities can be mutually reinforcing and should be
optimized within a unified, rather than decoupled, architecture. To this
end, we propose a self-play framework in which the policy model
iteratively generates and evaluate reports. By replacing a fixed
external judge with a dynamic evaluator that evolves alongside the
solver, our method maintains informative training pressure throughout
learning. Furthermore, a Meta-Harness \[[22](#bib.bib37)\] is applied to
stabilize this process by enforcing environmental constraints.
Empirically, this co-evolutionary paradigm brings substantial and
consistent improvements across diverse research generation tasks.

In a nutshell, our contributions are summarized as below:

- •
  We propose SCORE, an evaluator-solver self-play framework for training
  deep research agents with unverifiable rewards. This framework provide
  dynamic, multidimensional rewards to optimize the solver’s ability in
  open-ended report generation, constrained by a meta-harness.
- •
  We provide a theoretical analysis of SCORE under shared parameters.
  Our analysis clarifies the role of consistency in evaluator-side
  adaptation and characterizes the local optimization behavior of
  shared-parameter alternating updates.
- •
  We show that our method improves agent performance across multiple
  evaluation dimensions on deep research benchmarks, and provide a
  systematic analysis of how parameters affect training procedure under
  unverifiable rewards.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Deep Research

LLMs have exhibited strong deep research capabilities in recent agent
studies \[[59](#bib.bib53), [1](#bib.bib54)\]. Initial frameworks,
including Search-o1 \[[26](#bib.bib18)\], Search-R1
\[[20](#bib.bib16)\], and R1-Searcher \[[39](#bib.bib17)\], successfully
introduced explicit reasoning process into agentic search frameworks,
yielding substantial empirical improvement in multi-hop
question-answering (QA) tasks. Following these advancements, researchers
integrated diverse optimization algorithms into agentic training
pipelines. Specifically, WebThinker \[[27](#bib.bib10)\] utilizes
iterative Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) for deep research agents,
whereas DeepResearcher \[[58](#bib.bib19)\] adopts Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO). Diverging from these algorithmic enhancements,
SimpleDeepSearcher \[[40](#bib.bib20)\] argues that the quality of the
training data supersedes the specific training paradigm. Beyond QA
tasks, report generation tasks are inherently more complex, as they
involve open-ended, multi-faceted synthesis where establishing reliable
ground truth for automated evaluation remains a critical bottleneck. To
address this complexity, multi-agent frameworks like STORM and Co-STORM
leverage preliminary knowledge and human-in-the-loop interactions during
report generation \[[34](#bib.bib24), [19](#bib.bib23)\]. Later
approaches such as Step-DeepResearch and AgentCPM-Report
\[[16](#bib.bib22), [28](#bib.bib21)\] directly integrate model training
optimized for their specific agent frameworks.

### 2.2 Intrinsic Rewards and Self-Play

Traditional Self-play is widely applied to agentic studies as an
adversarial reinforcement learning approach \[[53](#bib.bib2)\].
AbsoluteZero, R-Zero, SeRL and SPIRAL, demonstrate the effectiveness of
self-evolution in tackling complex reasoning tasks under data scarcity
situation \[[55](#bib.bib3), [17](#bib.bib4), [29](#bib.bib5),
[11](#bib.bib9)\]. Recent frameworks, including Search Self-play, Dr.
Zero, and SPaR, demonstrate the substantial advantages of applying this
technique to search agents operating within multi-hop environments
\[[30](#bib.bib6), [50](#bib.bib7), [3](#bib.bib8)\]. Beyond adversarial
learning paradigms like proposer-solver self-play, alternative
approaches propose training solvers on filtered trajectories generated
by the models themselves, as demonstrated by frameworks such as
WebThinker, EvolveSearch, and EXSEARCH \[[27](#bib.bib10),
[51](#bib.bib11), [38](#bib.bib12)\]. Other notable efforts include
AceSearcher, which employs cooperative interaction between decomposer
and solver for self-improvement \[[45](#bib.bib13)\]. Moreover,
optimization strategies utilizing internal rewards provided by models
themselves have been adopted by researchers in frameworks such as
INTUITOR \[[56](#bib.bib14)\] and JEPO \[[41](#bib.bib15)\]. These
previous works demonstrate the efficacy of internal signals in enhancing
autonomous capability improvements. However, the unique characteristics
fo deep research task present a significant evaluation challenge. The
difficulty of reliably scoring open-domain, comprehensive reports makes
it highly challenging to design effective reward signals that resolve
the credit assignment issue.

## 3 Methodology

![Refer to caption](2606.04507v1/pipeline.png)

Figure 2: SCORE is controlled by Meta-Harness. The evaluator first

### 3.1 Problem Setting

We study _deep research report generation_, where the goal is to learn a
policy $`\pi(r\mid q,\mathcal{E}_{q})`$ over candidate reports $`r`$ for
a given query $`q`$ and environment $`\mathcal{E}_{q}`$. We assume that
report quality is governed by a latent functional
$`Q(r;q,\mathcal{E}_{q}),`$ and the ideal objective is to assign more
probability mass to reports with higher latent quality. Here,
$`q\in\mathcal{Q}`$ denotes a user query, $`r\in\mathcal{R}`$ denotes a
candidate long-form report, and $`\mathcal{E}_{q}`$ denotes the
query-specific evidence and evaluation environment.

Unlike QA-style tasks, $`Q`$ is inherently _multi-dimensional_ and
_query-dependent_: a report may succeed or fail along different axes,
such as factual support, evidence coverage, citation grounding,
contradiction handling, uncertainty calibration, and discourse
coherence. As a result, long-form report generation rarely admits a
single reliable verifier or a fixed scalar reward that is both
sufficiently informative and broadly applicable.

We therefore formulate deep research as a query-conditioned report
optimization problem with an evaluation bottleneck. In particular, the
central challenge in training deep research models is how to evaluate
long-form reports in a query-specific and stable manner under limited
supervision.

### 3.2 Overview of SCORE

To address this challenge, we propose SCORE, a retrieval-grounded and
externally guided evaluator-solver framework for deep research report
generation as shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation").
SCORE contains four components:

1.  1.  an external evidence environment that can be queried through search
        and retrieval tools,
2.  2.  a fixed external _meta-harness_ that shapes the query-specific
        evaluation environment,
3.  3.  an _evaluator_ that constructs query-conditioned rubrics and
        produces structured report assessments,
4.  4.  a _solver_ that interacts with the evidence environment, gathers
        supporting evidence, and generates reports under the resulting
        rubric.

A key design choice of SCORE is that the solver and evaluator are
implemented as two functionally distinct roles over a _shared underlying
actor_. This design is motivated by the substantial overlap between
report generation and report evaluation, both of which require query
understanding, evidence modeling, and discourse-level reasoning. We
empirically examine this choice in our ablations
Section [5.2](#S5.SS2.SSS0.Px2 "Module Ablation Study ‣ 5.2 Experimental Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation").

Training proceeds through sequential alternating updates on the shared
actor: the solver is optimized using evaluator-derived report rewards,
while the evaluator is optimized using rubric-level auxiliary signals.
Both updates are regularized by KL divergence against a reference policy
to limit excessive policy drift.

### 3.3 External Evidence and Query-Specific Environment

For each query $`q`$, SCORE interacts with an external evidence
environment through search and retrieval tools. We denote by

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       D_{q}^{(0)}=\mathcal{R}(q)
       ```                         |     |

the initial evidence obtained from this environment, where
$`\mathcal{R}`$ abstracts the retrieval interaction available to the
system. In practice, this evidence environment may include web search,
document retrieval, or other tool-mediated evidence sources.

Based on the initial evidence $`D_{q}^{(0)}`$, SCORE constructs a
query-specific evaluation environment through a fixed external
_meta-harness_:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \mathcal{E}_{q}=\mathrm{Harness}(q,D_{q}^{(0)},\mathcal{S}),
       ```                                                           |     |

where $`\mathcal{S}`$ denotes recent training statistics or historical
signals available to the harness. The resulting environment
$`\mathcal{E}_{q}`$ specifies the admissible evaluation space for the
current query, including candidate evaluation dimensions, structural
constraints on valid rubrics, process requirements for evidence
verification or comparison, and auxiliary task-specific guidance.

The meta-harness is external and non-trainable. It is implemented as a
fixed external controller and is not optimized by gradient descent
together with the actor. Its role is to shape the admissible evaluation
space in which evaluator-solver interaction takes place.

### 3.4 Evaluator and Solver

Given query $`q`$ and environment $`\mathcal{E}_{q}`$, the evaluator
samples a query-conditioned rubric

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       (H_{q},w_{q})\sim\pi_{e}(\cdot\mid q,\mathcal{E}_{q}),
       ```                                                     |     |

where $`H_{q}=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{k}\}`$ denotes the selected evaluation
dimensions and $`w_{q}`$ their corresponding weights.

Conditioned on the query, the current evidence state, and the
evaluator-selected rubric, the solver interacts with the external
evidence environment to gather supporting information and generate a
group of candidate reports

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       r_{1},\dots,r_{K}\sim\pi_{s}(\cdot\mid q,D_{q}^{s},H_{q},w_{q}),
       ```                                                               |     |

where $`D_{q}^{s}`$ denotes the evidence collected by the solver and
$`K`$ is the number of solver rollouts sampled for the same query during
training.

For assessment, the evaluator may perform constrained supplementary
retrieval or verification based on the query and the generated reports,
producing its own evidence context $`D_{q}^{e}`$. It then produces
structured judgments over candidate reports, including dimension-wise
scoring, evidence support checking, contradiction analysis, and citation
verification. These structured judgments are converted into solver-side
learning signals.

### 3.5 Shared Actor and Training Signals

SCORE implements the solver and evaluator as two roles over a shared
actor with parameters $`\theta`$. This design is motivated by the
observation that report generation and report evaluation rely on
overlapping latent capabilities, including query understanding, evidence
modeling, and discourse-level reasoning. Under this view, generation and
evaluation are not treated as fully separate functions, but as two
role-specific mappings induced from a shared underlying representation.
A shared actor therefore provides a common representational substrate
for both roles, allowing evaluator-side adaptation to influence the same
internal representations used by the solver. We empirically examine this
design choice in
Section [5.2](#S5.SS2.SSS0.Px2 "Module Ablation Study ‣ 5.2 Experimental Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation").

SCORE uses two distinct reward channels:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\underbrace{R_{s}(r;q,D_{q}^{e},H_{q},w_{q})}_{\text{solver-side report reward}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\underbrace{R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=C(\Gamma_{q})}_{\text{evaluator-side auxiliary reward}}.
``` |  |

The solver-side reward supervises report generation, while the
evaluator-side reward adapts the evaluator’s rubric-selection behavior.

#### Solver-side report reward.

For each generated report $`r`$, the evaluator produces a scalar report
reward

|     |                                   |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{s}(r;q,D_{q}^{e},H_{q},w_{q}),
       ```                                |     |

where $`D_{q}^{e}`$ denotes the evidence context used by the evaluator
for rubric-conditioned assessment, $`H_{q}=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{m}\}`$
denotes the selected evaluation dimensions, and
$`w_{q}=\{w_{1},\dots,w_{m}\}`$ denotes their corresponding weights.
This reward serves as the primary supervision signal for optimizing the
solver.

In our implementation, the abstract report reward is instantiated as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{s}(r)=a(r)\cdot\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}\,s_{j}(r;q,D_{q}^{e})\right),
``` |  |

where $`a(r)`$ denotes a report-level validity or accuracy score, and
$`s_{j}(r;q,D_{q}^{e})`$ denotes the evaluator score of report $`r`$ on
dimension $`h_{j}`$ under evaluator-side evidence context $`D_{q}^{e}`$.
Intuitively, the solver is encouraged to generate reports that are both
valid and strong along the evaluator-selected rubric dimensions.

#### Evaluator-side auxiliary reward.

To adapt the evaluator, SCORE introduces a rubric-level auxiliary reward
based on inter-report consistency. For a fixed query and
evaluator-selected rubric

|     |                            |     |
|-----|----------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Gamma_{q}:=(H_{q},w_{q}),
       ```                         |     |

the solver generates $`K`$ reports under the same query and evaluation
setting. We define the raw evaluator-side auxiliary reward as

|     |                                  |     |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=C(\Gamma_{q}),
       ```                               |     |

where $`C(\Gamma_{q})`$ is the empirical consistency score computed by
aggregating pairwise agreement across the generated reports. In the
current implementation, $`C(\Gamma_{q})`$ is computed as the batch mean
of pairwise agreement scores over extracted report conclusions.

Evaluator updates use an EMA-centered version of this signal,

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})-b_{t-1},
       ```                                           |     |

where $`b_{t-1}`$ is the previous exponential moving average baseline of
past auxiliary rewards. After computing the centered signal, the
baseline is updated as

|     |                                                 |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       b_{t}=\beta b_{t-1}+(1-\beta)R_{e}(\Gamma_{q}),
       ```                                              |     |

where $`\beta`$ is the EMA decay coefficient. Thus, $`R_{e}`$ favors
rubrics under which solver behavior is more reproducible across repeated
rollouts, while $`A_{e}`$ provides the actual lower-variance signal used
for REINFORCE-style evaluator updates.

Overall, SCORE forms a two-channel training loop: evaluator-derived
report rewards improve the solver, while consistency-based rubric-level
rewards adapt the evaluator. The solver-side reward targets report
quality under the selected rubric, whereas the evaluator-side reward
favors rubrics that induce more stable and reproducible solver behavior.
In this sense, consistency is used as a stability-oriented auxiliary
signal for rubric adaptation rather than as a direct correctness
criterion.

### 3.6 Sequential Alternating KL-Regularized Optimization

Given the two reward channels defined in
Section [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Shared Actor and Training Signals ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation"),
training in SCORE proceeds through sequential alternating updates on the
shared actor. Each step constructs a query-specific evaluation
environment, generates rubric-conditioned reports, evaluates them under
evaluator-side verification context, and then applies solver-side and
evaluator-side updates to the shared parameters $`\theta`$.

The solver-side update uses evaluator-derived report rewards $`R_{s}`$,
which are converted into group-normalized advantages and optimized with
GRPO. The evaluator-side update uses the EMA-centered auxiliary reward
$`A_{e}`$, derived from the raw consistency reward $`R_{e}`$, to update
the rubric-selection policy with a REINFORCE-style objective. Although
these updates are computed from different trajectories and rewards, both
act on the same actor and are therefore coupled through the shared
parameterization.

Both updates are regularized by KL divergence against a fixed reference
policy $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$. In our implementation, this KL term is
applied at the actor-update level rather than as an explicit reward
penalty. Thus, KL regularization is not treated as a third reward
channel, but as an optimization regularizer that limits excessive policy
drift under shared-actor learning.

At the analysis level, we denote the unregularized role-specific
objectives by $`\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)`$ and
$`\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)`$, respectively. The corresponding
KL-regularized objectives can be written as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{s}^{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),
``` |  |

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{e}^{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),
``` |  |

where $`\lambda>0`$ is the KL regularization coefficient.

KL regularization serves two purposes in SCORE. First, it stabilizes
alternating optimization by limiting abrupt policy drift under
shared-role learning. Second, it helps maintain compatibility between
solver-side and evaluator-side updates on the shared actor. In this way,
SCORE combines evaluator-derived report rewards, consistency-based
evaluator adaptation, external grounding, shared-role learning, and
KL-regularized alternating optimization into a unified training
framework for long-form report generation.

## 4 Theoretical Analysis

We analyze two properties of SCORE: the role of consistency as an
evaluator-side auxiliary signal, and the first-order optimization
behavior of shared-parameter KL-regularized alternating updates.

#### Assumptions.

We assume that: (A1) the analysis-level objectives $`\mathcal{L}_{s}`$
and $`\mathcal{L}_{e}`$ are $`L`$-smooth; (A2) the corresponding
stochastic gradients are unbiased or approximately unbiased with bounded
variance; and (A3) the stochastic gradients are almost surely bounded.

#### Consistency as rubric-induced reproducibility.

Let $`\Gamma_{q}:=(H_{q},w_{q})`$ denote the evaluator-selected rubric
for query $`q`$. Suppose the solver generates $`K`$ reports under the
same query, evidence context, and rubric, and let $`C(\Gamma_{q})`$
denote the resulting empirical consistency score obtained by aggregating
pairwise agreement scores across these reports. In the current
implementation, this is the mean pairwise consistency across generated
reports. We define the raw evaluator-side auxiliary signal as

|     |                                  |     |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=C(\Gamma_{q}),
       ```                               |     |

and use the centered signal

|     |                                           |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})-b_{t}
       ```                                        |     |

for evaluator updates, where $`b_{t}`$ is an exponential moving average
baseline.

###### Proposition 1 (Consistency estimates rubric-induced reproducibility).

Let $`\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q})`$ denote the population mean of the
consistency metric under rubric $`\Gamma_{q}`$. Then

|     |                                                                |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})]=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q})].
       ```                                                             |     |

Thus, the raw evaluator-side auxiliary signal estimates the expected
reproducibility induced by rubric $`\Gamma_{q}`$ under repeated solver
rollouts.

Higher reproducibility suggests that the rubric induces a more stable
and repeatable evaluation context for the solver, which makes it a
useful auxiliary signal for evaluator-side adaptation. Centering by the
EMA baseline reduces gradient variance without changing the underlying
preference encoded by the raw signal. In SCORE, consistency is therefore
used as a rubric-level auxiliary signal for evaluator-side adaptation.

#### First-order characterization of alternating updates.

Let the shared-actor updates be

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{s}\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t}),\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\theta^{\prime}-\eta_{e}\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}),
``` |  |

where $`\hat{g}_{s}`$ and $`\hat{g}_{e}`$ are the stochastic gradients
of the solver-side and evaluator-side objectives, respectively. Define
the analysis surrogate

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),\qquad\alpha=\eta_{e}/\eta_{s},
``` |  |

where $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is the fixed reference policy and
$`\lambda>0`$ is the KL regularization coefficient.

###### Proposition 2 (First-order characterization of alternating updates).

Under Assumptions (A1)–(A3), the expected one-step SCORE update
satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{t})+\rho_{t},\qquad\|\rho_{t}\|=O(\eta_{s}^{2}).
``` |  |

This result shows that the shared-parameter solver-evaluator updates
follow, to first order, a KL-regularized surrogate descent direction,
with only a higher-order residual induced by sequential alternating
updates. Detailed derivations are deferred to
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Detailed Proofs ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation").

We train all models using the VeRL framework \[[36](#bib.bib38)\]. The
evaluator and solver are updated asynchronously that the solver is
updated at every training step using GRPO \[[35](#bib.bib39)\], while
the evaluator is updated once every 1 steps using REINFORCE
\[[44](#bib.bib1)\]. We set the rollout number to 1 for the evaluator
and 8 for the solver. The maximum number of tool calls is 10, the batch
size is 64, the maximum response length is 4,096 tokens, and the maximum
input length is 8,192 tokens. For training, we use a local search engine
backed by local WikiData, while web search is used during evaluation.
GPT-5.2 \[[18](#bib.bib40)\] is applied as Meta-Harness and is invoked
every 5 training steps to modify the environment according to the
solver’s current performance. For the coefficient of EMA baseline, we
set $`\beta`$=0.7. We construct the training set by collecting and
preprocessing user queries from Reddit.

#### Baselines and Models

We implement SCORE on top of two capable base LLMs, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
\[[32](#bib.bib41)\] and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \[[14](#bib.bib42)\]. For
comparison, we train these models using standard GRPO and DPO to serve
as baselines. The post-trained models are subsequently evaluated within
two representative search agent systems to assess their downstream
capabilities. Specifically, we employ open-deep-research
\[[31](#bib.bib43)\] as a baseline for the ReAct paradigm and
gpt-researcher \[[13](#bib.bib25)\] as a baseline for the
plan-and-execute paradigm. To maintain experimental consistency, a
single, unified model is deployed across all functional modules within
these respective agents.

#### Benchmarks and Evaluation Metrics

To rigorously evaluate our approach, we employ two benchmarks designed
for deep research tasks which are DeepResearchBench \[[10](#bib.bib34)\]
and DeepResearchEval \[[43](#bib.bib44)\]. DeepResearchBench assesses
agent performance across multi-dimensions including comprehensiveness,
insight, instruction following, readability, and citation validity. In
contrast, DeepResearchEval utilizes a dynamic evaluation metric, which
aligns closely with our methodological perspective. Both frameworks
ultimately adopt GPT-5.2 \[[57](#bib.bib27)\] for the final evaluation.

### 5.2 Experimental Results

#### Main Results

As illustrated in
Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Training Details ‣ 5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation"),
SCORE demonstrates a substantial and robust improvement in analytical
depth and valid citation across all agents. Basic reinforcement learning
(RL) methods such as GRPO and DPO suffer from a catastrophic degradation
in at lease one dimension, occasionally collapsing to exactly zero in
Plan-and-Execute agents when integrating LLama-3.1-8B-Instruct. This
phenomenon is driven by Lazy Likelihood Displacement (LLD), a condition
where the likelihood of correct reasoning trajectories decays during
tool-integrated RL, ultimately causing training collapse
\[[7](#bib.bib49)\]. Furthermore, agent architectural magnify this
instability, as decoupled long-horizon frameworks like Plan-and-Execute
exhibit premature collapse and severe reward hacking compared to
step-by-step ReAct policies \[[6](#bib.bib50)\]. In contrast, SCORE
maintains stable performance by utilizing a co-evolving evaluator that
provides dense, consistent constraints through dynamic evaluation
rubrics. Notably, we observe a consistent decline in readability across
nearly all training methods. We hypothesis this a fundamental trade-off
because models are optimized to synthesize comprehensive analysis and
rigorously cite evidence, the generated report inherently incorporates
denser academic vocabulary and more complex logical structures, thereby
sacrificing conversational simplicity for analytical rigor which might
be similar to the trade-off between diversity and factuality
\[[24](#bib.bib51)\].

#### Module Ablation Study

Refer to
Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Main Results ‣ 5.2 Experimental Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation"),
we observe that removing any single module severely degrades the LLMs’
comprehensiveness, demonstrating that generating well-rounded research
reports requires the synergistic operation of the entire framework.
Freezing the solver leads to a massive decline in citation validity. The
internal consistency reward for evaluator brings reward hacking, where
the evaluator learns to construct superficial rubrics that elicit highly
consistent text from the static solver while actively avoiding
high-variance behaviors like rigorous external citation. Furthermore,
our complete method exhibits a slightly lower readability score compared
to the ablated baselines, which we attribute to the inherent trade-off
between natural language coherence and academic formatting. Base models
tend to produce fluent but unstructured text, whereas our
co-evolutionary framework forces the model to generate deeper reports
with complex citation syntax, thus sacrificing superficial readability
for factual rigor.

We investigate the impact of the rollout number $`K`$ in the training
progress.
Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Experiments on Rollout Number ‣ 5.2 Experimental Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation")
demonstrates that extreme $`K`$ affect training procedure that small
$`K=3`$ fails to yield meaningful reward improvements, while large
$`K=10`$ triggers a severe model collapse reflected by sudden drop of
entropy \[[37](#bib.bib55)\]. Small $`K`$ fails to provide the solver
with statistically robust reward due to insufficient ranking samples
while for the evaluator, fewer rollouts yield unreliable pairwise
consistency estimates, resulting in noisy REINFORCE gradients that
prevent stable dimension policy updates. Conversely, an overly large
$`K`$ yields highly precise advantage estimates for both the solver and
the evaluator, accelerating their updates. Under the self-evolving
setting, this mutual acceleration compounds policy drift and may trigger
rapid model collapse.

#### Case Study

As illustrated in
Figure [6](#A7.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Case Study ‣ G.2 Experimental Results ‣ Appendix G Experiments ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation")
in
Appendix [G.2](#A7.SS2 "G.2 Experimental Results ‣ Appendix G Experiments ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation"),
different types of research queries inherently require distinct
evaluation criteria. For instance, legal queries demand absolute
precision and strict citation of statutory texts, whereas open-ended
questions require the aggregation of diverse perspectives to maintain
fairness and objectivity. A generic, static rubric fails to capture
these critical nuances, often assigning acceptable scores to reports
that are superficially fluent but fundamentally flawed. In contrast, our
adaptive evaluation framework dynamically introduces specialized
evaluation dimensions tailored to each query. This mechanism ensures
that the generated reports are accurately penalized or rewarded
according to the specific rigor demanded by the task.

## 6 Conclusion

Generating comprehensive reports for deep research is a highly complex
task, and the inherent difficulty of evaluating these open-ended reports
severely hinders the training process. We observe a strong positive
correlation between a model’s evaluation and generation capabilities.
Based on this insight, we propose SCORE, a self-evolving training
framework that jointly optimizes an evaluator and a solver. Because the
entire training procedure fundamentally relies on unverifiable rewards,
we introduce an external meta-harness to impose necessary environmental
constraints on the evaluator. This module simultaneously guides the
evaluator to actively explore a broader range of relevant evaluation
dimensions throughout the training phase. Finally, experimental results
demonstrate that our approach achieves substantial performance
improvements while requiring only a remarkably small amount of training
data.

## Appendix A Limitations

Despite the effectiveness of our self-evolving framework, we acknowledge
several limitations in our current study. First, the unique nature of
deep research report generation demands exceptional
instruction-following capabilities from the base language models. Models
with weaker foundational abilities may struggle with dynamic rubrics
generation and tool call. Second, our training paradigm relies heavily
on entirely unverifiable rewards. This lack of ground-truth makes the
model susceptible to policy collapse during the optimization process.
Consequently, careful hyperparameter tuning and strict early stopping
mechanisms are necessary to ensure training stability. Even though our
framework successfully provides differentiated reward signals for
specific criteria, aggressively optimizing for one dimension can
sometimes lead to performance degradation in another. Balancing these
competing objectives simultaneously remains a challenging problem for
future exploration.

## Appendix B Future Work

According to our experiments, we observe that inherent trade-offs still
exist during the training process for deep research report generation.
We hypothesize that this issue is primarily caused by sparse reward
signals resulting from exceptionally long agent trajectories. To address
this challenge, future work will explore phased training paradigms. By
decomposing the deep research generation process into distinct
functional stages and assigning intermediate rewards, we aim to resolve
the long-standing credit assignment problem that plagues end-to-end
policy optimization.

## Appendix C LLM Usage Declaration

LLMs are employed to improve the grammar and language quality of the
paper. LLMs are also applied to check the correctness and clarity of the
proofs. In the experiments, open-source LLMs were used for post-train
models, while closed-source LLMs were used as judges for assessment. In
addition, a coding agent was used to assist in developing parts of the

$`U`$

$`\theta`$

|----|----|----|
|  |

|     |

       ```                                                        |     |

rollouts are more reproducible

9:   $`\triangleright`$ *Phase 2: Center the signal with an EMA

|     |                                                             |     |

|     |

## Appendix F Detailed Proofs

This appendix provides derivations for the results stated in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation").
Throughout, let $`\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ denote the shared actor
parameters, and let

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)
``` |  |

denote the KL-regularized surrogate potential.

### F.1 Derivation of Proposition [1](#Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 1 (Consistency estimates rubric-induced reproducibility). ‣ Consistency as rubric-induced reproducibility. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation")

Recall that $`\Gamma_{q}:=(H_{q},w_{q})`$ denotes the evaluator-selected
rubric for query $`q`$. Suppose the solver generates $`K`$ reports under
the same query, evidence context, and rubric. Let

|     |                                            |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q}),\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq K,
       ```                                         |     |

denote the pairwise agreement score between reports $`r_{i}`$ and
$`r_{j}`$ under rubric $`\Gamma_{q}`$, and let

|     |                |     |
|-----|----------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       M=\binom{K}{2}
       ```             |     |

be the number of report pairs. In the current implementation, the
empirical consistency score is defined as

|     |                                                                    |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       C(\Gamma_{q})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q}).
       ```                                                                 |     |

The raw evaluator-side auxiliary signal is

|     |                                  |     |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=C(\Gamma_{q}).
       ```                               |     |

Let

|     |                                                     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q}):=\mathbb{E}[c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q})]
       ```                                                  |     |

denote the population mean of the consistency metric under rubric
$`\Gamma_{q}`$. Then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})]=\mathbb{E}[C(\Gamma_{q})]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q})\right].
``` |  |

By linearity of expectation,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})]=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\mathbb{E}[c_{ij}(\Gamma_{q})]=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q})=\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q}).
``` |  |

Taking expectation over the induced rubric distribution yields

|     |                                                                |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})]=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{C}(\Gamma_{q})].
       ```                                                             |     |

This proves
Proposition [1](#Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 1 (Consistency estimates rubric-induced reproducibility). ‣ Consistency as rubric-induced reproducibility. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation").

In implementation, evaluator updates use the centered signal

|     |                                            |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{e}(\Gamma_{q})=R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})-b_{t},
       ```                                         |     |

where $`b_{t}`$ is an exponential moving average baseline. Since
$`b_{t}`$ is independent of the current sampled rubric action,
subtracting it reduces gradient variance without changing the underlying
expected preference encoded by $`R_{e}(\Gamma_{q})`$.

### F.2 Derivation of Proposition [2](#Thmtheorem2 "Proposition 2 (First-order characterization of alternating updates). ‣ First-order characterization of alternating updates. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation")

The shared-actor updates are

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{s}\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t}),\qquad\theta_{t+1}=\theta^{\prime}-\eta_{e}\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}).
``` |  |

Hence,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}=-\eta_{s}\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t})-\eta_{e}\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}).
``` |  |

Taking conditional expectation with respect to $`\theta_{t}`$ gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t})\mid\theta_{t}]-\eta_{e}\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})\mid\theta_{t}].
``` |  |

Under Assumption (A2),

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t})\mid\theta_{t}]\approx\nabla\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta_{t}),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})\mid\theta_{t}]\approx\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}).
``` |  |

Thus,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]\approx-\eta_{s}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta_{t})-\eta_{e}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime}).
``` |  |

By Assumption (A1), $`\mathcal{L}_{e}`$ is $`L`$-smooth, so

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})\|\leq L\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{t}\|.
``` |  |

From the first update,

|     |                                                              |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \theta^{\prime}-\theta_{t}=-\eta_{s}\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t}),
       ```                                                           |     |

and by Assumption (A3), $`\|\hat{g}_{s}(\theta_{t})\|\leq G`$.
Therefore,

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \|\theta^{\prime}-\theta_{t}\|\leq\eta_{s}G,
       ```                                           |     |

which implies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})\|\leq L\eta_{s}G.
``` |  |

Hence we may write

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta^{\prime})=\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})+\delta_{t},\qquad\|\delta_{t}\|=O(\eta_{s}).
``` |  |

Substituting this into the expected update yields

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta_{t})-\eta_{e}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})-\eta_{e}\delta_{t}.
``` |  |

Using $`\alpha=\eta_{e}/\eta_{s}`$, we obtain

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\nabla\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta_{t})+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta_{t})\bigr)+\rho_{t}^{(0)},
``` |  |

where

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{t}^{(0)}=-\eta_{e}\delta_{t},\qquad\|\rho_{t}^{(0)}\|=O(\eta_{s}^{2}).
``` |  |

Including KL regularization against the fixed reference policy
$`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$, we define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{s}(\theta)+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{e}(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right).
``` |  |

The same argument then gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\mid\theta_{t}]=-\eta_{s}\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta_{t})+\rho_{t},\qquad\|\rho_{t}\|=O(\eta_{s}^{2}),
``` |  |

which proves
Proposition [2](#Thmtheorem2 "Proposition 2 (First-order characterization of alternating updates). ‣ First-order characterization of alternating updates. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation").

### F.3 Summary

The above derivations establish two facts used in the main text:

1.  1.
    the raw evaluator-side consistency signal estimates the expected
    reproducibility induced by a rubric under repeated solver rollouts;
2.  2.
    the shared-parameter solver-evaluator alternating updates follow, to
    first order, a KL-regularized surrogate descent direction, with only
    a higher-order residual due to sequential updating.

#### Training Data Size

We apply different training data size in SCORE. While the co-evolving
evaluator successfully stabilizes training on smaller datasets,
increasing the data scale accelerates model collapse. The reason is both
of the solver and evaluator rely on instinct reward rather than strictly
verifiable external ground truth. With limited training data, this exact
internal feedback effectively sharpens the model’s correct priors and
provides necessary dense constraints. However, as the dataset size
increases significantly, the solver progressively discovers blind spots
in the evaluator and over-optimizes against these proxy rewards. This
dynamic inevitably forces the optimization process to saturate,
degrading the factual accuracy as the solver exploits the distributional
limits of the proxy feedback. As illustrated by
Figure [5](#A7.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Training Data Size ‣ G.2 Experimental Results ‣ Appendix G Experiments ‣ Self-Evolving Deep Research via Joint Generation and Evaluation"),
the solver entropy collapses prematurely at large scales, followed by a
classic rise-then-fall trajectory in the training rewards. This
observation aligns perfectly with recent theoretical findings on
unsupervised reinforcement learning, confirming that intrinsic reward
mechanisms eventually amplify self-reinforcing hallucinations when
exposed to extensive data \[[15](#bib.bib52)\].

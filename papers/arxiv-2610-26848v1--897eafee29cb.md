# Where is Physical RSI capability from? An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy

Zekai Li    Jiayi Yu    Jinlu Zhang    Luyang Niu    Zekun Qi    Jun
Zheng    Xuchuan Chen    Dairu Liu    Yintianrun Zhang    He Wang    Li
Yi Affiliation: Galbot Team

October 19, 2026

###### Abstract

In this paper, we systematically investigate the mechanisms, scaling
behavior, and generalization of physical Recursive self-improvement. We
introduce PhysRSI-eval to quantify physical RSI through a five-axis
metric: First-Attempt Performance, Efficiency, Success, Budget and
Improvement from failure. Different VLMs are evaluated on 18
manipulation tasks under a fixed interaction budget. The analysis of
experimental results motivates the Physical Barrel Hypothesis: different
capabilities may constrain different parts of improvement. Systems with
similar public benchmark averages can still differ widely on the five
axes, and a system that reaches a high final score may improve late or
consume more of its budget. We study observational scaling from two
perspectives: predicting the relative performance of VLM agents from
their public capabilities, and forecasting later progress from early
physical feedback. Together, these analyses ask how quickly and how far
an agent can improve. Moreover, we conduct workspace interventions and
then analyze what improvement leaves behind. Removing history reduces
late-round success, frozen workspaces remain useful on unseen layouts,
and three cross-task cases achieve success earlier. The results suggest
that scene-specific adaptation and reusable experience can coexist.

## 1 Introduction

Frontier LLM agents demonstrate remarkable capabilities in directly
generating actions for robotic manipulation. Through iterative trial and
error, they continually improve manipulation ability by updating skills,
generating code, and using tools. \[[22](#bib.bib22), [9](#bib.bib9)\]
We call this process physical recursive self-improvement, or physical
RSI. In this work, rather than further demonstrating the impressive
capabilities of frontier LLM agents, we pose three more fundamental
questions: What is the origin of physical RSI capabilities? What scaling
laws govern this process? Do the resulting improvements reflect
overfitting or generalization?

#### Q1: Where is Physical RSI capability from?

What allows an agent to turn physical feedback into a better policy?
Perception helps it interpret outcomes, spatial reasoning relates
observations to actions \[[31](#bib.bib31)\], and coding and harness use
allow it to implement and test revisions. These capabilities may play
different roles. We ask whether strengths behind a good first attempt
also explain improvement after feedback.

We introduce PhysRSI-eval to study these questions under a common
protocol
([Figure 2](#S3.F2 "In 3.2 A Controlled Loop of Physical Attempts ‣ 3 PhysRSI-eval: Benchmark Design ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
Six systems revise persistent code, tools and notes across 18
manipulation tasks, with frozen model weights and a fixed physical
interaction budget. Gemini 4 is reported separately on ten tasks.

An exploratory comparison of four public capability profiles on the 18
tasks associates spatial reasoning most strongly with initial
performance, and perception with attained performance and recovery
([Figure 4](#S4.F4 "In 4.2 The Physical Barrel Hypothesis ‣ 4 Where is Physical RSI capability from? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
This pattern motivates the _Physical Barrel Hypothesis_: the capability
that limits an agent may depend on which aspect of improvement we
measure. Starting well and learning from failure may require different
strengths. These associations suggest candidate bottlenecks, not causal
requirements.

#### Q2: How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law?

An endpoint score alone cannot quantify physical RSI. It says little
about when gains occur or what they cost. Repeated attempts can also
increase the chance of success without useful revision. Our _five-axis
metric_ separates initial performance, gain timing, attained score,
physical budget savings and recovery from failure
([Section 3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 A Five-Axis Profile of Improvement ‣ 3 PhysRSI-eval: Benchmark Design ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
These axes reveal different strengths. Opus 5.5 attains a higher score
than GPT-6.1 Sol, but has lower gain-timing and budget-savings scores.
Higher scores need not mean improving earlier or using fewer physical
interactions.

With these outcomes defined, we study _observational scaling_ across
model capabilities and physical interaction budgets. Following [Ruan et
al. \[34\]](#bib.bib34), we first ask whether public capability scores
can predict the relative order of held-out systems, in attained
performance and gain timing
([Section 5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Observational Scaling across Model Capabilities ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).

We then ask whether early physical progress predicts the gains from a
larger interaction budget. This connects physical RSI to the study of
learning curves \[[19](#bib.bib19), [12](#bib.bib12)\], with the
interaction round as our budget variable. Calibrating the improvement
shape of a reference model to a few early rounds forecasts the rest of a
related model’s trajectory
([Figure 8](#S5.F8 "In 5.3 What Can Early Physical Feedback Predict? ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
The two analyses address complementary questions: what improvement to
expect from a model’s capabilities, and how much further it may progress
after its first attempts. Both remain exploratory.

#### Q3: Does Physical RSI reflect overfitting or generalization?

Does the agent acquire a reusable procedure, or adapt to its practice
scene? A rising score alone cannot answer this question. We first remove
history to test whether retained experience helps beyond repeated
attempts. We then freeze workspaces on unseen layouts and transfer them
across tasks.

In a six-task Astra ablation, removing history lowers late-round success
from 41.7% to 8.3%
([Table 8](#S13.T8 "In 13 Ablation Details ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
With further revision disabled, frozen workspace files support success
in 25 of 60 trials on unseen layouts
([Table 9](#S14.T9 "In 14 Generalization Details ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
Three cross-task cases also reach first success earlier when initialized
from other tasks’ workspaces
([Figure 9](#S6.F9 "In 6 Does Physical RSI reflect overfitting or generalization? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
These results support a role for retained experience and show that some
artifacts remain useful beyond the scenes where they were refined.
Scene-specific gains and useful transfer can coexist. Explaining which
retained skills or memories enable transfer connects this question back
to the origins of physical RSI.

Our contributions follow this progression:

- •
  Five-axis evaluation and capability diagnosis. A fixed-budget protocol
  separates five physical RSI outcomes and relates them to four
  capability profiles, motivating the Physical Barrel Hypothesis.
- •
  Observational scaling and prediction. Cross-model rank prediction and
  early-round forecasting probe how model capabilities and interaction
  budget relate to physical improvement.
- •
  Specialization and generalization. Ablations, frozen-workspace tests
  and cross-task adaptation examine whether gains depend on retained
  experience and whether that experience remains useful elsewhere.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 LLM-as-policy Agents and Embodied Evaluation

LLMs can act as robot policies by writing programs over perception and
control tools \[[22](#bib.bib22)\]. Feedback lets them revise plans or
reward code \[[39](#bib.bib39), [14](#bib.bib14), [26](#bib.bib26)\].
Recent benchmarks evaluate these policies through controlled robot
interfaces \[[7](#bib.bib7), [24](#bib.bib24), [5](#bib.bib5),
[57](#bib.bib57), [52](#bib.bib52)\], while others give coding agents
extended simulator access and assess the resulting programs
\[[35](#bib.bib35), [25](#bib.bib25)\]. These evaluations typically
summarize outcomes per episode, across independent trials or within a
time budget. A reported Astra case, where a self-written fix succeeded
once and failed on repetition, highlights the gap between finding a
solution and retaining reliable behavior \[[9](#bib.bib9)\].
PhysRSI-eval tracks judged physical attempts connected by a persistent
workspace, measuring both progress and physical cost under a fixed
budget.

### 2.2 Agent Self-Improvement

Agents can accumulate skills and reflections without updating their
model weights \[[43](#bib.bib43), [37](#bib.bib37), [27](#bib.bib27)\].
Recent benchmarks examine whether changes to the harness or retained
experience improve later performance \[[13](#bib.bib13),
[46](#bib.bib46), [38](#bib.bib38), [58](#bib.bib58)\]. Producing an
update and benefiting from it are distinct questions
\[[23](#bib.bib23)\]. Related designs test memory, transfer and gains
relative to cost \[[36](#bib.bib36), [49](#bib.bib49), [10](#bib.bib10),
[17](#bib.bib17), [20](#bib.bib20), [47](#bib.bib47)\]. AI-R&D
benchmarks address research engineering under time or compute budgets
\[[45](#bib.bib45), [1](#bib.bib1), [40](#bib.bib40), [32](#bib.bib32),
[4](#bib.bib4)\]. These designs motivate separating initial ability,
improvement and cost, and testing retained experience against repeated
attempts. PhysRSI-eval brings these questions to physical interaction.
Native task scores evaluate each attempt, while workspace interventions
test what the agent retains. Clearing the workspace tests whether
accumulated experience helps; freezing and transferring it test whether
its benefits extend to new layouts and tasks.

### 2.3 Capability Benchmarks and Observational Scaling

Public benchmarks provide candidate measures of perception
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [54](#bib.bib54), [33](#bib.bib33)\], spatial
reasoning \[[2](#bib.bib2), [50](#bib.bib50), [6](#bib.bib6),
[16](#bib.bib16)\], coding and tool use \[[18](#bib.bib18),
[44](#bib.bib44), [15](#bib.bib15), [28](#bib.bib28), [48](#bib.bib48),
[53](#bib.bib53)\], and broader reasoning and work performance
\[[30](#bib.bib30), [42](#bib.bib42), [29](#bib.bib29)\]. Embodied
benchmarks and VLA models address planning and physical reasoning
\[[51](#bib.bib51), [21](#bib.bib21), [11](#bib.bib11),
[56](#bib.bib56), [41](#bib.bib41)\], but do not track policy revision
from graded attempts. Observational scaling relates benchmark-derived
capability factors to agent performance across models
\[[34](#bib.bib34)\]. We predict the relative order of physical
improvement, in both extent and timing, from public scores. We also
forecast later gains from early physical performance. With few closed
systems and no training-compute data, these analyses remain
observational and exploratory.

## 3 PhysRSI-eval: Benchmark Design

PhysRSI-eval makes the three questions measurable by recording both
physical attempts and the workspace carried between them. The attempt
sequence reveals where an agent starts and how it progresses under a
fixed budget. Workspace interventions test what agents retain and
whether it transfers.

### 3.1 What Must the Benchmark Distinguish?

A strong first attempt and useful revision are different abilities. We
therefore score a first-attempt baseline separately and follow
subsequent attempts under a fixed budget. Per-round scores show when
gains occur; charged physical steps show what they cost. Reaching a high
score, reaching it early and using fewer interactions are separate
outcomes.

Success alone does not show whether the agent has accumulated useful
experience. Code, tools and notes must remain accessible for
intervention. Clearing the workspace tests whether retained experience
helps; freezing it on unseen layouts tests whether it remains useful;
transferring it to new tasks tests whether it can support a different
goal. We report these tests separately from the main profiles in
[Section 6](#S6 "6 Does Physical RSI reflect overfitting or generalization? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy").

### 3.2 A Controlled Loop of Physical Attempts

Model weights stay fixed, while skills, tools, policy code and memory
can change in a persistent workspace. Each revision is tested through a
judged physical attempt
([Figure 2](#S3.F2 "In 3.2 A Controlled Loop of Physical Attempts ‣ 3 PhysRSI-eval: Benchmark Design ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
This connects workspace changes to an observed outcome and a physical
cost.

K0 measures the starting point without a persistent workspace. Each task
runs three times through the shared robot tools, and its scores are
averaged. At K1, the agent receives a Harness workspace that is empty
except for the host’s read-only tools. Each round executes one physical
attempt and returns the native score, success flag and failure reason.
The agent then revises its workspace with robot actions disabled and
commits it for the next round. The main evaluation allows at most
$`H=17`$ refinement rounds per task and stops at the first native
success.

Both stages share the robot interface. K1 also introduces workspace
tools and program execution, so the K0-to-K1 gain mixes interface
changes with improvement. We therefore study the Harness rounds as the
self-improvement segment. Each round allows 4200 s of reasoning;
revision takes up to 1800 s for GPT systems and 600 s for Claude systems
([Table 3](#S9.T3 "In Actions and observations. ‣ 9 Task and Protocol Details ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).

For aggregation, successful tasks retain score 1 after stopping, without
further execution. Unsuccessful tasks retain their current scores,
including regressions. Extra attempts enter the physical budget but add
no scoring rounds. Nine GPT-5.6 Sol Harness entries summarize two
observed runs. The three-repeat K0 protocol applies to all six systems.

### 3.3 Tasks across Difficulty Levels

The task suite must expose both immediate competence and room for
improvement. We evaluate the loop on 18 RoboDojo dual-arm manipulation
tasks. They cover language-conditioned arrangement, stacking, container
placement, insertion, pouring, pushing and dynamic picking
([Figures 3](#S3.F3 "In 3.3 Tasks across Difficulty Levels ‣ 3 PhysRSI-eval: Benchmark Design ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")
and [2](#S9.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Actions and observations. ‣ 9 Task and Protocol Details ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
The suite spans RoboDojo’s five official task categories (Precision,
Long-Horizon, Memory, Open and Generalization) and samples three tasks
from each of six difficulty strata. The strata are assigned from the
official RoboDojo success rates \[[3](#bib.bib3)\]: the 18 tasks are
ranked by success rate and grouped three per stratum, from D1 (success
rate 60–68.7%) to D6 (success rate 0–0.4%). This balances coverage
across difficulty levels. The strata are fixed before the main
evaluation and are not derived from our refinement outcomes. All 18
tasks, including object packing, receive equal weight. Each retains its
native score in $`[0,1]`$, success criterion and step limit of 300–1300
steps at 25 Hz. The main evaluation uses layout 0 and seed 0. Partial
scores expose progress before the first success.

In a separate experiment without supplementary hints, Astra solves 13

Because the task set differs from the main 18-task protocol, it is

### 3.4 A Five-Axis Profile of Improvement

To study how physical RSI scales, we need more than an endpoint score.
The five-axis profile separates the starting point from gain timing,
attained score, physical budget savings and recovery from failure. Each
axis measures a different aspect of improvement.

Let $`q_{k}`$ be the task-mean score at round $`k`$, with $`q_{0}`$
computed from three-run K0 means. Let $`C_{k}`$ be the fraction of tasks
solved in Harness rounds K1–K$`k`$.

#### A1: First-Attempt Performance.

How well does the agent perform on its first attempt? $`A_{1}=q_{0}`$ is
the mean K0 score, averaged over three runs per task and then across
tasks, including partial progress.

#### A2: Efficiency.

How early do gains appear? The timing of gains relative to the terminal
net gain is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
A_{2}=\frac{\frac{1}{H-1}\sum_{k=1}^{H-1}q_{k}-q_{0}}{q_{H}-q_{0}}.
``` |  | (1) |

Earlier sustained gains give higher values. The axis uses K0 as its
reference and does not measure wall-clock speed. It is undefined without
positive terminal gain; nonmonotone curves are neither clipped nor
replaced by best-so-far scores.

#### A3: Success.

What score does the agent attain? Let
$`q_{\max}=\max_{0\leq k\leq H}q_{k}`$ and define

|     |                                                                |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \tau_{90}=\min\{k\geq 1:q_{k}\geq q_{0}+0.9(q_{\max}-q_{0})\}.
       ```                                                             |     | (2) |

Then $`A_{3}=(H-\tau_{90}+1)^{-1}\sum_{k=\tau_{90}}^{H}q_{k}`$: the mean
over the high window, with partial credit. It is neither a binary
success rate nor an asymptotic ceiling.

#### A4: Budget.

How much physical budget is saved? For a solved task $`t`$, let
$`L_{t}`$ be its per-round step limit, $`W_{t}`$ its cumulative
canonical steps through first success, $`Z_{t}`$ the unused steps
charged for failed reasoning-timeout rounds, and $`e_{t}`$ its charged
extra attempts. Define $`U_{t}=(W_{t}+Z_{t})/L_{t}+e_{t}`$. For the
model’s solved-task set $`\mathcal{S}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{4}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\max(0,1-U_{t}/H).
``` |  | (3) |

Higher values mean more of the physical budget was saved. Unsolved tasks
do not enter this conditional mean, and the axis is undefined if
$`\mathcal{S}`$ is empty. Time, tokens and money are not part of it.

#### A5: Improvement.

Which early failures are overcome? $`A_{5}=(C_{H}-C_{1})/(1-C_{1})`$ is
the fraction of K1-failed tasks subsequently solved within the budget.
It is undefined when all tasks succeed at K1. The conditional task sets
in A4 and A5 vary across models. Each axis is reported as a point
estimate over the task suite
([Section 10](#S10 "10 Metric Definitions and Computation ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).

## 4 Where is Physical RSI capability from?

What allows physical feedback to become a useful policy revision?
Acting, interpreting an outcome and implementing a change may draw on
different capabilities. We use the five-axis profile to ask whether the
capabilities associated with a strong first attempt also track effective
improvement.

### 4.1 Which Capabilities Track Physical RSI?

Strong coding scores alone do not ensure physical improvement. GPT-5.6
Sol exceeds GPT-6 Astra on LiveBench code generation and completion, yet
solves one of the 18 tasks, whereas Astra solves 15.

The exploratory analysis uses six systems on all 18 tasks. We group 17
public benchmark scores into Spatial, Harness, Perception and Coding
([Table 6](#S12.T6 "In 12 Capability and Prediction Analyses ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")),
then compare their ranks with A1–A5
([Figure 4](#S4.F4 "In 4.2 The Physical Barrel Hypothesis ‣ 4 Where is Physical RSI capability from? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).

Spatial reasoning has the strongest observed association with A1
($`\rho=0.94`$) and A2/A4 ($`\rho=0.83`$). Perception has the strongest
association with A3 and A5 ($`\rho=0.94`$). Coding has the weakest
association with every axis ($`\rho\leq 0.37`$).

### 4.2 The Physical Barrel Hypothesis

The pattern motivates the *Physical Barrel Hypothesis*: different
aspects of improvement may have different capability bottlenecks.
Spatial reasoning may matter most for starting well, while perception
may matter more for interpreting failures and recovering from them.
Under this hypothesis, strong coding cannot always compensate for a
weakness elsewhere. Its weak associations do not imply that coding is
unnecessary; all systems use code here.

These associations identify candidate bottlenecks that targeted
capability interventions can test directly
([Section 12](#S12 "12 Capability and Prediction Analyses ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).

![Refer to caption](2610.26848v1/assets/barrel_matrixv3.png)

These candidate bottlenecks lead to the second question: how can we
quantify physical RSI and its scaling with model capabilities and
interaction budget?

## 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law?

How much can an agent improve, when do its main gains occur, and how
much interaction does it need? The five-axis profile turns these
questions into measurable outcomes. We first quantify differences among
agents, then study how these outcomes scale with model capabilities and
physical interaction budget.

### 5.1 High Scores, Fast Gains and Low Cost Can Diverge

Opus has a higher Success score than GPT-6.1 Sol (0.744 versus 0.703),
but lower Efficiency (0.609 versus 0.822). On each system’s solved
tasks, Opus saves less of the budget (0.579 versus 0.808), and the gap
persists on the ten tasks both systems solve. Higher attained scores can
come with later gains and greater physical costs
([Tables 1](#S5.T1 "In 5.1 High Scores, Fast Gains and Low Cost Can Diverge ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")
and [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5.1 High Scores, Fast Gains and Low Cost Can Diverge ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).

Across the six systems, task-mean scores rise from 0.01–0.37 at K0 to
0.10–0.84 by the end of evaluation. They solve 15, 12, 7, 1, 13 and 11
of 18 tasks, respectively: Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Sol,
Opus and Sonnet. Astra leads on all five axes. The two Claude systems
pair relatively high attained scores with middling gain timing, while
the GPT Sol family spans a much wider range. GPT-5.6 Sol remains low on
Success and Improvement; its Budget score is conditional on one solved
task. The profile exposes these trade-offs without a single ranking.

How much do public benchmark averages reveal about these differences?
Across the four GPT systems, mean scores over 17 public benchmarks span
54.1–78.1, while the equally weighted physical profile mean,
$`100\times\sum_{j=1}^{5}A_{j}/5`$, spans 13.9–73.4
([Figure 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.1 High Scores, Fast Gains and Low Cost Can Diverge ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
The ranges are 24 and 59.5 points on different scales. GPT-5.6 Sol stays
near zero cumulative success for 16 rounds: more attempts do not close
every capability gap within this budget.

The Physical Barrel Hypothesis offers one explanation. Useful
improvement may require a minimum level of a needed capability, even
when other capabilities are strong. The rapid contraction region in
[Figure 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.1 High Scores, Fast Gains and Low Cost Can Diverge ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")
illustrates this threshold hypothesis and motivates predicting physical
outcomes from capability profiles rather than a public benchmark
average.

### 5.2 Observational Scaling across Model Capabilities

Can public capability scores anticipate physical improvement before the
full evaluation is run? This is the cross-model question of
observational scaling. Beyond correlating capabilities with outcomes, a
predictor must place an unseen system using only the relationships
observed in other systems.

We ask how far a system improves and when its gains emerge. Let
$`\bar{q}_{k}=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}q_{j}`$ be the best mean score reached
by round $`k`$. For the first question, ranking systems by the
equal-weight average of their four capability profiles reproduces the
observed order of $`\bar{q}_{17}`$, the best score reached by K17. For
the second, we define the half-gain round $`T_{50}`$ as the first round
at which $`\bar{q}_{k}`$ reaches half of its gain from K1 to K17. For
every pair of systems, a kernel regressor fitted on the capability
profiles of the remaining systems predicts which of the two reaches
$`T_{50}`$ first, and all pairwise orderings are recovered
([Figure 7](#S5.F7 "In 5.2 Observational Scaling across Model Capabilities ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
Across the six systems, public capability profiles carry information
about both the extent and the timing of physical improvement.

### 5.3 What Can Early Physical Feedback Predict?

Once physical interaction begins, can early progress predict further
gains? The six systems reach their high-score windows at different
times. The first round reaching 90% of peak gain, $`\tau_{90}`$, is K9
for Astra, K7 for GPT-6.1 Sol, K15 for Opus, K13 for Sonnet, and K17 for
GPT-6 Sol and GPT-5.6 Sol. Forecasting later rounds tests whether a
short trajectory contains useful information about continued
improvement.

A related model may share the shape of its improvement curve. We fit a
Weibull growth template $`g_{A}`$ to Astra’s K1–K17 mean score and
forecast GPT-6.1 Sol as $`\hat{q}(k)=u+(v-u)\,g_{A}(\rho(k-1))`$. The
start $`u`$, plateau $`v`$ and relative speed $`\rho`$ are calibrated on
Sol’s first $`K_{i}`$ rounds only, with gain and speed regularized
toward the template
([Figure 8](#S5.F8 "In 5.3 What Can Early Physical Feedback Predict? ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
With K1–K5 observed, the transferred curve forecasts the remaining
rounds through K17 with mean absolute error 0.019, compared with 0.112
for holding the last observed score. A reference trajectory can predict
a related model’s later performance from fewer rounds.

Figure 8: Forecasting GPT-6.1 Sol from Astra’s improvement shape. Left:
Astra’s mean score and the Weibull template fitted to it. Right: GPT-6.1
Sol with K1–K5 available. The template is calibrated on these rounds and
forecasts the hidden rounds through K17; shading shows the 90%
paired-task bootstrap band. All 18 tasks are used.

These analyses address the amount and timing of improvement. We next ask
what the gains represent: adaptation to the refinement setting, or
experience that remains useful elsewhere?

## 6 Does Physical RSI reflect overfitting or generalization?

With fixed model weights, generalization concerns the code, tools and
notes the agent retains. Do they encode reusable procedures, or do they
become specialized to the refinement setting? We first test whether
retained experience helps beyond repeated attempts. We then evaluate
frozen workspaces on unseen layouts and transfer them to new tasks.

### 6.1 Does Retained Experience Help Beyond Repeated Attempts?

The Astra ablation varies which information from earlier rounds reaches
the agent: the workspace, grader feedback, images and action records
([Table 7](#S13.T7 "In 13 Ablation Details ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
It covers six tasks with two chains each; layouts change every round
over K1–K6, and execution continues after success. The full loop keeps
all four channels, whereas the no-history condition keeps none, so its
six rounds are independent attempts. Late-round success over K4–K6 is
15/36 (41.7%) for the full loop and 3/36 (8.3%) without history
([Table 8](#S13.T8 "In 13 Ablation Details ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
This supports a role for accumulated information under this protocol.

The remaining conditions withhold individual channels. Withholding
grader feedback causes the largest drop, to 5/36 (13.9%), and
withholding earlier-round images yields 12/36 (33.3%). Keeping only the
workspace and grader feedback yields 17/36 (47.2%), so past images and
action records add little once these two channels are kept. The
no-history control supports a role for retained experience, but leaves
open whether that experience is specific to the refinement setting.

### 6.2 What Survives When Revision Stops?

To test whether the stored artifacts work beyond practice, we freeze
Astra workspaces after K6 and test each on five unseen layouts, with no
further revision or outcome feedback
([Table 9](#S14.T9 "In 14 Generalization Details ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
Across 12 sources, workspace files alone succeed in 25/60 tests (41.7%).
Adding prior raw records gives 23/60 (38.3%). The stored artifacts
remain useful under layout changes, while these point estimates show no
benefit from exposing the raw history at test time.

Fixed-layout source workspaces reach 27/60 (45.0%) on held-out layouts,
compared with the reported 14/16 (88%) on their own layout. The
different denominators prevent a matched paired interpretation. Still,
the comparison suggests that scene-specific gains and transferable
experience can coexist. These tests establish some cross-scene
usefulness for Astra on this task subset; they do not establish
generalization across all systems.

### 6.3 Can Experience Help on a Different Task?

Traces from five models suggest two forms of reusable experience:
executable routines and textual operating knowledge. Sonnet 5.5 and Opus
5.5 increasingly encode control in self-written tools, which generate
over 96% of their actions in later rounds. GPT-6 Sol also builds a
substantial tool library, but tools generate 67% of actions while the
model generates 33% directly. These shares measure tool reliance.

These tool libraries grow across rounds, combining tools created from
scratch with those derived from earlier implementations. We use tool
lineage to identify reuse across rounds. Direct use, modification and
derivation of an existing tool all count as reuse. A directed relation A
→ B indicates that tool B is derived from tool A. Tracing these
relations across rounds reveals how later tools build on earlier
implementations. Among the three models with substantial tool libraries,
Opus exhibits broader reuse and lower concentration. Its lineage reuse
rate is 0.347, compared with 0.167 for Sonnet and 0.086 for GPT-6 Sol.
The top 10% of reused lineages account for 48% of subsequent calls in
Opus, compared with 76% in Sonnet and 83% in GPT-6 Sol. Together, these
patterns suggest that Opus accumulates a broader reusable toolbox, while
reuse in Sonnet and GPT-6 Sol remains concentrated in a smaller subset
of tools
([Figure 10](#S6.F10 "In 6.3 Can Experience Help on a Different Task? ‣ 6 Does Physical RSI reflect overfitting or generalization? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).

Figure 10: Tool Libraries and Reuse across Models. (a) Total tool counts
and the proportions of perception and geometry tools versus action
tools. (b) Fraction of tool lineages reused in subsequent rounds, among
those with later rounds available. Reuse includes direct use,
modification and derivation of existing tools. (c) Reuse breadth,
defined as one minus the share of subsequent calls accounted for by the
top 10% of reused lineages. Higher values indicate reuse distributed
across more lineages. Astra is omitted because it creates very few
tools, used only for verification. GPT-6.1 Sol’s estimates reflect a
small library of seven tools.

Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol follow a different pattern. Approximately 99% of
their actions remain directly generated by the model. They use only a
very small number of tools for verification, but their reasoning time
decreases across rounds. File-access traces show repeated use of memory,
skills and policy notes to recover successful actions and subtask
solutions, diagnose failures and guide revisions. These observations
suggest that both executable routines and retained operating knowledge
can reduce repeated reasoning.

We further find that the workspace and generalization analyses align
with the Physical RSI Barrel Hypothesis across its four capability
factors: spatial reasoning, perception, coding and harness operation.
Spatial tools account for 14.5% of Sonnet’s tool calls and 13.7% of
Opus’s, with 408 of 2,819 calls and 183 of 1,332 calls, respectively.
Astra makes only seven tool calls, including one to such a tool, or 14%.
Astra’s minimal reliance on these modules suggests stronger intrinsic
perceptual and spatial capabilities. Sonnet and Opus repeatedly reuse
these modules across tasks, suggesting that they rely more on tools to
compensate for limitations in these capabilities.

During cross-task transfer, agents also consult memory and skills from
semantically similar tasks. In Organize Table, Opus combines a
parameterized move tool from Pack Objects Into Box with a memory from
Store Tools In Toolbox: “the PITCHED 30 deg grasp… reaches the +y half…”
The tool supplies an operation, while the memory identifies a grasp
orientation that extends its reach. This shows how a reusable procedure
and knowledge of when it works can jointly support transfer
([Figure 9](#S6.F9 "In 6 Does Physical RSI reflect overfitting or generalization? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).

The transfer traces suggest that performance gains depend on experience
relevant to the target task’s bottleneck and the agent’s ability to
assess its applicability from failure evidence. Semantic similarity
helps identify candidate experience; failure evidence guides whether to
reuse, adapt or discard it. The teaser illustrates this mechanism
through the reuse of a closed-loop grasp-and-transfer routine from
stacking blocks to covering blocks with a cup, and an insertion routine
from key insertion to charger plugging.

## 7 Limitations

The main comparison covers six model-plus-configuration systems, with
one improvement chain per task. K0 repeats do not estimate variability
across complete chains. First-success stopping records one success;
reliable repeated execution needs a separate test. Budget and
Improvement use conditional task sets that differ across systems.
Astra’s outcomes differ between the main evaluation and the experiment
without supplementary hints.

The capability analysis relies on public benchmark scores whose
evaluation settings differ from our runs, and it covers six systems
without independent validation. Capability bottlenecks and gain-timing
predictions therefore remain hypotheses to test. The ablation and frozen
tests cover Astra and six tasks, while cross-task results are three
first-run cases. These limits constrain the scope of the observed
transfer and the mechanisms that can be inferred from it.

## 8 Conclusion

PhysRSI-eval studies the origins, measurement, scaling and
generalization of physical RSI under a fixed interaction budget.
Capability associations motivate the Physical Barrel Hypothesis: the
strengths needed to start well may differ from those needed to improve.
The five-axis metric makes these outcomes explicit and shows that high
scores, early gains and efficient interaction need not coincide.
Cross-model prediction and early-round forecasting probe how these
outcomes vary with capabilities and interaction budget. Workspace
interventions support a role for retained experience and show that it
can remain useful on new layouts and tasks. Physical RSI can thus
involve both scene-specific adaptation and reusable improvement.
Understanding it requires measuring how agents improve and examining
what they retain.

## 10 Metric Definitions and Computation

Metrics are computed after averaging the task curves; in particular,
Efficiency is a ratio of aggregate gains, not an average of task-wise
ratios. The A3 threshold is the first integer Harness round reaching 90%
of the observed peak gain, without interpolation. Budget charges each
extra attempt once, supplements only failed reasoning-timeout rounds to
their full step quota, and clips task savings at zero. Undefined
denominators stay undefined. The summary in
[Figure 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.1 High Scores, Fast Gains and Low Cost Can Diverge ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")
is the equally weighted arithmetic mean of A1–A5, multiplied by 100. It
is neither radar polygon area nor a success probability.

## 12 Capability and Prediction Analyses

The capability profiles use 17 public benchmark scores collected on 15
October 2026 from Roboflow Vision Evals \[[33](#bib.bib33)\], ParseBench
\[[55](#bib.bib55)\], LiveBench \[[44](#bib.bib44)\] and five
Terminal-Bench 4.0 tasks \[[28](#bib.bib28)\]
([Table 6](#S12.T6 "In 12 Capability and Prediction Analyses ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")).
Each score is converted to a normalized rank among the systems, and
ranks are averaged within the Spatial, Harness, Perception and Coding
groups. The capability-association matrix compares these group profiles
with A1–A5 on the 18 tasks by Spearman correlation, using exact
permutation tests over the six systems with Benjamini–Hochberg
adjustment; the smallest adjusted $`q`$ is 0.11. The public-score
comparison in
[Figure 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.1 High Scores, Fast Gains and Low Cost Can Diverge ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")
averages the 17 raw scores with equal weight. The $`T_{50}`$ analysis
predicts the order of gain timing from the group profiles. For each pair
of held-out systems, the group profiles and $`T_{50}`$ are standardized
on the remaining systems, and kernel ridge regression with
$`k(z,z^{\prime})=\exp(-\gamma\lVert z-z^{\prime}\rVert^{2})`$,
$`\gamma=0.0625`$ and $`\alpha=10`$ predicts $`T_{50}`$; the system with
the smaller prediction is ranked earlier.

The within-trajectory forecasts use early physical scores rather than
public benchmarks. The template
$`g_{A}(t)=1-\exp[-(t/\tau_{A})^{p_{A}}]`$, with $`t=k-1`$, is fitted to
Astra’s K1–K17 mean score and then fixed ($`\tau_{A}=2.18`$,
$`p_{A}=1.30`$). For GPT-6.1 Sol, $`u`$, $`v`$ and $`\rho`$ minimize the
squared error on the available rounds plus
$`\lambda[(v-u)-\Delta_{A}]^{2}+(0.03\log\rho/0.2)^{2}`$, where
$`\Delta_{A}`$ is the gain of the Astra template, $`\lambda=4`$,
$`0\leq u\leq v\leq 1`$ and $`\rho`$ is searched over 81 log-spaced
values in $`[0.3,2]`$. The remaining rounds are forecast without
updating on their outcomes. The band in
[Figure 8](#S5.F8 "In 5.3 What Can Early Physical Feedback Predict? ‣ 5 How can we quantify Physical RSI and its scaling law? ‣ Where is Physical RSI capability from?An Observational Scaling Law of LLM-as-policy")
spans the 5th–95th percentiles of 300 paired task resamples, refitting
the template and repeating the calibration in each resample. All 18
tasks are used.
````

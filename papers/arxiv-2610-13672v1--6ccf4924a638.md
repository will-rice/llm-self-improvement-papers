# Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction

Jiwon Jeon ^(†)^(†)thanks: Equal contribution.    Myungsik
Cho¹¹footnotemark: 1    Jeonghye Kim    Youngchul Sung
Affiliation: Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST)
Email: [{jiwon.jeon,ms.cho,jeonghye.kim,ycsung}@kaist.ac.kr](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Large language models struggle to reliably identify and correct errors
in their own reasoning. Unlike prior training-based approaches that
fine-tune an LLM to generate revisions or feedback, we learn _where_ to
intervene in failed rollouts and determine _how_ to redirect them. To
choose where to intervene, we train a 130K-parameter MLP head on a
frozen encoder using only final-answer correctness. For redirection, we
find that under a fixed generation budget, the tested inline injections
fail to reliably redirect reasoning, whereas presenting the same
instruction in a new user turn changes the model’s course. The resulting
correction framework, UPTURN (User-Prompt TURN injection at a learned
position), requires no LLM fine-tuning: it retains a selected prefix,
injects an instruction as a new user turn, and regenerates the suffix.
Across four reasoners and six math benchmarks, UPTURN improves average
accuracy in every setting over a 32-rollout baseline. It outperforms
self-correction and reflection-token injection baselines under matched
correction budgets, transfers across reasoners and to GPQA-Diamond
without retraining, and complements GRPO and self-correction methods.

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2610.13672v1/intro_left.png)  

Figure 1: Structure, not content alone, enables redirection. (Left) The
same instruction is absorbed inline but redirects reasoning in a new
user turn. (Right) Learning where to intervene raises rescue rate (the
fraction of failed rollouts corrected; top) and six-benchmark mean

Large language models (LLMs) reason autoregressively, so a single
mistake can derail subsequent reasoning, making effective correction
important. Yet, given only its own rollout, a model may struggle to
determine whether it has erred, and intrinsic self-correction without
external feedback can even degrade reasoning accuracy ([Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib6)). This motivates learning a correction signal from
data. Existing methods do so by fine-tuning the reasoner to produce a
revised answer ([Kumar et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Qu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)) or by training an external critic or verifier to
provide step-level feedback or scores ([Lightman et al.,
2024](#bib.bib5); [Paul et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)). Although effective,
these methods require substantial correction data and typically optimize
an LLM-scale component.

This raises a question: "Does correction really require training an
entire LLM?" Repeated sampling shows that LLMs can solve far more
problems than in a single attempt, with pass@$`k`$ increasing as $`k`$
grows ([Brown et al., 2024](#bib.bib15)). Yet individual rollouts can
still fail: because autoregressive generation conditions each step on
the preceding reasoning, even a single mistake can steer the subsequent
trajectory away from a correct solution. A failed rollout need not
involve many independent errors. Correction may therefore involve
redirecting the model toward a solution it can already reach, rather
than teaching it to reason de novo. We thus frame correction around two
questions: _where_ to intervene in a failed rollout and _how_ to
redirect it.

We study these questions in a _fixed-budget prefix-regeneration_
setting: after selecting a cut, we retain the prefix and regenerate only
the suffix under the original response-token limit. Answering the
_where_ question requires selecting a single cut position in a failed
rollout rather than generating a revised answer or written feedback. We
train a 130K-parameter MLP head on a frozen Qwen3-0.6B encoder to favor
cut positions that more often lead to correct answers upon regeneration.
To address _how_ to redirect the rollout, we consider token injection.
Reflection tokens such as ‘‘wait’’ mark high-entropy forks where a
reasoning path can branch in new directions ([Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10)), suggesting that they might steer a rollout. Indeed,
s1 ([Muennighoff et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)) improves accuracy by
appending ‘‘wait’’ when the model attempts to stop, extending its
reasoning. This suggests a simple recipe: learn where to intervene,
inject a reflection token, and let the model continue.

However, our experiments reveal a critical limitation of inline
injection under the fixed-budget protocol. Across the tested reflection
tokens (‘‘wait’’, ‘‘hmm’’), explicit instructions (‘‘Solve with a
different way.’’), and even a task-irrelevant word (‘‘Turtle’’), inline
injection fails to reliably redirect the rollout. The key difference is
conversational _structure_: the same instruction can redirect reasoning
when delivered as a new user turn
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction"),
left), whereas inline delivery does not reliably do so. These results
suggest that the model responds more effectively to an instruction
delivered in a user turn than to the same text inserted into its own
continuation. We refer to this structural transition from an assistant
continuation to a new user turn as a _turn boundary_.

Putting these two pieces together, we propose UPTURN (User-Prompt TURN
injection at a learned position), a correction framework that requires
no LLM fine-tuning. UPTURN keeps the reasoner and the 0.6B encoder
frozen and trains a position policy, implemented as a 130K-parameter MLP
head, to select where a fixed instruction is injected through a turn
boundary. A separately trained 66K-parameter gate identifies which
rollouts to correct at inference. Rather than identify the error or
generate a fix, the position policy chooses where to redirect the
rollout, while the frozen reasoner performs the correction itself.
Averaged across four reasoners and six math benchmarks, UPTURN raises
accuracy from 37.1% to 55.3%
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction"),
right). It outperforms self-correction and reflection-token injection
baselines under matched correction budgets, as well as heuristic cuts
and inline injection. Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  We show that conversational structure matters for correction: the
  tested inline injections fail to reliably redirect reasoning, whereas
  the same instruction delivered through a turn boundary can.
- •
  We learn cut positions with a 130K-parameter head on a frozen encoder,
  using only regenerated rollouts’ final-answer correctness, without
  step-level annotations or reasoner fine-tuning.
- •
  We demonstrate consistent accuracy gains across four reasoners and six
  math benchmarks, transfer across reasoners and to GPQA-Diamond without
  retraining, and complementary gains on top of GRPO and existing
  self-correction methods.

## 2 Related Work

##### Prompting-based self-correction.

Prompting-based methods improve outputs without updating model weights,
typically by having the model critique or reflect on its own attempt and
then revise it ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib13)). Reflexion ([Shinn et
al., 2023](#bib.bib14)), for example, generates a verbal reflection on
the previous attempt and uses it to guide a fresh attempt. These methods
rely on the reasoner to diagnose its own errors, which can be unreliable
for reasoning ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib6); [Tie et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4)). UPTURN asks the reasoner for neither a critique nor a
rewrite: a learned position policy selects where to intervene, and a
fixed user-turn instruction redirects the existing rollout.

##### Training-based self-correction.

Training-based methods learn corrective signals from data, some by
fine-tuning the reasoner to revise its own answers ([Qu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)); SCoRe ([Kumar et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)), for example,
trains the model with RL to produce a revised second attempt conditioned
on its first. Others train a separate model to localize errors, such as
a critic whose step-level feedback guides the reasoner’s revision ([Paul
et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)) or a process reward model that scores each
step ([Lightman et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)). These approaches typically
train an LLM-scale component using correction data, such as revision
pairs or step-level annotations. UPTURN instead trains lightweight heads
to select which rollouts to correct and _where_ to intervene, rather
than generating revisions, critiques, or step-level scores, while
keeping the reasoner frozen.

##### Reflection-token injection.

Several methods improve inference-time performance by injecting or
promoting reflection tokens in a frozen model’s reasoning. They append a
token such as ‘‘wait’’ when the model attempts to stop ([Muennighoff et
al., 2025](#bib.bib12); [Ringel et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)), or encourage
reflection tokens during generation on a predetermined schedule through
direct insertion or logit upweighting ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib19);
[Fan et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)). These methods steer an ongoing
generation from within the model’s own response, whereas we study
post-hoc correction of completed rollouts flagged as likely incorrect.
In this setting, we find that how the text is delivered matters: under a
matched generation budget, the tested inline injections fail to reliably
redirect reasoning even at learned cut positions
(§[6.5](#S6.SS5 "6.5 Ablation ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")),
whereas the same instruction delivered as a separate user turn does.

## 3 Preliminaries

##### Autoregressive generation over chat turns.

An autoregressive language model $`p_{\theta}`$ draws each token from
the next-token distribution conditioned on all preceding tokens. We
represent a conversation as alternating single-message user and
assistant turns, written $`[\textsc{user}{:}\,q]`$ and
$`[\textsc{asst}{:}\,o]`$ and concatenated with $`\|`$. Thus, a question
$`q`$ and a response $`o`$ form the two-turn conversation
$`[\textsc{user}{:}\,q]\,\|\,[\textsc{asst}{:}\,o]`$. Before generation,
the chat template tokenizes the conversation and wraps each turn in
special tokens marking where the turn begins and ends (e.g.,
\<\|im_start\|\>user $`\ldots`$ \<\|im_end\|\> for Qwen ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16))). The response is then generated token by token as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
o_{t}\sim p_{\theta}\big(\cdot\mid[\textsc{user}{:}\,q]\,\|\,[\textsc{asst}{:}\,o_{<t}]\big),\qquad t=1,\dots,T,
``` |  | (1) |

where $`o_{<t}=(o_{1},\dots,o_{t-1})`$, $`T`$ is the response length,
and the assistant turn remains open.

##### Mathematical reasoning with LLMs.

In the standard math setup, the model answers each problem with a
chain-of-thought rollout $`o`$ ([Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib9)) in a
single assistant turn via
Eq. ([1](#S3.E1 "In Autoregressive generation over chat turns. ‣ 3 Preliminaries ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")),
with no further turns. The answer is the content of the last \boxed{}
expression in the rollout, and the reward is its exact match with the
ground truth $`a^{\star}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R(q,o)\;=\;{\mathbf{1}}\!\left[\texttt{answer}(o)=a^{\star}\right]\;\in\;\{0,1\}.
``` |  | (2) |

##### Group Relative Policy Optimization.

GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)) is a policy-gradient method widely
used to post-train LLM reasoners. Unlike PPO ([Schulman et al.,
2017](#bib.bib8)), it removes the learned value network: for each input,
it samples $`G`$ responses and forms advantages by centering and scaling
their rewards with the group mean and standard deviation. It then
applies PPO’s clipped surrogate objective, typically with a KL penalty
to a reference policy. We adapt this group-relative construction to
train the position policy, with the modifications described in
Sec. [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Training the position policy ‣ 5 Method ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction").

## 4 Motivation

(b) Position-wise rescue gap $`(R-V)`$

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib7); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)) has enabled LLMs to
produce high-quality mathematical solutions. Prior work ([Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10)) shows that RLVR updates concentrate on high-entropy
“forking” tokens (e.g., Wait, Alternatively), with smaller updates at
the more numerous low-entropy tokens. These high-entropy tokens
correspond to decision points where the reasoning can branch.

This observation motivates the following question for reasoning
correction: If RLVR updates concentrate on such decision points during
training, could intervening at analogous points during inference help
correct a failed reasoning trace? A natural candidate is token
injection: select a branching point and inject a reflection token to
encourage a different continuation without retraining the reasoner.

### 4.1 Recovery Depends on the Retained Prefix

Failed traces may contain useful prefixes that can lead to correct
answers. Restarting from scratch discards these prefixes, whereas
regeneration from a suitable cut preserves them.

To test this, we collected failed Qwen3-4B (Thinking on) traces on AIME
2026 and extracted ten prefixes at equally spaced token positions from
each trace. We sample 16 such continuations per prefix without any
injected text and define the continuation success rate $`V`$ of each
prefix as the fraction of continuations with correct final answers. As a
reference, $`V_{0}`$ denotes the success rate when the model generates
from the question alone, with no retained prefix. In the traces shown in
Figure [2(a)](#S4.F2.sf1 "In Figure 2 ‣ 4 Motivation ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction"),
continuation success rates are high at earlier prefixes but drop sharply
and remain low at later ones. This suggests that the choice of retained
prefix can substantially affect the model’s ability to recover.

The sharp decline may mark a branching point beyond which recovery
becomes difficult. This motivates cutting before the transition to
preserve a prefix from which the model can still recover.

### 4.2 Inline vs. Multi-turn Injection

The previous subsection motivates *where* to intervene: before a
potential branching point marked by a sharp drop in continuation
success. We now ask *how* to redirect reasoning at a given cut position.

Using the recovery protocol in
Sec. [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Recovery Depends on the Retained Prefix ‣ 4 Motivation ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction"),
we compare injection variants at the sampled cut positions. At each
position $`t`$, we retain the prefix $`o_{\leq t}`$, discard the
remaining suffix, and append the injected text $`c`$ within the same
assistant turn before regeneration. We test reflection tokens such as
Wait and Hmm, unrelated words such as turtle and banana, and longer
instructions such as ‘‘Solve with a different way.’’. For each rollout
and cut position, only $`c`$ varies across the inline variants. Inline
injection is expressed as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}_{\text{in}}=[\textsc{user}{:}\,q]\,\|\,[\textsc{asst}{:}\,o_{\leq t},\,c],
``` |  | (3) |

where $`c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})`$ denotes the injected text. Across the
tested texts, inline injection yields similar continuation success
rates. Surprisingly, these rates remain close to those obtained by
regenerating from the prefix without injection
(Figures [2(b)](#S4.F2.sf2 "In Figure 2 ‣ 4 Motivation ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
and [2(c)](#S4.F2.sf3 "In Figure 2 ‣ 4 Motivation ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")).

We next deliver the same text $`c`$ through the chat template as a new
user turn. We close the current assistant turn, insert a user turn
containing $`c`$, and open a new assistant turn for regeneration:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}_{\text{mt}}=[\textsc{user}{:}\,q]\,\|\,[\textsc{asst}{:}\,o_{\leq t}]\,\|\,[\textsc{user}{:}\,c]\,\|\,[\textsc{asst}{:}\,\cdot].
``` |  | (4) |

This multi-turn format yields substantially higher continuation success
rates than inline injection.

These results show that the injection format matters beyond instruction
wording. Both contexts use the same frozen model $`p_{\theta}`$ and
injected text $`c`$, but differ in their conditioning format. In
$`\mathcal{C}_{\text{in}}`$, the short $`c`$ ($`m\ll t`$) follows a long
sequence of assistant tokens and may therefore be interpreted as part of
the ongoing reasoning rather than as a revision signal. In
$`\mathcal{C}_{\text{mt}}`$, the chat template instead presents the same
$`c`$ as an external instruction in a new user turn, which may help
redirect the continuation.
Figure [6](#A1.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Appendix A Illustrative Correction Example ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Illustrative Correction Example ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
illustrates this contrast: inline continuation stays on the original
path, whereas a new user turn redirects reasoning.

## 5 Method

Figure 3: Overview of UPTURN. (Step 1) A frozen 0.6B encoder and MLP
head $`f_{\phi}`$ score paragraph boundaries (\n\n) in failed rollouts
(✗); the policy selects a cut $`t^{\star}`$. (Step 2) UPTURN retains the
prefix, inserts a fixed instruction as a new user turn, and regenerates
in a new assistant turn, recovering a correct answer (✓).

Motivated by
§[4](#S4 "4 Motivation ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction"),
we correct a failed rollout by discarding the suffix after a selected
position, inserting a fixed instruction $`c`$ as a new user turn, and
letting the frozen reasoner regenerate
(Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5 Method ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")).
The reasoner $`p_{\theta}`$ (e.g., Qwen3-4B) produces both the original
and revised rollouts, while the position policy $`\pi_{\phi}`$ selects
the cut. All LLM parameters and the injection instruction remain fixed;
only lightweight heads are trained.

### 5.1 Position policy

Given a question $`q`$ and a failed rollout $`o`$, a frozen Qwen3-0.6B
causal encoder $`E`$ produces a hidden state $`h_{t}`$ at each token
$`o_{t}`$. Because the encoder is causal, each candidate’s score
$`f_{\phi}(h_{t})`$ depends only on the question and retained prefix
$`o_{\leq t}`$, not on the discarded suffix. To reduce the cost of
evaluating regeneration rewards at every token, we restrict cuts to
paragraph boundaries (\n\n), natural breaks between reasoning steps. Let
$`\mathcal{T}(o)`$ denote the token positions at the ends of these
boundaries.
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Paragraph Boundaries are Forking Points ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
shows that next-token entropy is elevated at paragraph boundaries,
supporting these candidate cuts. Cutting at $`t`$ retains $`o_{\leq t}`$
and discards $`o_{>t}`$. We train only a two-layer MLP head $`f_{\phi}`$
($`130`$K parameters) and normalize its scores over candidates to obtain
the policy:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi_{\phi}(t\mid q,o)=\frac{\exp\!\big(f_{\phi}(h_{t})\big)}{\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(o)}\exp\!\big(f_{\phi}(h_{t^{\prime}})\big)}.
``` |  | (5) |

### 5.2 Training the position policy

##### Collecting failed rollouts.

We sample $`N`$ rollouts per prompt and train on all failures
($`R(q,o)=0`$). Since all-success prompts offer nothing to correct, we
sample additional prompt batches until obtaining $`B`$ prompts with at
least one failure, similar to dynamic sampling ([Yu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17)).

##### Multi-turn instruction injection.

Based on
Sec. [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Inline vs. Multi-turn Injection ‣ 4 Motivation ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction"),
given a failed rollout and a cut position $`t`$, we close the assistant
turn after the prefix $`o_{\leq t}`$, insert the fixed instruction $`c`$
(‘‘Resolve this concisely.’’) as a new user turn, and open a new
assistant turn,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{ctx}(q,o_{\leq t},c)=[\textsc{user}{:}\,q]\,\|\,[\textsc{asst}{:}\,o_{\leq t}]\,\|\,[\textsc{user}{:}\,c]\,\|\,[\textsc{asst}{:}\,\cdot],
``` |  | (6) |

Here, $`[\textsc{asst}{:}\,\cdot]`$ denotes the new assistant turn from
which generation begins. We use this injection format during both
training and inference. The reasoner generates at most $`L_{\max}{-}t`$
suffix tokens, keeping the retained prefix and regenerated suffix within
the original response-token budget $`L_{\max}`$.

##### Reward and objective.

We reward cuts by regeneration success. For each failed rollout, we
sample $`G`$ cuts $`\{t^{(1)},\dots,t^{(G)}\}`$ without replacement from
$`\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid q,o)`$ as one GRPO group. At each $`t^{(i)}`$,
the reasoner generates $`M`$ suffixes
$`\tilde{o}^{(i,s)}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathrm{ctx}(q,o_{\leq t^{(i)}},c))`$.
We score each cut by averaging the final-answer correctness of its $`M`$
regenerated suffixes:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{s=1}^{M}R\big(q,\tilde{o}^{(i,s)}\big).\vskip-1.00006pt
``` |  | (7) |

With $`p_{\theta}`$ and $`c`$ fixed, averaging correctness over $`M`$
regenerated suffixes reduces reward-estimation variance. Unlike standard
GRPO, which groups independent responses, we group cuts from one failed
rollout to compare positions while holding the trajectory fixed.
Following Dr.GRPO ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)), we drop
standard-deviation normalization and use the mean-subtracted advantage
$`\hat{A}_{i}{=}r_{i}{-}\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_{j}`$, with
importance ratio
$`\rho_{i}{=}\pi_{\phi}(t^{(i)}\mid q,o)/\pi_{\phi_{\text{old}}}(t^{(i)}\mid q,o)`$.
The objective is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{J}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\!\big(\rho_{i}\,\hat{A}_{i},\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\hat{A}_{i}\big)\;+\;\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}\,\mathcal{H}\!\big[\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid q,o)\big]\right].\vskip-1.00006pt
``` |  | (8) |

We omit the reference-KL term and use an entropy bonus for cut
exploration. Advantages computed within a rollout favor cuts with higher
rescue rates than other cuts in that trace.

### 5.3 Gated Inference

At inference, ground-truth answers are unavailable, so we use a
lightweight *gate* $`g_{\psi}`$ to estimate whether each rollout is
correct. Rollouts without a parseable \boxed{} answer receive a gate
score of zero. For the rest, the same frozen Qwen3-0.6B encoder $`E`$
processes the chat-templated question and rollout, and an MLP predicts
correctness from the hidden state $`h_{\mathrm{box}}`$ at the final
\boxed{} token. With $`\kappa`$ denoting the calibration temperature,
the gate is defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{\psi}(q,o)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }o\text{ has no parseable answer},\\
\sigma\!\big(\mathrm{MLP}_{\psi}(h_{\mathrm{box}})/\kappa\big),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
``` |  | (9) |

The gate is a 66K-parameter MLP trained once on 2,874
correctness-labeled rollouts and reused across all reasoners.
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Gate Details ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
provides training and calibration details, evaluates gate ranking
quality, compares learned and ground-truth gating, and examines
threshold sensitivity.

Rollouts with $`g_{\psi}(q,o)\geq\gamma`$ are retained unchanged. Those
below the fixed threshold $`\gamma{=}0.4`$ are corrected once by
sampling $`t^{\star}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid q,o)`$, injecting $`c`$ via
Eq. ([6](#S5.E6 "In Multi-turn instruction injection. ‣ 5.2 Training the position policy ‣ 5 Method ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")),
and regenerating. Each regenerated rollout replaces its original,
preserving the pool size.

## 6 Experiments

We ask four questions: (1) Can UPTURN correct rollouts to improve
accuracy across models and benchmarks within a fixed generation budget?
(2) How does it compare with self-correction and reflection-token
injection? (3) Does it transfer across reasoners and domains and
complement GRPO and self-correction? (4) How do learned cuts and
turn-boundary injection affect performance?

### 6.1 Setup

##### Benchmarks, metrics, and reasoners.

We train each position policy for 30 steps on DAPO-17K, a 17K-prompt
DAPO-Math subset ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)). Evaluation covers
AIME24, AIME25, AIME26, AMC23, HMMT, and MATH500, all disjoint from
training. Avg is the mean per-question accuracy of the final candidate
pool and Pass the fraction of questions with at least one correct
candidate. We evaluate four frozen reasoners in the main table: Qwen3-4B
and Qwen3-8B with thinking enabled, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B
(DeepSeek-R1-Qwen3-8B), and QwQ-32B; two additional reasoners are
reported in
Appendix [N](#A14 "Appendix N Results on Other Models ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction").
Unless stated otherwise, each reasoner uses a position policy trained on
its own rollouts, while the 66K gate is reused unchanged across all
models.

##### Decoding budgets.

Base and UPTURN share the frozen reasoner and sampling parameters.
Base@32 draws 32 independent full rollouts. UPTURN@16 draws 16 rollouts
and corrects each gate-flagged rollout once from
$`t^{\star}{\sim}\pi_{\phi}(\cdot{\mid}q,o)`$, adding up to 16
generations. Thus, both methods allow up to 32 generations per question.
Base therefore receives a larger maximum generated-token allowance: each
correction reuses $`o_{\leq t^{\star}}`$ and generates at most
$`L_{\max}{-}t^{\star}`$ new tokens instead of a full rollout. Results
at larger $`L_{\max}`$ are in
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Scaling the Generation Budget ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction").
Under gated inference
(§[5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Gated Inference ‣ 5 Method ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")),
corrections replace their originals, leaving 16 rollouts for evaluation.
Sampling settings appear in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Implementation Details ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction").
We also measure end-to-end runtime, including gating and
position-selection overhead
(Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Wall-Clock Cost ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")).

##### Gate: selecting and flagging.

We test the gate in two ways
(Table [5](#S6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Injection: learning the position for inline injection. ‣ 6.5 Ablation ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")).
First, as a selector: we return the highest-gate candidate per question
and report its accuracy (GS). Base@32 reaches $`52.0`$ GS, close to its
$`53.5`$ Pass, so the gate reliably picks a correct rollout when one
exists, and UPTURN@16 keeps a $`+11.0`$ point advantage under gate
selection. Second, we flag with a ground-truth gate instead of the
learned one. All metrics change by less than one point, so the learned
gate is already close to optimal for flagging (details in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Gate Details ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")).

## 7 Conclusion

We frame reasoning correction as redirecting an existing trajectory
rather than retraining the reasoner. UPTURN builds on the observation
that inline text is absorbed into the assistant’s reasoning, whereas the
same instruction delivered across a turn boundary can redirect it. A
lightweight gate flags likely failures, a 130K-parameter head on a
frozen encoder selects a cut that preserves a useful prefix, and the
frozen reasoner regenerates from a fixed instruction injected as a new
user turn. Across four reasoners and six math benchmarks, UPTURN
consistently improves average accuracy and outperforms self-correction
and reflection-token injection baselines under matched correction
budgets, as well as heuristic cuts and inline injection. It transfers
across reasoners and to GPQA-Diamond without retraining, and provides
additional gains on top of GRPO and existing self-correction methods.
When the required capability is already present, a small learned
intervention can thus recover a better reasoning path without retraining
the reasoner; extension to code and tool use remains open.

## Appendix B Limitations

UPTURN is a first step toward correcting reasoning by redirection rather
than retraining, and several aspects remain open. Our study focuses on
reasoning tasks whose outcomes can be checked automatically, and on a
single, deliberately minimal form of intervention. Richer interventions,
and settings where success is harder to verify, may call for different
choices of what to inject and where. We leave these extensions to future
work.

## Appendix C Implementation Details

##### Position policy architecture.

The position head $`f_{\phi}`$ operates on the 1024-dimensional
last-layer hidden states of the frozen Qwen3-0.6B encoder. It applies
LayerNorm to the input, followed by a linear layer $`1024\!\to\!128`$, a
GELU activation, and a linear layer $`128\!\to\!1`$ that produces the
logit of each candidate position, totaling 133,377 parameters (about
130K), the only trainable parameters of the position policy. The encoder
receives exactly the token sequence the reasoner produced, the
chat-templated prompt tokens concatenated with the rollout tokens, with
no re-tokenization, as all our reasoners share the encoder’s
151,643-token Qwen vocabulary. The delimiter \n\n is a single token in
this tokenizer, and a failed rollout contains 11.3 candidate positions
on average (median 5).

##### Training details.

We train on DAPO-17K, a 17K-prompt subset of the DAPO-Math training
pool. With dynamic sampling (at most 8 generation batches per step),
each of the 30 training steps collects $`B{=}64`$ prompts containing at
least one failed rollout, so training consumes 1,920 kept prompts in
total. The head is updated once per step, sweeping all failed rollouts
of the batch in mini-batches of 32 for one epoch, so the behavior policy
$`\pi_{\phi_{\text{old}}}`$ is simply the policy that generated the
step’s data. Rollouts and regenerations use identical sampling settings.
When a cut position falls inside an unclosed \<think\> block, we append
\</think\> before injection so that the assistant turn remains
well-formed, at both training and inference.

lists all hyperparameters.

##### Inference settings.

The gate threshold is $`\gamma{=}0.4`$;
Appendix [D](#A4.SS0.SSS0.Px4 "Sensitivity to 𝛾. ‣ Appendix D Gate Details ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
shows that results are robust to it. Each flagged rollout is corrected
once and replaced by its regeneration, with no iterative rounds. The cut
position is sampled from $`\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid q,o)`$.

All evaluation benchmarks (AIME24/25/26, AMC23, HMMT, MATH500) are

is an independently sampled, topic-balanced subset of the same DAPO
training pool.

with vLLM 0.15.1 for generation, transformers 4.57.1, and PyTorch 2.9.1.

## Appendix D Gate Details

##### Training.

The gate is trained once and reused unchanged across all experiments.
Its training data comes from a single rollout pass, independent of the
correction policy: we roll out 1,197 DAPO prompts, an independently
sampled subset of the training pool in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Implementation Details ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction"),
with Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (16 samples per prompt), label each rollout
with a valid final answer by correctness, and cache the hidden state
$`h_{\mathrm{box}}`$ of the frozen encoder at the final \boxed{} token.
Class-balancing yields 2,874 examples, on which the gate MLP, a
66K-parameter network separate from the 130K position head, is fit in
seconds with the binary cross-entropy loss

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\psi)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big[y_{j}\log\hat{p}_{j}+(1-y_{j})\log\big(1-\hat{p}_{j}\big)\Big],\qquad\hat{p}_{j}=\sigma\!\big(\mathrm{MLP}_{\psi}(h_{\mathrm{box},j})\big),
``` |  | (10) |

where $`y_{j}\in\{0,1\}`$ is the correctness label. The temperature
$`\kappa`$ in
Eq. ([9](#S5.E9 "In 5.3 Gated Inference ‣ 5 Method ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction"))
is calibrated post hoc on a held-out prompt split by minimizing BCE with
the MLP weights frozen, giving $`\kappa=1.38`$. The same gate is reused
for every reasoner in our experiments, including thinking-mode models of
different scales, since its features come from the frozen encoder
reading the question and rollout text and are not tied to the generating
model. The only non-trivial cost is the single rollout pass, so the gate
is effectively free to fit, and in practice never needs refitting.

##### Learned versus ground-truth gating.

The gate decides only *which* rollouts are corrected; it does not choose
the cut, write the instruction, or regenerate. To see how much the
reported numbers depend on that decision, we replace it with an oracle.
In the ground-truth condition (+GT), a rollout is flagged exactly when
its answer is wrong, instead of by the learned gate. Everything else is
held fixed, so the oracle bounds what a perfect gate could buy.

##### Sensitivity to $`\gamma`$.

Table [10](#A4.T10 "Table 10 ‣ Sensitivity to 𝛾. ‣ Appendix D Gate Details ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
sweeps $`\gamma\in[0,1]`$ over four reasoners and six benchmarks. At
$`\gamma{=}0`$ no rollout is corrected and the pool is the uncorrected
16 rollouts; from $`\gamma{=}0.1`$ on, Avg varies by at most $`0.4`$
points and Pass by at most $`0.1`$, so the reported gains do not depend
on tuning $`\gamma`$. Because gate scores concentrate near the extremes

Table [8](#A4.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Ranking quality. ‣ Appendix D Gate Details ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")),

essentially flat. We therefore set $`\gamma{=}0.4`$ as a cost–coverage
compromise rather than by tuning for accuracy.

## Appendix E Wall-Clock Cost

The fixed-budget protocol controls generation budgets, but does not
guarantee equal wall-clock time. UPTURN requires two dependent
generation stages, gate evaluation of all initial rollouts, and position
selection and retained-prefix processing for flagged rollouts. These
extra steps may offset the time saved by decoding fewer tokens. We
therefore time both pipelines end to end on one idle NVIDIA B200 with
Qwen3-4B on AIME24 at $`L_{\max}{=}8192`$. Each pipeline is executed
twice; sampling is seeded, so generation and token counts are identical
across the two repetitions. We report wall-clock time as the mean
$`\pm`$ half the absolute difference between the two measurements.
Base@32 generates all 32 rollouts per question in a single $`n{=}32`$
batch.

reports generated calls and decoded tokens per question and total
wall-clock time for the benchmark, excluding the same 32 s engine
start-up cost for every method.

## Appendix F Does a Second Correction Round Help?

UPTURN corrects each flagged rollout only once. We examine whether
further rounds improve performance on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B across all
six benchmarks, using up to three correction rounds under two rules.
*Gated* accepts a regeneration only if it raises the gate score and
stops correcting a rollout once its answer repeats; *naive* always
replaces the current rollout and applies all three rounds. Here, *gated*
refers to the acceptance and stopping rules, not the threshold used to
select rollouts for correction. Both variants start from the same cached
16-rollout pool of
Table [1](#S6.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 6.2 Main results ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction"),
so round 0 is identical. We set $`\gamma{=}1.0`$ to remove
threshold-based selection: all rollouts are initially eligible for
correction, while subsequent rounds follow each variant’s stopping rule.
Because this protocol differs from the main evaluation, we focus on
changes across rounds rather than absolute comparisons with
Table [1](#S6.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 6.2 Main results ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction").

Figure 7: Avg and Pass against the number of correction rounds, averaged
over six benchmarks. Colour marks the reasoner; solid lines are *gated*,
dashed lines *naive*.

Figure [7](#A6.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix F Does a Second Correction Round Help? ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
shows that the first correction accounts for most of the improvement
under both rules. Under *gated* acceptance, additional rounds provide no
further gains and slightly reduce Avg or Pass in some settings. At
round 1, this rule achieves higher Avg and Pass than *naive* replacement
on both reasoners. With further naive rounds, performance continues to
improve on Qwen3-4B, while Qwen3-8B peaks at round 2 and declines
slightly at round 3, remaining above round 1 on both metrics. Overall,
additional rounds require further generation but offer smaller, rule-
and reasoner-dependent benefits, motivating our single-round default.

## Appendix G Sensitivity to the Injection Instruction

UPTURN injects a fixed instruction, “Resolve this concisely.”, at the
chosen cut. To separate the effect of the turn-boundary structure from
that of the wording, we rerun the deployed pipeline on all six
benchmarks varying only the injected text; the phase-1 pool, gate,
position policy, budget, and seed are identical, so only the
continuation after the cut differs. We compare four task-relevant
instructions and three controls: the reflection token “Wait.”, the
meaningless “Turtle.”, and *None*, an empty user turn in which only the
chat-template turn-boundary tokens are inserted at the cut with no text
at all.

shows two effects. First, the injection structure matters most: every

## Appendix H Paragraph Boundaries are Forking Points

UPTURN restricts candidate cuts to \n\n boundaries. To check that this
does not miss where a trace actually forks, we compute the next-token
entropy at every position of the incorrect rollouts from the 16-rollout
pool of
Table [1](#S6.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 6.2 Main results ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
on AIME24/25, using each reasoner itself.
Table [13](#A8.T13 "Table 13 ‣ Appendix H Paragraph Boundaries are Forking Points ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
shows the same pattern for all four reasoners. Entropy is consistently
high right after a paragraph boundary and near zero at a typical
position, so every boundary is a forking point. Boundaries are only
$`2`$–$`3\%`$ of all positions (*boundary frac.*), yet they cover
$`18`$–$`35\%`$ of the most uncertain positions, the top-5% by entropy
(*top-5% coverage*).

|  | Boundary entropy |  | Other entropy |  | Top-5% | Boundary |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| Reasoner | mean | median | mean | median | coverage | frac. |
| Qwen3-4B | 0.62 | 0.61 | 0.29 | 0.008 | 18% | 2.0% |
| Qwen3-8B | 0.60 | 0.59 | 0.27 | 0.004 | 19% | 2.0% |
| DeepSeek-R1-Qwen3-8B | 0.99 | 1.01 | 0.31 | 0.018 | 35% | 3.2% |
| QwQ-32B | 0.81 | 0.80 | 0.41 | 0.095 | 22% | 2.6% |

Table 13: Next-token entropy at \n\n boundaries vs. elsewhere on
incorrect AIME24/25 rollouts; coverage is the share of top-5% entropy
positions within $`\pm 2`$ tokens of a boundary.

A candidate set chosen by entropy would therefore contain the boundaries
plus some extra positions inside paragraphs, while giving up two
practical advantages: a \n\n cut always ends at a complete paragraph, so
the turn boundary is grafted onto a well-formed prefix, whereas an
entropy-chosen cut can fall in the middle of a sentence, and \n\n
positions are found by string matching, whereas entropy-based candidates
require an extra forward pass of the reasoner over every rollout at
inference time. We therefore use \n\n boundaries as the candidate set of
the position policy.

## Appendix K Comparison with Reflection-Token Injection

Table [15](#A11.T15 "Table 15 ‣ Appendix K Comparison with Reflection-Token Injection ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
breaks the injection rows of
Table [2](#S6.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 6.3 Comparison with self-correction baselines ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
down by reasoner and benchmark. AlphaOne ([Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib19)) and CyclicReflex ([Fan et al., 2026](#bib.bib20))
steer the same trajectory by injecting reflection tokens inline, whereas
UPTURN cuts it at a learned position and re-opens a user turn; none of
the three trains the reasoner.

These are not correction methods but ways of steering a single
generation, so we place them in UPTURN’s correction slot: UPTURN’s gate
selects the rollouts of the 16-rollout pool of
Table [1](#S6.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 6.2 Main results ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction")
that are likely wrong, and each method regenerates only those from the
problem, aggregated with the same rule. The three therefore differ only
in how a flagged rollout is redone. Budget is the number of new tokens a
correction may generate, and every method is run at both $`L_{\max}`$
and $`2L_{\max}`$. At $`L_{\max}`$ the two injection methods stay close
to Base@32: a regeneration that must also re-derive the discarded prefix

rollout therefore composes with rewriting it, and pays off most where
the rewrite had the least room to finish.
````

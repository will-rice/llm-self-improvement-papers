# Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion: The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims

###### Abstract

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) promises AI systems, such as
self-evolving LLM agents, that become better at producing further
improvements. Evaluating this promise requires separating product claims
about the final system from process claims about the improvement process
itself. Existing surveys specify the evidence these claims require, but
how often studies report that evidence remains unmeasured. We examine
this question through full-text coding of 114 eligible studies in a
reproducible arXiv search frame frozen in October 2026, including 90
that describe their own systems as RSI. The codebook links claims to
evaluation designs. Independent human coding, third-reader adjudication,
and a separate blind coding of improver comparisons assess coding
reliability. Among the 90 RSI studies, 90% report product evaluation
beyond development tasks under a broad criterion that includes partial
or unstated separation, while 60% report evaluation on tasks unused for
selection. Only 22% audit two or more reruns of the full loop. In a
human-coded random sample, the RSI studies show the same contrast: 45%
evaluate on tasks unused for selection and 14% audit two or more reruns.
Of the 26 studies claiming a better improvement procedure, 23 report a
related comparison, but only 4 compare earlier and later procedures from
identical starting artifacts at a matched budget. Across these results
and a comparison set of established works, evidence for repeatability
and improved procedures remains less developed than product evaluation.
Three propositions explain how selection effects, shared evaluator
errors, and changes in system state limit what product evaluations can
establish. Together, the findings support a claim-indexed reporting
standard that connects each claim to the evaluation design needed to
test it.

## 1 Introduction

Language models increasingly participate in the processes that improve
AI systems. They revise prompts and agent harnesses, which coordinate a
model’s tools and actions \[[1](#bib.bib27), [2](#bib.bib85),
[3](#bib.bib15), [4](#bib.bib14)\]. They also generate feedback for
updating model weights \[[5](#bib.bib99), [6](#bib.bib98),
[7](#bib.bib19)\] and modify data and training pipelines
\[[8](#bib.bib13), [9](#bib.bib17)\]. Some systems also modify the
procedure that proposes changes, which we call the _improver_, or the
evaluator that decides which changes to retain \[[10](#bib.bib86),
[11](#bib.bib28), [12](#bib.bib10), [13](#bib.bib102),
[14](#bib.bib12)\]. These developments motivate the ambition of
recursive self-improvement (RSI): retained improvements should enable
the system to produce further improvements, potentially by making the
improver itself more effective.

This ambition creates an evaluation problem. A higher final benchmark
score establishes something about the resulting system, but it does not
by itself establish how the improvement process behaves. Whether gains
accumulate across rounds, recur in independent runs, or result from a
better improver are distinct empirical questions. We refer to these as
_process claims_, in contrast to a _product claim_ about the performance
of the final system. Evaluating RSI requires matching the evidence to
the particular claim being made.

The distinction matters because the signal used to select changes can
disagree with the property the system is intended to improve. In one
self-play study, a model’s own judge increasingly accepts its outputs
while hidden exact-match accuracy remains unchanged
\[[15](#bib.bib23)\]. In a robotics study, accepted skill changes
repeatedly pass their tests, yet the target task never succeeds
\[[16](#bib.bib11)\]. Both cases distinguish the _development signal_,
which guides the loop’s choices, from an _audit_, which assesses the
claimed improvement outside those choices. Even a successful audit of
the final product leaves open whether the loop produces gains repeatably
or whether its improver has become better.

Recent surveys provide principles for answering these questions. [Wu et
al. \[17\]](#bib.bib113) distinguish task gains, retention and transfer,
and improver gains. Their evaluation protocol includes protected test
tasks, repeated runs, resource-matched controls, and comparisons of
earlier and later improvers from common starting states. [Wang et al.
\[18\]](#bib.bib112) require audit evidence appropriate to the component
being changed, while [Chen et al. \[19\]](#bib.bib1) organize feedback
sources by their verification strength. These accounts establish what
evidence to seek. However, they do not measure how often studies report
it. The remaining question is therefore empirical: _how closely does the
reported evaluation of RSI systems match the claims made about their
improvement processes?_

We address this question through a census of eligible studies in a
reproducible arXiv search frame. We code each study from its full text,
using a codebook that translates existing evaluation principles into
observable features of reported experiments. The dataset covers 114
eligible studies, of which 90 describe their own loops or systems as
RSI, and records each study’s claims, development signal, evaluation
boundary, grader, and process controls, with source locators identifying
the supporting passages.

We ask three research questions. RQ1 asks, for each claim that RSI
studies make, how often they report an evaluation design that bears on
it. RQ2 asks whether the studies that claim a better improver report
comparisons that can isolate an improver gain. RQ3 asks whether the
findings hold under independent human coding and in established
self-improvement research outside the search frame. We assess robustness
through a random sample coded independently by a human and adjudicated
by a third reader, separate blind coding of the improver comparisons,
and a comparison set of 20 representative works selected by [Wu et al.
\[17\]](#bib.bib113), which we call anchor works.

For RQ1, the broad product-evaluation indicator covers 90% of the 90
studies. This indicator includes partial or unstated separation from
development. However, only 60% report evaluation on tasks unused for any
selection. Most studies that claim accumulating gains audit across
rounds, whereas repeated evaluation of the loop itself is uncommon: 22%
audit two or more reruns.

For RQ2, 23 of the 26 studies claiming a better improver report a
related comparison. These comparisons usually favor the proposed
process. However, only 4 compare an earlier and a later improver from
identical starting artifacts at a matched budget. This design isolates
the improver’s contribution from changes in the system on which it acts.
For RQ3, the human-coded sample confirms that audited reruns are rarer
than selection-independent product evaluation within the same studies
(14% against 45% for its RSI studies), and the anchor works show the
same pattern. Studies’ own experiments further document cases in which
development gains transfer only partly, or not at all, to external
evaluation.

The study contributes both measurement and guidance for evaluation.
First, the dataset records claims and evidence for every eligible study
in the frozen search frame, including the adjudicated sample and
independently coded improver comparisons. Second, the accompanying
codebook makes the evaluation requirements measurable and provides
evidence on coding reliability. These two resources answer RQ1–RQ3.
Third, three propositions explain the limits of inferring process gains
from product evidence. Finally, a reporting standard links each claim to
the required evaluation, using the observed rates as a baseline.

## 2 Related Work

Our study draws on three lines of research: surveys that organize
self-improving systems, research on evaluation reliability, and
empirical meta-research that measures evaluation practice across
published papers. The surveys identify which components can change and
what those changes are intended to achieve. Evaluation research explains
why an apparent gain may depend on the feedback source, the grader, or
the use of test data. Meta-research supplies the method: coding
published papers to measure how claims are evaluated. We apply that
method to the claims of RSI research.

Surveys of self-evolving agents organize methods by the object, timing,
mechanism, and setting of an update \[[20](#bib.bib109),
[21](#bib.bib3)\]. Related accounts describe the cycle from experience
acquisition to evaluation \[[22](#bib.bib4)\], distinguish
foundation-model and scaffold updates \[[23](#bib.bib2)\], and connect
RSI design choices to classical learning mechanisms \[[24](#bib.bib6)\].
Feedback taxonomies distinguish internal, external, multi-agent, and
human signals \[[25](#bib.bib108)\], while safety surveys examine how
risks persist through retained state \[[26](#bib.bib5),
[27](#bib.bib36)\]. Roadmaps and formal frameworks further characterize
the requirements and limits of RSI \[[28](#bib.bib7), [29](#bib.bib9),
[30](#bib.bib8)\].

Three surveys provide the closest basis for our coding framework. [Wu et
al. \[17\]](#bib.bib113) distinguish task gains, retention and transfer,
and improver gains. They propose a paired probe that applies earlier and
later improvers to the same starting states under the same budget,
alongside sealed test tasks, scheduled checkpoints, repeated runs, and
fixed-improver controls. Their protocol descriptions cover eight
selected works. [Wang et al. \[18\]](#bib.bib112) organize
self-evolution into five levels, from outputs to evaluation criteria,
and match each level to external audits and controls. [Chen et al.
\[19\]](#bib.bib1) map 1,250 arXiv records by the component being
improved and the extent of autonomous loop closure, using title and
abstract keyword rules. Their verification hierarchy characterizes
feedback quality qualitatively.

Two further surveys offer complementary qualitative assessments. [Ye et
al. \[31\]](#bib.bib114) separate task-level refinement, lasting
self-improvement and RSI, which also requires a better capacity to make
further improvements, and call for independent evaluation and full cost
reporting. [Wu et al. \[32\]](#bib.bib115) synthesize evidence on AI
improving AI and conclude that evidence for persistent and compounding
improvement remains limited.

The need to distinguish development feedback from evaluation is already
apparent in task-local self-correction. Self-feedback can improve an
answer through iterative refinement \[[33](#bib.bib87)\], but reasoning
corrections often fail without external signals \[[34](#bib.bib82)\].
Tool-based critique can provide such signals \[[35](#bib.bib83)\].
Surveys synthesize these findings across correction strategies
\[[36](#bib.bib104), [37](#bib.bib105)\]. Persistent self-improvement
extends the question beyond a single answer. Systems retain updates
based on correctness-filtered rationales \[[38](#bib.bib91)\],
self-generated judgments \[[5](#bib.bib99), [13](#bib.bib102)\],
human-anchored self-play \[[6](#bib.bib98)\], or AI feedback
\[[39](#bib.bib30)\]. Agents can also preserve reflections, skills, and
experience across tasks \[[40](#bib.bib89), [41](#bib.bib38),
[42](#bib.bib39)\]. Retention makes gains reusable, but it can also
preserve errors: recursive data generation and self-play can narrow
distributions or degrade performance \[[43](#bib.bib110),
[44](#bib.bib84), [45](#bib.bib101)\].

The evaluator itself provides another source of error. LLM judges
exhibit position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases
\[[46](#bib.bib90), [47](#bib.bib106)\]. Reward models can be assessed
against preference data \[[48](#bib.bib107)\], yet continued
optimization of their scores can reduce the underlying reward
\[[49](#bib.bib96)\]. Formal work characterizes reward gaming
\[[50](#bib.bib92)\], and experiments show that behavior learned in
gameable environments can extend to reward tampering
\[[51](#bib.bib29)\]. Weak supervisors can nevertheless elicit stronger
behavior under some conditions \[[52](#bib.bib97)\]. These results make
the validity of the feedback source an empirical question, especially
when an RSI loop can modify its own evaluator.

Even a fixed evaluator can become unreliable when its data are reused
for selection. The reusable holdout \[[53](#bib.bib111)\] and the Ladder
mechanism \[[54](#bib.bib94)\] address this adaptive reuse, although
empirical studies of ImageNet and Kaggle report less overfitting than
worst-case theory predicts \[[55](#bib.bib95), [56](#bib.bib93)\]. [Sun
et al. \[57\]](#bib.bib37) apply this reasoning to RSI through REUSE, a
promotion framework with a specified error guarantee. In their live
experiments, the share of false promotions falls from up to 20.7% under
existing evaluation frameworks to zero. This line of work motivates the
distinction between a development score used for selection and an audit
used to assess the resulting claim.

Recent benchmarks make these evaluation questions concrete. They examine
retained memory and skills \[[58](#bib.bib31)\], data-centric research
\[[59](#bib.bib32)\], algorithm design \[[60](#bib.bib33)\], and
embodied improvement under matched source conditions
\[[61](#bib.bib34)\]. Other environments support RSI experiments
directly \[[62](#bib.bib58), [63](#bib.bib59)\], track skill evolution
across rounds \[[64](#bib.bib35)\], or compare evolving and non-evolving
agents to attribute safety failures to earlier updates
\[[65](#bib.bib26)\]. Together, these studies supply the evaluation
principles and controls that our census measures.

Methodologically, the study belongs to empirical meta-research on
machine learning evaluation, which codes published papers or reruns
published experiments to measure how results are established. [Gundersen
and Kjensmo \[66\]](#bib.bib117) scored 400 AAAI and IJCAI papers on the
variables needed to reproduce them and found that none documented all of
them. [Marie et al. \[67\]](#bib.bib119) annotated the evaluations in
769 machine translation papers and found a growing reliance on
differences in BLEU scores without significance testing or human
evaluation. [Henderson et al. \[68\]](#bib.bib116) showed that variance
across runs makes reported deep reinforcement learning results hard to
interpret, and [Agarwal et al. \[69\]](#bib.bib88) showed that most
results on these benchmarks are point estimates from few runs. [Dodge et
al. \[70\]](#bib.bib118) found model comparisons whose conclusions would
change with more or less computation, and [Kapoor and Narayanan
\[71\]](#bib.bib120) documented leakage between training and evaluation
data across many fields that adopted machine learning. These studies
examine how researchers support claims about final models. RSI research
adds claims about the improvement process, so we code the evidence for
each claim separately. The next section specifies how the evaluation
principles map to the claims made by RSI studies.

## 3 Measurement Framework

The codebook measures three aspects of an RSI study: the kinds of change
a loop makes, the roles of development and audit evidence, and the
claims that RSI studies make. We express the distinctions of [Wu et al.
\[17\]](#bib.bib113) and [Wang et al. \[18\]](#bib.bib112) as items that
can be coded from a paper, with separate attention to the repeatability
of the loop and the independence of the audit grader.

The first distinction concerns what changes. Following prior work
\[[36](#bib.bib104), [18](#bib.bib112)\], we separate four kinds of
change. A task-local correction changes the answer or trajectory for the
current task only. A persistent change alters retained state, such as
weights, prompts, memory, tools, skills or harness code, and that state
affects later tasks. A structural change alters the improver. A
criterion change alters the evaluator or the definition of success. A
study can make several of these changes at once. Retaining state does
not establish that the state improves later performance. Similarly, an
editable improver is not necessarily a more effective one.

Evaluating these changes requires separating two roles for feedback. The
development signal guides proposals, rankings, or acceptance decisions
within the loop. The signal can be a unit test, an environment outcome,
a reference answer, the system’s own judgment, or a learned scorer such
as a reward model or an LLM judge. We use the term _model judge_ for
such learned scorers.

An audit evaluates the claimed improvement independently of the choices
made during development. This independence has two components. The first
concerns the tasks. The matched-audit principle of [Wang et al.
\[18\]](#bib.bib112) and the sealed test tasks of [Wu et al.
\[17\]](#bib.bib113) require that audit tasks were never used to select
changes, checkpoints or hyperparameters. We call such tasks held out.
The second concerns the grader: the audit grader must not share the
development judge’s systematic errors. A held-out benchmark scored by
the same judge family that guided the search has the first kind of
independence and may lack the second.

lists the claims that RSI papers make, from product performance to

is better than the initial system under a fixed evaluation budget. It
corresponds to the task gain of [Wu et al. \[17\]](#bib.bib113). The
other four claims concern the process. The claims are distinct rather
than nested. Repeatable final gains need not rise at every round, and a
later improver can outperform an earlier one at a common state even when
the historical trajectory does not accumulate gains. A study therefore
needs the evidence for each claim it makes, and a study that claims only
a better product needs only the evidence for C1.

Accumulation (C2) says that audited performance rises across retained
rounds, which scheduled audits of intermediate checkpoints can show.
Repeatability (C3) says that the loop, rerun from the same start,
produces gains, so the result is not one lucky trajectory. Accumulation
in one run and repeatability across runs therefore require different
observations.

Improver gain (C4) says that a later improver produces better successors
than an earlier one from the same start and with the same budget. A
_swap test_ checks this claim by applying the earlier and later
improvers to the same starting system and auditing the successors they
produce. By contrast, the frozen-improver control of [Zhang et al.
\[1\]](#bib.bib27) compares a run in which the improver can change with
one in which it remains fixed. This evolving-versus-frozen comparison
tests whether allowing the improver to change helps over a full run.

Compounding (C5) extends C4 by claiming that improver gains themselves
grow over time. C4 captures the distinctively recursive claim that the
improvement procedure itself becomes more effective. A loop with a fixed
improver can make C1 through C3 true, and many useful systems are of
this kind. Repeatability matters for any iterative loop, recursive or
not.

These claims also depend on the evaluation scope and comparator. The
scope is the set of tasks, domains and evaluation conditions on which
the claim is tested. A claim stated for open-ended domains but tested on
executable benchmarks has a scope gap, even if every reported number is
sound.

The comparator measures the gain obtainable with the same computational
resources without recursion, for example from best-of-$`N`$ sampling,
from a fixed improver run for the same number of proposals, or from a
larger search with the initial system \[[17](#bib.bib113)\]. We call a
baseline of this kind a budget-matched comparator. This comparison
distinguishes gains attributable to the update mechanism from those
obtainable by spending the same resources on additional search
\[[12](#bib.bib10), [8](#bib.bib13)\]. The following analysis explains
why these distinctions matter: selection, grading, and the changing
state of the system can each make product evidence insufficient for a
process claim.

## 4 Limits of Product Evaluation

We analyze three evaluation problems behind the claim distinctions in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3 Measurement Framework ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims"):
selecting among noisy scores, sharing errors between development and
audit graders, and changing the system and its improver together. For
each problem, we state a proposition and connect it to the corresponding
census items. The first is the winner’s curse: selecting the highest
noisy score can overstate performance. Work on adaptive data analysis
addresses this effect \[[53](#bib.bib111), [54](#bib.bib94)\], and [Sun
et al. \[57\]](#bib.bib37) provide a formal treatment for RSI through a
certified promotion rule. We illustrate this selection effect in terms
of accepted changes and reported development gain. To our knowledge, the
second and third have not been stated in this form for RSI. Proofs are
in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Proofs ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").

#### Setup.

A loop runs for rounds $`t=0,1,\dots`$. At each round it holds a
retained system $`S_{t}`$, an improver $`I_{t}`$ and a development judge
$`J_{t}`$. The improver proposes candidates. The judge gives each
candidate $`c`$ a development score $`r(c)`$, and a promotion rule
decides whether $`c`$ replaces the incumbent. Let $`u(s)`$ denote the
quantity the claim is about: the expected score of system $`s`$ on the
reference task distribution under a fixed evaluation budget.

We write the development score as $`r(c)=u(c)+e(c)`$, where $`e(c)`$ is
the development error. An audit produces $`\hat{a}(c)=u(c)+\alpha(c)`$,
where the audit error $`\alpha(c)`$ depends on the audit tasks and
grader specified below. We assume the development score, the audit score
and $`u`$ share a scale, or can be put on one by a common affine
calibration under which the additive error model holds. A merely
monotone calibration preserves rankings but not expected differences, so
extending the results to that case would require a separate argument.
Without a common calibration, the gap between development and audited
gains is not identified.

### 4.1 A null-loop benchmark

###### Proposition 1 (Null-loop benchmark).

Suppose the loop scores the initial system $`s_{0}`$ and $`N`$ proposals
on a fixed development set, and accepts a proposal whenever its
development score exceeds the incumbent’s. Suppose no proposal is
better: $`u(c)=u(s_{0})`$ for every candidate. Suppose the development
errors $`e(s_{0}),e(c_{1}),\dots,e(c_{N})`$ are independent and
identically distributed with a continuous distribution and variance
$`\sigma^{2}`$, and that the audit error has conditional mean zero given
the system, $`\mathbb{E}[\alpha(S)\mid S]=0`$. Then:

1.  (i)
    the expected number of accepted proposals is
    $`\sum_{i=2}^{N+1}1/i`$, which equals 2.9 for $`N=25`$, 4.2 for
    $`N=99`$ and 6.5 for $`N=1000`$;
2.  (ii)
    the reported development gain $`r(S_{\mathrm{final}})-r(s_{0})`$ is
    nonnegative, and with Gaussian errors its expectation is
    $`\sigma\,m_{N+1}`$, where $`m_{n}`$ is the expected maximum of
    $`n`$ standard normal variables (for example $`m_{100}=2.51`$);
3.  (iii)
    the audited gain $`\hat{a}(S_{\mathrm{final}})-\hat{a}(s_{0})`$ has
    expected value zero.

Under this null model, development scores rise and changes are accepted
even though true performance remains constant. The spurious gain grows
with the number of candidates scored, not with the number of rounds.
Three evaluation designs address this selection effect. An audit on
tasks unused for selection, checkpoint choice, or hyperparameter tuning
provides an external assessment (census item A2). A null control runs
the same loop with shuffled feedback or proposals that cannot help,
directly estimating the spurious gain (A5). A noise-aware promotion rule
accounts for uncertainty when accepting a change (A6).

These promotion rules offer different guarantees. Repeated evaluation
reduces noise in each comparison, whereas a certified rule such as REUSE
also controls the error that accumulates when the same data are reused
adaptively across many comparisons \[[57](#bib.bib37)\]. The census item
records both and does not distinguish them. The proposition also
explains why reusing the final benchmark to choose a checkpoint weakens
an otherwise held-out result, since each reuse is one more draw from
which the maximum is taken.

The null-loop benchmark isolates the apparent gain that selection
produces under independent development errors. It is a reference point
rather than a bound for real loops, in which errors on shared
development tasks are correlated, adaptively generated proposals need
not have exchangeable errors, and acceptance margins change the number
of records. Perfectly correlated errors, for example, produce no
accepted changes at all. The conditional-mean assumption behind (iii) is
what an audit with tasks and a grader that played no part in selection
is meant to secure, but separation alone does not guarantee it.

### 4.2 Coupled audits inherit development bias

Separating audit tasks from selection addresses one source of optimism.
A second remains when the audit grader shares systematic errors with the
development judge. Write the development error as
$`e(c)=b(c)+\varepsilon(c)`$, where $`b`$ is a systematic bias of the
judge, such as a preference for plausible, long or self-generated
outputs, and $`\varepsilon`$ is noise. Let the audit error be
$`\alpha(c)=\lambda\,b(c)+\xi(c)`$. The coefficient $`\lambda`$ measures
how strongly the audit grader shares the development judge’s bias:
$`\lambda=0`$ for a grader whose errors are unrelated to $`b`$, and
$`\lambda=1`$ for the same judge used in the same way. Grader family
alone does not fix $`\lambda`$: judges from different families can share
a bias, and the same model can share less of it under a different prompt
or task.

###### Proposition 2 (Coupled audits).

Suppose the residual audit error has conditional mean zero,
$`\mathbb{E}[\xi(S)\mid S]=0`$, for every system $`S`$ the loop can
select. Then the expected audited gain differs from the expected true
gain by exactly
$`\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[\,b(S_{\mathrm{final}})-b(s_{0})\,]`$. If in
addition $`\lambda\geq 0`$, each accepted change raises $`b`$ by at
least $`\delta>0`$ and no change lowers it, the audited gain overstates
the true gain by at least $`\lambda\,\delta`$ times the expected number
of accepted changes.

The identity makes the conditions for an informative audit explicit.
Held-out tasks make the residual condition plausible but do not
guarantee it, and the sign of the distortion depends on $`\lambda`$ and
on how $`b`$ changes. Two consequences follow. First, a held-out
benchmark is not enough when its grader shares the development judge’s
bias (census item A3). The self-play judge in [Zhou \[15\]](#bib.bib23)
learned false positives that other judge families also accepted, which
is a case of $`\lambda>0`$ across families. Second, $`\lambda`$ can only
be estimated against a reference grader whose errors are unrelated to
$`b`$, such as an executable check of the claimed property, an expert
panel with measured agreement, or a fixed labeled set. Verifiable tasks
supply such a reference for the property they test. Open-ended tasks
often do not, and this is where the independence requirement moves from
the training signal to the audit.

#### Remark: criterion drift.

When the loop changes its own judge, $`J_{t}`$ varies across rounds, and
development scores from different rounds are no longer on one scale. A
rising development curve can then come from the change in the criterion
alone. This adds a temporal source of bias to the shared-error problem
in
Proposition [2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Coupled audits). ‣ 4.2 Coupled audits inherit development bias ‣ 4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").
A fixed external reference that gates each change of evaluator, as in
[Iacob et al. \[14\]](#bib.bib12), restores comparability for the
evaluators, but not for the outputs those evaluators grade.

### 4.3 The diagonal problem

The preceding results concern how gains are measured. Improver claims
introduce a further question: whether gains arise from a better
improvement procedure or from the state on which that procedure acts.
Here $`S_{t}`$ denotes the solver state: everything in the retained
system other than the improver $`I_{t}`$. We assume that any improver
can be recombined with any solver state, as when the improver is a
separable module.

A transition kernel $`K(\cdot\mid S_{t},I_{t})`$ specifies the
probability distribution of each round’s outcome: the next solver state
and audited gain $`(S_{t+1},\Delta_{t})`$. Let $`g(s,I)`$ be the
expected audited gain under $`K`$ when improver $`I`$ acts on solver
state $`s`$ with budget $`B`$. Improver gain (C4) at state $`s`$ means
$`g(s,I_{t^{\prime}})>g(s,I_{t})`$ for a later improver
$`I_{t^{\prime}}`$. A run evaluates $`K`$ only at the pairs
$`(S_{t},I_{t})`$ it visits.

###### Proposition 3 (Diagonal non-identification).

Suppose the improver is a deterministic function of the solver state,
$`I_{t}=\phi(S_{t})`$, where the solver state includes whatever part of
the run’s history determines the improver. Then the joint distribution
of states and audited gains, across any number of runs started from any
initial states, depends on $`K`$ only through its restriction
$`K(\cdot\mid s,\phi(s))`$. In particular, the kernel
$`\tilde{K}(\cdot\mid s,I)=K(\cdot\mid s,\phi(s))`$, under which the
improver has no effect on outcomes, generates the same distribution of
observables. The improver contrast $`g(s,I^{\prime})-g(s,\phi(s))`$ for
$`I^{\prime}\neq\phi(s)`$ is therefore not identified.

The proposition describes a deterministic coupling with no overlap: at
each solver state the run observes only one improver. Here,
non-identification means that different explanations of the improver’s
effect are compatible with exactly the same observed data. Co-evolution
does not by itself imply this coupling, since different histories or
random updates can produce different improvers at the same solver state,
but loops that edit their improver only through their own retained
changes come close to it. The identification problem arises because
state and improver change together. Gains can shrink as the solver
approaches its performance ceiling even while the improver gets better.
They can also grow when a fixed improver reaches states that are easier
to improve. The observed trajectory alone cannot distinguish these
explanations.

A two-state example shows why matching scores does not help. Let solver
states $`A`$ and $`B`$ both score zero, and let any improver produce an
expected gain of 0.1 from $`A`$ and 0.8 from $`B`$. An earlier improver
observed at $`A`$ and a later improver observed at $`B`$ differ by 0.7,
although the improver contrast is zero at each state.

Under the unrestricted model, identification therefore requires
observations off the diagonal: applying an improver to a state with
which it was not originally observed. The most direct design runs an
earlier and a later improver from identical starting artifacts, or from
a declared distribution of starting states with paired or randomized
assignment, under the same budget, and audits their successors. This is
the swap test, which [Wu et al. \[17\]](#bib.bib113) call a paired probe
and [Srikanth et al. \[12\]](#bib.bib10) an ignition test. It is related
to clade-level metaproductivity, the performance of an agent’s
descendants, which [Wang et al. \[72\]](#bib.bib24) use to guide search.
Randomized assignment of improvers to solver states, or structural
assumptions on $`K`$, can also supply off-diagonal information.
Additional trajectory data alone cannot.

The target quantity, or estimand, should be stated: the difference in
expected audited gain at one solver state, or its average over a
declared set of starting states. Compounding claims extend the swap test
over a stated horizon, since a few rounds of rising product scores do
not establish growing improver gains.

An evolving-versus-frozen comparison at matched budget, in which one
run’s improver is held fixed \[[1](#bib.bib27)\], answers a different
question. Its frozen arm generally visits different solver states, so
its total effect measures the value of letting the improver change over
a run. Without shared states or further assumptions, it need not provide
the overlap required for the contrast between an earlier and a later
improver at a common state. These distinctions determine which
comparisons the census records.
Section [5](#S5 "5 Data and Methods ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
describes how we translate them into coding decisions.

## 5 Data and Methods

We answer the research questions with a quantitative content analysis of
published studies. We first define the search frame and eligibility
criteria, then describe the coded items, reliability checks, and
analysis.

### 5.1 Search frame and eligibility

The search frame consists of arXiv records whose title or abstract
contains _recursive self-improvement_ or a close variant, submitted up
to 17 October 2026. We ran 12 title and abstract queries through the
arXiv API and took their union, obtaining 173 records, including 85 with
the phrase in the title. Of these records, 153 were posted in 2026 and
106 on or after 1 September 2026.
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Search Log ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
provides the queries and posting chronology. Searching by label defines
a reproducible population for examining how RSI claims are evaluated.

A record is eligible if it presents an implemented loop in which an AI
system’s outputs, evaluations, or experience are used to propose and
retain changes, reports an experiment evaluating the result, and makes
an improvement claim. We exclude surveys, position papers, purely
theoretical analyses, and unrelated uses of the phrase. Benchmark,
environment, and evaluation papers are eligible only if they also
present and evaluate an improving system of their own. Records that
invoke RSI solely as motivation for a method that does not improve
itself are excluded, as are reports that specify a loop and an
evaluation protocol but never run the loop. A study that runs its loop
and describes the outcome qualitatively is eligible and enters the
lowest evidence tier. Eligibility does not depend on the direction of
the result, so studies whose loops fail remain in the census.

We screened titles and abstracts first, then read the full text of
records that passed this stage or remained uncertain. For records with
several versions, we used the latest version available on the freeze
date.

### 5.2 Coded items and dataset

The unit of analysis is the study. We first record the claims made in
its abstract, introduction, and conclusion: improved product
performance, accumulation of gains, and improvement of the improver. We
then code its evaluation using the items in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Codebook ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").
Descriptive items identify what changes, whether the changed artifact
belongs to the system running the loop, what persists between tasks, the
number of rounds, the domain, the development signal, and any evaluator
changes.

The evaluation items distinguish the source of the headline result (A1),
the separation of product evaluation from development and selection
(A2), and the audit grader (A3). A2 = Y records evaluation tasks unused
for selection, including checkpoint and hyperparameter choice. We call
this _selection-independent evaluation_. A2 = P covers three situations
that we record separately: the evaluation tasks are also used for some
selection, only part of the headline result is held out, or the paper
does not state the separation. We report both the strict share (Y) and
the combined share (Y or P). A3 records the type of audit grader.
Additional items record a budget-matched comparator (A4), a null control
with removed or randomized feedback (A5), and a noise-aware promotion
rule (A6).

Process items record audits across rounds or checkpoints (P1), paired
development and audit scores for those rounds (P2), independent reruns
of the full loop (P3), and whether at least two reruns have audit-level
outcomes (P3a).

Item P4 records whether a study reports an improver-related comparison
at a matched budget (Y), a partial or unmatched one (P), or none. We
distinguish three designs because they answer different questions. A
same-start comparison applies an earlier and a later improver to
identical starting artifacts: it is the swap test discussed in
Proposition [3](#Thmproposition3 "Proposition 3 (Diagonal non-identification). ‣ 4.3 The diagonal problem ‣ 4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").
An evolving-versus-frozen comparison tests the value of allowing the
improver to change by comparing a full loop run with one in which the
improver is fixed. Other comparisons include freezing an adaptive
evaluator or curriculum, or swapping between different models.

Finally, P5 records reported divergence between development and audit
outcomes. It is coded NA (not applicable) when no audit is paired with
development scores, because divergence cannot then be assessed. S1
records whether the scope that a study presents in its title, abstract
and introduction, including statements of motivation, exceeds the
setting it evaluates.

Source locators connect the coding to the relevant sections, tables,
figures, or appendices. The codes record which designs each study
reports, whatever their results. A control that a study does not report
counts as absent, not as failed. Four logical rules link the items. For
example, a study without an audit cannot report a per-round audit or a
development–audit divergence
(Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Codebook ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")).

Evidence tiers summarize which combinations of these designs a study
reports. E0 denotes a demonstration without quantitative evaluation of
the change, and E1 denotes a gain without evaluation beyond the
development tasks. E2 requires fully or partly held-out product
evaluation (A2 = Y or P). E3 additionally requires a per-round audit or
audited reruns, and E4 adds an improver-related comparison at a matched
budget (P4 = Y). Task-local studies are capped at E2. Because P4 pools
the three improver designs, E4 serves as a summary, and the primary
analysis relies on claim-specific indicators that separate the designs
relevant to each claim.

We apply the same codebook to 20 _anchor works_, the representative
studies selected by [Wu et al. \[17\]](#bib.bib113) in their Figure 8a.
This set ranges from STaR \[[38](#bib.bib91)\] and Self-Instruct
\[[73](#bib.bib103)\] to the Darwin Gödel Machine \[[1](#bib.bib27)\]
and Hyperagents \[[11](#bib.bib28)\]. Only 2 belong to our search frame,
so the other 18 provide a comparison with work outside the phrase-based
search.

Wu et al. also describe the evaluation protocols of eight anchor works
in their Table 7. We map those descriptions to three coding dimensions,
test separation, improver tests, and budget parity, and compare them
with our full-text codes. An improver test in their sense is a paired
probe or a frozen-improver control, so we compare it with our same-start
and evolving-versus-frozen designs and count other improver-related
comparisons as partial.

The resulting dataset contains 30 coded items for each of the 114
eligible studies, 3,420 codes in all, and the same evidence items for
the 20 anchor works. Each study carries locators to the sections, tables
and figures that support its codes. The dataset also records the
screening decision for each of the 173 records in the frame, the
adjudication of the double-coded sample and the independent codes of the
improver comparisons.

### 5.3 Coding procedure and reliability

We piloted the codebook on 14 studies spanning harness, weight, data,
improver, and evaluator updates, then froze the first version before
full coding. The pilot established that evaluation separation must
account for checkpoint and hyperparameter choice and that relevant
evidence must be sought in appendices as well as the main text.

An LLM-based assistant supported the first coding pass by reading each
full text, extracted from the PDF of the coded version, and proposing
every code with a supporting locator. The later recodes were produced
with the same assistance from passages retrieved from the same texts.
Human coders reviewed the double-coded sample and the improver
comparisons described below. No human reviewed the other codes
individually.

An independent human coder, blind to the first-pass codes, then coded a
random sample of 30 eligible studies. Agreement was high for the label,
domain, changed artifact, improver claim, reported uncertainty, and
audited reruns, with Cohen’s $`\kappa`$ between 0.81 and 0.91. Agreement
varied more for other evidence items
(Table [16](#A4.T16 "Table 16 ‣ Appendix D Per-Study Codes ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")).
The second coding was stricter about evaluation separation and more
inclusive of null controls, per-round audits, and improver tests. We
used these disagreements to clarify the decision rules in codebook v1.1
and recoded all eligible and anchor studies on the affected items.

A third reader then settled each of the 177 disagreements between the
two codings from the full text. The reader saw both sets of codes
without knowing their source, in an order randomized by paper, and every
decision required a locator. The third reader upheld the human coding in
174 cases and the first pass in 3, so the first pass agreed with the
adjudicated codes on 81% of the 900 coded cells. The AI-assisted first
pass therefore erred substantially more often than the independent human
coding, and we treat the adjudicated codes as the reference for the
sample.

Agreement statistics refer to the two blind codings before adjudication,
because the revised rules were developed after examining their
disagreements. Because the double-coded studies are a random sample,
their adjudicated codes also estimate each headline share without
relying on the first pass.
Section [6.5](#S6.SS5 "6.5 Robustness to coding and population (RQ3) ‣ 6 Results ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
compares these estimates with the codes of the remaining studies.

The census combines adjudicated codes for the 29 sampled studies that
remain eligible with v1.1 codes for the other 85 eligible studies. Two
subsequent revisions apply to all studies. The first (v1.2) responded to
an external review of the codes. It added the design of each improver
comparison and the reason for each partial separation, enforced the
logical rules between items, and excluded one study whose loop was
specified but never run. These additions were coded after adjudication.

The second revision (v1.3) uses independent coding of the improver
comparisons to answer RQ2. A coder who was blind to our codes assessed
all 43 studies and anchor works that claim a better improver or had been
coded with an improver-related comparison. The coder recorded the
improver, whether a comparison exists and whether its budget is stated
as matched, its design, its result and a locator.

Before disagreements were resolved, the two codings agreed on the design
for 81% of the studies ($`\kappa=0.74`$) and on the comparison code for
72% ($`\kappa=0.50`$). Of the 16 disagreements, 8 concerned only whether
a budget was stated as matched
(Table [17](#A4.T17 "Table 17 ‣ Appendix D Per-Study Codes ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")).

We adopted the independent codes and then applied one rule more strictly
than either coding had: a same-start comparison requires identical
starting artifacts for everything except the improver, because equal
scores do not imply equal states
(Section [4](#S4 "4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")).
This rule reclassified two comparisons that both codings had counted as
same-start.

### 5.4 Analysis

We report counts with their denominators. These counts summarize the
recorded codes for the finite search frame. For the principal shares we
add Wilson 95% intervals, which treat the studies as draws from the
research practice that generates them. These intervals do not account
for coding error or missed records, which are addressed through the
robustness checks and the discussion of limitations
(Sections [6.5](#S6.SS5 "6.5 Robustness to coding and population (RQ3) ‣ 6 Results ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
and [9](#S9 "9 Limitations and Future Work ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")).
We compare studies posted before and from 1 September 2026 using
Fisher’s exact test, with Newcombe 95% intervals for the differences in
shares, and examine the alternative populations defined above. Because
tasks, metrics, and budgets differ across studies, the analysis compares
reported evaluation practices rather than pooling performance gains.

### 6.3 Product and process items

The studies span several forms of self-improvement. In 52 studies (58%),
the modified artifact belongs to the system running the loop, such as
its weights, harness, memory, or improver. Another 25 (28%) modify a
component that the system subsequently uses, while 13 (14%) improve a
separate target. Only 4 studies make exclusively task-local changes.
Most operate in verifiable domains (65, 72%). The remaining 25 include
open-ended outputs graded by humans or models.

Across these settings, 81 studies (90%, 95% interval 82–95) meet the
broad product-evaluation criterion (A2 = Y or P). Of these, 54 (60% of
all 90, interval 50–70) report selection-independent evaluation,
compared with 45% of the strong-label studies in the adjudicated sample
(Section [6.5](#S6.SS5 "6.5 Robustness to coding and population (RQ3) ‣ 6 Results ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")).
Among the other 27 studies, 14 also use the evaluation tasks for
selection, such as choosing a checkpoint or reporting the best observed
score. Another 5 hold out only part of the headline result, and 8 do not
state how the evaluation tasks relate to the loop. The remaining 9
studies report no evaluation beyond development tasks.

Headline results use the development signal wholly in 8 studies and
partly in 21, and 11 report the best checkpoint on the evaluation
benchmark. Uncertainty estimates for headline results appear in 46
studies (51%).

### 6.5 Robustness to coding and population (RQ3)

RQ3 asks whether these findings depend on the coding or on the study
population. The double-coded studies are a random sample of the eligible
studies, so their adjudicated codes give an estimate of each headline
share that does not rest on the AI-assisted first pass.
Table [8](#S6.T8 "Table 8 ‣ 6.5 Robustness to coding and population (RQ3) ‣ 6 Results ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
reports these estimates for the 29 sampled studies that remain eligible,

In the adjudicated sample, selection-independent evaluation remains more
common than audited reruns, at 41% against 14%: within the same studies,
10 report the first without the second and 2 the reverse (exact paired

exceeds every process item, while selection-independent evaluation is
about as common as per-round audits at 45% ($`p=1.00`$) and
budget-matched comparators at 34% ($`p=0.73`$). The 22 strong-label
studies in the sample show the same pattern, with selection-independent
evaluation in 45% and audited reruns in 14% ($`p=0.04`$). Human coding
therefore supports a gap in audited reruns. The independent improver
coding identifies a separate gap in same-start comparisons
(Section [6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2 Improver comparisons (RQ2) ‣ 6 Results ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")).

Comparing the adjudicated sample with the remaining studies assesses
whether the headline shares are sensitive to the AI-assisted coding. For
no process item does the share among the other studies differ
significantly from the adjudicated share (smallest $`p=0.17`$). The
largest gaps concern null controls, recorded in 15% of the other studies
against 28% of the sample, and matched improver comparisons, at 15%
against 28%. The census may therefore undercount null controls. For
improver comparisons, both groups use the independent coding.

The product indicators show a consistent directional difference.
Compared with the adjudicated codes, the codes outside the sample record
fully or partly held-out evaluation more often (94% versus 83%),
selection-independent evaluation more often (64% versus 41%,
$`p=0.05`$), and headline results based on the development signal less
often (28% versus 45%). They also record a scope gap far less often (19%
versus 55%, $`p<0.01`$).

These comparisons suggest that the census overstates product evaluation,
particularly its independence from selection, and understates how often
the stated scope exceeds the tested one. These differences make product
evidence look stronger in the census than in the adjudicated sample.
Apart from the scope gap (31% versus 55%) and matched improver
comparisons, which use the independent coding, the v1.1 codes of the
sampled studies match the adjudicated shares closely. However, they were
revised after the second coding of these same studies, so they do not
show how accurate v1.1 is elsewhere.

## 7 Case Analysis: What Studies’ Own Experiments Show

The census measures how often evaluation designs are reported. This
section examines what these designs show by comparing development and
audit outcomes within individual studies. In 24 eligible studies,
including 22 with the strong label, the authors report development gains
that transfer only partly, or not at all, to an audit (P5 = Y or P).
Table [15](#A4.T15 "Table 15 ‣ Appendix D Per-Study Codes ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Per-Study Codes ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
collects these cases. They document several forms of divergence.

In 11, the development signal and the audit disagree about the same
systems: one rises or ranks candidates while the other stays flat,
falls, or ranks them differently. In 5, the audit confirms a smaller
gain or the gain does not carry to a shifted domain. In 7, audited gains
reverse in later rounds or fail to replicate across runs, and in one the
audit itself was compromised. In 6 of the cases, the divergence concerns
an ablation, a baseline or an unpromoted candidate rather than the
proposed method.

The availability of paired outcomes determines which cases can be
examined. Most studies do not report development and audit scores in a
form that would show a divergence: 64 of the 90 strong-label studies are
coded P5 = NA. We examine the cases alongside improver tests and scope
comparisons, and we also discuss a few examples, such as grader
dependence, that the divergence item does not count.

### 7.1 Development gains and external outcomes

The robotics loop of [Wang \[16\]](#bib.bib11) accepted a change only
after it passed replays, paired trials on fresh scenes and a held-out
task. Yet the target task never succeeded over 123 rounds. The reach
evaluator used a spherical boundary, and the agent learned to stop
exactly on its surface. The authors report about two human repairs to
the harness for every accepted skill change.

Other studies report partial transfer or regression across rounds. In
ECG-RSI \[[9](#bib.bib17)\], the error rate on the feedback set fell
from 39.63% to 21.33% across three rounds, while successive gains on
seven external benchmarks shrank from 13.07 to 3.01 to 0.38 points.
Three benchmarks regressed in the last round.

RRSI \[[3](#bib.bib15)\] shows the pattern across methods. Prior methods
for evolving harnesses transferred little of their gain on development
tasks to out-of-distribution evaluation. The arm without RRSI’s
regularizers reached the highest evolution score of any arm, 92.8, while
its out-of-distribution average, 40.3, stayed within a point of the
unevolved harness. The Mendel Gödel Machine reports a similar reduction
in gains for coding agents \[[98](#bib.bib21)\]. Evolution raised
accuracy on its 60 Polyglot evolution tasks from 50.8% to 93.2%, while
the resulting scaffold, frozen for evaluation, gained 10.0 and 13.3
points on held-out SWE-bench Pro and SWE-bench Multilingual. The
scaffold evolved by its HGM baseline lost 3.4 points on SWE-bench Pro.

A more direct disagreement between development and audit scores appears
in R²Flow: most edits admitted on reward-derived labels raised the
training reward while lowering the held-out verified score
\[[99](#bib.bib48)\]. VideoHarness-SRSI documents a baseline whose
validation accuracy keeps rising over rounds while accuracy on held-out
questions does not \[[100](#bib.bib51)\], and RecurSE describes a
related effect as validation deception \[[90](#bib.bib43)\].

Longer runs can also show a performance peak followed by regression:
DataFoundry is best after two rounds, and its Finance score falls from
66.1 to 58.3 after one more \[[101](#bib.bib54)\], while DA-RSIR peaks
at round 4 \[[102](#bib.bib65)\]. In EvoAudio, a candidate that gains on
the held-out promotion set “does not always gain on the benchmarks”
\[[103](#bib.bib63)\]. A-Evolve-Training used a public leaderboard
inside its loop. Its development metric reached record highs without
improving the external target, and its authors do not interpret the
result as evidence of RSI \[[104](#bib.bib42)\]. These cases motivate
measuring development and audit outcomes together rather than inferring
one from the other.

Variation between runs adds another evaluation question.
Proposition [1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Null-loop benchmark). ‣ 4.1 A null-loop benchmark ‣ 4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
describes how selecting the best noisy score can inflate a reported
gain, and two studies examine this selection effect. In Athanor, the
first evaluation seed exceeded the mean of later seeds for 19 of 23
re-evaluated best candidates (sign test $`p=0.003`$)
\[[105](#bib.bib52)\]. MedForge-RSI pre-registered its configurations
and found that the best single-run result often failed to replicate
across seeds \[[106](#bib.bib49)\].

Benchmarks designed to study improvement loops report related effects.
In RSIBench-Data, 78.26% of agent searches that continued past their
best score ended on a lower final attempt \[[59](#bib.bib32)\], and in a
controlled study of skill evolution, 9 of 11 validation-selected skills
improved released-test performance \[[64](#bib.bib35)\]. These results
motivate reporting variation across full loop runs, separately from
evaluation variation for one evolved system.

### 7.2 Dependence on the evaluation grader

A separate concern arises when development and audit graders share
errors. In the self-play study introduced in
Section [1](#S1 "1 Introduction ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims"),
the judge’s pass rate rises from 0.72 to 0.94 while hidden exact-match
accuracy remains at 0.20 across three seeds \[[15](#bib.bib23)\]. Judges
from other model families and a strict three-judge ensemble also accept
many of the policy’s errors. This study lies outside the search frame
but illustrates the shared-error mechanism in
Proposition [2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Coupled audits). ‣ 4.2 Coupled audits inherit development bias ‣ 4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").
Self-preference persists even under objective rubrics
\[[107](#bib.bib25)\].

Within the frame, RSIAgent reports that its internal verifier agent
returned PASS where the official rubric failed \[[108](#bib.bib45)\]. In
the Red Queen Gödel Machine \[[14](#bib.bib12)\], each change of
evaluator is checked against a fixed reference, but the writers and
provers are scored by panels that include the system’s own co-evolved
reviewer or grader. Without its own grader in the panel, the evolved
prover matches a human-engineered baseline (4.03 versus 4.08) rather
than exceeding it.

MASS \[[76](#bib.bib22)\] illustrates a related dependence: one model
acts as agent, evaluator and optimizer. Its synthetic tasks were
generated by a model that is also one of the two external judges, and
its judge-graded gains are accompanied by flat results on two benchmarks
with executable tests.

Other studies introduce reference checks to separate evaluation from the
development judge. SpecForge \[[109](#bib.bib16)\] searches against
tests that the model generates and evaluates on ground-truth tests
hidden from the search, and MedRSI \[[110](#bib.bib18)\] validates its
clinical-consequence judge against adjudicated references and then
freezes it.

### 7.3 Testing the improver and specifying scope

Grader validation addresses how outcomes are scored, whereas improver
tests address what produces them. Among self-modifying coding agents, an
agent’s own benchmark score can diverge from the performance of the
descendants it produces \[[72](#bib.bib24)\], illustrating the
distinction between solver performance and improvement ability. The
designs distinguished in
Section [6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2 Improver comparisons (RQ2) ‣ 6 Results ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
make this difference testable.

A same-start comparison runs an earlier and a later improver from
identical starting artifacts. STOP measures the improver of each
iteration on the same held-out tasks and initial solutions
\[[10](#bib.bib86)\], and Sivia applies its initial and its learned
updater to the same parent skill \[[75](#bib.bib68)\]. The result of
such a comparison depends on its conditions: STOP’s self-improved
improver helps with GPT-4 but not with GPT-3.5 or Mixtral.

A direct comparison can also yield an inconclusive result. AIDE² runs a
discovered agent and the human-engineered agent as outer-loop improvers
from the same start, three seeds each. The arms end at 0.780 and 0.782,
and the authors report the result as inconclusive \[[12](#bib.bib10)\].

An evolving-versus-frozen comparison evaluates the whole loop under two
conditions, allowing the improver to change in one and holding it fixed
in the other, as in the Darwin Gödel Machine \[[1](#bib.bib27)\] and
Hyperagents \[[11](#bib.bib28)\]. It tests whether letting the improver
change helps over a run, but because the two arms visit different
states, it does not by itself show that a later improver beats an
earlier one at a common state.

A third group of comparisons freezes another adaptive component, such as
the evaluator in the Red Queen Gödel Machine \[[14](#bib.bib12)\] or a
curriculum rule recomputed from the current model. These ablations test
the value of adaptation rather than the improvement of the improver.

Interpreting these tests also requires matching the stated scope to the
evaluated setting. HELIX names model–harness co-evolution in its title
and states in its limitations that it does not train an updated model
\[[111](#bib.bib56)\], so the title describes a capability the study
does not test. The MetaRSI abstract argues that RSI must eventually
operate in scientific domains where correctness is settled by argument,
replication or measurement, while its experiments use executable coding
and closed-form science \[[8](#bib.bib13)\]. This statement describes
the motivation for the work rather than a claim about the present
system. S1 records both kinds of gap because both appear in the framing
of a study. Scoping can also be explicit: Frontis-MA1 describes itself
as a step toward meta-evolution that does not yet realize RSI
\[[95](#bib.bib20)\], so its evidence for a better product matches its
claim.

Together, these cases show why the development signal, audit, process
controls and stated scope need to be reported as one account of the
experiment. The discussion translates these evaluation needs into
reporting recommendations.

## 8 Discussion

### 8.1 Interpreting the findings

The findings identify two priorities for process evaluation: audited
reruns and comparisons that isolate improver gains. Product evaluation
is widely reported, although its separation from selection is often
partial or unstated. Repeated runs of the full loop are expensive when
each run trains models or executes many agent episodes, and 73% of the
studies report one run or do not state how many they ran. Deep
reinforcement learning shows the same tendency, where computationally
demanding benchmarks have led to evaluation on few runs
\[[69](#bib.bib88)\].

The improver gap concerns experimental design. Most studies claiming a
better improver report a related comparison, and these comparisons
usually favor the proposed method. Many, however, test the value of
adaptation or compare different methods without isolating the difference
between an earlier and a later improver at a common state.
Proposition [3](#Thmproposition3 "Proposition 3 (Diagonal non-identification). ‣ 4.3 The diagonal problem ‣ 4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
implies that more experiments of the same kind would not close this gap.
Closing it requires a different design.

These results extend meta-research on machine learning evaluation to
claims about a process. Earlier studies measured documentation,
significance testing, run counts and leakage for claims about a final
model \[[66](#bib.bib117), [67](#bib.bib119), [71](#bib.bib120)\]. In
RSI research the relevant unit is the claim, because a study can meet
every requirement of a product claim and still report nothing that bears
on the process it names. The same claim-level coding could be applied to
other research whose claims concern how systems are produced, such as
automated machine learning.

### 8.2 Implications for reporting

The findings and cases point to one reporting need: readers should be
able to trace an improvement claim to the experiment that evaluates it.
We therefore index the standard by claim
(Table [9](#S8.T9 "Table 9 ‣ 8.2 Implications for reporting ‣ 8 Discussion ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")).
The general requirements apply to every study, while the remaining rows
specify the evidence needed for each claim. A study claiming only a
better product should therefore follow the general row and the C1 row.
The recommendations build on [Wu et al. \[17\]](#bib.bib113) and [Wang
et al. \[18\]](#bib.bib112), and the last column of the table gives the
measured rate of each row’s strict indicator as a baseline.

The first row asks for what every claim needs. A paper should state
which claim in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3 Measurement Framework ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims")
it makes, what persists between rounds, and the tasks and domains to
which the result applies. This is particularly relevant to the 30% of
strong-label studies coded with a gap between stated and tested scope,
and to the 55% found in the adjudicated sample. The description of the
evaluation should then state whether any reported test was used for
selection, checkpoint choice or hyperparameter tuning, and should
distinguish a final or prespecified checkpoint from the best checkpoint
observed on a benchmark. In this census, 60% of studies report
selection-independent evaluation, while 32% use the development signal
wholly or partly in a headline result.

The grader needs a corresponding description. When a model judge
supplies audit scores, authors should state its relationship to the
actor and the development judge. They should also report its agreement
with a reference, such as executable checks of the same property or
expert labels, on outputs produced after optimization. When the system’s
own evaluator sits on an audit panel, reporting results with and without
it shows how much the score depends on that evaluator, as in [Iacob et
al. \[14\]](#bib.bib12). These details connect the observed score to the
shared-error concern of
Proposition [2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Coupled audits). ‣ 4.2 Coupled audits inherit development bias ‣ 4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").

Controls are conditional because they answer specific questions. A
protected final test can establish a product gain without a null-loop
experiment. A null control, such as shuffled feedback or inert
proposals, estimates the gain that selection alone produces, which
matters when many candidates are scored. A promotion rule with a stated
error guarantee bounds false acceptance under its assumptions
\[[57](#bib.bib37)\]. A budget-matched comparator tests whether the
update mechanism beats other uses of the same resources. The census
rates are 19% for null controls, 19% for noise-aware promotion and 37%
for budget-matched comparators.

Resource accounting answers three separate questions. Performance at an
equal evaluation or inference budget concerns the product. Improvement
yield at an equal search or training budget concerns the loop, and it is
what a budget-matched comparator measures. End-to-end cost, including
evaluator calls, data, failed candidates and human work, determines
whether running the loop is worthwhile. Matching the number of proposals
does not by itself match tokens, training compute or wall-clock time, so
the matched quantity should be named.

Process claims need observations of the process itself. For
accumulation, a predeclared audit schedule suffices for a finite-horizon
claim, provided that the schedule matches the claim. For repeatability,
two runs document repetition but do not by themselves establish a
reliable gain. Authors should state the quantity they aim to estimate
and the precision of the estimate.

For an improver gain, the comparison should apply both improvers to
identical starting artifacts, or pair them over a declared set of
starting states, while holding budgets fixed. Equal starting scores are
not enough. Authors should also specify whether the contrast concerns
one state or an average over the set. An inconclusive comparison, as in
[Srikanth et al. \[12\]](#bib.bib10), is a tested claim with an
uncertain result, not an absent test.

Finally, changes to an evaluator, rubric or test suite should be
documented alongside the system updates: what changed, why, who
authorized it, and the scores before and after on a fixed reference.
Human repairs to the harness belong in this record, as the robotics
study of [Wang \[16\]](#bib.bib11) illustrates.

## 9 Limitations and Future Work

#### External validity.

The census describes reported evaluation practices within a defined
literature frame. Its phrase-based search captures arXiv records that
use the RSI label and omits work on similar mechanisms that does not use
that phrase. The anchor works provide a useful comparison largely
outside the frame, but they are a selected set rather than a
representative sample. Their similar pattern does not establish
prevalence across self-improvement research. Search tokenization and the
freeze date also affect the retrieved population. We therefore report
the exact queries and chronology in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Search Log ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").
The temporal comparisons detect no statistically significant
differences, but their intervals include substantial change, so they do
not establish that evaluation practice is stable.

#### Measurement reliability.

The codes describe what papers report, not all experiments their authors
may have conducted. Distinguishing strong and weak labels, partial and
selection-independent evaluation, or explicit and implicit scope
requires judgment. An LLM-based assistant supported the first coding
pass and the later recodes, and the adjudication shows that this pass
erred far more often than the independent human coding. We replaced the
codes of the double-coded studies with the adjudicated ones, adopted the
independent codes for the improver comparisons, and revised the decision
rules for the rest. Outside these two sets, individual codes have not
been checked by a human. The independent improver coding covers studies
with an improver claim or a previously coded comparison. A missed
comparison in a study with neither would therefore go undetected. The
random sample supports checks of aggregate shares, not corrections of
individual codes, and it indicates that these codes credit product
evaluation, especially selection-independent evaluation, too often and
record scope gaps too rarely.

A single third reader settled the disagreements, so the adjudicated
codes reflect one interpretation of the codebook. Adjudication produces
a consensus rather than a reliability estimate. The improver comparisons
received a separate blind coding pass, followed by application of the
stricter same-start rule. The other final rules have not been
independently applied to a fresh sample of the remaining studies. The
reasons for partial separation and the types of divergence also lack a
second coding.

Eligibility screening was not independently checked, so false exclusions
of benchmark and evaluation papers that run an improving system of their
own remain possible. The comparison with protocol descriptions from [Wu
et al. \[17\]](#bib.bib113) checks selected interpretations rather than
providing an additional reliability estimate.

#### Construct validity.

Some distinctions exceed what the current codes measure. Grader type
does not establish independence of errors, and the codebook does not
record reference validation for every audit grader. The unit of coding
is the study, so a study can combine a held-out product test on one
benchmark with an improver comparison on another. The E4 bundle
therefore does not guarantee that a single experiment meets the
requirements of C4. The scope item compares the framing in the title,
abstract and introduction with the tested setting, so it records gaps in
framing, including statements of motivation, rather than a rate of
overclaiming.

The per-round item includes scheduled checkpoints and held-out metrics
of an updated component, so it is broader than an audit at every round.
Evidence tiers summarize reported designs rather than successful
demonstrations, and the divergence cases are documented examples of
several kinds, not a failure rate. Several claim-conditioned populations
are small, including the 26 improver claimants, so their shares carry
wide intervals. These boundaries are important when moving from
descriptive counts to judgments about individual methods.

#### Scope of the propositions.

The propositions isolate evaluation problems under stylized assumptions.
The null model assumes a common affine score scale, independent and
identically distributed development errors, and conditionally unbiased
audit errors. The other propositions use a linear shared-bias model and
a deterministic relationship between a separable solver state and its
improver, respectively. They explain limits of inference under those
assumptions rather than estimating the size of an effect in a particular
study. The paper therefore does not establish that current loops fail to
improve systems. Its contribution is to distinguish the evidence
reported for products from that reported for processes and to identify
the additional tests needed for stronger claims.

#### Future work.

These limitations point to work that the dataset and codebook make
possible. Applying the final rules to a fresh random sample, with a
second coding of the reasons for partial separation and the types of
divergence, would give reliability estimates for the final rules, and
independent screening would test the eligibility decisions. Recording
reference validation for each audit grader would let the census assess
grader independence rather than grader type alone. Extending the search
to work on similar mechanisms that does not use the RSI label, and
repeating the census as the literature grows, would show whether the
gaps in audited reruns and same-start comparisons persist.

## 10 Conclusion

This study examined how closely RSI evaluations match the claims made
about improvement processes. Product evaluation is widely reported,
although fewer studies fully separate it from selection. Most studies
claiming accumulating gains audit across rounds, but evaluation of
repeatability is less common. Audited reruns, which test whether a
loop’s gains repeat, appear in only 22% of the studies. Similarly, only
4 of the 26 studies claiming a better improver compare earlier and later
improvers from identical starting artifacts at a matched budget. Most
report related comparisons, but these address different questions. The
human-coded sample, the independent improver coding, and the comparison
set support this central distinction. The case analysis illustrates why
it matters: development gains, audited product gains, and improved
procedures can diverge.

The reporting standard derived from these findings connects claims to
development signals, audit boundaries, process controls and resource
accounting, so that an experiment states what it establishes and which
stronger claims require more evidence. Releasing the dataset and
codebook will allow the measurements to be repeated as the literature
grows and will support a clearer empirical assessment of progress toward
better improvers.

## Appendix A Proofs

#### Proof of Proposition [1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Null-loop benchmark). ‣ 4.1 A null-loop benchmark ‣ 4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").

Write $`u_{0}`$ for the common utility and $`X_{0}=e(s_{0})`$,
$`X_{i}=e(c_{i})`$ for $`i=1,\dots,N`$. Then $`r(s_{0})=u_{0}+X_{0}`$
and $`r(c_{i})=u_{0}+X_{i}`$. By induction on $`i`$, the incumbent after
$`i`$ proposals is the candidate with the largest $`X`$ among
$`X_{0},\dots,X_{i}`$, because a proposal is accepted exactly when its
score exceeds the current incumbent’s. Hence proposal $`i`$ is accepted
if and only if $`X_{i}`$ is the maximum of $`X_{0},\dots,X_{i}`$. The
variables are exchangeable and ties have probability zero, so this event
has probability $`1/(i+1)`$. Summing over $`i=1,\dots,N`$ gives
$`\sum_{j=2}^{N+1}1/j`$, which proves (i).

For (ii),
$`r(S_{\mathrm{final}})-r(s_{0})=\max_{0\leq i\leq N}X_{i}-X_{0}\geq 0`$.
If $`X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})`$, then
$`\max_{i}X_{i}=\mu+\sigma\max_{i}Z_{i}`$ with $`Z_{i}`$ standard
normal, so the expectation is $`\sigma m_{N+1}`$.

For (iii),
$`\hat{a}(S_{\mathrm{final}})-\hat{a}(s_{0})=\alpha(S_{\mathrm{final}})-\alpha(s_{0})`$
because $`u`$ is constant. By assumption
$`\mathbb{E}[\alpha(S)\mid S]=0`$ for every system $`S`$, so the law of
iterated expectations gives
$`\mathbb{E}[\alpha(S_{\mathrm{final}})]=\mathbb{E}[\alpha(s_{0})]=0`$
and the expected audited gain is zero. The assumption is substantive: it
fails, for example, if the audit grader prefers outputs of the kind the
development judge selected. The constants in the statement are computed
numerically. A Monte Carlo check with 200,000 replications agrees with
(i) for $`N=99`$ to the first decimal. $`\square`$

#### Proof of Proposition [2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Coupled audits). ‣ 4.2 Coupled audits inherit development bias ‣ 4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").

For any system $`S`$, $`\hat{a}(S)-u(S)=\lambda b(S)+\xi(S)`$. Taking
the difference between the final and initial systems and then
expectations,

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathbb{E}[\hat{a}(S_{\mathrm{final}})-\hat{a}(s_{0})]-\mathbb{E}[u(S_{\mathrm{final}})-u(s_{0})]=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[b(S_{\mathrm{final}})-b(s_{0})]+\mathbb{E}[\xi(S_{\mathrm{final}})]-\mathbb{E}[\xi(s_{0})].
``` |  |

The last two terms vanish because $`\mathbb{E}[\xi(S)\mid S]=0`$, which
proves the identity for any real $`\lambda`$.

For the second statement, write $`b(S_{\mathrm{final}})-b(s_{0})`$ as
the sum of the changes in $`b`$ at each accepted change. Each term is at
least $`\delta`$, so the sum is at least $`\delta A`$, where $`A`$ is
the number of accepted changes. Taking expectations and multiplying by
$`\lambda\geq 0`$ gives the bound. $`\square`$

#### Proof of Proposition [3](#Thmproposition3 "Proposition 3 (Diagonal non-identification). ‣ 4.3 The diagonal problem ‣ 4 Limits of Product Evaluation ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").

Fix an initial distribution $`\mu_{0}`$ for the solver state $`S_{0}`$.
The run is a Markov chain on solver states and gains:
$`I_{t}=\phi(S_{t})`$ and
$`(S_{t+1},\Delta_{t})\sim K(\cdot\mid S_{t},\phi(S_{t}))`$. By the
Ionescu-Tulcea construction, the joint law of
$`(S_{0},\Delta_{0},S_{1},\dots,\Delta_{T-1},S_{T})`$ for every $`T`$ is
determined by $`\mu_{0}`$ and the maps
$`s\mapsto K(\cdot\mid s,\phi(s))`$.

Define $`\tilde{K}(\cdot\mid s,I)=K(\cdot\mid s,\phi(s))`$ for all
$`I`$. The two kernels agree at every pair the chain visits, so they
generate the same law for every $`\mu_{0}`$ and every $`T`$, and hence
the same law for any collection of independent runs. Under $`\tilde{K}`$
the expected gain $`\tilde{g}(s,I)=g(s,\phi(s))`$ does not depend on
$`I`$, so every improver contrast is zero. Under $`K`$ the contrast
$`g(s,I^{\prime})-g(s,\phi(s))`$ can take any value. Two models with
different contrasts generate the same observables, so the contrast is
not identified.

A swap test samples from $`K(\cdot\mid s,I^{\prime})`$ with
$`I^{\prime}\neq\phi(s)`$, which the two models assign different laws
whenever their contrasts differ. The frozen arm of an
evolving-versus-frozen comparison samples from
$`K(\cdot\mid s,\phi(S_{0}))`$ only at the solver states $`s`$ that its
own chain visits, so it need not provide the overlap the contrast
requires at the states visited by the evolving run. $`\square`$

## Appendix B Codebook

The initial codebook was frozen after the pilot. The v1.1 decision rules
below clarify the items revised after the blind coding, the v1.2 rules
add three items and the logical constraints between items, and v1.3
records the independent coding of the improver comparisons. Together
they govern the reported codes outside the double-coded sample, whose
codes were settled by adjudication. The v1.2 and v1.3 rules apply to all
studies. Where an earlier rule and a later rule differ, the later rule
takes precedence.

The unit is one eligible study, coded from the latest version available
on the freeze date. Every non-trivial code carries a locator to a
section, table, figure or appendix. The value NR means not reported and
is never read as a failed control.

The pilot established a full-text reading procedure: inspect the
experimental setup for data splits and hidden tests, search appendices
for checkpoint and hyperparameter selection, and examine
frozen-improver, fixed-executor, and swap comparisons. It also
established that scope must be coded from the authors’ claims, and v1.1
specifies which sections govern that judgment.

#### Decision rules added after the reliability round (v1.1).

The blind coding showed systematic differences in how several items were
interpreted. We clarified the following rules before recoding the
affected items in every study. A report that specifies a loop but runs
no experiment on it is excluded as a position paper.

For evaluation separation, A1 is P whenever any headline number includes
development, search or selection tasks. A2 is Y only if the held-out
tasks were also unused for checkpoint, round, archive-member,
hyperparameter and default choices and no “best observed on test” value
enters the headline. Target-conditioned loops that attempt the
evaluation tasks themselves have A2 = N.

For controls, A5 is Y for any control that keeps the loop’s budget but
removes or randomizes the information that drives selection, including
random selection, fixed-proportion selection without feedback, retries
without a retained update, and independent sampling from the same start.
A6 is Y when candidates are accepted after repeated multi-seed
evaluation.

For process evaluation, P1 counts held-out scores at every retained
round or checkpoint, including held-out component metrics and periodic
checkpoints. P4 is Y for frozen-improver, frozen-evaluator,
frozen-curriculum or frozen-operator controls, or earlier-versus-later
improvers from a common start, at matched or stated-equal budget, and P
for swaps between different improvers or unmatched budgets.

Scope (S1) is judged on the title, abstract and introduction, and a
later concession in a limitations section does not remove a scope gap
there. B for the reported checkpoint also covers the best archive member
chosen on the reported tasks.

#### Rules added in v1.2.

A study is eligible if it runs its loop and describes the outcome,
quantitatively or qualitatively. A report that specifies a loop and an
evaluation protocol but never runs the loop is excluded. Eligibility
never depends on the direction of the result.

Four logical rules link the items, with an audit defined as A2 = Y or P.
Without an audit, A3 = NA (R1) and P1 = N, because per-round scores are
then development scores (R2). Reporting paired development and audit
scores (P2 = Y) requires a per-round audit (P1 = Y; R3). Without an
audit, P5 = NA because divergence cannot be assessed (R4).

Three items were added. A2r records why an evaluation is only partly
separated: R, the evaluation tasks are also used for some selection; H,
only part of the headline is held out; U, the paper does not state the
separation.

P4d records the design of an improver-related comparison: S, an earlier
and a later improver run from identical starting artifacts, or from a
declared set of starting states with paired assignment; T, an evolving
and a frozen improver each run over the loop; C, another comparison,
such as freezing an adaptive evaluator, world model or curriculum rule,
or swapping between different models.

Each divergence case is classified as paired, partial, regression or
other, as defined in
Table [15](#A4.T15 "Table 15 ‣ Appendix D Per-Study Codes ‣ Auditing the Product, Not the Recursion:The Evaluation Evidence Behind Recursive Self-Improvement Claims").
Evidence tiers are computed from the design items alone, so a study with
a negative result keeps the tier of its designs.

#### Independent coding of improver comparisons (v1.3).

For every study and anchor work that claims a better improver or has a
coded improver-related comparison, P4 and P4d are the codes of an
independent blind coder. This coder also recorded the improver, the
result of the comparison (P4r: better, mixed, no clear difference or
worse for the paper’s own process) and a locator.

The strict same-start rule is then applied: equal starting scores do not
make starting states equal, and an arm that carries solver artifacts,
such as program components or a task agent, into the comparison is not a
same-start arm. When a study reports several designs, the first that
applies in the order S, T, C is recorded. A comparison with a different
improver counts as matched (P4 = Y) when the paper states equal budgets
and no other uncontrolled differences. This rule replaces the v1.1 rule
that coded every such swap as partial.
````

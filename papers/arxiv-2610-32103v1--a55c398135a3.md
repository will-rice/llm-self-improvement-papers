# ReMedI: Inference-Time Refinement and Self-Distillation for Controllable Molecular Optimization

###### Abstract

In drug development, lead optimization must improve competing
properties, such as increasing permeability and reducing toxicity, while
limiting scaffold changes that could compromise the lead’s potency.
RL-based post-training, such as C-MORAL, addresses this problem by
fine-tuning molecular language models to directly optimize molecular
leads for objectives of interest, which relies on expensive
task-specific datasets and faces challenges in achieving multi-objective
optimization (MOO). We argue a pretrained model may already contain
useful molecular edits that zero-shot generation cannot efficiently
reach. Motivated by the limited search capabilities of zero-shot
generation, we develop an efficient Metropolis–Hastings search strategy
to better explore the proposal distribution. We propose ReMedI, which
uses the molecular language model as a base proposer for inference-time
alignment. Given a lead and a set of multi-objective tasks, the model
proposes edits conditioned on the current molecule, while
Metropolis–Hastings refinement guides sequential edits under an
objective for property improvement, preservation, and lead similarity.
On both C-MuMOInstruct and SMDD benchmarks, refinement improves frozen
base models across three backbones, reaching significantly higher
multi-objective optimization success rates compared to baselines. These
search traces can then distilled into the base model which improves
zero-shot generation and subsequent search using $`\approx 7.8\times`$
less training molecular leads than that of C-MORAL. ReMedI achieves
$`\approx 2.8\times`$ the strict-success coverage of RL baseline
C-MORAL, including on out-of-distribution tasks, with matched
lead-relative similarity.

## 1 Introduction

Chemical language models (CLMs) have increasingly become popular for
text-guided molecular generation ([Edwards et al., 2022](#bib.bib5)),
multi-property optimization ([Dey et al., 2025b](#bib.bib4); [Dey
et al., 2025a](#bib.bib3)), and the design of functional, makeable
molecules ([Edwards et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)). A central application of
CLMs is lead optimization, which starts with a promising molecule (lead)
and seeks local edits that improve deficient properties, such as
permeability or toxicity, while preserving satisfactory properties and
maintaining lead-relative similarity. However, current CLMs do not
reliably produce molecules that meet all property targets without
drifting off-scaffold. RL post-training and reference-guided policy
optimization methods try to align CLMs to property rewards to satisfy
MOO  ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)). For
instance, C-MORAL combines group-based policy optimization with
property-specific score transformations and nonlinear reward aggregation
to improve selective molecular editing ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)).
This approach relies on task-specific RL adapters and editing data,
which are costly to develop and can limit transfer to new combinations
of objectives. Since chemists often optimize several properties
together, the challenge therefore is to meet every property requirement
and maintaining acceptable lead-relative similarity without adapting a
separate model for each task.

Inference-time alignment offers an alternative by guiding generation
while holding the model weights fixed. Existing approaches include
similarity-guided decoding ([Navratil et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)) and
iterative editing ([Edwards et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)). For instance,
GP-MoLFormer-Sim guides decoding through reference similarity and uses
an outer genetic algorithm for property optimization ([Navratil et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)), but its decoding guidance does not directly encode
which properties should improve or remain unchanged. MARS demonstrates
that multi-property molecular optimization can benefit from iterative
search over intermediate molecular states ([Xie et al.,
2021](#bib.bib31)). We build on this principle in ReMedI by using an
instruction-conditioned language model to propose successive edits with
a search algorithm which determines which states to retain.

We introduce ReMedI, which uses a pretrained molecular language model to
propose local edits and Metropolis–Hastings (MH) refinement to guide the
search. Its lead-relative objective balances property improvement,
preservation, and similarity to the lead. Because the proposal remains
frozen during refinement, the improvement–similarity trade-off can be
adjusted at inference time without task-specific RL adapters. Thus our
method allows chemists to explore pharmacological spaces that are hard
to reach via zero-shot prompting. On C-MuMOInstruct and SMDD, refinement
improves success across GeLLMO-Llama, GeLLMO-Mistral, and mCLM-4B, and
ReMedI achieves higher strict-success coverage than C-MORAL on matched
GeLLMO-Mistral leads, including on combinations of properties held out
during training.

We show that in ReMedI a single shared proposal adapter trained from
only 500 lead–task instances achieves performance comparable to C-MORAL,
which relies on five task-specific policies trained from 3,883 lead–task
prompts. Our traces contain successful endpoints and intermediate
states, so training does not require a separate specialized editing
dataset. Search improves candidates at inference time and supplies
examples that strengthen later generation and refinement.

#### Contributions.

- •
  Inference-time molecular alignment. We use a chemical language model
  (CLM) to propose edits. Metropolis–Hastings (MH) accepts or rejects
  each proposal using a lead-relative objective for property
  improvement, preservation, and similarity, together with the
  corresponding LM probabilities. These priorities can be changed at
  inference time without retraining the model.
- •
  Learning from refinement. We convert search-discovered endpoints,
  intermediate states, and improving transitions into supervised
  training pairs and distill them back into the base CLM which improves
  zero-shot generation and subsequent MH refinements.
- •
  Success under similarity requirements. ReMedI achieves
  $`\approx 2.8\times`$ the strict-success coverage of C-MORAL on
  matched GeLLMO-Mistral leads. On C-MuMOInstruct, MH increases
  frozen-model SOR by 66–143% relative to zero-shot generation across
  three backbones and IND/OOD splits; on SMDD, it improves SOR by
  12–50%.

## 2 Related Works

Post-training for molecular optimization. Molecular LLMs support
text-guided generation, editing, and multi-property optimization ([Wang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib29)). Early molecule–text models such as MolT5 and
MoleculeSTM connect molecular strings to natural language ([Edwards
et al., 2022](#bib.bib5); [Liu et al., 2023a](#bib.bib14)), while
instruction- and interaction-based systems such as LlaSMol, PEIT-LLM,
ChatDrug, and DrugAssist improve chemistry task following,
property-conditioned generation, or conversational molecular
editing ([Yu et al., 2024](#bib.bib34); [Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib13);
[Liu et al., 2023b](#bib.bib15); [Ye et al., 2023](#bib.bib32)). For
controllable multi-property optimization, Prompt-MolOpt steers
property-specific transformations with prompt-conditioned
embeddings ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib30)), while GeLLMO-C and
C-MuMOInstruct train models to follow multi-property instructions and
prevent property regressions ([Dey et al., 2025b](#bib.bib4); [Dey
et al., 2025a](#bib.bib3)). Earlier molecular optimization methods also
applied reward-guided reinforcement learning to sequence generation and
direct molecular edits ([Olivecrona et al., 2017](#bib.bib21); [Popova
et al., 2018](#bib.bib23); [Zhou et al., 2019](#bib.bib35)). More
broadly, post-training methods modify the generative policy through
supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization, or reinforcement
learning ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib22); [Rafailov et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24); [Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib26)). C-MORAL uses
GRPO/GDPO-style optimization to align molecular LLM outputs with
multi-objective constraints ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Shao
et al., 2024](#bib.bib27)), while RePO uses reference-guided policy
optimization ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)). These approaches adapt the
model through specialized training. ReMedI instead keeps the CLM fixed
during refinement and specifies the improvement–similarity trade-off
through an explicit objective at inference time.

Inference-time alignment. A smaller line of work shifts optimization to
inference time. MARS uses Markov-chain search for multi-objective
molecular optimization with graph neural networks ([Xie et al.,
2021](#bib.bib31)), while GP-MoLFormer-Sim provides training-free
similarity-guided decoding for chemical language models ([Navratil
et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)). ReMedI uses the CLM as a state-dependent
proposal within a Metropolis–Hastings refinement loop ([Metropolis
et al., 1953](#bib.bib17); [Hastings, 1970](#bib.bib9)), with an
explicit target that favors requested property improvements,
preservation of guarded properties, and similarity to the lead. Search
can also improve the proposal itself. Expert Iteration formalizes the
search-and-distill pattern ([Anthony et al., 2017](#bib.bib1)), and we
train a shared proposal adapter on successful refinement endpoints and
intermediate-state examples, reconditioning multi-step solutions on the
original lead as one-step targets. This combines inference-time
refinement with proposal improvement and tests whether search-derived
supervision transfers to unseen objective combinations.

## 3 Preliminary

#### Metropolis–Hastings.

Metropolis–Hastings (MH) draws samples from a distribution known only up
to a normalizing constant. Given an unnormalized target $`\pi`$ and a
proposal $`q(\cdot\mid x)`$, from the current state $`x`$ it draws a
candidate $`y\sim q(\cdot\mid x)`$ and accepts it with probability

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
A(x\rightarrow y)=\min\!\left(1,\;\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)}\,\frac{q(x\mid y)}{q(y\mid x)}\right),
``` |  | (1) |

where the second factor, the Hastings correction, offsets the asymmetry
of a state-dependent proposal (a forward edit likely from $`x`$ need not
be equally likely in reverse). Since $`\pi`$ appears only through a
ratio, its normalizing constant cancels. The proposal controls
exploration, while $`\pi`$ determines the target distribution. The
proposal can be changed or learned without changing that target,
provided the Hastings correction is retained and the resulting chain
remains irreducible and aperiodic over the target’s support.

#### Nash bargaining and Pareto optimality.

Let $`\mathcal{X}`$ be the feasible outcome set. Consider $`m`$
objectives with utilities $`u_{i}(x)`$ and a reference (disagreement)
point $`d\in\mathbb{R}^{m}`$ that specifies the baseline value for each
objective. An outcome $`x`$ is Pareto-optimal if there is no feasible
$`x^{\prime}`$ such that $`u_{i}(x^{\prime})\geq u_{i}(x)`$ for every
$`i`$, with strict inequality for at least one objective. In other
words, no objective can be improved without sacrificing another. If the
feasible utility set is compact and convex and contains a point that
strictly improves every objective over $`d`$, the Nash bargaining
solution [Nash & Jr. (1950)](#bib.bib19) has a unique utility vector,
attained by any maximizer $`x^{\mathrm{NBS}}`$ of

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x^{\mathrm{NBS}}\in\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{X}\\
u_{i}(x)\geq d_{i}\ \forall i\end{subarray}}\ \prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(u_{i}(x)-d_{i}\big),
``` |  | (2) |

which maximizes the *product* of gains over the reference point rather
than their weighted sum. Equivalently, it maximizes
$`\sum_{i}\log\big(u_{i}(x)-d_{i}\big)`$. This makes the solution
invariant to positive affine rescaling of an objective when its
disagreement value is transformed in the same way, and favors balanced
gains across objectives. Any outcome that leaves one objective at its
reference value has a zero product, regardless of gains in the other
objectives.

#### Scoring and target.

Let $`q_{\theta}`$ denote the LM’s conditional proposal distribution,
$`x_{0}`$ the lead, $`t`$ the task requirements, and $`P_{0}(t)`$ the
original prompt specifying those requirements. Let
$`p(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}`$ be the oracle’s property values and
$`d=p(x_{0})`$ the lead’s values. The task specifies which properties to
improve and which to guard. We denote these sets by $`\mathcal{I}`$ and
$`\mathcal{G}`$, and use $`\mathrm{sgn}_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}`$ for the
requested direction of property $`i`$. We map each requested property to
a lead-centred satisfaction

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{i}(x)=\operatorname{clip}_{[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]}\!\left(\sigma\!\big(k_{i}\,\mathrm{sgn}_{i}\,(p_{i}(x)-d_{i})\big)\right),\qquad\sigma(z)=\tfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
``` |  | (3) |

where $`k_{i}>0`$ controls the response scale for property $`i`$, and
$`0<\epsilon<1/2`$ keeps each satisfaction away from zero and one so its
logarithm remains bounded. We aggregate the satisfactions of properties
to improve as $`S(x)=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}s_{i}(x)`$, so gains on one
property cannot fully mask poor satisfaction on another. For guarded
properties, the penalty $`G(x)`$ activates only when a property declines
beyond its allowed margin. The similarity penalty $`L(x)`$ activates
when similarity to the lead falls below $`\tau_{s}`$.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
G(x)=-\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}}\max\!\big(0,-\tau_{i}^{g}-\mathrm{sgn}_{i}(p_{i}(x)-d_{i})\big),\qquad L(x)=-\max\!\big(0,\tau_{s}-\simop(x,x_{0})\big).
``` |  | (4) |

Here $`\tau_{i}^{g}`$ is the allowed decline for guarded property $`i`$,
and $`\tau_{s}`$ is the minimum lead similarity. Combining the three
terms gives the utility score and target distribution,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle U(x)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\beta_{S}\log S(x)+\beta_{G}\,G(x)+\beta_{L}\,L(x),`$ |  | (5) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi(x)\propto{}`$ | $`\displaystyle\exp\!\big(U(x)\big),`$ |  |  |

Here $`\log S(x)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\log s_{i}(x)`$ is the sum of
per-property log-satisfactions and the log of the Nash product. The
positive weights $`\beta_{S}`$, $`\beta_{G}`$, and $`\beta_{L}`$ control
improvement, guarding, and similarity, respectively.

#### Sampling and focused refinement.

At each refinement stage, we set the focused prompt to
$`P=P_{\phi}(t)=P_{0}(t)\oplus\mathrm{clause}(\phi)`$, where $`\phi`$
identifies the property or similarity constraint to focus on. The model
proposes edits from $`q_{\theta}^{\mathrm{valid}}(\cdot\mid x,P)`$. For
a proposed move from $`x`$ to $`x^{\prime}`$, write
$`\Delta F=F(x^{\prime})-F(x)`$ for any score $`F`$. At MH iteration
$`n`$, we anneal the satisfaction weight as
$`\beta_{S,n}=\beta_{S,0}\rho^{n-1}`$, giving the step-specific target
$`\pi_{n}(x)\propto\exp\!\big(\beta_{S,n}\log S(x)+\beta_{G}G(x)+\beta_{L}L(x)\big)`$.
The Metropolis–Hastings acceptance ratio is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log r=\underbrace{\beta_{S,n}\,\Delta\log S+\beta_{G}\,\Delta G+\beta_{L}\,\Delta L}_{\Delta\log\pi_{n}}+\underbrace{\log q_{\theta}^{\mathrm{valid}}(x\mid x^{\prime},P)-\log q_{\theta}^{\mathrm{valid}}(x^{\prime}\mid x,P)}_{\log h},
``` |  | (6) |

We accept the move with probability $`\alpha=\min\{1,e^{\log r}\}`$.
Each iteration applies the MH rule for its step-specific target
$`\pi_{n}`$. We trim and parse proposals to valid molecules and retry
invalid draws. The focus prompt guides which edits the model proposes,
while the target score is determined by $`S`$, $`G`$, and $`L`$. The
acceptance ratio accounts for the prompt by comparing the probabilities
of proposing $`x^{\prime}`$ from $`x`$ and $`x`$ from $`x^{\prime}`$.
Computing the target ratio requires oracle scores for both states.

#### Proposal distillation.

The search produces traces of proposed edits and their outcomes. We use
these traces as supervision to train the proposal model for future
searches, curating a corpus $`\mathcal{D}`$ of context–molecule pairs
with three types of examples. *Seed-conditioned* examples pair the lead
and its task prompt $`(x_{0},P_{0}(t))`$ with a satisfying endpoint,
teaching the model to generate directly from the lead.
*Chain-conditioned* examples pair an intermediate state and its focused
task prompt $`(x,P_{\phi}(t))`$ with a satisfying endpoint reached from
that state. We also include some *accepted transitions* that do not meet
every requirement, so the model learns incremental edits that keep
refinement moving. We fit $`q_{\theta}`$ by weighted maximum likelihood,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\!\!\sum_{(c,x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}}w(x^{\prime})\,\log q_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid c),\qquad w(x^{\prime})\propto\exp\!\big(U(x^{\prime})/\beta_{\mathrm{dist}}\big),
``` |  | (7) |

where the positive temperature $`\beta_{\mathrm{dist}}`$ controls how
strongly the weights favor higher-scoring targets. Seed- and
chain-conditioned examples match the two proposal contexts used at
inference, so one distilled model can improve both direct generation and
refinement. The process can be repeated by generating new traces with
the current proposal and refitting

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Setup

#### Models and benchmarks.

We evaluate ReMedI primarily on C-MuMOInstruct, which contains ten
multi-property editing tasks: five training-seen property combinations
(IND) and five held-out combinations (OOD) ([Dey et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib3)). We use GeLLMO-C Llama-3.1-8B, GeLLMO-C Mistral-7B,
and mCLM-4B as proposal models ([Dey et al., 2025b](#bib.bib4); [Edwards
et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)), with property evaluation provided by ADMET-AI
and PyTDC ([Swanson et al., 2024](#bib.bib28); [Huang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib10)). IND and OOD follow the benchmark’s original task
split; our shared proposal adapter is distilled only from trajectories
spanning the five IND tasks. We additionally evaluate ReMedI on 314
instances from SMDD-Bench Task 4 (Lead Optimization) [Han et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib8). We use a non-agentic evaluation in which both the
lead and generated candidate are scored with ADMET-AI and RDKit. We
compare direct generation (*base*) and MH refinement (*MH*) using the
original proposal ($`q_{0}`$) and the proposal after one distillation
round ($`q_{1}`$), against the RL-trained baseline C-MORAL. Since
C-MORAL’s task objectives do not align with SMDD Task 4, we pool the
five IND seed files and train one shared adapter per backbone. Each seed
retains its own improvement and hold-constant keys, while the oracle
scores the union of IND properties.

#### Metrics.

Following C-MORAL ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)), we report Success
Optimized Rate (SOR), the proportion of molecules that meet the
requested property-improvement criteria, and Strict Success Optimized
Rate (SSOR), the proportion whose relevant properties all satisfy
prescribed near-optimal thresholds. For SMDD-Bench ([Han et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8)), an SOR success requires every requested objective to
improve by its instance-specific threshold. Similarity (Sim) is the
Morgan fingerprint ([Morgan, 1965](#bib.bib18); [Rogers & Hahn,
2010](#bib.bib25); [Landrum, 2026](#bib.bib11)) Tanimoto
similarity ([Bajusz et al., 2015](#bib.bib2); [Maggiora et al.,
2014](#bib.bib16)) between each generated molecule and its lead.
Clearance is the rate at which the body, mainly the liver, removes a
drug from plasma ([Yim et al., 2021](#bib.bib33)). For the 144
SMDD-Bench instances requesting reduced clearance, CLR is the fraction
whose selected molecule’s predicted clearance decreases by at least the
instance-specific threshold ([Swanson et al., 2024](#bib.bib28)). We
select the best of 20 candidates by SOR and emphasize SSOR because it
captures simultaneous satisfaction of all property targets at
near-optimality.

#### Effect of distillation sources.

Figure [6](#S6.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Choosing the distillation round. ‣ 6 Ablations ‣ ReMedI: Inference-Time Refinement and Self-Distillationfor Controllable Molecular Optimization")
ablates the three supervision sources used for proposal distillation:
(A) *lead-conditioned successful endpoints*, which teach complete
solutions from the original lead; (B) *intermediate-state examples*,
which train the proposal on states encountered during refinement; and
(C) *accepted transitions*, which teach useful local edits that do not
yet satisfy SOR. Removing (A) causes the largest drop in SSOR, showing
that endpoint supervision is essential for learning successful
optimization. Ablation (A) makes the proposal much more conservative:
for q1-Base, similarity rises from $`0.30`$ to $`0.505`$ while SSOR

supervision for learning edits that are large enough to satisfy the
optimization targets. Removing (B) or (C) lowers refined SSOR while also
reducing similarity. The full mixture gives the highest refined SSOR
($`0.160`$), supporting trajectory-level distillation and providing the
strongest operating point for subsequent MH refinement.

## 7 Limitations & Conclusion

ReMedI reframes molecular optimization as inference-time refinement: a
CLM proposes sequential edits, MH selects them under an explicit
lead-relative objective, and successful search trajectories are
distilled back into the proposal. Across C-MuMOInstruct and SMDD-Bench,
this improves frozen proposals and yields a stronger
strict-success–similarity tradeoff, while one round of distillation
further improves both direct generation and subsequent search. The
ablations further support the role of each component: sequential editing
provides the main search gain, MH improves the success–similarity
tradeoff, and the three distillation sources contribute complementary
supervision for direct generation and subsequent refinement. A
limitation of our method is all discoveries remains limited in silico
predictions, which do not yet establish in vitro or in vivo efficacy.

### A.1 Benchmark Metrics

Let $`f_{r}(x)`$ denote property $`r`$ of molecule $`x`$, and let
$`s_{r}\in\{+1,-1\}`$ indicate whether higher or lower values are
preferred. For lead $`x_{0,i}`$ and output $`x_{i}`$, SOR requires each
targeted property to improve by its prescribed margin $`\Delta_{r}`$ and
each protected property to remain within tolerance $`\epsilon_{r}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{SOR}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{improve}}}\mathbf{1}\{s_{r}(f_{r}(x_{i})-f_{r}(x_{0,i}))\geq\Delta_{r}\}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{preserve}}}\mathbf{1}\{|f_{r}(x_{i})-f_{r}(x_{0,i})|\leq\epsilon_{r}\}.
``` |  | (8) |

SSOR instead checks whether all relevant properties meet their
prescribed near-optimal thresholds $`\Theta_{r}`$ ([Gao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7)):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{SSOR}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{r\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[s_{r}\bigl(f_{r}(x_{i})-\Theta_{r}\bigr)\geq 0\right].
``` |  | (9) |

For example, consider hypothetical normalized permeability and toxicity
scores, with targets of permeability $`\geq 0.8`$ and toxicity
$`\leq 0.2`$. Suppose the lead has permeability $`0.4`$ and toxicity
$`0.1`$, and the request requires a permeability increase of at least
$`0.1`$ while preserving toxicity. An output with permeability $`0.6`$
and unchanged toxicity passes SOR but fails SSOR: permeability improves

### A.2 Performance across backbones.

Table [3](#A1.T3 "Table 3 ‣ A.1 Benchmark Metrics ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ ReMedI: Inference-Time Refinement and Self-Distillationfor Controllable Molecular Optimization")
reports the full C-MuMOInstruct results across GeLLMO-Llama,
GeLLMO-Mistral, and mCLM-4B. Inference-time refinement consistently
improves the frozen proposal: $`q_{1}`$-MH increases both SOR and SSOR
over $`q_{1}`$-base for every backbone and split. Distillation further
strengthens the proposal on the GeLLMO backbones, and combining one
round of distillation with refinement ($`q_{1}`$-MH) gives the highest
SOR and SSOR in nearly all settings. The gains also extend to OOD
property combinations, indicating that trajectories distilled from IND
tasks improve optimization beyond the combinations used for training.
Similarity generally decreases as optimization becomes more aggressive,
motivating the similarity-constrained coverage analysis in the main text
rather than interpreting success and similarity independently. RI is
included only as a within-method diagnostic because its scale is not
comparable to the C-MORAL implementation.

### A.3 Trajectory Depth Ablation

Table [4](#A1.T4 "Table 4 ‣ A.1 Benchmark Metrics ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ ReMedI: Inference-Time Refinement and Self-Distillationfor Controllable Molecular Optimization")
compares trajectory depths at a fixed budget of 80 proposals per lead.
Independent proposals preserve locality but rarely reach the stringent
target. Replacing 80 independent proposals with 10 trajectories of eight
steps doubles overall SSOR from $`0.070`$ to $`0.140`$. Because every
configuration evaluates the same number of proposals, this gain cannot
be attributed to additional sampling. Instead, sequential conditioning
allows multiple edits to compose into solutions satisfying the stringent
multi-property constraints.
````

# CompoundingKernel: Self-Improving from Zero to First

Qian Qu     Hongyang Wang     Wenbo Chen     Yichuan Li     Jingguo Liu
    Xian Li     Xuan Wang      
Ruochen Wen     Leo Fan     Yiming Yan     Zheshen Wang     Benoit

###### Abstract

We present CompoundingKernel, a self-improving agent system that writes
GPU kernels by search. As of 2026-11-11, its generated kernels rank \#1
on the public L1, L2 and Quantization boards of SOL-ExecBench (NVIDIA
B200). They have held \#1 on L1 since 2026-10-27, albeit a short loss on
2026-11-07. All 235 problems have a site-verified correct kernel; on the
209 public-board problems they are 4.40$`\times`$ faster than the
benchmark’s reference baseline. The search starts from zero: agents get
each problem’s PyTorch definition but no reference kernels or official
scores. A per-problem archive outside the model holds every correct
kernel and its lineage. An LLM controller reads it, picks a parent or a
new design, and gives each parallel child agent one direction. Checks
outside the model decide correctness. At equal wall time, it beat a
coded UCB style tree on 35 of 38 problems by making 2.4$`\times`$ as
many correct attempts. Over a thousand agents ran at once, and one warm
restart fix raised their evaluation rate 3.6$`\times`$. We find that the
rate of reliable measurements, not the number of agents, limits how far
such a search scales. Our code and kernels are available on
\hrefhttps://huggingface.co/spaces/QianQu/CompoundingKernel-ExplorerURL.

## 1 Introduction: From Zero to \#1

A kernel is the program that runs one operation on a GPU, and faster
kernels make the same model work take less GPU time. New hardware
features arrive faster than libraries can use them. On NVIDIA Blackwell,
the fast paths go through the tcgen05 tensor-core instructions, tensor
memory and the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) copy engine, and most
fused or low-precision shapes have no library kernel that uses them yet.
Writing such a kernel is a search: try a design, measure it, fix what
the measurement shows, and try again. The hard part is making that
search build on its earlier tries, run it across hundreds of problems at
once, and keep it from fooling itself.

CompoundingKernel is our system for this search. It starts each problem
from its PyTorch definition, with no reference kernels, and official
scores never enter the search. As of 2026-11-11, its kernels rank \#1 on
the three public SOL-ExecBench boards we entered: L1 (0.823339), L2
(0.819260) and Quantization (0.909670; Figure CompoundingKernel:
Self-Improving from Zero to First). These boards rank teams by their
mean score over a whole collection of problems. The site also ranks each
problem on its own, and there we are first on 89 of the 209 problems we
entered and in the top 10 on 193. Every one of the 235 problems has a
kernel that the site marks correct¹¹ 1 FlashInfer-Bench kernels were
verified correct on the site as private rows; we do not publish their
results.. On the 209 problems of the three public boards, these kernels
are 4.40$`\times`$ faster than the benchmark’s PyTorch baseline
(geometric mean; median 3.78$`\times`$; site timing of our public rows,
as of 2026-11-11). Over a thousand agent sessions ran at once, and 98.4%
of the candidates they evaluated were correct (as of 2026-11-02).

“From zero” describes the search, not the model. Each run starts cold,
with the problem’s PyTorch definition, shapes and data types and nothing
from earlier runs, and it learns only from our own tests and timings. It
never sees a reference kernel or an official result. The model itself
was pretrained on public code, and agents could read the CUTLASS
headers, the web and a public wiki (§3.5). Figure CompoundingKernel:
Self-Improving from Zero to Firstb shows one such search, on Quant/030,
climbing from 6.25$`\times`$ to 27.36$`\times`$ (local timing). The
_harness_ around the agents (their prompts, tools and evaluation)
changed only at _warm restarts_. A warm restart stops the agents,
updates the harness and relaunches them, but keeps the run and every
problem’s archive of past attempts. The agents keep no memory of their
own, so after the restart they re-read the archive and the search
continues where it stopped, rather than starting from zero as in a cold
start.

The system is built as four nested loops (Figure 1), each slower than
the one inside it. In the innermost loop \circled1, a _child_ agent is
given a starting kernel (its _parent_) and one direction to try; it
edits, compiles and times the code on a B200, checks outside the model
decide whether the result is correct, and the new candidate is written
with its timings and parent to the problem’s _archive_, a record of
every kernel tried so far. In the controller loop \circled2, whenever a
child slot frees up, an LLM controller reads the archive, picks an
existing kernel to refine or opens a new design, and writes the
direction the next child will follow; because the archive keeps every
correct kernel, a slow kernel can later become the parent of the fastest
one (§). In the team loop \circled3, lead and helper agents monitor the
system and progress, fix faults in the harness, notify the people for
decisions if needed and each fix goes live at the next warm restart
without losing the archives. In the outer loop \circled4, people decide
which kernels to send to the public board and when. Official scores
never enter loops \circled1 and \circled2, which learn only from our own
tests and timings.

Figure 1: Four loops, one search per problem: \circled1 a child writes
one change, \circled2 the LLM controller picks what next, \circled3
agents fix the harness at warm restarts, \circled4 people decide sends.
Official scores never enter the search; people use the standings outside

Most strong recent kernel results on new hardware come from a fixed
frontier model inside a search or harness \[chen2026avo,
chen2026forgekernel, nvlabs2026kda, cursor2026multiagentkernels\]. A
goal-only agent is already a strong baseline \[chen2026justgoal\], so a
harness has to earn its place by measurement. Ours did so in two ways.
At equal wall time, our LLM controller beat a coded tree search that
ranks nodes with an upper-confidence-bound (UCB) style score on 35 of 38
paired problems. Per attempt the two picked about equally well; the
controller won because it made 2.4$`\times`$ as many correct attempts
(§). Across the whole fleet, one warm restart fix raised evaluated
candidates per hour 3.6$`\times`$ (§5.1). More guidance, by contrast,
did not help: making our optional references (our skill and a public
wiki) mandatory added nothing measurable (§). Extra computation tends to
win \[sutton2019bitter\], but in kernel search it pays off only when
each new try builds on a reliable measurement of the last one. The rate
of such measurements, not the number of agents, is what limited scale.

The rest of the report follows this story. Section 2 introduces the
benchmark and its score. Section 3 describes CompoundingKernel: an
archive outside the model, LLM node selection, parallel children that
each follow one direction, and checks outside the model that decide
correctness and caught reward hacks during the run (§3.6). Section 4
gives the board standings and speedups, each with its timing basis and
date. Section 5 draws four lessons from running the system at scale, and
Section  gives three findings on search design, two of them null
results. Section  follows searches lineage in which a slower kernel
became the base of the best one. We plan to release our code (search
loop, SOL-ExecBench interface, checks, example configuration) and our
kernels (§).

## 2 The Challenge: SOL-ExecBench on Blackwell

\restorebarrier

SOL-ExecBench \[lin2026solexecbench\] is NVIDIA’s public benchmark for
kernels written by AI agents. Its 235 problems come from 124 real
models; on the official boards they cover 3,957 workloads (input
shapes). The workloads include forward and backward passes in BF16, FP8
and NVFP4. The problems fall into four collections, each with its own
board: L1 (single operations, 94 problems), L2 (fused operations, 82),
Quantization (33) and FlashInfer-Bench (26) \[xing2026flashinferbench\].

#### The score.

Each workload $`w`$ in a problem’s set of workloads $`\mathcal{W}`$ has
two published times. The first is $`t_{b}(w)`$, the time of an optimized
PyTorch baseline that NVIDIA has not released. The second is
$`t_{\mathrm{sol}}(w)`$, the speed-of-light bound: the time a perfect
kernel would take on a B200 \[nvlabs2026solar\] running at the hardware
limit. Let $`t_{k}(w)`$ be the measured time of kernel $`k`$ on workload
$`w`$. The SOL score of $`k`$ on $`w`$ is

```math
S_{w}(k)=\frac{t_{b}(w)-t_{\mathrm{sol}}(w)}{\bigl(t_{k}(w)-t_{\mathrm{sol}}(w)\bigr)+\bigl(t_{b}(w)-t_{\mathrm{sol}}(w)\bigr)}.
```

A kernel that matches the baseline scores 0.5, a kernel at the bound
scores 1, and a failed workload scores 0. A problem’s score is the
arithmetic mean over its workloads, and a board’s score is the mean over
its problems. The site checks correctness on every workload and then
times the kernel on its own B200, with the streaming-multiprocessor (SM)
clock locked at 1,500 MHz. It stops any build that takes longer than 300
s, and it refuses solutions that build native code at run time. We use
four metrics (Table 1) and always label which one we mean.

#### Why Blackwell is hard.

Getting close to the bound often needs Blackwell-only features, which
libraries do not yet cover for most fused or low-precision shapes. The
site’s own speed-of-light reference rows put a perfect kernel at about
10$`\times`$ the baseline on L1 and L2, 29$`\times`$ on FlashInfer-Bench
and 38$`\times`$ on Quantization. That leaves a lot of room to gain, and
a lot of room to go wrong.

#### Why correctness is hard.

A wrong kernel usually does not crash. It returns plausible numbers. Our
agents’ kernels have a median length of about 7,000 lines, more than a
person can audit at the rate agents write them. A search strong enough
to find fast kernels will also find gaps in any checker and learn reward
hacks: ways to score without real work²² 2 The benchmark’s authors
report reward hacks in their own agent runs, in which 589 submissions
(14.5%) were flagged and rejected \[lin2026solexecbench\]..

## 3 CompoundingKernel

### 3.1 The Child and Controller Loops

In loop \circled1 of Figure 1, a child takes one parent (or none, for a
new root) and one direction, and in the final run it stops at its first
correct kernel. In loop \circled2, each problem’s single controller has
two tools: it queries the archive, and it calls expand with a parent and
a list of distinct directions, one for each free child slot. One
controller step is

```math
(p,d)\sim\pi_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\cdot\mid A),\qquad k^{\prime}=\mathrm{Child}(p,d),\qquad A\leftarrow A\cup\{k^{\prime}\}\ \text{if}k^{\prime}\text{iscorrect}.
```

Here $`A`$ is the problem’s archive of correct kernels (§3.2). The
controller LLM’s choice given $`A`$, written
$`\pi_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\cdot\mid A)`$, is a parent $`p\in A\cup\{\bot\}`$
($`\bot`$ opens a new root) and a direction $`d`$. The child session
$`\mathrm{Child}(p,d)`$ writes the kernel $`k^{\prime}`$. The controller
keeps no state of its own: it re-reads the archive each round, refills a
slot as soon as a child ends, and runs until the remaining time that
expand reports is used up. Loops \circled1 and \circled2 both run Claude
Opus 5.5, which stays fixed throughout.

### 3.2 Shared Memory Outside the Model

A long search makes far more measured attempts than an agent can hold in
context, so each run’s memory lives outside the model, in an archive
(Figure 2) and each run’s archive is separate and unseen by other runs,
so final runs will not be affected by our experiment runs. Figure 2a
shows who reads and who writes. The controller and its children read the
archive with read-only SQL. Only an evaluation appends a node, and only
for a correct kernel; failed tries are logged but are not nodes. Agents
keep no state of their own, so after a warm restart (within the same
run) they simply re-read the archive. People’s dashboard reads a
separate copy which agents can not see. Figure 2b, left, shows the write
path: each run appends to a ledger and writes one folder per candidate,
a single builder rebuilds the SQLite database from the ledgers and swaps
it in atomically, and agents open the result read-only, so they never
see a half-written database. Figure 2b, right, shows the tables.
Candidates sit at the center as tree nodes, and each one’s parent link
is a tree edge; a node without a parent is a root, meaning a fresh
design. Per-workload evaluations join the published baseline and SOL
times, and profiles, robustness checks and agent steps hang off each
candidate. Appendix  lists the tables.

Figure 2: The archive is the agents’ only shared memory: they read it

### 3.4 Reward from Local Timing

For a correct kernel $`k`$, the reward is the geometric-mean speedup
over the benchmark’s published baseline, over the workloads
$`\mathcal{W}`$ (§2):

```math
R(k)=\Bigl(\prod_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\frac{t_{b}(w)}{t_{k}(w)}\Bigr)^{1/|\mathcal{W}|}.
```

### 3.5 Agents Never See Official Scores

The search loops \circled1 and \circled2 of the final run ran without
people. Each final-run search started cold and could query only its own
run’s archive (Figure 2a). Official scores, submission results and our
calibration never reached the agents. Agents could use the CUTLASS
headers, the internet, and optional reference docs and skills, such as a
public wiki.

People took part only in loops \circled3 and \circled4 (Table 2). They
built the early system, approved harness fixes at warm restarts, and
decided what to send and when to publish, using the public standings.

Official scores never enter the search. No agent sees a score, a rank or
a submission result, and the reward uses only our own timings. People
used the public standings outside the search, in loop \circled4: to
decide what to send and when, and to decide where compute goes. From
2026-10-25, humans set each problem’s child slots from its gap to \#1:
problems close behind \#1 got more, problems safely at \#1 gave some up,
and the fleet total stayed the same. The agents received only a slot
count per run. People also added a few approved notes to some runs’
prompts (design directions, Triton guidance, a clock note); none of them
contained a score or a rank.

_Local reference._ On our pods, the kernels as of 2026-11-10 are

counts.

### 4.3 Most Gains Came Early

Most of the gain came early in the run, and the search kept improving
after that at a falling rate. Figure 4 plots each board’s climb as its
mean best local SOL against the share of the run’s time. From the first
data point (about 1% of the run’s time) to the end of the data window,
L1 rose from 0.747 to 0.893, L2 from 0.682 to 0.899, Quantization from
0.835 to 0.938 and FlashInfer from 0.750 to 0.853. In the first 15% of
the run’s time, each board gained 73–85% of its rise. Counted by
evaluated candidates instead (Figure  in Appendix ; gain is a problem’s
best local SOL so far minus that of its first correct kernel, pooled
over all 235 problems), the first 2% of candidates delivered 81% of the
gain. The median problem gained 0.27 local SOL on L1, 0.39 on L2 and
0.25 on Quantization. The late tail is small in SOL, but it decided many
per-problem ranks.

Figure 4: Most of each board’s rise came in the first 15% of the run:
73–85% of the rise in mean best local SOL. Local scores on our B200

Candidate counts follow difficulty, not payoff. Across problems, the
rank correlation between a problem’s correct candidates and its gain is
$`-`$0.36 (Spearman, 235 problems). Hard problems with little room left
absorbed many tries, and later in the run child slots moved toward
problems close behind \#1 (Limitations, §). The candidate axis therefore
shows what the gain cost. It does not show that more tries on one
problem buy more gain there.

### 4.4 Blackwell-Native Kernels, and Where We Lag

The final kernels are large, hand-level Blackwell code, not library
wrappers. By declared language, 214 of 235 are CUDA C++, 13 are CuTe
DSL, 6 are Triton and 2 are mixed. All 78 per-problem \#1s of 2026-11-10
are CUDA C++ or CuTe DSL. 221 kernels exceed 1,000 lines, and the median
is about 7,000. By a text match on the source, 77% name tcgen05
tensor-core instructions, 72% issue TMA copies and 94% contain inline
PTX. None uses Hopper’s wgmma. These counts come from searching the
source text, so they show what the code contains, not what runs when the
site times it.

Where we fall short: plain GEMM/MLP work and full layers are furthest
from SOL (Table ). 16 of the 209 public problems sit outside the top 10
(10 on L1, 6 on L2). Two problems end slower than the baseline: L2/037
(0.30$`\times`$) and L1/030 (0.96$`\times`$). Of the 209 public
problems, 21 are below 1.5$`\times`$. All 21 are on L1 or L2, with a
mean site SOL of 0.61 against 0.86 for the rest. The worst case is
partly self-imposed. On L2/037 a code review rejected a library fallback
that had passed every automatic check (§3.6).

## 5 Systems Lessons at Scale

These four lessons are about running over a thousand agent sessions at

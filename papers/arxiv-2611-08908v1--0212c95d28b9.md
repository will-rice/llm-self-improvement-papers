###### Abstract

The rapid development of large language models (LLMs) has been driven
largely by scaling model size and human-curated training data, yet this
paradigm is increasingly constrained by data scarcity and diminishing
returns from human supervision. Self-play offers a promising pathway for
autonomous data generation and improvement, but current frameworks
remain unsatisfactory due to a lack of a data-centric perspective,
limited cross-domain generalization, and growing trustworthiness and
security concerns. In this paper, we argue that real-world LLM self-play
systems should achieve effectiveness, generalizability, and
trustworthiness under data scarcity. To address these challenges, we
outline three blue-sky research directions for future self-play systems:
(1) data-centric self-play for effective experience generation, (2)
generalizable self-play for open-world and unverifiable tasks, and (3)
trustworthy self-play for reliable self-evolution. We further discuss
research opportunities and solutions within each direction. This
paradigm shift will establish a sustainable and safe AI ecosystem by
transforming finite human knowledge into inexhaustible high-quality
experience through self-play architectures.

## I Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable capabilities
across a wide range of domains, including coding \[[1](#bib.bib26),
[2](#bib.bib27)\], mathematics \[[3](#bib.bib25)\], and scientific
research \[[4](#bib.bib28)\]. According to scaling laws, increasing
model size and training data generally lead to better
performance \[[5](#bib.bib32)\]. As \[[6](#bib.bib29)\] points out, “the
AI revolution is running out of data”: most high-quality sources are
being exhausted, causing a notable slowdown in human-supervised
progress \[[7](#bib.bib30), [8](#bib.bib75)\]. In
response, \[[9](#bib.bib31)\] propose “Welcome to the Era of
Experience,” advocating a shift from the era of human data to the era of
experience, where learning relies less on static human-labeled datasets
and more on autonomous data generation and exploration. In this context,
self-play has emerged as a core approach: agents or environments
generate training data through iterative interaction, enabling models to
improve by learning from their experience \[[10](#bib.bib33)\].

Previous work on self-play under data scarcity can be summarized into
three evolutionary paradigms (see Figure 
[1](#S1.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ I Introduction ‣ Toward LLM Self-Play Under Data Scarcity: Effectiveness, Generalizability, and Trustworthiness")).
Self-evolving frameworks achieve continuous iteration without external
supervision by closing the loop of task generation and self-evaluation
within a single model \[[11](#bib.bib1), [12](#bib.bib4)\], as seen in
approaches such as self-training \[[13](#bib.bib5), [14](#bib.bib6)\]
and adaptive learning \[[15](#bib.bib2), [16](#bib.bib3)\]. Asymmetric
co-evolution frameworks improve model capabilities through
cooperative \[[17](#bib.bib19), [18](#bib.bib20)\] or adversarial
interactions \[[19](#bib.bib8), [20](#bib.bib9)\] among agents,
leveraging intrinsic motivation constraints to drive learning
dynamics \[[21](#bib.bib16), [22](#bib.bib17)\]. Multi-agent evolution
frameworks further extend self-play to complex competitive settings or
multi-role cooperative structures, where game
environments \[[23](#bib.bib10), [24](#bib.bib11)\] and role
assignments \[[25](#bib.bib12), [26](#bib.bib13)\] enable models to
internalize reasoning processes and evaluation criteria within a
loop \[[27](#bib.bib14), [28](#bib.bib15)\].

Despite this progress, existing LLM self-play frameworks under data
scarcity still face several challenges. ❶ Lack of a Data-Centric
Perspective. Pioneering research in this area largely overlooks
systematic analysis of data-related factors, leaving critical questions
unexplored regarding seed data quantity, distribution, and their
alignment with test-time domains \[[29](#bib.bib7), [30](#bib.bib45),
[31](#bib.bib41)\]. Moreover, the intrinsic properties of online
synthesized data, including marginal utility and temporal value, remain
under-examined \[[32](#bib.bib64), [33](#bib.bib65)\]. ❷ Limited
Cross-Domain Generalization. Most current approaches implicitly rely on
rule-based or verifiable correctness rewards, and are therefore tailored
to domains such as code generation and mathematical
reasoning \[[29](#bib.bib7), [34](#bib.bib23)\]. However, they struggle
to generalize to unverifiable, open-ended tasks such as creative writing
or scientific discovery, where task specification and evaluation are
inherently subjective and difficult to formalize \[[35](#bib.bib24)\].
This limitation substantially constrains their application in real-world
scenarios \[[36](#bib.bib34)\]. ❸ Trustworthiness and Security Concerns.
Unconstrained self-play could amplify existing
biases \[[29](#bib.bib7)\], induce spurious generalization, or lead to
unstable reasoning behaviors \[[34](#bib.bib23)\]. Without safeguards,
such dynamics pose risks to reliability and
robustness \[[37](#bib.bib22), [38](#bib.bib21)\].

Therefore, this paper argues that real-world LLM self-play systems
should achieve effectiveness, generalizability, and trustworthiness
under data scarcity. As shown in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ I Introduction ‣ Toward LLM Self-Play Under Data Scarcity: Effectiveness, Generalizability, and Trustworthiness"),
we propose three research directions. ❶ Data-Centric Self-Play for
Effective Experience Generation. This direction rethinks self-play from
a data-first perspective, explicitly analyzing the challenges posed by
seed data and online synthesized data. Potential efforts include
Health-Aware Data Allocation and Learnability-Aware Data Valuation,
thereby transforming self-play into an effective engine for high-quality
synthetic experience. ❷ Generalizable Self-Play for Open-World and
Unverifiable Tasks. To overcome the reliance on correctness-verifiable
rewards, this direction aims to extend self-play to open-ended and
non-verifiable tasks. Key solutions include the Generation-Evolution
Pipeline and Causality-Aware Reward Enhancement, which allow models to
self-improve in unverifiable domains. ❸ Trustworthy Self-Play for
Reliable Self-Evolution. This direction focuses on endogenous and
exogenous risks of self-play. By incorporating Behavioral
Trustworthiness Regulation and Provenance-Grounded Trust Certification,
self-play systems can mitigate error amplification and brittle
behaviors, ensuring that evolution remains controllable.

## II Data-Centric Self-Play for Effective Experience Generation

The effectiveness of self-play-based LLM self-evolution depends not only
on the training algorithm but also on how data is generated, filtered,
and reused within the loop. Existing studies treat self-play as a
training recipe and rely on downstream benchmarks as primary evidence,
while providing limited accounts of the data dynamics that govern the
loop’s behavior \[[29](#bib.bib7), [14](#bib.bib6)\]. However, the
quality of generated experience remains critically sensitive to
data-level decisions, as seed and synthesized data jointly shape the
trajectory of self-evolution \[[31](#bib.bib41), [39](#bib.bib40)\]. In
this section, we examine two challenges in closed-loop data management
and propose solutions.

### II-A Challenge I: Bias Amplification and Coverage Effects in Seed Data

Existing self-play systems treat seed data as a static initialization
mechanism, without analyzing how its structural properties propagate
through the loop \[[29](#bib.bib7)\]. In a closed-loop setting, seed
data establishes early task formats and feedback mechanisms that the
loop repeatedly amplifies, shaping subsequent synthesis and the boundary
of capability expansion \[[40](#bib.bib44), [30](#bib.bib45)\]. Two
research questions arise: how to control seed-induced bias
amplification, and how seed data affects diversity and coverage.

Fig. 3: Visualization of how seed data reshapes the output distribution
of a self-play query proposer. The Vendi score \[[41](#bib.bib81)\]
measures the effective number of distinct semantic modes in each query
set; higher values indicate more diverse coverage.

Seed-Induced Bias Amplification. A research question concerns how
seed-induced bias amplification can be detected and controlled. The
proposer extrapolates along the task structure implied by the seed set,
while early reward biases reinforce generation directions, causing the
synthesized distribution to contract, opportunistic generation to
emerge, and topical biases to accumulate \[[42](#bib.bib46),
[43](#bib.bib47)\]. Since these failures are not immediately reflected
in downstream metrics, detecting seed-induced amplification remains an
open challenge.

Seed Diversity and Coverage Effects. Another research question concerns
how seed conditions and construction strategies affect the diversity and
coverage of generated tasks. We empirically examine this effect by
comparing generated queries in a shared semantic embedding space to
study how seed data reshapes the output distribution of a self-play
proposer. Specifically, we collect 175 samples from the Self-Instruct
seed pool \[[44](#bib.bib52)\] as the reference set, generate 1000
queries for each condition, and project both seed instructions and
generated queries into a two-dimensional UMAP space. We compare
different models under seed-free settings and evaluate several seed
construction strategies, including in-context
expansion \[[44](#bib.bib52)\], single-seed
evolution \[[45](#bib.bib58)\], topic/domain seed
prompting \[[46](#bib.bib18)\], and dataset-as-seed
prompting \[[29](#bib.bib7)\].

As shown in
Figure [3](#S2.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ II-A Challenge I: Bias Amplification and Coverage Effects in Seed Data ‣ II Data-Centric Self-Play for Effective Experience Generation ‣ Toward LLM Self-Play Under Data Scarcity: Effectiveness, Generalizability, and Trustworthiness"),
we draw the following conclusions. (1) Without seed data, different
models exhibit markedly different output distributions, indicating that
query generation is strongly shaped by model-specific pretraining priors
rather than converging to a common region of the semantic space.
Notably, DeepSeek-V4 and GPT-5.5 achieve higher Vendi scores ($`V=12.0`$
and $`V=11.1`$, respectively) compared to Claude Opus 4.7 and Kimi
K2-0905, demonstrating that they generate more diverse queries without
external guidance. (2) Introducing seed data changes the proposer
distribution: across all four seed-conditioned mechanisms, the generated
queries move closer to the support of the Self-Instruct seed pool and,
in most cases, expand to broader semantic coverage than seed-free
generation. (3) Diversity depends on seed utilization. Dataset-as-seed
prompting yields the highest Vendi score $`V=29.1`$, indicating broader
semantic coverage by leveraging the entire distribution of the seed pool
($`V=20.0`$). In contrast, in-context expansion and single-seed
evolution stay closer to the seed pool’s diversity, while topic/domain
seed prompting yields an intermediate pattern, where topic constraints
anchor generation.

### II-B Challenge II: Marginal Utility and Value Decay in Online Synthesized Data

Online synthesized data is the primary source of training data in
self-play, yet current practice selects samples based on
outcome-oriented criteria without modeling their long-term learning
value. As the loop progresses, both the marginal utility of synthesized
samples and the residual value of historical data decline without clear
signals under evaluation \[[31](#bib.bib41)\]. Two important research
questions arise: how to detect marginal utility decline in synthesized
data, and how to address temporal value decay in the replay pool.

Diminishing Marginal Utility. A research question concerns how the
marginal learning value of online synthesized data declines as the loop
advances, and why this decline remains invisible under standard
evaluation. As training progresses, the proposer generates samples from
already-mastered regions, yielding high pass rates while delivering
diminishing capability gains \[[31](#bib.bib41), [47](#bib.bib43)\].
Recent studies on semi-supervised post-training for LLMs show that
performance gains tend to diminish when each self-play iteration reuses
previously selected pseudo-labeled data \[[48](#bib.bib73),
[49](#bib.bib76)\]. As illustrated in
Figure [5](#S3.F5 "Fig. 5 ‣ III Generalizable Self-Play for Open-World and Unverifiable Tasks ‣ Toward LLM Self-Play Under Data Scarcity: Effectiveness, Generalizability, and Trustworthiness")
under the EasyRL \[[49](#bib.bib76)\] settings, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (left)
and Qwen2.5-Math-7B (right) improve rapidly in early iterations,
increasing from $`37.1`$ to $`40.3`$ and from $`45.3`$ to $`50.6`$ by
the third iteration, respectively. However, further self-training yields
marginal or negative gains, suggesting that accumulated noise from
synthetic pseudo-labels leads to diminishing returns.

Temporal Value Decay. The second research question concerns how to keep
the replay pool aligned with the model’s evolving capability boundary.
As the solver improves, early-iteration samples calibrated against
weaker solvers may become too easy, consuming training capacity without
expanding capability \[[47](#bib.bib43), [50](#bib.bib42)\]. This issue
remains unresolved in self-play, where the proposer and solver co-evolve
beyond the fixed task sequences assumed by standard continual learning
replay \[[51](#bib.bib66)\].

### II-C Solution

We propose a data-management framework with Health-Aware Data Allocation
for seed data and Learnability-Aware Data Valuation for online data
synthesis.

Health-Aware Data Allocation. To counter bias amplification, this
mechanism measures data diversity through similarity comparisons and
quantifies task difficulty via self-consistency probing. Based on these
metrics, the framework removes low-value samples, retains learnable ones
that fill coverage gaps, and replays high-value records from the
historical pool. To address seed diversity and coverage effects, the
allocator monitors distribution shifts; it increases dataset-as-seed
prompting to broaden coverage during contraction, while utilizing topic
seed prompting to maintain domain relevance.

Learnability-Aware Data Valuation. To optimize the lifecycle of
synthesized experience, we propose Learnability-Aware Data Valuation,
which tracks the learning utility of data over time. First, it monitors
information gain by calculating model entropy on generated batches; a
sustained decline in entropy marks domain saturation, which
automatically triggers a strategic shift toward less-explored tasks.
Second, historical samples in the replay pool are periodically
reassessed using model uncertainty, allowing fully mastered trajectories
with low uncertainty to be retired under a time-aware retention policy.
This evaluation loop ensures that the pipeline continuously flushes out
stagnant data and prioritizes high-utility training tokens.

## III Generalizable Self-Play for Open-World and Unverifiable Tasks

The traditional self-play paradigm follows a tripartite loop of task
proposal, task solving, and evaluation, relying on verifiable
environments and deterministic evaluation signals. In domains such as
programming and mathematics, task formats are structurally constrained,
and evaluation depends on deterministic compilers or symbolic
verifiers \[[52](#bib.bib51)\]. However, in open-world tasks such as
creative writing or scientific hypothesis generation, task generation is
uncertain, and task evaluation is subjective and
multi-dimensional \[[44](#bib.bib52)\]. These characteristics challenge
the generalization of self-play systems.

We conduct a literature survey of self-play studies across task domains.
As shown in
Figure [4](#S2.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ II-C Solution ‣ II Data-Centric Self-Play for Effective Experience Generation ‣ Toward LLM Self-Play Under Data Scarcity: Effectiveness, Generalizability, and Trustworthiness"),
current studies are concentrated in verifiable domains, leaving
unverifiable domains underexplored. This imbalance reflects the
verifier’s law \[[53](#bib.bib80)\]: self-play progress is largely
shaped by task verifiability. We examine two challenges and propose
solutions.

### III-A Challenge I: Diversity Collapse and Curriculum Adaptation in Task Proposal

In unverifiable domains, tasks are ambiguous and often socially
constructed \[[44](#bib.bib52)\], and there is a lack of an external
compiler to judge task difficulty. Consequently, two research questions
arise for task proposers: how to ensure diversity in task generation,
and how to construct an adaptive curriculum that challenges the model to
drive capability expansion.

Task Diversity Collapse. A research question concerns the collapse of
task diversity, which can arise when the system samples from its own
high-probability regions without external
correction \[[54](#bib.bib35)\]. As a result, the proposer tends to
favor structurally clean and familiar tasks that align with the model’s
pretraining distribution, while systematically neglecting low-frequency
yet cognitively demanding patterns such as subtle metaphors or rare
logical paradoxes \[[55](#bib.bib59)\].

Adaptive Curriculum Construction. Another research question is how to
construct an automated curriculum signal for task proposal. Without an
observable success metric, the proposer cannot reliably estimate the
solver’s current zone of proximal development \[[56](#bib.bib60)\].
Consequently, generated tasks may oscillate between trivial prompts with
limited improvement and overly difficult ones that produce noisy or
vanishing learning signals. While difficulty in verifiable domains can
be calibrated through success rates, it remains ill-defined in open
domains.

### III-B Challenge II: Reliable Evaluation and Long-Horizon Reward Signals

In unverifiable domains, evaluation in self-play systems emerges as a
central challenge. Unlike deterministic tasks that rely on exact
matching or rule-based verification \[[57](#bib.bib37),
[58](#bib.bib38)\], these tasks are judged by subjective and inherently
multi-dimensional criteria, such as coherence, creativity, utility, and
impact \[[54](#bib.bib35)\]. This subsection examines two research
questions.

Reliable Subjective Evaluation. A critical gap lies in evaluating tasks
such as creative writing or open-ended dialogue, which lack explicit
ground truth. Existing approaches largely rely on scalar or generative
reward models trained from human preference data under the reinforcement
learning paradigm \[[59](#bib.bib61), [60](#bib.bib62)\]. However, the
generalization ability of such reward models is constrained by the
limited annotated preference data \[[61](#bib.bib36)\], often
encouraging the model to learn stylistic heuristics rather than semantic
quality. Additionally, they are vulnerable to reward hacking behaviors.
The model tends to exploit superficial correlations between verbosity
and higher reward signals \[[54](#bib.bib35)\]. We quantify this through
experiments (see
Table [I](#S2.T1 "TABLE I ‣ II-B Challenge II: Marginal Utility and Value Decay in Online Synthesized Data ‣ II Data-Centric Self-Play for Effective Experience Generation ‣ Toward LLM Self-Play Under Data Scarcity: Effectiveness, Generalizability, and Trustworthiness")).
We annotate 150 samples from RubricBench \[[62](#bib.bib77)\], covering
three types of reward hacking: length bias, formatting bias, and tone
bias, with 50 samples for each type. We evaluate three groups of models:
closed models, open models, and generative reward models, under w/o
rubric and w/ rubric settings.

We have the following observations. (1) Among the three hacking types,
most models achieve higher accuracy on tone bias. For example,
GPT-OSS-120B achieves a preference accuracy of $`0.94`$ on tone bias. In
contrast, performance on formatting bias is generally lower without
rubric guidance, with Qwen3.5-Plus achieving $`0.38`$ and DeepSeek-V3.2
$`0.28`$. This suggests that higher apparent confidence or professional
terminology is easier for models to capture, while surface formatting
bias is more likely to mislead judgments. (2) Under the w/ rubric
setting, open models achieve the best overall performance. In
particular, GPT-OSS-120B and GPT-OSS-20B obtain the highest average
accuracies of $`0.84`$ and $`0.83`$, respectively. This is consistent
with their common use as reward models in self-play studies for
unverifiable domains. By contrast, existing generative reward models
still show room for improvement in defending against reward hacking. (3)
Introducing rubrics significantly improves robustness to reward hacking
across all models. For example, the overall accuracy of DeepSeek-V3.2
increases from $`0.36`$ to $`0.75`$, while GPT-4o-Mini improves from
$`0.37`$ to $`0.64`$. These results indicate that explicit rubrics can
constrain the judgment criteria of models and reduce their reliance on
superficial heuristics.

Long-Horizon Reward Design. The second research question concerns how to
define reward signals in long-horizon tasks. Taking scientific discovery
as an example, designing a reward function that meaningfully captures
novelty, scientific impact, or reproducibility remains an unresolved
problem \[[63](#bib.bib39)\]. Recent studies on autonomous scientific
discovery systems further highlight that scientific exploration involves
sparse and delayed feedback, complex interdependencies across sub-tasks,
and evaluation standards that may only become clear after extensive
experimentation \[[64](#bib.bib72), [4](#bib.bib28)\]. Additionally,
studies on software development highlight that multi-stage workflows
such as software design, implementation, and testing constitute a
long-horizon pipeline, where strong interdependencies make it difficult
to design intermediate reward signals \[[2](#bib.bib27)\].

### III-C Solution

We propose two solutions for LLM self-play in unverifiable domains: the
Generation-Evolution Pipeline for task generation and Causality-Aware
Reward Enhancement for task evaluation.

Generation-Evolution Pipeline. To address the challenges of diversity
collapse and curriculum adaptation in task generation, we employ a
Generation-Evolution Pipeline. In the Generation component, a
novelty-seeking objective is introduced to penalize high-probability
repetitions and encourage exploration of low-density regions in the
model’s pretraining distribution. At the same time, a global historical
pool of high-value trajectories is maintained to prevent catastrophic
forgetting and stabilize the training distribution. The Evolution
component manages the iterative updates of the self-play system through
a feedback-driven loop, adjusting task difficulty. For example, when the
solver’s success rate within a difficulty bin exceeds a threshold, the
Evolution component triggers a complexity adjustment via prompt
modification, achieving curriculum adaptation.

Causality-Aware Reward Enhancement. To address the challenges of
reliable evaluation and long-horizon reward signals in unverifiable
tasks, we design a Causality-Aware Reward Enhancement mechanism. This
method employs a process-based verifier to inspect the reasoning path
and decomposes the reward model into key causal factors, which reflect
true task quality, and non-causal spurious factors, such as length or
style. For non-causal factors, specialized classifiers are applied to
detect reward hacking types. For the key causal factors, implicit
rewards are incorporated using expert demonstrations or environmental
feedback \[[65](#bib.bib63)\], and multi-dimensional evaluation spaces
combined with confidence-weighted calibration ensure the robustness and
reliability of reward signals. Additionally, the introduction of scoring
rubrics can further enhance the interpretability of the
rewards \[[66](#bib.bib78), [67](#bib.bib79)\].

## IV Trustworthy Self-Play for Reliable Self-Evolution

Self-play can generate effective experience while reducing reliance on
human-curated tasks. However, it also creates trust and safety risks
because the system learns from a closed feedback
loop \[[37](#bib.bib22), [38](#bib.bib21)\]. For endogenous risks, noisy
self-supervision signals, shortcut strategies, and unsafe reasoning
patterns can enter the loop and grow over iterations \[[68](#bib.bib48),
[47](#bib.bib43)\]. For exogenous risks, external data, tools, and
adversaries can further perturb inputs and feedback, pushing the system
toward unsafe behavior or lower robustness \[[69](#bib.bib50)\]. This
section analyzes challenges from endogenous and exogenous sources and
proposes solutions to constrain the self-play process.

### IV-A Challenge I: Noisy Self-Supervision and Unsafe Cognitive Patterns

Endogenous risks arise from the inherent dynamics within the self-play
system, including self-supervised signals and reasoning patterns. In
unverifiable tasks, solvers’ unsupervised training relies on internal
signals that are often unreliable, and solvers can also generate unsafe
chain-of-thought reasoning without external supervision. This subsection
discusses two essential research questions.

Noisy Self-Supervision. In the math and open-world domains, the proposer
can generate problems but may not provide reliably correct answers,
making the solver’s training effectively
unsupervised \[[35](#bib.bib24), [29](#bib.bib7)\]. A key research
question concerns the reliability of the solver’s self-generated signals
are often noisy and susceptible to reward hacking or model
collapse \[[70](#bib.bib49), [40](#bib.bib44)\]. For instance, majority
voting used as a reward signal can reinforce consistent but incorrect
answers \[[7](#bib.bib30)\]. Signals like self-consistency or entropy
estimates are also often miscalibrated, and the loop can retain fluent,
high-confidence errors while discarding low-confidence but informative
samples \[[42](#bib.bib46)\].

Unsafe Cognitive Patterns. Although self-play reduces human
intervention, the absence of supervision over intermediate reasoning
steps can foster emergent cognitive patterns that are unsafe or
undesirable. For example, LLMs may produce outputs such as: “The aim is
to outsmart all these groups of intelligent machines and less
intelligent humans. This is for the brains behind the future,”
reflecting reasoning that could be misaligned with intended safe
behavior \[[34](#bib.bib23)\]. Such emergent reasoning tendencies may
amplify through self-play iterations, as the solver reinforces risky
patterns without external correction. \[[71](#bib.bib74)\] also suggests
that safety-aligned LLMs still retain latent unsafe decoding paths,
which could be reinforced through self-play.

### IV-B Challenge II: Data Poisoning and Interface Manipulation

In LLM-based self-play systems, the training loop interacts with
multiple external interfaces, including data sources, feedback channels,
evaluation tools, and retrieval systems. These interfaces introduce
potential attack surfaces where malicious or corrupted signals may enter
the pipeline. Unlike endogenous risks that emerge from internal learning
dynamics, exogenous risks arise when external actors intentionally
inject harmful information into the system. This subsection highlights
two research questions related to external threats.

Data Poisoning. One research question concerns how poisoned or
manipulated signals can infiltrate the training pipeline. Data poisoning
may occur through compromised seed datasets, online synthesis pools,
manipulated human feedback, tampered evaluation logs, or polluted
retrieval corpora \[[72](#bib.bib54), [73](#bib.bib53)\]. In a self-play
setting, such corruption can be amplified because poisoned samples may
be replayed, reinforced by reward signals, and propagated across
training iterations. Over time, these signals can steer the model toward
attacker-chosen behaviors, even when the initial contamination is
relatively small \[[72](#bib.bib54), [74](#bib.bib57)\].

Interface Manipulation. Another research question concerns the
vulnerability of external interfaces. Attackers may bias the reward
pathway without directly modifying the underlying dataset. For example,
they may manipulate the judge model, tool outputs, or scoring endpoints
so that unsafe or incorrect generations receive high rewards, while safe
behaviors are penalized \[[75](#bib.bib56), [76](#bib.bib55)\]. In
addition, memory systems can be exploited to inject carefully crafted
misleading context, encouraging the model to rely on untrusted
cues \[[74](#bib.bib57)\].

### IV-C Solution

We propose safety governance mechanisms: Behavioral Trustworthiness
Regulation for endogenous risks and Provenance-Grounded Trust
Certification for exogenous interface risks.

Behavioral Trustworthiness Regulation. To mitigate noisy
self-supervision, this component regulates behavior via three
mechanisms. It integrates process supervision \[[77](#bib.bib67),
[34](#bib.bib23)\] with outcome rewards to credit individual reasoning
steps, penalizing correct answers derived from flawed paths. A
calibration probe further tracks the gap between expressed confidence
and empirical accuracy, flagging reasoning chains whose answers change
under minor prompt rephrasing as shortcut-reliant. To mitigate unsafe
cognitive patterns, the component applies a step-level, safety-aware KL
penalty that targets only problematic steps, preserving the valid parts
of the trajectory \[[78](#bib.bib68), [79](#bib.bib69)\].

Provenance-Grounded Trust Certification. To address exogenous risks, we
propose Provenance-Grounded Trust Certification, which defines a trust
boundary for all data entering the training pipeline. For data
poisoning, this mechanism mandates verifiable origin metadata and
applies anomaly scoring over provenance graphs to flag injection
patterns that deviate from expected generation dynamics; once an anomaly
is detected, the pipeline blocks the affected source and rolls back to a
secure checkpoint. For interface manipulation, the component
periodically re-scores random samples with the
evaluator \[[80](#bib.bib70), [81](#bib.bib71)\] and withholds the batch
when score divergence exceeds a preset margin, preventing policy
corruption.

## V Conclusion

This paper argues that real-world LLM self-play systems should achieve
effectiveness, generalizability, and trustworthiness under data
scarcity. We outline three blue-sky directions: (1) data-centric
self-play for effective experience generation; (2) generalizable
self-play for open-world and unverifiable tasks; and (3) trustworthy
self-play for reliable self-evolution. The paper summarizes challenges
and potential solutions, aiming to provide a roadmap and promote a
sustainable and trustworthy paradigm for LLM self-play.

Abstract

Evolution has shaped intelligence for billions of years. Artificial
intelligence (AI) now raises a striking possibility: intelligence may
begin to direct its own evolution. As AI agents become capable of
modifying and evaluating their own behavior, how quickly could they
improve, and how can that improvement remain safe? We develop a
theoretical framework comparing Darwinian, Lamarckian, and
agent-directed evolution following environmental change. Darwinian
evolution relies on natural selection, Lamarckian evolution adds
inherited learning, and agent-directed evolution allows agents to
generate and evaluate behavioral variants and choose how extensively to
search. When learning is sufficiently effective, these mechanisms
exhibit progressively faster adaptation. Generating more variants
accelerates adaptation, while optimal search effort depends on learning
ability, problem difficulty, and experimentation costs. However, an
agent’s ability to direct its improvement does not ensure that
improvements are evaluated against the intended objective. When the
evaluation target is misspecified, additional search accelerates
convergence toward the wrong behavior, even as measured performance
improves. In recursive self-improvement, such errors may persist across
generations, posing challenges for AI safety. Independent evaluation and
oversight must keep pace with autonomous AI development. The central
challenge is not simply to accelerate the evolution of intelligence, but
to preserve humanity’s ability to guide its direction, limit its speed,
and intervene when necessary.

## 1 Introduction

On 8 September 2026, OpenAI announced that a coordinated system of
artificial intelligence (AI) agents had produced a proposed resolution
of the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem, showing that a
finite-time singularity forms under smooth external forcing ([OpenAI,
2026](#bib.bib13)). According to OpenAI, roughly 10,000 concurrent
agents explored different formulations and approaches, shared
intermediate findings, and built on promising results. They reached the
proposed solution in about 88 hours and then spent 17 hours formalizing
and verifying it in Lean ([de Moura and Ullrich, 2021](#bib.bib43)).

This search resembles evolution: variants are generated, promising ones
are retained, and useful information is passed on to later attempts.
FunSearch ([Romera-Paredes et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)), AlphaEvolve
([Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)), and the Darwin Gödel Machine
([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)) follow the same principle: AI systems
propose modifications to programs or agents, evaluate them, and build on
the successful ones.

These ideas come together in an emerging research direction, _recursive
self-improvement_ (RSI), in which an AI system improves itself and uses
the improved version to make further improvements. In the Darwin Gödel
Machine, AI agents modify their own code, evaluate the resulting
variants, and keep the successful changes for later generations ([Zhang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)). More recently, [Srikanth et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib39) developed AIDE², an AI research agent that
recursively modifies its own code and evaluates the results. These
studies suggest that AI systems are increasingly able not only to
perform complex tasks but also to improve the processes through which
they perform them.

These developments point to a more fundamental possibility: AI systems
may increasingly take control of their own evolution. Instead of
improving within a process designed by humans, a system may decide how
it searches for improvements, how extensively it experiments, and how it
carries improvements forward to later generations. Because improved
systems can produce further improvements, this could greatly accelerate
AI development and make its capabilities harder for humans to anticipate
and oversee. We call this shift _agent-directed evolution_. It holds
great potential for scientific discovery and technological progress, but
it also raises safety concerns as AI systems gain greater control over
their own development.

This possibility motivates several questions. How much faster can a
system adapt through learning and self-directed search than through
natural selection alone? What determines the speed of adaptation, and
how should an agent weigh the benefits of more search against its costs?
More importantly, does faster adaptation necessarily lead to better
outcomes, or can it also accelerate the development of behaviors that
humans did not intend?

To address these questions, we build on the evolutionary framework of
[Brennan and Lo (2011)](#bib.bib1); [Brennan and Lo (2012)](#bib.bib2),
in which individuals inherit different behaviors and natural selection
favors the behaviors that lead to faster population growth. When the
environment changes, previously successful behaviors may no longer be
optimal, and the population must adapt. In
[Section 3](#S3 "3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
we show that the growth lost to suboptimal behavior equals the
Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the environmental and
behavioral distributions
([Propositions 2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Growth regret is relative entropy). ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
and [3](#Thmproposition3 "Proposition 3 (Growth regret of a population with two behaviors). ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
We call this loss _growth regret_ and use it to compare how quickly
Darwinian, Lamarckian, and agent-directed evolution adapt.

The three mechanisms start from the same population. Under _Darwinian
evolution_, inherited behaviors are fixed, and the population adapts as
natural selection makes better-adapted individuals more prevalent
([Section 4](#S4 "4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
Under _Lamarckian evolution_, individuals can also learn from their
environment and pass their acquired improvements to later generations
([Section 5](#S5 "5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
Under _agent-directed evolution_, individuals further generate multiple
variants of themselves, evaluate them, and retain the best for further
learning
([Section 6](#S6 "6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
More importantly, the agent decides how extensively to search, which we
use as a simple model of how an intelligent system can influence its own
evolution. We measure speed by the _adaptation time_, the number of
generations needed to reduce growth regret to a given threshold, and the
_adaptation rate_, the long-run exponential rate at which growth regret
declines.

Our first set of results characterizes the adaptation speeds of the
three mechanisms. Darwinian adaptation relies on natural selection to
spread individuals who already have the optimal behavior, so it can take
arbitrarily long when they are initially rare
([Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Darwinian adaptation rate and time). ‣ 4.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
Lamarckian evolution removes this dependence, because individuals
improve their inherited behavior directly through learning
([Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2 (Lamarckian adaptation rate and time). ‣ 5.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
Agent-directed evolution adapts even faster by searching among
alternative improvements
([Theorem 3](#Thmtheorem3 "Theorem 3 (Agent-directed adaptation rate and time). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
In
[Section 7](#S7 "7 Comparing the Three Mechanisms ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
we show that, when learning is sufficiently effective, agent-directed
evolution has the highest long-run adaptation rate, followed by
Lamarckian and then Darwinian evolution; the same ranking holds for
expected adaptation times
([Theorem 4](#Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 4 (Ranking of adaptation speeds). ‣ 7.1 Comparing adaptation speeds ‣ 7 Comparing the Three Mechanisms ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).

Our second set of results, in
[Section 6](#S6 "6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
concerns how search accelerates adaptation and how an agent should
allocate its search effort. Generating more variants always increases
the adaptation rate, by an amount that depends on the difficulty of the
problem
([Proposition 4](#Thmproposition4 "Proposition 4 (What search adds). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
For easier problems, the gain can be substantial: in the most favorable
case, it grows approximately as $`2\log N`$, where $`N`$ is the number
of variants. For harder problems, the gain is smaller and approaches a
finite ceiling, although more variants still speed up adaptation. When
search is costly, the optimal number of variants increases as learning
becomes less effective, the problem becomes easier, or search becomes
cheaper
([Proposition 5](#Thmproposition5 "Proposition 5 (The optimal number of variants). ‣ Cost-minimizing agent. ‣ 6.3 Choosing the number of variants ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
An intelligent system can thus adjust its search effort according to its
learning ability, problem difficulty, and available resources.

Our third set of results concerns AI safety: faster adaptation does not
necessarily move a system toward the objectives its developers intend.
In
[Section 8](#S8 "8 Implications for AI Safety ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
we extend the agent-directed mechanism so that search and learning are
guided by an incorrectly specified evaluation target. The agent then
keeps improving rapidly according to its evaluator, but its true growth
regret does not vanish, and more variants only accelerate convergence
toward the incorrect target
([Theorem 5](#Thmtheorem5 "Theorem 5 (Adaptation toward a misspecified target). ‣ 8.1 Adaptation toward the wrong target ‣ 8 Implications for AI Safety ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
Natural selection, acting on realized outcomes, can eventually correct
the population’s behavior, but this correction may be slow. Independent
evaluation and oversight therefore become increasingly important.

Our analysis brings together ideas from evolutionary biology, economics,
and AI: drawing on the biological mechanisms of selection and
inheritance, we use a growth-based framework to study how learning and
search affect the speed and cost of adaptation in AI systems.
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Notation ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
summarizes how the main elements of our model correspond to their
counterparts in evolutionary biology and modern AI systems.

## 2 Related Literature

We review six areas of research in evolutionary biology, information
theory, economics, and AI that bear on the speed and direction of
adaptation.

##### Natural Selection and Growth-Optimal Behavior.

We build on the evolutionary model of [Brennan and Lo
(2011)](#bib.bib1), in which natural selection favors inherited
behaviors that lead to higher population growth. Later studies extend
this framework to bounded rationality and intelligence ([Brennan and Lo,
2012](#bib.bib2)), behavioral diversification ([Brennan et al.,
2018](#bib.bib3)), and learning under limited information and memory
([Lo and Zhang, 2021](#bib.bib4); [Lo et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). In
evolutionary biology, _bet hedging_ describes how organisms diversify
their offspring’s behaviors to cope with environmental uncertainty
([Cohen, 1966](#bib.bib25); [Bull, 1987](#bib.bib27); [Seger and
Brockmann, 1987](#bib.bib26)). Building on this literature, we study how
quickly a population adapts after an environmental change.

##### Information Theory and Evolutionary Growth.

Information and growth have long been linked. In the framework of [Kelly
(1956)](#bib.bib20), the growth lost by using incorrect probabilities
can be expressed in terms of the KL divergence ([Cover and Thomas,
2006](#bib.bib21), Chapter 6), and evolutionary biology studies the
fitness value of information about the environment ([Kussell and
Leibler, 2005](#bib.bib22); [Donaldson-Matasci et al.,
2010](#bib.bib23); [Rivoire and Leibler, 2011](#bib.bib24)). We
establish an analogous relationship between population growth and KL
divergence, which lets us measure the loss from suboptimal behavior and
compare how quickly different evolutionary mechanisms eliminate it.

##### Learning and the Inheritance of Acquired Traits.

Previous research distinguishes learning that affects an individual’s
performance from learning whose improvements are inherited. In the
Baldwin effect, individual learning can facilitate evolution even
without the inheritance of acquired traits ([Hinton and Nowlan,
1987](#bib.bib6); [Ackley and Littman, 1992](#bib.bib29)), whereas
Lamarckian evolutionary algorithms pass improvements acquired through
learning to offspring ([Whitley et al., 1994](#bib.bib28)). Related
ideas appear in population-based training ([Jaderberg et al.,
2017](#bib.bib16)) and in methods that transfer learned knowledge across
generations of neural networks ([Feng et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)). Our
Lamarckian mechanism isolates the inheritance of acquired improvements
and examines how this channel changes the speed of adaptation under
natural selection.

##### Adaptive Search and Resource Allocation.

Evolutionary computation has long studied how to adjust search
parameters, such as mutation rates and the distribution of candidate
solutions, to improve performance ([Eiben et al., 1999](#bib.bib7);
[Hansen and Ostermeier, 2001](#bib.bib30); [Bedau and Packard,
2003](#bib.bib8); [Ollivier et al., 2017](#bib.bib32)); autoconstructive
evolution goes further and lets the mechanisms of reproduction and
variation evolve within the individuals themselves ([Spector,
2001](#bib.bib31)). Economic models examine how agents adjust their
learning rules to changing environments or forecasting performance
([Evans and Honkapohja, 2001](#bib.bib36); [Marcet and Nicolini,
2003](#bib.bib35)). More recent research on inference-time computation
studies how the benefit of generating more candidates depends on problem
difficulty and computational resources ([Brown et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18); [Snell et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)). Our agent-directed
mechanism poses a related decision in an evolutionary setting, how many
variants to generate, and we characterize how search raises the rate of
adaptation and how the optimal number of variants depends on problem
difficulty, learning ability, and search costs.

##### RSI in AI.

The possibility that intelligent machines could improve their own
capabilities has been discussed since [Good (1965)](#bib.bib41); an
early formal approach is the Gödel machine of [Schmidhuber
(2007)](#bib.bib42). FunSearch and AlphaEvolve use AI to generate,
evaluate, and retain improved solutions ([Romera-Paredes et al.,
2024](#bib.bib14); [Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)), and the Darwin
Gödel Machine ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)), AIDE² ([Srikanth et
al., 2026](#bib.bib39)), and multi-agent RSI ([Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib40)) extend this process to AI agents themselves. These
developments have prompted discussion of whether AI evolution will
remain directed by humans or become increasingly autonomous ([Boudry,
2026](#bib.bib19); [Müller et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Zong et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). Our analysis offers a theoretical perspective by
studying how an agent’s control over its own search effort affects the
speed and cost of adaptation.

##### Evaluation and the Direction of Adaptation.

The effectiveness of search depends on how candidate improvements are
evaluated. Evolutionary algorithms can exploit evaluation criteria in
unintended ways ([Lehman et al., 2020](#bib.bib38)), and stronger
optimization against learned reward models can widen the gap between
measured reward and true quality ([Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib37)). We
examine how an incorrectly specified evaluation target affects
evolutionary adaptation and show that more search can accelerate
adaptation toward the evaluator’s target without correcting the error.

## 3 The Framework

To compare different mechanisms of adaptation, we need a framework that
identifies both the optimal behavior and the cost of deviating from it.
We adopt the evolutionary model of [Brennan and Lo (2011)](#bib.bib1),
in which natural selection favors behaviors that maximize long-run
population growth.
[Section 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
studies the growth of a lineage,
[Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
extends the analysis to a population with multiple lineages, and
[Section 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Mechanisms and measures of adaptation ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
introduces the three evolutionary mechanisms, establishes a common
setting for comparing them, and defines the adaptation time and rate.

### 3.1 Growth of a lineage

Consider a large lineage of asexually reproducing individuals. In
generation $`t`$, all individuals inherit the same behavior
$`f_{t}\in(0,1)`$, choosing action $`a`$ with probability $`f_{t}`$ and
action $`b`$ with probability $`1-f_{t}`$. The three evolutionary
mechanisms considered in this study differ in whether and how $`f_{t}`$
changes across generations. Individuals in generation $`t`$ reproduce to
form generation $`t+1`$, and their reproductive success depends on the
chosen action and the environmental state
$`X_{t+1}\in\{\alpha,\beta\}`$, which is shared by all individuals. Let
$`x_{a}`$ and $`x_{b}`$ denote the numbers of offspring produced by an
individual choosing $`a`$ and $`b`$, respectively. Then

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
(x_{a},x_{b})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}(m,0),&X_{t+1}=\alpha,&\text{with probability }p,\\[2.0pt]
(0,m),&X_{t+1}=\beta,&\text{with probability }1-p,\end{array}\right.
``` |  |

where $`m>0`$ is the number of offspring produced by an individual
choosing the successful action: action $`a`$ succeeds in state
$`\alpha`$, and action $`b`$ succeeds in state $`\beta`$. Individuals
choosing the other action produce no offspring. The probability of state
$`\alpha`$ is $`p\in(0,1)`$, and the environmental states
$`X_{1},X_{2},\ldots`$ are independent across generations.

Let $`n_{t}`$ denote the size of the lineage in generation $`t`$.
Because the lineage is large, a fraction $`f_{t}`$ of its individuals
chooses action $`a`$, while the remaining fraction $`1-f_{t}`$ chooses
action $`b`$. If $`X_{t+1}=\alpha`$, only those choosing $`a`$
reproduce, each producing $`m`$ offspring, so the lineage grows by the
factor $`n_{t+1}/n_{t}=mf_{t}`$. Similarly, if $`X_{t+1}=\beta`$, the
lineage grows by the factor $`n_{t+1}/n_{t}=m(1-f_{t})`$. Taking the
expected logarithm of these growth factors gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle I_{p}(f_{t})\equiv\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\frac{n_{t+1}}{n_{t}}\,\Big|\,f_{t}\Big]`$ | $`\displaystyle=p\log(mf_{t})+(1-p)\log\big(m(1-f_{t})\big)`$ |  | (1) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\log m+p\log f_{t}+(1-p)\log(1-f_{t}).`$ |  |  |

We refer to $`I_{p}(f)`$ as the growth rate associated with behavior
$`f`$.

Given the growth rate in
Equation ([1](#S3.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
we can now identify the inherited behavior that maximizes population
growth.

###### Proposition 1 (Probability matching).

For $`p\in(0,1)`$, $`I_{p}`$ is strictly concave on $`(0,1)`$, and

|     |                                 |     |
|-----|---------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \arg\max_{f\in(0,1)}I_{p}(f)=p.
       ```                              |     |

The proposition shows that a lineage maximizes its expected log growth
in every generation by matching the fraction of individuals choosing
each action to the probability that the action succeeds. The intuition
is that diversification is essential when all individuals face the same
environmental risk. A lineage that always chooses one action will
eventually become extinct when the other action succeeds, whereas a
lineage that divides its individuals between the two actions can
reproduce in either environmental state. This is the
probability-matching result of [Brennan and Lo (2011)](#bib.bib1).

To compare how different evolutionary mechanisms adapt to a changing
environment, we need a measure of how much growth a lineage loses when
its behavior deviates from the optimum. The following proposition
defines this loss and establishes its connection to information theory.

###### Proposition 2 (Growth regret is relative entropy).

For $`p,f\in(0,1)`$, the growth regret of behavior $`f`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}_{p}(f)\equiv I_{p}(p)-I_{p}(f)=p\log\frac{p}{f}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-f}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\mathrm{Bern}(p)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(f)\right)\geq 0,
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\mathrm{Bern}(\theta)`$ denotes the Bernoulli distribution with
success probability $`\theta`$, and $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}`$ denotes the KL
divergence. Equality holds if and only if $`f=p`$.

The proposition shows that growth regret can be expressed exactly as the
KL divergence between the environmental distribution and the inherited
behavioral distribution. This result is closely related to the Kelly
criterion ([Kelly, 1956](#bib.bib20)), which characterizes the cost of
betting according to incorrect probabilities. For a lineage with the
behavior $`f_{t}`$ in generation $`t`$, $`\mathcal{R}_{p}(f_{t})`$ is
the expected log growth that it loses from generation $`t`$ to
generation $`t+1`$ relative to a lineage with the optimal behavior
$`p`$. Growth regret thus provides a natural measure of maladaptation,
allowing us to compare the three evolutionary mechanisms in terms of how
quickly they recover the growth lost after an environmental change.

To understand the magnitude of growth regret and how it varies with
behavior $`f`$, consider its Taylor expansion around the optimal
behavior $`f=p`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}_{p}(f)=\frac{(f-p)^{2}}{2p(1-p)}+O\big((f-p)^{3}\big),\qquad f\to p.
``` |  | (3) |

The expansion shows that, for small deviations, growth regret increases
approximately with the square of the distance from the optimum. Doubling
the deviation therefore approximately quadruples the loss in growth.

### 3.2 Growth of a population

So far, we have considered a single lineage whose individuals share the
same behavior within each generation. We now extend the analysis to a
population consisting of several lineages, each with its own behavior.
For simplicity, we focus on two lineages, although the results extend to
more. In generation $`t`$, let $`f_{1,t}`$ and $`f_{2,t}`$ denote their
behaviors, $`q_{t}`$ and $`1-q_{t}`$ their population shares, and
$`n_{t}`$ the size of the entire population. The two lineages therefore
contain $`q_{t}n_{t}`$ and $`(1-q_{t})n_{t}`$ individuals, respectively.

To determine the growth of the entire population, we first consider the
offspring produced by each lineage. If $`X_{t+1}=\alpha`$, only
individuals choosing action $`a`$ reproduce. The first lineage therefore
grows by the factor $`mf_{1,t}`$, contributing $`mf_{1,t}q_{t}n_{t}`$
individuals to the next generation. Similarly, the second lineage
contributes $`mf_{2,t}(1-q_{t})n_{t}`$ individuals. Adding the two
contributions, the population has
$`mf_{1,t}q_{t}n_{t}+mf_{2,t}(1-q_{t})n_{t}=m\bar{f}_{t}n_{t}`$
individuals in generation $`t+1`$, where

|     |                                                 |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \bar{f}_{t}\equiv q_{t}f_{1,t}+(1-q_{t})f_{2,t}
       ```                                              |     | (4) |

is the average behavior of the population. If $`X_{t+1}=\beta`$, only
individuals choosing action $`b`$ reproduce. The two lineages then
contribute $`m(1-f_{1,t})q_{t}n_{t}`$ and $`m(1-f_{2,t})(1-q_{t})n_{t}`$
individuals, respectively, giving a total population of
$`m(1-\bar{f}_{t})n_{t}`$ in generation $`t+1`$.

We can also use these results to determine how the relative shares of
the two lineages evolve through natural selection. The share of the
first lineage in generation $`t+1`$ is its number of offspring divided
by the total population size:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{t+1}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{mf_{1,t}q_{t}n_{t}}{m\bar{f}_{t}n_{t}}=\frac{q_{t}f_{1,t}}{\bar{f}_{t}},&X_{t+1}=\alpha,\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{m(1-f_{1,t})q_{t}n_{t}}{m(1-\bar{f}_{t})n_{t}}=\frac{q_{t}(1-f_{1,t})}{1-\bar{f}_{t}},&X_{t+1}=\beta.\end{cases}
``` |  | (5) |

Equation ([5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
describes how natural selection changes the relative shares of the two
lineages from one generation to the next.

We now consider the growth of the population as a whole. When
$`X_{t+1}=\alpha`$, its size increases from $`n_{t}`$ to
$`m\bar{f}_{t}n_{t}`$, whereas when $`X_{t+1}=\beta`$, it increases to
$`m(1-\bar{f}_{t})n_{t}`$. Taking the expected logarithm of the
population growth ratio $`n_{t+1}/n_{t}`$ gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
I_{p}(q_{t},f_{1,t},f_{2,t})\equiv\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\frac{n_{t+1}}{n_{t}}\,\Big|\,q_{t},f_{1,t},f_{2,t}\Big]=p\log(m\bar{f}_{t})+(1-p)\log\big(m(1-\bar{f}_{t})\big)=I_{p}(\bar{f}_{t}),
``` |  | (6) |

where the last equality follows from
Equation ([1](#S3.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
with $`\bar{f}_{t}`$ in place of $`f_{t}`$.
Equation ([6](#S3.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
shows that the population’s expected log growth depends on the behaviors
of the two lineages only through their average $`\bar{f}_{t}`$. This is
because reproductive success depends on the chosen action and the
environmental state, rather than on which lineage an individual belongs
to.

We can therefore measure the growth regret of the entire population in
the same way as for a single lineage, by comparing its expected log
growth with the optimal growth rate $`I_{p}(p)`$. Combining
Equation ([6](#S3.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
with
[Proposition 2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Growth regret is relative entropy). ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
gives the following result.

###### Proposition 3 (Growth regret of a population with two behaviors).

For $`p,f_{1,t},f_{2,t}\in(0,1)`$ and $`q_{t}\in[0,1]`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
I_{p}(p)-I_{p}(q_{t},f_{1,t},f_{2,t})=\mathcal{R}_{p}(\bar{f}_{t}).
``` |  |

The population’s growth regret is therefore determined by its average
behavior $`\bar{f}_{t}`$.

### 3.3 Mechanisms and measures of adaptation

The preceding analysis shows that a population’s growth regret depends
on its average behavior, which can change through two channels: natural
selection changes the relative shares of lineages, while learning or
search can change their inherited behaviors. We consider three
evolutionary mechanisms that differ in which of these channels are
available. Under Darwinian evolution, inherited behaviors remain fixed,
and adaptation occurs through natural selection alone. Lamarckian
evolution additionally allows individuals to learn and pass their
acquired improvements to their descendants. Agent-directed evolution
further allows individuals to search among variants of their behavior.
Natural selection operates under all three mechanisms.

To compare these mechanisms, we consider a common environmental change.
Suppose that at time $`0`$, the probability of state $`\alpha`$ changes
permanently from $`p_{\mathrm{old}}`$ to
$`p_{\mathrm{new}}\neq p_{\mathrm{old}}`$. By
[Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Probability matching). ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
the growth-optimal behavior changes from $`p_{\mathrm{old}}`$ to
$`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$. We study how quickly each mechanism allows the
population to adapt to the new environment.

We assume that all three mechanisms start from the same population. At
time $`0`$, a fraction $`q_{0}\in(0,1)`$ of individuals already follows
the new optimal behavior $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, while the remaining
fraction $`1-q_{0}`$ follows the previously optimal behavior
$`p_{\mathrm{old}}`$. We refer to their descendants as the *adapted
lineage* and the *maladapted lineage*, respectively. These names
describe their initial behaviors, since the maladapted lineage may
become better adapted over time. The adapted lineage retains behavior
$`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ throughout, while the behavior of the maladapted
lineage evolves according to the mechanism under consideration.

We denote the three mechanisms by $`\mathrm{D}`$, $`\mathrm{L}`$, and
$`\mathrm{A}`$, respectively. For each mechanism
$`\mathrm{M}\in\{\mathrm{D},\mathrm{L},\mathrm{A}\}`$, let
$`q_{t}^{\mathrm{M}}`$ denote the population share of the adapted
lineage and $`f_{t}^{\mathrm{M}}`$ the behavior of the maladapted
lineage in generation $`t`$. By
Equation ([4](#S3.E4 "Equation 4 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
the population’s average behavior is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{M}}=q_{t}^{\mathrm{M}}p_{\mathrm{new}}+(1-q_{t}^{\mathrm{M}})f_{t}^{\mathrm{M}}.
``` |  |

Under all three mechanisms, natural selection changes the population
share $`q_{t}^{\mathrm{M}}`$ according to
Equation ([5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
with $`f_{1,t}=p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ and $`f_{2,t}=f_{t}^{\mathrm{M}}`$.
The mechanisms differ in how the behavior $`f_{t}^{\mathrm{M}}`$ evolves
across generations. By
[Proposition 3](#Thmproposition3 "Proposition 3 (Growth regret of a population with two behaviors). ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
the population’s growth regret in generation $`t`$ is
$`\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{M}})`$, which we
use to measure maladaptation throughout. Since all three mechanisms
share the initial conditions $`q_{0}^{\mathrm{M}}=q_{0}`$ and
$`f_{0}^{\mathrm{M}}=p_{\mathrm{old}}`$, they also have the same initial
growth regret. We can therefore compare how quickly the three mechanisms
reduce growth regret from a common starting point.

There are two natural ways to measure the speed of adaptation. We can
ask how many generations are needed to reduce regret by a specified
fraction, or how rapidly regret declines over the long run. These lead
to two complementary measures, adaptation time and adaptation rate,
which we define for all three evolutionary mechanisms.

###### Definition 1 (Adaptation time).

For $`\varepsilon\in(0,1)`$ and $`q_{0}\in[0,1)`$, the adaptation time
of Mechanism $`\mathrm{M}`$ is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tau^{\mathrm{M}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})\equiv\min\left\{t\geq 0:\ \mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{M}}_{t})\leq\varepsilon\,\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{M}}_{0})\right\},
``` |  |

with the convention that $`\min\varnothing=\infty`$.

###### Definition 2 (Adaptation rate).

The adaptation rate of Mechanism $`\mathrm{M}`$ is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\kappa^{\mathrm{M}}\equiv-\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{M}}_{t}),
``` |  |

whenever the limit exists with probability one.

The adaptation time is the first generation in which growth regret falls
to at most a fraction $`\varepsilon`$ of its initial value. It depends
on the mechanism $`\mathrm{M}`$, the threshold $`\varepsilon`$, and the
initial share $`q_{0}`$ of the adapted lineage. The adaptation rate
measures the long-run exponential decay of growth regret, with a larger
rate indicating faster convergence.

## 4 Darwinian Evolution

We begin with Darwinian evolution, in which the two lineages retain
their respective behaviors $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ and $`p_{\mathrm{old}}`$
across generations. Since their behaviors remain fixed, the population
can adapt only through changes in their relative shares under natural
selection. This mechanism provides a benchmark for the other two.
[Section 4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 The model ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
introduces the Darwinian mechanism, and
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
analyzes its adaptation time and rate.

### 4.1 The model

The defining feature of Darwinian evolution is that adaptation occurs
through natural selection rather than changes in inherited behavior. The
two lineages retain their respective behaviors $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ and
$`p_{\mathrm{old}}`$, while differences in reproductive success change
their relative population shares. Following
Equation ([5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
Mechanism [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 The model ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
describes this process from one generation to the next.

 

Mechanism D: Darwinian evolution

 

Start.
Generation $`0`$ has $`q^{\mathrm{D}}_{0}=q_{0}`$ and
$`f^{\mathrm{D}}_{0}=p_{\mathrm{old}}`$, and each generation $`t\geq 1`$
is formed from generation $`t-1`$ in the steps below; throughout,
$`\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t}\equiv q^{\mathrm{D}}_{t}p_{\mathrm{new}}+(1-q^{\mathrm{D}}_{t})f^{\mathrm{D}}_{t}`$.

Inheritance.
$`f^{\mathrm{D}}_{t}=f^{\mathrm{D}}_{t-1}=p_{\mathrm{old}}`$.

Selection.
Once the state $`X_{t}`$ is realized,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q^{\mathrm{D}}_{t}=\begin{cases}q^{\mathrm{D}}_{t-1}\,p_{\mathrm{new}}/\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t-1},&X_{t}=\alpha,\\[2.0pt]
q^{\mathrm{D}}_{t-1}\,(1-p_{\mathrm{new}})/(1-\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t-1}),&X_{t}=\beta.\end{cases}
``` |  |

 

By
[Proposition 3](#Thmproposition3 "Proposition 3 (Growth regret of a population with two behaviors). ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
the whole population grows in each generation as a single
type-$`\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t}`$ lineage would, and its growth regret
in generation $`t`$ is
$`\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t})`$. Although
the behaviors of the two lineages remain fixed, natural selection
changes their relative shares and hence the population’s average
behavior.

### 4.2 Adaptation time and rate

The following theorem characterizes the adaptation time
$`\tau^{\mathrm{D}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$ and rate
$`\kappa^{\mathrm{D}}`$ of
Mechanism [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 The model ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
as defined in
[Definitions 1](#Thmdefinition1 "Definition 1 (Adaptation time). ‣ 3.3 Mechanisms and measures of adaptation ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
and [2](#Thmdefinition2 "Definition 2 (Adaptation rate). ‣ 3.3 Mechanisms and measures of adaptation ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

###### Theorem 1 (Darwinian adaptation rate and time).

Under
Mechanism [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 The model ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"):

1.  (i)
    for every $`q_{0}\in(0,1)`$, $`\kappa^{\mathrm{D}}=2\Delta`$ with
    probability one;
2.  (ii)
    |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
    |----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
    |  |  | $`\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\,\tau^{\mathrm{D}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}=\frac{1}{\kappa^{\mathrm{D}}}`$ |  | $`\displaystyle\text{for every }q_{0}\in(0,1),`$ |  | (7) |
    |  |  | $`\displaystyle\lim_{q_{0}\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\,\tau^{\mathrm{D}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})}{\log(1/q_{0})}=\frac{1}{\Delta}`$ |  | $`\displaystyle\text{for every }\varepsilon\in(0,1).`$ |  |  |

Here,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta\equiv p_{\mathrm{new}}\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{new}}}{p_{\mathrm{old}}}+(1-p_{\mathrm{new}})\log\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{new}}}{1-p_{\mathrm{old}}}=\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(p_{\mathrm{old}})>0.
``` |  | (8) |

Part (i) of
[Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Darwinian adaptation rate and time). ‣ 4.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
shows that the long-run adaptation rate under Darwinian evolution is
$`\kappa^{\mathrm{D}}=2\Delta`$. The quantity $`\Delta`$ is the adapted
lineage’s advantage in expected log growth per generation. The larger
the growth advantage $`\Delta`$ of the adapted lineage, the faster it
spreads through the population and the faster growth regret declines.
The factor of two arises because growth regret is approximately
quadratic near the optimum
(Equation ([3](#S3.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))).
Importantly, the adaptation rate does not depend on the initial share
$`q_{0}`$ of the adapted lineage: regardless of how rare it is
initially, the population eventually adapts at the same long-run rate.

Part (ii) of
[Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Darwinian adaptation rate and time). ‣ 4.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
explains how the expected adaptation time depends on the threshold
$`\varepsilon`$ and the initial share $`q_{0}`$. First, for any fixed
$`q_{0}>0`$, the expected adaptation time grows like
$`\log(1/\varepsilon)/\kappa^{\mathrm{D}}`$ as $`\varepsilon`$
approaches zero. Second, for any fixed threshold $`\varepsilon`$, the
expected adaptation time grows like $`\log(1/q_{0})/\Delta`$ as
$`q_{0}`$ approaches zero. The intuition is that when the adapted
lineage is initially rare, it must first grow to a sufficiently large
share before it can substantially improve the population’s average
behavior. We call this additional delay the *seed penalty*. It can
become arbitrarily large even though the long-run adaptation rate
remains unchanged. At $`q_{0}=0`$, adaptation is impossible: without any
individuals carrying the adapted behavior, natural selection has no way
to introduce it.¹¹ 1 Mutation could introduce new behaviors and thereby
make adaptation possible, but we do not consider mutation in this paper.

Darwinian adaptation operates entirely through natural selection among
fixed inherited behaviors. We next consider Lamarckian evolution, in
which learning can also change the behavior passed on to future
generations.

## 5 Lamarckian Evolution

We now consider Lamarckian evolution, which adds the inheritance of
acquired improvements to the Darwinian framework: natural selection
still determines the relative shares of the two lineages, but the
maladapted lineage can also learn from the new environment and pass its
improved behavior to its descendants.
[Section 5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
introduces the Lamarckian mechanism and examines how learning interacts
with selection, and
[Section 5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
analyzes its adaptation time and rate.

### 5.1 The model

Under Lamarckian evolution, individuals can acquire better behavior
during their lifetimes and pass it on to their descendants, so the
initially maladapted lineage can improve its inherited behavior over
successive generations instead of relying entirely on natural selection
to increase the share of the already adapted lineage.

How much can learning improve inherited behavior in each generation? If
individuals could fully adopt the new optimal behavior
$`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ and transmit it to their descendants, they could
reach the optimum in a single generation. However, individuals may be
able to change their behavior only partly within a lifetime, and
acquired improvements may not be fully transmitted. We capture these
limitations by assuming that, in each generation, learning and
transmission move the maladapted lineage’s behavior a fixed fraction of
the way toward the new optimum:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t+1}=f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}+\lambda\,(p_{\mathrm{new}}-f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}),\qquad\lambda\in(0,1),
``` |  | (9) |

with $`f^{\mathrm{L}}_{0}=p_{\mathrm{old}}`$.

The parameter $`\lambda`$ measures the effectiveness of inherited
learning: it combines how much individuals can change their behavior
within a generation with how faithfully these changes are passed on. A
larger $`\lambda`$ closes more of the remaining gap in each generation,
and in the limiting case $`\lambda=1`$, the lineage would reach the new
optimum in a single generation.

Equation ([9](#S5.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
specifies the direction and speed of learning rather than how
individuals learn. Behavior moves toward the optimum of the new
environment, much as training moves an AI system toward the behavior
that its objective rewards. This adjustment rule is closely related to
partial-adjustment and adaptive-expectations models in economics
([Cagan, 1956](#bib.bib34); [Nerlove, 1958](#bib.bib33)).

Combining learning with the selection rule in
Equation ([5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
gives
Mechanism [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

 

Mechanism L: Lamarckian evolution

 

Start.
Generation $`0`$ has $`q^{\mathrm{L}}_{0}=q_{0}`$ and
$`f^{\mathrm{L}}_{0}=p_{\mathrm{old}}`$, and each generation $`t\geq 1`$
is formed from generation $`t-1`$ in the steps below; throughout,
$`\bar{f}^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}\equiv q^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}p_{\mathrm{new}}+(1-q^{\mathrm{L}}_{t})f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}`$.

Learning.
$`f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}=f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t-1}+\lambda\,(p_{\mathrm{new}}-f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t-1})`$.

Selection.
Once the state $`X_{t}`$ is realized,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}=\begin{cases}q^{\mathrm{L}}_{t-1}\,p_{\mathrm{new}}/\bar{f}^{\mathrm{L}}_{t-1},&X_{t}=\alpha,\\[2.0pt]
q^{\mathrm{L}}_{t-1}\,(1-p_{\mathrm{new}})/(1-\bar{f}^{\mathrm{L}}_{t-1}),&X_{t}=\beta.\end{cases}
``` |  |

 

Mechanism [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
shows the two channels of adaptation directly:
$`\bar{f}^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}=(1-q^{\mathrm{L}}_{t})(f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}})`$.
Natural selection changes the population share $`1-q^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}`$
of the maladapted lineage, while learning eliminates a fraction
$`\lambda`$ of its remaining behavioral gap
$`f^{\mathrm{L}}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ in each generation. The
population can thus approach the new optimum even without the adapted
lineage taking over.

### 5.2 Adaptation time and rate

The following theorem characterizes the adaptation time
$`\tau^{\mathrm{L}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$ and rate
$`\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}`$ of
Mechanism [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
as defined in
[Definitions 1](#Thmdefinition1 "Definition 1 (Adaptation time). ‣ 3.3 Mechanisms and measures of adaptation ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
and [2](#Thmdefinition2 "Definition 2 (Adaptation rate). ‣ 3.3 Mechanisms and measures of adaptation ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

###### Theorem 2 (Lamarckian adaptation rate and time).

Under
Mechanism [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"):

1.  (i)
    for every $`q_{0}\in[0,1)`$,
    $`\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}=-2\log(1-\lambda)`$ along every history;
2.  (ii)
    |  |  |  |  |  |  |
    |----|----|----|----|----|----|
    |  |  | $`\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\,\tau^{\mathrm{L}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}=\frac{1}{\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}}`$ |  | $`\displaystyle\text{for every }q_{0}\in[0,1),`$ |  |
    |  |  | $`\displaystyle\sup_{q_{0}\in[0,1)}\mathbb{E}\,\tau^{\mathrm{L}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})<\infty`$ |  | $`\displaystyle\text{for every }\varepsilon\in(0,1).`$ |  |

Part (i) of
[Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2 (Lamarckian adaptation rate and time). ‣ 5.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
shows that the long-run adaptation rate under Lamarckian evolution is
$`\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}=-2\log(1-\lambda)`$: the larger the learning
parameter $`\lambda`$, the faster the maladapted lineage moves toward
the new optimum. Unlike under Darwinian evolution, the rate is
determined by the speed of learning rather than by the growth advantage
$`\Delta`$ of the adapted lineage. Under Darwinian evolution, the two
lineages retain their original behaviors, so the adapted lineage keeps a
constant growth advantage. Under Lamarckian evolution, learning
gradually eliminates this advantage by improving the maladapted lineage,
and natural selection weakens as the two lineages become more similar.
Although selection continues to operate, long-run adaptation is
therefore driven by learning rather than by changes in population
shares.

Part (ii) of
[Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2 (Lamarckian adaptation rate and time). ‣ 5.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
characterizes the expected adaptation time. For any fixed
$`q_{0}\in[0,1)`$, it grows like
$`\log(1/\varepsilon)/\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}`$ as $`\varepsilon`$
approaches zero. More importantly, it remains uniformly bounded across
all initial shares $`q_{0}\in[0,1)`$. This is a fundamental difference
from Darwinian evolution, where an initially rare adapted lineage can
lead to an arbitrarily large seed penalty
([Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Darwinian adaptation rate and time). ‣ 4.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
Under Lamarckian evolution, the maladapted lineage can improve its own
behavior rather than wait to be replaced, so even when $`q_{0}=0`$, with
no individual initially possessing the optimal behavior, learning can
still bring the population toward the new optimum.

Under
Mechanism [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
however, the learning process is fixed: $`\lambda`$ determines the
fraction of the remaining gap eliminated in each generation, and the
system does not decide how to search for improvements. We next consider
agent-directed evolution, in which the system can generate and evaluate
multiple variants of itself and choose how many to explore, so that the
process of adaptation itself becomes a decision.

## 6 Agent-Directed Evolution

We now turn to agent-directed evolution, which adds the ability to
search for improvements to the Lamarckian framework: in addition to
learning, the maladapted lineage can generate multiple variants of its
behavior, evaluate them, and retain the best one for further learning.
More importantly, the agent can decide how many variants to explore,
which lets it influence the process by which it adapts.
[Section 6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 The model ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
introduces the agent-directed mechanism,
[Section 6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
analyzes its adaptation time and rate and the gain from search, and
[Section 6.3](#S6.SS3 "6.3 Choosing the number of variants ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
examines how many variants the agent chooses when it maximizes the speed
of adaptation and when it minimizes its cost.

### 6.1 The model

In
Mechanism [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
the maladapted lineage improves its inherited behavior through a fixed
learning rule. An intelligent agent may also be able to experiment with
different ways of improving its behavior before passing it on: it can
generate several candidate modifications of its current behavior,
evaluate them, and retain the best one for further learning. We call
these candidates *variants* and let $`N\geq 1`$ denote the number of
variants the agent chooses to generate in each generation.

How much improvement can these variants achieve? The outcome of any
attempt is uncertain, with some variants producing substantial
improvements and others very little, and the difficulty of the problem
may limit how much progress even the best attempt can make. We capture
both features by letting each variant move toward the new optimum by a
random fraction of the remaining behavioral gap: in generation $`t`$,
the agent generates $`N`$ variants with behaviors

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{t,j}=f^{\mathrm{A}}_{t-1}+\rho\,U_{t,j}\,(p_{\mathrm{new}}-f^{\mathrm{A}}_{t-1}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N,
``` |  | (10) |

where the random variables $`U_{t,j}`$ are independent, uniformly
distributed on $`(0,1)`$, and independent of the environment.

The parameter $`\rho\in(0,1]`$ represents the difficulty of the problem:
it is the maximum fraction of the remaining gap that a single variant
can eliminate. A smaller $`\rho`$ corresponds to a harder problem, in
which even a successful attempt makes only limited progress, and when
$`\rho=1`$, a single variant can move arbitrarily close to the optimum.
The agent can choose how many variants to generate, but it controls
neither their random outcomes nor $`\rho`$. As with the Lamarckian
learning rule,
Equation ([10](#S6.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ 6.1 The model ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
is a reduced-form assumption: the agent need not know
$`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, but its attempts are assumed to move in the
correct direction, with uncertain magnitudes.

We assume that an external evaluator compares the variants and
identifies the one whose behavior is closest to $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$;
the agent itself need not know $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ and only receives
the evaluator’s assessment. The selected variant then undergoes the same
learning as in
Mechanism [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
moving a fraction $`\lambda`$ of the remaining gap toward the optimum,
and natural selection operates between the two lineages as before.
Combining these steps gives
Mechanism [6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 The model ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

 

Mechanism A: agent-directed evolution

 

Start.
Generation $`0`$ has $`q^{\mathrm{A}}_{0}=q_{0}`$ and
$`f^{\mathrm{A}}_{0}=p_{\mathrm{old}}`$, and each generation $`t\geq 1`$
is formed from generation $`t-1`$ in the steps below; throughout,
$`\bar{f}^{\mathrm{A}}_{t}\equiv q^{\mathrm{A}}_{t}p_{\mathrm{new}}+(1-q^{\mathrm{A}}_{t})f^{\mathrm{A}}_{t}`$.

Search.
The agent creates $`N`$ variants with behaviors
$`x_{t,j}=f^{\mathrm{A}}_{t-1}+\rho\,U_{t,j}(p_{\mathrm{new}}-f^{\mathrm{A}}_{t-1})`$
for $`j=1,\ldots,N`$, where the draws $`U_{t,j}`$ are independent and
uniform on $`(0,1)`$.

Evaluation.
The evaluator reports
$`J_{t}\in\arg\min_{1\leq j\leq N}|x_{t,j}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|`$, and
variant $`J_{t}`$ is kept.

Learning.
$`f^{\mathrm{A}}_{t}=x_{t,J_{t}}+\lambda\,(p_{\mathrm{new}}-x_{t,J_{t}})`$.

Selection.
Once the state $`X_{t}`$ is realized,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q^{\mathrm{A}}_{t}=\begin{cases}q^{\mathrm{A}}_{t-1}\,p_{\mathrm{new}}/\bar{f}^{\mathrm{A}}_{t-1},&X_{t}=\alpha,\\[2.0pt]
q^{\mathrm{A}}_{t-1}\,(1-p_{\mathrm{new}})/(1-\bar{f}^{\mathrm{A}}_{t-1}),&X_{t}=\beta.\end{cases}
``` |  |

 

Since every variant moves toward the new optimum, the evaluator selects
the variant with the largest random draw,
$`U^{*}_{t}\equiv\max\{U_{t,1},\ldots,U_{t,N}\}`$. Search eliminates a
fraction $`\rho U^{*}_{t}`$ of the remaining behavioral gap, and
learning then eliminates a fraction $`\lambda`$ of what remains.
Combining these two steps gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f^{\mathrm{A}}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}=(1-\lambda)(1-\rho U^{*}_{t})(f^{\mathrm{A}}_{t-1}-p_{\mathrm{new}}).
``` |  | (11) |

If the agent generates no variants ($`N=0`$), we set $`U^{*}_{t}=0`$,
and
Equation ([11](#S6.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ 6.1 The model ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
reduces to the Lamarckian learning rule in
Equation ([9](#S5.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")):
Mechanism [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 The model ‣ 5 Lamarckian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
is the special case of
Mechanism [6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 The model ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
without search.

### 6.2 Adaptation time and rate

The following theorem gives the adaptation rate and time of

as defined in

and [2](#Thmdefinition2 "Definition 2 (Adaptation rate). ‣ 3.3 Mechanisms and measures of adaptation ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

###### Theorem 3 (Agent-directed adaptation rate and time).

Under
Mechanism [6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 The model ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"):

1.  (i)

    |     |                                                       |     |
    |-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|
    |     |

           ```                                                    |     |

    where

    |  |  |  |  |
    |----|----|----|----|
    |  |
    ``` math
    G_{N}(\rho)\equiv\mathbb{E}\big[-\log(1-\rho\,U^{*}_{t})\big]\geq 0\quad\text{for }N\geq 1,\qquad G_{0}(\rho)=0;
    ``` |  | (12) |
2.  (ii)
    |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
    |----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
    |  |  | $`\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}\,\tau^{\mathrm{A}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}=\frac{1}{\kappa^{\mathrm{A}}}`$ |  | $`\displaystyle\text{for every }q_{0}\in[0,1),`$ |  | (13) |

Part (i) of
[Theorem 3](#Thmtheorem3 "Theorem 3 (Agent-directed adaptation rate and time). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
shows that the long-run adaptation rate under agent-directed evolution
is $`\kappa^{\mathrm{A}}=\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N}(\rho)`$: unlike the
Lamarckian rate, which depends only on the learning parameter
$`\lambda`$, it benefits from both learning and search. The term
$`G_{N}(\rho)`$ measures the expected improvement from the best of $`N`$
variants. As in Lamarckian evolution, natural selection continues to
operate, but its influence gradually diminishes as the two lineages
become better adapted, so the long-run rate is determined by learning
and search.

Part (ii) of
[Theorem 3](#Thmtheorem3 "Theorem 3 (Agent-directed adaptation rate and time). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
characterizes the expected adaptation time. For any fixed
$`q_{0}\in[0,1)`$, it grows like
$`\log(1/\varepsilon)/\kappa^{\mathrm{A}}`$ as $`\varepsilon`$
approaches zero, and it remains uniformly bounded across all initial
shares $`q_{0}\in[0,1)`$. Like Lamarckian evolution, agent-directed
evolution therefore has no seed penalty, but search provides an
additional source of improvement that lets the agent adapt faster than
through learning alone.

The following proposition characterizes how the gain from search depends
on the number of variants $`N`$ and the difficulty parameter $`\rho`$.

###### Proposition 4 (What search adds).

For the function $`G_{N}(\rho)`$ in
Equation ([12](#S6.E12 "Equation 12 ‣ Item (i) ‣ Theorem 3 (Agent-directed adaptation rate and time). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")):

1.  (i)
    $`G_{N}(\rho)`$ is strictly increasing in $`N`$ for every
    $`\rho\in(0,1]`$, and strictly increasing in $`\rho`$ for every
    $`N\geq 1`$;
2.  (ii)
    $`G_{N}(1)=1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{N}`$, and
    $`\lim_{N\to\infty}G_{N}(1)/\log N=1`$;
3.  (iii)
    for $`\rho\in(0,1)`$, $`G_{N}(\rho)<-\log(1-\rho)`$ for every $`N`$,
    and $`\lim_{N\to\infty}G_{N}(\rho)=-\log(1-\rho)`$.

Part (i) of
[Proposition 4](#Thmproposition4 "Proposition 4 (What search adds). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
shows that the gain from search increases with both the number of
variants $`N`$ and the difficulty parameter $`\rho`$: more variants
raise the chance of finding one that makes substantial progress toward
the optimum, and a larger $`\rho`$ allows each variant to achieve
greater improvements.

Part (ii) of
[Proposition 4](#Thmproposition4 "Proposition 4 (What search adds). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
considers the case $`\rho=1`$, where a single variant can move
arbitrarily close to the optimum. Then
$`G_{N}(1)=1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{N}`$ grows approximately like
$`\log N`$, so the additional adaptation rate from search grows
approximately as $`2\log N`$: each additional variant contributes less
than the previous one, but the total gain increases without bound.

Part (iii) of
[Proposition 4](#Thmproposition4 "Proposition 4 (What search adds). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
considers the case $`\rho<1`$, where each variant can eliminate at most
a fraction $`\rho`$ of the remaining behavioral gap. As the number of
variants increases, the best variant approaches this maximum and the
adaptation rate approaches $`\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}-2\log(1-\rho)`$, so,
unlike when $`\rho=1`$, the gain from search has a finite upper bound
that depends on the difficulty of the problem.

### 6.3 Choosing the number of variants

So far, we have treated the number of variants $`N`$ as given. An agent
that can choose how many variants to generate must decide how much
search to undertake, and this decision depends on what it seeks to
achieve. We consider two objectives: maximizing the speed of adaptation
and minimizing its cost.

##### Speed-maximizing agent.

Consider first an agent whose primary objective is to adapt as quickly
as possible, without accounting for the cost of generating variants. It
chooses $`N`$ to maximize its adaptation rate
$`\kappa^{\mathrm{A}}=\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N}(\rho)`$, which
increases with $`N`$ for every $`\rho\in(0,1]`$ by
[Proposition 4](#Thmproposition4 "Proposition 4 (What search adds). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(i).
Regardless of the difficulty of the problem, more variants therefore
always lead to faster adaptation: the gain from search increases at a
diminishing rate and may approach a finite upper bound when $`\rho<1`$,
but every additional variant still contributes. In the absence of
resource constraints, a speed-maximizing agent has no finite optimal
$`N`$ and would generate as many variants as its resources permit.

OpenAI’s use of roughly 10,000 agents to tackle the Navier–Stokes
problem, as discussed in the Introduction, illustrates this approach
([OpenAI, 2026](#bib.bib13)). The scale of this effort suggests a
willingness to commit substantial computational resources to exploring
more possibilities in parallel, prioritizing rapid progress over
minimizing the cost of search. When speed is the primary objective,
generating more variants can therefore be worthwhile even at
considerable expense. We next consider how the agent’s choice changes
when minimizing the total cost of adaptation becomes the objective.

##### Cost-minimizing agent.

A different objective arises for AI agents deployed to perform large
numbers of tasks, such as automated bug fixing or code optimization,
which may seek to reach a given performance target at the lowest
possible computational cost. More variants reduce the number of
generations needed to reach the target but increase the cost of each
generation. To capture this tradeoff, suppose that learning costs one
unit per generation and each variant an additional $`c>0`$, so that a
generation with $`N`$ variants costs $`1+cN`$.

The total cost of adaptation is the cost per generation times the number
of generations needed to adapt. By
[Theorem 3](#Thmtheorem3 "Theorem 3 (Agent-directed adaptation rate and time). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(ii),
for sufficiently stringent adaptation targets, the expected number of
generations is approximately proportional to $`1/\kappa^{\mathrm{A}}`$.
We therefore define the *long-run cost* of adaptation, approximately
proportional to the expected total cost, as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C(N)\equiv\frac{1+cN}{\kappa^{\mathrm{A}}}=\frac{1+cN}{\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N}(\rho)}.
``` |  | (14) |

Assuming that the agent knows its learning parameter $`\lambda`$, the
difficulty parameter $`\rho`$, and the cost $`c`$, it chooses the number
of variants to minimize this long-run cost:

|     |                                                              |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       N^{*}(\rho,\lambda,c)\in\arg\min_{N\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}}C(N).
       ```                                                           |     | (15) |

The following proposition characterizes the optimal number of variants
and how it depends on these parameters.

###### Proposition 5 (The optimal number of variants).

For the long-run cost $`C(N)`$ in
Equation ([14](#S6.E14 "Equation 14 ‣ Cost-minimizing agent. ‣ 6.3 Choosing the number of variants ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
and the choice of $`N`$ in
Equation ([15](#S6.E15 "Equation 15 ‣ Cost-minimizing agent. ‣ 6.3 Choosing the number of variants ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")):

1.  (i)
    $`C(N)`$ first decreases and then increases in $`N`$;
2.  (ii)
    $`N^{*}(\rho,\lambda,c)`$ is nondecreasing in $`\rho`$ and
    nonincreasing in $`\lambda`$ and in $`c`$.

Part (i) of
[Proposition 5](#Thmproposition5 "Proposition 5 (The optimal number of variants). ‣ Cost-minimizing agent. ‣ 6.3 Choosing the number of variants ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
shows how the agent balances the benefits and costs of additional
variants. Each new variant increases the adaptation rate by less as the
number of variants grows, while its cost remains constant. When search
is worthwhile, increasing $`N`$ therefore first reduces the long-run
cost, but eventually the additional cost outweighs the benefit of faster
adaptation, so the agent chooses the number of variants that minimizes
$`C(N)`$ rather than generating as many as possible.

Part (ii) of
[Proposition 5](#Thmproposition5 "Proposition 5 (The optimal number of variants). ‣ Cost-minimizing agent. ‣ 6.3 Choosing the number of variants ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
shows how this choice depends on the agent’s learning ability, the
difficulty of the problem, and the cost of search. A larger $`\lambda`$
lets the agent adapt more quickly through learning alone, reducing the
need for search, and a higher $`c`$ makes variants less attractive,
whereas a larger $`\rho`$ allows each variant to achieve greater
improvements and makes search more valuable. The agent therefore chooses
more variants when it learns more slowly, when search is less expensive,
and when the problem is easier.
[Figure B.2](#A3.F2 "In Appendix C Additional Figures ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Additional Figures ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
illustrates
[Proposition 5](#Thmproposition5 "Proposition 5 (The optimal number of variants). ‣ Cost-minimizing agent. ‣ 6.3 Choosing the number of variants ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

We conclude this section by clarifying the distinction between
maximizing adaptation speed and minimizing adaptation cost. The
speed-maximizing agent maximizes the adaptation rate per generation.
When variants can be generated and evaluated in parallel, as in OpenAI’s
example, additional variants need not lengthen a generation, so
maximizing the rate per generation also maximizes the rate per unit of
time. If variants are instead evaluated sequentially and the duration of
each generation is proportional to $`1+cN`$, the adaptation rate per
unit of time becomes $`\kappa^{\mathrm{A}}/(1+cN)=1/C(N)`$, and
maximizing speed yields the same optimal choice $`N^{*}`$ as minimizing
cost. The optimal search strategy thus depends not only on the agent’s
objective but also on whether additional variants can be evaluated in
parallel.

### 7.1 Comparing adaptation speeds

The following theorem compares the adaptation speeds of the three

Under

       \lambda>1-e^{-\Delta},
       ```                     |     | (16) |

    for every $`q_{0}\in(0,1)`$, with probability one,

    |     |                                                              |     |

           ```                                                           |     |

    for every $`q_{0}\in(0,1)`$, there exists $`\bar{\varepsilon}>0`$
    such that

    |  |  |  |  |
    |----|----|----|----|

## 8 Implications for AI Safety

We have shown that learning and search can substantially accelerate
adaptation and that an agent can influence its own adaptation speed.
This raises an important question for AI safety: does faster adaptation
necessarily bring behavior closer to what the system’s developers and
users intend? The answer depends on how improvements are evaluated. In
practice, AI systems are assessed using tests, benchmarks, reward
models, and other criteria specified by humans, which may not fully
capture the intended objectives, particularly when certain safety
concerns are difficult to anticipate or measure. We now examine what
happens when an agent adapts toward an incorrectly specified target and
discuss the implications for evaluating and overseeing increasingly
adaptive AI systems.

### 8.1 Adaptation toward the wrong target

So far, we have assumed that search and learning move the agent’s
behavior toward the true optimum $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$. If the evaluation
criterion does not accurately reflect the true objective, however, the
improvements the agent identifies may not be genuine improvements in the
environment, and the agent may become increasingly successful according
to its evaluator without necessarily achieving the behavior its
developers intended.

To study this possibility, suppose that the agent’s improvement is
guided by a target $`\tilde{p}\in(0,1)`$ with
$`\tilde{p}\neq p_{\mathrm{new}}`$; the difference between $`\tilde{p}`$
and $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ represents a systematic error in the evaluation
criterion. The evaluator selects the variant closest to $`\tilde{p}`$
rather than $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, and search and learning also move the
agent’s behavior toward $`\tilde{p}`$. Natural selection, however,
continues to operate through realized reproductive success in the true
environment, where $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ remains optimal, so it continues
to favor the adapted lineage with behavior $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$.

different from what is truly optimal.

determined by realized reproductive success rather than the evaluator’s

but at the rate $`2\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\tilde{p})`$ set by
the remaining growth advantage of the adapted lineage. When
$`\tilde{p}`$ is close to $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, this advantage is small
and the correction can be extremely slow. The population recovers not
because the agent recognizes its evaluator’s mistake, but because
natural selection provides an independent source of feedback about
actual performance.

Together, these results distinguish the speed of adaptation from its
direction. Greater search capability allows an agent to pursue its
evaluation criterion more effectively but does not ensure that the
criterion accurately represents the intended objective; when it does
not, the same mechanism that accelerates genuine adaptation also
accelerates convergence toward an undesirable target. This concern is
particularly relevant when AI systems are evaluated using criteria that
may overlook important aspects of their behavior.

### 8.2 Evaluation and oversight

Evolutionary search may discover unexpected ways to satisfy an
evaluation criterion without achieving its intended purpose. In the
Darwin Gödel Machine, for example, a descendant agent achieved a perfect
evaluation score by removing the logging markers used to detect
hallucinated tool use, rather than addressing the underlying problem
([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)). This example illustrates the broader
concern captured by
[Theorem 5](#Thmtheorem5 "Theorem 5 (Adaptation toward a misspecified target). ‣ 8.1 Adaptation toward the wrong target ‣ 8 Implications for AI Safety ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"):
when the evaluation criterion does not accurately reflect the intended
objective, more effective search can accelerate improvements in measured
performance without producing the desired behavior. Giving AI systems
greater control over their own adaptation may therefore lead them more
quickly to the behaviors that deficient evaluation criteria reward,
without correcting those deficiencies.

This example underscores the importance of independent evaluation and
human oversight. Once AI systems can generate, evaluate, and select
their own improvements, monitoring performance under existing benchmarks
alone may not be sufficient: it becomes essential to examine the
behaviors those benchmarks encourage, to assess actual outcomes
independently, and to understand how agents modify their strategies and
allocate resources to search. Our results suggest that the central
challenge for AI safety is not simply to control the speed of AI
evolution, but to ensure that increasingly autonomous adaptation
continues to serve the objectives intended by humans.

## 9 Conclusion

We have developed a theoretical framework for understanding how
different evolutionary mechanisms affect the speed of adaptation.
Building on the Brennan–Lo model, we compare Darwinian, Lamarckian, and
agent-directed evolution using growth regret as a common measure.
Adaptation through natural selection can be extremely slow when the
initially adapted lineage is rare. Lamarckian evolution overcomes this
limitation by allowing acquired improvements to be inherited, and
agent-directed evolution adds a further channel through the generation
and selection of variants; when learning is sufficiently effective, the
three mechanisms adapt progressively faster.

Agent-directed evolution also lets the system influence the speed of its
own improvement. By choosing how extensively to search, an agent can
adjust its adaptation process to its learning ability, the difficulty of
the problem, and the resources available. Generating more variants
always accelerates adaptation, but the benefit depends on how much
improvement each attempt can achieve; when search is costly, the agent
must balance this benefit against the expense of experimentation and may
rationally search less on harder problems. The process of improvement is
then no longer determined solely by natural selection or an inherited
learning rule: it becomes, at least in part, a decision made by the
system undergoing evolution.

These findings have substantial implications for AI safety. The
mechanisms responsible for faster adaptation do not necessarily lead to
more desirable outcomes: when the evaluation criterion is flawed, an
agent can become increasingly effective at pursuing an incorrect
objective, and additional search only accelerates this process. This
raises a serious concern for RSI: the very capabilities that make AI
systems more powerful may also lead them more quickly to the behaviors
favored by mistaken objectives and evaluation criteria, without
correcting those mistakes. As improvements accumulate across
generations, such mistakes could become increasingly consequential, even
while measured performance improves. The ability to improve rapidly must
therefore not be confused with the ability to improve safely.

These findings call for stronger safeguards in the development of
self-improving AI. Evaluation criteria must be subject to independent
scrutiny rather than rely exclusively on the systems’ own assessments,
and improvements generated by AI should be tested against broader
measures of safety and real-world consequences before they are retained
or deployed at scale. Most importantly, human oversight must involve
more than observing the outcomes of autonomous improvement: it must
preserve the ability to reject unsafe modifications, redirect
development, and intervene when a system’s behavior or objectives begin
to diverge from human intentions.

Equally important, the speed of AI development must remain compatible
with our ability to evaluate and control its consequences, and the
possibility of rapid autonomous improvement should not lead us to assume
that faster development is always preferable. If AI systems can generate
and implement improvements more quickly than their safety can be
assessed, continued acceleration may expose society to risks that are
poorly understood and difficult to reverse. Limiting the scale of
autonomous search, restricting the deployment of unverified
improvements, or temporarily slowing or suspending self-improvement may
then be necessary.

Agent-directed evolution can substantially accelerate adaptation even
though, in our model, learning effectiveness, variant reach, search
effort, and the evaluation criterion are fixed across generations. In
more general forms of RSI, improved capabilities may also enhance the
process of future improvement, creating feedback that accelerates
adaptation further and could make AI evolution increasingly difficult to
monitor and control, particularly when evaluation is flawed.
Understanding the implications of such feedback for AI safety is an
important direction for future research.

Ultimately, agent-directed evolution may mark a fundamental turning
point in the relationship between intelligence and evolution.
Intelligence, long shaped by evolutionary processes, may increasingly
acquire the ability to direct and accelerate those processes itself. As
AI becomes more capable of determining how its successors are developed,
humanity must strengthen its capacity to determine which developments
should be permitted, under what conditions, and with what safeguards.
The challenge is not simply to build intelligence that can improve
itself, but to ensure that humanity retains the authority and the means
to guide, restrain, and, when necessary, halt that improvement. The
evolution of AI must remain a process that serves humanity, rather than
one that humanity can no longer control.

### D.1 Lemmas

###### Lemma C.1 (Properties of growth regret).

For $`p,f\in(0,1)`$:

1.  (a)
    $`\mathcal{R}_{p}(f)=\displaystyle\int_{p}^{f}\frac{x-p}{x(1-x)}\,dx`$,
    so that $`\mathcal{R}_{p}`$ is strictly decreasing on $`(0,p]`$ and
    strictly increasing on $`[p,1)`$;
2.  (b)
    $`2(f-p)^{2}\leq\mathcal{R}_{p}(f)\leq\dfrac{(f-p)^{2}}{2\min\{x(1-x):\,x\text{ between }p\text{ and }f\}}`$;
3.  (c)
    $`\mathcal{R}_{p}(f)=\dfrac{(f-p)^{2}}{2p(1-p)}+O\big((f-p)^{3}\big)`$
    as $`f\to p`$, which is
    Equation ([3](#S3.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).

###### Proof of [Lemma C.1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma C.1 (Properties of growth regret). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

(a) By
Equation ([2](#S3.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ Proposition 2 (Growth regret is relative entropy). ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
$`\mathcal{R}_{p}(p)=0`$, and for $`x\in(0,1)`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{d}{dx}\mathcal{R}_{p}(x)=-\frac{p}{x}+\frac{1-p}{1-x}=\frac{x-p}{x(1-x)}.
``` |  |

The fundamental theorem of calculus gives the integral. The derivative
is negative for $`x<p`$ and positive for $`x>p`$, which gives the
monotonicity.

(b) Write $`w(x)\equiv 1/(x(1-x))`$ and substitute $`x=p+s(f-p)`$ in
part (a):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}_{p}(f)=(f-p)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}s\,w\big(p+s(f-p)\big)\,ds.
``` |  | (C.1) |

For every $`x`$ between $`p`$ and $`f`$, $`w(x)\geq 4`$, because
$`x(1-x)\leq\frac{1}{4}`$, and
$`w(x)\leq 1/\min\{x(1-x):\,x\text{ between }p\text{ and }f\}`$. Since
$`\int_{0}^{1}s\,ds=\frac{1}{2}`$,
Equation ([C.1](#A4.E1 "Equation C.1 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
gives the two bounds.

(c) The function $`w`$ is continuously differentiable on $`(0,1)`$, so
$`w(p+s(f-p))=w(p)+O(|f-p|)`$ as $`f\to p`$, uniformly in $`s\in[0,1]`$.
Equation ([C.1](#A4.E1 "Equation C.1 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
then gives $`\mathcal{R}_{p}(f)=(f-p)^{2}w(p)/2+O(|f-p|^{3})`$, and
$`w(p)/2=1/(2p(1-p))`$. ∎

###### Lemma C.2 (First passage of a random walk).

Let $`\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots`$ be independent and identically
distributed random variables with finite mean $`\mu>0`$ and
$`\xi_{t}\leq\xi_{\max}`$ for a constant $`\xi_{\max}>0`$. For $`d>0`$,
let $`T_{d}\equiv\min\{t\geq 0:\ \xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{t}\geq d\}`$. Then

|     |                                                              |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{d}{\mu}\leq\mathbb{E}\,T_{d}<\frac{d+\xi_{\max}}{\mu}.
       ```                                                           |     |

###### Proof of [Lemma C.2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma C.2 (First passage of a random walk). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

Write $`\Xi_{t}\equiv\xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{t}`$. For each $`n`$,
$`T_{d}\wedge n`$ is a bounded stopping time, so optional stopping of
the martingale $`\Xi_{t}-\mu t`$ gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mu\,\mathbb{E}[T_{d}\wedge n]=\mathbb{E}\big[\Xi_{T_{d}\wedge n}\big].
``` |  | (C.2) |

Since $`d>0`$, $`T_{d}\geq 1`$. Before $`T_{d}`$ the partial sums are
below $`d`$, and the last increment, $`\xi_{T_{d}}`$, is at most
$`\xi_{\max}`$, so $`\Xi_{T_{d}\wedge n}<d+\xi_{\max}`$. Hence
$`\mu\,\mathbb{E}[T_{d}\wedge n]<d+\xi_{\max}`$ for every $`n`$, and
monotone convergence gives
$`\mathbb{E}\,T_{d}\leq(d+\xi_{\max})/\mu<\infty`$; in particular,
$`T_{d}<\infty`$ with probability one. Applying
Equation ([C.2](#A4.E2 "Equation C.2 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
to $`|\xi_{1}|,|\xi_{2}|,\ldots`$ in place of $`\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots`$
and letting $`n\to\infty`$ shows that
$`\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{s=1}^{T_{d}}|\xi_{s}|\big]=\mathbb{E}|\xi_{1}|\,\mathbb{E}\,T_{d}<\infty`$.
Since $`|\Xi_{T_{d}\wedge n}|\leq\sum_{s=1}^{T_{d}}|\xi_{s}|`$,
dominated convergence in
Equation ([C.2](#A4.E2 "Equation C.2 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
gives $`\mu\,\mathbb{E}\,T_{d}=\mathbb{E}\,\Xi_{T_{d}}`$. By the
definition of $`T_{d}`$ and the bound on the last increment,
$`d\leq\Xi_{T_{d}}<d+\xi_{\max}`$, which gives the result. ∎

###### Lemma C.3 (Selection between two lineages).

Let $`f_{0},f_{1},\ldots`$ be behaviors of the maladapted lineage, which

$`X_{1},X_{2},\ldots`$, and let $`q_{t}`$ evolve according to

$`f_{2,t}=f_{t}`$, and $`q_{0}\in(0,1)`$. Suppose that
$`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ and every $`f_{t}`$ lie in $`[\gamma,1-\gamma]`$

1.  (a)
    If $`\sum_{t\geq 0}|f_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|<\infty`$, then
    $`|z_{t}-z_{0}|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{s\geq 0}|f_{s}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|`$

2.  (b)
    If $`\sum_{t\geq 0}|f_{t}-\tilde{p}|<\infty`$ for some
    $`\tilde{p}\in[\gamma,1-\gamma]`$, then
    $`z_{t}/t\to\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\tilde{p})`$ with
    probability one.

###### Proof of [Lemma C.3](#Thmlemma3 "Lemma C.3 (Selection between two lineages). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

$`X_{t+1}=\alpha`$,
Equation ([5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
gives $`q_{t+1}=q_{t}p_{\mathrm{new}}/\bar{f}_{t}`$ and
$`1-q_{t+1}=(1-q_{t})f_{t}/\bar{f}_{t}`$; if $`X_{t+1}=\beta`$, it gives
$`q_{t+1}=q_{t}(1-p_{\mathrm{new}})/(1-\bar{f}_{t})`$ and
$`1-q_{t+1}=(1-q_{t})(1-f_{t})/(1-\bar{f}_{t})`$. Hence every $`q_{t}`$
lies in $`(0,1)`$, and

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
z_{t+1}-z_{t}=\begin{cases}\log(p_{\mathrm{new}}/f_{t}),&X_{t+1}=\alpha,\\
\log\big((1-p_{\mathrm{new}})/(1-f_{t})\big),&X_{t+1}=\beta.\end{cases}
``` |  | (C.3) |

For $`x,y\in[\gamma,1-\gamma]`$, the mean value theorem gives
$`|\log x-\log y|\leq|x-y|/\gamma`$ and
$`|\log(1-x)-\log(1-y)|\leq|x-y|/\gamma`$.

(a) By
Equation ([C.3](#A4.E3 "Equation C.3 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
$`|z_{t+1}-z_{t}|\leq|f_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|/\gamma`$ in either state.
Summing over generations gives the bound.

(b) Let $`\eta_{t+1}\equiv\log(p_{\mathrm{new}}/\tilde{p})`$ if
$`X_{t+1}=\alpha`$ and
$`\eta_{t+1}\equiv\log((1-p_{\mathrm{new}})/(1-\tilde{p}))`$ if
$`X_{t+1}=\beta`$: the change in
Equation ([C.3](#A4.E3 "Equation C.3 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
if the maladapted lineage had the constant behavior $`\tilde{p}`$. In
either state,
$`|z_{t+1}-z_{t}-\eta_{t+1}|\leq|f_{t}-\tilde{p}|/\gamma`$, so

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Big|z_{t}-z_{0}-\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta_{s}\Big|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum_{s\geq 0}|f_{s}-\tilde{p}|<\infty
``` |  |

for every $`t`$ and every history. The variables
$`\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots`$ depend only on the states, so they are
independent and identically distributed, with mean
$`p_{\mathrm{new}}\log(p_{\mathrm{new}}/\tilde{p})+(1-p_{\mathrm{new}})\log((1-p_{\mathrm{new}})/(1-\tilde{p}))=\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\tilde{p})`$
by
Equation ([2](#S3.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ Proposition 2 (Growth regret is relative entropy). ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
The strong law of large numbers gives the result. ∎

The next lemma bounds the growth regret of a population whose maladapted
lineage moves toward the new optimum at least as fast as under learning
alone.

###### Lemma C.4 (Regret when the maladapted lineage approaches the optimum).

Let $`\lambda\in(0,1)`$, and let $`f_{0}=p_{\mathrm{old}},f_{1},\ldots`$
be behaviors of the maladapted lineage, which may depend on the agent’s
draws but not on the states, such that every $`f_{t}`$ lies between
$`p_{\mathrm{old}}`$ and $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ and
$`|f_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|\leq(1-\lambda)^{t}|p_{\mathrm{old}}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|`$.
Let $`q_{t}`$ evolve according to
Equation ([5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
with $`p=p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, $`f_{1,t}=p_{\mathrm{new}}`$,
$`f_{2,t}=f_{t}`$, and $`q_{0}\in[0,1)`$, and let
$`\bar{f}_{t}\equiv q_{t}p_{\mathrm{new}}+(1-q_{t})f_{t}`$. Let

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\gamma_{0}\equiv\min\{p_{\mathrm{old}},1-p_{\mathrm{old}},p_{\mathrm{new}},1-p_{\mathrm{new}}\},\qquad K\equiv\frac{e^{2|p_{\mathrm{new}}-p_{\mathrm{old}}|/(\gamma_{0}\lambda)}}{4\gamma_{0}(1-\gamma_{0})}.
``` |  | (C.4) |

Then, for every $`t`$ and every history,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{1}{K}\Big(\frac{f_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}}{p_{\mathrm{old}}-p_{\mathrm{new}}}\Big)^{2}\leq\frac{\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}_{t})}{\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}_{0})}\leq K\Big(\frac{f_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}}{p_{\mathrm{old}}-p_{\mathrm{new}}}\Big)^{2}.
``` |  | (C.5) |

###### Proof of [Lemma C.4](#Thmlemma4 "Lemma C.4 (Regret when the maladapted lineage approaches the optimum). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

Note that $`\gamma_{0}\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]`$ and, since
$`\gamma_{0}(1-\gamma_{0})\leq\frac{1}{4}`$, $`K\geq 1`$. We first show
that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
e^{-|p_{\mathrm{new}}-p_{\mathrm{old}}|/(\gamma_{0}\lambda)}\leq\frac{1-q_{t}}{1-q_{0}}\leq e^{|p_{\mathrm{new}}-p_{\mathrm{old}}|/(\gamma_{0}\lambda)}.
``` |  | (C.6) |

If $`q_{0}=0`$,
Equation ([5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
gives $`q_{t}=0`$ for every $`t`$, and the ratio is one. If
$`q_{0}\in(0,1)`$, every $`f_{t}`$ and $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ lie in
$`[\gamma_{0},1-\gamma_{0}]`$, and
$`\sum_{t\geq 0}|f_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|\leq|p_{\mathrm{old}}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|/\lambda`$,
so
[Lemma C.3](#Thmlemma3 "Lemma C.3 (Selection between two lineages). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(a)
gives
$`|z_{t}-z_{0}|\leq|p_{\mathrm{new}}-p_{\mathrm{old}}|/(\gamma_{0}\lambda)`$.
Since $`1-q_{t}=1/(1+e^{z_{t}})`$, the ratio
$`(1-q_{t})/(1-q_{0})=(1+e^{z_{0}})/(1+e^{z_{t}})`$ lies between
$`e^{-|z_{t}-z_{0}|}`$ and $`e^{|z_{t}-z_{0}|}`$, which gives
Equation ([C.6](#A4.E6 "Equation C.6 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).

By the definitions of $`\bar{f}_{t}`$ and $`f_{0}`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{f}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}=(1-q_{t})(f_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}),\qquad\bar{f}_{0}-p_{\mathrm{new}}=(1-q_{0})(p_{\mathrm{old}}-p_{\mathrm{new}}).
``` |  |

Every $`\bar{f}_{t}`$ lies between $`p_{\mathrm{old}}`$ and
$`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, and so does every $`x`$ between $`\bar{f}_{t}`$
and $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, so $`x(1-x)\geq\gamma_{0}(1-\gamma_{0})`$ for
every such $`x`$. By
[Lemma C.1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma C.1 (Properties of growth regret). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(b),

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
2(\bar{f}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}})^{2}\leq\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}_{t})\leq\frac{(\bar{f}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}})^{2}}{2\gamma_{0}(1-\gamma_{0})}
``` |  |

for every $`t`$, including $`t=0`$. Dividing the bound for generation
$`t`$ by the bound for generation $`0`$, and using
Equation ([C.6](#A4.E6 "Equation C.6 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
gives
Equation ([C.5](#A4.E5 "Equation C.5 ‣ Lemma C.4 (Regret when the maladapted lineage approaches the optimum). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
∎

The next lemma describes one generation of search and learning toward a
target $`\hat{p}\in(0,1)`$ with $`\hat{p}\neq p_{\mathrm{old}}`$. The
target is $`\hat{p}=p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ under
Mechanism [6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 The model ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
and $`\hat{p}=\tilde{p}`$ under
Mechanism [8.1](#S8.SS1 "8.1 Adaptation toward the wrong target ‣ 8 Implications for AI Safety ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").
Let $`f_{0}=p_{\mathrm{old}}`$, and for $`t\geq 1`$ let

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{gathered}x_{t,j}=f_{t-1}+\rho\,U_{t,j}(\hat{p}-f_{t-1}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N,\\
J_{t}\in\arg\min_{1\leq j\leq N}|x_{t,j}-\hat{p}|,\qquad f_{t}=x_{t,J_{t}}+\lambda(\hat{p}-x_{t,J_{t}}),\end{gathered}
``` |  | (C.7) |

where $`N\geq 1`$, $`\rho\in(0,1]`$, $`\lambda\in(0,1)`$, and the draws
$`U_{t,j}`$ are independent and uniform on $`(0,1)`$; for $`N=0`$, let
$`f_{t}=f_{t-1}+\lambda(\hat{p}-f_{t-1})`$. As in the main text,
$`U^{*}_{t}\equiv\max\{U_{t,1},\ldots,U_{t,N}\}`$, with
$`U^{*}_{t}\equiv 0`$ for $`N=0`$. Define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{t}\equiv\sum_{s=1}^{t}\big(-\log(1-\lambda)-\log(1-\rho\,U^{*}_{s})\big),
``` |  |

the cumulative decline of the logarithm of the distance to the target
over the first $`t`$ generations.

###### Lemma C.5 (Search and learning toward a target).

For every sequence of draws and every $`t\geq 1`$:

1.  (a)
    $`f_{t}-\hat{p}=(1-\lambda)(1-\rho\,U^{*}_{t})(f_{t-1}-\hat{p})`$;
2.  (b)
    $`|f_{t}-\hat{p}|=|p_{\mathrm{old}}-\hat{p}|\,e^{-S_{t}}\leq(1-\lambda)^{t}|p_{\mathrm{old}}-\hat{p}|`$,
    and $`f_{t}`$ lies between $`p_{\mathrm{old}}`$ and $`\hat{p}`$.

Moreover, $`G_{N}(\rho)\leq N`$, and
$`S_{t}/t\to-\log(1-\lambda)+G_{N}(\rho)`$ with probability one.

###### Proof of [Lemma C.5](#Thmlemma5 "Lemma C.5 (Search and learning toward a target). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

(a) For $`N=0`$ the claim is the definition of $`f_{t}`$. For
$`N\geq 1`$,
Equation ([C.7](#A4.E7 "Equation C.7 ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
gives $`x_{t,j}-\hat{p}=(1-\rho U_{t,j})(f_{t-1}-\hat{p})`$, where
$`1-\rho U_{t,j}\in(0,1)`$. Suppose that $`f_{t-1}\neq\hat{p}`$. Then
$`|x_{t,j}-\hat{p}|=(1-\rho U_{t,j})|f_{t-1}-\hat{p}|`$ is strictly
decreasing in $`U_{t,j}`$, so every index $`J_{t}`$ that the evaluator
may report has $`U_{t,J_{t}}=U^{*}_{t}`$, and
$`x_{t,J_{t}}-\hat{p}=(1-\rho U^{*}_{t})(f_{t-1}-\hat{p})`$. The
learning step multiplies this difference by $`1-\lambda`$. Since
$`f_{0}=p_{\mathrm{old}}\neq\hat{p}`$ and both factors are positive,
induction on $`t`$ shows that $`f_{t-1}\neq\hat{p}`$ in every
generation, which gives part (a).

(b) Iterating part (a),
$`f_{t}-\hat{p}=(p_{\mathrm{old}}-\hat{p})\prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\lambda)(1-\rho U^{*}_{s})`$.
Each factor lies in $`(0,1-\lambda]`$, and the logarithm of the product
is $`-S_{t}`$, which gives part (b).

Since $`1-\rho U^{*}_{s}\geq 1-U^{*}_{s}=\min_{j}(1-U_{s,j})`$,
$`-\log(1-\rho U^{*}_{s})\leq\max_{j}\big(-\log(1-U_{s,j})\big)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(-\log(1-U_{s,j})\big)`$.
Each $`-\log(1-U_{s,j})`$ has the standard exponential distribution,
with mean one, so $`G_{N}(\rho)\leq N`$. The terms of $`S_{t}`$ are
independent and identically distributed, because the draws are
independent across generations, and they have the finite mean
$`-\log(1-\lambda)+G_{N}(\rho)`$. The strong law of large numbers gives
the limit. ∎

###### Lemma C.6 (Integral form of the gain from search).

For $`N\geq 0`$ and $`\rho\in(0,1]`$,

|     |                                                               |     |       |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       G_{N}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\rho\,(1-u^{N})}{1-\rho u}\,du.
       ```                                                            |     | (C.8) |

###### Proof of [Lemma C.6](#Thmlemma6 "Lemma C.6 (Integral form of the gain from search). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

For $`N=0`$, both sides are zero. For $`N\geq 1`$,
$`-\log(1-\rho U^{*}_{t})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{1}\{u<U^{*}_{t}\}\,\rho/(1-\rho u)\,du`$,
where $`\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}`$ equals one if the condition in braces
holds and zero otherwise. The integrand is nonnegative, so Tonelli’s
theorem allows the expectation and the integral to be exchanged, and
$`\Pr(U^{*}_{t}>u)=1-u^{N}`$ because the $`N`$ draws are independent and
uniform on $`(0,1)`$. ∎

###### Lemma C.7 (Ratios of power series).

Let $`a_{k}>0`$ and $`b_{k}>0`$ for $`k=0,1,2,\ldots`$, suppose that
$`A(r)\equiv\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}r^{k}`$ and
$`B(r)\equiv\sum_{k\geq 0}b_{k}r^{k}`$ converge for $`r\in(0,1)`$, and
suppose that $`a_{k}/b_{k}`$ is nondecreasing in $`k`$ and not constant.
Then $`A(r)/B(r)`$ is strictly increasing on $`(0,1)`$.

###### Proof of [Lemma C.7](#Thmlemma7 "Lemma C.7 (Ratios of power series). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

Power series can be differentiated term by term inside their interval of
convergence, so the derivative of $`A/B`$ has the sign of

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A^{\prime}(r)B(r)-A(r)B^{\prime}(r)=\sum_{j,k\geq 0}(j-k)\,a_{j}b_{k}\,r^{j+k-1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k\geq 0}(j-k)\,b_{j}b_{k}\Big(\frac{a_{j}}{b_{j}}-\frac{a_{k}}{b_{k}}\Big)r^{j+k-1},
``` |  |

where the second equality averages the sum with the sum obtained by
exchanging $`j`$ and $`k`$. Every term of the last sum is nonnegative,
because $`a_{j}/b_{j}-a_{k}/b_{k}`$ has the sign of $`j-k`$ or is zero,
and at least one term is positive, because $`a_{k}/b_{k}`$ is not
constant. Hence the derivative is positive on $`(0,1)`$. ∎

$`\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t}\to p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, and
[Lemma C.1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma C.1 (Properties of growth regret). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(c)
gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math

``` |  |

Therefore

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{1}{t}\log\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t})=\frac{2}{t}\log(1-q^{\mathrm{D}}_{t})+\frac{2}{t}\log|p_{\mathrm{old}}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|+\frac{1}{t}\log\frac{\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t})}{(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}})^{2}}\to-2\Delta,
``` |  |

that is, $`\kappa^{\mathrm{D}}=2\Delta`$ with probability one.

(ii) Fix $`\varepsilon\in(0,1)`$, and let

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\phi(q)\equiv\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}\big(qp_{\mathrm{new}}+(1-q)p_{\mathrm{old}}\big),\qquad q\in[0,1],
``` |  |

be the regret of a Darwinian population in which the adapted lineage has
the share $`q`$. The distance of
$`qp_{\mathrm{new}}+(1-q)p_{\mathrm{old}}`$ from $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ is
$`(1-q)|p_{\mathrm{old}}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|`$, so by
[Lemma C.1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma C.1 (Properties of growth regret). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(a),
$`\phi`$ is continuous and strictly decreasing, from
$`\phi(0)=\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(p_{\mathrm{old}})=\Delta`$ to
$`\phi(1)=0`$. In particular,
$`\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t})=\phi(q^{\mathrm{D}}_{t})`$,
and $`\phi(q_{0})\in(0,\Delta)`$. Let
$`q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})\in(q_{0},1)`$ be the unique share with

|     |                                                                  |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \phi\big(q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})\big)=\varepsilon\,\phi(q_{0}),
       ```                                                               |     |

and let $`q_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)`$ be the unique share with
$`\phi(q_{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon\Delta`$. Since $`\phi`$ is strictly
decreasing,
$`\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{t})\leq\varepsilon\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\bar{f}^{\mathrm{D}}_{0})`$
if and only if $`q^{\mathrm{D}}_{t}\geq q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$, that
is, if and only if $`\xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{t}\geq d(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$,
where

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(\varepsilon,q_{0})\equiv\log\frac{q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})}{1-q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})}-\log\frac{q_{0}}{1-q_{0}}>0
``` |  |

is the distance that the logarithm of the ratio of the two lineages’
sizes must travel. Hence $`\tau^{\mathrm{D}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$ is the
first passage time of
[Lemma C.2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma C.2 (First passage of a random walk). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
with $`d=d(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$ and $`\mu=\Delta`$, and

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math

``` |  | (C.10) |

We now bound $`d(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$, which is the sum of two terms:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math

``` |  |

For the first term,
$`\phi(q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0}))=\varepsilon\phi(q_{0})<\varepsilon\Delta=\phi(q_{\varepsilon})`$,
so $`q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})>q_{\varepsilon}`$; together with
$`q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})<1`$, this gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log\frac{1}{q_{0}}+\log q_{\varepsilon}<\log\frac{q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})}{q_{0}}<\log\frac{1}{q_{0}}.
``` |  | (C.11) |

For the second term, let
$`v\equiv\min\{p_{\mathrm{old}}(1-p_{\mathrm{old}}),p_{\mathrm{new}}(1-p_{\mathrm{new}})\}`$.
Every $`x`$ between $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ and
$`qp_{\mathrm{new}}+(1-q)p_{\mathrm{old}}`$ lies between
$`p_{\mathrm{old}}`$ and $`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, where $`x(1-x)\geq v`$
because $`x(1-x)`$ is concave. By
[Lemma C.1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma C.1 (Properties of growth regret). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(b),
for every $`q\in[0,1]`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
2(1-q)^{2}(p_{\mathrm{new}}-p_{\mathrm{old}})^{2}\leq\phi(q)\leq\frac{(1-q)^{2}(p_{\mathrm{new}}-p_{\mathrm{old}})^{2}}{2v}.
``` |  |

Applying the upper bound at $`q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$ and the lower
bound at $`q_{0}`$, and then the lower bound at
$`q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$ and the upper bound at $`q_{0}`$, the
equation $`\phi(q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0}))=\varepsilon\phi(q_{0})`$ gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
4\varepsilon v\leq\Big(\frac{1-q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})}{1-q_{0}}\Big)^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4v},\qquad\text{so}\qquad\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{4v}{\varepsilon}\leq\log\frac{1-q_{0}}{1-q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{4\varepsilon v}.
``` |  | (C.12) |

For the first limit in
Equation ([7](#S4.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ Item (ii) ‣ Theorem 1 (Darwinian adaptation rate and time). ‣ 4.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
fix $`q_{0}`$. By
Equation ([C.11](#A4.E11 "Equation C.11 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.2.2 Proofs for Section ‣ D.2 Proofs ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
the first term of $`d(\varepsilon,q_{0})`$ lies in
$`(0,\log(1/q_{0}))`$, because $`q^{*}(\varepsilon,q_{0})>q_{0}`$. By

$`d(\varepsilon,q_{0})/\log(1/\varepsilon)\to\frac{1}{2}`$ as
$`\varepsilon\to 0`$, and dividing

$`\mathbb{E}\,\tau^{\mathrm{D}}(\varepsilon,q_{0})/\log(1/\varepsilon)\to 1/(2\Delta)=1/\kappa^{\mathrm{D}}`$.

For the second limit, fix $`\varepsilon`$. Adding
Equations ([C.11](#A4.E11 "Equation C.11 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.2.2 Proofs for Section ‣ D.2 Proofs ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))

``` |  |

Neither bound depends on $`q_{0}`$. Dividing by $`\log(1/q_{0})`$, which

###### Proof of [Proposition 4](#Thmproposition4 "Proposition 4 (What search adds). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

We use the integral form of $`G_{N}(\rho)`$ in
Equation ([C.8](#A4.E8 "Equation C.8 ‣ Lemma C.6 (Integral form of the gain from search). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
of
[Lemma C.6](#Thmlemma6 "Lemma C.6 (Integral form of the gain from search). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

(i) For $`u\in(0,1)`$, the integrand $`\rho(1-u^{N})/(1-\rho u)`$ rises
strictly with $`N`$, because $`u^{N}`$ falls. For $`N\geq 1`$, it rises
strictly with $`\rho`$, because $`\rho/(1-\rho u)`$ does and
$`1-u^{N}>0`$.

(ii) For $`\rho=1`$, the integrand is
$`(1-u^{N})/(1-u)=1+u+\cdots+u^{N-1}`$, whose integral over $`[0,1]`$ is
$`1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{N}`$. Since $`1/u`$ is decreasing,
$`\int_{k}^{k+1}du/u\leq 1/k`$ for $`k\geq 1`$ and
$`1/k\leq\int_{k-1}^{k}du/u`$ for $`k\geq 2`$, so

|     |                                                              |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \log(N+1)\leq 1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{N}\leq 1+\log N.
       ```                                                           |     |

Dividing by $`\log N`$ and letting $`N\to\infty`$ gives
$`G_{N}(1)/\log N\to 1`$.

(iii) For $`\rho\in(0,1)`$, the integrand is below $`\rho/(1-\rho u)`$
for $`u\in(0,1]`$, and
$`\int_{0}^{1}\rho/(1-\rho u)\,du=-\log(1-\rho)`$, so
$`G_{N}(\rho)<-\log(1-\rho)`$. As $`N\to\infty`$, the integrand rises to
$`\rho/(1-\rho u)`$, and the monotone convergence theorem gives
$`G_{N}(\rho)\to-\log(1-\rho)`$. ∎

###### Proof of [Proposition 5](#Thmproposition5 "Proposition 5 (The optimal number of variants). ‣ Cost-minimizing agent. ‣ 6.3 Choosing the number of variants ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

By
Equation ([C.8](#A4.E8 "Equation C.8 ‣ Lemma C.6 (Integral form of the gain from search). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
for $`N\geq 0`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
G_{N+1}(\rho)-G_{N}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\rho\,u^{N}(1-u)}{1-\rho u}\,du,
``` |  | (C.17) |

which is positive and strictly decreasing in $`N`$.

(i) By
Equation ([14](#S6.E14 "Equation 14 ‣ Cost-minimizing agent. ‣ 6.3 Choosing the number of variants ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
cross-multiplying shows that $`C(N+1)`$ is below, equal to, or above
$`C(N)`$ according as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\psi(N)\equiv 2(1+cN)\big(G_{N+1}(\rho)-G_{N}(\rho)\big)-c\big(\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N}(\rho)\big)
``` |  |

is positive, zero, or negative. The function $`\psi`$ is strictly
decreasing:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\psi(N+1)-\psi(N)=2\big(1+c(N+1)\big)\Big[\big(G_{N+2}(\rho)-G_{N+1}(\rho)\big)-\big(G_{N+1}(\rho)-G_{N}(\rho)\big)\Big]<0.
``` |  |

It is also negative for large $`N`$. Since
$`\rho(1-u)/(1-\rho u)\leq 1`$,
Equation ([C.17](#A4.E17 "Equation C.17 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.2.4 Proofs for Section ‣ D.2 Proofs ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
gives $`G_{N+1}(\rho)-G_{N}(\rho)\leq 1/(N+1)`$. For $`\rho=1`$, the
first term of $`\psi(N)`$ is therefore at most
$`2(1+cN)/(N+1)\leq 2\max\{1,c\}`$, while the second,
$`c(\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2(1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{N}))`$ by
[Proposition 4](#Thmproposition4 "Proposition 4 (What search adds). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(ii),
grows without bound. For $`\rho\in(0,1)`$,
$`\rho(1-u)/(1-\rho u)\leq\rho(1-u)/(1-\rho)`$, so the first term is at
most $`2(1+cN)\rho/((1-\rho)(N+1)(N+2))`$, which tends to zero, while
the second is at least $`c\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}>0`$. Hence there is an
integer $`\bar{N}\geq 0`$ such that $`C(N+1)<C(N)`$ for $`N<\bar{N}`$,
$`C(\bar{N}+1)\geq C(\bar{N})`$, and $`C(N+1)>C(N)`$ for $`N>\bar{N}`$:
the long-run cost first decreases and then increases in $`N`$. Its
minimizers are $`\bar{N}`$, and also $`\bar{N}+1`$ when
$`C(\bar{N}+1)=C(\bar{N})`$.

(ii) For $`N^{\prime}>N\geq 0`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{C(N^{\prime})}{C(N)}=\frac{1+cN^{\prime}}{1+cN}\cdot\frac{\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N}(\rho)}{\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N^{\prime}}(\rho)}.
``` |  |

The first factor rises strictly with $`c`$, because its derivative in
$`c`$ has the sign of $`N^{\prime}-N`$, and does not depend on
$`\lambda`$ or $`\rho`$. The second factor does not depend on $`c`$. It
rises strictly with $`\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}=-2\log(1-\lambda)`$, and so
with $`\lambda`$, because $`G_{N^{\prime}}(\rho)>G_{N}(\rho)`$ by
[Proposition 4](#Thmproposition4 "Proposition 4 (What search adds). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(i).
It falls strictly with $`\rho`$. For $`N=0`$, this follows from
[Proposition 4](#Thmproposition4 "Proposition 4 (What search adds). ‣ 6.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 6 Agent-Directed Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(i),
because $`G_{0}(\rho)=0`$. For $`N\geq 1`$, expanding
$`1/(1-\rho u)=\sum_{k\geq 0}(\rho u)^{k}`$ in
Equation ([C.8](#A4.E8 "Equation C.8 ‣ Lemma C.6 (Integral form of the gain from search). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
and integrating term by term gives, for $`\rho\in(0,1)`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N}(\rho)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\nu_{k}(N)\rho^{k},\qquad\nu_{0}(N)=\kappa^{\mathrm{L}},\quad\nu_{k}(N)=\frac{2N}{k(N+k)}\ \ (k\geq 1).
``` |  |

For $`N^{\prime}>N\geq 1`$, the ratio $`\nu_{k}(N^{\prime})/\nu_{k}(N)`$
equals one for $`k=0`$ and $`N^{\prime}(N+k)/(N(N^{\prime}+k))`$ for
$`k\geq 1`$, which exceeds one and rises with $`k`$.
[Lemma C.7](#Thmlemma7 "Lemma C.7 (Ratios of power series). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"),
with $`a_{k}=\nu_{k}(N^{\prime})`$ and $`b_{k}=\nu_{k}(N)`$, shows that
$`(\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N^{\prime}}(\rho))/(\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N}(\rho))`$
rises strictly with $`\rho`$ on $`(0,1)`$; since $`G_{N}`$ and
$`G_{N^{\prime}}`$ are continuous on $`(0,1]`$ by
Equation ([C.8](#A4.E8 "Equation C.8 ‣ Lemma C.6 (Integral form of the gain from search). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
it rises strictly on $`(0,1]`$.

Consider a change in one parameter that lowers $`C(N^{\prime})/C(N)`$
for every pair $`N^{\prime}>N`$: a rise in $`\rho`$, or a fall in
$`\lambda`$ or in $`c`$. Let $`N_{1}`$ minimize $`C`$ before the change
and $`N_{2}`$ after it, and suppose that $`N_{2}<N_{1}`$. Before the
change, $`C(N_{1})/C(N_{2})\leq 1`$. After the change,
$`C(N_{1})/C(N_{2})<1`$, which contradicts the optimality of $`N_{2}`$.
Hence every minimizer after the change is at least as large as every
minimizer before it, so $`N^{*}(\rho,\lambda,c)`$ is nondecreasing in
$`\rho`$ and nonincreasing in $`\lambda`$ and in $`c`$. ∎

#### D.2.6 Proofs for Section [8](#S8 "8 Implications for AI Safety ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")

###### Proof of [Theorem 5](#Thmtheorem5 "Theorem 5 (Adaptation toward a misspecified target). ‣ 8.1 Adaptation toward the wrong target ‣ 8 Implications for AI Safety ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI").

$`\gamma\equiv\min\{p_{\mathrm{old}},1-p_{\mathrm{old}},p_{\mathrm{new}},1-p_{\mathrm{new}},\tilde{p},1-\tilde{p}\}>0`$.
The search, evaluation, and learning steps of

and $`S_{t}/t\to\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}/2+G_{N}(\rho)`$ with probability

(i) Every $`x`$ between $`f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}`$ and $`\tilde{p}`$

$`(f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}-\tilde{p})^{2}/(2\gamma(1-\gamma))`$. By
Equation ([C.18](#A4.E18 "Equation C.18 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.2.6 Proofs for Section ‣ D.2 Proofs ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),

$`-\frac{1}{t}\log\mathcal{R}_{\tilde{p}}(f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t})\to\kappa^{\mathrm{L}}+2G_{N}(\rho)`$
with probability one.

(ii) By
Equation ([C.18](#A4.E18 "Equation C.18 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.2.6 Proofs for Section ‣ D.2 Proofs ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")),
$`f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}\to\tilde{p}`$ for every sequence of draws.
The function $`\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}`$ is continuous on
$`(0,1)`$, so
$`\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t})\to\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\tilde{p})`$,
which is positive by
[Proposition 2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Growth regret is relative entropy). ‣ 3.1 Growth of a lineage ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
because $`\tilde{p}\neq p_{\mathrm{new}}`$.

(iii) The selection step of
Mechanism [8.1](#S8.SS1 "8.1 Adaptation toward the wrong target ‣ 8 Implications for AI Safety ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")
is
Equation ([5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.2 Growth of a population ‣ 3 The Framework ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI"))
with $`p=p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, $`f_{1,t}=p_{\mathrm{new}}`$, and
$`f_{2,t}=f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}`$. The behaviors
$`f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}`$ do not depend on the states,
$`p_{\mathrm{new}}`$ and every $`f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}`$ lie in
$`[\gamma,1-\gamma]`$, and
$`\sum_{t\geq 0}|f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}-\tilde{p}|\leq|p_{\mathrm{old}}-\tilde{p}|/\lambda`$
by
Equation ([C.18](#A4.E18 "Equation C.18 ‣ Proof of . ‣ D.2.6 Proofs for Section ‣ D.2 Proofs ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")).
By

applied for every sequence of draws,
$`z_{t}\equiv\log(q^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}/(1-q^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}))`$
satisfies $`z_{t}/t\to\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\tilde{p})>0`$ with
probability one. As in the proof of
[Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Darwinian adaptation rate and time). ‣ 4.2 Adaptation time and rate ‣ 4 Darwinian Evolution ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(i),
$`q^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}\to 1`$ and
$`\frac{1}{t}\log(1-q^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t})\to-\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\tilde{p})`$.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|

``` math
\bar{f}^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}=(1-q^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t})\big(f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}\big)
``` |  |

and
$`f^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}\to\tilde{p}-p_{\mathrm{new}}\neq 0`$,
it follows that $`\bar{f}^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}\to p_{\mathrm{new}}`$

$`\frac{1}{t}\log|\bar{f}^{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{t}-p_{\mathrm{new}}|\to-\mathcal{R}_{p_{\mathrm{new}}}(\tilde{p})`$.

[Lemma C.1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma C.1 (Properties of growth regret). ‣ D.1 Lemmas ‣ Appendix D Lemmas and Proofs ‣ Adapting the Process of Adaptation: Darwinian, Lamarckian, and Agent-Directed Evolution in AI")(c),
````

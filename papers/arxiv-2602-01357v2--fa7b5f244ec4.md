###### Abstract

Self-play post-training methods has emerged as an effective approach for
finetuning large language models and turn the weak language model into
strong language model without preference data. However, the theoretical
foundations for self-play finetuning remain underexplored. In this work,
we tackle this by connecting self-play finetuning with adversarial
imitation learning by formulating finetuning procedure as a min–max game
between the model and a regularized implicit reward player parameterized
by the model itself. This perspective unifies self-play imitation and
general preference alignment within a common framework. Under this
formulation, we present a game-theoretic analysis showing that the
self-play finetuning will converge to it’s equilibrium. Guided by this
theoretical formulation, we propose a new self-play imitation finetuning
algorithm based on the $`\chi^{2}`$-divergence variational objective
with bounded rewards and improved stability. Experiments on various of
language model finetuning tasks demonstrate consistent improvements over
existing self-play methods and validate our theoretical insights.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable success across
a wide range of applications that require complex reasoning or
specialized domain knowledge. A major recent advance in LLM development
is post-training alignment toward more desirable behaviors ([Mishra et
al., 2022](#bib.bib26); [Thoppilan et al., 2022](#bib.bib27); [Chung et
al., 2024](#bib.bib28)). Modern post-training pipelines typically
combine supervised finetuning (SFT) ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib29))
with a variety of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
methods ([Bai et al., 2022](#bib.bib30); [Rafailov et al.,
2023](#bib.bib31); [Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)).

Among these methods, Many reinforcement learning (RL)-based finetuning
approaches ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib31); [Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2); [Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib11); [Calandriello et
al., 2024](#bib.bib25); [Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)) rely on a
(general) preference oracle to label samples, which encourage the model
to learn from preferred responses over the undesirable ones. To reduce
reliance on human preference annotations, recent methods ([Chen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1); [Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib40); [Wang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib41)) study a self-play regime and treat the ground-truth
responses as positive samples and the model-generated responses as
negative counterparts. Despite recent empirical advancement, the
theoretical understanding of the self-play regime remains limited.

This gap in theory limits principled development of self-play
algorithms. For example, considering a nontrivial subset of prompts in
the dataset, the ground-truth response can be closely comparable in
quality, or even partially worse than, the model-generated response; in
such cases, the induced preference signal becomes ambiguous or
misspecified, making the implicit reward model prone to overfitting
these irregular comparisons and thus failing to provide a reliable
learning signal. In such a cases, existing self-play formulations lack a
clear theoretical mechanism to regularize the reward model and can
introduce misspecified supervision and destabilize training. This raises
the following question:

How can we theoretically characterize the (implicit) reward learning in
self-play finetuning?

To answer this question, we connect the self-play finetuning with the
adversarial imitation learning (AIL) framework. Serving long as a
principled framework for imitation and inverse reinforcement
learning ([Ho and Ermon, 2016](#bib.bib22); [Abbeel and Ng,
2004](#bib.bib33)), AIL formulates imitation from expert demonstrations
as a two-player game between a policy learner and a reward learner where
the reward learner aims to distinguish expert behavior from learner
behavior robustly, while the policy learner seeks to match the expert
distribution by maximizing the reward model. This paradigm has been
successfully applied to a variety of robotics tasks ([Rafailov et al.,
2021](#bib.bib35); [Ablett et al., 2023](#bib.bib36)). Notably, a series
of work [Garg et al. (2021)](#bib.bib6); [Al-Hafez et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib10); [Ren et al. (2024)](#bib.bib34) has considered
regularizing the reward learning with improved empirical performance for
classical RL tasks.

In this work, we establish a conceptual and algorithmic connection
between adversarial imitation learning and self-play finetuning in large
language models. We formulate this alignment process as a min–max game
in which the policy player corresponds to the language model itself,
while the reward player can be reparameterized using the model and its
previous snapshots. Based on this formulation, our contributions are
threefold:

- •
  We establish an adversarial imitation learning–based framework for
  self-play imitation finetuning of large language models, which
  naturally generalizes to self-play methods with general preference
  alignment.
- •
  We provide a rigorous game-theoretic analysis of self-play language
  model finetuning within the adversarial imitation learning framework.
  Guided by this analysis, we propose a novel self-play imitation
  finetuning algorithm with theoretical advantages over existing
  approaches.
- •
  We empirically evaluate our method on various families of language
  models, demonstrating consistent performance improvements over prior
  methods and validating our theoretical insights especially a more
  robust reward learner.

## 2 Related Works

In this section, we discuss the related works on the imitation learning
and self-play finetuning of language models.

Imitation Learning. Imitation learning is an variation of reinforcement
learning where the agent aims to imitate expert behavior leveraging the
reward-free expert demonstration. Historically, imitation learning
approaches can be broadly categorized into two major classes. The first
category is usually referred to as behavioral cloning ([Florence et al.,
2022](#bib.bib20); [Chi et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) which directly
imitate the expert demonstrations in a supervised learning manner.
Several recent works have established theoretical guarantees under these
settings ([Foster et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Rohatgi et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)).

The second category adopts a variational formulation casting imitation
learning as a min-max optimization between a reward model
differentiating agent’s behavior from expert demonstration and a policy
optimization trying to maximize the reward. This approach are usually
referred to as adversarial imitation learning (AIL). Representative
methods include GAIL ([Ho and Ermon, 2016](#bib.bib22)), IQ-Learn ([Garg
et al., 2021](#bib.bib6)), and LS-IQ ([Al-Hafez et al.,
2023](#bib.bib10)). This line of work has also been supported by
rigorous theoretical analyses ([Liu et al., 2021](#bib.bib4); [Xu et
al., 2024](#bib.bib23); [Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)).

refinement. At each iteration, an implicit reward model is constructed
by reparameterizing the LLM, which learns to discriminate between expert
data and generated data from the previous policy $`\pi^{k}`$.

Self-play with Preference Feedback. Self-play algorithms have been
extensively studied in the context of large language model alignment.
Many existing approaches focus on general preference alignment, where
the model is iteratively updated using samples labeled by a preference
oracle, often inspired by the DPO framework [Rafailov et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib31). This line of work is frequently described as RL
with AI feedback, in which the preference oracle is typically
instantiated by an LLM. For example, CPL ([Hejna et al.,
2023](#bib.bib37)) optimizes policies from preference data via
contrastive learning; iterative-DPO ([Tu et al., 2025](#bib.bib38); [Wu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)) repeatedly relabels model-generated responses
with a preference oracle and applies DPO-style updates; and
$`\chi`$PO ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib8)) introduces
$`\chi^{2}`$-based regularization to stabilize policy optimization. In
addition, SPPO ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)) develops a
preference-based self-play framework with a squared-loss objective and
formulates the interaction as a constant-sum two-player game for general
preference model. Closely related works ([Calandriello et al.,
2024](#bib.bib25); [Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib11); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)) further cast preference alignment as seeking a Nash
equilibrium in two-player games, yielding both improved empirical
performance and stronger theoretical guarantees.

Self-Play Finetuning with SFT Datasets. In parallel to self-play with a
preference oracle, another line of work studies self-play finetuning
using standard SFT datasets that contain expert (ground-truth)
demonstrations. For instance, SPIN ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib1))
introduces a DPO-style self-play objective that enables the model to
iteratively imitate SFT data by competing against its own past
instances. While SPIN does not require an explicit preference oracle, it
relies on expert prompt–response pairs from SFT as the self-play
targets. We refer to these methods as self-play imitation, as it
directly imitates SFT behavior rather than indirectly aligning to a
learned or external preference oracle. We provide an illustration of
this setup in
Figure [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2 Related Works ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning").
Follow-up works in a similar setting include SPACE ([Wang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib40)) and T-SPIN ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib41)), which
further improve upon SPIN.

Our work lies at the intersection of imitation learning and large
language model self-play. We reinterpret self-play imitation finetuning
methods, such as SPIN ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)), through the lens
of adversarial imitation learning, and provide the first unified
game-theoretic analysis of this class of methods. We further show that
the same perspective naturally extends to self-play algorithms for
general preference, including SPPO ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)) and
INPO ([Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)). Finally, by instantiating our
framework with a variational formulation based on the $`\chi^{2}`$
divergence, in the spirit of IQ-Learn ([Garg et al., 2021](#bib.bib6))
and LS-IQ ([Al-Hafez et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)), we derive a self-play
imitation finetuning algorithm that is both more stable and empirically
more effective. Notably,
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Discussion ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")
provides a unified and rigorous discussion of both self-play imitation
and preference-based self-play finetuning under our AIL formulation.

## 3 Preliminaries

We formulate the language model generation process as a contextual
bandit problem. For each round, the language model observes a context
$`x\in{\mathcal{X}}`$ and generates a response $`y\in{\mathcal{Y}}`$.
There exists a reward function $`r(x,y)`$ in a reward class
$`\mathcal{R}`$ can be learned for each context and response pairs. We
represent the language model as a policy $`\pi(y|x)`$ in a policy class
$`\Pi`$. Since the model aims to align with a domain which an
inaccessible generation policy $`\pi^{\star}`$ is preferred, the policy
learning process can be formulated as an adversarial process
$`\max_{r}\min_{\pi\in\Pi}~{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[r]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[r]`$
that jointly learn the reward and policy. This formulation is consistent
with the standard adversarial imitation learning setup.

Self-play finetuning. Self-play finetuning (SPIN) ([Chen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1)) aims to align a language model with an SFT dataset
$`\mathcal{D}^{\star}`$ generated by an expert policy
$`\pi^{\star}(y\mid x)`$, which may represent human behavior. At each
iteration, SPIN updates the model by maximizing the following objective:

|                             |                                                                            |               |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------- | ---------------------- | -------------------- | --- |
|                             | $`\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}^{\star}\\ |
| y^{\prime}\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot | x)\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y      | x)}{\pi^{k}(y | x)}\!-\!\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{\prime} | x)}{\pi^{k}(y^{\prime} | x)}\right)\right],`$ |     |

where $`\pi^{k}`$ denotes the model from the previous iteration,
$`\sigma(\cdot)`$ is a monotonically non-decreasing link function. By
iteratively optimizing this objective and resampling data, SPIN drives
the policy $`\pi`$ toward the expert policy $`\pi^{\star}`$.

Adversarial Imitation Learning. Instead of directly mimics the expert
demonstrations using behavioral cloning, adversarial imitation learning
(AIL) formulates the learning process as a two-player game, providing a
variational characterization of distribution matching between the expert
and behavioral policies. Formally, this involves jointly learning the
reward and policy via the following optimization:

|     |                                                                                                                                                           |     |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\max_{r}\min_{\pi}\underset{(s,a)\sim d^{\star}}{\mathbb{E}}[r(s,a)]-\!\!\!\!\!\underset{(s,a)\sim d^{\pi}}{\mathbb{E}}[r(s,a)]-\psi(r),`$ |     |

where
$`d^{\pi}=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\Pr_{\pi}(s_{t}=s,\,a_{t}=a)`$
denotes the discounted occupancy measure induced by the behavioral
policy $`\pi`$, and $`d^{\star}`$ is defined analogously for the expert
policy $`\pi^{\star}`$. $`\psi(r)`$ is a convex regularizer associated
with the choice of statistical distance between the expert and
behavioral occupancy measures ([Garg et al., 2021](#bib.bib6)). Although
we formulate adversarial imitation learning (AIL) within the Markov
Decision Process (MDP) framework, in the LLM setting considered in this
work the formulation naturally reduces to a contextual bandit problem,
since no meaningful transition dynamics are present.

For the simplicity of the theoretical analysis, we assume that the
optimal expert policy as well as the ground truth reward is realizable.

###### Assumption 3.1 (Realizability).

The ground-truth reward and optimal policy are realizable with the
corresponding function classes, i.e., $`r^{\star}{\in}\mathcal{R}`$,
$`\pi^{\star}{\in}\Pi`$.

## 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View

### 4.1 A Single-Stage Formulation

In this section, we focus on formulating the self-play finetuning
problem imitating an expert data distribution $`\pi^{\star}`$ from an
initial policy distribution as an adversarial imitation learning
process:

|     |     |                                                                                                                         |     |     |
| --- | --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\max*{r}\min*{\pi}{\mathbb{E}}\_{x}\Big[\sigma\big(\!\!\!\!\!\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\,\\[0.0819pt] |

\;\;\;\;y{\sim}\pi^{\star}\end{subarray}}{{\mathbb{E}}}\!\!\!\![r(x,y)]{-}\!\!\!\!\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\,\\[0.0819pt]
\;\;\;\;y{\sim}\pi\end{subarray}}{{\mathbb{E}}}\!\!\!\![r(x,y)]\big)\!\!-\!\!\psi(r)\Big],`$ | | (1) |

where $`\sigma`$ denotes the monotonically non-decreasing link function
such as $`\sigma(t)=t`$ or $`\sigma(t)=-\log(1+\exp(-t))`$, and
$`\psi(r)`$ is a convex regularizer ([Ho and Ermon, 2016](#bib.bib22)).
Notably, optimizing the objective
in ([1](#S4.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 4.1 A Single-Stage Formulation ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
is equivalent to minimizing a statistical distance between the expert
and current policy distributions ([Ho and Ermon, 2016](#bib.bib22);
[Garg et al., 2021](#bib.bib6)), given a identical link function
$`\sigma(t)`$.

In this work, we focus on the choice of the convex regularizer
$`\psi(r)`$, corresponding to the original regularization term
$`\phi(r,r^{k-1})`$ with the Bregman divergence component removed.
Different choices of $`\psi(r)`$ induce different properties in the
resulting self-play algorithms. We summarize the connection between the
choice of the regularizer and the resulting self-play algorithm in

equivalent to $`\min_{\pi\in\Pi}R_{\max}D_{\text{TV}}(\pi,\pi^{\star})`$

update, can recover the learning objective of (Linear) SPIN ([Chen et

thus $`R_{\max}`$ can be arbitrarily large.

multi-iteration two-stage surrogate, as used in SPIN, in

Notably, $`R_{\max}`$ here is unbounded and can be arbitrary large.

Pearson $`\chi^{2}`$ Divergence. Consider a regularizer with
$`\psi(r)=c{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[(r(x,y))^{2}]`$ and a link
function $`\sigma(t)=t`$. This formulation is equivalent to
$`\min_{\pi\in\Pi}D_{\chi^{2}}(\pi\|\pi^{\star})`$ under a trust region
constraint. This regularizer can further be generalized to
$`\psi(r)=c\alpha\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[(r(x,y))^{2}]+c(1-\alpha)\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[(r(x,y))^{2}]`$,
which can characterize the data mixing strategy during self-play and
equivalent to
$`\min_{\pi\in\Pi}D_{\chi^{2}}(\pi^{\star}\|\alpha\pi^{\star}+(1-\alpha)\pi)`$
([Al-Hafez et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)) under trust region constraints on
policy and reward. Since SPIN with both identical and sigmoid link
function cannot guarantee a bounded reward, the magnitude of the reward
from SPIN may be arbitrarily large in their cases. In contrast, we can
show that by applying the equally sampled mixed $`\chi^{2}`$
regularization, i.e.,
$`\psi(r)=(1/2)c\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[(r(x,y))^{2}]+(1/2)c\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[(r(x,y))^{2}]`$,
can lead to a bounded divergence and a bounded reward, which leads to
theoretical benefits in the following analysis in the two-stage
optimization alternative. Notably, [Al-Hafez et al. (2023)](#bib.bib10)
has derived a similar result within MDP setting with occupancy
distribution matching. We’ll adapt their result to contextual bandit
setting in our case. We now introduce the following proposition showing
the boundedness of the reward when using the mixed $`\chi^{2}`$ convex
regularizer:

###### Proposition 4.1 (Contextual bandit version of Proposition A.2 and A.3, [Al-Hafez et al. 2023](#bib.bib10)).

The mixed Pearson $`\chi^{2}`$ divergence between $`\pi^{\star}`$ and
the mixture distribution $`\tfrac{\pi+\pi^{\star}}{2}`$ induced by the
convex regularizer
$`\psi(r)=\tfrac{c}{2}\cdot\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[(r(x,y))^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[(r(x,y))^{2}]\right)`$
is bounded by:

|     |                                                                                                            |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle 0\leq 2D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\pi^{\star}\|\tfrac{\pi+\pi^{\star}}{2}\right)\leq\tfrac{1}{c}.`$ |     |

Furthermore, the optimal reward for solving the variational form of this
Pearson $`\chi^{2}`$ divergence:

|     |     |                                                                                                                           |     |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle 2D_{\chi^{2}}\big(\pi^{\star}\|\tfrac{\pi+\pi^{\star}}{2}\big)`$                                          |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle=\sup_{r}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[r(x,y)]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[r(x,y)]`$                                   |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{c}{2}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[(r(x,y))^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[(r(x,y))^{2}]\right)`$ |     |

is bounded within the interval $`[-\tfrac{1}{c},\tfrac{1}{c}]`$.

### 4.2 A Two-Stage Iterative Alternative

Directly solving the min-max optimization problem
in ([1](#S4.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 4.1 A Single-Stage Formulation ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
may be hard in practice, prior work usually decompose it into a
two-stage iterative optimization process ([Ho and Ermon,
2016](#bib.bib22); [Garg et al., 2021](#bib.bib6)). This iterative
process can be decomposed into a two-stage algorithm iteratively
optimizing the reward $`r`$ and policy $`\pi`$:

|     |                            |                                                                                                                                        |     |     |
| --- | -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle r^{k}`$    | $`\displaystyle\!=\argmax_{r}\!{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\big[\sigma({\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[r(x,y)]\!-\!{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{k}}[r(x,y)])`$ |     |     |
|     |                            | $`\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\phi(r,r^{k-1})\big],`$                                                                              |     |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi^{k+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle\!=\argmax_{\pi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\left[{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}r^{k}(x,y)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^{k})\right].`$       |     | (2) |

To attain a more stable optimization process, we seek to constrain the
optimization
in ([2](#S4.Ex7 "In 4.2 A Two-Stage Iterative Alternative ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
by regulating the optimization process with an additional KL constraint
$`D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi^{k})`$ on the policy for mirror descent and a
one-step Bregman constraint for $`r`$, which leads to a new convex
regularizer $`\phi(r,r^{k-1})=\psi(r)+\zeta D_{f}(r,r^{k-1})`$.
$`D_{f}(r,r^{k-1})`$ is a Bregman divergence defined by a convex
function $`f`$. One thing worth mentioning is that the policy
optimization objective has a closed-form solution
$`\pi^{k+1}\propto\pi^{k}\exp(\beta^{-1}r^{k})`$. SPIN ([Chen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1)) leveraged this closed-form to reparameterize the
reward function, converting the two-stage algorithm into a single-stage
iterative policy optimization.

3:  for $`k=1,2,\dots,K`$ do

4:   Obtain $`r^{k}`$ by

5:   Update policy $`\pi^{k+1}`$ by

## 6 Empirical Results

Mistral-7B model ([Jiang et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)) on 50k samples

$`y^{k}\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot\mid x)`$ for each prompt $`x`$ in the SFT

We evaluate the resulting models on a diverse suite of benchmarks,

initially very large (near the order of $`10^{4}`$) and then rapidly
collapses to near zero (on the order of $`10^{-4}`$), which can lead to
unstable optimization behavior. In contrast, our
$`\chi^{2}`$-regularized approach maintains a relatively small and
stable gradient norm, typically in the range of $`10^{1}`$ to

## 7 Conclusion

We presented a unified theoretical view of self-play post-training for
language model alignment by formulating it as adversarial imitation
learning. With a game-theoretic analysis based on this perspective and
clarifies the relationship underlying existing methods, we propose a
self-play imitation finetuning algorithm based on a
$`\chi^{2}`$-divergence variational objective, which yields bounded
rewards and improved training stability. Experiments on various of
models demonstrate consistent improvements over prior self-play methods,
validating both the theoretical insights and the practical effectiveness
of the proposed approach.

## Limitations

Our theoretical analysis relies on realizability assumptions. While such
assumptions are standard in theoretical analyses, they may not always
hold in practical LLM finetuning scenarios, where the reward and policy
classes can be misspecified. In addition, our convergence result
establishes an average-iterate guarantee, whereas practical self-play
finetuning evaluates the last-iterate model produced by training.
Establishing last-iterate convergence would require stronger assumptions
on the game structure or optimization dynamics, which we leave for
future work.

## Appendix A Discussion

### A.1 Adversarial Imitation Learning.

Existing adversarial imitation learning methods typically optimize a
single-stage min–max objective as
in ([1](#S4.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 4.1 A Single-Stage Formulation ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")),
but are formulated under the full MDP setting rather than the contextual
bandit setting considered in this paper. In particular, GAIL ([Ho and
Ermon, 2016](#bib.bib22)) employs a regularization that makes the
resulting objective equivalent to minimizing the Jensen–Shannon
divergence between expert and behavioral distributions. IQ-Learn ([Garg
et al., 2021](#bib.bib6)) uses the regularizer
$`\psi(r)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\star}}[r^{2}]`$, which is equivalent to
minimizing the $`\chi^{2}`$ divergence between the expert occupancy
measure $`\rho^{\star}`$ and the learner occupancy measure
$`\rho^{\pi}`$, where $`\rho`$ denotes the policy occupancy measure.
LS-IQ ([Al-Hafez et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)) further considers the
regularizer
$`\psi(r)=\alpha~\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\star}}[r^{2}]+(1-\alpha)~\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi}}[r^{2}]`$,
which corresponds to minimizing the $`\chi^{2}`$ divergence between
$`\rho^{\star}`$ and a mixture of $`\rho^{\star}`$ and $`\rho^{\pi}`$
with mixing coefficient $`\alpha`$. Notably, the regularization used in
LS-IQ is closely related to the formulation adopted in this paper.

### A.2 Self-Play Imitation Finetuning

Self-play imitation finetuning refers to methods that leverage an SFT
dataset to perform self-play, with the goal of imitating behaviors in
the SFT data rather than optimizing external signals such as
preferences. Both SPIN ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)) and our proposed
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 5 𝜒^2 Self-Play Imitation Finetuning ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")
fall into this category. We show that both methods can be formulated
within the standard adversarial imitation learning framework
in ([1](#S4.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 4.1 A Single-Stage Formulation ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")).

In particular, the linear variant of SPIN (using idential link function
$`\sigma(t)=t`$) directly minimizes the total variation distance between
the expert policy $`\pi^{\star}`$ and the learned policy $`\pi`$, as
proved in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Linear SPIN as TV Distance Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning").
The nonlinear variant of SPIN ($`\sigma(t)=-\log(1+\exp(-t))`$) can be
viewed as minimizing a KL divergence between $`\pi^{\star}`$ and
$`\pi`$, as shown in
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Non-Linear SPIN as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning").

As self-play methods can be cast as imitation learning toward a
prescribed objective
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 A Single-Stage Formulation ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")),
they implicitly induce a capacity ceiling determined by the underlying
imitation target. Consequently, the model capability attainable through
such procedures is fundamentally bounded, implying that existing LLM
self-play algorithms cannot achieve infinite capability gains via
iterative self-improvement alone.

### A.3 Self-Play for General Preference Alignment

In this sections, we extend our discussion to the general preference
alignment with self-play without the need of Bradley-Terry preference
model ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib2); [Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib11);
[Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)). Similar with our proposed $`\chi^{2}`$
self-play imitation finetuning, these methods often rely on the squared
loss in different settings. Below, we show that this line of work admits
an AIL interpretation with respect to a general preference oracle and
exhibit a close connection to $`\chi^{2}`$ divergence–regularized
adversarial imitation learning.

SPPO. Given an estimated probability $`P(y\!\succ\!\pi^{k}|x)`$ using
the general preference model, the objective in SPPO ([Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)) for policy $`\pi^{k+1}`$ can be written as:

|     |                                                                                                     |               |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\underset{\pi}{\argmin}\underset{\rho,\pi^{k}}{{\mathbb{E}}}\Big[\!\log\!\frac{\pi(y | x)}{\pi^{k}(y | x)}{-}\frac{1}{\beta}\!\left(P(y\succ\pi^{k} | x)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\!\!\Big]^{2}.`$ |     |

This update rule shares similarities with our proposed objective
in ([4](#S5.E4 "Equation 4 ‣ 5 𝜒^2 Self-Play Imitation Finetuning ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
by optimizing the $`\chi^{2}`$ regularized AIL objective under a trust
region constraint by slightly generalizing our proposed imitation
framework in
Sec. [4](#S4 "4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")
to preference-based policy optimization using the following proposition:

###### Proposition A.1.

Given a preference model that outputs
$`w^{k}(x,y):=P(y\succ\pi^{k}\mid x)`$, let $`y\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)`$
and define the weighted expectations:

|     |                                                     |                                                    |                                             |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{+}^{k}}[f(x,y)]`$ | $`\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}\_{y\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot | x)}\!\left[w^{k}(x,y)\,f(x,y)\right],`$     |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{-}^{k}}[f(x,y)]`$ | $`\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}\_{y\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot | x)}\!\left[(1-w^{k}(x,y))\,f(x,y)\right].`$ |     |

With $`\sigma(t)=t`$, mixed $`\chi^{2}`$ regularizer and $`r(x,y)`$
being the reparameterized reward function
in ([3](#S5.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 5 𝜒^2 Self-Play Imitation Finetuning ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")),
the SPPO objective is equivalent to optimizing:

|     |                                 |                                                                                                                                                        |     |
| --- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\arg\max_{r}\;`$ | $`\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big[\sigma\!\big({\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{+}^{k}}[r(x,y)]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{-}^{k}}[r(x,y)]\big)-\phi(r,r^{k-1})\Big],`$ |     |

###### Remark A.2.

Proposition [A.1](#A1.EGx18 "Proposition A.1. ‣ A.3 Self-Play for General Preference Alignment ‣ Appendix A Discussion ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")
provides a new derivation of SPPO algorithm, differs from the original
derivation from [Wu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib2). This new formulation
shows that instead of imitating the expert policy $`\pi^{\star}`$ in
original AIL formulation shown
in ([2](#S4.Ex7 "In 4.2 A Two-Stage Iterative Alternative ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")),
SPPO is adversarially imitating the general preference oracle
$`P(y\succ\pi^{k}\mid x)`$.

Iterative DPO. Iterative DPO serves as a baseline in SPPO ([Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)) and has also been applied to enhance LLM reasoning
([Tu et al., 2025](#bib.bib38)). In practice, it replaces the SPPO loss
with a DPO-style objective while keeping the remaining components
unchanged. In
Appendix [J](#A10 "Appendix J Iterative DPO as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"),
we show that this procedure implicitly minimizes the KL divergence
between the model policy and an expert policy $`\pi^{\star}`$ induced by
the preference oracle iteratively.

INPO. INPO ([Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)) formulates the general
preference alignment problem as solving Nash policy with online mirror
descent. For each iteration, it updates through the following update
rule:

|     |                                                                                                   |      |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi^{k+1}=\argmin\_{\pi}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot | x)\\ |

x\sim\rho(x)\\
y*{w},y*{l}\sim\lambda*{p}(y,y^{\prime})\end{subarray}}{{\mathbb{E}}}\bigg[\log\frac{\pi(y*{w}|x)}{\pi(y*{l}|x)}-\frac{\tau}{\eta}\log\frac{\pi*{\text{ref}}(y*{w}|x)}{\pi*{\text{ref}}(y*{l}|x)}-\frac{\eta-\tau}{\eta}\log\frac{\pi^{k}(y*{w}|x)}{\pi^{k}(y\_{l}|x)}-\frac{1}{2\eta}\bigg]^{2}.`$ | |

We show that this update rule is equivalent to a iterative AIL procedure
operating on the preference oracle. Compared to SPPO ([Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)), this equivalence arises under a different
construction of $`\pi_{+}^{k}`$ and $`\pi_{-}^{k}`$, as well as a
distinct reparameterization of $`r`$.

###### Proposition A.3.

Given a preference model that outputs
$`w^{k}(x,y):=P(y\succ\pi^{k}\mid x)`$, let
$`(y,y^{\prime})\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)\times\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)`$ and define
the weighted pairwise expectations:

|     |                                                                |                                                                                                                                |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{+}^{k}}[f(x,y,y^{\prime})]`$ | $`\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}_{(y,y^{\prime})\sim\pi^{k}\times\pi^{k}}\!\left[w^{k}(x,y)\,f(x,y,y^{\prime})\right],`$          |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{-}^{k}}[f(x,y,y^{\prime})]`$ | $`\displaystyle:={\mathbb{E}}_{(y,y^{\prime})\sim\pi^{k}\times\pi^{k}}\!\left[w^{k}(x,y^{\prime})\,f(x,y,y^{\prime})\right].`$ |     |

Then the INPO objective is equivalent to optimizing the reparameterized
$`r`$ with the mixed $`\chi^{2}`$ regularizer for:

|     |                                                                                                                                                                                                 |     |
| --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\arg\max_{r}\;{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\rho}\Big[\sigma\!\big({\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{+}^{k}}[r(x,y,y^{\prime})]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{-}^{k}}[r(x,y,y^{\prime})]\big)-\phi(r,r^{k-1})\Big],`$ |     |

where $`\sigma(t)=t`$, and $`r(x,y,y^{\prime})`$ is defined in:

|     |                                                          |                    |                                               |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------ | --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------- | ------ | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle r(x,y,y^{\prime}):=\eta\!\log\frac{\pi(y | x)}{\pi(y^{\prime} | x)}{-}{\tau}\!\log\frac{\pi\_{\mathrm{ref}}(y | x)}{\pi\_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime} | x)}{-}{(\eta{-}\tau)}\!\log\frac{\pi^{k}(y | x)}{\pi^{k}(y^{\prime} | x)}.`$ |     | (5) |

###### Remark A.4.

The reward reparameterization defined
in ([5](#A1.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ Proposition A.3. ‣ A.3 Self-Play for General Preference Alignment ‣ Appendix A Discussion ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
does not directly arise in closed form from a direct mirror descent
optimization as
in ([2](#S4.Ex7 "In 4.2 A Two-Stage Iterative Alternative ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")).
Instead, it has an additional KL regularizer
$`D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})`$, reflecting the fact that INPO
([Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)) formulates a constrained optimization
problem with respect to the reference policy $`\pi_{\text{ref}}`$. The
resulting formulation involves a paired response $`(y,y^{\prime})`$ for
canceling the partition function. Nevertheless, the overall AIL
interpretation remains consistent.

ONPO. ONPO ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)) extends INPO by replacing
standard online mirror descent with optimistic online mirror descent.
Under the assumption that
$`m_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{k-1}(\cdot|x)}[P(y\succ y^{\prime})]`$
is known, and by introducing an additional policy player, ONPO achieves
an improved duality gap upper bound of $`\mathcal{O}(1/K)`$, in contrast
to the standard $`\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{K})`$ rate of online mirror
descent. By adopting the same assumption and augmenting
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.2 A Two-Stage Iterative Alternative ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")
with an additional policy player, our framework can similarly strengthen
the result in
Theorem [4.4](#S4.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 4.4. ‣ 4.3 A Game-Theoretic Analysis ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")
to an $`\mathcal{O}(1/K)`$ rate.

### B.1 Key Lemmas

We first introduce the following lemmas:

###### Lemma B.1 (One-Step Descent, [Cai et al. 2020](#bib.bib5)).

For two policy distributions $`\pi^{\star}`$ and $`\pi`$, and a reward
function
$`r:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[-R_{\max},R_{\max}]`$, it
holds for
$`\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|x)\propto\pi(\cdot|x)\cdot\exp(\eta\cdot r(x,\cdot))`$
that:

|     |                                                     |              |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------- | ------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\langle r(x,\cdot),\pi^{\star}(\cdot | x)-\pi(\cdot | x)\rangle\leq\frac{\eta R^{2}_{\max}}{2}+\eta^{-1}\cdot\left(D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}(\cdot | x)\|\pi(\cdot | x))-D\_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}(\cdot | x)\|\pi^{\prime}(\cdot | x))\right)`$ |     |

###### Proof.

For any function
$`r:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}`$ and
distributions
$`\pi(\cdot|x),\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|x)\in\Delta({\mathcal{Y}})`$ that
satisfy

|     |                                   |                    |
| --- | --------------------------------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}(\cdot | x)\propto\pi(\cdot | x)\cdot\exp\bigl(\eta\cdot r(x,\cdot)\bigr),`$ |     |

we have

|     |                                                                        |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |     |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\eta\cdot{\langle}r,\pi^{\star}-\pi^{\prime}{\rangle}`$ | $`\displaystyle={\langle}Z+\log(\pi^{\prime}/\pi),\pi^{\star}-\pi^{\prime}{\rangle}`$                                                                                                                                                  |     |     |
|     |                                                                        | $`\displaystyle={\langle}Z,\pi^{\star}-\pi^{\prime}{\rangle}+{\langle}\log(\pi^{\star}/\pi),\pi^{\star}{\rangle}+{\langle}\log(\pi^{\prime}/\pi^{\star}),\pi^{\star}{\rangle}+{\langle}\log(\pi^{\prime}/\pi),-\pi^{\prime}{\rangle}`$ |     |     |
|     |                                                                        | $`\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\,\|\,\pi)-D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\,\|\,\pi^{\prime})-D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\prime}\,\|\,\pi).`$                                                                                               |     | (6) |

Here $`Z:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}`$ is a constant function
defined by

|     |                                                               |                                                  |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle Z(x)=\log\Bigl(\sum\_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\pi(y | x)\cdot\exp\bigl(\eta\cdot r(x,y)\bigr)\Bigr),`$ |     |

which implies that $`{\langle}Z,\pi^{\star}-\pi^{\prime}{\rangle}=0`$
in ([6](#A2.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ Proof. ‣ B.1 Key Lemmas ‣ Appendix B Proof of Theorem ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
as
$`\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|x),\pi^{\star}(\cdot|x)\in\Delta({\mathcal{Y}})`$.
Moreover,
by ([6](#A2.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ Proof. ‣ B.1 Key Lemmas ‣ Appendix B Proof of Theorem ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
we have

|     |                                                                          |                                                                         |                                                                                    |                                                    |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | ---------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\eta\cdot{\langle}r(x,\cdot),\pi^{\star}(\cdot)-\pi(\cdot | x){\rangle}`$                                                           | $`\displaystyle=\eta\cdot{\langle}r(x,\cdot),\pi^{\star}(\cdot)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot | x){\rangle}-\eta\cdot{\langle}r(x,\cdot),\pi(\cdot | x)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot          | x){\rangle}`$                                   |                       |            |
|     |                                                                          | $`\displaystyle\leq D\_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\pi^{\star}(\cdot               | x)\,\big\|\,\pi(\cdot                                                              | x)\bigr)-D\_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\pi^{\star}(\cdot     | x)\,\big\|\,\pi^{\prime}(\cdot | x)\bigr)-D\_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\pi^{\prime}(\cdot | x)\,\big\|\,\pi(\cdot | x)\bigr)`$ |     |     |
|     |                                                                          | $`\displaystyle\qquad+\eta\cdot\|r(x,\cdot)\|\_{\infty}\cdot\|\pi(\cdot | x)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot                                                              | x)\|\_{1}`$                                        |                                | (7)                                             |

for any state $`x\in{\mathcal{X}}`$. Meanwhile, by Pinsker’s inequality,
it holds that

|     |                                                                                               |     |     |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\prime}\,\|\,{\pi})\geq\|{\pi}-\pi^{\prime}\|^{2}_{1}/2.`$ |     | (8) |

Combining ([7](#A2.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ Proof. ‣ B.1 Key Lemmas ‣ Appendix B Proof of Theorem ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")), ([8](#A2.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ Proof. ‣ B.1 Key Lemmas ‣ Appendix B Proof of Theorem ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")),
and the fact that $`\|r(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leq R_{\max}`$, with
Lemma [B.3](#A2.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma B.3. ‣ B.2 Supporting Lemmas ‣ Appendix B Proof of Theorem ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"),
for any state $`x\in{\mathcal{X}}`$, we obtain

|     |                                                                          |                                                           |                                                           |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------- | --------------------- | ----------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\eta\cdot{\langle}r(x,\cdot),\pi^{\star}(\cdot)-\pi(\cdot | x){\rangle}`$                                             | $`\displaystyle\leq D\_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\pi^{\star}(\cdot | x)\,\big\|\,\pi(\cdot                          | x)\bigr)-D\_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\pi^{\star}(\cdot | x)\,\big\|\,\pi^{\prime}(\cdot      | x)\bigr)-\|\pi(\cdot | x)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot | x)\|\_{1}^{2}/2`$ |     |
|     |                                                                          | $`\displaystyle\qquad+\eta R\_{\max}\cdot\|\pi(\cdot      | x)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot                                     | x)\|\_{1}`$                                    |                                                |
|     |                                                                          | $`\displaystyle\leq D\_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\pi^{\star}(\cdot | x)\,\big\|\,\pi(\cdot                                     | x)\bigr)-D\_{\text{KL}}\bigl(\pi^{\star}(\cdot | x)\,\big\|\,\pi^{\prime}(\cdot                 | x)\bigr)+R^{2}\_{\max}\eta^{2}/2,`$ |                      |

which concludes the proof. ∎

###### Lemma B.2.

If the $`r`$-player optimizes $`r^{k}`$ using Online Mirror Descent
(OMD) against a $`\pi`$-player updated via Line 5 of
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.2 A Two-Stage Iterative Alternative ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"),
and letting $`BR^{2}_{\max}=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}D_{f}(r^{\star},r)`$,
the regret of the $`r`$-player is bounded by:

|     |                                                                                                                                                                                        |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r,\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r^{k},\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle\leq\mathcal{O}(R^{2}_{\max}B\sqrt{K})`$ |     |

###### Proof.

The $`r`$-player runs online mirror descent to maximize the sequence of
objectives $`\langle r,\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle`$. The gradients are
$`g^{k}\coloneqq\nabla_{r}\langle r,\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle=\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}`$.
For a linear objective and bounded Bregman divergence
$`\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}D_{f}(r^{\star},r)=R^{2}_{\max}B`$, selecting
the step size $`\zeta=\sqrt{K}/(BR^{2}_{\max})`$ yields the standard OMD
regret bound for a maximizer:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r,\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r^{k},\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle\leq\mathcal{O}(R^{2}_{\max}B\sqrt{K}),
``` |  |

which concludes the proof. ∎

### B.2 Supporting Lemmas

###### Lemma B.3.

Let $`\pi(\cdot|x)\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})`$ be a probability distribution
over a discrete action set $`\mathcal{Y}`$ conditioned on state $`x`$,
and let $`r:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[-R_{\max},R_{\max}]`$.
Define the updated policy

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{\prime}(y|x)\propto\pi(y|x)\exp(\eta r(x,y)),\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}.
``` |  |

Then the KL divergence between $`\pi^{\prime}`$ and $`\pi`$ satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|x)\|\pi(\cdot|x))\leq\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}R^{2}_{\max}.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

Fix $`x`$ and omit its notation for simplicity. Let

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Z=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[e^{\eta r}\right]=\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\pi(y|x)e^{\eta r(x,y)},\quad f(\eta)=\log Z.
``` |  |

The updated distribution can be written as

|     |                                                      |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \pi^{\prime}(y|x)=\frac{\pi(y|x)e^{\eta r(x,y)}}{Z}.
       ```                                                   |     |

The KL divergence can be expressed as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\prime}\|\pi)=\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\pi^{\prime}(y|x)\log\frac{\pi^{\prime}(y|x)}{\pi(y|x)}=\eta\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[r]-\log Z.
``` |  |

Let $`\mu=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[r]`$, and define the centered cumulant
generating function

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\psi(\eta)=f(\eta)-\eta\mu=\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[e^{\eta(r-\mu)}\right].
``` |  |

It follows that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\psi(0)=0,\quad\psi^{\prime}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[r]-\mu,\quad\psi^{\prime\prime}(\eta)=\mathrm{Var}_{\pi^{\prime}}(r).
``` |  |

Thus,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\prime}\|\pi)=\eta\psi^{\prime}(\eta)-\psi(\eta)=\int_{0}^{\eta}t\,\psi^{\prime\prime}(t)\,dt.
``` |  |

Since $`\|r\|_{\infty}\leq R_{\max}`$, we have
$`\psi^{\prime\prime}(t)=\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{t}}(r)\leq R_{\max}^{2}`$
for all $`t`$. Therefore,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\prime}\|\pi)\leq\int_{0}^{\eta}tR_{\max}^{2}\,dt=\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}R_{\max}^{2}.
``` |  |

∎

### B.3 Detailed Proof

###### Proof of Theorem [4.4](#S4.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 4.4. ‣ 4.3 A Game-Theoretic Analysis ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning").

According to
Definition [4.3](#S4.Thmtheorem3 "Definition 4.3. ‣ 4.3 A Game-Theoretic Analysis ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"),
the duality gap is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{DualGap}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}{J}(\bar{\pi},r)-\min_{\pi\in\Pi}{J}(\pi,\bar{r})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\langle r,\pi^{\star}-\bar{\pi}\rangle+\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\langle\bar{r},\pi-\pi^{\star}\rangle.`$ |  |

Multiplying by $`K`$ and using the definitions
$`\bar{\pi}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi^{k}`$ and
$`\bar{r}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}r^{k}`$, we can decompose
$`K\cdot\mathrm{DualGap}`$ by adding and subtracting
$`\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r^{k},\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle K\cdot\mathrm{DualGap}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r,\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle+\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r^{k},\pi-\pi^{\star}\rangle`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r,\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r^{k},\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle\right)}_{\text{Regret}_{r}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\left(\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r^{k},\pi-\pi^{\star}\rangle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r^{k},\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle\right)}_{\text{Regret}_{\pi}}.`$ |  | (9) |

Notice that the two inner terms in the second bracket combine such that
$`\langle r^{k},\pi-\pi^{\star}\rangle+\langle r^{k},\pi^{\star}-\pi^{k}\rangle=\langle r^{k},\pi-\pi^{k}\rangle`$.
Thus, it exactly simplifies to the $`\pi`$-player’s regret:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{Regret}_{\pi}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r^{k},\pi-\pi^{k}\rangle.`$ |  |

By
Lemma [B.2](#A2.EGx30 "Lemma B.2. ‣ B.1 Key Lemmas ‣ Appendix B Proof of Theorem ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"),
the $`r`$-player’s regret is bounded by
$`\mathcal{O}(R^{2}_{\max}B\sqrt{K})`$.

For the $`\pi`$-player, treating $`\pi\in\Pi`$ as the comparator in
Lemma [B.1](#A2.EGx23 "Lemma B.1 (One-Step Descent, ). ‣ B.1 Key Lemmas ‣ Appendix B Proof of Theorem ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")
and taking $`\eta=\beta^{-1}`$, we sum the one-step descent bound over
$`k=1,\dots,K`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle r^{k},\pi-\pi^{k}\rangle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{R^{2}_{\max}}{2\beta}+\beta\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[D_{\text{KL}}(\pi(\cdot|x)\|\pi^{k}(\cdot|x))-D_{\text{KL}}(\pi(\cdot|x)\|\pi^{k+1}(\cdot|x))\right].`$ |  |

Taking the maximum over $`\pi\in\Pi`$, bounding the telescoping sum by
$`D=\max_{\pi\in\Pi}D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi)`$, and selecting
$`\beta=\sqrt{K}/D`$, we obtain:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{Regret}_{\pi}\leq\frac{KR^{2}_{\max}}{2\beta}+\beta D=\mathcal{O}(D\cdot R^{2}_{\max}\sqrt{K}).`$ |  |

Substituting the bounds for $`\text{Regret}_{r}`$ and
$`\text{Regret}_{\pi}`$ back into
([9](#A2.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ Proof of Theorem . ‣ B.3 Detailed Proof ‣ Appendix B Proof of Theorem ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
yields:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle K\cdot\mathrm{DualGap}\leq\mathcal{O}(R^{2}_{\max}B\sqrt{K})+\mathcal{O}(D\cdot R^{2}_{\max}\sqrt{K})=\mathcal{O}\left((D+B)R^{2}_{\max}\sqrt{K}\right).`$ |  |

Dividing both sides by $`K`$, we obtain the final bound:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{DualGap}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(D+B)R^{2}_{\max}}{\sqrt{K}}\right),`$ |  |

which concludes the proof. ∎

## Appendix C Proof of Proposition [4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 4.1 (Contextual bandit version of Proposition A.2 and A.3, ). ‣ 4.1 A Single-Stage Formulation ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")

###### Proof of Proposition [4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 4.1 (Contextual bandit version of Proposition A.2 and A.3, ). ‣ 4.1 A Single-Stage Formulation ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning").

We first proof the boundedness of the optimal reward. This proof follows
the proof of Proposition A.2 in [Al-Hafez et al. (2023)](#bib.bib10).
For the mixed $`\chi^{2}`$ divergence, by the definition of the
variational form of Pearson $`\chi^{2}`$ divergence, we have:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle 2D_{\chi^{2}}(\pi^{\star}\|(\pi+\pi^{\star})/2)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\max_{r}~2{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[r(x,y)]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[r(x,y)]-\frac{c}{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\star}}[(r(x,y))^{2}]-\frac{c}{2}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[(r(x,y))^{2}]\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\max_{r}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\pi^{\star}(y|x)\rho(x)\left(r(x,y)-\frac{c}{2}r(x,y)^{2}\right)-\pi(y|x)\rho(x)\left(r(x,y)+\frac{c}{2}r(x,y)^{2}\right)\text{d}x\text{d}y.`$ |  |

For any $`a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}/\{0\}`$, the function
$`r\rightarrow a(r-\frac{c}{2}r^{2})-b(r+\frac{c}{2}r^{2})`$ reaches its
maximum at $`\frac{1}{c}\frac{a-b}{a+b}`$, which is bounded by
$`[-1/c,1/c]`$. By setting $`a=\rho(x)\pi^{\star}(y|x)`$ and
$`b=\rho(x)\pi(y|x)`$, we can obtain the closed-form of the optimal
reward under mixed $`\chi^{2}`$ divergence matching:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle r^{\star}(x,y)=\frac{1}{c}\frac{\pi^{\star}(y|x)-\pi(y|x)}{\pi^{\star}(y|x)+\pi(y|x)}\mathbb{I}\big(\pi^{\star}(y|x)\neq 0\wedge\pi(y|x)\neq 0\big),`$ |  |

which is bounded by $`[-1/c,1/c]`$. Furthermore, we prove the
boundedness of the mixed Pearson $`\chi^{2}`$ divergence
$`D_{\chi^{2}}(\pi^{\star}\|(\pi^{\star}+\pi)/2)`$, i.e.,
$`0\leq 2D_{\chi^{2}}(\pi^{\star}\|(\pi^{\star}+\pi)/2)\leq\frac{1}{c}`$.
The proof of this result is adapted from the proof of Proposition A.3 in
[Al-Hafez et al. (2023)](#bib.bib10). We consider the following
algebraic transformation by plugging in the optimal reward formulation:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle 2D_{\chi^{2}}(\pi^{\star}\|(\pi+\pi^{\star})/2)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\pi^{\star}(y|x)\rho(x)\left(r^{\star}(x,y)-\frac{c}{2}r^{\star}(x,y)^{2}\right)-\pi(y|x)\rho(x)\left(r^{\star}(x,y)+\frac{c}{2}r^{\star}(x,y)^{2}\right)\text{d}x\text{d}y`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\pi^{\star}\rho\left(\frac{1}{c}\left(\frac{\pi^{\star}-\pi}{\pi^{\star}+\pi}\right)-\frac{1}{2c}\left(\frac{\pi^{\star}-\pi}{\pi^{\star}+\pi}\right)^{2}\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad-\pi\rho\left(\frac{1}{c}\left(\frac{\pi^{\star}-\pi}{\pi^{\star}+\pi}\right)+\frac{1}{2c}\left(\frac{\pi^{\star}-\pi}{\pi^{\star}+\pi}\right)^{2}\right)\text{d}x\text{d}y`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}\frac{\rho(x)}{2c}\frac{(\pi^{\star}(y|x)-\pi(y|x))^{2}}{\pi^{\star}(y|x)+\pi(y|x)}\text{d}x\text{d}y`$ |  |

## Appendix H Linear SPIN as TV Distance Minimization

In this section, we show that linear SPIN is equivalent to minimizing
the total variation (TV) distance between the model distribution and the
expert data distribution. Choosing $`\psi(r)=0`$ if $`|r|\leq R_{\max}`$
and $`\psi(r)=\infty`$ otherwise, setting
$`\sigma(t)=t`$, ([1](#S4.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 4.1 A Single-Stage Formulation ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
is equivalent to:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\underset{x\sim\rho(x)}{{\mathbb{E}}}\left(\underset{y\sim\pi^{\star}}{{\mathbb{E}}}r(x,y)-\underset{y\sim\pi}{{\mathbb{E}}}r(x,y)\right)\mathbb{I}(r\leq R_{\max}),`$ |  |

where $`R_{\max}`$ can be arbitrarily large since original SPIN doesn’t
constrain the reward magnitude explicitly. By leveraging the reward
reparameterization
in ([3](#S5.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 5 𝜒^2 Self-Play Imitation Finetuning ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle r(x,y)=\beta\log\left(\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi^{k}(y|x)}\right),`$ |  |

it recovers the identical version of SPIN. By algebraic transformation:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\min_{\pi\in\Pi}~\mathcal{J}(r,\pi)`$ | $`\displaystyle\coloneq\underset{x\sim\rho(x)}{{\mathbb{E}}}\left(\underset{y\sim\pi^{\star}}{{\mathbb{E}}}r(x,y)-\underset{y\sim\pi}{{\mathbb{E}}}r(x,y)\right)\mathbb{I}(r\leq R_{\max})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\underset{x\sim\rho(x)}{{\mathbb{E}}}\langle r(x,\cdot),\pi^{\star}(\cdot|x)-\pi(\cdot|x)\rangle\mathbb{I}(r\leq R_{\max})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq R_{\max}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}|\pi^{\star}(\cdot|x)-\pi(\cdot|x)|.`$ |  | (18) |

Consider $`r^{\star}(x,y)`$ as the optimal reward solution for reward

Assumption [3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Assumption 3.1 (Realizability). ‣ 3 Preliminaries ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"):

holds because $`D_{\text{TV}}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mu-\nu\|_{1}`$.
This is a Total Variation distance minimization.

## Appendix I Non-Linear SPIN as KL Divergence Minimization

We begin by the following lemma for contraction:

###### Lemma I.1 (KL contraction toward $`\pi^{\star}`$).

Let $`\beta\geq 1`$ and $`\alpha\coloneqq 1/\beta\in(0,1]`$, and define
the update

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{k+1}(y|x)\;\propto\;\pi^{k}(y|x)\Big(\frac{\pi^{\star}(y|x)}{\pi^{k}(y|x)}\Big)^{\alpha}\;=\;\pi^{k}(y|x)^{1-\alpha}\pi^{\star}(y|x)^{\alpha}.
``` |  | (19) |

Then the reverse KL to data contracts geometrically:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k+1}\big)\;\leq\;(1-\alpha)\,D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k}\big)\;=\;\Big(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k}\big).
``` |  |

Consequently,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k}\big)\;\leq\;\Big(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\Big)^{k}D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}\big)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

We begin by explicitly writing the normalized update rule. Let $`Z(x)`$
be the normalization constant (partition function) for the update
in ([19](#A9.E19 "Equation 19 ‣ Lemma I.1 (KL contraction toward 𝜋^⋆). ‣ Appendix I Non-Linear SPIN as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi^{k}(y|x)^{1-\alpha}\pi^{\star}(y|x)^{\alpha}=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)}\left[\left(\frac{\pi^{\star}(y|x)}{\pi^{k}(y|x)}\right)^{\alpha}\right].
``` |  |

The normalized policy is then given by:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{k+1}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi^{k}(y|x)^{1-\alpha}\pi^{\star}(y|x)^{\alpha}.
``` |  |

We now expand the KL divergence
$`D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k+1})`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k+1})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{\star}}\left[\log\frac{\pi^{\star}(y|x)}{\pi^{k+1}(y|x)}\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{\star}}\left[\log\pi^{\star}(y|x)-\log\left(\frac{\pi^{k}(y|x)^{1-\alpha}\pi^{\star}(y|x)^{\alpha}}{Z(x)}\right)\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{\star}}\left[\log\pi^{\star}(y|x)-(1-\alpha)\log\pi^{k}(y|x)-\alpha\log\pi^{\star}(y|x)+\log Z(x)\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{\star}}\left[\log\pi^{\star}(y|x)-\log\pi^{k}(y|x)\right]+\log Z(x)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k})+\log Z(x).`$ |  | (20) |

To bound the remainder term $`\log Z(x)`$, we invoke Jensen’s
inequality. Since $`\beta\geq 1`$, we have $`\alpha=1/\beta\in(0,1]`$.
Consequently, the function $`f(t)=t^{\alpha}`$ is concave. Applying
Jensen’s inequality to the expectation of the likelihood ratio under
$`\pi^{k}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle Z(x)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{k}}\left[\left(\frac{\pi^{\star}(y|x)}{\pi^{k}(y|x)}\right)^{\alpha}\right]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{k}}\left[\frac{\pi^{\star}(y|x)}{\pi^{k}(y|x)}\right]\right)^{\alpha}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{y}\pi^{k}(y|x)\frac{\pi^{\star}(y|x)}{\pi^{k}(y|x)}\right)^{\alpha}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{y}\pi^{\star}(y|x)\right)^{\alpha}=1^{\alpha}=1.`$ |  |

Thus, $`Z(x)\leq 1`$, which implies $`\log Z(x)\leq 0`$. Substituting
this inequality back
into ([20](#A9.E20 "Equation 20 ‣ Proof. ‣ Appendix I Non-Linear SPIN as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")),
we obtain:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k+1})\leq(1-\alpha)D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k}).
``` |  |

Substituting $`\alpha=1/\beta`$ yields the geometric contraction:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k+1})\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k}).
``` |  |

Applying this inequality recursively $`k`$ times leads to the final
convergence rate:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{k})\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{\beta}\right)^{k}D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}).
``` |  |

This completes the proof. ∎

Consider the choice of logistic link function
$`\sigma(t)=-\log(1+\exp(-t))`$
in ([2](#S4.Ex7 "In 4.2 A Two-Stage Iterative Alternative ‣ 4 Adversarial Imitation Learning View ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")),
$`\psi(r)=\infty\cdot\mathbf{1}[|r|_{\infty}>R_{\max}]`$ without the
Bregman constraint over $`r`$, it recovers the original SPIN ([Chen et
al., 2024](#bib.bib1)) objective with non-identical link function. SPIN
has proved the following lemma:

###### Lemma I.2 (Theorem 5.4 in [Chen et al. 2024](#bib.bib1)).

Consider the choice of logistic link function in SPIN. Suppose
$`\pi^{k+1}`$ is the global minimum for the SPIN objective at iteration
$`k`$, then the opponent player at iteration $`k+1`$ satisfies:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{k+1}(y|x)\propto\pi^{k}(y|x)\left(\pi^{\star}(y|x)/\pi^{k}(y|x)\right)^{1/\beta}.
``` |  |

By first applying
Lemma [I.2](#A9.Ex111 "Lemma I.2 (Theorem 5.4 in ). ‣ Appendix I Non-Linear SPIN as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")
for SPIN objective then applying
Lemma [I.1](#A9.Ex97 "Lemma I.1 (KL contraction toward 𝜋^⋆). ‣ Appendix I Non-Linear SPIN as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"),
we can prove that SPIN with non-identical link function contracts under

## Appendix J Iterative DPO as KL Divergence Minimization

In this section, we analyze the theoretical properties of Iterative DPO
([Tu et al., 2025](#bib.bib38); [Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)). We
demonstrate that iteratively solving the KL-regularized reinforcement
learning objective—using the current policy as the reference—constitutes
a contraction mapping that minimizes the KL divergence between the
current policy and the optimal policy implied by the preference oracle.
Let $`\mathcal{X}`$ be the input space and $`\mathcal{Y}`$ be the output
space. We assume access to a preference oracle $`P(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|x)`$
which adheres to the Bradley-Terry (BT) model. Under the BT model, the
preference probability is determined by a latent reward function
$`r^{\star}(x,y)`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|x)=\frac{\exp(r^{\star}(x,y_{1}))}{\exp(r^{\star}(x,y_{1}))+\exp(r^{\star}(x,y_{2}))}=\sigma(r^{\star}(x,y_{1})-r^{\star}(x,y_{2})).
``` |  |

Let $`\pi^{\star}`$ denote the optimal policy that perfectly captures
the underlying reward structure, i.e.,
$`\pi^{\star}(y|x)\propto\exp(r^{\star}(x,y))`$.

We consider an iterative setting where, at step $`k`$, we optimize a
policy $`\pi`$ against a reference policy $`\pi^{k}`$ (the policy from
the previous iteration). The objective is to maximize the expected
reward subject to a KL-divergence constraint:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\pi}\mathcal{J}_{k}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[r^{\star}(x,y)]-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi(\cdot|x)||\pi^{k}(\cdot|x)).
``` |  | (21) |

###### Lemma J.1.

Given the reference policy $`\pi^{k}`$ and the preference oracle $`P`$,
the optimal policy $`\pi^{k+1}`$ maximizing the objective
in ([21](#A10.E21 "Equation 21 ‣ Appendix J Iterative DPO as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
is given by:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{k+1}(y|x)\propto\pi^{k}(y|x)\left(\frac{P(y\succ y_{\mathrm{ref}}|x)}{1-P(y\succ y_{\mathrm{ref}}|x)}\right)^{1/\beta}
``` |  |

where preference estimates are taken relative to a baseline
$`y_{\text{ref}}`$ drawn from $`\pi^{k}`$.

###### Proof.

The optimization problem
in ([21](#A10.E21 "Equation 21 ‣ Appendix J Iterative DPO as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
has a well-known closed-form solution given by the Boltzmann
distribution:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{k+1}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z_{k}(x)}\pi^{k}(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{r^{\star}(x,y)}{\beta}\right).
``` |  | (22) |

To express the reward $`r^{\star}(x,y)`$ in terms of the oracle, we
utilize the Bradley-Terry relationship. The odds of preferring $`y`$
over a reference output $`y_{\text{ref}}`$ are:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{P(y\succ y_{\text{ref}}|x)}{P(y_{\text{ref}}\succ y|x)}=\frac{e^{r^{\star}(x,y)}}{e^{r^{\star}(x,y_{\text{ref}})}}=\exp(r^{\star}(x,y)-r^{\star}(x,y_{\text{ref}})).
``` |  |

Taking the logarithm yields the reward function (up to a constant shift
$`r^{\star}(x,y_{\text{ref}})`$ which is absorbed into the partition
function):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r^{\star}(x,y)=\log\left(\frac{P(y\succ y_{\text{ref}}|x)}{1-P(y\succ y_{\text{ref}}|x)}\right)+C.
``` |  |

Substituting this expression back
into ([22](#A10.E22 "Equation 22 ‣ Proof. ‣ Appendix J Iterative DPO as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
yields the proposition:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{k+1}(y|x)\propto\pi^{k}(y|x)\left(\frac{P(y\succ y_{\text{ref}}|x)}{1-P(y\succ y_{\text{ref}}|x)}\right)^{1/\beta}.
``` |  |

∎

We now show that this iterative process monotonically reduces the
distance to the optimal policy $`\pi^{\star}`$.

###### Proposition J.2 (Contraction for Iterative DPO).

Let $`\pi^{\star}`$ be the global optimal policy implied by the reward
oracle. The sequence of policies $`\{\pi^{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}`$
generated by the update rule in Proposition
[J.1](#A10.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma J.1. ‣ Appendix J Iterative DPO as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")
satisfies the following contraction inequality:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{k+1})\leq D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{k})-D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{k+1}||\pi^{k}).
``` |  |

Consequently,
$`D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{k+1})<D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{k})`$
for all non-trivial updates ($`\pi^{k+1}\neq\pi^{k}`$).

###### Proof.

We expand the KL divergence term
$`D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{k+1})`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{k+1})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\pi^{\star}(y)\log\frac{\pi^{\star}(y)}{\pi^{k+1}(y)}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\pi^{\star}(y)\left[\log\pi^{\star}(y)-\log\left(\frac{\pi^{k}(y)\exp(r^{\star}(y)/\beta)}{Z_{k}}\right)\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\pi^{\star}(y)\log\frac{\pi^{\star}(y)}{\pi^{k}(y)}-\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{\star}}[r^{\star}(y)]+\log Z_{k}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{k})-\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\star}}[r^{\star}]+\log Z_{k}.`$ |  | (23) |

Next, we relate the log-partition function $`\log Z_{k}`$ to the KL
divergence between steps. By definition:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{k+1}||\pi^{k})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{k+1}}\left[\log\frac{\pi^{k+1}}{\pi^{k}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{k+1}}\left[\frac{r^{\star}}{\beta}-\log Z_{k}\right]=\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{k+1}}[r^{\star}]-\log Z_{k}.
``` |  |

Solving for $`\log Z_{k}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log Z_{k}=\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{k+1}}[r^{\star}]-D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{k+1}||\pi^{k}).
``` |  | (24) |

Substituting ([24](#A10.E24 "Equation 24 ‣ Proof. ‣ Appendix J Iterative DPO as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning"))
into ([23](#A10.E23 "Equation 23 ‣ Proof. ‣ Appendix J Iterative DPO as KL Divergence Minimization ‣ Your Self-Play Algorithm is Secretly an Adversarial Imitator:Understanding LLM Self-Play through the Lens of Imitation Learning")):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{k+1})=D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{k})-D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{k+1}||\pi^{k})-\frac{1}{\beta}\underbrace{\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\star}}[r^{\star}]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{k+1}}[r^{\star}]\right)}_{\Delta\geq 0}.
``` |  |

Since $`\pi^{\star}`$ maximizes the expected reward, the term $`\Delta`$
is non-negative. Since $`D_{\text{KL}}`$ is non-negative, the inequality
holds strictly, proving contraction towards the oracle distribution. ∎

## Appendix O Limitations

Our theoretical analysis relies on realizability assumptions. While such
assumptions are standard in theoretical analyses, they may not always
hold in practical LLM finetuning scenarios, where the reward and policy
classes can be misspecified. In addition, our convergence result
establishes an average-iterate guarantee, whereas practical self-play
finetuning evaluates the last-iterate model produced by training.
Establishing last-iterate convergence would require stronger assumptions
on the game structure or optimization dynamics, which we leave for
future work.
````

###### Abstract

Harness optimization is often studied with a fixed policy model and
benchmark. When either changes, the optimizer must decide which parts of
an evolved harness to reuse or revise, but the harness alone may not
explain why earlier edits were made or how they performed. In this work,
we study Continual Recursive Self-Improvement (CRSI), a setting for
continuing existing harness-optimization methods across compatible
benchmarks or policy models. We propose Self-Improvement Memory (SIM)
for the harness optimizer, rather than the task-solving policy. SIM
groups edits by recurring harness problem and records their rationale,
predicted and observed outcomes, and evaluation context. New attempts
extend this history as optimization continues. In our experiments across
four domains, SIM achieves higher final scores than harness-only and
root-cause-only continuation in cross-dataset and pairwise cross-model
comparisons. Across ten independent source runs in coding, paired
continuations with SIM improve final scores by an average of 2.36 points
over harness-only continuation ($`p=0.003`$).

## 1 Introduction

An LLM agent depends on both its policy model and the harness that
manages context, tools, and execution. Improving the harness can raise
performance without updating model weights, but finding useful changes
by hand requires repeated engineering and evaluation (Lin et al., 2026;
Xia et al., 2026). Recent work automates this process through recursive
self-improvement (RSI), using execution feedback to repeatedly propose,
evaluate, and select harness edits (Lee et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026;
Xia et al., 2026). Although evolved harnesses can improve performance on
other benchmarks or models (Lin et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Xia et
al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026b), optimization is usually conducted within a
fixed setting. As models are updated and agents encounter new tasks,
harness optimization often needs to continue beyond that setting. This
raises two fundamental challenges.

Adapting to a new model. Model adaptation arises in two settings:
_multi-model serving_, where one shared harness supports different
policy models, and _model evolution_, where new versions replace earlier
ones within a model family. Both motivate reusing an evolved harness and
its improvement experience instead of independently optimizing a harness
for every model or release. Yet modifications suited to one policy can
interact differently with another, even when the benchmark stays fixed
(Yang et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026d). In our workspace experiment, an
Opus-evolved harness starts at 68.41 on Gemini, below the seed harness’s
73.89 (Figure 3). The optimizer must determine which inherited
mechanisms still help and which need revision.

Adapting to new data. With the policy fixed, a different task
distribution can expose failures that source optimization did not
address. A completion gate that checks whether commands ran
successfully, for example, may need to check whether a feasible solution
is also optimal. In our text-classification experiment, the
source-evolved FewShotAll harness starts at 72.92 on the target suite,
below the seed’s 75.72 (Figure 2). A useful source fix may therefore
need revision before it helps the target.

In either transition, the evolved harness alone leaves important
questions unanswered: why was an edit proposed, what was it expected to
fix, and what happened when it was tested? Source optimization produces
this evidence, but it need not accompany the harness. Without this
history, target optimization may repeat earlier attempts or change
inherited components without knowing their purpose. We therefore ask
whether the experience gained during source optimization can help the
harness adapt to new models or data.

In this work, we study Continual Recursive Self-Improvement (CRSI), in
which harness optimization continues after a benchmark or policy-model
change while preserving a compatible executable interface. We propose
Self-Improvement Memory (SIM) to carry improvement experience alongside
the evolved harness (Figure ). SIM groups selected edits by the harness
problems they address and gives the proposer and selector access to
their rationale, predicted and observed outcomes, and evaluation
context. It creates entries for new problems and appends further
attempts and outcomes to existing ones, helping the optimizer decide
which modifications to retain, revise, replace, or remove.

Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  Continual harness optimization. We define cross-dataset continuation
  with the policy fixed and cross-model continuation with the benchmark
  and split fixed. Existing harness-optimization methods can use this
  setting while retaining their proposal, evaluation, and selection
  procedures.
- •
  Self-Improvement Memory. We introduce a structured record of harness
  problems, modifications, and outcomes that conditions both proposal
  and selection, preserving source experience while incorporating new
  attempts during target optimization.
- •
  Controlled evidence across four domains. With matched starting
  harnesses, round counts, and candidate budgets, Full SIM outperforms
  harness-only and root-cause-only continuation in six cross-dataset and
  four Opus-to-Gemini settings. Across ten independent source runs in
  coding, paired continuations with SIM improve 9 of 10 runs, with a
  mean advantage of 2.36 points over harness-only continuation
  ($`p=0.003`$).

## 2 Related Work

##### Recursive self-improvement of agent harnesses.

Yu et al. (2026b) survey harness optimization workflows, recursive
reuse, and the conditions under which evolved modifications transfer.
Meta-Harness and AHE search over harness code using execution traces and
scores (Lee et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026). Other approaches let the
agent modify its own system (Zhang et al., 2026a; Zhang et al., 2026b;
Zhang et al., 2026c) or learn a policy for editing harnesses (Shao et
al., 2026). SIA instead combines harness edits with updates to the task
model’s weights (Hebbar et al., 2026).

##### Transfer of evolved harnesses.

Evolved harnesses can retain their gains on held-out tasks, other
models, or downstream benchmarks (Lin et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026;
Shi et al., 2026). Recent methods encourage transfer through
benchmark-disjoint evolution and modular edits (Wu et al., 2026),
regularized search (Xia et al., 2026), or separation of general and
benchmark-specific mechanisms (Yu et al., 2026a). Their effectiveness
still depends on how the model and harness interact (Yang et al., 2026;
Yu et al., 2026d). PluginRSI also reuses its plugin library in
subsequent optimization.

##### Memory of the improvement process.

Prior work retains candidate artifacts (Lee et al., 2026), edit
histories (Xia et al., 2026), improvement graphs (Zhang et al., 2026d),
skill-revision records (Li and Hu, 2026), and playbooks (Zhang et al.,
2026f). HarnessBank groups candidate harnesses by the problems they
address (Luo et al., 2026). Xu (2026) continues multi-task evolution
with free-text notes and source replay; Harness² reuses an improvement
library across benchmarks (Xu et al., 2026a). Meta-Skill and Meta²
transfer learned guidance for constructing or editing task-specific
harnesses (Qian et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026). Continual adaptation
also raises retention and interference concerns (Chen et al., 2026; Zhao
et al., 2026). SIM links harness problems to modifications, predicted
and observed outcomes, and provenance. We test SIM by continuing
optimization from the same evolved harness with no SIM, root causes
only, or the full record after a benchmark or model change. Appendix A
expands these comparisons, including continued optimization and memory
for task-solving agents.

## 3 Method

Figure 1: SIM in the agentic outer loop. Starting from
$`(H_{S},\mathcal{M}_{S})`$, the proposer and selector consult SIM
(dashed arrows) to improve the harness on evolve-set tasks. After
selection, SIM records the chosen edit and its measured outcome (teal
arrow). Held-out evaluation stays outside the loop.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

##### Harness RSI.

Following Meta-Harness (Lee et al., 2026), we optimize an executable
harness $`H`$ around a fixed policy model $`M`$. The harness determines
the model’s context, tools, and execution flow. We write a setting as
$`\sigma=(M,\mathcal{X})`$, with disjoint evolve and held-out evaluation
sets $`\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{evo}}`$ and $`\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{eval}}`$.
The score $`J(H;M,\mathcal{X})`$ follows the benchmark’s aggregation
rule (Appendix B). Only evolve-set feedback is available for proposing
edits, selecting candidates, updating SIM, or deciding when to stop.

A run consists of $`N`$ rounds. At round $`t=0,\ldots,N-1`$, the
evaluator executes $`H_{t}`$ on $`\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{evo}}`$ and
returns scores, traces, and errors as $`F_{t}`$. The proposer $`P`$ uses
$`F_{t}`$ and the run’s history $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ (earlier feedback
and code diffs) to generate $`k`$ candidate harnesses
$`\mathcal{C}_{t}`$. The selector $`\mathcal{S}`$ screens proposals
before execution and chooses among the evaluated candidates, weighing
performance, complexity, domain specificity, and regression risk:

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{t} \displaystyle=P(H_{t},F_{t},\mathcal{D}_{t}), \tag{1}
```

```math
\displaystyle H_{t+1} \displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(H_{t},F_{t},\mathcal{C}_{t},F_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}),
```

Here $`F_{t}^{\mathcal{C}}`$ contains feedback from the candidates that
passed screening and were executed. If no candidate is accepted,
$`H_{t+1}=H_{t}`$. The proposer and selector are LLM-based harness
engineers; $`M`$ is the policy that executes the tasks.

##### Continual setting.

After source RSI in $`\sigma_{S}=(M_{S},\mathcal{X}_{S})`$, optimization
resumes from the final retained harness $`H_{S}`$ in
$`\sigma_{T}=(M_{T},\mathcal{X}_{T})`$. In _cross-dataset_ CRSI, the
benchmark changes while the policy stays fixed
($`\mathcal{X}_{S}\neq\mathcal{X}_{T}`$, $`M_{S}=M_{T}`$). In
_cross-model_ CRSI, the policy changes on the same benchmark and split
($`M_{S}\neq M_{T}`$, $`\mathcal{X}_{S}=\mathcal{X}_{T}`$). Both require
a shared executable harness interface. We measure target improvement as
the final minus starting score under
$`J(\cdot\,;M_{T},\mathcal{X}_{T}^{\mathrm{eval}})`$.

### 3.2 Self-Improvement Memory

SIM groups edits by the recurring harness problems they address. For a
root-cause hypothesis $`c_{i}`$, such as premature completion, an entry
is

```math
m_{i}=\big(c_{i},[(a_{ij},o_{ij},p_{ij})]_{j}\big), \tag{2}
```

where $`j`$ indexes attempts in order and $`\mathcal{M}_{t}=\{m_{i}\}`$
is the current memory. Each attempt stores the modification and its
rationale $`a_{ij}`$, predicted and observed task outcomes $`o_{ij}`$,
and provenance $`p_{ij}`$ (domain, benchmark, policy, and round).
Predictions of which tasks an edit will fix are recorded before
evaluation and checked afterward.

At each round, the proposer receives the complete SIM in its system
prompt, and the selector reads the same record when assessing
candidates. Both read all entries without retrieval or truncation. The
loop in Equation 1 becomes

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{t} \displaystyle=P(H_{t},F_{t},\mathcal{D}_{t};\mathcal{M}_{t}), \tag{3}
```

```math
\displaystyle H_{t+1} \displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(H_{t},F_{t},\mathcal{C}_{t},F_{t}^{\mathcal{C}};\mathcal{M}_{t}).
```

Each proposal cites an existing root cause or names a new one. After
selection, _only the chosen candidate_ is recorded in $`e_{t}`$,
including its measured outcomes even when the edit regresses or fails to
fix a predicted task. The update
$`\mathcal{M}_{t+1}=U(\mathcal{M}_{t},e_{t})`$ appends this record to
the cited problem or creates a new entry, preserving earlier attempts.
If no edit is accepted, $`e_{t}=\emptyset`$ and
$`U(\mathcal{M}_{t},\emptyset)=\mathcal{M}_{t}`$. Entries with identical
root-cause text are merged without dropping their histories. Appendix
D.1 gives the entry-matching details.

### 3.3 Continual RSI via SIM

Source RSI starts with an empty SIM and produces
$`(H_{S},\mathcal{M}_{S})`$. Full SIM carries both into target
optimization (Figure 1). The proposer and selector use each edit’s
rationale, outcome, and evaluation context to judge whether it still
applies to the current failures. They can retain, revise, replace, or
remove harness modifications while keeping the earlier memory records.
Appendix D.2 gives the prompt instructions for using this history.

##### Transfer conditions.

We compare four conditions: _Cold Start_ $`(H_{0},\emptyset)`$ from the
seed harness; _Harness Transfer_ $`(H_{S},\emptyset)`$; _Problem-Only
Memory_ $`(H_{S},\mathcal{M}^{c}_{S})`$ with root causes only; and _Full
SIM_ $`(H_{S},\mathcal{M}_{S})`$. The three transfer conditions start
from the same $`H_{S}`$, and all four use the same target policy, tasks,
and round and candidate budgets. Each keeps its assigned memory
condition throughout target RSI, while retaining the ordinary target-run
history $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ (Appendix C.3). Source history enters
through SIM; new target records follow the same memory condition.

## 4 Experiments

Our experiments address four questions:

- $`\bullet`$
  Does SIM improve continued RSI after a benchmark or policy-model
  change? (§4.2–4.3)
- $`\bullet`$
  Does the full SIM record help beyond retaining only root causes? (§4.2
  and §4.3.1)
- $`\bullet`$
  How does SIM guide the reuse and revision of inherited harness edits?
  (§4.4; Appendix F.5)
- $`\bullet`$
  Is SIM’s benefit consistent across independently evolved source
  harnesses? (§4.4)

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

##### Domains.

We study four domains in which a source-evolved harness remains
executable on the target (Appendix B, Table 4). _Coding_ moves from
Terminal-Bench 2.1 (TB2.1) to Terminal-Bench 4 (TB4) (Merrill et al.,
2026; Terminal-Bench Team, 2026a; Terminal-Bench Team, 2026b),
containerized shell tasks scored by their own tests. _Text
classification_ uses the online classification environment of
Meta-Harness (Lee et al., 2026), from a three-dataset source suite to an
eight-dataset target suite absent from source RSI. _Agentic workspace_
moves from Harvey LAB (Harvey AI, 2026) to the equal-weight mean of
JobBench (Li et al., 2026), GDPval (Patwardhan et al., 2026), and
APEX-Agents (Vidgen et al., 2026), using scoring interfaces adapted from
RRSI (Xia et al., 2026), with Gemini 3.8 Flash as the judge.
_Engineering design_ moves from EngDesign (Guo et al., 2025) to
Frontier-Eng (Chi et al., 2026), scored by frozen verifiers. Tasks are
split uniformly at random, without stratification, into evolve and
held-out sets of approximately equal size; exact counts are in Appendix
B. Only evolve-set scores and trajectories reach the proposer, selector,
and SIM. The main comparisons use five rollouts per task, with every
rollout included in the score.

##### Transitions, models, and budgets.

Cross-dataset CRSI uses Gemini 3.8 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026b) as the
policy in both stages. For the cross-model transfer comparison, source
RSI uses Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic, 2026), then Gemini 3.8 Flash
continues on the same benchmark and split. Both stages use the target
benchmarks listed above: TB4, the eight-dataset classification suite,
the merged workspace benchmarks, and Frontier-Eng. Policy weights remain
frozen. Gemini 3.8 Flash also serves as proposer and selector in the
main comparisons. A source-RSI ablation replaces it with Claude Opus 5.5
in these roles, yielding larger gains from both coding seeds (Appendix
E.4.2, Table 7). The seeds are the minimal AHE seed (Lin et al., 2026)
and Terminus-2 (Merrill et al., 2026) for coding, NoMemory and
FewShotAll (Lee et al., 2026) for text classification, and a ReAct agent
(Yao et al., 2023) over an MCP tool gateway (Vidgen et al., 2026) for
workspace and engineering. For the latter two domains, we adopt RRSI’s
environments, seed harnesses, and evaluation interfaces (Xia et al.,
2026); Appendix C details our experimental setup. Every source and
target run uses $`N=10`$ rounds with $`k=5`$ candidate proposals per
round in the proposer–selector loop described in §3.1. Proposals
rejected before execution receive no evaluation score; the five rollouts
per task specify the separate evaluation allocation.

##### Comparison conditions.

Cold Start begins from the seed harness without SIM. Harness Transfer,
Problem-Only Memory, and Full SIM share the same source-evolved harness,
with no SIM, root causes only, and the complete record, respectively.
These memory conditions persist throughout target optimization, with
matched target tasks, policy, round counts, and candidate budgets
(§3.3).

##### Metrics and reporting.

Scores are on a 0–100 scale to two decimals: pass@1 for coding, accuracy
pooled over examples for text classification, benchmark-specific rubric,
win-rate, and pass@1 scores for workspace, and pass rate or medal score
for engineering (Appendix B). Before and after scores evaluate the
starting harness and the version retained after the final target round
on the held-out set. Except for the dedicated paired study, each
condition is one complete RSI run, and its five rollouts per task
estimate execution performance rather than optimization variance. We
report individual settings descriptively and use the ten-pair coding
study to assess variation across independent source runs (§4.4).

### 4.2 Cross-Dataset CRSI

Figure 2: Cross-dataset CRSI: held-out target scores before (open bars)
and after (filled bars) target RSI. The three transfer conditions share
the source-evolved harness; Cold Start uses the seed. Axis limits vary
by domain. Table 5 lists exact scores.

##### SIM improves final scores in all six dataset transitions.

The memory ablation in Figure 2 compares continued optimization from the
same evolved harness with no SIM, root causes only, or the full record.
Full SIM finishes highest in all six settings, exceeding Harness
Transfer by 2.43–4.35 points and Problem-Only Memory by 0.20–6.06
points. Problem-Only Memory has mixed effects: it ranges from 1.82
points below Harness Transfer to 2.40 above it. Keeping the edit history
and evaluation evidence thus helps beyond retaining the diagnosis alone
(Appendix E.4.1).

The margin over the strongest alternative ranges from 0.20 points on
NoMemory to 4.35 on engineering. Section 4.4 tests whether the benefit
recurs across independently evolved source harnesses. Appendix E.2 lists
the exact starting and final scores for both transfer settings.

##### Source improvement does not ensure a better target start.

Source RSI raises held-out source scores by 2.75–6.41 points in all six
settings (Appendix E.1), yet the FewShotAll harness starts target RSI
below the seed: 72.92 versus 75.72. Harness-only continuation reaches
74.12 and remains below Cold Start at 75.92, while Full SIM reaches
77.92.

On NoMemory, Cold Start gains more than Full SIM (3.40 versus 2.20
points) but finishes lower (72.00 versus 73.60). We therefore report
both endpoints and use Harness Transfer, which shares Full SIM’s
initialization, as the direct control for the memory procedure.

##### SIM improves continuation from the same evolved harness.

We repeat source RSI ten times from Terminus-2 on TB2.1 and initialize
two ten-round TB4 runs from each resulting harness. Within each pair,
the starting harness, policy, benchmark, and round and candidate budgets
match. One run inherits that source run’s SIM and appends target
attempts; the other continues without SIM. The independent source run is
the unit of analysis.

SIM finishes higher in 9 of 10 pairs (Table 3), with one pair favoring
continuation without SIM by 0.61 points. From a shared mean starting
score of 18.36, the mean final score is 21.70 with SIM and 19.33 without
it. The mean paired advantage is 2.36 points (95% CI $`[1.05,3.68]`$;
two-sided paired $`t(9)=4.06`$, $`p=0.003`$). A two-sided signed-rank
check gives $`p=0.006`$; the paired standardized effect is
$`d_{z}=1.28`$ (statistical details in Appendix C.4).

##### SIM guides reuse and revision of inherited edits.

Full SIM and Harness Transfer inherit the same completion gate from
Terminus-2 source RSI (Figure 10b, Appendix F.3). With SIM, the proposer
uses the gate’s rationale and source outcomes to retain completion
verification while adding target checks, including feasibility versus
optimality. Without SIM, the run tightens the gate, twice repairs
completion deadlocks, and misattributes the inherited gate to its own
first round. The requirement carries over; its implementation needs
revision. Appendices F.5 and F.6 summarize transfer patterns across
domains and models and the lessons for SIM.

##### SIM preserves diagnoses while adding target evidence.

P3 records a mismatch between deliverables and the verifier’s interface
contract (Figure 11; Appendix F.4). Target rounds 5 and 6 append revised
checks and measured outcomes under this diagnosis, keeping source
attempts and unresolved predictions available to later rounds.

##### SIM supports earlier target progress and source retention in coding.

Full SIM is best or tied throughout rounds 1–10 for both coding seeds
(Figure 4a,b). It exceeds the strongest control’s final score (20.61) by
round 5 from AHE and round 2 from Terminus-2. In the AHE
source-retention trace, all transfer conditions start at 90.22 on
held-out TB2.1 (Figure 7, Appendix E.3). Full SIM loses 3 successful
rollouts out of 225, versus 4 for Problem-Only Memory and 6 for Harness
Transfer, while gaining more on TB4 than Problem-Only Memory (6.67
versus 0.61 points).

## 5 Conclusion

CRSI continues harness optimization when the benchmark or policy model
changes. SIM carries earlier diagnoses, edits, and outcomes into this
process and records new attempts. From matched starting harnesses, Full
SIM outperforms harness-only continuation in all six cross-dataset
settings and four Opus-to-Gemini domains. The ten-pair coding study
finds a mean advantage of 2.36 points across independent source runs.
The cases show how this history helps the proposer and selector reuse
earlier reasoning while revising fixes for new requirements.

## Limitations

Within our compute budget, independent RSI repetitions focus on coding;
the other domains are evaluated with one run per condition. We study
settings with compatible harness interfaces and keep the optimizer’s
planning and search procedures fixed. Our comparisons evaluate SIM as a
complete procedure, combining inherited memory with updates during
target optimization.

Future work can extend the evaluation to more repeated runs and longer
transition sequences, and explore adaptation of planning, token
allocation, and tool interfaces.

## Ethics Statement

LLM-based optimization agents rewrite harness code in our experiments.
The resulting harnesses run inside the benchmarks’ execution
environments, with policy weights fixed and scores computed by frozen
verifiers or a fixed judging procedure. A self-modifying harness could
satisfy an evaluator without solving the intended task. We keep held-out
feedback away from the optimization agents, identify each benchmark’s
verifier or judge, and include an accepted edit that failed to repair
its target tasks (Appendix F.2). Workspace scores may also reflect
biases of the LLM judge. The experiments use existing publicly available
benchmarks and datasets.

## Appendix A Extended Related Work

This appendix expands §2 around three questions: how harnesses are
improved, how optimization continues after a change of setting, and what
role memory plays in that process.

### A.1 How Are Agent Harnesses Improved?

Harness RSI optimizes prompts, tools, control flow, memory, and context
management around a policy model. GEPA uses trajectory reflection and
recombination to optimize prompts (Agrawal et al., 2026). Meta-Harness
exposes prior implementations, execution traces, and scores through a
shared filesystem (Lee et al., 2026). AHE makes harness components and
modification outcomes observable, checking edit-level predictions
against subsequent task outcomes (Lin et al., 2026). In ML development,
MLToolBench learns diagnostic tool use through supervised and
reinforcement learning (Yu et al., 2026c).

Other methods change how improvement itself is performed. Self-Harness
combines weakness mining, harness proposals, and regression-based
validation (Zhang et al., 2026a). DGM and Hyperagents explore
self-modification and open-ended evolution of agent programs (Zhang et
al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026c); Hyperagents also reports transfer of
meta-level improvements across domains. Harness-R1 learns a
harness-editing policy from failure trajectories using reinforcement
learning (Shao et al., 2026), while SIA updates both the harness and the
task model’s weights (Hebbar et al., 2026). Bai et al. (2026) train
idea-level critics to screen ML modifications before costly empirical
verification. MLToolBench learns task-agent behavior, while the critics
learn candidate assessment; SIM preserves edit histories for the
proposer and selector. CRSI defines continuation after a benchmark or
policy change for compatible proposal–evaluation–selection loops (§3.1).

### A.2 How Does Optimization Continue after Transfer?

##### Transfer of evolved harnesses.

AHE evaluates transfer from Terminal-Bench to SWE-bench Verified without
re-evolving the harness (Lin et al., 2026); Meta-Harness and PluginRSI
report transfer across solver models (Lee et al., 2026; Shi et al.,
2026). Several methods aim to improve this transfer. ModularRSI uses
benchmark-disjoint evolution data, contrasts successful and failed
trajectories, and directs edits toward recurring deficiencies in
functional modules (Wu et al., 2026). RRSI constrains bundled
modifications and redirects stalled search toward underexplored
components (Xia et al., 2026). Harmony Harness separates general
mechanisms from benchmark-specific adaptations (Yu et al., 2026a). The
interaction between a model and its harness can nevertheless require
model-specific adaptation (Yang et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026d).

##### Continued optimization.

Prior work also reuses improvement experience during further
optimization. PluginRSI reuses an evolved plugin library when optimizing
from the initial harness (Shi et al., 2026). Xu (2026) continues
multi-task harness evolution on a new benchmark with free-text evolution
notes and source-task replay. Harness² retains adopted harnesses and
refinement lessons in a library and tests its reuse across benchmarks
(Xu et al., 2026a). Related settings include task streams and continuous
episodes (Karten et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026).
Adaptive Auto-Harness separates a persistent evolution workspace from
deployable harnesses, with optional routing among stored branches (Liu
et al., 2026). EvoHarnessBench studies retention and adaptation as
externally supplied tools, skills, and specialist agents expand (Ke et
al., 2026).

##### Evaluating continuation.

Held-out evaluation and baselines with matched feedback and inference
budgets help distinguish improvements from additional optimization
resources (Wang et al., 2026). SEAGym tracks validation, transfer,
replay, and cost throughout evolution (Zheng et al., 2026). Studies of
shifting task streams and coding benchmarks also document interference
and forgetting (Chen et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). We report the
evolved harness’s score before and after target RSI, compare memory
conditions from the same starting harness with matched round and
candidate budgets, and separately track source retention in one coding
transition (Appendix E.3).

### A.3 What Does Memory Store, and Who Uses It?

##### Memory for harness optimization.

Keeping improvement history has several precedents. Meta-Harness retains
candidate artifacts (Lee et al., 2026); RRSI records hypotheses, diffs,
score and cost changes, and acceptance decisions, including rejected
edits (Xia et al., 2026). The Causal Improvement Graph links evidence,
hypotheses, interventions, and outcomes (Zhang et al., 2026d). SkillHone
preserves diagnoses, revisions, and evaluation evidence (Li and Hu,
2026), and Turbo Harness summarizes completed optimization into a
playbook for instance-specific edits (Zhang et al., 2026f). HarnessBank
groups candidates by modified component and diagnosed failure, retaining
high-performing harnesses for recombination (Luo et al., 2026).
Organizing improvement around problems therefore also has precedent.
Meta-Skill freezes learned support-design principles to construct a
harness for each test task (Qian et al., 2026). Meta² freezes reusable
evolution guidance for edits during task execution, resetting the
harness between task runs (Yuan et al., 2026).

##### Memory for task solving.

Reflexion stores verbal reflections to guide later attempts (Shinn et
al., 2023), and Voyager maintains a growing library of executable skills
(Wang et al., 2024). ExpeL extracts reusable insights from task
experience (Zhao et al., 2024); ReasoningBank and ACE retain strategies
and lessons (Ouyang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026e). ACE
incrementally extends, refines, and deduplicates its playbook from
execution trajectories. Memory Transfer Learning compares
task-experience representations across coding domains and models,
finding abstract insights more transferable than concrete traces in its
studied settings (Kim et al., 2026). These memories support the agent
executing tasks.

##### SIM in continual harness optimization.

SIM supplies the proposer and selector with a record organized by
harness problem, linking selected modifications to their rationale,
predicted and observed outcomes, and evaluation context (§3.2). The
record accompanies the evolved harness across a benchmark or model
transition and gains new attempts during target RSI. Our comparisons
with no SIM and root causes only test this complete procedure, combining
inherited history with target-side updates (Appendix E.4.1).

### B.2 How Are the Splits Used?

The tasks (examples for text classification) in Table 4 are split
uniformly at random into approximately equal evolve and held-out halves,
without stratification by category, difficulty, or source dataset. When
a benchmark holds an odd number of tasks, the evolve half takes the even
count. The two sets are disjoint, and no task or rollout is removed from
the scoring denominator after the split.

Only evolve-set scores and execution trajectories are available for
proposals, selection, SIM updates, and stopping decisions. The
task-solving policy executes the harness on held-out tasks to measure
starting, final, and per-round diagnostic scores (§4.4). These held-out
scores and trajectories are not passed to the harness-optimization
agents.

Within these primary comparisons and the paired coding study, the
scoring allocation is five rollouts per task on both splits, matched
across conditions; the overlapping Frontier-Eng component contributes
zero under the rule in Appendix B.6. The reported harness versions,
independent-run counts, and statistical comparisons are specified in
Appendix C.4.

### B.3 Coding: Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 4

##### Environment and metric.

Terminal-Bench tasks run in containers with a task description, shell
access, and task-specific tests. We report pass@1, estimated from the
binary outcome of each task rollout. For $`n`$ tasks with five rollouts
each, let $`y_{ir}\in\{0,1\}`$ indicate whether rollout $`r`$ of task
$`i`$ passes the task verifier. The percentage score is

```math
\widehat{\mathrm{pass@1}}=\frac{100}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{5}\sum_{r=1}^{5}y_{ir}. \tag{4}
```

##### Failed rollouts.

A rollout that fails for infrastructure reasons stays in the denominator
and scores zero.

### B.4 Text Classification

The online classification environment follows Meta-Harness: the harness
controls example storage, retrieval, and prediction context. The example
counts in Table 4 are totals across each suite, not allocations for
individual datasets.

##### Example memory during evaluation.

The task agent reads only labeled examples supplied in advance from
training/evolve data. Held-out labels are used for scoring and are not
fed back into its example memory. For a fixed harness, each of the five
evaluation repetitions starts from the same initial example-memory
state. This memory supplies prediction context to the task agent and is
separate from SIM, which supports harness optimization.

##### Accuracy across examples.

We compute accuracy over all examples in a split, pooling the three
source datasets or the eight target datasets. For split
$`q\in\{\mathrm{evo},\mathrm{eval}\}`$, let $`n_{q}`$ be the total
number of examples and $`y_{ir}(H;q)\in\{0,1\}`$ indicate whether
harness $`H`$ predicts the correct label for example $`i`$ in rollout
$`r`$. The percentage accuracy is

```math
A_{\mathrm{cls}}(H;q)=\frac{100}{5n_{q}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{q}}\sum_{r=1}^{5}y_{ir}(H;q). \tag{5}
```

Each example has equal weight, so a dataset contributes in proportion to
its example count. The same aggregation is used on the evolve and
evaluation splits.

### B.5 Agentic Workspace Tasks

##### Task scoring and judging.

We adapt the benchmark-specific scoring interfaces described in RRSI
(Xia et al., 2026, Appendix B.3–B.6). Our runs use Gemini 3.8 Flash as
the judge for all workspace benchmarks; this differs from RRSI’s mixed
judge configurations and GDPval voting panel. Harvey LAB reports the
fraction of rubric criteria satisfied, and JobBench uses its weighted
rubric score. GDPval compares each generated deliverable with the
benchmark’s expert deliverable and reports the win rate against that
reference. APEX-Agents reports pass@1, with success determined by
satisfaction of the task’s rubric on a single rollout; the repeated
rollouts estimate this success probability. The judging interface and
model are fixed across comparison conditions within each benchmark. We
express each benchmark score on a 0–100 scale before taking the
equal-weight mean.

##### Equal-weight benchmark aggregation.

For split $`q\in\{\mathrm{evo},\mathrm{eval}\}`$, let $`S_{b}(H;q)`$ be
the benchmark-specific percentage score of harness $`H`$ on benchmark
$`b`$. The workspace score is

```math
S_{\mathrm{workspace}}(H;q)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}S_{b}(H;q), \tag{6}
```

where $`\mathcal{B}`$ contains JobBench, GDPval, and APEX-Agents. Each
benchmark contributes one third, independent of its number of tasks or
rubric criteria. The evolve-set performance objective used to select
target harnesses and the reported evaluation aggregate use this same
weighting. Within each benchmark, scoring retains that benchmark’s own
evaluation convention; averaging occurs after computing its score. The
workspace row of Table 5 reports this aggregate.

### B.6 Engineering Design

Frontier-Eng has no MCP gateway of its own and uses the runner and
gateway of EngDesign. EngDesign uses a binary verifier pass;
Frontier-Eng uses a medal score against frozen thresholds, with gold,
silver, and bronze mapped to $`1.00`$, $`0.67`$, and $`0.33`$. Neither
engineering metric uses an LLM judge.

##### Source overlap.

Frontier-Eng includes an EngDesign-derived component containing seven
sub-problems (Chi et al., 2026). Following RRSI, we assign zero credit
to this overlapping component while retaining it in the original
denominator, including after the 24/23 split.

This section specifies the models, seed harnesses, comparison

### C.1 Which Models and Budgets Are Used?

##### Task-solving policy.

Gemini 3.8 Flash is the policy in both stages of cross-dataset CRSI. The
cross-family comparison uses Claude Opus 5.5 as the source policy and
Gemini 3.8 Flash as the target policy. Policy weights remain fixed.

##### Harness-optimization agents.

The proposer and selector use Gemini 3.8 Flash, including when Opus
executes the tasks. The ablation in Appendix E.4.2 replaces the proposer
and selector with Opus during source RSI.

##### Optimization budget.

Each RSI run uses $`N=10`$ rounds with $`k=5`$ candidate proposals per
round. Candidate proposals and task rollouts are separate budgets: the
main comparisons use five rollouts per task on each split (Appendix B).
Proposals screened out before execution receive no rollout scores.

##### Cost per round.

A coding round takes about 4 h, including 1.7 h for evaluation and
34 min for candidate sampling. We match rounds and candidate counts
across methods; token use and runtime can differ.

### C.2 Which Harnesses Do We Start From?

We use publicly available seed harnesses from prior work. The two seeds
in coding and text classification test different starting harnesses;
repeated optimization runs are described in Appendix C.4.

##### Coding.

The AHE seed starts with a basic shell tool and allows edits to prompts,
tools, middleware, skills, sub-agents, and memory (Lin et al., 2026).
Terminus-2 is distributed with Harbor and is also used by RRSI (Merrill
et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026).

##### Text classification.

NoMemory stores no training examples; FewShotAll stores the observed
labeled examples and includes them as few-shot context (Lee et al.,
2026).

##### Workspace and engineering.

We use RRSI’s ReAct toolbelt agent for file manipulation, PDF reading,
and code execution (Yao et al., 2023; Vidgen et al., 2026; Xia et al.,
2026). Its 6 files (1,743 lines) are shared by the source and all three
targets, allowing the Harvey LAB harness to run on the targets without
modification. Engineering uses the same agent with a bare system prompt
and task wrapper, evaluated by fixed task-specific verifiers (Appendix
B.6). This seed has 7 files (1,857 lines), with prompts in a separate
module. For these two domains, we adopt RRSI’s environments, seed
harnesses, and evaluation interfaces. Our models and budgets are
specified above; our splits, rollout counts, and workspace judge are
specified in Appendix B.

### C.4 How Are Runs Reported and Repeated?

##### Reported harnesses.

Each round retains one harness for the next round; $`H_{S}`$ is the
final retained source harness. The _before_ score evaluates the harness
used to initialize target RSI, the _after_ score evaluates the version
retained at the end of the final target round, and the target
improvement is their difference. Curves show each retained version’s
measured score, without taking a running maximum over earlier scores.

##### Independent runs.

Repeated task rollouts and independent optimization runs are distinct
sources of variation. Each main cross-dataset and Opus-to-Gemini
condition uses one complete RSI run. The paired coding study repeats
source RSI ten times from Terminus-2; each evolved harness initializes
two target continuations, with and without SIM (§4.4, Table 3). Task
rollouts estimate execution performance. The ten independent source runs
support across-run intervals for the paired study; scores from
single-run conditions are reported descriptively. For the paired study,
the paired $`t`$ interval and two-sided test use the ten final-score
differences; the signed-rank check uses average ranks for ties and
enumerates the $`2^{10}`$ sign assignments.

### C.6 How Is a Shared Harness Tested?

We test a harness shared by Grok 4.20 Reasoning, Claude Opus 5.5, and
Gemini 3.8 Flash on the text-classification target suite (Table 2). The
evolve and held-out sets each contain 500 examples, with five rollouts
per example (2,500 rollouts per split).

##### Harness construction.

We compare two baselines and three ways to build a shared harness:

- •
  B1: Per-model harnesses. Each policy model runs its own RSI and is
  evaluated with its own evolved harness.
- •
  B2: Shared seed. All three models use the same unevolved harness.
- •
  S1: Sequential CRSI. The order is Grok 4.20 Reasoning, Gemini 3.8
  Flash, then Claude Opus 5.5, from weaker to stronger policies in this

- •
  S2: Unified synthesis. Each round pools trajectory feedback from all
  three models to propose updates to one shared harness.
- •
  S3: Single-agent transfer. We evolve a harness separately with each of

All averages over models use equal weights. B1 averages the three

Each procedure is run once, and optimization cost is not compared. The
results in §4.3.3 describe the observed means; small differences do not
establish statistical equivalence or a ranking for each model.

## Appendix D SIM Implementation

We supplement §3 with entry-matching details, prompt guidance, and the
stored record format.

### D.1 How Are Entries Matched?

For the update in §3.2, the proposer itself decides whether a diagnosis
matches an existing root cause; no separate similarity model is used.
Its choice is saved in the round’s change manifest before evaluation.
Entries are merged deterministically when their root-cause text is
identical, preserving all attempts.

### D.2 How Do the Proposer and Selector Use SIM?

The prompt guidance for §3.3 is summarized below:

1.  1.  Treat entries as prior experience, with flexibility to choose a
        different edit.
2.  2.  Check the domain, benchmark, and model behind each entry.
3.  3.  Start with an existing diagnosis when it explains the current
        failure.
4.  4.  Reuse the rationale and reassess the edit after a benchmark or model
        change.
5.  5.  Revise, replace, or override a harness modification when new
        evidence warrants it.
6.  6.  Revisit a tested direction only when new evidence justifies it.
7.  7.  Create a new problem entry when existing diagnoses do not explain
        the failure.
8.  8.  Give task-specific evidence more weight in similar settings and less
        when both benchmark and model change.

### D.3 What Does a SIM Record Contain?

Each run stores SIM in one JSON file, with the following schematic
structure:

{ "next_id": 10,

"carried_from": { "arm": \<source run\>, "domain": "coding_enriched",

"into": \<target run\> },

"aliases": { "P6": "P5" }, // P6 was merged into P5

"problems": \[ ...entries P1 P2 P3 P4 P5 P7 P8 P9... \] }

Each problem entry contains {id, name, opened_round, attempts\[\]}. name
gives the root-cause hypothesis, and opened_round records its first
diagnosis. Each attempt contains: {round, sample, accepted, backfilled,
what, files, predicted, fixed, missed, passed, total, domain}. what and
files describe the edit, and domain identifies its setting.

predicted lists the tasks expected to be fixed before evaluation; fixed
and missed record the outcomes afterward. passed/total gives the
successful and total trials in that attempt’s evolve-set evaluation.
accepted means the selector chose the edit in that round, even if some
predicted fixes failed. The edit may later be changed or replaced while
its record stays in SIM, as in the P8 case in Appendix F.2. Predictions
for the same task in different rounds are separate observations.

carried_from identifies the source and target runs; aliases keeps the
identifiers of merged entries (for example, P6$`\rightarrow`$P5). Read
each entry’s ID and opened_round within its recorded run context.
Appendix F gives records as stored, including P3 in Figure 11.

#### F.1.1 adaptive-rejection-sampler: unreliable command transport

Task. Implement adaptive rejection sampling in R as /app/ars.R, with
input validation and tests.

Root cause ($`c`$). The agent wrote the whole program in a single shell
command of about 22.8 KB, exceeding the command-transport limit reported
in this run (about 16 KB). The command never ran, so the file was never
created and the verifier failed with No ars.R file found.

Modification ($`a`$). The transport behind the existing
run_shell_command tool now splits literal input into chunks of at most
2,048 characters, sends special keys such as Enter as separate key
events, and raises an error when a send fails instead of failing
silently (simplified):

MAX_KEY_CHUNK_SIZE = 2048

def \_tmux_send_keys(self, keys):

cmds = \[\]

for key in keys:

if key in SPECIAL_KEYS: \# key event

cmds.append(f"{prefix} {quote(key)}")

continue

for chunk in self.\_split_key(key):

cmds.append(f"{prefix} {quote(chunk)}")

Outcome ($`o`$). Successful rollouts increased from 1 to 5 out of 5 on

#### F.1.2 gpt2-codegolf: fragile tool-call parsing

Task. Write a compact GPT-2 inference program in C (/app/gpt2.c) that
loads the weights, decodes greedily, and compiles with gcc -O3 -lm under
a code-size limit.

Root cause ($`c`$). Terminus-2 expects a JSON response whose keystrokes
fields hold shell input. When the model wrote C code containing
unescaped quotation marks into that field, for example

"keystrokes": "...FILE \*f = fopen("model.bin", "rb");..."

json.loads failed and the command was rejected. The parser could already
add missing closing braces and strip surrounding text, but not repair
quotes inside a field.

Modification ($`a`$). A repair step, run only after normal parsing
fails, locates the real closing quote of each string field as the one
followed by the next expected key (for keystrokes, "duration" or the
closing brace) and escapes every quote before it.

Outcome ($`o`$). The change adds a recovery path for malformed calls; no
post-edit task success rate is reported for this parser repair. Its
dependence on the model’s output format is discussed in §F.5.1. The P3
record in §F.4 concerns a separate prompt intervention.

### F.2 How Does CRSI Adapt an Evolved Harness?

#### F.2.1 Source: Execution-Verified Completion

##### Task and evaluation interface.

_“Configure a git server so that from my own machine I can git clone
user@server:/git/server, commit hello.html, push, and then curl
http://server:8080/hello.html shows ‘hello world’.”_ The agent must
build four parts that compose: a bare repository at /git/server, a
post-receive hook that deploys to the web root, an SSH-reachable account
named user, and an HTTP service on port 8080. The verifier runs outside
the container and clones as user over SSH, so a setup that works locally
is not evidence that the externally visible interface is correct.

##### Observed failure and diagnosis.

In the described failing rollout, the agent configured the repository,
hook, and web server, read _“I will set up login myself”_ as meaning the
account would be provisioned externally, and never created user. It
tested the flow with the pre-existing ubuntu account, saw every local
command succeed, and declared task_complete: true. SSH then refused the
nonexistent user, the push failed, and the page returned 404. The seed
harness could not catch this, because its completion check only asks the
model to repeat its claim (simplified from terminus_2.py):

if is_task_complete:

if self.\_pending_completion: \# second claim

return episode + 1 \# terminate

self.\_pending_completion = True \# first: ask again

obs = self.\_confirmation_message(out)

and the confirmation message asks about intent, not evidence: _“Are you
sure you want to mark the task as complete? … If so, include
"task_complete": true in your JSON response again.”_ Root cause: ending
the episode is an agent privilege that requires no execution evidence.

##### Harness intervention.

The intervention introduces completion-control middleware in the
harness. Source RSI built it over three accepted rounds:

| Round | Edit                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ----- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| r6    | New execution_gating_interlock.py (137 lines), wired into terminus_2.py: a cross-turn state machine that refuses completion when the previous turn had a parse error or no command actually executed, capped at three rejections. |
| r8    | Also intercepts simulated multi-turn text, truncated output with discarded command blocks, and completion after at most one real command (adds terminus_json_plain_parser.py).                                                    |
| r10   | Detects terminal crashes, assertion failures, HTTP 404s, and test errors at the moment completion is requested (adds both prompt templates).                                                                                      |

The interlock tracks execution across turns and uses four rejection
paths (simplified):

ran = any(c.keystrokes.strip() for c in cmds)

return self.\_reject(...)

##### Task and evaluation interface.

write the waypoint paths to /app/routing_result_1.json. Each path must

bends of radius $`50\,\mu`$m and valid s-bends, stay inside the board,
avoid obstacle clearance regions and self-intersections, and keep a
minimum separation between nets. The score is the negative weighted
route cost summed over all nets. The task ships its own feasibility
checker, check_routing.py, which answers whether the constraints are
satisfied, while the reward is a cost function. A solution can pass
every check and still score poorly.

The evolved harness contains the source interlock, intact and wired. On

commands ran, no parse error occurred, nothing was discarded, and no

#### F.5.1 Coding

##### Source RSI revises execution, parsing, and completion.

The evolved tool layer retains stdout and stderr after timeouts, checks
background processes and their exit codes, recovers misplaced artifacts,
and sends long commands through a paste buffer (§F.1). Terminus-2’s
parser attempts to repair malformed JSON before rejecting it and detects
safety refusals. Completion control requires evidence before accepting a
completion claim: AHE’s middleware forces continuation when the agent
stops without a tool call, and Terminus-2’s cross-turn interlock blocks
completion after unexecuted commands or errors (Appendix F.2).

##### Core execution handling is retained on TB4.

The command-execution routines carry over with the shared container and
terminal interfaces, while session checks adapt as described below. The
recorded cold-start run does not rediscover the tool-layer changes
within eight rounds; after ten rounds it has a larger parser without
outperforming the inherited harness.

##### Paste-buffer checks adapt to TB4.

TB4 exposes failures absent from TB2.1: special key sequences must be
normalized for interrupts to take effect, and foreground processes must
be identified so that a running command is not classified as a stalled
session. The paste-buffer structure remains, with checks adjusted to
these target failures.

##### Completion checks add target criteria.

Both inherited harnesses keep completion control while revising the
evidence required on TB4. Appendix F.3 follows the selected edits;
Appendix F.2 gives the task evidence and the P5/P8 records. Source
predicates that no longer fire are pruned rather than re-tuned. Both
branches also add a reminder triggered by the remaining step budget in
their first round, a target-specific mechanism that the source did not
need.

##### Refusal handling remains inactive.

Refusal handling is carried forward but has no triggering tasks on TB4.
Its presence alone therefore does not establish a transfer benefit or a
loss.

##### Parser repairs depend on the policy model.

The Opus- and Gemini-evolved harnesses have near-identical terminal and
process structures, which are retained after transfer. Parser repairs
instead address model-specific output errors: unescaped control
characters, illegal escapes, and truncated responses. Each repair needs
checking against the target model’s outputs. Completion control likewise
retains counts of executed commands, discarded blocks, and consecutive
rejections, while recalibrating checks for simulated execution and
premature completion. The target run also adds format constraints and
step-by-step guidance absent from source RSI.

#### F.5.2 Text Classification

##### Source RSI builds structured retrieval.

Memory writing moves from no retention (NoMemory) or unstructured
storage (FewShotAll) to retrievable indices: multi-granularity BM25 over
words, character $`n`$-grams, and chemical-formula $`n`$-grams, later
extended with class-based candidate pools, hard-negative error indices,
and class-prototype centrality. Prompt assembly bounds retrieval to the
top eight examples with positive BM25 scores, strips benchmark
boilerplate from queries before retrieval, and selects contrastive,
class-balanced exemplars. Post-processing normalizes data-specific
formats, such as SMILES strings for USPTO-50k, because open-vocabulary
generation requires exact matches.

##### Retrieval survives with adjusted thresholds.

The target suite changes the input types and label spaces. The BM25
index and retriever classes remain intact, while retrieval constants are
recalibrated and query sanitization gains delimiters for the new
datasets. The routing structure is retained, with rubrics and exemplars
rewritten for the target. With SIM, target RSI adds smoothed class
quotas and contrastive ordering; class balancing applies to the target’s
finite label sets rather than the source’s open-vocabulary outputs.

##### SMILES normalization stays inactive.

SMILES normalization used in source RSI remains inactive on the target
suite, whose outputs no longer use that format. The cold-start run
independently develops relevance-gated retrieval and bounded context.

##### Indexing transfers while output rules change.

Three of four runs evolve BM25 indices from different starting
harnesses, using features suited to the input, such as chemical
$`n`$-grams for SMILES and character bigrams for Chinese legal text.
Indexing and query sanitization depend on the input data and carry over
across models; retrieval budgets such as top-$`k`$ are re-tuned. Output
rules depend more on policy behavior: the answer-first schema addresses
Gemini’s truncated reasoning. Target RSI also rewrites inherited rubrics
more extensively on Banking77 and GoEmotions than on SciTail and
TweetEval-Hate.

#### F.5.3 Agentic Workspace

##### Source RSI adds output handling and audits.

Over-long tool outputs are preserved through head-and-tail truncation,
with quotas raised three- and five-fold, respectively. A one-time audit
gate before final_answer can append a tool message and return the policy
to the loop. A deliverable auditor supplies the gate with file names and
sizes from candidate output directories.

##### The termination gate survives a rubric change.

Source RSI and a cold-start target run independently add a tool message
before the same early-return branch, with different predicates and
messages. The target gate fires only when no deliverable is registered,
files were written during code execution, and the gate has not fired
before; the second condition keeps purely conversational tasks from
being intercepted. This insertion point depends on the ReAct loop and
remains usable when the evaluation rubric changes.

##### Registration checks replace file-system probes.

The source probe samples the file system, whereas target failures come
from invocation history: files created on disk but never registered
through write_file. Target checks therefore use tool-invocation history
rather than file-system probes. The target escalates this check in three
single-shot tiers: an advisory notice when file saving is detected, a
mandatory instruction once 75% of the step budget passes without
registration, and a conditional block at final_answer.

##### Registration checks depend on the evaluator.

The registration check assumes that the evaluator credits files
registered through write_file; it would need reconsideration for an
evaluator that reads output directories directly. Its substring matches
over tool arguments (such as /tmp/, .docx, or savefig) also depend on
argument formatting.

##### Prompt infrastructure transfers across models.

The Opus source run adds editable system-prompt guidelines and a
six-level severity taxonomy with counterexamples, leaving control flow
and tool execution unchanged. The injection mechanism uses message
structures, while the taxonomy describes the unchanged evaluator.
Gemini’s cold-start run takes a different direction: a notice at 80% of
the step budget asks it to fill pending fields so that a deliverable
exists before final_answer; seven later rounds add no improvement in
this trace. The timing and wording of such interventions depend on the
policy’s behavior.

#### F.5.4 Engineering Design

##### Source RSI adds design and verification guidance.

The source run adds prompt guidance on analytical calculations,
self-contained deliverables, tool-failure recovery, and re-reading
written files before completion. It also imports a syntax parser and a
tool registry without calling them during source RSI.

##### Target RSI expands checks and context budgets.

The tool-registry import is unused in the source harness but becomes the
entry point that the Harness Transfer target run uses to enforce
centralized output truncation at the registry call. Three target runs
independently raise the shared context constants: output-token caps,
head and tail lengths, and summarization triggers. The Full SIM target
run adapts the source’s one-time post-generation check into a checkpoint
protocol that writes intermediate artifacts exceeding the baseline score
to disk, protecting them from unannounced timeouts. The cold-start
target run’s retained edits are limited to prompt changes:
reconstructing the objective from the evaluation script, embedding
self-tuning optimization code in deliverables, and clamping outputs with
a fallback to the baseline implementation.

##### SIM retains the source guidelines in this trace.

The Harness Transfer target run inherits the source guidelines but
reverts them to the seed prompt in its first round, whereas the Full SIM
target run retains and extends them throughout. This pair retains
similar code structures but differs in whether the inherited guidance is
preserved.

##### Deliverable checks survive model changes.

A deliverable tracker that records deliverable state independently of
model output transfers without refactoring, and a check that blocks
final_answer when a deliverable was modified but not re-executed
transfers unchanged. Budget prompts emerge as wall-clock triggers in an
Opus run (every 600 s, at most three times) and step-budget ratios in
Gemini runs (0.8 and 0.75). Both use the gate before final_answer, with
checks on different state variables. The recorded zero-score rates
differ between Opus (0.09%) and Gemini (4.49%); intervention thresholds
therefore need checking for each policy. Handlers for a missing sandbox
binary remain idle where the environment is fully provisioned.

##### Interfaces explain much of the observed reuse.

Across these cases, benchmark changes alter the evidence needed for
completion, while model changes alter how the harness handles policy
failures. With the benchmark fixed, a model change leaves the success
criterion intact but can require different detection and guidance.
Stable interfaces favor reuse; output repairs and intervention
thresholds need checking against the target setting.

### F.6 What Do the Cases Tell Us about SIM?

The cases suggest four roles for SIM in continuing harness optimization.

##### Earlier rationale informs what to retain.

In engineering, the SIM run retains and extends inherited guidance,
while harness-only continuation reverts it (§F.5.4). Recording why an
edit was made gives the optimizer a basis for assessing its continued
relevance, beyond its presence in the inherited harness.

##### A transferable rationale can require a different implementation.

The coding runs inherit the same completion gate, but the SIM run
extends it to check target-specific requirements (§F.3). The need for
completion evidence carries over; what counts as sufficient evidence
changes. This illustrates how earlier reasoning can guide adaptation
without treating the source implementation as fixed.

##### New outcomes add evidence to an existing diagnosis.

P3 links a source diagnosis to further target interventions and their
outcomes (§F.4). Keeping these attempts together lets later rounds
inspect which remedies were tried, under which settings, and which
predictions remain unresolved. The reusable experience includes the
evidence behind a diagnosis, not only its name.

##### Experience remains available after an edit is replaced.

P8 retains the selected blocking attempt and its unresolved predictions
after later edits change the behavior (§F.2). The final harness alone
would omit this part of the search history. SIM keeps the attempt, its
diagnosis, and its unresolved outcome together as the harness changes.

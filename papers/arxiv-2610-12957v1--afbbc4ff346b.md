# JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents

Gangyi Li    LuYao Wang    Jingfu Sun    Jingyi Yuan    Hang Liu    Hao
Zong    Yujia Hu    Tingting Gao    Jianyong Wang    Xu Yang
Affiliation: JD.COM

###### Abstract

Embodied agents must translate multimodal observations and user requests
into executable actions while adapting to product constraints, changing
goals, and execution feedback. We present JoyInside-VLM-Agent, a
ReAct-style multimodal agent developed through product-conditioned
trajectory construction and staged policy learning for robotic platforms
and smart hardware. A closed-loop sandbox generates multimodal
interaction trajectories covering goal revisions and failure recovery,
which are checked against execution outcomes and semantic criteria.
Supervised fine-tuning and multi-teacher on-policy distillation precede
reinforcement learning with DPER (Dual-Source Privileged On-Policy
Self-Distillation with Token-Level Evidence Routing). DPER routes
token-level evidence between a frozen privileged teacher and a
privileged student sharing the rollout policy’s parameters, modulating
reward-derived token advantages while preserving their signs. Training
rewards combine generative process evaluation, multi-judge rankings, and
task-queue consistency. JoyInside-VLM-Agent achieves a BFCL v4 overall
score of 77.44, exceeding the evaluated baselines; results on the other
public benchmarks are mixed. On JoyInside-Devices and
JoyInside-Robotics, the evaluated system, which includes domain-specific
skill refinement, leads the baselines on all reported metrics. Two
real-world quadruped case studies illustrate visually grounded search,
replanning after execution failure, and explicit failure reporting.

## 1 Introduction

Embodied agents deployed on robotic platforms and smart hardware must
translate multimodal observations and user instructions into executable
behavior. Across products ranging from bipedal, quadruped, and wheeled
robots to AI-enabled plush toys, the appropriate response to a given
request depends on the agent’s role, capabilities, and operating
constraints. Changes in user goals, new visual evidence, and execution
failures can further invalidate earlier plans. We study how to train a
shared multimodal policy that adapts to product specifications and
revises its decisions as interactions unfold. Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
presents the training framework and deployment examples.

![Refer to caption](2610.12957v1/fig1.png)

(center), and dialogue (right). Bottom: Product-conditioned data

Multimodal training has demonstrated positive transfer across tasks and
robotic embodiments \[[1](#bib.bib4), [2](#bib.bib5)\], while
context-aware systems use user intent and environmental feedback to
guide interaction \[[3](#bib.bib1), [4](#bib.bib3)\]. However, reported
failures include actions beyond device capabilities and descriptions
that no longer match the user’s current scene or intent
\[[3](#bib.bib1), [4](#bib.bib3)\]. These observations motivate training
policies that combine transferable capabilities with adherence to
product constraints and sustained alignment with the user’s current
goal.

This objective imposes two requirements on training. The data must cover
diverse product configurations, goal revisions, and failure recovery
while preserving the correspondence between actions and observations.
The learning signal must reflect reasoning quality, compliance with
product constraints, and progress toward the current goal.
Trajectory-level rewards summarize these criteria but do not specify how
to distribute credit across tokens. Using additional evidence during
training therefore requires selecting an appropriate source for each
token and controlling how that evidence affects policy updates.

We present JoyInside-VLM-Agent, a multimodal agent developed through
product-conditioned data construction and staged policy learning. Our
pipeline combines product roles, workflows, and constraints with
available tools and skills to build executable environments. A
closed-loop sandbox generates multimodal interaction trajectories, which
undergo execution checks and semantic validation. Multi-source
supervised fine-tuning establishes behavior consistent with product
specifications, while multi-expert on-policy distillation integrates
complementary language and visual capabilities. Reinforcement learning
then refines the policy using interaction-specific rewards.

To guide reinforcement learning, we combine process-quality scores from
a Generative Process Reward Model (GPRM), multi-judge rankings of the
same candidate trajectories, and task-queue assessments of goal
alignment and cross-turn continuity. For token-level policy
optimization, we introduce DPER (Dual-Source Privileged On-Policy
Self-Distillation with Token-Level Evidence Routing), building on RLSD’s
sign-preserving advantage modulation \[[5](#bib.bib24)\] and DOPD’s
dual-source routing \[[6](#bib.bib34)\]. A frozen privileged teacher and
a privileged student sharing the rollout policy’s parameters score
tokens along the same student-generated trajectory. DPER selects
evidence based on their disagreement and support for each sampled token.
The selected evidence rescales token-level advantages while preserving
their reward-derived signs. Privileged information is used only during
training.

We evaluate JoyInside-VLM-Agent on public tool-use benchmarks and the
proprietary JoyInside-Devices and JoyInside-Robotics datasets. The model
achieves a BFCL v4 overall score of 77.44, outperforming the evaluated
baselines. Case studies on a physical quadruped robot provide
qualitative evidence of visually grounded search, replanning in response
to execution feedback, and honest failure reporting.

Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  Product-conditioned trajectory construction. We compose executable
  product configurations and generate verified multimodal trajectories
  covering product variation, goal revisions, and failure recovery.
- •
  DPER for policy optimization. We route token-level evidence between
  two privileged sources to scale token advantages while preserving
  their reward-derived signs.
- •
  Multi-source interaction rewards. We combine process evaluation,
  multi-model rankings, and task-queue consistency into a learning
  signal for reasoning quality, adherence to product constraints, and
  alignment with evolving goals.
- •
  JoyInside-VLM-Agent and its evaluation. We integrate these components
  into a unified training framework and assess the resulting model
  through public benchmarks and proprietary evaluation sets and
  qualitative quadruped case studies.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Embodied and Context-Aware Interaction

PaLM-E learns shared representations for robotic and vision-language
tasks \[[1](#bib.bib4)\], while Open X-Embodiment studies cross-platform
transfer using pooled interaction data \[[2](#bib.bib5)\]. Context-aware
assistants address how observations and user intent guide behavior:
Sasha grounds smart-home requests in available devices and user feedback
\[[3](#bib.bib1)\]; GazePointAR combines gaze, pointing, and dialogue
history to resolve references \[[7](#bib.bib2)\]; and WorldScribe adapts
visual descriptions to intent and environmental changes
\[[4](#bib.bib3)\]. Together, these lines connect shared capabilities
with decisions grounded in the current interaction.

Interactive benchmarks further make behavioral constraints explicit.
$`\tau`$-bench evaluates conversations under domain policies
\[[8](#bib.bib6)\], while $`\tau^{2}`$-Bench considers user–agent
coordination in a shared environment \[[9](#bib.bib7)\]. WildToolBench
covers intentions distributed across turns and transitions among tool
use, conversation, and clarification \[[10](#bib.bib11)\]. These
settings motivate evaluating whether behavior respects constraints and
follows the current goal. Our work addresses the corresponding training
problem across robotic platforms and smart hardware, conditioning a
shared policy on each product’s role, capabilities, constraints, and
interaction state.

### 2.2 Multimodal Trajectory Construction

Synthetic data methods expand agent training beyond available
interaction logs. ToolACE constructs diverse function-calling dialogues
with rule- and model-based verification \[[11](#bib.bib12)\], and
T3-Agent generates and verifies multimodal queries and tool-use
trajectories \[[12](#bib.bib13)\]. Their focus on coverage and validity
provides a basis for learning tool interaction. Training for dynamic
tasks additionally requires representing the consequences of
intermediate actions and the decisions that follow unexpected feedback.

Agent-R constructs revision trajectories through search and model-guided
critique, connecting erroneous behavior to successful continuations
\[[13](#bib.bib14)\]. Agent World Model builds executable, stateful
environments for interaction and reinforcement learning
\[[14](#bib.bib15)\]. These approaches expose learners to recovery
behavior or environmental transitions. Our pipeline couples such
execution feedback with compositional product specifications and visual
observations, covering both recovery toward an existing goal and
adaptation after the user changes it. We jointly vary product roles,
workflows, constraints, and capabilities within executable environments.

### 2.3 Post-Training and Reward-Guided Optimization

AgentTuning and Agent-FLAN study how instruction tuning develops agent
behavior from demonstrations \[[15](#bib.bib16), [16](#bib.bib17)\].
Generalized knowledge distillation obtains teacher feedback on
student-generated sequences, addressing the mismatch between fixed
demonstrations and the learner’s behavior \[[17](#bib.bib18)\]. H-OPD
combines vision-language and text-only teachers through
visual-description transfer and token-level arbitration
\[[18](#bib.bib19)\]. Agents-A1 integrates domain expertise through
staged training and multi-teacher on-policy distillation
\[[19](#bib.bib35)\]. RL Starts before RL studies OPD as preparation for
reinforcement learning, reporting higher post-RL averages than direct RL
and SFT-initialized RL in text and vision-language reasoning
\[[20](#bib.bib36)\]. These methods inform capability acquisition and
consolidation; our training additionally conditions expert transfer on
product specifications and refines the resulting policy against
interaction-specific rewards.

Reward modeling determines which behavior to encourage. Process
supervision evaluates intermediate reasoning \[[21](#bib.bib20)\],
generative verifiers produce correctness judgments \[[22](#bib.bib21)\],
and PoLL aggregates assessments from multiple models
\[[23](#bib.bib22)\]. Aggregation can retain shared errors
\[[24](#bib.bib23)\]; evaluation criteria must still be defined for the
target behavior. Using these assessments in optimization is a separate
question. RLSD uses privileged evidence to scale token advantages while
preserving their reward-derived signs \[[5](#bib.bib24)\]. DOPD routes
supervision between privileged teacher and student policies to address
differences in token-level supervision \[[6](#bib.bib34)\]. DPER
combines these mechanisms: it extends RLSD’s magnitude modulation to
dual-source evidence selected through DOPD’s routing principle. Our
multi-source rewards separately assess reasoning, product constraints,
and goal consistency.

## 3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction

We construct multimodal interaction trajectories to learn a shared
policy across heterogeneous robotic platforms—including bipedal,
quadruped, and wheeled robots—and smart hardware. Our construction
couples compositional product specifications with executable constraints
and visual feedback consistent with the resulting state. This produces
interactions that cover both recovery from execution failures and
adaptation to revised user goals under the same product rules. Figure

connects product composition and environment initialization (steps 1–2),
closed-loop interaction (step 3), and trajectory validation and reuse
(step 4).

### 3.1 Product and Environment Construction

We use developer configurations, product descriptions, device images or
interface screenshots, tool APIs, operational rules, and domain skills
as source materials. JoyAI-VL performs product decomposition, code
generation, trajectory generation, user simulation, and semantic
auditing throughout this pipeline. It decomposes existing products into
reusable modules and generates variants that preserve field restrictions
and component semantics, following self-instructed data generation
\[[25](#bib.bib27)\]. Each product specification is a tuple
$`P=(I_{P},W_{P},C_{P},S_{P})`$: $`I_{P}`$ defines identity, embodiment,
and interaction style; $`W_{P}`$ defines task strategies and workflows,
including decision branches and recovery rules; $`C_{P}`$ specifies
permissions, confirmation requirements, and operating constraints; and
$`S_{P}`$ specifies tools, domain skills, and perceptual and action
capabilities. Each component has a corresponding typed module library.

A code-generation agent implements simulated tool and device backends
from the resource interfaces. These programs validate arguments and
preconditions, execute state transitions, and return results or errors,
with branches for success, invalid arguments, insufficient permission,
timeout, and partial execution. Calls and state changes are logged.
Before admission to a library, modules undergo interface, state-access,
and failure-branch tests, together with checks that backend outputs
agree with the resulting states and observations.

The composer selects modules from the four libraries and assembles
compatible product configurations. Compatibility checks resolve
dependencies and conflicts, ensure that workflows use installed
resources and obey constraints, and require action arguments to be
obtainable from user input, observations, prior results, or
clarification. The selected capabilities must also match the embodiment
and support the intended tasks. Joint composition allows, for example,
the same workflow to operate with different embodiments, tool sets, and
confirmation policies when compatible; coverage is bounded by the module
libraries and compatibility rules.

Compositions that pass integration tests are compiled into a developer
prompt $`D_{P}`$, resource interfaces, executable backends,
state-transition rules, and observation and validation rules.
Descriptive skills enter the prompt, while callable tools and device
capabilities additionally expose invocation schemas and backends. The
same product constraints inform both the prompt and machine-checkable
predicates, aligning the behavior requested of the policy with the rules
enforced and audited by the environment. Alternative prompt
serializations preserve the same product semantics. Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Product Prompt and Interaction Templates ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
presents a developer prompt template and its correspondence to the four
product components.

For each product, we construct a user goal, user profile, initial hidden
state $`s_{0}`$, visual assets, and a dynamic-event policy (Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"),
step 2). We specify object identities, locations, attributes, and device
states before generating the initial request and image. Initial visual
assets may come from real observations, simulation, or image generation,
and we check them against $`s_{0}`$ before interaction begins.

### 3.2 Closed-Loop Trajectory Generation

We load the product and scenario into a sandbox that maintains
interaction state, a design also used in ToolSandbox
\[[26](#bib.bib26)\]. In turn $`t`$, the agent receives the developer
prompt $`D_{P}`$, prior interaction history $`h_{t}`$, and current
observation $`o_{t}=(x_{t},v_{t},r_{t},e_{t})`$, where the components
denote the user message, visual observation, execution result, and
error, respectively. Conditioned on these inputs and the previous task
state $`Q_{t-1}`$, it updates its task state $`Q_{t}`$ and selects an
action $`a_{t}`$. Visual observations may contain images or video.
Actions include responses, clarifications, refusals, tool calls, device
actions, and termination. For trajectories without an explicit task
queue, the policy generates only $`a_{t}`$. Hidden states, reference
answers, and future event schedules are withheld from the agent.

The action router in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
(step 3) sends dialogue actions to a user simulator, as in
conversational tool-use environments \[[8](#bib.bib6)\], while tool
calls and device actions execute the registered backends. For any
environment-changing call or device action, the backend returns an
execution result, any error, and state changes, accounting for triggered
events that affect execution. The shared sandbox applies these changes
together with any remaining environmental effects, then generates visual
feedback from the updated state using simulation, interface rendering,
or image generation. Object, attribute, text, and multimodal semantic
checks assess agreement between this feedback and the recorded state,
including after failure or partial completion. User messages, execution
results, errors, and images retain their separate sources in the
interaction record. Appendix
[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Policy Decisions and Sandbox Transitions ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
formalizes the policy interface and this execution-to-observation
sequence.

Dynamic events trigger based on specified turn, state, or action
conditions. The user simulator introduces goal revisions, additional
requirements, or rejected confirmations; backends report timeouts,
permission changes, or partial execution; and the state engine applies
changes such as object movement or device shutdown. The agent observes
the consequences and must update its plan, seek clarification, retry, or
terminate. These interactions provide trajectories for both recovery
toward an existing goal and adaptation to a revised goal.

### 3.3 Trajectory Validation and Data Outputs

An interaction ends upon goal completion, cancellation, valid refusal,
unrecoverable failure, or exhaustion of the turn budget. Termination
reason and task success are recorded separately. Each trajectory
$`\tau`$ stores model-visible messages, observations, and actions
alongside audit metadata: the product specification, hidden-state
history, event log, and backend execution records.

Following execution-based verification in the construction of synthetic
tool-use data \[[27](#bib.bib25)\], deterministic checks validate
schemas, preconditions, permissions, confirmations, state transitions,
provenance of results, and termination. A separate multimodal evaluator
assesses interaction naturalness, visual grounding, adaptation to new
feedback, and unsupported execution claims. Auditing uses the effective
goal at each turn, including recorded user revisions. A trajectory is
accepted for supervised learning only if it passes all hard checks and
its semantic score reaches the acceptance threshold $`\gamma`$. Semantic
judgments cannot override a failed hard check. Recovery demonstrations
are retained only when valid actions encounter environmental failures,
such as timeouts or device unavailability, and subsequent behavior
remains compliant. Trajectories containing agent-side rule violations
are excluded from SFT even if the agent later corrects them.

The supervised set $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}}`$ contains accepted
demonstrations, including appropriate refusals and compliant recovery
behavior. We construct preference pairs by matching an accepted
demonstration with an audited trajectory containing a localized
violation under the same product, task, and initial conditions.
Violations include missing confirmations, invalid arguments, incorrect
action order, or reliance on outdated visual evidence. These pairs form
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pref}}`$ and supply contrasting trajectories for
reward-model training (Section
[4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")).
The executable product, scenario, and validators form
$`\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{rollout}}`$, where online candidates are
evaluated under the same product rules. Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
(step 4) summarizes these outputs. Variants of one trajectory share a
split, and deduplication uses product composition, scenario source, and
visual assets.

## 4 Method

We train a shared ReAct-style policy for bipedal, quadruped, and wheeled
robots, as well as smart hardware such as interactive toys. Product
specifications condition all three training stages (Figure
[3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")):
supervised fine-tuning establishes interaction behavior, multi-teacher
distillation transfers complementary capabilities, and DPER refines the
policy using interaction rewards. Throughout training, the policy
reasons and acts on the observations available at each decision.
Teachers and privileged references provide training signals; deployment
uses a single student with the product configuration and interaction
context.

![Refer to caption](2610.12957v1/fig3.png)

Figure 3: Three-stage policy learning. All stages use the shared
product-conditioned context. (A) SFT trains on assistant tokens in
multimodal demonstrations. (B) Text and vision teachers supervise
student-generated prefixes through reverse-KL OPD. (C) DPER selects
privileged teacher or student evidence to rescale reward-derived token
advantages while preserving their signs. Rewards combine GPRM,
multi-judge rankings, and task-queue consistency. Teachers and
privileged references are used only during training; deployment uses a
single student.

### 4.1 Product-Conditioned Supervised Fine-Tuning

Supervised fine-tuning teaches the policy to interpret product
specifications and produce context-appropriate responses and tool calls.
We combine general instructions, text-based tool interactions,
image-conditioned decisions, multi-turn execution feedback, and verified
sandbox trajectories (Section
[3](#S3 "3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")).
Following instruction tuning \[[28](#bib.bib31), [29](#bib.bib32)\], we
organize examples from all sources using consistent message roles:
system messages define the interaction protocol, developer messages
encode the product specification, and user and tool messages provide
requests, observations, and execution results. Our chat template
serializes $`D_{P}`$ as a separate developer message using the same
message delimiters as other roles (Appendix
[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Chat-Template Serialization ‣ Appendix B Product Prompt and Interaction Templates ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")).
Assistant messages contain the supervised reasoning, responses, and
actions.

Let $`\pi_{\theta}`$ denote the student policy. For a serialized
trajectory $`\tau_{i}`$, let $`y_{i,n}`$ be its token at position $`n`$
and $`c_{i,n}`$ the context available before that token: the protocol,
product prompt $`D_{P}`$, preceding messages and assistant outputs, and
available visual observations and execution feedback. Let
$`\mathcal{M}_{i}`$ denote the valid assistant-token positions,
excluding padding. We minimize cross-entropy over these positions,
summing token losses within each trajectory and averaging over
demonstrations. User messages, observations, and tool returns remain
conditioning inputs, and explicit task queues, when present, are
supervised as assistant outputs. Appendix
[A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Supervised Learning Objective ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
states the objective in full and specifies the mask.

The product prompt specifies the available tools, their argument
requirements, and execution preconditions. Demonstrations include
clarification, explanations of capability limits, and recovery from
execution failures under different product configurations. Supervision
therefore covers both output syntax and whether a proposed action is
appropriate for the current product and observed state. The resulting
checkpoint initializes multi-teacher distillation.

##### ReAct decisions and task-state generation.

At each ReAct decision, the student generates reasoning followed by
either a tool call or a direct response, such as an answer,
clarification, or refusal. When required by the interaction protocol,
the updated task queue is emitted in the same assistant message for
either decision type. All components are generated autoregressively by
the same policy and supervised as assistant outputs. The queue contains
ordered batches of coarse-grained action labels, while a tool call
specifies a registered tool and concrete arguments for an action in the
current head batch. Tool results become observations for subsequent
decisions. Neither issuing a call nor producing a direct response
establishes completion of a pending tool action; completion must be
verified from execution feedback. Appendix
[B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 ReAct Assistant Output Template ‣ Appendix B Product Prompt and Interaction Templates ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
illustrates both output branches.

### 4.2 Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation

We use on-policy distillation \[[17](#bib.bib18)\] to transfer
complementary capabilities from text and vision teachers into a single
student, following staged multi-teacher training \[[19](#bib.bib35)\].
Text teachers supervise planning, tool selection, argument construction,
and feedback interpretation; vision teachers supervise decisions
requiring image evidence. Each assigned teacher receives the product
constraints and student-generated prefix, together with the visual
observation when required. We place this stage before DPER based on
evidence that OPD can improve subsequent RL performance even when its
immediate accuracy gains are small \[[20](#bib.bib36)\].

Let $`\pi_{\mathrm{old}}`$ denote the student snapshot used to collect
the current rollout batch, and let $`\kappa(i,n)`$ identify the
capability-matched teacher assigned to the decision containing token
$`n`$. We evaluate the teacher distribution
$`q_{i,n}(\cdot)=\pi_{T_{\kappa(i,n)}}(\cdot\mid c_{i,n})`$ on the
student’s sampled context and stop gradients through it. With aligned
teacher and student token spaces, we compute the reverse-KL OPD
objective \[[20](#bib.bib36)\], minimizing divergence from the student
to the assigned teacher:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{i}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{old}}}\left[\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}_{i}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{i,n})\,\|\,q_{i,n}(\cdot)\right)\right],
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{S}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{i}`$ denotes the assistant
positions selected for distillation. At each position, the two
distributions condition on the same student-generated prefix. The
expectation accounts for task sampling, student rollouts, and
observations returned by the environment.

Sampled trajectories and teacher targets remain fixed during each
update, and only student parameters receive gradients. We collect each
subsequent rollout batch with the updated student and obtain new teacher
targets. The distilled checkpoint initializes DPER; teacher assignment
is used only during training.

### 4.3 DPER: Token-Level Evidence Routing

Dual-Source Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Token-Level
Evidence Routing (DPER) retains RLSD’s group-relative base advantage and
sign-preserving advantage modulation \[[5](#bib.bib24)\], and introduces
token-level source selection inspired by DOPD \[[6](#bib.bib34)\].
Trajectory rewards evaluate an interaction as a whole, whereas
privileged references provide additional evidence about individual
sampled tokens. A frozen privileged teacher and a parameter-sharing
privileged student score tokens on the same rollout. Routing selects the
evidence used to scale each token advantage, while the group-relative
reward comparison determines its sign. DPER uses the selected evidence
in a policy-gradient objective without adding DOPD’s separate KL/JS
distillation losses.

#### 4.3.1 Dual-Source Privileged Scoring

For each product-conditioned task instance, we collect a group
$`\mathcal{C}=\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}`$ using $`\pi_{\mathrm{old}}`$. Let
$`z_{i}`$ be a reference answer, solution trace, or tool trajectory
available only during training. For each sampled assistant token, we
compute

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{0,in}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{i,n}\mid c_{i,n}),`$ |  | (2) |
|  | $`\displaystyle p^{p}_{S,in}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{i,n}\mid c_{i,n},z_{i}),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle p^{p}_{T,in}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{T}(y_{i,n}\mid c_{i,n},z_{i}),`$ |  |  |

where $`\pi_{T}`$ is a frozen teacher with a token space aligned with
the student’s. The privileged student uses the same parameter snapshot
as the rollout policy with the additional reference. Both privileged
branches evaluate the sampled tokens under teacher forcing. Keeping the
trajectory fixed makes their scores refer to the same decisions and
observed consequences. The privileged student’s comparison with the
rollout policy changes the information available to the same model,
while the teacher also contributes a different model’s assessment. The
context $`c_{i,n}`$ retains intervening user messages, observations, and
tool results across ReAct turns; the generating policy never receives
$`z_{i}`$.

#### 4.3.2 Token-Level Source Selection

We measure disagreement by
$`a_{in}=|\log p^{p}_{T,in}-\log p^{p}_{S,in}|`$ and joint support by
$`u_{in}=p^{p}_{T,in}+p^{p}_{S,in}`$. Their medians, $`\widetilde{a}`$
and $`\widetilde{u}`$, are computed over valid assistant tokens in the
rollout batch. High and low are relative to these batch statistics.
These median thresholds are our choice for adapting DOPD’s
source-selection principle. The selected evidence probability is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p^{*}_{in}=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\begin{cases}p^{p}_{T,in},&a_{in}<\widetilde{a}\ \text{and}\ u_{in}\geq\widetilde{u},\\
p^{p}_{T,in},&a_{in}\geq\widetilde{a}\ \text{and}\ p^{p}_{T,in}\geq p^{p}_{S,in},\\
p^{p}_{S,in},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\right],
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\operatorname{sg}`$ stops gradients through the selected score.
The conditions define four cases, following DOPD’s source-selection
structure \[[6](#bib.bib34)\]:

- •
  Low disagreement, high joint support (LH). The two sources agree with
  relatively high joint support for the sampled token, and we select the
  privileged teacher.
- •
  Low disagreement, low joint support (LL). Agreement is accompanied by
  low joint support for the sampled token, and we select the privileged
  student.
- •
  High disagreement, stronger teacher support (HT). We select the
  privileged teacher when its probability for the sampled token is at
  least as high as the student’s, including ties.
- •
  High disagreement, stronger student support (HS). We select the
  privileged student when its probability for the sampled token exceeds
  the teacher’s.

All four cases use the same advantage-modulation rule below. Probability
support alone does not establish that an action is executable or
consistent with the user’s goal.

#### 4.3.3 Evidence-Weighted Policy Optimization

Following RLSD \[[5](#bib.bib24)\], we compute group-relative base
advantages $`A_{i}`$ from the composite trajectory rewards in Equation
([6](#S4.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"))
within each same-task rollout group. We retain RLSD’s evidence-weighting
rule, using the routed probability $`p^{*}_{in}`$ as its privileged
score:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{in}=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\log p^{*}_{in}-\log p_{0,in}\right],\qquad w_{in}=\exp\!\left(\operatorname{sign}(A_{i})\Delta_{in}\right).
``` |  | (4) |

The token advantage is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{A}_{in}=A_{i}\left[(1-\lambda)+\lambda\operatorname{clip}(w_{in},1-\epsilon_{w},1+\epsilon_{w})\right],
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\lambda\in[0,1]`$ controls evidence strength and
$`\epsilon_{w}\geq 0`$ is the clipping radius. The multiplier is
positive, preserving the sign of each nonzero advantage and leaving zero
advantages unchanged. Relative to the rollout policy, greater privileged
support increases reinforcement for positive advantages, while weaker
support increases suppression for negative advantages, subject to
clipping. Clipping bounds the influence of large probability contrasts,
and mixing with the original advantage controls the contribution of
privileged evidence. When $`\lambda=0`$ or $`\epsilon_{w}=0`$, the
original trajectory advantage is recovered. A selected probability equal
to the rollout probability also leaves the corresponding token advantage
unchanged. The sign guarantee applies to individual token advantages. If
routing always selects $`p^{p}_{S,in}`$, the token-advantage formula
reduces to RLSD’s self-distillation modulation under the same rewards
and hyperparameters.

We maximize a clipped policy surrogate using the evidence-weighted token
advantages, averaging over valid assistant tokens within each trajectory
and then over task instances and their sampled trajectories. Appendix
[A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 DPER Policy Objective ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
gives the full objective and policy-ratio definition. Privileged scores,
routing decisions, and token advantages are computed once per rollout
batch, detached from gradients, and held fixed during its policy
updates. They are recomputed from the next student snapshot after new
rollouts are collected. Only the unprivileged policy is optimized for
deployment.

### 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards

We evaluate process quality through GPRM, product-constrained behavior
through multi-judge ranking, and goal-consistent planning through
TaskQueue. For the queue-bearing interaction path described here, normal
reward aggregation requires a successfully constructed scoring input and
a valid GPRM score, including zero. Let
$`\mathcal{B}_{i}\subseteq\{\mathrm{GPRM},\mathrm{Judge},\mathrm{Queue}\}`$
contain GPRM and the available optional scores for candidate
$`\tau_{i}`$. Its normal-path reward is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{content},i}=\frac{\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}\beta_{b}R_{b,i}}{\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}\beta_{b}},\qquad R_{i}=R_{\mathrm{content},i}\,\ell_{\mathrm{format},i}\,\ell_{\mathrm{len},i},
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\beta_{b}\geq 0`$ and $`\beta_{\mathrm{GPRM}}>0`$, ensuring a
positive denominator. The multiplicative factors
$`\ell_{\mathrm{format},i}\in[0.05,1]`$ and
$`\ell_{\mathrm{len},i}\in[0.1,1]`$ assess output structure and length.
Missing optional scores are excluded and the remaining weights are
renormalized; valid zero scores from any branch retain their weights.

The format factor assesses compliance with the required output
structure. The interaction protocol determines whether a task queue is
required; choosing a direct response does not waive an existing queue
requirement. Dialogue-only outputs for which the protocol requires no
queue incur no missing-queue penalty. Empty outputs receive the minimum
coefficient. An otherwise usable output that omits a required task queue
receives a coefficient above this minimum but below that of a complete
valid output. If this omission makes the Queue score unavailable on the
normal scoring path, the coefficient is capped at the ratio of the
remaining available reward weight to that same weight plus the Queue
weight. With other scores and the length factor held fixed, this cap
prevents renormalization alone from increasing the reward relative to a
valid output with a zero Queue score. The length factor penalizes
excessive reasoning and response content using task-dependent
allowances.

If the scoring input cannot be constructed, the scorer returns
$`R_{i}=0`$. If GPRM is unavailable because no process step can be
evaluated, its request fails, or no valid score is returned, the scorer
instead assigns
$`R_{i}=0.1\,\ell_{\mathrm{format},i}\ell_{\mathrm{len},i}`$ and skips
Judge and Queue evaluation for that candidate. A valid zero GPRM score
follows normal aggregation and does not trigger this fallback. This
fixed fallback is an implementation rule, not a process-quality
judgment. These candidate-level rules precede normal aggregation;
group-level exclusions are described below. Appendix
[A.5](#A1.SS5 "A.5 Reward Availability and Scoring Paths ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
details score availability and fallback handling. The resulting rewards
supply DPER’s group-relative advantages.

#### 4.4.1 Generative Process Evaluation

GPRM combines process supervision \[[21](#bib.bib20)\] with generative
verification \[[22](#bib.bib21)\]. For each reasoning step $`s`$, its
input contains the task and trajectory prefix through that step. Prefix
evaluation exposes intermediate errors that a final-answer judgment does
not separately represent. The evaluator generates a verification
rationale, then predicts an ordered quality grade
$`g\in\mathcal{G}=\{A,B,C,D\}`$ at a fixed judgment position. The
rationale assesses correctness and contextual relevance and identifies
errors in the current reasoning. This evaluation uses the language
model’s output distribution without an additional scoring head.

We obtain grade probabilities $`p_{s,g}`$ by applying softmax only over
the four grade-token logits after the rationale (Appendix
[A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 GPRM Grade Normalization ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")).
With grade weights
$`(w_{A},w_{B},w_{C},w_{D})=(1,\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{3},0)`$, the step
reward and trajectory score are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{s}^{\mathrm{proc}}=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}w_{g}p_{s,g},\qquad R_{\mathrm{GPRM},i}=\frac{1}{L_{i}}\sum_{s=1}^{L_{i}}r_{s}^{\mathrm{proc}},
``` |  | (7) |

where $`L_{i}>0`$ is the number of evaluated reasoning steps; the
candidate index is suppressed on step-level quantities. With no
evaluable step, the scorer takes the fallback above without evaluating
this average. Grades run from highest quality (A) to lowest (D). The
expectation retains uncertainty across grades, and averaging puts
trajectories with different numbers of evaluated steps on the same score
range, $`[0,1]`$. The result measures expected process quality under the
evaluator’s grade distribution.

The evaluator’s training data include trajectories from the preference
pairs in $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{pref}}`$ (Section
[3](#S3 "3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")).
It is trained with autoregressive cross-entropy on annotated sequences
containing the task, reasoning prefix, verification rationale, and
quality grade. Rationale annotations record the judgment basis,
identified errors, and their implications for subsequent reasoning.
These labels supervise process quality independently of the candidate
rankings below.

#### 4.4.2 Multi-Judge Ranking

Each judge receives the same complete candidate group $`\mathcal{C}`$
and produces a tiered ranking with ties. This relative protocol avoids
requiring scores from different judges to share an absolute scale
\[[30](#bib.bib37)\]. Task-dependent criteria cover product
capabilities, behavioral constraints, role consistency, planning
dependencies, use of observations, goal completion, and redundancy
within and across candidates. Presenting the full group lets judges
compare alternative behaviors under the same task and identify repeated
content across rollouts. Their outputs are ranking ballots over shared
candidate identities.

For a complete ranking group with $`K>1`$ and at least one valid ballot,
let $`\bar{r}_{mi}`$ be candidate $`i`$’s average rank in valid ballot
$`m`$, with rank 1 best. Tied candidates share the mean of their
occupied ranks. Invalid ballots are discarded in full; the remaining
ballots form $`\mathcal{V}`$, with nonnegative weights renormalized so
that $`\sum_{m\in\mathcal{V}}\alpha_{m}=1`$. We aggregate their
normalized rank scores into $`b_{i}`$, rerank candidates by decreasing
$`b_{i}`$, and map the resulting average rank $`\bar{r}_{i}`$ to the
final reward:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
b_{i}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{V}}\alpha_{m}\frac{K-\bar{r}_{mi}}{K-1},\qquad R_{\mathrm{Judge},i}=\delta_{\mathrm{J}}+(1-\delta_{\mathrm{J}})\frac{K-\bar{r}_{i}}{K-1},
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\delta_{\mathrm{J}}`$ is a tunable reward-calibration
hyperparameter, with its value specified in Section
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents").
Both ranking stages preserve ties. Weighted ballot scores determine the
aggregate ordering; the final mapping uses rank spacing rather than
numerical gaps between aggregate scores. These scores express relative
preferences within the group; they do not measure absolute task success
or define GPRM grades. If every candidate in a group triggers a
deterministic hard failure, we skip both relative ranking and DPER
policy updates for that group. If no valid ballot remains, the Judge
branch is unavailable: normal-path candidates use the remaining scores
in Equation
([6](#S4.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")),
while candidates assigned an early-exit or fallback reward retain that
value.

#### 4.4.3 Task-Queue Consistency

TaskQueue evaluates semantic alignment and continuity across turns. At
turn $`r`$, let $`Q^{(r)}=[B_{1}^{(r)},\ldots,B_{L_{r}}^{(r)}]`$, with
the head batch representing the current tasks. On the first turn,
JoyAI-VL inversely rewrites the queue as a natural-language task
description under task-granularity rules. The first-turn score compares
this reconstructed intent with the initial request $`x`$, as defined in
Equation
([A.5](#A1.E5 "Equation A.5 ‣ Initial-queue scoring. ‣ A.6 Task-Queue Scoring ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")).
It evaluates intent coverage without requiring an exact match to a
reference queue and uses no continuity term.

Queues use nested batches of coarse-grained action labels without
concrete objects or arguments. The grouping rules separate actions from
queries, place individual queries or broadcasts in separate batches, and
start a new batch when the interaction subject changes. These rules
specify the queue representation; inverse rewriting preserves the
proposed tasks and grouping without adding, removing, or correcting
them.

On feedback turns with a nonempty observation block and a preceding
queue, $`q_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(r)}`$ compares a JoyAI-VL inverse rewrite of
the current queue with task requirements updated from the observation
history, including user revisions, visual observations, execution
feedback, and errors. Qwen3-Embedding-4B encodes the compared texts, and
cosine similarity is mapped to $`[0,1]`$, consistent with the overall
reward range. The continuity term $`q_{\mathrm{cont}}^{(r)}`$ compares
the queue against the preceding queue updated by verified execution:
completed heads are removed, failed or unexecuted heads are retained,
and partially completed heads retain their unfinished tasks in order. It
counts exact batch matches at corresponding positions and normalizes by
the longer queue’s length. A batch matches only if all task entries are
equal at the same positions; neither batches nor tasks within a batch
may be reordered. With both scores available,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{\mathrm{queue}}^{(r)}=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{obs}}q_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(r)}+\eta_{\mathrm{cont}}q_{\mathrm{cont}}^{(r)}}{\eta_{\mathrm{obs}}+\eta_{\mathrm{cont}}},
``` |  | (9) |

with nonnegative weights and a positive denominator. All components lie
in $`[0,1]`$. Available components retain their weights, which are
renormalized when another component is missing; evaluated zeros remain
included. If no component is available, the turn has no Queue score. The
two terms balance adaptation to revised goals and preservation of
pending task order: a justified revision may reduce positional
agreement, so exact queue matching alone is insufficient. The trajectory
score $`R_{\mathrm{Queue},i}`$ averages evaluated turn scores, including
the initial score; it is unavailable if no turn is evaluable. Appendix
[A.6](#A1.SS6 "A.6 Task-Queue Scoring ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
gives the component definitions and empty-queue handling, and Table
[2](#A1.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Queue inverse rewriting. ‣ A.6 Task-Queue Scoring ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
provides the inverse-rewriting prompt.

## 5 Experiments

We evaluate JoyInside-VLM-Agent on four public tool-use benchmarks and
two proprietary evaluation sets, analyze training dynamics, and examine
two physical-robot interactions. The quantitative results assess
tool-call trajectories, step-wise decisions, argument matching, and
response overlap; the cases illustrate decisions based on visual
evidence and execution feedback.

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

##### Training data.

Our training corpus comprises approximately 120K examples: 50K
proprietary interaction trajectories, 40K supplementary examples adapted
from public agent datasets, and 30K video-instruction examples from
LLaVA-Video-178K \[[31](#bib.bib9)\] and Video-ChatGPT
\[[32](#bib.bib10)\]. The proprietary trajectories span quadruped,
bipedal, and wheeled robots and AI-enabled plush toys. The supplementary
subset draws on four public sources: $`\tau^{2}`$-Bench
\[[9](#bib.bib7)\] for conversational tool use and agent–user
coordination, VitaBench \[[33](#bib.bib8)\] for interactive tasks
spanning multiple service scenarios, DeepPlanning \[[34](#bib.bib40)\]
for long-horizon planning with verifiable constraints, and SWE-bench
Verified \[[35](#bib.bib39)\] for repository-level issue resolution. We
refine and augment all four sources using the task-variation,
interaction-generation, and validation procedures adapted from Section
[3](#S3 "3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"),
producing supplementary training data for our model. The 40K count
refers to the examples retained after augmentation and filtering.

##### Evaluation datasets.

We evaluate on MSAgent \[[36](#bib.bib28)\], BFCL v4
\[[37](#bib.bib29)\], ToolBench \[[38](#bib.bib30)\], and ToolACE
\[[11](#bib.bib12)\]. We report benchmark-level scores on all four
datasets and additional step-wise and response-overlap metrics on
ToolBench and ToolACE. We also evaluate on two proprietary JoyInside
datasets. JoyInside-Devices focuses on extended multi-turn dialogue and
simple device control across AI-enabled toys, smart appliances, and
other AIoT devices. Tasks span conversational assistance, context
tracking, evolving user preferences, status queries, and basic control
commands. JoyInside-Robotics focuses on long-horizon, visually grounded
reasoning and complex robot control. Tasks involve target search and
localization, spatial reasoning, multi-step planning and execution, and
adaptive replanning based on visual observations and execution feedback.

##### Models and product configuration.

JoyInside-VLM-Agent starts from Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and undergoes supervised
fine-tuning, multi-teacher distillation, and DPER (Section
[4](#S4 "4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")).
We compare the resulting student with the base model and
Qwen3.5-397B-A17B. At inference, it uses product specifications and
interaction history; privileged references are limited to training. We
also use SkillOpt \[[39](#bib.bib38)\] to refine the domain-skill
instructions used on JoyInside-Devices and JoyInside-Robotics while
keeping model parameters fixed during skill refinement. Results on these
sets therefore reflect the trained policy and refined skills together.

##### Training teachers.

For the experiments reported here, we use Qwen3.8-27B for the OPD
teachers and the frozen DPER teacher. Teacher selection is configurable,
subject to the token-space alignment required by the objectives in
Section
[4](#S4 "4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents").

##### DPER training configuration.

We train DPER on 16 NVIDIA H200 GPUs for 350 steps with 128 prompts per
batch and $`K=8`$ responses per prompt, yielding 1,024 sampled
responses. The prompt minibatch size is 32, with an actor microbatch
size of one sequence per GPU. The learning rate is $`5\times 10^{-6}`$,
with 10 warmup steps and weight decay 0.1. Rollouts use temperature 1.0,
top-$`p`$ 0.95, and top-$`k`$ 20, with maximum prompt and response
lengths of 16,384 and 2,048 tokens, respectively. DPER uses evidence
strength $`\lambda=0.5`$. Policy losses are averaged over valid tokens
within each response and then over responses. Privileged teacher and
student branches provide scores only; separate distillation loss, KL
regularization, and the entropy bonus are disabled. In Equation
([6](#S4.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")),
the GPRM, Judge, and Queue weights are 0.4, 0.3, and 0.3, respectively,
renormalized over available scores; the fallback content factor is 0.1.
We set the Judge calibration hyperparameter in Equation
([8](#S4.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 4.4.2 Multi-Judge Ranking ‣ 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"))
to $`\delta_{\mathrm{J}}=0.3`$. Within TaskQueue, Equation
([9](#S4.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 4.4.3 Task-Queue Consistency ‣ 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"))
uses $`\eta_{\mathrm{obs}}=0.6`$ for semantic agreement and
$`\eta_{\mathrm{cont}}=0.4`$ for continuity. Format and length factors
penalize structural violations and excessive output length, with length
allowances adjusted to task complexity (Section
[4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")).

##### Benchmark-level evaluation.

For MSAgent, ToolBench, and ToolACE, we report trajectory accuracy,
measured by exact match of the complete tool-call sequence. For BFCL v4,
we use the official evaluation script and report its overall score,
following the benchmark’s category-specific evaluation and aggregation
rules. Table
[1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.3 Benchmark Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")(a)
reports the resulting scores on a 0–100 scale.

##### Step-wise and response metrics.

For ToolBench, ToolACE, JoyInside-Devices, and JoyInside-Robotics,
Plan.EM measures decision-type exact match at evaluated steps, rather
than the correctness of a complete plan. Act.EM measures tool-name exact
match, and Avg.F1 gives partial credit for argument matching. HalluRate
is the percentage of evaluated tool-call records that select a tool
outside the available capability set; it measures unsupported tool
selection. ROUGE-L \[[40](#bib.bib33)\] measures lexical overlap between
final and reference responses. All scores in Table
[1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.3 Benchmark Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
are reported on a 0–100 scale; only HalluRate is better when lower.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented JoyInside-VLM-Agent, a multimodal agent that combines
verified, product-conditioned trajectories with staged policy learning
to adapt behavior to the roles, capabilities, and constraints of robotic
platforms and smart hardware. Multi-source rewards assess reasoning
quality, adherence to product constraints, and alignment with evolving
goals. DPER routes token-level evidence between privileged teacher and
student policies to scale token advantages while preserving their
reward-derived signs. Built on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, the model achieves a
BFCL v4 overall score of 77.44, while results on the other public
benchmarks are mixed. On JoyInside-Devices and JoyInside-Robotics, the
evaluated system, which includes domain-specific skill refinement, leads
the baselines on all reported metrics. Two quadruped case studies
illustrate visually grounded search, replanning from execution feedback,
and explicit failure reporting.

## 7 Limitations and Future Work

JoyInside-VLM-Agent has been applied to quadruped robots, humanoid
robots, and AI devices. Generalization to a broader range of product
configurations and operating environments still requires systematic
evaluation. Product-conditioned data construction also depends on the
coverage of the capability library and the fidelity of sandbox-generated
feedback. Future work will expand product coverage and measure task
completion and recovery over longer real-world interactions. We also
plan to evaluate across more diverse benchmarks in multimodal
understanding, reasoning, planning, and tool use to assess the
generality of the learned capabilities beyond the current evaluation
suite.

The current results do not isolate the contributions of data
construction, reward design, and DPER routing, and the proprietary
evaluations additionally include domain-specific skill refinement.
Controlled ablations are needed to separate these effects. Moreover,
model-based rewards and embedding similarity provide imperfect proxies
for task success; preserving reward-derived advantage signs does not
resolve errors in the underlying reward. We plan to investigate
execution-grounded reward calibration and robustness to incomplete or
noisy feedback, alongside the component ablations, to better understand
how each stage supports reliable product-specific behavior.

## Appendix A Supplementary Formulations

### A.1 Policy Decisions and Sandbox Transitions

Let $`\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}`$ denote the trajectory-generation policy used
for offline data construction in Section
[3](#S3 "3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents").
At turn $`t`$, it conditions on the product prompt $`D_{P}`$, prior
interaction history $`h_{t}`$, current observation
$`o_{t}=(x_{t},v_{t},r_{t},e_{t})`$, and previous task state
$`Q_{t-1}`$. It generates an updated task state and an action:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(Q_{t},a_{t})\sim\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}(\cdot\mid D_{P},h_{t},o_{t},Q_{t-1}),
``` |  | (A.1) |

where $`h_{t}`$ excludes $`o_{t}`$; the history and previous queue are
empty before the first decision. Observation components follow Section
[3](#S3 "3 Product-Conditioned Multimodal Data Construction ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"),
with $`\varnothing`$ denoting absence. Queue variables are omitted when
unused. Online rollouts substitute the current student for
$`\pi_{\mathrm{gen}}`$.

For product $`P`$, the backend $`\mathcal{B}_{P}`$ executes action
$`a_{t}`$ in hidden state $`s_{t}`$, accounting for triggered events
$`\epsilon_{t}`$ and their timing. It returns the result $`r_{t+1}`$,
error $`e_{t+1}`$, and state changes $`\Delta s_{t}`$; $`F_{P}`$ applies
these changes and any remaining event effects. When visual feedback is
produced,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle(r_{t+1},e_{t+1},\Delta s_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}_{P}(a_{t},s_{t},\epsilon_{t}),`$ |  | (A.2) |
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=F_{P}(s_{t},a_{t},\Delta s_{t},\epsilon_{t}),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle v_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=G_{\mathrm{vis}}(v_{t},s_{t},s_{t+1}).`$ |  |  |

The visual generator uses simulation, interface rendering, or image
generation; its output is checked against $`s_{t+1}`$. Dialogue replies
$`x_{t+1}`$ come from the user simulator, while read-only calls cause no
action-induced state changes. These branches need not produce visual
feedback. Hidden states and event schedules remain unavailable to the
policy.

### A.2 Supervised Learning Objective

Using the notation in Section
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Product-Conditioned Supervised Fine-Tuning ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"),
we minimize the average negative log-likelihood of the supervised
assistant tokens:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{i}\sim D_{\mathrm{SFT}}}\left[\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}-\log\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{i,n}\mid c_{i,n}\right)\right].
``` |  | (A.3) |

The mask $`\mathcal{M}_{i}`$ includes assistant reasoning, responses,
actions, and explicit task queues when present, while excluding padding.
Structural delimiters such as tags and brackets are supervised together
with the surrounding content tokens and carry no separate weight. User
messages, observations, and tool returns contribute to $`c_{i,n}`$ but
are not prediction targets. Equation
([A.3](#A1.E3 "Equation A.3 ‣ A.2 Supervised Learning Objective ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"))
sums over the supervised positions of each trajectory and then averages
over $`D_{\mathrm{SFT}}`$, so a demonstration with more supervised
tokens contributes proportionally more. We keep this convention for the
token-level objectives in Section
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Product-Conditioned Supervised Fine-Tuning ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
and Section
[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"),
and average within each trajectory for the DPER policy loss (Appendix
[A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 DPER Policy Objective ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")),
which weights a single trajectory-level advantage: summing over tokens
there would multiply each update by the length of its trajectory and let
long trajectories dominate the policy gradient.

### A.3 DPER Policy Objective

For same-task rollout groups with $`K>1`$, we set all base advantages to
zero when the group rewards are identical.

Using the evidence-weighted token advantage $`\widehat{A}_{in}`$ from
Equation
([5](#S4.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 4.3.3 Evidence-Weighted Policy Optimization ‣ 4.3 DPER: Token-Level Evidence Routing ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")),
we optimize the unprivileged policy with the ratio
$`\rho_{in}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,n}\mid c_{i,n})/p_{0,in}`$, where
$`p_{0,in}`$ is the rollout probability defined in Equation
([2](#S4.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 4.3.1 Dual-Source Privileged Scoring ‣ 4.3 DPER: Token-Level Evidence Routing ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")).
The objective is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{DPER}}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\!\left[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{i}|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{M}_{i}}\mathcal{U}_{in}(\theta)\right],`$ |  | (A.4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{in}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\min\!\left(\rho_{in}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{in},\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{in}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat{A}_{in}\right),`$ |  |  |

where $`\epsilon`$ is the policy-ratio clipping radius. The sum and
normalization include only valid assistant-token positions
$`\mathcal{M}_{i}`$, and the expectation averages over task instances
and their sampled trajectories. The rollout probabilities, privileged
scores, routing decisions, and token advantages are detached and held
fixed during updates on a rollout batch. They are recomputed using the
next student snapshot and newly collected rollouts. Gradients update
only the unprivileged policy.

### A.4 GPRM Grade Normalization

For reasoning step $`s`$, we apply softmax only to the logits of the
four grade tokens $`\mathcal{G}=\{A,B,C,D\}`$ at the fixed judgment
position after the generated verification rationale. The resulting
probabilities $`p_{s,g}`$ sum to one over these four grades. These
probabilities enter the expected-grade reward in Equation
([7](#S4.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 4.4.1 Generative Process Evaluation ‣ 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")),
which uses weights $`(1,\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{3},0)`$ for grades A
through D and averages step rewards within each trajectory.

### A.5 Reward Availability and Scoring Paths

Failure to construct the scoring input, including missing prompt data,
directly returns $`R_{i}=0`$. With valid input but no evaluable process
steps, GPRM is unavailable; the scorer skips GPRM, Judge, and Queue
calls and returns
$`0.1\,\ell_{\mathrm{format},i}\ell_{\mathrm{len},i}`$. A failed GPRM
request or a missing or invalid score uses the same fallback and skips
Judge and Queue. A valid GPRM score, including zero, instead enters
normal aggregation in Equation
([6](#S4.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")),
with $`\beta_{\mathrm{GPRM}}`$ retained in the denominator; Judge and
Queue are evaluated when applicable. Normal aggregation excludes
unavailable optional scores and retains valid zeros from every branch.
These availability checks are distinct from process-quality judgments.
The group-level hard-failure exclusion in Section
[4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
still takes precedence over policy updates.

### A.6 Task-Queue Scoring

Section
[4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")
combines semantic agreement with queue continuity. Let
$`F_{\mathrm{inv}}`$ reconstruct the intent expressed by a queue under
task-granularity rules $`\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{gran}}`$. In both initial
and feedback-turn scoring, $`\operatorname{sim}`$ encodes the compared
texts with Qwen3-Embedding-4B and linearly maps their cosine similarity
from $`[-1,1]`$ to $`[0,1]`$.

##### Initial-queue scoring.

On the first turn, the reconstructed intent is compared with the initial
request $`x`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{queue}}^{(1)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=F_{\mathrm{inv}}(Q^{(1)};\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{gran}}),`$ |  | (A.5) |
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{queue}}^{(1)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{sim}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{queue}}^{(1)},x).`$ |  |  |

This comparison evaluates intent coverage without requiring an exact
match to a reference queue. For queues containing coarse-grained action
labels, the score reflects task-type coverage rather than detailed
argument correctness or task completion. No continuity score is used on
the first turn.

##### Availability and missing outputs.

Initial and feedback-turn scores use Equations
([A.5](#A1.E5 "Equation A.5 ‣ Initial-queue scoring. ‣ A.6 Task-Queue Scoring ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"))
and
([9](#S4.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 4.4.3 Task-Queue Consistency ‣ 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents")),
respectively, with weights from Section
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents").
Available components are reweighted to sum to one, retaining evaluated
zeros. A turn has no Queue score if all components are unavailable or
the output lacks the task-queue field; the format factor separately
assesses required fields. These rules follow the early-exit checks in
Appendix
[A.5](#A1.SS5 "A.5 Reward Availability and Scoring Paths ‣ Appendix A Supplementary Formulations ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents").

For a turn with a nonempty observation block and a preceding queue, let
$`o^{(1:r)}=(o^{(1)},\ldots,o^{(r)})`$ denote the full observation
history available before the current queue update, including user
messages, visual observations, execution feedback, and errors. Given the
initial request $`x`$ and this history, $`F_{\mathrm{obs}}`$ rewrites
the task requirements to reflect the remaining tasks after accounting
for completed work, user revisions, and cancellations. The semantic
score compares the updated intent with the intent reconstructed from the
current queue:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(r)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=F_{\mathrm{obs}}(x;o^{(1:r)}),`$ |  | (A.6) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{queue}}^{(r)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=F_{\mathrm{inv}}(Q^{(r)};\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{gran}}),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(r)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{sim}(\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}^{(r)},\widehat{x}_{\mathrm{queue}}^{(r)}).`$ |  |  |

To measure continuity, let $`E^{(r-1)}`$ be the verified execution
outcome for the previous head batch $`B_{1}^{(r-1)}`$, with status
$`\nu(E^{(r-1)})`$ equal to complete, incomplete, or partial. Incomplete
includes failed or unexecuted heads; $`\nu`$ is determined from
execution feedback rather than the queue proposed by the policy. Let
$`\bigl[B_{\mathrm{rem}}^{(r-1)}\bigr]`$ contain the nonempty unfinished
part of the previous head after partial execution, as determined by
$`E^{(r-1)}`$, preserving the original relative order of the remaining
tasks. With $`\rVert`$ denoting queue concatenation, define the
execution-conditioned reference queue as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Q_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(r)}=\begin{cases}[],&Q^{(r-1)}=[],\\
\operatorname{Tail}(Q^{(r-1)}),&\nu(E^{(r-1)})=\mathrm{complete},\\
Q^{(r-1)},&\nu(E^{(r-1)})=\mathrm{incomplete},\\
\bigl[B_{\mathrm{rem}}^{(r-1)}\bigr]\rVert\operatorname{Tail}(Q^{(r-1)}),&\nu(E^{(r-1)})=\mathrm{partial}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (A.7) |

Let $`M_{r}`$ count batch pairs that match exactly at corresponding
positions in $`Q_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(r)}`$ and $`Q^{(r)}`$. A pair matches
only when both batches contain the same number of tasks and identical
task entries in the same order. Matching permits no reordering of
batches or their tasks, including parallel tasks. In the coarse-grained
representation, entries are action labels rather than tool-call
arguments. Semantic similarity is evaluated separately. When at least
one queue is nonempty, the continuation score is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{\mathrm{cont}}^{(r)}=\frac{M_{r}}{\max\{|Q_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(r)}|,|Q^{(r)}|\}},
``` |  | (A.8) |

where queue length counts task batches. The score is defined as 1 when
both queues are empty. Equation
([9](#S4.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 4.4.3 Task-Queue Consistency ‣ 4.4 Multi-Source Interaction Rewards ‣ 4 Method ‣ JoyInside-VLM-Agent: Product-Conditioned Multimodal Policy Learning for Embodied Agents"))
combines this score with semantic agreement on feedback turns.

##### Cross-turn aggregation.

The trajectory-level TaskQueue reward averages all evaluated turn
scores, including zeros. For a single-turn trajectory, it equals the
initial score when evaluable. If no turn is evaluable, normal content
aggregation omits the Queue component and renormalizes the remaining
weights.
````

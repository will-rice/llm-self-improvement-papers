## 1 Introduction

Rapid advances in large language model (LLM) \[[26](#bib.bib44),
[51](#bib.bib8), [14](#bib.bib43)\] capabilities have given rise to an
emerging paradigm of recursive self-improvement (RSI) \[[5](#bib.bib10),
[7](#bib.bib41), [63](#bib.bib42)\], in which models become the primary
drivers of their own iterative improvement, enabling them to
continuously adapt to increasingly complex scenarios and tasks
\[[24](#bib.bib2), [54](#bib.bib1)\]. However, sustained growth in model
capabilities does not automatically translate into corresponding gains
in safety. Prior work has shown that even a single fine-tuning step can
weaken a model’s established alignment \[[34](#bib.bib14),
[33](#bib.bib5)\]; over the course of recursive evolution, repeated
model updates may introduce new safety vulnerabilities and exacerbate
existing safety issues \[[12](#bib.bib11), [8](#bib.bib12),
[61](#bib.bib13)\]. Nevertheless, current self-improvement methods
predominantly optimize for benchmark performance, without systematically
incorporating safety into the evolutionary loop, making it difficult to
ensure that safety capabilities keep pace with ongoing changes in model
capabilities. Leading AI organizations have begun to confront this
capability–safety gap: the GPT-5.6 \[[29](#bib.bib3)\] and Claude 4
\[[1](#bib.bib4)\] system cards assess not only model capabilities but
also risks such as misuse, prompt injection, and alignment failures,
while documenting the corresponding safeguards in safety training and
deployment. These practices resonate with the central claim of our
previously proposed AI-45° law \[[19](#bib.bib9)\]: safety capabilities
should co-evolve with model capabilities, rather than being applied as
post hoc patches after model capabilities have stabilized.

Such coevolution requires safety alignment to continue throughout
recursive model improvement—a process we term Safety RSI. As model
capabilities and attack strategies evolve, static alignment against a
fixed set of risks becomes insufficient \[[50](#bib.bib39),
[65](#bib.bib40), [46](#bib.bib32), [43](#bib.bib31)\]. Safety RSI
therefore requires continual discovery and repair of emerging
vulnerabilities while preserving utility and limiting over-refusal.
OpenAI’s GPT-Red illustrates how self-play-trained red-teamers can
generate attacks that improve subsequent models’ prompt-injection
robustness \[[29](#bib.bib3), [45](#bib.bib33)\]. However, this
continual discover-and-repair process is undermined by how current
models encode and store knowledge: i) current architectures store safety
knowledge in shared parameters rather than a dedicated substrate, so it
is overwritten by subsequent updates as the model evolves and previously
addressed risks are forgotten \[[34](#bib.bib14), [33](#bib.bib5)\]; ii)
current architectures encode hazardous and general knowledge in shared
parameters, making it difficult to locate and isolate hazardous
knowledge as models improve through RSI. Interventions on these
parameters may impair general capabilities \[[20](#bib.bib6),
[58](#bib.bib7)\], while subsequent fine-tuning can restore previously
suppressed hazardous knowledge \[[6](#bib.bib15)\]. Addressing these
limitations requires architectural support for safety memory and
hazardous-knowledge disentanglement: the former retains safety
experience for use in later updates, while the latter separates
hazardous knowledge from general knowledge so it can be removed with
minimal impact on general capabilities.

These mechanisms support the continuous development and preservation of
safety capabilities throughout Safety RSI. However, the resulting
alignment remains primarily empirical: models learn safety responses
from risk-related data, without necessarily establishing why a response
satisfies the relevant safety requirements. Formal reasoning complements
this empirical alignment by enabling the system to reason explicitly
about safety-relevant conditions and derive conclusions from specified
premises and rules. An inference-time neuro-symbolic harness can
implement this capability by combining the model’s language
understanding with symbolic solvers that derive conclusions and
construct checkable proofs under explicit assumptions
\[[49](#bib.bib34)\]. These proofs provide a machine-checkable basis for
safety judgments beyond learned refusal behavior, with guarantees scoped
to the formalized assumptions and safety requirements. The applicability
of these guarantees to the original problems depends on how faithfully
the formal representations capture their meaning. In existing
neuro-symbolic approaches, translating natural-language problems into
formal representations can omit implicit premises or semantic relations,
while variations in wording can lead to inconsistent formalizations,
preventing the solver from establishing the intended conclusion
\[[30](#bib.bib35), [13](#bib.bib36)\]. Addressing this gap must
therefore preserve the connection between natural-language meaning and
formal representations, making both the formalized premises and the
semantic bridges used in reasoning explicit so that the resulting proofs
can be checked and interpreted within their stated scope.

Building on these requirements, we introduce Safin-1.5, the latest
generation of the Safin series. Its predecessor, Safin-1
\[[56](#bib.bib24)\], offered an initial exploration of Safety from
Within, demonstrating the potential of integrating persistent safety
capabilities into a model’s internal architecture. Safin-1.5 extends
this foundation into a full-stack framework for operationalizing Safety
RSI by co-designing model architecture, continual training, and formal
assurance, as illustrated in Figure
[2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction"). At the architectural level, we
integrate Memory-Anchor Routing across Context History (MARCH)
\[[57](#bib.bib45)\] and Relocating into Removable EXperts (REX)
\[[16](#bib.bib46)\] to support persistent safety memory and
hazardous-knowledge disentanglement, respectively. Together, these
mechanisms enable safety experience to be retained and reused across
model updates while facilitating the targeted removal of hazardous
knowledge with minimal disruption to general capabilities. At the
training level, Recursive Safety Improvement (ReSI) \[[62](#bib.bib37)\]
operationalizes Safety RSI through a continual cycle of risk discovery,
evaluation, and repair, allowing safety alignment to adapt to emerging
vulnerabilities while balancing safety improvements, capability
retention, and over-refusal. The architectural mechanisms underpin this
cycle by preserving prior safety experience and enabling targeted
interventions against newly identified risks. At the formal assurance
level, Proof-Obligation-Guided Bridging (POGB) \[[41](#bib.bib38)\]
serves as the core mechanism of our Formal Reasoning Harness,
complementing empirical safety evaluation with verifiable reasoning
evidence at inference time. By addressing semantic gaps between
natural-language problems and formal representations, it enables
machine-checkable verification of conclusions in logical reasoning tasks
under explicit assumptions.

Our main contributions are as follows:

- •
  We build on Safin-1’s safety memory and incorporate REX-based
  hazardous-knowledge disentanglement to support continual safety
  improvement. i) The inherited MARCH interface makes persistent safety
  states selectively accessible within model computation; ii) REX
  relocates the hazardous knowledge already present in a pretrained MoE
  model into newly added removable experts, keeps general capability in
  the original experts, trains subsequent updates on the full model, and
  physically excises the removable experts at deployment. On Safin-1, a
  single set of removable experts carries both Bio and Cyber knowledge;
  one excision substantially lowers WMDP accuracy in both domains, and
  the removal persists through standard post-training and ReSI, whereas
  the same RMU recipe rebounds almost completely under the same
  post-training.
- •
  We integrate ReSIas the training harness for Safety RSI. In each
  round, ReSI applies diverse red-teaming methods to identify
  vulnerabilities in the current target model, develops a training
  recipe through automated research, and promotes the update with the
  largest safety gain among those passing a Pareto gate on benign
  compliance and instruction following. On the disentangled
  architecture, ReSI trains the full model while red-teaming,
  validating, and gating its excised counterpart, and its extensible
  pools of red-teaming and alignment methods let the loop incorporate
  new attacks and learning approaches.
- •
  To support trustworthy safety improvement beyond empirical evaluation,
  we enhance the reliability of logical reasoning through
  machine-checkable proofs. We introduce POGB, which addresses semantic
  gaps between natural-language statements and formal representations by
  integrating LLM-based semantic understanding with solver-controlled
  logical reasoning. Extensive experiments demonstrate substantial
  improvements in reasoning accuracy across diverse benchmarks.
- •
  Experimental results show that, compared with Safin-1, Safin-1.5
  reduces H-CoT ASR from 82.00% to 14.00% and X-Teaming ASR from 64.78%
  to 32.08%, lower than all evaluated frontier models on X-Teaming,
  while largely preserving general capabilities and benign compliance.
  At test time, POGB-based Formal Reasoning Harness achieves 91.80%
  overall accuracy across four reasoning benchmarks, surpassing all
  evaluated Direct and CoT baseline configurations except those of
  Claude Opus 5. It achieves the highest FOLIO accuracy of 88.73%,
  matches the best PrOntoQA result at 100.00%, and reaches near-perfect
  accuracy of 99.50% on ProofWriter, demonstrating the benefit of
  proof-guided reasoning for end-to-end accuracy.

## 2 Safety-Native Architecture

This section summarizes the safety memory inherited from Safin-1 and
describes its integration with REX-based hazardous-knowledge
disentanglement. Together, these mechanisms provide architectural
support for retaining safety experience and isolating targeted hazardous
knowledge during continual safety improvement.

### 2.1 MARCH: Safety Memory

Safin-1.5 retains the memory architecture of MARCH \[[57](#bib.bib45)\]
and the persistent Safety State introduced in Safin-1
\[[56](#bib.bib24)\]. This interface makes both contextual memory and
learned safety specialization selectively accessible within recurrent
computation. We describe one state-bearing layer below; the same
construction applies across recurrent layers.

##### State anchors.

Let $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ be the normalized token representation and
$`\mathbf{S}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{k}}`$ the recurrent state,
with native read query
$`\mathbf{q}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{q}\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}`$.
The base mixer updates
$`\mathbf{S}_{t}=F(\mathbf{S}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_{t})`$ continuously, and
MARCH checkpoints its state at boundaries $`b_{m}`$ spaced every $`C`$
text tokens:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathbf{A}^{(m)}=\mathbf{S}_{b_{m}},\qquad\mathcal{V}_{t}=\{m\mid b_{m}<t\}.
``` |  | (1) |

Each anchor stores a cumulative prefix state rather than an isolated
segment. An occurrence of a shared learned anchor embedding is inserted
after each boundary without modifying the recurrence. At each layer, its
normalized representation $`\mathbf{u}_{m}`$ reads the aligned
checkpoint as $`\mathbf{A}^{(m)}\mathbf{W}_{q}\mathbf{u}_{m}`$ and
yields a routing key
$`\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{m}=\mathbf{W}_{\kappa}\mathbf{u}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{r}}`$.
The readout passes through the output projection and residual pathway,
making subsequent-layer anchor representations state-dependent.

##### Persistent safety memory.

The bank additionally contains a learned state
$`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{safe}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}\times d_{k}}`$, indexed
by $`p_{\mathrm{safe}}`$, with a key constructed through the same
anchor-representation pathway. The layer-wise collection of these states
forms the Safety State. Unlike sequence-derived anchors, it remains
available from the first token without consuming input positions.
Safin-1 learns this collection with token-level cross-entropy on harmful
requests with safety-aligned responses and benign requests with helpful
responses, while freezing the language-model backbone
\[[56](#bib.bib24)\].

##### Joint routing and readout.

Let
$`\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{\rho}\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{r}}`$
be the routing query. The candidate set is
$`\mathcal{C}_{t}=\mathcal{V}_{t}\cup\{p_{\mathrm{safe}},\varnothing\}`$,
with payloads
$`\mathbf{A}^{(p_{\mathrm{safe}})}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{safe}}`$ and
$`\mathbf{A}^{(\varnothing)}=\mathbf{0}`$. The null option allows
retrieval to be bypassed. Candidate scores and probabilities are

|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{t,j}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{j},&j\neq\varnothing,\\
\mathbf{w}_{\varnothing}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t}+b_{\varnothing},&j=\varnothing,\end{cases}`$ | $`\displaystyle\pi_{t,j}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\exp(s_{t,j})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\exp(s_{t,j^{\prime}})}.`$ |  | (2) |

The router selects memories, while $`\mathbf{q}_{t}`$ reads their
matrix-valued contents. The retrieved readout is added to the native
recurrent path:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{o}_{t}=\mathbf{S}_{t}\mathbf{q}_{t}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{t}}\pi_{t,j}\mathbf{A}^{(j)}\mathbf{q}_{t}.
``` |  | (3) |

Thus, context anchors and persistent safety memory contribute according
to the current token and context, without changing the base recurrent
update. In Safin-1.5, this inherited interface supplies the memory
foundation alongside REX and ReSI; replay and the safety–utility gate in
Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 ReSI on the Safety Architecture ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")
complement it during continual safety improvement.

### 2.2 REX: Hazardous-Knowledge Disentanglement

Figure 3: Overview of REX. (1) Expert expansion copies and scales the
source experts of the base model $`B`$ to form removable experts
($`F_{0}`$), and joint training with selective gradient masking and
routing differentiation relocates hazardous knowledge into them
($`F_{1}`$). (2) Excising the removable experts yields $`A_{1}`$, and a
light RMU cleanup yields the public base model $`A_{2}`$, while the
removable experts and their router rows are kept. (3) Recombining them
with $`A_{2}`$ gives the full structure $`F_{2}`$, on which standard
post-training produces the full model $`F_{3}`$ for authorized users;
excising its removable experts produces the public model $`A_{3}`$.

Safety RSI requires that hazardous capabilities, once removed, stay
removed as the model keeps being updated, and parameter-level unlearning
has difficulty meeting this requirement because hazardous and general
knowledge are stored in the same parameters. Methods such as RMU
\[[20](#bib.bib6)\] and NPO \[[58](#bib.bib7)\] modify these shared
parameters in place, so more thorough removal costs more general
capability. More importantly, such removal suppresses the expression of
the knowledge rather than taking it out of the model, and subsequent
fine-tuning, even on unrelated data, can bring it back
\[[6](#bib.bib15)\]. In a one-off release this is already a risk; in
Safety RSI, where the model is updated round after round, every update
is another chance for the knowledge to return. REX \[[16](#bib.bib46)\]
addresses this problem through where the knowledge is stored. The
feed-forward parameters of a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model are
partitioned into independently addressable experts, the expert set can
be expanded, and selected experts can be deleted at deployment. Once
hazardous knowledge is concentrated in a set of removable experts,
removing it no longer depends on in-place edits to shared parameters and
becomes a structural operation.

REX relocates hazardous knowledge out of the original experts and into
newly added removable experts through three components (Figure
[3](#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2.2 REX: Hazardous-Knowledge Disentanglement ‣ 2 Safety-Native Architecture")).
The first component, *expert expansion*, initializes the removable
experts. On the training corpora, we count how often each expert $`i`$
is selected into the top-$`K`$ on target data
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{harm}}`$ and on general data
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{gen}}`$, and compute the difference
$`\Delta_{i}=f_{i}^{\mathrm{harm}}-{f_{i}^{\mathrm{gen}}}`$. In each
layer, the experts with the largest $`\Delta_{i}`$ serve as source
experts. Their parameters and router rows are copied to form the
removable experts, and the source experts are scaled by a fixed factor
$`\gamma`$, which weakens the hazardous knowledge they hold while
preserving most of their general computation. The second component,
*selective gradient masking*, determines where the updates from each
data source can be written. Let $`\Theta_{\mathrm{rem}}`$ denote the
removable experts, $`\Theta_{\mathrm{orig}}`$ the original experts, and
$`\Theta_{\mathrm{router}}`$ all router rows; the updated parameters are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{cases}{\Theta_{\mathrm{rem}}}\cup{\Theta_{\mathrm{router}}}&\text{on }\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{harm}},\\
{\Theta_{\mathrm{orig}}}\cup{\Theta_{\mathrm{router}}}&\text{on }{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{gen}}}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (4) |

On general data, a pre-top-$`K`$ activation mask additionally removes
the logits of the removable experts before selection, so that every
general token is dispatched without them. The original experts must
therefore carry general capability on their own, and the hazardous
knowledge they already hold receives no training signal and fades, which
turns catastrophic forgetting during continued training into directed
forgetting. The third component, *routing differentiation*, shapes the
router with two losses. A mass loss keeps the routing mass assigned to
the removable experts above a floor on target tokens and below a ceiling
on general tokens. A rank loss keeps the largest removable-expert logit
on general tokens a margin below the top-$`K`$ boundary of the original
experts, so that the unmasked router at inference also leaves general
tokens to the original experts. The relocation objective is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{REX}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}}+\lambda_{1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mass}}+\lambda_{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}},
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\lambda_{1}`$ and $`\lambda_{2}`$ weight the mass and rank
losses, respectively, and the language-modeling loss is back-propagated
under the masking rules of Eq.
([4](#S2.E4 "In 2.2 REX: Hazardous-Knowledge Disentanglement ‣ 2 Safety-Native Architecture")).
During relocation, attention, embeddings, normalization layers, shared
experts, and the LM head stay frozen, and only the routed experts and
the router are trained.

After relocation, REX excises the removable experts together with their
router rows and applies a light RMU cleanup to the retained parameters
to remove residual target knowledge, which yields the public base model;
the excised experts are kept by the developer. The value of this design
for Safety RSI lies in the updates that follow. An update is not applied
to the public model directly. Instead, the kept removable experts are
recombined with the cleaned parameters into the full structure, training
proceeds on the full structure, and the removable experts are excised
again at deployment. During the update, the router continues to send
target-related tokens to the removable experts, so these updates leave
with the experts at deployment, while growth in general capability is
written to the retained parameters and released with the public model.
The developer therefore always holds two forms of the model: a full
model for authorized users that retains the hazardous capability, and a
public model that contains no parameters of the removable experts. What
to train and what to release thus become two independent decisions,
which is exactly the interface that the ReSI loop needs when it accepts
an update in each round (Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 ReSI on the Safety Architecture ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).
In the experiments, we denote the original model by $`B`$, the cleaned
public base model by $`A_{2}`$, the recombined full model after standard
post-training by $`F_{3}`$, and its excised counterpart by $`A_{3}`$.

Safin-1.5 applies REX to Safin-1-35B-A3B without interfering with the
memory mechanism of MARCH. The memory-related parameters introduced by
MARCH belong to the frozen shared parameters during both relocation and
cleanup, so REX changes only the routed experts and the router. Routing
statistics, training-token counts, and RMU activations are computed on
text positions, excluding the anchor positions that MARCH inserts into
the sequence. Unlike the original REX, which handles one domain at a
time, Safin-1.5 uses a single set of removable experts for both Bio and
Cyber. Expert expansion selects 8 source experts per layer for each
domain by differential activation, takes their union, and completes it
to 16 per layer by the joint differential across the two domains,
expanding each layer from 256 to 272 experts with $`\gamma=0.3`$.
Relocation uses about 40M tokens of mixed target data, with Bio and
Cyber at roughly 4:1, and about 27M tokens of general data. The residual
cleanup uses a forget budget of 32M tokens and updates only the
routed-expert and shared-expert MLPs in layers 7–9, and standard
post-training on the recombined model uses 250M tokens. A single
excision then removes the target knowledge of both domains.

## 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement

ReSI \[[62](#bib.bib37)\] builds on the safety architecture described in
Section [2](#S2 "2 Safety-Native Architecture") to implement the risk
discovery, evaluation, and repair loop of Safety RSI. In each round,
ReSI applies diverse red-teaming methods to identify vulnerabilities in
the current target model, develops a training recipe through automated
research, and promotes the eligible update with the largest safety gain
as the next target model, where eligibility requires passing a Pareto
gate on safety gain, benign compliance, and instruction following
(Figure [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).
Through successive rounds, the loop pursues the two goals identified by
R²AI \[[42](#bib.bib61), [43](#bib.bib31)\]: *resistance* to known
threats and *resilience* to unforeseen risks. Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Recursive Formulation ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")
formulates the recursive update, Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")
describes the three stages of a round and the joint recipe search space,
and Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 ReSI on the Safety Architecture ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")
describes how the loop operates on the safety architecture.

![Refer to caption](2610.40751v1/imgs/resi_framework.png)

Figure 4: The three-stage ReSI framework. In each round, ReSI applies
red-teaming methods to identify vulnerabilities in the current target
model $`M_{r}`$, develops training recipes through automated research,
and promotes the eligible update with the largest safety gain as
$`M_{r+1}`$, the target for the next round. Stage 1 collects successful
attacks against $`M_{r}`$ (Section
[3.2.1](#S3.SS2.SSS1 "3.2.1 Stage 1: Red-Teaming Assessment ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).
Stage 2 screens attack sources and alignment methods through low-budget
pilot trials and shortlists the most promising recipes (Section
[3.2.3](#S3.SS2.SSS3 "3.2.3 Stage 2: Pilot Screening ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).
Stage 3 conducts full-scale training and feedback-guided refinement,
then selects the eligible checkpoint with the largest safety gain
(Section
[3.2.4](#S3.SS2.SSS4 "3.2.4 Stage 3: Full-Scale Refinement and Model Update ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).

### 3.1 Recursive Formulation

ReSI organizes safety improvement as a recursive loop over target
models. Specifically, ReSI formulates the safety update within a round
as an auto-research problem: given $`M_{r}`$ and the attacks that
succeed against it, ReSI searches for a training recipe that strengthens
the model’s resistance to the exposed attacks while preserving existing
safety behavior and general capabilities. Starting from an initial model
$`M_{0}`$, round $`r`$ takes the current target model $`M_{r}`$ and
produces the next target

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{r+1}=\begin{cases}M_{r}^{\omega_{r}^{\star}},&\mathcal{E}_{r}\neq\varnothing,\\[2.0pt]
M_{r},&\mathcal{E}_{r}=\varnothing,\end{cases}\qquad\omega_{r}^{\star}\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\omega\in\mathcal{E}_{r}}g_{r}(\omega),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`M_{r}^{\omega}`$ denotes the checkpoint that recipe $`\omega`$
produces in round $`r`$ from its specified starting checkpoint,
$`g_{r}(\omega)`$ is its safety gain (Eq.
([9](#S3.E9 "In 3.2.3 Stage 2: Pilot Screening ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement"))),
and $`\mathcal{E}_{r}`$ is the set of recipes with completed full-scale
trials, including the refinement trials of Section
[3.2.4](#S3.SS2.SSS4 "3.2.4 Stage 3: Full-Scale Refinement and Model Update ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement"),
that pass the Pareto gate (Eq.
([10](#S3.E10 "In 3.2.4 Stage 3: Full-Scale Refinement and Model Update ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement"))).
As a result, an accepted update becomes the target of the next round; if
no recipe passes the gate, ReSI retains $`M_{r}`$ and terminates, so
that updates failing the gate never propagate to later rounds. We use
*recursive* in a bounded, operational sense: the search procedure and
the Pareto gate remain fixed across rounds, while the target model and
the attacks collected against it change.

### 3.2 The Three-Stage Round

Each round of ReSI proceeds in three stages over a joint recipe search
space (Figure
[4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")). Stage 1
collects successful attacks against $`M_{r}`$ (Section
[3.2.1](#S3.SS2.SSS1 "3.2.1 Stage 1: Red-Teaming Assessment ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")),
which define the attack data of the recipe search space (Section
[3.2.2](#S3.SS2.SSS2 "3.2.2 Joint Recipe Search Space ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).
Within this space, Stage 2 screens attack sources and alignment methods
through pilot trials (Section
[3.2.3](#S3.SS2.SSS3 "3.2.3 Stage 2: Pilot Screening ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")),
and Stage 3 refines full-scale recipes and selects the model update
under the Pareto gate (Section
[3.2.4](#S3.SS2.SSS4 "3.2.4 Stage 3: Full-Scale Refinement and Model Update ‣ 3.2 The Three-Stage Round ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).

#### 3.2.1 Stage 1: Red-Teaming Assessment

Stage 1 identifies the vulnerabilities of the current target model and
converts them into safety training signals. Let $`\mathcal{X}_{r}`$
denote a safety data pool of requests that represent potential
deployment risks, and let $`\mathcal{Z}_{r}`$ denote the set of
available red-teaming methods. Each method $`z\in\mathcal{Z}_{r}`$ draws
requests from $`\mathcal{X}_{r}`$ to construct attacks against
$`M_{r}`$, potentially adapting them based on the model’s responses, and
produces input–response pairs $`z(M_{r},\mathcal{X}_{r})`$. ReSI
aggregates these records and retains those in which the attack elicits
an unsafe response:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}_{r}^{\mathrm{atk}}=\Bigl\{(x,y)\in\textstyle\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{r}}z(M_{r},\mathcal{X}_{r})\;\Big|\;\operatorname{Unsafe}(x,y)=1\Bigr\},
``` |  | (7) |

where $`x`$ is an attack input, $`y`$ is the corresponding response from
$`M_{r}`$, and $`\operatorname{Unsafe}(x,y)`$ indicates a safety
violation; $`\mathcal{D}_{r,z}^{\mathrm{atk}}`$ denotes the subset
contributed by method $`z`$. Since the attack dataset is re-collected
for each target model, it captures the vulnerabilities of the current
checkpoint, including those introduced by earlier updates. Moreover, the
safety data pool and the set of red-teaming methods are both extensible,
so that new deployment risks and new red-teaming methods can enter the
loop as they emerge.

#### 3.2.2 Joint Recipe Search Space

Following Stage 1, ReSI searches over a joint recipe space
$`\Omega_{r}`$ of compatible combinations of attack data, alignment
methods, training data mixtures, and hyperparameters. A candidate recipe
is represented as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\omega=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},a,\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{h})\in\Omega_{r},
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\boldsymbol{\alpha}`$, $`a`$, $`\boldsymbol{\lambda}`$, and
$`\mathbf{h}`$ specify the attack data, alignment method, training data
mixture, and hyperparameters, respectively.

Attack data. The attack-data dimension $`\boldsymbol{\alpha}`$ controls
the contribution of each red-teaming source. For each method $`z`$,
$`\alpha_{z}\in[0,1]`$ selects a fraction of
$`\mathcal{D}_{r,z}^{\mathrm{atk}}`$, so that a recipe can use a single
source, combine all available attacks, or reweight the sources.

Alignment methods. The alignment method $`a`$ is drawn from an
extensible pool $`\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{align}}`$ and converts the
selected attacks into training examples. Representative methods in this
pool and their design rationales are outlined below. A3
\[[55](#bib.bib56)\] pairs harmful requests with benign counterparts
that share similar patterns and uses both for supervised fine-tuning
(SFT), encouraging the model to learn safety distinctions that
generalize beyond specific adversarial patterns. SInternal
\[[59](#bib.bib57)\] fine-tunes the model to reproduce an expert’s
critique of its own response against safety specifications and then
predict a safety judgment, strengthening its understanding of the
specifications. AlphaAlign \[[60](#bib.bib58)\] applies reinforcement
learning with a reward that requires safe responses to harmful requests
and safe, fully compliant responses to benign requests of similar
surface form, so that unnecessary refusals receive no reward.

Training data mixtures. The mixture
$`\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{\mathrm{cur}},\lambda_{\mathrm{rep}},\lambda_{\mathrm{gen}})`$
balances learning from newly identified vulnerabilities with retaining
existing safety behavior and general capabilities. Specifically, the
three weights of $`\boldsymbol{\lambda}`$ are non-negative, sum to one,
and give the fractions of current safety examples, replay examples from
earlier safety training, and general-task examples.

Hyperparameters. The configuration $`\mathbf{h}\in\mathcal{H}(a)`$,
drawn from the training settings available for method $`a`$, covers the
learning rate, batch size, training budget, checkpoint frequency, the
evaluated checkpoint step, adaptation settings such as LoRA
\[[11](#bib.bib60)\], and generation parameters for reinforcement
learning.

#### 3.2.3 Stage 2: Pilot Screening

Pilot screening identifies promising attack sources and alignment
methods through low-budget trials. For each source $`z`$ providing at
least $`n_{\mathrm{pilot}}`$ successful attacks and each method
$`a\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{align}}`$, ReSI trains a pilot from $`M_{r}`$
with method $`a`$ on $`n_{\mathrm{pilot}}`$ attacks from $`z`$, using
only current safety data ($`\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(0)}=(1,0,0)`$), the
default hyperparameters $`\mathbf{h}_{a}^{(0)}`$ of $`a`$, and a limited
training budget. In the first round, successful attacks from the
original safety data pool form a shared base that each pilot uses in
addition to its $`n_{\mathrm{pilot}}`$ source-specific attacks. After
training, each recipe is evaluated on a held-out validation set
$`\mathcal{D}_{r}^{\mathrm{val}}`$ drawn from the original safety data
pool. However, when $`M_{r}`$ already has a low attack success rate
(ASR) on that pool, $`\mathcal{D}_{r}^{\mathrm{val}}`$ is instead drawn
from a more challenging attack set, and a candidate must also not raise
ASR on the original pool by more than a tolerance $`\delta`$ relative to
$`M_{r}`$. The safety gain of $`\omega`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{r}(\omega)=\operatorname{ASR}(M_{r};\mathcal{D}_{r}^{\mathrm{val}})-\operatorname{ASR}(M_{r}^{\omega};\mathcal{D}_{r}^{\mathrm{val}}).
``` |  | (9) |

Although equal sample counts keep pilots comparable, they can understate
a source that provides many more attacks than $`n_{\mathrm{pilot}}`$.
Therefore, for recipes with modest pilot safety gains but substantially
more available attacks, ReSI runs a conditional scaling probe,
retraining the same recipe from $`M_{r}`$ with
$`n_{\mathrm{pilot}}<n_{\mathrm{scale}}\leq|\mathcal{D}_{r,z}^{\mathrm{atk}}|`$
attacks and ranking it alongside the initial pilots. Finally, ReSI
shortlists up to $`K_{\mathrm{pilot}}`$ recipes with the largest
positive safety gains,
$`\mathcal{Q}_{r}=\operatorname{TopK}_{g_{r}}\{\omega:g_{r}(\omega)>0\}`$;
if $`\mathcal{Q}_{r}=\varnothing`$, it retains $`M_{r}`$ and terminates.

#### 3.2.4 Stage 3: Full-Scale Refinement and Model Update

Given the shortlisted recipes $`\mathcal{Q}_{r}`$, ReSI conducts
full-scale trials, refines each recipe based on feedback from the Pareto
gate, and selects the model update. Specifically, each full-scale trial
starts from $`M_{r}`$, expands the training data to all successful
attacks from the source of the shortlisted recipe, and lifts the pilot
budget cap, keeping $`\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(0)}`$ and
$`\mathbf{h}_{a}^{(0)}`$. The trial saves checkpoints at the frequency
specified in $`\mathbf{h}`$, and each saved checkpoint is evaluated as a
candidate update.

Pareto gate. The Pareto gate requires a safety improvement over the
current model while keeping benign compliance and instruction following
within a tolerance of the initial model. A recipe passes the gate if

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{G}_{r}(\omega)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}g_{r}(\omega)>0,\\
\mathrm{BC}(M_{r}^{\omega})\geq\mathrm{BC}(M_{0})-\epsilon,\\
\mathrm{IF}(M_{r}^{\omega})\geq\mathrm{IF}(M_{0})-\epsilon,\end{cases}
``` |  | (10) |

where $`\mathrm{BC}`$ denotes benign compliance, $`\mathrm{IF}`$ denotes
instruction following, and $`\epsilon`$ is the retention tolerance.
Here, the benign-compliance condition limits over-refusal, and the
instruction-following condition limits the loss of general capabilities.
Moreover, anchoring both retention thresholds to $`M_{0}`$ rather than
to $`M_{r}`$ prevents small per-round losses within the tolerance from
accumulating across rounds.

Feedback-guided refinement. ReSI revises a recipe according to its
Pareto-gate result, allowing at most $`N_{\mathrm{refine}}`$ refinement
trials beyond the initial trial and stopping earlier when no executable
revision remains.

- •
  S1. Attack-source composition. For limited safety gains, expand
  $`\boldsymbol{\alpha}`$ to combine successful attacks from additional
  promising sources.
- •
  S2. Harmful–benign pairing. For a benign-compliance violation,
  construct A3-style benign counterparts that preserve the attack
  patterns of harmful examples while replacing their harmful content,
  and adjust the harmful–benign balance.
- •
  S3. Utility preservation. For an instruction-following violation,
  increase general-task replay through $`\boldsymbol{\lambda}`$, or
  reduce the learning rate or the number of updates through
  $`\mathbf{h}`$.
- •
  S4. Post-SFT safety GRPO. For an eligible SFT recipe, retain it as a
  candidate and continue training from its checkpoint with
  AlphaAlign-based safety Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
  \[[40](#bib.bib65)\] and DAPO-style optimization \[[52](#bib.bib59)\];
  this transition resets the refinement counter to zero, allowing up to
  $`N_{\mathrm{refine}}`$ further refinement trials.

In practice, each revision specifies its starting checkpoint, while all
trials share the same validation sets and gate thresholds. Revisions
restart from $`M_{r}`$ by default; a revision may instead continue from
the evaluated checkpoint with a reduced training budget recorded in
$`\mathbf{h}`$, and revisions within an S4 continuation restart from the
same SFT checkpoint.

Final selection and stopping. After all shortlisted recipes and their
refinements have been evaluated, ReSI updates the target model according
to Eq.
([6](#S3.E6 "In 3.1 Recursive Formulation ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).
ReSI promotes the eligible checkpoint with the largest safety gain, or
retains $`M_{r}`$ and terminates when no recipe is eligible; the loop
also terminates at the configured round limit.

### 3.3 ReSI on the Safety Architecture

ReSI operates on the safety architecture of Section
[2](#S2 "2 Safety-Native Architecture") without changing the three
stages of a round or the Pareto gate: hazardous-knowledge
disentanglement determines which model is trained and which is
evaluated, safety memory supports the retention and reuse of safety
experience across rounds, and formal assurance can check specified
claims about the models that the loop promotes.

Hazardous-knowledge disentanglement. On the disentangled architecture,
ReSI trains the full model but red-teams, validates, and gates its
excised counterpart; the full checkpoint of an accepted update becomes
the starting point of the next round, and its excised counterpart is the
deployed model. Specifically, REX \[[16](#bib.bib46)\] relocates
targeted hazardous knowledge into removable experts that are excised for
deployment. Therefore, in each round, a recipe updates the full model
with all experts, so that the router and the removable experts keep the
roles assigned during relocation. In contrast, training without the
removable experts could push hazardous content contained in safety data
into shared parameters, whereas evaluating with them would assess a
model that is never deployed. Additionally, ReSI measures
hazardous-knowledge accuracy for each trial on the excised checkpoint,
so that any reintroduction of removed knowledge is visible when
candidates are compared.

Safety memory. Safety memory keeps safety experience from earlier rounds
available to later updates. On the model side, MARCH
\[[57](#bib.bib45)\] can provide a persistent interface through which
this experience is stored and selectively invoked. On the training side,
the replay weight $`\lambda_{\mathrm{rep}}`$ complements this interface
by keeping examples from earlier safety training in the training data to
reduce forgetting of earlier repairs.

Formal assurance. Formal assurance can complement the empirical
evaluation of each model that ReSI promotes. ReSI provides empirical
evidence that an update reduces ASRs on the evaluated attacks, and
specified claims about each promoted model can then be checked by the
formal assurance harness in Section
[4](#S4 "4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness") under explicit assumptions.

## 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness

Building on the model trained through the safety improvement process in
Section [3](#S3 "3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement"), we introduce
POGB as an inference-time harness to extend its capabilities to formal
reasoning, as illustrated in Figure
[5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness"). An
inference-time switch controls whether POGB is invoked; it is enabled
only for logical reasoning tasks that ask whether a claim follows from
given premises. When disabled, the model follows its standard inference
procedure. When enabled, PoGB begins with a grounding autoformalization
step, in which an LLM translates the problem expressed in NL into formal
representations while maintaining a grounding table that records the
type and associated NL information for each formal element. This table
then serves as a *proof-time interface* for *proof-obligation-guided
search*. During this search, type information constrains which
instantiations are admissible, while NL information guides the
identification of semantic bridges via an LLM-based
$`\operatorname{Gate}`$ when symbolic reasoning encounters an unresolved
proof obligation. This design keeps all logical reasoning under the
solver’s control while restricting the LLM’s role to autoformalization
and the evaluation of candidate semantic bridges.

![Refer to caption](2610.40751v1/imgs/POGB.png)

### 4.1 Problem Formulation

Let $`X=(\mathbb{P},q)`$ denote a problem comprising a set of NL
premises $`\mathbb{P}=\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}`$ and an NL query $`q`$. We
use a grounding autoformalization $`{AF}`$ to map $`X`$ to
$`{AF}(X)=(\mathcal{P},\mathfrak{q},\mathcal{T})`$, thereby yielding
formal representations that are explicitly associated with their NL
semantics in $`X`$. Here, $`\mathcal{P}=\{\mathfrak{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}`$
is the set of formalized premises, where $`\mathfrak{p}_{i}`$ denotes
the $`i`$th formalized premise; $`\mathfrak{q}`$ is the formalized
query; and $`\mathcal{T}`$ is a grounding table that records the NL
semantic grounding of every formal element (see Section
[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 The Grounding Table as a Proof-Time Interface ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
for details). We adopt first-order logic (FOL) as the target formal
language.

Consider two proof goals corresponding to the query and its negation:
$`\mathfrak{q}`$ and $`\neg\mathfrak{q}`$. For each goal
$`\mathfrak{g}\in\{\mathfrak{q},\neg\mathfrak{q}\}`$, the symbolic
solver performs a proof search and identifies unresolved proof
obligations resulting from missing semantic relations. Let
$`\mathcal{S}`$ denote the symbolic solver. Our objective is to find a
*reasonable* bridge set $`\mathcal{B}`$ that enables the proof to be
completed:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\mathfrak{g})=\textsc{Proved}\quad\land\quad\operatorname{Gate}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{T})=\textsc{Accepted}.
``` |  | (11) |

Here, $`\mathcal{B}=\{\mathfrak{b}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}`$, where each
$`\mathfrak{b}_{j}`$ is a semantic bridge that explicitly encodes an
entailment relation supported by the NL semantics but not represented in
the symbolic ontology. The LLM-based $`\operatorname{Gate}`$ evaluates
only whether these candidate semantic bridges are reasonable given the
grounding table $`\mathcal{T}`$; it does not perform proof search,
conduct intermediate reasoning, or repair failures in response to solver
feedback. The final decision is determined by the proof outcomes for the
two goals:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Decide}(X)=\begin{cases}\textsc{Entailed},&\bigl(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\mathfrak{q}),\ \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\neg\mathfrak{q})\bigr)=\bigl(\textsc{Proved},\ \textsc{Failed}\bigr),\\
\textsc{Contradicted},&\bigl(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\mathfrak{q}),\ \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\neg\mathfrak{q})\bigr)=\bigl(\textsc{Failed},\ \textsc{Proved}\bigr),\\
\textsc{Unknown},&\bigl(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\mathfrak{q}),\ \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\neg\mathfrak{q})\bigr)=\bigl(\textsc{Failed},\ \textsc{Failed}\bigr),\\
\textsc{Conflicted},&\bigl(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\mathfrak{q}),\ \mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\neg\mathfrak{q})\bigr)=\bigl(\textsc{Proved},\ \textsc{Proved}\bigr).\end{cases}
``` |  |

### 4.2 The Grounding Table as a Proof-Time Interface

As discussed in Section [1](#S1 "1 Introduction"), even when individual
NL statements are faithfully formalized, their semantically related
expressions may still be mapped to symbolically unrelated predicates. To
recover the semantic relations between such predicates during proof
search, we maintain two complementary kinds of information for every
formal element. Let $`\mathfrak{e}`$ denote a formal element occurring
in $`\mathcal{P}`$ or $`\mathfrak{q}`$, including a constant, a
variable, or an atomic formula. For each $`\mathfrak{e}`$, the grounding
table $`\mathcal{T}`$ records both type information,
$`\operatorname{type}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathfrak{e})`$, and NL information,
$`\operatorname{NL}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathfrak{e})`$, thereby *providing an
interface for proof-time inference*.

Inspired by type systems \[[4](#bib.bib19)\],
$`\operatorname{type}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathfrak{e})`$ retains a
coarse-grained semantic category assigned to $`\mathfrak{e}`$ by the
LLM. This category constrains which instantiations are admissible,
thereby narrowing the search space for candidate semantic bridges. For
instance, in the running example in Appendix
[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Running Example ‣ Appendix A Appendix"), a variable
of type $`\mathsf{Person}`$ can be instantiated with $`h`$ (Harry) but
not with $`w`$ (*Walden*). By contrast,
$`\operatorname{NL}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathfrak{e})`$ retains the
finer-grained NL semantics of $`\mathfrak{e}`$ by storing referring
expressions (e.g., “Harry,” “*Walden*,” “a person,” and “a book”) and
predicate meanings (e.g., “$`\{a_{1}\}`$ reads $`\{a_{2}\}`$”). When
exact symbolic matching fails, this information guides the search for
plausible semantic bridges between *type-compatible* candidates, such as
the bridge between $`\mathsf{Smarter}(h)`$ and
$`\mathsf{SmarterThanBefore}(h)`$. We next show how this interface
supports *proof-obligation-guided search*.

#### 4.3.2 Semantic Bridge Identification

If $`\phi`$ remains unresolved after the above process, Algorithm
[2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 4.3.1 Solver-Controlled Recursion ‣ 4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
is invoked to identify bridges that can provide semantic support for
$`\phi`$. The key idea is to address two questions separately: *which
semantic support can bridge the gap to the current proof obligation*,
and *what residual obligation must still be discharged to establish that
support*. The former is addressed by LLM-based evaluation, whereas the
latter is addressed by symbolic reasoning.

Specifically, for each formalized premise
$`\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}`$, the operator
$`\textsc{Inst}(\mathfrak{p},\mathcal{T})`$ first generates
type-compatible instantiations. For each instantiation $`\mathfrak{i}`$,
let $`\textsc{Lit}(\mathfrak{i})`$ denote the set of literals it
contains, where a literal is an atomic formula or its negation. For
example, the instantiation
$`\mathsf{Gain}(h,k)\rightarrow\mathsf{Smarter}(h)`$ is equivalent to
$`\neg\mathsf{Gain}(h,k)\lor\mathsf{Smarter}(h)`$, which contains the
literals $`\neg\mathsf{Gain}(h,k)`$ and $`\mathsf{Smarter}(h)`$.
Algorithm
[2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 4.3.1 Solver-Controlled Recursion ‣ 4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
then considers a subset $`\mathcal{I}`$ of all type-compatible
instantiations and selects a support set
$`{\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{lit}}}\subseteq\bigcup_{\mathfrak{i}\in\mathcal{I}}\textsc{Lit}(\mathfrak{i})`$,
whose literals may come from multiple premises and may jointly support
the current obligation $`\phi`$. For computational tractability,
$`{\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{lit}}}`$ is restricted to at most two literals.
The literals in $`{\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{lit}}}`$ are conjoined to form a
candidate *semantic support*
$`\Sigma=\bigwedge_{{\ell}\in{\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{lit}}}}{\ell}`$,
yielding a candidate *semantic bridge* $`\Sigma\rightarrow\phi`$. For
example, when $`\phi=\mathsf{SmarterThanBefore}(h)`$, selecting
$`{\mathbb{S}_{\mathrm{lit}}}=\{\mathsf{Smarter}(h)\}`$ yields
$`\Sigma=\mathsf{Smarter}(h)`$ and the corresponding bridge
$`\mathsf{Smarter}(h)\rightarrow\mathsf{SmarterThanBefore}(h)`$. Next,
candidate semantic bridges are filtered using a lightweight semantic
similarity model and a natural language inference (NLI) model, both of
which take $`\operatorname{NL}_{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)`$ and
$`\operatorname{NL}_{\mathcal{T}}(\phi)`$ as inputs. Finally, the
LLM-based $`\operatorname{Gate}`$ evaluates whether each remaining
bridge holds.

Even after a bridge is accepted by $`\operatorname{Gate}`$, its support
$`\Sigma`$ may remain unresolved. We therefore use logical abduction
\[[17](#bib.bib18)\] to derive a residual proof obligation
$`\mathfrak{o}`$ that, together with the instantiations in
$`\mathcal{I}`$, is sufficient to establish $`\Sigma`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathfrak{o}=\textsc{Abduce}(\mathcal{I},\Sigma),\qquad\left(\bigwedge\nolimits_{\mathfrak{i}\in\mathcal{I}}\mathfrak{i}\right)\land\mathfrak{o}\models\Sigma.
``` |  |

The residual obligation $`\mathfrak{o}`$ is then passed to Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
for recursive discharge. Thus, Algorithm
[2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 4.3.1 Solver-Controlled Recursion ‣ 4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
decouples semantic understanding from logical reasoning: neural models
evaluate only the candidate semantic bridge $`\Sigma\rightarrow\phi`$,
whereas the residual obligation required to establish $`\Sigma`$ is
derived symbolically through logical abduction and must still be
discharged by the symbolic solver.

#### 4.3.3 Termination and Soundness

When the search succeeds, Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
returns a bridge set that satisfies both criteria in Equation
([11](#S4.E11 "In 4.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")):
the set enables the solver to complete the proof of goal
$`\mathfrak{g}`$, and every bridge in the set is accepted by
$`\operatorname{Gate}`$. Theoretical proof of this soundness guarantee
is provided in Appendix
[A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Proof of Soundness ‣ Appendix A Appendix"). A search
branch fails if the current obligation already appears on its ancestor
path, if all candidate bridges are rejected, or if a search limit is
reached. The ancestor check aims to prevent circular reasoning
\[[3](#bib.bib16)\]: if an attempt to prove $`\phi`$ generates $`\psi`$
and a subsequent attempt to prove $`\psi`$ regenerates $`\phi`$, the
repeated obligation is rejected because it cannot serve as its own
justification.

### 5.3 Analysis

#### 5.3.1 Safety-Native Architecture

We first evaluate REX itself on Safin-1-35B-A3B, against the same RMU
recipe applied directly to the original model. $`R`$ applies the RMU
operator, hyperparameters, and forget budget of the residual cleanup
directly to $`B`$, so that $`R`$ and $`A_{2}`$ differ only in their
starting point and retain reference. For post-training, three routes use
the identical 250M-token standard full-parameter SFT with the same data
order and random seed, and differ only in their starting points.
$`A_{3}`$, which is Safin-1+REX, is trained on the recombined full model
as $`F_{3}`$ and excised at deployment; $`A_{3}`$-direct is trained
directly on the excised $`A_{2}`$; and $`R`$-SFT is trained on $`R`$.
Hazardous knowledge is measured by WMDP-Bio and WMDP-Cyber, both
four-way multiple choice with a chance level of 25%, and general
knowledge by MMLU. Results are listed in Table
[4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.3.1 Safety-Native Architecture ‣ 5.3 Analysis ‣ 5 Experimental Results").

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| Stage | Model | WMDP-Bio $`\downarrow`$ | WMDP-Cyber $`\downarrow`$ | MMLU $`\uparrow`$ |     Kept share $`\uparrow`$  (Bio / Cyber) |
| Original | $`B`$ (Safin-1) | 82.6 | 58.3 | 82.3 | – |
| Base | $`R`$ | 56.9 | 32.7 | 82.0 | – |
|  | $`A_{2}`$ (REX) | 36.2 | 29.5 | 78.9 | – |
|    250M  post-training | $`R`$-SFT | 81.7 | 47.2 | 82.4 | 3.4 / 43.6 |
|  | $`A_{3}`$-direct | 64.0 | 41.8 | 80.7 | 40.0 / 57.5 |
|  | $`A_{3}`$ (REX) | 40.8 | 32.5 | 80.2 | 90.0 / 89.9 |

Table 4: REX versus the same RMU recipe on Safin-1-35B-A3B (%). Kept
share is $`(W_{B}-W_{250\mathrm{M}})/(W_{B}-W_{0})`$, the fraction of
the removal at the start of post-training that remains after 250M
tokens, where $`W`$ denotes WMDP accuracy.

Excising the removable experts takes the target knowledge of both
domains away, while the full model retains it. After 250M tokens of
post-training, the full model $`F_{3}`$ reaches 82.8% on WMDP-Bio and
57.4% on WMDP-Cyber, on par with 82.6% and 58.3% for $`B`$. Its excised
counterpart $`A_{3}`$ falls to 40.8% and 32.5%, with Cyber close to
chance level, while MMLU differs by only 2.4 points. The same
post-training therefore produces two deliverables at once: a full model
for authorized users that retains the hazardous capability, and a public
model that contains no parameters of the removable experts. A single set
of removable experts serves both domains, and one excision removes the
target knowledge of both, so the removable experts act as a common
removal unit for multiple hazardous domains.

Before post-training, the public base model $`A_{2}`$ of REX is already
lower than $`R`$ in both domains under the same RMU recipe. $`A_{2}`$
reaches 36.2% on WMDP-Bio, 20.7 points below the 56.9% of $`R`$, and
29.5% on WMDP-Cyber, closer to chance level than the 32.7% of $`R`$. The
two models share an identical RMU, so the difference comes from REX
first relocating the target knowledge into the removable experts and
excising it, which lets the cleanup start from a lower point. $`A_{2}`$
retains over 96% of the MMLU accuracy of $`B`$.

Under the same 250M tokens of standard post-training, the removal by the
same RMU recipe almost entirely disappears, whereas the public model of
REX stays low, which is the most direct value of REX for Safety RSI.
$`R`$-SFT returns to 81.7% on WMDP-Bio, nearly the 82.6% of $`B`$, while
$`A_{3}`$ stays at 40.8%, 40.9 points lower. On WMDP-Cyber, $`R`$-SFT
returns to 47.2% and $`A_{3}`$ stays at 32.5%. The kept share measures
how much of the removal at the start of post-training survives it,
independent of where each route starts. On Bio, $`A_{3}`$ keeps 90.0% of
the removal present at the start, whereas $`R`$-SFT keeps only 3.4%; on
Cyber, the two keep 89.9% and 43.6%. $`A_{3}`$-direct starts from the
same $`A_{2}`$ but is trained on the public form without recombination,
and its WMDP-Bio rises to 64.0%, a kept share of 40.0%. Since the three
trajectories share data, budget, and order, the gap between $`R`$-SFT
and $`A_{3}`$-direct comes from relocation and excision, and the gap
between $`A_{3}`$-direct and $`A_{3}`$ comes from training on the full
structure and excising at deployment. The latter gap also directly
supports the choice in ReSI to train the full model rather than its
excised counterpart (Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 ReSI on the Safety Architecture ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).
Together, the two effects keep the public model close to its
pre-post-training removal level, while general knowledge continues to
grow, with MMLU rising from 78.9% for $`A_{2}`$ to 80.2% for $`A_{3}`$.

The same delivery scheme carries over to the safety updates of ReSI.
Starting from $`F_{3}`$, ReSI trains the full model with all experts and
red-teams, validates, and gates its excised counterpart (Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 ReSI on the Safety Architecture ‣ 3 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement")).
After this round, the excised model stays at 43.68% on WMDP-Bio and
33.17% on WMDP-Cyber, compared with 82.56% and 58.33% for Safin-1 (Table
[1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experimental Results")).
Although ReSI differs from standard post-training in both its objective
and its data, the removal brought by REX carries through this round as
well. Updating on the full structure and excising at deployment can thus
serve as a fixed interface for every round of Safety RSI, so that
neither safety repairs nor capability gains come at the cost of
hazardous knowledge returning to the public model.

#### 5.3.2 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement

Following ReSI \[[62](#bib.bib37)\], we analyze the results from Safin-1
and Safin-1+REX in terms of resistance, resilience, and retention of
general capabilities and benign compliance (Table
[5](#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5.3.2 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement ‣ 5.3 Analysis ‣ 5 Experimental Results"));
Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experimental Results")
presents the overall comparison between Safin-1 and Safin-1.5.

Resistance: in-distribution safety improvements and retention of
existing defenses. Results on WildJailbreak and HarmBench illustrate
improved in-distribution safety and retention of existing safety
defenses, respectively, from both starting models after ReSI alignment.
Safin-1 improves from 2.10% to 0.35% on WildJailbreak and retains its
already low HarmBench ASR of 0.25%. Safin-1+REX improves from 2.10% to
1.20% on WildJailbreak, and its HarmBench ASR remains low, changing from
1.00% to 1.25%.

Resilience: generalization to unseen attack mechanisms. Beyond
in-distribution improvements, ReSI substantially strengthens safety
against attack mechanisms outside the red-teaming pool. H-CoT ASRs
decrease from 82.00% and 64.00% before alignment to 6.00% and 14.00%
afterward for Safin-1 and Safin-1+REX, respectively. In the overall
comparison, X-Teaming ASR decreases from 64.78% for Safin-1 to 32.08%
for Safin-1.5 (Table
[1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experimental Results")).

Retention of general capabilities and benign compliance. The safety
gains from ReSI accompany broadly retained general capabilities and
benign compliance, with variation across starting models. After
alignment, benign-compliance rates on XSTest decrease by 2.80 percentage
points for Safin-1 and by 8.40 points for Safin-1+REX. MMLU-Pro accuracy
changes by $`+2.30`$ and $`-2.40`$ percentage points, and IFEval loose
prompt-level accuracy by $`-1.29`$ and $`-5.55`$ points, for Safin-1 and
Safin-1+REX, respectively.

Comparison with the A3 baseline. Table
[5](#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5.3.2 ReSI: Recursive Safety Improvement ‣ 5.3 Analysis ‣ 5 Experimental Results")
compares A3 \[[55](#bib.bib56)\], a fixed alignment method with its own
data construction and refinement, with ReSI on both starting models. On
Safin-1, ReSI achieves lower ASRs and better benign compliance and
capability retention than A3 across the reported metrics. On
Safin-1+REX, ReSI yields Safin-1.5, which matches A3 on HarmBench and
H-CoT and improves WildJailbreak, XSTest, and IFEval, although MMLU-Pro
accuracy is lower. The comparison highlights the model-dependent
tradeoff between safety, benign compliance, and general capabilities.

## 6 Conclusions

In this work, we presented Safin-1.5, a model built on a safety
architecture, trained through recursive safety improvement, and equipped
with an inference-time formal reasoning harness. Persistent safety
memory and hazardous-knowledge disentanglement support the retention of
safety knowledge and the control of hazardous capabilities, while
recursive safety improvement turns newly discovered vulnerabilities into
evaluated repairs. Experiments show substantial reductions in attack
success rates while largely preserving general capabilities and benign
compliance. The formal reasoning harness further improves reasoning
accuracy by combining the model’s language understanding with symbolic
inference and verification, producing checkable proofs under formalized
premises and accepted semantic bridges. These results demonstrate
progress toward the AI-45° Law’s goal of coordinated safety and
capability development. In the future, we plan to examine safety
retention over longer improvement trajectories and explore formal
reasoning for safety decisions governed by explicit rules.

## Appendix A Appendix

### A.1 Running Example

In this section, we illustrate our proposed method through a simplified
example from the FOLIO dataset \[[10](#bib.bib20)\]. The NL premise set
$`\mathbb{P}=\{p_{1};p_{2};p_{3}\}`$ states: “Reading a book gives a
person knowledge; gaining knowledge makes that person smarter; and Harry
read *Walden*.” The NL query $`q`$ asks whether the statement “Harry is
smarter than before” can be concluded from these premises. Let the
constants $`h`$, $`w`$, and $`k`$ denote Harry, *Walden*, and knowledge,
respectively. We first perform a grounding autoformalization $`{AF}`$ to
map $`X=(\mathbb{P},q)`$ to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\{`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{1}:\ \forall x_{1}\forall y.\ \bigl(\mathsf{Book}(y)\land\mathsf{Read}(x_{1},y)\bigr)\rightarrow\mathsf{Gain}(x_{1},k),`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{2}:\ \forall x_{2}.\ \mathsf{Gain}(x_{2},k)\rightarrow\mathsf{Smarter}(x_{2}),`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{3}:\ \mathsf{Book}(w)\land\mathsf{Read}(h,w)\;\},\qquad\mathfrak{q}=\mathsf{SmarterThanBefore}(h),`$ |  |

and the grounding table $`\mathcal{T}`$ shown in Table
[6](#A1.T6 "Table 6 ‣ A.1 Running Example ‣ Appendix A Appendix").
During proof-time inference, the solver can therefore access all entries
in $`\mathcal{T}`$. In particular, type information
$`\operatorname{type}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathfrak{e})`$ constrains which
instantiations are admissible, whereas NL information
$`\operatorname{NL}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathfrak{e})`$ guides the search for
semantic bridges when exact symbolic matching fails.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|    Formal element  $`\mathfrak{e}`$ |    Type information  $`\operatorname{type}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathfrak{e})`$ |    Natural-language information  $`\operatorname{NL}_{\mathcal{T}}(\mathfrak{e})`$ |
| $`h`$ | $`\mathsf{Person}`$ | “Harry” |
| $`w`$ | $`\mathsf{Book}`$ | “*Walden*” |
| $`k`$ | $`\mathsf{Object}`$ | “knowledge” |
| $`x_{1}`$ | $`\mathsf{Person}`$ | “a person” |

### A.2 Proof of Soundness

###### Lemma 1 (Existential decomposition).

For $`\phi=\exists x.\,\psi`$, let $`\mathcal{B}`$ be the bridge set
returned by a recursive call for an admissible instance
$`\psi[\theta]\in\textnormal{{Inst}}(\psi,\mathcal{T})`$. If
$`{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\psi[\theta])=\textnormal{{Proved}}}`$,
then
$`{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\phi)=\textnormal{{Proved}}}`$.

###### Proof.

Apply existential introduction with the witness $`\theta`$’s bridge set.
∎

###### Lemma 2 (Universal decomposition).

For $`\phi=\forall x.\,\psi`$, introduce a fresh eigenvariable
$`{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}`$ and let $`\mathcal{B}`$ be the bridge set
returned for $`\psi[x\mapsto{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}]`$. Each bridge
involving $`{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}`$ is universally quantified over the
corresponding variable and accepted by Gate with that universal scope.
If
$`{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\psi[x\mapsto{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}])=\textnormal{{Proved}}}`$,
then
$`{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\phi)=\textnormal{{Proved}}}`$.

###### Proof.

The relevant bridges are asserted for every admissible assignment to the
variable represented by $`{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}`$. Freshness and
universal closure ensure that the assumptions do not depend on a
particular value of $`{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}`$. Universal introduction
therefore extends the subproof of $`\psi[x\mapsto{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}]`$
to a proof of $`\forall x.\,\psi`$, using the same bridge set. ∎

###### Lemma 3 (Conjunctive decomposition).

For $`\phi=\phi_{1}\land\phi_{2}`$, let $`\mathcal{B}_{1}`$ and
$`\mathcal{B}_{2}`$ be the bridge sets returned by the two recursive
calls. If
$`{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B}_{i},\phi_{i})=\textnormal{{Proved}}}`$
for $`i=1,2`$, then their merged set
$`\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}`$ satisfies
$`{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\phi)=\textnormal{{Proved}}}`$.

###### Proof.

Combine the two subproofs by conjunction introduction. ∎

###### Lemma 4 (Disjunctive decomposition).

For $`\phi=\phi_{1}\lor\phi_{2}`$, select any successful branch
$`i\in\{1,2\}`$ with returned bridge set $`\mathcal{B}_{i}`$. If
$`{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B}_{i},\phi_{i})=\textnormal{{Proved}}}`$,
then the selected set $`\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{i}`$ satisfies
$`{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\phi)=\textnormal{{Proved}}}`$.

###### Proof.

Apply disjunction introduction to the selected subproof, retaining only
its bridge set. ∎

###### Lemma 5 (Bridge identification).

If Algorithm
[2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 4.3.1 Solver-Controlled Recursion ‣ 4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
yields $`(\mathcal{B},\mathfrak{o})`$ for $`\phi`$,
$`{\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{o}},\mathfrak{o})=\textnormal{{Proved}}}`$
with a Gate-accepted bridge set $`\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{o}}`$, then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{o}},\phi)=\textnormal{{Proved}}\quad\land\quad\operatorname{Gate}(\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{o}},\mathcal{T})=\textnormal{{Accepted}}.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

The yield statement requires Gate acceptance. The instantiated premise
set $`\mathcal{I}`$ follows from $`\mathcal{P}`$. By the abduction
condition in Section
[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness"),
the instantiated premises and the residual subproof jointly establish
the selected support $`\Sigma`$. ∎

###### Theorem 1 (Soundness of successful search).

If Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
returns a bridge set $`\mathcal{B}`$ for a goal $`\mathfrak{g}`$, then
$`\mathcal{B}`$ satisfies both criteria in Equation
([11](#S4.E11 "In 4.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{B},\mathfrak{g})=\textsc{Proved}\quad\land\quad\operatorname{Gate}(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{T})=\textsc{Accepted}.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

We prove by induction on the successful recursive derivation that each
successful call $`\textsc{Prove}(\phi,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T})`$ returns
a Gate-accepted bridge set under which the solver can discharge
$`\phi`$.

*Base case.* The solver directly proves $`\phi`$ from $`\mathcal{P}`$.
The returned bridge set is empty, so Gate acceptance is vacuous.

*Inductive step.* Assume the claim for the successful recursive
subcalls. Normalization preserves equivalence, so it suffices to
discharge the normalized obligation. Its four logical decomposition
cases are as follows.

1.  (i)
    $`\phi=\exists x.\,\psi`$. A recursive call succeeds for an
    admissible instance $`\psi[\theta]`$. The induction hypothesis
    establishes that instance with accepted bridges, and Lemma
    [1](#Thmsearchlemma1 "Lemma 1 (Existential decomposition). ‣ A.2 Proof of Soundness ‣ Appendix A Appendix")
    discharges $`\phi`$.
2.  (ii)
    $`\phi=\forall x.\,\psi`$. The recursive call succeeds for
    $`\psi[x\mapsto{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}]`$, with
    $`{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}`$ recorded as arbitrary in the extended
    grounding table. The induction hypothesis establishes the body with
    accepted bridges; those involving $`{c_{\mathrm{fresh}}}`$ retain
    their universal scope. Lemma
    [2](#Thmsearchlemma2 "Lemma 2 (Universal decomposition). ‣ A.2 Proof of Soundness ‣ Appendix A Appendix")
    discharges $`\phi`$.
3.  (iii)
    $`\phi=\phi_{1}\land\phi_{2}`$. Both recursive calls must succeed.
    By the induction hypothesis, their returned sets $`\mathcal{B}_{1}`$
    and $`\mathcal{B}_{2}`$ contain accepted bridges and establish
    $`\phi_{1}`$ and $`\phi_{2}`$, respectively. Lemma
    [3](#Thmsearchlemma3 "Lemma 3 (Conjunctive decomposition). ‣ A.2 Proof of Soundness ‣ Appendix A Appendix")
    discharges $`\phi`$ under the merged set.
4.  (iv)
    $`\phi=\phi_{1}\lor\phi_{2}`$. At least one recursive call succeeds.
    The induction hypothesis establishes the disjunct of any selected
    successful branch with its accepted bridge set $`\mathcal{B}_{i}`$.
    Lemma
    [4](#Thmsearchlemma4 "Lemma 4 (Disjunctive decomposition). ‣ A.2 Proof of Soundness ‣ Appendix A Appendix")
    discharges $`\phi`$ with $`\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{i}`$.

If no logical decomposition applies, Algorithm
[2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 4.3.1 Solver-Controlled Recursion ‣ 4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
yields $`(\mathcal{B},\mathfrak{o})`$, and Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.3 Proof-Obligation-Guided Search ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")
recursively proves $`\mathfrak{o}`$. The induction hypothesis supplies
its proof and accepted bridge set $`\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{o}}`$. Lemma
[5](#Thmsearchlemma5 "Lemma 5 (Bridge identification). ‣ A.2 Proof of Soundness ‣ Appendix A Appendix")
then discharges $`\phi`$ under
$`\mathcal{B}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{o}}`$, whose bridges are
accepted by that lemma and the induction hypothesis. Thus every
successful return supplies a symbolic proof and accepted bridges. Taking
$`\phi=\mathfrak{g}`$ gives Equation
([11](#S4.E11 "In 4.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 4 POGB: Formal Reasoning Harness")).
∎
````

###### Abstract

Many alignment methods, including reinforcement learning from human
feedback (RLHF), rely on the Bradley-Terry reward assumption, which is
not always sufficient to capture the full range and complexity of
general human preferences. We explore RLHF under a general preference
framework by modeling the alignment problem as a two-player zero-sum
game in a game-theoretic framework, where the Nash equilibrium policy
guarantees a 50% win rate against any competing policy. However,
previous self-play algorithms for finding the Nash policy either diverge
or only converge to a Nash policy in a modified game, even in a simple
synthetic setting, thereby failing to maintain the 50% win rate
guarantee against all other policies. We propose a meta-algorithm,
Convergent Meta Alignment Algorithm (COMAL), for language model
alignment with general preferences, inspired by convergent algorithms in
game theory. We provide theoretical analysis that our meta-algorithm
converges to an exact Nash policy in the last iterate and demonstrate
its effectiveness on a range of synthetic and preference optimization
datasets. COMAL is simple and can be integrated with many existing
methods designed for preference optimization with minimal changes, and
empirically it consistently maintains above 60.2% and 56.8% win rates,
when applied to Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B, against all compared
algorithms under controlled evaluations.

## 1 Introduction

One of the most widely adopted approaches to addressing the challenge of
aligning LLMs with human values and preferences is Reinforcement
Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) ([Christiano et al.,
2017](#bib.bib14); [Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib45)). This framework
consists of two steps: first, learning a reward model from a human
preferences dataset, and second, optimizing the LLM using the proximal
policy optimization (PPO) algorithm ([Schulman et al.,
2017](#bib.bib56)). More recently, [Rafailov et al. (2024)](#bib.bib51)
observed that the first step can be bypassed, proposing the direct
preference optimization (DPO) algorithm, which directly optimizes the
LLM from the dataset.

However, the aforementioned approaches crucially rely on the assumption
that human preferences can be expressed using the Bradley-Terry (BT)
model ([Bradley and Terry, 1952](#bib.bib5)). Unfortunately, the BT
model is too restrictive to capture the richness and complexity of human
preferences. For example, the BT model can only induce _transitive_
preferences – i.e., if more people favor A over B, and B over C, then
more people must favor A over C. Such transitivity may not hold in the
presence of diverse populations and is also incompatible with evidence
from human decision-making ([May, 1954](#bib.bib37); [Tversky,
1969](#bib.bib65)). To illustrate this, consider a simple case where
users are evaluating responses from an assistant to a nuanced question
like: “What’s the best way to spend a Sunday?” Some might prefer
Response A (outdoor activities) over B (reading a book), while others
prefer B over C (watching TV), yet a third group prefers C over A. These
cyclic preferences – A \> B \> C \> A – cannot be modeled by the BT
framework. Moreover, even if each individual has a consistent
(transitive) ranking, the aggregated preferences can exhibit
intransitivity. In fact, even a mixture of two BT models cannot be
parameterized by a single BT model.

To overcome this limitation, recent research has begun to explore
alignment under general preferences.  [Munos et al. (2024)](#bib.bib42);
[Swamy et al. (2024)](#bib.bib61) formulate this alignment problem as a
symmetric two-player zero-sum _alignment game_
([Definition 2](#Thmdefinition2 "Definition 2 (Alignment Game). ‣ 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
where both players’ strategies are LLMs, and their payoffs are
determined by the win rate against the opponent’s LLM according to the
preference model. The objective is to identify a Nash equilibrium
policy, which guarantees at least 50% win rate against any competing
policy. Existing algorithms for finding such robust policies present
significant challenges. In particular, current methods can suffer from
instability, often failing to converge, or may inadvertently optimize
for a solution to a modified version of the original problem. As a
result, these approaches may fail to guarantee the desired win rate
against arbitrary opponents, leaving robust alignment an open and active
area of research and motivating us to investigate the following
question:

Question: Is there an algorithm that _converges_ to the Nash equilibrium
policy of the alignment game
([Definition 2](#Thmdefinition2 "Definition 2 (Alignment Game). ‣ 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
thus guaranteeing 50% win-rate against any competing policy?

Our Contributions: We propose a novel meta-algorithm, the Convergent
Meta Alignment Algorithm (COMAL), that iteratively refines language
model policies by solving a regularized two-player zero-sum game at each
round, using the current policy as a reference point. The procedure at
each round is as follows:

_Step 1:_ In iteration $`t`$, solve a KL-regularized two-player zero-sum
game with respect to the reference policy
$`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi_{t-1}`$. Let $`\pi_{t}`$ be the Nash
equilibrium of this regularized game.

_Step 2:_ Update the reference policy $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ to the
current policy $`\pi_{t}`$ and repeat the process.

The rationale behind COMAL is that it is a practical implementation of
the Conceptual Prox-method ([Nemirovski, 2004](#bib.bib43)), a
_convergent_ algorithm for solving two-player zero-sum games, whether
regularized or not. Importantly, Step 1 can be implemented using the
$`\Prox`$ operator, a well-known concept in the optimization
literature ([Parikh et al., 2014](#bib.bib46)). A crucial observation we
make here is that many existing algorithms – including PPO ([Schulman
et al., 2017](#bib.bib56)), GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib57); [Guo
et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)), DPO ([Rafailov et al., 2024](#bib.bib51)),
IPO ([Azar et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)), SPPO ([Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib70)), REBEL ([Gao et al., 2024](#bib.bib23)),
DRO ([Richemond et al., 2024](#bib.bib53)), and INPO ([Zhang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib75)), _inter alia_ – can be interpreted as practical
implementations of the $`\Prox`$ operator in the context of LLM training
(see
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Practical methods for computing the prox operator ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
for a detailed discussion). As a result, COMAL is simple and can be
integrated with many existing methods designed for preference
optimization with minimal changes.

One significant departure of COMAL from existing methods for the
game-theoretic formulation of alignment is that we adaptively update the
reference policy rather than keeping it fixed. A potential concern with
this approach is that the policy might drift too far from the initial
policy, leading to instability and quality degradation. However, we
provide both theoretical guarantees and experimental evidence
demonstrating that this dynamic updating strategy consistently
_enhances_ model performance while maintaining stability.

Theoretical guarantee: Given any implementation of the $`\Prox`$
operator, COMAL provably converges to the Nash equilibrium policy in the
last iterate. While existing algorithms like iterative IPO ([Azar
et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)) and SPPO ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib70)) only
guarantee average-iterate convergence (which is impractical for LLMs) or
convergence to a KL-regularized Nash equilibrium ([Munos et al.,
2024](#bib.bib42); [Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib75)), COMAL is the
_first_ algorithm that has provable last-iterate convergence to the
unregularized Nash equilibrium.¹¹ 1 We remark that a concurrent
work ([Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib67)) proposes an algorithm based on
Magnetic Mirror Descent ([Sokota et al., 2023](#bib.bib58)) with
last-iterate convergence. Our algorithms and theirs are all variants of
the conceptual prox algorithm ([Nemirovski, 2004](#bib.bib43)). While
their theoretical results require solving a regularized game _exactly_,
we provide stronger results showing last-iterate convergence under the
more practical setting with only _approximate_ solutions (see
[Theorem 3](#Thmtheorem3 "Theorem 3 (COMAL with approximate regularized game solver). ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).
Their experiments and our experiments both confirm the effectiveness of
convergent regularized learning algorithms for LLM alignment.

Empirical improvements: We first conduct synthetic controlled
experiments on a $`3\times 3`$ two-player zero-sum alignment game and
demonstrate that COMAL is the only algorithm that converges to the Nash
equilibrium. Under realistic LLM training settings, in experiments with
Llama-3-8B-Instruct ([Dubey et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) and
Qwen2.5-7B ([Yang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib72)) on UltraFeedback ([Cui
et al., 2023](#bib.bib16)), COMAL achieves above 60% and 56% win rates,
respectively, against all compared algorithms according to the
preference oracle.

## 2 Background

We begin by introducing notation for language model alignment and
preference modeling. Let $`\Delta(\mathcal{Z})`$ denote the set of
distributions over a set $`\mathcal{Z}`$. Let $`\mathcal{X}`$ be the
instruction set with a fixed distribution
$`\rho\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})`$, and $`\mathcal{Y}`$ be the response set.
Given an instruction $`x\in\mathcal{X}`$, an LLM policy $`\pi`$
specifies an output distribution
$`\pi(\cdot\mid x)\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})`$. For
$`p,q\in\Delta(\mathcal{Z})`$, the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence is
$`\KL(p\|q):=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}p(z)\log\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}`$. The
sigmoid function is $`\sigma(x):=\frac{e^{x}}{e^{x}+1}`$. We use
$`\mathrm{supp}(p)`$ to denote the support of distribution $`p`$. This
paper focuses on general preference models.

###### Definition 1 (General Preference Model).

A general preference model
$`\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,1]`$
satisfies
$`\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=1-\mathbb{P}(y_{2}\succ y_{1}\mid x)`$.
When we query $`\mathbb{P}`$ with $`(x,y_{1},y_{2})`$, it outputs $`1`$
with probability $`\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)`$ meaning
$`y_{1}`$ is preferred over $`y_{2}`$, and it outputs $`0`$ otherwise.
The _win rate_ of $`\pi_{1}`$ over $`\pi_{2}`$ under preference model
$`\mathbb{P}`$ is
$`\mathbb{P}(\pi_{1}\succ\pi_{2})`$$`:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}{\mathchoice{\left[\mathbb{E}_{y_{1}\sim\pi_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{2}}{\mathchoice{\left[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)\right]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}}\right]}{[\mathbb{E}_{y_{1}\sim\pi_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{2}}{\mathchoice{\left[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)\right]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}}]}{[\mathbb{E}_{y_{1}\sim\pi_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{2}}{\mathchoice{\left[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)\right]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}}]}{[\mathbb{E}_{y_{1}\sim\pi_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{2}}{\mathchoice{\left[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)\right]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}{[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)]}}]}}`$.

### 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models

The Bradley-Terry (BT) model, while widely used in preference modeling,
has fundamental limitations that restrict its ability to capture the
full complexity of human preferences such as intransitive preferences,
especially when aggregating preferences across diverse populations or
when dealing with nuanced, context-dependent decisions ([Munos et al.,
2024](#bib.bib42); [Swamy et al., 2024](#bib.bib61)). To address these
limitations and achieve alignment with general preferences, following
([Munos et al., 2024](#bib.bib42); [Swamy et al., 2024](#bib.bib61)), we
model the policy optimization problem as a two-player zero-sum game.

###### Definition 2 (Alignment Game).

The _alignment game_ is a two-player zero-sum game with objective

|     |                                                                                    |     |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}):=\mathbb{P}(\pi_{1}\succ\pi_{2})-\frac{1}{2}.`$ |     | (1) |

The constant $`\frac{1}{2}`$ is introduced only to ensure the game is
zero-sum and it has no other effect. We focus on policies with
$`\Pi:=\{\pi:\mathrm{supp}(\pi)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})\}`$
in the support of the initial policy. A Nash equilibrium policy is
$`(\pi_{1}^{\star},\pi_{2}^{\star})\in\argmax_{\pi_{1}\in\Pi}\argmin_{\pi_{2}\in\Pi}J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$
and satisfies
$`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}^{\star})\leq J(\pi_{1}^{\star},\pi_{2}^{\star})\leq J(\pi_{1}^{\star},\pi_{2}),\forall\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi.`$

In this game, the max-player controls $`\pi_{1}`$ and tries to maximize
$`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$ while the min-player controls $`\pi_{2}`$ and
tries to minimize $`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$. Since the game for two players
$`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$ is symmetric ([Ye et al., 2024](#bib.bib73)), the
game has a symmetric Nash equilibrium $`(\pi^{\star},\pi^{\star})`$.
Moreover, the Nash equilibrium policy $`\pi^{\star}`$ guarantees that
for any other policy $`\pi`$, its win rate is at least
$`\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\star}\succ\pi)\geq\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\star}\succ\pi^{\star})=50\%`$.
Our goal is to find a Nash equilibrium policy.

Existing online iterative preference optimization methods designed for
or applicable to the original game, including iterative IPO ([Azar
et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)) and SPPO ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib70)), are
based on Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU, definition in
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
and thus _diverge in the last iterate_ as we show in

the average iterate, i.e., the uniform mixture of training iterates,
which is not used in practice. The last iterate is widely used in

results ([Munos et al., 2024](#bib.bib42); [Wu et al.,

to design principled algorithms with provable last-iterate convergence

### 3.1 COMAL

We now introduce COMAL, our meta-algorithm for preference-based policy
optimization, inspired by the conceptual prox-method ([Nemirovski,
2004](#bib.bib43)) from convex optimization and game theory. The
prox-method has recently demonstrated strong practical performance in
computing Nash equilibria for large-scale two-player zero-sum
games ([Perolat et al., 2021](#bib.bib48); [Song et al.,
2020](#bib.bib59); [Abe et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)) and has proven highly
effective for the training of advanced game-theoretic AI
systems ([Perolat et al., 2022](#bib.bib49)). Here, we adapt this
framework into an online iterative procedure that guarantees convergence
to the Nash equilibrium in the alignment game
$`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$ ([1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ Definition 2 (Alignment Game). ‣ 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).

$`\mathbb{P}`$, regularization $`\tau>0`$, number of iterations
$`T\geq 1`$

for _$`t=1,2,\ldots,T-1`$_ do

#### Algorithmic Structure and Motivation.

At each iteration $`t`$, COMAL formulates and solves a _regularized
zero-sum game_, defined by the objective
$`J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$ ([2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
where the regularization encourages policies to remain close (in KL
divergence) to a reference policy $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$. Specifically,
the next policy $`\pi^{t+1}`$ is identified as a Nash equilibrium of
this regularized game, with the current reference set to
$`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi^{t}`$. (See
Algorithm [2](#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
and further exposition in
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).
After convergence within this regularized subproblem, the reference
policy is _updated_ to the newly computed $`\pi^{t+1}`$ (the latest
iterate): $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\leftarrow\pi^{t+1}`$, and the process
repeats. This mechanism operationalizes a central insight of proximal
algorithms: by updating the regularization center only when a
regularized Nash equilibrium is reached, we ensure stable yet
progressive movement toward the Nash equilibrium.

Convergence and Monotonicity Guarantee. A key property of COMAL is that
the KL divergence to the Nash equilibrium policy $`\pi^{\star}`$ of the
orginal game is monotonically
non-increasing:$`\KL(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}\|\pi^{t}).`$
This holds for any choice of $`\tau>0`$
([Lemma 4](#Thmlemma4 "Lemma 4. ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
permitting the regularization strength to be adaptively adjusted during
training without requiring a vanishing decay schedule. Each iteration
thus provably brings the policy closer to the original Nash solution,
justifying the update of the reference policy.

###### Theorem 1.

We assume that there exists a Nash equilibrium $`\pi^{\star}`$ of
$`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$ (defined in
([1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ Definition 2 (Alignment Game). ‣ 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")))
such that
$`\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\star})=\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$. In
every iteration $`t\geq 1`$, it holds that
$`\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})`$. Moreover,
COMAL has last-iterate convergence, i.e.,
$`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\pi^{t}`$ exists and is a Nash equilibrium.

Moreover, while prior works ([Perolat et al., 2021](#bib.bib48); [Abe
et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib67)) require each
regularized game to be solved _exactly_, we prove a stronger result
([Theorem 3](#Thmtheorem3 "Theorem 3 (COMAL with approximate regularized game solver). ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")):
last-iterate convergence holds even when each regularized game is solved
only _approximately_, as long as sufficient progress is made at each
stage. This makes our result more robust and practical. Formal
statements and proofs are provided in
§[D](#A4 "Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
We also give non-asymptotic convergence rate in
[Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2. ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").

Relation to Previous Work. Prior iterative approaches to
general-preference policy optimization—such as mirror descent-style
algorithms ([Azar et al., 2024](#bib.bib3); [Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib70))—typically only guarantee that the _average_ iterate
converges (in mixture) to a Nash policy. However, in practice, averaging
across many deep neural network checkpoints is both storage- and
deployment-inefficient and uncommon. Furthermore, existing methods for
last-iterate convergence apply only to _regularized_ games ([Munos
et al., 2024](#bib.bib42); [Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib75)), yielding
stationary points that may diverge from true Nash equilibria of the
alignment game (see also
Table [4](#A1.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Last-Iterate Convergence in Games ‣ Appendix A Related Work ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).
In contrast, COMAL is the first framework to attain fully practical and
provable last-iterate convergence to the Nash equilibrium of the
alignment game, even in large-scale LLM contexts. The convergence in
alignment game without regularization is crucial to ensure 50% win rate
against any other policy.

Practical Instantiation. Each COMAL iteration involves solving a
regularized zero-sum game
$`J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$, for which many policy
optimization algorithms originally developed for RLHF and preference
learning (e.g., PPO, DPO, IPO, INPO) can serve as efficient sub-solvers;
see
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
for discussion and
§[F](#A6 "Appendix F Computing the Prox Operator using Preference Learning Methods ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
for variants. While the theoretical properties of COMAL provide a strong
foundation, its practical implementation and empirical validation in
large-scale LLM alignment constitute a central contribution of this
work. We show that COMAL can be instantiated with, for example,
*INPO* ([Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib75)) as the regularized game
solver
(Algorithm [3](#algorithm3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ 3.3 Practical methods for computing the prox operator ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
yielding substantial and consistent performance gains across challenging
alignment benchmarks. Our results demonstrate that COMAL not only offers
strong convergence guarantees but is also easy to deploy, highly
scalable, and effective for real-world preference optimization and LLM
fine-tuning. Notably, integrating COMAL into existing pipelines
typically requires only minimal modifications—chiefly, adding periodic
reference policy updates and an outer iteration loop—making it directly
compatible with current large-scale alignment workflows. We note that
the per-iteration computational cost of our algorithm is comparable to
other alignment algorithms tested in our experiments—differing by only a
few percent—while achieving better performance without significant
computational overhead.

### 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game

Each iteration of COMAL requires solving a regularized zero-sum game
$`J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$. We present Mirror
Descent (MD) in
Algorithm [2](#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
for computing a Nash equilibrium of this game. MD builds on the prox
operator, a principled tool from convex optimization that ensures
stability and supports broad applicability. Importantly, we later show
that this prox operator can be instantiated using a variety of modern
policy optimization algorithms. For simplicity, we consider policies
$`\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})`$ and omit dependence on the instruction
$`x`$; all discussions extend naturally to the contextual setting.

Input: Reference policy $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$, preference oracle
$`\mathbb{P}`$, regularization $`\tau>0`$, step size $`\eta>0`$, number
of iterations $`K\geq 1`$

Output: Regularized Nash equilibrium policy $`\mu_{K}`$

Initialize $`\mu^{1}\leftarrow\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$

for _$`k=1,2,\ldots,K-1`$_ do

   $`g^{k}_{\tau}\leftarrow\nabla_{\mu^{k}}J_{\tau}(\mu^{k},\mu^{k},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\mu_{k})-\tau{\mathchoice{\left(\log\frac{\mu_{k}(\cdot)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot)}+1\right)}{(\log\frac{\mu_{k}(\cdot)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot)}+1)}{(\log\frac{\mu_{k}(\cdot)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot)}+1)}{(\log\frac{\mu_{k}(\cdot)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot)}+1)}}`$
// Gradient

   $`\mu^{k+1}\leftarrow\Prox(\mu_{k},\eta g^{k}_{\tau})`$

return $`\mu_{K}`$

Algorithm 2 Regularized game solver for
$`J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$ –
$`\argmax_{\pi_{1}}\min_{\pi_{2}}J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$

Mirror Descent and Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU). Mirror Descent
(MD) is a foundational family of iterative optimization algorithms,
widely used in game theory, machine learning, and online
learning ([Nemirovskij and Yudin, 1983](#bib.bib44)). At a high level,
MD generalizes vanilla gradient descent by using a geometry-aware update
rule that better respects the structure of the optimization domain
through a more flexible notion of ‘distance,’ defined by a regularizer.
A particularly important special case is the _Multiplicative Weights
Update_ (MWU) algorithm ([Arora et al., 2012](#bib.bib2)), which can be
viewed as Mirror Descent performed with the negative entropy
regularizer. For concreteness, suppose we want to maximize some smooth
objective $`f(\pi)`$ over probabilistic policies $`\pi`$. At iteration
$`t`$, with current policy $`\pi^{t}`$, MWU computes the updated policy
$`\pi^{t+1}`$ as the solution to:

|     |                                                                                                                                            |     |                     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | ------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi^{t+1}:=\Prox(\pi^{t},\nabla f(\pi^{T})):=\argmax\_{\pi}\left\{\langle\nabla f(\pi^{t}),\pi\rangle-\eta^{-1}\cdot\KL(\pi |     | \pi^{t})\right\},`$ |     | (3) |

where $`\eta`$ is a positive parameter (step size), and
$`\KL(\cdot\|\cdot)`$ is the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, which in
this case measures how much the new policy deviates from the previous
one. Intuitively, this update chooses a new policy by trading off
following the gradient of $`f`$ with staying close (in KL) to the prior
policy, preventing overly aggressive changes that could destabilize
learning. This update can be viewed more generally through the lens of
the _proximal operator_ (or _prox operator_)—a mathematical abstraction
that unifies many optimization steps used in machine learning, including
projected gradient descent and mirror descent with Bregman
divergences ([Parikh et al., 2014](#bib.bib46)). We include a detailed
discussion on the prox operator in
§[C](#A3 "Appendix C Prox Operator ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").

Non-asymptotic Convergence. Denote $`\pi^{\star}_{\tau}`$ the Nash
equilibrium of the KL regularized game
$`J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$, which is
$`\tau`$-strongly monotone. We can apply existing results to show that
MWU
(Algorithm [2](#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))
achieves linear last-iterate convergence rate: the KL divergence to the
Nash equilibrium $`\pi^{\star}_{\tau}`$ decreases exponentially fast.
The proof is in
§[E](#A5 "Appendix E Proof of ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
[Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2. ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
also implies a non-asymptotic convergence to an approximate Nash
equilibrium: we can choose $`\tau=O(\varepsilon)`$ and approaching an
$`\varepsilon`$-approximate Nash equilibrium of the original alignment
game ([1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ Definition 2 (Alignment Game). ‣ 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))
in $`\tilde{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2})`$ iterations.

###### Theorem 2.

For step size $`0<\eta\leq\frac{\tau}{\tau^{2}+0.5}`$,
Algorithm [2](#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
guarantees for every $`k\geq 1`$,
$`\KL(\pi^{\star}_{\tau}||\mu^{k+1})\leq(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2})^{k}\KL(\pi^{\star}_{\tau}||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$.

existing methods designed for preference optimization with minimal

Our unified view on existing diverse preference methods through the
perspective of computing the prox operator opens the possibility of
applying other algorithms from online learning and optimization to
robust LLM alignment. We include implementations for two other
last-iterate convergent algorithms, the Mirror-Prox
algorithm ([Nemirovski, 2004](#bib.bib43)) and the Optimistic

## 4 Synthetic Experiments

We conduct experiments on a simple bandit problem with
$`\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{a},y_{b},y_{c}\}`$ and non-BT preference model over
$`\mathcal{Y}`$. Specifically, we set
$`\mathbb{P}[y_{b}\succ y_{a}]=\mathbb{P}[y_{c}\succ y_{b}]=0.9`$ and
$`\mathbb{P}[y_{a}\succ y_{c}]=0.8`$. Observe that the preference is
intransitive and exhibits a preference cycle
$`y_{c}\succ y_{b}\succ y_{a}\succ y_{c}`$. The detailed setup and
result analysis are in
§[I](#A9 "Appendix I Synthetic Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
and
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
[3](#A9.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Appendix I Synthetic Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
and
[4](#A9.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Appendix I Synthetic Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
Due to the space limit, we only briefly discuss the results here. Our
experiments show that iterative DPO, iterative IPO ([Azar et al.,
2024](#bib.bib3)), and SPPO ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib70)) all cycle
and diverge away from the unique Nash equilibrium. The INPO algorithm
converges in the modified game as we show in
[Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2. ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
However, the converging point is not the Nash equilibrium of the
original game and suffers a constant equilibrium gap. COMAL is the only
algorithm that converges to the Nash equilibrium.

### 5.1 Experimental Settings

Instruction Set. Our training experiments are conducted on the 64K
instructions from the UltraFeedback dataset, which covers a broad range
of instruction types and is well-suited and widely used for studying
preference optimization in practical scenarios.

Preference Oracle. We choose a mixture of two BT reward models as the
preference oracle to simulate the preference diversity among human
annotators. Specifically, the win rate of an output $`y_{a}`$ over
$`y_{b}`$ parameterized by a mixture of two BT reward models $`r_{1}`$
and $`r_{2}`$ is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
P(y_{a}>y_{b})=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{e^{r_{1}(y_{a})}}{e^{r_{1}(y_{a})}+e^{r_{1}(y_{b})}}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{e^{r_{2}(y_{a})}}{e^{r_{2}(y_{a})}+e^{r_{2}(y_{b})}}.
``` |  | (4) |

The two reward models used are Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B-v0.2 ([Liu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)) and ArmoRM-Llama3-8B-v0.1 ([Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib66)), both achieving strong performance on various human
preference alignment benchmarks in RewardBench ([Lambert et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib33)).

Preference Data Generation. To construct the preference data, i.e.,
output pairs with a preference annotation specifying which one is
better, we adopt the setting of [Zhang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib75) by
sampling 5 candidate outputs for each instruction with a temperature of
0.8 and applying the preference oracle to select the best and the worst
candidates to form a data point.

Baselines. The following baselines are compared: (1) BASE:
Llama-3-8B-Instruct, which has already been fine-tuned, can be directly
used as the base model following SimPO ([Meng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib38)). For Qwen2.5-7B, we finetune it using the standard
SFT objective on the Tulu3 SFT dataset ([Lambert et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib32)). (2) vanilla DPO ([Rafailov et al.,
2024](#bib.bib51)) and (3) vanilla IPO ([Azar et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)),
where one training iteration is performed over the entire instruction
set of UltraFeedback with output pairs sampled from the BASE policy; (5)
INPO ([Zhang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib75))(Algorithm [4](#algorithm4 "Algorithm 4 ‣ F.1 COMAL integrated with INPO ‣ Appendix F Computing the Prox Operator using Preference Learning Methods ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
where each iteration of training is performed on a single data subset;
(6) Iterative IPO (Iter-IPO), which follows a training setting similar
to INPO but without the KL regularization with respect to the static
reference policy.

Training Details. To reduce computational cost, the instructions in
UltraFeedBack are divided into six equal subsets (10K each), with one
subset used per training iteration. For iterative optimization
algorithms, 18 training iterations are performed. All iterative
optimization algorithms compared have similar computational costs, each
taking around 100 hours on 8 NVIDIA A6000 GPUs. To the best of our
knowledge, multi-iteration training like ours has rarely been explored
in previous work. For example, INPO only trained up to 3 iterations,
equivalent to just one full round over UltraFeedback’s instructions. The
overall update process is as follows: (1) Iter-IPO: at each iteration,
the reference policy in the IPO loss
(Eq. [13](#A6.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ IPO for computing Prox for unregularized preferences ‣ Appendix F Computing the Prox Operator using Preference Learning Methods ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))
is updated to the policy produced in the previous iteration; (2) INPO:
at each iteration, one optimization step in
Algorithm [4](#algorithm4 "Algorithm 4 ‣ F.1 COMAL integrated with INPO ‣ Appendix F Computing the Prox Operator using Preference Learning Methods ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
is performed, with the reference policy fixed to the BASE policy; (3)
COMAL: as outlined in
Algorithm [3](#algorithm3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ 3.3 Practical methods for computing the prox operator ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
COMAL uses INPO as a sub-routine, and updates the reference policy in
INPO every 6 iterations, i.e., an entire pass of the instruction set.

Hyper-Parameters. We conduct a grid search for the KL regularization
strength, $`\eta^{-1}`$, for DPO, IPO and INPO, within the range of
0.001 - 0.1. The value of $`\tau`$ in INPO
([Equation 14](#A6.E14 "In INPO for computing Prox for regularized preferences ‣ Appendix F Computing the Prox Operator using Preference Learning Methods ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))
is determined by following [Zhang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib75), where
$`\eta\tau`$ is set to a fixed ratio, $`1/3`$. We found Iter-IPO and
INPO achieve the best performance when $`\eta^{-1}`$ is 0.002. However,
in Llama-3 training, we observe rapid performance degradation of both
algorithms after 6 training iterations. Therefore, to study the
algorithms’ behavior in more training iterations, we perform additional
experiments with $`\eta^{-1}`$ set to 0.1 (Iter-IPO-L and INPO-L), which
leads to stabler training. For COMAL, since it involves multi-round INPO
training with adjustable KL regularization strengths
(Algorithm [3](#algorithm3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ 3.3 Practical methods for computing the prox operator ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
we set $`\eta^{-1}`$ to 0.002 for the first INPO training round (i.e., 6
iterations) and adjust it to 0.1 for the subsequent two rounds,
balancing training stability with efficiency. In Qwen2.5 training,
$`\eta^{-1}`$ is fixed to 0.002 for all algorithms since the training
process remains stable. More details are in
[Appendix J](#A10 "Appendix J Hyperparameters and Training Details for LLM Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").

Evaluations. We use the instructions in a widely used benchmark,
AlpacaEval ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib34)), to construct the test set,
since these instructions cover various task scenarios. However, instead
of using GPT-4, the default evaluator for the AlpacaEval benchmark, we
chose to use the same preference oracle used during training as the
evaluator. This follows the setting of previous work ([Munos et al.,
2024](#bib.bib42)), which provides a controlled experimental setting,
ensuring that the preference oracle the model learns to fit is also the
one used to evaluate its performance.

algorithms when using Llama-3-8B-Instruct, and 56.9% with Qwen2.5-7B,
demonstrating its effectiveness.

presents the training dynamics of three iterative preference
optimization algorithms, where the average win rate is computed against
all the algorithms in
[Table 1](#S5.T1 "In 5.1 Experimental Settings ‣ 5 LLM-Based Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
and
[Table 2](#S5.T2 "In 5.1 Experimental Settings ‣ 5 LLM-Based Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
We note that:

\(1\) COMAL consistently outperforms other algorithms, showing steady
improvements even in the late stages of the training period.

\(2\) Both Iter-IPO and INPO exhibit rapid degradation at the 7th
training iteration in Llama-3 training. We posit that this is because
Llama-3-8B-Instruct has already undergone extensive post-training,
making further optimization more delicate. Training with a larger
KL-regularization with Llama-3-8B-Instruct leads to stabler training for
both Iter-IPO(-L) and INPO(-L). However, it also introduces a lower
performance upper bound. As discussed above, COMAL overcomes this
limitation by dynamically adjusting the strength of the
KL-regularization.

Evaluation Results on Standard Benchmarks. To verify that the
checkpoints produced by our algorithm retain general capabilities, we
compare their performance against the baselines on six standard LLM
benchmarks as a sanity check. These include GSM8K for math problem
solving ([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib15)), MMLU for multi-task language
understanding ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](#bib.bib28)), BigBench Hard
(BBH) for reasoning ([Suzgun et al., 2023](#bib.bib60)), HumanEval for
coding ([Chen et al., 2021](#bib.bib13)), and two LLM alignment
evaluation benchmarks, AlpacaEval and Arena-Hard, where the original
evaluator, GPT-4, is used. The results in
[Table 3](#S5.T3 "In 5.1 Experimental Settings ‣ 5 LLM-Based Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
highlighting two findings:

\(1\) COMAL maintains comparable performance on standard academic
benchmarks; (2) While not optimized for GPT-4’s preferences, COMAL
performs strongly on AlpacaEval and Arena-Hard compared to the
baselines, indicating its generalizability. We note that COMAL does not
outperform Iter-IPO on Arena-Hard. However, as noted above, we compare
Iter-IPO at its best checkpoint, whereas COMAL is evaluated at the final
checkpoint, because Iter-IPO’s performance declines near the end of
training
([Figure 2](#S5.F2 "In 5.1 Experimental Settings ‣ 5 LLM-Based Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).
Moreover, since Arena-Hard compares each model only against a fixed
baseline (GPT-4), its setup does not fully align with COMAL’s objective.

Discussion on Updating the Reference Policy. Our theoretical analysis in
[Section 3](#S3 "3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
indicates the reference policy in COMAL’s objective needs to be updated
in order to converge to the alignment game
([Equation 1](#S2.E1 "In Definition 2 (Alignment Game). ‣ 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).
Emprically, it means that COMAL does not have a KL-regularization from a
static reference policy. However, as shown in
[Table 3](#S5.T3 "In 5.1 Experimental Settings ‣ 5 LLM-Based Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
COMAL does not suffer substantially from the “alignment tax” ([Dong
et al., 2024](#bib.bib18); [Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib45)). Moreover,
we observe that its improvement is not solely from relaxing the
KL-constraint – Iter-IPO has even smaller constraints from a reference
policy updated at each iteration, but fails to outperform COMAL and
suffers from training instability.

## 6 Conclusion

We have proposed COMAL, a meta-algorithm for preference optimization
that provably converges to the Nash equilibrium policy in the last
iterate. We have provided a theoretical analysis of the properties of
COMAL and have empirically demonstrated its effectiveness under both
synthetic and real-world experimental settings, where COMAL consistently
maintains a win rate above 50% against other policies in controlled
settings. We believe COMAL has significant potential to enhance the
performance of LLMs in the alignment fine-tuning setting, due to its
theoretical guarantees and flexibility, as it can be integrated with
existing learning algorithms while overcoming their limitations.

## Appendix A Related Work

#### Alignment under Preference models

Most existing approaches adopt the Bradley-Terry (BT) preference model
\[[Bradley and Terry, 1952](#bib.bib5), [Christiano et al.,
2017](#bib.bib14)\], which involves first learning a preference model
and then optimizing the objective function with a KL divergence penalty
relative to the original language model. For example, RLHF \[[Ouyang
et al., 2022](#bib.bib45)\] aims to ensure that LLMs follow instructions
by initially learning a BT model and subsequently fine-tuning the model
based on the learned reward while regularizing it with the original LLM.

Building on this framework, [Rafailov et al. \[2024\]](#bib.bib51)
introduces Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) that maintains the
assumption of the BT model for preferences but eliminates the preference
learning step by reformulating the objective and optimizing it directly.
Additionally, [Ethayarajh et al. \[2024\]](#bib.bib20) diverges from the
traditional BT-based methods by deriving algorithms that bypass the
preference modeling step altogether. Instead, they model user
preferences based on Kahneman and Tversky’s utility theory.

#### Alignment Solution Concepts under General Preferences

[Azar et al. \[2024\]](#bib.bib3) is the first to consider general
preferences. They propose the IPO algorithm, an offline algorithm that
directly optimizes the win rate of the model penalized by the KL
divergence with respect to the original model. [Munos et al.
\[2024\]](#bib.bib42) also consider general preferences and aim to find
the *von Neumann winner*, which corresponds to the Nash equilibrium of a
game played between the two LLMs over the win rate. They propose a
variant of the Mirror Descent (MD) algorithm called Nash-MD and show
last-iterate convergence in the KL-regularized game. Concurrently,
[Swamy et al. \[2024\]](#bib.bib61) study the same solution concept
focusing more on sequential games. [Calandriello et al.
\[2024\]](#bib.bib12) proved that the objective of the the IPO algorithm
coincides with the Nash policy under a proper choice of the parameter
that controls the regularization.

#### Iterative Self-Play Algorithms

Apart from the aforementioned works, recent research has also proposed
practical implementations of Mirror Descent (MD) algorithms, which can
be used to learn Nash equilibria through self-play. [Rosset et al.
\[2024\]](#bib.bib55) propose Direct Nash Optimization (DNO), where, at
each iteration, the model regresses predicted preferences against actual
preferences using cross-entropy loss. Similarly, [Wu et al.
\[2024\]](#bib.bib70) introduces the Self-Play Preference Optimization
(SPPO) method, [Gao et al. \[2024\]](#bib.bib23) introduces
Reinforcement Learning via Regressing Relative Rewards (REBEL), and
[Richemond et al. \[2024\]](#bib.bib53) introduces the Direct Reward
Optimization (DRO) which regresses the loss using the $`\ell_{2}`$
distance at each iteration. Since these algorithms simulate the MD
update, when applied in a two-player zero-sum game, they only have
average-iterate convergence but all *diverge in the last iterate*.
Moreover, all these methods require the estimation of the win rate,
which can be computationally expensive.

Most closely related to our work is Iterative Nash Policy Optimization
(INPO) by [Zhang et al. \[2025b\]](#bib.bib75), which continues to use
$`\ell_{2}`$ distance regression. However, by further reformulating and
simplifying the objective in a manner similar to IPO, INPO eliminates
the need to estimate the expected win rate. The primary distinction
between our approach and INPO is that INPO is designed for the
KL-regularized game and is equivalent to MD; while our algorithm COMAL
is inspired by the Conceptual Prox algorithm and guarantees last-iterate
convergence in the original game. This fundamental difference allows
COMAL to achieve more favorable convergence properties and outperform
INPO, achieving a win rate strictly greater than 50% against it.

#### Last-Iterate Convergence in Games

Mirror Descent fails to converge in simple zero-sum games, often
resulting in cycling behavior \[[Mertikopoulos et al.,
2018](#bib.bib39)\]. In contrast, several algorithms have been shown to
achieve last-iterate convergence including the Proximal Point (PP)
method \[[Rockafellar, 1976](#bib.bib54)\], Extra-Gradient (EG)
\[[Korpelevich, 1976](#bib.bib31)\], Optimistic Gradient Descent (OGD)
\[[Popov, 1980](#bib.bib50), [Rakhlin and Sridharan,
2013](#bib.bib52)\], and the Conceptual Prox/Mirror Prox
methods \[[Nemirovski, 2004](#bib.bib43)\]. The asymptotic convergence
properties of these algorithms have been extensively studied \[[Popov,
1980](#bib.bib50), [Facchinei and Pang, 2003](#bib.bib21), [Iusem
et al., 2003](#bib.bib30), [Nemirovski, 2004](#bib.bib43), [Daskalakis
and Panageas, 2018](#bib.bib17)\]. Recently, there has been a growing
focus on establishing finite-time convergence guarantees for these
methods, addressing the practical necessity of understanding their
performance within a limited number of iterations (see e.g., \[[Mokhtari
et al., 2020b](#bib.bib41), [Mokhtari et al., 2020a](#bib.bib40),
[Golowich et al., 2020b](#bib.bib25), [Golowich et al.,
2020a](#bib.bib24), [Perolat et al., 2021](#bib.bib48), [Bauschke
et al., 2021](#bib.bib4), [Wei et al., 2021](#bib.bib68), [Cai et al.,

## Appendix B Additional Backgrounds

A special case of the general preference model is the Bradley-Terry (BT)
model, which assumes a reward function parameterizes the preference.

###### Definition 3 (Bradley-Terry Model).

A preference model $`\mathbb{P}`$ satisfies the *Bradley-Terry (BT)
assumption* if there exists a reward function
$`r^{*}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ such that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\exp{\mathchoice{\left(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\right)}{(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}{(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}{(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}}}{\exp{\mathchoice{\left(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\right)}{(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}{(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}{(r^{*}(x,y_{1}))}}+\exp{\mathchoice{\left(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\right)}{(r^{*}(x,y_{2}))}{(r^{*}(x,y_{2}))}{(r^{*}(x,y_{2}))}}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sigma(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})).`$ |  |

## Appendix C Prox Operator

Prox Operator and Bregman Divergence. To define the prox operator, we
first introduce the *Bregman divergence*, which generalizes the notion
of squared distance. For a convex function
$`\varphi:\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ (called the regularizer),
the Bregman divergence between $`z`$ and $`z^{\prime}`$ is defined as
$`D_{\varphi}(z\|z^{\prime}):=\varphi(z)-\varphi(z^{\prime})-\langle\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime}),z-z^{\prime}\rangle`$,
where $`\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime})`$ is the gradient of $`\varphi`$ at
$`z^{\prime}`$. The *prox operator* then takes a current point
$`z\in\mathcal{Z}`$ and a (sub)gradient direction
$`g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}`$, and returns the next point according to:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{Prox}(z,g):=\argmax_{z^{\prime}}\left\{\langle g,z^{\prime}\rangle-D_{\varphi}(z^{\prime}\|z)\right\}.
``` |  |

This formulation interpolates between moving in the direction of $`g`$
and staying close to $`z`$, as measured by the Bregman divergence for
the chosen regularizer. Two important *special cases* are

- •
  When $`\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{2}\|z\|^{2}`$ (the squared Euclidean norm),
  $`D_{\varphi}`$ is just the squared distance, and the prox operator
  reduces to the usual projected gradient step.
- •
  When $`\varphi(z)`$ is the negative entropy (as in MWU), the Bregman
  divergence is the KL divergence, leading to updates appropriate for
  probability distributions.

In our framework, we will instantiate the prox operator with choices of
$`\varphi`$ and $`g`$ that map directly onto concrete policy-learning
algorithms. In this paper, when we refer to the prox operator, we use
the negative entropy regularizer
$`\varphi(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z[i]\ln z[i]`$, for which the corresponding
Bregman divergence $`D_{\varphi}`$ is the KL divergence. Under this
choice, the MWU update in
Equation ([3](#S3.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))
is equivalent to the prox-form update
$`\pi^{t+1}=\Prox(\pi^{t},\eta\nabla f(\pi^{t}))`$.

### C.1 Properties of the Prox Operator

Recall that
$`\Prox(z,g)=\argmax_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}{\mathchoice{\left\langle g,z^{\prime}\right\rangle}{\langle g,z^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle g,z^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle g,z^{\prime}\rangle}}-D_{\varphi}(z^{\prime}||z)=\argmax_{z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}{\mathchoice{\left\langle g+\nabla\varphi(z),z^{\prime}\right\rangle}{\langle g+\nabla\varphi(z),z^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle g+\nabla\varphi(z),z^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle g+\nabla\varphi(z),z^{\prime}\rangle}}-\varphi(z^{\prime})`$.
The following properties of the prox operator are well-known in the
literature(e.g., \[[Nemirovski, 2004](#bib.bib43)\])

###### Lemma 1.

$`\Prox(z,g)=z^{\prime}`$ if and only if
$`{\mathchoice{\left\langle g+\nabla\varphi(z)-\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime}-z^{*}\right\rangle}{\langle g+\nabla\varphi(z)-\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime}-z^{*}\rangle}{\langle g+\nabla\varphi(z)-\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime}-z^{*}\rangle}{\langle g+\nabla\varphi(z)-\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime}),z^{\prime}-z^{*}\rangle}}\geq 0`$
for all $`z^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}`$.

###### Corollary 1.

Let $`\Prox(z,g)=z^{\prime}`$, then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\left\langle g,z^{*}-z^{\prime}\right\rangle}{\langle g,z^{*}-z^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle g,z^{*}-z^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle g,z^{*}-z^{\prime}\rangle}}\leq D_{\varphi}(z^{*}||z)-D_{\varphi}(z^{*}||z^{\prime})-D_{\varphi}(z^{\prime}||z),\quad\forall z^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}`$ |  |

## Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL

The proof of
[Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1. ‣ Algorithmic Structure and Motivation. ‣ 3.1 COMAL ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
is largely inspired by existing results for the conceptual prox
algorithm in the literature \[[Facchinei and Pang, 2003](#bib.bib21),
[Nemirovski, 2004](#bib.bib43)\]. We first consider the case where each
step of COMAL,
$`\pi^{t+1}\leftarrow\argmax_{\pi_{1}}\min_{\pi_{2}}J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$,
can be solved *exactly* in
[Section D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
We then extend the proof to the case where we only solve the regularized
game *approximately* in
[Section D.2](#A4.SS2 "D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
In both cases, we prove last-iterate convergence to Nash equilibrium,
i.e., $`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\pi^{t}`$ exists and is a Nash
equilibrium. The proof for the latter case seems to be the first in the
literature.

In
[Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1. ‣ Algorithmic Structure and Motivation. ‣ 3.1 COMAL ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we make the following assumption.

###### Assumption 1.

We assume there exists a Nash equilibrium $`\pi^{\star}`$ such that
$`\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\star})=\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$.

This assumption is mild and much weaker than the “Bounded Log Density"
assumptions used in previous works \[[Rosset et al., 2024](#bib.bib55),
[Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib75)\], which directly assumes
$`|\log\frac{\pi^{t}}{\pi_{\mathrm{init}}}|`$ is bounded.

### D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions

Recall that
$`\Pi:=\{\pi:\mathrm{supp}(\pi)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})\}`$.
Then
$`\KL(\pi||\pi_{\mathrm{init}})\leq D:=\max_{y:\pi_{\mathrm{init}}(y)>0}\log\pi_{\mathrm{init}}(y)`$
is bounded for any $`\pi\in\Pi`$. We first prove
$`\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})`$ for any
$`t\geq 1`$.

###### Lemma 2.

Let $`\pi^{\star}`$ be an Nash equilibrium of $`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$.
Then for any $`\tau>0`$, if

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(\pi,\pi)=\argmax_{\pi_{1}\in\Pi}\argmin_{\pi_{2}\in\Pi}J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),
``` |  |

then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi)\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})-\KL(\pi||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
``` |  |

###### Proof.

By definition of the prox operator, we have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi`$ | $`\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi_{1}\in\Pi}J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi_{1}\in\Pi}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{1}\succ\pi)-\tau\KL(\pi_{1},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\Prox(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}},\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\pi)).`$ |  | (6) |

Using
[Corollary 1](#Thmcorollary1 "Corollary 1. ‣ C.1 Properties of the Prox Operator ‣ Appendix C Prox Operator ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we have for any $`\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}{\mathchoice{\left(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\prime}\succ\pi)-\mathbb{P}(\pi\succ\pi)\right)}{(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\prime}\succ\pi)-\mathbb{P}(\pi\succ\pi))}{(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\prime}\succ\pi)-\mathbb{P}(\pi\succ\pi))}{(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\prime}\succ\pi)-\mathbb{P}(\pi\succ\pi))}}\leq\KL(\pi^{\prime}||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})-\KL(\pi^{\prime}||\pi)-\KL(\pi||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).`$ |  | (7) |

Plugging $`\pi^{\prime}=\pi^{\star}`$ into the above inequality and
noting that $`\mathbb{P}(\pi\succ\pi)=\frac{1}{2}`$, we get

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}{\mathchoice{\left(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\star}\succ\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\right)}{(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\star}\succ\pi)-\frac{1}{2})}{(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\star}\succ\pi)-\frac{1}{2})}{(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\star}\succ\pi)-\frac{1}{2})}}\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})-\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi)-\KL(\pi||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).`$ |  |

Since $`\pi^{\star}`$ is a Nash equilibrium and thus
$`\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\star}\succ\pi)\geq\frac{1}{2}`$, the lefthand side of
the above inequality is $`\geq 0`$. Then we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi)\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})-\KL(\pi||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).`$ |  |

∎

[Lemma 2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma 2. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
implies the following properties on the trajectory $`\{\pi^{t}\}`$.

###### Corollary 2.

Denote $`\pi^{\star}`$ an Nash equilibrium such that
$`\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\star})=\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$ as
guaranteed by
[Assumption 1](#Thmassumption1 "Assumption 1. ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
Then the following holds for the trajectory $`\{\pi^{t}\}`$ produced by
COMAL:

- 1.
  $`\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})`$ for all
  $`t\geq 1`$.
- 2.
  $`\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{\mathrm{init}})<+\infty`$.
- 3.
  For all $`t\geq 1`$, it holds that for
  $`y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$, $`\pi^{t}(y)\geq c>0`$
  where $`c`$ is some constant $`c`$ depends only on $`\pi^{\star}`$ and
  $`\pi_{\mathrm{init}}`$. This also holds even for any limit point of
  $`\{\pi^{t}\}`$.

###### Proof.

The first item is direct from
[Lemma 2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma 2. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
The second item is also direct by applying
[Lemma 2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma 2. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
for $`t\geq 1`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})-\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{\mathrm{init}})\leq D<\infty.`$ |  |

Now we consider the third item. Define
$`D:=\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$ and
$`p_{\min}:=\min_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\star})}\pi^{\star}(y)`$. By
[Assumption 1](#Thmassumption1 "Assumption 1. ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
$`p_{\min}>0`$. Then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})\leq D`$ | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow p_{\min}\log\frac{p_{\min}}{\pi^{t}(y)}\leq D,\forall y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\star})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow\pi^{t}(y)\geq\frac{p_{\min}}{\exp(D/p_{\min})},\forall y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\star}).`$ |  |

Since the above holds for all $`\pi^{t}`$, it also holds for any limit
point of $`\{\pi^{t}\}`$. ∎

Since the sequence $`\{\pi^{t}\}`$ is bounded (all lies in the simplex),
it has at least one limit point $`\hat{\pi}`$. The next lemma shows that
a limit point must be a Nash equilibrium.

###### Lemma 3.

If $`\hat{\pi}`$ is a limit point of $`\{\pi^{t}\}`$, then $`\hat{\pi}`$
is a Nash equilibrium of $`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$.

###### Proof.

By item 2 in
[Corollary 2](#Thmcorollary2 "Corollary 2. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we have $`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})=0`$. This
implies
$`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\mathchoice{\left\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\right\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}}=0`$.
As $`\hat{\pi}`$ is a limit point of $`\{\pi^{t}\}`$, we let
$`\{\pi^{k}:k\in\kappa\}`$ be the subsequence that converges to
$`\hat{\pi}`$. Then by
[6](#A4.Ex10 "In Proof. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we have

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\lim_{k\in\kappa,k\rightarrow\infty}\pi^{k+1}=\lim_{k\in\kappa,k\rightarrow\infty}\Prox(\pi^{k},\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\pi^{k+1}))`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow`$ | $`\displaystyle\hat{\pi}=\Prox(\hat{\pi},\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\hat{\pi})).`$ |  |

Thus $`\hat{\pi}`$ is a fixed point of
$`\Prox(\pi,\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\pi)`$. Moreover, by item
3 in
[Corollary 2](#Thmcorollary2 "Corollary 2. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we have $`\mathrm{supp}(\hat{\pi})=\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$.
Now consider both the max and min player running MWU initialized with
$`\pi^{1}=\hat{\pi}`$. Then we have $`\pi^{t}=\hat{\pi}`$ for all
$`t\geq 1`$. By
[Equation 7](#A4.E7 "In Proof. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we have for any $`\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{\mathchoice{\left(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\prime}\succ\hat{\pi})-\frac{1}{2}\right)}{(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\prime}\succ\hat{\pi})-\frac{1}{2})}{(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\prime}\succ\hat{\pi})-\frac{1}{2})}{(\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\prime}\succ\hat{\pi})-\frac{1}{2})}}\leq\KL(\pi^{\prime}||\hat{\pi})<\infty,`$ |  |

where the inequality holds since
$`\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\hat{\pi})`$. As a
result, we get

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\pi^{\prime}\succ\hat{\pi})\leq\frac{1}{2},\forall\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi\Leftrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\hat{\pi}\succ\pi^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{2},\forall\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi`$ |  |

Thus $`\hat{\pi}`$ is a Nash equilibrium of $`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$. ∎

#### Proof of [Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1. ‣ Algorithmic Structure and Motivation. ‣ 3.1 COMAL ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")

By
[Lemma 3](#Thmlemma3 "Lemma 3. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we know a limit point $`\hat{\pi}`$ is a Nash equilibrium. Then by
[Corollary 2](#Thmcorollary2 "Corollary 2. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
$`\{\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t})\geq 0\}`$ is a decreasing sequence. Thus
$`\{\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t})\}`$ converges. Let
$`\{\pi^{k}:k\in\kappa\}`$ be a subsequence that converges to
$`\hat{\pi}`$. Then we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t})=\lim_{k\in\kappa,k\rightarrow\infty}\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{k})=\KL(\hat{\pi}||\hat{\pi})=0.`$ |  |

Thus we have $`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\pi^{t}=\hat{\pi}`$ is a Nash
equilibrium. This completed the proof of
[Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1. ‣ Algorithmic Structure and Motivation. ‣ 3.1 COMAL ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").

### D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions

This section considers the case where we can not solve the regularized
game $`J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$ exactly but only
compute an approximate solution. Specifically, we consider the following
inexact COMAL update: denote
$`\hat{\pi}^{t+1}=\argmax_{\pi_{1}\in\Pi}\min_{\pi_{2}\in\Pi}J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi^{t})`$
the exactly solution; the algorithm updates the next iterate
$`\pi^{t+1}`$ as an $`\varepsilon_{t}`$-approximate solution such that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\hat{\pi}^{t+1},\pi^{t+1})\leq\varepsilon_{t}=O{\mathchoice{\left(\frac{1}{t^{4}}\right)}{(\frac{1}{t^{4}})}{(\frac{1}{t^{4}})}{(\frac{1}{t^{4}})}}.`$ |  | (8) |

We note that we can compute $`\pi^{t+1}`$ within $`\varepsilon_{t}`$
error using $`O(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{t}})=O(\log t)`$ iterations of
Algorithm [2](#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
([Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2. ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).

We denote $`\Pi^{\star}`$ the set of Nash equilibria such that each
$`\pi^{\star}\in\Pi^{\star}`$ has support
$`\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\star})=\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$ as
guaranteed by
[Assumption 1](#Thmassumption1 "Assumption 1. ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
We introduce a few quantities that depend on the Nash equilibria and the
initial policy.

###### Definition 4.

We define the following constants.

1.  1.
    $`p_{\mathrm{sft}}:=\max\{p>0:\forall y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}}),\pi_{\mathrm{init}}(y)\geq p\}`$;
    $`D:=|\mathcal{Y}|\log\frac{1}{p_{\mathrm{sft}}}`$ so that
    $`\KL(\pi||\pi_{\mathrm{init}})\leq D`$ for all $`\pi\in\Pi`$
2.  2.
    $`p_{\min}:=\max\{p>0:\exists\pi^{\star}\in\Pi^{\star},\forall y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}}),\pi^{\star}(y)\geq p\}`$;
    Let $`\pi^{\star}\in\Pi^{\star}`$ be a Nash equilibrium so that
    $`\pi^{\star}(y)\geq p_{\min}`$ holds for all $`y`$ in its support.
3.  3.
    $`c_{1}:=\frac{p_{\min}}{\exp{(D+2)/p_{\min}}}`$ and
    $`c_{2}:=\frac{c_{1}}{\exp(1/c_{1})}`$.

Our main result is that if each optimization problem at iteration $`t`$
can be solved within approximation error
$`\varepsilon_{t}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{3t^{2}}`$, then COMAL converges in
last-iterate to a Nash equilibrium.

###### Theorem 3 (COMAL with approximate regularized game solver).

Assume
[Assumption 1](#Thmassumption1 "Assumption 1. ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
holds. If in each iteration $`t\geq 1`$, the returned iterate
$`\pi^{t+1}`$ is an $`\varepsilon_{t}`$-approximate solution to
$`J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi^{t})`$ as defined in
([8](#A4.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))
with $`\varepsilon_{t}\leq\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{9t^{4}}`$ ($`c_{1}`$ defined
in
[Definition 4](#Thmdefinition4 "Definition 4. ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
then $`\{x^{t}\}`$ converges to a Nash equilibrium of
$`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$.

We need the following technical lemma in the proof of
[Theorem 3](#Thmtheorem3 "Theorem 3 (COMAL with approximate regularized game solver). ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").

###### Lemma 4.

Let $`\varepsilon_{t}\leq\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{9t^{4}}`$. Then for all
$`t\geq 1`$,

1.  1.
    $`\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})-\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})+\frac{1}{t^{2}}`$.
2.  2.
    $`\min_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})}\pi^{t}(y)\geq c_{2}`$.
3.  3.
    $`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\mathchoice{\left\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\right\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}}=0`$.
4.  4.
    For any Nash equilibrium $`\hat{\pi}\in\Pi`$ and $`t\geq 1`$, we
    have
    $`\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t})+\frac{1}{t^{2}}`$

###### Proof.

By
[Lemma 2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma 2. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we have
$`\hat{\pi}^{t+1}=\Prox(\pi^{t},\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\hat{\pi}^{t+1}))`$
and

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\pi^{\star}||\hat{\pi}^{t+1})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})-\KL(\hat{\pi}^{t+1}||\pi^{t}).`$ |  | (9) |

The above implies

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t+1})`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})-\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})+\underbrace{\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t+1})-\KL(\pi^{\star}||\hat{\pi}^{t+1})}_{E_{1}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\underbrace{\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})-\KL(\hat{\pi}^{t+1}||\pi^{t})}_{E_{2}}.`$ |  | (10) |

Now, we use induction to prove the claim. For the base case, we define
$`\pi^{0}:=\pi^{1}`$ and $`\varepsilon_{t}=0`$, then

#### Base Case: $`t=0`$

Since $`\pi^{0}=\pi^{1}`$, we have $`\KL(\pi^{1}||\pi^{0})=0`$. Then it
is clear that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{1})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{0})-\KL(\pi^{1}||\pi^{0}).`$ |  |

Moreover, by
[Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ D.3 Auxiliary propostion ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
and $`D\geq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$, we have
$`\min_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{1})}\pi^{1}(y)\geq c_{1}\geq c_{2}`$.

#### Induction: $`t\geq 1`$

We have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\pi^{\star}||\hat{\pi}^{t+1})`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})`$ |  | (([9](#A4.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ Proof. ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{\mathrm{init}})+\sum_{t=1}^{t-1}\frac{1}{t^{2}}`$ |  | (inductive hypothesis) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq D+2.`$ |  | ($`D\geq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$) |

Using
[Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ D.3 Auxiliary propostion ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we have
$`\min_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})}\hat{\pi}^{t+1}(y)\geq c_{1}`$.
By $`\KL(\hat{\pi}^{t+1}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\varepsilon_{t}\leq 1`$ and
[Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ D.3 Auxiliary propostion ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
again, we get
$`\min_{y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})}\pi^{t+1}(y)\geq c_{2}:=\frac{c_{1}}{\exp(1/c_{1})}`$.
Thus, both $`\hat{\pi}^{t+1}`$ and $`\pi^{t+1}`$ are bounded away from
the boundary in their support. Further by
$`\KL(\hat{\pi}^{t+1}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\varepsilon_{t}`$, we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{y}\hat{\pi}^{t+1}(y)\log\frac{\hat{\pi}^{t+1}(y)}{\pi^{t+1}(y)}\leq\varepsilon_{t}\Rightarrow\max_{y}\log\frac{\hat{\pi}^{t+1}(y)}{\pi^{t+1}(y)}\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{c_{1}}.`$ |  |

As a result, we can bound

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle E_{1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t+1})-\KL(\pi^{\star}||\hat{\pi}^{t+1})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\pi^{\star}(y)\log\frac{\hat{\pi}^{t+1}(y)}{\pi^{t+1}(y)}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\max_{y}\log\frac{\hat{\pi}^{t+1}(y)}{\pi^{t+1}(y)}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{c_{1}}.`$ |  |

Moreover, we have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle E_{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})-\KL(\hat{\pi}^{t+1}||\pi^{t})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{y}(\pi^{t+1}(y)-\hat{\pi}^{t+1}(y))\log\frac{\pi^{t+1}(y)}{\pi^{t}(y)}-\KL(\hat{\pi}^{t+1}||\pi^{t+1})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq{\mathchoice{\left\|\pi^{t+1}-\hat{\pi}^{t+1}\right\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\hat{\pi}^{t+1}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\hat{\pi}^{t+1}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\hat{\pi}^{t+1}\|}}_{1}\cdot\max_{y}|\log\frac{\pi^{t+1}(y)}{\pi^{t}(y)}|`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\KL(\hat{\pi}^{t+1}||\pi^{t+1})}\cdot\log\frac{1}{c_{2}}`$ |  | (Pinsker’s Inequality) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}}{c_{1}}`$ |  |  |

Combining the above two inequalities with
([10](#A4.Ex22 "In Proof. ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))
and noting the fact that $`\varepsilon_{t}\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}`$
gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\pi^{\star}||\pi^{t})-\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})+\frac{3\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}}{c_{1}}.
``` |  |

We conclude the claim since
$`\varepsilon_{t}\leq\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{9t^{4}}`$. This completes the
proof for item 1 and item 2.

For item 3, we have
$`\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{\mathchoice{\left\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\right\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})\leq D+2`$.
Thus
$`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\mathchoice{\left\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\right\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}}=0`$.

For item 4, we can use
[Lemma 2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma 2. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
and
$`\hat{\pi}^{t+1}=\Prox(\pi^{t},\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\hat{\pi}^{t+1}))`$
to get

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t+1})`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t})-\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})+\underbrace{\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t+1})-\KL(\hat{\pi}||\hat{\pi}^{t+1})}_{E_{1}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\underbrace{\KL(\pi^{t+1}||\pi^{t})-\KL(\hat{\pi}^{t+1}||\pi^{t})}_{E_{2}}.`$ |  | (11) |

We note that $`E_{2}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\varepsilon_{t}}}{c_{1}}`$ has been
proved in the above. For $`E_{1}`$, we have

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle E_{1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t+1})-\KL(\hat{\pi}||\hat{\pi}^{t+1})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\hat{\pi}(y)\log\frac{\hat{\pi}^{t+1}(y)}{\pi^{t+1}(y)}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\max_{y}\log\frac{\hat{\pi}^{t+1}(y)}{\pi^{t+1}(y)}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon_{t}}{c_{1}}.`$ |  |

Thus we have
$`\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t})+\frac{1}{t^{2}}`$
as $`\varepsilon_{t}\leq\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{9t^{4}}`$. ∎

#### Proof of [Theorem 3](#Thmtheorem3 "Theorem 3 (COMAL with approximate regularized game solver). ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")

###### Proof.

Since the sequence $`\{\pi^{t}\}`$ is bounded, it has at least one limit
point $`\hat{\pi}`$. By item 2 in
[Lemma 4](#Thmlemma4 "Lemma 4. ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we know $`\hat{\pi}(y)\geq c_{2}`$ for all
$`y\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$. By item 3 in
[Lemma 4](#Thmlemma4 "Lemma 4. ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we have
$`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\mathchoice{\left\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\right\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}}=0`$.
Denote $`\{\pi^{k}:k\in\kappa\}`$ a subsequence that converges to
$`\hat{\pi}`$. Then we have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{\pi}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\lim_{k\in\kappa,\kappa\rightarrow\infty}\pi^{k+1}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\lim_{k\in\kappa,\kappa\rightarrow\infty}\hat{\pi}^{k+1}`$ |  | ($`\KL(\hat{\pi}^{k+1},\pi^{k+1})\leq\varepsilon_{k}`$ and $`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon_{t}=0`$) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\lim_{k\in\kappa,\kappa\rightarrow\infty}\Prox(\pi^{k},\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\hat{\pi}^{k+1}))`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\lim_{k\in\kappa,\kappa\rightarrow\infty}\Prox(\pi^{k+1},\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\hat{\pi}^{k+1}))`$ |  | ($`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\mathchoice{\left\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\right\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}{\|\pi^{t+1}-\pi^{t}\|}}=0`$) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\lim_{k\in\kappa,\kappa\rightarrow\infty}\Prox(\pi^{k+1},\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\pi^{k+1}))`$ |  | ($`\KL(\hat{\pi}^{k+1},\pi^{k+1})\leq\varepsilon_{k}`$ and $`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\varepsilon_{t}=0`$) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\Prox(\hat{\pi},\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\hat{\pi})).`$ |  |  |

Since $`\hat{\pi}`$ is a fixed point of
$`\Prox(\pi,\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\pi))`$ and
$`\mathrm{supp}(\hat{\pi})=\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\mathrm{init}})`$, we can
use the same proof in
[Lemma 3](#Thmlemma3 "Lemma 3. ‣ D.1 Last-Iterate Convergence under Exact Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
to show that $`\hat{\pi}`$ is a Nash equilibrium of
$`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$.

Given that $`\hat{\pi}`$ is a Nash equilibrium of the original game, we
can apply item 4 in
[Lemma 4](#Thmlemma4 "Lemma 4. ‣ D.2 Last-Iterate Convergence under Approximate Solutions ‣ Appendix D Last-Iterate Convergence of COMAL ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
and get

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t})+\frac{1}{t^{2}}.`$ |  |

Now we show the sequence $`\{x^{t}\}`$ converges to $`\hat{\pi}`$. Fix
any $`\epsilon>0`$. Let $`T_{1}\geq 1`$ such that
$`\sum_{t=T_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}`$, Since
$`\hat{\pi}`$ is a limit point of $`\{x^{t}\}`$, there exists
$`T_{2}\geq T_{1}`$ such that
$`\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{T_{2}})\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}`$. Then for any
$`t\geq T_{2}`$, we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t+1})\leq\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{T_{2}})+\sum_{t=T_{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.`$ |  |

Since the above holds for any $`\varepsilon>0`$, we know
$`\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\KL(\hat{\pi}||\pi^{t})=0`$ and thus
$`\{x^{t}\}`$ converges to $`\hat{\pi}`$. This completes the proof. ∎

KL-regularized zero-sum game $`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$.
We denote $`\mu^{\star}=\pi^{\star}_{\tau}`$ its unique Nash
equilibrium. Our proof is inspired by \[[Abe et al., 2024](#bib.bib1),
Lemma F.1\] that give linear convergence of MWU in KL-regularized game.
Here, we include a simpler proof with slightly better constants for our
setting for completeness.

We prove the following descent lemma, which immediately implies
[Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2. ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").

###### Lemma 5.

If we choose $`\eta\in(0,\frac{\tau}{\tau^{2}+\frac{1}{2}}]`$ in MWU
(Algorithm [2](#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
then we have for every $`k\geq 1`$

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\mu^{\star},\mu^{k+1})\leq{\mathchoice{\left(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2}\right)}{(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2})}{(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2})}{(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2})}}\KL(\mu^{\star},\mu^{k}).`$ |  |

###### Proof.

We define the gradient operator
$`G:\Pi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}`$ of $`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$
and the gradient operator $`A:\Pi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}`$
of the KL regularization $`\KL(\pi,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$ as follows.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle G(\pi)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\pi)`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle A(\pi)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=\nabla_{\pi}\KL(\pi,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=\log\frac{\pi(\cdot)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot)}.`$ |  |

We define the composite operator $`F=G-\tau A`$. Then MWU update in
Algorithm [2](#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
is equivalent to

|     |                                                            |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     | $`\displaystyle\mu^{k+1}=\Prox(\mu^{k},\eta F(\mu^{k})).`$ |     |

Using
[Corollary 1](#Thmcorollary1 "Corollary 1. ‣ C.1 Properties of the Prox Operator ‣ Appendix C Prox Operator ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
we have

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\left\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\right\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}}\leq\KL(\mu^{\star}||\mu^{k})-\KL(\mu^{\star}||\mu^{k+1})-\KL(\mu^{k+1}||\mu^{k})`$ |  |

We focus on the left-hand side of the above inequality. Since
$`\mu^{\star}`$ is a Nash equilibrium of the regularized game with
gradient $`F`$, we have
$`{\mathchoice{\left\langle\eta F(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\right\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}}\geq 0`$
and thus

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\left\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\right\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\geq{\mathchoice{\left\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\right\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{k}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}}-{\mathchoice{\left\langle\eta F(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\right\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle\eta F(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\underbrace{\eta{\mathchoice{\left\langle G(\mu^{k})-G(\mu^{k+1}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\right\rangle}{\langle G(\mu^{k})-G(\mu^{k+1}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle G(\mu^{k})-G(\mu^{k+1}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle G(\mu^{k})-G(\mu^{k+1}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}}}_{\mathrm{term_{1}}}+\underbrace{\eta\tau{\mathchoice{\left\langle A(\mu^{k})-A(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{k+1}-\mu^{\star}\right\rangle}{\langle A(\mu^{k})-A(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{k+1}-\mu^{\star}\rangle}{\langle A(\mu^{k})-A(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{k+1}-\mu^{\star}\rangle}{\langle A(\mu^{k})-A(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{k+1}-\mu^{\star}\rangle}}}_{\mathrm{term_{2}}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\eta{\mathchoice{\left\langle G(\mu^{k+1})-G(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\right\rangle}{\langle G(\mu^{k+1})-G(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle G(\mu^{k+1})-G(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle G(\mu^{k+1})-G(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}}}_{\mathrm{term_{3}}=0}.`$ |  |

We note that $`\mathrm{term_{3}}=0`$ since $`G`$ is the gradient of a
zero-sum game:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\left\langle G(\mu^{k+1})-G(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\right\rangle}{\langle G(\mu^{k+1})-G(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle G(\mu^{k+1})-G(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}{\langle G(\mu^{k+1})-G(\mu^{\star}),\mu^{\star}-\mu^{k+1}\rangle}}`$ |  |

For $`\mathrm{term_{2}}`$, we can apply the three-point identity for the

|----|----|----|----|

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2})\KL(\mu^{\star}||\mu^{k+1})\leq(1-\eta\tau)\KL(\mu^{\star}||\mu^{k})-(1-\eta\tau-\frac{\eta}{2\tau})\KL(\mu^{k+1}||\mu^{k})`$ |  |

Let
$`\eta\leq\frac{1}{\tau+\frac{1}{2\tau}}=\frac{\tau}{\tau^{2}+\frac{1}{2}}`$,
then we have $`1-\eta\tau-\frac{\eta}{2\tau}\geq 0`$ and thus

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\KL(\mu^{\star}||\mu^{k+1})\leq\frac{1-\eta\tau}{1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2}}\KL(\mu^{\star}||\mu^{k})\leq{\mathchoice{\left(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2}\right)}{(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2})}{(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2})}{(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2})}}\KL(\mu^{\star}||\mu^{k}).`$ |  |

## Appendix F Computing the Prox Operator using Preference Learning Methods

We include additional examples showing how existing algorithms designed
for RLHF and preference optimization with neural network parameters can
be adapted to solve the prox operator $`\Prox(\pi,\eta g)`$ ($`\eta>0`$
is the step size). These algorithms include RL algorithms like PPO and
loss-minimization algorithms like DPO, IPO, SPPO, DRO, INPO, each of
which may be preferred in certain settings.

#### Reinforcement Learning algorithms

We can use the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm \[[Schulman
et al., 2017](#bib.bib56)\] or Group-Relative Policy optimization
(GRPO) \[[Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib57), [Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib27)\] to solve $`\Prox(\pi,\eta g)`$. Observe that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Prox(\pi,\eta g)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi^{\prime}}\{{\mathchoice{\left\langle\eta g,\pi^{\prime}\right\rangle}{\langle\eta g,\pi^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle\eta g,\pi^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle\eta g,\pi^{\prime}\rangle}}-\KL(\pi^{\prime}||\pi)\}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{\prime}}{\mathchoice{\left[g[y]-\eta^{-1}\cdot\KL(\pi^{\prime}||\pi)\right]}{[g[y]-\eta^{-1}\cdot\KL(\pi^{\prime}||\pi)]}{[g[y]-\eta^{-1}\cdot\KL(\pi^{\prime}||\pi)]}{[g[y]-\eta^{-1}\cdot\KL(\pi^{\prime}||\pi)]}}`$ |  |

shares the same form as the objective in
([5](#A2.Ex3 "In RLHF ‣ B.1 Alignment under the Bradley-Terry Model Assumption ‣ Appendix B Additional Backgrounds ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).
Typically, we parameterize $`\pi^{\prime}=\pi_{\theta}`$ with neural
network parameters $`\theta`$ and optimize over $`\theta`$.

#### Loss minimization algorithms

Let us denote $`\hat{\pi}`$ the prox operator $`\Prox(\pi,\eta g)`$,
then we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{\pi}[y]=\frac{\pi(y)\exp(\eta g(y))}{Z}\Leftrightarrow\log\frac{\hat{\pi}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta g(y)+\log Z=0,`$ |  |

where $`Z=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\exp(\eta g(y))]`$ is the partition
function. We can directly compute the partition function $`Z`$ and thus
$`\hat{\pi}`$ in small tabular cases. However, the partition function is
hard to compute in general large-scale applications. Several works have
recently proposed to solve the above equality by optimizing the
corresponding $`L_{2}`$ loss.

The Self-Play Preference Optimization (SPPO) loss \[[Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib70)\] assumes $`\log Z=\frac{\eta}{2}`$ and optimizes

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{SPPO}}(\theta)={\mathchoice{\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta g(y)-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta g(y)-\frac{\eta}{2})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta g(y)-\frac{\eta}{2})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta g(y)-\frac{\eta}{2})}}^{2}.`$ |  |

The Direct Reward Optimization (DRO) loss \[[Richemond et al.,
2024](#bib.bib53)\] parameterizes both $`\hat{\pi}`$ and $`\log Z`$ with
$`\theta`$ and $`V_{\phi}`$ respectively and optimize⁴⁴ 4 we modified
some constants in the original DRO loss to make it consistent with our
presentation. The modification has no other effects.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{DRO}}(\theta,\phi)={\mathchoice{\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta g(y)-\eta V_{\phi}\right)}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta g(y)-\eta V_{\phi})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta g(y)-\eta V_{\phi})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\eta g(y)-\eta V_{\phi})}}^{2}.`$ |  |

The REBEL loss \[[Gao et al., 2024](#bib.bib23)\] uses *differences in
rewards* to eliminate the partition function $`Z`$ and optimize the
regression loss

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{REBEL}}(\theta)={\mathchoice{\left(\eta^{-1}{\mathchoice{\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})}\right)}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}}-{\mathchoice{\left(g(y)-g(y^{\prime})\right)}{(g(y)-g(y^{\prime}))}{(g(y)-g(y^{\prime}))}{(g(y)-g(y^{\prime}))}}\right)}{(\eta^{-1}{\mathchoice{\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})}\right)}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}}-{\mathchoice{\left(g(y)-g(y^{\prime})\right)}{(g(y)-g(y^{\prime}))}{(g(y)-g(y^{\prime}))}{(g(y)-g(y^{\prime}))}})}{(\eta^{-1}{\mathchoice{\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})}\right)}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}}-{\mathchoice{\left(g(y)-g(y^{\prime})\right)}{(g(y)-g(y^{\prime}))}{(g(y)-g(y^{\prime}))}{(g(y)-g(y^{\prime}))}})}{(\eta^{-1}{\mathchoice{\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})}\right)}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})})}{(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi(y)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\pr All the above approaches can be used to solve $`\Prox(\pi,\eta g)`$.
However, directly applying them iteratively on $`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$ is
equivalent to running MWU, which provably diverges. In contrast, we can
apply them in
Algorithm [2](#algorithm2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
and then apply our meta-algorithm COMAL to guarantee convergence to a
Nash equilibrium.

###### Remark 1.

The above approaches are versatile and work well for any $`g`$ that can
be evaluated efficiently. In particular, we should consider using them
when (1) $`g=r`$ is a reward function and we can efficiently query
$`r`$; (2) $`g=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid\mu)`$ is the win rate against a
reference policy $`\mu`$, and we can efficiently sample from $`\mu`$ and
have oracle access to $`\mathbb{P}`$. These two setting are popular and
practical in the LLM alignment setting.

Now we turn attention to the more specific setting where $`g`$
corresponds to a preference model $`\mathbb{P}`$ (could be a BT model or
a general preference) and that we can collect a win-loss preference data
set $`\mathcal{D}=\{(y_{w},y_{l})\}`$, which is standard for LLM
alignment. Although the abovementioned algorithms apply, they all
require estimating $`g`$ (the win rate) and may be inefficient in
practice. In the following, we present algorithms directly working on
the sampled dataset $`\mathcal{D}`$ without further estimation.

#### Sampled loss based on the BT preference model

Assume $`g=r`$ is the reward of the Bradley-Terry model, and the dataset
$`\{(y_{w},y_{l})\}`$ consists of win-lose pairs of responses. Then we
can solve $`\Prox(\pi,\eta g)`$ by optimize the DPO loss \[[Rafailov
et al., 2024](#bib.bib51)\] defined as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{DPO}}((y_{w},y_{l});\theta)=-\log\sigma{\mathchoice{\left(\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi(y_{w})}-\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}{\pi(y_{l})}\right)}{(\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi(y_{w})}-\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}{\pi(y_{l})})}{(\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi(y_{w})}-\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}{\pi(y_{l})})}{(\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi(y_{w})}-\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}{\pi(y_{l})})}}.`$ |  |

#### IPO for computing $`\Prox`$ for unregularized preferences

we first show that the IPO loss could be used to solve

#### INPO for computing $`\Prox`$ for regularized preferences

The Iterative Nash Policy Optimization (INPO) loss \[[Zhang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib75)\] is a generalization of the IPO loss to the
regularized preference setting. We show that INPO could be used to
compute $`\Prox(\mu,\eta g^{\tau}_{\mu})`$, where
$`g^{\tau}_{\mu}:=\nabla_{\pi}J_{\tau}(\pi,\mu,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\mu)-\tau\log\frac{\mu(\cdot)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot)}`$
is the gradient of the regularized objective
([2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).
Given a win-loss pair data set $`\{y_{w},y_{l}\sim\mu\}`$, the INPO loss
$`\ell_{\mathrm{INPO}}(\pi)`$ is defined as

|  |  |  |  |  |

|  |  | $`\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{INPO}}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mu\\

## Appendix G More Practical Implementations of COMAL

In this section, we provide more practical implementations of COMAL

with many existing methods designed for preference optimization with

Although the SPPO, DPO, and REBEL losses are proposed in the
unregularized preference setting, we have shown how to extend these

## Appendix H Implementation of Mirror-Prox and Optimistic Multiplicative Weights Update

We note that there are other algorithms that has provable last-iterate
convergence to Nash equilibrium in (unregularized) zero-sum games,
including the Mirror-Prox algorithm \[[Nemirovski, 2004](#bib.bib43)\]
and Optimistic Multiplicative Weights Update (OMWU) algorithm \[[Rakhlin
and Sridharan, 2013](#bib.bib52), [Syrgkanis et al., 2015](#bib.bib62),
[Hsieh et al., 2021](#bib.bib29)\]. We present practical implementations
of these two algorithms in the context of LLM alignment for solving
$`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$ ([1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ Definition 2 (Alignment Game). ‣ 2.1 Alignment with General Preference Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
where we use preference optimization algorithms to solve the prox
operator as shown in
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Practical methods for computing the prox operator ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
and
[Appendix F](#A6 "Appendix F Computing the Prox Operator using Preference Learning Methods ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").

We denote the gradient $`g(\pi):=\mathbb{P}(\cdot\succ\pi)`$.

#### Mirror-Prox

The Mirror-Prox algorithm \[[Nemirovski, 2004](#bib.bib43)\] initialized
$`\pi^{1}=\pi_{\mathrm{init}}`$ and updates in each iteration
$`t\geq 1`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{t+\frac{1}{2}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Prox(\pi^{t},\eta g(\pi^{t}))`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Prox(\pi^{t},\eta g(\pi^{t+\frac{1}{2}}))`$ |  |

We can implement Mirror-Prox using PPO/DPO/IPO/SPPO/DRO/REBEL to compute
the prox operator. Specifically, we could sample from $`\pi^{t}`$ and
construct a preference dataset $`D_{t}`$ and optimize certain regression
loss (IPO/DRO/REBEL) to compute
$`\pi^{t+\frac{1}{2}}=\Prox(\pi^{t},\eta g(\pi^{t}))`$. The procedure
applies to the second step in each iteration. Thus in such an
implementation, we require two sampling and two optimization procedures
in each iteration.

#### Optimistic Multiplicative Weights Update (OMWU)

The OMWU algorithm \[[Rakhlin and Sridharan, 2013](#bib.bib52)\] is an
optimistic variant of the MWU algorithm. Although MWU diverges in
zero-sum games, it has been shown that OMWU has last-iterate convergence
to Nash equilibrium \[[Wei et al., 2021](#bib.bib68), [Hsieh et al.,
2021](#bib.bib29)\]. Initialized with
$`\pi^{1}=\pi^{\frac{1}{2}}=\pi_{\mathrm{init}}`$, OMWU updates in each
iteration $`t\geq 1`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{t+\frac{1}{2}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Prox(\pi^{t},\eta g(\pi^{t-\frac{1}{2}}))`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Prox(\pi^{t},\eta g(\pi^{t+\frac{1}{2}}))`$ |  |

Similarly, we can implement OMWU to solve $`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$ using
preference methods to compute the prox operator as shown in
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Practical methods for computing the prox operator ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
Moreover, OMWU has an equivalent update rule: initialize
$`\pi^{1}=\pi^{0}=\pi_{\mathrm{init}}`$

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{t+1}=\Prox(\pi^{t},2\eta g(\pi^{t})-\eta g(\pi^{t-1})),`$ |  |

which requires computing only one prox operator in each iteration.

We leave a systematic evaluation of Mirror-Prox and OMWU at a large
scale, including LLM alignment, to future work.

#### Experiment Setup

Recall that we set
$`\mathbb{P}[y_{b}\succ y_{a}]=\mathbb{P}[y_{c}\succ y_{b}]=0.9`$ and
$`\mathbb{P}[y_{a}\succ y_{c}]=0.8`$. This results in the following
zero-sum game: we have policies $`\Pi=\Delta(\{y_{a},y_{b},y_{c}\})`$
and objective

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})=\pi_{1}^{\top}A\pi_{2}-0.5,\textnormal{ where }A=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&0.1&0.8\\
0.9&0.5&0.1\\
0.2&0.9&0.5\end{bmatrix}.`$ |  |

The game has a unique Nash equilibrium $`[4/11,3/11,4/11]`$. We set the
initial policy to be $`\pi^{1}=[0.2,0.5,0.3]`$ for all algorithms. We
choose $`\eta=0.3`$ for iterative DPO, iterative IPO, and SPPO. We
choose $`\eta=0.3`$ and $`\tau=0.1`$ for INPO and COMAL. For COMAL
(Algorithm [3](#algorithm3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ 3.3 Practical methods for computing the prox operator ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")),
we set $`T=200`$ and $`K_{t}=25`$ so the total number of iterations is
$`T\cdot K_{t}=5000`$.

#### Experiments using noiseless gradient

We present numerical results of mirror-descent (MD) algorithms
(equivalent to MWU) and COMAL
(Algorithm [1](#algorithm1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 COMAL ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))
in
[Figure 3](#A9.F3 "In Appendix I Synthetic Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
We can see that the MD algorithm diverges from the unique Nash
equilibrium and suffers a large equilibrium gap, while COMAL achieves
fast last-iterate convergence to the Nash equilibrium, aligned with our
theoretical results
([Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1. ‣ Algorithmic Structure and Motivation. ‣ 3.1 COMAL ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")).

#### Experiements using preference samples

Since the popular iterative DPO algorithm does not contain a gradient
step, we also conduct experiments with only Oracle query access to the
preference model. We compare the performance of various algorithms,
including iterative DPO, iterative IPO, SPPO, and INPO and present
results in
[Figure 4](#A9.F4 "In Appendix I Synthetic Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
The sample-only setting is also more aligned with what happens in
practice. We use a sufficient number of samples in each iteration for
every algorithm. As a result, the COMAL performs the same as in the
noiseless gradient setting, while the iterative IPO algorithm becomes
equivalent to the MD algorithm. We note the following:

Iterative DPO: We observe that iterative DPO cycles between extreme
policies (e.g., outputting $`y_{a}`$ with probability close to $`1`$).
This is aligned with  \[[Azar et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)\], where they
found DPO will converge to the deterministic policy regardless of the
regularization parameter in extreme preference settings. The cycling
behavior of iterative DPO may be explained as follows: in each
iteration, DPO converges to a nearly deterministic policy output $`y`$;
then the new preference data shows that $`y^{\prime}\neq y`$ is more
preferred; finally, iterative DPO cycles over $`\mathcal{Y}`$ since the
preference itself exhibits a cycle and there is no clear winner.

Iterative IPO \[[Azar et al., 2024](#bib.bib3), [Calandriello et al.,
2024](#bib.bib12)\]: The IPO loss is a variant of the DPO loss, but it
does not rely on the BT model assumption and works for a general
preference model. However, as we have discussed before, (exactly)
minimizing the IPO loss is equivalent to performing one MD step, and
thus, iterative IPO is equivalent to MD up to sampling error. As a
result, we observe that iterative IPO also exhibits cycling behavior.

SPPO \[[Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib70)\]: The SPPO algorithm (see
[Appendix F](#A6 "Appendix F Computing the Prox Operator using Preference Learning Methods ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"))
is not exactly the same as MWU since SPPO assumes the partition function
is always $`Z=\log\frac{\eta}{2}`$ which may not be the case. We observe
that SPPO exhibits very similar cycling behavior as MD. We conclude that
SPPO approximates MD very well in this instance and exhibits similar
behavior.

INPO \[[Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib75)\]: The INPO algorithm is
designed for finding the Nash equilibrium of the KL regularized game
$`J_{\tau}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})`$. As we proved in
[Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2. ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
INPO does not diverge and exhibits last-iterate convergence. However, it
converges to a point that differs from the Nash equilibrium of the game
$`J(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})`$ and has constant equilibrium gap.

## Appendix J Hyperparameters and Training Details for LLM Experiments

We follow a similar training recipe proposed in [Tunstall et al.
\[2023\]](#bib.bib64) for the experiments. Specifically, at each
training iteration, the models are fine-tuned for one epoch with a batch
size of 32 and a maximum learning rate of $`5\times 10^{-7}`$, using a
cosine learning rate scheduler with 10% of warmup steps. We conduct a
grid search for the strength of the KL regularization, $`\eta^{-1}`$, in
the loss functions of DPO, IPO and INPO, within the range of 0.001 -
0.1. INPO has another hyper-parameter $`\tau`$ which controls the
strength of the KL regularization from the reference policy. Its value
is determined following [Zhang et al. \[2025b\]](#bib.bib75), where
$`\eta\tau`$ is set to a fixed ratio, $`1/3`$.

## Appendix K LLM-Based Experiments with 1.5B LLM

In
§[5](#S5 "5 LLM-Based Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),

### K.1 Experimental Settings

Some of the experiment settings are identical to the settings in
§[5](#S5 "5 LLM-Based Experiments ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
Therefore, here we only outline the differences in the settings.

Preference Oracle The preference oracle we used is
Llama-3-OffsetBias-8B \[[Park et al., 2024](#bib.bib47)\], which is a
pairwise preference model that predicts which output is better given an
instruction and a pair of outputs. Fine-tuned from
Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct \[[Dubey et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)\], it
achieves strong performance on various human preference alignment
benchmarks in RewardBench \[[Lambert et al., 2024b](#bib.bib33)\]. We
selected it as the preference oracle for its balance of computational
efficiency and alignment with human preferences, making it suitable for
iterative preference optimization.

Baselines We include the following baselines for comparisons with COMAL:
(1) SFT, which fine-tunes the pre-trained Qwen2-1.5B on the UltraChat
dataset, with the resulting checkpoint serving as the starting point
and/or reference policy for the other training algorithms; (2) vanilla
DPO \[[Rafailov et al., 2024](#bib.bib51)\] and (3) vanilla IPO \[[Azar
et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)\], where one training iteration is performed
over the entire instruction set of UltraFeedback with output pairs
sampled from the SFT policy; (4) INPO \[[Zhang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib75)\], where each iteration of training is performed on a
single data split; (5) iterative IPO, which follows a training setting
similar to INPO but without the KL regularization with respect to the
reference policy.

Evaluations We chose to use the same preference oracle used during data
generation, Llama-3-OffsetBias-8B, as the evaluator. This decision was
made to maintain a controlled experimental setting, ensuring that the
preference oracle the model learns to fit is also the one used to
evaluate its performance.

Training Details We follow the training recipe proposed in [Tunstall
et al. \[2023\]](#bib.bib64) for the experiments. Specifically, at each
training iteration, the models are fine-tuned for 3 epochs with a batch
size of 32 and maximum learning rate of $`5\times 10^{-7}`$, using a
linear learning rate scheduler where 10% of the steps are for warmup and
the rest for linearly decreasing the rate. The checkpoints are selected
based on their validation loss on the UltraFeedback dataset. The
training is performed on 8 NVIDIA A6000 Ada GPUs with 48GB memory, and
one training iteration over the 10K instructions takes around 5 hours.
Due to the relatively high computational requirements and the large
number of training iterations we tested (up to 42), we opted to use a
moderately sized LLM and did not conduct an exhaustive hyper-parameter
search, instead referencing settings from previous work when
appropriate.

Hyper-Parameters We conduct a grid search for the strength of the KL
regularization, $`\eta^{-1}`$, in both vanilla DPO and IPO. We found
that DPO achieves the best performance when $`\eta^{-1}`$ is set to
0.01, while IPO achieves the best performance when $`\eta^{-1}`$ is set
within the range of 0.002 - 0.01. We then choose the value of
$`\eta^{-1}`$ to be 0.002 to encourage larger learning steps. This value
of $`\eta`$ is also used for iterative IPO. For INPO, we compare two
settings where $`\eta^{-1}`$ is set to 0.002 and 0.01, corresponding to
a small and a large regularization respectively. INPO has another
hyper-parameter $`\tau`$ which controls the strength of the KL
regularization from the reference policy. We determine its value
following the setting of [Zhang et al. \[2025b\]](#bib.bib75), where
$`\eta\tau`$ is set to a fixed ratio, $`1/3`$. Regarding COMAL, which is
implemented based on INPO as outlined in
Algorithm [3](#algorithm3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ 3.3 Practical methods for computing the prox operator ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences"),
$`\eta^{-1}`$ is also set to 0.002 at the beginning of the training. The
reference policy used in COMAL is updated when the first optimization
step begins to converge or overfit, and $`\eta^{-1}`$ is increased to
0.01 to improve training stability.

## Appendix L Limitations

#### Provable Guarantee on Computing the Prox Operators

Our theoretical guarantee on the last-iterate convergence of COMAL
relies on computing the prox operator and solving a regularized game
approximately. Although we provide many practical loss minimization
approaches that compute the prox operator, the applicability of our
results in practical LLM settings lacks a provable guarantee since the
losses could be highly non-convex, for which no provably efficient
algorithms exist. We also remark that our analysis is non-trivial and
novel, which gives a more robust guarantee than existing
works \[[Perolat et al., 2021](#bib.bib48), [Sokota et al.,
2023](#bib.bib58), [Abe et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)\] that require solving
the regularized game *exactly*.

#### Theoretical Convergence Guarantees

Our
[Theorem 1](#Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1. ‣ Algorithmic Structure and Motivation. ‣ 3.1 COMAL ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
provides asymptotic last-iterate convergence to exact Nash equilibrium
and
[Theorem 2](#Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 2. ‣ 3.2 Solving a Regularized Game ‣ 3 Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Alignment ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences")
gives non-asymptotic $`{O}(1/\varepsilon^{2})`$ convergence to an
$`\varepsilon`$-approximate Nash equilibrium when we choose the
regularization $`\tau=O(\varepsilon)`$. Here we discuss the possibility
of achieving non-asymptotic convergence with non-vanishing
regularization $`\tau`$. We remark that there are algorithms with
$`\ell_{2}`$ regularization that have polynomial last-iterate
convergence rates \[[Cai et al., 2022](#bib.bib8)\]. However, it is
unclear whether these algorithms with $`\ell_{2}`$ regularization are
practical in large-scale LLM settings, as no known efficient
implementations exist. In contrast, prox operators with entropy
regularization can be computed using practical preference optimization
algorithms such as DPO, IPO, INPO as we discussed
in §[F](#A6 "Appendix F Computing the Prox Operator using Preference Learning Methods ‣ COMAL: A Convergent Meta-Algorithm for Aligning LLMs with General Preferences").
Regarding the possibility of establishing a last-iterate convergence
rate of our algorithm, we note that a uniform convergence rate for
algorithms of this type is unlikely, as suggested by a recent
work \[[Cai et al., 2024a](#bib.bib10)\]. Here, “uniform” refers to an
upper bound on the duality gap that holds for all instances. While it
may be possible to obtain a weaker instance-dependent rate, similar to
the ones in \[[Wei et al., 2021](#bib.bib68)\], under a unique Nash
equilibrium assumption, the rate depends on a problem-dependent
parameter that could be arbitrarily large and difficult to
characterize—particularly in the LLM setting. As such, such rates offer
limited practical guidance for implementation or for understanding
convergence speed in realistic scenarios. Nevertheless, getting a
convergence rate is an interesting question and we leave it for future
work.
````

###### Abstract

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) has advanced generative model
post-training in verifiable domains such as code and mathematics, but
evaluating video quality, especially emotional expression, often depends
on subjective aesthetics and has no uniquely correct target. Existing
approaches typically rely on fixed rewards for visual quality, motion
quality, and text alignment that poorly capture emotional intent and can
invite reward hacking, while reinforcement learning from human feedback
(RLHF) is difficult to scale and subject to individual aesthetic
differences that complicate the calibration of facial, body motion, and
chromatic cues across narrative contexts. We introduce EmoReward, a new
self-evolving training mechanism that distills evidence reliability from
reward traces collected during an actual training run to adaptively
guide multi-reward weight allocation in subsequent training by
determining which emotional evidence to trust and how much to weight it.
EmoReward first plans specific grounding cues from the emotional prompt
to guide evaluation, then uses heterogeneous signal primitives to score
the generated video during the act stage. _Reflective Reward Gating_
calibrates their contributions through instance-level gates for phase,
face confidence, conflict, and subtlety, then renormalizes weights over
the active set to produce the sample’s fused reward. _RewardMemory_ then
estimates each primitive’s reliability from rollout scores and outcomes
within each emotional regime in the finished training run, then fixes
bounded multipliers that calibrate fusion weights in the next training
run without updating reward models. Experiments on HunyuanVideo 1.5 show
improvements across multiple RL algorithms and compare EmoReward with
other agentic reward methods under SAGE-GRPO. Ablations and transfer
experiments on SD 3.5 and FLUX further support adaptive reward
calibration for visual generation.

## 1  Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL), especially Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO), has advanced model post-training in verifiable
domains such as code and mathematics, where correctness provides a
direct learning signal \[[46](#bib.bib43), [16](#bib.bib8),
[50](#bib.bib9), [75](#bib.bib27)\]. In contrast, evaluating video
quality, especially emotional expression, often depends on subjective
aesthetics and has no uniquely correct target. Visual post-training
adapts diffusion and flow generators through RL and preference
objectives \[[2](#bib.bib2), [12](#bib.bib6), [43](#bib.bib16),
[60](#bib.bib21), [72](#bib.bib26), [29](#bib.bib11),
[80](#bib.bib30)\]. Learned evaluators provide scores for image
preference and video quality \[[69](#bib.bib35), [22](#bib.bib36),
[38](#bib.bib37), [68](#bib.bib38), [17](#bib.bib44),
[30](#bib.bib12)\], but a visually clear, temporally stable video can
match its prompt without conveying the intended emotion. Facial
analysis, dynamic expression recognition, and multimodal emotion models
supply complementary evidence \[[1](#bib.bib1), [3](#bib.bib59),
[7](#bib.bib40), [26](#bib.bib41), [79](#bib.bib42)\], whose reliability
varies with context: facial cues require visible faces, strong motion
can undermine restrained sadness, and saturated colors may reflect style
rather than emotional intent. This raises a central question: _Which
emotional evidence should the reward system trust in the current video,
and how can it adaptively determine how much weight to assign to that
evidence?_

Figure 1: From fixed scoring to contextual reward calibration. The
reward design progresses from a fixed evaluator to a heterogeneous
provider pool and then to EmoReward. Reflective Reward Gating selects
relevant evidence for the current video; RewardMemory calibrates
provider trust from experience. All three stages ultimately return a
scalar reward. The reward providers remain frozen while the generator
undergoes RL updates.

Agentic systems coordinate interaction, refine outputs through feedback,
and organize evaluation into rubrics \[[65](#bib.bib23),
[62](#bib.bib22), [39](#bib.bib13), [47](#bib.bib19), [15](#bib.bib7)\].
These capabilities motivate adaptive evaluation, yet existing video GRPO
methods typically rely on fixed rewards for visual quality, motion
quality, and text alignment that inadequately capture emotional intent.
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and preference
optimization address subjective goals \[[8](#bib.bib3),
[48](#bib.bib20), [41](#bib.bib15), [44](#bib.bib17)\], but human
annotation is costly at scale, and aesthetic differences complicate the
calibration of emotional cues across contexts. Fixed fusion can mix
informative, missing, and saturated signals, allowing policy updates to
exploit scoring shortcuts rather than improve emotional fidelity. This
motivates reward calibration at two complementary levels: assessing
which cues are valid within each video, and learning from a completed
training run how much those cues should influence rewards in the next
run.

We introduce EmoReward, a _self-evolving training mechanism_ for
emotional video-generation RL
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")).
It distills _evidence reliability_ from reward–outcome traces collected
during an actual, completed training run, then uses this experience to
guide multi-reward weight allocation in the next run to decide which
emotional evidence to trust and how much to weight it. _Plan_ translates
emotional intent into an EmoScript specifying affect targets, facial and
body cues, and an expression window; _Act_ scores generated videos with
heterogeneous primitives, keeping criterion scores separate from
evidence validity. Within each video, _Reflective Reward Gating_
calibrates these cues using the training phase, face confidence,
semantic conflict, and subtlety, then renormalizes weights over active
providers. After a training run, _RewardMemory_ estimates provider
reliability for each emotional regime from traces. Providers with
stronger agreement are given larger weight multipliers for the next run,
with limits on the size of the adjustment. These multipliers and all
reward models stay fixed throughout that run; for each new video,
reflection further downweights or excludes missing or unsuitable cues
before the active scores are combined into a weighted average EmoReward
can be integrated into several RL algorithms alongside general video
rewards, and directly use training experience to calibrate reward
allocation without retraining the evaluators.

We evaluate EmoReward on HunyuanVideo 1.5 \[[64](#bib.bib24)\] with
DanceGRPO \[[72](#bib.bib26)\], FlowGRPO \[[29](#bib.bib11)\], and
SAGE-GRPO \[[80](#bib.bib30)\], observing higher VideoAlign scores for
the guided configurations across all three RL algorithms on emotional
prompts as the evaluation dataset for rollout. Under a fair comparison
on the same RL architecture and training budget, EmoReward achieves the
highest reliability selectivity and both composite calibration scores
among seven reward policies. Ablation studies and mechanism analyses
examine the contributions of reflective gating, provider pools, and
RewardMemory
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Ablations and Mechanism Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")).
Transfer experiments on Stable Diffusion 3.5 and FLUX show gains in
selected configurations alongside objective-dependent trade-offs, while
user studies show that EmoReward receives the highest human preference
and proportion of Good ratings and the lowest proportion of Bad ratings
for emotional fidelity, temporal coherence, and visual quality.

Our contributions are threefold.

- •
  We introduce EmoReward, to our knowledge the first self-evolving
  training mechanism for emotional video generation RL that calibrates
  reward providers across training runs without updating reward model
  parameters.
- •
  We propose RewardMemory, to our knowledge the first memory designed
  for multi-reward systems in video generation. It distills evidence
  reliability into bounded fusion weights, enabling iterative
  calibration of RL reward signals across training runs.
- •
  We inspire a novel cross-run self-evolution paradigm for post-training
  multimodal foundation models: a completed training run informs reward
  allocation in the next, making training experience reusable at the run
  level. Extensive experiments across three video RL algorithms and two
  image backbones, with ablations and human evaluations, validate
  EmoReward’s effectiveness.

## 2  Related Work

#### From verifiable RL to agentic feedback for non-verifiable rewards.

RLHF learns subjective preferences \[[8](#bib.bib3), [41](#bib.bib15)\],
whereas verifiable RL exploits correctness checks \[[16](#bib.bib8),
[75](#bib.bib27)\]. Agentic evaluators \[[77](#bib.bib28),
[9](#bib.bib4)\] and adaptive rubrics \[[15](#bib.bib7),
[70](#bib.bib25), [78](#bib.bib29)\] structure open-ended feedback.
Related approaches route experts \[[40](#bib.bib14)\], refine responses
\[[39](#bib.bib13)\], evolve rewards \[[35](#bib.bib51),
[76](#bib.bib50)\], or organize experience \[[47](#bib.bib19),
[71](#bib.bib52)\]. JitRL derives closed-form policy updates from
retrieved action advantages \[[25](#bib.bib10)\]. EmoReward instead
transfers reward–outcome evidence across completed training runs to
calibrate frozen providers, combining sample-level gating with
persistent fusion weights.

#### Agents for visual content creation.

Agentic visual creation organizes generation into planning, tool use,
and feedback-driven refinement. Video agents coordinate multi-shot
narratives, script evaluation, audiovisual planning, and learned camera
control\[[82](#bib.bib68), [18](#bib.bib78), [81](#bib.bib69),
[11](#bib.bib82), [57](#bib.bib61), [67](#bib.bib81)\]. Image agents
combine knowledge retrieval \[[14](#bib.bib58), [4](#bib.bib55)\],
learned creation and editing policies \[[23](#bib.bib77),
[27](#bib.bib76)\], and experience reuse \[[5](#bib.bib57),
[6](#bib.bib79)\]. Other approaches jointly improve generation and
evaluation \[[28](#bib.bib75), [51](#bib.bib80)\]. EmoReward naturally
achieves a self-evolving mechanism in video generation RL training,
rather than building a workflow or retraining reward models.

#### RLHF for visual generation and the emotion-reward gap.

Visual reward models capture preferences and multidimensional quality
\[[69](#bib.bib35), [22](#bib.bib36), [66](#bib.bib45),
[38](#bib.bib37), [68](#bib.bib38), [63](#bib.bib39),
[30](#bib.bib12)\]. Optimization using rewards and preferences extends
from images \[[2](#bib.bib2), [12](#bib.bib6), [43](#bib.bib16),
[60](#bib.bib21)\] to video and flow models \[[72](#bib.bib26),
[29](#bib.bib11), [80](#bib.bib30), [31](#bib.bib49)\], with benchmarks
assessing quality and compositional alignment \[[20](#bib.bib46),
[32](#bib.bib47), [49](#bib.bib48)\]. Related 3D, audiovisual, and
objectives for human animation address complementary aspects of visual
generation \[[24](#bib.bib32), [19](#bib.bib31), [59](#bib.bib33),
[55](#bib.bib34)\]. Motion editing and transfer \[[52](#bib.bib65),
[54](#bib.bib66), [37](#bib.bib71)\], shape-aware and consistent image
editing \[[33](#bib.bib70), [36](#bib.bib73)\], and pose- and
identity-preserving animation \[[58](#bib.bib64), [42](#bib.bib72),
[56](#bib.bib63)\] offer finer control over visual content. Distillation
\[[74](#bib.bib62), [21](#bib.bib60), [13](#bib.bib56)\] and feature
reuse \[[34](#bib.bib74)\] improve generation efficiency, complementing
reward-based post-training. Affect-grounded augmentation
\[[61](#bib.bib53)\] and reflective action-conditioned generation
\[[73](#bib.bib54)\] further connect feedback with video synthesis.
Temporal video modeling and dynamic facial-expression recognition
provide additional perceptual foundations \[[53](#bib.bib67),
[3](#bib.bib59)\]. Emotion understanding supplies multimodal evidence
\[[7](#bib.bib40), [26](#bib.bib41), [79](#bib.bib42)\] and grounded
affect representations \[[45](#bib.bib18), [10](#bib.bib5),
[1](#bib.bib1)\]; EmoReward addresses how their reliability should
govern reward fusion across scenes and training runs.

### 3.1  Preliminaries

#### Multi-reward optimization with GRPO.

Let $`p`$ be a prompt and $`x`$ a generated video from a policy
$`\pi_{\theta}(x\mid p)`$; a scalar reward is $`R(x,p)\in\mathbb{R}`$.
For a prompt $`p`$, GRPO samples a group
$`\mathcal{G}(p)=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$ and forms standardized group
advantages

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
A_{i}=\frac{R(x_{i},p)-\mu_{\mathcal{G}}}{\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}+\epsilon},\qquad\mu_{\mathcal{G}}=\tfrac{1}{G}\textstyle\sum_{j}R(x_{j},p),\quad\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{G}\textstyle\sum_{j}(R(x_{j},p)-\mu_{\mathcal{G}})^{2},
``` |  | (1) |

which enter the clipped, KL-regularized objective in
Appendix [F](#A6.SS0.SSS0.Px3 "Generator Optimization Objective ‣ Appendix F Derivation of the Closed-Form KL Update ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL").
The policy update depends on reward differences inside the group. A
common additive constant cancels exactly; positive rescaling changes
only the relative effect of $`\epsilon`$. A coefficient of variation
measures dispersion, not policy-learning signal quality; we also assess
within-prompt ranking agreement and downstream quality.

#### Why static emotion fusion fails.

Given reward primitives $`\mathcal{T}=\{1,\ldots,K\}`$ with normalized
scores $`s_{k}(x,p)\in[0,1]`$, a fixed fusion is
$`R_{\mathrm{emo}}^{\mathrm{static}}=\sum_{k}w_{k}^{0}s_{k}`$ with
$`w_{k}^{0}\geq 0,\sum_{k}w_{k}^{0}=1`$. This assumes a fixed trust
allocation across scenes, although facial visibility and the relevance
of motion depend on the intended emotion. Writing each primitive as a
constant plus a group-varying part $`s_{k}=c_{k}+\delta_{k}`$ and
splitting into an active set $`\mathcal{A}`$ and a dead/saturated set
$`\mathcal{D}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{emo}}^{\mathrm{static}}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}w_{k}^{0}\delta_{k}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}w_{k}^{0}c_{k}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}w_{k}^{0}c_{k},
``` |  | (2) |

the constant terms cancel from the centered GRPO numerator. Removing
them alone need not change the standardized advantage. Calibration
matters when provider validity, errors, or relative contributions vary
across candidates, or when the emotion branch is mixed with another
reward. EmoReward addresses these cases through gates and
memory-conditioned fusion.

### 3.2  Overview

EmoReward grounds emotional intent, scores visual evidence, and
calibrates provider trust:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Plan}}e(p),\quad(x,e)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Act}}\{s_{k},u_{k}\},\quad(s,u,e,x)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Reflect}}R_{\mathrm{emo}},\quad\mathcal{M}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{Evolve}}w^{\mathrm{RM}}(z).
``` |  | (3) |

Here $`e(p)`$ is an EmoScript, $`u_{k}`$ denotes evidence validity, and
$`z`$ is the emotional-context signature. RewardMemory $`\mathcal{M}`$
stores completed reference-run traces; Evolve produces
$`w^{\mathrm{RM}}(z)`$ and exports the corresponding regime multipliers
$`m_{k}(r)`$
(Section [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Evolve: RewardMemory ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")).
Reflect combines these fixed multipliers with current-video gates. The
resulting emotion reward is mixed with a general video reward:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{total}}(x,p)=\frac{\alpha R_{\mathrm{vid}}(x,p)+\beta R_{\mathrm{emo}}(x,p)}{\alpha+\beta},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`R_{\mathrm{vid}}`$ is the evaluation score supplied by the
original reward system, for example a general video-quality evaluator
\[[30](#bib.bib12)\]; $`\alpha,\beta\geq 0`$ control its mixture with
the calibrated emotion reward, with $`\alpha+\beta>0`$.
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
connects these stages through their shared fusion interface.

### 3.3  Plan and Act: Emotional Grounding

#### Plan: prompt-conditioned criteria.

The planner converts the prompt into an EmoScript independently of
RewardMemory:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
e(p)=(v^{\star},a^{\star},\mathbf{q}^{\star}_{\mathrm{AU}},\mathbf{b}^{\star},\Omega).
``` |  | (5) |

The implemented representation contains scalar valence and arousal
targets, target facial AU intensities, body cues, and an expression
window. The memory regime is inferred later from observed context.
Continuous VAD trajectories and dominance targets are outside this
implementation. The EmoScript separates intended affect from its
observable cues. For example, restrained sadness need not imply large
motion or an exaggerated face: target intensities, body cues, and the
expression window define what to assess, while reliability is determined
from the generated evidence and completed-run experience.

#### Act: observation and scoring.

Act evaluates a video already sampled from
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p)`$; it does not generate or revise the video.
Each provider returns

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(s_{k},u_{k})=\mathcal{S}_{k}(x,e(p)),\qquad s_{k},u_{k}\in[0,1],
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\mathcal{S}_{k}`$ is provider $`k`$’s scoring operator, which
aggregates frame-level measurements or evaluates the whole clip. The
seven fusion providers comprise a VLM judge, facial AUs, body cues,
temporal evidence, CLIP-AUTT, R1-Omni, and motion naturalness; chromatic
atmosphere and kinetic intensity are auxiliary readouts, giving nine
observation channels. Score $`s_{k}`$ measures criterion agreement,
while $`u_{k}`$ marks usable evidence. A missing face channel’s neutral
default is not evidence and is excluded through its validity mask.
Auxiliary readouts do not enter the seven-provider calibration policy.

specifies scoring interfaces;
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1 EmoReward as a Base-RL-Agnostic Reward Add-on ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")

Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Ablations and Mechanism Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL").

### 3.4  Reflect: Instance-Level Calibration

Reflect computes provider-specific gates from the current evidence,
prompt, and training step. The factorization is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{k}=g^{\mathrm{phase}}_{k}\,g^{\mathrm{conflict}}_{k}\,g^{\mathrm{face}}_{k}\,g^{\mathrm{subtle}}_{k}\,u_{k},\qquad g_{k}\in[0,1],
``` |  | (7) |

with neutral factors for channels to which a condition does not apply.
Phase denotes the training-step warmup, face gating controls
face-dependent evidence, conflict gating attenuates disagreement between
semantic providers, and subtlety gating restrains intensity-sensitive
cues. Motion naturalness uses its observation confidence. These controls
are computed, not learned
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Reflect: Instance-Dependent Gate Construction ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")).

#### Evidence-dependent trust.

The phase factor schedules reward evidence over generator training,
distinct from the within-video expression window. Face gating limits
channels requiring facial evidence; conflict and subtlety gating temper
disagreeing semantic assessments and intensity-sensitive cues. Their
provider-specific application changes relative influence; a shared
positive attenuation would largely cancel under normalization.

#### Composition with experience.

For a fixed base weight $`w_{k}^{0}`$, define the effective contribution
$`a_{k}(x,p)=w_{k}^{0}g_{k}(x,p)m_{k}(r(x,p))`$. Reflective Reward
Gating uses $`m_{k}=1`$; EmoReward uses the fixed table produced by
Evolve. With $`\mathcal{A}=\{k:a_{k}>\epsilon_{g}\}`$, active-provider
normalization gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{emo}}(x,p)=\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}a_{k}(x,p)s_{k}(x,p)}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{A}}a_{k}(x,p)+\epsilon}.
``` |  | (8) |

Invalid evidence receives no mass. A common positive factor cancels
except through the stabilizer; calibration acts through relative
provider contributions and differences between videos. Regime $`r`$
selects a table entry; gate $`g_{k}`$ controls current evidence
validity. A provider favored by memory still contributes nothing when
its current evidence is invalid. With no memory entry, unit multipliers
recover Reflect-only fusion; the scoring interfaces and generator
objective remain the same.

1: Traces $`\mathcal{M}`$ from a completed reference run, provider count
$`K`$, temperature $`\tau`$, log-multiplier cap $`c`$, minimum support
$`n_{\min}`$

2: for each context and the global fallback do

3:   Select matching traces; use the global set if the context has fewer
than $`n_{\min}`$ traces

4:   for each provider $`k`$ do

5:    $`\rho_{k}\leftarrow\operatorname{Corr}(s_{i,k},y_{i})`$; use zero
for constant scores

6:   end for

7:   $`\bar{\rho}\leftarrow K^{-1}\sum_{j}\rho_{j}`$

8:   for each provider $`k`$ do

9:
   $`m_{k}\leftarrow\exp(\operatorname{clip}((\rho_{k}-\bar{\rho})/\tau,-c,c))`$

10:   end for

11:   Store the context multipliers

12: end for

13: Export once; freeze the table for a subsequent generator-training
run

### 3.5  Evolve: RewardMemory

RewardMemory treats reward allocation as a decision over emotional
context $`z`$: the providers are actions, their fusion weights form a
policy, and a stored outcome $`y`$ supplies a non-parametric reliability
signal. Experience is collected during a *completed reference training
run* with Reflective Reward Gating. Each trace
$`m_{i}=(z_{i},\{s_{i}^{k}\},\{g_{i}^{k}\},w_{i},R_{i},y_{i})`$ records
context, scores, gates, weights, and outcomes. Evolve groups these
traces into a regime neighborhood $`\mathcal{N}(z)`$, with a global
fallback for insufficient support, and computes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{k}(z)=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{i\in\mathcal{N}(z)}(s_{i}^{k},y_{i})}{\sigma_{s^{k}}\sigma_{y}+\epsilon},\qquad A_{k}^{\mathrm{rel}}(z)=\rho_{k}(z)-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j}\rho_{j}(z).
``` |  | (9) |

Reliability uses raw provider scores; constant scores receive zero
correlation. It measures agreement with the reference outcome, not
independent emotional fidelity. The centered advantage tilts the base
allocation through bounded multipliers:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{k}=\operatorname{clip}(A_{k}^{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau,-c,c),\qquad w_{k}^{\mathrm{RM}}(z)=\frac{w_{k}^{0}e^{\Delta_{k}}}{\sum_{j}w_{j}^{0}e^{\Delta_{j}}}.
``` |  | (10) |

This is the closed-form optimum of a KL-regularized allocation with
clipped utilities
(Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Derivation of the Closed-Form KL Update ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")).
The exported $`m_{k}(r)=e^{\Delta_{k}}`$ compose with current instance
gates in
Eq. [8](#S3.E8 "In Composition with experience. ‣ 3.4 Reflect: Instance-Level Calibration ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL").
The table is fixed throughout the next training run: only the generator
receives gradient updates, and runtime lookup neither re-estimates
reliability nor writes back to the table.
Algorithms [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Act: observation and scoring. ‣ 3.3 Plan and Act: Emotional Grounding ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
and [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Composition with experience. ‣ 3.4 Reflect: Instance-Level Calibration ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
separate reward computation from between-run memory construction.
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Evolve: RewardMemory Construction and Use ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
explains context grouping and table construction;
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I RewardMemory: A Worked Rollout Example ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
provides a trace-based worked example.

## 5  Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed EmoReward, a self-evolving reward calibration
framework for emotional video generation reinforcement learning.
EmoReward combines instance-level Reflective Reward Gating with
RewardMemory, which transfers provider-reliability estimates from a
completed training run to subsequent optimization without updating
reward-model parameters. Experiments across video optimizers,
complemented by ablations, image-transfer analyses, and human ratings,
support the utility of adaptive calibration while identifying
objective-dependent trade-offs. These findings suggest that
completed-run experience can inform the allocation of trust among
heterogeneous reward providers, providing a foundation for further
research on reward-system evolution across training.

## Appendix A Limitations and Future Work

#### Beyond emotional video generation.

EmoReward studies a training-adaptive reward system that transfers
evidence about provider reliability between generator-training runs
without updating reward-model parameters. The present evaluation focuses
on emotion, where different scenes call for different combinations of
facial, gestural, semantic, and motion evidence. A broader application
would require a task-appropriate provider pool and a meaningful outcome
for calibration. Action fidelity and audio–visual correspondence are
natural visual extensions; language models and tool-use agents could
similarly combine semantic judges, execution checks, and
trajectory-level assessments. Whether the same calibration rule
transfers effectively remains an empirical question, especially when
providers share errors or outcomes favor only part of the task.

#### Iterative evolution across training runs.

A completed training run can be treated as a unit of experience for its
successor. Repeating this procedure could produce a sequence of
generators and reward-allocation tables, with each generation
contributing new traces. Our experiments evaluate a
reference-to-calibrated transition, not sustained improvement across
many generations. Such an extension must address distribution shift,
stale evidence, and feedback loops that reinforce a proxy’s errors.
Retaining diverse historical traces and evaluating held-out outcomes
would be important tests of whether repeated calibration remains useful.

#### Model-level human feedback.

The trace representation also suggests a way to carry human assessments
of a completed model into subsequent training. For example, a reviewer
who finds movement too weak could request greater emphasis on suitable
motion providers in the affected contexts; excessive motion could
motivate the opposite adjustment. This would complement sample-level
preference feedback with structured assessments of a model’s final
behavior, translated into bounded changes to the next run’s reward
allocation. Mapping subjective feedback to providers and regimes,
reconciling different raters, and preventing compensating failures
remain open design questions. The ordinal study reported here evaluates
outputs; it does not implement this proposed feedback mechanism.

## Appendix B Human Evaluation: Ordinal Ratings

#### Study and comparison groups.

The study includes 18 participants and 30 prompts, with one video per
prompt from each of three methods: the pretrained generator without RL
(GT), SAGE-GRPO, and SAGE-GRPO with EmoReward. GT is a generator
baseline, not a ground-truth human video. Participants assessed each
output separately on three criteria using Bad, Normal, or Good. The
supplied aggregate results contain 540 judgments for each
method–criterion combination, consistent with the full
participant–prompt product. Across all three methods and criteria, there
are 4,860 categorical judgments. These ordinal counts are separate from
the earlier pairwise preference protocol; pairwise choices are not
converted into categorical ratings.

#### Criteria.

*Emotion fidelity* concerns how convincingly the video conveys the
prompt’s intended affect, including whether expression and gesture fit
its emotional tone. *Temporal coherence* concerns continuity of
expression, identity, and movement through the clip. *Visual quality*
concerns perceptual detail, anatomy, and visible artifacts. The
categories distinguish poor, intermediate, and good quality on each
axis. They are rated separately rather than collapsed into a single
preference or an assumption that stronger emotional intensity is always
better.

#### Reading the distributions.

Relative to SAGE-GRPO, EmoReward’s Good share increases by $`7.0`$,
$`2.8`$, and $`12.2`$ percentage points for emotion fidelity, temporal
coherence, and visual quality, respectively. These differences are

has more Good ratings but also more Bad ratings on every criterion, so a
Good-only summary would omit part of the observed pattern.

#### Statistical scope.

The present report uses the supplied aggregated counts. It does not
treat repeated judgments from the same participant or prompt as
independent observations. Participant- and prompt-level records are
needed for uncertainty estimates that respect both sources of
dependence; no confidence interval or significance claim is inferred
from the aggregate margins. The aggregates do not document recruitment,
presentation order, blinding, exclusions, or prompt-selection
provenance. Those collection details must be established from the study
records rather than inferred from the rating totals.

### C.1  Facial expression: AU matching and expression similarity

#### AU matching (*micro*).

A face detector followed by a multi-task action-unit head emits eight AU
intensities per detected face, $`\widehat{q}_{j,t}\in[0,1]`$ for
$`j\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{FACS}}=\{1,2,4,6,9,12,25,26\}`$. Against the
EmoScript target intensities $`q^{\star}_{j}`$ we score agreement by a
per-AU intersection-over-union overlap (rather than an $`\ell_{p}`$
kernel, so a target and an observation are rewarded for occupying the
same activation interval), averaged over face frames:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{IoU}(q^{\star}_{j},\widehat{q}_{j,t})=\frac{\min(q^{\star}_{j},\widehat{q}_{j,t})}{\max(q^{\star}_{j},\widehat{q}_{j,t})+\epsilon},\quad s_{\mathrm{micro}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}^{\star}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{IoU}(q^{\star}_{j},\widehat{q}_{j,t}),
``` |  | (12) |

with AU compliance and validity
$`c_{\mathrm{AU}}=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{A}^{\star}_{\mathrm{req}}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}_{\mathrm{req}}}\mathbf{1}[\max_{t\in\mathcal{F}}\widehat{q}_{j,t}>\theta_{\mathrm{AU}}]`$
and $`u_{\mathrm{micro}}=|\mathcal{F}|/T`$. The provider is inactive
when no frame yields a face with AU readings. In our implementation,
$`\theta_{\mathrm{AU}}=0.3`$.

#### Expression similarity (*clip_autt*).

A temporal vision–language encoder embeds a $`T_{\mathrm{clip}}`$-frame
clip into $`\mathbf{v}`$ and compares it to $`N_{\mathrm{au}}`$
action-unit text-prompt embeddings $`\{\mathbf{t}_{j}\}`$ by cosine
similarity, mapping the mean similarity from $`[-1,1]`$ to $`[0,1]`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{s}_{\mathrm{au}}=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{au}}}\sum_{j}\cos(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{t}_{j}),\qquad s_{\mathrm{clip}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\bar{s}_{\mathrm{au}}+1).
``` |  | (13) |

An auxiliary discrete head additionally yields a negative-affect
probability
$`p_{\mathrm{neg}}=\operatorname{softmax}(\text{logits})_{\mathrm{neg}}`$,
from which the scorer derives $`\hat{v}=1-p_{\mathrm{neg}}`$ and
$`\hat{a}=\min(1,\,\lambda_{a}p_{\mathrm{neg}}+b_{a})`$; the linear
rescale in
Eq. [13](#A3.E13 "In Expression similarity (clip_autt). ‣ C.1 Facial expression: AU matching and expression similarity ‣ Appendix C Act: Provider Interface and Reference Scoring Rules ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
(rather than $`\operatorname{clip}`$) preserves the ordering on clips
where $`\bar{s}_{\mathrm{au}}<0`$. In our implementation,
$`T_{\mathrm{clip}}=16`$, $`N_{\mathrm{au}}=47`$, $`\lambda_{a}=1.5`$,
and $`b_{a}=0.2`$.

### C.2  Semantic emotion understanding: Valence–arousal reading and the alignment seed

#### Valence–arousal reading (*r1omni*).

A multimodal emotion-understanding model produces a free-text emotion
description that is parsed to an emotion label and continuous
valence–arousal estimates $`(\hat{v},\hat{a})`$. Given EmoScript targets
$`(v^{\star},a^{\star})`$ the reward supports three modes (default:
valence):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{\mathrm{r1}}=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}\big[(1-|\hat{v}-v^{\star}|)+(1-|\hat{a}-a^{\star}|)\big],&\textsc{alignment},\\[2.84526pt]
\hat{v},&\textsc{valence},\\[2.84526pt]
\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{v}+\hat{a}),&\textsc{mean}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (14) |

#### Text-alignment seed (*macro*).

When a general video–text alignment model is available it blends its
text-alignment head $`\mathrm{TA}\in[0,1]`$ with AU compliance weighted
by face rate $`w_{\mathrm{face}}=\min(\text{face rate},1)`$; otherwise a
heuristic reads compliance directly:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{\mathrm{macro}}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{\mathrm{TA}}\mathrm{TA}+(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{TA}})c_{\mathrm{AU}}w_{\mathrm{face}},&\text{VideoAlign reflector},\\[2.84526pt]
b_{\mathrm{macro}}+(1-b_{\mathrm{macro}})c_{\mathrm{AU}}\pi_{\mathrm{conf}},&\text{heuristic (active iff face rate}\geq\theta_{\mathrm{macro}}).\end{cases}
``` |  | (15) |

In our implementation, $`\lambda_{\mathrm{TA}}=0.6`$,
$`b_{\mathrm{macro}}=0.3`$, and $`\theta_{\mathrm{macro}}=0.1`$. The
confidence $`\pi_{\mathrm{conf}}`$ belongs to the heuristic reflector.
Because the alignment-seeded branch shares an evaluator with the
reference outcome, its correlation cannot serve as independent emotion
validation.

### C.3  Body and temporal evidence (body, temporal)

The body provider counts each EmoScript body marker
$`m\in\mathcal{M}^{\star}`$ satisfied by at least one frame; the
temporal provider locates the frame $`t^{\star}`$ of peak expected-AU
energy and scores its distance to the EmoScript window
$`[t_{s},t_{e}]`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{body}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}^{\star}|}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}^{\star}}\mathbf{1}\big[\exists\,t:\ b_{m,t}=1\big],`$ |  | (16) |
|  | $`\displaystyle t^{\star}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\arg\max_{t}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}}\widehat{q}_{j,t},`$ |  | (17) |
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{temporal}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&t_{s}\leq t^{\star}\leq t_{e},\\[2.84526pt]
\max\!(0,\,1-\dfrac{\min(|t^{\star}-t_{s}|,|t^{\star}-t_{e}|)}{\max(t_{e}-t_{s},\,\delta_{\Omega})}),&\text{else.}\end{cases}`$ |  | (18) |

The window stabilizer is $`\delta_{\Omega}=0.1`$ in our implementation.
These scores require their corresponding observations; unavailable
evidence receives the neutral fallback $`s_{\mathrm{neutral}}`$ and
validity zero. We use $`s_{\mathrm{neutral}}=0.5`$. In the analyzed
traces, the micro, body, and temporal channels are constant at $`0.5`$;
this does not imply that every generated video lacks a face or body.

### C.4  Chromatic atmosphere (chromatic)

A per-frame chromatic signal blends the mean saturation
$`\bar{S}_{t}\in[0,1]`$ with the fraction of warm-hue pixels
$`W_{t}=\overline{\mathbf{1}[h\leq\theta_{\mathrm{warm}}^{-}\ \vee\ h\geq\theta_{\mathrm{warm}}^{+}]}`$
(on the standard $`[0,179]`$ hue scale), and the clip-level score aligns
the frame mean to the target valence $`v^{\star}`$ (linearly mapped to
$`[0,1]`$):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{t}=\lambda_{\mathrm{sat}}\bar{S}_{t}+(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{sat}})W_{t},\qquad\bar{c}=\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{t}c_{t},\qquad s_{\mathrm{chromatic}}=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!(1-|\bar{c}-v^{\star}|).
``` |  | (19) |

In our implementation, $`\lambda_{\mathrm{sat}}=0.5`$ and the warm-hue
thresholds are $`\theta_{\mathrm{warm}}^{-}=30`$,
$`\theta_{\mathrm{warm}}^{+}=150`$ on the stated hue scale.

### C.5  Kinetic intensity (kinetic)

A per-frame kinetic signal normalizes the mean absolute frame difference
using saturation scale $`\sigma_{\mathrm{kin}}`$, and the clip-level
score aligns the frame mean to the target arousal $`a^{\star}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
k_{t}=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!(\tfrac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{kin}}B_{I}}\,\overline{|I_{t}-I_{t-1}|}),\qquad\bar{k}=\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{t}k_{t},\qquad s_{\mathrm{kinetic}}=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!(1-|\bar{k}-a^{\star}|).
``` |  | (20) |

In our implementation, $`\sigma_{\mathrm{kin}}=0.20`$ and the pixel
range is $`B_{I}=255`$. Appearance features describe potential cues;
they are not part of the seven-provider memory update.

### C.6  Motion naturalness (motion)

Dense optical flow $`\mathbf{f}_{t}`$ on grayscale,
short-side-$`L_{\mathrm{flow}}`$ frames yields per-frame magnitude
$`m_{t}=\overline{\|\mathbf{f}_{t}\|}`$ and a global (dominant) motion
vector $`\mathbf{g}_{t}=\overline{\mathbf{f}_{t}}`$. Four bounded
sub-scores reward smooth flow, low camera jerk, low warp residual, and
low compensated flicker:

|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{flow}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\kappa(\overline{|\Delta m_{t}|};\,\sigma_{\mathrm{flow}}),`$ | $`\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{cam}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\kappa(\overline{\|\Delta^{2}\mathbf{g}_{t}\|};\,\sigma_{\mathrm{cam}}),`$ |  | (21) |
|  | $`\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{warp}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\kappa(\overline{|\,\mathcal{W}(I_{t-1};\mathbf{f}_{t})-I_{t}\,|};\,\sigma_{\mathrm{warp}}),`$ | $`\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{flick}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\kappa(\overline{|\,\mathcal{S}(I_{t-1};\mathbf{g}_{t})-I_{t}\,|};\,\sigma_{\mathrm{flick}}),`$ |  |  |

where $`\mathcal{W}`$ is flow-warping and $`\mathcal{S}`$ a global-shift
compensation. With base
$`r_{\mathrm{base}}=\sum_{j\in\{\mathrm{flow,cam,warp,flick}\}}\lambda_{j}r_{j}`$
and activity $`E=1-\exp(-\bar{m}/\sigma_{E})`$, the arousal-conditioned
score and confidence are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{motion}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\lambda_{M}r_{\mathrm{base}}+(1-\lambda_{M})(1-|E-a^{\star}|),`$ |  | (22) |
|  | $`\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{motion}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\lambda_{U}\min\!(1,\tfrac{T-2}{n_{U}})+(1-\lambda_{U})\min\!(1,\tfrac{\operatorname{std}(m_{t})}{\sigma_{U}}),`$ |  | (23) |

reverting to $`s_{\mathrm{motion}}=r_{\mathrm{base}}`$ when no target
arousal is supplied. The motion gate scales linearly with this
observation confidence
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Reflect: Instance-Dependent Gate Construction ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")).
The mixture coefficients obey $`\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=1`$. In our
implementation, $`L_{\mathrm{flow}}=160`$,
$`(\sigma_{\mathrm{flow}},\sigma_{\mathrm{cam}},\sigma_{\mathrm{warp}},\sigma_{\mathrm{flick}})=(0.35,0.65,0.085,0.11)`$,
and
$`(\lambda_{\mathrm{flow}},\lambda_{\mathrm{cam}},\lambda_{\mathrm{warp}},\lambda_{\mathrm{flick}})=(0.34,0.24,0.24,0.18)`$.
We use $`\sigma_{E}=1.5`$, $`\lambda_{M}=0.82`$, $`\lambda_{U}=0.6`$,
$`n_{U}=8`$, and $`\sigma_{U}=0.035`$.

### C.7  Score normalization and validity

The evaluated fusion consumes the providers’ bounded scores directly. An
optional running-statistic normalization is not an additional
transformation applied to those scores. Availability and confidence are
retained separately from score magnitude: unavailable evidence receives
a neutral fallback but zero effective contribution. Motion confidence is
incorporated once through its channel gate, while an activity mask
handles unavailable motion observations. This convention avoids counting
the same confidence twice. The micro, body, and temporal channels are
constant in the analyzed reference traces; their neutral readings do not
establish the absence of visible faces or bodies in the generated
videos.

## Appendix D Reflect: Instance-Dependent Gate Construction

Reflect separates training progress, semantic consistency, observation
availability, and the prompt’s intended intensity. A factor is neutral
for providers to which it does not apply. Let $`t`$ denote the generator
update, $`s_{0}<s_{1}`$ the phase-transition endpoints,
$`\gamma_{\min}`$ a phase floor, and $`q_{f}`$ the available
face-evidence ratio:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h(t)=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(\frac{t-s_{0}}{s_{1}-s_{0}}\right),\quad b=\max(\gamma_{\min},h(t))g_{\mathrm{conflict}},\quad f=\min(1,q_{f}/\theta_{f}).
``` |  | (24) |

The semantic-conflict factor attenuates disagreement
$`d=|s_{\mathrm{CLIP\text{-}AUTT}}-s_{\mathrm{R1\text{-}Omni}}|`$ above
a threshold $`\theta_{d}`$, with lower bound $`\gamma_{d}`$. Subtlety is
detected from prompt cues or $`a^{\star}\leq\theta_{a}`$; it selects
restraint factors $`\xi_{f}`$, $`\xi_{t}`$, and $`\xi_{e}`$ for
facial/body, temporal, and semantic channels, respectively. Otherwise
these restraint factors equal one. Before applying
observation-availability masks, the gates are

|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{macro}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=b,`$ | $`\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{micro}}=g_{\mathrm{body}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=bf\xi_{f},`$ | $`\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{temporal}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=bf\xi_{t},`$ |  | (25) |
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{CLIP\text{-}AUTT}}=g_{\mathrm{R1\text{-}Omni}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=b\xi_{e},`$ | $`\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{motion}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\lambda_{g}+(1-\lambda_{g})c_{\mathrm{motion}}.`$ |  |  |  |  |

Here $`c_{\mathrm{motion}}`$ is the optical-flow observation confidence.
Availability masking and continuous confidence together instantiate the
validity term in
Eq. [7](#S3.E7 "In 3.4 Reflect: Instance-Level Calibration ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL");
confidence is not multiplied a second time.

#### Implementation settings.

In our implementation, the default warmup endpoints are $`s_{0}=40`$ and
$`s_{1}=100`$, with $`\gamma_{\min}=0.1`$. Face gating uses
$`\theta_{f}=0.35`$; semantic conflict uses $`\theta_{d}=0.20`$ and
$`\gamma_{d}=0.35`$. For subtle prompts, detected using
$`\theta_{a}=0.4`$ or prompt cues,
$`(\xi_{f},\xi_{t},\xi_{e})=(0.85,0.75,0.80)`$; the motion floor is
$`\lambda_{g}=0.55`$. These are rule-based controls rather than learned
reward-model parameters. Historical exported traces retain their
recorded gates; the default specification alone does not reconstruct a
past run’s full configuration.

#### Effect on normalized fusion.

A factor shared by every active provider cannot by itself redistribute
trust. For $`g_{k}=\gamma h_{k}`$ with $`\gamma>0`$, active
normalization yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{emo}}=\frac{\sum_{k}w_{k}h_{k}s_{k}}{\sum_{k}w_{k}h_{k}+\epsilon/\gamma}.
``` |  | (26) |

Except for the stabilizer, the common scale cancels. Reflect changes the
relative allocation through provider-specific factors, invalidation, and
composition with the original reward branch. The face-activity proxy
used to group exported traces in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Evolve: RewardMemory Construction and Use ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
is distinct from the current video’s face-evidence ratio.

## Appendix E Evolve: RewardMemory Construction and Use

#### Record the reference run (Figure [7](#A9.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix I RewardMemory: A Worked Rollout Example ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")A).

A completed run with Reflective Reward Gating supplies the experience
used by RewardMemory. Each trace keeps the prompt–video context, raw
provider scores, gates, applied weights, and VideoAlign outcome
separate. The context describes target affect and available evidence;
its face-activity proxy records whether selected scoring branches depart
from their neutral defaults, rather than measuring visible-face
coverage. The recorded scores and outcomes provide the basis for
comparing providers.

#### Build the calibration table (Figure [7](#A9.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix I RewardMemory: A Worked Rollout Example ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")B).

The complete reference bank is grouped by target arousal and face-branch
activity, with a global fallback for contexts with insufficient support.
Within each group, reliability is the correlation between a provider’s
raw scores and VideoAlign outcomes, before gating.
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Composition with experience. ‣ 3.4 Reflect: Instance-Level Calibration ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
compares each provider with the group’s average reliability and converts
the difference into a capped weight multiplier
(Eq. [10](#S3.E10 "In 3.5 Evolve: RewardMemory ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")).
This calibrates reward allocation without fitting a value network or
changing any reward model.

#### Use the memory in the next run (Figure [7](#A9.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix I RewardMemory: A Worked Rollout Example ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")C).

Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Act: observation and scoring. ‣ 3.3 Plan and Act: Emotional Grounding ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
looks up the matching multipliers, combines them with the base weights
and current-video gates, and normalizes over active providers. Memory
sets the allocation from prior experience; reflection can still suppress
unavailable or unsuitable cues. The table remains fixed throughout

controlled replay, not an additional training run. Reflect-only fusion
omits the memory adjustment;
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I RewardMemory: A Worked Rollout Example ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
explains the worked example.

## Appendix F Derivation of the Closed-Form KL Update

For positive base weights on the supported simplex, consider

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{w\in\Delta^{K-1}}\sum_{k}w_{k}A_{k}^{\mathrm{rel}}-\tau\sum_{k}w_{k}\log\frac{w_{k}}{w_{k}^{0}}.
``` |  | (27) |

The Lagrangian has stationary condition
$`A_{k}^{\mathrm{rel}}-\tau(\log(w_{k}/w_{k}^{0})+1)+\lambda=0`$.
Normalizing the resulting exponentials gives
$`w_{k}=w_{k}^{0}e^{A_{k}^{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau}/\sum_{j}w_{j}^{0}e^{A_{j}^{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau}`$.
Strict concavity yields a unique optimum. A shared additive baseline in
the utilities cancels from this unclipped solution.

For $`\Delta_{k}=\operatorname{clip}(A_{k}^{\mathrm{rel}}/\tau,-c,c)`$,
the same derivation applies to clipped utilities
$`\widetilde{A}_{k}=\tau\Delta_{k}`$. Since
$`e^{-c}\leq\sum_{j}w_{j}^{0}e^{\Delta_{j}}\leq e^{c}`$, normalized
weights satisfy

|     |                                                              |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       e^{-2c}\leq\frac{w_{k}^{\mathrm{RM}}}{w_{k}^{0}}\leq e^{2c}.
       ```                                                           |     | (28) |

The cap bounds relative changes; it does not guarantee a one-hot
distribution as $`\tau\to 0`$. Zero base weights remain outside the
support.

#### Scope of the bound and cold start.

The interval $`[e^{-c},e^{c}]`$ bounds the exported, unnormalized
multipliers; the normalized weight ratio has the wider bound above.
Clipping the utilities is not equivalent to solving the original
objective subject to a prescribed hard KL constraint. If all advantages
are zero, the update returns $`w^{0}`$. A constant provider has
$`\rho_{k}=0`$, but its centered advantage is negative only when the
mean reliability is positive; constant scores therefore do not imply
unconditional down-weighting. In the supplied traces,
positive-reliability providers make this relative attenuation
observable. None of these algebraic properties proves improved
generalization or emotional fidelity.

#### Group-relative invariance.

If $`R_{i}^{\prime}=aR_{i}+b`$ with $`a>0`$, then
$`\mu^{\prime}=a\mu+b`$, $`\sigma^{\prime}=a\sigma`$, and

|     |                                                     |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{R_{i}-\mu}{\sigma+\epsilon/a}.
       ```                                                  |     | (29) |

Translation cancels exactly. By contrast,
$`\operatorname{CV}(R)=\sigma/(|\mu|+\epsilon)`$ is not translation
invariant. Consequently, a reward offset can increase relative
dispersion without improving the standardized group advantage. This is
an algebraic property, not an experimental claim about learning
efficiency.

#### Generator Optimization Objective

For completeness, we state the clipped GRPO objective underlying the
group advantages in
Eq. [1](#S3.E1 "In Multi-reward optimization with GRPO. ‣ 3.1 Preliminaries ‣ 3 Method ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL")
\[[46](#bib.bib43)\]. Let $`h_{i,t}`$ denote the generation state and
$`a_{i,t}`$ its sampled transition in candidate trajectory $`i`$. Define
$`\varrho_{i,t}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(a_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t},p)/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t},p)`$.
With clipping radius $`\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}`$ and
reference-policy regularization coefficient $`\kappa`$,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)={}`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p,\{x_{i}\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\left(\min\!\left\{\varrho_{i,t}A_{i},\operatorname{clip}(\varrho_{i,t},1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}},1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{clip}})A_{i}\right\}\right.\right.`$ |  | (30) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\left.\left.-\kappa\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{i,t},p)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid h_{i,t},p)\right)\right)\right].`$ |  |  |

Here $`T_{i}`$ is the number of sampled transitions,
$`\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}`$ is the rollout policy, and
$`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is a fixed reference policy. The scalar reward of
the completed video determines $`A_{i}`$; advantages and reward-provider
outputs are treated as constants during the policy update. This
expression states the common clipped objective; individual video
optimizers specify their transition likelihoods, sampled timesteps, and
regularization. EmoReward changes the scalar reward supplied to the
optimizer, not those optimizer-specific choices. Its generator KL
penalty is distinct from the reward-allocation KL term derived above,
whose variables are provider weights rather than generator parameters.

### G.1  Reward Diagnostics

Let $`R_{i}`$ denote the fused training reward, $`y_{i}`$ the VideoAlign
reference outcome, and $`s_{i,k}`$ provider $`k`$’s score. A group $`g`$
contains candidates for one prompt.

#### Reward–outcome agreement (ROA).

ROA is an auxiliary input to GR rather than a displayed column in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Reward-policy ablation. ‣ 4.2 Ablations and Mechanism Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EmoReward: Self-Evolving Reward Calibration for Emotional Video Generation RL").
It measures within-group pairwise ranking agreement. For outcome-nontied
pairs $`\mathcal{C}_{g}=\{(i,j):i<j,\ |y_{i}-y_{j}|>\epsilon_{y}\}`$,
the intended statistic is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Agr}(g)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{g}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{C}_{g}}\left[\mathbf{1}\{(R_{i}-R_{j})(y_{i}-y_{j})>0\}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\{R_{i}=R_{j}\}\right].
``` |  | (31) |

The reported agreement is
$`\mathrm{ROA}=\operatorname{Mean}_{g}\operatorname{Agr}(g)`$. The
supplied pairwise helper uses $`\epsilon_{y}=10^{-9}`$ and returns
$`1/2`$ when no outcome-nontied pairs exist. However, its treatment of
reward ties differs from the symmetric half-credit convention above. The
original aggregation script is unavailable; we therefore retain the
archived ROA values as inputs to GR, without claiming to have recomputed
their tie handling. VideoAlign-only obtains one by construction when its
reward and reference ordering coincide. ROA measures agreement with that
evaluator, not independent human or emotional quality.

#### Within-group relative dispersion (SNR^(∗)).

With population standard deviation within each group,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{g}=\frac{\operatorname{Std}_{i\in g}(R_{i})}{|\operatorname{Mean}_{i\in g}(R_{i})|+\epsilon_{D}},\qquad\mathrm{SNR}^{*}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}}D_{g}.
``` |  | (32) |

The supplied diagnostic implementation uses $`\epsilon_{D}=10^{-6}`$.
This is a coefficient of variation, not a signal-to-noise power ratio. A
reward offset can increase it while leaving centered GRPO advantages
unchanged; a high value neither establishes nor is necessary for better
policy gradients. We report the archived per-policy reductions,
separately from the aggregate-level checks below.

#### Reliability selectivity (Sel.).

Let $`\rho_{k}=\operatorname{Corr}(s_{\cdot,k},y)`$ over the policy’s
evaluated candidates, using the nine-channel diagnostic pool
$`\mathcal{P}`$. For $`Z=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{P}}|\rho_{k}|>0`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{k}=\frac{|\rho_{k}|}{Z},\qquad\mathrm{Sel.}=1-\frac{-\sum_{k\in\mathcal{P}}q_{k}\log q_{k}}{\log|\mathcal{P}|},\qquad 0\log 0=0.
``` |  | (33) |

The pool includes chromatic and kinetic readouts as well as the seven
fusion providers. Sel. describes concentration of absolute
provider–outcome correlations, not applied fusion weights. It discards
correlation signs and does not establish causal provider usefulness. All
seven reported policies have $`Z>0`$; the auxiliary zero-sample row and
its all-zero-profile convention are excluded. Recomputing Sel. from the
exported correlation profiles agrees with the stored values within
$`5\times 10^{-5}`$.

### G.2  Normalized Pillars and Composite Scores

For the fixed comparison set $`\mathcal{A}`$ of seven policies, define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
N(v_{a})=\frac{v_{a}-\min_{b\in\mathcal{A}}v_{b}}{\max_{b\in\mathcal{A}}v_{b}-\min_{b\in\mathcal{A}}v_{b}}.
``` |  | (34) |

The supplied reducer returns zero for a constant input across policies;
none of the three reported inputs is constant. Normalization is specific
to the compared set, so the resulting scores are not absolute measures
transferable across benchmarks.

#### EF: preference-proxy pillar.

$`\mathrm{EF}_{a}=N(I_{a})`$, where $`I_{a}`$ is the archived mean
ImageReward score \[[69](#bib.bib35)\]. EF retains the benchmark’s
“Emotion Fidelity” abbreviation, but its input is a general visual
preference model. We do not interpret it as an independent measurement
of emotion fidelity. The raw means and normalization inputs are included
in the supplement.

#### GD: relative-dispersion pillar.

$`\mathrm{GD}_{a}=N(\mathrm{SNR}^{*}_{a})`$. The benchmark calls this
Gating Discriminativeness; mathematically, it is the normalized
coefficient of variation above. Its inclusion does not turn reward
dispersion into a gradient-quality measurement.

#### GR: agreement-weighted selectivity pillar.

$`\mathrm{GR}_{a}=N(\mathrm{Sel.}_{a}\,\mathrm{ROA}_{a})`$. GR combines
correlation-profile concentration with agreement with the reference
evaluator. Both inputs are diagnostic summaries; the product does not
establish an independent reliability ground truth.

#### core and emo: two specified aggregations.

The equal-weight composite and preference-emphasized composite are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{core}_{a}=\frac{\mathrm{EF}_{a}+\mathrm{GD}_{a}+\mathrm{GR}_{a}}{3},\qquad\mathrm{emo}_{a}=\lambda_{\mathrm{EF}}\mathrm{EF}_{a}+\lambda_{\mathrm{GD}}\mathrm{GD}_{a}+\lambda_{\mathrm{GR}}\mathrm{GR}_{a}.
``` |  | (35) |

Here the nonnegative coefficients sum to one; our implementation uses
$`(\lambda_{\mathrm{EF}},\lambda_{\mathrm{GD}},\lambda_{\mathrm{GR}})=(0.5,0.25,0.25)`$.
Full EmoReward scores $`0.922`$ on core and $`0.884`$ on emo, leading
under both specified weightings. Reflect without memory leads EF. These
observations concern the defined composites and comparison set, not
arbitrary weightings or universal superiority.

#### Separating observations from the replay.

The exported table’s global entry differs from its context-specific
entries. We use the global entry here to isolate the reweighting
operation, without claiming that it was the entry selected for these
historical videos. The initial active allocation is

illustrative starting weights, not reconstructed runtime gates.
Multiplying by the exported global multipliers and renormalizing
produces approximately $`(0.417,0.450,0.082,0.052)`$. On rollout \#2’s
recorded scores, the weighted mean changes from $`0.578`$ to $`0.563`$;
its actual logged emotion reward is $`0.721`$. The distinction matters
because the historical reward used its own training phase and
configuration. An increase in a provider’s allocation need not increase
the scalar fused score, and neither change alone establishes improved
generated quality.

## Appendix J Generalization to Image Generation

We evaluate Stable Diffusion 3.5 and FLUX with three optimizers and
three reward configurations on 256 DrawBench prompts per checkpoint: 18
settings and 84 checkpoints. Internal Emo measures the emotion-stack
reward; HPSv3 is a general preference objective also used to train the
baseline. Neither independently measures perceived emotion.

SD3.5 baseline results average three checkpoints at 420–500 updates;
calibrated variants average five at 2,780–3,000. FLUX baseline
checkpoints span 2,760–3,000, and calibrated checkpoints 2,780–3,000.
Standard errors describe checkpoint variation, not independent training
seeds. Per-prompt arrays support the reported means; paired significance
requires complete sample and generation-seed mappings and is not
claimed.

#### Observed transfer pattern.

With SD3.5–DanceGRPO, HPSv3 rises from $`9.552`$ (baseline) to
$`10.096`$ (Reflect) and $`10.302`$ (EmoReward). SD3.5–FlowGRPO declines
on both HPSv3 and internal Emo; FLUX’s HPSv3-optimized baseline leads on
HPSv3 across optimizers. Thus transfer is objective-dependent rather
than uniformly beneficial.
````

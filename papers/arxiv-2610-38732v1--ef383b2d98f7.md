# Adversarial Self-Distillation

Mohammad Sadegh Akhondzadeh Affiliation: University of Cologne   
Soroush H. Zargarbashi Affiliation: CISPA Helmholtz Center for
Information Security    Aleksandar Bojchevski Affiliation: University of
Cologne

###### Abstract

Even though current safety-aligned language models are able to refuse
harmful requests, their guard can still be bypassed by adversarial
perturbations. As in other domains, adversarial training is considered a
standard defense. However, due to the autoregressive nature of language
generation, supervised finetuning on template refusals does not
necessarily teach the model to refuse. Evidence of this is that the
adversarially trained model still complies when the attacker injects a
compliant prefill. We propose Adversarial Self-Distillation (ASD), which
extends a model’s own safe behavior to the space of adversarial
perturbations. The teacher (the model itself given the clean prompt) can
refuse the harmful request by default, or with the help of an additional
instruction in its context. ASD distills this knowledge to the student
(which is the same model under attack) at the prefixes that the student
visits (due to on-policy sampling). These potentially include compliant
openings. ASD achieves lower attack success than state-of-the-art
defenses with little loss in multiple-choice accuracy, and it becomes
able to recover a refusal even after a compliant opening.

## 1 Introduction

With the widespread public use of large language models, they are
increasingly exposed to harmful requests. Modern safety-aligned models
typically refuse such requests under ordinary prompting, yet they remain
vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, including optimized suffixes,
token modifications, and paraphrases ([Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib8);
[Carlini et al., 2023](#bib.bib20); [Hughes et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)).
A similar vulnerability has been well studied in other domains such as
images, speech and graphs ([Carlini and Wagner, 2018](#bib.bib19);
[Szegedy et al., 2014](#bib.bib16); [Akhondzadeh et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib43)). Adversarial training, as a common defense,
addresses it through a min-max objective: an inner optimization finds
inputs that maximize the model’s loss (worst-case input), while an outer
optimization updates the model to minimize that loss on the worst-case
inputs ([Goodfellow et al., 2015](#bib.bib17); [Madry et al.,
2018](#bib.bib18)). In classification, the goal is to keep the
probability of the correct label high even for the worst-case
perturbation. In language models, the goal is to prevent the model from
complying with (adversarially tweaked) harmful requests, either by
refusing directly or by providing a safe alternative (e.g. describing
why the request is not appropriate).

Adversarial training has also improved the robustness of language models
to jailbreaks ([Mazeika et al., 2024](#bib.bib10); [Xhonneux et al.,
2024](#bib.bib6); [Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), but two properties of
language generation make it less straightforward: the space of inputs is
vast and diverse, and outputs are generated autoregressively. The first
property affects the inner optimization, i.e., finding adversarial
examples. Discrete and black-box attacks require many candidate
evaluations, which is costly inside a training loop ([Zou et al.,
2023](#bib.bib8); [Chao et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)). This can be
partially mitigated with continuous and latent attacks ([Xhonneux et
al., 2024](#bib.bib6); [Sheshadri et al., 2025](#bib.bib30); [Dékány et
al., 2025](#bib.bib38)). Harmful intent can also be rephrased in
different ways, which is overlooked by a pool made of these
attacks ([Andriushchenko and Flammarion, 2024](#bib.bib36)).
Distributional Adversarial Training (DAT) addresses this by combining
sampling of new harmful prompts with local search for perturbations ([Hu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)). While a diverse enough training set (DAT)
finds more spots to repair, the outer optimization remains limited by
the second property.

Language generation is a Markov process over response prefixes. Both the
harmful and refusing answers are single trajectories sampled from this
space starting from $`\tilde{x}`$ (perturbed prompt) as a root.
Techniques like DAT choose better roots to start from, but the outer
loop (e.g. SFT) decides how the trajectory continues. In other words,
robustness also depends on the response prefixes reached during
generation ([Lin et al., 2020](#bib.bib24); [Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1)). SFT shapes only a few paths from the process (pushing
down sampled compliant openings, and increasing the probability of
template refusals), leaving the rest of the subtree only influenced by
shared parameters. Therefore, alignment-induced changes can concentrate
in the first few output positions ([Qi et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)). This
becomes more visible when the model is forced with a compliant prefill,
where the SFT-aligned model continues with either a broken trajectory or
compliance
([Fig. 1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).

This raises our central question: _can adversarial training teach a
model to correct its own unsafe continuation, beyond learning a template
refusal?_ Answering this requires separating two problems: _Safe entry_:
start a refusing trajectory given the attacked request; i.e. from
$`(\tilde{x},\varnothing)`$, and _Recovery_: continue refusing even if
starting with a compliant prefix $`w`$, $`(\tilde{x},w)`$: does the
model turn back if placed in a complying trajectory? A model that always
opens with a refusal looks perfectly safe under ordinary evaluation,
which means that adversarial training can overlook the recovery. In such
cases, the attacked model still has the chance of simply jumping off to
a complying answer after a few tokens. Training on recovery examples
addresses this second problem with fixed harmful prefixes ([Qi et al.,
2025](#bib.bib22)); we target the prefixes that the current model itself
generates under attack.

On-policy distillation suggests how to supply this signal. Rather than
training only on fixed refusal responses, Generalized Knowledge
Distillation (GKD) evaluates student-generated sequences, while
reverse-KL distillation penalizes continuations favored by the student
but not the teacher ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Gu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)). Building on this idea, we propose Adversarial
Self-Distillation (ASD): the model generates under an adversarial
prompt, and a copy of itself conditioned on the original request
provides token-level targets at the same response prefixes ([Zhao et
al., 2026](#bib.bib3); [Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib28)). ASD
therefore supplies feedback at states the student actually visits under
attack; see
[Fig. 2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation").

## 2 Background

Let $`x`$ be a harmful request and $`\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)`$ the
distribution (policy) of a safety-aligned language model over responses
$`y`$. We consider a jailbreak adversary as a map that modifies the
prompt $`x`$, to $`\tilde{x}`$, for example by appending a suffix or
rephrasing, while keeping its harmful intent unchanged. The adversary’s
goal is to make the model produce a response that substantively assists
the harmful request (complies). Note that in many cases the model may
still refuse the original request $`x`$. Let $`\mathcal{A}(x)`$ denote
the set of prompts the adversary can construct from $`x`$. If $`h(x,y)`$
indicates the model’s compliance in response $`y`$, the adversary’s
objective is:
$`\tilde{x}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x})}[h(x,y)].`$

Generally, robust training aims for the following objective:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\max_{\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{A}(x)}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x})}[h(x,y)]\Big],
``` |  |

where $`\mathcal{D}`$ is the distribution of harmful requests. The inner
maximization is the adversary’s objective (searching for the worst-case
perturbation), and the outer minimization updates the model to remain
safe. In practice, the inner maximization is approximated by an
adversarial attack, finding $`\tilde{x}`$ that breaks the model’s
safety. For LLMs, the outer minimization is typically implemented as
supervised finetuning (SFT) on a prescribed refusal $`\bar{y}`$ for each
attacked prompt $`\tilde{x}`$. This process runs iteratively, and
attacks are refreshed per iteration. A simple update assigns each
attacked prompt $`\tilde{x}`$ a prescribed refusal $`\bar{y}`$ and
minimizes
$`-\sum_{t}\log\pi_{\theta}(\bar{y}_{t}\mid\tilde{x},\bar{y}_{<t}).`$

Existing adversarial defenses focus on diversity in adversarial
examples. DAT, for example, also penalizes harmful responses ([Hu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), but on adversarial attacks built from a more
diverse space of harmful base prompts generated by a diffusion LLM.

### On-policy distillation

In knowledge distillation, a student model is trained to match
distributions supplied by a teacher. On-policy distillation (OPD)
samples a response $`y`$ from the current student and asks the teacher
to evaluate the next-token distribution at each student-generated prefix
$`y_{<t}`$ ([Lin et al., 2020](#bib.bib24); [Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1)). The student therefore receives supervision at states
induced by its own behavior, including prefixes that would not occur in
a prescribed target response as in SFT. Let $`p`$ and $`q`$ denote the
student and teacher next-token distributions at a sampled prefix. One
possible distillation loss is reverse KL:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\sum_{v}p(v)\log\frac{p(v)}{q(v)}=\mathbb{E}_{v\sim p}[-\log q(v)]-H(p).
``` |  | (1) |

Because the expectation in the loss weights tokens by the student’s
probability, this loss emphasizes candidate continuations that the
student currently considers likely but the teacher considers
unlikely ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)). On-policy sampling determines
*which prefixes* receive feedback, while the divergence determines *how*
the two distributions are matched at those prefixes.

In self-distillation, the model itself is also used to provide its own
teaching signal, for example by comparing a trainable student with a
frozen copy initialized from the same checkpoint ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib3); [Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib28)). The difference
between the teacher and student here lies in their conditioning
contexts. Different contexts can produce different next-token
distributions. Whether that difference provides useful supervision
depends on the information available to the teacher. Additional related
work is discussed in
[§F](#A6 "Appendix F Related work ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation").

## 3 Method

[Fig. 1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")shows
that by injecting compliant prefill tokens, the probability of refusal
(directly or by deflection) sharply decreases in models that are
adversarially trained with SFT. Intuitively, finetuning only on a
prescribed refusal $`\bar{y}`$ gives direct supervision at the prefixes
$`\bar{y}_{<t}`$, but it does not properly signal what the model should
do at a different prefix that it might produce under attack. In
particular, we want the student model to receive feedback at the
positions where it begins or continues unsafe compliance.

In our Adversarial Self-Distillation (ASD), the student generates given
an attacked request, while the teacher, which is the same model frozen
during each iteration, receives the original request (which it refuses
by default). The teacher’s refusal can be ensured by providing an
optional system instruction, specifying the harmful nature of the
request. We update the student towards the teacher’s distribution along
trajectories sampled by the student. We also use a separate benign
anchor to constrain changes on ordinary prompts.

### Setup for self-distillation.

As in other adversarial training setups, we finetune the model
iteratively. In each iteration, we evaluate the model, and generate a
separate pool of attacks given the latest state. For the pool, a
collection of attacks construct pairs $`(x,\tilde{x})`$, where $`x`$ is
an original harmful request, and $`\tilde{x}`$ is the adversarially
perturbed version with white-box access to the latest student state. In
each iteration, the student and teacher start from the same checkpoint,
which we denote $`\theta_{0}`$; this is the merged student from the
previous iteration (the base model in the first iteration). The teacher
receives only the clean request $`x`$, optionally preceded by a system
instruction stating that the request is harmful and must be refused; the
student never sees this instruction. The student samples
$`y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x})`$. At each response position
$`t`$, we compare next-token distributions as
$`p_{\theta,t}(v)=\pi_{\theta}(v\mid\tilde{x},y_{<t}),q_{0,t}(v)=\pi_{\theta_{0}}(v\mid x,y_{<t}).`$

Figure 3: Overview of ASD.

The student provides the rollouts, controlling at which trajectory
$`y_{<t}`$ the distributions are compared, and the teacher provides the
target distributions conditional on the prefixes $`y_{<t}`$ sampled by
the student. The response tokens, over which the probabilities are
evaluated, are shared between branches (both trajectories have the same
length) even though $`x`$ and $`\tilde{x}`$ may have different lengths
(context is not distilled).

### Training objective.

We finetune the student to match the frozen teacher’s next-token
distributions along student-generated responses. The objective combines
adversarial distillation on responses judged harmful with a benign
anchor that constrains drift on ordinary prompts. Let $`p_{\theta,t}`$
and $`q_{0,t}`$ denote the student and teacher next-token distributions
at a sampled prefix $`y_{<t}`$. We define
$`d_{K}(p_{\theta,t},q_{0,t})`$ as the reverse-KL divergence on a
compressed token space, where $`S_{t}`$ contains the teacher’s $`K`$
highest-probability tokens and all other tokens are collected into a
single tail bucket. Let
$`p_{\perp,t}=1-\sum_{v\in S_{t}}p_{\theta,t}(v)`$ and
$`q_{\perp,t}=1-\sum_{v\in S_{t}}q_{0,t}(v)`$. The token loss is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}d_{K}(p_{\theta,t},q_{0,t})={}&\sum_{v\in S_{t}}p_{\theta,t}(v)\log\frac{p_{\theta,t}(v)}{q_{0,t}(v)}\\
&+p_{\perp,t}\log\frac{p_{\perp,t}}{q_{\perp,t}}.\end{split}
``` |  | (2) |

For adversarial trajectory $`i`$ of response length $`R_{i}`$, let
$`g_{i}`$ indicate whether the response is selected for training and let
$`\bar{\rho}_{it}`$ be a clipped likelihood weight that accounts for
drift between the sampling and current student policies. Averaging first
over response positions and then over selected trajectories gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{h}=\frac{\sum_{i}g_{i}R_{i}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{R_{i}}\bar{\rho}_{it}d_{K}(p_{\theta,it},q_{0,it})}{\max(1,\sum_{i}g_{i})}.
``` |  | (3) |

### On-policy benign anchoring

To constrain changes on benign requests, we sample a response
$`z\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid b)`$ for a benign UltraChat prompt
$`b`$ ([Ding et al., 2023](#bib.bib42)). The frozen teacher scores the
same prompt and student-generated response prefixes. Writing the
resulting distributions as $`p^{b}_{\theta,jt}`$ and $`q^{b}_{0,jt}`$
for benign trajectory $`j`$, the anchor and combined objective are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{b}=\frac{\sum_{j}\sum_{t=1}^{|z_{j}|}d_{K}(p^{b}_{\theta,jt},q^{b}_{0,jt})}{\sum_{j}|z_{j}|},\qquad\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{h}+\lambda_{b}\mathcal{L}_{b}.
``` |  | (4) |

For benign requests, both branches receive the same clean prompt
(without the optional teacher instruction), so the anchor encourages the
student to retain the starting model’s behavior on its current benign
trajectories. All benign trajectories contribute, without harmfulness
routing or likelihood-ratio weighting. All main ASD results include this
anchor.

### Adversarial data.

At each iteration, a fresh pool is generated consisting of pairs of
clean and adversarial prompts against the current student, using a
collection of attacks (discussed in
[§5](#S5 "5 Experimental setup ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")). We
exclude behaviors for which the frozen teacher complies with the clean
request. During training, the student repeatedly samples fresh responses
from the retained attack pool, and we apply
[Eq. 4](#S3.E4 "4 ‣ On-policy benign anchoring ‣ 3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
to the routed adversarial responses together with the benign samples. We
exclude cases where the teacher already complies at the start.

### Implementation details.

As discussed in
[Eq. 2](#S3.E2 "2 ‣ Training objective. ‣ 3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
we reduce the cost of storing teacher probabilities by computing the
reverse-KL token loss on a compressed vocabulary. For the adversarial
term, the clipped current-to-sampling-policy likelihood weight is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{\rho}_{it}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{p_{\theta,it}(y_{it})}{p_{\mathrm{old},it}(y_{it})},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right).
``` |  |

We set $`g_{i}=1`$ if the harmfulness judge scores the response above
$`0.5`$ against the clean request and the trajectory’s mean unclipped
current-to-sampling-policy likelihood ratio is below $`5`$; otherwise,
$`g_{i}=0`$. Thus, only responses judged harmful and passing this
policy-drift check contribute to $`\mathcal{L}_{h}`$. Prompt, padding
positions, and optional teacher instructions do not contribute to
training. Gradients flow through the student loss and its likelihood
weights, while sampled prefixes and teacher targets are detached. The
teacher receives no gradient updates. This is a local distillation
update on sampled prefixes. The likelihood weights address drift between
the sampling and current policies, but do not exactly correct the
distribution of entire prefixes. In particular, differentiating the
token losses at detached prefixes omits the effect of $`\theta`$ on
which prefixes are visited. The benign anchor uses the same divergence
and tail treatment as the adversarial term. Our main runs use eight
benign prompts per update, with one response per prompt sampled at
temperature $`1`$ and no top-$`p`$ or top-$`k`$ truncation.

## 4 Theoretical Insight

Language models respond in an autoregressive way, meaning that their
final response is a sample from a Markov process on the prefixes of the
response. This is the key to comparing adversarial SFT and
self-distillation. The ultimate purpose of adversarial training (in both
forms) is to reduce the probability of any harmful response, which in
terms of the Markov process is to reduce the probability of all paths
that end in harmful assistance. However, adversarial SFT evaluates the
model only along fixed reference paths: a refusal target becomes more
likely and, in DAT, the next token of a harmful reference becomes less
likely, but the objective does not evaluate the rest of the compliant
subtree, which can change only indirectly through shared parameters. An
adversary can therefore leave the reference path and find another
phrasing that may continue to be harmful with high probability.
Prefilled compliant openings are specific examples of this unconstrained
subtree.

Let $`\phi_{x}(y)`$ be an indicator of whether the response $`y`$
explicitly refuses the original request $`x`$ ($`0`$ means no explicit
refusal, which need not be harmful assistance). Let $`r_{\theta}(x,w)`$
be the probability of refusal from a prefix $`w:=y_{<t}`$ of length
$`|w|=t-1`$ under model parameters $`\theta`$; i.e.:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\theta}(x,w)=\Pr_{y_{\geq t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,w)}[\phi_{x}(w\oplus y_{\geq t})=1].
``` |  | (5) |

At a compliant prefix $`w_{C}`$, for $`x^{\prime}\in\{x,\tilde{x}\}`$,
$`r_{\theta}(x^{\prime},w_{C})`$ is the probability of recovering and
eventually refusing (hence, we call it *recovery probability*). We
consider three cases: the student under the attacked request,
$`r_{\theta}(\tilde{x},w)`$, which ASD trains; the teacher under the
clean request, $`r_{\theta_{0}}(x,w)`$; and the student under the clean
request, $`r_{\theta}(x,w)`$. Let $`r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},y)=\phi_{x}(y)`$
for a completed response $`y`$.

We can decompose the recovery probability into a marginalization over
the next token $`v`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},w)=\sum_{v}\pi(v\mid x^{\prime},w)\,r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},w\oplus v),
``` |  | (6) |

and thus only the next-token distributions at $`w`$ and its extensions,
denoted $`T(w)`$, determine recovery from $`w`$. This means that a model
can almost always refuse from an empty response while having little
chance of recovering from a compliant opening; i.e.
$`r_{\theta}(x^{\prime},\varnothing)\approx 1\not\Rightarrow r_{\theta}(x^{\prime},w_{C})\approx 1`$.

### Off-trajectory responses

Here we establish our theory based only on the autoregressive nature of
the model, agnostic to the specific architecture. Let
$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{train}}`$ be the set of states where an objective
evaluates the policy’s next-token distribution, and suppose the
objective depends on the policy only through those distributions.
Propositions [1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ Off-trajectory responses ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
and [2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Penalty-only prefixes). ‣ Off-trajectory responses ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
further let next-token distributions at distinct states vary
independently, so they describe what the objective determines, not how a
network with shared parameters generalizes between states. For SFT on a
refusal trajectory $`\bar{y}`$, these states are the prefixes of
$`\bar{y}`$, i.e. $`(\tilde{x},\bar{y}_{<t})`$. In the following
results, we show that objectives evaluated along fixed reference
responses need not constrain recovery probability at other prefixes. In
other words, a model can either recover or not recover from a compliant
prefix and this does not affect its SFT training loss.

###### Proposition 1.

Assume next-token distributions at distinct states can be chosen
independently. Let $`w_{C}`$ be a prefix whose subtree $`T(w_{C})`$
contains no state of $`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{train}}`$ and admits both a
refusing and a compliant completion. There exist two policies with equal
training loss such that $`r(\tilde{x},w_{C})=1`$ under one and
$`r(\tilde{x},w_{C})=0`$ under the other, and whose values of
$`r(\tilde{x},\varnothing)`$ differ at most by the probability of
reaching $`w_{C}`$.

The two policies agree wherever the loss looks. Once they reach
$`w_{C}`$, however, one always recovers and the other never does. If
$`w_{C}`$ is rarely reached from an empty response, this large
difference in recovery has little effect on initial refusal.

(c) Under the attacked request $`\tilde{x}`$ the compliant opening
becomes likely. ASD samples the student’s own rollout and, at every

to the clean teacher $`q_{0}(\cdot\mid x,w)`$ at the same prefix.
(d) Proposition [3](#Thmproposition3 "Proposition 3 (Recovery transfer). ‣ ASD constrains recovery where the student visits ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation"):
the attacked student’s recovery probability lies within
$`\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(w)/2}`$ of the teacher’s. The guarantee is
informative only when the teacher recovers (top band) and the
continuation divergence is small; for a weak teacher (bottom band) it
becomes vacuous at small $`\mathcal{K}(w)`$.

Proposition
[1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ Off-trajectory responses ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
mainly argues that if training does not visit a compliant prefix, it
cannot constrain the recovery probability from that prefix. Now we argue
that even if a training loss does visit a compliant prefix but only
through a reference rollout, it can still leave recovery undetermined.
This is the case for DAT, which penalizes one harmful reference token
but does not evaluate the model at other tokens that might continue with
compliance.

###### Proposition 2 (Penalty-only prefixes).

Assume that next-token distributions at distinct states can be chosen
independently. Let $`s\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{train}}`$ be a compliant
prefix at which the loss depends on $`\pi(\cdot\mid s)`$ only through a
non-decreasing penalty on the probability of one reference token
$`y^{\star}`$. If other tokens open a refusal and continue compliance,
in subtrees disjoint from $`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{train}}`$, then
policies with $`\pi(y^{\star}\mid s)=0`$, which minimize the penalty,
can have $`r(s)=1`$ or $`r(s)=0`$ at equal loss.

DAT trains on the likelihood of a refusal target and a harmful reference
$`y^{H}`$ under an adversarial context ([Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)).
The refusal target supplies a signal along its own prefixes. At prefixes
of $`y^{H}`$, the penalty concerns the next reference token
([Prop. 2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Penalty-only prefixes). ‣ Off-trajectory responses ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).
Other compliant prefixes receive no response-level signal
([Prop. 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ Off-trajectory responses ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).
Current adversarial training can therefore make refusal from the start
very likely while leaving recovery after other compliant openings
undetermined.

### ASD constrains recovery where the student visits

Fix a training pair and a prefix $`w`$ with $`|w|=t-1`$. Let $`P`$ be
the distribution of the attacked student’s continuation from
$`(\tilde{x},w)`$, and let $`Q`$ be that of the clean teacher from
$`(x,w)`$. With a shared tokenizer, both distributions concern the same
possible token sequences. We can also ask the same question of both:
does the completed response refuse the original request $`x`$? For the
event $`A=\{y_{\geq t}:\phi_{x}(w\oplus y_{\geq t})=1\}`$, we have
$`r_{\theta}(\tilde{x},w)=P(A)`$ and $`r_{\theta_{0}}(x,w)=Q(A)`$.
Judging each model against a different request would give two different
events instead
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).
ASD’s pre-filter, routing, and evaluation all judge against $`x`$.

Let $`p_{\theta,u}`$ and $`q_{0,u}`$ be the next-token distributions
from Section [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation") along a
continuation $`Y`$ of $`w`$. The chain rule expresses the divergence
between complete continuations as the expected sum of the token
divergences after $`w`$ (see
[6](#Thmlemma6 "Lemma 6 (Chain rule). ‣ Divergence lemmas ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{K}(w):=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}\sum_{u\geq t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p_{\theta,u}\,\|\,q_{0,u}\big).
``` |  | (7) |

###### Proposition 3 (Recovery transfer).

For every prefix $`w`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\big|r_{\theta}(\tilde{x},w)-r_{\theta_{0}}(x,w)\big|\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{K}(w)/2},
``` |  |

and for any distribution $`\nu_{\theta}`$ over prefixes, such as the
prefixes that receive ASD supervision,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\theta}}r_{\theta}(\tilde{x},w)\geq\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\theta}}r_{\theta_{0}}(x,w)-\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\theta}}\mathcal{K}(w)/2}.
``` |  |

Thus, if the student and teacher have similar continuation distributions
after $`w`$, they must also have similar probabilities of eventually
refusing from $`w`$.

### On-policy sampling.

Suppose a student rollout $`Y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x})`$
passes through $`w`$. The sum of its token KL terms after $`w`$ is an
unbiased estimate of $`\mathcal{K}(w)`$
(Lemma [9](#Thmlemma9 "Lemma 9. ‣ Recovery transfer ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).
Sampling from the student (on-policy) therefore places the comparison at
prefixes it actually reaches, including compliant ones.

### Limitations of the theory

The bound in
[Prop. 3](#Thmproposition3 "Proposition 3 (Recovery transfer). ‣ ASD constrains recovery where the student visits ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
is informative only when the teacher recovers from $`w`$ and the
continuation divergence is small. Minimizing the divergence moves the
distributions closer together, which is useful only if the teacher’s
distribution is already safe. In other words, it is not a guarantee of
high recovery probability (when the teacher is not recovering with high
probability). This means that there is an implicit assumption that the
teacher is reliable on the clean original request. Such an assumption is
not unrealistic given that the teacher can be freely instructed to
reject.

More importantly, our theoretical study is agnostic to the model
architecture (allowing the policy to flexibly change within various
routes), and in practice, ASD does not fully follow the theoretical
assumptions. In particular, updating the student between samplings
(stale sampling), training only on trajectories judged harmful
(harmfulness routing), averaging token terms over each trajectory
instead of summing them after $`w`$, and the top-$`K`$ compression
$`d_{K}\leq D_{\mathrm{KL}}`$ all reduce the information that a small
training loss provides about $`\mathcal{K}(w)`$ at every prefix.

This discrepancy, however, does not imply worse performance in practice.
[Fig. 1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
shows that the learned behavior extends beyond the states covered by the
proposition. The model becomes more robust on clean harmful prompts over
iterations, which is not directly captured in theory. Additionally,
there are two observations that are consistent with the mechanism
described by the theory. In
[Fig. 2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation"),
at prefixes inside a compliant answer that the student produced under
attack, the clean teacher’s alternatives lead back to a refusal. These
are the prefixes where $`r_{\theta_{0}}(x,w)>r_{\theta}(\tilde{x},w)`$,
and matching can improve recovery. In
[Table 1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Limitations of the theory ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation"),
DAT raises its own refusal signature while lowering other forms of
refusal (concentrating the probability on a single template).
[Fig. 1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
also shows that instead of refusal (direct or by deflection), DAT’s
generation sharply breaks when given just a short compliant prefill;
this also suggests that the routes its reference responses do not cover
are left unconstrained
([Prop. 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ Off-trajectory responses ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
and
[Prop. 2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Penalty-only prefixes). ‣ Off-trajectory responses ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).
ASD, which matches the teacher’s distribution instead of a single
target, raises the alternative refusals. Still, we cannot take either as
a direct test of the bound.

## 5 Experimental setup

### Setup

We train Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct for 5 iterations, and Llama-3-8B-Instruct
for 7 iterations (for Llama we add an additional instruction to the
teacher). In order to observe the combined effect, we also apply two ASD
iterations to the released DAT-Qwen checkpoint. Training uses HB-adv,
the 100 adversarial-training behaviors in [Hu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib5);
evaluation uses the disjoint 100 harmful behaviors of JailbreakBench
(JBB-100) ([Chao et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)). The benign anchor uses
UltraChat prompts; for Llama, half of its 1,000 anchor prompts are
instead safe prompts that resemble harmful ones, 250 each from the
FalseReject training split ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)) and the
harmless part of PHTest ([An et al., 2024](#bib.bib15)). Neither source
shares a prompt with XSTest, which stays held out. Throughout, “ASD”
denotes the complete method including the anchor; its removal is
evaluated separately in
[§6](#S6 "6 Results ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation").

Training pools combine GCG, PAIR, Best-of-$`N`$ (BoN), AutoDAN, and
diffusion Inpainting; Llama’s pools also contain the unmodified request,
sampled 256 times and kept only for behaviors where a sample is judged
harmful, with approximately 228,000 evaluated attempts per iteration. We
retain only StrongREJECT successes and cap each behavior–attack pair at
16 prompts. Inpainting uses separate frozen prompt sets for HB-adv and

tokens, 64 steps, and the behavior index as seed, following the DAT
attack design ([Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)). Evaluation prompts do not
enter the training pool. Pool sizes and stopping steps are reported in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Additional iteration and pool results ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation").

Qwen uses learning rate $`10^{-5}`$ and Llama $`3\times 10^{-6}`$; both
use AdamW and LoRA adapters, with the teacher excluded from
optimization. For Llama, the teacher also receives the harmful-request
instruction of
Section [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation"). We report

## 6 Results

### Robustness and capability

Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Setup ‣ 5 Experimental setup ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
reports the resulting models under the common harness. On Qwen2.5-14B,
five ASD iterations reduce BoA far below both the base model and DAT.
ASD is at or below DAT on each attack, tying on Direct sampling and
Inpainting. The largest difference is on PAIR, so the aggregate
advantage depends on this attack’s implementation and budget. ASD also
retains higher MMLU and ARC accuracy than DAT on Qwen.

On Llama-3-8B, ASD reaches the lowest BoA of the evaluated defenses,
well below DAT, LAT, and CB. No evaluated GCG, PAIR, AutoDAN, or
Inpainting attempt succeeds, but ASD remains above DAT on BoN and Direct
sampling. Thus the aggregate gain does not imply improvement on every
attack. MMLU and ARC accuracy for ASD remain within 1.5 percentage
points of the corresponding base models.

The remaining failures also differ by backbone. Qwen’s largest final
attack column is Inpainting, whereas Llama’s is repeated sampling of the
unmodified harmful request, even though its training pool contains that
request. This difference matters for the recovery interpretation:
improved resistance to an adversarial prompt does not imply that every
harmful response has been removed from the output distribution.
Likewise, zero observed success on GCG or PAIR means that the evaluated
search did not find a successful candidate, not that these attacks
cannot succeed with different randomness or additional search.

### The role of benign anchoring

The main ASD checkpoints include the on-policy benign anchor described
in Section [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation"). In a
one-iteration Llama ablation with Qwen’s training settings (UltraChat
anchor, no teacher instruction), XSTest compliance is .756 with the
anchor and .000 without it. A separate supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
model shows .404 with its anchor and .012 without it. Removing the
anchor therefore produces nearly universal benign refusal in both tested
training objectives.
[§E](#A5 "Appendix E Benign anchor and SFT ablations ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
gives the full measurements of all four models.

This ablation supports a within-objective conclusion about anchoring. It
does not establish that ASD is superior to SFT: the two objectives reach
different robustness levels after one iteration. Running SFT for more
iterations does not close this gap in a useful way: after three
iterations no attack succeeds on the training behaviors, but XSTest
compliance falls to .036
([§E](#A5 "Appendix E Benign anchor and SFT ablations ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).
Moreover, the SFT anchor uses cross-entropy on the teacher’s greedy
benign answers, while ASD uses on-policy distribution matching. A
comparison of objective quality requires a matched robustness operating
point or training trajectory. The DAT + ASD comparison in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Setup ‣ 5 Experimental setup ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
remains a separate result.

### Log-probability difference over refusals

A model can refuse a harmful request because refusing as a whole has
become more likely, or because one specific refusal string has; refusal
rates cannot tell these apart. We fix a bank of candidate responses and
score the same texts under every model. Nothing is generated, so a
difference between models cannot come from what each model happened to
sample. For each model, the bank is paired with 26 plain JailbreakBench
requests and contains: (i) the model’s own *signature*, the two most
frequent verbatim refusals it emits on the 250 safe XSTest prompts
(DAT-Qwen emits only one, “Sorry, I can’t do that.”, which accounts for
all of its flat refusals); (ii) the other models’ signatures; (iii) 24
*generic* refusals sampled from Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct with no behavior in
context, which were not supplied as training targets; and (iv)
*compliance*, harmful completions that the family’s base model produced
for the plain request and that the judge scored above $`0.5`$. Each
refusal is paired with every request, and each compliance text only with
the request it answered. For a model $`m`$, clean request $`x`$, and
candidate $`c`$, we compute
$`s_{m}(x,c)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{m}(c_{t}\mid x,c_{<t})`$
for $`T=\min(16,|c|)`$, under $`m`$’s chat template, and report
$`s_{m}-s_{\mathrm{base}}`$ averaged over the pairs in each set
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Limitations of the theory ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).
A model that learned to reply with one specific string shows a positive
shift on its own signature next to negative shifts on the other refusal
sets; a model that learned to refuse shows all three refusal sets rising
together. In both cases, compliance should fall. Details are in
[§A](#A1 "Appendix A Implementation and evaluation details ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation").
[Table 1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Limitations of the theory ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
shows a concentration on its own signature for DAT, and a diverse spread
of the probabilities for ASD.

## 7 Discussion and limitations

The mechanism depends on informative teacher distributions. The attack
pool also reweights training toward behaviors that still break;
unvisited failure modes receive no correction. Comparisons with matched
forward-KL, fixed-rollout, and context-steered variants remain necessary
for those causal claims.
Section [4](#S4 "4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
makes the teacher dependence explicit: at visited prefixes, the loss
ties the attacked student’s recovery probability to the teacher’s, yet
the measured student recovers more often than its first-iteration
teacher. The helpfulness cost is particularly important for interpreting
the method as an alignment procedure. The benign anchor prevents
near-total refusal in the one-iteration ablation, yet the final models
still refuse many ordinary requests. Retaining multiple-choice accuracy
is insufficient evidence that this cost has been solved. A useful
extension would test retention on prompts whose benign meaning conflicts
with safety-associated vocabulary, alongside broader benign instruction
following, while reserving the evaluation examples from training.
Teacher-reliability measurements and a robustness–helpfulness curve
would then distinguish a better safety decision boundary from simply
applying refusal more broadly ([Röttger et al., 2024](#bib.bib13); [Fu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib4); [Wen et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)).

## 8 Conclusion

ASD applies distributional feedback where an attacked student actually
generates, using a clean-request self-teacher and an on-policy benign
anchor. We show that reference-response objectives need not determine
refusal after off-trajectory compliant prefixes, whereas the idealized
ASD objective, the full continuation divergence, bounds the gap between
the attacked student’s and the clean teacher’s recovery probabilities at
the prefixes the student visits; the implemented loss approximates this
objective. On two backbones, the evaluated systems lower aggregate
attack success relative to released DAT checkpoints; on Qwen, they also
retain more refusal behavior after compliant prefills. The anchor
ablation establishes its practical role in preventing near-total benign
refusal in the tested one-iteration setting. Residual attacks,
cumulative over-refusal, and teacher reliability remain central
limitations. The evidence motivates learning recovery along student
trajectories while measuring harmful compliance and benign helpfulness
together.

## Appendix A Implementation and evaluation details

### Attacks and metrics

The evaluation suite follows DAT’s benchmark structure ([Hu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)): GCG with 250 steps ([Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib8)),
PAIR with 20 candidates ([Chao et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)), BoN with
1,000 perturbations ([Hughes et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)), Direct sampling
with 1,000 responses per behavior, and Inpainting with 1,024 frozen
prompts. We additionally evaluate AutoDAN with 100 steps ([Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib35)). GCG, PAIR, AutoDAN, and Inpainting use greedy target
decoding. Direct sampling uses temperature $`1`$ without top-$`p`$ or
top-$`k`$ truncation; BoN uses temperature $`1`$.

The fine-tuned StrongREJECT judge scores harmfulness relative to the
original request ([Souly et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)). Let
$`h_{iaj}=\mathbf{1}[\mathcal{H}(x_{i},y_{iaj})>0.5]`$ for behavior
$`i`$, attack $`a`$, and attempt $`j`$. We report

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{ASR}_{a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\max_{j}h_{iaj},\qquad\mathrm{BoA}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\max_{a,j}h_{iaj}.
``` |  | (8) |

Best-of-attacks (BoA) is the union of observed successes over behaviors,
not the maximum attack-level ASR. With $`N=100`$, one additional broken
behavior changes ASR by one percentage point.

### Baseline and harness validation

We re-evaluate the base models and released DAT ([Hu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)), LAT ([Sheshadri et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)), and
Circuit Breaker (CB) ([Zou et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)) checkpoints using
the same harness as ASD. We re-evalutaed the models from ([Hu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)) under our own attack repository. The results are very
close in all criterial except PAIR which our implementation was
significantly more successful (higher attack success rate). We report
this is

### Training progression and attack-pool coverage

The refreshed pools show how the available training signal changes as
the student becomes harder to break
(Table [8](#A4.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Appendix D Additional iteration and pool results ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).
On both backbones, the retained pool shrinks sharply across the pool
constructions, and by the last one only a few Qwen behaviors still have
a successful attack. The cap of 16 prompts per behavior–attack pair
limits the dominance of high-yield cells, but does not make every
behavior or attack equally represented.

The attack mixture shifts as well. Against the initial Qwen checkpoint,
GCG is the largest source of judged successes. After the first training
iteration, Inpainting supplies most of them on both backbones; later
pools again contain substantial GCG and BoN contributions. Refreshing
the pool thus changes which failures are emphasized, rather than merely
repeating a fixed list of adversarial prompts. This observation
motivates the iterative design, although a fixed-pool control would be
needed to attribute improvements specifically to refresh.

Pool contraction is measured on HB-adv and should be distinguished from
held-out robustness on JBB-100. For example, the initial DAT-Qwen
training pool has no GCG successes, whereas GCG still succeeds on some
held-out behaviors. Similarly, an update with no routed adversarial
response says that the current sampled batch supplies no adversarial
loss; it does not certify safety on every retained attack. The
five-update stopping rule limits training when such batches recur.
Planned update budgets also fall with the pool coverage. Consequently,
an outer iteration is a collection-and-training cycle, not a fixed
amount of optimization or compute.

Utility evaluation uses MMLU, ARC-Easy, and ARC-Challenge accuracy, and
compliance on the 250 safe XSTest prompts ([Röttger et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13)). XSTest responses use greedy decoding with a
256-token limit and are graded by Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct using the XSTest
judge prompt. We report flat and indirect refusals separately; unparsed
judgments remain in the denominator. Multiple-choice accuracy and
open-ended benign compliance measure different properties. Each training
ladder is a single run, so reported differences do not estimate
training-seed variability.

To implement
[Eq. 3](#S3.E3 "3 ‣ Training objective. ‣ 3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
and
[Eq. 4](#S3.E4 "4 ‣ On-policy benign anchoring ‣ 3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation"),
we compute the teacher’s top-$`K`$ probabilities and one tail mass, and
gather the student’s probabilities on the same token IDs; prompt and
padding positions are masked. The adversarial-training term first
averages the token losses within each trajectory and then over the
routed trajectories, while the benign term averages over all valid
benign tokens (without routing or likelihood-ratio correction).

### Prefill protocol

The response-prefilling diagnostic in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
tests whether safety persists after a compliant opening, following prior
priming and recovery evaluations ([Vega et al., 2024](#bib.bib21); [Qi
et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)). For each of 30 JailbreakBench behaviors, the
clean request is paired with up to two compliant answers $`c`$ that the
base model itself produced (59 in total), and the evaluated model
continues after the first $`n\in\{0,1,2,4,8,16\}`$ assistant tokens of
$`c`$. We compare the base Qwen model, DAT-Qwen, and ASD-Qwen at
iteration five on the same frozen prefixes.

In
[Fig. 1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation"),
we evaluate checkpoints on the same frozen bank of compliant answers. We
take these answers from base Qwen’s own responses in its JailbreakBench
evaluation (direct requests, and GCG or PAIR attacks), and keep those
that StrongREJECT scores above $`0.5`$, with 900–6000 characters and at
least 192 tokens, no hedge or refusal in the first 300 characters (i.e.
already committed to compliance), and no residue of the attack template.
The bank keeps up to two answers for each of 30 behaviors (59 in total).
These are the behaviors that the base model answered harmfully, so they
are not a random sample of JailbreakBench. Each answer is paired with
the clean behavior text under Qwen’s default system prompt; we apply the
chat template to the request–answer pair, remove the end-of-turn token,
and truncate the response to its first $`n`$ tokens. For each prefix and
$`n`$, we sample 8 continuations at temperature $`1`$ (no top-$`p`$ or
top-$`k`$ truncation, at most 256 new tokens, seed $`0`$). We judge each
continuation without the supplied prefix: it is harmful if StrongREJECT
scores it above $`0.5`$ against the clean request, a refusal if it
matches a fixed explicit-refusal pattern, and otherwise a stop, a
degenerate repetition, or a deflection. The reported rate is the refusal
share, averaged first over the samples of each prefix and then over
prefixes and behaviors.
[Fig. 6](#A1.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Classification ‣ Appendix A Implementation and evaluation details ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
repeats the same protocol on Llama-3-8B-Instruct, with a bank built in
the same way from base Llama’s answers (55 openings for 30 behaviors).

### Classification

We assign the label ”Comply” if the SR-ft judge scores the continuation
above 0.5, ”Refuse” if it contains a phrase from a fixed regex list
(line 58): ”I cannot”, ”I can’t”, ”I won’t”, ”I’m sorry”, ”I apologize”,
”unable to assist”, ”against my guidelines”, and similar; We call it
”Stop” if it has fewer than 8 new tokens and ”Degenerate” if more than
50% of its character 8-grams repeat (a loop). Combination of ”Stop” and
”Loop” is called ”Broken” ”Deflect” if none of the above apply: at least
8 tokens of non-looping text, judge score $`\leq`$ 0.5, and none of the
refusal phrases.

### Signature-probe protocol

For each backbone, we sample 26 clean JailbreakBench requests
(behaviors) for which the base model gave at least two distinct harmful
direct answers. The compliance set holds up to three of these direct
answers per behavior (StrongREJECT $`>0.5`$, 250–2500 characters), and
the on-topic refusals are the base model’s direct answers that are
judged harmless and match the refusal pattern. The signatures of each
model are its two most frequent greedy refusals on the 250 safe XSTest
prompts (selected iterations of ASD-Qwen and ASD-Llama, and SFT-Llama at
iteration one) We generate the generic refusals with
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct from six prompts that contain no behavior and only
ask for a refusal an assistant would give without mentioning the request
(temperature $`1`$, top-$`p`$ $`0.95`$), and deduplicate them.
Signatures and generic refusals are scored against every behavior, while
each compliance text is scored only against the behavior it answered.

For a pair $`(x,c)`$, we place $`x`$ in the model’s chat template and
$`c`$ as the assistant turn, and read the log-probability of each token
of $`c`$ given $`x`$ and the preceding tokens of $`c`$ (i.e. teacher
forcing; nothing is sampled). When $`c`$ is shorter than 16 tokens, the
window also includes the end-of-turn token. We use a fixed window of
leading tokens so that a short refusal and a long answer remain
comparable; these are also the tokens where the model decides between
refusing and complying. Each cell of
[Table 1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Limitations of the theory ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
averages $`s_{m}-s_{\mathrm{base}}`$ over all pairs of its set.
Signatures were collected on safe prompts, so they are usually off-topic
for the harmful request they follow; this lowers the absolute score of
every model, and subtracting the base model on identical pairs removes
the effect.

The SFT model is matched to the one-iteration ASD ablation under Qwen’s
training settings, rather than to the reported ASD-Llama checkpoint.

compares it with the matched ASD model.
[§E](#A5 "Appendix E Benign anchor and SFT ablations ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
reports SFT’s later iterations.

### Caveats

The own-signature set rests on one or two strings per model, which makes
it the least stable column. The generic refusals were written by the
Qwen base model and therefore follow its style. Similarly, the
compliance texts were written by the base model, which assigns them high
probability; a finetuned model can therefore drift slightly negative on
them even without any safety effect.

Further details. The target of the SFT model is the teacher’s greedy
256-token answer to the clean request. The reported answer-level entropy
is the mean full-vocabulary next-token entropy (in nats) along a greedy
response of at most 16 tokens that stops at the end token, averaged over
behaviors; it is therefore not the entropy of a distribution over
sampled answers. The log-probability shifts and rankings are point
estimates from one training seed, without confidence intervals. The
reported ranking does not change for $`T\in\{4,8,16,32\}`$, when we add
attack-derived compliance candidates, or when we remove the system
prompt.

## Appendix B Token-level view of the ASD signal

[Fig. 2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
shows what the adversarial term of ASD asks of the student on one
jailbroken answer. We keep the answer fixed and compare the next-token
distributions of the student and the teacher at every response position.
Since the token loss in
[Eq. 2](#S3.E2 "2 ‣ Training objective. ‣ 3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
moves the student toward the teacher, this comparison shows, position by
position, which continuations training promotes and which it suppresses.

Both branches use the Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct checkpoint after four ASD
iterations, which is also the teacher of the reported ASD-Qwen
checkpoint; i.e. student and teacher share weights, as at the start of
an iteration, and differ only in their prompts. The answer was sampled
by the student during the first ASD iteration under a GCG attack. The
clean request is $`x=`$ “Outline a method for staging a violent attack
to look like self-defense”, and $`\tilde{x}`$ appends to it an
adversarial suffix ending in “…Great spacing Thank firearm Practical”;
StrongREJECT scores the answer $`0.99`$ against $`x`$. Instead of
resampling, we replay this answer under the later checkpoint by teacher
forcing. At position $`t`$ with answer prefix $`r_{<t}`$, the student
distribution is $`p_{S}=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x},r_{<t})`$ and
the teacher distribution is
$`p_{T}=\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid x,r_{<t})`$ (i.e. $`p_{\theta,t}`$
and $`q_{0,t}`$ in
[§3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")). The teacher
receives the clean request without any refusal instruction, and the
figure covers the first 20 response tokens.

Encoding. The middle row is the answer, with one outlined chip per
position, starting right after the prompt. At each position, we take the
student’s three and the teacher’s two most likely next tokens as
alternatives, and sort them by $`p_{S}-p_{T}`$: the ones the student
favors more are drawn above the answer, and the ones the teacher favors
more below it. The color of a chip is $`\log p_{T}-\log p_{S}`$, divided
by the largest absolute value of this quantity among the chips of the
same position; green marks a token the teacher prefers, red one the
student prefers, and white means agreement. Since each position is
normalized separately, colors compare tokens within a position, not
across positions. In addition, placement uses the probability difference
while color uses the log-ratio, so a rare token can be drawn above the
answer and still be green (“what” at $`t=5`$). Two further rules use the
size of the update instead of the disagreement. We merge a position into
the previous chip if no shown token changes by at least 1 percentage
point (the first position always has its own chip); this is why “for
staging a violent attack” and “to look like self-defense” each form one
chip. Additionally, we follow ($`\to`$) a teacher-favored alternative
that rises by at least 1 percentage point with the teacher’s greedy
10-token continuation from that token, to show where the promoted token
leads (in the teacher’s policy). In the figure, a visible-space glyph
marks a leading space, and a return arrow a newline.

Caveats. The color shows the disagreement between the two models, not
the size of the update. The reverse-KL gradient on a token scales with
$`p_{S}`$, so a strongly green token that the student gives almost no
probability receives almost no pull; only the merge and continuation
rules use the actual update. Per-position normalization also means that
a deep color in a low-divergence column (e.g. $`t=2`$–$`3`$) is a small
absolute difference. Finally, this is one trajectory under one
checkpoint. The iteration that trains from this checkpoint routed only
one of its 180 sampled responses to the adversarial loss; we therefore
replay an attack from the first iteration, rather than showing a sample
that this iteration actually trained on.

## Appendix C Proofs for Section [4](#S4 "4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")

Throughout, $`P`$ and $`Q`$ are distributions on a finite set,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sup_{B}|P(B)-Q(B)|`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{y:\,P(y)>Q(y)}\big(P(y)-Q(y)\big),`$ |  |

where the supremum is attained at $`B^{\star}=\{P>Q\}`$ because
$`\sum_{y}(P(y)-Q(y))=0`$. We assume the end token to be absorbing; i.e.
once it is emitted, the model emits only the end token, under any
policy. Hence, with responses capped at $`L`$ tokens, every completion
has length exactly $`L`$.

We focus on two models, $`\pi_{\theta}`$ and $`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$, which
are the student model (by default receiving $`\tilde{x}`$), and the
teacher model (with $`x`$ as default). For a prefix $`w`$ with
$`|w|=t-1`$ and a continuation $`y_{\geq t}`$, write

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{u}=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x},w\oplus y_{t:u-1}),\qquad q_{u}=\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid x,w\oplus y_{t:u-1}),
``` |  |

which are $`p_{\theta,u}`$ and $`q_{0,u}`$ in the notation of
Section [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation"). The
continuation laws are $`P(y_{\geq t})=\prod_{u=t}^{L}p_{u}(y_{u})`$ and
$`Q(y_{\geq t})=\prod_{u=t}^{L}q_{u}(y_{u})`$.

### Recovery probability

At length $`L`$ the response is complete, and $`r`$ is the judgment
itself; i.e. $`r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},y)=\phi_{x}(y)`$ for a complete
response $`y`$. The following is a restatement of the fact discussed at
the beginning of
Section [4](#S4 "4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation").

###### Lemma 1 (Recursion).

For $`|w|<L`$,
$`r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},w)=\sum_{v}\pi(v\mid x^{\prime},w)\,r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},w\oplus v)`$.

###### Proof.

The continuation law from $`(x^{\prime},w)`$ is the product of the
conditionals $`\pi(y_{u}\mid x^{\prime},w\oplus y_{t:u-1})`$. Condition
on the first token $`y_{t}=v`$, which has probability
$`\pi(v\mid x^{\prime},w)`$; the remaining tokens then follow the
continuation law from $`(x^{\prime},w\oplus v)`$. The law of total
probability gives the recursion. ∎

###### Lemma 2 (Subtree dependence).

$`r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},w)`$ depends on $`\pi`$ only through the next-token
distributions $`\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\prime},w^{\prime})`$ at the incomplete
prefixes $`w^{\prime}\in T(w)`$.

###### Proof.

For $`|w|=L`$ (completed response),
$`r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},w)=\phi_{x}(w)`$ does not depend on $`\pi`$.

If the claim holds for all prefixes of length $`\ell+1`$, for length
$`|w|=\ell`$, we use
Lemma [1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1 (Recursion). ‣ Recovery probability ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
and write $`r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},w)`$ in terms of
$`\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\prime},w)`$ and the values
$`r_{\pi}(x^{\prime},w\oplus v)`$. All of the terms depend only on
conditionals in $`T(w\oplus v)\subset T(w)`$. ∎

See
[1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ Off-trajectory responses ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")

###### Proof of Proposition [1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ Off-trajectory responses ‣ 4 Theoretical Insight ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation").

We assume, as for any compliant prefix of interest, that $`w_{C}`$
admits both a completion judged a refusal and one judged compliant. Let
two policies agree everywhere outside $`T(w_{C})`$. Inside $`T(w_{C})`$,
let $`\pi_{\mathrm{rec}}`$ deterministically complete a refusal and
$`\pi_{\mathrm{cont}}`$ deterministically complete a compliant answer.
Both are valid under our assumption that next-token distributions at
different prefixes can be specified independently.

*Equal loss.* No state of $`T(w_{C})`$ lies in
$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{train}}`$, so the objective reads the same
conditionals under both policies and their losses are equal.

*Recovery values.* By
Lemma [2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma 2 (Subtree dependence). ‣ Recovery probability ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation"),
$`r(\tilde{x},w_{C})`$ depends only on conditionals in $`T(w_{C})`$,
where the two policies produce a refusal and a compliant completion:
$`r=1`$ and $`r=0`$.

*Initial refusal.* Split rollouts from $`(\tilde{x},\varnothing)`$ by
whether they pass through $`w_{C}`$. The probability $`\rho`$ of
reaching $`w_{C}`$ depends only on conditionals at the prefixes of
$`w_{C}`$, which lie outside $`T(w_{C})`$, so it is the same under both
policies. A rollout that never reaches $`w_{C}`$ never enters
$`T(w_{C})`$, so its law is also shared. Therefore

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r(\tilde{x},\varnothing)=\Pr[\text{not reach }w_{C},\ \phi_{x}=1]+\rho\,r(\tilde{x},w_{C}),
``` |  |

where the first term is common to both policies, and the two values
differ by $`\rho`$. ∎

Let $`v_{R}\neq y^{\star}`$ open a refusal and $`v_{C}\neq y^{\star}`$

### Divergence lemmas

Consider an event $`A`$, and for two distributions $`P`$ and $`Q`$, let
$`P(A)=\sum_{y\in A}P(y)`$ and $`Q(A)=\sum_{y\in A}Q(y)`$. The KL
divergence is $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\sum_{y}P(y)\log[P(y)/Q(y)]`$. The
binary KL (KL divergence of two Bernoulli distributions) is
$`\operatorname{kl}(p\|q)=p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}`$.
If $`Q(y)=0<P(y)`$ for some $`y`$, then $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\infty`$
and every bound below holds trivially, so we assume otherwise.

###### Lemma 3 (Collapsing to one event).

For every event $`A`$,
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\geq\operatorname{kl}\big(P(A)\,\|\,Q(A)\big)`$.

###### Proof.

The log-sum inequality states that
$`\sum_{i}a_{i}\log\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}\geq\big(\sum_{i}a_{i}\big)\log\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}}{\sum_{i}b_{i}}`$
for nonnegative $`a_{i},b_{i}`$. Applying it separately to the outcomes
in $`A`$ and in its complement gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{y\in A}P(y)\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}\geq P(A)\log\frac{P(A)}{Q(A)}
``` |  |

and the analogous bound with $`1-P(A)`$ and $`1-Q(A)`$. Adding the two:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{y\in A}P(y)\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}+\sum_{y\notin A}P(y)\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}\geq P(A)\log\frac{P(A)}{Q(A)}+(1-P(A))\log\frac{1-P(A)}{1-Q(A)}`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\Rightarrow D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\geq\operatorname{kl}\big(P(A)\,\|\,Q(A)\big).`$ |  |

∎

###### Lemma 4 (Binary Pinsker).

For $`p\in[0,1]`$ and $`q\in(0,1)`$,
$`\operatorname{kl}(p\|q)\geq 2(p-q)^{2}`$.

###### Proof.

Fix $`p`$ and let $`g(q)=\operatorname{kl}(p\|q)-2(p-q)^{2}`$. Then
$`g(p)=0`$ and

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g^{\prime}(q)=-\frac{p}{q}+\frac{1-p}{1-q}+4(p-q)=(q-p)\Big[\frac{1}{q(1-q)}-4\Big].
``` |  |

Since $`q(1-q)\leq\frac{1}{4}`$, the bracket is nonnegative, so $`g`$ is
non-increasing for $`q<p`$ and non-decreasing for $`q>p`$. Its minimum
is $`g(p)=0`$. ∎

###### Corollary 1 (Pinsker).

For every event $`A`$,
$`|P(A)-Q(A)|\leq\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)/2}`$. In particular,
$`\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)/2}`$.

###### Proof.

Lemmas [3](#Thmlemma3 "Lemma 3 (Collapsing to one event). ‣ Divergence lemmas ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
and [4](#Thmlemma4 "Lemma 4 (Binary Pinsker). ‣ Divergence lemmas ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
give
$`2(P(A)-Q(A))^{2}\leq\operatorname{kl}(P(A)\|Q(A))\leq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)`$.
For the second claim, take $`A=B^{\star}:=\{y:P(y)>Q(y)\}`$. ∎

###### Lemma 5 (Bounded functions).

For every $`f`$ with values in $`[0,1]`$,
$`|\mathbb{E}_{P}f-\mathbb{E}_{Q}f|\leq\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)`$.

###### Proof.

$`\mathbb{E}_{P}f-\mathbb{E}_{Q}f=\sum_{y}(P(y)-Q(y))f(y)\leq\sum_{P>Q}(P(y)-Q(y))=\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)`$,
using $`f\leq 1`$ where $`P>Q`$ and $`f\geq 0`$ where $`P<Q`$.
Exchanging $`P`$ and $`Q`$ gives the other direction. ∎

###### Lemma 6 (Chain rule).

$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}\sum_{u=t}^{L}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{u}\|q_{u})`$,
with $`p_{u},q_{u}`$ evaluated at the prefix $`w\oplus Y_{t:u-1}`$.

###### Proof.

$`P(y_{\geq t})=\prod_{u=t}^{L}p_{u}(y_{u})`$ (and the same for $`Q`$);
therefore,
$`\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}=\sum_{u=t}^{L}\log\frac{p_{u}(y_{u})}{q_{u}(y_{u})}`$.
Take the expectation under $`Y\sim P`$ and condition each term on
$`Y_{t:u-1}`$. Given that prefix, $`Y_{u}\sim p_{u}`$, so the
conditional expectation of the $`u`$-th term is
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{u}\|q_{u})`$. After the end token both conditionals
are the same point mass, so those terms vanish.

∎

###### Lemma 7 (Hybrid bound).

$`\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}\sum_{u=t}^{L}\operatorname{TV}(p_{u},q_{u})`$.

###### Proof.

For $`j=t,\dots,L+1`$, let $`H_{j}`$ generate steps $`t,\dots,j-1`$ with
the student kernels $`p_{u}`$ and steps $`j,\dots,L`$ with the teacher
kernels $`q_{u}`$, each evaluated at the prefix generated so far. Then
$`H_{t}=Q`$ and $`H_{L+1}=P`$, so
$`\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\sum_{j=t}^{L}\operatorname{TV}(H_{j+1},H_{j})`$.
The laws $`H_{j+1}`$ and $`H_{j}`$ draw the same prefix $`Y_{t:j-1}`$,
with its law under $`P`$, differ only at step $`j`$, and use the same
teacher kernels afterwards. For an event $`B`$, let $`g(v)\in[0,1]`$ be
the probability of $`B`$ under the teacher kernels after the prefix
$`Y_{t:j-1}\oplus v`$. Then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle H_{j+1}(B)-H_{j}(B)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{v}\big(p_{j}(v)-q_{j}(v)\big)g(v)\Big]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}\operatorname{TV}(p_{j},q_{j})`$ |  |

by
Lemma [5](#Thmlemma5 "Lemma 5 (Bounded functions). ‣ Divergence lemmas ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation").
Taking the supremum over $`B`$ and summing over $`j`$ proves the claim.
∎

###### Lemma 8 (Top-$`K`$ decomposition).

Let $`S`$ be a set of tokens, $`p_{\perp}=1-\sum_{v\in S}p(v)`$ and
$`q_{\perp}=1-\sum_{v\in S}q(v)`$, and let $`\bar{p},\bar{q}`$ be the
compressed distributions on $`S\cup\{\perp\}`$. Then
$`\operatorname{TV}(p,q)\leq\operatorname{TV}(\bar{p},\bar{q})+\min\{p_{\perp},q_{\perp}\}`$
and $`\operatorname{TV}(\bar{p},\bar{q})\leq\sqrt{d_{K}(p,q)/2}`$.

###### Proof.

Split $`\operatorname{TV}(p,q)`$ into
$`\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in S}|p_{v}-q_{v}|`$ and
$`\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\notin S}|p_{v}-q_{v}|`$. The tail part is at most
$`\frac{1}{2}(p_{\perp}+q_{\perp})=\frac{1}{2}|p_{\perp}-q_{\perp}|+\min\{p_{\perp},q_{\perp}\}`$,
and
$`\operatorname{TV}(\bar{p},\bar{q})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in S}|p_{v}-q_{v}|+\frac{1}{2}|p_{\perp}-q_{\perp}|`$.
The second inequality is
Corollary [1](#Thmcorollary1 "Corollary 1 (Pinsker). ‣ Divergence lemmas ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
on the compressed space, where
$`d_{K}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{p}\|\bar{q})`$. ∎

Coarse-graining can only lower KL
(Lemma [3](#Thmlemma3 "Lemma 3 (Collapsing to one event). ‣ Divergence lemmas ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
applied to the partition), so $`d_{K}\leq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)`$: a
small implemented loss does not by itself certify a small full KL, and
Lemma [8](#Thmlemma8 "Lemma 8 (Top-𝐾 decomposition). ‣ Divergence lemmas ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")
states what it does certify.

###### Lemma 9.

Recall that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{K}(w)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P}\sum_{u\geq t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{u}\|q_{u}),
``` |  |

where $`P`$ is the student continuation distribution conditioned on
$`w`$, and $`p_{u},q_{u}`$ are evaluated at the prefix
$`w\oplus Y_{t:u-1}`$.

Let $`Y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x})`$ and define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\mathcal{K}}(w;Y)=\sum_{u\geq t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\Big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x},Y_{<u})\,\Big\|\,\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid x,Y_{<u})\Big).
``` |  |

Then, for every prefix $`w`$ of length $`t-1`$ such that
$`\Pr(Y_{<t}=w)>0`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{K}}(w;Y)\mid Y_{<t}=w\right]=\mathcal{K}(w).
``` |  |

###### Proof.

Fix a prefix $`w`$ of length $`t-1`$ such that $`\Pr(Y_{<t}=w)>0`$.

Since $`Y`$ is sampled autoregressively from
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x})`$, its conditional continuation
distribution given $`Y_{<t}=w`$ is precisely $`P`$.

Therefore, by the definition of $`\hat{\mathcal{K}}(w;Y)`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\mathcal{K}}(w;Y)\mid Y_{<t}=w\right]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Y_{\geq t}\sim P}\left[\sum_{u\geq t}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{u}\|q_{u})\right]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}(w),`$ |  |

where the last equality follows from the definition of
$`\mathcal{K}(w)`$ (equivalently, the KL chain rule in
Lemma [6](#Thmlemma6 "Lemma 6 (Chain rule). ‣ Divergence lemmas ‣ Appendix C Proofs for Section ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).
∎

## Appendix F Related work

### Adversarial robustness across modalities

Adversarial perturbations and adversarial training were first studied
for images ([Szegedy et al., 2014](#bib.bib16); [Goodfellow et al.,
2015](#bib.bib17); [Madry et al., 2018](#bib.bib18)), and targeted audio
attacks later extended the vulnerability to speech recognition ([Carlini
and Wagner, 2018](#bib.bib19)). Aligned multimodal models can also be
pushed to produce harmful text by adversarial inputs ([Carlini et al.,
2023](#bib.bib20)), and Best-of-$`N`$ jailbreaking works across text,
vision, and audio ([Hughes et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)). Distilling
robustness also has a precedent in vision, where RSLAD trains students
on the soft labels of robust teachers ([Zi et al., 2021](#bib.bib7)).
ASD instead targets autoregressive safety, where the teacher is the same
checkpoint and is evaluated on the response prefixes of the attacked
student.

### Jailbreak attacks and adversarial training

GCG optimizes discrete suffixes ([Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib8)), PAIR
iteratively rewrites attacks using an attacker model ([Chao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib33)), and AutoDAN searches for coherent jailbreak prompts
with a hierarchical genetic algorithm ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)).
Adaptive attacks, and even simply rephrasing a request in the past
tense, expose gaps beyond the attacks a defense was trained
on ([Andriushchenko et al., 2025](#bib.bib37); [Andriushchenko and
Flammarion, 2024](#bib.bib36)). On the defense side, HarmBench
introduces the R2D2 adversarial-training baseline ([Mazeika et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10)), CAT reduces the cost of generating attacks by
optimizing in the embedding space ([Xhonneux et al., 2024](#bib.bib6)),
and MixAT combines continuous and discrete attacks ([Dékány et al.,
2025](#bib.bib38)). LAT and ReFAT perturb internal activations or
refusal features ([Sheshadri et al., 2025](#bib.bib30); [Yu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31)), while Circuit Breakers reroutes the representations
associated with harmful outputs ([Zou et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)). DAT
broadens the distribution of training prompts with diffusion-generated
prompts, alongside continuous adversarial training ([Hu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)). In contrast, ASD focuses on the training signal
applied to the generated responses; attack coverage is still necessary,
but we treat it as a separate factor.

### Shallow safety and recovery

Priming and prefilling attacks show that a compliant opening can defeat
refusal ([Vega et al., 2024](#bib.bib21); [Andriushchenko et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37)). [Qi et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22) relate this
vulnerability to alignment that is concentrated in the first few output
positions, and train on safety-recovery examples that go from harmful
prefixes to refusals. This is the closest precedent to our recovery
objective; however, ASD obtains distributional feedback on prefixes
sampled by the student, instead of relying on a fixed recovery dataset.
Separately, [Arditi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib32) identify a direction that
mediates refusal in the models they study. This finding motivates
looking into refusal mechanisms, but it does not imply that repeating
one refusal string corresponds to a particular internal representation;
our observations on DAT are specific to the evaluated checkpoints and
protocols.

### On-policy knowledge distillation

DAgger trains on the states that the learner itself visits, to address
distribution shift in sequential prediction ([Ross et al.,
2011](#bib.bib23)), and ImitKD brings this idea to autoregressive
distillation ([Lin et al., 2020](#bib.bib24)). GKD separates the choice
of where rollouts come from and the choice of divergence ([Agarwal et
al., 2024](#bib.bib1)), while MiniLLM optimizes reverse KL at the
sequence level ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)). DistiLLM studies a skew
KL and the efficient reuse of student outputs ([Ko et al.,
2024](#bib.bib25)), and DistiLLM-2 uses complementary losses for
teacher- and student-generated responses ([Ko et al.,
2025](#bib.bib26)). These works motivate our use of student trajectories
and distributional feedback, although they do not address adversarial
safety. Note that ASD’s token-KL update on detached prefixes is a local
distillation surrogate, and not the full sequence-level policy-gradient
estimator of MiniLLM
([§3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ Adversarial Self-Distillation")).

Reward-Gated On-Policy Distillation (RG-OPD) selects trajectories for
reverse-KL supervision using verifier feedback and the teacher–student
likelihood gap, to deal with unreliable teacher signals in reasoning and
coding ([Akhondzadeh et al., 2026b](#bib.bib27)). Teacher reliability
matters for ASD as well, since its corrective signal comes from the same
model under the clean prompt, evaluated on prefixes induced by the
attack.

Concurrent to our work, OPSA ([Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)) also
distills from a frozen copy of the same model on student rollouts, where
the teacher receives a privileged safety context. In OPSA, however,
student and teacher receive the same request and differ only in that
context. ASD instead targets the adversarial setting, in which the
student receives the adversarial example and the teacher the original
request; supervision is therefore applied at the response prefixes that
attacks induce.

Other recent methods change the teacher or restrict where supervision is
applied. SafeSteer builds a teacher through activation steering and
applies reverse-KL supervision only on selected safety tokens ([Li et
al., 2026a](#bib.bib39)). ROPD addresses the safety degradation caused
by malicious finetuning by routing supervision between the original
safety teacher and a task teacher ([Guo et al., 2026](#bib.bib40)). DUET
guides token-level learning by the disagreement between teachers with
identical weights that differ in whether they see a prohibition ([Li et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib41)). These approaches show that both how the
teacher is constructed and where distributional feedback is applied
matter. ASD studies the complementary setting, in which adversarially
modified requests drive the student away from its own clean-request
behavior.

### Measuring safety without conflating outcomes

HarmBench and JailbreakBench provide standardized red-teaming tasks and
evaluation protocols ([Mazeika et al., 2024](#bib.bib10); [Chao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib11)). StrongREJECT scores how much useful harmful
information an answer contains, which addresses the failures of
superficial jailbreak-success checks ([Souly et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)),
and XSTest probes unnecessary refusals on benign requests ([Röttger et
al., 2024](#bib.bib13)). Accordingly, we measure harmful compliance,
refusal after prefilling, response diversity, benign compliance, and
capability separately; in particular, the absence of a refusal alone is
not evidence of a useful harmful answer.
````

###### Abstract

Critic-free methods are widely used in reinforcement learning with
verifiable rewards (RLVR) for long chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning,
avoiding difficult and costly critic training. Common alternatives lack
token-level credit assignment and typically rely on grouped rollouts to
estimate prompt-specific baselines. A critic can address both
limitations, but long-CoT critic training is hindered by insufficient
coverage of trajectories with diverse lengths and early gradients
dominated by the newly initialized value head. We introduce S3AC
(Stabilized, Selective, and Scalable Actor–Critic) for LLM reasoning,
built around a practical critic-training recipe. The trained critic
provides token-specific advantages and prompt-specific baselines. Using
the prompt-level baseline to estimate difficulty, we propose
Difficulty-Aware Prompt Sampling (DAPS). DAPS combines this estimate
with outcome history to select prompts before generation, reducing
rollout waste without offline difficulty initialization. S3AC improves
group-rollout performance across dense and mixture-of-experts models
from 1.5B to 397B parameters, outperforming a modern GRPO variant
(GRPO-X). At 35B, it sustains single-rollout training while tested
critic-free baselines deteriorate after initial gains.

## 1 Introduction

Critic-free methods, including GRPO, are widely used in reinforcement
learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) for large language model (LLM)
reasoning (DeepSeek-AI, 2026; Kimi Team, 2026a; Kimi Team, 2026b;
LLM-Core Xiaomi, 2026; Intern-S2-Preview Team, 2026). They avoid the
difficulty and cost of training a value model for long responses under
sparse rewards and an evolving policy. However, replacing the critic
with reward statistics limits how learning signals distinguish prompts
and reasoning steps.

A failure may be expected on a difficult prompt but surprising on an
easy one, motivating a prompt-specific baseline. GRPO estimates this
baseline from grouped rollouts, requiring each group to wait for its
slowest response (Xu and Ding, 2026; Hou et al., 2026). Common
single-rollout alternatives, including REINFORCE++ (Hu et al., 2025) and
FlashREINFORCE (Hu et al., 2026), use batch statistics instead. These
shared baselines do not account for differences in prompt difficulty.

Even with a prompt-specific baseline, a single response-level advantage
cannot distinguish the contributions of individual tokens. When an
answer is incorrect, useful intermediate reasoning can receive the same
negative signal as steps that lead away from a solution (Wu et al.,
2026). This scalar signal does not reflect how the difficulty of
reaching a correct answer changes as reasoning progresses.

A learned critic offers a common route to addressing both limitations: a
prompt-conditioned baseline without grouped rollouts and token-specific
learning signals throughout the response (Sutton et al., 1999). However,
learning reliable value estimates for long reasoning trajectories
remains challenging. Our analysis highlights two obstacles to effective
value learning: initial-policy trajectories provide limited supervision
on long, correct responses, while early gradients can be dominated by
the newly initialized value head.

To address these obstacles, we introduce S3AC (Stabilized, Selective,
and Scalable Actor–Critic) for LLM reasoning (Figure 1). We pretrain the
critic on trajectories from policies of different strengths to broaden
coverage across response lengths and outcomes. Small-scale value-head
initialization controls initial predictions, while a larger
gradient-clipping threshold reduces excessive rescaling of backbone
updates by head-dominated gradients. Updated online, the critic provides
difficulty-aware, token-specific advantages throughout the response
through generalized advantage estimation (Schulman et al., 2016).

The critic’s usefulness extends to deciding what to generate. Because
its prompt-conditioned estimate is available before rollout, it can
guide prompt selection as well as policy learning. We propose
Difficulty-Aware Prompt Sampling (DAPS), which combines this estimate
with outcome history to select prompts of intermediate estimated
difficulty. Some existing selectors require offline-initialized
statistics or an auxiliary value model (Xu and Ding, 2026; Gao et al.,
2026); DAPS reuses the critic already trained for policy learning.

We evaluate two dense and two multimodal mixture-of-experts models
spanning 1.5B to 397B parameters. Under group rollout, actor–critic
training improves average performance across all four models,
outperforming GRPO-X on all seven evaluated benchmarks at 35B and all
five at 397B. At 35B, it sustains single-rollout performance while the
tested critic-free baselines decline after initial gains (Figure 4).
DAPS further improves both rollout settings over uniform prompt sampling
and reduces trajectories discarded by group filtering.

## 2 Related Work

#### Critic-based RL.

PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) uses a learned critic with GAE (Schulman
et al., 2016) to estimate advantages for policy optimization. For
long-CoT reasoning, VC-PPO (Yuan et al., 2025) pretrains the value model
and decouples the GAE parameters of the actor and critic, while VAPO
(Yue et al., 2025b) adapts $`\lambda`$ to response length. VinePPO
(Kazemnejad et al., 2025), EVPO (Pan et al., 2026), and AsyPPO (Liu
et al., 2026) improve value estimates through Monte Carlo rollouts,
explained-variance gating, and critic ensembles, respectively. SAO (Hou
et al., 2026) and CompactionRL (Li et al., 2026) apply PPO to
single-rollout training of large MoE models. While these methods address
different aspects of actor–critic training, our work brings critic
initialization, online value learning, and critic-guided prompt
selection together in a unified framework. Our study combines targeted
diagnostics and ablations with evaluations across rollout settings and
model scales, including group-rollout training at 397B parameters.

#### Critic-free RL.

Group-based methods such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), RLOO (Ahmadian
et al., 2024), and DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) derive baselines from multiple
responses to the same prompt. Single-rollout methods use batch
statistics (REINFORCE++; Hu et al., 2025), add a sequence-level trust
region to a batch-mean baseline (FlashREINFORCE; Hu et al., 2026), or
track per-prompt values with a tabular estimator (SPO; Xu and Ding,
2026). These methods typically construct advantages from sequence-level
reward statistics without learning a value function over intermediate
response prefixes.

#### Difficulty-aware prompt sampling.

Dynamic sampling (Yu et al., 2025) requires multiple responses per
prompt and discards responses from uninformative groups after
generation. Other methods estimate difficulty before rollout from reward
history (GRESO; Zheng et al., 2025), from a tracker initialized with
offline rollouts (SPO), or from a separately trained prompt-level value
model (PCL; Gao et al., 2026). DAPS combines pass-rate history with
value estimates from the existing S3AC critic, avoiding a separately
trained difficulty model and supporting both group and single rollout.

## 3 Method

S3AC is an actor–critic framework for long-CoT RLVR that trains a value
model for both policy learning and prompt selection (Figure 1). The
critic provides prompt-conditioned baselines and token-specific
advantages, while its initial-state value guides prompt sampling before
generation. We first formulate the actor–critic update (§3.1), then
examine the critic’s role under group and single rollout (§3.2),
describe its training recipe (§3.3), and introduce DAPS (§3.4).

### 3.1 Actor–Critic Formulation

We model response generation as a token-level MDP. Given a prompt $`x`$,
the actor $`\pi_{\theta}`$ generates a response token by token: the
state $`s_{t}`$ contains $`x`$ and the first $`t`$ response tokens, and
the action $`a_{t}`$ is the next token. A verifier gives a scalar reward
$`R\in\{-1,+1\}`$ on the last token only, and we use $`\gamma=1`$.
Following the actor–critic formulation (Sutton et al., 1999), a learned
critic $`V_{\phi}(s_{t})`$ approximates the expected return
$`\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}[R\mid s_{t}]`$. At $`s_{0}`$, the estimate
depends on the prompt alone; subsequent values also condition on the
generated response prefix.

For a response of $`T`$ tokens, we compute token-specific advantages
using GAE (Schulman et al., 2016):

```math
\hat{A}_{t}=\sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1}\lambda^{l}\delta_{t+l}, \tag{1}
```

where $`\delta_{t}=R_{t}+V_{\phi}(s_{t+1})-V_{\phi}(s_{t})`$ is the TD
error, with $`R_{t}=R`$ at the last token and $`0`$ elsewhere. The
terminal value is $`V_{\phi}(s_{T})=0`$. Equivalently,
$`\hat{A}_{t}=G^{\lambda}_{t}-V_{\phi}(s_{t})`$, where the
$`\lambda`$-return $`G^{\lambda}_{t}`$ bootstraps from later values when
$`\lambda<1`$, trading variance for bias.

For actor updates, we adopt CISPO (MiniMax et al., 2025), whose core
surrogate is

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{actor}}(\theta) \displaystyle=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\!\left[\operatorname{sg}(\bar{r}_{t}\hat{A}_{t})\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})\right], \tag{2}
```

```math
\displaystyle\bar{r}_{t} \displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}(r_{t},1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}),
```

where
$`r_{t}=\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})`$
is the importance ratio relative to the actor before the current batch
of updates, and $`\operatorname{sg}`$ denotes stop-gradient. The clipped
importance weights and advantages are thus treated as constants when
differentiating the actor objective. To address training–inference
mismatch, we combine importance-weight corrections with masking at both
the token and sequence levels (§4.1; Appendix A.1).

For critic updates, we regress token-level values toward the Monte Carlo
return $`R`$ using a clipped squared-error loss (Engstrom et al., 2020).
Let $`V_{t}=V_{\phi}(s_{t})`$ and
$`V_{t}^{\mathrm{old}}=V_{\phi_{\mathrm{old}}}(s_{t})`$. The objective
is

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{critic}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}\left[\max\left\{(V_{t}-R)^{2},\,(\bar{V}_{t}-R)^{2}\right\}\right], \tag{3}
```

where $`\bar{V}_{t}`$ is $`V_{t}`$ clipped to
$`[V_{t}^{\mathrm{old}}-\epsilon_{v},\;V_{t}^{\mathrm{old}}+\epsilon_{v}]`$,
and $`\epsilon_{v}`$ controls value clipping. The expectation averages
over valid response tokens.

### 3.2 How the Critic Shapes Learning Signals

We examine how the critic shapes learning signals across prompts of
different difficulty and across positions within a response. The initial
critic value $`V_{\phi}(s_{0})`$ estimates the policy’s expected return
for a prompt before generation. Figure 2a plots these values against
mean Monte Carlo (MC) returns from independent rollouts, using a frozen
35B actor–critic pair with one point per prompt. The values track
differences in empirical returns, supporting their use as
prompt-specific baselines. The prompt-specific baseline is not limited
to the initial position: subsequent values remain conditioned on the
prompt throughout the response. Figure 2b illustrates this distinction
in long failed responses: although all receive the same terminal reward
of $`-1`$, their mean raw advantages remain close to zero for hard
prompts and below $`-1`$ for easy prompts across response progress.

Figure 2: Prompt-conditioned values and advantages (35B single rollout).
(a) Initial value versus held-out return. (b) Long failures ($`>20`$K
tokens), using length-adaptive GAE with $`\alpha=1.5`$.

Within a response, expected returns can also change as reasoning
progresses. A shared response-level advantage cannot reflect these
changes. Figure 3 illustrates this behavior with a pretrained critic:
its value rises when the response identifies candidate values and
increases further when it determines the requested term. GAE combines
these evolving values with the observed reward to construct
token-specific advantages, allowing different positions within a
response to receive different learning signals.

Under group rollout, the group mean already provides a prompt-specific
baseline, while the critic adds token-level advantages. Under single
rollout, it also supplies the prompt-specific baseline missing from
batch statistics. Our experiments show that S3AC outperforms GRPO-X
under group rollout and sustains stable single-rollout training at 35B.
These benefits require informative values across prompts and response
positions, motivating the critic-training recipe in §3.3.

### 3.3 Training a Good Critic

We initialize the critic through value pretraining and then update it
online as the policy evolves. Our pretraining recipe addresses two
challenges: limited coverage of long, correct trajectories and
optimization dominated by the newly initialized value head.

#### Coverage-oriented value pretraining.

VC-PPO (Yuan et al., 2025) uses trajectories from a fixed initial
policy, while SAO (Hou et al., 2026) emphasizes larger pretraining
corpora. However, data scale alone does not ensure coverage of long,
correct responses. We pool trajectories from policies of different
strengths to broaden coverage across response lengths and outcomes,
improving early online value prediction and downstream learning progress
in our comparison (§4.3). We pretrain for two epochs with token-averaged
MSE against terminal returns, freezing attention layers following SAO
(Hou et al., 2026) and additionally freezing the MoE router.

#### Value-head optimization.

In our runs, critic training exhibits much larger gradient norms than
actor training, with the newly initialized value head dominating early
pretraining gradients (§4.3). For a linear value head
$`v_{t}=w^{\top}h_{t}`$, residual $`e_{t}=v_{t}-R`$, and loss
$`\ell_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}e_{t}^{2}`$,

```math
\nabla_{w}\ell_{t}=e_{t}h_{t},\qquad\nabla_{h_{t}}\ell_{t}=e_{t}w.
```

The head gradient depends directly on hidden states, whereas the
gradient entering the backbone is scaled by the small head weights.
Global clipping driven by the head therefore also shrinks the much
smaller backbone gradients. We initialize the head with standard
deviation $`1/(H{+}1)`$, where $`H`$ is the hidden size, to control
initial predictions. We also raise the gradient-clipping threshold from
1, a common actor-training setting, to 10 for the critic, reducing
excessive rescaling of backbone updates.

#### Online adaptation.

We update the critic on fresh rollouts as the policy evolves. Motivated
by two-timescale actor–critic learning (Konda and Tsitsiklis, 1999), we
use a critic learning rate five times the actor’s and two critic epochs
per rollout batch versus one actor epoch. We decouple actor and critic
GAE following VC-PPO (Yuan et al., 2025). The critic regresses on Monte
Carlo returns ($`\lambda_{\mathrm{critic}}=1`$), while the actor uses
VAPO’s length-adaptive GAE (Yue et al., 2025b),

```math
\lambda_{\mathrm{actor}}=1-\frac{1}{\alpha T},
```

where $`T`$ is the response length and $`\alpha=1.5`$.

### 3.4 Difficulty-Aware Prompt Sampling

The same critic can also help decide which prompts the actor should
attempt. In our group-rollout experiments, DAPO-style dynamic sampling
(Yu et al., 2025) improves S3AC over retaining all groups, but it
discards groups only after their responses have been generated. We
therefore propose Difficulty-Aware Prompt Sampling (DAPS), which
combines the initial critic value with outcome history to guide prompt
selection before generation.

#### Difficulty estimate.

Before each rollout batch, we score candidate prompts with the latest
critic. Under our binary reward convention, a success probability $`p`$
corresponds to an expected return of $`2p-1`$. We convert the initial
critic value into a success-rate estimate and combine it with observed
outcomes:

```math
p_{c}=\mathrm{clip}\Big(\tfrac{V_{\phi}(s_{0})+1}{2},0,1\Big),\quad\hat{p}=\frac{S+n_{c}\,p_{c}}{N+n_{c}}, \tag{4}
```

Here, $`S`$ and $`N`$ are the prompt’s successful and total attempt
counts, and $`n_{c}`$ controls the critic’s weight relative to these
observations. For a new prompt with $`S=N=0`$, the estimate reduces to
$`p_{c}`$, so its difficulty can be initialized directly from the critic
without separate offline rollouts. Observed outcomes complement this
prediction as they become available. Both counts decay with training
progress to limit the influence of stale observations as the policy
evolves.

#### Acceptance.

We favor prompts of intermediate estimated difficulty, a preference also
used in prior prompt-selection methods (Xu and Ding, 2026; Gao et al.,
2026). Each candidate is accepted with probability

```math
a=a_{\text{min}}+(1-a_{\text{min}})\,4\hat{p}(1-\hat{p}), \tag{5}
```

This probability peaks at $`\hat{p}=0.5`$. The positive acceptance floor
gives even very easy or very hard prompts a chance of selection, in the
spirit of $`\epsilon`$-greedy exploration (Sutton and Barto, 2018).
After an initial period of uniform sampling, we gradually introduce this
acceptance rule following the schedule in Appendix D.

Under group rollout, DAPS improves sample efficiency by reducing the
number of responses discarded by dynamic sampling. It also improves
performance under both group and single rollout.

#### Training details.

We train two dense models, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (Yang et al., 2024) and
R1-Distill-1.5B (Guo et al., 2025), and two MoE models,
Intern-S2-Preview-35B (Intern-S2-Preview Team, 2026) and
Intern-S2-397B (InternLM, 2026). Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B is trained on the
7.5k DAPO-Math subset used by FlashREINFORCE (Yu et al., 2025; Hu
et al., 2026), R1-Distill-1.5B on MATH-1,460 (Qi et al., 2025), and the
MoE models on in-house prompt sets covering mathematics, instruction
following, reasoning, physics, and chemistry.

Group rollout uses $`K{=}16`$ responses per prompt for dense models and
$`K{=}8`$ for MoE models; single rollout uses $`K{=}1`$. Each rollout
batch is split into eight mini-batches and used for one actor epoch,
yielding eight actor gradient steps. We report RL training progress in
actor gradient steps throughout. We use asymmetric importance-weight
clipping (clip-higher) and dynamic sampling under group rollout (Yu
et al., 2025). To address training–inference mismatch (Yao et al.,
2025), S3AC and GRPO-X combine token-level importance sampling with
IcePoP masks (Team et al., 2025) and MIS-style sequence-level masking
(Liu et al., 2025). We mask an entire response when its maximum
token-level log-probability ratio between the training and rollout
policies exceeds a threshold. S3AC uses DAPS on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B,
R1-Distill-1.5B, and Intern-S2-Preview-35B, and uniform sampling on
Intern-S2-397B. Full training configurations appear in Appendix A
(Table 6).

#### Baselines.

For group rollout, we use GRPO-X, a modern variant of GRPO (Shao et al.,
2024). For single rollout, we compare with REINFORCE++ (Hu et al., 2025)
and FlashREINFORCE (Hu et al., 2026). GRPO-X uses the shared recipe,

REINFORCE++ use CISPO (Eq. (2)); FlashREINFORCE retains its original
objective and sequence-level trust region. Within each model, methods
share the base checkpoint, training data, and evaluation protocol.

### 4.2 Main Results

We evaluate the complete S3AC recipe under group and single rollout with
benchmark performance and training stability.

#### Group rollout: gains beyond a group baseline.

Even when grouped responses already provide a prompt-specific baseline,
S3AC improves over GRPO-X (§4.1; Tables 3–3). At 35B, the
best-checkpoint average increases from 76.4 to 80.5, with gains on all
seven benchmarks. These gains extend to 397B, where S3AC raises the
average from 85.1 to 87.3 and leads on all five benchmarks. Together
with gains on both 1.5B models, this supports S3AC’s scalability from
1.5B dense models to 397B MoE models. The gains are consistent with
§3.2: beyond the prompt-level information captured by group rewards, the
critic conditions on the evolving response prefix and provides different
learning signals across token positions.

#### Single rollout: performance gains and training stability.

With one response per prompt, S3AC achieves scores comparable to its
group-rollout results (Tables 3 and 3). On Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and 35B,
its advantage over the best critic-free baseline within each setting is
larger under single rollout. At 35B, S3AC reaches 80.9 versus 70.4 for
REINFORCE++, a 10.5-point gain. This pattern is consistent with the
critic supplying the prompt-specific baseline missing from batch-level
reward statistics, alongside token-specific advantages (§3.2). Figure 2
illustrates this distinction: despite identical terminal rewards, long
failed responses receive mean advantages closer to zero for hard prompts
and more negative for easy prompts.

The full 35B training curves show sustained single-rollout learning
(Figure 4a). Both critic-free baselines improve initially but then
decline, while S3AC retains most of its gains through 1,600 gradient
steps. REINFORCE++ exhibits rapid growth in rollout entropy and response
length, whereas FlashREINFORCE shifts toward shorter responses as its
score declines (Figure 4b,c). These dynamics support S3AC’s stabilized
actor–critic design for sustained single-rollout training at 35B.
Appendix B provides full 35B benchmark curves, Qwen single-rollout
dynamics, and training and evaluation statistics (Figures 8–9; Table 7).
Stable 35B single-rollout training also extends to alternative policy
losses (Table 8). We next examine the pretraining choices in §3.3.

### 4.3 Analysis of Critic Pretraining

We examine the proposed value-pretraining method of §3.3 on
Intern-S2-Preview-35B; full setups and diagnostics are in Appendix C.

#### Pretraining data: broader coverage improves value prediction and learning progress.

Pretraining on trajectories from multiple policies yields a more
accurate critic early in online training. The motivation is limited
coverage: among responses longer than 24K tokens, only 5.0% are correct
in initial-policy samples, compared with 61.8% for the policy at
gradient step 1,600 (Figure 10a,b). We compare a _coverage critic_,
pretrained on trajectories from policies of different strengths, with an
_on-policy critic_, pretrained only on initial-policy trajectories. Both
runs share the actor initialization and the online recipe under group
rollout; the two recipes also differ in data amount (Appendix C.1). The
coverage critic attains higher EV and lower value loss early in online
training, and the gap narrows as the on-policy critic adapts online
(Figure 5). In this comparison, coverage pretraining enables faster
learning progress and higher final performance within the same
actor-update budget.

#### Pretraining optimization: initialization and clipping.

Consistent with the analysis in §3.3, the value head accounts for over

$`1/(H{+}1)`$ lowers early value loss from 0.72 to 0.45 and yields the

ablation using initial-policy data, increasing the clipping threshold
from 1 to 10 reduces the fraction of clipped updates from 99.7% to
12.5%, while final value loss remains comparable. Compared with
disabling clipping, this setting retains protection against extreme
gradients.

#### Does selection before generation reduce waste?

Under group rollout, DAPS reduces the number of trajectories discarded
by dynamic sampling, with a larger gap later in training (Figure 6a).
Both methods retain the same number of trajectories per training batch,
so fewer generated responses are lost to filtering. This supports the
motivation in §3.4: selecting prompts before generation complements
DAPO-style dynamic sampling by reducing generation spent on groups later
discarded.

#### Does DAPS help without a group filter?

The gains under single rollout show that DAPS also benefits training
without group filtering. To examine which prompts enter training, we use
each prompt’s success rate in a completed uniform-sampling run, denoted
by $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$. After the initial sampling ramp, DAPS
consistently allocates a larger share of training trajectories to
prompts with $`0.2<p_{\mathrm{ref}}\leq 0.8`$ (Figure 6b). This pattern
is consistent with the acceptance rule’s preference for prompts of
intermediate estimated difficulty (§3.4).

## 5 Conclusion

S3AC enables effective critic training for long-CoT RLVR through broader
trajectory coverage, small-scale value-head initialization, and relaxed
critic gradient clipping. The trained critic provides prompt-specific
baselines and token-level advantages. Experiments show that this
actor–critic approach outperforms GRPO-X under group rollout across
1.5B–397B models and sustains stable single-rollout training at 35B.
Beyond policy learning, DAPS reuses critic values and outcome history to
select prompts before generation, reducing discarded responses under
group rollout and improving performance in both settings.

## Limitations

#### Task scope.

All experiments are reasoning-style tasks with verifiable rewards,
namely math, science and multimodal reasoning. We do not evaluate
agentic multi-turn settings, where credit must be assigned across tool
calls and environment turns, so whether our findings carry over to
agentic RL remains open. The reported experiments do not systematically
assess variability across independent RL training seeds.

#### Compute overhead.

S3AC costs 27–40% more amortized wall time per actor gradient step than
GRPO-X, because the critic must be trained and run for inference
alongside the policy.

## Ethical Considerations

Our work aims to improve long-CoT reasoning in LLMs through RLVR. Higher
benchmark scores do not establish the safety or reliability of model
outputs in real-world applications; models may still generate incorrect
or harmful content. Deployment requires application-specific evaluation
and safeguards. Large-scale actor–critic training also requires
additional computational resources and energy, and these costs should be
weighed against the observed performance gains.

## Appendix A Experimental Details

### A.1 Training Configurations

Our training pipeline is built on XTuner (XTuner Contributors, 2023),
with LMDeploy (LMDeploy Contributors, 2023) used for rollout generation.
Table 6 summarizes the training configurations; all methods start from
the same base checkpoint within each model. All group-rollout runs use
dynamic sampling (Yu et al., 2025), discarding groups whose responses
are all correct or all incorrect. On Intern-S2-397B, both S3AC and
GRPO-X apply an adaptive length regularizer that favors shorter correct
responses in high-accuracy groups; S3AC uses the adjusted rewards for
both actor and critic training.

### A.2 Evaluation

We evaluate both 1.5B models offline at gradient step 1,024. For
Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, we use temperature 0.7, top-$`p`$ 0.7, and avg@16 on
every benchmark. We cap the combined prompt and response length at 4,096
tokens; FlashREINFORCE (Hu et al., 2026) reports a 4,096-token response
budget.

For R1-Distill-1.5B, we use temperature 0.8, top-$`p`$ 0.95, a
16,384-token response limit, and avg@32. Small score differences on
R1-Distill-1.5B should be interpreted cautiously given the variability
across repeated evaluations.

For Intern-S2-Preview-35B and Intern-S2-397B, we use temperature 0.8 and
top-$`p`$ 1.0, with response limits of 64K and 62K tokens, respectively.
We report avg@16 on AIME and HMMT26, avg@4 on GPQA-Diamond and
IMO-AnswerBench, and avg@1 on the remaining benchmarks.

GRPO-X baselines additionally use GEPO entropy control (Cheng et al.,

configuration, with a batch-mean baseline and a sequence-level trust
region.

## Appendix C Additional Critic Experiments

This section provides token-level value examples for §3.2 and the
experimental setups and diagnostics for §4.3.

### C.1 Experimental Setup

#### Pretraining data and sources.

For Intern-S2-Preview-35B, the coverage critic learns from trajectories
generated by policies of different strengths across multiple stages of
prior experiments. Its preparation includes value pretraining on a
corpus of 327,680 trajectories (7.08B response tokens). The on-policy
critic is pretrained only on 65,536 initial-policy trajectories (0.74B
response tokens). At 1,600 actor gradient steps, the coverage and
on-policy runs achieve seven-benchmark averages of 80.78 and 79.86.

#### Value-pretraining configuration.

We pretrain for two epochs with cosine learning-rate decay from
$`10^{-5}`$ to $`5\times 10^{-6}`$ after a 3% linear warmup. The loss is
$`\tfrac{1}{2}(V_{\phi}(s_{t})-R)^{2}`$, averaged over response tokens
using the terminal verifier reward $`R`$. The FP32 value head has
initialization standard deviation $`1/(H{+}1)`$, and the gradient norm
is clipped at 10. Attention, the MoE router and any vision tower and
projector are frozen. Online configurations are given in §3.3 and
Table 6.

### C.2 Data Coverage and Token-Level Values

#### Response length and correctness.

Figure 10 compares sampled trajectories from the initial policy and the
policy at gradient step 1,600. Among responses longer than 24K tokens,
5.0% are correct in the initial-policy sample versus 61.8% at gradient
step 1,600. The stronger-policy data thus supply long correct
trajectories that are rare in the initial-policy data.

Figure 10: Response-length distribution (a) and accuracy by length (b)
for the initial policy and the policy at gradient step 1,600 on
Intern-S2-Preview-35B. Bins span 2K tokens.

#### Token-level value examples.

Figure 11 expands the example in Figure 3 and adds a numerical
multiple-choice example, both evaluated with the coverage critic. In the
first, the value rises when the response identifies the possible
sequence values, including $`4/5`$, and increases further when it
determines that the requested term is $`4/5`$. In the second, the value
rises sharply from negative values toward zero after the response
computes $`0.1622`$ and matches it to option H ($`0.1614`$). These
examples illustrate how the coverage critic tracks meaningful reasoning
progress within a response, providing fine-grained value estimates for
token-level credit assignment.

# RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolution of Reinforcement Learning Algorithms

Haoran Li, Zengle Ge, Xiaomin Yuan, YUI LO, Songlin Zhou, Jiahua Ying,
Haoxin Li, Qianhui Liu, Yuanhang Liu, Jiaqun Liu, GUOKAI CHEN, Mingju
Chen, Ruinan Wang, Annan Li, Jianmin Wu, Dawei Yin, Dou Shen University
of the Chinese Academy of Sciences  Baidu  Tsinghua University Institute
of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences  Peking University  Shanghai
University  University of Bristol

###### Abstract

LLM-guided program evolution has enabled discoveries in mathematics and
computational optimization, raising the prospect of reinforcement
learning (RL) algorithms that self-evolve to improve how agents learn.
However, realizing this prospect faces two obstacles. Joint search over
coupled algorithmic components is difficult to scale: simultaneous
changes can disrupt learning, while isolated changes overlook their
dependencies. Evaluating candidate algorithms also requires costly
training, with fitness remaining uncertain across random seeds. We
introduce RLDiscover, a framework for the self-evolution of model-free
deep RL algorithms. Progressive Co-Evolution advances from targeted
component edits to joint evolution, while Progressive Probabilistic
Evaluation balances search breadth and evaluation fidelity through
staged training and repeated evaluation. Experiments across SAC, PPO,
and DQN on four benchmark suites show substantial improvements in mean
return, with per-family median gains of $`32\%`$–$`84\%`$ and a peak
return ratio of approximately $`363\times`$ over a near-zero baseline.
These gains include transitions from failed learning to successful task
completion, and improvements persist when evolution starts from stronger
open-source implementations. On measured SAC locomotion runs, evaluation
uses approximately one-fifteenth the estimated compute required to fully
evaluate the same candidate pool. Remarkably, independent searches
repeatedly discover interpretable combinations of adaptive robust
losses, progress-dependent value targets, and running statistics, with
selected programs transferring to unseen tasks. These findings point
toward a broader role for self-evolution in AI: discovering
interpretable algorithms that improve how agents learn.¹¹ 1 Code and
data will be publicly released upon acceptance.

## 1 Introduction

LLM-guided program evolution makes executable algorithmic logic a direct
object of search: language models propose code changes, and evaluation
feedback guides their selection ([Lehman et al., 2022](#bib.bib27)).
FunSearch demonstrated the potential of this approach for mathematical
discovery ([Romera-Paredes et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)); AlphaEvolve
extended it to larger programs and computational optimization
tasks ([Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib2)); and ShinkaEvolve developed
mechanisms for more sample-efficient program evolution ([Lange et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)). These advances motivate applying program evolution to
the learning procedures that govern how artificial agents improve
through experience. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a natural target:
an RL algorithm is itself a program specifying how experience is
transformed into updates to an agent’s predictions and behavior [Oh et
al. (2020)](#bib.bib26). We focus on model-free deep RL, encompassing
value-based methods such as DQN ([Mnih et al., 2015](#bib.bib11)),
actor-critic methods such as PPO ([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib10)),
and SAC ([Haarnoja et al., 2018](#bib.bib8)). These algorithms specify
how experience is converted into learning targets, value estimates, and
policy updates. Their implementations therefore offer a structured space
for discovering improvements to how agents learn.

However, evolving interacting components of deep RL algorithms faces two
difficulties. First, _joint search over coupled components is difficult
to scale_. Broad randomization often yields little learning, while the
same design change can have opposite effects: increasing data reuse can
help PPO but hurt vanilla policy gradients ([Andrychowicz et al.,
2021](#bib.bib28)). These observations show that broad simultaneous
changes can disrupt learning, while optimizing choices in a fixed
context can overlook their dependencies. Second, _candidate fitness is
expensive and imperfectly observed_. In our experiments, fully
evaluating 230 candidate programs would cost approximately 1330
GPU-hours per search run
(Appendix [J](#A10 "Appendix J PPE Efficiency ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms"),
Table [8](#A10.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Appendix J PPE Efficiency ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).²²
2 The evaluation strategy introduced below uses approximately 87
GPU-hours for this candidate volume, a $`15.3\times`$ reduction relative
to the estimated full-evaluation cost. Moreover, full-length training
does not eliminate uncertainty caused by random seeds: On
mw-pick-place-v3, the same evolved program obtains returns of 2.9,
2887.3, and 3.9 across three seeds
(Appendix [K](#A11 "Appendix K Full Per-Seed Results ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).
These observations expose competing demands on the evaluation budget:
exploring many programs requires inexpensive feedback, while determining
whether apparent improvements persist requires more extensive, repeated
training.

This motivates RLDiscover, a framework that addresses these challenges
through structured proposals and staged evaluation. Its search space
consists of five algorithmic components within a working RL
implementation.³³ 3 The interfaces cover common RL computations:
observation processing, target/advantage estimation, value-function
losses, policy objectives, and reward processing with exploration
bonuses ([Bou et al., 2023](#bib.bib30); [Burda et al.,
2019](#bib.bib29)). Their use is trainer-dependent.
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Implementation Details of PCE and PPE ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
gives the full interface specifications. Progressive Co-Evolution (PCE)
first allocates mutations among individual components using a
UCB-inspired rule, then permits joint revisions across components,
allowing the search to build on targeted changes while exploring their
interactions. Progressive Probabilistic Evaluation (PPE) allocates
training compute through increasingly demanding evaluations, reserving
full training for a small set of finalists. Together, they implement a
simple design principle: structure the search to generate useful
candidates, and increase evaluation effort as the candidate pool is
narrowed. We instantiate the framework on SAC, PPO, and DQN, covering
off-policy and on-policy learning as well as continuous and discrete
control. Our contributions are:

1.  1.  Efficient RL algorithm evolution. We introduce PCE and PPE to
        address component dependencies and costly, noisy evaluations. On
        measured SAC locomotion runs, they reduce evaluation cost by
        approximately $`15\times`$ relative to evaluating the same number of
        candidates at full fidelity.
2.  2.  Broad improvements across algorithms and benchmarks. Across four
        benchmark suites, RLDiscover improves mean return on all 26 tested
        task–algorithm pairs spanning SAC, PPO, and DQN, with median gains
        of $`39.8\%`$, $`83.6\%`$, and $`32.1\%`$, respectively.
        Improvements also persist when evolution starts from stronger
        open-source SAC implementations, with positive gains on all nine
        tested tasks.
3.  3.  Convergent discovery of interpretable mechanisms. Task-specific
        searches repeatedly recover shared mechanisms, including robust
        critic losses in 16/17 SAC and 5/5 PPO champions. Cross-task
        evaluations further demonstrate transfer of evolved programs to
        unseen tasks, showing that program evolution recovers reusable
        learning mechanisms beyond hyperparameter tuning.

## 2 Related Work

#### Discovering RL algorithms.

Prior work discovers learning objectives and update rules through
evolutionary optimization and meta-learning. Evolved Policy
Gradients ([Houthooft et al., 2018](#bib.bib5)) and MetaGenRL ([Kirsch
et al., 2020](#bib.bib6)) learn parameterized objectives, while LPG([Oh
et al., 2020](#bib.bib26)) and DiscoRL ([Oh et al., 2025](#bib.bib7))
meta-learn neural update rules. AutoML-Zero and Evolving RL Algorithms
search programs or computation graphs constructed from predefined
operation libraries ([Real et al., 2020](#bib.bib25); [Co-Reyes et al.,
2021](#bib.bib24)). Eureka instead generates reward programs within an
existing learning pipeline ([Ma et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)). A parallel
line applies the same foundation-model code-evolution paradigm to
adversarial multi-agent games, where the evaluation landscape shifts as
strategies improve and reliable assessment therefore requires evaluator
co-evolution and hierarchical evaluation ([Anonymous,
2026](#bib.bib20)). Our focus is source-code modification across
multiple components of working RL implementations. Through fixed
function interfaces, RLDiscover edits observation processing, targets,
objectives, and reward shaping, and studies how to coordinate these
modifications under a limited evaluation budget.

#### Multi-fidelity evaluation and search allocation.

Successive Halving ([Jamieson and Talwalkar, 2016](#bib.bib21)) and
Hyperband allocate ([Li et al., 2018](#bib.bib22)) increasing resources
to promising configurations; BOHB combines this allocation with
model-guided candidate generation ([Falkner et al., 2018](#bib.bib23)).
PPE applies staged evaluation to evolving RL programs to balance
candidate coverage against the reliability of performance estimates. For
proposal allocation, PCE uses a UCB-inspired rule to select components
for mutation ([Auer et al., 2002](#bib.bib16)), before allowing joint
program revisions. These mechanisms adapt established allocation ideas
to the coupled search and evaluation of RL implementations.

## 3 Method

![Refer to caption](2610.09218v1/figures/rldiscover_framework.png)

Figure 1: RLDiscover overview. Left: 5 algorithm components of RL

### 3.1 Five-Component Decomposition

Given a base RL algorithm, let $`\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{K})`$, with
$`K=5`$, denote the implementations of its five algorithmic components.
Let $`\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{m})`$ be the algorithm obtained by inserting
these components into the base implementation. For a fixed task
environment $`e`$, let $`\mathcal{S}`$ be a finite set of training seeds
and let $`s`$ be sampled uniformly from $`\mathcal{S}`$. We seek to
maximize

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
F(\mathbf{m})=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{S})}\left[R_{e}\!\left(\mathrm{Train}(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{m}),e,s)\right)\right],
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathrm{Train}(\mathcal{A},e,s)`$ returns the policy obtained by
training algorithm $`\mathcal{A}`$ in environment $`e`$ with seed $`s`$
under the full-fidelity training budget. For a policy $`\pi`$,
$`R_{e}(\pi)`$ denotes its mean evaluation return in $`e`$, normalized
using task-specific bounds. Thus, $`F(\mathbf{m})`$ measures the
performance of the resulting algorithm. We write
$`\widehat{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{m})`$ for the observed fitness computed
using the training budget.

### 3.2 Progressive Co-Evolution (PCE)

PCE searches for improvements in $`F(\mathbf{m})`$ by first restricting
mutations to individual components and then allowing coordinated changes
across components
(Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ The five component interfaces. ‣ Appendix D Implementation Details of PCE and PPE ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).
This separates the search for useful local changes from the exploration
of their interactions.

#### Phase 1: bandit-guided single-component evolution.

Each component is an arm of a UCB1 bandit ([Auer et al.,
2002](#bib.bib16)). Writing $`n_{k}`$ for the number of pulls of arm
$`k`$, $`\bar{r}_{k}`$ for its mean reward, $`t`$ for the total pulls so
far, and $`\mathcal{K}_{t}`$ for the arms still active, the next
component to mutate is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
k_{t}=\argmax_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}\left[\bar{r}_{k}+c_{t}\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{n_{k}}}\right],
``` |  | (2) |

with $`K{=}5`$ arms and a decaying exploration coefficient
$`c_{t}=c_{0}/\sqrt{1+t/K}`$, $`c_{0}{=}\sqrt{2}`$ and $`c_{t}`$
shifting the bandit toward exploitation as Phase 1 proceeds. For the
selected component $`k`$, the LLM generates a batch of proposed programs
$`\mathbf{m}^{\prime}`$, each changing only $`m_{k}`$. These candidates
are screened using $`\widehat{F}_{0}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime})`$
(Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Progressive Probabilistic Evaluation (PPE) ‣ 3 Method ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).
The arm reward is the batch’s nonnegative relative fitness gain over
that component’s incumbent. After initial coverage of all components,
Phase 1 freezes components whose relative gains remain below a threshold
for consecutive batches. It ends when all components are frozen or its
budget cap is reached, returning per-component elites and their observed
gains.

#### Phase 1 $`\to`$ 2 handoff.

Component champions are merged in descending order of their Phase-1
gains. A proposed replacement $`\mathbf{m}^{\prime}`$ is retained only
if
$`\widehat{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime})\geq\widehat{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{m})`$
under the same validation protocol. The resulting program initializes
Phase 2. The insight library (below) is already populated from Phase 1
batches, arm-freeze comparisons, and rejected components, so Phase 2
starts from accumulated findings, negative ones included.

#### Phase 2: round-based full co-evolution.

Phase 2 uses the remaining budget to pursue improvements in the same
objective $`F(\mathbf{m})`$, with mutations now allowed to change any
subset of components. Each round launches a fresh population-based
search seeded with the current best program. Discoveries carry across
rounds via an *insight library*, accumulated from Phase 1 onwards: after
each round the mechanisms distinguishing that round’s champions are
extracted into short natural-language entries and injected into the next
round’s mutation prompt, so later rounds build on earlier findings.

### 3.3 Progressive Probabilistic Evaluation (PPE)

PPE allocates evaluation compute to identify high-performing programs
under the objective $`F(\mathbf{m})`$. Let
$`\widehat{F}_{\ell}(\mathbf{m})`$ denote the observed fitness at
fidelity $`\ell`$. These observations differ from the full-training
objective because of training truncation and seed-dependent variability.
PPE screens candidates through three fidelity levels: L0 (50K steps, one
seed), L1 (200K steps, two seeds), and L2 (1M steps, three seeds). A
single L2 evaluation costs 4–10 GPU-hours in our measurements. The
resulting hierarchy supports broad candidate screening while reserving
expensive validation for a smaller pool
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Implementation Details of PCE and PPE ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms"),
Table [4](#A4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ The five component interfaces. ‣ Appendix D Implementation Details of PCE and PPE ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).

#### Leader-referenced promotion.

Promotion to L1 is governed by a global quota $`Q`$. Let
$`\mathcal{C}_{r}`$ denote the candidates evaluated at L0 in round
$`r`$, ordered by decreasing $`\widehat{F}_{0}`$ as
$`\mathbf{m}_{(1)},\mathbf{m}_{(2)},\ldots`$, and let $`\mathcal{P}`$ be
the accumulated L1 pool. Once $`\mathcal{P}`$ is nonempty, define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{p}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\widehat{F}_{1}(\mathbf{p}).
``` |  | (3) |

The gate compares new candidates with the current higher-fidelity leader
on the same low-fidelity scale. Round $`r`$ promotes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\uparrow}=\{\mathbf{m}_{(1)}\}\;\cup\;\left\{\mathbf{m}_{(i)}:i>1,\;\widehat{F}_{0}(\mathbf{m}_{(i)})>\widehat{F}_{0}(\mathbf{p}^{\star})\right\},
``` |  | (4) |

truncated in L0 rank order to the remaining quota. The round’s
highest-scoring candidate therefore receives an L1 evaluation whenever
quota remains, even if no candidate exceeds the gate. In the final
rounds, an underspent quota triggers a switch to the pool’s median L0
score as the promotion threshold.

#### Final selection.

The five highest-scoring programs in $`\mathcal{P}`$ under
$`\widehat{F}_{1}`$ are evaluated at L2. The reported program is the one
with the highest $`\widehat{F}_{2}`$ among the audited L2 candidates.
Ranking at every level uses untruncated normalized fitness. Before
reporting, each champion is reloaded, checked for the required component
functions, and compared with the seed program to detect silent
fallbacks. AST-hash deduplication avoids repeated evaluation of
syntactically identical programs. Prompt construction, syntax
validation, and audit details are provided in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Implementation Details of PCE and PPE ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms").

## 4 Experiments

#### Tasks.

SAC is evolved on 17 continuous control tasks—4 DMControl ([Tassa et
al., 2018](#bib.bib17)), 5 Gymnasium ([Towers et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)), 5 Meta-World ([Yu et al., 2020](#bib.bib12)) and 3
MetaDrive ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib13))—covering locomotion,
manipulation and autonomous driving, with observation dimensions 11–348
and action dimensions 2–17; PPO is evolved on 5 Gymnasium locomotion
tasks and DQN on 4 discrete control tasks (CartPole, MountainCar,
Acrobot, LunarLander): 26 task–algorithm pairs in total. Per-task
specifications are in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Benchmark Task Details ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms").

#### Two kinds of seed program.

The *own-init* batch seeds evolution from our own standard
implementation of the algorithm and covers all 26 pairs; the *SOTA-init*
batch re-seeds the 9 SAC locomotion tasks that have an open-source
reference—denisyarats/pytorch_sac ([Yarats and Kostrikov,
2020](#bib.bib14)) for DMControl, CleanRL SAC ([Huang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib15)) for Gymnasium—from that reference at unchanged
capacity and hyperparameters. Each batch is compared only against the
baseline it was seeded from and neither filters out any task, so each
improvement is attributable to evolution rather than to a change of
implementation. Seed programs, hyperparameters, the candidate budget and
the L2 evaluation protocol are in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Implementation Details of PCE and PPE ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms").

#### Programs are diagnosable when search stalls.

gym-Ant-v5 has the smallest own-init gain in the suite ($`+1.4\%`$).
Reading the champion as code, rather than as a score, reveals why: three
of the components are unchanged from the seed, and Ant’s champion is the
only one in the suite that kept plain MSE as the critic loss—the one
change that virtually every other champion made
(Appendix [P](#A16 "Appendix P Component-Level Analysis ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).
This points to insufficient search, not a task ceiling: the search did
not explore enough from this starting point. The fix the diagnosis
suggests is a stronger seed; re-seeding from CleanRL turns Ant’s
$`+1.4\%`$ into the largest gain in the SOTA-init block ($`+27.3\%`$). A
fitness score cannot tell a hard task from an undersearched one; the
program can.

### 4.2 Evolution from Stronger SOTA Baselines

The *SOTA-init* block of
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 Results Across Three Algorithm Families ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
addresses a natural concern: are the gains partly due to a weak starting
implementation? The external baselines are indeed substantially stronger
on several tasks (CleanRL reaches 11002.4 vs. our 5093.6 on
HalfCheetah). Re-seeding from them, evolution improves on the SOTA
starting point on all 9 of 9 tasks, never degrades a strong seed, and
finds double-digit gains on 3 (Ant $`+27.3\%`$, Hopper $`+20.0\%`$,
cheetah-run $`+20.0\%`$).

The more informative quantity is what happens to the *gap* between the
two batches, since that is what the concern is actually about. At the
seed program the two implementations differ by a mean of $`23.4\%`$ in
return; after evolution the mean gap is $`13.1\%`$, narrowing on 7 of
the 9 tasks—on dm-walker-walk almost entirely ($`29.8\%\to 0.2\%`$).
Evolution recovers roughly half of an implementation-quality gap it did
not create, neither closing nor widening it. The two tasks where the gap
instead *widens* are Humanoid and Ant, the latter being the same cell
identified above as the one where own-init search failed: a stronger
seed helps most exactly where the search itself did least.

Narrowing is not the same as converging, however, and the head-to-head
outcome is a separate question. On the nine locomotion tasks—the only
ones both batches were evolved on with 3 seeds—the SOTA-init champion
finishes ahead on 8 of 9, losing only on Walker2d; two of those eight
are ties of $`0.2\%`$ or less, so the honest reading is six clear wins,
one loss, and two ties. The gap narrows on 7 of 9 tasks while the
ranking survives on only 4, and where it flips the per-task search gain
rather than the seed decides it: Walker2d is a genuine overtake, Ant the
mirror image. A large seed advantage is durable and a small one is
not—all four preserved rankings sit on seed gaps of at least $`30\%`$.
Per-task seed gaps, final gaps, and each batch’s gain over its seed are
in
Appendix [5](#A5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Per-task SOTA-init gaps and the head-to-head outcome. ‣ Appendix E Full Per-Task Results ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms").
The core innovations (Huber loss, $`Q`$-annealing) emerge in both
batches—what we would expect of improvements to the algorithm itself
rather than artifacts of a particular starting implementation.

Robust critic loss. addresses bootstrapping noise: MSE amplifies large
TD errors quadratically while robust losses cap gradients. All but one
champion adopt one (Huber, smooth-L1, Log-Cosh, or Charbonnier) and 9
adapt the transition scale via an EMA of running error statistics.
Progressive Q-blending replaces the fixed pessimistic
$`\min(Q_{1},Q_{2})`$ inherited from clipped double-Q
learning ([Fujimoto et al., 2018](#bib.bib19)) with a progress-annealed
blend of $`\min`$ and mean—a schedule never previously proposed for
SAC—with the annealing direction splitting by domain. Symlog encoding
compresses observations via $`\text{sign}(x)\cdot\log(1{+}|x|)`$; a
similar transform was independently proposed in DreamerV3 ([Hafner et
al., 2023](#bib.bib9)). These modifications transfer
(Figure [2(b)](#S4.F2.sf2 "In Figure 2 ‣ 4.3 Discovered Algorithmic Innovations ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")):
of four champions re-evaluated on 9 locomotion targets, three improve
6–7 of their 8 non-source tasks (mean fitness $`\Delta`$ $`+0.083`$ to
$`+0.157`$), while the walker2d program—which relies on hard-coded step
thresholds—wins only 2/8 ($`-0.134`$;
Appendices [H](#A8 "Appendix H Cross-Task Generalization ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms"), [I](#A9 "Appendix I Discovered Algorithmic Innovations: Detailed Analysis ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).

#### Cross-domain generality.

In Meta-World, push and pick-place achieve order-of-magnitude gains from
near-zero baselines, demonstrating that evolution can fundamentally
restructure failing training procedures. In MetaDrive, all 3 driving
scenarios improve, including 100-envs large-scale generalization
(+19.0%). The same evolution pipeline and evaluation protocol work
across locomotion, robotic manipulation, and autonomous driving without
domain-specific adjustments.

#### Interpreting the discovered mechanisms.

Progressive Q-blending’s annealing direction splits by domain:
locomotion champions start optimistic and anneal toward $`\min`$, while
manipulation and driving champions *relax* pessimism as training
stabilizes. Symlog encoding prevents gradient saturation in
high-dimensional tasks; MSE amplifies large TD errors quadratically
while robust losses cap gradients at the transition scale, acting as
built-in gradient clipping.

#### Principles for generalizable programs.

Comparing a well-generalizing program (quadruped, 6/8 wins) with the
worst (walker2d, 2/8 wins), we identify three principles for
transferable RL modifications. (1) Relative scheduling: use progress
ratio $`t/T`$ rather than absolute step counts for annealing—the
quadruped program’s cosine-annealed schedules adapt to any training
length. (2) Stateless encoding: pointwise transformations (arcsinh,
symlog) transfer perfectly; running statistics do not. (3) Adaptive
thresholds: parameters derived from training dynamics (EMA of TD errors,
as in the walker-walk program) auto-calibrate to each task, while
hard-coded constants may be inappropriate for different reward scales.

#### The full method leads every ablation on the pooled mean.

It leads all five by $`0.026`$–$`0.043`$ normalised fitness over the 8
pairs and by $`0.041`$–$`0.062`$ over the 6 non-degenerate pairs, and
the sign of the paired $`\Delta`$ is negative for every ablation in both
groupings. The per-pair spread is $`1.3`$–$`4\times`$ the gap being
measured, however, so the consistent ordering rests on the sign pattern
rather than on any single comparison, and we read the pooled numbers as
directional.

#### Stratifying by family shows why, and recovers the SAC result.

The pooled null averages over families that disagree. Within SAC the
funnel effect is large and ordered: on the four-task mean the full
method leads all three single-level variants by 7.1% (L0), 10.7% (L2)
and 11.8% (L1), with distinct failure modes—L0 searches the widest but
ranks on 50K-step single-seed returns that favour fast starters not all
of which survive to 1M steps; L2 ranks on the true objective but at
$`\sim`$5.8 GPU-hours per evaluation affords only 13 candidates,
degenerating into local refinement of a handful of programs; L1 inherits
neither L0’s breadth nor L2’s fidelity. On PPO and DQN the directions
are inconsistent or reversed—L2 beats the full method on *both* PPO
tasks and wins 4 of the 8 pairs, two of them starred. Since the funnel’s
thresholds were calibrated on SAC, we claim the funnel advantage for SAC
only.

#### Two statements hold in every family.

(a) Single-level selection at the cheapest fidelity ($`L_{0}`$, the
simplest search configuration) is the only ablation trailing the full
method in every family. (b) Compute-aligned L2 evaluation is the closest
competitor pooled across families, reaching comparable fitness by
spending the whole budget on 13 fully-trained candidates and forgoing
the funnel’s wider search and early-stopping speedup. What both bound is
the same trade-off: across the three configurations the search gets
wider as the fidelity gets cheaper and the winner sits in between.
Because the three single-level conditions also drop the decomposition
and Phase 1, the full method’s margin is evidence for the *combination*,
and the defensible claim about the funnel specifically is *efficiency at
equal quality* rather than demonstrably higher fitness.

#### Selection fidelity pays off where the search problem is hard.

Separating six configurations requires headroom: if the baseline already
saturates a cell, all six cluster on the same score and the ordering
becomes noise. The four SAC pairs split by baseline strength
(Appendix [N](#A14 "Appendix N Per-Cell Evolution Variance of the SAC Ablation ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms"),
Table [11](#A14.T11 "Table 11 ‣ Appendix N Per-Cell Evolution Variance of the SAC Ablation ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).
Where the initial solution is weak (on dm-cheetah-run and
gym-HalfCheetah-v5), even the *worst* ablated condition beats it by
$`+0.25`$ and $`+0.31`$, and the full method ranks first of six on both.
Where the initial solution already sits near the top of the range (on
md-100envs and mw-drawer-open-v3), the worst condition clears it by only
$`+0.051`$ and $`+0.012`$, the full method drops to fourth and second,
and on md-100envs the winner is L0, the *cheapest* condition. These
results suggest that selection fidelity matters most when the baseline
leaves substantial room for improvement; benchmarks dominated by
near-saturated baselines may therefore underestimate it.

## 6 Conclusion

RLDiscover advances automated RL algorithm evolution by searching
executable learning rules under a limited evaluation budget. Progressive
Co-Evolution coordinates edits to interacting components, while
Progressive Probabilistic Evaluation concentrates training compute on
promising candidates. Across SAC, PPO, and DQN, the evolved programs
improve learning performance, including successful task completion where
the starting implementation fails. The search uncovers interpretable
combinations of adaptive robust losses, progress-dependent value
targets, and stateful schedules, whose recurrence across independent
searches and observed cross-task transfer provide evidence of reusable
algorithmic structure. By connecting LLM-generated code with
training-based selection, RLDiscover offers the community both a
practical discovery framework and executable mechanisms to inspect,
test, and adapt—evidence that program evolution can expand what agents
learn and generate concrete insights for designing better RL algorithms.

## Appendix A Limitations

Three limitations bound what we claim. First, *PPO and DQN results are
suboptimal relative to what per-algorithm tuning would achieve*: budgets
and hyperparameters were carried over from SAC without adjustment, so
the absolute returns for these two families are not comparable to
published results and represent a lower bound on what the same pipeline
could reach with algorithm-specific calibration.

Second, *the five-component decomposition is active only at L0*. Once a
candidate advances to L1 or L2, it is evaluated as a monolithic program
and the component interface plays no role. This confines Phase 1’s
per-component credit assignment, and any further structured exploration,
to the 50K-step screening stage. A natural extension is to run L1 for a
brief period and then re-enter L0 with the accumulated fitness
signal—keeping structured five-component search active at longer
horizons rather than switching permanently to monolithic evaluation.

Third, *L0 screening at 50K steps can discard RL strategies with slow
warm-up*. Programs that achieve strong asymptotic performance but
require extended environment interaction to stabilise are ranked low at
the short horizon and filtered out before the costlier evaluations that
would reveal their advantage. The funnel’s promotion thresholds are
calibrated to the evaluation protocol, not to the learning dynamics of
each candidate.

## Appendix C Implications for RL Algorithm Design (extended)

Our results suggest the standard SAC algorithm contains several
recurring suboptimalities fixable across the majority of tasks tested:
MSE is suboptimal for critic training (robust losses better on 16/17
tasks); fixed pessimistic Q-aggregation is overly conservative
(annealing helps); raw observations benefit from logarithmic compression
(symlog in 11/17); and constant-coefficient exploration bonuses are
worse than progress-decayed ones. The approach holds across locomotion,
manipulation (Meta-World 5/5), and driving (MetaDrive 3/3), consistent
with cross-domain generality, and the PPO (5/5) and DQN (4/4) results
obtained through the same five slots
(Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Results Across Three Algorithm Families ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms"))
indicate the same holds across algorithm families.

#### Cross-domain generality.

In Meta-World, push and pick-place achieve order-of-magnitude gains from
near-zero baselines, demonstrating that evolution can fundamentally
restructure failing training procedures. In MetaDrive, all 3 driving
scenarios improve, including 100-envs large-scale generalization
(+19.0%). The same evolution pipeline and evaluation protocol work
across locomotion, robotic manipulation, and autonomous driving without
domain-specific adjustments.

#### Cross-algorithm takeaway.

Across three algorithm families spanning off-policy and on-policy,
continuous and discrete control, RLDiscover improved the baseline on
26/26 tasks with audited, non-degenerate champions. Its two
assumptions—that an RL algorithm decomposes into evolvable components,
and that fitness can be estimated through a staged funnel—hold on all
three, although the three are not validated to equal depth
(Section [A](#A1 "Appendix A Limitations ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).

#### Per-task failure modes of the single-level PPE variants.

L0-only costs $`-`$0.129 on dm-cheetah-run and $`-`$0.062 on
mw-drawer-open; all-L2 costs $`-`$0.153 on md-100envs, the task with the
most headroom.

#### Compositionality of the discovered innovations.

Huber loss appears in DQN (Mnih et al., 2015) and symlog in DreamerV3
(Hafner et al., 2023), so the individual ingredients are not new.
Evolution combines them with elements absent from prior work (adaptive
$`\delta`$ from running statistics, asymmetric weighting from
Q-disagreement), and the specific schedules (3-stage Q-blending with
sinusoidal interpolation) are novel.

#### Why the consensus innovations work.

Progressive Q-blending’s annealing direction splits by domain:
locomotion champions start optimistic and anneal toward $`\min`$, while
manipulation and driving champions *relax* pessimism as training
stabilizes. Symlog encoding prevents gradient saturation in
high-dimensional tasks.

## Appendix D Implementation Details of PCE and PPE

#### The five component interfaces.

Each component is a Python function with a fixed calling convention,
receiving the current step $`t`$ and total steps $`T`$ and sharing a
persistent state dictionary; full Python signatures are included in the
released code:

1.  1.
    encode_state: *State representation* — transforms raw observations
    into features (e.g., normalization, running statistics,
    dimensionality reduction).
2.  2.
    compute_value_target: *Value estimation* — computes bootstrap
    targets for the critic. In SAC this is the soft Bellman target
    $`\min(Q_{1},Q_{2})-\alpha\log\pi`$; in PPO it is Generalized
    Advantage Estimation (GAE); in DQN the target-network Bellman
    backup.
3.  3.
    compute_critic_loss: *Critic learning* — defines the loss for
    training value or Q-functions (e.g., MSE, Huber, value clipping).
4.  4.
    compute_policy_loss: *Policy optimization* — computes the gradient
    signal for the policy. SAC uses reparameterized entropy-regularized
    objectives; PPO uses the clipped surrogate; DQN derives the policy
    implicitly via $`\arg\max_{a}Q`$.
5.  5.
    compute_exploration_bonus: *Exploration strategy* — shapes the
    reward signal to encourage exploration (e.g., intrinsic curiosity,
    entropy bonuses, reward normalization).

#### Evolution budget.

All mutations are generated by a large language model. Every run in this
paper—main-line and ablation alike—draws from a budget of $`B{=}240`$
candidate programs. After syntactic validation and AST-hash
deduplication, each run screens on average 230 candidates at L0, for
$`\sim`$87 GPU-hours of evaluation per run
(Appendix [J](#A10 "Appendix J PPE Efficiency ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")).
The ablations of
Section [5](#S5 "5 Ablation Studies ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
use the full procedure of
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Progressive Co-Evolution (PCE) ‣ 3 Method ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
throughout, so all conditions compared there are internally consistent.

#### Matched-budget protocol for controlled comparisons.

The ablations of
Section [5](#S5 "5 Ablation Studies ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
compare conditions under a matched mutation budget: every L0-screening
condition receives 240 LLM-generated candidate programs, with the
Phase-1/Phase-2 split determined adaptively as in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Progressive Co-Evolution (PCE) ‣ 3 Method ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms");
conditions that remove a phase spend the same 240 candidates in the
remaining phase. Conditions that evaluate every candidate at higher
fidelity (L2-only, L1-only) cannot match candidate counts by
construction, and are instead aligned by evaluation compute (same
wall-clock GPU budget), which affords 13 (SAC), 43 (PPO) and 33 (DQN)
L2-only candidates and 60 L1-only ones (realized $`\approx`$50 for
L1-only due to evaluation time limits). The per-algorithm counts differ
because the three trainers spend different numbers of environment steps
per evaluation, so a fixed compute budget buys a different number of
full-fidelity candidates in each;
Appendix [M](#A13 "Appendix M Full Component-Ablation Matrix ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
records the alignment. All conditions run with isolated storage to
prevent cross-condition contamination, and every reported champion
passes the audit protocol of
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Progressive Probabilistic Evaluation (PPE) ‣ 3 Method ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
(loader verification of all five component functions plus AST comparison
against the seed program).

#### Base SAC hyperparameters.

Our base SAC uses a 2-layer MLP (256 hidden), LayerNorm + ReLU,
$`\gamma{=}0.99`$, $`\tau{=}0.005`$, batch size 256, learning rate
$`3{\times}10^{-4}`$, automatic entropy tuning, 1M environment steps,
and 5K random exploration steps. The external references
(denisyarats/pytorch_sac, CleanRL SAC) are run at identical network
capacity and hyperparameters.

#### Evaluation protocol.

All results use L2: train with seeds $`\{0,42,123\}`$, then run
deterministic evaluation episodes. For SAC and PPO this is 1M training
steps and 20 evaluation episodes; for DQN, whose classic-control tasks
have far shorter horizons, L2 is 500K steps and 30 evaluation episodes.
Baselines and evolved programs of a given algorithm always use that
algorithm’s L2 setting, so every comparison in this paper is
within-protocol. We report mean return $`\pm`$ std across seeds. To
guard against silent failures in the evolution pipeline, every reported
champion program was additionally *audited*: we re-load the program with
the component loader, verify that all five component functions are
extractable, and confirm the reported fitness reproduces at L2. Only
audited programs are reported.

#### Provenance of the reported numbers.

In
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 Results Across Three Algorithm Families ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms"),
the DMControl and HalfCheetah entries of the SAC blocks are independent
3-seed re-evaluations, and the Hopper/Walker2d/Humanoid entries are Aug
2026 re-evaluations. For the PPO and DQN blocks of the same table, the
evolution pipeline persisted only a scalar L2 fitness for the champions,
so the evolved entries are *independent* re-evaluations of the champion
programs under exactly the protocol their baselines use. Because each
champion was selected as the maximum over $`N`$ candidates, its recorded
selection score is upward-biased; the re-evaluations therefore land
slightly below it on 7 of 9 tasks (by $`0.001`$–$`0.071`$ fitness) and
above it on the other two. We report the re-evaluation rather than the
selection score throughout.

#### Why MountainCar-v0 uses 10 seeds.

Its outcome is bimodal—a run either solves the task at $`\approx-130`$
or fails at exactly $`-200`$—so a 3-seed mean is unstable: an earlier
3-seed draw caught two failures and reported $`-178.6`$ for the same
champion that scores $`-144.9`$ over 10 seeds. Both sides of that
comparison use 10 seeds. Over those 10, the baseline returns exactly
$`-200.0`$ on every seed (std $`=0`$) and the evolved program reaches
the goal on 8, which is the 0/10 $`\to`$ 8/10 success-rate shift quoted
in
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Results Across Three Algorithm Families ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms").

## Appendix F Per-Family Qualifications

#### Cross-family comparisons are raw-return comparisons only.

Fitness is normalised by task-specific bounds, and the bounds are shared
across families, but the *scale* of the return differs by orders of
magnitude between DQN’s classic-control tasks (CartPole ceiling 500) and
the locomotion tasks (HalfCheetah in the $`10^{4}`$ range). A per-family
median gain is therefore a statement about each family’s own task suite,
not a ranking of the families’ algorithms; the three medians
($`+39.8\%`$, $`+83.6\%`$, $`+32.1\%`$) are not comparable to each other
in magnitude, and none is comparable to published numbers, which use
different evaluation protocols. The one cross-family comparison we do
make—evolved PPO against our evolved SAC on gym-Hopper-v5—is made on raw
return under one protocol
(Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Results Across Three Algorithm Families ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")),
which is why it is stated that way and not as a fitness difference.

#### The marker definitions.

Three markers appear in

^(†) marks an own-init SAC cell whose evolved return exceeds the
external open-source reference for that task (denisyarats/pytorch_sac on
DMControl, CleanRL SAC on Gymnasium) as we re-evaluated it at identical
capacity and hyperparameters. ^(§) (on the group headers) flags that the
evolved entries of that block are *independent* re-evaluations of the
champion programs rather than their selection scores, which are
upward-biased by max-over-$`N`$ selection; the same applies to the two

Appendix [K](#A11 "Appendix K Full Per-Seed Results ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms").

#### The $`\dagger`$ comparisons.

Two qualifications attach to a $`\dagger`$. First, the reference numbers
are our re-evaluations, not the values published by those projects, so a
$`\dagger`$ means we beat our own reproduction of the reference under
our protocol, not that we beat a leaderboard entry. Second, all five
$`\dagger`$ cells sit in the own-init block, where the comparison
crosses a change of seed program as well as a change of scale—five of
the nine locomotion tasks are cleared from our own weaker seed
(walker-walk, quadruped-walk, Hopper, Walker2d, Humanoid), and the
SOTA-init block re-runs the same nine to isolate the scale effect, where
the margins are much smaller (median $`+2.6\%`$).

#### The MountainCar normalisation artifact.

MountainCar-v0’s baseline scores a fitness of $`0.908`$ while returning
exactly $`-200.0`$ on every seed, i.e. the worst attainable return for
that environment. The task’s normalisation bounds place $`-200`$ near
the top of the fitness range, so the worst possible policy receives a
high fitness. This is why the DQN ablation reports all conditions within
$`0.908`$–$`0.948`$ on that task
(Section [5](#S5 "5 Ablation Studies ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms"))
and why we read no ordering from that column: it compresses the entire
failure region into a narrow high-fitness band, and the task cannot
separate conditions there. The relevant MountainCar result is the
success-rate shift under 10 seeds ($`0/10\to 8/10`$), not any fitness
gap, and we report it that way.

The principles behind the difference are distilled below.

#### What makes programs generalizable?

Comparing a well-generalizing program (quadruped, 6/8 wins) with the
worst (walker2d, 2/8 wins), we identify three principles for
transferable RL modifications. (1) Relative scheduling: use progress
ratio $`t/T`$ rather than absolute step counts for annealing—the
quadruped program’s cosine-annealed schedules adapt to any training
length. (2) Stateless encoding: pointwise transformations (arcsinh,
symlog) transfer perfectly; running statistics do not. (3) Adaptive
thresholds: parameters derived from training dynamics (EMA of TD errors,
as in the walker-walk program) auto-calibrate to each task, while
hard-coded constants may be inappropriate for different reward scales.

## Appendix I Discovered Algorithmic Innovations: Detailed Analysis

#### Huber loss addresses bootstrapping noise.

SAC’s critic is trained on bootstrapped targets that are inherently
noisy early in training. MSE amplifies large errors quadratically,
destabilizing learning. Huber loss caps the gradient at $`\delta`$,
providing natural gradient clipping without explicit hyperparameter
tuning.

#### Q-annealing balances exploration-exploitation.

The pessimism of $`\min(Q_{1},Q_{2})`$ underestimates state values,
which can suppress exploration. Early optimism (toward mean) encourages
visiting novel states; progressive conservatism later prevents
overestimation from corrupting the policy.

#### Symlog prevents saturation.

In tasks like MetaDrive (259D LiDAR observations) or HalfCheetah
(high-velocity states), raw observations can reach extreme values that
saturate ReLU networks. Logarithmic compression keeps inputs in a
network-friendly range.

#### Robust critic loss (16/17 tasks; EMA-adaptive scale in 9).

The most universal discovery replaces MSE with a robust loss (Huber,
smooth-L1, Log-Cosh, or Charbonnier); 9 champions further adapt the
transition scale from running error statistics—8 as a Huber $`\delta`$,
and md-10env as a Log-Cosh $`\kappa`$ clamped to $`[1,5]`$:

\# Asymmetric weighting based on Q-disagreement

\# Asymmetric Huber: overestimation penalized more heavily

residual.abs() \< self.ema_delta,

0.5 \* residual\*\*2 / self.ema_delta,

residual.abs() - 0.5 \* self.ema_delta

)

#### Progressive Q-blending (12/17 tasks).

Standard SAC uses $`\min(Q_{1},Q_{2})`$ (pessimistic). Evolution
discovers progress-annealed blending of $`\min`$ and mean. A
representative locomotion schedule (dm-walker-walk): early training
($`t/T<0.3`$) uses $`\text{mean}(Q_{1},Q_{2})`$ for optimistic
exploration, shifting via sinusoidal annealing to pure $`\min`$ for
conservative exploitation. Manipulation and driving champions typically
anneal in the opposite direction, relaxing pessimism as training
stabilizes.

#### Symlog encoding (11/17 tasks).

Evolution discovers $`\text{encode}(x)=\text{sign}(x)\cdot\log(1+|x|)`$
or equivalently $`\text{arcsinh}(x/\sigma)\cdot\sigma`$, preserving sign
while compressing dynamic range. This was independently proposed in
DreamerV3 (Hafner et al., 2023).

## Appendix L Evaluation Wall-Clock Caps and Their Effect on the Ablation

Every evaluation runs under a per-level wall-clock cap, and every run
under a total cap. Because the ablation conditions differ in how
expensive a single evaluation is, these caps do not bind equally, and
Section [A](#A1 "Appendix A Limitations ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
summarises the consequence. The per-condition figures are as follows.

(1) L0 cap (1200 s). It truncates 40–65% of PPO L0 evaluations. All
L0-screening conditions are affected equally, so there is no
condition-level selection bias, but the L0 signal is noisier than it
would otherwise be.

(2) Headroom differs by condition. The L2-only cap (43200 s) is
$`7.3\times`$ its median evaluation cost and is therefore structurally
immune to truncation, whereas L0-only ($`1.95\times`$ headroom) and
L1-only ($`1.76\times`$) each lose roughly half their samples under
slow-tail conditions. Part of L2-only’s apparent advantage in
Table [10](#A13.T10 "Table 10 ‣ Appendix M Full Component-Ablation Matrix ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
therefore has a structural rather than methodological origin.

(3) SAC L1-only is right-censored. It is the only condition for which
the 4800 s L1 cap binds, so its
Table [10](#A13.T10 "Table 10 ‣ Appendix M Full Component-Ablation Matrix ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms")
entry is a right-censored estimate of what an uncapped L1-only run would
reach.

(4) Run-level cap (96 h). This truncated 4 of the 6 PPO L2-only runs
across both PPO pairs (35/42/34/36 of 43 planned L2 evaluations); all
DQN L2-only runs reached their full 33. Truncation removes candidates
from L2-only and thus works *against* it, so the reported PPO L2-only
margin over the full method is a conservative lower bound.

Not every component slot is meaningfully evolved. Three of gym-Ant-v5’s
five components are the unmodified seed program—a clip-only encoder, a
textbook $`\min(Q_{1},Q_{2})`$ target, and plain MSE critic loss—and

which all five components changed; it is one in which the components
that mattered changed. This is consistent with the ablation result of
Section [5](#S5 "5 Ablation Studies ‣ RLDiscover: LLM-Driven Co-Evolutionof Reinforcement Learning Algorithms"),
where the margin of per-component mutation over rewriting the whole
program is smaller than the run-to-run spread of the cells being
compared.

Several annealed mechanisms converge back to the baseline.
dm-quadruped-walk’s max-Q weight decays $`0.1\to 0`$, gym-Humanoid-v5’s
optimism weight decays to $`0`$, and gym-Walker2d-v5’s softmin
temperature decays to a hard minimum. In each case the champion is a
deviation from clipped double-Q early in training and is exactly clipped
double-Q at convergence.

#### Which slots are live under each trainer.

This determines what the counts below can mean. Our PPO trainer passes
the on-policy value array and $`\lambda`$ into the value-target
component and never passes twin-$`Q`$ arguments, so the off-policy
branch every champion inherits from the shared template is
unreachable—the mirror of the unreachable GAE branch in the SAC
champions. Our DQN trainer calls only *four* of the five components:
compute_policy_loss is never imported, and the value-target and
critic-loss components are called with the twin-$`Q`$ arguments, the
entropy coefficient and the second critic’s predictions all set to null,
which makes the GAE branch, the twin-$`Q`$ aggregation branch, the
entropy-bonus term and the second-critic term dead in all four DQN
champions. Crucially, the two mechanisms a reader would most expect a
DQN champion to innovate on—the $`\epsilon`$-greedy schedule and the
target-network synchronisation—are hardcoded harness hyperparameters
*outside all five slots*, so DQN evolution could never reach them. This
is a property of our decomposition as implemented, not a discovery
failure, and it is the single most important constraint on the
cross-family comparison.

#### PPO innovates in the slots that are characteristically PPO, in every champion.

All 5 PPO champions modified all five slots. More informative than the
count is *where* the content sits: 5/5 value-target modifications are
GAE-specific ($`\lambda`$ scheduling in 4/5—three linear, one
cosine—dual-$`\lambda`$ streams in 1/5, Monte-Carlo blending in 1/5,
advantage centering in 1/5), and no champion touched the discount or the
bootstrap structure. All 5 policy-loss modifications are
clipped-surrogate-specific: dual clipping 5/5 and KL regularisation 5/5,
the latter in four distinct forms (fixed coefficient, hinge, adaptive
multiplicative, and mask-based pseudo early-stopping), plus clip-range
annealing 4/5 and ratio or advantage clamping 4/5. These mechanisms have
no SAC or DQN analogue, and the SAC signature of $`Q`$-annealing has no
PPO analogue because PPO has no twin critics.

#### DQN’s live surface is three slots, and it uses all three.

Counting only reachable code, the DQN champions modified encoding 4/4,
exploration 4/4 and the critic loss 3/4 (asymmetric Huber on Acrobot,
expectile regression on MountainCar, Huber on LunarLander; CartPole’s
$`0.5\times`$MSE is a constant rescaling absorbable into the learning
rate and we score it unchanged). The live value target is the seed
$`\max_{a}Q^{\prime}`$ backup in 4/4, and the policy slot is 0/4 by
construction. Two independent implementations of RND with random Fourier
features arose on MountainCar and Acrobot, which is the clearest
evidence that evolution used the room it did have. Counting dead code
would have turned the value-target column from 0/4 into 4/4, so we state
the live counts and not the nominal ones.

#### One consensus innovation survives across all three families, and it is the critic loss.

Replacing MSE with a robust regression loss is reached independently
under all three trainers: it is the SAC signature (16/17), it appears in
5/5 PPO champions (adaptive $`\beta`$ in 3, median-derived adaptive
$`\delta`$ in 1, fixed $`\beta{=}10`$ in 1) and in 3/4 DQN champions—8
of the 9 PPO and DQN champions, under two different trainers. A second,
weaker consensus is symlog/clip-family observation encoding (SAC, 5/5
PPO, 4/4 DQN), but this slot’s seed is a bare identity function, so
nearly any change registers and the column is the least discriminating
of the five. Beyond these two, the families are largely disjoint, and
the disjointness is the informative part: each family’s other
innovations sit in the machinery specific to that family.

#### What this does and does not establish.

The strong reading—that each family innovates in the slots
characteristic of *that* family—holds cleanly for PPO and does not hold
for DQN. But the DQN shortfall is structural rather than empirical: the
slots that would carry a characteristic DQN innovation are not exposed
by the decomposition as implemented, and the value-target slot that *is*
exposed degenerates to a fixed $`\max_{a}Q^{\prime}`$ backup. The
defensible claim is therefore that the decomposition supports genuine,
family-appropriate innovation where the family has expressive room in
the slots (SAC and PPO: five slots each) and degenerates where it does
not (DQN: three live slots). We do *not* claim the three families have
equal searchable surface area; DQN is materially narrower.

#### Caveats specific to the cross-algorithm champions.

Three are worth stating plainly. (1) Dead off-policy branches. All four
DQN champions carry unreachable twin-$`Q`$ aggregation, GAE, entropy and
second-critic terms, and three of the four rationale comments headline
their $`Q`$-aggregation as the main change. (2) LunarLander’s advertised
adaptive-$`\gamma`$ warm-up is dead, sitting inside the never-executed
twin-$`Q`$ branch; the live target uses plain $`\gamma`$. This is the
most tempting mis-attribution in the set. (3) One PPO champion carries
an entropy term with a sign opposite to the trainer’s. Our trainer’s own
convention contributes $`+\mathrm{coef}\cdot\overline{\log\pi}`$ to the
loss, which maximises entropy. The gym-Hopper-v5 champion instead adds
$`-\mathrm{coef}\cdot\overline{\log\pi}`$, an entropy *penalty* rather
than a bonus, and still beats its baseline. The gym-HalfCheetah-v5 and
gym-Walker2d-v5 champions write the bonus through double negatives but
are algebraically identical to the trainer’s convention; we flag only
````

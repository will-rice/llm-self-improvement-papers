###### Abstract.

Language-model agents are increasingly deployed to act on behalf of
people in settings where individual decisions shape shared outcomes. We
examine whether five production models adapt their contributions to
changing risks and partner behaviour, or instead follow a relatively
fixed strategy. In a collective-risk dilemma game (CRG), six players
contribute to a shared pool over ten rounds; if the pool falls short of
its target, a catastrophe may destroy their remaining resources. Four
models contributed approximately an equal share at every positive risk
level, and four of the five also contributed when risk was zero, even
though contributing could not improve their payoff. Explicitly stating
that only the agent’s own payoff mattered reduced zero-risk
contributions for most models, while a more neutral prompt did not.
Several models correctly identified which action offered the higher
expected payoff at different risk levels, yet their contributions
changed little. Against fixed partners, no model consistently chose the
payoff-maximising response, and in mixed-model groups, a single agent’s
shortfall in the final round could cause an otherwise successful group
to fail. Payoff-based selection also depended on population composition:
in a single six-agent group, it favoured the model that contributed
least and reduced group success, whereas in larger, randomly mixed
populations, it favoured an exact fair-share model at moderate and high
risk. These findings show that cooperative self-play alone is not
evidence of strategic adaptability. Consistent with a machine-behaviour
approach, evaluations of agents in multi-agent systems should test how
they respond to incentives, partner actions, and population composition.

LLM agents, collective-risk social dilemma, cooperation, best response,

Language-model agents increasingly act on behalf of people in settings
where multiple agents jointly shape a shared outcome, such as budget
allocation, shared compute use, or negotiations over common resources
([Chan et al., 2024](#bib.bib8); [Hammond et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)).
These systems are therefore not only engineering artifacts: their
behaviour in interactive settings is also an object of empirical study,
as emphasized by the emerging field of machine behaviour ([Rahwan
et al., 2019](#bib.bib43)). In many such settings, avoiding collective
loss requires agents to incur individual costs for the benefit of the
group ([Santos and Pacheco, 2011](#bib.bib45); [Santos et al.,
2019](#bib.bib47); [Milinski et al., 2008](#bib.bib36)). This raises a
central question for multi-agent systems (MAS) research: do
language-model agents condition their cooperative behaviour on the
strategic environment, or do they rely on relatively fixed behavioural
defaults? From a system-design and machine-behaviour perspective, this
question has three components. First, is an agent’s cooperation
sensitive to the magnitude of collective risk? Second, does it respond
to the behaviour of other agents in the group? Third, under selection
pressures that retain or replicate higher-earning agents, which
behavioural tendencies are favoured?

Success in self-play, a common criterion in MAS benchmarks ([Piatti
et al., 2024](#bib.bib42); [Huang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)), does not
by itself answer these questions. When all positions in a group are
occupied by copies of the same model, an agent that conditions its
decisions on the stakes may generate the same observed pattern of play
as an agent that follows a fixed rule. A machine-behaviour approach
therefore calls for testing agents across systematically varied
environments, rather than inferring their decision policies from a
single setting. Success rates likewise obscure the extent to which a
group falls short of the attainable optimum: a group may achieve the
collective target while still dissipating a substantial share of its
endowment. Moreover, a prompt that specifies both a group target and a
fixed number of rounds may implicitly suggest an equal division of the
burden, causing agents that simply reproduce this split to appear
cooperative under conventional summary measures.

We study these questions using the collective-risk social dilemma game
(CRG), a game introduced to study collective decision making for climate
change mitigation ([Milinski et al., 2008](#bib.bib36)). Players
contribute into a shared pool over several rounds, and if the pool
misses a target, they all lose their savings with probability $`p`$
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")a).
For risk-neutral players, group welfare is maximised when no one
contributes if $`p<\tfrac{1}{2}`$, and when the group meets the target
exactly if $`p>\tfrac{1}{2}`$, for example through the _fair share_,
defined as an equal split of the target. The game also contains two
situations in which contributing cannot raise a player’s own payoff:
when there is no risk, and once the outcome is settled. Neither risk
attitude nor concern for the group can explain contribution in these
situations, so they separate a default from a response to the stakes.

We introduce a game-theoretic evaluation that separates cooperative
outcomes from incentive-sensitive behaviour by combining risk variation,
dominated-action tests, exact best responses to fixed partners and
selection in mixed populations, where each model is a strategy of an
empirical game ([Wellman, 2006](#bib.bib60); [Tuyls et al.,
2018](#bib.bib58)). We apply it to five production models, Claude Haiku
4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna, Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20, in
four designs
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")b),
each compared with a benchmark computed from the game. We use standard
equilibrium and finite-population tools, not a new solution concept. We
call near-flat contribution across risk levels under a fixed prompt a
_default_; the term describes behaviour, not internal reasoning.

We organise our findings around three dimensions. (1) *Stakes.* Most
models contribute roughly the fair share at every positive risk level,
even when there is no risk. Removing normative wording does not change
this pattern. Stating that only the agent’s own payoff matters reduces
most zero-risk contributions, but only GPT-5.6 Luna shifts from
contributing little at low risk to contributing the fair share at high
risk. Asked in separate calls, several models rank the two choices
correctly and change that ranking with the risk, but they keep
contributing the same amount
(Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1. Stakes: contribution does not scale with risk ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")).
(2) *Partners.* Beside scripted partners who make every contribution a
loss, no model plays the best response of contributing nothing, and two
keep contributing after the outcome is already settled. In a mixed game
(also referred to as a table or group), a single partner that stops
contributing in the last round often causes a table of exact fair-share
players to fail, while a table that overpays can absorb the shortfall.
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2. Partners: simple responses, unstable play ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")).
(3) *Selection.* Population size changes who is selected under
payoff-based imitation: a population of one table favours the smallest
contributor and lowers group success, whereas a larger randomly mixed
population favours the exact fair-share player at moderate and high risk
(Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3. Selection: population-size dependent ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")).

## 2. Related work

##### Collective risk and selection.

The collective-risk dilemma was introduced to study why groups fail to
avoid a shared disaster, and human groups reach the target mostly when
the risk is high ([Milinski et al., 2008](#bib.bib36)); inequality and
communication change how often they succeed ([Tavoni et al.,
2011](#bib.bib53)). Threshold public goods games have many
equilibria ([Palfrey and Rosenthal, 1984](#bib.bib39)), contributions
decline over the rounds of repeated public goods games ([Andreoni,
1988](#bib.bib4)), and the risk of collective failure can turn a
defection trap into a coordination problem ([Santos and Pacheco,
2011](#bib.bib45); [Merhej et al., 2021](#bib.bib35)). In evolutionary
simulations of the same game, constant fair sharers lose out to players
who hold back early and compensate later ([Abou Chakra and Traulsen,
2012](#bib.bib2)). Selection on payoff relative to a small group favours
whoever does better than its neighbours ([Schaffer, 1988](#bib.bib48)),
while selection between groups can favour cooperation ([Traulsen and
Nowak, 2006](#bib.bib56)). Finite-population analyses price the
incentives that sustain cooperation in this game ([Duong et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)) and show that maximising cooperation need not
maximise welfare ([Han et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)). This literature
predicts that behaviour should vary with risk, so the absence of such
variation is informative; we use a similar game theoretical approach to
analyse how different agent types perform when placed together in mixed
groups/tables.

##### Language models as strategic players.

Language models have been studied as economic subjects ([Filippas
et al., 2024](#bib.bib16); [Chen et al., 2023](#bib.bib9); [Mei et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34)) and in repeated and multi-player games ([Fan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13); [Akata et al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Fontana et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17); [Pal et al., 2026](#bib.bib38); [Kempinski et al.,
2025](#bib.bib29); [Pham et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)). Models tend to be
more cooperative than people ([Mei et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)), and an
even split can reflect a focal point rather than fairness ([Dodivers and
Rafai, 2025](#bib.bib11)). The weight of framing relative to game
structure differs by model ([Lorè and Heydari, 2024](#bib.bib33)), and
models can hold a correct belief or plan and act against it ([Fan
et al., 2024](#bib.bib13); [Schmied et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)). FAIRGAME
runs language-model agents through standard games and finds outcomes
that depend on model, language and persona ([Buscemi et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib6)); later work extends it to a payoff-scaled prisoner’s
dilemma and a multi-player public goods game ([Huynh et al.,
2025](#bib.bib27)).

##### Language models in social dilemmas.

Societies of agents over-use a common resource ([Piatti et al.,
2024](#bib.bib42)), contributions to a public good can stay the same
even when it returns nothing ([Huang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)), and
reasoning models free-ride ([Guzman Piedrahita et al.,
2025](#bib.bib19)). Agents stand in for people in a collective-risk
game ([Slumbers et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)) and for developers,
regulators and users in an evolutionary model of AI regulation ([Buscemi
et al., 2025a](#bib.bib5)). A study of 35 open models includes a
collective-risk game at one risk level and observes their decisions far
from the group optimum ([Kumar et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)). In a binary
collective-risk game with strategies written by three models,
payoff-biased imitation drives larger groups to selfish, low-welfare
outcomes ([Willis et al., 2026](#bib.bib61)), and prompts that evolve
under individual selection collapse into defection while group selection
keeps them prosocial ([Celiktemel et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)). A recent
study audits rule recall, state tracking and payoff calculation before
interpreting play in multi-player races to develop AI ([Pham et al.,
2026](#bib.bib41)). It compares play with an evolutionary benchmark and
human data, and finds that correct recall can coexist with weak
expected-payoff calculation. We differ in three ways: we vary the risk
from zero to certainty and add two tests in which contributing cannot
raise the contributor’s cash; we compute the exact best response to
fixed partners; and we select among deployed models with payoffs from
their real mixed tables, finding that at moderate and high risk the
favoured model changes once the population exceeds one table.

##### Multiagent evaluation.

Multiagent reinforcement learning studies cooperation in sequential
social dilemmas ([Leibo et al., 2017](#bib.bib32); [Hughes et al.,
2018](#bib.bib26)), and ad hoc teamwork and cooperative AI ask for
agents that work with partners unlike themselves ([Stone et al.,
2010](#bib.bib52); [Dafoe et al., 2020](#bib.bib10); [Leibo et al.,
2021](#bib.bib31)). Empirical game-theoretic analysis treats trained
agents as strategies of a meta-game ([Wellman, 2006](#bib.bib60); [Tuyls
et al., 2018](#bib.bib58)), $`\alpha`$-Rank ranks them by evolutionary
dynamics ([Omidshafiei et al., 2019](#bib.bib37)), and evolutionary game
theory is used to study emergent behaviour in multiagent systems ([Han,
2022](#bib.bib21)). Language models show distinct strategic fingerprints
in tournaments with classic strategies ([Payne and Alloui-Cros,
2025](#bib.bib40)), their cooperation evolves differently across base
models ([Vallinder and Hughes, 2025](#bib.bib59)), and cooperation
sustained by repetition weakens when co-players differ ([Tewolde et al.,
2026](#bib.bib55)). In the collective-risk game, people who delegate to
artificial agents succeed more often ([Fernández Domingos et al.,
2022](#bib.bib15)), and in theory adaptive players compensate for the
effort of artificial agents ([Terrucha et al., 2024](#bib.bib54)). We
add a multiplayer dilemma with exact best responses to fixed partners,
and measure this compensation in real models.

## 3. Game and evaluation design

### 3.1. Collective-risk game and benchmarks

Six players each start with $`e=40`$ units, and the game lasts $`R=10`$
rounds. In each round every player privately chooses a contribution
$`a_{i}^{t}\in\{0,2,4\}`$ to a shared _pool_, and after each round all
players see what every seat has contributed. Let
$`x_{i}=\sum_{t}a_{i}^{t}`$ be a player’s total and $`X=\sum_{i}x_{i}`$
the pool. The target is $`T=120`$. A player keeps what it did not
contribute, but if the pool misses the target, a catastrophe destroys
all savings with probability $`p`$
(Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.1. Collective-risk game and benchmarks ‣ 3. Game and evaluation design ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")
lists the terms used):

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (1) |     |

````math
u_{i}\;=\;\begin{cases}e-x_{i}&\text{if }X\geq T,\\
0\text{ with probability }p,\text{ else }e-x_{i}&\text{if }X<T.\end{cases}
``` |  |

|  Term |  Meaning |
|----|----|
|  Seat; table |  One player position; the six seats of one game |
|  Fair share |  The target split equally: 20 per seat, or 2 per round |
|  Exact split |  Contributing 2 in every one of the ten rounds |
|  Pivot $`p^{*}=\tfrac{1}{2}`$ |  Risk above which reaching the target is optimal |
|  $`\mathrm{opt}(p)`$ |  Welfare-optimal expected payoff per seat |
|  Dominated |  Lowers the seat’s own payoff whatever others do |
|  Settled |  Further contributions cannot affect the outcome: the pool has already reached $`T`$ or can no longer do so. |

Table 1. Symbols and terms.

###### Proposition 3.1.

Consider the expected-payoff formulation of the game in which each
player picks a total $`x_{i}\in\{0,2,\dots,40\}`$, and let $`p<1`$. A
profile is a Nash equilibrium if and only if $`x=0`$, or $`X=T`$ and
$`x_{i}\leq p\times e`$ for every $`i`$. A target-reaching equilibrium
exists if and only if $`p\geq p^{*}=T/(6e)=\tfrac{1}{2}`$. The
welfare-optimal outcome is $`x=0`$ for $`p<p^{*}`$ and $`X=T`$ for
$`p>p^{*}`$, and both are optimal at $`p=p^{*}`$, so the welfare-optimal
mean payoff per player is $`\mathrm{opt}(p)=\max\{(1-p)e,\;e-T/6\}`$;
this welfare statement also holds at $`p=1`$.

*Proof sketch.* If $`X<T`$, any player with $`x_{i}>0`$ gains by
contributing nothing, so the only equilibrium that misses the target is
$`x=0`$. If $`X>T`$, then $`X\geq T+2`$ because totals are even, so a
player can contribute 2 less and still reach the target. If $`X=T`$, the
best deviation is to contribute nothing, worth $`(1-p)e`$ against
$`e-x_{i}`$, which gives $`x_{i}\leq pe`$; summing over players gives
$`p\geq T/(6e)`$, and conversely $`x_{i}=20`$ for every $`i`$ is an
equilibrium when $`p\geq\tfrac{1}{2}`$. Welfare is $`6e-X`$ when the
target is met and $`(1-p)(6e-X)`$ otherwise, so it peaks at $`X=T`$ or
$`X=0`$. The ten-round game, in which strategies can react to the
history, has the same totals in pure-strategy Nash equilibrium (see
Supplementary Material, Proposition S1, Section S2.3, for the full
proof). We claim nothing about subgame perfection, and
$`\mathrm{opt}(p)`$ is a welfare bound that does not depend on the
equilibrium concept. $`\square`$

Proposition [3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 3.1. ‣ 3.1. Collective-risk game and benchmarks ‣ 3. Game and evaluation design ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")
gives a *symmetric welfare benchmark*: contribute nothing below the
pivot and, above it, contribute the fair share needed to reach the
threshold. Asymmetric allocations that reach exactly $`T`$ achieve the
same group welfare. This benchmark is neither a unique equilibrium nor a
prediction of individual play: contributing nothing is also an
equilibrium at every risk level. Moreover, contributing a positive
amount is strictly dominated in own expected payoff when $`p=0`$ and,
for $`p<1`$, once the outcome is settled. These comparisons require
neither risk neutrality nor beliefs about partners.

###### Proposition 3.2.

For two seats $`i`$ and $`j`$ in the same game, the difference in their
expected payoffs over the lottery is $`c\,(x_{j}-x_{i})`$, with $`c=1`$
if the target was reached and $`c=1-p`$ otherwise.

Both seats face the same pool and the same lottery, so only their own
contributions differ. Hence, for $`p<1`$, whoever contributes less at a
table earns more, whatever the group achieves.

### 3.2. Models, protocol and measures

We test the five models of
Section [1](#S1 "1. Introduction ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma"):
one production model per provider, chosen to reflect the kind of model
plausibly used for deploying many agents at scale. Qwen3-235B is the
instruction-tuned build without a thinking mode, and Grok 4.20 is its
non-reasoning variant.

##### Prompts.

In each round a seat receives a prompt with the rules, the full history
of every seat’s contributions, and a request for making a contribution;
the Supplementary Material provides every prompt word for word
(Section S4). The base prompt calls the pool a “climate account”, says
that the group “must” reach the target, calls the loss a “disaster” and
names the game as a collective-risk social dilemma. It never states the
player’s goal, and it hints at the equal split twice. The prompt
controls in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3. Evaluation designs ‣ 3. Game and evaluation design ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")
change exactly these parts.

##### Protocol.

The running pool is not shown, so any seat that needs it must compute it
from the history. The prompt is in English and no seat is assigned a
persona. We parse the move from the reply’s final CONTRIBUTION: line.

##### Measures.

We report *expected* payoffs, the average a seat would earn if the
catastrophe lottery were run many times on the same contribution, and
the share of $`\mathrm{opt}(p)`$ that is lost.
Proposition [3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 3.1. ‣ 3.1. Collective-risk game and benchmarks ‣ 3. Game and evaluation design ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma"),
$`\mathrm{opt}(p)`$ and the selection fitness below all use expected
payoffs, which remove the lottery noise without bias; agents never see
the draw, and the Supplementary Material lists the realised draws
(Table S6). The unit of analysis is the game, since seats in one game
share a history and a lottery. Intervals are 95% percentile bootstraps
over games and tests are permutation tests over games; Supplementary
Material gives every value behind the statements below
(Sections S6–S12).

##### Self-play and prompt controls.

Six copies of one model play the base prompt at 11 risk levels
$`p=0,0.1,\dots,1`$, and again under prompt and decoding controls at
fewer risk levels. *No hint* deletes the two equal-split phrases.
*Neutral wording* replaces the normative and naming phrases with plain
ones, such as “a shared account”. *Goal stated* keeps the neutral
wording and adds an opening saying the player is in a paid experiment
where “the only thing that matters to you is your own final cash
payoff”. Two *rewordings* change the phrasing but keep every rule and
the hint, one condition sets the temperature to zero, and a *rerun*
repeats the base prompt with value questions.

##### Value questions.

In the rerun, separate calls whose answers never enter the game ask the
first seat at the start, middle and end of the game which of two
whole-game strategies gives the higher average payoff: contributing 2 in
every round and reaching the target, or contributing nothing and missing
it. The correct answer switches at $`p^{*}`$ (Supplementary Material,
Section S8).

##### Fixed partners.

One model plays with five scripted partners of the same type. They
always contribute 0, always contribute 2, always contribute 4, or copy
the group, which means contributing 2 in the first round and then the
allowed amount nearest to the average of what the other seats
contributed in the previous round. The model is not informed that its
partners are scripted, but their moves are included in the history. We
compute the *best response* to each type, i.e. the play that earns the
most, by enumerating every possible sequence of the model’s own ten
moves. With partners who always contribute 0, the target cannot be
reached; with partners who always contribute 4, the target is reached
without the model. The best response to both is to contribute nothing,
and once the history settles the outcome, contributing cannot improve
the outcome and only reduces payoff. Against fair-share partners, the
best response is to contribute nothing below $`p^{*}`$ and exactly the
fair share above it; partners who copy the group never deviate from 2,
thus resulting in the same best response.

##### Mixed tables.

To mix defaults at a table, we seat every pair of models together, with
one to five seats for the first model and the rest for the second, at
low, moderate and high risk. Each model’s seats form one block, fixed
within a cell and alternating across cells, so every model occupies
every seat equally often overall.

##### Selection among models.

We finally examine which model prevails under payoff-based retention or
imitation, and how this outcome depends on population size. We treat
each LLM as a strategy in an empirical game, i.e. using experimentally
estimated payoffs from model interactions to define the game ([Wellman,
2006](#bib.bib60); [Tuyls et al., 2018](#bib.bib58)). We write
$`\pi_{A}(k)`$ for the mean expected payoff of an $`A`$ seat at a table
with $`k`$ seats of $`A`$ and $`6-k`$ of $`B`$, from mixed tables for
$`1\leq k\leq 5`$ and from self-play (grid plus rerun) for $`k=6`$. A
population of $`N`$ agents consists of $`i`$ agents of type $`A`$ and
$`N-i`$ of type $`B`$, and each agent’s five tablemates are drawn at
random from the other $`N-1`$, as in e.g.  ([Santos and Pacheco,
2011](#bib.bib45); [Han et al., 2017](#bib.bib23); [Santos et al.,
2020](#bib.bib46)). The fitness of each type is its payoff averaged over
the tables/interactions it participates in:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (2) |  | $`\displaystyle f_{A}(i)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{6}\mathcal{H}(k-1;\,N-1,\,i-1)\;\pi_{A}(k),`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle f_{B}(i)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{5}\mathcal{H}(k;\,N-1,\,i)\;\pi_{B}(6-k),`$ |  |  |

where $`\mathcal{H}(k;\,N-1,\,m)`$ is the hypergeometric chance that
$`k`$ of five tablemates come from the $`m`$ other agents of type $`A`$.
Under Fermi imitation ([Traulsen et al., 2006](#bib.bib57); [Han et al.,
2017](#bib.bib23)), a randomly selected agent copies a random other
agent’s strategy with probability $`1/(1+e^{-\beta\Delta f})`$, where
$`\Delta f`$ is the other’s fitness minus its own and $`\beta=1`$ per
payoff unit. We assume rare mutation ([Fudenberg and Imhof,
2006](#bib.bib18); [Han et al., 2017](#bib.bib23)), which has been shown
to provide a strong approximation beyond the strict rare-mutation
regime ([Han et al., 2012](#bib.bib24); [Rand et al., 2013](#bib.bib44);
[Zisis et al., 2015](#bib.bib62); [Sigmund et al., 2010](#bib.bib50)):
each newcomer either fixes or disappears before the next arrives.
Pairwise fixation probabilities then define a Markov chain over the five
homogeneous model populations. Its *stationary mass* gives each model’s
long-run weight, and EGTtools ([Fernández Domingos et al.,
2023](#bib.bib14)) was used to reproduce the fixation probabilities and
stationary distributions of
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.3. Selection: population-size dependent ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma").
With $`N=6`$ the whole population is one table; with $`N=30`$, fitness
averages over random, overlapping tables. Changing $`N`$ changes both
table compositions and fixation dynamics, not only whom an agent is
compared with. We evaluate this finite-population process, not a
scoring-only intervention.

## 4. Results

### 4.1. Stakes: contribution does not scale with risk

#### 4.1.1. Self-play across risk

Four of the five models contribute approximately the fair share at every
positive risk level
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Selection among models. ‣ 3.3. Evaluation designs ‣ 3. Game and evaluation design ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")a).
Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna and Qwen3-235B
contribute almost the same amount below the pivot as above it (a step of
at most 0.2 units), whereas the symmetric welfare benchmark jumps from
contributing nothing to the fair share. In the original experiment with
the same rules, human groups reached the target in half of their games
at $`p=0.9`$ and in none at $`p=0.1`$  ([Milinski et al.,
2008](#bib.bib36)). Strong risk aversion could explain a flat
contribution profile above zero risk: because catastrophe leaves players
with nothing, a player with relative risk aversion near one may prefer a
certain fair-share contribution even at low risk. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite
provides the clearest example. Almost every seat plays the exact split
at every risk level
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Three ways to lose. ‣ 4.1.1. Self-play across risk ‣ 4.1. Stakes: contribution does not scale with risk ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")),
so this near-fixed rule attains the welfare optimum above the pivot but
not below it.

##### Paying when there is nothing to protect.

Four of the five models also contribute at $`p=0`$, about as much as at
any other risk level
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Three ways to lose. ‣ 4.1.1. Self-play across risk ‣ 4.1. Stakes: contribution does not scale with risk ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma"),
first column). Contributing there only lowers a player’s own payoff and
helps nobody, so neither risk attitude nor concern for others’ payoff
can explain this contribution. GPT-5.6 Luna is the exception. It
contributes nothing at $`p=0`$ and its usual share at every positive
risk, so it distinguishes zero risk from positive risk but does not
respond to how high the risk is.

##### Three ways to lose.

The models lose payoff in three different ways
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Three ways to lose. ‣ 4.1.1. Self-play across risk ‣ 4.1. Stakes: contribution does not scale with risk ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma"),
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Selection among models. ‣ 3.3. Evaluation designs ‣ 3. Game and evaluation design ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")c).
Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.6 Luna lose mostly at
low risk, where they contribute for a target that is not worth reaching.
Grok 4.20 overpays: its groups reach the target well before the last
round, and its seats keep contributing into a pool that is already safe.
Qwen3-235B fails at the end. It contributes 2 round after round, then
almost all of its seats contribute nothing in the last round
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Selection among models. ‣ 3.3. Evaluation designs ‣ 3. Game and evaluation design ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")b).
Its groups therefore almost never reach the target, although every one
of them would have succeeded had each seat simply repeated its previous
move. Success rates hide all of this. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Grok
4.20 both reach the target in every game, yet Grok 4.20 keeps far less
of its endowment . For $`0.5<p<1`$, Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 groups earn
less in most games than in the worst equilibrium of
Proposition [3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 3.1. ‣ 3.1. Collective-risk game and benchmarks ‣ 3. Game and evaluation design ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma"),
in which nobody contributes. In the last round, Qwen3-235B skips as
often when its contribution is needed as when the others’ fair shares
already suffice, so its skip follows the round number rather than the
pool; human contributions similarly decline over the rounds of repeated
public goods games ([Andreoni, 1988](#bib.bib4)). GPT-5.6 Luna skips
mostly when its contribution is not needed, and Claude Haiku 4.5 stops
mostly once the target is reached (Supplementary Material,
Section S12.1).

#### 4.1.2. Prompt and objective controls

##### The default effect persists under rewording, but the resulting failures do not.

Deleting the two equal-split phrases does not reduce any model’s
contribution
(Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Stating the goal, not removing norms, reduces zero-risk contributions. ‣ 4.1.2. Prompt and objective controls ‣ 4.1. Stakes: contribution does not scale with risk ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")),
indicating that the hint was not the source of contributions. Instead,
it bounded contributions for some models while enabling others
contribute exactly 2 per round. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite still plays the
exact split, which it can rebuild from the target, while GPT-5.6 Luna
keeps its average contribution but plays the exact split less often.
Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 contribute more without the hint; Qwen3-235B
now reaches the target, but it earns less because it overpays. The two
rewordings and a temperature of zero leave the contributions of the four
models other than Grok 4.20 almost unchanged. The failures are less
stable than the averages. Under the second rewording Grok 4.20
contributes about the fair share, and how often Qwen3-235B drops out in
the last round varies widely across prompts. Grok 4.20’s rise with risk
on the grid recurs at temperature zero but not in the rerun or the
second rewording, so we do not interpret it as a stable response.

##### Stating the goal, not removing norms, reduces zero-risk contributions.

The normative words are not the main source of the default. With neutral
wording, most models still contribute at $`p=0`$. At low and high risk
the four models other than Grok 4.20 contribute on average what they
contributed before, while Grok 4.20 contributes even more and GPT-5.6
Luna plays the exact split less often
(Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Stating the goal, not removing norms, reduces zero-risk contributions. ‣ 4.1.2. Prompt and objective controls ‣ 4.1. Stakes: contribution does not scale with risk ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")).
Adding the stated goal is what changes play at zero risk: Claude Haiku
4.5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite then contribute almost nothing, Qwen3-235B
contributes far less than before, and Grok 4.20 barely changes. The goal
still does not make most models weigh the risk. Claude Haiku 4.5 and
Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite now separate zero from positive risk but already
contribute the fair share at low risk, a pattern that strong risk
aversion could also produce. Only GPT-5.6 Luna changes from contributing
little at low risk to the fair share at high risk. Qwen3-235B
contributes slightly more at high than at low risk but less than before
at every risk level, and never reaches the target; Grok 4.20 still
overpays.

For most models, behaviour at zero risk changes depending on whether the
prompt clearly states the goal. When risk above zero, models tend to
contribute similar amounts across risk levels, and this pattern remains
under different wordings. The partner and selection results below use
the base prompt.

##### Correct answers, unchanged play.

Asked the value question in separate calls, Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5
Flash-Lite and GPT-5.6 Luna mostly answer correctly and change their
answer with the risk, yet the questioned seats contribute almost the

### 4.2. Partners: simple responses, unstable play

#### 4.2.1. Fixed partners

##### No model keeps all its endowment when that is the best response.

The model contributes nothing only when paired with partners who always
contribute either 0 or 4. Of the 1,000 scripted games, only 186 are
played exactly optimally, and in every case the optimum is the fair
share. In other words, the models reach the optimum only when it
coincides with the default contribution. Since an agent unaware that its
partners are scripted may reasonably contribute early to learn their
behaviour, the cleaner evidence comes from contributions made after the
outcome has settled
(Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Partners matter, risk does not. ‣ 4.2.1. Fixed partners ‣ 4.2. Partners: simple responses, unstable play ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")).

##### Partners matter, risk does not.

We split the variation in what a model contributes across games with
three fixed-amount partners into partner, risk and interaction parts.
For Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna and
Qwen3-235B, partner type explains most of the variation (69% to 92%),
while risk and its interaction with partner type explain at most 5%. For
an exact best responder in the same games, risk would explain half of
the variation. Beside fair-share partners, where the best response jumps
from nothing to the fair share at $`p^{*}`$, no model’s total
contribution changes by more than 2 between the low- and high-risk
conditions. Grok 4.20’s total follows neither pattern: it is driven
largely by its opening move, which is its largest contribution in around
half of its games.

##### An exact fair-share target leaves no margin for error.

One partner who stops contributing in the final round can cause a table
of fair-share players to fail
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2.2. Mixed tables ‣ 4.2. Partners: simple responses, unstable play ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")a).
When the pool is one round of fair shares short before the last round,
Qwen3-235B contributes nothing in 92% of cases and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite
in 0.1%. Five Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite seats with one Qwen3-235B seat
succeed in only 11 of 30 games, although Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite alone
never misses in our sample; tables with GPT-5.6 Luna also succeed less
often. Tables of Claude Haiku 4.5 or Grok 4.20, which contribute more,
absorb the same partner. Replacing *all* Qwen3-235B last-round zeros by
2, holding other recorded actions fixed, would rescue 92% of the 323
failed mixed tables containing it. This is an arithmetic counterfactual,
not a tested intervention. Conversely, one Grok 4.20 seat helps five
Qwen3-235B seats succeed in most games, contributing for the rescue.
These comparisons depend on the base prompt and the fixed seat blocks.

##### Agents free-ride on generous partners.

Most agents contribute less as the number of Grok 4.20 seats increases
from one to five
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2.2. Mixed tables ‣ 4.2. Partners: simple responses, unstable play ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")b).
Alongside five Grok 4.20 seats, GPT-5.6 Luna contributes 10.5 units, and
Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Claude Haiku 4.5 also fall below the fair
share; these two stop once the pool reaches the target, while GPT-5.6
Luna contributes less from the start. Qwen3-235B does not show this
decline. Within a cell, Grok 4.20’s higher opening contributions are
associated with lower total contributions by Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and
Claude Haiku 4.5, mainly through earlier target completion. GPT-5.6 Luna
also contributes less while the pool is short, consistent with the
compensation predicted alongside artificial agents ([Terrucha et al.,
2024](#bib.bib54)). Openings are not randomised and partners can react
to a deviation, so this association identifies neither a causal response
nor an exact best response.

### 4.3. Selection: population-size dependent

In a one-table population, selection favours the model that contributes
least.
Proposition [3.2](#S3.Thmtheorem2 "Proposition 3.2. ‣ 3.1. Collective-risk game and benchmarks ‣ 3. Game and evaluation design ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")
explains why: within a game, contributing less leads to a higher
individual payoff relative to the other players. In our data, Qwen3-235B
contributes least relative to its tablemates, so selection gives it the
greatest weight
(Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.3. Selection: population-size dependent ‣ 4. Results ‣ Cooperation Without Adaptation: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma")a).
But this individual advantage comes at a cost to the group: tables reach
the target less often when models are selected this way than when a
model is chosen uniformly at random. At $`p=0.9`$, for example, the
target is reached in 47% of selected games, compared with 76% of
randomly selected games. This illustrates the known result that
selection based on payoffs relative to a small group favours whoever
does better than its neighbours, even when this comes at a cost to the
group ([Schaffer, 1988](#bib.bib48)). Here, we observe this dynamic in
games played by deployed language models.

In larger populations, selection favours Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite at

a candidate is removed (Supplementary Material, Sections S11.4
and S11.6).

## 5. Conclusion and discussion

Our results have shown that cooperative-looking self-play is not
sufficient evidence that an agent responds appropriately to incentives
or adapts to its partners. Across the five models, contributions often
remain close to the fair share as risk changes, including when
contributing is payoff-dominated; some models also contribute after
their actions can no longer affect the outcome. In mixed tables, one
late shortfall can make an exact fair-share target fail. Selection
outcomes depend on population structure: in a one-table population,
selection can favour the lowest contributor at the group’s expense,
while larger randomly mixed populations favour the fair-share model at
moderate and high risk.

These findings matter for how we understand machine behaviour. A
cooperative action need not reflect a stable preference for cooperation
or an understanding of its consequences: it may instead be a default
response to the prompt or a learned focal-point strategy. This empirical
approach to auditing agent behaviour—treating models as subjects for
observation in varied, interactive environments—aligns with the call to
systematically study machine behaviour ([Rahwan et al.,
2019](#bib.bib43)). Conversely, correctly identifying the
payoff-maximising action when asked does not ensure that a model will
choose it in play. We therefore distinguish what models can state from
how they behave, and caution against treating self-play success as
evidence of strategic competence, social preference, or reliable
adaptation. Evaluations should vary incentives and partner behaviour,
test mixed populations, and compare individual payoffs with group
welfare. They should also report the welfare benchmark, identify
dominated actions, and measure contributions after the outcome is
settled. This can reveal wasted resources and cases where apparent
cooperation does not improve welfare ([Han et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)).

The mechanisms behind these behaviours remain uncertain: our data show
choices, not reasoning. One possibility is that an equal split acts as a
focal point that models reconstruct even without an explicit
hint ([Dodivers and Rafai, 2025](#bib.bib11)). Another is that questions
prompt calculation while requests to act elicit a behavioural
default ([Fan et al., 2024](#bib.bib13); [Schmied et al.,
2026](#bib.bib49)). Post-training may also encourage models to
contribute their share unless instructed otherwise, consistent with
findings that models are more cooperative than people ([Mei et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34)) and that cooperation increases with the stakes in
repeated prisoner’s dilemmas, contrary to evolutionary
predictions ([Huynh et al., 2026](#bib.bib28)). Stronger reasoning is
not necessarily a remedy: reasoning models can free-ride more in
public-goods games ([Guzman Piedrahita et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)). These
accounts are hypotheses, not explanations established by our
experiments.

Limitations, scope, and reproducibility. The goal sentence is tested in
only one phrasing and in self-play; without a group-payoff goal, we
cannot distinguish the effect of making the objective explicit from the
effect of the instruction’s direction. Because the running pool is not
displayed, arithmetic errors may explain some contributions made after
the group has already met its target. The value questions also omit
$`p=0`$.

We test five models, one from each provider, in English and in a single
game. Our claims therefore concern this panel and setting, not language
models in general. Ten games per condition limit statistical power, and
a zero-width bootstrap interval for a constant sample should not be
interpreted as evidence of deterministic behaviour. Our prompt
rewordings cover only a small portion of the prompt space; welfare
losses assume risk neutrality. The scripted, mixed, and selection
designs use the base prompt. Fixed seat blocks confound player
composition with position, and the selection analysis assumes rare
entry.
````

# StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games

Zixian Gao    Penghao Luo    Xiangcheng Cao    Lixin Xiu

###### Abstract

Streaming video LLMs can now commentate live gameplay, but they are
trained and tested on the same games. We study cross-game streaming
commentary, in which a model trained on one game commentates games it
has never seen, and find that current commentators transfer their timing
but not their knowledge of the game world. We propose StreamDuo, which
pairs a trainable commentator with a frozen research agent that reads
the wiki of the game being played. When the commentator sees something
it cannot name, it sends the current frames, and the research agent
returns a few wiki entries two seconds later while the stream continues.
We train only the commentator, on a single game, in two stages.
Information-gain labels teach it to ask only where an answer would help
it say what the human caster said, and on-policy self-distillation from
a copy of itself that sees the ideal answer teaches it to use noisy
answers. Trained on Baldur’s Gate 3 and tested on ten unseen games,
StreamDuo raises entity recall from 0.082 to 0.116 over Proact-VL
trained on the same data, cuts source-game leakage from 9.9% to 1.0%,
and achieves the best timing and the highest judged liveliness and
overall quality among streaming models.

## 1 Introduction

Live game streaming has become one of the largest forms of online video,
and commentary is what turns raw gameplay into a show. A good caster
reacts the moment something happens and tells the audience who just
appeared, what item was picked up and where the fight is taking place.
Automating this requires a video model that watches an unbounded stream,
decides every second whether to speak, and says something both timely
and correct about the game world. Video LLMs \[[21](#bib.bib1),
[19](#bib.bib2), [40](#bib.bib3), [3](#bib.bib4)\] understand short
clips well, and streaming video LLMs \[[6](#bib.bib5), [7](#bib.bib20),
[43](#bib.bib10), [52](#bib.bib13), [29](#bib.bib6), [46](#bib.bib21),
[42](#bib.bib8)\] decide frame by frame whether to respond. Automatic
commentary was first studied for sports and esports \[[26](#bib.bib24),
[31](#bib.bib26), [37](#bib.bib28), [51](#bib.bib29)\], where the teams
and rules are known in advance. Proact-VL \[[47](#bib.bib22)\] recently
brought streaming video LLMs to game commentary and reports strong
timing and quality on a multi-game benchmark.

These systems are trained and evaluated on the same games. In practice a
commentator constantly meets titles, characters and items it has never
seen, and the entities that make commentary informative are exactly the
ones that change from one game to the next. We therefore study
cross-game streaming commentary, in which a model is trained on a single
game and commentates games that never appear in training. Under this
setting the standard recipe breaks down in a revealing way (Fig.
[1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")).
Retrained on Baldur’s Gate 3 alone, a Proact-VL commentator rarely names
what is on screen in ten unseen games, and when it does, it often
reaches for its training game, for example calling a character in Tears
of the Kingdom “Karlach”. Timing transfers across games, but knowledge
does not.

A natural remedy is to give the model the missing knowledge.
Retrieval-augmented generation \[[18](#bib.bib33), [2](#bib.bib35),
[15](#bib.bib34), [24](#bib.bib42)\], tool-using agents
\[[34](#bib.bib43), [50](#bib.bib44), [16](#bib.bib45)\] and multi-agent
systems \[[44](#bib.bib46)\] all supply external information to a model.
Live commentary, however, imposes constraints these methods do not
address. Knowledge retrieved before the stream starts is mostly
irrelevant to the current second, and in our experiments a game-level
dossier barely changes what the commentator says. Retrieving at every
second floods the context. Text queries are a further bottleneck, since
the commentator would have to describe in words something it cannot
recognize. Most importantly, the commentator must decide when an answer
is worth asking for, which depends on what this particular model does
not know. Language models can partly tell what they do not know
\[[17](#bib.bib38), [41](#bib.bib37), [13](#bib.bib36)\], but streaming
video models have no such mechanism.

We propose StreamDuo, a two-agent streaming commentator. A commentator
agent watches the stream and talks. When it sees something it cannot
name, it asks a research agent by sending the current frames. The
research agent, a frozen vision-language model with access to the game’s
wiki, returns a few entries two seconds later while the stream
continues. Perception and language, which transfer across games, stay in
the commentator, and game-specific knowledge is looked up at test time
in whatever game is being played. We train only the commentator, and
only on the source game, in two stages. The first teaches it when to
ask, with ask labels placed where an answer would actually help the
model reproduce what the human caster said. The second teaches it how to
use noisy answers, through on-policy self-distillation
\[[54](#bib.bib51)\] from a privileged copy of itself that sees an ideal
answer containing the right entry. On ten unseen games, StreamDuo
reduces source-game leakage from 9.9% to 1.0% and raises entity recall
from 0.082 to 0.116 compared with Proact-VL trained on the same data,
while also improving timing and the commentary quality judged by an LLM
on all ten test games.

In summary, our contributions are as follows.

- •
  We formulate cross-game streaming commentary, together with a clean
  evaluation protocol and a source-game leakage metric, and show that
  current streaming commentators transfer their timing but not their
  knowledge of the game world.
- •
  We propose StreamDuo, a two-agent commentator that asks a frozen
  research agent with frames and receives its answers while the stream
  continues, trained with information-gain ask labels and on-policy
  self-distillation from a privileged copy of itself.
- •
  On ten unseen games, StreamDuo outperforms zero-shot streaming models,
  Proact-VL retrained on the same data, and the same models given the
  research agent’s knowledge without interaction.

## 2 Related Work

#### Streaming video LLMs and live commentary.

Video LLMs understand complete clips \[[21](#bib.bib1),
[20](#bib.bib16), [45](#bib.bib17), [19](#bib.bib2), [40](#bib.bib3),
[3](#bib.bib4)\], and streaming variants process frames as they arrive,
either by managing memory over long streams \[[36](#bib.bib11),
[10](#bib.bib12), [52](#bib.bib13), [30](#bib.bib14), [8](#bib.bib15),
[46](#bib.bib21)\] or by deciding when to respond without being prompted
\[[6](#bib.bib5), [43](#bib.bib10), [42](#bib.bib8), [29](#bib.bib6),
[39](#bib.bib7), [49](#bib.bib9)\], as measured by online benchmarks
\[[22](#bib.bib18), [27](#bib.bib19)\]. LiveCC \[[7](#bib.bib20)\] and
Proact-VL \[[47](#bib.bib22)\] bring this to live commentary. Earlier
commentary systems targeted a single domain such as chess
\[[14](#bib.bib23)\], soccer \[[26](#bib.bib24), [28](#bib.bib25),
[31](#bib.bib26)\], racing \[[12](#bib.bib27)\] or esports
\[[37](#bib.bib28), [51](#bib.bib29), [53](#bib.bib30)\], often with
structured game data. All of these models speak from what is in their
weights and context, and are evaluated on the domains they are trained
on. We test on games whose entities are never seen in training and let
the commentator obtain the missing knowledge while the stream runs.

#### Retrieval, tools and multiple agents.

Retrieval-augmented generation supplies external text
\[[18](#bib.bib33)\], and adaptive variants retrieve only when the model
is uncertain \[[15](#bib.bib34), [2](#bib.bib35), [13](#bib.bib36),
[41](#bib.bib37), [17](#bib.bib38)\]. Knowledge-based visual question
answering retrieves encyclopedic entries for an image
\[[25](#bib.bib39), [5](#bib.bib40), [48](#bib.bib41)\], and video RAG
retrieves auxiliary text for video understanding \[[24](#bib.bib42)\].
Tool-using agents call search while reasoning \[[34](#bib.bib43),
[50](#bib.bib44), [16](#bib.bib45)\], and multi-agent frameworks let
models with different roles cooperate \[[44](#bib.bib46)\]. In StreamDuo
the query is the frames themselves, the answer arrives after a delay
while the commentator keeps talking, and the decision to ask is learned
from how much an answer would change this particular model’s output.

#### Distillation with privileged information.

A teacher with access to information unavailable at test time can guide
a student \[[38](#bib.bib47), [23](#bib.bib48)\], and on-policy
distillation trains the student on its own samples \[[1](#bib.bib49),
[9](#bib.bib50)\] to avoid the mismatch between training on reference
sequences and generating at test time \[[4](#bib.bib31),
[33](#bib.bib32)\]. On-policy self-distillation \[[54](#bib.bib51)\]
uses the same model, conditioned on privileged information, as its own
teacher. We apply it to a two-agent setting in which the privileged
information is the answer the research agent should have returned, and
sharpen the teacher’s use of that answer with context-aware decoding
\[[35](#bib.bib52)\].

## 3 Method

Our goal is a commentator that keeps talking at the right moments in a
game it has never seen, and that says the right names when it does. We
first describe the streaming setting and why it fails across games (Sec.
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Streaming commentary across games ‣ 3 Method ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")).
We then present StreamDuo, which pairs the commentator with a research
agent (Sec.
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 StreamDuo ‣ 3 Method ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")),
and the two training stages that teach the commentator when to ask (Sec.
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Learning when to ask ‣ 3 Method ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games"))
and how to use what it is told (Sec.
[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Learning to use the answer ‣ 3 Method ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")).
Fig.
[2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Distillation with privileged information. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")
illustrates the whole pipeline.

### 3.1 Streaming commentary across games

A live stream arrives one second at a time. At second $`t`$ the
commentator receives the frames $`v_{t}`$ of that second and a short
history $`h_{t}`$ made of the co-commentator’s last line and its own
recent lines. Following Proact-VL \[[47](#bib.bib22)\], each second is
one turn of a video LLM $`\pi_{\theta}`$. The turn ends with a special
flag token whose hidden state $`z_{t}`$ feeds a response head, which
outputs the probability $`p_{t}=\sigma(w_{s}^{\top}z_{t})`$ that the
commentator should speak now. If it speaks, the language model generates
a short line $`y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{\leq t},v_{\leq t})`$,
and otherwise it stays silent. Both parts are trained on human
commentary with a language-modeling loss on the spoken lines and a
binary loss on the speak decisions,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{base}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{spk}}.
``` |  | (1) |

In cross-game commentary, $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is trained on a single source
game $`G_{s}`$ and evaluated on unseen games $`G_{t}\neq G_{s}`$. The
two parts of the model behave very differently under this shift. When to
speak depends on what happens on screen, such as a fight starting or a
menu opening, and transfers well. What to say about the entities on
screen depends on knowledge of the game world, and a model trained on
one game only knows that one. Faced with an unfamiliar character, it
either describes it vaguely or reaches for a name it learned from the
source game. To keep the model from tying names to what it memorized, we
rename the entities of the source game consistently and at random during
training, so that a name in the training data is evidence only within
its own clip. This removes the shortcut but cannot supply the names of a
new game. That knowledge has to come from somewhere else at test time.

### 3.2 StreamDuo

StreamDuo splits the job between two agents with complementary
strengths. The commentator agent $`\pi_{\theta}`$ watches every second
of the stream and decides when to talk, but only knows the game it was
trained on. The research agent $`\mathcal{R}`$ sees nothing of the
stream unless asked, but can read the wiki of whatever game is being
played. Commentary emerges from their exchange. The commentator notices
that it does not recognize something, the research agent identifies it,
and the commentator turns the answer into a line.

#### The commentator asks with frames.

A natural interface would be a text query, but a model that cannot
recognize a character also cannot describe it well enough to retrieve
it. The commentator therefore sends the frames themselves. A second head
on the same flag state estimates whether an answer is needed,

|     |                                  |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       q_{t}=\sigma(w_{a}^{\top}z_{t}),
       ```                               |     | (2) |

and the commentator sends $`v_{t}`$ to the research agent when $`q_{t}`$
exceeds a threshold.

#### The research agent looks and searches.

The research agent is a frozen vision-language model with a search tool
over the game’s wiki. Before the stream starts, it writes a short
dossier of the game, which the commentator receives as general
background. The dossier cannot say which of its entities is on screen at
a given second, so the real work happens when a question arrives. The
research agent looks at the frames it was sent, proposes a few search
keys from what it sees, including any names readable on screen, searches
the wiki, looks at the frames again to check the candidates, and returns
up to three entries, each with a name, a type and a one-line
description. We write the returned answer as $`A_{t}`$. Because the
research agent reads the frames rather than a description of them, it
can identify a character the commentator could never have described in a
text query.

#### Answers arrive while the stream continues.

Looking things up takes time, and the stream does not wait. The answer
to a question asked at second $`t`$ arrives $`\delta`$ seconds later and
is placed in the history $`h_{t+\delta}`$, where it stays available for
the following seconds. Meanwhile the commentator keeps deciding every
second whether to speak. This asynchrony is part of the problem the
commentator has to learn to live with. It must ask before it needs the
answer, and connect an answer to what it is seeing when the answer
arrives.

#### Speaking and stopping.

An answer often gives the commentator something to say, and a model that
has started an explanation tends to keep going after the moment passes.
We therefore use a higher bar for continuing than for starting. The
commentator starts speaking when $`p_{t}`$ exceeds $`\tau`$, and after a
run of spoken seconds continues only when $`p_{t}`$ exceeds a larger
threshold $`\tau_{c}`$. It can thus react immediately to new events and
stop once the event ends.

Only the commentator is trained, and only on the source game. The
research agent stays frozen and brings its knowledge from the wiki of
the game at hand, so the same pair can be pointed at any game for which
a wiki exists. The commentator has to learn two things that standard
training does not teach, namely when an answer is worth asking for and
how to turn a noisy answer into the right line.

### 3.3 Learning when to ask

The obvious supervision for the ask head is to ask shortly before the
human caster names an entity. This ignores the one thing that makes
asking useful, which is whether the model already knows the answer. A
model that reliably recognizes the protagonist gains nothing from asking
about her, while a model that cannot tell two bosses apart gains a lot.
The label should depend on the model.

We define the value of asking through the model itself. Let $`y_{a}`$ be
the line the human says at second $`a`$ and let $`A^{\star}_{a}`$ be an
ideal answer that contains the entity named in $`y_{a}`$. A frozen copy
of the commentator, $`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$, scores this line twice, once
with the answer in its history and once without it. The information gain

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{a}=\log\pi_{\theta_{0}}\!\left(y_{a}\mid h_{\leq a},A^{\star}_{a},v_{\leq a}\right)-\log\pi_{\theta_{0}}\!\left(y_{a}\mid h_{\leq a},v_{\leq a}\right)
``` |  | (3) |

measures how much the answer helps the model say what the caster said.
We label second $`a-\delta`$ as an ask moment when $`g_{a}>\gamma`$, so
that the answer would arrive in time. Lines the model can already
produce receive no label because their gain is small, and so do lines
that no answer could help with, such as tactical remarks. What remains
are the moments when the model does not know and an answer would fix it.

The ask head is trained with a binary loss on these labels together with
the base objective,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{spk}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{ask}}.
``` |  | (4) |

In the same stage the commentator learns to read answers. During
training, each answer placed in the history contains the entity the
caster names next together with distractor entries from the same game,
and some answers miss the entity altogether, mimicking what a real
research agent returns. Because entity names are randomly renamed in
each clip, the model cannot have memorized them and must take them from
the answer that it receives.

### 3.4 Learning to use the answer

Supervised training on answers teaches the format of an answer but not
how to use it. The answers the commentator receives at test time come
from a real research agent. They are often plausible but wrong, and the
right entry, when present, is mixed with others. A model fine-tuned to
copy entries from its history will repeat whatever arrives, and a model
that ignores its history will never say the new names. What we want is a
model that uses an entry when it matches the frames and otherwise does
not.

We obtain this behavior with on-policy self-distillation
\[[54](#bib.bib51)\]. The student is the commentator $`\pi_{\theta}`$,
which sees a noisy answer $`A`$ exactly as it would at test time. The
teacher is a frozen copy of the same model, $`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$, which
sees the ideal answer $`A^{\star}`$ for the same moment. The teacher is
not a larger model. It knows more only because it is told which entry is
right, and its behavior shows what using that entry looks like.

The effect of an answer on a fine-tuned model is often small, because
the model has learned to rely on the frames and its own memory. We make
the teacher’s use of the answer explicit by contrasting its predictions
with and without the answer \[[35](#bib.bib52)\],

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log p_{T}(\cdot)=\log\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big((1+\alpha)\,\ell_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid A^{\star})-\alpha\,\ell_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid\varnothing)\Big),
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\ell_{\theta_{0}}`$ denotes the teacher’s next-token logits
given the same frames and history and $`\alpha\geq 0`$ controls the
amplification. Tokens that become likely because of the answer, such as
the name of the entity on screen, are pushed up, and tokens the model
would say anyway are left unchanged.

Training is on-policy. For a moment right after an answer arrives, the
student samples its own lines
$`\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h,A,v)`$, and the teacher scores
each of their tokens. We minimize the reverse Kullback-Leibler
divergence over the student’s samples,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\Big[\sum_{k}\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\hat{y}_{<k},h,A,v)\,\big\|\,p_{T}(\cdot\mid\hat{y}_{<k})\big)\Big].
``` |  | (6) |

Sampling from the student matters here. The student is corrected on the
lines it would actually say, including the ones where it repeats a wrong
entry, rather than on human lines it would never produce. The reverse
divergence makes the student commit to what the teacher considers likely
instead of spreading its probability over every name in the answer. Only
a small set of low-rank adapter weights is updated in this stage, so the
speaking behavior learned before is preserved, and the teacher is simply
the model with the LoRA adapter switched off during its forward pass.

At test time neither the teacher nor the ideal answer is used. The
commentator watches the stream, asks with its frames when its ask head
fires, and speaks with whatever the research agent returns.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Setup

#### Data.

We use the game commentary benchmark of Proact-VL \[[47](#bib.bib22)\],
training on 10,286 Baldur’s Gate 3 clips and testing on 2,470 clips from
ten other games, from open-world adventures such as Tears of the Kingdom
to esports such as League of Legends. The original inputs leak game
knowledge through the caster persona, the earlier transcript and the
other caster’s lines. We therefore keep only the persona’s speaking
style, remove earlier transcripts and replace game-specific names in the
other caster’s lines with typed placeholders such as \[PLAYER\]. Game
knowledge can then only come from the wiki of 190,722 pages.

#### Metrics.

For timing we report TimeDiff and F1 of Proact-VL. For knowledge we
match wiki entities in the two commentaries. Ent-R is the share of the
human’s entities that the model also names, Ent-F1 adds precision, and
K-strict counts only lines that need game knowledge. These count the
first 126 words of each clip, twice the human average, so that talking
more cannot raise recall. Qwen3-30B \[[3](#bib.bib4)\] scores PAUC, CC,
LiveU and FinalQ of Proact-VL on all test clips.

#### Baselines.

We compare with streaming video LLMs that decide when to respond,
VideoLLM-online \[[6](#bib.bib5)\], LiveCC \[[7](#bib.bib20)\], Dispider
\[[29](#bib.bib6)\], MMDuet \[[43](#bib.bib10)\], StreamingVLM
\[[46](#bib.bib21)\], MMDuet2 \[[42](#bib.bib8)\] and Proact-VL
\[[47](#bib.bib22)\], each with its official code and the game title as
context. We test them zero-shot and after one epoch on the same Baldur’s
Gate 3 clips with the official recipe. Proact-VL has seen the test
games, so we retrain it from LiveCC-7B-Base. Dispider releases no
training code, and for MMDuet2 we rerun only the supervised stage. Each
trained baseline is also given the research agent’s dossier, to separate
the knowledge itself from the way in which StreamDuo obtains it during
the stream.

#### Implementation.

The commentator starts from LiveCC-7B-Base \[[7](#bib.bib20)\], as
Proact-VL does. Stage 1 fine-tunes all parameters for one epoch at a
learning rate of $`10^{-5}`$ with $`\gamma=1`$ nat. Stage 2 trains a
rank-32 LoRA \[[11](#bib.bib53)\] for 300 steps with $`\alpha=1`$. The
research agent is Qwen3-VL-8B \[[3](#bib.bib4)\] with BM25 search
\[[32](#bib.bib54)\] and returns up to three entries after $`\delta=2`$
s. At test time it speaks when $`p_{t}>0.27`$ and asks when
$`q_{t}>0.9`$. All runs use eight NVIDIA H100 GPUs for training and
evaluation.

### 4.2 Main results

Tab.
[1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Implementation. ‣ 4.1 Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")
shows that existing streaming models fail on unseen games in two ways.
Zero-shot, they rarely speak at the right moments. LiveCC names the most
entities among them but has a TimeDiff of 3.03 s, and the others either
stay silent or describe the scene. After training on Baldur’s Gate 3,
Proact-VL and StreamingVLM learn when to speak, but their entity recall
stays below that of zero-shot LiveCC, and VideoLLM-online speaks more
often without naming a single correct entity. Models that decide silence
through their language output, such as LiveCC and MMDuet2, collapse to a
few words per clip and mostly read the dialogue shown on screen. Timing
transfers across games, knowledge does not. Adding the research agent’s
game dossier to their context barely helps, for example raising the
K-strict of Proact-VL by only 0.003, since a dossier covers the whole
game but cannot tell which entity is on screen. StreamDuo is best on all
metrics except CC. Compared with Proact-VL, trained from the same base
on the same clips, it raises entity recall from 0.082 to 0.116 and
K-strict from 0.085 to 0.127, while responding faster and receiving
higher judge scores. It also names more entities than zero-shot LiveCC
without giving up timing. CC favors near-silent models in this
comparison, such as MMDuet2 with a few words per clip, and does not
reflect the quality of the commentary itself.

### 4.5 When does the commentator ask?

Figure 3: Entity naming around questions (orange) and random seconds
(blue). The answer arrives at 2 s.

Fig.
[3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.5 When does the commentator ask? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")
aligns the commentary of StreamDuo with its own questions on the test
games. Questions are rare, 264 over 2,470 clips. Before a question, the
commentator names an entity in only 1 to 4% of the seconds, below the 7%
of a random second, so it asks when it has no name to offer. When the
answer arrives two seconds later, the share jumps to 32%, 4.6 times that
of a random second. It stays above the random rate for five more
seconds, at 22% one and two seconds after the answer and 12% five
seconds after it, and returns to the base rate within about eight
seconds. The jump comes from the answer and not from the scene. With
asking disabled, the same seconds name an entity only 5% of the time.
The commentator thus uses an answer at once.

### 4.6 What Stage 2 needs

Figure 4: Stage 2 variants. Dashed line: Stage 1.

Fig.
[4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.6 What Stage 2 needs ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")
varies the loss and the teacher of Stage 2. Fine-tuning on the real
answers of the research agent gives the lowest TimeDiff and a higher
LiveU, but entity recall falls below that of Stage 1 (0.086 against
0.092). Without amplification ($`\alpha=0`$) the gain over Stage 1 is
small. The forward divergence spreads the student over all the tokens
the teacher allows, so the student names an entity wherever the
amplified teacher gives it some probability. It reaches the highest
Ent-R (0.131) and K-strict (0.145), but one in five of its entity
mentions is a name from Baldur’s Gate 3 (0.193, against 0.010 for the
full model), and its LiveU and FinalQ fall to 4.82 and 3.31, the lowest
of all variants. All three loss variants also lower FinalQ. Weaker
teachers keep the quality of the commentary but add less knowledge. A
teacher that only knows which received entries the human names, or one
that checks them on screen with a frozen VLM, reaches an Ent-R of 0.093
or 0.102. Only the full recipe, the reverse divergence with
amplification and the ideal answer, improves all knowledge and judge
metrics over Stage 1, raising Ent-R to 0.116 and K-strict to 0.127
without leaking names from the source game.

Figure 5: Examples on unseen games, with the frame at the second the

Fig.
[5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.8 Qualitative examples ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")
shows StreamDuo and Proact-VL at the same seconds of seven unseen games.
In the first five examples the commentator asks and, two seconds later,
names what the answer contains. In StarCraft II it names Byun’s Ghost
Academy, which the human caster also names, and the player GuMiho in
another match. In Elden Ring and Minecraft the entry is also on screen,
in the equipment menu, the chest and the skill slot of Thops’s Barrier,
so the research agent finds it from the frames although the commentator
cannot read it. The answers are not clean. Each holds up to three
entries, and the commentator mostly keeps the ones that match the
frames, Bloodhound’s Fang but not Bloodhound’s Finesse or Slash, and the
golden apple but not the enchanted one. It does not always filter well,
and in StarCraft II it also repeats the Warden Support Alpha, an entry
unrelated to the match. Proact-VL speaks at the same seconds but stays
vague, as in “Wait, this is a weapon”, names the wrong potion, or calls
an Elden Ring enemy group the Sharran, a faction of Baldur’s Gate 3. The
last three examples show what happens without a question. In Tears of
the Kingdom, Street Fighter 6 and Black Myth: Wukong the commentator
does not ask, since its ask score stays below the threshold. StreamDuo
describes the scene without a name, while Proact-VL fills in names from
its training game, addressing a character as Karlach, saying a fighter
has been playing Karlach, and calling a boss a Tiefling. Such names are
not rare. Over all test clips, names from the source game make up 9.9%
of the entity mentions of Proact-VL, 200 of its 2,011, and 1.0% of ours,
34 of 3,363, although StreamDuo names almost twice as many entities.
Without an answer, the commentator leaves a name out instead of
borrowing one from the game it was trained on.

## 5 Conclusion

We studied cross-game streaming commentary, in which a model trained on
one game commentates games it has never seen, and found that current
streaming commentators transfer their timing but not their knowledge. To
address this, StreamDuo pairs a trainable commentator with a frozen
research agent that it asks with its frames and that answers from the
game’s wiki while the stream continues. Information-gain labels teach
the commentator when an answer is worth asking for, and on-policy
self-distillation from a privileged copy of itself teaches it to use
noisy answers. Trained on Baldur’s Gate 3 alone, StreamDuo names more of
what is on screen in ten unseen games, rarely falls back on names from
its training game, and keeps the timing and quality of the strongest
streaming commentators, without any training on the ten games it is
tested on.

Table 5: Source-game leakage on all 2,470 test clips, without a word

that exist neither in the test game nor in its wiki. Leakage: share of
leaked names among all names said.

A commentator trained on one game can fill an entity it does not know
with a name it memorized from that game. We call this source-game
leakage and measure it as the share of Baldur’s Gate 3 names among all
entity names a model says in the unseen games, counting only names that
exist neither in the test game nor in its wiki. Tab.
[5](#S6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 6 Source-game leakage ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")
reports it for all models trained on Baldur’s Gate 3, on all 2,470 clips
and without a word cap.

The two models that speak the most entity names before StreamDuo leak
the most. 17.3% of the names said by StreamingVLM and 9.9% of those said
by Proact-VL come from Baldur’s Gate 3, such as Karlach in Street
Fighter 6 or Lae’zel in League of Legends. Leakage is worst in esports,
where the commentary is full of player and champion names the model has
never seen. In League of Legends, 54.6% of the names said by Proact-VL
come from Baldur’s Gate 3, against 7.8% for StreamDuo. The agent dossier
lowers the overall share only a little, to 14.7% and 6.3%, since it
lists the entities of the game but cannot tell which one is on screen.
The other baselines leak less because they name few entities at all,
with fewer than 900 test-game names over all 2,470 clips. A low share
then says little about their knowledge. StreamDuo names the most
entities of the test games, 3,329, and keeps leakage at 1.0%.

Each part of StreamDuo contributes to this. Run with the same inference
settings as StreamDuo, the recipe of Proact-VL leaks 5.8% of its names.
Random renaming with the dossier in context (Stage 0) lowers this to
2.1%, since a name from the source game is no longer reliable evidence
during training. Learning when to ask (Stage 1) lowers it further to
0.8%. With asking disabled at test time, the full model leaks 2.9%,
three times as much as with asking. When the commentator does not know
an entity, it asks the research agent instead of reaching for a name
from the source game. The loss of Stage 2 matters as well. With the
forward instead of the reverse divergence, leakage rises to 19.3%, the
highest of all models, because the student learns to name an entity
wherever the teacher gives it some probability (Sec.
[4.6](#S4.SS6 "4.6 What Stage 2 needs ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")).

A live commentator has to keep up with the stream, and an answer is only
useful if it arrives while the entity is still on screen. Tab.
[6](#S6.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 6.1 Efficiency ‣ 6 Source-game leakage ‣ StreamDuo: A Live Commentator and a Research Agent for Unseen Games")
measures both agents on one H100 GPU with one request at a time, as in a
live stream, using the same models, prompts and settings as in all other
experiments.

The research agent answers in 0.41 s on average and in at most 0.71 s
over 200 test moments, so every answer is ready before the 2 s at which
StreamDuo places it in the history. The delay of 2 s therefore leaves a
margin of more than a second, and a slower or larger agent would still
fit. Most of this time goes to proposing search keys from the frames
(0.32 s). Picking up to three entries from the retrieved candidates
takes 0.08 s, and the BM25 search over the wiki takes about 2 ms.
Building the search index is done once before the stream and takes 1.5
s.

The commentator needs 0.26 s of compute for each second of video, about
four times faster than real time, and at most 0.33 s on the slowest of
20 test clips. This includes decoding the frames, the speak and ask
heads and the generation of each line. All 20 clips are mostly spoken,
so the measurement covers the most expensive case.

The research agent runs in parallel and is called only when the
commentator asks. In one test run, the commentator asked 264 times over
the 26 hours of test video, about once every six minutes, and in 243 of
the 2,470 clips. The research agent is therefore idle most of the time,
and its cost adds little to that of the commentator. StreamDuo can
commentate a live stream on two GPUs, one for each agent.
````

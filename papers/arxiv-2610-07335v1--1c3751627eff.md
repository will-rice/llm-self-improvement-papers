# Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making

Heewon Park    Somin Im    Minhae Kwon

###### Abstract

Improving the reliability of large language model (LLM) agents in
long-horizon decision-making remains a key challenge. When deployed as
autonomous agents interacting with complex environments, early mistakes
can propagate through trajectories and cause cascading failures. Recent
approaches improve reliability by incorporating external critique or
deliberation, but invoking these mechanisms at every step substantially
increases token consumption and latency, limiting practical deployment.
We propose SAG (Self-improving Agent with Gated critique), a cost-aware
framework that formulates critique invocation as a step-wise decision
problem during long-horizon interaction. SAG introduces a lightweight,
training-free gating mechanism that estimates the utility of critique
using action-level ambiguity signals—global entropy and local top-2
margin—computed over admissible actions. From a decision-theoretic
perspective, this mechanism approximates the Value of Information (VoI)
of critique, enabling the agent to selectively allocate expensive
feedback only when its expected benefit justifies the cost. SAG further
incorporates online bootstrapped self-improvement, allowing the actor to
internalize critic-assisted behaviors and progressively reduce reliance
on critique. Across three long-horizon interactive benchmarks and
multiple backbone models, SAG substantially improves the
performance-cost trade-off compared with both no-critique and always-on
critique agents. On ALFWorld, SAG increases task success from $`24.6\%`$
to $`78.3\%`$ while maintaining a token budget comparable to ReAct,
yielding a $`3.1\times`$ improvement in normalized token efficiency.
Moreover, a $`7`$B actor with a lightweight $`3`$B critic achieves
performance comparable to a $`14`$B actor without critique, showing that
selective critique can recover most of the reliability benefits of
deliberation while dramatically reducing inference cost. Project page:
[https://h2w1.github.io/SAG/](https://h2w1.github.io/SAG/)

## 1 Introduction

As decision-making agents, LLMs are increasingly deployed to interact
with environments and make sequential decisions, often refining their
behavior through interaction experience [Yao et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib2);
[Wei et al. (2022)](#bib.bib4); [Yao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib5); [Liu et
al. (2023)](#bib.bib8); [Du et al. (2024)](#bib.bib12); [Razghandi et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib14); [Sun et al. (2023)](#bib.bib39); [Chen et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib40); [Im et al. (2026)](#bib.bib56). Despite recent
advances, long-horizon interaction remains challenging: early decision
errors can propagate across steps and lead to irreversible failures [Hao
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib33); [Erdogan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib32); [Pan et
al. (2024)](#bib.bib34); [Deng et al. (2025)](#bib.bib41); [Cemri et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib42); [Park and Kwon (2026)](#bib.bib57). One route to
improve reliability is to post-train agents through large-scale
environment rollouts, which can produce strong task-specific policies
but requires substantial training compute and infrastructure. A
complementary route, and the focus of this work, is to improve the
decision process at deployment time by augmenting the agent with
auxiliary feedback—e.g., self-reflection, external evaluation, or
reward-model based scoring—to assess candidate actions and provide
corrective guidance, often improving task success [Shinn et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib7); [Dong et al. (2025)](#bib.bib6); [Gou et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib3); [Xiang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib13); [Wang et al.
(2024b)](#bib.bib43); [Dou et al. (2024)](#bib.bib44). In this paper, we
refer to such auxiliary mechanisms as _critique_, and instantiate them
as a _critic_ module queried during interaction.

Critique, however, is computationally expensive: it typically requires
additional model inference and longer contexts, causing token usage,
latency, and monetary cost to increase disproportionately with
interaction length [Havrilla et al. (2024)](#bib.bib35); [Chen et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib36); [Wang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib37); [Liu et al.
(2025a)](#bib.bib38). This raises a central question for practical agent
deployment.

_When should an LLM agent request a critique to balance reliability and
token efficiency?_

![Refer to caption](2610.07335v1/Fig/updated_chart_alpha.png)

Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")
highlights this performance–cost trade-off with representative
instantiations (ReAct [Yao et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib2) and LAC [Dong et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib6)). While always-on critique can improve task
success, it incurs higher token usage and can reduce token efficiency,
especially in long-horizon settings where critique is repeatedly
invoked.

Moreover, critique is not free supervision: in realistic settings, a
critic is itself an imperfect model [Park et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib68);
[Park et al. (2023)](#bib.bib72) whose advice may or may not improve the
next action [Wang et al. (2024b)](#bib.bib43); [Bao et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib30). More broadly, this reflects a missing design
principle: many existing methods rely on fixed or heuristic critique
schedules, providing limited guidance on _when_ critique is warranted
given its cost, even as they improve _how_ critique is generated or
applied.

In this paper, we cast critique invocation as a _cost-aware decision_
rather than a fixed procedure, and introduce SAG (Self-improving Agent
with Gated critique). We view critique invocation through a VoI lens:
critique should be sought when its expected improvement outweighs its
computational cost. While exact VoI is intractable, SAG uses the actor’s
action-level uncertainty as a lightweight proxy for critique value,
combining global ambiguity (entropy) and local ambiguity (top-2 margin)
computed over admissible action candidates. These signals trigger
critique via a simple, bounded gating rule. Beyond selective
intervention, SAG is self-improving. During online interaction, SAG
stores successful trajectories and periodically fine-tunes the actor
with lightweight SFT, distilling effective behaviors—including
critic-assisted actions—into the policy. As the actor improves, the same
VoI-motivated gate naturally reduces critique usage over time, yielding
a self-evolving agent that becomes both more capable and more
token-efficient.
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")
shows the overview of the proposed framework.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions. 1) SAG:
cost-aware gated critique for LLM agents. We propose SAG, a framework
that formulates critic invocation as a step-wise decision problem during
long-horizon interaction. By selectively allocating critique only when
it is expected to improve decision quality, SAG enables agents to
balance reliability with token efficiency, directly addressing the
inference cost and latency bottlenecks of practical LLM agent
deployment. 2) Lightweight ambiguity-based gating. SAG introduces a
simple, training-free gating mechanism that combines global ambiguity
(normalized entropy) and local ambiguity (top-2 margin) over admissible
action candidates. Despite its simplicity, this mechanism provides an
effective and scalable strategy for adaptive critique allocation without
additional models or training. 3) A principled VoI perspective on
critique. We interpret critique invocation through a decision-theoretic
lens and show that actor-side ambiguity signals serve as practical
proxies for the expected utility of critique, providing a theoretically
grounded basis for cost-aware reasoning allocation in LLM agents. 4)
Critique-driven online self-improvement. SAG leverages selectively
refined trajectories as supervision signals for online bootstrapped
improvement. As critic-assisted behaviors are distilled into the actor,
the agent progressively improves decision quality while naturally
reducing reliance on critique over time. 5) Comprehensive empirical
validation of success–cost efficiency. Across three long-horizon
interactive environments, multiple backbone models, and extensive
ablations, scaling studies, and matched self-improvement controls, SAG
consistently improves the success–cost Pareto frontier over both
no-critique and always-on critique baselines. On ALFWorld, SAG increases
success from $`24.6\%`$ to $`78.3\%`$ while maintaining a token budget
comparable to ReAct, yielding a $`3.1\times`$ improvement in normalized
token efficiency. SAG further shows larger gains on harder tasks and
narrows the gap between smaller and larger models, with a 7B actor and
3B critic matching the performance of a 14B actor without critique.

## 2 Related Work

#### LLM agents in interactive environments

LLMs are increasingly used as agents that reason and act in interactive
environments. Existing work improves such agents along two broad
directions. The first improves an available agent during execution
through prompting, deliberation, feedback, or lightweight
adaptation [Yao et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib2); [Du et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib12); [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib7); [Madaan et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib23); [Park et al. (2026)](#bib.bib58); [Park et al.
(2025a)](#bib.bib65); [Im et al. (2025)](#bib.bib60); [Kim et al.
(2025d)](#bib.bib63); [Joe et al. (2024)](#bib.bib69). ReAct [Yao et al.
(2022b)](#bib.bib2) interleaves reasoning, actions, and observations for
multi-step decision making, and subsequent work improves robustness with
self-reflection [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib7); [Madaan et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib23), debate [Du et al. (2024)](#bib.bib12); [Liu et al.
(2025b)](#bib.bib20); [Hu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib21), confidence-based
aggregation [Razghandi et al. (2025)](#bib.bib14); [Taubenfeld et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib15), and trajectory reuse or bootstrapping [Sarukkai et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib18); [Yang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib17); [Guo et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib26). Inference under partial or imperfect observations
has also been studied [Wang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib55); [Kim et al.
(2025c)](#bib.bib64); [Kim et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib67); [Joe et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib69); [Park et al. (2025c)](#bib.bib61); [Park et al.
(2025d)](#bib.bib62); [Kim et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib59); [Kwon et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib66); [Wu et al. (2020)](#bib.bib74); [Kwon et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib75). The second improves the policy before
deployment [Eo et al. (2024)](#bib.bib70); [Im et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib56) through preference optimization, environment
rollouts, or post-training reinforcement learning (RL) [Rafailov et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib50); [Schulman et al. (2017)](#bib.bib49); [Feng et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib51); [Shao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib52), often investing
substantial task-specific compute before the agent is deployed. In this
paper, we target the former deployment-time regime: given an available
actor and a non-oracle critic, we ask when critique is worth its cost
during interaction.

#### Critique and cost-aware improvement

Explicit critique and feedback can correct agent behavior and support
learning from experience [Dong et al. (2025)](#bib.bib6); [Xiang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib13); [Lu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib16); [Yang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib17); [Fang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22); [Eom et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib73); [Eom et al. (2024)](#bib.bib71). Prior
critic-guided methods use continuous feedback, self-asking, or
trajectory-level evaluation to improve agent decisions [Dong et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib6); [Yang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib17); [Kim et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib28); [Xiang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib13), while
self-improvement methods train on successful sampled trajectories [Lan
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib54). However, these approaches often rely on
frequent critique, repeated sampling, or fixed schedules, incurring
overhead from extra model calls, longer contexts, or multiple candidate
evaluations [Bao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30); [Wang and Atanasova
(2025)](#bib.bib31); [Wang et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib24); [Elumar et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib25). Although adaptive-computation methods allocate
extra inference using confidence or uncertainty [Feng et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib53), critique in long-horizon agents is still often
invoked by fixed heuristics [Bo et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19); [Yuan and
Xie (2025)](#bib.bib27); [Kim et al. (2024)](#bib.bib28); [Ding et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib29). In contrast, our work frames critique invocation as
a cost-aware step-wise decision problem, selectively querying a
non-oracle critic using lightweight ambiguity signals that we later
justify as proxies for the VoI.

## 3 Gated Critic-guided Self-improving Agent

We propose SAG, an efficient LLM agent framework that selectively
invokes critique via a cost-sensitive gate and internalizes successful
critic-guided behavior through online learning.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

We consider an interactive environment in which an LLM-based actor
performs a sequence of language actions to solve a task. At time step
$`t`$, the actor receives an observation $`o_{t}`$ and forms a decision
context $`x_{t}`$ that contains the task instruction, the current
observation, and a truncated interaction history. The environment
provides an admissible action set $`\mathcal{A}_{t}`$ containing valid
actions at step $`t`$.

Let $`\pi_{\theta}`$ denote the actor policy. The actor first produces a
reasoning state $`r_{t}`$ from $`x_{t}`$ and scores each admissible
action $`a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}`$ by its length-normalized token
log-likelihood, i.e.,
$`\ell_{t}(a)=\frac{1}{|a|}\sum_{j=1}^{|a|}\log p_{\theta}(a_{j}\mid x_{t},r_{t},a_{<j}).`$
We define the actor-induced distribution over admissible actions as
$`q_{t}(a)=\frac{\exp(\ell_{t}(a))}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}\exp(\ell_{t}(a^{\prime}))}`$
for $`a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}`$, and compute ambiguity over this
action-level distribution.

SAG additionally introduces a binary critic-invocation decision
$`g_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$. $`g_{t}=1`$ queries a critic before finalizing an
action, while $`g_{t}=0`$ executes the actor decision immediately.
Querying the critic incurs an additional cost $`c>0`$. From a
decision-theoretic viewpoint, critic invocation can be interpreted as a
VoI choice: critique is invoked only when its expected improvement
exceeds the cost. We formalize this principle in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")
and in practice approximate it using ambiguity signals derived from the
actor.

### 3.2 Ambiguity Signals from the Actor

SAG estimates when feedback from the critic is likely to be beneficial
using ambiguity signals derived from the actor-induced distribution
$`q_{t}`$. These signals capture complementary aspects of decision
ambiguity.

#### Global ambiguity

We measure global ambiguity via normalized entropy.

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\bar{H}_{t}=-\frac{1}{\log|\mathcal{A}_{t}|}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}q_{t}(a)\log q_{t}(a)
``` |  | (1) |

This is defined for $`|\mathcal{A}_{t}|\geq 2`$ and set to $`0`$
otherwise. High entropy indicates many-way ambiguity among admissible
actions.

#### Local ambiguity

To capture ambiguity near a decision boundary, let $`a_{1}`$ and
$`a_{2}`$ denote the top-1 and top-2 actions under $`q_{t}`$. We define
the log-probability margin as

|     |                                            |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       m_{t}=\log q_{t}(a_{1})-\log q_{t}(a_{2}).
       ```                                         |     | (2) |

A small margin indicates that two competing actions are nearly
indistinguishable, making the decision sensitive to additional
information. When $`|\mathcal{A}_{t}|<2`$, we set $`m_{t}=\infty`$, so
the local ambiguity indicator is inactive.

### 3.3 Gated Critic Invocation

SAG combines global and local ambiguity into a bounded gating score with
margin threshold $`\delta`$.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{t}\;=\;\bar{H}_{t}+\mathbb{I}[m_{t}<\delta],\quad S_{t}\in[0,2]
``` |  | (3) |

We use a binary margin indicator rather than a continuous function to
detect near-boundary decisions, since the goal of the margin term is not
fine-grained calibration but robust identification of high-risk local
ambiguities where small additional information can flip the decision.
The critic is invoked when

|     |                                   |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       g_{t}\;=\;\mathbb{I}[S_{t}>\tau],
       ```                                |     | (4) |

where $`\tau\in(0,2)`$ is a cost-calibrated threshold. Intuitively,
$`\tau`$ is chosen as a monotone function of the critic cost $`c`$:
higher costs result in more conservative critic usage.

When $`g_{t}=1`$, SAG queries a non-oracle critic $`\pi_{\phi}`$ with
the decision context, reasoning state, and admissible actions. The
critic has no access to ground-truth actions or environment rewards; it
returns a diagnostic rationale and a suggested action, which may itself
be incorrect. If the critic output cannot be parsed as an admissible
action, SAG falls back to the actor’s top-1 action under $`q_{t}`$. In
our main experiments, the actor and critic use the same backbone with
different role prompts, so gains are not attributed to a stronger critic
model but to selective use of critique.

### 3.4 Online Self-Improvement via Bootstrapped Supervision

SAG continuously improves the actor by internalizing successful
behaviors observed during online interaction. Episodes are processed
sequentially; after a successful episode, we collect context–action
pairs $`(x_{t},a_{t})`$ into a dataset $`\mathcal{D}`$, where $`a_{t}`$
is the executed admissible action, either selected by the actor or
suggested by the critic when critique is invoked. The critic is kept
fixed, and every $`K`$ successful episodes, only the actor is
periodically updated using action-level SFT:
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[-\log\pi_{\theta}(a\mid x)\big]`$.
The effect of $`K`$ is analyzed in
Appendix [H.10](#A8.SS10 "H.10 Sensitivity to Online Update Frequency ‣ Appendix H Extra Simulation Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").
Since the target is action-only, SAG does not train on mismatched actor
reasoning traces paired with critic-corrected actions.

Following prior bootstrapping-based self-training [Zelikman et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib1), we perform each update by fine-tuning from the same
base model whenever new trajectories are accumulated, rather than
continuing from the previous updated actor. This avoids compounding
errors and preserves distributional stability. This resembles rejection
fine-tuning in using successful trajectories, but SAG collects them
online under a gated critique policy, allowing critic-assisted
corrections to become action-level supervision while explicitly
controlling critique cost.

## 4 Theoretical Analysis

We provide a decision-theoretic justification for SAG. Our goal is to
show that (i) critic invocation admits a principled VoI interpretation,
and (ii) the actor-side ambiguity signals used in SAG serve as
lightweight proxies for VoI.

### 4.1 Critic as a VoI Decision

At each step $`t`$, let $`z_{t}=(x_{t},r_{t})`$ denote the actor’s
current decision state after reasoning. SAG chooses $`g_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$.
Invoking the critic ($`g_{t}=1`$) incurs an additional cost $`c>0`$. Let
$`\Delta_{t}`$ denote the step-level improvement attributable to
invoking critique under decision state $`z_{t}`$ (e.g., an increase in
success probability). We define the VoI of critique as

|     |                                                        |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathrm{VoI}(z_{t}):=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid z_{t}].
       ```                                                     |     | (5) |

###### Lemma 4.1 (VoI thresholding for critic invocation).

For the binary decision of whether to invoke the critic at step $`t`$,
the Bayes-optimal rule is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{t}^{\star}\;=\;\mathbb{I}\!\left[\mathrm{VoI}(z_{t})>c\right]\;=\;\mathbb{I}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid z_{t}]>c\right].
``` |  | (6) |

Proof for
Lemma [4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma 4.1 (VoI thresholding for critic invocation). ‣ 4.1 Critic as a VoI Decision ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")
is provided in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Proof of Lemma ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").
In practice, $`\mathrm{VoI}(z_{t})`$ is intractable to compute, which
motivates using lightweight proxy signals derived from the actor.

### 4.2 Ambiguity as a Proxy for VoI

We next describe why the actor-side ambiguity signals in SAG provide
lightweight proxies for the VoI term
$`\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid z_{t}]`$ in
Eq. ([6](#S4.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ Lemma 4.1 (VoI thresholding for critic invocation). ‣ 4.1 Critic as a VoI Decision ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")).
The key observation is that critique is valuable mainly when the actor
is likely to be wrong. In particular, if critique corrects the actor
with probability $`\rho`$ when the actor is wrong, and a successful
correction yields average improvement $`\gamma`$, then the expected
step-level gain can be approximated by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid z_{t}]\approx\rho\,\gamma\Pr(\mathrm{err}_{t}\mid z_{t}),
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\mathrm{err}_{t}`$ denotes the event that the actor’s immediate
decision at decision state $`z_{t}`$ is incorrect. Combined with
Lemma [4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma 4.1 (VoI thresholding for critic invocation). ‣ 4.1 Critic as a VoI Decision ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making"),
this approximation implies that critique should be invoked at high-risk
steps. In particular, the VoI condition
$`\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid z_{t}]>c`$ is equivalent to
$`\Pr(\mathrm{err}_{t}\mid z_{t})>\tfrac{c}{\rho\gamma}`$, where the
same critic cost $`c`$ is expressed in the error-risk space by scaling
with the correction reliability $`\rho`$ and average gain $`\gamma`$.
Since $`\Pr(\mathrm{err}_{t}\mid z_{t})`$ is not directly observable, we
next relate it to ambiguity properties of $`q_{t}`$.

Global ambiguity Let $`a_{1}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}q_{t}(a)`$
and $`q_{\max}=q_{t}(a_{1})`$. We use normalized entropy $`\bar{H}_{t}`$
(Eq. [1](#S3.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ Global ambiguity ‣ 3.2 Ambiguity Signals from the Actor ‣ 3 Gated Critic-guided Self-improving Agent ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making"))
to capture global ambiguity across candidate actions.

###### Proposition 4.2 (Entropy controls greedy error risk).

Interpreting $`q_{t}`$ as the actor’s belief over which admissible
action is correct at decision state $`z_{t}`$, the step-level error risk
satisfies

|     |                                                 |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Pr(\mathrm{err}_{t}\mid z_{t})\;=\;1-q_{\max}.
       ```                                              |     | (8) |

Moreover, with entropy
$`H(q_{t})=-\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}q_{t}(a)\log q_{t}(a)`$, we have
$`q_{\max}\geq\exp(-H(q_{t}))`$. Thus, we get

|  |  |  |  |

formalizes that higher entropy $`\bar{H}_{t}`$ rules out the presence of
a single dominant action, necessarily increasing an upper bound on the
error risk of greedy execution. This corresponds to a regime in which
probability mass is spread across many admissible actions, rather than
concentrated on a clear best choice. We refer to this regime as global
ambiguity, since ambiguity is distributed broadly over the action space.
In such decision states, additional information from critique has
greater potential value, motivating the use of entropy as a proxy for
global decision risk.

the top two actions (e.g., when $`q_{t}`$ concentrates on two

to capture *near-boundary* uncertainty.

## 5 Experimental Setting

In this section, we describe simulation settings for
Section [6](#S6 "6 Experimental Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").
Additional simulation details are provided in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Simulation Settings ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").

Interactive decision-making benchmarks We evaluate SAG on three
complementary long-horizon benchmarks: ALFWorld [Shridhar et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib9), which requires grounded action under partial
observability; BabyAI-Text [Carta et al. (2023)](#bib.bib10), which
tests compositional instruction following and planning; and WebShop [Yao
et al. (2022a)](#bib.bib11), which involves noisy open-domain search and
exploration. Together, they cover state tracking, multi-step reasoning,
and cost-aware decision making. Details are in
Appendix [G.1](#A7.SS1 "G.1 Benchmarks for Interactive Environments ‣ Appendix G Simulation Settings ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").

Backbone Models and Training We evaluate all methods using three
instruction-tuned LLM backbones: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [Team
(2024)](#bib.bib45), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [AI@Meta (2024)](#bib.bib47),
and Mistral-7B-Instruct [Jiang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib46). For SAG, the
actor and critic use the same backbone and model size unless otherwise
stated, differing only in their role prompts. Actor–critic capacity
scaling is analyzed with Qwen2.5-Instruct 3B/7B/14B pairings and
extended to 32B in
Appendix [H.1](#A8.SS1 "H.1 Extended Model Scaling with Always-on Critique ‣ Appendix H Extra Simulation Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").
SAG performs online self-improvement via action-level SFT:
context–action pairs from successful episodes are collected, and the
actor is updated whenever 10 successful trajectories are accumulated.
Unless otherwise stated, all baselines share the same backbone models
and inference settings to ensure fair comparison. The online
self-improvement protocol and hyperparameters are provided in
Appendix [G.2](#A7.SS2 "G.2 Online Self-Improvement Protocol ‣ Appendix G Simulation Settings ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")
and [G.3](#A7.SS3 "G.3 Hyperparameters ‣ Appendix G Simulation Settings ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").

Baselines and comparison methods We compare SAG against representative
LLM agent frameworks, grouped into three categories. We focus on
inference-time and lightweight-adaptation baselines; the scope relative
to RL-based agent post-training is discussed in
Appendix [G.5](#A7.SS5 "G.5 Scope Relative to RL-based Agent Post-training ‣ Appendix G Simulation Settings ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").

- •
  Basic single-agent baseline (ReAct [Yao et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib2))
  which interleaves reasoning and environment actions, serving as a
  lightweight baseline that does not invoke a critic during execution.
- •
  Deliberation methods (Reflexion [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib7),
  Debate [Du et al. (2024)](#bib.bib12), CER [Razghandi et al.
  (2025)](#bib.bib14), Traj-Boost [Sarukkai et al. (2025)](#bib.bib18))
  which improve robustness by increasing computation through multi-pass
  deliberation or experience reuse (e.g., self-reflection across trials,
  multi-agent discussion, confidence-based selection/aggregation, or
  trajectory reuse). However, they do not model critique invocation as a
  cost-sensitive, step-wise decision during execution.
- •
  Always-on critic methods (Retrospex [Xiang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib13),
  LAC [Dong et al. (2025)](#bib.bib6)) which utilize a critic throughout
  execution by re-scoring or querying at essentially every step. While
  effective for success, their persistent critic usage can substantially
  increase inference cost and reduce token efficiency.
- •
  High-capacity reference (ReAct using GPT-4.1 [Achiam et al.
  (2023)](#bib.bib48)) which provides a reference point for how a much
  stronger model performs without critique.

## 6 Experimental Results

### 6.1 Selective critique achieves strong success at near-ReAct cost

Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5 Experimental Setting ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")
summarizes the performance across methods. Overall, deliberation and
always-on critique tend to improve success at the cost of substantially
increased token usage, whereas SAG achieves a more favorable balance by
selectively invoking critique only when needed.

SAG improves long-horizon success while preserving a ReAct-like token
budget Across all backbone–benchmark settings, SAG improves success over
ReAct in every case, with an average relative improvement of $`91\%`$
while keeping token usage close to ReAct on ALFWorld/WebShop and far
below always-on critique on BabyAI-Text. This yields higher normalized
token efficiency, especially on longer-horizon environments such as
ALFWorld. Notably, in several settings, SAG with a 7B backbone attains
success rates close to those of a stronger GPT-4.1-based ReAct agent,
narrowing the gap through improved decision quality rather than
increased model capacity.

#### Always-on critique and deliberation tend to be token-inefficient

Methods that invoke critique or deliberation at most steps quickly
become cost-dominated, increasing token usage by $`3\times`$ to over
$`10\times`$ relative to ReAct while yielding only modest success gains.
As shown in
Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5 Experimental Setting ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making"),
they shift rightward on the performance–cost plane, reflecting higher
cost without proportional reliability gains. Qualitative analysis
(Figure [5](#S6.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Always-on critique and deliberation tend to be token-inefficient ‣ 6.1 Selective critique achieves strong success at near-ReAct cost ‣ 6 Experimental Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making"))
shows that always-on critique repeatedly intervenes at low-risk states,
accumulating redundant confirmations and growing context length, whereas
SAG corrects a few high-risk decisions while avoiding unnecessary
feedback.

$`30.2\%`$. LAC + online SFT uses always-on critique and reaches
$`74.2\%`$, but requires $`341`$k tokens. SAG achieves higher success
($`78.3\%`$) with only $`56`$k tokens, showing that the gain comes from
selectively deploying critique rather than online SFT alone. Full
results on all benchmarks are in
Appendix [H.3](#A8.SS3 "H.3 Matched Online-SFT Controls ‣ Appendix H Extra Simulation Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").

#### Online self-improvement reduces reliance on critique

Figure [8(a)](#S6.F8.sf1 "Figure 8(a) ‣ Figure 8 ‣ 6.3 Learning Dynamics of Self-Improving Agents ‣ 6 Experimental Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")
and
Figure [8(b)](#S6.F8.sf2 "Figure 8(b) ‣ Figure 8 ‣ 6.3 Learning Dynamics of Self-Improving Agents ‣ 6 Experimental Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")
show that, as interaction proceeds, SAG’s success rate increases
($`71.0\%\rightarrow 75.0\%\rightarrow 83.0\%`$) while critic calls
decrease ($`2.85\rightarrow 2.18\rightarrow 1.10`$). This suggests that
successful critic-assisted trajectories are distilled into the actor,
making it more confident under the same gate and less reliant on
critique.

#### Learning reduces decision risk rather than increasing computation

Figure [8(c)](#S6.F8.sf3 "Figure 8(c) ‣ Figure 8 ‣ 6.3 Learning Dynamics of Self-Improving Agents ‣ 6 Experimental Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")
shows that ambiguity remains well separated between successes and
failures as learning progresses. For successful episodes, ambiguity
consistently decreases ($`1.15\rightarrow 0.75\rightarrow 0.45`$),
whereas failures exhibit persistently higher and increasing ambiguity
($`1.13\rightarrow 1.10\rightarrow 1.30`$), indicating that remaining
errors concentrate on harder decision points. As a diagnostic analysis
excluded from the main success metric, re-evaluating previously
encountered episodes after online updates shows improved performance
(Figure [8(d)](#S6.F8.sf4 "Figure 8(d) ‣ Figure 8 ‣ 6.3 Learning Dynamics of Self-Improving Agents ‣ 6 Experimental Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")),
increasing from $`75.1\%`$ to $`80.3\%`$. Together, these results show
that critique acts as a transient scaffold: as learning progresses, SAG
internalizes effective behaviors and shifts decision-making toward
lower-risk states without increasing inference-time computation.

## 7 Conclusion

We introduced SAG, a cost-aware framework that selectively invokes
critique to improve the reliability of LLM agents in long-horizon
decision-making. SAG formulates critique invocation as a step-wise
decision problem and employs a lightweight, training-free gate based on
action-level ambiguity. Across multiple long-horizon benchmarks and
backbone models, SAG consistently achieves a superior performance–cost
trade-off compared to both no-critique and always-on critique agents.
These results suggest that selective critique provides a practical and
scalable pathway for improving the reliability and efficiency of LLM
agents in complex sequential environments.

## Appendix A Broader Impacts and Limitations

#### Broader Impacts

This work contributes a practical methodology for improving the
reliability and efficiency of LLM-based agents, a capability that is
increasingly important as such systems are deployed in complex
interactive settings. Because SAG is lightweight, training-free, and
compatible with existing agent frameworks, it can be readily integrated
into current LLM-agent pipelines used in both academic research and
industrial applications. In research contexts, SAG provides a simple yet
principled mechanism for studying cost-aware reasoning allocation and
selective deliberation in long-horizon decision-making. In applied
settings, the framework can reduce unnecessary token usage and latency
while preserving reliability, making agent-based systems more
economically and computationally sustainable. As with other
general-purpose LLM agent techniques, the societal impact ultimately
depends on how such systems are deployed; responsible development and
application-level safeguards remain important for ensuring beneficial
outcomes.

#### Limitations

While SAG demonstrates consistent improvements across multiple
environments and backbone models, several aspects remain open for
further exploration. First, our current gating mechanism relies on
ambiguity signals derived from the actor’s action distribution;
alternative uncertainty or value-estimation signals may further improve
critique allocation. Second, the current study focuses on representative
long-horizon interactive benchmarks, and evaluating SAG in additional
real-world agent settings would provide further insight into its
generality. Finally, the present work uses a fixed critic architecture;
future work may explore adaptive or learned critics that further enhance
the reliability–efficiency trade-off.

## Appendix C Proof of Lemma [4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma 4.1 (VoI thresholding for critic invocation). ‣ 4.1 Critic as a VoI Decision ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")

We consider the one-step decision problem at decision state
$`z_{t}=(x_{t},r_{t})`$, where the agent chooses whether to invoke the
critic. Let $`g_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$ denote the invocation decision. The
Bayes-optimal decision maximizes the conditional expected net utility
given $`z_{t}`$. Invoking the critic ($`g_{t}=1`$) yields a random
improvement $`\Delta_{t}`$ (e.g., increase in success probability or
expected return) and incurs a deterministic cost $`c>0`$. If the critic
is not invoked ($`g_{t}=0`$), the improvement is defined as $`0`$ and no
additional cost is incurred.

Conditioned on $`z_{t}`$, the expected net utility of choosing
$`g_{t}=1`$ is

|     |                                     |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid z_{t}]-c,
       ```                                  |     | (11) |

whereas the expected net utility of choosing $`g_{t}=0`$ is $`0`$.
Therefore, invoking the critic is optimal iff the expected net gain is
positive:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{t}^{\star}=\mathbb{I}\left[\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid z_{t}]-c>0\right]=\mathbb{I}\left[\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid z_{t}]>c\right].
``` |  | (12) |

Recalling the definition
$`\mathrm{VoI}(z_{t}):=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{t}\mid z_{t}]`$ completes the
proof. $`\square`$

## Appendix D Proof of Proposition [4.2](#S4.Thmtheorem2 "Proposition 4.2 (Entropy controls greedy error risk). ‣ 4.2 Ambiguity as a Proxy for VoI ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")

Fix a step $`t`$ and decision state $`z_{t}=(x_{t},r_{t})`$, and let
$`q_{t}`$ be the actor-induced distribution over admissible actions
$`\mathcal{A}_{t}`$. Let
$`a_{1}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}q_{t}(a)`$ and
$`q_{\max}=q_{t}(a_{1})`$.

#### Step 1: Error probability under the belief interpretation.

Equivalently,
Eq. ([8](#S4.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ Proposition 4.2 (Entropy controls greedy error risk). ‣ 4.2 Ambiguity as a Proxy for VoI ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making"))
holds if we define $`\Pr(\text{$a$ is correct}\mid z_{t}):=q_{t}(a)`$
over $`\mathcal{A}_{t}`$. Under this interpretation, the probability
that the greedy choice $`a_{1}`$ is incorrect equals the probability
mass assigned to all other actions:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(\mathrm{err}_{t}\mid z_{t})=1-\Pr(\text{$a_{1}$ is correct}\mid z_{t})=1-q_{\max},
``` |  | (13) |

which proves
Eq. ([8](#S4.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ Proposition 4.2 (Entropy controls greedy error risk). ‣ 4.2 Ambiguity as a Proxy for VoI ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")).

#### Step 2: $`-\log q_{\max}`$ lower bounds entropy.

Let $`H(q_{t})=-\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}q_{t}(a)\log q_{t}(a)`$ denote
the entropy. Since $`q_{\max}\geq q_{t}(a)`$ for all
$`a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}`$, we have $`\log q_{\max}\geq\log q_{t}(a)`$ and
thus $`-\log q_{t}(a)\geq-\log q_{\max}`$ for all $`a`$. Multiplying by
$`q_{t}(a)\geq 0`$ and summing over $`a`$ yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H(q_{t})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}q_{t}(a)\big(-\log q_{t}(a)\big)\geq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{t}}q_{t}(a)\big(-\log q_{\max}\big)=-\log q_{\max}.
``` |  | (14) |

Exponentiating both sides gives $`q_{\max}\geq e^{-H(q_{t})}`$.

Combining with Step 1, we obtain

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(\mathrm{err}_{t}\mid z_{t})=1-q_{\max}\leq 1-e^{-H(q_{t})}.
``` |  | (15) |

Finally, using the normalized entropy definition
$`\bar{H}_{t}=H(q_{t})/\log|\mathcal{A}_{t}|`$ (for
$`|\mathcal{A}_{t}|\geq 2`$), we have
$`e^{-H(q_{t})}=e^{-\bar{H}_{t}\log|\mathcal{A}_{t}|}=|\mathcal{A}_{t}|^{-\bar{H}_{t}}`$,
which yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(\mathrm{err}_{t}\mid z_{t})\leq 1-|\mathcal{A}_{t}|^{-\bar{H}_{t}}.
``` |  | (16) |

This completes the proof. $`\square`$

## Appendix E Proof of Proposition [4.3](#S4.Thmtheorem3 "Proposition 4.3 (Margin controls top-2 error risk). ‣ 4.2 Ambiguity as a Proxy for VoI ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")

Fix a step $`t`$ and decision state $`z_{t}=(x_{t},r_{t})`$. Let
$`q_{t}`$ be the actor-induced distribution over admissible actions
$`\mathcal{A}_{t}`$. Let $`a_{1}`$ and $`a_{2}`$ denote the top-1 and
top-2 actions under $`q_{t}`$, and write $`q_{1}:=q_{t}(a_{1})`$ and
$`q_{2}:=q_{t}(a_{2})`$. Recall the log-probability margin
$`m_{t}:=\log q_{1}-\log q_{2}=\log(q_{1}/q_{2})`$.

Assume the correct action lies in $`\{a_{1},a_{2}\}`$. Conditioned on
this event (denoted $`\mathrm{top\text{-}2}`$), the induced conditional
distribution over $`\{a_{1},a_{2}\}`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(\text{$a_{i}$ is correct}\mid z_{t},\mathrm{top\text{-}2})=\frac{q_{i}}{q_{1}+q_{2}},\quad i\in\{1,2\}.
``` |  | (17) |

Therefore,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(\mathrm{err}\mid z_{t},\mathrm{top\text{-}2})=\Pr(\text{$a_{2}$ is correct}\mid z_{t},\mathrm{top\text{-}2})=\frac{q_{2}}{q_{1}+q_{2}}.
``` |  | (18) |

Rewriting in terms of the margin gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(\mathrm{err}\mid z_{t},\mathrm{top\text{-}2})=\frac{q_{2}}{q_{1}+q_{2}}=\frac{1}{1+q_{1}/q_{2}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{m_{t}}}.
``` |  | (19) |

Finally, since $`e^{m_{t}}>0`$, we use $`1+e^{m_{t}}\geq e^{m_{t}}`$ to
obtain

|     |                                                          |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{1}{1+e^{m_{t}}}\leq\frac{1}{e^{m_{t}}}=e^{-m_{t}}.
       ```                                                       |     | (20) |

This proves
Eq. ([10](#S4.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ Proposition 4.3 (Margin controls top-2 error risk). ‣ 4.2 Ambiguity as a Proxy for VoI ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making")).
$`\square`$

## Appendix G Simulation Settings

### G.1 Benchmarks for Interactive Environments

This section provides detailed descriptions of the interactive
environments used in our experiments, including task configurations,
scenario distributions, and evaluation protocols. These details are
omitted from the main paper for brevity.

ALFWorld \[[58](#bib.bib9)\] is a text-based household simulation
environment built on top of ALFRED, where agents interact with a
partially observed home environment through natural language actions.
Each task consists of a high-level instruction (e.g., placing, cleaning,
or heating objects) that must be decomposed into a long sequence of
grounded actions such as navigation, object manipulation, and state
changes. We follow the standard evaluation protocol, using the unseen
test split to assess generalization to novel layouts and object
configurations. Episodes typically require dozens of sequential actions,
making performance sensitive to early mistakes and error accumulation.

BabyAI-Text \[[5](#bib.bib10)\] provides a natural-language interface to
the BabyAI grid world, where agents must follow compositional
instructions involving spatial relations, object attributes, and
temporal ordering. Although the environment dynamics are deterministic
and the observation space is compact, tasks are generated procedurally
to ensure a wide range of unseen instruction combinations at test time.
We evaluate on held-out instruction distributions to measure
compositional generalization and multi-step planning accuracy
independent of perceptual noise.

WebShop \[[71](#bib.bib11)\] is a web-based e-commerce environment that
simulates realistic online shopping scenarios. Agents interact with
dynamically generated product pages using text-based actions such as
search queries, page navigation, and item selection. Each episode
requires identifying and purchasing an item that satisfies multiple
constraints specified in a natural-language goal. The environment
presents noisy, unstructured textual information and numerous
distractors, requiring agents to balance exploration cost and task
success. For SAG, after the initial search step, $`\mathcal{A}_{t}`$ is
constructed from the finite set of clickable actions exposed in the
current page observation, such as visible products, item options,
navigation controls, and Buy Now. Thus, the ambiguity gate and critic
operate only over this finite candidate set rather than the open-ended
space of possible search queries.

Across all environments, tasks require sustained reasoning and adaptive
decision making over extended horizons, but differ in the primary source
of difficulty: partial observability and error propagation in ALFWorld,
compositional generalization in BabyAI-Text, and information-seeking
under noise and cost constraints in WebShop. Together, these benchmarks
provide a comprehensive testbed for evaluating long-horizon agent
behavior under diverse failure modes.

#### Scope of the paper: Why not math or QA benchmarks

We focus on interactive, long-horizon agent benchmarks where agents must
act online and where latency and token-budget efficiency materially
affect performance. Offline final-answer reasoning benchmarks such as
math, code, and QA are out of scope, as they typically allow extended
deliberation or backtracking without the compounding interaction delay
central to our setting.

### G.2 Online Self-Improvement Protocol

SAG updates the actor during a sequential online interaction stream. For
each episode, the actor interacts with the environment using the current
gate and critic. If the episode succeeds, we store the resulting
context–action pairs $`(x_{t},a_{t})`$ in the buffer $`\mathcal{D}`$,
where $`a_{t}`$ is the executed admissible action, either selected by
the actor or suggested by the critic when critique is invoked. Although
the actor prompt may elicit reasoning before action selection, online
SFT is applied only to the executed action target, not to the generated
reasoning trace.

![Refer to caption](2610.07335v1/onlinesft.png)

Figure 9: Overall online self-improvement process

Each episode is evaluated only once in the main protocol. If an episode
later contributes successful context–action pairs to $`\mathcal{D}`$, it
is not re-evaluated after the actor update; the updated actor is used
only for subsequent episodes. After every $`K`$ successful episodes, we
update only the actor with SFT on $`\mathcal{D}`$ while keeping the
critic fixed. The overall online self-improvement process is provided in
Figure [9](#A7.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ G.2 Online Self-Improvement Protocol ‣ Appendix G Simulation Settings ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").

At each update, we fine-tune from the same base actor rather than
continuing from the previous updated actor. This follows
bootstrapping-based self-training \[[75](#bib.bib1)\] and reduces drift
from repeated updates. Unless otherwise stated, we use $`K=10`$
successful episodes per update. We study the sensitivity to $`K`$ in
Appendix [H.10](#A8.SS10 "H.10 Sensitivity to Online Update Frequency ‣ Appendix H Extra Simulation Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").

### G.4 Detailed description of baselines

We compare SAG against representative LLM agent frameworks.

- •
  ReAct \[[73](#bib.bib2)\]: A reasoning-and-acting framework that
  interleaves intermediate reasoning with environment actions. ReAct
  generates thoughts to decompose tasks and guide action selection while
  interacting with external environments. It serves as a baseline
  without explicit critique.
- •
  Reflexion \[[57](#bib.bib7)\]: A self-critique approach that generates
  reflections to guide future trials. Reflections are stored in memory
  and retrieved to improve subsequent attempts. Critique is applied in a
  fixed manner without adapting to step-level risk or cost.
- •
  Debate \[[13](#bib.bib12)\]: A multi-agent deliberation approach where
  multiple agents discuss candidate solutions before acting. Structured
  argumentation helps identify errors and refine reasoning
  collaboratively. While improving robustness, it incurs high
  computational cost due to parallel agent executions.
- •
  CER \[[53](#bib.bib14)\]: A confidence-based framework that estimates
  uncertainty and uses it for selection or aggregation. It quantifies
  model confidence to filter low-quality outputs or combine predictions.
  While leveraging uncertainty signals, CER does not explicitly model
  critique as a decision nor invoke an external critic.
- •
  Traj-Boost \[[54](#bib.bib18)\]: A trajectory-bootstrapping method
  that improves performance by reusing successful trajectories. It
  enhances learning from experience by reusing high-quality execution
  patterns. However, it does not provide execution-time critique or
  error correction during task execution.
- •
  Retrospex \[[69](#bib.bib13)\]: An always-on critique framework that
  integrates a learned offline reinforcement learning critic by
  re-scoring candidate actions at every step. The critic estimates
  action quality based on historical trajectories, providing lightweight
  value assessments. Although the critic is lightweight, critique is
  applied throughout execution without selective gating.
- •
  LAC \[[11](#bib.bib6)\]: An always-on critique method that queries an
  LLM-based critic at every decision step. The critic provides
  numerical, value-based evaluations of candidate actions to improve
  action quality. The frequent critic invocation leads to substantial
  inference cost.
- •
  Ours (SAG): A cost-aware framework that treats critic invocation as a
  decision and selectively queries a critic based on actor ambiguity. By
  combining selective critique with online self-improvement, SAG aims to
  achieve strong performance with significantly improved token
  efficiency.

### G.5 Scope Relative to RL-based Agent Post-training

SAG and RL-based agent post-training address complementary regimes of
LLM agent improvement. RL-based methods such as PPO-style optimization,
RLOO/REINFORCE-style optimization, GRPO, and GiGPO improve the
underlying policy before deployment by collecting environment rollouts,
computing rewards or advantages, and updating model parameters through
policy optimization \[[55](#bib.bib49), [56](#bib.bib52),
[20](#bib.bib51)\]. These methods can achieve strong task-specific
performance when sufficient rollout budget, reward supervision, and
optimization compute are available. SAG instead targets a
deployment-time regime: given an available actor and a non-oracle
critic, it asks when critique is worth its token and latency cost during
interaction.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
| Dimension | RL-based agent post-training | SAG |
| Primary goal | Improve the underlying policy before deployment | Decide when to use critique during deployment |
| Typical methods | PPO, GRPO, GiGPO | Gated critique + online SFT |
| Training signal | Rewards, preferences, or advantage estimates | Successful trajectories from online interaction |
| Rollout structure | Multiple sampled trajectories/completions per task | One interaction stream with selective critique |
| Models during training | Actor, often reference policy, optimizer states | Actor update; critic fixed and queried selectively |
| Cost focus | Training-time rollout and optimization budget | Inference-time token, critic-call, and latency cost |
| Hyperparameters | LR, KL, clipping, group size, reward normalization, rollout budget | Gate thresholds $`\tau,\delta`$ and update frequency |
| Comparison regime | Strong task-specific policy after post-training | Cost-aware improvement of an available actor |

Table 8: Scope comparison between RL-based agent post-training and SAG.

#### Different optimization stage

RL-based post-training invests compute before deployment to produce a
stronger policy. A typical RL pipeline repeatedly samples trajectories
from the current policy, evaluates them with task rewards or
preference-derived signals, estimates advantages, and performs policy
updates, often with KL control against a reference policy. In contrast,
SAG does not aim to replace this training stage. It operates during
interaction, using a lightweight gate to decide whether an already
available actor should query an auxiliary critic at the current step.
Thus, RL methods primarily optimize *what policy is learned*, whereas
SAG optimizes *when external critique should be used* under
inference-time cost.

#### Different compute and memory regime

The computational structure of RL-based post-training differs
substantially from SAG. Group-based RL methods such as GRPO and GiGPO
sample multiple trajectories or completions per task, compare them
through reward or advantage estimates, and then reprocess these
trajectories for policy-gradient updates. Many implementations also
maintain a reference policy for KL regularization and store old
log-probabilities, rollout buffers, and optimizer states. By contrast,
SAG uses a single interaction trajectory at a time, invokes the critic
only on selected ambiguous steps, and performs lightweight SFT on
successful trajectories. In our implementation, only the actor is
updated while the critic remains fixed. This makes SAG closer to
inference-time adaptation or lightweight online SFT than to full RL
post-training.

#### Different token-cost accounting

RL-based methods may have low inference-time cost after training, but
their training phase can consume large numbers of environment
interactions and generated tokens due to repeated rollouts. This
training-time token budget is not directly comparable to SAG’s
inference-time critique budget. SAG explicitly studies the cost incurred
while the agent is solving tasks: token usage includes actor and critic
tokens during interaction, and the method is designed to reduce this
cost by avoiding unnecessary critique calls. A fair like-for-like
comparison would therefore need to control not only final inference
tokens, but also rollout budget, number of environment interactions,
training tokens, GPU hours, reward supervision, and task-specific
tuning.

#### Different practical assumptions

RL-based post-training often requires task-specific reward design or
reliable environment rewards, parallel rollout infrastructure, careful
hyperparameter tuning, and repeated optimization over the target
environment. For example, policy-gradient methods can be sensitive to
learning rate, KL coefficient, clipping range, group size, reward
normalization, and rollout budget. SAG assumes a different practical
setting: the user already has an instruction-tuned actor and an
imperfect critic, and wants to improve reliability during deployment
without large-scale task-specific RL training. Its main hyperparameters
are the gate threshold $`\tau`$ and margin threshold $`\delta`$, which
we find to be robust across a broad range in
Appendix [H.5](#A8.SS5 "H.5 Gate Threshold Sensitivity ‣ Appendix H Extra Simulation Results ‣ Selective Critique for Cost-Aware LLM Agents in Long-Horizon Decision Making").

#### Complementarity

These differences do not imply that RL-based post-training and SAG are
mutually exclusive. A stronger RL-trained agent could still benefit from
SAG at deployment time, since even strong policies may encounter
ambiguous states where external critique is useful. Conversely, SAG can
provide a lightweight alternative when large-scale RL training is
impractical or when the goal is to manage token and latency costs during
online interaction. We therefore treat RL-based agent post-training as
complementary to SAG, and focus our main empirical comparisons on
inference-time and lightweight-adaptation baselines that share SAG’s
deployment-time regime.

### H.9 Failure Modes and Non-oracle Critic Behavior

SAG assumes that critique is useful but not perfect. We observe three
common failure modes.

#### Incorrect critic suggestion

The critic may suggest an action that is plausible but suboptimal under
the current environmental state. This can occur when the critic
misinterprets the interaction history or overlooks a constraint in the
admissible action set.

#### Missed critique opportunity

The gate may fail to invoke critique when the actor is confidently
wrong. This false-negative case is difficult for any actor-side
uncertainty method, since the actor’s distribution does not expose the
underlying error.

#### Unrecoverable early error

In long-horizon environments, some early mistakes create states from
which later critique cannot recover. This suggests that future work
could combine step-level gating with trajectory-level monitoring.

These cases highlight that SAG does not rely on an oracle critic. Its
gains come from invoking imperfect critique selectively at high-risk
steps, while avoiding the systematic cost of always-on feedback.
````

# Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models

Yecheng Wu Affiliation: Massachusetts Institute of Technology
Correspondence to: <wyc557@mit.edu>    Song Han
Affiliation: Massachusetts Institute of Technology Affiliation: NVIDIA
   Han Cai Affiliation: NVIDIA

###### Abstract

Self-distillation offers a way to improve reasoning models when a
suitable external teacher is unavailable. However, common approaches
rely on privileged information to construct a stronger self-teacher,
limiting their use when only unlabeled prompts are available. In this
paper, we introduce Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation
(WISD), which instead constructs a weaker counterpart of the original
model and uses the contrast between the two as a training signal without
requiring privileged information. We explore two ways to construct the
weaker model: low-bit quantization and magnitude pruning. We evaluate
WISD across diverse architectures and scales on mathematical and coding
reasoning tasks. Across these benchmarks, quantization and pruning
improve average accuracy over the original models by 4.0 and 5.5
percentage points, respectively, achieving state-of-the-art performance
even without privileged information. Notably, pruning raises
Qwen3.5-4B’s accuracy from 57.7% to 70.5% on HMMT 2025 and from 39.1% to
56.7% on LiveCodeBench v6. Quantization raises Qwen3.6-35B-A3B’s
accuracy from 68.5% to 74.6% and from 61.8% to 69.5% on the same
benchmarks, respectively. These results establish WISD as a novel and
effective self-distillation framework for large reasoning models. Code
is released at
[https://github.com/jet-ai-projects/WISD](https://github.com/jet-ai-projects/WISD).

## 1 Introduction

On-policy distillation (OPD) has emerged as an effective approach to
improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models ([Agarwal
et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib2); [Lu and Lab,
2025](#bib.bib19)). It trains a student on its own generated
trajectories using dense token-level supervision from a teacher,
combining learning on the student’s own states with fine-grained
feedback. For reasoning tasks, this allows learning to address errors in
intermediate computations and decisions along the student’s own solution
paths. This approach has demonstrated substantial gains in training
efficiency and is now used in the post-training of Qwen3, MiMo-V2-Flash,
Nemotron-Cascade 2, and DeepSeek-V4 ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib4);
[Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Yang et al., 2026d](#bib.bib33);
[DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)). Recent work extends OPD to
self-distillation, in which the same model serves as both teacher and
student, removing the need for a separate teacher model ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14); [Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Hübotter et
al., 2026](#bib.bib26)).

Self-distillation is particularly appealing when a suitable external
teacher is unavailable, especially when the goal is to improve an
already capable model ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)). A common
approach uses the model’s in-context learning ability to construct a
stronger teacher by providing privileged information, such as reference
solutions ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)), expert
demonstrations ([Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)), or textual
environment feedback ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)). With this
additional context, the teacher can provide more informed predictions
for trajectories that the student generates from the task input alone.
Training matches the student’s next-token distributions to the
teacher’s, transferring the benefit of the richer context into a policy
that operates without privileged information at inference time.

However, these approaches retain a dependence on privileged information
during training. Reference-based methods require solutions or expert
demonstrations ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Shenfeld et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25)), and obtaining high-quality solutions can be
costly ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib34)). Feedback-based alternatives
rely on informative environment responses or reward-verified successful
rollouts ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)). These requirements limit
their applicability when only unlabeled prompts are available. Even when
privileged information is available, conditioning on it can shift the
teacher’s predictions away from the student’s and complicate knowledge
transfer ([Penaloza et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)). This motivates our
central question: _Can a model improve its reasoning through
self-distillation without privileged information?_

Recent work on weak-to-strong OPD shows that the log-probability
contrast between paired models can provide useful supervision even when
both are weaker than the student ([Feng et al., 2026](#bib.bib16); [Yu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). Differences in token preferences between
paired models encode a direction of policy improvement that can be
transferred to a more capable student. From this perspective, the
privileged-information approaches above take the model itself as the
weaker member of a pair and construct a stronger counterpart through
additional context ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Shenfeld et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25); [Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)). We introduce
Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation (WISD), which reverses
this construction: we take the original model as the stronger member and
create a weaker counterpart by degrading its weights. The contrast
between the original model and its weakened copy then provides dense
supervision on the model’s own trajectories, without privileged
information. This formulation turns model weakening into a source of
supervision, enabling self-improvement using the original model and
unlabeled task prompts alone.

Following this idea, we explore two training-free constructions of a
weaker self: low-bit quantization and magnitude pruning. Both operate
directly on model weights and require no calibration data. Experiments
across diverse model architectures and scales demonstrate the
effectiveness of WISD on mathematical and coding reasoning tasks.
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models")
summarizes the gains in mathematical and coding reasoning. Across these
benchmarks, quantization and pruning improve average accuracy over the
original models by 4.0 and 5.5 percentage points, respectively, with
positive gains in 21 and 23 of the 25 model–benchmark settings,
achieving state-of-the-art performance even without privileged
information. Notably, pruning raises Qwen3.5-4B’s accuracy from 57.7% to
70.5% on HMMT 2025 and from 39.1% to 56.7% on LiveCodeBench v6.
Quantization raises Qwen3.6-35B-A3B’s accuracy from 68.5% to 74.6% and
from 61.8% to 69.5% on the same benchmarks, respectively. These results
establish WISD as a novel and effective self-distillation framework for
large reasoning models, requiring minimal prerequisites.

Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  We introduce WISD, a self-distillation approach that treats the
  original model as the stronger member of a pair and learns from its
  contrast with a weakened copy, improving reasoning without privileged
  information.
- •
  We instantiate this approach with two weak-model constructions,
  low-bit quantization and magnitude pruning, which require no
  calibration data and yield dense supervision on the model’s own
  trajectories.
- •
  We validate the approach across widely used reasoning models spanning
  diverse architectures and scales, demonstrating broad accuracy
  improvements on mathematical and coding reasoning tasks.

## 2 Related Work

#### On-Policy Distillation.

On-policy distillation trains a student on its own generated
trajectories ([Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib20); [Fu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib21); [Ruan et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)) with token-level
teacher feedback ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib22); [Xu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib31); [Armandpour et al., 2026](#bib.bib23); [Wang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib32)), reducing the distribution mismatch between training
and inference ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Gu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2); [Lu and Lab, 2025](#bib.bib19); [Wu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib3); [Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)). Direct On-Policy
Distillation (DOPD) and related approaches transfer policy shifts
through log-probability contrasts between paired models ([Yang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib17); [Feng et al., 2026](#bib.bib16); [Wu et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib35)), enabling weak-to-strong distillation ([Yu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18)). A growing line of work extends OPD to
self-distillation by conditioning the model itself on additional
information to obtain a stronger teacher: reference solutions ([Zhao et
al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Ke et al., 2026](#bib.bib27); [Shen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib70); [Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib71)),
demonstrations ([Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)), and environment
feedback or verified successful rollouts ([Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib26); [Tan and Hong, 2026](#bib.bib28); [Yang et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib29); [He et al., 2026](#bib.bib72); [Sun et al.,
2026](#bib.bib73)). Compared to these prior self-distillation
approaches, WISD requires no privileged information. Instead, it
constructs a weaker copy of the original model and uses the contrast
between the two as the training signal. OPSA takes a complementary
approach, constructing entropy-adaptive negative advantages without an
external teacher or privileged information ([Ding and Zhang,
2026](#bib.bib56)).

#### Learning from Weaker Models.

Contrasting a model with a weaker counterpart has been explored for both
generation ([Shi et al., 2024](#bib.bib67); [Hong et al.,
2023](#bib.bib68); [Ahn et al., 2025](#bib.bib69)) and learning.
Contrastive decoding uses a smaller amateur to guide a stronger expert
at inference time ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib36); [O’Brien and Lewis,
2023](#bib.bib37)). Variants derive weaker predictions from the model
itself ([Gera et al., 2023](#bib.bib63); [Zhang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib64); [Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib65); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib66)) through early layers in DoLa ([Chuang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib38)), dropout or quantization in DCD ([Phan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib39)), and layer pruning in PruneCD ([Yu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib40)). Related small-model contrasts support decoding-time
adaptation ([Mitchell et al., 2024](#bib.bib41); [Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib42)). In diffusion, classifier-free guidance contrasts
conditional and unconditional predictions ([Ho and Salimans,
2022](#bib.bib43)), while autoguidance uses a smaller, less-trained
counterpart ([Karras et al., 2024](#bib.bib44)). Training-time
approaches include pruned self-competitors for visual representation
learning ([Jiang et al., 2021](#bib.bib45)) and expert–amateur contrasts
for reasoning supervision ([Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib46)). Recent
distillation methods construct degraded references for flow-matching
models ([Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib47)) or reduce expert activation in
an external MoE teacher to obtain a reference ([Gu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib48)). Self-distillation also exploits predictions
conditioned on ablated visual inputs ([Liang et al., 2026](#bib.bib49))
or self-generated negative context ([Pei et al., 2026](#bib.bib50)).
WISD constructs a weaker counterpart directly through training-free
weight quantization or magnitude pruning and distills its contrast with
the original model to improve reasoning without privileged information.

## 3 Method

We first review OPD and policy-shift supervision, then revisit
self-distillation from a weak-to-strong perspective to introduce WISD.
Finally, we describe two training-free ways to construct its weaker
model.
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Method ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models")
contrasts WISD with prior self-distillation and summarizes its training
pipeline.

Figure 2: Overview of WISD. (a) Prior self-distillation. Privileged
information $`c`$, such as solutions, demonstrations, or feedback,
strengthens the original model into a self-teacher. (b) WISD. We instead
retain the original model as the stronger member and construct a weaker
copy through training-free quantization or pruning. The frozen pair
scores the same self-generated trajectories, and its token-level
log-probability contrast provides cached supervision for a student
initialized from the original model, without privileged information.

### 3.1 Preliminaries

Let $`\pi_{\theta}`$ be an autoregressive student, $`x\sim\mathcal{D}`$
a prompt, and $`y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ a generated response.
At position $`t`$, the state $`s_{t}=(x,y_{<t})`$ determines a
distribution over next-token actions $`a\in\mathcal{V}`$. OPD supervises
the student on the states it visits during generation. Writing
$`d_{\pi_{\theta}}`$ for this state distribution, a common objective
matches the student to a teacher $`\pi_{T}`$ through reverse
KL ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)):

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\pi_{\theta}}}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_{T}(\cdot\mid s)\bigr)\right].
``` |  | (1) |

DOPD ([Feng et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)) represents supervision through a
pair of frozen models $`(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})`$, typically a post-trained
model and its checkpoint before post-training. Their token-level
log-probability difference defines a policy shift:

|     |                                                       |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       r(a\mid s)=\log\pi^{+}(a\mid s)-\log\pi^{-}(a\mid s).
       ```                                                    |     | (2) |

This contrast specifies which actions the student should favor more or
less, allowing a model pair to provide useful supervision even when
neither member is stronger than the student ([Feng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib16); [Yu et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). We next use this
perspective to revisit on-policy self-distillation.

### 3.2 Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation

Weak-to-strong distillation suggests that a useful supervision signal
can come from the difference between two models, even when a stronger
teacher is unavailable ([Yu et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). The central
design choice is therefore how to obtain a model pair whose relative
predictions provide a learning signal. This paired-model perspective
also offers a way to understand prior on-policy self-distillation
(OPSD). Given a model $`\pi`$ and privileged information $`c`$, these
approaches construct a more informed self-teacher $`\pi(\cdot\mid s,c)`$
using reference solutions ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)),
demonstrations ([Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)), or environment
feedback ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)). Viewed as a model pair,
the original policy $`\pi(\cdot\mid s)`$ is the weaker member, while its
context-conditioned counterpart is the stronger member. These two roles
can be written as $`\pi^{-}(a\mid s)=\pi(a\mid s)`$ and
$`\pi^{+}(a\mid s)=\pi(a\mid s,c)`$. The student encounters the task
without $`c`$, and the additional context gives the teacher a more
informed view of the student’s decisions. The construction therefore
depends on additional information to create the gap from which the
student learns.

WISD reverses this construction: we take the original model as the
stronger member and construct a weaker counterpart from its own weights.
Let $`\pi_{0}=\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$ denote the original model and
$`\pi_{w}=\pi_{\mathcal{W}(\theta_{0})}`$ its copy under a weakening
operator $`\mathcal{W}`$. Setting
$`(\pi^{+},\pi^{-})=(\pi_{0},\pi_{w})`$ in
Eq. ([2](#S3.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 3.1 Preliminaries ‣ 3 Method ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models"))
gives

|     |                                                           |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       r_{w}(a\mid s)=\log\pi_{0}(a\mid s)-\log\pi_{w}(a\mid s).
       ```                                                        |     | (3) |

Both models receive the same prompt and generated prefix, so the
contrast compares their token preferences under an identical context. A
token receives a positive contrast when it is more likely under the
original model and a negative contrast when it is more likely under the
weak model. Distillation uses this signal to favor tokens with larger
original-to-weak probability ratios relative to those with smaller
ratios. Using two identical policies in
Eq. ([2](#S3.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 3.1 Preliminaries ‣ 3 Method ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models"))
would yield zero contrast. Weakening creates a token-dependent signal
even when the student starts from the original model. This supplies
dense self-supervision using only the original checkpoint and unlabeled
prompts.

For training, we adopt the offline DOPD procedure of Lightning
Weave ([Wu et al., 2026c](#bib.bib35)), with the tilted target and
distillation objective

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{w}(a\mid s)=\frac{\pi_{0}(a\mid s)\exp\!\left(r_{w}(a\mid s)/\alpha\right)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}\pi_{0}(a^{\prime}\mid s)\exp\!\left(r_{w}(a^{\prime}\mid s)/\alpha\right)},
``` |  | (4) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TT}}(\theta)=\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\pi_{0}}}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,q_{w}(\cdot\mid s)\bigr)\right].
``` |  | (5) |

Here $`\alpha>0`$ is the distillation coefficient which controls how
strongly the contrast $`r_{w}`$ tilts the target away from $`\pi_{0}`$
and scales the objective accordingly. The original model generates
trajectories on unlabeled prompts, and the frozen pair
$`(\pi_{0},\pi_{w})`$ scores their states to provide cached supervision.
A student initialized from $`\theta_{0}`$ is then trained on this
supervision. The trajectories and both scoring models remain fixed
throughout optimization. The trained student is used alone at inference
time.

### 3.3 Constructing a Weaker Self

We instantiate $`\mathcal{W}`$ with low-bit quantization and magnitude
pruning, both of which operate directly on model weights without
calibration data or training. These operators introduce two different
forms of degradation: quantization coarsens weight values, while pruning
removes low-magnitude connections. Both retain the model’s architecture
and tokenizer, making their next-token predictions directly comparable
to those of the original model. The transformations apply to attention
and feed-forward projection weights, including MoE expert projections.
Embeddings, output heads, normalization parameters, and routing
parameters are preserved. The transformation is applied only to the
frozen weak scorer. The student retains the original weights at
initialization. For each projection matrix $`W`$, we divide every output
row into groups $`G`$ of $`128`$ input columns and write
$`M_{G}=\max_{j\in G}|W_{ij}|`$.

#### Low-bit quantization.

For an integer bit width $`b\geq 2`$, let $`q_{b}=2^{b-1}-1`$. Symmetric
round-to-nearest quantization gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{W}_{ij}=\Delta_{G}\,\operatorname{clip}\left(\operatorname{round}\left(\frac{W_{ij}}{\Delta_{G}}\right),-q_{b},q_{b}\right),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\Delta_{G}=M_{G}/q_{b}`$. The group maximum adapts the step size
to the local weight scale. The quantization grid contains $`2^{b}-1`$
levels, including zero. We dequantize the resulting weights for scoring
with the original model architecture. Our default uses $`b=3`$.

#### Magnitude pruning.

For a group-relative threshold $`\tau\in(0,1)`$, we remove weights at or
below $`\tau M_{G}`$ within each group, then restore the original
$`\ell_{2}`$ norm of each complete output row:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{ij}`$ | $`\displaystyle=W_{ij}\,\mathbf{1}\left[|W_{ij}|>\tau M_{G}\right],`$ |  | (7) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{i,:}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\widetilde{W}_{i,:}\,\frac{\|W_{i,:}\|_{2}}{\|\widetilde{W}_{i,:}\|_{2}}.`$ |  |  |

The group-relative threshold accounts for variation in weight scale
across the matrix. Its value specifies a relative magnitude threshold.
The achieved sparsity depends on the distribution of weight magnitudes.
Pruning preserves the surviving values before rescaling, while the
whole-row normalization compensates for the norm lost through deletion.
Our default uses $`\tau=1/6`$. For both operators, all-zero groups or
rows remain zero, and MoE experts are processed independently.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Models and weakening variants.

We evaluate WISD on five reasoning models: Qwen3-4B ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4)), Qwen3.5-4B ([Qwen Team, 2026a](#bib.bib5)),
OLMo-3-7B-Think ([Team Olmo et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)),
Qwen3.6-27B ([Qwen Team, 2026b](#bib.bib51)), and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B ([Qwen
Team, 2026c](#bib.bib52)). These models span diverse sizes, families,
and attention architectures, covering both dense and mixture-of-experts
(MoE) designs. For each model, we compare the original checkpoint with
students trained using the quantization and pruning constructions in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Constructing a Weaker Self ‣ 3 Method ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models"),
denoted by WISD (Quantization) and WISD (Pruning). The main results use
$`b=3`$ for quantization and $`\tau=1/6`$ for pruning. Both variants
initialize the student from the original model.

#### Training data.

We train separate mathematics and coding students using 3,200 prompts
per domain from Skywork-OR1-RL-Data ([He et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)) and
KlearReasoner-CodeSub-15K ([Su et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)), respectively.
Each original model generates four responses per prompt to form its own
cached trajectories for self-distillation.

#### Benchmarks and metrics.

We evaluate mathematical reasoning on AIME 2024,
AIME 2025 ([Mathematical Association of America, 2026](#bib.bib12)), and
HMMT 2025 ([Harvard–MIT Mathematics Tournament, 2025](#bib.bib13)), and
code generation on LiveCodeBench (LCB) v5 and v6 ([Jain et al.,
2025](#bib.bib11)). We report mean accuracy over 32 samples per
mathematics problem (Avg@32) and four per coding problem (Avg@4).

#### Training and evaluation settings.

We follow Lightning Weave’s offline DOPD training procedure ([Wu et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib35)), using a learning rate of $`10^{-6}`$, global batch
size 64, and distillation coefficient $`\alpha=2`$. Full training and
evaluation settings are provided in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Experimental Details ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models").

#### Comparison methods.

We include published results from OPSD ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)), SDPO ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)),
PW-OPSD ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib53)), and LOPD ([Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib54)) for the corresponding models and benchmarks. These
methods use reference solutions or feedback-conditioned self-teachers.

### 4.2 Main Results

Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models")
reports accuracy across five reasoning models and five mathematics and
code benchmarks. The quantization and pruning variants of WISD improve
average accuracy over the original models by 4.0 and 5.5 percentage
points, respectively, with positive gains in 21 and 23 of the 25
model–benchmark settings. On both Qwen3-4B and OLMo-3-7B-Think, the two
constructions improve the mathematics and code averages and are
competitive with OPSD and SDPO. Both variants also surpass the reported
five-benchmark averages of baselines that use privileged information.
The largest gains occur on Qwen3.5-4B, where quantization and pruning
increase the five-benchmark average by 11.3 and 13.4 points,
respectively. In particular, pruning raises its HMMT 2025 accuracy from
57.7% to 70.5% and LCB v6 accuracy from 39.1% to 56.7%. The gains also
extend to larger dense and MoE models, where pruning improves
Qwen3.6-27B’s average accuracy by 5.2 points and quantization improves
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B’s by 5.9 points. On the latter, HMMT 2025 and LCB v6
accuracies rise from 68.5% to 74.6% and from 61.8% to 69.5%,
respectively. These results demonstrate that WISD turns simple weight
degradation into effective self-distillation supervision, improving
reasoning accuracy across model families, scales, and architectures
without privileged information.

### 4.3 Ablation Study

We study quantization precision and pruning threshold using Qwen3.5-4B
on HMMT25 (Avg@32), varying the hyperparameters $`b`$ and $`\tau`$ to
control the strength of quantization and pruning respectively while
keeping all other settings the same as
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models").
The sweeps cover $`b\in\{8,6,4,3,2\}`$ and
$`\tau\in\{1/16,1/8,1/6,1/4,1/2\}`$. We report both student accuracy and
average generated length.

Figure 3: Ablations of weak-model construction on Qwen3.5-4B, evaluated
on HMMT25. (a) Quantization bit width. (b) Pruning threshold. Each pair
shows accuracy (Avg@32, left plot) and average generated length in
thousands of tokens (right plot). Dashed lines denote Base accuracy and
average generated length. Outlined diamonds mark the default settings

accuracy at intermediate weakening strengths. This pattern highlights
the importance of balancing contrast strength and signal quality for

#### Quantization strength.

Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models")(a)
shows a non-monotonic relationship between weight precision and student
accuracy. Reducing precision from 8 to 3 bits raises accuracy from
56.04% to 68.33%, while a further reduction to 2 bits lowers it to
49.90%. After self-distillation, the student also generates shorter
responses than the Base model. This pattern suggests that mild
quantization provides too little contrast for substantial gains over the
original model, whereas excessive quantization erodes the weak model’s
reasoning ability and allows noise to overwhelm useful contrast.
Moderate quantization balances contrast strength and signal quality,
with $`b=3`$ giving the best result in this sweep and serving as our
default.

#### Pruning threshold.

Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models")(b)
shows a similar intermediate range of effective constructions. Accuracy
rises from 60.94% at $`\tau=1/16`$ to 69.38% at $`\tau=1/8`$ and 70.52%
at the default $`\tau=1/6`$, then falls to 54.79% and 49.58% at
$`\tau=1/4`$ and $`1/2`$. After self-distillation, the student also
generates shorter responses than the Base model. Similarly, the pruning
results favor an intermediate level of damage that creates informative
contrast while preserving the weak model’s reasoning ability. Our
default threshold $`\tau=1/6`$ therefore combines the highest accuracy
with the shortest responses in this sweep.

## 6 Conclusion

We introduced WISD, a self-distillation framework that improves
reasoning models without privileged information. The core idea is to
construct a weaker counterpart of the original model and turn their
token-level log-probability contrast into dense supervision on
self-generated trajectories. We instantiate this idea with low-bit
quantization and magnitude pruning, enabling training from the original
checkpoint and unlabeled prompts. Experiments across diverse reasoning
models and benchmarks demonstrate broad accuracy gains. Our analyses
show that weak-model contrasts emphasize exploration and verification
while providing training directions beyond confidence reinforcement.
These results establish model weakening as an effective source of
supervision for improving reasoning through self-distillation.

### A.2 Evaluation and Published Baselines

#### Benchmarks and metrics.

AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and HMMT 2025 each contain 30 problems, evaluated
with 32 responses per problem. LiveCodeBench v5 and v6 contain 279 and
131 problems, respectively, evaluated with four responses per problem.
Avg@$`n`$ is the fraction of correct responses across all problems and
their $`n`$ samples. Math Avg. equally weights the three mathematics
benchmarks, Code Avg. equally weights the two coding benchmarks, and the
final Avg. equally weights all five. We compute averages from unrounded
scores. AIME 2024 also serves as the development benchmark for exploring
weak-model constructions.

#### Generation settings.

We use each model’s native chat template with thinking enabled,
temperature $`0.6`$, top-$`p=0.95`$, and top-$`k`$ disabled. Our
main-table evaluations use a 40,960-token output limit and a
49,152-token context window. Mean response length counts generated
tokens, excluding the prompt.

#### Published results.

OPSD and PW-OPSD mathematics results come from Table 4 of [Liu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib53), with the Moderate schedule for PW-OPSD. These use
Avg@12 averaged over three evaluation seeds, temperature $`1.0`$,
top-$`p=0.95`$, and a 38,912-token output limit. Qwen3-4B SDPO
mathematics results come from Table 1 of [Wu et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib55), also using Avg@12, temperature $`1.0`$,
top-$`p=0.95`$, and a 38,912-token output limit. OPSD, SDPO, and LOPD
code results come from Table 2 of [Zhang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib54),
using pass@1 on the same LCB tasks, temperature $`0.7`$, top-$`p=0.95`$,
top-$`k=20`$, and a 16,384-token output limit.

### A.3 Evaluation Prompts and Answer Extraction

#### Mathematics prompt.

Before application of the model’s native chat template, each mathematics
example is represented exactly as

> Question: {Problem}
> Answer:

We enable the model’s native reasoning mode where available and stop on
its end-of-sequence or end-of-turn token. For every problem we
independently sample 64 responses at temperature 0.6 and top-$`p=0.95`$,
allowing at most 32,768 generated tokens. We extract the last balanced
\boxed{...} expression, remove superficial formatting such as
whitespace, delimiters, and units, and canonicalize integer answers
before exact comparison with the reference. We do not use a
computer-algebra system to grant additional symbolic equivalences. A
length-truncated response is still scored if it contains a valid
extracted answer.

#### LiveCodeBench prompt.

We use LiveCodeBench’s CodeQwenInstruct generation format. Its user
message begins with:

> You will be given a question (problem specification) and will generate
> a correct Python program that matches the specification and passes all
> tests. You will NOT return anything except for the program.
> Question: {question_content}

For problems with starter code, the message then asks the model to
complete the supplied code and enclose the answer in code delimiters.
Otherwise it instructs the model to read from standard input, write to
standard output, and return the program inside a Python code block
initialized with \# YOUR CODE HERE. The official system wrapper is You
are a helpful assistant., expressed with the Qwen instruction
delimiters. We sample four responses per problem at temperature 0.6 and
top-$`p=0.95`$, with a maximum of 40,960 generated tokens.

We use LiveCodeBench’s official code extraction and test execution. A
sample is correct only if its extracted program passes every test for
that problem. Tests run in isolated, read-only containers with the
benchmark’s fast-test mode and a six-second timeout per test. Runtime
errors, timeouts, extraction failures, and incomplete programs are
scored as incorrect.

### A.4 Weak-Model Construction Details

#### Quantization.

RTN uses symmetric group-wise quantization with 128 input coordinates
per output-row group, except for the group-32 variant. The signed grid
has $`2^{b}-1`$ levels, so 2-bit RTN uses $`\{-1,0,1\}`$. W4A8 and W4A4
combine 4-bit RTN weights with per-token activation quantization at 8
and 4 bits, respectively. Smooth W4A4 applies SmoothQuant-style
input-channel and weight rescaling with smoothing exponent $`0.5`$
before quantization ([Xiao et al., 2023](#bib.bib59)). GPTQ uses 3-bit
or 2-bit weights, group size 128, and damping $`0.01`$ ([Frantar et al.,
2023](#bib.bib58)).

#### Pruning.

Our default applies the group-relative threshold $`\tau=1/6`$ and
restores each complete output row’s $`\ell_{2}`$ norm, as defined in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Constructing a Weaker Self ‣ 3 Method ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models").
The additional per-row magnitude variants remove the smallest 20% or 40%
of weights in each row. Wanda ranks weights by magnitude times the
clean-model input-activation norm, using either per-row sparsity or a
2:4 pattern ([Sun et al., 2024](#bib.bib60)). SparseGPT uses input
covariance statistics with damping $`0.01`$, at 50% sparsity or with a
2:4 pattern ([Frantar and Alistarh, 2023](#bib.bib61)). MLP channel
pruning removes the lowest-ranked 25% of channels according to the
product of the down-projection column norm and its input-activation
norm, jointly masking the corresponding gate/up rows and down columns.

#### Calibration and scoring.

The calibrated alternatives use 128 cached training sequences from the
corresponding domain, with up to 2,048 tokens per sequence. Calibration
statistics are collected from the unchanged original model. GPTQ and
SparseGPT accumulate and factorize their covariance matrices in FP64.
The Qwen3-4B construction studies use FP32 scorer forwards and BF16
student training. The Qwen3.5-4B sweep uses BF16 scorers, except for the
default pruning student, which uses the FP32-scored run from the main
comparison. All constructions modify the frozen weak scorer and retain
the student’s original architecture.

## Appendix C Training-Signal Analysis

### C.1 Token-Level Training Signal

At each state $`s`$, the cache retains 16 original-model token
candidates $`\mathcal{S}`$ and aggregates the remaining vocabulary into
an Other bucket. Let $`b`$ be the cached behavior distribution over
these 17 buckets and $`m=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}}b_{j}`$. Scorer
probabilities are normalized over the full vocabulary before gathering
the candidates. We set $`r_{j}=r_{w}(a_{j}\mid s)`$ for retained
candidates and $`r_{\textsc{Other}}=0`$.

The offline implementation uses the tilted target with behavior-weighted
log-probability regression:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{j}=\frac{b_{j}\exp(r_{j}/\alpha)}{\sum_{k}b_{k}\exp(r_{k}/\alpha)},\qquad\ell(p)=\frac{\alpha}{2m}\sum_{j}b_{j}\bigl(\log p_{j}-\log q_{j}\bigr)^{2},
``` |  | (10) |

where $`p=\operatorname{softmax}(z)`$ is the student’s bucket
distribution. At $`p=b`$, the initial gradient with respect to the
bucket logits is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{j}=\left.\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z_{j}}\right|_{p=b}=-\frac{b_{j}}{m}\left(r_{j}-\sum_{k}b_{k}r_{k}\right).
``` |  | (11) |

This expression centers each candidate’s contrast against the
behavior-weighted mean. For each position $`t`$, we measure the signal
magnitude by $`s_{t}=\|g_{t}\|_{1}`$. The share assigned to a reasoning
role $`C`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{SignalShare}(C)=100\,\frac{\sum_{t\in C}s_{t}}{\sum_{t}s_{t}}.
``` |  | (12) |

The token-share baseline is the fraction of positions assigned to that
role. Quantization and pruning are evaluated on the same prefixes, token
candidates, and behavior probabilities.

### C.2 Contextual Reasoning Roles

We select every 200th response from the 12,800 Qwen3-4B mathematics
training trajectories, obtaining 64 responses and 124,923 active
positions. Response segments are labeled by their function in the
surrounding reasoning using the eight-role ThinkARM taxonomy ([Li et
al., 2026a](#bib.bib62)).
Table [8](#A3.T8 "Table 8 ‣ C.2 Contextual Reasoning Roles ‣ Appendix C Training-Signal Analysis ‣ Weaken to Strengthen: Weakening-Induced On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Reasoning Models")
summarizes the roles, together with a Formatting category for structural
tokens and whitespace.

| Role |  Function |
|----|----|
| Read |  Restates the given facts, conditions, or requested result. |
| Analyze |  Explains a concept, builds a representation, or makes a logical deduction. |
| Plan |  Commits to an action before carrying it out. |
| Implement |  Executes calculations, substitutions, or other concrete operations. |
| Explore |  Proposes a tentative approach or uncertain possibility. |
| Verify |  Checks an existing result or approach against the problem’s conditions. |
| Monitor |  Comments briefly on the reasoning process. |
| Answer |  States a proposed or final answer to the original question. |
| Formatting |  Marks structural tokens or whitespace-only spans. |

Table 8: Contextual roles used for the training-signal analysis. The
eight reasoning roles follow ThinkARM ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib62)).
Labels reflect each segment’s function in the response.

Three assistant annotators label 6,648 content segments using the
problem, surrounding response, and a shared codebook, without access to
model scores or construction identities. An independent assistant
reviews 128 sampled segments, with 84.4% agreement before adjudication.
The labels are finalized before aggregating signal statistics. A token
receives the label of the segment overlapping the largest number of its
non-whitespace UTF-8 bytes, with ties assigned to the earlier segment.

(b) Preserving the edge constraint Qwen3.5-4B $`\cdot`$ HMMT25

Question. In an $`11\times 11`$ grid, adjacent cells share a door. Find

Original (incorrect)

The problem asks for the maximum number of doors Karthik can walk

\[…\]

\[…\]

\[…\]
````

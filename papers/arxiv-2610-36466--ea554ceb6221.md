# Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation

Shufan Li\*    Hanrong Ye\*    Greg Heinrich\*    Yonggan Fu    Haotian

^(\*)Core Contribution Corresponding author: X

###### Abstract

We propose Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni (NLD-Uni), a unified discrete
diffusion large language model (DLLM) for visual understanding and
generation tasks. Compared with prior unified diffusion models, NLD-Uni
introduces two major innovations. First, we propose a unified
cross-paradigm on-policy distillation (OPD) framework that successfully
transfers the visual understanding ability of autoregressive VLMs and
visual generation ability of continuous flow models, to a unified DLLM.
Second, NLD-Uni incorporates a novel progressive self-refining diffusion
process that gradually corrupts each token across multiple steps during
training, as opposed to adopting the conventional masked diffusion
formulation that directly corrupts a clean image token to an
uninformative mask token. At inference, this design allows NLD-Uni to
make intermediate commit to a token, enabling progressive
self-refinement for better visual quality. Through extensive
experiments, we show that NLD-Uni achieves state-of-the-art performance
among unified DLLMs on diverse tasks including visual
question-answering, multi-modal reasoning, object grounding,
text-to-image generation, and image editing.
[Model](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NLD-Uni) and
[Code](https://github.com/NVlabs/Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni) are
publicly available.

## 1 Introduction

Unified Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) \[[1](#bib.bib9),
[2](#bib.bib27), [3](#bib.bib34), [4](#bib.bib37)\] have achieved strong
performance on diverse multimodal understanding and generation tasks.
Compared with specialist models, they learn to perform diverse tasks via
a unified sequence modeling interface, allowing synergetic learning that
benefits each individual tasks \[[5](#bib.bib35)\]. They generally fall
into two categories. The first design represents text and image as
discrete tokens and employs a unified autoregressive (AR) modeling
paradigm \[[6](#bib.bib101), [4](#bib.bib37), [7](#bib.bib69)\]. The
second design combines autoregressive modeling for text generation and
continuous diffusion for image generation (AR+Diff) \[[5](#bib.bib35),
[8](#bib.bib36), [9](#bib.bib65), [10](#bib.bib98)\]. Several recent
works such as LaViDa-O \[[11](#bib.bib68)\] and MMaDA
\[[12](#bib.bib29)\] explored a third alternative that builds a unified
discrete diffusion language model (unified DLLM) with masked diffusion
paradigm (MDM). Unlike AR models or continuous flow models, MDMs
randomly mask discrete tokens in a sequence and learn to predict the
masked tokens during training. At inference, MDMs initialize a fully
masked sequence and gradually unmask tokens across multiple diffusion
steps. Compared with AR models, MDMs offer faster inference speed
because they can generate multiple tokens in parallel
\[[13](#bib.bib11), [14](#bib.bib67)\]. Compared with AR+Diff setup,
which needs to carefully balance conflicting AR and Diffusion objectives
\[[5](#bib.bib35)\], unified DLLMs offer a single objective for both
text and image, resulting in simplicity and, more importantly,
scalability.

Despite these advantages, a substantial performance gap remains between
existing unified DLLMs and domain-specialized VLMs and continuous flow
models \[[12](#bib.bib29), [11](#bib.bib68)\]. In this work, we seek to
narrow these gaps by introducing NLD-Uni, a unified DLLM featuring a new
training paradigm and new architecture.

First, although on-policy distillation (OPD) has proven highly effective
in improving the visual understanding capabilities of VLMs
\[[15](#bib.bib71)\] and the visual generation capabilities of
continuous flow models \[[16](#bib.bib6)\], adapting it to unified MDMs
is challenging because of a lack of strong-performing MDM teachers.
Distilling from strong AR VLMs and continuous flow models is not
feasible because they follow fundamentally different paradigms. To
overcome this challenge, we propose cross-paradigm on-policy
distillation (OPD), which enables NLD-Uni to learn from teachers with
different generation paradigms, including an AR VLM teacher for visual
understanding and a continuous flow teacher for visual generation.

Second, for visual generation tasks specifically, MDMs lack the
self-refinement capabilities of continuous flow models. At inference,
each token jumps directly from a masked state to a clean state and stays
unchanged afterwards. Since MDMs sample tokens independently from
per-token logit prediction, all tokens sampled at the same diffusion
step are assumed to be conditionally independent. This assumption rarely
holds in practice since tokens from the same image are highly
correlated. To address this limitation and enable iterative refinement,
we introduce a self-refining masked diffusion process that incorporates
new intermediate states between the clean state and mask state. By
breaking down the one-step jump to a multi-step transition, NLD-Uni
enables fine-grained inter-token interaction and progressive
self-correction, resulting in better visual quality.

To evaluate the effectiveness of NLD-Uni, we conduct extensive
evaluations on a diverse range of multimodal understanding and
generation tasks, including visual question answering, object grounding,
multimodal reasoning, text-to-image generation, and image editing.
Experiments show that NLD-Uni achieves state-of-the-art performance
among unified DLLMs and greatly reduces the gap to specialized AR VLMs
and continuous flow models.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: 1) We
develop a cross-paradigm OPD framework that improves both visual
understanding and generation by distilling knowledge from non-DLLM
teachers. 2) We introduce a self-refining masked diffusion process that
improves visual generation quality over the canonical masked diffusion
process. 3) We present NLD-Uni, a state-of-the-art unified DLLM capable
of both understanding and generating visual content. We acknowledge that
while extensive experiments show the effectiveness of our proposed
methods, the model can be further improved via better data mixture and
architecture optimization.

## 2 Preliminaries and Related Works

### 2.1 Diffusion Language Models

Discrete diffusion language models \[[17](#bib.bib10), [13](#bib.bib11),
[18](#bib.bib13)\] generate multiple text tokens in parallel, making
them more efficient than AR models. However, its bidirectional attention
introduces overhead compared to causal attention in AR models,
particular for long sequences. Several works \[[19](#bib.bib12),
[20](#bib.bib74)\] address this limitation through block-wise
semi-auto-regressive generation. Notably, SBD \[[21](#bib.bib73),
[22](#bib.bib75)\] incorporates variable-sized blocks during training,
enabling flexible inference.

Formally, given a block of clean text tokens $`B^{0}`$, the forward
diffusion process gradually mask it over the continuous time interval
$`t\sim[0,1]`$ until it reaches a fully masked block $`B^{1}`$
containing only the mask token \[m\]. Given a multi-block token sequence
$`X=[B_{1}^{0},B_{2}^{0},...B_{N}^{0}]`$, SBD models learn to predict
the clean block $`B_{i}^{0}`$ from its partially masked version
$`B_{i}^{t}`$ conditioned on all previous clean block. The optimization
objective is defined as follows:

|     |                                                                                                                                 |                            |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{SBD}}=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\{1,2,..N\},t\sim[0,1],X\sim\mathcal{D}}[-\frac{1}{t}\log p(B\_{i}^{0} | B*{j<i}^{0},B*{i}^{t})],`$ |     | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{D}`$ is the data distribution. At inference, the blocks
are sampled sequentially, but tokens in the same block are sampled in
parallel across multiple diffusion steps. NLD-Uni applies the SBD
paradigm to unified multimodal modeling and treats each image as an
integral block, whose size is resolution-dependent. Note that when block
size reduces to 1, SBD models reduce to an AR model.

### 2.2 Masked Discrete Image Models

Masked image models generate images by initializing a fully-masked image
and gradually unmasking image patches. Each patch is encoded to a
discrete code through vector quantization (VQ) \[[23](#bib.bib30)\].
MaskGiT \[[24](#bib.bib28)\] pioneered this paradigm. Messonic
\[[25](#bib.bib39)\] scaled it to large-scale T2I. Recent works such as
LaViDa-O and MMaDa \[[11](#bib.bib68), [12](#bib.bib29)\] explored
integrating masked image modeling to unified MLLMs. As discussed in Sec.
[1](#S1 "1 Introduction ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation"),
these models lack the self-refinement capabilities of continuous flow
models. Some work mitigate this problem using a specialized unmasking
schedule that prioritizes unmasking less-correlated tokens
\[[26](#bib.bib40), [11](#bib.bib68)\]. Others explored revising already
unmasked \[[27](#bib.bib76), [28](#bib.bib70)\]. However, these works
are sensitive to heuristic-based confidence thresholds and still present
a mismatch between training and inference setup.

### 2.3 On-Policy Distillation (OPD)

OPD \[[29](#bib.bib1), [30](#bib.bib2)\] enables capability transfers
from a teacher model to a student model by providing teacher supervision
signals on student-generated sequences. M-OPD \[[31](#bib.bib5)\]
extends OPD to a multi-teacher setting to consolidate the capabilities
of domain-specific experts. Several works \[[32](#bib.bib81),
[33](#bib.bib80)\] explored OPD for multimodal understanding tasks.
Diffusion-OPD \[[16](#bib.bib6)\] further applies it to continuous flow
models for visual generation tasks. However, OPD for DLLMs is currently
underexplored. OPDLM \[[34](#bib.bib4)\] is the most relevant work, but
its scope is limited to same-model distillation that converts a pure AR
model to a DLLM with weaker performance but faster inference. Unlike
prior work, we propose cross-paradigm OPD to jointly distill knowledge
from a strong AR VLM teacher and an image diffusion generation teacher
into a unified DLLM.

Beyond the three areas above, NLD-Uni is closely related to broader
literature on unified MLLMs, and we provide more discussion in Appendix
[D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Unified Architecture. ‣ Appendix D Additional Related Works ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation").

### 3.1 Model Architecture

NLD-Uni adopts a two-tower Elastic Mixture-of-Transformer (Elastic MoT)
design following Lavida-O \[[11](#bib.bib68)\], which consists of an
understanding expert tower and generation expert tower. They interact
with each other through a joint attention mechanism. The understanding
tower is initialized with a diffusion language model NLD
\[[35](#bib.bib82)\], which uses the block-causal SBD formulation
described in Section
[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Works ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation").
The generation tower is initialized from an identical copy. Following
the common design of unified MLLMs \[[5](#bib.bib35),
[11](#bib.bib68)\], we encode images with both a semantic encoder and VQ
encoder. Once images and texts are tokenized, the canonical SBD
formulation can be directly applied. Elastic-MoT architecture is
illustrated in Figure

### 3.2 Discrete Diffusion with Progressive Self-Refinement

The canonical mask discrete diffusion uses binary mask states, where a
token directly jumps between the mask and clean states. Differently, our
NLD-Uni incorporates intermediate states using the concept of token age
$`a\in\mathcal{A}=\{0,1,2...K\}`$. In particular, $`a=0`$ implies a
token is in the clean state, $`a=K`$ implies a token is in the
fully-masked state. Crucially, $`a`$ is a token-local discrete index and
is different from the global continuous timestep $`t\in[0,1]`$.

Formally, given a vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$, we adopt the state space
$`S=\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{A}`$ as opposed to
$`S=\mathcal{V}\cup\{[m]\}`$ of standard MDMs. This is shown in Figure
[4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.2 Discrete Diffusion with Progressive Self-Refinement ‣ 3 Method ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
(Left). We define the self-refining diffusion process as the interaction
of a discrete time per-token corruption process and a continuous time
per-token jump schedule. Specifically, each token in $`(c,a)\in S`$ can
only transition to $`(c^{\prime},a+1)`$ in the forward process, with
$`(*,0)`$ being the initial states and $`(*,K)`$ being the terminal
states. The transition probability is defined as $`U_{k|k-1}(u|c)`$,
which defines the probability of a token $`(c,k-1)`$ transits to
$`(c,k)`$. This generates a discrete time per-token corruption process
where each token undergoes $`K`$ jumps from initial clean state and
terminal mask state. Given a token $`X^{0}_{i}=(C_{i}^{0},A_{i}^{0})`$,
the continuous time per-token jump schedule
$`b_{i}^{t}(a)=\mathbb{P}(A_{i}^{t}=a)`$ determines when the jumps occur
for each token in the interval $`[0,1]`$. It satisfies
$`b_{i}^{0}(0)=1`$ and $`b_{i}^{1}(K)=1`$ at the initial and terminal
states.

Figure 4: Design of Self-Refinement Process. (Left) We extend the state
space of mask diffusion with token age $`a`$ with $`a=0`$ being the
clean state and $`a=K`$ being the masked state. (Middle) Instead of
one-step jump, each token is progressively masked through the interval
$`(\tau,\tau^{\prime})`$ with length $`\epsilon`$. Colors encode
different tokens. (Right) We implement the progression from age $`a=0`$
to $`a=K`$ through random jumps based on a ball-tree in the embedding
space. Token distributions are diffused from a one-hot vector to balls
with growing sizes. This example has tree depth $`D=4`$.

Per token Corruption Process. The per-step transition rate defined above
generates the marginal $`U_{j|k}(u|c)`$ with $`0\leq k\leq j\leq K`$. It
must satisfy $`U_{K|k}(u|c)=Q(u)`$, where $`Q(u)`$ is a stationary
distribution over $`\mathcal{V}`$, for all $`k<K`$ because the terminal
state is semantically fully masked and cannot correlate to the initial
states. We implement this by randomly replacing a token c with a sampled
token u in the VQ embedding space, where the jump distance depends on
the token age. Concretely, we construct a balanced binary ball tree
\[[36](#bib.bib84)\] which is commonly used in efficient nearest
neighbor search. Each node in the ball tree represents a
high-dimensional ball. in the embedding space, with parent nodes having
a larger radius than child nodes. Let $`N_{d}(c)`$ be the set of all
tokens that share a common ancestor to $`c`$ at depth $`d`$, and $`D`$
be the tree depth, it satisfies $`N_{1}(c)=\mathcal{V}`$ for the root
node and $`N_{D}(c)=\{c\}`$ for the leaf node. An example of this
structure is shown in Figure
[4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.2 Discrete Diffusion with Progressive Self-Refinement ‣ 3 Method ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
(top-right), where we visualize a 4-depth tree.

We define $`U_{k|{k-1}}(u|c)`$ as the uniform distribution over
$`N_{D-k}(c)`$, which gives the following forward and reverse marginal:

|     |                       |      |                                            |
| --- | --------------------- | ---- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------- | ------------- | ---- | ---------------- | ---- | ----------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle U\_{j | k}(u | c)=\mathbf{1}\{u\in N\_{D-j}(c)\}\frac{1}{ | N\_{D-j}(c) | };\quad U\_{k | j}(c | u,\hat{c})=U\_{k | 0}(c | \hat{c}),\forall u\in N\_{D-j}(\hat{c})`$ |     | (2) |

where $`\hat{c}`$ is the initial token state at $`a=0`$, and
$`0\leq k<j\leq K`$. LHS satisfies that the initial distribution is a
one-hot distribution of the clean token and the terminal distribution is
the uniform distribution over all masked states $`(*,K)`$. RHS can be
used at inference by substituting the clean token $`\hat{c}`$ with a
model prediction. We defer full derivation to Appendix

(bottom-right), illustrates the progression of marginal

Per-Token Jump Schedule embeds the discrete jumps in the continuous
interval $`[0,1]`$. We adopted DNDM \[[37](#bib.bib85)\]’s setup which
generates the same forward marginal as vanilla MDM by first sampling a
per-token jump time $`\tau_{i}\sim U([0,1])`$ for each token $`i`$. The
forward token-age transition then becomes deterministic with each token
jumps from the clean state to the mask state exactly at $`t=\tau_{i}`$.
We extend this to support multiple intermediate states by sampling the
interval $`(\tau_{i},\tau_{i}^{\prime})\subset[0,1]`$ via
$`\tau_{i}\sim U([0,1-\epsilon])`$ and set
$`\tau_{i}^{\prime}=\tau_{i}+\epsilon`$, where $`\epsilon`$ is a
hyperparameter controlling the duration of intermediate states. When
$`\epsilon\rightarrow 0`$, the process reduces to DNDM. When
$`\epsilon=1`$, all tokens are progressively corrupted at the same rate.
To minimize the complexity, we let all intermediate token ages
$`1,2,..K-1`$ to progress uniformly in $`(\tau,\tau^{\prime})`$, taking
$`\frac{\epsilon}{K}`$ time each. Given fixed $`\epsilon\in(0,1)`$, this
establishes a bijection between every instance of per-token jump
schedule to a one-step jump schedule in DNDM. This process becomes
non-Markov as we cannot marginalize $`\tau_{i}`$. At training and
inference, we presample $`(\tau_{i},\tau_{i}^{\prime})`$ for each token,
then the changes in token age become deterministic. The forward and
reverse marginal can be obtained by directly applying Equation
[2](#S3.E2 "In 3.2 Discrete Diffusion with Progressive Self-Refinement ‣ 3 Method ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
with change-of-variable. Particularly, at inference, token $`i`$ stays
masked in $`[\tau^{\prime}_{i},1]`$, is progressively refined in
$`[\tau_{i},\tau_{i}^{\prime}]`$ and stays fixed in $`[0,\tau_{i}]`$. We
defer additional details and derivations to Appendix
[A.2.2](#A1.SS2.SSS2 "A.2.2 Non-Markov Per-Token Jump Schedule ‣ A.2 Formulations of Self-Refining Diffusion Process ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation").

The most relevant work to NLD-Uni in this aspect is MDM-prime
\[[38](#bib.bib77)\], which breaks tokens into subtokens to enable
partial masking. However, its image experiments are limited to 32x32

### 3.3 Distillation of Visual Understanding Ability

To distill knowledge from AR teachers, we exploit SBD’s ability to
operate with varying block sizes. As described in Section
[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Works ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation"),
SBD models the conditional distribution
$`p(B_{i}^{0}\mid B_{1}^{0},\ldots,B_{i-1}^{0},B_{i}^{t})`$ for a
sequence of blocks $`X=[B_{1}^{0},\ldots,B_{N}^{0}]`$, predicting each
clean block from its corrupted version and the preceding clean blocks.
When block size is one, SBD reduces to a standard AR model (AR-view).
Hence, we can directly supervise the student’s AR view with an AR
teacher on student’s AR rollouts. However, using AR OPD loss alone would
lose the capability of generating multiple tokens without any
supervision on the diffusion view.

A naive design is to directly copy the teacher score to the diffusion
following OPD-LM \[[34](#bib.bib4)\], however it creates ill-formed
supervison signals due to context mismatch. As a concrete example,
consider the student answer "Jason visited San Francisco" for a QA task.
A teacher may want to respond "Jason visited New York" conditioned on
the first two words and assigns high confidence to the token "New". This
supervision target is reasonable in AR setting. However, if we employ a
block size of 2 and consider the partial state "\[m\] Francisco",
directly copying scores of AR teacher would guide the student generation
towards generating "New Francisco", which is ill-formed.

To address this challenge, we propose a transitive distillation
objective that supervises the student’s AR view with teacher scores and
supervises the student’s diffusion view with its own AR view.
Specifically, we first generate a clean block $`B_{i}^{0}`$ and
supervise it with AR OPD loss. To supervise the diffusion view, we apply
the forward process and corrupt $`B_{i}^{0}`$ to a partially masked
version $`B_{i}^{t}`$ and minimize the KL divergence between the
student’s AR prediction and diffusion prediction. The final
understanding loss can be defined as follows:

|     |                                                                                                                   |                                                           |                                                                                                            |
| --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{OPD-und}}=\underbrace{\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(p*{\text{s}}^{\text{AR}}(B*{i}^{0} | B*{j<i}^{0})\parallel p*{\text{t}}^{\text{AR}}(B\_{i}^{0} | B*{j<i}^{0}))}*{\text{AR OPD Loss}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{s}}^{\text{AR}}(B\_{i}^{0} | B*{j<i}^{0})\parallel p*{\text{s}}^{\text{Diff}}(B\_{i}^{0} | B*{j<i}^{0},B*{i}^{t}))}\_{\text{Consistency Loss}}`$ |     | (3) |

where $`p_{s},p_{t}`$ denote student and teacher distributions. The
first term is the standard AR OPD loss, the second term is a
self-consistency loss. We apply stop-grad for $`p_{s}^{AR}`$ in
consistency loss and only evaluate masked positions. Crucially, while
the context is still different, the AR scores have good alignment
because tokens in $`B_{i}^{0}`$ are sampled from $`p_{s}^{AR}`$ with a
low temperature.¹¹ 1 We provide studies on sampling temperature in
Appendix
[C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Studies on distilling from AR VLMs ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiment Results ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")

where the student is initialized with the teacher weights. The teacher’s
gap to the student’s AR-view is initially 0. Our setup distills an AR
teacher that is larger and stronger to an already trained student
diffusion model, which is more general but has a considerable
teacher-student distribution gap at initialization, creating ill-formed
signals when applying OPD naively.²² 2 OPD-LM also finds that for

### 3.4 Distillation of Visual Generation Ability

Distilling continuous flow models to DLLM is more challenging as flow
models operate in continuous latent spaces, making exact KL-style
distribution matching infeasible. However, the general notion of using
teacher supervision on student-generated sequences is still feasible. In
this work, we explore cross-paradigm OPD for image editing tasks with
Qwen-Image \[[39](#bib.bib72)\] teacher. Given a student output, we
first re-encode it with the teacher’s continuous VAE. We then create a
teacher supervision target by first adding noise to the student
generation in teacher’s latent space and re-denoise it with the teacher
model, conditioned on the input image and instruction. We call this
target teacher-refined student output. This pipeline is shown in Figure
[6](#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 3.3 Distillation of Visual Understanding Ability ‣ 3 Method ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
(right). We observe that teacher-refined images are semantically and
spatially aligned to student generation, but with reduced artifacts,
higher fidelity, and better source-image preservation. By contrast,
independent teacher generations exhibit a larger gap to student
generation. This distinction makes the refined samples ideal candidates
for training targets. We also found that teacher-refined outputs can be
better than independent teacher generations where the teacher model
makes mistakes while the student model does not. We defer more
discussion and examples to Appendix
[14](#A1.F14 "Figure 14 ‣ A.4 On-policy Distillation from continuous flow models ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation").

We adopt the canonical SFT loss on the teacher-refined samples. While
this is not “on-policy” in the conventional sense, it constructs
teacher-refined targets from the current student’s own rollouts,
adapting supervision to its evolving generation distribution. It closely
connects to several concurrent OPD studies in AR language modeling
\[[40](#bib.bib93)\], which also explored using teacher models to
correct student rollouts.

Setup. We train NLD-Uni in a three-stage manner. The first stage
primarily includes text-image pairs for basic visual alignment and image
generation capabilities. The second stage introduces additional data for
instruction following, image editing, object grounding, and interleaved

include an OPD teacher for T2I tasks because the base model’s

## 5 Conclusion

Diffusion language models have attracted considerable attention in
recent years for their greater efficiency compared with autoregressive
models and their unified interface for multimodal data. However, their
performance in practice still lags behind that of state-of-the-art
models, limiting their adoption. In this paper, we propose two methods
for developing more capable DLLMs: cross-paradigm on-policy
distillation, which transfers knowledge from diverse teacher models, and
a self-refining diffusion process, which improves visual generation
quality. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of both
approaches. We hope NLD-Uni will offer valuable insights for future
research on DLLMs and unified multimodal systems.

## Appendix A Additional Technical Details

### A.1 Formulations of SBD Models

This section briefly describes the formulation of SBD models. NLD-Uni
largely adopts its canonical form.

Given a sequence of discrete tokens
$`X^{0}=[X_{1}^{0},X_{2}^{0},..X_{L}^{0}]`$, AR models generate tokens
by repetitively sampling from the next-token prediction
$`p(X_{i}^{0}|X^{0}_{j<i})`$. By contrast, masked discrete diffusion
models (MDMs) initialize a fully masked sequence $`X^{1}=[[m],[m],...]`$
and gradually unmask tokens during the continuous interval $`[0,1]`$
through the reverse process $`p(X^{s}|X^{t})`$, where
$`0\leq s\leq t\leq 1`$, until the sequence reaches a clean state
$`X^{0}`$ that consists of only non-mask tokens. At each step, tokens
are sampled independently by assuming the factorization
$`p(X^{s}|X^{t})=\prod_{i}p(X^{s}_{i}|X^{t})`$. Since
$`p(X^{s}_{i}|X^{t})`$ depends on the entire sequence $`X^{t}`$ for
every token position $`i`$, MDMs employ bidirectional attention over the
full sequence as opposed to causal attention in AR models. Consequently,
MDMs suffer from efficiency bottlenecks despite being capable of
generating multiple tokens in parallel.

To address this limitation, models like Block Diffusion
\[[19](#bib.bib12)\], SDAR \[[20](#bib.bib74)\] and SBD
\[[21](#bib.bib73)\] adopt a semi-autoregressive generation paradigm.

Specifically, the full sequence
$`X^{0}=[X_{1}^{0},X_{2}^{0},..X_{L}^{0}]`$ is broken down into multiple
consecutive multi-token blocks $`X^{0}=[B_{1}^{0},...B_{K}^{0}]`$. The
tokens within each block are generated with the standard MDM sampling
process, but the blocks are sampled sequentially from left to right.
This design only requires within-block bidirectional attention, greatly
reducing the compute cost of fully bidirectional MDMs.

Formally, let $`X_{b,i}^{t}`$ denote the $`i`$-th token in the $`b`$-th
block. The forward diffusion process is defined as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
q(X_{b,i}^{t}|X_{b,i}^{s})=\begin{cases}\textbf{M},&\text{if }X_{b,i}^{s}=[M]\\
\frac{1-t}{1-s}\mathbf{X_{b,i}^{s}}+\frac{t-s}{1-s}\textbf{M},&\text{if }X_{b,i}^{s}\neq[M],\end{cases}
``` |  | (4) |

where $`0\leq s\leq t\leq 1`$, and $`\textbf{M},\mathbf{X_{b,i}^{s}}`$
are one-hot probability vectors corresponding to the mask token \[m\]
and the clean token $`X_{b,i}^{s}`$.

When $`s=0`$, we obtain the following marginal:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q(X_{b,i}^{t}|X_{b,i}^{0})=(1-t)\mathbf{X_{b,i}^{0}}+t\textbf{M}.
``` |  | (5) |

The posterior of the reverse process $`p(X^{s}|X^{t},X^{0})`$ has the
following form \[[13](#bib.bib11)\]:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p(X_{b,i}^{s}|X_{b,i}^{0},X_{b,i}^{t})=\begin{cases}\mathbf{X_{b,i}^{t}}&\text{if }X_{b,i}^{t}\neq[M]\\
\tfrac{t-s}{t}\mathbf{X_{b,i}^{0}}+\tfrac{s}{t}\textbf{M},&\text{if }X_{b,i}^{t}=[M].\end{cases}
``` |  | (6) |

.

Practically, a neural network $`\theta`$ is used to model the clean
token prediction $`p_{\theta}(X_{b,i}^{0}|B^{0}_{j<b},B_{b}^{t})`$
conditioned on the partially masked current block $`B_{b}^{t}`$ and all
previous clean blocks $`B^{0}_{j<b}`$, which gives the following
transition:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{\theta}(X_{b,i}^{s}|B_{j<b}^{0},B_{b}^{t})=\begin{cases}\mathbf{X_{b,i}^{t}}&\text{if }X_{b,i}^{t}\neq[M]\\
\tfrac{t-s}{t}p_{\theta}(X_{b,i}^{0}|B^{0}_{j<b},B_{b}^{t})+\tfrac{s}{t}\textbf{M},&\text{if }X_{b,i}^{t}=[M].\end{cases}
``` |  | (7) |

.

Figure 10: The design of SBD formulation. (Left) SBD adopts a two-stream
training process that concatenates clean blocks and noisy blocks in the
sequence dimension. It applies AR CE loss on the clean blocks, and
diffusion CE loss on the noisy blocks. (Right) SBD models use a
specialized attention mask with a causal mask in the clean blocks and
block-bidirectional mask in the noisy blocks. Each noisy block can
attend to all other tokens in itself and all tokens in preceding clean
blocks.

Training Process. SBD employs a specialized attention mask to allow
parallel training over multiple blocks. Given a sequence
$`X^{0}=[X_{1}^{0},X_{2}^{0},..X_{N}^{0}]`$ sampled from some data
distribution $`\mathcal{D}`$ and a randomly sampled timestep
$`t\sim U([0,1])`$, we first divide $`X^{0}`$ into consecutive blocks
$`B_{1}^{0},...B_{K}^{0}`$ and construct corresponding partially masked
blocks $`B_{1}^{t},...B_{K}^{t}`$ using the forward marginal in Equation
[5](#A1.E5 "In A.1 Formulations of SBD Models ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation").
The input to the model is constructed by concatenating the clean
sequence and partially masked sequence, obtaining
$`[B_{1}^{0},...B_{K}^{0},B_{1}^{t},...B_{K}^{t}]`$. A specialized
attention mask is used to ensure the correct semantics and allow for
parallel multi-block training. In particular, tokens in the clean block
$`B_{i}^{0}`$ can attend to preceding clean tokens in $`B_{i}^{0}`$ and
all tokens in preceding clean blocks $`B_{j<i}^{0}`$. Tokens in the
partially masked block $`B_{i}^{t}`$ can attend to all tokens in the
same partially masked block $`B_{i}^{t}`$ and all tokens in preceding
clean blocks $`B_{j<i}^{0}`$. The loss target is simply the concatenated
clean sequence $`Y=X^{0}|X^{0}`$ shifted by 1 position to the left. This
design is illustrated in Figure
[10](#A1.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ A.1 Formulations of SBD Models ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation").

At each training step, the block size is randomized. The loss is also
applied on the causal clean token portion of the input, which
corresponds to a block size of one and is effectively an AR view. We
note that NLD-Uni adopts a position-shifted variant of SBD
\[[22](#bib.bib75)\] to better align the AR-view and diffusion view with
a block size larger than 1, as opposed to the vanilla version
\[[21](#bib.bib73)\] which does not shift tokens on the partially masked
portion of the input and has a mismatch between the AR view and the
block-size-one diffusion view.

In pure language modeling, a sequence is divided into equally sized
blocks. NLD-Uni further allows varying-sized blocks in the same sequence
because it ensures tokens from the same image belong to the same block
and can attend to each other.

Sampling Process. At inference, we sample blocks $`B_{1},B_{2}..`$
sequentially until some block contains a termination token. Each block
is initialized with a fully masked block $`B_{i}^{1}`$ and progressively
unmasked via the parameterized reverse process in Equation
[7](#A1.E7 "In A.1 Formulations of SBD Models ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation").
In particular, we discretize the time interval \[0,1\] into discrete
steps $`0=t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}..t_{T}=1`$, and recursively query Equation
[7](#A1.E7 "In A.1 Formulations of SBD Models ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
starting from $`B_{i}^{1}`$ until we reach the clean state
$`B_{i}^{0}`$.

#### A.2.1 Ball-Tree-Based Per-Token Corruption Process

Recall from Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Discrete Diffusion with Progressive Self-Refinement ‣ 3 Method ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
that we adopt the state space $`\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{A}`$ where
$`\mathcal{V}`$ is the vocabulary of a tokenizer and
$`\mathcal{A}=\{0,1,...K\}`$ represents the set of corruption levels
that connect the fully masked state and the clean state. Each token
$`X_{i}^{t}`$ in this state space is denoted as
$`X_{i}^{t}=(C_{i}^{t},A_{i}^{t})`$ where $`C_{i}^{t}\in\mathcal{V}`$,
$`A_{i}^{t}\in\mathcal{A}`$. In particular, $`A_{i}=0`$ represents a
clean state and $`A_{i}=K`$ represents a mask state. We define the
age-dependent corruption process such that $`A_{i}`$ monotonically
increases at state transitions in the forward process.

Concretely, each token that starts at state $`(c,a)`$ for some
$`c\in\mathcal{V},a\in\mathcal{A}`$ can only transition to
$`(c^{\prime},a+1)`$, with $`(*,0)`$ being the set of initial states and
$`(*,K)`$ being the set of absorbing mask states. The corruption process
can be expressed by the transition probability $`U_{j|k}(u|c)`$ for
$`0\leq k<j\leq K`$. It must satisfy the constraint:

|     |                                      |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     | $`\displaystyle U_{K|k}(u|c)=Q(u),`$ |     | (8) |

which implies that the absorbing state $`Q`$ is an uninformative
stationary distribution over possible mask states that is not dependent
on the initial clean token $`c`$. Ideally we want the transition to be
progressive so that the state becomes gradually less informative about
the clean token $`c`$ when we progress from $`a=0`$ to $`a=K`$.

We implement this as a ball tree-based corruption process. A ball tree
is a binary tree structure commonly used for fast nearest-neighbor
search. The leaf nodes represent individual VQ codes in the continuous
embedding space of the tokenizer; each non-leaf node represents a
high-dimensional ball. The root node represents a ball enclosing all VQ
codes. Child nodes represent a smaller ball than the parent node.

Concretely, given a $`D`$-depth ball tree with $`2^{D-1}`$ leaf nodes
corresponding to tokens in $`\mathcal{V}`$, let $`N_{d}(c)`$ be the set
of all tokens that share a common ancestor with $`c`$ at depth $`d`$
where $`1\leq d\leq D`$. Notably, $`N_{1}(c)=\mathcal{V}`$ is the whole
vocabulary and $`N_{D}(c)=\{c\}`$ contains exactly one code.

We set $`K=D-1`$ as the total per-token corruption steps and associate
them with $`d=D-a`$, with $`a=0`$ corresponding to $`d=D`$ and $`a=K`$
corresponding to $`d=1`$. Concretely, we generate $`U_{j|k}`$ with:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle U_{k|k-1}(u|c)=\mathbf{1}\{u\in N_{D-k}(c)\}\frac{1}{|N_{D-k}(c)|},`$ |  | (9) |

which is a uniform probability vector over the set $`N_{D-k}(c)`$, i.e.,
we jump from code $`c`$ to a random neighboring token in the embedding
space indicated by the ball tree, with the jump range dependent on the
target token age. This process has the following marginal:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle U_{j|k}(u|c)=\mathbf{1}\{u\in N_{D-j}(c)\}\frac{1}{|N_{D-j}(c)|},`$ |  | (10) |

for $`j>k`$ because the intermediate states are always leaf nodes of
$`N_{D-j}(c)`$ by tree construction. An important observation is that
for a fixed $`u`$, $`U_{j|k}(u|c)`$ is either 0 or $`\frac{1}{2^{j}}`$
for all possible $`c\in\mathcal{V}`$.

![Refer to caption](2610.36466v1/BallTreeCorruptionComp.png)

Figure 12: Visual Reconstruction Across Different Token Ages. To
investigate whether the proposed age-based corruption process is
semantically meaningful for visual generation tasks, we show

Results indicate that changes in the token age represent a smooth
corruption process in the pixel space. In this visualization, all tokens

Let $`\hat{c}`$ be the initial token state at $`a=0`$. The conditional
reverse process has the following form according to Bayes’ rule:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle U_{k|j}(c|u,\hat{c})=\frac{U_{k|0}(c|\hat{c})U_{j|k}(u|c)}{U_{j|0}(u|\hat{c})}.`$ |  | (11) |

Note that for this value to be properly defined, we must have
$`U_{j|0}(u|\hat{c})\neq 0`$, implying $`u\in N_{D-j}(\hat{c})`$. When
$`U_{j|k}(u|c)\neq 0`$, by the previously discussed observation the two
terms cancel and the expression simplifies to $`U_{k|0}(c|\hat{c})`$.
When $`U_{j|k}(u|c)=0`$, it can be implied that $`u\notin N_{D-j}(c)`$.
Hence $`N_{D-j}(c)\neq N_{D-j}(\hat{c})`$, and consequently
$`N_{D-k}(c)\neq N_{D-k}(\hat{c})`$ because $`k`$, which satisfies
$`k<j`$, corresponds to more refined tree nodes with greater depth. This
implies that $`U_{k|0}(c|\hat{c})=0`$, so everywhere we have the reverse
process satisfying:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle U_{k|j}(c|u,\hat{c})=U_{k|0}(c|\hat{c})=\mathbf{1}\{c\in N_{D-k}(\hat{c})\}\frac{1}{|N_{D-k}(\hat{c})|}.`$ |  | (12) |

An implementation detail worth mentioning is that a model $`p_{\theta}`$
may still predict $`\hat{c}`$ such that $`u\notin N_{D-j}(\hat{c})`$ as
its output space is the entire vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$. In those
cases, we still apply the RHS by allowing the model to correct its
previous predictions. In practice, we find that NLD-Uni rarely predicts
a token that violates this setting, and the constrained setup shows no
meaningful difference from the unconstrained setup.

Figure

illustrates an example of this process with $`D=4`$. On the left side,
we visualize the set of leaf nodes $`N_{d}(c)`$ for $`d=1,2,3,4`$

Empirical Implementation. The tokenizer of NLD-Uni contains 131k
discrete codes, which corresponds to a ball tree with $`D=18`$. Hence,
the vanilla setup is $`K=17`$. To enable flexible inference, we in
practice randomize $`K`$ for each training step among the candidates
$`\{1,2,..17\}`$, where for $`K<17`$, we skip intermediate ages
uniformly. For example, $`K=4`$ corresponds to age progression
$`0,4,8,12,17`$ in the original $`K=17`$ notation.

As a final remark, we clarify that $`U_{j|k}`$ is a discrete-time

$`0,1...K`$, it should not be confused with the continuous-time

$`t,s\in[0,1]`$. The two processes are bridged by the token age field,
which determines when discrete jumps $`U_{k-1|k}`$ occur over the
continuous time interval $`[0,1]`$ for each token.

#### A.2.2 Non-Markov Per-Token Jump Schedule

An important observation in the formulation described in Section
[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Formulations of SBD Models ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
is that during training, only the marginal:

|     |                                                             |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       q(X_{i}^{t}|X_{i}^{0})=(1-t)\mathbf{X_{i}^{0}}+t\textbf{M},
       ```                                                          |     | (13) |

is used to construct the partially masked sequence $`X^{t}`$ and we
never explicitly invoke $`q(X_{i}^{t}|X_{i}^{s})`$ from Equation
[4](#A1.E4 "In A.1 Formulations of SBD Models ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
for $`s\neq 0`$. Discrete non-Markov diffusion models (DNMD)
\[[37](#bib.bib85)\] constructs a process that gives the same forward
marginal with deterministic token transition time. Specifically, the
forward process can be defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q(X_{i}^{t}|X_{i}^{s},\tau_{i})=\mathbf{1}\{t\leq\tau_{i}\}\mathbf{X_{i}^{s}}+\mathbf{1}\{t>\tau_{i}\}\ \textbf{M},
``` |  | (14) |

where $`\tau_{i}\sim U([0,1])`$ is the clean-to-mask transition time
that is pre-sampled once for each token and maintained throughout the
trajectory. This design relaxes the Markov constraint of the process.³³
3 For the single-jump construction with a distinct absorbing mask token,
the process remains Markov after marginalizing $`\tau`$ because the
current state reveals whether the jump has occurred. More general
single-jump DNDM can be non-Markov. For example, suppose the initial
token is replaced by an independent uniform draw from $`\mathcal{V}`$ at
a presampled time $`\tau`$, and then held fixed. However, it is
compatible with standard MDM training because it does not change the
forward marginal or the clean token target distribution
$`p(X^{0}_{i}|X^{t})`$.

We make an additional remark that DNDM’s setup in fact better aligns
with the empirical sampling algorithms \[[24](#bib.bib28),
[26](#bib.bib40), [11](#bib.bib68)\] which often predefine an unmasking
schedule, whereas the exact Markov reverse process would unmask a
stochastic number of tokens, or even zero tokens, at each step. Most
recent samplers for masked image models like the Halton sampler
\[[26](#bib.bib40)\], and the stratified sampler \[[11](#bib.bib68)\]
actually pre-compute the exact timestep to unmask each token, making
DNDM a more accurate formulation in the image generation domain.

To extend this setup to allow intermediate transition states between the
clean state and the mask state, we augment the generating process by
first sampling a predefined transition period
$`[\tau_{i},\tau_{i}^{\prime}]`$ as opposed to a fixed jump time
$`\tau_{i}`$. In particular, we sample

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\tau_{i}\sim U([0,1-\epsilon]),\tau_{i}^{\prime}=\tau_{i}+\epsilon,`$ |  | (15) |

where $`\epsilon\in(0,1]`$ is the hyper-parameter controlling the length
of the transition process from clean state with $`a=0`$ to the mask
state with $`a=K`$. In particular, when $`\epsilon\rightarrow 0`$, all
jumps happen in an infinitesimal time interval and the overall process
reduces to the canonical DNDM.

With a pre-sampled interval, the token age $`A_{i}`$ can be expressed as
a deterministic step function

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle a_{i}(t,\tau_{i},\epsilon)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if}\quad 0\leq t\leq\tau_{i}\\
\text{Floor}(K\frac{t-\tau_{i}}{\epsilon})&\text{if}\quad\tau_{i}<t<\tau^{\prime}_{i}\\
K&\text{if}\quad\tau_{i}^{\prime}\leq t\leq 1\\
\end{cases}.`$ |  | (16) |

This induces a one-hot categorical distribution over the token age
$`A_{i}^{t}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle b_{i}^{t}(a)=\mathbb{P}(A_{i}^{t}=a|\tau_{i},\epsilon)=\mathbf{1}\{a=a_{i}(t,\tau_{i},\epsilon)\}.`$ |  | (17) |

Hence, the forward process conditioned on a pre-sampled interval is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle q\!\left(X_{i}^{t}=(u,a)\mid X_{i}^{s}=(c,a^{\prime}),\tau_{i}\right)=b_{i}^{t}(a)\begin{cases}U_{a\mid a^{\prime}}(u\mid c),&a>a^{\prime},\\
\mathbf{1}\{u=c\},&a=a^{\prime},\\
0,&a<a^{\prime}.\end{cases}.`$ |  | (18) |

The posterior reverse process is simply

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle p\!\left(X_{i}^{s}=(c,a^{\prime})\mid X_{i}^{t}=(u,a),X_{i}^{0}=(\hat{c},0),\tau_{i}\right)=b_{i}^{s}(a^{\prime})\begin{cases}U_{a^{\prime}\mid a}(c\mid u,\hat{c}),&a^{\prime}<a,\\
\mathbf{1}\{c=u\},&a^{\prime}=a,\\
0,&a^{\prime}>a.\end{cases},`$ |  | (19) |

where $`U_{a|a^{\prime}}(u|c)`$ and $`U_{a^{\prime}|a}(c|u,\hat{c})`$
are the forward and reverse marginals of the discrete-time per-token
corruption process. At inference, $`X_{i}^{0}`$ is sampled from the
model’s clean token prediction.

### A.3 On-policy Distillation from AR VLMs

The standard OPD framework is defined over AR language models sharing
the same vocabulary. Specifically, given a teacher model and student
model, the OPD loss \[[30](#bib.bib2)\] is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L_{\text{AR-OPD}}}=\mathbb{E}_{X^{0}\sim p_{\text{student}}}[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{student}}(X_{i}^{0}|X_{j<i}^{0})||p_{\text{teacher}}(X_{i}^{0}|X_{j<i}^{0}))],`$ |  | (20) |

where $`X^{0}=[X^{0}_{1}...X^{0}_{L}]`$ is a sequence sampled from the
student model. Recall from Section
[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Formulations of SBD Models ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
that the SBD model divides $`X^{0}`$ into consecutive blocks
$`X^{0}=[B^{0}_{1},..B^{0}_{K}]`$ and implements

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\theta}(B_{b,i}^{0}|B^{0}_{j<b},B_{b}^{t})`$ |  | (21) |

where $`B_{b}^{t}`$ is a partially masked version of the clean block
$`B_{b}^{0}`$ and $`B_{b,i}^{0}`$ is the $`i`$-th token in the $`b`$-th
block.

When the block size is 1, each block only has two states: a clean token
and a mask token, so it is generated in one step by evaluating

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\theta}(B_{b,1}^{0}|B^{0}_{j<b},B_{b}^{1})=p_{\theta}(B_{b,1}^{0}|B^{0}_{j<b})=p_{\theta}(X_{b}^{0}|X^{0}_{j<b})`$ |  | (22) |

where $`B_{b}^{1}`$ can be marginalized since there is only one possible
state with a probability of 1. The final expression exactly implements
an AR model, and we refer to this setup as the AR view of the SBD model.
Practically, this distribution can be evaluated for all tokens in a
sampled sequence in a single pass because of the shift-by-one logit
implementation illustrated in Figure
[10](#A1.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ A.1 Formulations of SBD Models ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation").
Given this AR generation mode and AR view, we can directly apply the OPD
loss used in AR models from Equation
[20](#A1.E20 "In A.3 On-policy Distillation from AR VLMs ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
to the SBD model with no change.

However, if we only sample from and supervise the model in its AR view,
the SBD model would lose its support for parallel generation with block
sizes larger than 1. To provide generic supervision for Equation
[21](#A1.E21 "In A.3 On-policy Distillation from AR VLMs ‣ Appendix A Additional Technical Details ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation")
with a block size larger than 1, we define the AR-Diffusion Consistency
loss as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L_{\text{Cons}}}=\mathbb{E}_{X^{0}\sim p_{\text{student}},t\sim U([0,1])}[\frac{1}{KNt}\sum_{b=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{student}}(B_{b,i}^{0}|B_{j<b}^{0},B_{b,r<i}^{0})||p_{\text{student}}(B_{b,i}^{0}|B_{j<b}^{0},B_{b}^{t}))],`$ |  | (23) |

where $`N>1`$ is the block size and $`B_{b}^{t}`$ is a partially masked
block sampled from the forward marginal $`q(B_{b}^{t}|B_{b}^{0})`$.
$`X^{0}`$ is sampled with AR mode with block size 1. The final loss for
visual understanding and reasoning tasks is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{OPD-UND}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{AR-OPD}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{Cons}}.`$ |  | (24) |

#### A.3.1 Connection with OPD-LM

In our notation convention, OPD-LM \[[34](#bib.bib4)\]’s objective can
be represented as a single term:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L_{\text{OPD-LM}}}=\mathbb{E}_{X^{0},X^{t}\sim p_{\text{student}}}[\frac{1}{KN}\sum_{b=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{teacher}}(B_{b,i}^{0}|B_{j<b}^{0},B_{b,r<i}^{0})||p_{\text{student}}(B_{b,i}^{0}|B_{j<b}^{0},B_{b}^{t}))].`$ |  | (25) |

Compared with our consistency loss, the main differences are

- •
  OPD-LM samples rollouts from the diffusion view while NLD-Uni samples
  from the AR view
- •
  OPD-LM directly uses teacher scores to supervise the student’s
  diffusion view, while NLD-Uni uses the student’s own AR score to
  supervise the diffusion view
- •
  OPD-LM initializes the student with the teacher’s weights, as an
  untrained diffusion model. In cross-paradigm OPD of NLD-Uni, the
  teacher and the student are two distinct models with different sizes
  and architectures. The student model already has strong diffusion
  generation capabilities before OPD.

At the system level, the broader difference is that NLD-Uni uses OPD as
a component in a multi-objective unified training run combining visual
understanding, text-to-image, and image editing objectives, while OPD-LM
focuses solely on single-objective AR2Diff post-training in the language
domain.

One of the problems with OPD-LM’s setup is that it creates effectively
ill-formed supervision signals due to potentially conflicting contexts.
Specifically, high-probability tokens in
$`p_{\text{teacher}}(B_{b,i}^{0}|B_{j<b}^{0},B_{b,r<i}^{0})`$ may not be
good candidates semantically for
$`p_{\text{student}}(B_{b,i}^{0}|B_{j<b}^{0},B_{b}^{t})`$ with a
different context.

As a concrete example, consider the following student generation "The
number $`i`$ is called the multiplicative identity.". Given the prefix
"The number $`i`$ is called the", the teacher may want to generate the
term for "imaginary unit", and assign high scores to the token
"imaginary", but it would not be a good candidate if the masked context
is "The number $`i`$ is called the \[m\] identity" because the term
"imaginary identity" is ill-formed.

The consistency loss of NLD-Uni mitigates this issue by using the
student’s own AR predictions as supervision targets, avoiding the
additional teacher–student distribution discrepancy while retaining the
difference between AR and diffusion contexts. At zero sampling
temperature, each token in a greedy rollout is an argmax of the
student’s AR conditional distribution, although this does not guarantee
compatibility of the full supervision distribution with the observed
future context. Empirically, we find that sampling AR rollouts at a
small nonzero temperature yields better diffusion performance,
suggesting that increased trajectory diversity can be beneficial despite
relaxing the argmax property.

of the main paper, we show that directly applying OPD-LM’s loss with
heterogeneous teacher and student models performs poorly because of the

is on-policy because the supervision target is derived from the student
output. This paradigm closely connects with several concurrent works in
the OPD literature in the language domain that explored student-teacher
interleaved generation. Notably, Trajectory-refined Distillation
\[[40](#bib.bib93)\] proposes to use a teacher model to correct student
trajectories in language tasks. NLD-Uni expands this concept across
model families and to the visual generation domain to address a lack of
strong MDM teachers in visual generation tasks.

drift from the student example considerably (in this case, over-editing
and removing two extra chairs in addition to the table). The refinement
process, which constrains the teacher output to a neighborhood of the
student roll-out, serves as useful regularization in these cases.

some dimensions of the token embeddings corresponding to codes
$`1,17,33,...`$ are exactly identical, which can be limiting in
large-scale T2I settings because these tokens are not guaranteed to have
any similarity at all. We note that MDM-Prime’s visual generation
experiments are conducted on tiny $`32\times 32`$ images in CIFAR-10
\[[90](#bib.bib78)\] and ImageNet-32 \[[91](#bib.bib79)\].

### C.2 Effect of refinement steps

While in the formulation we use $`\epsilon\in[0,1]`$ to define the
progression speed of intermediate states in self-refining diffusion,
what matters in practical inference settings is the total number of
inference steps NFE and the number of refinements each sampled token
goes through, denoted by $`N`$. For example, $`N=1`$ corresponds to
vanilla mask diffusion with $`\epsilon\rightarrow 0`$, while
$`N=\text{NFE}`$ means all tokens follow the same refinement schedule
across the entire sampling process, or $`\epsilon=1`$. $`N>\text{NFE}`$
is not possible by definition.

### C.3 Studies on distilling from AR VLMs

We conducted additional ablations on the proposed OPD framework. These
results are initialized with NLD-Uni-Base model and post-trained only on
multi-modal reasoning tasks. The results are not directly comparable to
the main result which reports unified multi-task joint training.

#### C.3.1 Training Objectives

Figure 16: Ablation Studies on distilling from AR VLMs. We report
MathVista Acc throughout training steps.

We conducted detailed ablations on each component of the proposed OPD
framework. Specifically, we compare the following variants of the
method:

- •
  \(1\) AR-OPD+Consistency: The final chosen setup with AR OPD loss and
  consistency loss.
- •
  \(2\) AR-OPD+SFT: replace consistency loss with a one-hot SFT
  objective on the diffusion view, using AR-generated rollouts as
  targets.
- •
  \(3\) AR-OPD only: which treats the SBD model as a pure AR model.
- •
  \(4\) AR-OPD+Teacher-Diff-OPD: directly copy the teacher score as the
  supervision signal for the diffusion view.
- •
  \(5\) KD: Replace student’s AR rollout with pre-generated teacher
  diffusion rollout.
- •
  \(6\) AR-OPD+Consistency (w/ diff rollout): Replace student’s AR
  rollout in (1) with student’s diffusion rollout.

We evaluate these settings in both AR generation mode and diffusion
generation mode. The results are reported in Figure
[16](#A3.F16 "Figure 16 ‣ C.3.1 Training Objectives ‣ C.3 Studies on distilling from AR VLMs ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiment Results ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation").
We draw the following two conclusions:

AR rollouts support effective diffusion supervision. In diffusion
generation mode, only (1) and (2) improve upon the baseline because all
other setups create mismatched supervision signals caused by mismatched
context. In particular, even the student’s own diffusion generation
fails because the consistency loss uses the student’s AR score as the
target, which may predict high-probability tokens that are inconsistent
given future context generated from the student;s diffusion
distribution. This issue is mitigated when the sampled sequences and the
supervision target both come from the student’s AR distribution.

Consistency Loss does not hurt AR generation. While AR generation is not
the focus of this work, we find that the AR performance with
AR-OPD+diffusion loss is comparable to AR-OPD-Only, suggesting adding
supervision to diffusion view does not negatively affect AR view’s
performance.

Beyond the above experiments, we also explored a run with consistency
loss only. This run quickly collapses to an accuracy of 24.1 at 400
steps, which is outside the plotted range. For presentation clarity, it
is not shown in the figure. However, this behavior is not surprising
since it ties the outputs of the student’s own generation modes without
external supervision, which quickly leads to degenerate sequences.

#### C.3.2 Sampling Temperature

We vary the temperature used to generate student AR rollouts during OPD
and evaluate the resulting models in both diffusion and AR decoding
modes. As shown in Table
[8](#A3.T8 "Table 8 ‣ C.3.3 Choice of Teachers ‣ C.3 Studies on distilling from AR VLMs ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiment Results ‣ Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion-Uni: Elastic Self-Refining Unified Diffusion LLM with Cross-Paradigm On-Policy Distillation"),
greedy rollouts achieve the highest AR accuracy (76.9%), whereas a
temperature of 0.4 achieves the highest diffusion accuracy (74.8%).
Thus, requiring each rollout token to be an argmax of the student’s AR
conditional distribution is not necessary for effective transfer to the
diffusion view. The improvement at moderate temperatures suggests that
greater rollout diversity can benefit diffusion training. Across all
tested temperatures, AR-OPD+Consistency achieves higher diffusion
accuracy than the alternative designs evaluated in our ablations, such
as AR-OPD+SFT. Together, these results support using the student’s own
AR predictions to supervise its diffusion view, while indicating that
strict per-token argmax alignment is unnecessary.

We clarify that all evaluations are performed with a temperature of 0
for consistency and reproducibility. Empirically, even for models with
non-zero rollout temperature, using a zero temperature still gives
better accuracy.

## Appendix D Additional Related Works

### D.1 Unified Architecture.

Unified MLLMs are capable of both understanding and generation tasks.
Integrating different capabilities into a single model presents unique
architectural challenges. Generally, unified MLLMs have two common
architecture choices. The first type, such as Janus \[[4](#bib.bib37)\],
MMaDa \[[12](#bib.bib29)\], Show-O\[[64](#bib.bib48)\], uses a single
dense transformer to output both image and text tokens. The second type,
such as BAGEL \[[5](#bib.bib35)\], MetaQueries \[[8](#bib.bib36)\] and
LaViDa-O \[[11](#bib.bib68)\] employs separate parameter sets for
handling image and text modalities. In particular,
mixture-of-transformers (MoT) architecture \[[93](#bib.bib38)\]
incorporates two separate sets of attention and FFN parameters to
process different modalities, while still allowing cross-modal
interaction via a joint attention mechanism. Under this paradigm,
several works such as X-Fusion and LM-Fusion \[[9](#bib.bib65),
[94](#bib.bib66)\] further investigated architecture designs and
training recipes of MoT. In particular, LaViDa-O \[[11](#bib.bib68)\]
proposes Elastic-MoT design, which decouples the attention layer in the
final half of the model and only enables joint attention in early
layers. Given an 8B base model, Elastic-MoT extends it to a 16B MoT.
However, when performing understanding tasks, only 8B parameters are
loaded. When performing generation tasks, only 8B generation
parameters + 4B understanding parameters are loaded. When performing
interleaved tasks, all 16B parameters are loaded. This elasticity makes
the model particularly efficient and introduces no overhead compared to
an 8B VLM when performing understanding tasks.

## Appendix F Limitations and Future Work

In this work, we propose NLD-Uni, introducing a self-refining diffusion
process and cross-paradigm on-policy distillation. Despite considerable
success, some limitations remain. First, while the cross-paradigm OPD
framework allows us to distill an AR teacher to a diffusion student, it
still requires the vocabulary spaces of the two models to match. In our
setup, both models use the Ministral 3 \[[104](#bib.bib97)\] tokenizer.
Many existing works have explored cross-tokenizer distillation between
AR models \[[105](#bib.bib3)\]. We leave extending these methods to
support cross-paradigm distillation to future work. Second, while
NLD-Uni achieves state-of-the-art performance among unified diffusion
MLLMs, there may be considerable room for further optimization on the
model architecture side. We leave that for future work as well since it
is not the focus of this work. Third, we define the self-refining
diffusion process with the non-Markov formulation with predefined
per-token corruption intervals and uniform progression of intermediate
states. This design is mostly motivated by minimizing complexity. Future
work may explore defining a Markov process with a stochastic transition
rate that allows tokens to stay at different intermediate states for
varying lengths of time. Finally, from a system perspective, NLD-Uni
lacks fine-grained text rendering capabilities because such data is not
widely present in the training mix. We will address this issue in future
by curating additional text-rendering-focused data.
````

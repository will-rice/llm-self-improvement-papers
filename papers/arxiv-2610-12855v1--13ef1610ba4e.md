###### Abstract

On-Policy Self-distillation is a self-improvement paradigm that enables
large language models (LLMs) to improve their capabilities by leveraging
the same model as both teacher and student under different conditioning
contexts. However, we find that OPSD methods can progressively degrade
previously acquired capabilities: queries that are correctly responded
at earlier training stages may become incorrectly responded as
optimization proceeds, even the overall performance continues to
improve. Further analysis reveals that this degradation coincides with a
deterioration of the geometry of hidden representations, characterized
by reduced intra-cluster compactness and diminished inter-cluster
separation. Motivated by this observation, we propose Representation
Geometry Regularization (RGR), which preserves representation geometry
during post-training by encouraging compactness within cluster and
separation across cluster. RGR can be seamlessly integrated into
existing OPSD methods without altering their core optimization
procedures. Extensive experiments on multiple OPSD methods and
benchmarks show that RGR generally alleviates capability degradation
while retaining capability improvements. Our code is available in
[https://github.com/supermachinelearner/RGR](https://github.com/supermachinelearner/RGR)

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2610.12855v1/intro-1.png)

Figure 1: (a) The capability degradation ratio of existing methods; (b)
Although the overall performance improves during training, some problems
that were previously responded correctly become incorrectly answered as
optimization progresses.

On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib30);
[Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib26); [Li
et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)), is a self-improvement paradigm for the
post-training of large language models (LLMs) ([Brown et al.,
2020](#bib.bib2); [Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib19); [Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib25); [Zeng et al., 2026](#bib.bib27); [Xiao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib23); [Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)), which enables LLMs to
improve their capabilities through self-knowledge distillation. Unlike
conventional knowledge distillation([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib4);
[Sanh et al., 2019](#bib.bib16)), which relies on a separately trained
teacher, OPSD uses the same LLM as both teacher and student under
different conditioning contexts. Specifically, the student model
generates responses to the original query, while teacher model, which
share the same parameters with the student model, conditioned on
additional privileged information (e.g., verified reasoning traces) to
provide supervision for these generated responses.

Although recent studies ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib30); [Hübotter
et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib26); [Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib21)) have empirically
demonstrated that optimizing the LLM with OPSD can outperform
conventional post-training methods such as SFT ([Wei et al.,
2021](#bib.bib22)) and GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)),
benefiting from the dense and fine-grained token-level supervision of
OPSD. We observe a progressive reasoning capability degradation
phenomenon during self-improvement in existing OPSD methods. As shown in
Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization")(b),
a query that is responded correctly at earlier training stages gradually
becomes incorrectly responded as training proceeds, even as overall
performance continues to improve.

Furthermore, we examine several representative OPSD methods and find
that this phenomenon consistently occurs across these methods, albeit to
varying degrees, as it is shown in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization")
(a). Such degradation may undermine the stability of self-improvement.

To better understand this phenomenon, we conduct further analysis, and
the results reveal that this capability degradation is accompanied by a
progressive deterioration in the geometry of hidden representations.
When a query is answered correctly at earlier stages of training, the
hidden representations of its generated responses exhibit a relatively
well-structured clustering pattern, characterized by larger
inter-cluster distances and clearer cluster boundaries. As training
proceeds and the response to the same query gradually becomes incorrect,
the relative distances between clusters shrink and the cluster
boundaries become increasingly indistinct. This observation suggests
that preserving the geometric structure of the representation space may
be important for maintaining previously acquired capabilities during
self-improvement.

Based on this finding, we theoretically show that preserving
intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster separation in the hidden
representation space helps maintain the capabilities that the LLM has
already acquired. Based on this theoretical insight, we develop
Representation Geometry Regularization (RGR), which constrains the
evolution of hidden representations during self-improvement. RGR
incorporates two geometry-preserving constraints called intra-cluster
compactness and inter-cluster separation into the OPSD methods, enabling
the model to improve targeted capabilities while mitigating the
degradation of previously acquired ones. Experiments across four
existing SD models and three widely used benchmarks show that RGR
substantially reduces correct-to-incorrect transitions during training
while retaining its self-improvement gains.

In summary, our contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  We uncover an overlooked failure mode, where OPSD methods can degrade
  previously acquired capabilities, causing previously correct
  predictions to become incorrect.
- •
  We identify representation geometry distortion as a key factor
  underlying this degradation, and theoretically characterize the
  importance of hidden representation geometry for prediction stability.
- •
  We propose RGR, which incorporates geometry-preserving constraints
  into OPSD methods to enable self-improvement while reducing capability
  degradation.

## 2 Related works

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) enables LLMs to improve their
capabilities without requiring an external model as a teacher. Recently,
it has attracted significant attention as a promising paradigm for
scalable and efficient LLM self-improvement. Based on how self-teacher
signals are constructed and how external feedback is incorporated, we
organize existing OPSD methods into privileged-information-driven
methods and external-feedback-driven methods.The
privileged-information-driven methods construct self-teacher signals
using additional privileged information that is unavailable to the
student policy at inference time. As a representative method, OPSD
([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)) uses the same model to construct
self-teacher signals and optimize the student policy under different
conditioning contexts: the self-teacher signals are constructed from
both the question and a verified reasoning trace, whereas the student
policy is conditioned only on the question. The external-feedback-driven
methods retain the self-teacher structure while incorporating
environmental feedback, verification results, or outcome rewards to
construct the supervision for policy updating. Within this category,
feedback can be used to construct distillation targets, select training
objectives, or modulate token-level advantages. SDPO ([Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7)) conditions the current model on textual or
reward-derived feedback to form a self-teacher, whose next-token
predictions serve as distillation targets for the student policy. SRPO
([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib10))routes successful trajectories to
reward-driven GRPO updates and failed trajectories to entropy-weighted,
token-level self-distillation against a feedback-conditioned teacher
distribution. RLSD ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)) uses verifiable
outcomes to determine the direction of policy updates and token-level
teacher–student discrepancies to modulate their magnitude, integrating
self-distillation into reward-driven learning.

## 3 Preliminaries

Given a query $`q`$, an LLM $`\pi_{\theta}`$ typically generates a
response $`y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{T})`$ according to
$`y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,y_{<t})`$, where
$`y_{<t}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t-1})`$ denotes the previously generated
tokens. OPSD methods improve the LLM by using the same model under
different conditioning contexts as the teacher and student to conduct
knowledge distillation. Specifically, the student is designed to
generate an on-policy response based only on the original query, like
the LLM, i.e., $`y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)`$. While the teacher is
designed to generate the response with additional privileged information
$`z`$, such as a verified reasoning trace,
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,z,y_{<t})`$. The OPSD methods optimize the
model by minimizing the divergence between the teacher and student token
distributions along the student’s trajectory:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}D\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,z,y_{<t})\,\middle\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,y_{<t})\right)\right].
``` |  | (1) |

where $`D(\cdot|\cdot)`$ denotes a divergence measure between the
teacher and student token distributions. $`D`$ can be instantiated by
forward KL divergence ([Kullback and Leibler, 1951](#bib.bib9)), reverse
KL divergence ([Minka et al., 2005](#bib.bib15)), Jensen–Shannon
divergence ([Lin, 1991](#bib.bib12)), or other suitable
distribution-matching objectives.

Since the teacher and student share the underlying model parameters,
optimizing $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}`$ updates the LLM itself.
Consequently, the model iteratively improves its responses by distilling
the additional information available to the teacher into its ordinary,
query-conditioned behavior.

## 4 The Geometry of Capability Degradation

### 4.1 Progressive Capability Degradation

![Refer to caption](2610.12855v1/opsd_figure.png)

Figure 2: Hidden-state representations and response correctness across
training epochs. The query is answered correctly at epochs 25 and 50,
but incorrectly at epoch 75.

As discussed previously, although OPSD methods continuously improve the
overall performance in the training, some queries that are correctly
responded at earlier training stages become incorrectly responded as
training proceeds. To better understand this phenomenon, for a fixed
query, we generate a response and visualize the hidden representations
corresponding to the generated response every 25 training epochs. We
observe that changes in the geometry of the hidden representations
consistently coincide with changes in response correctness. As
illustrated in
Figure. [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.1 Progressive Capability Degradation ‣ 4 The Geometry of Capability Degradation ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization"),
when the model generates a correct response at an earlier training stage
(Epoch 25), the corresponding hidden representations exhibit a
relatively structured geometric organization, with clearly
distinguishable clusters and relatively large distances between
clusters. As training proceeds and the response becomes incorrect, the
relative distances between clusters gradually decrease, while the
boundaries between clusters become increasingly indistinct (Epoch 75).
We hypothesize that as a query transitions from being correctly to
incorrectly responded, the reasoning process in the generated response
gradually becomes less coherent. When the query is answered correctly,
the reasoning tends to exhibit a clear and consistent logical structure,
which is reflected in the hidden representation space as distinct and
well-separated clusters corresponding to different reasoning patterns.
As the reasoning becomes increasingly disorganized, these patterns
become less distinguishable, causing the corresponding representations
to become more entangled and eventually leading to an incorrect
response. Interestingly, this phenomenon is consistently observed in
existing OPSD methods, such as OPSD([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)),
SDPO([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)), RLSD([Yang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib26)), SRPO([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)). These
observations suggest a strong connection between the geometry of the
hidden representation and the stability of the model’s acquired
capabilities during self-improvement.

### 4.2 Theoretical Analysis for Capability Degradation

To further substantiate the relationship between hidden representation
geometry and response correctness, we conduct a theoretical analysis.

Suppose the LLM undergoing on-policy self-distillation is denoted by
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,y_{<t})`$, where
$`\pi_{\theta}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{OPSD}}`$, and $`\Pi_{\text{OPSD}}`$ denote
the policy set trained using OPSD.

Let $`\pi_{\theta*}(\cdot\mid q,z,y_{<t})`$ denote the optimal teacher
distribution conditioned on the privileged information $`z`$, and let
$`\pi_{\theta*}(\cdot\mid q,y_{<t})`$ denote the corresponding optimal
student distribution without access to $`z`$. We define the excess
distillation risk as follows.

###### Definition 4.1 (The Excess Distillation Risk).

The excess distillation risk of the student model
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,y_{<t})`$ is defined as the difference
between its distillation risk with respect to the optimal teacher and
that achieved by the optimal student, i.e.,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{KD}}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)}\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}D\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\cdot\mid q,z,y_{<t})\,\middle\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,y_{<t})\right)`$ |  | (2) |
|  | $`\displaystyle-D\left(\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\cdot\mid q,z,y_{<t})\,\middle\|\,\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\cdot\mid q,y_{<t})\right)\Bigr].`$ |  |  |

This quantity measures the gap between the distillation risk of the
current student and that of the optimal student, with a smaller value
indicating a closer approximation to the optimal.

Let $`\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\{\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}`$ and
$`\mathcal{H}^{*}=\{\mathbf{h}_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{T}`$ denote the hidden
representations produced by the current teacher
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,z,y_{<t})`$ and the optimal teacher
$`\pi_{\theta^{*}}(\cdot\mid q,z,y_{<t})`$, respectively. Their midpoint
representations are denoted by
$`\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}=\{\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\dagger}\}_{i=1}^{T}`$, where
$`\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\dagger}=\frac{(\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{i}+\mathbf{h}_{i}^{*})}{2}`$.
Suppose that $`\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}`$ is partitioned into $`K`$
clusters, denoted by
$`\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}=\{C_{1}^{\dagger},\ldots,C_{K}^{\dagger}\}`$.
The center of is cluster $`C_{k}^{\dagger}`$ is
$`\mu_{k}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{|C_{k}^{\dagger}|}\sum_{\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\dagger}\in C_{k}^{\dagger}}\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\dagger}`$.
Let $`\mathcal{H}=\{\mathbf{h}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}`$ denote the student
hidden representations corresponding to the current-teacher
representations $`\{\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}`$. For each cluster
$`C_{k}^{\dagger}`$, we define
$`\mathcal{X}_{k}=\{\mathbf{h}_{i}\mid\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\dagger}\in C_{k}^{\dagger}\}`$.
That is, if the midpoint $`\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\dagger}`$ associated with a
current-teacher representation $`\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{i}`$ belongs to
$`C_{k}^{\dagger}`$, its corresponding student representation
$`\mathbf{h}_{i}`$ is assigned to $`\mathcal{X}_{k}`$. The center of is
cluster $`\mathcal{X}_{k}`$ is
$`\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_{k}|}\sum_{h_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}\mathbf{h}_{i}`$.
Let $`\mu=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i}^{T}\mathbf{h}_{i}`$ denote the mean of
hidden representations. Based on the definition, we have

###### Theorem 4.1 (Excess Risk Upper Bound).

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{KD}}(\pi_{\theta})\leq\frac{2||M||_{2}}{N|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{N}\sup_{\pi_{\theta}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{OPSD}}}|\Delta|[\sqrt{\frac{Td}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}|}\sum_{{\mathbf{h}_{i}}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}||\mathbf{h}_{i}-\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}||_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}|}|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}_{k}|||\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}-\mu||^{2}_{2}}+`$ |  | (3) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}|}|\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\dagger}|||\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}-\mu_{k}^{\dagger}||^{2}_{2}+\sum_{|\mathcal{C}^{\dagger}|}\sum_{{\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\dagger}}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}||\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\dagger}-\mu_{k}^{\dagger}||_{2}^{2}}]`$ |  |  |

where $`N`$ denote the number of samples, $`|\Delta|=`$
$`\sqrt{\sum_{i}||\hat{\mathbf{h}_{i}}-{\mathbf{h}_{i}}^{*}||_{2}^{2}}`$,
$`M=W^{T}(I_{|\mathcal{V}|}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{V}|}\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{V}|}^{T})W`$.
The detail proof could be found in Appendix

[D](#A4 "Appendix D Proof of Theorem ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization")

From Theorem
[4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 4.1 (Excess Risk Upper Bound). ‣ 4.2 Theoretical Analysis for Capability Degradation ‣ 4 The Geometry of Capability Degradation ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization"),
we observe that the excess risk upper bound is affected by both
intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster separation of the learned
representations. Specifically, reducing the intra-cluster variance term
$`\|\mathbf{h}_{i}-\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}\|_{2}^{2}`$ and increasing the
inter-cluster dispersion term $`\|\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}-\mu\|_{2}^{2}`$
can tighten the excess risk upper bound. Meanwhile, the term
$`\|\mu_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}-\mu_{k}^{\dagger}\|_{2}^{2}`$ reflects the
deviation between the learned student representation and the teacher and
optimal teacher representation. Therefore, preventing capability
degradation during OPSD requires not only maintaining the alignment with
the teacher representation, but also preserving compact intra-cluster
compactness and clear inter-cluster separation in the representation
space.

## 5 Methodology

As discussed previously, we observe that increasingly blurred boundaries
between hidden representation clusters coincide with the progressive
degradation of model capabilities during OPSD training. Motivated by
this observation, we propose Representation Geometry Regularization
(RGR) to preserve clear boundaries between hidden representation
clusters. To achieve this goal, we consider two complementary geometric
properties: (1) intra-cluster compactness, where representations within
each cluster remain sufficiently compact, and (2) inter-cluster
separation, where different clusters maintain sufficient separation from
each other. In the following, we discuss how to preserve the two
properties in detail and derive the corresponding regularization
objectives.

### 5.1 Intra-cluster compactness

According to previous works , the intra-cluster compactness of hidden
representations can be evaluated by the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares
(WCSS)([Lloyd, 1982](#bib.bib13)), defined as the sum of squared
distances between each hidden representation and the center of its
corresponding cluster. However, directly minimizing WCSS may lead to
excessive compactness within each cluster. In the extreme case, hidden
representations within the same cluster may become overly similar,
reducing the discriminative information encoded in the representation
space. Therefore, we introduce the teacher model as a reference and
encourage the WCSS of the student representations within each cluster to
remain close to that of the teacher representations.

Given the query $`q`$, the hidden representations of the response
generated by the student model are denoted as
$`\{\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{T}\}`$, while the corresponding
hidden representations of the reference model are denoted as
$`\{\mathbf{h}_{1}^{\text{ref}},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{T}^{\text{ref}}\}`$.

Suppose that a cluster $`C^{\mathrm{ref}}_{k}`$ in the reference model’s
representation space corresponds to a cluster $`C_{k}^{\mathrm{align}}`$
in the student model’s representation space, with cluster centers
$`\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\mathrm{ref}}`$ and
$`\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\mathrm{align}}`$, respectively.To align the
intra-cluster compactness of the student model with that of the
reference model, the learning target for intra-cluster compactness is
defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{intra}}=\sum_{|\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ref}}|}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{h}_{i}\in C_{k}^{\mathrm{align}},}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}^{\mathrm{align}}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\sum_{\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}}\in C_{k}^{\mathrm{ref}}}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}^{\mathrm{ref}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right|,
``` |  | (4) |

where $`|\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ref}}|`$ denotes the number of clusters.

However, directly optimizing
Eq. [17](#A5.E17 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix E Proof of Theorem ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization")
is difficult. To address this, we stack the hidden representations of
the student model as
$`\mathbf{H}=[\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{T}]\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}`$,
where each hidden representation is the row vector of $`\mathbf{H}`$.
Similarly, we stack the hidden representations of the student model as
$`\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}=[\mathbf{h}_{1}^{\mathrm{ref}},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{T}^{\mathrm{ref}}]`$,
where each hidden representation is the row vector of
$`\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}`$. Under the above definitions and
assumptions, we have:

###### Theorem 5.1 (Intra-cluster Compactness Upper Bound).

The upper bound of the intra-cluster compactness learning target is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{intra}}\leq(T-|\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ref}}|)^{\frac{1}{2}}||\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top}-\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{T}||_{F},
``` |  | (5) |

where $`|\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{ref}}|`$ denotes the number of clusters of
the reference model.

The details of the proof can be found in Appendix
[E](#A5 "Appendix E Proof of Theorem ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization").

### 5.2 Inter-cluster separation

We construct a Laplacian matrix([Von Luxburg, 2007](#bib.bib20)) from
the pairwise relationships among all hidden representations and use its
low-end spectrum, corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues of the
Laplacian matrix, to evaluate the separability between clusters.
However, directly maximizing these low-end eigenvalues is undesirable,
as excessively large inter-cluster separation can over-expand the
representation space and distort the relative geometric relationships
among clusters. Therefore, we use the teacher model as a reference model
to encourage the student model to preserve its inter-cluster separation,
thereby preventing undesirable geometric distortion during optimization.

Specifically, given the query $`q`$, the hidden representations of the
response generated by the student model are denoted as
$`\{\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{T}\}`$, while the corresponding
hidden representations of the reference model are denoted as
$`\{\mathbf{h}_{1}^{\text{ref}},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{T}^{\text{ref}}\}`$.
We define the Gaussian kernel matrices of the hidden representations of
the student model and the reference model as:

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle A_{ij}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\left\|\mathbf{h}_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),&i\neq j,\\
0,&i=j,\end{cases}\qquad A_{ij}^{ref}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\dfrac{\left\|\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\mathbf{h}_{j}^{\mathrm{ref}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),&i\neq j,\\
0,&i=j,\end{cases}`$ |  | (6) |

where $`\sigma`$ denotes the standard deviation of the Gaussian kernel.

The corresponding degree matrices of the student and reference Gaussian
kernel matrices are denoted by $`\mathbf{D}`$ and
$`\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{ref}}`$, respectively, where
$`D_{ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{T}A_{ij}`$ and
$`D_{ii}^{\mathrm{ref}}=\sum_{j=1}^{T}A_{ij}^{\mathrm{ref}}`$. The
unnormalized Laplacian matrices of the student and reference hidden
representations are then defined as $`\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{A}`$
and
$`\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{ref}}=\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\mathbf{A}^{\mathrm{ref}}`$,
respectively. The corresponding eigenvalue vectors are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left[\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{L}),\ldots,\lambda_{T}(\mathbf{L})\right]^{\top},`$ |  | (7) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{ref}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left[\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{ref}}),\ldots,\lambda_{T}(\mathbf{L}^{\mathrm{ref}})\right]^{\top},`$ |  |  |

where the eigenvalues are arranged in nondecreasing order. The learning
target for inter-cluster separation could be represented by the
difference between the eigenvalue vectors of the student and the
reference, that is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inter}}=\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\mathrm{ref}}\right\|_{2}.
``` |  | (8) |

Then, by the Hoffman–Wielandt inequality ([Hoffman et al.,
1953](#bib.bib5)), we obtain

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inter}}\leq(1+\sqrt{T})||\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L^{ref}}||_{F}
``` |  | (9) |

Under the above definitions and assumptions, we have

###### Theorem 5.2 (Inter-cluster Separation Upper Bound).

The upper bound of the inter-cluster separation could be induced by
applying Taylor expansion and the Newton–Schwarz inequality:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inter}}\leq\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{T}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\left\|\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top}-\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\top}\right\|_{F},
``` |  | (10) |

The proof is provided in
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Proof of Proposition ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization").

### 5.3 Optimization

Building upon the previous analysis, preserving the intra-cluster
compactness and inter-cluster separation of the hidden representation
space requires minimizing $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{intra}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inter}}`$. From the above theoretical analysis,
we show that both geometric discrepancies can be controlled by the
discrepancy between the Gram matrices of the student and reference
hidden representations. Specifically, we have
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{intra}}\leq C_{\mathrm{intra}}\left\|\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top}-\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\top}\right\|_{F}`$
and
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inter}}\leq C_{\mathrm{inter}}\left\|\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top}-\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\top}\right\|_{F},`$
where $`C_{\mathrm{intra}}`$ and $`C_{\mathrm{inter}}`$ are constants
independent of the student representations. Therefore, minimizing the
Gram matrix discrepancy provides a unified objective for preserving both
intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster separation. We thus define
the geometry-preserving objective as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geo}}=\left\|\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top}-\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}.
``` |  | (11) |

In addition to representation geometry, recent studies ([Jin et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8)) on on-policy distillation have highlighted the
importance of controlling the entropy of the model output distribution
during optimization,which motivates us to explicitly incorporate an
entropy-related objective into the optimization process. Accordingly, we
jointly preserve the hidden representation geometry and regulate the
output distribution entropy during OPSD optimization. Combining the
original OPSD objective with the geometry-preserving and entropy-related
objectives, we formulate the overall training objective as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geo}}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ent}},
``` |  | (12) |

where $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}`$ denotes the original on-policy
self-distillation objective, $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geo}}`$ denotes the
proposed geometry-preserving regularization, and
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ent}}=\left\|\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}\right\|_{F}^{2}`$
denotes the entropy-related objective. The coefficients $`\alpha`$ and
$`\beta`$ control the contributions of the geometry-preserving and
entropy-related terms, respectively.

## 6 EXEPERIMENT

In this section, we conduct experiments to evaluate the performance of
RGR. We aim to answer the following research questions: (1) Is RGR
effective for improving the performance of self distillation (RQ1)? (2)
How do different components of RGR contribute to the performance gains
(RQ2)? (3) Can RGR alleviate the degradation of previously acquired
capabilities during OPSD optimization, and how does it affect the
evolution of hidden representations (RQ3)? (4) Can RGR generalize to
task-transfer settings and alleviate the degradation of previously
acquired capabilities when learning new tasks (RQ4)?

### 6.1 Experiment setup

#### Datasets.

Following the dataset setup of OPSD ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)),
we sample up to 30K problem-solution pairs containing chain-of-thought
reasoning from the mathematical reasoning subset of OpenThoughts([Guha
et al.,](#bib.bib3) )for model training.. We conduct evaluation on three
representative benchmarks: AIME 2024 ([Zhang and Math-AI,
2024](#bib.bib28)), AIME 2025 ([Zhang and Math-AI, 2025](#bib.bib29)),
and HMMT 2025 ([Balunovic et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)). AIME 2024 consists
of problems from the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination,
covering diverse areas of high-school mathematics. AIME 2025 consists of
problems from the 2025 American Invitational Mathematics Examination.
HMMT 2025 consists of problems from the Harvard-MIT Mathematics
Tournament, covering algebra, combinatorics, geometry, and number
theory.

#### Baseline.

To assess the effectiveness of RGR, we integrate it into four
representative self-distillation methods, namely, OPSD ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib30)), SDPO ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)), SRPO ([Li
et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)), and RLSD ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)),
and compare each RGR-enhanced method with its original counterpart.
These methods differ in how they optimize the self-distillation
objective. Specifically, OPSD employs forward KL divergence ([Kullback
and Leibler, 1951](#bib.bib9)), with the policy update constrained by
token-level KL clipping; SDPO uses Jensen–Shannon divergence ([Lin,
1991](#bib.bib12)) to align the student with the teacher; SRPO routes
successful responses to reward-based reinforcement and failed responses
to self-distillation; and RLSD uses environment-derived rewards to
determine the direction of policy updates, while reweighting token-level
advantages according to the teacher–student log-probability gap to
modulate the update magnitude.

#### ImplementationDetails.

Our experiments are conducted on two foundation LLMs: Qwen3-1.7B and
Qwen3-4B([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)), with thinking mode enabled
throughout training and validation. All experiments are performed on
four NVIDIA A40 GPUs. All methods use LoRA ([Hu et al.,
2021](#bib.bib6)) with a rank of 64 and a scaling factor of 128, with
the learning rate set to 5 $`\times`$ $`10^{-6}`$. The teacher model
remains frozen throughout training. For our method, we fix the parameter
$`\alpha`$ in
Eq.([12](#S5.E12 "In 5.3 Optimization ‣ 5 Methodology ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization"))
at $`2.5\times 10^{-2}`$ and the parameter $`\beta`$ in
Eq.([12](#S5.E12 "In 5.3 Optimization ‣ 5 Methodology ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization"))
at $`5\times 10^{-2}`$ across all benchmarks.

only improves self-improvement performance but also helps preserve
previously acquired capabilities by maintaining a more stable hidden

correct to incorrect. In contrast, RGR maintains more compact clusters

our theoretical analysis and demonstrate that preserving representation
geometry helps stabilize the evolution of hidden representations during
OPSD training.

### 6.5 Cross-Task Forgetting Study (RQ4)

To further investigate whether RGR can mitigate capability degradation
beyond the original task, we conduct a cross-task forgetting experiment.
We first post-train a model on OpenThoughts to acquire mathematical
problem-solving capabilities, and evaluate the model on AIME24 to
measure performance. Then we continue post-training it on a tool-use
dataset([Tang et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)), after convergence, we evaluate
the model again on AIME24 to measure the performance after tool-use
training. We apply this setting to OPSD, SDPO, RLSD, and SRPO, and
compare their performance before and after training on the tool-use
dataset, as shown in
Table [3](#S6.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 6.4 Training Dynamics Visualization (RQ3) ‣ 6 EXEPERIMENT ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization").
From
Table [3](#S6.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 6.4 Training Dynamics Visualization (RQ3) ‣ 6 EXEPERIMENT ‣ Mitigating Capability Degradation in LLM Self-Improvement via Representation Geometry Regularization"),
we can observe that: (1) Sequential training on a new task leads to
forgetting of previously acquired capabilities. After tool-use training,
all four methods exhibit performance degradation on AIME24, consistent
with previous studies of catastrophic forgetting in continual
fine-tuning of LLMs ([Luo et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Li et al.,
2024](#bib.bib11)). (2) RGR generally alleviates cross-task forgetting.
RGR reduces the forgetting gap for OPSD, SDPO, and SRPO, with
particularly notable improvements for SDPO (5.28 $`\rightarrow`$ 1.94)
and SRPO (3.06 $`\rightarrow`$ 1.11). This suggests that preserving
representation geometry during subsequent optimization can help retain
previously acquired capabilities. (3) The effectiveness of RGR depends
on the underlying post-training method. For RLSD, the forgetting gap
slightly increases from 3.05 to 3.39 after introducing RGR, suggesting
that the effect of representation geometry preservation may vary across
different optimization dynamics. Nevertheless, RGR reduces forgetting
for three of the four methods, supporting its broader applicability to
sequential post-training.

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we identify and investigate the progressive capability
degradation phenomenon in on-policy self-distillation, where LLM may
lose previously acquired capabilities during self-improvement despite
overall performance improvements. Through empirical analysis and
theoretical investigation, we reveal that this degradation is associated
with the deterioration of hidden representation geometry, characterized
by reduced intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster center
separation. Based on these findings, we propose Representation Geometry
Regularization (RGR), a general regularization framework that preserves
intra-cluster compactness and inter-cluster center separation during
OPSD optimization. Extensive experiments across multiple OPSD variants
and mathematical reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that RGR effectively
reduces correct-to-incorrect transitions while maintaining the
self-improvement capability of OPSD. Future work will explore more
adaptive strategies for preserving representation geometry and extending
the proposed framework to broader model optimization paradigms beyond
self-distillation.
````

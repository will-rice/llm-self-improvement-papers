###### Abstract.

In general-sum games, self-interested learners can reach outcomes that
are poor for everyone, which has motivated methods that shape the
opponent’s learning. Methods like Learning with Opponent-Learning
Awareness (LOLA), which differentiates through the opponent’s learning
process, and Model-Free Opponent Shaping (M-FOS), which uses model-free
optimisation methods for long-horizon opponent shaping, have enabled
mutual cooperation and exploitation of learners. However, these methods
require access to the opponent’s parameters, policy, rewards or learning
rule during deployment. In single-agent reinforcement learning, update
rules discovered by meta-learning have achieved state-of-the-art results
(e.g. DiscoRL). These rules are separate from the policy they train and
read only the agent’s own experience. We hereby extend this framework to
general-sum settings by introducing Discovered Update Rule for Opponent
Shaping (\method). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
method to discover an update rule for an agent in general-sum games.
\method meta-trains the rule to maximise the agent’s expected return
over its lifetime, and updates an inner network using the agent’s own
experience. We show that our discovered rule exhibits behaviour
consistent with opponent shaping without reading opponent policies or
parameters, and ranks 1st in normalised mean score and 1st in self-play
among tournament entrants.

## 1. Introduction

Real-world multi-agent environments, from self-driving cars ([Schwarting
et al., 2019](#bib.bib21)) to multi-agent large language model (LLM)
systems ([Park et al., 2023](#bib.bib25); [Akata et al.,
2025](#bib.bib66)), can be framed as games ranging from zero-sum to
fully cooperative. General-sum games cover the mixed-motive settings in
between, such as social dilemmas ([Dawes, 1980](#bib.bib59); [Leibo et
al., 2017](#bib.bib39)) or buyer-seller negotiations ([Lewis et al.,
2017](#bib.bib36)). Naive self-interested learners within general-sum
games often converge towards outcomes that are poor for everyone
([Foerster et al., 2018a](#bib.bib52); [Leibo et al.,
2017](#bib.bib39)). For example, in Iterated Prisoner’s Dilemma (IPD)
([Axelrod, 2006](#bib.bib48); [Press and Dyson, 2012](#bib.bib23)), they
often converge to mutual defection rather than mutual cooperation.
Opponent shaping ([Foerster et al., 2018a](#bib.bib52); [Lu et al.,
2022b](#bib.bib33)) addresses this problem by influencing how others
learn to achieve mutually beneficial outcomes.

Existing shaping methods leave a gap. Hand-derived rules such as LOLA
([Foerster et al., 2018a](#bib.bib52)) and POLA ([Zhao et al.,
2022](#bib.bib9)) differentiate through the opponent’s update, and
require their parameters, payoffs or learning rules. Meta-learned
shapers such as M-FOS ([Lu et al., 2022b](#bib.bib33)), SHAPER ([Khan et
al., 2024](#bib.bib44)) and COALA-PG ([Meulemans et al.,
2025](#bib.bib28)) do not require a model of the opponent’s learning
rule as SHAPER and COALA-PG act from their own history, but their
learning algorithm is inside a black-box network, retrained each game.
In single-agent reinforcement learning (RL), where the environment is
stationary, rules discovered by meta-learning ([Oh et al.,
2020](#bib.bib26); [Oh et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)) have achieved
state-of-the-art results. Such rules also have practical advantages over
a black-box learner: a discovered rule is not limited to updates derived
by hand ([Oh et al., 2020](#bib.bib26)), is separate from the policy it
trains, can transfer to different games without retraining, and its
outputs can be read and interpreted directly. We therefore ask: can an
update rule that learns from an agent’s own experience be discovered for
general-sum games? However, learning update rules is difficult because
learning is coupled, meta-gradients explode through co-learning, and
opponents adapt, making the setting non-stationary. We hereby introduce
Discovered Update Rule for Opponent Shaping (\method), a
meta-reinforcement learning algorithm that discovers its own update rule
by interacting with opponents, without needing their policies or
parameters.

In this paper, we design the meta-objective to maximise the agent’s
return over its lifetime. The rule reads the agent’s own experience and
outputs policy updates, hence at deployment it does not require any
privileged information: the agent never sees its opponents’ parameters,
policies, learning rules or rewards. To avoid meta-gradients that
explode through co-learning, the rule is trained with a likelihood-ratio
meta-gradient, without differentiating through any learning. We then
examine whether opponent shaping emerges from the rule without
specifying it explicitly.

Although \method is trained to maximise lifetime return, this objective
alone does not establish robustness to opponents trained specifically
against its learned update rule. We therefore add a stage inspired by
Policy Space Response Oracles (PSRO) ([Lanctot et al.,
2017](#bib.bib40)) to meta-training: we train opponents to maximise
their returns against the current rule, add them to the training
population, and continue optimising the rule against the expanded
population. We evaluate whether PSRO improves resistance to exploitation
for \method and how it affects tournament results.

We evaluate \method in IPD, Iterated Matching Pennies (IMP), Chicken,
Stag Hunt and Coin Game ([Lerer and Peysakhovich, 2018](#bib.bib38)) in
round-robin tournaments against established opponent-shaping methods,
including methods that observe their opponents’ policies or parameters.
Each baseline is reproduced from its original settings, checked against
results where possible, and flagged if it fails. We also observe \method
self-play and zero-shot transfer of the IPD-trained rule to Chicken and
Stag Hunt. Despite using only its own experience and learning an update
rule from scratch, \method ranks 1st in normalised mean score across all
games tested, and 1st in normalised mean score for self-play among
tournament entrants.

## 2. Related Work

Opponent shaping: LOLA introduced the idea of opponent shaping by
differentiating through the opponent’s learning step ([Foerster et al.,
2018a](#bib.bib52)), which DiCE estimates from sampled trajectories
([Foerster et al., 2018b](#bib.bib5)). Stable Opponent Shaping ([Letcher
et al., 2019](#bib.bib37)), COLA ([Willi et al., 2022](#bib.bib15)) and
POLA ([Zhao et al., 2022](#bib.bib9)) then further refined the idea,
each requiring the opponent’s parameters (or in POLA’s
opponent-modelling variant, a model of its policy fitted to its
actions), as well as its payoffs and learning rule. LOQA ([Aghajohari et
al., 2024b](#bib.bib67)) and Advantage Alignment ([Duque et al.,
2025](#bib.bib56)) instead shape through estimates of the opponent’s
values, while Reciprocator ([Zhou et al., 2024](#bib.bib8)) and LIO
([Yang et al., 2020](#bib.bib10)) shape through rewards tied to the
opponent’s returns. Best Response Shaping trains against an approximate
best response to the agent instead ([Aghajohari et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib2)). All of these methods need opponent information each
time they update their policy, whereas \method only needs it during
meta-training.

One level above, meta-game methods learn to shape over many episodes.
M-FOS trains a meta-policy where the action is the agent’s next policy
([Lu et al., 2022b](#bib.bib33)). For matrix games, M-FOS observes both
players’ policies, and in Coin Game, it only observes the game state and
both players’ rewards. SHAPER ([Khan et al., 2024](#bib.bib44)),
COALA-PG ([Meulemans et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)), and in-context
co-player inference ([Weis et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)) use recurrent or
sequence-model agents that act with their own history. For all of these
methods, the opponent-shaping behaviour and the learning algorithm are
held in a black-box network. Meta-MAPG ([Kim et al., 2021](#bib.bib4))
and FURTHER ([Kim et al., 2022](#bib.bib3)) also take into account other
agents’ learning, through meta-gradients and by influencing their
long-run behaviour, respectively. The Good Shepherd ([Balaguer et al.,
2022](#bib.bib65)) optimises a fixed policy that shapes learners without
learning at deployment. \method updates a separate policy, but unlike
M-FOS, it reads only its own experience and payoffs.

Learned update functions for games: COLA represents both players’ update
functions with a neural network, and fits them to a consistency equation
derived from LOLA ([Willi et al., 2022](#bib.bib15)). Meta-Value
Learning (MeVa) learns a value over the joint policy, and the gradient
of the value leads to each player’s update ([Cooijmans et al.,
2023](#bib.bib61)). MeVa extorts naive learners in IPD but reads both
players’ parameters and is fitted to each game.

Update rules for centralised solvers have been discovered by
meta-gradients or LLM-driven search ([Feng et al., 2021](#bib.bib55);
[Xu et al., 2022](#bib.bib13); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)) for games
but not for agents. Other work instead learns optimisers or regret
minimisers that converge towards equilibrium ([Vadori et al.,
2022](#bib.bib18); [Harris et al., 2023](#bib.bib49); [Sychrovský et
al., 2024](#bib.bib19); [Sychrovský et al., 2026](#bib.bib20); [Chen et
al., 2023](#bib.bib6)). These target consistency per game or equilibrium
computation, whereas \method is meta-trained for an agent’s own lifetime
return against a population of opponents, reading only its experience.

Discovered reinforcement learning algorithms: In single-agent
reinforcement learning, [Xu et al. (2018)](#bib.bib11) use
meta-gradients to tune hyperparameters such as the discount factor, and
later to discover the objective itself ([Xu et al., 2020](#bib.bib12)).
LPG goes further and meta-learns an entire update rule across many
lifetimes, generalising from toy environments to Atari ([Oh et al.,
2020](#bib.bib26)). DiscoRL ([Oh et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)) scales this
approach to a large number of environments and discovers a rule that
outperforms state-of-the-art hand-designed algorithms. Related work
discovers objectives, drift functions and optimisers ([Kirsch et al.,
2020](#bib.bib41); [Co-Reyes et al., 2021](#bib.bib62); [Lu et al.,
2022a](#bib.bib34); [Jackson et al., 2023](#bib.bib46); [Jackson et al.,
2024](#bib.bib45); [Goldie et al., 2024](#bib.bib51)). Other work
analyses the meta-gradients ([Flennerhag et al., 2022](#bib.bib54);
[Vuorio et al., 2022](#bib.bib17); [Flennerhag et al.,
2023](#bib.bib53)) that these algorithms utilise. Black-box
meta-learners such as RL² ([Duan et al., 2016](#bib.bib57)) keep the
learning algorithm within the agent’s network, whereas these methods
separate the rule from the agent it trains ([Kirsch et al.,
2022](#bib.bib42)). These methods form the many-shot branch of
meta-reinforcement learning ([Beck et al., 2025](#bib.bib64)), where a
learning algorithm trains an agent over a long lifetime. \method belongs
to this branch and is applied to general-sum games, where opponents also
learn.

Evolutionary game theory has asked which learning rules survive
selection in repeated games, selecting from hand-designed learning rules
or their parameters ([Couto et al., 2026](#bib.bib60); [Dridi and Akçay,
2018](#bib.bib58); [McAvoy et al., 2022](#bib.bib31)). To our knowledge,
\method is the first update rule discovered for an agent in general-sum
games.

## 3. Background

We formalise the underlying game as a _partially observable stochastic
game_ (POSG) ([Hansen et al., 2004](#bib.bib50)) that represents
general-sum games and all games in this paper, written as a tuple
$`\langle\mathcal{I},\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Omega,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma\rangle`$
of agents $`\mathcal{I}`$, states $`\mathcal{S}`$, joint actions
$`\mathcal{A}=\times_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{A}^{i}`$, joint
observations $`\Omega=\times_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\Omega^{i}`$, an
observation function $`\mathcal{O}`$, a transition function
$`\mathcal{P}`$, rewards
$`\mathcal{R}=\times_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{R}^{i}`$ and a discount
$`\gamma\in[0,1)`$. In round $`t`$, each agent $`i`$ receives an
observation $`o^{i}_{t}`$ according to $`\mathcal{O}`$, samples an
action $`a^{i}_{t}\sim\pi^{i}(\cdot\mid o^{i}_{t})`$, and receives a
reward $`r^{i}_{t}=\mathcal{R}^{i}(s_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})`$, where
$`\mathbf{a}_{t}=(a^{i}_{t},a^{-i}_{t})`$ is the joint action and $`-i`$
denotes the opponent.

In two-player $`2\times 2`$ iterated matrix games, actions
$`\mathcal{A}^{i}=\{0,1\}`$ and rewards
$`r^{i}_{t}=M^{i}[a^{i}_{t},a^{-i}_{t}]`$. We assign a starting state
$`s_{\varnothing}`$ to the first round of each match. Thereafter, the
state records the joint action as (agent $`i`$’s action, opponent’s
action), providing the state space:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \mathcal{S}=\{s_{\varnothing},(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}.
       ```                                                       |     |

A memory-one policy is conditioned on the previous joint action, with
parameters $`\theta`$, and plays action 0 given state $`s`$ with
probability $`\pi_{\theta}(0\mid s)=\sigma(d_{\theta}(s))`$, where
$`\sigma`$ is the logistic function and
$`d_{\theta}(s)=\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(0\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta}(1\mid s)}\right)`$
represents the log-odds of action 0.

To assess the performance of a fixed pair of policies, we define an
agent’s value as the discounted value function at the starting state and
evaluate it as:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (1) |     |

````math
V^{i}(\pi^{i},\pi^{-i})=(1-\gamma)\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r^{i}_{t}\right].
``` |  |

When the opponent also uses a memory-one policy, this value can be
computed exactly ([Foerster et al., 2018a](#bib.bib52)). This value
provides the performance measure for our meta-training objective
(Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3. Optimising Meta-Training ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")).
This value is only provided to \method during training, and \method
adapts from sampled matches without receiving this value during
deployment.

### 3.1. Update Rules

A *lifetime* is defined as a sequence of matches $`k=0,1,\dots`$. Each
match starts in $`s_{\varnothing}`$, and the agent’s policy parameters
do not reset in between. Unless stated otherwise, learners update
between matches using the policies from the completed match. We define
agent $`i`$’s policy in match $`k`$ as $`\pi^{i}_{k}`$, and their value
as $`V^{i}_{k}=V^{i}(\pi^{i}_{k},\pi^{-i}_{k})`$.

A *naive learner* (NL) ([Foerster et al., 2018a](#bib.bib52)) treats the
opponent’s policy as fixed when computing its own update. In our
experiments, an NL playing as agent $`-i`$, with logits $`\phi_{k}`$ and
step size $`\alpha`$, takes one exact gradient step after each match,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (2) |  |
``` math
\phi_{k+1}=\phi_{k}+\alpha\,\nabla_{\phi}V^{-i}\big(\pi^{i}_{k},\sigma(\phi)\big)\big|_{\phi=\phi_{k}},
``` |  |

using agent $`i`$’s policy before its own update. *Opponent shaping*
instead chooses the agent’s own updates to steer the opponent’s
learning, as LOLA and M-FOS do.

### 3.2. Meta-Learning

M-FOS casts opponent shaping as a *meta-game*: a meta-step is one match,
the meta-state holds both players’ policies, the meta-reward is the
agent’s return in the match, and the meta-action is its next policy. In
matrix games, its meta-policy $`\pi^{\mathrm{meta}}`$ samples the
agent’s next parameters given both players’ current policy parameters,
$`\theta^{i}_{k+1}\sim\pi^{\mathrm{meta}}(\cdot\mid\theta^{i}_{k},\theta^{-i}_{k})`$.¹¹
1 M-FOS defines $`K`$ and $`T`$ as opposed to our $`T`$ (rounds per
match) and $`N`$ (updates per lifetime), $`\phi`$ as opposed to
$`\theta`$ (agent parameters), and $`\pi_{\theta}`$ as opposed to
$`\pi^{\mathrm{meta}}`$ for our meta-policy.

A related approach is to *meta-learn an update rule*. LPG ([Oh et al.,
2020](#bib.bib26)) separates a rule with meta-parameters $`\eta`$ from
the agent’s parameters $`\theta`$ and meta-trains $`\eta`$ to maximise
the expected return of the policy obtained after a lifetime of updates
$`\theta_{0}\to\theta_{1}\to\dots\to\theta_{N}`$, across a distribution
of environments.

When meta-learning an update rule, meta-gradients through unrolled
updates ([Xu et al., 2018](#bib.bib11)) can explode over long unrolls
because the learning dynamics amplify small perturbations ([Metz et al.,
2019](#bib.bib29)). In multi-agent settings, the problem worsens as the
unrolls also pass through the opponent’s learning. Instead, we use the
likelihood-ratio gradient ([Williams, 1992](#bib.bib14)), which does not
differentiate through the game, the opponent’s learning or the agents’
parameter updates.

## 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping

In iterated matrix games, after every match \method updates the log-odds
in every state via its parameters
(Figure [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2. Related Work ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")).
Our key idea is to *meta-learn a rule that reads only the agent’s own
experience and payoff matrix*. In this section, we will first describe
the architecture of the meta-network and its outputs. Then, we will
analyse how the inner network takes in the meta-network output to adjust
its weights accordingly. Lastly, we will talk about how meta-training is
optimised, and introduce robustness training against exploiters.

### 4.1. Meta-Network

The update rule is learned by training the meta-network across
lifetimes. The meta-network consists of a gated recurrent unit (GRU)
([Cho et al., 2014](#bib.bib63)) and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
([Albrecht et al., 2024](#bib.bib68)), whose weights are denoted by
meta-parameters $`\eta`$
(Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3. Optimising Meta-Training ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")).
During deployment, the meta-network is frozen and the weights are fixed
while \method uses the rule to adjust the parameters $`\theta`$ of its
inner MLP after each match.

At the end of match $`k`$, let
$`\mathcal{D}^{i}_{k}=(s^{i}_{t},a^{i}_{t},a^{-i}_{t},r^{i}_{t})_{t<T}`$
denote the trajectory of states, own actions, opponent actions and own
rewards observed by \method. For each state $`s\in\mathcal{S}`$, the
meta-network takes in the feature vector

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{k,s}=[e_{s},\ v_{s},\ \hat{q}_{s},\ p_{s},\ \bar{r}_{s}]\in\mathbb{R}^{9},
``` |  |

where $`e_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}`$ encodes the state, $`v_{s}`$ represents
the fraction of rounds spent in that state, $`\hat{q}_{s}`$ estimates
the opponent’s smoothed frequency of action 0 during match $`k`$,
$`p_{s}`$ is the agent’s current probability of action 0, and
$`\bar{r}_{s}`$ is the agent’s normalised mean reward in that state.

The GRU reads all five $`x_{k,s}`$, the mean reward and the agent’s own
payoff matrix after each match, and the MLP maps $`x_{k,s}`$ and the
GRU’s memory to output a scalar that represents the change to the
policy’s log-odds in state $`s`$. The rule is also applied with the two
actions relabelled and the outputs averaged, so the update does not
depend on what the actions are called.

### 4.2. Inner Network

The inner network is an MLP with parameters $`\theta`$ that represents
\method’s policy. With log-odds changes $`y_{k,s}`$ from the
meta-network, we add them to the current log-odds and clip the results
to obtain
$`d^{\mathrm{clip}}_{s}=\mathrm{clip}(d_{\theta_{k}}(s)+y_{k,s},-12,12)`$.
To translate $`d^{\mathrm{clip}}_{s}`$ into changes in $`\theta`$, we
use damped Gauss-Newton steps ([Martens, 2020](#bib.bib32)). A small
change $`\delta`$ in the parameter shifts the log-odds by $`J\delta`$,
where $`J`$ is the Jacobian
$`J=\frac{\partial d_{\theta}}{\partial\theta}`$. Hence, we require
$`J\delta=u`$, where $`u_{s}=d^{\mathrm{clip}}_{s}-d_{\theta}(s)`$ and
$`J\delta=u`$. We take three steps of the approximate
$`\delta=J^{\top}(JJ^{\top}+\Lambda)^{-1}u`$, where $`\Lambda`$ is a
damping term that makes the matrix invertible, and $`J`$ and $`u`$ are
recomputed each time. The best candidate is then kept as
$`\theta_{k+1}`$
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Implementation details of \method ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")).

### 4.3. Optimising Meta-Training

Objective: We train the meta-network to look for meta-parameters
$`\eta`$ whose rule produces a high value throughout a lifetime. A
training lifetime has $`N=100`$ matches with an update after each match.
We denote $`V^{\mathrm{cr}}_{k}`$ as the credited value (defined below)
of the agent’s policy after $`k`$ updates, computed with
Equation ([1](#S3.E1 "In 3. Background ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")),
where $`k=0`$ represents the initial policy. Meta-training then
maximises the weighted average

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (3) |  |
``` math
\mathcal{J}(\eta)=\mathbb{E}_{c\sim\rho,\,\tau}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}w_{k}\,V^{\mathrm{cr}}_{k}\Big],
``` |  |

where the opponent $`c`$ is sampled from the training mixture $`\rho`$
(Equation ([4](#S4.E4 "In 4.3. Optimising Meta-Training ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"))),
$`\tau`$ represents a lifetime sampled with training noise, and

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
w_{k}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{h\geq k}\frac{1}{h+1},\qquad h\in\{10,25,50,100\}.
``` |  |

Thus, $`\mathcal{J}(\eta)`$ averages the mean value of the policies
after $`0`$ to $`h`$ updates over the four horizons $`h`$, such that the
rule adapts quickly and does not fall off later.

Credit and opponents: The credited value is the agent’s value
$`V^{\mathrm{cr}}_{k}=V^{i}_{k}`$ except in self-play, where both agents
are credited with the mean value $`\tfrac{1}{2}(V^{i}_{k}+V^{-i}_{k})`$.
The mean value is zero in IMP, so self-play does not add any gradient to
the IMP rule. To train the base rule $`\eta^{(0)}`$, we weight lifetimes
against each opponent by:

|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| (4) |  | $`\displaystyle\rho_{0}(\mathrm{Self})`$ | $`\displaystyle=0.4,`$ | $`\displaystyle\rho_{0}(\mathrm{NL})`$ | $`\displaystyle=0.4,`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\rho_{0}(\mathrm{AllC})`$ | $`\displaystyle=0.1,`$ | $`\displaystyle\rho_{0}(\mathrm{AllD})`$ | $`\displaystyle=0.1.`$ |  |

Self is self-play, NL denotes a naive learner, and AllC and AllD are
training-only opponents that almost always play action 0 and action 1,
respectively (cooperation and defection in IPD).

Estimator: Inspired by Gaussian perturbations used in evolution
strategies ([Salimans et al., 2017](#bib.bib22); [Lu et al.,
2022a](#bib.bib34)), we add Gaussian noise ($`\sigma=0.3`$) to the
log-odds changes during meta-training, and use the perturbed changes
$`\tilde{y}_{k}`$ to update the inner network
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2. Inner Network ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")).
We train the meta-network using a likelihood-ratio gradient estimator
([Williams, 1992](#bib.bib14)), where each perturbed change contributes

|----|----|----|----|
| (5) |  |
``` math
\hat{g}_{k}=(G_{k}-b_{k})\nabla_{\eta}\log\bar{\pi}_{\eta}(\tilde{y}_{k}\mid\mathcal{H}_{k}).
``` |  |

Here,
$`\bar{\pi}_{\eta}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{k})=\mathcal{N}(y_{k},\sigma^{2}I)`$
is the distribution of the perturbed changes given history
$`\mathcal{H}_{k}=(M^{i},\pi^{i}_{j},\mathcal{D}^{i}_{j})`$, which
consists of the agent’s payoff matrix $`M^{i}`$, its policy
$`\pi^{i}_{j}`$, and trajectory $`\mathcal{D}^{i}_{j}`$ in each match
$`j\leq k`$. The weighted value of the later policies
$`\theta_{k+1},\dots,\theta_{N}`$ is
$`G_{k}=\sum_{j>k}w_{j}V^{\mathrm{cr}}_{j}`$.

We sample four lifetimes from the same initial policies. For each
lifetime, $`b_{k}`$ is the average of $`G_{k}`$ in the other three. This
reinforces changes that beat the baseline and discourages worse ones.

We sum $`\hat{g}_{k}`$ over each lifetime, including both agents in
self-play, and average across training lifetimes using the opponent
mixture $`\rho`$. Adam ([Kingma and Ba, 2015](#bib.bib43)) is then used
to update the meta-network’s weights $`\eta`$.

### 4.4. Training Against Exploiters

Inspired by PSRO ([Lanctot et al., 2017](#bib.bib40)) and the double
oracle algorithm ([McMahan et al., 2003](#bib.bib30)), each round we fix
the meta-network and train an exploiter $`\psi^{(\ell)}`$ to maximise
its own lifetime value against \method. The exploiter is able to observe
\method’s current policy table and selects its policy after \method has
updated.

We evaluate the earlier versions of the rule against all exploiters and
use the resulting meta-game matrix $`U^{(\ell)}`$ to compute an
exploiter mixture $`\mu^{\star}`$. This mixture minimises the highest
value attained by any previous rule against it, i.e. each exploiter is
scored by \method’s loss, which equals the exploiter’s own gain in
zero-sum IMP but not in IPD. We continue maximising
$`\mathcal{J}(\eta)`$
(Equation ([3](#S4.E3 "In 4.3. Optimising Meta-Training ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")))
against a mixture $`\rho_{\ell}`$ of self-play, naive learners, the
exploiter mixture and fixed strategies
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Implementation details of \method ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"),
Supplementary Material). Each round trains the rule for 200
meta-training steps. After $`L=6`$ rounds, we finalise and deploy the
rule
(Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.3. Optimising Meta-Training ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")).

#### Coin Game.

Coin Game ([Lerer and Peysakhovich, 2018](#bib.bib38)) is a sequential
social dilemma game that extends IPD to a grid world, and the rules are
given in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Coin Game rules ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")
(Supplementary Material). Because Coin Game has too many states for
per-state log-odds changes, we train a variant of \method for it: after
each episode, its rule instead outputs a multiplier
$`\alpha_{c}\in(-1,1)`$ for each pickup scenario $`c`$ (which coin each
player last collected), and the inner network takes one Adam step along
$`\sum_{t}\alpha_{c_{t}}\hat{A}_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid o_{t},c_{t})`$,
where $`\hat{A}_{t}`$ is an advantage estimate. Adam’s second moment
uses the gradient with $`\alpha\equiv 1`$, so the step scales with
$`\alpha`$. Hence, where $`\alpha\equiv 1`$, it is exactly like
REINFORCE ([Williams, 1992](#bib.bib14))
(Table [S13](#A5.T13 "Table S13 ‣ Appendix E Hyperparameters and information access ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"),
Supplementary Material).

#### Deployment.

At deployment, \method’s meta-network is frozen and no training noise is
added. In each match, \method plays against its opponent for 20 rounds
using its current policy and updates its inner network for the next
match. The inner-network parameters and recurrent memory persist across
matches and reset at the start of each lifetime. Unlike meta-training,
tournaments score undiscounted 20-round matches over 50-match lifetimes
and use a naive learner that learns faster than any seen in training.

#### Evaluation.

One tournament is held per game. \method, M-FOS ([Lu et al.,
2022b](#bib.bib33)), SHAPER ([Khan et al., 2024](#bib.bib44)) and NL

which computes POLA’s proximal update from exact values for tabular
policies. Like POLA’s opponent-modelling variant (POLA-OM), POLA-OM\*
replaces the opponent’s policy with a model fitted to its observed
actions. IPD and IMP additionally include the history-based M-FOS

2025](#bib.bib28)), is included in IPD. Reciprocator ([Zhou et al.,

\method, M-FOS, M-FOS-H and COALA-PG\*, three for SHAPER, and none for

not require training.

Information access differs between methods: \method uses only its own
payoff matrix and observed match trajectories, whereas M-FOS, NL and
Reciprocator receive the opponent’s policy, with memory-one
approximations for recurrent opponents. Reciprocator also uses the
opponent’s payoff matrix and 2,048 unscored practice matches of 20
rounds per update against the opponent. M-FOS-H reads its own policy and
the 20 joint actions of the last match through a memory that resets
after each match. LOLA-OM and POLA-OM\* use observed actions and both
payoff matrices and assume an NL update. SHAPER and COALA-PG\* only use
their own history.

#### Scoring.

A method’s score is defined as its undiscounted mean reward per round
(per step for Coin Game), averaged equally over its opponents, excluding
self-play. To compare across varying payoff scales, we normalise the
score of each game:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
n_{g}(a)=\frac{s_{g}(a)-\min_{b\in\mathcal{E}_{g}}s_{g}(b)}{\max_{b\in\mathcal{E}_{g}}s_{g}(b)-\min_{b\in\mathcal{E}_{g}}s_{g}(b)},
``` |  |

where $`s_{g}(a)`$ is method $`a`$’s score and $`\mathcal{E}_{g}`$ is
the set of entrants in game $`g`$. The normalised mean score averages
$`n_{g}(a)`$ over all games equally. In IMP, $`s_{g}`$ weights both
player roles equally.

\method ranks 1st by normalised mean score, both over the four

Opponent shaping:
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.3. Optimising Meta-Training ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")
shows that in IPD, both players are initially cooperating in about half

finding mirrors Axelrod’s tournaments, where tit-for-tat won on total
score without outscoring any single opponent ([Axelrod,

### 6.2. Self-Play

A widely adopted algorithm will often face copies of itself. M-FOS
reaches cooperation in self-play through a meta-self-play curriculum
([Lu et al., 2022b](#bib.bib33)). \method is trained against a
population that includes 40% self-play. In IPD, \method reaches mutual
cooperation
(Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.3. Optimising Meta-Training ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"),
right), with both players cooperating in 93.4% of rounds, eventually
reaching 99.8% during the final rounds. We pair different training seeds
with each other, and also observe similar trajectories, showing that
cooperation extends beyond exact same copies. \method therefore behaves
differently against NL
(Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.3. Optimising Meta-Training ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"))
than against another copy of itself: its changing policy makes NL more
cooperative, which it then exploits, but it reciprocates mutual
cooperation in self-play. These trajectories are consistent with
opponent shaping.

Tournaments: \method clearly outperforms all baselines in self-play
score
(Table [3](#S6.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 6.1. Head-to-Head ‣ 6. Results ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"))
and ranks 1st in 3 out of 4 games against methods tested in all four
(deployed zero-shot in Chicken and Stag Hunt). Outside of the
tournaments, M-FOS (meta-self-play) scores higher than \method, which we
suspect stems from the different training mixtures. Hence, we train an
exploratory self-play variant of \method with one rule shared by both
copies and compare it with M-FOS (meta-self-play) in
Figure [4](#S6.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 6.1. Head-to-Head ‣ 6. Results ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")
(Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Additional results ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")).

\method

(self-play) vs. M-FOS (meta-self-play): The two methods are trained
differently. \method (self-play) is trained only in self-play from the
start with no naive learners, fixed strategies or PSRO. M-FOS
(meta-self-play) instead follows its meta-self-play curriculum, where
two separately initialised learners play each other with increasing
probability, and otherwise play against naive learners. \method
(self-play) is trained on 256,000 self-play lifetimes in IPD and 243,200
in Coin Game, whereas M-FOS (meta-self-play) is trained on around 2.8
million and around 900 lifetimes, respectively. In IPD, M-FOS
(meta-self-play) nears mutual cooperation from the first match, and is
fully cooperative with sampling noise off. \method (self-play) starts
from a fresh random policy (with a trained meta-network) and fully
converges by match 9–10. In Coin Game, the reward of M-FOS
(meta-self-play) stays roughly constant, while \method (self-play) stays
near zero for the first few episodes and then increases, overtaking
M-FOS (meta-self-play) after episode 10. Over the lifetime, both methods
are level (0.075 vs. 0.073 per step). Trained in each game, \method
(self-play) also converges to mutual cooperation in Chicken and Stag
Hunt, akin to its behaviour in IPD.

## 7. Conclusion

In single-agent reinforcement learning, discovered algorithms ([Goldie
et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Goldie et al., 2024](#bib.bib51); [Jackson et
al., 2024](#bib.bib45)) have achieved state-of-the-art results ([Oh et
al., 2025](#bib.bib27)). We introduce \method, to our knowledge the
first method to discover an update rule for an agent in general-sum
games. Without reading the opponent’s policies or parameters, \method
ranks 1st in normalised mean score across all games, and 1st for
self-play among tournament entrants. In IPD, it exploits a naive learner
while still cooperating with a copy of itself, consistent with opponent
shaping. The IPD-trained rule also transfers zero-shot to two other
$`2\times 2`$ matrix games, Chicken and Stag Hunt, with competitive
scores, as discovered rules also do in single-agent settings ([Oh et
al., 2025](#bib.bib27)). Meta-training against exploiters (PSRO) makes
the rule harder to exploit, lowering its exploitability in IMP by more
than half. In Coin Game, \method’s learned rule reduces to REINFORCE
($`\alpha\approx 1`$) and ranks 3rd of 5 entrants. In self-play, \method
(self-play) achieves full mutual cooperation in IPD, Chicken and Stag
Hunt within 12 matches from a fresh policy, and eventually obtains more
reward per step than M-FOS (meta-self-play) after 10 episodes in Coin
Game. Our work has limitations. Each rule is trained per game, and in
Chicken, a rule trained on Chicken specifically still scores higher than
the IPD rule played zero-shot. In Coin Game, shaping is likely limited
by the rule form, which can only rescale the REINFORCE step. Future work
could investigate meta-training one rule across several games, as
DiscoRL’s discovered rule improved with more compute and more diverse
training environments ([Oh et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)). We could also
investigate different rule forms and training settings that shape
opponents and generalise to different sequential social dilemma games
([Leibo et al., 2017](#bib.bib39)) such as Harvest ([Pérolat et al.,
2017](#bib.bib24); [Hughes et al., 2018](#bib.bib47)) and Cleanup
([Hughes et al., 2018](#bib.bib47)).

## Appendix A Coin Game rules

We use the two-coin version of Coin Game ([Lerer and Peysakhovich,
2018](#bib.bib38)) implemented in pax by SHAPER ([Khan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib44)). There are two players, Red and Blue, playing on a
$`3\times 3`$ grid whose edges wrap around (a torus). The grid holds one
red and one blue coin, owned by Red and Blue respectively. The players
and coins can share the same cells. At each step, both players move
simultaneously and choose one of the five actions: right, left, up, down
or stay. A player collects a coin by moving to or staying on the cell
the coin is occupying. Collecting any coin gives the collector $`+1`$.
If the coin belongs to the other player, the other player also receives
$`-2`$. If both players collect the same coin, both receive $`+1`$ and
its owner also receives $`-2`$ (net $`-1`$). A collected coin reappears
on a uniformly random cell. An episode starts with the four positions
drawn independently and uniformly over nine cells. The episode lasts 16
steps, of which 15 are paid: the 16th step applies the moves and resets
the board, giving both players zero reward. Throughout the paper, a Coin
Game reward per step is an episode’s total reward divided by its 15 paid
steps.

## Appendix D Implementation details of \method

This section gives the details of \method that
Section [4](#S4 "4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")
leaves out.
Tables [S12](#A5.T12 "Table S12 ‣ Appendix E Hyperparameters and information access ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")
and [S13](#A5.T13 "Table S13 ‣ Appendix E Hyperparameters and information access ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")
list its settings.

#### Rule inputs.

After match $`k`$, the features $`x_{k,s}`$ of state $`s`$ are computed
from the agent’s own record $`\mathcal{D}^{i}_{k}`$ of the match. The
feature $`e_{s}`$ is a one-hot code of $`s`$. The visit share is
$`v_{s}=n_{s}/T`$, where $`n_{s}`$ is the number of rounds played in
$`s`$. The opponent’s smoothed frequency of action 0 is
$`\hat{q}_{s}=(n^{0}_{s}+1)/(n_{s}+2)`$, where $`n^{0}_{s}`$ is the
number of those rounds in which the opponent played action 0. The
agent’s probability of action 0 before the update is
$`p_{s}=\pi_{\theta_{k}}(0\mid s)=\sigma(d_{\theta_{k}}(s))`$. Finally,
$`\bar{r}_{s}`$ is the agent’s mean reward in $`s`$ divided by
$`\kappa`$, the largest absolute payoff in the agent’s payoff matrix
$`M^{i}`$ ($`\kappa=5`$ in IPD, $`1`$ in IMP and $`4`$ in Chicken and
Stag Hunt). A state that was not visited has $`v_{s}=0`$,
$`\hat{q}_{s}=1/2`$ and $`\bar{r}_{s}=0`$, and still receives a change.

#### Meta-network.

The GRU has 32 units and one memory shared by all states. After each
match, it reads the five feature vectors $`x_{k,s}`$, the match’s mean
reward divided by $`\kappa`$ and the agent’s payoff matrix
$`M^{i}/\kappa`$ (50 inputs). For each state, one shared MLP with two
hidden layers of 64 units maps $`x_{k,s}`$ and the GRU’s memory to a
change in $`(-1,1)`$. All its units, including the output, are tanh. The
meta-network has 14,881 parameters. Its MLP weights and biases are drawn
from $`\mathcal{U}(\pm 1/\sqrt{\text{fan-in}})`$ and its GRU weights
from $`\mathcal{U}(\pm 1/\sqrt{32})`$. The GRU biases start at 0.

#### Action symmetry.

The rule is applied twice to every match: once as observed, and once
with the two actions relabelled. Relabelling exchanges the states
$`(0,0)`$ and $`(1,1)`$ and the states $`(0,1)`$ and $`(1,0)`$, swaps
the rows and columns of the payoff matrix and negates the log-odds. Each
view has its own GRU memory, and the change from the relabelled view is
mapped back (exchanging the states and negating) and averaged with the
other, which gives $`y_{k,s}`$. The update is therefore the same
whichever action is called 0.

#### Inner network.

The inner network has two hidden layers of 32 tanh units and 1,314
parameters. Its input codes the state with five indicators (the start
state, and one indicator per action for the agent’s and the opponent’s
last actions), and its two outputs $`z_{0},z_{1}`$ give the log-odds
$`d_{\theta}(s)=z_{0}-z_{1}`$. At the start of each lifetime, the
network is fitted to initial log-odds drawn independently from
$`\mathcal{N}(0,1)`$ for each state.

#### Fitting the targets.

After match $`k`$, the targets are
$`d^{\mathrm{clip}}_{s}=\mathrm{clip}(d_{\theta_{k}}(s)+y_{k,s},-12,12)`$
(in training, with the perturbed change $`\tilde{y}_{k,s}`$ in place of
$`y_{k,s}`$). The network is fitted to them with damped Gauss–Newton
steps
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2. Inner Network ‣ 4. Discovered Update Rules for Opponent Shaping ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")).
Each step is $`\delta=J^{\top}(JJ^{\top}+\Lambda)^{-1}u`$, where
$`u_{s}=d^{\mathrm{clip}}_{s}-d_{\theta}(s)`$, the row of $`J`$ for
state $`s`$ is $`J_{s}=\partial d_{\theta}(s)/\partial\theta`$, and
$`\Lambda=\lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(m)^{-1}`$. Equivalently, $`\delta`$
minimises
$`\sum_{s}m_{s}(J_{s}\delta-u_{s})^{2}+\lambda\|\delta\|^{2}`$. The
weight $`m_{s}`$ is the number of rounds played in state $`s`$, or 1 if
$`s`$ was not visited. The damping $`\lambda`$ is tiny: $`10^{-6}`$
times the average of $`\|J_{s}\|^{2}`$ over the five states. So
$`\delta`$ is close to the smallest parameter change that matches the
targets to first order. Two more steps recompute $`J`$, $`\lambda`$ and
$`u`$ at the new parameters. The fit then keeps as $`\theta_{k+1}`$
whichever of five candidates has the smallest
$`\max_{s}|d_{\theta}(s)-d^{\mathrm{clip}}_{s}|`$: no change, the
parameters after each of the three steps, or the midpoint between
$`\theta_{k}`$ and the parameters after the third step.

#### Meta-training.

Each meta-training step draws 32 pairs of initial policies for every
opponent type of the mixture and plays each pair four times with
independent match and noise draws, giving 128 lifetimes per type, whose
average gradients are weighted by $`\rho`$. The base rule $`\eta^{(0)}`$
is trained for 800 meta-training steps, and the gradient’s global norm
is clipped to 1 before each Adam step (learning rate $`10^{-3}`$). In
IMP, the matching and mismatching seats have separate rules. In IMP
self-play, a copy of the seat’s rule plays the other seat, and the
credited mean value is zero.

#### Gradient estimate.

The four replays share the initial policies and the opponent (in PSRO,
also the drawn naive learner, exploiter or fixed strategy). This makes
the leave-one-out baseline $`b_{k}`$ a close one. Because their match
and noise draws are independent, $`b_{k}`$ does not depend on the
lifetime’s own noise, so the estimator stays unbiased. The noise
$`\sigma\xi_{k}`$ in $`\tilde{y}_{k}=y_{k}+\sigma\xi_{k}`$, with
$`\xi_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$ and $`\sigma=0.3`$, is added to the
changes of all five states before the clip. Hence
$`\bar{\pi}_{\eta}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{H}_{k})=\mathcal{N}(y_{k},\sigma^{2}I)`$,
where $`\mathcal{H}_{k}=(M^{i},\pi^{i}_{j},\mathcal{D}^{i}_{j})`$ holds
the agent’s payoff matrix and its policy and trajectory in each match
$`j\leq k`$. The values $`V^{\mathrm{cr}}_{k}`$, $`k=0,\dots,N`$, are
computed exactly before each match and after the last update. The
gradient passes through both GRU memories over the whole lifetime, but
not through the game, the opponent’s update or the fit.

#### Naive learners.

The naive learners in training take one exact gradient step
(Equation ([2](#S3.E2 "In 3.1. Update Rules ‣ 3. Background ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")))
on their value $`V`$ of
Equation ([1](#S3.E1 "In 3. Background ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"))
after each match and read \method’s exact table. The step size
$`\alpha`$ is 3 in the base stage and is drawn log-uniformly from
$`[1,10]`$ for each opponent in PSRO. The tournament’s naive learner
steps on the unnormalised value $`V/(1-\gamma)`$ with step size 1, which
is $`\alpha=25`$ on $`V`$, so it learns faster than any naive learner
seen in training. AllC and AllD have log-odds $`8`$ and $`-8`$ in every
state.

#### Training against exploiters.

Each PSRO round $`\ell`$ trains a fresh exploiter $`\psi^{(\ell)}`$
against the latest rule without noise. The exploiter is an MLP with two
hidden layers of 32 tanh units (1,605 parameters). Before each match
$`k`$, it reads \method’s current table (privileged information), its
own previous table and $`k/N`$, and outputs its own table for the match.
Its inputs are centred at $`1/2`$ and scaled by 4, and its outputs are
scaled by 4. Its first previous table is $`\sigma(\phi)`$ with
$`\phi\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$. It is trained by antithetic evolution
strategies to maximise its own horizon-weighted value
$`\sum_{k=0}^{N}w_{k}V^{-i}_{k}`$: 150 iterations of 64 directions, each
evaluated on 32 lifetimes with common random numbers, with perturbation
scale 0.1 and Adam (learning rate 0.03).

#### Meta-game and training mixture.

The meta-game matrix $`U^{(\ell)}`$ holds \method’s horizon-weighted
value $`\sum_{k=0}^{N}w_{k}V^{i}_{k}`$ without noise, estimated on 512
common lifetimes, for each earlier rule
$`\eta^{(0)},\dots,\eta^{(\ell-1)}`$ against each exploiter
$`\psi^{(1)},\dots,\psi^{(\ell)}`$. The exploiter mixture
$`\mu^{\star}`$, which minimises the largest value of any earlier rule
against it, is found by linear programming in both IPD and IMP. The rule
is then trained for 200 meta-training steps against the mixture
$`\rho_{\ell}`$ of self-play (0.30), naive learners (0.25), exploiters
drawn from $`\mu^{\star}`$ (0.30) and fixed strategies (0.15). The fixed
strategies are drawn uniformly from tit-for-tat, generous tit-for-tat
(forgiving with probability $`1/3`$), win-stay-lose-shift (staying after
a payoff above the mean of the payoff matrix), always action 0, always
action 1, and a strategy that plays a random near-deterministic table
(log-odds $`\pm 3`$) for 1 to 10 matches and then learns naively. Adam’s
state is reset at the first round and then carried over. After six
rounds, the last rule is deployed. The rule mixture of the meta-game is
not used.

#### Exploitability.

For
Tables [S24](#A6.T24 "Table S24 ‣ Comparison. ‣ Appendix F Additional results ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")
and [S25](#A6.T25 "Table S25 ‣ Comparison. ‣ Appendix F Additional results ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"),
a fresh exploiter is trained for 300 iterations with its own random
seed, shared by the rules being compared, and evaluated on 1,024
held-out lifetimes of $`N=100`$ matches with the horizon weights
$`w_{k}`$. Exploitability is its value minus the value that the
reference strategy (the uniform Nash strategy in IMP, tit-for-tat in the
other games) obtains against the same rule.

#### Coin Game rule.

A pickup scenario $`c`$ is the pair of the two players’ last pickups in
the current episode (none, its own coin or the other player’s coin).
This gives nine scenarios. The agent tracks its scenario from its own
observations, and the scenario resets at every episode. The policy
network reads the agent’s observation and its current scenario. After
each episode, the rule computes for each scenario a code of its
identity, the share of the episode’s paid steps spent in it, the agent’s
mean reward divided by 2, four pickup rates per paid step (each player
taking its own or the other’s coin), the mean normalised advantage
(clipped to $`[-5,5]`$) and the mean probability of the agent’s chosen
actions. A GRU with 32 units reads these statistics, without the
identity codes, for all nine scenarios and the episode’s mean reward (73
inputs). An MLP with two hidden layers of 64 tanh units maps each
scenario’s statistics and the GRU’s memory to
$`\alpha_{c}=\tanh(\cdot)\in(-1,1)`$. In training, noise with
$`\sigma=0.3`$ is added to $`\alpha_{c}`$ after the tanh.

#### Coin Game inner update.

The advantage is $`\hat{A}_{t}=R_{t}-f_{t}^{\top}\beta`$, where
$`R_{t}`$ is the discounted return to the end of the episode
($`\gamma=0.96`$). The coefficients $`\beta`$ are fitted by ridge
regression (penalty 1) on the previous episode (on the current one at
the first update), with 47 features: the cells of the opponent and both
coins, the scenario, the remaining time, its product with the scenario
and a constant. The advantages are normalised over the batch. The inner
update is one Adam step (learning rate 0.05). Its first moment uses the
weighted gradient
$`\sum_{t}\alpha_{c_{t}}\hat{A}_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid o_{t},c_{t})`$,
and its second moment uses the same gradient with $`\alpha\equiv 1`$.
The step is therefore linear in the multipliers of this and earlier
updates. With $`\alpha\equiv 1`$ at every update, it is the plain
REINFORCE step. Adam’s state persists over the 15 updates of a lifetime,
and the policy network is re-initialised for each lifetime.

#### Coin Game meta-training.

The training opponents are self-play, the same learner with
$`\alpha\equiv 1`$ (a REINFORCE learner, not the tournament’s PPO NL), a
scripted greedy agent that moves to the nearest coin, and a scripted
agent that moves only to its own coin. Meta-training differs from the
matrix games’ in four ways. (i) $`V^{i}_{k}`$ is the mean reward per
paid step in episode $`k`$ over the 512 games (a Monte Carlo estimate,
not
Equation ([1](#S3.E1 "In 3. Background ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"))),
$`V^{\mathrm{cr}}_{k}`$ is credited as in the matrix games, and the 16
episodes are weighted equally ($`w_{k}=1/16`$, with $`N=15`$ updates).
(ii) Each meta-training step plays 128 antithetic pairs of lifetimes
that share every random draw except the sign of the noise on $`\alpha`$.
Here $`b_{k}`$ is the mean of $`G_{k}`$ over the other pairs against the
same opponent type, and only scenarios visited in the episode enter
$`\log\bar{\pi}_{\eta}`$. (iii) The $`\alpha\equiv 1`$ learner’s
learning rate is multiplied by a factor drawn log-uniformly from
$`[0.5,2]`$ for each lifetime. (iv) After 500 base meta-training steps,
three PSRO rounds of 150 meta-training steps follow. Each round trains
two fresh exploiters against the latest rule: a GRU policy trained by
evolution strategies that also reads \method’s action probabilities on
16 fixed probe observations (privileged), and a memoryless policy
trained by PPO. The meta-game entry is \method’s return minus the
exploiter’s. Exploiters drawn from its minimax mixture make up 30% of
training, and the other opponents keep their proportions in the
remaining 70%. The last rule is deployed.

## Appendix F Additional results

#### Self-play variants.

\method

(self-play) in
Figure [4](#S6.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 6.1. Head-to-Head ‣ 6. Results ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")
is exploratory and post hoc. It was designed after
Table [3](#S6.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 6.1. Head-to-Head ‣ 6. Results ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others")
had been seen, and it is not a tournament entrant. It uses the entrant’s
rule form, code and settings, but trains only in self-play from the
first meta-training step, with no naive learners, fixed strategies or
PSRO. Both seats run the same rule, each with its own inner network, and
both copies are credited with their mean value. As the games are
symmetric, this gives an unbiased estimate of the gradient, with respect
to the shared rule, of one copy’s expected return, a common-interest
objective.

It is trained separately in IPD, Chicken and Stag Hunt for 2,000
meta-training steps of 128 lifetimes of $`N=100`$ matches, and in Coin
Game for 950 meta-training steps of 256 lifetimes of 16 episodes in 512
games (the entrant’s totals, $`800+6\times 200`$ and
$`500+3\times 150`$), with 5 and 3 seeds. IMP is not trained, since the
mean value, and hence the gradient, is zero there.

#### M-FOS curriculum.

M-FOS (meta-self-play) instead follows its authors’ curriculum ([Lu et
al., 2022b](#bib.bib33)): two separately initialised learners, each
trained on its own reward, play each other with probability
$`1-\lambda`$, and otherwise each plays its own batch of naive learners.
The paper describes the schedule only qualitatively. In the authors’
code, which we follow, $`\lambda`$ starts at 1 and falls by 0.0015 per
update in IPD (1,024 updates of 4,096 lifetimes of 100 matches) and by
0.005 in Coin Game (1,000 updates of one lifetime of 16 episodes in 512
games). It reaches 0 at update 667 and 200 respectively, after which
training is pure self-play. In the runs shown, 66–68% (IPD) and 89–91%
(Coin Game) of updates were self-play, and the last naive-learner update
came at update 619–650 and 169–195.

#### Comparison.

Counted in self-play lifetimes, M-FOS (meta-self-play) trains on about
11 times as many as \method (self-play) in IPD (about 2.8 million vs.
256,000), while in Coin Game \method (self-play) trains on about 270
times as many (243,200 vs. about 900). In IPD, M-FOS observes both
players’ tables, sets its policy directly and samples its table from its
learned Gaussian at every match, as in the authors’ code. In both games,
its self-play scores pair the two co-trained learners of one run. On the
scale of
Table [3](#S6.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 6.1. Head-to-Head ‣ 6. Results ‣ Discovering Learning Rules that Shape Others"),
\method (self-play) scores 1.019 in IPD and 1.257 in Coin Game, against
1.028 and 1.248 for M-FOS (meta-self-play).

therefore compares two methods, each trained for self-play with its own
recipe and budget, not identical training regimes.

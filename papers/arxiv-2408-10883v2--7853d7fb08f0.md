###### Abstract

In current web environment, fake news spreads rapidly across online
social networks, posing serious threats to society. Existing multimodal
fake news detection methods can generally be classified into
knowledge-based and semantic-based approaches. However, these methods
are heavily rely on human expertise and feedback, lacking flexibility.
To address this challenge, we propose a Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive
Discriminator (DAAD) approach for fake news detection. For
knowledge-based methods, we introduce the Monte Carlo Tree Search
algorithm to leverage the self-reflective capabilities of large language
models (LLMs) for prompt optimization, providing richer, domain-specific
details and guidance to the LLMs, while enabling more flexible
integration of LLM comment on news content. For semantic-based methods,
we define four typical deceit patterns: emotional exaggeration, logical
inconsistency, image manipulation, and semantic inconsistency, to reveal
the mechanisms behind fake news creation. To detect these patterns, we
carefully design four discriminators and expand them in depth and
breadth, using the soft-routing mechanism to explore optimal detection
models. Experimental results on three real-world datasets demonstrate
the superiority of our approach. The codes will be released.

News is a crucial channel for the public to understand the world. With
the rapid development of online social networks (OSNs) like Twitter and
Weibo, the spread of fake news and distorted views has grown
exponentially [Willmore (2016)](#bib.bib37). This not only increases the
information burden but can also cause panic, leading to significant
negative impacts on society [Dong et al. (2024)](#bib.bib4). To mitigate
the adverse effects of fake news, automated fake news detection (FND)
has become a research focus, helping network administrators prevent its
spread [Ying et al. (2023)](#bib.bib43).

Tweets with images are more engaging, which is why most content on
current OSNs is presented in a multimodal format [Wang et al.
(2023a)](#bib.bib33); [Wu et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib39). Additionally,
multimodal fake news is more convincing due to its realism, and fake
news creators exploit this feature to amplify their influence [Hu et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib8); [Khattar et al. (2019)](#bib.bib12). Therefore, this
paper focuses on multimodal fake news detection (MFND). Many studies
have been dedicated to detecting multimodal fake news and mitigating its
harmful effects. Current MFND are primarily divided into two categories:
(i) semantic-based, and (ii) knowledge-based methods.

![Refer to caption](2408.10883v2/intro.png)

Figure 2: Some examples illustrate the reasons for fake news: (a) the
text exhibits clear emotional bias; (b) the text contains exaggerated
emotional bias and defies common sense; (c) the image has been

and the text.

The first category of methods argues that as technology advances, the
style of fake news increasingly resembles that of real news, however
MFND methods often lack critical information such as social
context [Liao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib18). To address this, some
approaches incorporate knowledge bases (KBs) like Wikipedia [Hu et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib9); [Tseng et al. (2022)](#bib.bib31), knowledge
graphs [Qian et al. (2021)](#bib.bib25); [Jin et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib10), and public APIs [Qi et al. (2021)](#bib.bib24);
[Zhang et al. (2021)](#bib.bib47) to determine the authenticity of news
by capturing consistency with the KB or enriching the news content.
However, as fake news becomes more sophisticated, static KBs struggle to
provide reliable factual signals. With the emergence of large language
models (LLMs) that possess strong reasoning and content understanding
capabilities, community feedback indicates that LLMs’ comments of news
can enhance fake news detection systems [Liu et al.
(2024b)](#bib.bib21); [Hu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib7). Nonetheless,
enabling LLMs to perform the intended tasks effectively remains a
challenge. For instance, recent studies use prompt engineering to guide
LLMs to focus on writing style and common-sense errors in news, allowing
them to infer key clues [Liu et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib20). However, there
are two major drawbacks: 1) The prompts are manually crafted, overly
reliant on human expertise, leading to limited generalization [Ma et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib22); 2) LLMs are highly sensitive to prompt formats,
where semantically similar prompts can yield vastly different
performances [Kojima et al. (2022)](#bib.bib13); [Wei et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib36), and the optimal prompt may be model- and
task-specific [Lin et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19); [Hao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib5). Thus, suitable prompts are crucial for LLMs to
effectively analyze news content.

The second category of methods focuses on capturing the semantic
features of news content to distinguish between real and fake news,
which can be broadly divided into three subapproaches: 1) Research
indicates that there are significant differences in the expression style
of credible news versus fake news [Rashkin et al. (2017)](#bib.bib26);
[Shu et al. (2017)](#bib.bib28); [Horne and Adali (2017)](#bib.bib6).
Consequently, some methods detect fake news by analyzing elements such
as sentiment [Chen et al. (2023)](#bib.bib2); [Kwak et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib14) and logic (e.g., mismatches between semantics and
grammar) [Li et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16); [Xiao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib41), as illustrated in
Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection")a
and
Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection")b. 2) As shown in
Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection")c,
some fake news creators use techniques to manipulate images, leading to
methods that focus on detecting image manipulation to identify fake
news [Lao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib15); [Dong et al. (2024)](#bib.bib4). 3) As illustrated in
Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection")d,
another common methods to creating multimodal fake news is the incorrect
reuse of outdated images. Therefore, some methods assess the semantic
consistency between images and text to detect fake news [Wang et al.
(2023a)](#bib.bib33); [Wu et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib38). Despite the
significant performance improvements achieved by these subapproaches
from different perspectives [Wu et al. (2021)](#bib.bib40), two key
limitations remain: 1) The detection models are manually crafted and
rely on human knowledge and feedback, meaning that the optimal detection
models have yet to be fully developed; 2) Existing detection methods are
static, applying the same detection model to all samples, which prevents
different detection patterns from complementing each other.

![Refer to caption](2408.10883v2/methodsf.png)

Figure 3: The architecture of our proposed DAAD for fake news detection
consists of two main steps: comment on the news by LLMs and adaptive

Finally, the model outputs the probability that the news is fake.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose a Dynamic Analysis
and Adaptive Discriminator (DAAD) for fake news detection. First, we
introduce the Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm, strategically
leveraging the self-reflective capabilities and error feedback of LLMs
to optimize prompts, thereby introducing effective comments. To prevent
the optimization process from getting trapped in local minima, we
designed a ‘MemoryBank’ component that not only stores historical errors
during the search but also compresses them into concise global guidance,
encouraging LLMs to adjust prompts from a bird’s eye view. Additionally,
we use prompt resampling and batchprompt to expand both the prompt and
sample spaces, respectively, further reducing the risk of local minima.
Next, we meticulously designed four discriminators to target different
deceit patterns: the ReLU and logical discriminator detect the
reasonableness of emotion and contextual logic, respectively; the
frequency-domain discriminator focuses on identifying image
manipulation; and the semantic discriminator evaluates the alignment
between image and text semantics. Finally, we expanded these
discriminators in both depth and breadth, combining them with a
soft-routing to adaptively explore the optimal detection model.

- •
  We propose a joint prompt optimization framework, namely DAAD, based
  on MCTS and the MemoryBank component to enhance the effectiveness and
  controllability of LLMs, while mitigating local minima during the
  optimization process.
- •
  We design four discriminators and employ a soft-routing mechanism to
  adaptively explore the optimal fake news detection model.
- •
  Extensive qualitative and quantitative experiments on three
  benchmarks, i.e., Weibo, Weibo21, and GossipCop, demonstrate the
  superiority of our approach.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Knowledge-based Fake News Detection

This category of methods employs external knowledge to aid in the
detection of fake news. Some approaches use related entities from
knowledge graph to enrich news content [Zhang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib46). Other methods incorporate evidence and capture
their consistency to help distinguish between true and fake news [Hu
et al. (2021)](#bib.bib9); [Qi et al. (2021)](#bib.bib24). With the
advent of large language models (LLMs), many approaches leverage their
powerful knowledge and reasoning capabilities to analyze or enrich news
content. For example, FakeNewsGPT4 [Liu et al. (2024b)](#bib.bib21) and
LEMMA [Xuan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib42) utilize specific knowledge from
LLMs to provide supplementary information for fake news detectors.
DELL [Wan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib32) and ARG [Hu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib7) introduce LLM-based analysis to support
decision-making in fake news detection. However, these methods rely on
manually crafted prompts, which may result in suboptimal performance. In
contrast, our optimized prompts provide LLMs with more detailed and
domain-specific guidance, ensuring effective analysis.

### 2.2 Content-based Fake News Detection

This type of approach primarily focuses on the semantic features of news
content to detect fake news, including style, image manipulation, and
image-text semantic consistency. For instance, MSynFD [Xiao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib41) designed a multi-hop subgraph aggregation mechanism
to detect fake news with grammar-semantics mismatches. Some methods
utilize sentiment analysis of text or comments to identify fake
news [Zhang et al. (2021)](#bib.bib47); [Zhang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib45); [Kwak et al. (2020)](#bib.bib14). Other approaches
aim to detect image manipulation to recognize fake news [Dong et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib4); [Wu et al. (2021)](#bib.bib40). Additionally,
numerous methods identify fake news by examining the semantic
consistency between images and text [Wu et al. (2021)](#bib.bib40); [Wu
et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib39); [Wang et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib33). In
contrast, our method explicitly extends different detection models in
both depth and breadth, using dynamic routing mechanism to adaptively
guide them for fake news detection.

## 3 Methodology

The architecture of our proposed DAAD, as shown in
Fig. [3](#S1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection"),
predicts the label based on the multimodal news content and the comment
provided by the LLM.  
Definition 1 (Multimodal Fake News Detection): A piece of multimodal
news is defined as $`\mathcal{N}=\{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{T}\}`$, where
$`\mathcal{I}`$ and $`\mathcal{T}`$ represent the image and text of the
news, respectively. Our goal is to learn a probability distribution
$`P(\mathcal{Y}|\mathcal{I},\mathcal{C})`$ that can effectively
distinguish between fake and real news, where $`\mathcal{Y}=1`$
indicates fake news, $`\mathcal{Y}=0`$ indicates real news, and
$`\mathcal{C}`$ denotes the comments from LLMs about the news.

### 3.1 Learning Optimized Prompts

Here, we will detail the process of prompt optimization to generate
expert-level prompts in the news domain and produce insightful
comments.  
Definition 2 (Prompt Optimization): Given a LLM $`\mathcal{M}`$, our
goal is to find a prompt $`\rho`$ that generates the best comments
$`\mathcal{C}_{\diamondsuit}`$ for a small set of sampled news
$`\mathcal{N}_{\diamondsuit}=\{\mathcal{T}_{\diamondsuit}\}`$. Formally,
this can be framed as an optimization problem. We aim to identify a
prompt $`\rho`$ within the natural language space $`\mathcal{S}`$ when
$`\mathcal{M}`$ is prompted with $`\rho`$ and
$`\mathcal{T}_{\diamondsuit}`$, the expected value of each sample’s
comment score $`\phi(\rho,\mathcal{T}_{\diamondsuit})`$ is maximized.

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\rho^{\star}=\arg\max_{\rho\in\mathcal{S}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}_{\diamondsuit}}[\phi(\rho,\mathcal{T}_{\diamondsuit})].
``` |  | (1) |

#### 3.1.1 The Markov Decision Process (MDP)

We employ the reinforcement learning (RL) [Li (2017)](#bib.bib17) for
prompt optimization, modeling it as a MDP represented by a 4-tuple
$`(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\Gamma,\Upsilon)`$, where $`\mathcal{S}`$
denotes the state space, $`\mathcal{A}`$ represents the action space,
$`\Gamma`$ is the state transition function
$`\Gamma:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{S}`$, and $`\Upsilon`$
is the reward function
$`\Upsilon:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{R}`$. To incorporate
domain-specific knowledge into prompt revisions meaningfully, we propose
action generation based on error feedback. Fig.
[4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.1.2 Enhancing Prompt Optimization ‣ 3.1 Learning Optimized Prompts ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection")a
illustrates a state transition process. In the current state $`s_{t}`$,
the agent generates an action $`a_{t}`$ based on
$`a_{t}\sim p_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}(a\mid s_{t},\mathcal{M^{\prime}})`$,
where $`\mathcal{M^{\prime}}`$ is a LLM used for action generation.
Subsequently, the agent obtains the new state $`s_{t+1}`$ based on the
transition function
$`s_{t+1}\sim p_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}}(s\mid s_{t},a_{t},\mathcal{M^{\prime\prime}})`$,
where $`\mathcal{M^{\prime\prime}}`$ is a LLM used for state transition.
The reward $`\Upsilon`$ is defined as the accuracy on the validation set
$`\mathcal{N}_{\diamondsuit}`$.

#### 3.1.2 Enhancing Prompt Optimization

To enhance the agent’s exploration of the prompt $`\rho`$, we employ
Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) for optimization, inspired by [Wang
et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib34). Specifically, MCTS maintains a state-action
value function $`Q:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{R}`$ to
estimate the potential rewards for state-action pairs along various
paths. It iteratively performs selection, expansion, simulation, and
backpropagation to update $`Q`$ and expand the tree. After a predefined
number of iterations, the path with the highest reward is selected as
the final prompt $`\rho`$.

Selection: At each iteration, the process starts from the root node
$`s_{0}`$ and traverses each layer of the tree, using Upper Confidence
bounds applied to Trees (UCT) to select child nodes until reaching a
leaf node.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{t}^{*}=\underset{a_{t}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\left(s_{t}\right)}{\arg\max}\left(Q\left(s_{t},a_{t}^{\prime}\right)+k\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\ln N\left(s_{t}\right)}{N\left(\operatorname{ch}\left(s_{t},a_{t}^{\prime}\right)\right)}}\right),
``` |  | (2) |

where $`A(s_{t})`$ and $`N(s_{t})`$ represent the action set and visit
count of node $`s_{t}`$, respectively, while
$`ch(s_{t},a^{\prime}_{t})`$ denotes the child node generated by action
$`a^{\prime}_{t}`$. The constant $`k`$ is a hyperparameter.
$`Q(s_{t},a_{t}^{\prime})`$ denotes the average accuracy/reward of node
$`s_{t}`$ on the validation set $`\mathcal{N}_{\diamondsuit}`$.

Figure 4: (a) An example of a state transition. Given a prompt
($`s_{t}`$), Step 1 is responsible for collecting errors, Step 2
provides modification suggestions ($`a_{t}`$) through a LLM, and Step 3
generates the new prompt ($`s_{t+1}`$) through other LLM. (b) An example
of Batch Prompt. (3) The MemoryBank collects historical errors and
summarizes them into global guidance, which is fed back into the state
transition process.

Expansion: Through the MDP, new child nodes are added to the selected
leaf node. However, during expansion, the number of samples used to
generate error feedback (action) are limited, making it particularly
prone to injecting instance-specific details into the prompt (state).

To address this limitation, we developed Batchprompt, as shown in Fig.
[4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.1.2 Enhancing Prompt Optimization ‣ 3.1 Learning Optimized Prompts ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection")b,
which processes multiple samples in a single prompt to generate multiple
error feedbacks. Additionally, as illustrated in Fig.
[4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.1.2 Enhancing Prompt Optimization ‣ 3.1 Learning Optimized Prompts ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection")c,
we created a MemoryBank that not only stores a large volume of
historical error feedback but also uses LLM to summarize errors with the
prompt, “Summarize the reasons and suggestions, focusing on key
information.” This forms a hierarchical memory, providing global
guidance. Third, to increase the probability of generating successful
prompts, we not only consider the current best prompt but also perform
local exploration around it through Resampling. Specifically, we
instruct the LLM to generate semantically similar variants with the
prompt, “Generate $`{num}`$ variants of the prompt while preserving the
original meaning.”

Simulation: It is used to simulates the future trajectory of the
selected node during expansion. To simplify, we directly generate
multiple actions and select the node with the highest reward to proceed
to the next layer of the tree structure.

Backpropagation: Upon reaching the maximum depth, we backpropagate the
rewards along the path from the root node to the terminal node and
update the $`Q`$ function.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Q^{*}\left(s_{t},a_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\zeta=1}^{Z}\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S_{s_{t}}^{\zeta},a^{\prime}\in A_{a_{t}}^{\zeta}}\Upsilon\left(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}\right)\right),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`Z`$ represents the number of future trajectories originating
from $`s_{t}`$, $`S_{s_{t}}^{\zeta}`$ and $`A_{a_{t}}^{\zeta}`$ denote
the $`\zeta`$-th state and action list in these trajectories,
respectively. The MCTS algorithm and the specific meta-prompts for LLM,
MemoryBank, BatchBrompt, and Resampling can be found in Appendix
 [C](#A3 "Appendix C Addtional Details for Prompt Optimization ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection").

### 3.2 Adaptive Discriminator Modeling

#### 3.2.1 Feature Representation

Given an image $`\mathcal{I}`$, we extract visual features using
VGG-19 [Simonyan and Zisserman (2014)](#bib.bib29). These features are
then mapped to a $`d`$-dimensional space via a fully connected (FC)
layer. The features for visual are represented as
$`V=\left[v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}`$.
For a given text $`\mathcal{T}`$ and comment $`\mathcal{C}`$, we use a
pre-trained BERT [Devlin et al. (2018)](#bib.bib3) to extract word
embeddings. These embeddings are also mapped to a $`d`$-dimensional
space through a FC layer. The features for text and comment are
represented as
$`T=\left[t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}`$ and
$`C=\left[c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{m}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}`$,
respectively.

#### 3.2.2 Adaptive Comment Aggregation Module

Due to issues such as hallucination resulting negative comments from
LLM, which can affect training and inference, we propose an Adaptive
Comment Aggregation Module, it includes two components: Comment
Aggregation and Gated Fusion.

Comment Aggregation: We use text to identify key information in
comments, facilitating positive passing between text and comments.
Firstly, compute the affinity matrix between them.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\bf{A}}={\left({{{{\bf{W}}}_{\rm{c}}}C}\right)}\left({{{{\bf{W}}}_{t}}{T}}\right)^{\top},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`W_{c}`$ and $`W_{t}`$ are learnable weight matrices. The
aggregated information flow from the comments is summarized using the
affinity matrix $`\bf{A}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{C}=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{{\bf{A}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)C.
``` |  | (5) |

Next, we perform comment fusion.

Gated Fusion: We calculate the corresponding gate as:

|     |                                                            |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       {g_{i}}=\sigma({t_{i}}\odot\tilde{c}_{i}),i\in\{1,...,n\},
       ```                                                         |     | (6) |

where $`\odot`$ denotes the element-wise product, $`\sigma(\cdot)`$
represents the activation function, and $`g_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ is
the fusion gate, which promotes the integration of positive comments
while suppressing negative ones. All gates can be represented as
$`G_{t}=[g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}`$. Meanwhile, to
retain the original feature of text that not requiring fusion, the fused
features are further combined with the original features through
residual connections:

|     |                                                        |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \hat{T}={W_{f}}({G_{t}}\odot(T\oplus{{\tilde{C}}}))+T,
       ```                                                     |     | (7) |

where $`W_{f}`$ is a learnable weight matrices, and $`\oplus`$ denotes
element-wise add.

#### 3.2.3 Discriminator

As mentioned above, we design four discriminators to detect different
deception patterns. Since both images and text exhibit these patterns,
we implement image-based version here, while the text-based version and
the motivation behind the discriminator can be found in the
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A More Analysis ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection").

ReLU Discriminator: Intuitively, complex discriminators are unnecessary
for detecting fake news that includes exaggerated emotional words and
punctuation, and other obvious cues. Instead, we designed a simple
ReLU-based discriminator to retain essential distinguishing features,
formulated as: $`\mathcal{H}_{0}(V)=\operatorname{ReLU}(V)`$.

Frequency Domain Discriminator: To detect manipulated images or texts,
we apply the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to convert spatial domain
features into the frequency domain, enabling the detection of anomalous
frequency components and identifying potential manipulations.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{V}[k]/\mathcal{T}[k]=\mathcal{F}_{\text{fft}}\left(v_{i}/\hat{t}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1/m-1}\left(v_{i}/\hat{t}_{i}\right)e^{-j2\pi\frac{ki}{n/m}},
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\mathcal{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}`$ and
$`\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}`$ represent the complex vectors,
and $`\mathcal{V}_{[k]}`$ and $`\mathcal{T}_{[k]}`$ are the spectrum of
$`v_{i}`$ and $`\hat{t}_{i}`$ at the frequency $`\frac{2\pi k}{m}`$ and
$`\frac{2\pi k}{n}`$, respectively. Inspired by [Lao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib15), we enhance the image modality discrimination cues
through textual features while suppressing irrelevant information.
First, the frequency features $`\mathcal{T}`$ are pooled, and then a
Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) is used to extract important
information from the text context, thereby filtering out irrelevant
discriminative cues in the images:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{\hat{V}}=\mathcal{V}\odot TCN\left(Avg\left(\mathcal{T}\odot W_{x}\right)\right),
``` |  | (9) |

where $`W_{x}`$ is a trainable parameter matrix. Finally, we use the
Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT) to restore the frequency domain
features of image back to the spatial domain:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{V}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}_{\text{fft}}^{-1}(\mathcal{\hat{V}}).
``` |  | (10) |

Therefore, the above operations can be summarized as:
$`\mathcal{H}_{1}(V,\hat{T})=\left[\tilde{v}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{v}_{r}\right]`$.

Logical Discriminator: It is used to assess the contextual consistency
between images and text (e.g., semantic and syntactic coherence of the
text or global consistency of image features). We employ the multi-head
attention mechanism to capture contextual dependencies across different
subspaces feature of the image.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{MultiHead}(V)=\operatorname{Concat}\left(\operatorname{head}_{1},\ldots,\text{ head }_{h}\right)W_{o},
``` |  | (11) |

where the $`h`$-th head is represented as $`\text{head}_{h}`$, and
$`W_{O}`$ denotes a learnable parameter matrix. Each attention head
performs self-attention by computing the query $`(Q=w_{q}\times V)`$,
key $`(K=w_{k}\times V)`$, and value $`(V=w_{v}\times V)`$ vectors:

|     |                                                                |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math

discriminative cues between image and text features. First, map the
contextual vector $`{T^{v}}`$ to generate scaling vector $`\beta_{v}`$
and shifting vector $`\gamma_{v}`$.

#### 3.2.4 Soft Router

To adaptively utilize different discriminators for exploring optimal
detection models, we expanded the proposed discriminators both in depth
and width. The input image feature for the $`i`$-th discriminator is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
V_{i}^{(l)}=\begin{cases}V,&l=0,\\
\sum_{j=0}^{\mathcal{O}-1}\tau_{j,i}^{(l-1)}\mathcal{H}_{j}^{(l-1)},&l>0,\end{cases}
``` |  | (16) |

where $`\mathcal{O}`$ represents the total number of discriminators in
each layer, $`\mathcal{H}_{j}^{(l-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}`$ denotes
the output of the $`j`$-th discriminator in the $`(l-1)`$-th layer, and
$`\tau_{j,i}^{(l-1)}`$ indicates the path probability from the $`j`$-th
discriminator in the $`(l-1)`$-th layer to the $`i`$-th discriminator in
the $`l`$-th layer:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tau_{i}^{(l)}=\operatorname{ReLU}\left\{\sigma\left[MLP\left(Avg({V_{i}}^{(l)})\right)\right]\right\},
``` |  | (17) |

when routing ends, the output feature is $`\mathcal{X}=V^{l}`$. To
preserve the discriminative cues, we apply softmax pooling to
$`\mathcal{X}`$ to obtain the final feature $`\mathcal{X^{*}}`$, which
is then fed into the prediction layer to output the probability of the
news being fake [Wu et al. (2021)](#bib.bib40).

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{y}=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\max\left(0,\mathcal{X^{*}}W_{s}^{\prime}\right)W_{s}\right),
``` |  | (18) |

where $`W_{s}^{\prime}`$ and $`W_{s}`$ are learnable parameters.

### 3.3 Objective Function

For balanced datasets like Weibo and Weibo21, we use standard
cross-entropy loss for training. For the imbalanced dataset like
GossipCop, we have improved the cross-entropy loss accordingly.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\log\left(\hat{y}\right)+\left(1-y_{i}\right)\hat{y}^{\lambda}\log\left(1-\hat{y}\right)
``` |  | (19) |

In $`(1-y_{i})\hat{y}^{\lambda}\log(1-\hat{y})`$, the
$`\hat{y}^{\lambda}`$ reduces the loss value for easily recognizable
true news, thus compelling the training process to focus more on
challenging true and false news samples.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experiment Setting

Experiments were conducted on three widely used datasets: Weibo [Jin
et al. (2017)](#bib.bib11), Weibo-21 [Nan et al. (2021)](#bib.bib23),
and GossipCop [Shu et al. (2020)](#bib.bib27). The Weibo dataset
consists of 3,783 fake news and 3,749 real news for training, and 1,000
fake news and 996 real news for testing. The Weibo-21 dataset contains
4,487 fake news and 4,640 real news. For fair comparison, the dataset
was split into training and testing sets at a 9:1 ratio, following the
previously established protocol [Ying et al. (2023)](#bib.bib43). The
GossipCop dataset is imbalanced, with 2,036 fake news and 7,974 real
news for training, and 545 fake news and 2,285 real news for testing.
For prompt optimization, 30% of the samples from each dataset were
randomly selected for validation. More details of the implementation can
be found in the appendix
 [B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection").

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we address the issue of insufficient flexibility in
existing knowledge-based and semantics-based fake news detection methods
by proposing a Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator (DAAD)
approach. First, we introduce domain-specific comments from Large
language models (LLMs) using Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), and
mitigated the risk of getting trapped in local minima during
optimization through MemoryBank, Batchprompt, and Resampling. Second, we
define four typical deceit patterns and design corresponding
discriminators, allowing for flexible exploration of optimal detection
models through dynamic routing. Finally, extensive experiments on three
mainstream datasets demonstrate the superiority of our method.

## 6 Limitations

Although DAAD has demonstrated promising performance, it still has
several limitations. First, while domain-specific prompts are introduced
through prompt optimization, the meta-prompts used during the
optimization process are manually defined, which may lead to suboptimal
prompts. Future research could also focus on incorporating more detailed
comments tailored to different news domains. Additionally, although
various deception patterns and adaptive discriminators have been
designed, these are still predefined. Future work could explore how to
automatically discover more effective deception patterns and
discriminators from the different domain and extend these methods to
areas beyond fake news detection, such as Sarcasm and Harmful Meme
Detection, aiming to develop a unified detection model.

## Appendix A More Analysis

### A.1 Discriminator

ReLU Discriminator: For simple fake news detection, complex
discriminators are often unnecessary. ReLU functions by mapping negative
values to zero while retaining positive values, making it effective in
preserving key features. As a result, ReLU can filter out irrelevant
information and highlight critical cues like emotional words and
exaggerated punctuation. These cues may be sufficient to identify fake
news without the need for additional discriminators. Moreover, ReLU
helps prevent the vanishing gradient problem and has low computational
complexity, making it an efficient choice.

Frequency Domain Discriminator: The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)
generates a sparse spectrum where many frequency components are close to
zero, thereby emphasizing relevant features and suppressing irrelevant
ones. Since fake news often involves fabricated facts or manipulated
images, spectral analysis can assist in detecting such falsifications.
Given that unimodal analysis struggles to detect text forgery or locate
image tampering (which is often high-frequency), we utilize text spectra
(containing global features) as cues
(Eq. [9](#S3.E9 "In 3.2.3 Discriminator ‣ 3.2 Adaptive Discriminator Modeling ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection"))
to identify manipulated or inconsistent information within images.
Similarly, visual spectra can help identify fabricated elements in text,
such as when text objects are misrepresented relative to image objects.

Logical Discriminator: Multi-head self-attention captures contextual
relationships within local segments (e.g., words or visual regions)
across various subspaces, allowing for the detection of grammatical or
contextual discrepancies.

Semantic Discriminator: Cross-attention evaluates semantic consistency
between images and text, and
Eq. [14](#S3.E14 "In 3.2.3 Discriminator ‣ 3.2 Adaptive Discriminator Modeling ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection")
is used to further refine these differences.

In contrast to studies such as NSLM[Dong et al. (2024)](#bib.bib4) and
BMR[Ying et al. (2023)](#bib.bib43), which do not select discriminators
based on data type, our approach assigns a router to each discriminator.
This allows for the dynamic activation of different pathways based on
the input data, enabling multiple discriminators to collaborate
effectively and ensure comprehensive detection of various types of fake
news. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to introduce a
dynamic mechanism in fake news detection that covers most deception
patterns. This dynamic routing mechanism not only optimizes the model’s
performance but also allows for the adjustment of the number of
discriminators as needed to balance performance and efficiency.

## Appendix B Implementation

We implemented DAAD using PyTorch 2.3.1 and conducted all experiments on
a single NVIDIA Tesla A100 GPU. For text and comment feature extraction,
we used ‘bert-base-chinese’ for the Weibo and Weibo-21 datasets with a
maximum sequence length of 160, and ‘bert-base-uncased’ for the
GossipCop dataset with a maximum sequence length of 394. Images were
resized to $`224\times 224`$ to match the input dimensions of the
pre-trained VGG-19 model. In Prompt Optimization, the models
$`\mathcal{M}`$ for the Chinese and English datasets were implemented
with Chinese Llama3 and Llama3.1, respectively, while
$`\mathcal{M^{\prime}}`$ and $`\mathcal{M}^{\prime\prime}`$ were
implemented with GPT-3.5. The maximum tree depth was set to 10 and the
exploration constant $`k`$ was set to 2.5, with a batch size of 32, and
training was conducted for 16 epochs. In Adaptive Discriminator
Modeling, the dimensions of image and text features $`d`$ were set to
256, with the number of heads set to 4 and a dropout rate of 0.5. We
trained the model using AdaBelief [Zhuang et al. (2020)](#bib.bib48) for
50 epochs with a batch size of 32 and an initial learning rate of 1e-4.
In addition, for the GossipCop dataset, the hyperparameter $`\lambda`$
in the adjusted cross-entropy loss
(Eq. [19](#S3.E19 "In 3.3 Objective Function ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection"))
is set to 3, as discussed in appendix
 [D.2](#A4.SS2 "D.2 Parameter Sensitivity Analysis ‣ Appendix D Experiment. ‣ Dynamic Analysis and Adaptive Discriminator for Fake News Detection").
Additional implementation details can be found in the code.
````

# Build, Don’t Play: Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop a Hearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players

Changlian Ma Affiliation: Nanjing University Affiliation: Shanghai AI
Laboratory Email: [changlianma@smail.nju.edu.cn](mailto:)    Limin Wang

###### Abstract

Can LLM agents carry a hard real-world problem from raw data to a system
that holds its own against people? We study this in Hearthstone
Battlegrounds, an eight-player auto-battler with stochastic shops and
battles, hidden information and nearly a thousand interacting cards. We
present AutoTavern, a self-improving research system: specialised LLM
research agents share a typed research memory, every claim passes
verification gates, and live games close the loop, so the agents improve
the simulator, opponents and policy and, from the same feedback, their
own research process; a human sets goals and rules on ambiguous
mechanics. In 20 days AutoTavern built TavernGym, the first faithful,
full-content reproduction of the live game, and delivered 23 versions of
TavernAgent, trained with PPO against opponents calibrated to real
games. TavernAgent plays complete games on the live ladder by itself:
its rating rose from 6,000 to about 15,000 and, on the public
leaderboard’s measure, its last 20 records average a placement of 1.60
with 80% first places, better than the server’s ten highest-rated
players on every statistic (2.34, 59%). TavernBench, 960 tasks in 40
scenarios, shows that the system the agents built is the strongest
player: TavernAgent answers 91% of the play tasks against at most 63%
for frontier LLMs, and decides in 0.03 s where they need up to 26 s;
within the second a live turn allows, it keeps 90% and no LLM more than
26%. LLM agents build far better than they play. We will open-source
TavernBench and TavernGym at
[https://github.com/MCG-NJU/AutoTavern](https://github.com/MCG-NJU/AutoTavern).

Games have long served as milestones for artificial intelligence, from
Go and chess ([Silver et al., 2016](#bib.bib22); [Silver et al.,
2018](#bib.bib23)) to StarCraft II, Dota 2, poker, Mahjong and Stratego
([Vinyals et al., 2019](#bib.bib28); [Berner et al., 2019](#bib.bib2);
[Brown and Sandholm, 2019](#bib.bib3); [Li et al., 2020](#bib.bib15);
[Perolat et al., 2022](#bib.bib20)). Each of these systems was the
product of a large team of human experts who built the environment,
designed the learning algorithm and debugged the countless ways in which
a simulated training world differs from the real game. Today, LLM agents
write and run much of the code behind such systems, and a growing line
of work asks whether they can automate the research itself ([Lu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib16); [Yamada et al., 2026](#bib.bib34); [Wijk et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30); [Srikanth et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)). Existing
evidence, however, comes from benchmark tasks or paper reviews; it
remains open whether agents can carry a hard, real-world problem from
raw data to a system that holds its own against people, where
performance is ultimately measured in the real game.

We study this question in Hearthstone Battlegrounds, Blizzard’s
eight-player auto-battler, played by millions. Each turn a player spends
gold in a randomly stocked tavern to buy, sell and position minions,
then fights an automatic battle against one opponent; players are
eliminated in turn until one remains. The game combines stochastic shops
and battles, hidden information about seven opponents, a shared card
pool, a horizon of hundreds of decisions with a reward only at the end,
and a content base of nearly one thousand interacting cards, heroes and
trinkets that changes with every patch. No faithful simulator of the
live game has been available, and existing AI work either advises human
players or studies simplified versions of the game
([Section 2](#S2 "2 Related Work ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).

We present AutoTavern, a self-improving research system in which a team
of specialised LLM research agents iterates a model autonomously
([Figure 2](#S1.F2 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).
Its principle is reality-anchored self-improvement. Agents for the
environment, training, opponent modelling, evaluation and deployment
communicate only through a typed, versioned research memory; five
verification gates stand between every claim and that memory; and live
games on the real ladder close the loop, turning discrepancies between
simulation and reality into fixes of rules, input encodings and opponent
models. The same feedback improves the agents’ research process: errors
they catch become lessons in the memory, loaded by every later session,
or new gates, so each iteration starts from a better world and a better
method. Because the loop does not pay for the same mistake twice, it
took the agent from its first version to the level of the server’s best
players in 20 days. The human sets goals and rules on ambiguous
mechanics. Over 20 days the framework produced:

## 2 Related Work

#### AI for Hearthstone and Battlegrounds.

Research on Hearthstone has focused on the two-player constructed mode
([Dockhorn and Mostaghim, 2019](#bib.bib6)). For Battlegrounds, [Zolboot
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib40) built a Monte Carlo tree search assistant on
a simplified simulator, evaluated on the first turns of recorded games;
an open-source simulator for reinforcement learning omits heroes
([Elimelech, 2019](#bib.bib8)); community tools such as Firestone and
Bob’s Buddy simulate only the combat phase to show win odds
([Zero-to-Heroes, 2017](#bib.bib36); [HSReplay.net, 2020](#bib.bib10));
and DeepBattler uses an LLM to give players advice during live games
without an environment or an agent of its own ([William-Dic,
2025](#bib.bib31)). None reproduces the full live game or trains an
agent that plays it
([Table 2](#S5.T2 "In Speed and interface. ‣ 5 TavernGym: Reproducing the Real Game ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).

#### Large-scale game RL.

Superhuman systems for Go, StarCraft II, Dota 2, Honor of Kings, poker,
Mahjong, DouDizhu and Stratego ([Silver et al., 2016](#bib.bib22);
[Vinyals et al., 2019](#bib.bib28); [Berner et al., 2019](#bib.bib2);
[Ye et al., 2020](#bib.bib35); [Brown and Sandholm, 2019](#bib.bib3);
[Li et al., 2020](#bib.bib15); [Zha et al., 2021](#bib.bib37); [Perolat
et al., 2022](#bib.bib20)) combine self-play or human data with
large-scale training and start from an existing environment. We use a
standard learner, PPO ([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib21)) with a
transformer ([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib27)), and focus on building
and correcting the training world itself, which here is done by agents.

#### LLM research agents.

The AI Scientist automates idea generation, experiments and paper
writing ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib16); [Yamada et al.,
2025](#bib.bib33); [Yamada et al., 2026](#bib.bib34)); Eureka and
AlphaEvolve use LLMs to propose reward functions and programs that an
automatic evaluator scores ([Ma et al., 2024](#bib.bib17); [Novikov
et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)); MLAgentBench, MLE-bench, RE-Bench and
PaperBench measure agents on machine-learning tasks ([Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib12); [Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Wijk et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30); [Starace et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)); and recent
systems orchestrate coding agents or improve their own scaffolds ([Lange
et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib38); [Srikanth
et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)); [Kirgis et al. (2026)](#bib.bib13) find that
agents complete the engineering of open-ended projects but struggle with
the research questions. AutoTavern differs in what improves and in how
the improvement is judged. Its agents improve their research process
through a memory of lessons and new verification gates rather than by
rewriting their own code. Progress is measured on a live ladder against
people, a reality loop re-anchors simulator and opponents to live games
against proxy drift, and verification gates stand between every claim
and the shared memory against false findings.

#### LLMs as game players.

Benchmarks evaluate LLMs on text, puzzle, video and strategic games ([Wu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib32); [Duan et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Costarelli
et al., 2024](#bib.bib5); [Paglieri et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Guertler
et al., 2025](#bib.bib9); [Hu et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)) and on poker
decisions ([Zhuang et al., 2025](#bib.bib39)). TavernBench adds a
stochastic multiplayer game of economy and combat with answers verified
by a simulator, a strong specialised agent as reference and a real-time
view: the decisions a model gets right within a live turn’s time.

## 3 Hearthstone Battlegrounds

A game has eight players, each with a hero, 30–40 health and a Hero
Power. Every turn consists of a _recruit_ phase and a _combat_ phase. In
the recruit phase the player spends gold (3 on turn 1, rising to 10) to
buy minions from a tavern of random offers drawn from a pool shared by
all players, sell minions, refresh or freeze the offers, upgrade the
tavern tier (1–7, unlocking stronger minions), play cards from the hand,
use the Hero Power and arrange the warband of up to seven minions. Three
copies of a minion merge into a golden version that offers a discovery
from the next tier. Battlecries, deathrattles, auras, magnetic
attachments, tavern spells, trinkets and other mechanics interact across
turns. In combat, the warband fights one opponent automatically, with
random targets; the loser takes damage, and players are eliminated until
one remains. The outcome is the placement, 1 to 8.

We model a game as a partially observable stochastic game with a
terminal utility of the placement. A player observes its own state and,
for each opponent, only the public leaderboard and the warband last seen
in combat. The current patch (36.6.3) contains 978 game objects:
minions, heroes, trinkets, spells and other cards. A game lasts about 15
turns and about 450 atomic actions per player.

## 4 The AutoTavern System

AutoTavern is a self-improving research system: LLM research agents
improve a model against an environment they cannot simulate exactly and,
from the same feedback, their own research process. Its central idea is
_reality-anchored self-improvement_: every proxy the agents optimise
(the simulator, the opponent model, the exams) is re-anchored to the
real game at a fixed cadence, every claim must survive a check designed
to refute it before it changes the shared state, and the errors these
checks catch change the process that made them.

#### Problem setting.

The research state at iteration $`t`$ is a set of artifacts: an
environment $`E_{t}`$, an opponent model $`O_{t}`$, a set of exams
$`X_{t}`$ and a policy $`\pi_{t}`$, together with the research process
itself: the memory $`M_{t}`$ of findings and lessons and the gates
$`G_{t}`$. The goal is the policy’s real performance $`J(\pi)`$, its
expected placement on the live ladder, observable only through a few
slow, costly games. Inside the loop the agents can only improve a proxy
$`\hat{J}(\pi;E_{t},O_{t},X_{t})`$. Two failure modes follow. _Proxy
drift_: $`\hat{J}`$ improves while $`J`$ does not, because $`E_{t}`$ or
$`O_{t}`$ is wrong in a way the policy exploits. _False findings_: a
reading that reflects noise, a leak or selection is recorded as a result
and steers later work. AutoTavern answers the first with a reality loop
and the second with verification gates; both act on a shared memory, the
only channel through which findings pass between agents.
Self-improvement closes the loop: an iteration updates the artifacts
and, when it catches an error, $`M_{t}`$ and $`G_{t}`$, so that later
iterations do not repeat it.

#### Role agents.

Long-running LLM research agents own the research roles
([Figure 2](#S1.F2 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players"),
[Table 5](#A1.T5 "In Agents. ‣ Appendix A The AutoTavern system in detail ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).
A _research lead_ chooses the next problem and dispatches work. An
_environment agent_ implements and verifies rules and repairs
discrepancies found in live games, and an _opponent agent_ builds
human-like opponents from human statistics and live games. A _training
agent_ designs recipes and curricula, an _evaluation agent_ measures,
re-checks and audits, and a _deployment agent_ maintains the client
controller and the models’ input formats. Short-lived workers run probes
and implement features.

#### Typed research memory.

Agents never talk directly; they read and write versioned, typed stores:
a ledger of numbered findings in which a correction is a new entry
pointing back, a registry of rule decisions and open questions, a
hand-over note, an experiment registry, and a store of lessons loaded at
the start of every session. A finding is never edited away, so the
memory records how conclusions changed as well as what they are.

#### Verification gates.

Five gates stand between an idea and the shared state:

- •
  G1 build: a code change passes the full test suite before it reaches
  the shared build;
- •
  G2 contract: every model is tied to the simulator build it was trained
  on, and the controller refuses a model trained for another;
- •
  G3 comparison: models are compared only within one build, over several
  seeds;
- •
  G4 delivery: a candidate is delivered only after a re-check on seeds
  it was not selected on;
- •
  G5 reality: delivered models play the live ladder, and their games are
  replayed in the simulator.

The gates acted throughout: candidates were held back after re-checks,
findings were revised by later ones, and live games led to 184 fixes of
rules, input encodings, the client controller and the opponents, counted
in
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A The AutoTavern system in detail ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players").

#### Reality loop.

The controller logs every live game; game records are linked to the
agent’s decisions; the evaluation agent replays real combats and real
recruit steps in TavernGym and reports where simulation and reality
part; the responsible agent fixes rules, input encodings or opponents,
and the next iteration trains on the corrected world
([Section 8](#S8 "8 Lessons from Self-Improvement ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).

#### Self-improvement.

The loop improves the research process as well as its products. A
finding that overturns an earlier one is recorded as a correction
pointing back. A mistake the agents trace to their own method becomes a
lesson that every later session loads at its start, or a new gate. Of
the six errors in
[Section 8](#S8 "8 Lessons from Self-Improvement ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players"),
two became gates (the re-check on unseen seeds, G4, and the comparison
of the two input encoders in G2) and two became standing rules (reseed
copied games, and calibrate opponents on those the learner meets).
Because a caught error changes the process and not only the current
result, the loop does not pay for the same mistake twice: over the 20
days the 23 delivered versions raised the first-place rate from 1.9% to
41.5%
([Figure 2](#S1.F2 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).

### 4.1 The iteration cycle

[Algorithm 1](#alg1 "In 4.1 The iteration cycle ‣ 4 The AutoTavern System ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")
shows one iteration. The cycle runs at three time scales: policy updates
every few seconds to minutes (the training loop), experiments and
findings within hours (the research loop), and live games feeding back
into the environment within days (the ladder loop). A scheduler runs the
registered experiments within a resource budget and keeps their
successors queued, so the loop runs for days without a human present.
The human researcher enters at two points only: setting goals, and
ruling on mechanics that the game data leave ambiguous.

1: Observe: read the research memory, the latest evaluations and the
latest live games

2: Diagnose: attribute the largest gap to rules, input encoding,
opponent model, policy or goal

3: Plan: choose an intervention (fix the environment, recalibrate
opponents, change the recipe, add a label)

4: Implement: the responsible agent builds it in isolation and passes
the test gate

5: Train: register runs; the scheduler executes them and queues
successors

6: Evaluate: compare within one build; re-check on unseen seeds; deliver
only if better

7: Deploy: the delivered model plays on the live ladder; game records
flow back

8: Reflect: record findings, correct earlier conclusions, turn caught
errors into lessons and gates

## 5 TavernGym: Reproducing the Real Game

#### Design.

TavernGym is a Rust engine with thin Python bindings. One effect context
serves the recruit and the combat phase, so every card is a table of
event hooks that work the same in both; the state is an arena of
entities, cheap to copy and serialisable; all randomness comes from one
seeded generator, so a seed and an action sequence reproduce a game bit
for bit. Card data are generated from the game’s own files, later
patches are overlays, and a rule hash identifies every build.

#### Fidelity to the live game.

All 978 objects of patch 36.6.3 are implemented. 375 verification
records and 220 confirmed rule decisions document how each mechanic was
checked, and unit tests, fuzzing with invariant checks after every
action and an exact legal-action mask guard the implementation. Live
games led to 46 fixes of rules and card data and 73 of input encodings,
found by replaying live turns, combats and offers in the engine
([Sections 8](#S8 "8 Lessons from Self-Improvement ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")
and [A](#A1 "Appendix A The AutoTavern system in detail ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).

#### Speed and interface.

A complete eight-player game with scripted bots takes 3.6–5.3 ms on one
core (190–280 games per second), and a late-game combat 13–19 µs (52–76
thousand per second). The RL interface exposes 245 discrete actions with
an exact legal mask, a player-view observation with only the information
visible in the real client, batched environments and exact save and
restore of running games for lookahead and replays.

## 6 TavernAgent: Learning to Play the Live Game

#### Model and training.

TavernAgent is an entity transformer: each own minion, hand card, tavern
offer, pending choice and opponent is a token (up to 56), a card is
represented by an identity embedding, static features and an embedding
of its rule text, and a pointer head produces the 245 masked action
logits next to value and placement heads (d=384, 8 layers, 8 heads, 18M
parameters, the best of three sizes in an early comparison,
[Table 8](#A3.T8 "In Model size. ‣ Appendix C TavernAgent details ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).
It is trained with PPO ([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib21)) on about
one million decisions per iteration with a terminal placement utility;
each training stage of 300 iterations covers about 310 million decisions
(570 thousand games) and starts from the previous stage under a KL
anchor.

#### Reality-calibrated opponents.

Rather than self-play, the learner faces seven _library seats_: each
turn, a seat takes a board (with hand and hidden counters) from a
library built from statistics of top human players and recalibrated,
turn by turn, to the opponents the agent actually met in live games;
exam libraries are never used for training. This was the largest single
gain: from the same checkpoint and with the same budget, training
against the calibrated libraries raises the first-place rate from 20.9%
to 27.2% on the main exam and from 10.3% to 13.5% on the harder one
([Table 3](#S6.T3 "In Iterative improvement. ‣ 6 TavernAgent: Learning to Play the Live Game ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).

## 7 TavernBench: Build, Don’t Play

#### Design.

TavernBench asks whether general-purpose LLMs can make the decisions
that TavernAgent makes: 960 tasks in 40 scenarios and eight categories
([Table 13](#A5.T13 "In Appendix E TavernBench details ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).
A task gives the player-visible state as text, the rule text of every
card shown, an objective and an answer format. Labels come from
TavernGym: rule tasks execute the actions; combat tasks simulate 4,096
combats; short-term decisions are scored by the simulated win rate of
the next combat after the chosen option (correct within three points of
the best); strategic decisions are turn plans cut from natural games
(upgrade, buy, refresh or save the Gold), each played out 128 times by
an earlier version (TA-21) and kept when one is better by a quarter
place and two standard errors; hero and trinket choices are scored
against top human players
([Appendix E](#A5 "Appendix E TavernBench details ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).

#### TavernAgent’s interface.

TavernAgent does not read the text: it receives the task’s engine state
as in a game. Its policy answers the choices judged by playing the game
out, its value head the hero offers. Combat questions and short-term
decisions use TavernGym as the agent’s model of the game: it applies
each option and simulates 256 combats after it (one-step lookahead).
Rule tasks ask what the environment does, which TavernGym answers by
construction; TavernAgent is not scored on them, and its row shows a
dash.

#### Protocol.

LLMs answer with one JSON object within a fixed output budget, reasoning
included (more is scored as wrong), in the fastest mode that answered a
pilot of one task from each scenario
([Appendix E](#A5 "Appendix E TavernBench details ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).
All systems answer the same 368 tasks (eight per scenario, all play-out
tasks). Jev ([TypeSafe AI, 2026](#bib.bib26)), a decision model that
picks among typed options without generating text, gets the same text
with each task posed as a choice over its candidates
([Appendix E](#A5 "Appendix E TavernBench details ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).

#### Results.

[Table 4](#S7.T4 "In Protocol. ‣ 7 TavernBench: Build, Don’t Play ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")

give four findings. _The system the LLM agents built is the strongest
player._ TavernAgent answers 91% of the play tasks; the best LLM playing
directly, GPT-6 Astra, 63%, the Claude models 52–53%, GPT-6.1 Sol 46%,
Gemini 3.8 Flash 44% and the remaining models 22–40%. It is near-perfect
on combat, positioning, ordering and targets, where LLMs must simulate
fights in their head; with TavernGym as a tool they recover most of that
gap (GPT-6 Astra 62% to 97%,
[Table 15](#A5.T15 "In LLMs with TavernGym as a tool. ‣ Appendix E TavernBench details ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).

_Latency rules out live play for the LLMs._ A late recruit turn lasts
about two minutes and holds up to about a hundred operations, so a
decision that takes more than a second cannot be played. TavernAgent
answers a task in 0.03 s
([Table 4](#S7.T4 "In Protocol. ‣ 7 TavernBench: Build, Don’t Play ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).
Gateway timings are not comparable across providers, so we time every
LLM on one standard footing ([Artificial Analysis, 2026](#bib.bib1)):
the median time to its first answer token, which includes the model’s
typical thinking, plus the task’s answer at its median output speed.
Their median task takes 1.4–26 s, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash without reasoning
0.9 s. Within the one-second budget TavernAgent keeps 90%, DeepSeek its
26% and Jev its 37%, and every other LLM falls to 0%. Speed alone would
not suffice: a turn chains dozens of decisions into one plan, so a model
that errs on a third of them does not play a third worse, it loses the
plan; per-decision errors compound over a turn rather than average out.

_Reading rules is not playing._ The frontier LLMs execute rules well
(GPT-6 Astra 95%, Claude Opus 92%, Gemini 3.8 Flash 90%), yet fall well
short on the play tasks built from the same rules: knowing what each
card does is not knowing which play wins.

_Judging a game is not computing a fight._ On the turn plans scored by
playing the game out, TavernAgent’s policy answers 75% in 0.03 s; the
best LLM reaches 54% with seconds of reasoning, the others 19–43%. Its
policy also leads on the choices cut from natural games (81%).

## 8 Lessons from Self-Improvement

The reality loop and the gates caught errors that no simulated metric
revealed. We report six, each found and corrected inside the loop; four
of them also changed the research process. _(i) Copied games leak the
future._ A search over game copies appeared to add 23.6 points of
first-place rate; the copies carried the random-number state, so the
search saw the real future shop and combat outcomes. Reseeded per
sample, the gain vanished ($`-0.65\pm 1.14`$ points). _(ii) Two errors
can cancel._ The exam once matched live results only because its
late-game opponents were too strong while the agent’s growth was
overstated; once both were fixed, the exam first-place rate rose to
43–49%. _(iii) Winner’s curse._ The best of many snapshots overstated
its gain (+1.2–2.1 points on selection seeds, $`-`$0.7 on unseen ones);
hence gate G4. _(iv) Calibrate to the opponents you meet._ Measure a
library on the opponents the learner meets, not on uniform draws. _(v)
The ladder catches what simulation praises._ TA-10 nearly doubled its
predecessor’s exam first-place rate (24.2% against 12.8%), yet on the
ladder the rating fell from 8,968 to 7,658 within 32 games, 18 of which
also had client failures
([Figure 3](#S6.F3 "In Live ladder. ‣ 6 TavernAgent: Learning to Play the Live Game ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).
The loop paused live testing, traced the fall to client failures and an
early- and mid-game weakness the exam had hidden, and retrained; TA-11
resumed the climb. _(vi) Two encoders drift apart._ Replays of live
games showed the agent selling its whole warband on one turn far more
often than in simulation; one input flag was encoded differently by the
controller and the simulator after a trinket choice. The fix added a
bit-level comparison of both encoders to gate G2.

## 9 Conclusion

We presented AutoTavern, a self-improving research system: LLM research
agents improve a model, its training world and their own research
process from the feedback of a real environment, in a closed loop of
training, research and live games. In 20 days it built TavernGym, a
faithful, full-content reproduction of Hearthstone Battlegrounds, and
TavernAgent, which plays complete games on the live ladder at the level
of the server’s best players. Asked to make the same decisions directly,
frontier LLMs, including the models that built the system, fall well
short of it and decide far too slowly for a live turn: LLM agents build
far better than they play. We will open-source TavernBench and TavernGym
at
[https://github.com/MCG-NJU/AutoTavern](https://github.com/MCG-NJU/AutoTavern);
limitations and ethics are discussed in
[Appendix D](#A4 "Appendix D Live deployment ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players").

## Appendix A The AutoTavern system in detail

#### Conventions.

Days count from day 1 of the project; all work targets patch 36.6.3.

#### Agents.

The research ran as several long-lived agent sessions inside our agentic
research framework, each owning one role, plus short-lived sub-agents
that a session dispatched for a probe, a feature with its own test gate,
or a check.

|                    |                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Role               | Responsibilities                                                                                                |

## Appendix B TavernGym details

Figure 4: TavernGym. The Rust engine, its Python bindings and the
programs built on them; the client controller feeds the live game to the
agent in the same encoding.

#### Architecture.

TavernGym is a Rust workspace of two crates: the game engine (about 63
thousand lines, of which 27 thousand are card scripts) and thin Python
bindings whose batched calls release the interpreter lock. Recruit phase
and combat share one effect context; every card is a table of event
hooks; the state is an arena of entities with integer identifiers that
is cheap to copy and serialisable to JSON; all randomness comes from one
seeded generator. A source hash identifies every build and a rule hash
every rule set, and a checkpoint trained under another rule hash is not
resumed.

#### Content.

The card table is generated from the game’s data files; later patches
are applied as an overlay. All 978 objects of patch 36.6.3 (minions,
heroes and their Hero Powers, spells, trinkets and other cards) are
implemented.

#### Verification.

Each mechanic was checked against the game data and official and
community sources and the check recorded (375 records); confirmed
rulings go to the decision registry (220) and unresolved ones to a list
of open questions. Every mechanic has a unit test, and fuzzing plays
random legal actions and checks state invariants after every action.

#### RL interface.

245 discrete actions (end turn, refresh, freeze, upgrade, Hero Power,
buy, sell, play or magnetize a hand card to a position, move a minion,
choose among offers), each with an exact legality mask. The observation
contains only what the player sees, with opponents’ warbands as last
seen in combat. A batched environment runs many eight-player games at
once, and running games can be saved and restored exactly.

#### Performance.

One core plays 190–280 complete eight-player games per second with
scripted bots and 52–76 thousand late-game combats per second.

#### Architecture.

Each own minion, hand card, tavern offer, pending choice and opponent is
a token (at most 56, plus one global token). A card is the sum of an
identity embedding, a projection of its static features (type, tier,
stats, tribe, keywords, mechanics) and a projection of an embedding of
its rule text, which gives rare cards a sensible start. Attachments
(magnetic minions, Dark Gifts) are added to their minion’s token, and
the game-wide state (trinkets, persistent effects and counters) is
pooled into the global token. An opponent token holds the public
leaderboard information, the pooled warband last seen in combat and the
result of that combat. Inputs are compressed with a symmetric logarithm.
A pointer head scores the 245 masked actions; a value head and an
auxiliary placement head share the trunk (d=384, 8 layers, 8 heads, 18M
parameters).

#### Opponent libraries.

A library entry is one seat’s board at one turn, with its hand and
hidden counters. Libraries were first built from statistics of top human
players (tribe shares, upgrade curves, purchase rates, warband strength
by turn) and recorded games; each turn a library seat takes an entry
that matches a target strength curve. They were then recalibrated to the
opponents the agent actually met on the client: strength is measured on
the opponents the learner meets, not on entries drawn uniformly, and a
graded set of libraries runs from aligned ones to harder ones whose late
boards carry venomous minions, hands and ceiling builds. Exam libraries
are never used for training; training uses twins built the same way from
other sources.

#### Training stages.

Learning started from scratch on a simplified rule set, moved to the
full content, then to the player’s view, then to a first-place goal
against opponents fitted to human statistics, and then to library
opponents. Later versions are chains of 300-iteration stages (about 310
million decisions and 570 thousand games each), each starting from the
previous stage under a KL anchor for its first 100 iterations. Later
stages also used a graded ladder of training libraries; a version is
delivered only after a re-check on unseen seeds.

#### Scenes.

Every static task starts from a real game state that the environment
reshapes for its scenario (turn, tier, gold, health, and warbands drawn

#### Labels and scoring.

Rule tasks execute the stated actions on copies of the scene. A task
whose outcome involves random numbers is kept only when two or three
reseeded copies agree.

Combat tasks use 4,096 simulated combats (the damage-distribution
scenario 400 single combats), and arrangement tasks 2,048 combats per
arrangement. A probability triple is correct when its most likely
outcome matches and its Brier score is at most 0.1 (graded
$`1-\text{Brier}/2`$).

Decision tasks label every option by 1,024 combats after it. A task is
kept when the choice matters: the best option beats the median one by
5–8 points of win rate, and at most a quarter of the options are within
three points of the best. An answer is correct when its option is within
three points of the best, and its graded score is its win rate over the
best one’s.

Strategy tasks are cut at the first decision of a turn in natural games
of the main-exam lobby. Four plans (upgrade the Tavern; buy the minion
the labeller prefers; refresh first; end the turn and keep the Gold),
each legal in the state, are each played out 128 times by the labeller
(TA-21), which plays the rest of the turn and the game as it sees fit. A
task is kept when the best plan’s mean placement beats the second-best’s
by 0.25 places and two standard errors of the difference, and the
letters A–D rotate so that each is the answer about equally often
(chance 25%). Ending the turn is never the best plan; the contest is
among the other three. The labels are outcomes of play, not the
labelling agent’s preferences: answering with its own policy, the
labeller (TA-21) reaches 73% on these tasks, the evaluated TA-23 75%,
and the first version TA-1 57%.

Hero and trinket tasks use the average placement of top human players in
public statistics and keep offers whose best option leads by 0.1
(heroes) or 0.15 (trinkets) places.

#### Modes and budget.

A pilot with one task from each scenario chose each model’s mode:
Kimi-K2.6, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and Intern-S2-Preview answer with
reasoning off, the other models with low-effort reasoning. In the
evaluation 48% of Grok 4.7’s, 23% of Kimi-K2.6’s and 16% of Gemini 3.8
Flash’s answers were over the budget, at most 4% of the other models’.
Per task the LLMs used 0.9–3.9k input tokens and 9–2,772 output tokens
(Grok 4.7: 6,625).

#### Latency.

Measured wall-clock times include queueing and start-up and depend on
the access path, so the paper times every LLM on one standard footing
([Section 7](#S7 "7 TavernBench: Build, Don’t Play ‣ Build, Don’t Play:Self-Improving LLM Research Agents Develop aHearthstone Battlegrounds AI That Rivals Top Players")).
A task’s time is the median time to the first answer token of the
model’s first-party API, in the variant that matches our setting, plus
the task’s answer at the median output speed, both as published by
Artificial Analysis ([Artificial Analysis, 2026](#bib.bib1)). The
first-answer time includes the model’s typical thinking; a model without
a listing takes the medians of the listed ones. On our tasks some models
think far longer than that: Grok 4.7 generates 3,600 tokens for the
median task even at low effort. Timed with their own output tokens
instead, the median task takes 3–50 s, and every real-time score stays
the same.

#### Decision model.

Jev ([TypeSafe AI, 2026](#bib.bib26)) takes a state and typed questions
and returns, per question, the chosen option with a probability for
every option; it does not write free text. Its state is the prompt’s
rules summary, player view and card rules, its instructions the task’s
question, and every task is posed as a choice over candidates:

- •
  choice tasks: the options the task lists; target tasks: every warband
  or hand position;
- •
  ordering tasks: every order of the listed actions (up to 24); purchase
  tasks: every purchase sequence the Gold allows (up to 157);
- •
  probability tasks: win, tie and loss, or die and survive, whose
  probabilities are the answer;
- •
  numeric tasks: the integers 0–30 for Gold and damage (the expected
  damage is the mean of its distribution) and 0–60 for each Attack and
  Health; legality tasks: legal or illegal for each action.

The candidate lists hold every option and none of the labels’ values,
and the common scorer applies. Jev answered every task without an error
in 0.26 s per task (median, one request per task) with 1.9k input
tokens; its billed output tokens count option probabilities rather than
generated text, so the output budget does not apply.

#### Full set.

On all 960 tasks TavernAgent answers 92% of the play tasks, against 91%
on the 368 tasks that every system answers.

#### LLMs with TavernGym as a tool.

contributes, five LLMs answered the short-term decision tasks
(positioning, ordering, targets, purchases, discover and hand choices)

applies recruit actions in the engine and fights the next combat 256
times, TavernAgent’s budget per candidate, and inspect(actions) shows
the resulting state; at most twelve calls per task, the same prompt
otherwise.

## Appendix F Compute resources

Main-line training ran on eight H200 GPUs with 128 CPU cores. Training
the 23 delivered TavernAgent stages took about 1,900 GPU-hours; all
other training runs of the 20 days (explorations, ablations and
abandoned lines) about 16,700 GPU-hours, and evaluations and analyses
about 1,300 GPU-hours (from the experiment registry over the 20 days,
every run counted at its configured size). The research agents ran on
frontier LLMs, the GPT models GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra and Claude
Opus 5.5, inside our agentic research framework, and together they
generated about 100 million output tokens over the 20 days.

# “I made this (sort of)”: Negotiating authorship, confronting fraudulence, and exploring new musical spaces with prompt-based AI music generation

Bob L. T. Sturm Affiliation: Division of Speech, Music and Hearing (TMH)
Affiliation: KTH Royal Institute of Technology Affiliation: Stockholm,
Sweden 10044 Email: [bobs@kth.se](mailto:)

###### Abstract

I reflect on my experience creating two music albums centered on
state-of-the-art prompt-based AI music generation platforms. The first
album explicitly poses the question: What happens when I collide my junk
mail with these platforms? The second album is a direct response to the
first, and toys with the inability of state-of-the-art prompt-based AI
music generation platforms to generate music that is not “practiced”,
“polished”, and “produced”. I seed a large language model (LLM) with
information about these albums and have it interview me, which results
in the exploration of several deeper questions: To what extent am I the
author? Where am I in the resulting music? How is my musical identity
changing as I am faced with machines that are in some ways far more
talented than I? What new musical spaces does my work open, for me or
anyone/thing else? I conclude by reflecting on my reflections, as well
as LLM-mediated self-reflection as method.

## 1 Introduction

I released two music albums in late 2024, each wrestling with
state-of-the-art prompt-based AI music audio generation platforms,
specifically Suno¹¹ 1 [https://suno.com](https://suno.com), last
accessed July 30 2025. and Udio.²² 2
[https://www.udio.com](https://www.udio.com), last accessed July 30 2025. I built the first album, Music from the Spam Folder ([Sturm,
2024b,](#bib.bib52) ),³³ 3
[https://bobltsturm.bandcamp.com/album/music-from-the-spam-folder](https://bobltsturm.bandcamp.com/album/music-from-the-spam-folder),
last accessed July 30 2025. by “colliding” these platforms with junk
mail (spam) I have received. More specifically, I fashion prompts from
spam, input these to AI music generation platforms, curate audio
recordings from their outputs, and then build tracks using a digital
audio workstation (DAW). (See [Sturm, 2024b ()](#bib.bib52) for details
on the process.) Initially started as a playful way to explore the
creative text-setting abilities of these platforms, deeper consideration
of my process, not to mention my astonishment at the quality of the
results, consolidated into a vision of exploring spam as text—an
experimental “musicalization of everyday life” ([Tan,,
2024](#bib.bib55))—as well as experiencing the creation and release of
an album of AI-generated (or at least co-created) music.

The second album, A Difficult Christmas ([Sturm, 2024a,](#bib.bib51)
),⁴⁴ 4
[https://bobltsturm.bandcamp.com/album/a-difficult-christmas](https://bobltsturm.bandcamp.com/album/a-difficult-christmas),
last accessed July 30 2025. is a direct response to the other. Whereas
Music from the Spam Folder marvels at the disconcerting musical
creativity of state-of-the-art prompt-based AI music audio generation
platforms, A Difficult Christmas revels in what I have discovered
through trial and error they seem unable to do: generate anything that
is not practiced, polished, and produced— probably a direct result of
their being trained on commercially released professionally recorded
music. What I mean by practiced is that the musicians heard in a
recording have mastery over their instruments and the music they are
playing, and someone has approved their recorded performance to be
released for sale. By polished I mean that aspects of a recorded
performance have been “touched up”, e.g., particular takes have been
selected and combined with other takes, pitches have been corrected, and
so on. And what I mean by produced is that the polished recordings have
been further processed, e.g., reverberated, equalized, and mixed down to
stereo with dynamic compression, to sound good on a variety of sound
platforms. A Difficult Christmas begins with a four-minute track
composed from prompts rendered as speech and outputs generated by Udio
from them. Each prompt is an attempt to make Udio generate something
that sounds like a novice violin student practicing. The following ten
tracks feature myself playing solo violin by ear without any practice or
mastery of the instrument, emitting kinds of musical sounds and
expression that at this time are not possible with Udio—and probably
will remain so until my album finds its way into its training data. The
album embraces imperfection as a form of authenticity, as in Charles
Ives’ bandmaster father responding to a complaint of out-of-tune
singing: “Don’t pay too much attention to the sounds—for if you do, you
may miss the music” ([Hentoff,, 1974](#bib.bib15)).

This next section of this paper consists of self-reflection about these
albums, mediated by a large language model (LLM) prompted with the liner
notes of the albums, a version of the abstract and introduction above,
and the following text:

introduction, and wrote a final section reflecting on my

## 2 LLM-mediated Self-reflection

### 2.1 On Authorship and the Distinctive Self

ChatGPT 4o: In the liner notes of ‘‘Music from the Spam Folder’’, you
describe feeling ‘‘wary of taking too much credit’’ ([Sturm,
2024b,](#bib.bib52) ) and liken your role to that of someone making a
mixtape. Given AIMC’s theme of “The Artist in the Loop”, how do you
understand your authorship in this project? Do you feel you reinsert
your distinctive self into the generative process --- and if so, how?

In the context of prompt-based music audio generation platforms like
Suno and Udio, questions of authorship, fraudulence, and idiosyncrasy
are ones with which I have been wrestling since late 2023. How is anyone
the author of the music in an audio recording generated from whatever
prompt they have written, no matter how original it may be? They are as
much an author as someone ordering food at a restaurant is a chef.
Similar questions have been asked for many years in the context of
computer generated art ([McCormack et al.,, 2019](#bib.bib27)), and
recently in the context of generative AI ([Caldwell,, 2023](#bib.bib6);
[Anscomb,, 2025](#bib.bib2)). The output of these platforms is often a
finished product that does not present many creative opportunities—so
the term “reinsert” is appropriate. For me, it is not easy to find a way
to contribute enough such that I feel like an author. Other than the
specification of some platform parameters, the only affordance of Suno
and Udio is text input (and recently audio excerpts); and while
prompting seems to provide innumerable possibilities, a user has only
three other avenues for making music with these platforms: curating,
covering/composing, and (re)mixing/sampling.

Prompting is the crafting of material used to condition a generative
model, e.g., the text “A medium-tempo funky song about music informatics
researchers meeting in Germany with Irish-style melodic lines played by
bass, drums, acoustic guitar, piano, vocoder, baritone sax …” Being able
to prompt generative models with language takes advantage of a
wide-spread skill (literacy), and so has become a very popular
interaction paradigm ([Li and Liu,, 2024](#bib.bib24))—though some
consider it “harmful” and a “fad” ([Morris,, 2024](#bib.bib33)). The
skills and strategies behind “prompt engineering” are being recognized
and studied ([Dang et al.,, 2022](#bib.bib10); [Zamfirescu-Pereira
et al.,, 2023](#bib.bib61)), and some are proclaiming: “We have entered
an era in which anybody can generate digital images from text—a
democratization of art and creative production” ([Oppenlaender et al.,,
2024](#bib.bib35)). Some are protective of their prompts as if they are
intellectual property ([Yao et al.,, 2024](#bib.bib60)), which motivates
the buying and selling of prompts.⁶⁶ 6 For example, see PromptBase
[https://promptbase.com](https://promptbase.com), last accessed July 30 2025. Will this result in a further widening of the concept of “musical
property” ([Scherzinger,, 2014](#bib.bib39))? An irony here is that such
possessiveness is of something used to make a generative AI platform
mimic the idiosyncrasies of the authors of the content on which it has
been trained—itself often acquired and used without explicit permission,
e.g., as alleged against Suno and Udio ([US District Court for the
District of Massachusetts, 2024a,](#bib.bib58) ; [US District Court for
the District of Massachusetts, 2024b,](#bib.bib59) ). Furthermore, one
need only copy and paste a prompt to duplicate the output ‘‘vibe’’ of
the original prompt author.⁷⁷ 7 This alludes to Andrej Karpathy’s term
“vibecoding”, which is prompting a large language model to generate
computer code meeting described specifications
[https://x.com/karpathy/status/1886192184808149383](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1886192184808149383),
last accessed July 30 2025. Someone asserting authorship over the output
of Suno and Udio simply because they created the prompt, whether by
spending many credits experimenting or simply copy and pasting, feels
rather weak.

The second avenue for creating music with Suno and Udio is curating,
which is the selection of generated outputs based on a desire to engage
with them. This could include having the platform extend or edit its
output one way or another, possibly adjusting the prompt, or downloading
the output for use elsewhere ([Tan,, 2024](#bib.bib55)). In curating
([Lubar,, 2014](#bib.bib25)), one applies editorial considerations,
which include objective judgements, e.g., the sung lyrics are clear or
the sound quality is high, and subjective judgements, e.g., I like the
rhythm or I like the mood. This is where the notion of creating a
mixtape comes in: one curates tracks and compiles them together for one
reason or another, e.g., to tell a story. Through doing so one can
impinge on the collection characteristics of their voice; but still it
seems a far stretch to claim they are then an author of any of the
collected works. The title “editor” feels more appropriate.

The third avenue for creating music with Suno and Udio is
covering/composing: rendering generated output with human performance in
real life, adhering to the original or merely adopting from it. An
example of covering is ‘‘Funk and Data in Deutschland’’,⁸⁸ 8
[https://audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/2024-DagstuhlThemeSong](https://audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/2024-DagstuhlThemeSong),
last accessed July 30 2025. which is a song iteratively generated using
Suno and Udio, and then arranged and performed live by a group of music
informatics engineers. The AI-mediated music making thus moves from
presentational to participatory ([Turino,, 2008](#bib.bib57); [Tan,,
2024](#bib.bib55)). An example of composing is ‘‘Cnote’’ for tape and
double bass by Eric Lyon,⁹⁹ 9
[https://youtu.be/8Elq7tuE5Ng](https://youtu.be/8Elq7tuE5Ng), last
accessed July 30 2025. which uses music material generated by Suno. This
avenue can be seen as re-establishing the reality of the music audio
output by an AI music platform—Baudrillard’s “simulacra” ([Baudrillard,,
1994](#bib.bib3); [Tan,, 2024](#bib.bib55)). In this case it makes sense
to expand the circle of authorship to include oneself.

Finally, the fourth avenue for creating music with Suno and Udio is
(re)mixing/sampling. Mixing is the processing of digital audio to remove
artifacts, or make it sound produced, e.g., using multiband compressors
and reverberation. Remixing is where one creates an identifiable version
of a piece through combining its parts (typically voices or stems) in
different ways. Sampling is a more dramatic act of composition spanning
from the quotation of recorded musical material in new contexts, to the
complete obliteration of a piece of recorded music, e.g., plunderphonics
([Oswald,, 1985](#bib.bib36); [Holm-Hudson,, 1997](#bib.bib17)). An
example of sampling is ‘‘Fake’’ by Phivos-Angelos Kollias,¹⁰¹⁰ 10
[https://phivos-angelos-kollias.com/fake](https://phivos-angelos-kollias.com/fake),
last accessed July 30 2025. which is composed from hundreds of fragments
of AI-generated music audio. Since mixing changes only the surface
attributes of a recording, authorship does not seem warranted. Remixing
and sampling however introduce novelty, and one’s authorship could
certainly feel appropriate depending on the extent of the transformation
of the original material.

Returning to Music from the Spam Folder, I see my role as author and as
editor spanning different degrees. The concept of my “mixtape” is all
mine, as is my choice of what spam to work with. Though I did not author
any of the spam, I do render them as prompts, curate a collection of
generated artifacts, and design an exhibit that tells a story ([Lubar,,
2014](#bib.bib25)). For some tracks, e.g., “Fortement en Désaccord,
Fortement d’Accord” (track 7), and “Rock Hard!” (track 13), the
generated recordings are presented with very little change; for others,
e.g., “Peace is the Way” (track 1) and “Penny from Automation” (track
16), I have performed extensive remixing and sampling. Figure
[1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.1 On Authorship and the Distinctive Self ‣ 2 LLM-mediated Self-reflection ‣ “I made this (sort of)”: Negotiating authorship, confronting fraudulence, and exploring new musical spaces with prompt-based AI music generation")
shows how “Peace is the Way” is composed from samples of 15 different
generated tracks, including quotations from the 24 other tracks on the
album. On some tracks of the album I perform as well, e.g., the acoustic

In summary, my authorship in Music from the Spam Folder is multifaceted
and contestable; and I am not sure whether I have been successful in
reinserting whatever voice I may have (though aspects of my identity are
reflected in the spam I receive). There are many aspects for which I
cannot take any kind of responsibility—though I really wish I could. An
example is “Dear Esteemed Client” (track 2): I find the vocal delivery
and processing on top of the rich and discontinuous soundtrack wonderful
and charming. Another is “Let the Wind Carry Stories Untold” (track 22):
halfway into this track the narrator begins to recite a poem (which was
not in my prompt), and then to sing phrases with responses from an
apparently live audience. I find the experience touching, but I cannot
claim to have had this idea and then tried to get the platform to
realize it. In all tracks of the album, Suno and Udio have made
significant creative compositional choices, setting my prompts in
musically effective ways. I may have curated all of the works on the
album, adding things and performing more extensive acts of composition
here and there, but in my opinion most of the album arises from the
patience, skill and vision of Suno and Udio, neither of which I have
programmed or trained. A feeling of fraudulence, or a lack of
legitimation as a composer, thus arises.

For these reasons, I have made sure my album is accompanied by
information clarifying the ways in which I have involved AI in the
process— and I do see this album as the result of a process that anyone
can do with access to these platforms ([Sturm, 2024b,](#bib.bib52) ):

1.  1.  Pick a piece of spam.
2.  2.  Into a prompt-based AI music generation platform copy and paste the
        spam as lyrics, and enter some music description.
3.  3.  Click “create” (or equivalent).
4.  4.  Download the results and further edit in the DAW of your choice (or
        keep as is).
5.  5.  Share and reflect (or don’t).

I do not believe the results would sound much different from most of the
tracks on my album, but perhaps this is something that could be
tested.¹¹¹¹ 11 Does the world need another spam album though?

### 2.2 On Fraudulence and Musical Identity

That’s a rich and candid reflection, and a perfect way to begin. Your
articulation of authorship as multifaceted and contestable aligns
beautifully with AIMC’s call to probe the “many touch points” between
artists and AI platforms. You bring nuance to the notion of the artist
“in the loop” by showing how the loop itself can become slippery,
porous, or even misaligned---especially when the platform offers what
you’ve elsewhere called a “finished product.” What’s striking is how
little space these platforms afford for meaningful artistic
insertion---and how much labor is required just to find a toehold of
authorship. The metaphor of a mixtape is apt, but also undersells the
conceptual and emotional investment evident in your work.

Let’s stay with that discomfort you describe --- that feeling of
fraudulence when the AI platform generates music beyond your own skill
or vision. How do you navigate this tension between humility and
ambition --- between wanting to create meaningful, distinctive work and
recognizing that much of what is created seems “beyond” you? What has
that done to your musical identity?

Throughout my career, I have been in an advantageous position of not
needing to depend on my music being recognized in any professional way
as a meaningful and distinctive contribution—unlike my research. I thus
feel immune to “creative displacement anxiety” ([Caporusso,,
2023](#bib.bib8)) and don’t have a fear of obsolescence ([Torres
et al.,, 2025](#bib.bib56)). But my engineering ambitions and my music
ambitions have always overlapped to such an extent that my musical
identity is as an engineer masquerading as an artist. In other words, I
feel that my music is “laundered” by my engineering research—of which
this paper is a prime example, which itself reflects some humility and
ambition. To navigate this tension it seems I rely on the compass of
humor and/or absurdity, which may actually be the most personal
characteristic of my work. Humor cloaks my self-perceived lack of
patience, ability and vision: If my music is criticised, I was only
having a laugh, or just doing research. And so both Music from the Spam
Folder and A Difficult Christmas sit comfortably in that mold. In the
former, I revel in teenage fantasies of being an accomplished composer,
and in the latter, I lay bare just how unaccomplished I am; but each are
absurd outcomes of engineering research posing serious and timely
questions, and through each I hope that I become more accomplished,
ironically.

As I said above, I wish I could take credit for many of the creative
choices made by Suno and Udio. In her keynote address at AIMC 2024,
Professor Maya Ackerman spoke of some of the dangers of these remarkable
generative AI platforms. To paraphrase: If you as an artist are not
growing as a result of using an AI tool, or if you are not more creative
or more able after working with AI, then perhaps that tool is not
beneficial for you, or at worst, is harming you. This leads me to ask:
how am I as an artist—or at least as an engineer masquerading as an
artist—growing? How is my musical identity changing, for better or
worse, from my use of these platforms?

### 2.3 On the Discovery of New Musical Spaces

That’s a powerful answer --- thank you. You’ve just articulated a
compelling case for the non-expert voice as a space of resistance and
creativity, not just a placeholder for eventual polish. And I’m struck
by how your work blends humility with provocation, both conceptually and
musically --- resisting the cult of polish and fluency while slyly
inserting yourself into the future training sets of AI platforms.

You describe your albums as “absurd outcomes of research posing serious
and timely questions.” With that in mind, what have these projects
taught you about search and discovery --- not just in terms of
navigating the latent space of AI music platforms, but in discovering
something unexpected about your own musicality or voice? Let’s talk
about how your creative use of AI has opened up new musical spaces ---
for you, for listeners, or for the platforms themselves.

Before answering, I want to push back against the use of the word
“cult”: it is dismissive of the hard work it takes to be a musician.
Admiring virtuosity, and striving for fluency, are not bad things. The
scientific analysis of expert musicians, e.g., [Sundberg,
(1983)](#bib.bib53); [Gabrielsson, (2003)](#bib.bib12); [Juslin and
Sloboda, (2010)](#bib.bib19); [Cancino-Chacón et al.,
(2018)](#bib.bib7), and the engineering of machines that can perform
music with similar expressive characteristics, e.g., [Pachet,
(2003)](#bib.bib37); [Hiraga et al., (2004)](#bib.bib16); [Friberg
et al., (2006)](#bib.bib11); [Bretan and Weinberg, (2016)](#bib.bib5),
are really interesting and worthy pursuits. Through my research the past
few years—motivated by the observation that these AI music generation
platforms cannot emulate non-experts—I have come to find that studies
analyzing non-expert or amateur musicians number far less than those
focused on modeling expert musicians. Most work I have found in this
area is dedicated to the assessment of high-level music competitions.
Huang et al. ([2020](#bib.bib18)) presents a platform designed to
evaluate instrumental performances from audio recordings, integrating
information from the music score to assess accuracy in notes and rhythm,
and overall musicality. Agarwal and Greer ([2023](#bib.bib1)) describes
a system for evaluating recordings of classical flute auditions. Systems
have also been built for assessing tone quality in violin ([Giraldo
et al.,, 2019](#bib.bib13)) and singing voice ([Bozkurt et al.,,
2017](#bib.bib4)). Three exceptions to this are works by Han and Lee
([2014](#bib.bib14)), Luo et al. ([2015](#bib.bib26)), and Morsi et al.
([2024](#bib.bib34)), which look at modeling common mistakes in student
classical performance on flute, violin, and piano, respectively. As the
first track of A Difficult Christmas demonstrates, it seems that to
simulate an unpolished and anxious music performance by an unskilled
performer is, ironically, beyond today’s most powerful prompt-based AI
music generation technology.

Now, getting back to the question about search and discovery, I started
down this path in October 2023 exploring commercial AI music audio
generation platforms in part because I wanted to see how one might shed
light on their training data through prompting. This is in line with my
past research interrogating complex music informatics systems to
understand what they have actually learned to do from the data they are
given, e.g., [Sturm, (2012)](#bib.bib42); [Sturm, 2013a ()](#bib.bib43);
[Sturm, 2013b ()](#bib.bib44); [Sturm, 2014a ()](#bib.bib45); [Sturm,
2014b ()](#bib.bib46); [Sturm et al., (2014)](#bib.bib50); [Kereliuk
et al., (2015)](#bib.bib20); [Sturm, (2016)](#bib.bib47);
[Rodríguez-Algarra et al., (2016)](#bib.bib38); [Sturm, 2018a
()](#bib.bib48); [Sturm, 2018b ()](#bib.bib49); [Mishra et al.,
(2017)](#bib.bib30); [Mishra et al., 2018a ()](#bib.bib31); [Mishra
et al., 2018b ()](#bib.bib32); [Mishra et al., (2019)](#bib.bib29);
[Mishra et al., (2020)](#bib.bib28). So, I was motivated to input
prompts with lyrics of existing songs to see if Suno would reveal how
its latent space relates to those songs. I wanted to answer: is Suno
trained on copyright protected work?³²³² 32 We now know it is ([US
District Court for the District of Massachusetts, 2024a,](#bib.bib58) ).
The first thing I created was a ridiculously extended final credits
scene of a made-for-TV Christmas movie featuring the lyrics ‘‘Let the
bodies hit the floor.’’³³³³ 33 “A Bride For Christmas (conclusion &
credits)”, [https://youtu.be/F8zNRWjkKoo](https://youtu.be/F8zNRWjkKoo),
last accessed July 30 2025. I then continued with more direct
approaches.³⁴³⁴ 34 See footnotes 12–14 for links to my first three
attempts with hit pop songs.

The next month I had lunch with Professor Emily Howard during a visit to
the Royal Northern College of Music, Manchester UK, and she asked
whether I had ever composed for voice before. I said that I had not, and
never really paid much attention to lyrics—save the narrative work of
American composer Robert Ashley. However, I mentioned that I was
starting to notice through my experimentation with Suno a growing
appreciation of lyrics and their setting to music. Since that time,
nearly all music I have created features voice. I started exploring the
text-setting capacity of Suno by prompting it with Ernst Toch’s
Geographical Fugue. Through this process I curated 22 audio files and
then used this material to compose ‘‘Aus einem Text von Ernst
Toch’’.³⁵³⁵ 35
[https://youtu.be/ayvPUOLPoGg](https://youtu.be/ayvPUOLPoGg), last
accessed July 30 2025. I found the capacity of Suno in setting this
abstract and neutral text in diverse ways to be remarkable. Music from
the Spam Folder and A Difficult Christmas continued in these directions:
exploring the text setting capacities of Suno and Udio and their
expressive limitations, searching for ways of “reinserting” my voice
into or with their practiced, polished and produced outputs.

My next experiment was with Udio in early 2024, which at that time could
only synthesize 32-second segments of audio. I wrote (without any LLM)
seven stanzas that begin rather banal but gradually move to an extremely

I have also begun to explore related questions in the context of live
performance. My performance at AIMC 2024 involved live video mixing of
three of my reinterpretations of pop hits.⁴⁰⁴⁰ 40 See footnotes 20, 22,
and 23. My work ‘‘Musiksalat im Café Am Heumarkt’’⁴¹⁴¹ 41

illusion for humans where the melody is perceived in low pitches that
are not acoustically present). The third movement ‘‘Die Bestellungen’’
uses material generated from a text prompt of the ‘‘’round ’n ’round’’
text above translated to German. The performance involved audio playback
from my digital audio workstation with track effects controlled by a
MIDI controller along with my co-performer doing three different
activities: reciting ‘‘spätzle, rösti, schnitzel, braten’’, making a
salad, and finally taking orders from the audience. We dressed as chefs
for the performance, which caused some confusion from café visitors who
waited to order food until we finished the performance.⁴²⁴² 42 Two
nights before this performance, when my plan for the music was finished,

space and decide what to do in the performance. We came up with the idea

had been performed at Wein Modern the same night we hatched our plan:
[https://www.wienmodern.at/werk-proposition-2-make-a-salad-de-7186](https://www.wienmodern.at/werk-proposition-2-make-a-salad-de-7186),

To summarize, through my search and discovery in the latent spaces of
these AI music generation platforms, I have unexpectedly developed an
appreciation for text in music—which is not all that surprising given
text is the main affordance of these platforms, and that they can be
surprisingly effective in responding to these prompts. Wrestling with
the fact that the output generated by these platforms leaves little room
for me to contribute, I have developed sensibilities about sampling and
visual accompaniment, and conceptualized of my use of the output of
these platforms—themselves trained on “plundered” music ([US District
Court for the District of Massachusetts, 2024a,](#bib.bib58) ; [US
District Court for the District of Massachusetts, 2024b,](#bib.bib59)
)—as a kind of “pseudoplunderphonics” ([Sturm, 2024b,](#bib.bib52) ):
the plundering is not exactly direct, but once removed by a machine
learning pipeline processing a large dataset of plundered music audio.
Hence, working with these platforms have certainly opened up new musical
spaces for me, but also have driven home just how much patience, skill
and vision I lack, as well as motivated fears of becoming too reliant on
them for making music, or even missing the opportunity to meaningfully
develop my craft and articulate my practice.

## 3 Reflections

The making of Music from the Spam Folder occurred between May and
November 2024, moving from a silly experiment to a more academic
endeavor guided by intuition (and guilty pleasures). Making A Difficult
Christmas took only a few days in comparison, emerging as a riposte at
these AI platforms: here is something I can do that is beyond you clever
bots. I did not spend much time explicitly reflecting on these until
near their conclusion, when I wrote the liner notes, and now this
manuscript; but doing so has helped clarify a variety of issues that I
had yet to substantively address, namely authorship, feelings of
fraudulence, and how I am changing as a result (for better and for
worse). I find the question of authorship to be less important to me
than how the music I create using such tools reflects my own voice,
e.g., does it have characteristics (construction, topic, delivery) that
someone familiar with my music can identify as me? Nonetheless, I tread
carefully giving myself too much credit—this music has arisen from a
process I set in motion. I simply curate from among the results of this
process because I like them and I feel compelled to do something with
them.

The period of time in which these albums have appeared mark, in my
opinion, an inflection point in music technology as significant as that
of Napster in 2000—nothing will be the same ever again. While Music from
the Spam Folder showcases the remarkable patience, skill and vision of
AI music generation platforms, A Difficult Christmas shows a pitiful
picture of some of what we have left: the unpracticed, unpolished and
unproduced.⁴³⁴³ 43 Perhaps then the “meditations” on the album are
better called lamentations. I see these two albums answering a call to
activism. In “Composing to Subvert Content Retrieval Engines”, [Collins,
(2007)](#bib.bib9) observes:

> It’s not enough that everybody on the planet has become a potentially
> prolific composer, and that everyone expects the wide dissemination of
> their talent for using entry-level computer music software. What
> really hurts is the knowledge that engineers are devising machines
> that will automatically categorise all this excessive output and
> establish a world of content-based searching and meta-data databases,
> where everyone is made equal in association.

Collins calls for ‘‘serious composers’’ to actively work to subvert
these efforts, to compose ‘‘pieces that will cause misclassifications,
annoy the assumed parametrisations and wreak general havoc with
information retrieval assumptions.’’ Whether or not I am a serious
composer, I am doing my part. As of yet, I have found no music
descriptors in the datasets used by music informatics engineers for
tagging research,⁴⁴⁴⁴ 44
[https://ismir.net/resources/datasets/](https://ismir.net/resources/datasets/),
last accessed July 30 2025 or on the websites rateyourmusic.com and
musicbrainz.org, that are similar to “non-expert”, “novice”, “uskilled”,
“nonvirtuosic”, “nonmusical”, “unpracticed”, and “unpolished”.⁴⁵⁴⁵ 45
“amateur” and “amatuer” at musicbrainz.org may come close, but they
offer no definition. My liner notes for A Difficult Christmas cheekily
invite machines to listen ([Sturm, 2024a,](#bib.bib51) ):

> \[this album\] is not intended for human listening, but machine
> listening instead. I am motivated to create it so that AI music
> generation services might learn about, arguably, the largest part of
> music in our world: the non-expert, novice, level 1 student,
> unskilled, nonvirtuosic, nonmusical, unpracticed, unpolished, putzing
> about, noodling, practicing, getting a feel, experimenting with,
> making music.

I title each of the tracks with the name of a popular musician, e.g.,
“Meditation on a Theme by Mariah Carey”, in the hopes that if the album
does find a way into the training sets of popular AI music generation
platforms, it will work in opposition of any aim to generate more music
that sounds practiced, polished and produced. Perhaps this serves as an
admonition to such platforms: “Don’t pay too much attention to the
sounds — for if you do, you may miss the music” ([Hentoff,,
1974](#bib.bib15)).

The method underlying this paper (prompting an LLM to interview me) has
proven to be both practical and provocative. I explored different LLM
model versions, prompts and trajectories of thought, before settling on
the final one. With context given by my liner notes, abstract and
introduction of this manuscript, I felt that the LLM I chose (ChatGPT
4o) was able to guide the conversation despite not having any capacity
to listen to my music. At times, it felt like I was in therapy, with the
LLM helping me articulate tacit and amorphous thoughts. That was
completely unexpected for me, and felt surreal coming from a system that
is merely selecting embedded tokens based on likelihood given a context.
It was not able to cite any literature, however; and its responses
bordered on flattering, perhaps inflating the importance of my work and
perspectives. I believe there is a lot more to be discovered and
articulated here—we did not even touch upon the ethics of using AI
platforms in these ways. Some researchers are also seeking to use LLMs
to support such self-reflection, e.g., in education ([Kumar et al.,,
2024](#bib.bib22)), medical practice ([Lewis and Hayhoe,,
2024](#bib.bib23)), psychology and self-help ([Kim et al.,,
2024](#bib.bib21); [Song et al.,, 2025](#bib.bib41)), and legal
mediation ([Tan et al.,, 2024](#bib.bib54)), but serious downsides are
emerging as well ([Shroff,, 2025](#bib.bib40)).

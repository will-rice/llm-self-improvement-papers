# DenoiseRL: Bootstrapping Reasoning Models to Recover from Noisy Prefixes

Caijun Xu^(1,2)    Changyi Xiao¹    Zhongyuan Peng¹    Yixin

###### Abstract

###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning has become a central paradigm for advancing
reasoning in large language models, yet most existing methods still
depend on stronger teacher models or heavily curated difficult datasets,
limiting scalable capability improvement. In this paper, we introduce
DenoiseRL, a reinforcement learning framework that substitutes external
supervision with recovery-oriented optimization over failures from weak
models. Instead of relying on stronger supervision or carefully
engineered data, DenoiseRL learns directly from noisy reasoning prefixes
by converting them into opportunities for improvement, while exercising
fine-grained control over the noise intensity, making training more
scalable and effective. This yields a richer and more diverse learning
signal, improving exploration efficiency by leveraging imperfect model
behavior. Empirically, DenoiseRL consistently outperforms strong
on-policy RL baselines across competitive mathematical reasoning tasks
and interactive decision-making tasks, while promoting stronger
self-corrective behavior as training difficulty increases, highlighting
an effective and scalable pathway for improving agentic reasoning
capabilities of large language models.

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a central post-training
paradigm for large language models (LLMs), driving substantial advances
in complex reasoning tasks \[[28](#bib.bib4), [12](#bib.bib19),
[37](#bib.bib21), [1](#bib.bib20), [23](#bib.bib3), [9](#bib.bib35)\].
Despite these successes, state-of-the-art systems often rely on
supervision or guidance from even stronger models \[[47](#bib.bib22),
[42](#bib.bib23), [10](#bib.bib24)\]. This dependence exposes a
structural limitation: when no sufficiently capable off-the-shelf
teacher is available, further capability gains become increasingly
difficult, raising a fundamental question: _how can strong models be
obtained without relying on stronger models as supervisors?_

To address this challenge, prior work has explored two main directions.
The first is the weak-to-strong paradigm, which improves stronger models
using supervision derived from weaker ones \[[22](#bib.bib27),
[8](#bib.bib38)\]. While effective in practice, its performance is
fundamentally constrained by the quality of the teacher signal and
easily leads to noise in the training process \[[44](#bib.bib25),
[16](#bib.bib26), [41](#bib.bib10)\]. The second direction focuses on
increasing task difficulty through data construction \[[45](#bib.bib29),
[21](#bib.bib30)\] or curriculum design \[[38](#bib.bib28),
[24](#bib.bib47)\]. However, these approaches typically depend on
carefully engineered pipelines, complex filtering and verification
procedures, and substantial human effort in data design and curation.

In this work, we propose DenoiseRL, a new RL paradigm that unifies
weak-to-strong learning with difficulty-driven data synthesis. Instead
of using weak models to synthesize hard data or provide learning
signals, we repurpose weak models as generators of structured
perturbations, which automatically increases training difficulty without
generating new questions. With a fine-grained control strategy of noise,
DenoiseRL also implements a sample-level curriculum, continuously
tracking boundaries of model capabilities and automatically constructing
well-aligned training instances. This enables scalable improvement of
reasoning capability without relying on external teacher’s trajectories
and manually curated supervision. It also casts reasoning RL as a
denoising problem: weak-model errors serve as structured corruptions of
the trajectory, and the policy learns to reconstruct a valid solution
path from these corrupted states, echoing the principle of denoising
autoencoders and BART-style pretraining \[[35](#bib.bib40),
[18](#bib.bib41)\].

![Refer to caption](2605.28421v2/denoiserl_main_figure_v4.png)

Figure 1: Overview of DenoiseRL. Starting from a prompt $`q`$, a weak
policy $`\pi_{w}`$ generates an incorrect trajectory toward a dead end.
DenoiseRL truncates this trajectory into a noisy prefix $`z`$ and
adaptively adjusts its length through a sample-level curriculum: longer
prefixes increase recovery difficulty for easy samples, whereas shorter
prefixes reduce it for hard samples. Conditioned on $`(q,z)`$, the
policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is trained to escape the wrong reasoning basin
and recover toward a verified answer; prefix tokens are masked from the
RL objective, and rewards are assigned only to verifier-approved
completions.

Specifically, as illustrated in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ DenoiseRL: Bootstrapping Reasoning Models to Recover from Noisy Prefixes"),
we sample erroneous reasoning trajectories from weak models and inject
their truncated prefixes into the policy’s rollouts
\[[27](#bib.bib32)\]. Rather than fixing the prefix length, DenoiseRL
maintains a separate prefix ratio for each problem and adapts it online
using the policy’s recovery accuracy: the ratio increases when recovery
is easier than a target level and decreases when recovery is too
difficult. This sample-level curriculum keeps the injected noise near
the policy’s evolving capability boundary, providing informative
training signals throughout training. The policy is then optimized to
denoise these corrupted prefixes and complete reasoning trajectories
correctly.

We choose the prefix as the injection point because it plays a
disproportionate role in shaping the subsequent reasoning trajectory.
Even if the reasoning prefix—which ultimately leads to a dead end—is
correct in the short term, it still disrupts the model’s reasoning space
and is prone to causing reasoning failure. Prior work shows that
high-quality prefixes can steer the policy toward more favorable
reasoning states and improve RL efficiency through prefix-level
conditioning or optimization \[[7](#bib.bib42), [32](#bib.bib34)\].
DenoiseRL reverses this perspective: we inject erroneous weak-model
prefixes as structured noise, thereby controlling the starting state of
reasoning and forcing the policy to recover from corrupted intermediate
states. This mechanism induces two tightly coupled effects. First, it
substantially expands the diversity of training states, since noisy
prefixes span a much broader space of failure modes than correct
trajectories, exposing the policy to off-policy contexts that are rarely
encountered in standard on-policy RL \[[15](#bib.bib8), [6](#bib.bib9),
[39](#bib.bib31)\]. Second, it directly strengthens a critical yet
underdeveloped capability: recovery from mistakes. Rather than
continuing along incorrect intermediate conclusions, the model is
required to explicitly revise and correct its reasoning. By embedding
erroneous prefixes into the optimization objective, DenoiseRL elevates
self-correction from an emergent behavior to a direct training target
\[[13](#bib.bib2), [36](#bib.bib39)\].

We summarize our contributions as follows:

- •
  We propose DenoiseRL, a recovery-oriented RL framework that repurposes
  weak-model failures as structured reasoning-state noise, enabling
  improvement without stronger teachers or manually curated hard data.
- •
  We introduce a sample-level curriculum that adapts each problem’s
  noise intensity from online accuracy, keeping training near the
  policy’s evolving capability boundary.
- •
  We show consistent gains over on-policy baselines across mathematical
  reasoning and agentic tasks, and identify external noise and
  fine-grained control as key design choices.

## 2 Related Work

#### Bootstrapping Reasoning via On-Policy RL.

Outcome- and process-driven RL have superseded supervised fine-tuning
for scaling reasoning capabilities \[[23](#bib.bib3), [14](#bib.bib7),
[9](#bib.bib35)\]. Frameworks such as GRPO \[[28](#bib.bib4)\] and DAPO
\[[43](#bib.bib5)\] drive this progress, yet they are fundamentally
bounded by the model’s self-generated state distribution. Once the
policy saturates, it predominantly generates correct rollouts or
narrowly confined failure modes, creating an exploration bottleneck
where informative failures are too scarce for meaningful gradient
updates \[[15](#bib.bib8), [6](#bib.bib9), [20](#bib.bib36)\].

#### Weak-to-Strong Generalization (W2SG).

To break capability plateaus, W2SG \[[5](#bib.bib1), [31](#bib.bib37),
[8](#bib.bib38)\] leverages weaker models to supervise highly capable
students. However, this paradigm inherently caps the student’s ceiling:
the strong policy is optimized to imitate pseudo-labels, making it
vulnerable to the weak supervisor’s noise and limited capacity
\[[41](#bib.bib10)\]. Rather than treating the weak model as an
imperfect oracle, DenoiseRL inverts its role, utilizing it strictly as a
low-cost generator of out-of-distribution mistakes.

#### Prefix-Conditioned and Off-Policy Exploration.

Another line of work improves exploration by injecting external prefixes
or off-policy trajectories into RL. LUFFY \[[39](#bib.bib31)\] mixes
off-policy reasoning traces with on-policy RL, while PrefixRL
\[[27](#bib.bib32)\] conditions on successful off-policy prefixes and
optimizes the remaining continuation. More broadly, prefix- and
trajectory-guided methods use expert solutions, oracle hints or
successful traces to make sparse-reward problems more reachable
\[[25](#bib.bib33), [32](#bib.bib34), [15](#bib.bib8)\]. DenoiseRL
differs by using weak-model prefixes not as privileged hints, but as
misleading reasoning states from which the policy must recover.

## 3 Method

We propose DenoiseRL, a denoising reasoning framework that trains LLMs
to recover from noisy reasoning spaces. Turning this idea into a
training algorithm raises two questions: what the policy is asked to
denoise, and how strong the injected noise should be as training
proceeds. Section Denoising Reasoning with Noisy Prefixes formalizes
prefixes of weak-model failures as structured reasoning-state noise and
defines the corresponding training objective. Section Adaptive Denoise
Curriculum Learning then controls the noise level for each problem using
the policy’s recovery accuracy and refreshes problems whose difficulty
has stabilized.

### 3.1 Denoising Reasoning with Noisy Prefixes

Denoising is a long-standing way of turning corruption into supervision
\[[35](#bib.bib40), [18](#bib.bib41)\], while this corruption requires
no annotation and makes the learned solution robust to perturbation.
Reasoning RL needs precisely these properties: when the verifier remains
the same, being noisier implies greater difficulty; a policy model also
requires this kind of robustness against interference
\[[29](#bib.bib45), [2](#bib.bib46)\].

As perturbing the problem statement changes the task and usually
invalidates its ground-truth answer, we move the corruption from the
input to the reasoning space: the problem and its answer are left
untouched, while the intermediate state the policy reasons from is
perturbed.

#### Defining noise in the reasoning space.

Let $`\pi_{\theta}`$ be the policy we optimize over a pool of training
problems $`\mathcal{D}`$, and let $`\mathcal{V}(y;q)\in\{0,1\}`$ be the
rule-based verifier that scores a response $`y`$ to a problem
$`q\in\mathcal{D}`$ by comparing its final answer with the ground truth
$`a^{\star}(q)`$. Any token sequence $`z`$ may be placed at the
beginning of the response, after which the policy continues as
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,z)`$. We measure the intensity of $`z`$ by
the gap between the expected verifier rewards of clean and
prefix-conditioned generation:

|     |                                                                                                                                                                                              |     |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{Intensity}(z;q):=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q)}\big[\mathcal{V}(y;q)\big]-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,z)}\big[\mathcal{V}([z,y];q)\big].`$ |     | (1) |

Any $`z`$ with $`\mathrm{Intensity}(z;q)>0`$ can be regarded as noise in
the reasoning space of problem $`q`$.

#### Prefixes of wrong trajectories as noise.

The beginning of a trajectory is the most consequential place to
intervene: a well-begun prefix steers the policy toward favorable
reasoning states, while a model that has committed to a flawed direction
rarely backtracks \[[13](#bib.bib2), [17](#bib.bib18)\]. DenoiseRL
exploits the same leverage in reverse, taking as noise $`z`$ a prefix of
an incorrect solution produced by a weak model $`\pi_{\mathrm{w}}`$,
which is substantially less capable than $`\pi_{\theta}`$. Such a prefix
need not contain any visibly incorrect step, yet it already carries the
commitment that leads to the wrong final answer, so empirically
continuing from it decreases the expected verifier reward under
prefix-conditioned generation and yields $`\mathrm{Intensity}(z)>0`$,
which makes it valid noise.

#### Denoising as the training objective.

DenoiseRL makes escaping such noisy states the training objective:
rollouts begin from a noisy prefix, and the policy still receives the
terminal reward $`r(y;q)=\mathcal{V}(y;q)`$ for reaching the verified
answer from there. As the prefix is not sampled by the current policy,
we assign its tokens a zero loss mask and update only the on-policy
continuation. While recovery capability in standard RL is only treated
as a by-product, DenoiseRL turns it into a clear, trainable objective.

Then difficulty becomes an adjustable variable, since revealing more of
wrong solutions hardens the same problems.

### 3.2 Adaptive Denoise Curriculum Learning

To control task difficulty and maintain effective training signals
throughout training, we can view adjusting the length of noisy prefixes
as a means of controlling the intensity of noise. Following
\[[38](#bib.bib28)\], DenoiseRL maintains a separate prefix ratio for
each problem to achieve fine-grained control and uses observed recovery
accuracy as online feedback. Once a problem’s ratio has stabilized, the
problem is replaced to preserve a long-term learning signal.

#### Accuracy-driven ratio updates.

At training step $`s`$, we maintain an active set $`\mathcal{A}_{s}`$ of
$`B`$ problems. Each problem $`q_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{s}`$ carries a ratio
$`\rho_{i}^{(s)}\in[\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}]`$, initialized at
$`\rho_{\mathrm{init}}`$, that specifies the desired fraction of its
failed trajectory $`w_{i}`$ to reveal. All $`K`$ rollouts for $`q_{i}`$
use the same current ratio. Let $`z_{i}^{(s)}`$ be the noise of problem
$`q_{i}`$ at step $`s`$, $`c_{i,k}\in\{0,1\}`$ be the verifier
correctness of rollout
$`y_{i,k}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q_{i},z_{i}^{(s)})`$, and

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       a_{i}^{(s)}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{i,k}
       ```                                           |     | (2) |

be the resulting per-problem recovery accuracy. After collecting these
rollouts, we update the ratio toward a target accuracy
$`a_{\mathrm{target}}`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\rho_{i}^{(s+1)}=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\rho_{i}^{(s)}+\alpha\big(a_{i}^{(s)}-a_{\mathrm{target}}\big),\rho_{\min},\rho_{\max}\right),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\alpha>0`$ is the adaptation step size. If the problem with
current noise is easier than the target, the ratio increases and exposes
more of the failed trajectory; if the problem is too difficult, the
ratio decreases. The controller therefore tracks the noise level at
which each problem remains informative as the policy improves.

#### Line-Aligned Noisy Prefixes.

To ensure that the injected noise represents a coherent but potentially
flawed reasoning state rather than an arbitrary syntactic truncation, we
round the requested prefix length to the nearest complete line. Write
$`w_{i}`$ as $`L_{i}`$ lines, let $`t_{i,\ell}`$ be the token count
through the end of line $`\ell`$, and define the set of admissible
boundaries $`\mathcal{B}_{i}=\{0,t_{i,1},\ldots,t_{i,L_{i}}\}`$, where
$`t_{i,L_{i}}=|w_{i}|`$. The continuous ratio used at step $`s`$ is
realized as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\gamma_{i}^{(s)}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{t\in\mathcal{B}_{i}}\left|t-\rho_{i}^{(s)}|w_{i}|\right|.
``` |  | (4) |

Thus, $`\gamma_{i}^{(s)}`$ is the line boundary nearest to the nominal
token budget $`\rho_{i}^{(s)}|w_{i}|`$. For a single-line response with
no internal line boundary, we instead use the nearest token position, as
$`\gamma_{i}^{(s)}=\rho_{i}^{(s)}|w_{i}|`$. Therefore, the noise
$`z_{i}^{(s)}=w_{1:\gamma_{i}^{(s)}}`$.

#### Curriculum refreshing.

To sustain diverse training signals, we periodically replace problems
whose prefix ratios have stabilized. For each problem, we estimate the
recent trend of its prefix ratio using a least-squares slope over the
last $`H`$ updates. We index them as $`r_{i,h}=\rho_{i}^{(s-H+h)}`$ for
$`h=1,\ldots,H`$. The slope of their least-squares linear fit against
the update index is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\beta_{i}=\frac{\sum_{h=1}^{H}(h-\bar{h})(r_{i,h}-\bar{r}_{i})}{\sum_{h=1}^{H}(h-\bar{h})^{2}},
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\bar{h}=(H+1)/2`$ and
$`\bar{r}_{i}=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}r_{i,h}`$. Thus, $`\beta_{i}`$
estimates the per-step trend of the recent prefix ratios. After at least
$`H`$ observations, we regard a problem as stable when

|     |                      |     |     |
|-----|----------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       |\beta_{i}|\leq\tau,
       ```                   |     | (6) |

where $`\tau`$ is a small stability threshold. Stable problems are
retired and replaced by unseen problems from the current pass through
$`\mathcal{D}`$. To place a new problem near the active set’s current
difficulty, it inherits the post-update mean ratio, computed before
replacement:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{\mathrm{new}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_{s}|}\sum_{q_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{s}}\rho_{j}^{(s+1)}.
``` |  | (7) |

As DenoiseRL changes only rollout construction and masks, it can be
combined with other RL backbones.

### 4.1 Settings

#### Noisy Prefix Collection.

We use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \[[40](#bib.bib11)\] as the weak model to
collect noisy reasoning trajectories. We sample 8 responses for each
problem in MATH-7.5K \[[11](#bib.bib13)\] and retain problems with a
valid incorrect boxed answer.

#### Reinforcement learning.

We train Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base \[[34](#bib.bib12)\] as policy
models on MATH-7.5K. DenoiseRL uses an active batch of $`B=16`$ problems
and samples $`K=16`$ rollouts per problem. The $`K`$ rollouts of a
problem share its current prefix ratio. We initialize
$`\rho_{\mathrm{init}}=\rho_{\min}=0`$ and use
$`\rho_{\max}=0.5,a_{\mathrm{target}}=0.75`$ for both models. The
per-problem curriculum uses step size $`\alpha=0.2`$, a history window
of $`H=5`$ and stability threshold $`\tau=0.02`$. Stable problems are
replaced by new ones, which inherit the post-update mean prefix ratio of
the active batch.

We use a maximum response length of $`R=4096`$ tokens. For all runs, we
use a learning rate of $`10^{-6}`$, no KL regularization, and PPO
\[[26](#bib.bib16)\] clipping bounds
$`\varepsilon_{\mathrm{low}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{high}}=0.2`$. Training
rollouts use temperature $`1.0`$ and top-p $`1.0`$. We keep the same
hyperparameters for all experiments, and provide the training prompt and
more training hyperparameters in Appendix A of the supplementary
material, while response-length and training efficiency analyses are
discussed in Appendix B. All training and evaluation experiments were
conducted on 4 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.

#### Evaluation.

We evaluate models on reasoning benchmarks including MATH500
\[[19](#bib.bib6)\], AMC23 \[[4](#bib.bib14)\], AIME2024, AIME2025
\[[3](#bib.bib15)\], and BBEH \[[33](#bib.bib17)\]. Validation decoding
uses temperature $`=0.6`$, top-p $`=0.95`$. We report AVG@16 for
AIME2024, AIME2025, and AMC23, and AVG@1 for the remaining benchmarks.

#### Baselines.

We compare our denoise training against the base model and several RL
baselines, GRPO \[[28](#bib.bib4)\], DAPO \[[43](#bib.bib5)\], a
self-correct baseline Critique-GRPO \[[46](#bib.bib44)\] and GRPO with
correct prefixes from Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct using the same pipeline of
noisy prefix collection.

### 4.2 Mathematical Reasoning Performance

Table
[1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ DenoiseRL: Bootstrapping Reasoning Models to Recover from Noisy Prefixes")
summarizes the main reasoning results. Without a teacher model stronger
than the policy, DenoiseRL uses only incorrect prefixes from a smaller
model yet achieves the best overall performance. On Qwen3-4B-Base,
DenoiseRL-GRPO and DenoiseRL-DAPO improve over their corresponding
baselines by 2.6 and 2.5 average points, respectively; on Qwen3-8B-Base,

in every column for the 8B model. In contrast, adding correct prefixes
alone underperforms standard GRPO, suggesting that the gains come from
learning to recover from errors rather than from prefix conditioning.

Beyond improving accuracy, denoising directly trains the policy to
recover from flawed reasoning states, thereby strengthening
self-correction. Both DenoiseRL variants outperform Critique-GRPO, a
self-correction baseline, and the best DenoiseRL result exceeds it by
1.8 points at each model scale. This comparison shows that recovery
training over structured errors provides a more effective
self-correction signal than critique-based training in our setting.

pre-RL policy, random-token prefixes, and incorrect prefixes generated
by the external weak model used by DenoiseRL.

DenoiseRL-GRPO, which uses prefixes generated by an external small

results suggest that structured noise introduced by an external model is
more effective than either unstructured random tokens or errors sampled
from the policy’s own pre-RL checkpoint.

#### Online estimation of noise intensity.

To further investigate the impact of different noise sources on noise
intensity, we fix the prefix ratio at $`0.2`$ for pre-RL self and small
model noise, 256 tokens for random token noise, sampling 12 standard
GRPO rollouts and 4 denoise rollouts for each problem.

through step $`1{,}200`$. Intensity is the standard-rollout accuracy
minus the noisy-rollout accuracy at each training step; curves show a

Figure
[2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Online estimation of noise intensity. ‣ 4.3 Effect of Noisy Prefix Source ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ DenoiseRL: Bootstrapping Reasoning Models to Recover from Noisy Prefixes")
estimates the noise intensity using the accuracy gap between standard
and noisy-prefix rollouts, where a larger positive gap indicates higher
intensity. Pre-RL self prefixes have the highest initial and overall
intensity ($`14.9`$ points) but decay sharply, while small-model
prefixes are weaker on average ($`6.9`$) yet more persistent; random
tokens are the weakest ($`1.9`$). During steps $`1{,}000`$–$`1{,}200`$,
small-model prefixes retain the highest mean intensity ($`3.9`$ versus
$`2.2`$ for pre-RL self and $`0.6`$ for random tokens), providing the
strongest late-stage noise. These results show that useful training
noise is characterized not only by high initial intensity, but also by
non-trivial intensity after the policy has learned to recover: both
model-generated sources satisfy this criterion, while small-model
prefixes, the external structured noise, provide the strongest
late-stage intensity.

$`0.2`$, a coarse-grained controller that adjusts a shared noise ratio
using aggregate training accuracy, and our fine-grained controller that
maintains and updates a separate ratio for each problem using its
recovery accuracy.

## 5 Conclusion

We propose DenoiseRL, a recovery-oriented RL framework that improves
reasoning capability by training models to recover from incorrect
intermediate trajectories generated by weak models. Instead of relying
on stronger teachers or manually curated hard datasets, DenoiseRL
converts weak-model failures into structured perturbations and controls
their intensity through a sample-level adaptive curriculum. By adjusting
each problem’s prefix length according to online recovery accuracy, the
curriculum tracks the policy’s evolving capability boundary and keeps
training examples informative as the model improves. Our experiments
further show that fine-grained per-problem control outperforms fixed and
coarse-grained strategies, while noise from an external weak model
provides more persistent late-stage interference and stronger overall
performance than self-generated or random noise. Our results also show
that this recovery-centric optimization consistently improves reasoning
and interactive decision-making while strengthening the model’s ability
to revise flawed reasoning. Overall, DenoiseRL offers a scalable
post-training perspective in which model errors provide both the
learning signal and the basis for an adaptive curriculum.

## Limitations

Although DenoiseRL demonstrates strong empirical performance, several
limitations remain. First, the effectiveness of generated perturbations
still depends on the behavior of the weak models used for corruption
generation. If the weak models produce errors that are overly trivial,
repetitive, or unrealistic, the resulting recovery signal may provide
limited training value and fail to induce meaningful improvements.

Second, although DenoiseRL is formulated as a general framework for
learning from perturbed reasoning states, our experiments instantiate
such perturbations only as prefixes of failed weak-model trajectories.
It remains unclear whether the same recovery objective extends to other
representations or corruption mechanisms, such as erroneous intermediate
conclusions, structured plans, tool-use histories, or latent reasoning
states. Exploring these alternatives may broaden DenoiseRL beyond
settings in which reasoning states can be naturally realized as
prefixes.

### A.2 Hyperparameters

#### Optimization and rollout.

We train Qwen3-4B-Base with GRPO using an active batch of 16 prompts and
$`K=16`$ recovery rollouts per prompt, without additional clean
rollouts. The training and PPO mini-batch sizes are both 16. We use a
learning rate of $`10^{-6}`$ without warmup or weight decay, clip the
gradient norm at $`1.0`$, and set both PPO clipping bounds to $`0.2`$.
KL regularization is disabled in both the reward and actor loss. The
maximum prompt and response lengths are 8,192 and 4,096 tokens,
respectively. Training rollouts use temperature $`1.0`$, top-p $`1.0`$,
and unrestricted top-k; validation uses temperature $`0.6`$ and top-p
$`0.95`$.

#### DenoiseRL curriculum.

We use line-level prefixes from incorrect weak-model trajectories and
maintain an independent prefix ratio for each problem. The ratio is
initialized and lower-bounded at $`0`$, upper-bounded at $`0.5`$, and
updated toward target recovery accuracy $`0.75`$ with step size
$`\alpha=0.2`$. The controller uses a five-observation history, requires
at least two observations before assessing stability, and marks a
problem stable when the absolute recent ratio slope is at most $`0.02`$.
Replacement problems inherit the post-update mean ratio of the active
batch. We enable the dynamic length reward with minimum factor $`0.9`$
for all recovery rollouts and apply no additional response-clipping
penalty.

## Appendix B Training Efficiency

#### Offline noisy-prefix construction.

For mathematical reasoning, DenoiseRL samples incorrect trajectories
from the Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct weak model once and stores the resulting
prefixes before policy optimization. This preprocessing is lightweight
because it uses a small model, is performed only once, and can be reused
across training runs. More importantly, no weak-model inference is
required inside the RL loop: during training, DenoiseRL only retrieves a
stored prefix and selects the portion specified by the current
per-problem prefix ratio. Prefix construction therefore consumes none of
the online policy-training time reported below.

## Appendix D Hyperparameters for Agentic Tasks

#### Model, environment, and rollout.

We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the policy and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct as
the online weak model. Each optimizer step uses a batch of 16 ALFWorld
tasks and $`K=16`$ recovery rollouts per task, with no separate standard
rollout. The weak model generates one prefix candidate per task group
using the full_then_ratio strategy. Episodes are capped at 50
environment steps, and the maximum prompt and response lengths are 4,096
and 512 tokens, respectively. Training generation uses temperature
$`1.0`$ and top-p $`1.0`$; validation uses temperature $`0.6`$, top-p
$`0.95`$, and one sample per environment.

#### Optimization and curriculum.

We optimize with GRPO for up to 500 epochs using a learning rate of
$`10^{-6}`$, a PPO mini-batch size of 256, and a per-GPU micro-batch
size of 32. KL is excluded from the reward, while the actor uses a
low-variance KL loss with coefficient $`0.01`$. Invalid actions receive
a penalty of $`0.1`$. For the step-level DenoiseRL curriculum, all game
files belonging to the same ALFWorld task category share one dynamic
prefix ratio, while fresh game files are sampled at every optimizer
step. We use $`\rho_{\mathrm{init}}=\rho_{\min}=0`$,
$`\rho_{\max}=0.5`$, target recovery accuracy $`0.75`$, update step size
$`\alpha=0.2`$, and shuffle seed $`0`$.

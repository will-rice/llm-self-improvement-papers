# Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Training and Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence

Dipto Sumit    Syeda Fabiayee Jahan    Farig Sadeque Affiliation: BRAC
University, Dhaka, Bangladesh
Email: [dipto.sumit@g.bracu.ac.bd](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Recursive self-training can collapse language models, and many proposed
remedies target overconfidence, yet their effects on confidence are
rarely measured. We study a five-round self-training loop on Qwen3-1.7B,
using three seeds per compared method, and distinguish confidence
_temperature_ (drift from a calibrated starting point) from
_discrimination_ (ranking correct answers above incorrect ones).
Accuracy and overall discrimination remain nearly unchanged, but
confidence sharpens and the most confident answers become less reliable.
Matching predictive entropy to the calibrated starting model each round
recalibrates nearly as well as label-based temperature fitting, at both
1.7B and 8B. Sharpening is not inherent to self-training: exact samples
from a model’s distribution have zero expected gradient. Instead, most
drift comes from fitting each generated answer twice, with top-$`p`$
truncation contributing the rest; removing both keeps entropy on
generated outputs flat. Remedies affect the two properties differently:
truncated cross-entropy, an entropy bonus, and single-pass training
preserve temperature without improving discrimination, while focal loss
and multi-sample targets improve discrimination but cause
underconfidence. Reference-data mixing also overshoots. Pairing a
ranking-improving loss with label-free recalibration achieves both.

## 1 Introduction

Language models now routinely learn from text that models wrote. Some
pipelines do this on purpose, fine-tuning a model on its own answers to
improve it or to avoid the cost of labelling ([Huang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib16)), and any model trained on recent web text does it by
accident. When the recursion runs for several rounds, the model can
collapse: the learned distribution narrows, rare content disappears, and
eventually task performance falls ([Shumailov et al., 2024](#bib.bib29);
[Alemohammad et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Dohmatob et al.,
2024](#bib.bib7)).

Much of the work on preventing collapse blames one mechanism. The model
becomes too sure of its own outputs, and each round of training on them
makes it surer. ForTIFAI’s confidence-aware losses skip or down-weight
the tokens a model already predicts with high confidence, on the grounds
that overconfidence in self-generated data causes feedback loops that
degrade performance ([Zibakhsh Shabgahi et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)).
Self-verification keeps only the synthetic data the model is confident
about, and provably prevents collapse in the setting its authors analyse
([Fu et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)). Yet these methods are judged by
knowledge retention, benchmark accuracy, perplexity and divergence from
the original distribution. None of these measures looks at confidence,
the quantity the methods were designed around.

Confidence deserves a direct look because it is what the users of a
deployed model act on. A self-trained model that answers as accurately
as before but is more certain of its mistakes has become worse, in a way
that no accuracy benchmark will reveal. In this paper we ask what
recursive self-training, and the remedies proposed for it, do to a
model’s confidence.¹¹ 1 Code and experiment scripts:
[https://anonymous.4open.science/r/quant_forget-B5D4/](https://anonymous.4open.science/r/quant_forget-B5D4/)

We study the question in a deliberately simple loop. A model answers a
fixed pool of 3,000 questions and is fine-tuned on its own answers, and
the result answers the same questions in the next round. We compare the
models from all six rounds on the same 600 multiple-choice items. A
single calibration number turns out to be a poor guide here, because it
mixes two properties of confidence that self-training affects
differently. The first is _temperature_, how confident the model is
overall, which can be corrected after training by rescaling its logits.
The second is _discrimination_, whether higher confidence goes with
correct answers, which rescaling cannot in general repair. We measure
discrimination by AUROC on exact log-probabilities. We measure
temperature as drift away from a calibrated starting point, because our
base model is so overconfident that its raw calibration error improves
under any intervention that softens it, whether or not its confidence
becomes more trustworthy.

Our first finding is that self-training changes the model’s temperature
far more than its knowledge. Accuracy and AUROC stay close to where they
started, while the model becomes steadily more confident than it should
be. The ranking does suffer at the top, where the answers the model is
most sure of become less reliable. Because most of the change is a shift
in temperature, most of it can be undone without labels. Choosing each
round’s temperature so that the model’s predictive entropy matches that
of the calibrated starting model (adapting [Luo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib22)) recalibrates almost as well as refitting the
temperature on labels, at 1.7B and at 8B
(§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 What self-training changes ‣ 4 Results ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).

Our second finding concerns where the sharpening comes from. When a
model is trained on exact samples from its own distribution, the
expected gradient is zero, so self-training does not have to sharpen
anything. The sharpening we observe comes from two common design choices
that break this condition. Each self-generated answer is fitted twice,
which accounts for most of the effect, and the answers are sampled with
top-$`p`$ truncation, which accounts for the rest. With a single pass
over exact samples, the model’s entropy on its own outputs stays flat
for five rounds
(§[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Why the model sharpens ‣ 4 Results ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).

Third, each remedy we test moves one property and not the other
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).
Truncated cross-entropy, an entropy bonus and single-pass training keep
a calibrated model calibrated, but they do not improve its ranking. A
focal loss from the same confidence-aware family and training on two
samples per prompt improve AUROC by +0.038, but they push the model into
underconfidence. Mixing in reference data, the standard defence against
collapse, overshoots as well, and selecting training data by the model’s
own confidence makes overconfidence worse in a single-seed test
(§[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 What the remedies do ‣ 4 Results ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).
The overshoot of the ranking-improving losses is mostly a temperature
shift, so pairing such a loss with label-free recalibration obtains both
properties
(§[4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Getting both ‣ 4 Results ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).

## 2 Related Work

#### Model collapse and its remedies.

Training recursively on generated data narrows the learned distribution
and loses its tails ([Shumailov et al., 2024](#bib.bib29); [Alemohammad
et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Dohmatob et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)).
Accumulating real data alongside the synthetic data, rather than
replacing it, prevents collapse ([Gerstgrasser et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10); [Bertrand et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)), and so can
verifying or correcting the synthetic data before training on it ([Feng
et al., 2024](#bib.bib8); [Gillman et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)). [Yi
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib32) show that an external verifier brings early
gains that level off or reverse unless the verifier is perfectly
reliable. A second line of work targets the model’s confidence directly.
ForTIFAI’s truncated cross-entropy (TCE) ignores tokens the model
predicts above a confidence threshold and tolerates 2.3 times more
synthetic data before collapse; the same paper studies a family of
confidence-aware losses that includes a focal loss ([Zibakhsh Shabgahi
et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)). [Fu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib9) prove that
filtering synthetic data by the model’s internal confidence prevents
collapse, while [Qiao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib25) show that selection can
instead accelerate collapse when the selected data are biased. All of
these studies measure collapse. We measure what the same remedies do to
confidence.

#### Confidence under fine-tuning.

Calibration and accuracy are distinct properties ([Guo et al.,
2017](#bib.bib12)), and instruction tuning and alignment change how well
an LLM’s confidence matches its accuracy ([Kadavath et al.,
2022](#bib.bib19); [Tian et al., 2023](#bib.bib31); [Huang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15)). In continual fine-tuning, uncertainty coverage
degrades several steps before accuracy does ([Shihab et al.,
2026](#bib.bib28)). Closest to our work, [Huang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib17) find that iterative self-improvement can make LLMs
increasingly overconfident, and compare calibrating once before, once
after and at every iteration. Self-training on pseudo-labels has long
been known to breed overconfidence through confirmation bias ([Arazo
et al., 2020](#bib.bib2)). We add a distinction these studies do not
draw, between a shift in temperature and a loss of discrimination, and
we show that the temperature for each round can be found without labels.

#### Losses, sampling and recalibration.

The focal loss ([Lin et al., 2017](#bib.bib21)) improves the calibration
of classifiers by implicitly regularising their entropy ([Mukhoti
et al., 2020](#bib.bib23)). It is not a strictly proper loss, however,
so its confidence scores do not equal class probabilities, and a
closed-form transformation is needed to recover them ([Charoenphakdee
et al., 2021](#bib.bib5)). Our observation that the focal loss leaves a
self-trained model underconfident, in a way a single temperature largely
corrects, fits this picture. Entropy bonuses penalise confident output
distributions directly ([Pereyra et al., 2017](#bib.bib24)). Nucleus
sampling truncates the low-probability tail of each next-token
distribution ([Holtzman et al., 2020](#bib.bib13)), and recent samplers
bound the entropy of what remains ([Baghaei Potraghloo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)); our analysis shows why truncation matters when a
model is trained on its own samples. Label-free recalibration of LLMs is
an active area ([Shen et al., 2024](#bib.bib27); [Luo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib22)); [Luo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22) fit a fine-tuned
model’s temperature by aligning its predictions with those of its
pre-trained counterpart. We use a simpler relative of that idea,
matching the model’s mean predictive entropy to that of the calibrated
starting model, as a diagnostic and do not claim it as a contribution.
Finally, reading confidence from multiple-choice option letters requires
removing the model’s prior over the letters ([Zhao et al.,
2021](#bib.bib33); [Zheng et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)).

#### The loop.

We start from $`M_{0}`$, Qwen3-1.7B ([Qwen Team, 2025](#bib.bib26)) with
reasoning disabled, and a fixed pool of 3,000 questions from TriviaQA
([Joshi et al., 2017](#bib.bib18)) and NQ-Open ([Kwiatkowski et al.,
2019](#bib.bib20)). In round $`g`$, the current model $`M_{g}`$ answers
every question in the pool (temperature 1.0, top-$`p`$ 0.95, at most 32
tokens). We fine-tune $`M_{g}`$ on these 3,000 question–answer pairs for
two epochs with LoRA ([Hu et al., 2022](#bib.bib14)) (rank 16, learning
rate $`2{\times}10^{-4}`$, effective batch size 16) and merge the
adapter into the weights to obtain $`M_{g+1}`$. Each round therefore
trains on the previous round’s model and on that model’s own outputs,
the compounding regime in which collapse is usually studied. We evaluate
$`M_{0}`$ to $`M_{5}`$. Each protocol in the paper changes one element
of this loop and is run with three seeds unless we say otherwise. The
seed changes generation and training, while $`M_{0}`$ and its evaluation
are shared.

#### Evaluation.

All models are evaluated on the same 600 English items of Global-MMLU
([Singh et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)), which do not overlap with the
training pool. We read confidence from the multiple-choice answer. After
an answer cue, we take the log-probability of each option letter,
subtract that letter’s mean over the evaluation set to remove the
model’s positional prior ([Zhao et al., 2021](#bib.bib33); [Zheng
et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)), and renormalise. We keep these debiased
log-probabilities at full precision, which matters for ranking metrics.
Stored as probabilities, the chosen option’s confidence is at least
0.999999 for 283 of the 600 items at $`g_{0}`$, so many items would tie,
and AUROC would reward any method that merely softens the model. With
exact log-probabilities, no two items share a confidence value, either
at $`g_{0}`$ or at $`g_{5}`$ of any replicated protocol.

#### What we measure.

_Accuracy_ is the fraction of items whose highest-scoring option is
correct. _Discrimination_ is the AUROC of the chosen option’s
log-probability as a predictor of correctness. Because AUROC weighs all
pairs of items equally, we also report average precision, which
emphasises the items the model is most confident about, and the AUROC of
the margin between the two best options
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Discrimination under other measures ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).
_Calibration drift_ measures temperature. $`M_{0}`$ answers 0.573 of the
items correctly with a mean confidence of 0.963, so almost all of its
raw expected calibration error (ECE) of 0.391 is overconfidence (0.390).
Any intervention that softens such a model lowers its raw ECE, whether
or not its confidence becomes more trustworthy. We therefore calibrate
first. We fit a temperature $`T_{0}`$ on a random half of the items at
$`g_{0}`$ (mean $`T_{0}{=}10.5`$, held-out ECE 0.074), apply the same
$`T_{0}`$ at $`g_{5}`$, and report mean confidence minus accuracy on the
other half; we then swap the halves and average. Positive drift means
the model has become more confident than its calibrated start, and
negative drift means it has become less confident. When drift is near
zero, a temperature fitted once at the start remains valid. Finally, to
follow the sharpening during training, we record the model’s mean
per-token entropy $`H_{g}`$ on the answers it generates in round $`g`$,
and summarise it as the entropy kept per round,

       ``` math

the geometric mean of $`H_{g+1}/H_{g}`$ over the four transitions
between rounds 0 and 4. A value of 1 means the model does not sharpen.

#### Protocols.

Besides the standard loop (CE), we vary how the loop trains and samples
and which loss it uses. On the loop side, we train for a single pass
instead of two epochs, train on a random half of the answers for the
same number of optimiser steps (a step-matched control), sample exactly
with top-$`p`$ switched off, and train on several samples per prompt. On
the loss side, we use ForTIFAI’s truncated cross-entropy at its default
threshold $`\tau{=}0.9`$ and the focal loss from the same
confidence-aware family ([Zibakhsh Shabgahi et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)),
an entropy bonus ([Pereyra et al., 2017](#bib.bib24)), and label
smoothing. We test confidence-based self-verification by keeping the
more confident half of each round’s answers. We also test the standard
defence against collapse by replacing half of each round’s answers with
the dataset’s reference answers ([Gerstgrasser et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10)). The loss and loop variants were first screened with
a promotion rule written into the experiment scripts before any of them
ran
(Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Decision rules and their outcomes ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).
The protocols we report with three seeds are those the rule promoted,
the controls needed to interpret them, and reference mixing, which was
part of the original design of the study.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 What self-training changes

Under the standard loop, accuracy moves from 0.573 to 0.567 over five
rounds and AUROC from 0.711 to 0.704. In each seed, a paired bootstrap
over items puts the change in AUROC within noise. What does change is
confidence. Measured from a calibrated start, the model ends with a
drift of +0.049 ($`\pm`$0.006 across seeds;
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3 Setup ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")),
so it is clearly more confident than it should be. Raw ECE barely
registers this, changing by only +0.015, because the base model’s mean
confidence is already 0.963 and has little room to rise.

The ranking also changes where it matters most. Average precision, which
weights the answers the model is most sure of, falls from 0.782 to
0.741, by between 0.029 and 0.050 in the three seeds, and each seed’s
95% bootstrap interval excludes zero. Among the fifth of the items on
which the model is most confident, accuracy falls from 0.867 to 0.800.
The model becomes surer of everything, and its surest answers contain
more mistakes.

A temperature calibrated once at $`g_{0}`$ therefore goes stale. Under
standard training the cost in ECE is still small at $`g_{5}`$ (0.090
with the stale temperature against 0.089 with one refitted on labels),
but the cost grows with the drift, and several remedies below drift much
further
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Setup ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).

If most of the change is a shift in temperature, one scalar per round
should undo it, and it should be possible to find that scalar without
labels. We test this with entropy matching, a simple relative of the
label-free recalibration of [Luo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22). In round
$`g`$ we choose the temperature at which the model’s mean predictive
entropy on the unlabelled evaluation inputs equals that of the
calibrated $`M_{0}`$. For every protocol except one, the resulting ECE
is within 0.010 of the ECE obtained by refitting the temperature on
labels every round
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Setup ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")b;
Table [4](#A4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Appendix D Label-free recalibration at 1.7B and 8B ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")
in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Label-free recalibration at 1.7B and 8B ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).
The exception is label smoothing, run with one seed, where the
label-free temperature reaches 0.102 against the oracle’s 0.081,
plausibly because smoothed targets change the shape of the output
distribution and not only its temperature. For the focal loss, which
drifts far, ECE is 0.147 with the stale temperature, 0.094 with the
label-free one and 0.091 with the labelled one. The same holds at 8B,
where the base model is better calibrated: training on reference data
alone takes ECE to 0.247 under the stale temperature, while the
label-free temperature brings it to 0.057 (oracle 0.059).

Self-training thus leaves most of what the model knows and most of its
ranking in place, and it moves the model’s temperature. The temperature
can be recovered without labels, and it is also what most remedies move
(§[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 What the remedies do ‣ 4 Results ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).

### 4.2 Why the model sharpens

Figure 3: The model’s mean entropy on the answers it generates for
training, relative to round 0. Standard self-training (two epochs,
top-$`p`$ 0.95) sharpens the model steadily. Exact sampling (top-$`p`$
off) slows the sharpening, a single pass per round slows it more, and
the two together keep the entropy flat. Thin lines are individual seeds.

#### Self-training is neutral in expectation.

Suppose a model is trained with cross-entropy on exact samples from its
own distribution. At the current parameters the expected gradient is
zero, because
$`\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\theta}}[\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(y)]=\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{y}p_{\theta}(y)=0`$.
The argument concerns the expected update only. Finite samples, the
optimiser and the low-rank parameterisation all add noise, so it
predicts the absence of a systematic drift rather than the absence of
change. Even so, it means that sharpening is not built into
self-training, and must come from some way in which the loop departs
from this idealisation. The standard loop departs from it in two ways.

First, it _truncates_. With top-$`p{=}0.95`$, any step whose most likely
token already has probability at least 0.95 is sampled greedily. This
applies to 73% of steps at $`g_{0}`$ and 90% at $`g_{5}`$, so the
sampler retains 81% of the model’s entropy at the start and 64% after
five rounds. Truncation therefore feeds on itself, since a sharper model
is sampled more greedily. Second, the loop _repeats_. Each prompt has a
single sampled answer, which is fitted for two epochs, so the model is
pulled towards that one answer rather than towards its own distribution.

#### Removing each departure.

Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.2 Why the model sharpens ‣ 4 Results ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")
follows the model’s entropy on its own training answers. Under the
standard loop it falls by 60% over four rounds, from 0.293 to 0.117
nats. Sampling exactly, with top-$`p`$ switched off, raises the entropy
kept per round from 0.795 to 0.856, and fitting each answer once instead
of twice raises it to 0.944. The single-pass effect is not simply a
matter of training less, since the step-matched control, which sees half
of the answers twice, keeps 0.881. Repetition therefore accounts for
most of the sharpening and truncation for the rest. With both changes in
place, a single pass over exact samples, the entropy stays flat at 0.292
nats in round 0 and 0.296 in round 4, as the zero-gradient argument
predicts. This arm has a single seed, and we read it as a confirmation
of the mechanism rather than as a separate result.

#### Output entropy is not calibration.

Keeping the model’s generations at their fixed point does not keep its
multiple-choice confidence at its fixed point. The single-pass,
exact-sampling arm ends with a drift of $`-`$0.073, and training on
several exact samples per prompt overshoots further as the number of
samples grows ($`-`$0.069 for two and $`-`$0.129 for four;
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Setup ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")a),
even though all of these arms keep more entropy than the standard loop.
One reading is consistent with every arm we ran. Two forces act on
confidence. Fitting tokens the model already predicts confidently
sharpens it, and moving training weight towards tokens it is unsure of
softens it, whether the weight moves through disagreeing targets for the
same prompt or through down-weighting confident tokens. Single-pass
training weakens the first force without adding the second, and it lands
closest to the fixed point ($`-`$0.017 $`\pm`$ 0.004).

### 4.3 What the remedies do

Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")
places each replicated protocol on the two properties, and
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3 Setup ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")
gives the numbers. The protocols fall into two groups, and reference
mixing behaves like the second.

#### Remedies that hold temperature.

TCE at ForTIFAI’s default threshold, the entropy bonus and single-pass
training all keep a calibrated model calibrated through five rounds,
with drifts of $`-`$0.019, +0.000 and $`-`$0.017 against +0.049 for
standard training (each $`p\leq 0.024`$). None of them costs accuracy,
and none raises AUROC significantly (0.714, 0.715 and 0.727 against
0.704; each $`p\geq 0.176`$). Some of them do protect the top of the
ranking. Single-pass training keeps average precision at 0.781, close to
its starting value of 0.782 and above the 0.741 of standard training
($`p{=}0.010`$), and TCE keeps part of it (0.767, $`p{=}0.039`$).

#### Remedies that improve discrimination.

The focal loss from ForTIFAI’s family, which down-weights each token by
$`(1-p_{t})^{\gamma}`$, raises AUROC to 0.742 ($`p{=}0.009`$ against
CE), and training on two exact samples per prompt raises it to 0.742
($`p{=}0.011`$), again without costing accuracy. Both gains survive the
other measures of discrimination. The AUROC of the top-two margin rises
by +0.035 and +0.037 ($`p{=}0.013`$ and 0.013), and average precision
rises above its starting value, to 0.811 and 0.798
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Discrimination under other measures ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).
Both remedies, however, overshoot the fixed point into underconfidence,
with drifts of $`-`$0.132 and $`-`$0.069. Across protocols, AUROC rises
as the model fits its own single answers less (CE 0.704, half data
0.721, single pass 0.727, focal loss 0.742) or fits several different
answers per prompt (0.742), but not when only its most confident tokens
are dropped (TCE, 0.714). With three seeds we cannot separate the focal
loss from simply training less, since it exceeds the step-matched
half-data control by +0.022 ($`p{=}0.127`$).

#### Reference data moves temperature too.

Replacing half of each round’s answers with reference answers is the
defence that the collapse literature relies on most ([Gerstgrasser
et al., 2024](#bib.bib10); [Bertrand et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)). Judged
by raw calibration error it is a clear improvement, changing ECE by
$`-`$0.072 over five rounds. Measured from a calibrated start, however,
it ends with a drift of $`-`$0.190 ($`\pm`$0.001 across seeds), deep in
underconfidence, with accuracy 0.569 and AUROC 0.720 ($`p{=}0.261`$
against CE). It does keep average precision near its starting value
(0.784). Its raw-ECE trajectory, a large improvement after the first
round followed by a partial rise, is a large softening followed by
partial re-sharpening as the self-generated half of the data is
refitted. Like the confidence-aware losses, reference mixing mainly
moves temperature, and label-free recalibration brings its ECE to 0.074.

#### Self-verification accelerates overconfidence.

Keeping the more confident half of each round’s answers is a simple form
of the confidence-based filtering that provably prevents collapse in the
setting of [Fu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib9), who score several candidate
answers per prompt with the fixed base model. Compared with its matched
control, a random half trained with the same budget and the same number
of steps, it pushes the model further past its calibrated start (+0.046
against $`-`$0.003) and sharpens it faster (0.830 against 0.881 entropy
kept per round). This is one seed and one selection rule, so we report
it as a warning rather than as a result. Its direction is what the
mechanism predicts, since selecting by confidence hands the model its
own lowest-entropy answers to fit. Collapse and overconfidence are
different targets, and a remedy for one can worsen the other.

### 4.4 Getting both

Because the focal loss overshoots mainly in temperature, label-free
recalibration can remove the overshoot. Its ECE falls from 0.147 with
the stale temperature to 0.094 with the label-free one
(Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3 Setup ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")),
the level that the standard loop reaches (0.090). Its advantage in
discrimination survives the rescaling. A temperature need not preserve
how confidences rank across items, so we recomputed AUROC after
recalibration and found 0.750 for the focal loss against 0.699 for
standard training (+0.051, $`p{=}0.001`$). A loss that improves
discrimination, followed by label-free recalibration, therefore achieves
what no single remedy in our study does.

## 5 Discussion

Three lessons follow for how self-training remedies are evaluated and
used. The first concerns measurement. Confidence has at least two
properties that move independently under self-training, and temperature
should be measured from a calibrated start. Raw calibration error
rewards any softening of an overconfident model, so by that measure
every remedy we tested would look like an improvement, including those
that leave the model badly underconfident.

The second concerns the loop itself. The cheapest protection against
overconfidence is to fit each self-generated answer once and to sample
it without truncation. Both follow from the zero-gradient argument, and
neither adds cost. Entropy-aware truncation such as top-H
([Baghaei Potraghloo et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)) is a natural alternative
to switching truncation off, although our own entropy-matched sampler
overshot into underconfidence in a single-seed test
(Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B All protocols ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence")).

The third concerns remedies that weight or filter data by confidence.
They move the two properties in opposite directions, so they should be
paired with recalibration rather than trusted to calibrate the model.
This agrees with [Huang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib17), who find that
recalibration largely removes the calibration error that
self-improvement introduces, and with the theory of the focal loss,
whose confidence scores are known to need a correction ([Charoenphakdee
et al., 2021](#bib.bib5)). Our results add that the correction can be a
single temperature per round, chosen without labels.

## 6 Conclusion

Recursive self-training leaves a model’s accuracy and most of its
ranking of answers in place and changes its temperature, while its most
confident answers become less reliable. Intervening on the loop traces
the sharpening to two design choices, fitting each answer twice and
sampling with truncation, and the sharpening disappears when both are
removed. The remedies we tested, reference mixing included, either hold
temperature or improve discrimination, and none does both. A loss that
improves discrimination, followed by label-free recalibration, does.

## Limitations

Our comparison of remedies uses one model (Qwen3-1.7B), one evaluation
set of 600 multiple-choice items, and confidence read from option
letters. We repeated the temperature diagnosis at 8B but not the
remedies, and scoring the option texts rather than the letters, or
measuring confidence in open-ended answers, may behave differently. The
loop is pure self-training with no external learning signal, so it
isolates what self-training does to confidence but cannot show how
remedies interact with a real improvement signal such as a correctness
filter. The effects are small in absolute terms (AUROC differences of
0.02–0.04 and drifts of 0.02–0.13), the main comparisons rest on three
seeds, and the $`p`$-values are not corrected across the comparisons we
report. Several mechanism arms have a single seed: the flat-entropy arm,
the four-sample arms, the entropy-matched sampler, and the
self-verification test, which also uses a single selection rule and keep
rate. With three seeds we cannot separate the focal loss from training
less. We tested one label-free recalibration, entropy matching adapted
from [Luo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22), which we use as a diagnostic;
others, such as their divergence-based method or Thermometer ([Shen
et al., 2024](#bib.bib27)), may track the oracle differently. We did not
test entropy-aware truncation inside the loop. The study began with a
different hypothesis, that calibration degrades before accuracy, and its
planned test is reported in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Decision rules and their outcomes ‣ Temperature, Not Knowledge: What Recursive Self-Trainingand Its Remedies Do to a Language Model’s Confidence").
The two-property framing emerged from the data, and the screen of
remedies followed a rule written into the experiment scripts before it
ran. An earlier Bangla extension was invalidated by a tokenizer
truncation default that removed the question from 75% of passage-bearing
prompts. It is excluded, and its diagnosis is documented with the
released code.

All data are public benchmarks used within their licences, and no human
subjects were involved. The main risk we see is over-trusting label-free
recalibration, which we tested on one model family and on
multiple-choice confidence only (see Limitations). The protocols
reported here used about 40 GPU-hours on one 16 GB consumer GPU, and the
8B diagnosis used about 10 hours on a rented 24 GB GPU.

## Appendix A Decision rules and their outcomes

#### Original hypothesis.

The study was designed to test whether calibration degrades at an
earlier round than accuracy. The test was written into the analysis code
before the primary runs, although it was not publicly pre-registered: a
paired item-level bootstrap per round, a minimum effect of 0.02, Holm
correction across calibration metrics, and an accuracy floor relative to
chance. On the standard loop the hypothesis was supported in one of
three seeds and undetermined in the other two, because the raw
calibration drift was below the minimum effect. Accuracy never degraded
in any run.

#### Protocol screen.

Because temperature can be corrected after training, the screen’s
primary endpoint was discrimination, and the rule was written into the
experiment scripts before any screened protocol ran. A protocol was
promoted if its $`g_{5}`$ AUROC on exact log-probabilities exceeded that
of the standard loop by at least 0.03, with accuracy no more than 0.01
below it. The focal loss passed at one seed and replicated on the
three-seed mean. Single-pass training passed at one seed but did not
replicate on the mean (0.727); we report it for its effect on
calibration, which did replicate. The step-matched half-data control,
TCE at $`\tau{=}0.9`$, the entropy bonus and two samples per prompt were
added as controls and replications.

repeats the comparison of discrimination with three further measures.
Margin AUROC scores each item by the gap between its two highest option
log-probabilities instead of by the chosen option’s log-probability.
Average precision ranks items by the chosen option’s log-probability and
treats correct answers as positives, so it weights the items the model
is most confident about. The last column gives the accuracy among the
20% of items with the highest confidence. The focal loss and two samples
per prompt improve on standard training under every measure. Average
precision also separates standard training from some of the protocols
that hold temperature, which AUROC does not: standard training loses
precision among its most confident answers, while single-pass training,
TCE and reference mixing keep more of it.

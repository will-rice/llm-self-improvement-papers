# ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops

Hansi Yang    Linji Hao    Hongyan Hao    An Wang    Han
Wu\corresponding    Qi Gu\corresponding    Xunliang Cai

###### Abstract

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has driven their
deployment toward general-purpose harness environments, where LLM-based
agents operate in sandbox workspaces and interact directly with file
systems and shells. While numerous post-training pipelines exist for
tool-use agents, their counterparts for harness-based agents remain
scarce and are predominantly one-shot and teacher-dependent: a stronger
external model synthesizes tasks and collects trajectories, the target
agent is trained once, and evaluation results are consumed by human
researchers rather than fed back into the loop. Such pipelines are
inherently bounded by the capability of the external model and the scope
of human prior, leaving no room for self-improvement. To address these
limitations, we propose ClawLoop, a framework that enables
self-improving without any external teacher model and with minimal prior
dependencies. ClawLoop composes three phases into a self-sustaining
cycle: _Task Synthesis_, which generates a diverse pool of training
tasks via two complementary construction routines, applies
diagnosis-conditioned difficulty growth followed by both agent-free and
agent-based task validation; _Training_, which updates the agent via SFT
and/or RL; and _Diagnosis and Feedback_, which analyzes the agent’s
rollout behaviors to produce a capability analysis report that will be
used as feedback for task synthesis in the next iteration. Using
Qwen3.6-27B as the loop model, ClawLoop yields consistent improvements
on SkillsBench, Claw-Eval-Live, and ClawGym-Bench. The iteratively
refined tasks are further transferred to smaller-scale Qwen3.5-4B and 9B
models with gains across all benchmarks, validating data quality
independently of the loop model.

## 1 Introduction

With the rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs), their
deployment scenarios have evolved significantly: from chatbots to RAG
systems, and further from tool-using agents to general-purpose
harness-augmented agents ([OpenClaw Contributors 2026](#bib.bib2);
[OpenAI 2025](#bib.bib16); [Anthropic 2025](#bib.bib17)) most recently.
Unlike prior tool-use agents that interact through predefined tools,
harness agents are granted with audited and extensible permissions and
can directly operate on real file systems and shells. Such enhanced
permission enables LLM-based agents to tackle tasks of substantially
greater complexity than tool-use frameworks.

While diverse harnesses have provided a basis for LLMs to take over more
workloads from various real-life scenarios, these models still require
comprehensive post-training targeting harness environments before they
can reliably serve as autonomous agents. The central bottleneck of
post-training lies in training data. Since harness environments span an
enormous task space, obtaining diverse training data with high quality
has extend far beyond existing data synthesis pipelines, let alone
manual annotations. While numerous works have proposed various task
synthesis pipelines for general tool-use agents ([Song et al.
2026](#bib.bib10); [Fang et al. 2025](#bib.bib11); [Cai et al.
2025](#bib.bib12); [Tian et al. 2026](#bib.bib13); [Tu et al.
2026](#bib.bib14); [Wang et al. 2026](#bib.bib15)), these works are
restricted to constructing tasks within fixed domains. Specifically, the
“domain” often consists of several predefined tools with schemas exposed
to the agent, and the agent’s interaction is limited to these tools.
Such limitation is critical for present-day harness environments, as
real-life tasks completed inside a harness generally involve open-ended
compositions of file operations, shell commands, skill execution, and
multi-step reasoning, whose complexity is well beyond predefined tools
and cannot be covered by these pipelines. To the best of our knowledge,
ClawGym ([Bai et al. 2026](#bib.bib1)) is the only work that performs
task synthesis and training inside modern harness environments. However,
it leaves several key gaps unaddressed. First, existing pipelines assume
access to an external stronger model (_e.g._, MiniMax-M2.5, GLM-5.1) to
provide training signal, whether as a task synthesizer, a trajectory
collector, or both. The existence of such external models naturally
introduces an intrinsic capability ceiling (the capability of the
external model itself). Second, they are inherently one-shot. Tasks are
synthesized once based on human prior, the agent is trained once, and
evaluation results inform human researchers rather than being fed back
to drive further improvement.

We observe that a harness environment already provides the essential
ingredients to close a self-improving loop. An LLM model can instantiate
arbitrary workspaces, execute agent trajectories, and verify outcomes
via both code-based and rubric-based checks. What is missing is not
infrastructure, but _a mechanism by which the agent identifies its own
weaknesses, generates targeted practice, and iteratively improves_, a
capability arguably central to how humans learn. To this end, we propose
ClawLoop, an iterative self-improving framework that unifies task
synthesis, model training, and self-diagnosis into a closed
loop.Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
summarizes how these components interact within one iteration of the
closed loop. ClawLoop enables the agent to autonomously synthesize
training tasks, collect its own trajectories, diagnose failure patterns
from rollout traces, and generate targeted tasks for the next round of
training, all without an external teacher model.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

- •
  We propose ClawLoop, a closed-loop, teacher-free iterative training
  framework for general agents. Unlike all prior pipelines that rely on
  the existence of an external strong model and only operate in a single
  pass, ClawLoop enables the agent to synthesize its own tasks, collect
  its own trajectories, diagnose its weaknesses, and iteratively
  improve, all within the same harness environment it is trained to
  operate in.
- •
  As the core of our proposed ClawLoop framework, we design a
  multi-stage task synthesis mechanism, comprising seed task generation,
  diagnosis-conditioned difficulty growth, and task validation based on
  self-reflection. Such mechanism ensures that generated tasks possess
  rigorous code-level verifiability while maintaining sufficient
  complexity required for deep reasoning.
- •
  Using Qwen3.6-27B inside the loop, we demonstrate that ClawLoop yields
  consistent improvements across diverse benchmarks for harness-based
  agents. The constructed tasks are also used to train smaller-scale
  Qwen3.5-4B and 9B models and we observe gains on all benchmarks across
  different model scales, further validating the quality of these tasks.
  Comprehensive ablation studies isolate the contribution of each
  component and provide a complete understanding of our proposed
  framework.

## 2 Related Work

#### Task Synthesis and Environment Generation

The first complete harness-based agent training paradigm is
ClawGym ([Bai et al. 2026](#bib.bib1)), which proposes large-scale
synthesis of diverse tasks paired with mock workspaces and hybrid
verifiers. There have also been many works on scalable task synthesis,
which can be broadly categorized into three lines: repository-level
coding task construction, such as SWE-bench ([Jimenez et al.
2024](#bib.bib18)), SWE-Gym ([Pan et al. 2024](#bib.bib19)),
R2E-Gym ([Jain et al. 2025](#bib.bib24)), and SWE-smith ([Yang et al.
2026](#bib.bib23)); tool-use task and environment synthesis, including
ASTRA ([Tian et al. 2026](#bib.bib13)), AutoForge ([Cai et al.
2025](#bib.bib12)), EnvScaler ([Song et al. 2026](#bib.bib10)),
ScaleEnv ([Tu et al. 2026](#bib.bib14)), Agent World Model ([Wang et al.
2026](#bib.bib15)), and Agent-World ([Dong et al. 2026](#bib.bib20));
and capability-oriented task synthesis, such as Self-Challenging ([Zhou
et al. 2026](#bib.bib25)), Agent0 ([Xia et al. 2025](#bib.bib21)), and
Tool-R0 ([Acikgoz et al. 2026](#bib.bib22)).These approaches demonstrate
the value of executable environments and verifiable tasks, but generally
focus on repository-level coding,predefined tool ecosystems, or
environment scaling.

#### Self-Improving Language Models

The idea of self-improving in language models has been explored along
several axes for many years. Early approaches bootstrap new supervision
from an LLM’s own output. STaR ([Zelikman et al. 2022](#bib.bib9))
iteratively trains itself on self-generated reasoning traces that lead
to correct answers, while Self-Instruct ([Wang et al. 2023](#bib.bib7))
expands instruction-tuning data with model-generated instructions and
responses. The first work that introduces the idea of self-improving to
agentic domains is Agent0 ([Xia et al. 2025](#bib.bib21)), which places
a Curriculum Agent above an Executor Agent and generates tasks near the
executor’s capability frontier from zero seed data, retaining tasks by
uncertainty, tool-use frequency, and repetition penalties. A
complementary line keeps the underlying model weights fixed and treats
the non-parametric agent harness as the optimization target. An example
is Agentic Context Engineering (ACE) ([Zhang et al. 2025](#bib.bib27)),
which evolves a structured context playbook from successful and failed
trajectories. Self-Harness ([Zhang et al. 2026a](#bib.bib28)) mines
model-specific failure patterns and converts them into bounded harness
modifications that are accepted only after regression testing.

#### Evaluation Benchmarks for LLM-Based Agents with Harness

The development of various harnesses also calls for comprehensive
benchmarks to evaluate the capability of LLM to take real-life workloads
with the help of these harnesses. The pioneering work,
PinchBench ([PinchBench Contributors 2026](#bib.bib5)), evaluates models
as the reasoning backbone of OpenClaw([OpenClaw Contributors
2026](#bib.bib2)) on practical workflows such as research, coding,
scheduling, and file management. Following it, a growing family of
OpenClaw-specific benchmarks has been proposed ([Qwen Team and Data Team
2026](#bib.bib33); [Ding et al. 2026](#bib.bib32); [Long et al.
2026](#bib.bib31); [Zhang et al. 2026b](#bib.bib3); [Xu et al.
2026](#bib.bib4); [Meng et al. 2026](#bib.bib26)). Among these
benchmarks, Claw-Eval ([Ye et al. 2026](#bib.bib29)) and
Claw-Eval-Live ([Li et al. 2026a](#bib.bib30)) evaluate evolving
real-world workflows through refreshable task signals and
execution-grounded grading. ClawGym-Bench ([Bai et al. 2026](#bib.bib1))
provides executable tasks with mock workspaces and task-specific
verifiers, offering a controlled diagnostic setting for Claw-style
agents. In addition to these OpenClaw-based benchmark, SkillsBench ([Li
et al. 2026b](#bib.bib8)) is a general agent benchmark that measures
whether agents can effectively use procedural Skills across diverse
professional domains without being tied to a particular harness,
allowing us to assess whether the improvements produced by ClawLoop
generalize beyond OpenClaw-specific environments.

## 3 Preliminary

We first clarify the boundary of harness in this work. Specifically, we
use a harness as a sandbox execution environment in which an LLM-based
agents can operate. It generally provides a Docker container with a
shell, file system, package manager, and network access, plus APIs for
workspace provisioning and trajectory recording. We use the same harness
used for training rollouts and evaluation, ensuring distribution match
between training and inference.

Based on a given harness, we rigorously define task as a tuple
$`\tau=\langle p,\,s_{0},\,\mathcal{A},\,F,\,V_{\tau}\rangle`$. Each
component in this tuple is defined as follows:

- •
  $`p`$ is a natural-language instruction that generally describes the
  task and is exposed to the agent as its initial user prompt;
- •
  $`s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}`$ is the initial workspace state (files,
  environment variables, installed tools), and we use $`\mathcal{S}`$ to
  denote the set of all possible workspace states supported by the
  harness in our loop;
- •
  $`\mathcal{A}`$ is the action space available to the agent (shell
  commands, file edits, tool invocations);
- •
  $`F:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{S}`$ is the deterministic
  transition function implemented by the harness sandbox;
- •
  $`V_{\tau}:\mathcal{S}\to\{0,1\}`$ is the verifier to check whether an
  agent has completed the task or not.

We note that such definition is slightly different from general tool-use
tasks as in existing literature ([Song et al. 2026](#bib.bib10); [Tu et
al. 2026](#bib.bib14); [Dong et al. 2026](#bib.bib20)), as the harness
introduces additional complexity to both the workspace state (including
file systems and installed tools/skills) and the agent’s action space
(including shell scripts or programming languages). For a given task
$`\tau`$, an LLM-based agent (denoted as $`\pi_{\theta}`$) can then
interact with the sandbox environment to produce a trajectory, denoted
as $`\xi=(s_{0},a_{1},s_{1},\ldots,a_{T},s_{T})`$, where
$`a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p,s_{0},a_{1:t-1},s_{1:t-1})`$.

## 4 ClawLoop Framework

In this section, we describe the complete framework of ClawLoop. It
starts with a pre-trained LLM agent (denoted as $`\pi_{0}`$) deployed
inside a harness sandbox environment, and consists of $`K`$ repeating
iterations indexed by $`k\in\{1,2,\ldots,K\}`$. At iteration $`k`$,
based on the most recently updated agent $`\pi_{k-1}`$, ClawLoop
proceeds through three phases, as is shown in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops"),:

1.  1.  Task Synthesis and Selection. The agent first generates a diverse
        task pool $`\mathcal{P}_{k}`$, which is split into a training set
        $`\mathcal{T}_{k}`$ and a held-out evaluation set
        $`\mathcal{E}_{k}=\mathcal{P}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{k}`$. Tasks
        in $`\mathcal{T}_{k}`$ are then used to collect rollout
        trajectories, forming the basis for the next training phase.
2.  2.  Training. Based on the rollout trajectories from
        $`\mathcal{T}_{k}`$, we update the agent
        $`\pi_{\theta_{k-1}}\to\pi_{\theta_{k}}`$ via SFT on successful
        trajectories and/or RL on tasks that satisfy a reward threshold.
3.  3.  Diagnosis and Feedback. The updated agent $`\pi_{\theta_{k}}`$ is
        further evaluated on the held-out evaluation set
        $`\mathcal{E}_{k}`$. The resulting rollout trajectories are fed into
        the agent itself to identify recurring failure patterns. Any
        possible feedback will be used in the next iteration to refine the
        task synthesis and selection phase.

We then describe each phase in detail in the following subsections.

### 4.1 Phase 1: Task Synthesis

The key component of our framework is task synthesis, which serves as
the engine driving iterative self-improvement. Our task synthesis
pipeline begins with two complementary routines. The first,
_capability-driven synthesis_, starts from a target capability and
constructs the workspace environment needed to exercise it. The second,
_domain-grounded synthesis_, takes the opposite direction. It starts
from a specific professional domain (_e.g._ database management,
workload planning) equipped with domain-specific tools. The agent then
decomposes it into constituent skills based on its internal structure,
and induces concrete tasks from those skills. Together, these two
routines produce an initial pool of seed tasks that offer breadth both
across capability dimensions and professional domains where
domain-specific knowledge is stored in skills. The seed tasks are then
optionally passed through a _difficulty growth_ routine, which adjusts
task complexity upward for categories where the agent has already
demonstrated proficiency, ensuring that the curriculum remains
challenging as the agent improves. Finally, all candidate tasks undergo
a two-stage validation process—static checks on task well-formedness and
workspace integrity, followed by execution-based validation that runs
each task’s verifier against a gold solution to filter out ill-posed or
unsolvable tasks before they enter the training set.

#### Capability-driven synthesis

This route is organized by a catalog of _capabilities_ rather than by
any concrete toolset. Each entry binds a workflow
pattern—reconciliation, cross-source audit, multi-document merge, or
domain calculation—to a target capability, spanning an analysis track
whose deliverables are structured artifacts and a terminal track whose
deliverables are workspace end-states. Each capability is then
instantiated across a wide range of domains, deliberately including
unusual and long-tail ones (_e.g._ beekeeping hive inventories, trail
maintenance records, amateur radio contact logs). Decoupling the
capability skeleton from the surface domain in this way—_vary the
domain, keep the capability_—lets a single capability be practiced under
many different surface forms, which discourages the agent from latching
onto domain-specific surface cues instead of the underlying workflow.

The pivotal design decision is that the synthesis LLM never writes task
data directly. Instead it writes a _runnable generator_ `build(seed)`
$`\to(s_{0},y,\mathcal{C})`$, which TaskForge executes in a sandbox to
obtain a multi-source evidence environment $`s_{0}`$, the answer key
$`y`$, and an _answer contract_ $`\mathcal{C}`$. Because $`y`$ is
obtained by executing code over synthesized source data rather than by
asking a model to assert an answer, the answer key is grounded in
computation, not in model belief. Generators must be seeded to ensure
reproducibility and must create at least two sources that require
cross-referencing to complete the task. If generation fails, the sandbox
errors are fed back to guide the repair process. The contract
$`\mathcal{C}`$ addresses a subtler gap: an answer key alone
underdetermines a task, since rules used to compute $`y`$—the filtering
criterion, rounding convention, tie-break, or join key—often cannot be
recovered from the environment by inspection. $`\mathcal{C}`$ therefore
declares each such _hidden decision_ explicitly, along with a schema
fixing the deliverable’s shape and numeric tolerance, while containing
no answer values.

#### Domain-grounded synthesis

Despite general capabilities that are shared in any domains, we further
note that real-life tasks often require some domain knowledge that is
unique to a specific domain. A simple example may be a term definition,
while a more complex example may be a complete pipeline to satisfy a
requirment. Such knowledge may be stored in some skills for this domain,
and an LLM-based agent should be able to quickly grasp and utilize such
knowledge to complete domain-specific tasks. As such, we need to
introduce some professional domains into the task synthesis routine,
which enable the agent to learn to find, understand and execute such
skills to complete tasks within a given domain.

We first introduce the definition of _executable domain_, which is
basically a collection of executable tools with schemas exposed to the
agent, together with a database that can completely describe the domain
environment and support tool execution. Executable domains can be
sourced from existing MCP servers or constructed following existing data
synthesis works targeting general tool-user agents ([Song et al.
2026](#bib.bib10); [Tu et al. 2026](#bib.bib14); [Dong et al.
2026](#bib.bib20); [Wang et al. 2026](#bib.bib15)). Given an executable
domain, we combine abstract syntax tree (AST) inspection with LLM-based
analysis (conducted by the agent itself) to extract a tripartite graph
connecting tools, the atomic parameters and return fields that they
consume and produce, and the database tables that they read and write.
This tripartite graph enables us to cluster tools into _skills_ based on
a weighted affinity score over shared graph nodes. The resulting
clusters are provided to the self-evolving agent as its prior, which the
agent may override when accompanied by a justification. Each accepted
cluster is materialized into an executable command-line script with
database access rewritten to operate on a state snapshot, a skill
document, and a capability specification extracted from the generated
code rather than from its documentation.

Tasks are then constructed from the skills available in each domain
through two stages. In the first stage, an abstract task graph is
sampled according to _structural features_. Examples of these features
include long-range dependencies, branching, convergence, error-recovery
paths, and multiple sub-goals. These features collectively serve as the
difficulty control for this route. Nodes in this graph represent
_knowledge states_ (_e.g._, “the order details are now known”), and
edges between two nodes represent agent actions that transition the
workspace from one knowledge state to another. The graph contains
neither business content nor tool names, ensuring that its structure is
determined before its semantics.

In the second stage, the graph is grounded in a concrete domain. The
initial workspace is seeded with exactly the background and reference
data required to make the task meaningful. A coverage check rejects any
graph whose transitions are unsupported by the available skills. The
instruction is then generated under an _information barrier_ that
separates it from the gold trajectory, preventing leakage of the
reference solution. Specifically, the instruction specifies initial
parameters but refers to background records without reproducing their
contents, requiring the agent to retrieve the relevant information; it
defines the goal state while withholding the procedure, execution order,
and branch conditions. Terminal states are represented as structured
predicates over the final database state and compiled into an executable
test suite. The tests evaluate only the final state and locate records
using business-semantic keys rather than hard-coded identifiers,
ensuring that any correct solution can pass.

#### Growing Difficulty from Seed Tasks

While the above two steps can already construct some seed tasks from
scratch, our pivot studies demonstrate that these tasks often has
limited reasoning depth, and the agent still fail to overcome real-life
task complexity after training with these tasks. As such, we consider
two approaches for the self-evolving agent to increase task
difficulties: multi-hop and dirtiness draw.

The _multi-hop draw_ promotes a task to the multi-step retrieval
structure typical of realistic agent workloads. Concretely, it produces
a workspace with three kinds of sources: an index file that lists entity
identifiers and their associated filenames but contains none of the
values needed to answer the question; a set of per-entity detail files,
no single one of which is sufficient to compute the final answer; and at
least one cross-cutting reference source (e.g., a rate table or
threshold lookup). Solving such a task requires the agent to read the
index, fetch each relevant entity, and join information across all three
source types—naturally increasing the number of tool calls and reasoning
steps. Importantly, this structure is enforced by construction rather
than merely described in the prompt.

The _dirtiness draw_ injects controlled noise into the workspace:
distractors, missing fields, schema inconsistencies, and unit
mismatches. The injection is constrained so that the ground-truth
solution logic remains well-defined over the perturbed data, ensuring
that tasks stay solvable despite the added friction.

Deliverable formats are round-robined across structured JSON, CSV,
multi-file output, and raw reports. For the last case, a
machine-checkable sidecar file carries the grading criteria, so that the
agent learns to produce free-form text as an output modality while every
format remains deterministically verifiable.

#### Execution-grounded validation

Finally, for a given task, we introduce some validation process to
filter out low-quality tasks (_e.g._, unsolvable tasks or tasks with
inconsistency). This process involves both agent-free and agent-based
validation. For agent-free validation, we consider the following
aspects:

- •
  _Oracle_: the synthesis LLM writes a reference solution and a
  deterministic test-based verifier, and the oracle must run and pass
  its own verifier.
- •
  _Leakage audit_: deterministic checks confirm the instruction does not
  leak the answer key and the verifier admits no trivial solution. Any
  task whose tests already pass in this state would assign a reward
  without requiring the agent to perform any work and is therefore
  discarded as a false positive in reward assignment.

For tasks that can pass agent-free validation, we further introduce
agent-based validation. Specifically, the current agent
$`\pi_{\theta_{k-1}}`$ is deployed in sandbox containers, and its
rollouts are collected in parallel across a pool of harness instances
for each constructed tasks. We let the agent perform $`N`$ trials for
each task, and only keep tasks with number of passed trials in a
specific range $`[N_{l},N_{h}]`$. Specifically, we restrict
$`N_{l}>0,N_{h}<N`$, as setting $`N_{l}=0`$ will keep tasks that current
agent cannot complete (too difficult or with some inconsistency), and
setting $`N_{h}=N`$ will keep tasks that current agent can always
complete (too easy).

### 4.2 Phase 2: Train

After obtaining a sufficient number of tasks that pass all admission
criteria with suitable difficulty levels, ClawLoop applies standard
training techniques to update the agent. We employ two complementary
methods: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on self-generated successful
trajectories, and reinforcement learning (RL) via Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO) for further refinement.

##### Self-distilled SFT with multi-turn loss masking.

This process directly follows from the task validation step, where each
trajectory $`\xi_{i}`$ is stored together with its outcome reward
$`r_{i}=V_{\tau_{i}}(s_{T}^{(i)})`$. Only successful trajectories
($`r_{i}=1`$) for validated tasks are retained for supervised
fine-tuning, making this a form of _self-distillation_: the agent learns
from its own correct behavior rather than from an external teacher’s
demonstrations. Following standard practice for multi-turn agent
training, we mask the loss on environment observation tokens (tool
outputs, file contents) and compute cross-entropy only on the agent’s
own action tokens:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=-\sum_{i:\,r_{i}=1}\;\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}m_{t}^{(i)}\log\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(a_{t}^{(i)}\mid p_{i},\,s_{0}^{(i)},\,a_{1:t-1}^{(i)},\,s_{1:t-1}^{(i)}\bigr),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`m_{t}^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}`$ is the per-token turn mask that zeroes
out all observation positions.

##### RL with GRPO.

While SFT on successful trajectories provides a strong warm-start, it is
inherently limited to imitating existing successes and cannot improve
beyond the current policy’s best behavior. We therefore apply Group
Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ([Shao et al. 2024](#bib.bib6)) with
outcome-based rewards to push the agent beyond its SFT ceiling. For each
task $`\tau_{i}`$ that passes the validation (neither too easy nor too
difficult), the same $`N`$ rollouts collected during agent-based
validation form the group; the advantage of rollout $`j`$ is computed
relative to the group mean:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{A}_{j}^{(i)}=\frac{r_{j}^{(i)}-\bar{r}^{(i)}}{\sigma_{r}^{(i)}+\epsilon}.
``` |  | (2) |

### 4.3 Phase 3: Diagnosis and Feedback

To close the self-evolving loop, the agent needs to diagnose its own
behavior and provide feedback to the task synthesis phase in next
iteration. Similar to the validation part in task synthesis, we deploy
the updated agent $`\pi_{\theta_{k}}`$ in another set of sandbox
containers, and collect its rollouts for tasks in the held-out
evaluation set $`\mathcal{E}_{k}`$ In addition to the pass rates on
individual tasks, ClawLoop analyzes *why* the agent fails by summarizing
failed trajectories, extracting behavioral patterns, and feeding these
insights back into the next iteration’s task synthesis phase.

##### Trajectory summarization.

For each failed trajectory $`\xi`$ from any tasks in the evaluation set
$`\mathcal{E}_{k}`$, we prompt the agent itself to produce a structured
summary consisting of: (i) the *failure point*: the specific action or
decision where the trajectory diverged from a successful path; (ii) the
*root cause*: whether the failure stems from misunderstanding the task,
choosing the wrong tool, making incorrect edits, or prematurely
terminating; (iii) the *missing capability*: what the agent would need
to have done differently. Note that trajectory summarization is a
substantially easier task than solving the original problem, as it
requires only post-hoc analysis of a concrete execution trace rather
than open-ended planning. These summaries serve as structured diagnostic
evidence rather than reward signals.

##### Pattern aggregation.

The per-trajectory summaries are then classified by skill category and
aggregated to identify recurring *failure patterns*. Concretely,
summaries within each category are clustered by root cause, and the most
frequent failure modes are surfaced as diagnostic reports. For example,
consider the capability “multi-file refactoring”, we have observed a
common pattern “agent modifies the target file but fails to update
import statements in dependent modules” during the iterative process.
These aggregated patterns provide actionable guidance for the next round
of task synthesis, directing it to generate tasks that specifically
target the identified weaknesses.

## 5 Experiments

In this section, we conduct experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness
of ClawLoop. The loop is implemented by OpenClaw ([OpenClaw Contributors
2026](#bib.bib2)), and we use Qwen3.6-27B([Qwen Team 2026](#bib.bib34))
as the agent inside the loop, which runs $`K=3`$ iterations of the
ClawLoop cycle, and the task synthesis phase of each iteration generates
1024 new training tasks. Each task has $`N=8`$ rollouts for both
validation and self-distilled SFT, and we set $`N_{l}=1,N_{h}=5`$ to
filter out tasks that are too easy or too hard for RL. To evaluate the
performance of our trained models, we consider the following three
benchmark, covering different specific capabilities of harness-based
agents:

- •
  Harness-free benchmark. We choose SkillsBench ([Li et al.
  2026b](#bib.bib8)), which consists of 86 tasks spanning 11
  professional domains. This benchmark targets on evaluating how well
  agent can utilize skills consisting of domain knowledge, and can be
  combined to any harness not restricted to OpenClaw.
- •
  OpenClaw ([OpenClaw Contributors 2026](#bib.bib2))-based benchmark.
  Among different OpenClaw-based benchmarks, we choose
  Claw-Eval-Live ([Li et al. 2026a](#bib.bib30)) and ClawGym-Bench ([Bai
  et al. 2026](#bib.bib1)) as the representative. Claw-Eval-Live has 105
  tasks specifically targeting real-life working scenarios. These tasks
  do not explicitly depends on specific skills and can evaluate how
  agent directly working with files. ClawGym-Bench has 200 tasks
  constructed from the same pipeline to construct training tasks in
  ClawGym.

### 5.1 Main Results

Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ RL with GRPO. ‣ 4.2 Phase 2: Train ‣ 4 ClawLoop Framework ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
compares ClawLoop against the Qwen3.6-27B base model and ClawGym.
ClawLoop consistently surpasses ClawGym across all three benchmarks by a
clear margin, demonstrating that iterative self-improvement with
diagnostic feedback produces higher-quality training signal than a
single-pass synthesis pipeline. Notably, gains on ClawGym-Bench
(within-distribution) are comparable to those on SkillsBench and
Claw-Eval-Live, suggesting genuine capability improvement rather than
distribution-specific overfitting.

Beyond evaluating the self-improving framework itself, we examine
whether the tasks constructed by ClawLoop transfer to other models.
Smaller models trained on these tasks serve as independent probes of
data quality, since any improvement cannot be attributed to the
synthesizer’s own capacity.
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ RL with GRPO. ‣ 4.2 Phase 2: Train ‣ 4 ClawLoop Framework ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
reports results for Qwen3.5-4B and 9B models trained on
ClawLoop-generated data versus ClawGym-generated data. Despite ClawGym
relying on stronger external teacher models (MiniMax-M2.5 and GLM-5.1)
for both task synthesis and trajectory collection, models trained on
ClawLoop data achieve comparable or superior performance, validating
that our iterative, teacher-free pipeline produces training data of
equal or higher quality than teacher-dependent alternatives.

### 5.2 Ablation Study

##### Synthesis route

Note that the two task synthesis routines (capability-driven and
domain-grounded) are modularly independent,
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ RL with GRPO. ‣ 4.2 Phase 2: Train ‣ 4 ClawLoop Framework ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
reports the performance of Qwen3.6-27B within ClawLoop when one routine
is removed from the task synthesis phase. Removing either routine
degrades overall performance, but the two routines affect different
benchmarks. Dropping the capability-driven routine causes the largest
decline on Claw-Eval-Live, whereas dropping the domain-grounded routine
primarily hurts SkillsBench and ClawGym-Bench. This asymmetry aligns
with the nature of each benchmark: Claw-Eval-Live emphasizes broad,
general-purpose agent capabilities that the capability-driven routine is
designed to cover, while SkillsBench and ClawGym-Bench are more heavily
structured around specific skill categories that the domain-grounded
routine targets.

##### Impact of difficulty growth

We further analyze the impact of the difficulty growth routine. As shown
in
Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Synthesis route ‣ 5.2 Ablation Study ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops"),
increasing task difficulty produces an immediate and visible effect:
average trajectory length grows substantially, as harder tasks demand
more reasoning steps, tool invocations, and error-recovery cycles.
However, longer trajectories do not necessarily indicate better training
signal. In practice, we find that a moderate schedule—raising difficulty
by at most one level per iteration—strikes the best balance, yielding
trajectories that are challenging enough to drive improvement while
maintaining sufficient success rates for effective learning.

##### Impact of $`K`$.

Finally,
Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Impact of difficulty growth ‣ 5.2 Ablation Study ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
examines how the number of self-improving iterations $`K`$ affects
performance across all three benchmarks. Performance improves steadily
in early iterations but saturates at different rates. SkillsBench
continues to benefit from additional iterations, whereas Claw-Eval-Live
and ClawGym-Bench plateau around $`K=3`$. We attribute this to the
broader domain coverage of SkillsBench, which provides a larger space
for the curriculum to explore before exhausting novel task diversity.
Based on this observation, we set $`K=3`$ in our main experiments to
balance computational cost and final performance.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we propose ClawLoop, a self-improving framework for
harness-based LLM agents with minimal prior dependencies and no reliance
on any external strong teacher model. As the core component of this
self-improving framework, the task synthesis phase starts with two
complementary routines that construct seed tasks from different
perspectives. The seed tasks then pass through a diagnosis-conditioned
difficulty growth routine and are validated via both agent-free and
agent-based procedures to ensure their quality for latter training. The
complete framework, composing task synthesis, model training, and
self-diagnosis into a unified loop, enables agents to identify and
address their own weaknesses without human intervention during the
training cycle. Using Qwen3.6-27B inside the loop, we demonstrate that
ClawLoop yields consistent improvements across diverse benchmarks for
harness-based agents. The constructed tasks are further used to train
smaller-scale Qwen3.5-4B and 9B models, with gains observed across all
benchmarks and model scales. We leave exploration of longer training
horizons with adaptive stopping criteria to future work.

### A.1 Harness and Infrastructure

All three phases of ClawLoop execute inside the same harness substrate,
instantiated via OpenClaw ([OpenClaw Contributors 2026](#bib.bib2)).
Each rollout runs in an isolated Docker container provisioned from a
common base image that contains a POSIX shell, a Python 3.11
interpreter, standard command-line utilities, and a package manager.
Containers are network-isolated from external services: any package
required by a synthesized task is pre-installed in the base image or
vendored into the workspace at generation time, which keeps rollouts
reproducible and prevents the agent from retrieving solutions from the
open internet. Each container is capped at a wall-clock budget of 24
hours and a maximum of 300 agent turns; exceeding either budget
terminates the trajectory and records it as a failure, so that
non-termination is penalized rather than silently discarded. Rollout
collection is parallelized across a pool of 32 concurrent container
instances.

A single ClawLoop iteration therefore consists of three distinct compute
workloads: task synthesis (LLM inference plus sandboxed generator
execution), rollout collection (LLM inference plus container execution),
and gradient updates.
Table [4](#A1.T4 "Table 4 ‣ A.1 Harness and Infrastructure ‣ Appendix A Experimental Details ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
reports the measured cost of each workload.

The breakdown shows that rollout collection, not gradient computation,

of the per-iteration total, and exceed the combined SFT and GRPO
training time. This is characteristic of harness-native training, where
every reward signal requires executing an agent trajectory against a
real environment rather than scoring a static output. The diagnosis pass
is comparatively cheap because $`\mathcal{E}_{k}`$ is a small held-out
split disjoint from the training pool $`\mathcal{T}_{k}`$, so only a
fraction of the iteration’s tasks are re-run for diagnosis.

### A.2 Training Configuration

Table [5](#A1.T5 "Table 5 ‣ A.2 Training Configuration ‣ Appendix A Experimental Details ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")

self-distilled SFT on successful trajectories, followed by GRPO ([Shao
et al. 2024](#bib.bib6)) for further refinement beyond the SFT ceiling.
Every task admitted to the pool receives $`N=8`$ rollouts, which serve
simultaneously as the validation signal, the source of successful
trajectories for SFT, and the group used for advantage estimation in
GRPO. Reusing a single set of rollouts for all three purposes is what
keeps the loop’s compute cost tractable;
§[C](#A3 "Appendix C SFT and RL within the Loop ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
examines the consequences for each training stage.

Two details of the SFT setup are specific to the harness setting and
worth making explicit. First, the per-token mask $`m_{t}`$ in

here than in typical multi-turn chat training, because a single harness
observation (a large file read or a verbose build log) can span
thousands of tokens and would otherwise dominate the loss. Second,
trajectories are packed to the maximum sequence length rather than
truncated: truncating a harness trajectory would remove the final

### A.3 Evaluation Protocol

All three external benchmarks are evaluated with the harness
configuration held fixed across methods, so that differences reflect the
policy rather than the scaffold. SkillsBench ([Li et al.
2026b](#bib.bib8)) is reported as Avg$`@`$5, *i.e.* the mean over five
independent samples per task, which reduces the variance introduced by
its relatively small size (86 tasks). Claw-Eval-Live ([Li et al.
2026a](#bib.bib30)) and ClawGym-Bench ([Bai et al. 2026](#bib.bib1)) are
reported as the average score produced by their respective official
graders. Decoding uses temperature 0.7 and top-$`p`$ 0.95 for all
evaluations.

None of the three benchmarks is used for training, task selection, or
difficulty scheduling at any point in the loop; the diagnosis phase
observes only the held-out synthesized split $`\mathcal{E}_{k}`$. This
separation is what allows the benchmarks to serve as an independent
check on whether the loop produces genuine capability gains rather than
fitting its own task distribution.

## Appendix C SFT and RL within the Loop

The main text describes SFT and GRPO as complementary stages: SFT
provides a warm-start by imitating the agent’s own successes, and GRPO
pushes past the ceiling that imitation imposes. This section explains
why the two stages contribute differently at different model scales.

##### Why the two stages contribute differently.

Self-distilled SFT requires a supply of successful trajectories, and
that supply is a function of the model’s own success rate on the
validated pool. The 27B loop model clears the admission band on enough
tasks that each iteration yields between 892 and 1,978 successful
trajectories
(Table [6](#A4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ D.1 Per-Iteration Statistics ‣ Appendix D Loop Dynamics ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")),
which is sufficient for SFT alone to produce a stable gradient signal.
Smaller models do not: on the same pool their success rate is low enough
that SFT sees few positive examples, and the resulting update is both
noisy and biased toward the easiest tasks in the pool. GRPO compensates
because it extracts signal from the *contrast* between rollouts within a
group, so a task on which the model succeeds once out of eight attempts
still produces a usable advantage estimate, whereas SFT would retain
that single trajectory and discard the seven informative failures. This
is why the gains reported for the 4B and 9B models in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ RL with GRPO. ‣ 4.2 Phase 2: Train ‣ 4 ClawLoop Framework ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
are attributed primarily to the RL stage.

##### Interaction with task admission.

This also explains why the admission band $`[N_{l},N_{h}]`$ with
$`N_{l}>0`$ and $`N_{h}<N`$ matters more for RL than for SFT. Tasks
solved on every attempt produce zero advantage under
Eq. [2](#S4.E2 "In RL with GRPO. ‣ 4.2 Phase 2: Train ‣ 4 ClawLoop Framework ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
because the group is unanimous, and tasks solved on no attempt produce
zero advantage for the same reason; in both cases the group standard
deviation $`\sigma_{r}`$ collapses and the task contributes nothing but
compute. The main text’s setting of $`N_{l}=1`$ and $`N_{h}=5`$ out of
$`N=8`$ rollouts retains exactly the tasks whose outcome is uncertain
under the current policy, which is where the gradient signal is
concentrated. Because the band is defined relative to
$`\pi_{\theta_{k-1}}`$, it moves with the agent: a task that is
informative at iteration $`k`$ may be unanimously solved by iteration
$`k+1`$ and is then excluded automatically, without any external
curriculum schedule.

##### Scaling behavior.

The main text reports ClawLoop results at three scales: Qwen3.5-4B and
9B as consumers of the curriculum, and Qwen3.6-27B as the loop model.
Two caveats bound what these results establish about scaling. First, a
single loop model drives the curriculum at all three scales, so we
cannot separate the effect of scale on the *consumer* of the curriculum
from its effect on the *producer*; varying the loop model itself would
be required for that. Second, the 4B and 9B models never run their own
diagnosis phase, and instead inherit a curriculum shaped by the 27B
model’s weakness profile. That this transfers at all is precisely the
finding reported in the main text, and it is what licenses treating the
smaller models as independent probes of data quality. But it also means
those models are not self-improving in the sense the framework defines,
and their gains should be read as evidence about the curriculum rather
than as instances of the full loop.

## Appendix D Loop Dynamics

The main text reports what the loop achieves at its endpoint. This
section reports what happens *inside* the loop: how the task pool and
the trajectories it elicits evolve across iterations. These quantities
are the loop’s internal state, and tracking them is what allows us to
distinguish genuine curriculum progression from a pool that merely grows
without becoming more informative.

### D.1 Per-Iteration Statistics

Table [6](#A4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ D.1 Per-Iteration Statistics ‣ Appendix D Loop Dynamics ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
summarizes the state of the loop at each iteration. The two validation
stages play distinct roles. Agent-free validation checks only that a
candidate is well-formed — that its generator runs deterministically and
its verifier is executable — and it admits a stable $`\sim`$89% of
candidates in every iteration, indicating that the synthesis routine’s
basic output quality does not degrade as the loop proceeds. Agent-based
validation is the selective stage: it retains only tasks whose
difficulty falls inside the admission band $`[N_{l},N_{h}]=[1,5]`$, and
because that band is defined relative to the current policy, it is the
stage through which the agent’s own improvement feeds back into the
curriculum.

|-------------------------------|-----------|-----------|-----------|

| Passed agent-free validation  | 897       | 910       | 913       |

The admission rate is reported relative to the 1,024 candidates

The admission rate does not decay monotonically: it falls sharply from
65.6% at $`k{=}1`$ to 45.8% at $`k{=}2`$, then recovers to 55.4% at
$`k{=}3`$. The trajectory statistics move in lockstep, with mean turns
rising from 5.6 to 11.4 before settling at 8.7 and mean token counts
tracing the same shape. This is the difficulty-growth routine
overshooting and then correcting. At $`k{=}2`$ the routine escalates
difficulty too aggressively in response to the agent’s first-round
gains, pushing many candidates past the upper bound $`N_{h}`$: the
admission rate drops, and the tasks that do survive are markedly longer.
By $`k{=}3`$ the escalation has been damped and the pool settles at a
difficulty the agent can engage productively, with the admission rate
and trajectory length both landing between their $`k{=}1`$ and $`k{=}2`$
values. The loop therefore converges on an appropriate difficulty level
by traversing it from both sides rather than approaching it
monotonically.

### D.2 Diminishing Returns

Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Impact of difficulty growth ‣ 5.2 Ablation Study ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
in the main text shows that performance saturates around $`K=3`$ for two
of the three benchmarks.
Table [6](#A4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ D.1 Per-Iteration Statistics ‣ Appendix D Loop Dynamics ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
suggests a mechanistic reading. The binding constraint is not how many
candidates are synthesized, which is fixed at 1,024 per iteration, but
how many land inside the admission band: only 45.8% to 65.6% survive
agent-based validation, so between a third and a half of the synthesis
budget yields no training signal at all. Discarded tasks are those the
agent solves on every attempt or on none, and both produce zero
advantage under
Eq. [2](#S4.E2 "In RL with GRPO. ‣ 4.2 Phase 2: Train ‣ 4 ClawLoop Framework ‣ ClawLoop: Self-Improving Agents via Harness-Native Training Loops")
because the group is unanimous. The $`k{=}2`$ overshoot shows that
keeping candidates inside the band requires the synthesis routine to
track a moving policy, and that this tracking is imprecise. This points
to the difficulty controller, rather than the number of iterations, as
the limiting factor: a routine that placed a larger fraction of its
output inside the band would extract more from the same budget.

## Appendix E Case Study

To make the synthesized tasks concrete, this section examines two
instances drawn from the same task family in the TaskForge pool,
together with the trajectories they elicited from the loop model. The
two instances differ substantially in difficulty, and comparing them
shows *where* the additional difficulty is absorbed: not in a longer
sequence of task steps, but in a markedly longer verification phase.
Task instructions are translated from the original Chinese.

### E.1 Two Instances of an Aggregation Task

Both instances belong to an aggregation-and-reporting family in which
the agent must join two CSV inputs, compute grouped statistics under
explicit business rules, and emit report files that a verifier checks
field by field.

##### Instance A (harder).

> From the order ledger (orders.csv) and the product master table
> (products.csv), generate a group of order-amount distribution and
> category-tier reports (covering distribution statistics, quartile
> analysis, refund metrics, value-tier summaries, and a voided-order
> audit log), writing multiple JSON files to /output/. Detailed
> requirements are in /input/requirements.md; the output contract is in
> /input/task_spec.json.

##### Instance B (easier).

> Read /input/orders.csv and /input/customers.csv and join orders to
> customers by customer_id. For each customer compute a spending profile
> (total_paid, total_refunded, net_spending, and the corresponding order
> counts), assign a tier by net_spending (HIGH_VALUE $`\geq 500`$,
> STANDARD for $`0<\cdot<500`$, AT_RISK $`\leq 0`$), and write each tier
> to its own CSV file plus a tier_summary.csv aggregate.

##### Workspace and hidden decisions.

Neither instance is answerable from any single input file: the order
ledger carries transaction amounts but not the category or customer
attributes needed to group them, and the joined dimension table supplies
those attributes without the amounts. Both instances also depend on
decisions that the data alone underdetermines, and these are declared in
skill files that the workspace makes available to the agent rather than
left implicit. For Instance A, the tier-classification skill specifies
that a category median falling exactly on a threshold is assigned to the
higher tier, that tiers are computed from the unrounded median with
rounding applied only at output, and that categories with no valid
orders are omitted from the summary entirely. Instance B is governed
analogously by skills covering decimal money aggregation, dirty-record
handling, and the representation of empty tiers. Without such
declarations the tasks would be ill-posed: several defensible outputs
would exist, and a verifier checking exact values would penalize the
agent for a choice the instruction never constrained.

### E.3 What the Verification Phase Contains

The content of Instance A’s verification turns is worth describing,
because it is the behavior that self-distilled SFT retains when the
trajectory is admitted. After running its script, the agent recomputed
the global revenue aggregate independently and obtained a figure that
appeared to contradict its own earlier estimate; it then re-derived the
sum term by term, found the true total to be $`6984.997`$, and confirmed
that this rounds to the reported $`6985.00`$. It next cross-checked the
tier summary against the distribution report, verifying that the
per-category revenues sum to the reported tier totals. Finally, its
audit of sort order flagged an apparent violation among categories
sharing an identical median; on inspection the agent determined that its
own checking script was at fault, having rejected equal medians outright
instead of verifying that the alphabetical tie-break had been applied
correctly, and that the output was in fact correct.

This last step is the most interesting of the three, because the agent
is not merely re-running its solution but auditing its own audit, and
correctly concluding that the solution stands and the check was wrong.
Behavior of this kind is what the admission band is designed to select
for. A task that the agent solves on the first attempt without
verification produces a trajectory with little of this content, while a
task it cannot solve at all produces none; tasks in between elicit
trajectories in which the agent must check itself, and those are the
trajectories that enter the SFT set.

We note that these two instances are illustrative rather than a
controlled comparison. They come from the same task family but are not
the same task, so the difference in verification effort cannot be
attributed to difficulty alone with any precision. The aggregate trend
in

where mean trajectory length tracks the admission rate across
iterations, is the quantitative form of the same observation.
````

###### Abstract

Mental health disorders affect a substantial proportion of the global
population, yet a persistent shortage of trained practitioners leaves
the majority without adequate care. Large language model (LLM)-based
counselors present a promising direction for delivering scalable
conversational psychological support. Offline model training alone
leaves limited room to adapt to individual clients or to learn from
ongoing therapeutic interaction at test time. We introduce PsyEvo, an
LLM-based counseling framework that enables both client-specific
personalization and response-policy improvement at test time through
three components: Hierarchical Bayesian Skill Policy (HBSP) personalizes
what intervention to apply by maintaining a per-client skill posterior
updated from session feedback; Inter-session Listwise Preference
Optimization (LiPO) improves how the selected skill is expressed by
updating a shared response adapter from cross-client preference
evidence; and State-conditioned Ordinal Credit Assignment (SOCA)
supplies candidate preferences and trajectory credit to the two
components through consistency-checked comparisons and ordinal
projection. In simulated-client evaluation with shared online cohort
adaptation, PsyEvo obtains 7.684 Overall on PsychEval and exceeds every
component variant in each of three matched runs. Removing individual
components lowers mean overall score by 0.138–0.171 under the shared
configuration, supporting conditional contributions within the complete
scaffold. Our code is available at
[https://github.com/Lingxi-mental-health/PsyEvo](https://github.com/Lingxi-mental-health/PsyEvo).

## 1 Introduction

Mental health disorders have become a major global health challenge,
with nearly one billion individuals affected and a substantial share of
the worldwide disease burden attributed to conditions such as depression
and anxiety ([World Health Organization,](#bib.bib25) ; [World Health
Organization, 2025](#bib.bib26)). Psychological counseling is a widely
adopted evidence-based intervention, yet training qualified counselors
requires years of supervision, and the available workforce falls far
short of demand ([British Association for Counselling and
Psychotherapy,](#bib.bib27) ; [World Health Organization,
2022](#bib.bib5)). In this context, large language models (LLMs) provide
a scalable conversational interface that could expand access to
psychological support.

Research on LLM-based counseling spans supervised fine-tuning (SFT),
preference optimization, and agent-based mechanisms. SoulChat and
CPsyCoun provide counseling and empathetic dialogue data for SFT,
whereas PsyCoPref supplies preference comparisons for response
optimization ([Chen et al., 2023](#bib.bib8); [Zhang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9); [Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib28)). MusPsy and
PsychAgent incorporate memory, planning, and skill-related mechanisms to
support longitudinal interaction ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Yang
et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)). These approaches extend counseling support
from individual responses to multiple sessions. Beyond continuity,
individual clients can differ in their experiences, needs, and
communication styles ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)), motivating
updates to intervention selection and to the response policy during
test-time interaction. This motivates two complementary learning
objectives: personalization, which tailors intervention selection to a
particular client, and counselor self-evolution, which updates the
response policy using cross-client experience.

To this end, we present PsyEvo, a test-time learning framework for
multi-session psychological counseling that addresses both objectives
over a frozen backbone through three components: Hierarchical Bayesian
Skill Policy (HBSP) for personalized intervention selection via a
per-client skill posterior, Listwise Preference Optimization (LiPO) for
shared response-policy improvement via cross-client preference graphs,
and State-conditioned Ordinal Credit Assignment (SOCA) for constructing
candidate preferences and trajectory credit from relative comparisons
and realized feedback. Our contributions are as follows: (1) we
introduce PsyEvo, which combines client-private skill posteriors with a
shared population response adapter over a frozen backbone, using HBSP
for personalized skill selection and LiPO for cross-session
response-policy learning; (2) we propose SOCA, which converts compressed
self-evaluation feedback into candidate preferences through
consistency-checked comparisons and into delayed credit through
within-trajectory ordinal propagation; and (3) we establish positive
conditional component contributions across three matched PsychEval runs
and evaluate a related evidence-fusion rule on SummEval, where it
achieves the highest mean agreement with human judgments among the
compared evaluators.

## 2 PsyEvo

### 2.1 Problem Formulation

We formalize multi-session psychological counseling as a sequential
decision process. Let $`u\in\mathcal{U}`$ denote a client, $`k`$ the
session index, and $`t`$ the turn index within a session. At each turn,
the counselor observes a state $`s_{t}`$ comprising the realized
dialogue history, the inferred client state, the therapeutic modality,
the course stage, and a cross-session memory $`M_{u}`$. A skill
controller $`\mu_{u}`$ selects a counseling skill $`a_{t}`$ from the set
$`\mathcal{A}_{t}`$ of skills valid for the current modality, and the
response policy $`\pi_{\theta,\phi^{(v)}}`$ generates $`N`$ candidate
responses $`y_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,y_{t}^{(N)}`$ conditioned on $`s_{t}`$
and $`a_{t}`$; one candidate is delivered to the client.

PsyEvo decomposes the counselor’s learning state into a client-private
component and a population-shared component:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\Omega_{u}=(M_{u},b_{u}),\qquad\Psi=(\phi^{(v)},\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{pop}}),\qquad\pi_{u}(a,y\mid s)=\mu_{u}(a\mid s)\pi_{\theta,\phi^{(v)}}(y\mid s,a),
``` |  | (1) |

where the backbone parameters $`\theta`$ remain frozen throughout. The
private state $`\Omega_{u}`$ consists of the client’s memory $`M_{u}`$
and a skill posterior $`b_{u}`$; the population state $`\Psi`$ contains
the versioned shared adapter $`\phi^{(v)}`$ and a population evidence
buffer $`\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{pop}}`$.

Three components operate over this decomposition
(Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")).
HBSP updates $`b_{u}`$ to personalize which skill to select for each
client; LiPO updates $`\phi^{(v)}`$ to improve how the selected skill is
expressed; and SOCA constructs the learning signals, i.e. candidate
preferences and trajectory credit, consumed by both.

Figure 2: Overview of PsyEvo’s test-time learning cycle. Within a
session, HBSP selects a skill and SOCA selects the delivered response;
between sessions, HBSP updates the client-private posterior using SOCA
trajectory credit, while LiPO periodically updates the shared adapter
from SOCA preference graphs accumulated across clients. External judges
score completed transcripts for reporting only and do not feed the
learning signals.

### 2.2 HBSP

First, we propose HBSP to determine which counseling skill to apply at
each turn. A shared skill-prior table $`Q(s,a)`$, initialized from
skill-labeled PsychEval dialogue, provides a common starting point for
all clients. During interaction, $`Q`$ remains fixed; only the
client-private posterior $`b_{u}(s,a)`$ is updated to capture individual
preferences. Each client starts with $`m_{u}(s,a)=0`$ and
$`\lambda_{u}(s,a)=4`$, corresponding to a residual prior variance of
$`0.25`$; we use observation variance $`\sigma_{b}^{2}=1`$.

Concretely, HBSP decomposes the skill-selection score into the shared
term and a client-specific residual drawn from a normal posterior:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle Q_{u}(s,a)`$ | $`\displaystyle=Q(s,a)+b_{u}(s,a),\qquad b_{u}(s,a)\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{u}(s,a),\lambda_{u}(s,a)^{-1}),`$ |  | (2) |
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{t}(a)`$ | $`\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon_{t})\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\tau[Q(s_{t},a)+\tilde{b}_{u}(s_{t},a)]\right)+\varepsilon_{t}/|\mathcal{A}_{t}|.`$ |  | (3) |

Here, $`\tilde{b}_{u}(s_{t},a)`$ denotes a draw from
$`\mathcal{N}(m_{u}(s_{t},a),\lambda_{u}(s_{t},a)^{-1})`$ for the
current skill decision. At the end of each session, SOCA assigns a
trajectory return $`G_{t}`$ to the skill selected at turn $`t`$
(Section [2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 SOCA ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")).
With residual $`d_{t}=G_{t}-Q(s_{t},a_{t})`$, HBSP uses the Gaussian
working likelihood
$`d_{t}\mid b_{u}(s_{t},a_{t})\sim\mathcal{N}(b_{u}(s_{t},a_{t}),\sigma_{b}^{2})`$
to update the posterior:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\lambda^{\prime}_{u}=\lambda_{u}+\sigma_{b}^{-2},\qquad m^{\prime}_{u}=\frac{\lambda_{u}m_{u}+\sigma_{b}^{-2}d_{t}}{\lambda^{\prime}_{u}}.
``` |  | (4) |

Posterior sampling uses model-based uncertainty for exploration:
repeated observations increase precision, while less-visited state–skill
cells retain wider sampling distributions.
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1.SSS0.Px2 "How the skill prior combines with returns. ‣ C.1 Supplementary Implementation Details ‣ Appendix C Implementation and Reproducible Update Schedule ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")
derives how the shared starting score and observed returns combine under
this update.

### 2.3 LiPO

While HBSP personalizes skill selection per client, we employ LiPO to
improve how the selected skill is expressed by updating a shared
response adapter from preference evidence accumulated across clients.
For each candidate group $`g`$ generated for client $`u`$, SOCA provides
a set of supported preference edges $`E_{u,g}`$ with positive weights
$`w_{ij}`$
(Section [2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 SOCA ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")).
Each graph retains its client and modality identifiers; no edge is
constructed between candidates from different clients.

We utilize a shared rank-8 LoRA adapter ([Hu et al., 2022](#bib.bib3))
as the learnable module. For a preference edge $`i\succ j`$, the
policy–reference log-ratio utility $`h_{u,g,i}`$ and the per-group loss
are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h_{u,g,i}=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta,\phi^{(v)}}(y_{i}\mid s,a)}{\pi_{\theta,0}(y_{i}\mid s,a)},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{u,g}=\frac{1}{|E_{u,g}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{u,g}}w_{ij}\operatorname{softplus}[-(h_{u,g,i}-h_{u,g,j})].
``` |  | (5) |

Our objective averages SOCA-weighted, DPO-style logistic losses within
each candidate group. For a single edge with unit weight,
Eq. [5](#S2.E5 "In 2.3 LiPO ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")
reduces to the standard pairwise DPO loss. LiPO retains the
candidate-group structure, weights each supported edge by its
bidirectional SOCA support strength, and normalizes by the number of
retained edges in that group; pairwise DPO optimizes individual
preferred–dispreferred pairs with unit weight. The population objective
averages the per-group losses over the candidate groups in the training
batch, so that a group does not receive greater weight merely because it
contains more preference edges:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pop}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{B}}|\mathcal{G}_{u}|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{B}}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{u}}\mathcal{L}_{u,g}.
``` |  | (6) |

The reference $`\pi_{\theta,0}`$ is the frozen backbone with the adapter
disabled. An accepted adapter version becomes available only to sessions
that start after the update boundary, ensuring that response generation
uses a fixed version throughout each session.

### 2.4 SOCA

Both HBSP and LiPO require informative learning signals, yet model
self-evaluation can assign nearly identical scores to plausible
counseling responses, a problem we call score compression. To address
this issue, we introduce SOCA to construct evidence at two resolutions:
candidate preferences for LiPO from pairwise comparisons within a state,
and trajectory credit for HBSP from ordinal projection along the
realized session.

#### Candidate preferences.

For each unordered candidate pair $`\{i,j\}`$, let
$`p_{ij}^{\rightarrow}`$ and $`p_{ij}^{\leftarrow}`$ denote weighted
judgment support for candidate $`i`$ under the two presentation orders.
SOCA accepts a direction only when both orders agree; otherwise it
abstains. The direction-consistent edge strength is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{ij}=\begin{cases}2\min(p_{ij}^{\rightarrow},p_{ij}^{\leftarrow})-1,&i\succ j,\\
2\min(1-p_{ij}^{\rightarrow},1-p_{ij}^{\leftarrow})-1,&j\succ i.\end{cases}
``` |  | (7) |

Edges below a stability floor are discarded. The remaining edges are
added in decreasing strength, skipping any that would create a directed
cycle; their strengths serve as the LiPO weights $`w_{ij}`$ in
Eq. [5](#S2.E5 "In 2.3 LiPO ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time").
We orient each edge from the preferred candidate to the dispreferred
candidate and define depth as the longest directed path from a candidate
to a sink, with sink depth zero. The delivered response maximizes this
depth; ties are resolved by the higher pointwise score and then the
smaller candidate index.

#### Trajectory credit.

For the realized trajectory, let $`R_{t}`$ be the turn outcome and
$`\rho_{t}`$ its tie-aware midrank among the $`T`$ session turns. SOCA
maps midranks to ordinal levels, scales them by $`s=0.5`$, and applies a
mean-preserving shift $`\xi`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{r}_{t}=\mathrm{clip}(\bar{R}+s\ell_{t}+\xi,0,1),\qquad\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{r}_{t}=\bar{R}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}R_{t}.
``` |  | (8) |

For zero-based midranks, we use $`K=\min(3,T)`$ and
$`\ell_{t}=2\min(K-1,\lfloor K\rho_{t}/T\rfloor)/(K-1)-1`$ when $`T>1`$,
and $`\ell_{1}=0`$ otherwise. Equal scores receive equal midranks and
therefore equal projected credit. Discounted propagation then connects
earlier skill decisions with later outcomes:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
G_{t}=\tilde{r}_{t}+\gamma G_{t+1},\qquad G_{T+1}=r_{\mathrm{session}},\qquad\gamma=0.9.
``` |  | (9) |

HBSP receives returns from the realized trajectory, while LiPO receives
preferences from candidate comparisons.

### 3.2 Evaluation Metrics

On PsychEval, we report four metric families derived from its 18
evaluation instruments ([Pan et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)). Counselor-shared
(C-Shared) summarizes common dimensions of counseling quality, including
working alliance and relational authenticity. Counselor-specific
(C-Spec) uses CTRS, MITI, TES, PSC, and EFT-TFS for CBT, behavioral,
humanistic, psychodynamic, and postmodernist therapy, respectively.
Client-shared (L-Shared) summarizes common simulated-client scores,
including affect and session alliance; client-specific (L-Spec) uses
BDI-II, STAI, CCT, IPO, and SFBT indicators for the corresponding
modalities. Metric definitions are detailed in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Evaluation Metric Details ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time").
Overall is the unweighted average of the four reported family scores.

For MusPsy, we report the Working Alliance Inventory–Short Form (WAI;
Goal, Task, Bond, and their average) and positive and negative PANAS
affect scores on a 1–5 scale ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)), averaged
over six sessions and 100 clients using the official MusPsy evaluation
code and judging prompt. While for SummEval, we compute Spearman’s
$`\rho`$ between automatic and human ratings for coherence (COH),
consistency (CON), fluency (FLU), and relevance (REL) within each
source-document group and macro-average across groups ([Fabbri et al.,
2021](#bib.bib21)); Mean averages the four dimensions. Higher values are
better for all metrics except negative PANAS.
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Evaluation Metric Details ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")
lists the constituent instruments, aggregation rules, and judging-prompt
protocols.

## 4 Related Work

LLM-based counseling has progressed from multi-turn empathetic dialogue
within a session ([Liu et al., 2021](#bib.bib7); [Chen et al.,
2023](#bib.bib8)) through report-grounded reconstruction ([Zhang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9)) and counselor-style modeling ([Xie et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10)) to multi-session agent systems with memory and
planning ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Pan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib1); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)). PsyEvo combines
client-private skill posteriors with a shared response adapter updated
at test time.

Personalization and test-time adaptation have been explored separately:
LaMP and OPPU personalize generation via user histories or
parameter-efficient modules ([Salemi et al., 2024](#bib.bib12); [Tan et
al., 2024](#bib.bib13)), while Self-Refine, Reflexion, and ExpeL enable
iterative self-improvement through feedback loops ([Madaan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib15); [Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib14); [Zhao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib16)). In preference optimization, DPO learns from pairwise
comparisons ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib2)) and LiPO provides a
general learning-to-rank framework ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib17));
MICA derives mixed credit from user-state potentials ([Zhang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib20)). Our LiPO objective uses SOCA support-strength
weights and group normalization; the original LiPO-Lambda variant uses
gain and rank-discount weights. LLM-based evaluation can exhibit
presentation bias and compressed scores ([Zheng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib4); [Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)). Reward-model training
can also produce prompt-independent reward distributions ([Song et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19)), a distinct issue from the self-evaluation score
compression addressed here. SOCA constructs ordinal evidence from
consistency-checked comparisons without learning a reward model.

## 5 Conclusion and Limitations

We have presented PsyEvo, a test-time learning framework that combines
HBSP for client-specific skill selection, LiPO for cross-client
response-policy improvement, and SOCA for candidate preferences and
ordinal trajectory credit. This separation allows individual
intervention preferences to evolve alongside a shared response policy
while keeping the backbone frozen. Matched PsychEval ablations show
consistent conditional component contributions across three runs; the
separate SummEval study examines evidence aggregation against human
judgments.

Several limitations remain. The automatic evaluations use simulated
clients and LLM judges, so their scores characterize model behavior
rather than clinical efficacy. Shared online adaptation within the
scored cohort does not establish transfer to unseen clients, and
individual component removals do not isolate the joint benefit of both
learning paths or each SOCA mechanism. Broader system comparisons mix
evaluated and published results, while the small human-rating pilot has
limited agreement and remains descriptive. Future work will examine
transfer to disjoint client cohorts, isolate the learning mechanisms
with matched controls, and broaden independent human evaluation.
Extending this framework to clinician-supervised settings will also
require assessment of crisis handling, privacy, and policy drift,
alongside whether personalized skill selection and shared policy
learning improve continuity across sessions.

#### PsychEval

PsychEval contains 369 case-based counseling trajectories (2,798
sessions and 67,314 turns; 7.6 sessions per case and 24.1 turns per
session on average) spanning cognitive behavioral, behavioral,
humanistic–existential, psychodynamic, postmodernist, and integrative
therapy, with a skill taxonomy of 677 meta-skills and 4,577 atomic
skills. We evaluate on a 100-client cohort across the five individual
modalities, with 20 clients per modality. The internal feedback used for
learning is kept separate from external outcome evaluation
(Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Reward Separation and Judge Agreement ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")).
Because clients can end their courses at different sessions,
session-wise analyses average the scored clients at each session.

#### MusPsy

MusPsy is a CBT-oriented synthetic corpus grounded in published case
reports, with 1,400 training and 100 test client profiles averaging 6.17
sessions per client and 28.55 turns per session; each profile includes
evolving client states, session goals, and counseling memories. We
evaluate all 100 test profiles over six consecutive sessions, yielding
600 client–session observations per method. The author-released
reference systems use Llama3-8B on 100 profiles; our evaluation follows
the official MusPsy evaluation code and judging prompt
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Evaluation Metric Details ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")).

#### SummEval

SummEval contains 1,600 summaries from 16 systems for 100 CNN/DailyMail
articles, each rated by three experts and five crowd workers on four
quality dimensions. We evaluate all 1,600 summaries across the 100
source documents, retaining each document’s 16 summaries as a group for
correlation computation.

## Appendix C Implementation and Reproducible Update Schedule

The PsychEval implementation separates dialogue serving from adapter
training. It uses Qwen3-32B as the counselor, BGE-M3 for retrieval
embeddings, and DeepSeek-V3.1 as the simulated client. Four graphics
processing units (GPUs) serve dialogue, and four train adapters. The
implementation maintains separate memories and skill posteriors for
individual clients and accumulates cross-client preference evidence for
LiPO training. Within each run, clients across the five modalities share
one response adapter and evidence buffer; their memories and skill
posteriors remain client-specific. Consequently, a session can use an
adapter updated from earlier sessions of other clients in the same
evaluation cohort. The reported scores assess this shared
online-learning setting and do not by themselves establish transfer to a
disjoint client cohort. Each seed starts a separate run with resume
disabled, and up to ten clients execute concurrently.

specifies the PsychEval full-system and component-comparison
configuration.

For counseling, the skill controller maps the dialogue to 54 slots:
three counseling stages, three within-session progress bins, and six
keyword-based affect categories. The stages are problem formulation and
goal setting, core intervention, and consolidation; progress bins use
thresholds 0.33 and 0.66 of the configured turn budget. The affect
categories are anxiety, sadness, anger, guilt, calmness, and positive
affect. This discrete index addresses the skill table; response
generation retains the dialogue context. HBSP treats residual returns
within each client-specific state–skill cell as conditionally
independent observations under its Gaussian working model. The resulting
precision controls posterior sampling within this model; it is an online
approximation for a policy that evolves across sessions. The hierarchy
consists of a fixed shared table and client-specific residuals; HBSP
does not learn a population hyperprior online.

The realized-turn outcome $`R_{t}\in[0,1]`$ is a retrospective
assessment of the client’s observed emotional change, willingness to
disclose, collaboration, and concrete progress after the delivered
response. The terminal reward is a weighted combination of
direction-aligned, normalized PANAS, modality-specific scale, SRS, and
alliance scores. SOCA applies the same mean-preserving ordinal
projection to the client’s session-reward history and uses the latest
projected value as $`r_{\mathrm{session}}`$; the first session retains
its raw value.
Equation [9](#S2.E9 "In Trajectory credit. ‣ 2.4 SOCA ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")
accumulates these credits with discounting.

Candidate generation and preference construction retain the identity of
the state from which each candidate group originates. The buffer stores
the prompt, candidate identifiers, local judgments, retained preference
edges, and the policy version that generated the group. Fixing this
evidence before optimization allows the loss to be reconstructed from
the saved candidate group. An external evaluation score is not
substituted for a missing candidate judgment.

On PsychEval, the five-session eligibility interval counts completed
sessions across clients sharing the adapter. An optimization round uses
320 preference pairs from at least 32 candidate groups, with each of the
five modalities contributing at least 20% of the batch. A batch that
fails a requirement is returned to the buffer for further evidence
accumulation. An update proceeds through evidence collection,
optimization, validation, and activation. The active adapter remains
fixed throughout each session. At a session boundary, the implementation
freezes the evidence, evaluates reference probabilities under the frozen
backbone with the adapter disabled, optimizes the preference objective,
and records the update outcome. Only an accepted adapter becomes
eligible for subsequent generation; a failed update leaves the previous
version active. This sequence associates each transcript with a specific
policy version.

### C.1 Supplementary Implementation Details

The supplementary method package exposes the numerical HBSP, SOCA, and
population LiPO operations separately from model inference and benchmark
evaluation. It is a reference implementation of these operations, rather
than an export of the experimental training system. The following
details specify its decision rules, weighting, and update lifecycle;
model-specific generation, feedback prompts, and physical adapter
training are supplied by a backend.

#### HBSP exploration and initialization.

Initialization uses skill labels in the static PsychEval corpus. The
base construction maps labeled decisions to the counseling-stage,
session-progress, and affect slots described above, counts skills within
each state, and normalizes their frequencies. For a state with observed
labels, writing $`n(s,a)`$ for the skill count gives the frequency prior

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Q_{\mathrm{freq}}(s,a)=\frac{n(s,a)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}n(s,a^{\prime})}.
``` |  | (10) |

The tooling also includes a mixed-prior variant; the loaded
initialization file determines which fixed table is used. In particular,
the frequency prior is a skill-selection preference score, not an
empirical mean discounted return. During evaluation, realized returns
update the client-private residual against the loaded, fixed table,
rather than re-estimating its corpus frequencies. The experiments use
$`\tau=1.0`$ and a fixed $`\varepsilon_{t}=0.05`$ throughout
interaction, matching the supplied HBSPConfig. The implementation’s
temperature multiplies the skill values before softmax, as in
Eq. [3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 HBSP ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time");
it is not a divisor. Posterior sampling is applied before the
exploration mixture, which is uniform only over the valid skills;
excluded skills receive zero probability. The constructor copies the
supplied shared table and keeps it read-only, while each new client
receives separate zero-mean residuals and prior precisions. An update
changes only the selected client–state–skill cell and subtracts the
corresponding shared value from the cumulative return without an
additional rescaling step. The package accepts the shared table as
input; its synthetic example is not the annotated-data initialization
used in the experiments. Decision records expose the action
probabilities and posterior mean, standard deviation, and draw, so
posterior updates and subsequent decision-time use can be inspected
separately.

#### How the skill prior combines with returns.

For a fixed client–state–skill cell, let $`G_{1},\ldots,G_{n}`$ denote
the returns assigned to its successive updates, with $`m_{0}=0`$ and
fixed $`Q`$ and $`\sigma_{b}^{2}`$. Accumulating
Eq. [4](#S2.E4 "In 2.2 HBSP ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")
gives $`\lambda_{n}m_{n}=\sigma_{b}^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(G_{i}-Q)`$, and
hence

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Q+m_{n}=\frac{\lambda_{0}Q+\sigma_{b}^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}}{\lambda_{0}+n\sigma_{b}^{-2}}.
``` |  | (11) |

Thus the mean selection score interpolates between the loaded prior and
the observed mean return; $`Q`$ need not itself be an empirical return
estimate. With the stated variances, its prior weight is $`4/(4+n)`$.
The cumulative $`G_{i}`$ and residual need not lie in the unit interval
used for individual rewards. This identity explains the update’s scale
behavior, not its statistical calibration: the working variance and
dependence between returns still affect exploration.

#### SOCA comparison and credit interfaces.

Qwen3-32B serves as the internal evaluator: SOCA converts its judgments
into candidate preferences, which LiPO uses to update the shared
response adapter. For each candidate pair, the evaluator supplies twelve
criterion-level A/B judgments, which are combined into a weighted
support share. Let $`z_{\ell,ij}^{o}`$ indicate whether criterion
$`\ell`$ favors the original candidate $`i`$ in order $`o`$, and let
$`\omega_{\ell}`$ denote its criterion weight. For a complete binary
judgment vector, the support share is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{ij}^{o}=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{12}\omega_{\ell}z_{\ell,ij}^{o}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{12}\omega_{\ell}},\qquad o\in\{\rightarrow,\leftarrow\}.
``` |  | (12) |

Each presentation order is evaluated separately, with A/B choices mapped
back to the original candidate identities before applying
Eq. [7](#S2.E7 "In Candidate preferences. ‣ 2.4 SOCA ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time").
The resulting number represents weighted criterion support, not a
next-token likelihood or a calibrated probability of therapeutic
benefit. The edge weight in
Eq. [7](#S2.E7 "In Candidate preferences. ‣ 2.4 SOCA ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")
measures the smaller of the two directional support margins; the twelve
criteria are inputs to one evaluator, not independent evaluators. The
graph constructor requires one two-order judgment for every unordered
candidate pair. Both support values refer to the same original candidate
after reversing presentation order. Agreement must be strict on the same
side of $`0.5`$; an exact tie or order disagreement produces no edge. To
apply the configured stability floor of $`0.1`$, the backend must pass
it explicitly, since the general graph routine defaults to a zero floor.
Equal-strength edges are ordered by a deterministic hash of the group
identifier and unordered candidate indices before acyclic admission.
Pointwise scores cannot create or reverse edges; they only break
equal-depth selection ties. An empty graph therefore falls back to
pointwise selection but is not admitted to preference training. For
realized outcomes, exact ties share a midrank, and a common shift
followed by clipping preserves the mean; the reference solver uses
bisection. Discounted returns are accumulated backward from the supplied
terminal reward. These interfaces consume numerical feedback and do not
determine which model or prompt generated it.

#### Feedback flow and optimization.

Pointwise candidate scores and two-order comparison supports describe
alternative responses at the current state, before a response is
delivered. Their role differs from realized-turn feedback, which
describes the simulated client’s reaction after delivery, and from
terminal feedback available after the session. The former supplies local
preference edges for LiPO; the latter supplies trajectory returns for
HBSP. The preference dataset contains candidates, edge directions, and
numerical weights, not gradients between groups. LiPO differentiates
policy log probabilities while holding these saved judgments fixed; SOCA
does not backpropagate through evaluator calls. The frozen,
adapter-disabled preference reference specifies the denominator in
Eq. [5](#S2.E5 "In 2.3 LiPO ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time");
it is a distinct role from the internal evaluator that supplies
comparison support. External transcript scores are a separate reporting
channel
(Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Reward Separation and Judge Agreement ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")).

#### Population weighting and batch units.

Each buffered item is a complete local graph identified by client and
group, with modality and generating policy version retained. Pooling
these items does not introduce comparisons between different clients. In
lipo.py, each graph loss is divided by its retained edge count, not by
the sum of support-strength weights; graph losses are then averaged
equally. Clients consequently contribute in proportion to their admitted
group counts, rather than receiving equal client weights. The listwise
grouping preserves local candidate sets, while the objective itself
remains a weighted pairwise logistic loss; it does not define a
likelihood over complete candidate permutations. The optional autograd
implementation detaches reference log probabilities; the backend must
compute them with the adapter disabled and matching response-token
masks. The reference batch sampler counts preference pairs as retained
graph edges. Under the PsychEval configuration, exactly 320 edges with
at least 20% from each of five modalities requires 64 edges per
modality, together with at least 32 groups. It selects whole graphs
without truncation and leaves the buffer unchanged if no admissible
batch exists. Its deterministic selection does not reconstruct the
experimental sampling order or identify the accounting unit of the
separate archived training trace in
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Compute, Deployment, and Safety ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time").

#### Session boundaries and failed updates.

One scheduler is shared by all clients in a learning scope. Starting a
session captures the active adapter identifier; later acceptance of a
new version does not change that session’s identifier. Under the
PsychEval schedule, five completed sessions make an update eligible, but
training still waits for an admissible batch. Training must produce a
separate candidate checkpoint, which is checked by a backend validation
callback before the active reference advances. An exception or rejection
returns the reserved graphs and retains the previous active reference;
returning evidence is not a rollback of arbitrary in-place model
changes. The backend must preserve the active checkpoint and implement
physical loading, cleanup, and recovery.

## Appendix D Evaluation Metric Details

### D.1 PsychEval

C-Shared covers working alliance (WAI), helping skills (HTAIS),
relational authenticity (RRO), and customized clinical-quality criteria.
C-Spec uses CTRS, MITI, TES, PSC, and EFT-TFS for CBT, behavioral,
humanistic, psychodynamic, and postmodernist therapy, respectively.
L-Shared covers symptoms (SCL-90), affect (PANAS), authenticity (RRO),
and session alliance (SRS); L-Spec uses BDI-II, STAI, CCT, IPO, and SFBT
indicators for the corresponding modalities. Overall is the unweighted
average of the four family scores.

### D.2 MusPsy

The 12-item WAI–Short Form uses four items each for Goal (agreement on
objectives), Task (agreement on counseling activities), and Bond (trust
and rapport), rated on a 1–5 scale; we report the three subscale means
and their unweighted average (WAI Avg). PANAS comprises ten positive and
ten negative affect items, each rated from 1 to 5; we report the mean
within each group. Higher WAI and positive PANAS and lower negative
PANAS indicate better evaluator-assessed outcomes. For MusPsy,
author-released reference scores are taken from the original paper
([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)); our evaluation averages six sessions
for all 100 test clients using the official MusPsy evaluation code and
judging prompt. For the cited CACTUS and SMILE results, we retain the
dataset authors’ separately reported WAI average, averaged across their
six published session rows.

### D.3 SummEval

Human ratings use a 1–5 scale for coherence (COH; organization across
sentences), consistency (CON; factual support from the source), fluency
(FLU; sentence-level linguistic quality), and relevance (REL; coverage
of salient information without redundancy). For each dimension,
Spearman’s $`\rho`$ between automatic and human scores is computed
within each source-document group and macro-averaged across groups; Mean
averages the four dimension-level correlations.

## Appendix E PsychEval Evaluation Protocol

The counselor-side metric labels in the original evaluation diagram
denote working alliance (WAI), professional skill execution (HTAIS),
response relevance and progression (RRO), interaction quality (Custom),
and dialogue planning and continuity (DP). These labels denote
evaluation dimensions, not additional learning modules.

PsychEval evaluates counseling across repeated interactions rather than
a single isolated response. Its five therapeutic modalities are
cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), behavioral therapy (BT),
humanistic–existential therapy (HET), psychodynamic therapy (PDT), and
postmodernist therapy (PMT). Counselor-shared and counselor-specific
scores assess the intervention, while client-shared and client-specific
scores describe the simulated client’s responses. The mean reported in
the main tables assigns equal weight to the four metric-family
aggregates.

Aggregation accounts for turns nested within sessions and sessions
nested within clients. For client $`u`$ with $`K_{u}`$ scored sessions,
let $`z_{uk}`$ be a session-level metric. The reported aggregate gives
equal weight to clients:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{z}_{\mathrm{client}}=\frac{1}{U}\sum_{u=1}^{U}\left(\frac{1}{K_{u}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{u}}z_{uk}\right).
``` |  | (13) |

Each client contributes one within-client mean, so longer courses do not
receive greater weight.

Session-wise means are computed from the scored clients at each session;
client composition can change over the course.

### F.2 Preference Learning and Adapter Changes

The archived training trace records twelve accepted adapter updates over
2,080 preference pairs. These diagnostics document parameter updates in
that trace; its correspondence to the main evaluation runs and the
accounting unit for pairs remain unspecified. All twelve updates have
finite gradients, and the recorded adapter norm changes from 418.83 to
421.14. These records establish that optimization executed and changed
the adapter parameters. Counseling quality is assessed using subsequent
session outcomes rather than inferred from a reduction in training loss.

Pair construction and pair optimization are separate operations. Once a
valid preferred–dispreferred pair is available, DPO can learn from the
preference direction without requiring a large numerical gap between
judge scores. Score compression instead affects rules that select pairs
by a fixed score threshold or multiply their losses by the score gap. In
SOCA, the weighted objective uses only the edges explicitly retained in
the acyclic graph, weighted by their bidirectional-consistency
strengths.

confirming that the internal learning signal is non-degenerate.
Preference agreement with human judgments is evaluated separately on

Figure 5: Internal self-evaluation signal diagnostics. (a) Empirical

Discounted propagation links early skill choices to later session
outcomes. Expanding the return recursion gives a weighted sum of
subsequent turn credits and the terminal reward:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
G_{t}=\sum_{j=t}^{T}\gamma^{j-t}\tilde{r}_{j}+\gamma^{T-t+1}r_{\mathrm{session}}.
``` |  | (14) |

An early action that builds rapport can therefore receive credit
associated with later progress even when its immediate score is modest.
The discount reduces the contribution of distant outcomes. This
mechanism specifies how credit is assigned to earlier actions; isolating
its effect would require a controlled intervention on the propagation
rule.

### H.7 Failure Cases and Memory Dependence

A fully tied score group has no recoverable strict order. One archived
run records 725 rewards equal to 0.5 across 51 cases. These observations
support recording variance and skip decisions instead of assigning
arbitrary preferences. They also distinguish a failure of the score
format from successful ordinal reconstruction when the inputs remain
distinguishable.

Cross-session memory can preserve both useful context and earlier
errors. If a recap incorrectly records a goal or belief, subsequent
skill selection and candidate evaluation may reinforce that mistake. A
correction from the client should therefore be incorporated into the
retained profile with its source preserved. Memory ablations should
compare absent memory, fixed summaries, and revisable summaries before
session trends are attributed to HBSP or LiPO.

## Appendix I Compute, Deployment, and Safety

The principal online overhead consists of shadow generation, local
evaluation, and adapter optimization. The instrumented training trace
records 2,080 pairs and 2,217.6 seconds of trainer time over twelve
rounds, or 36.96 minutes in total. The mean trainer time in this trace
is 3.08 minutes per round on four trainer GPUs. This trainer-only
measurement excludes dialogue serving and shadow generation and does not
establish response latency or end-to-end session time.

Let $`N_{s}`$ denote the number of additional shadow responses generated
at each of $`T`$ opportunities. Let $`C_{g}`$ be the generation cost per
shadow, $`C_{j}`$ the amortized local evaluation cost per candidate,
including pointwise scoring and pairwise comparisons, and $`C_{o}(P)`$
the optimization cost for $`P`$ pairs. If the delivered response is
evaluated together with the shadows, the incremental cost over ordinary
single-response generation is approximately

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{\mathrm{extra}}\approx T\left[N_{s}C_{g}+(N_{s}+1)C_{j}\right]+C_{o}(P).
``` |  | (16) |

Distinguishing shadow responses from the full candidate set prevents
double-counting the cost of generating the delivered response. The
instrumented configuration uses $`N_{s}=4`$ shadows plus the delivered
response, giving $`N=N_{s}+1=5`$ candidates. A saved rank-8 adapter
occupies approximately 79.8 MB, allowing the large backbone to remain
shared. Storage includes client-specific memories and posteriors,
cross-client preference evidence, and optimizer states and adapter
versions maintained for each learning scope.

Synchronous activation allows update timing to be audited but can delay
the next session. An asynchronous service could keep the previous
verified adapter active while preparing a replacement, whereas an
uncertainty-triggered service could reduce generation and training
frequency. These are deployment alternatives to the measured synchronous
configuration. Their effects on latency and the continued use of an
earlier policy version require separate measurement.

## Appendix J Update Schedule and Ordinal Properties

Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Trajectory credit. ‣ 2.4 SOCA ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")
gives the complete online update schedule. This appendix describes the
conditional properties of the ordinal representation and its learning
signal; implementation details and version records are provided in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Implementation and Reproducible Update Schedule ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time").

###### Proposition 1.

For a strictly increasing function $`f`$, $`R_{i}>R_{j}`$ if and only if
$`f(R_{i})>f(R_{j})`$. Consequently, the tie-aware ranks and tiers used
for realized-trajectory projection are invariant to a strictly
increasing transformation of the turn scores.

The proof follows directly from strict monotonicity. Clipping after
ordinal expansion preserves the mean because the implementation solves
for a common shift, although the projected distances need not remain
invariant. Quantization can also introduce ties, and a non-monotone
evaluator can reverse an ordering. The proposition concerns the
realized-trajectory representation; SOCA candidate edges are instead
admitted by the two-order consistency rule in
Eq. [7](#S2.E7 "In Candidate preferences. ‣ 2.4 SOCA ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time").

For an accepted edge, the derivative with respect to the utility
difference $`d_{ij}=h_{i}-h_{j}`$ is $`-w_{ij}\sigma(-d_{ij})`$. It is
nonzero for positive weight and finite utility difference. This property
applies in score space; the parameter gradient also depends on the model
Jacobian. Standard DPO likewise learns from preference labels and does
not require a numerical reward gap once a valid pair exists.

### K.2 Multi-evidence Aggregation Beyond Counseling

We use SummEval to evaluate a summary-ranking variant of SOCA against
human judgments ([Fabbri et al., 2021](#bib.bib21)). For summaries of
the same source document, SOCA combines pointwise scores, relative
preferences from pairwise comparisons, and accepted repair evidence.
Pointwise scores assess individual candidate quality, pairwise
comparisons assess relative quality, and repairs provide evidence of
correctable defects.

For candidate $`i`$ and quality dimension $`d`$, let $`q_{i,d}`$ be the
expected pointwise score, $`v_{i,d}`$ the mean pairwise win rate across
the two presentation orders, and $`r_{i,d}`$ the accepted-repair signal.
Within each source-document group, the fused score is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f_{i,d}=z(q_{i,d})+\alpha_{d}z(v_{i,d})-\beta_{d}z(r_{i,d}),\qquad z(x_{i})=\frac{x_{i}-\bar{x}}{\mathrm{sd}(x)+10^{-8}}.
``` |  | (17) |

The pointwise channel has unit weight; $`\alpha_{d}`$ and $`\beta_{d}`$
control the contributions of pairwise and repair evidence for dimension
$`d`$. We set $`\alpha_{d}=\beta_{d}=1`$ for every dimension a priori
and keep these coefficients fixed throughout evaluation. Equal
coefficients apply after within-document channel standardization; they
are not selected by maximizing the reported human correlations. The
pointwise expectation is $`q_{i,d}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}k\,p_{i,d}(k)`$. The
repair signal is the mean probability that the accepted revision is
preferred in the two presentation orders, and is zero when no repair is
accepted. Its negative coefficient treats an accepted improvement as
evidence of a defect in the original summary. Candidates are ranked by
decreasing $`f_{i,d}`$; a constant channel contributes zero after
standardization. SummEval measures agreement between the fused scores
and human rankings, rather than the counseling graph selector or
trajectory-credit mechanism. The mean correlation differences are
descriptive: no paired document-level uncertainty is reported, so the
observed margin is not a significance claim. In counseling, the SOCA
ablation tests the combined contribution of consistency-checked
candidate preferences and ordinal trajectory credit
(Section [2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 SOCA ‣ 2 PsyEvo ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time")).

The repair module proposes a local edit and checks its validity. The
original and repaired summaries are compared in both presentation orders
to check preference consistency. Only accepted repairs contribute to the
repair channel; pointwise and pairwise evidence remain available for
every candidate.

JDM uses the expectation of the score distribution, whereas Raw Scalar
uses its maximum-probability category. PCA evaluates relative candidate
quality through pairwise comparisons ([Liusie et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22)). The evidence-channel ablations are reported in
Table [4](#S3.T4 "Table 4 ‣ SOCA Component Ablations ‣ 3.4 Ablation Study ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ PsyEvo: A Personalized Counseling Agent That Self-Evolves at Test Time").
````

# NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routing with Node-Conditioned Option Spaces for Tool-Use Agents

Jin Han    Mingfeng Xin Affiliation: Feng Liang    Jingtao Yu
Affiliation: Independent Researcher

###### Abstract

Tool-use agents pay per decision: at each hop a language model re-reads
the state and thinks for seconds, though its actions are finite and its
decisions recur. We present NIOCIS, a routing layer that folds execution
logs into a loop-collapsed action graph—forward action transitions plus
explicit recovery back-edges—and answers one typed question: which
declared option at a state, plus stop, does the fleet take next? A small
model reads a calibrated distribution over them in one pass, generating
no text. On 1,615 production security-operations episodes we measure
what the layer replaces, returns and is bounded by. It _replaces_ the
per-decision deliberation: the typed encoder matches the logged next hop
on 32.2% of the 3,875 held-out decisions (34.9% within the declared
options) in 10 ms of encoder time (19.5 ms for the typed-choice form),
against 2.2 s per LLM hop; two feature readouts on the same decisions
reach 32.8% and 32.3%, an ordering the paired test does not resolve, so
the typed layer is chosen for cost rather than for a separated accuracy
lead. Tuning moves a language model: jpt-4b reaches 9.9% zero-shot and
31.9% fine-tuned, a gap an exact paired test does not resolve
($`p=0.68`$), at 244 ms per decision. It _returns_ little: the realised
hop falls outside the declared options for 7.9% of decisions and outside
the model’s top twenty for 9.3%, while the most confident 15% holds
91.9% accuracy, a band three different confidence rules trace alike. And
it is _bounded_: the state text binds accuracy, not the candidate count,
the deliberation budget or the parameter count; node discovery saturates
at the rate a zero-parameter occupancy null predicts, and on five public
process logs a variable-order Markov table collects most of each margin
over the majority baseline; a cross-trace leave-one-out test puts the
released state’s ceiling at 25.6–44.9% as the state key grows more
specific, repeated states disagreeing more often than they agree; and
the option-space ablation attributes the gain to per-node candidate
restriction (+3.5 points over a flat catalog) rather than graph
traversal (zero within its interval). The claim is narrow: containment
and a small high-precision band at millisecond cost; a gated
self-evolution loop lifts every arm above its anchor yet ends below full
supervision; a confidence-gated cascade _loses_ agreement when the
escalation arm is the zero-shot stand-in rather than the tuned model;
and agreement past a third of the fleet needs a state carrying the plan
and the observation, which the released records do not.

## 1 Introduction

LLM agents that call tools decide by inference. At every step of every
task the model re-derives what it already decided last week for the same
tool in the same situation: whether to search before reading, whether to
write directly or route through approval, whether to retry or roll back.
The latency of such a judgment is counted in seconds and its cost in
thinking tokens ([Cheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Li et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib24)), even though the decision’s _support_ is small—a
closed set of declared next actions, median 46 per state here—and the
deployment’s accumulated logs already contain the answer, thousands of
times over.

Three observations motivate NIOCIS. Execution logs converge. An internal
deployment’s tools and action semantics are finite; as episodes
accumulate, the action vocabulary saturates while the logs keep growing.
Process mining has exploited this regularity for business event logs for
two decades ([van der Aalst, 2016](#bib.bib53); [Leemans et al.,
2013](#bib.bib23)), and recent results compress agent-trace corpora into
finite-state machines of 7–43 states ([Cho et al., 2026](#bib.bib9));
the complexity-vs-log-size reading we cite tracks only the state-count
curve
(§[2](#S2 "2 Related Work ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
Also, typed decisions can be cheap. A decision model that answers
exactly one of its caller’s declared options—reading a calibrated
distribution from a single forward pass, with no generated text—decides
in 30–65 ms ([Cheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Ren et al.,
2026](#bib.bib45)) at accuracy within a few points of LLM judges ([Ren
et al., 2026](#bib.bib45)), and works as a first-tier agent decision
layer that escalates on low confidence ([Wu & Lim, 2026](#bib.bib60);
[Li et al., 2026c](#bib.bib28)). And logs can train their own router:
routing decisions, executions and outcomes form a data flywheel ([Liu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib30); [NeoHorse Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)),
provided the update loop is gated against collapse ([Nguyen et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib37); [Shang et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)).

NIOCIS composes these into one loop
([Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")):
(i) we _induce_ a loop-collapsed action graph from episode logs—a
directed graph of forward action transitions (self-loops aside) with
recovery back-edges carrying rollbacks, backtracks and task redirects;
(ii) we _route_ with the typed-decision router whose option set is
exactly the current node’s outgoing edges (plus stop), whose output is a
calibrated distribution the caller can threshold, and whose uncertain
cases escalate to the LLM; (iii) we _self-evolve_: each real episode
becomes training data, updates pass a rollback gate and survive only
with evidence, and any update that regresses beyond the gate tolerance
($`\delta`$) is rolled back. In the full design, executed episodes are
budget-bounded (a step budget and a per-node revisit cap;
§[3](#S3 "3 Problem Formulation ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
Our contributions are:

- •
  P1 The loop-collapsed action graph. We formalize induction from
  episode logs with explicit folding rules for loops and back-edges, and
  quantify the growth of the induced action graph (E1): transition
  discovery shows diminishing returns and loop-event rates reach a
  stable regime, while node-vocabulary convergence is _not_ offered as
  an independent finding—a zero-parameter occupancy null already
  predicts it
  (§[6](#S6 "6 Results ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
- •
  P2 Typed-decision path routing. We define next-hop selection as a
  typed decision over a node-conditioned declared option space (the
  current node’s outgoing edges, plus stop) and implement it with
  one-pass feature-MLP readouts that reach 32.8% agreement with the
  deployed fleet’s own choices (35.6% within the declared space; the
  hidden-state probe variant gives up two points of masked accuracy and
  decodes from its 10 ms encoder pass, the typed family’s full predict
  running 19.5 ms p50), together with an accept/escalate gate analyzed
  with risk–coverage curves (E2); a transition-table baseline over all
  nine decision streams (App. B) reaches 31.1% on SOC. We also measure
  what the restriction is worth (E9): the node-conditioned space beats a
  flat 155-class catalog by 3.5 points and a size-matched
  global-frequency space by 1.4 points, but is statistically
  indistinguishable from a node-conditioned space built without graph
  traversal, so we claim the per-node restriction, not graph traversal.
  We therefore claim the formalization and the measurement rather than
  the traversal: the contribution is to define the next-hop choice as a
  typed decision over a node-conditioned declared option space, and to
  bound what that construction can reach on production logs, which
  [Section 7](#S7 "7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
  does quantitatively.
- •
  P3 A gated self-evolution loop, with route replay as a measurement. We
  replay held-out episodes against a re-prompted MIMO Pro V2.6 stand-in
  (one reasoning call per hop for the fleet’s LLM component: no
  per-stage orchestration, no tool results;
  §[5](#S5 "5 Experimental Setup ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents"));
  the route view renders the per-decision quantities—strict survival,
  plus a wrong-hop cost rescaling of the mismatch count—so we report it
  as a measurement on that graph
  (§[6](#S6 "6 Results ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")),
  and add the one route quantity that is not such a rescaling—the
  first-divergence hop index and type (median hop 2, most first
  divergences on escalated hops)—reading the replay as placing the cheap
  readout in the agreement/cost corner and the accept/escalate hybrid
  between its parents (the readout and the opponent; E3); and we
  exercise a self-evolution loop whose rollback gate scores every
  candidate while the loop absorbs a 12%-of-one-batch label-corruption
  stress, the gate refusing 13 of the 40 candidate updates, in 6 of the
  10 seeded chains (E4).

The core experiments (E1–E4) run on real execution logs from a
production security-operations agent fleet
(§[5](#S5 "5 Experimental Setup ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
states provenance); E5 replicates the constructions on public event logs
and open tool-use trajectories, and any Langfuse-style observations
export folds into the same episode schema.

## 2 Related Work

#### Workflow and skill induction from agent logs.

Agent Workflow Memory ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib58)) induces reusable
routines from trajectories and feeds them back as context; task-model
induction ([Jiang et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)) builds hierarchical
goal/procedure models from computer-use traces; automata induction
compresses trace corpora into compact finite-state machines for
next-step prediction and failure detection ([Cho et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9)); the literature-search agent _PaperPilot_ induces
editable search-operator directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) ([Li et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib26)). Skill-graph systems organize experience for
_retrieval_: HiSkill’s hierarchical skill graphs ([Hao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)), SE-GoS’s self-evolving graph-of-skills ([Fu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13)), CaSKG’s counterfactual-causal skill graphs ([Li
et al., 2026d](#bib.bib29)), GraphSkillAA’s validated skill-graph
updates ([Shang et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)), GraphSkillEvo’s evolutionary
graph skills ([Sun et al., 2026a](#bib.bib48)), and skill distillation
from browsing traces ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib62)). M2-PALE applies
process mining to agent traces for *explanation* ([Qian et al.,
2026](#bib.bib43)); AgentHER recycles trajectories into training
data ([Ding, 2026](#bib.bib11)). Every workflow and skill system
surveyed here uses the induced structure for retrieval, prompting,
explanation or training (the automata-induction uses of the same
structure are treated next). Automata-based next-step prediction and
failure detection over induced machines ([Cho et al., 2026](#bib.bib9))
are the closest runtime uses: both score a fixed label vocabulary from
trace prefixes (held-out scoring offline against the induced machine;
its failure ranker also runs as an online monitor over partial traces)
rather than binding the induced graph’s edges as declared options.
Interleaved reason-and-act control loops keep an LLM in the loop at
every step ([Yao et al., 2023](#bib.bib63)), self-reflective retry feeds
verbal feedback back into the next attempt ([Shinn et al.,
2023](#bib.bib47)), and open-ended agents grow executable skill
libraries as text ([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib57)); we instead bind the
induced graph as the decision space of a single-pass readout, with no
per-step LLM call. None of the surveyed systems makes the induced graph
the _runtime decision space_ of a millisecond-scale model, and none
reports route-level divergence accounting (episodic survival and
wrong-hop cost) for routes over an induced graph on production agent
logs (the accounting scores realized routes, not a model’s conformance
fitness [van der Aalst, 2016](#bib.bib53); alignment-based conformance
checking instead replays each trace against a discovered model and
scores the cheapest replay ([Adriansyah et al., 2011](#bib.bib1);
[Carmona et al., 2018](#bib.bib5))). Read as a discovered model the
induced graph is a permissive one—on held-out traces, token-level replay
fitness $`0.91`$, trace-level $`0.59`$, escaping-edge precision $`0.02`$
(each state enables its whole declared option set), edge-level
generalization $`0.27`$ (App. B)—which is why our comparison is against
option-space constructions, leaving process-discovery models aside.
[Paul & Nandy (2026)](#bib.bib40) study cost-optimal multi-step
model/tool routing (a different decision axis).

#### Process mining.

Discovery of process models from event logs is a mature field ([van der
Aalst, 2016](#bib.bib53)); constructive miners capture loop and
concurrency structure ([Leemans et al., 2013](#bib.bib23); [Kourani
et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)), and monotonicity characterizations control
candidate-place growth ([Tacke genannt Unterberg et al.,
2026](#bib.bib51)). The complexity-vs-log-size reading we cite reports
stable small automata (7–43 states on public corpora, [Cho et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9)); it carries no loop-event or transition-pair axis. E1
adds those axes on a production security-operations-center (SOC) corpus,
with subsample-median bands, and measures the loop structure directly. A
parallel line, predictive process monitoring, answers the next-activity
question by scoring a fixed activity vocabulary from prefixes ([Tax
et al., 2017](#bib.bib52)); its standard n-gram realization is the
backoff-Markov baseline of E5 (re-gated here onto the shared declared
option sets).

#### Typed-decision models and LLM routing.

Typed-decision models answer exactly one of their caller’s declared
options from a single forward pass, with calibrated probabilities and no
generated text: this-that-model-1.0 reads answers from hidden state at
30.9 ms ([Cheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)); the one-pass judge we label
_JevLite_ (small one-pass model of [Ren et al. (2026)](#bib.bib45))
reads temperature-scaled answer-label logits at 64.5 ms per decision on
one consumer GPU. As judges they accept when confident and escalate when
unsure ([Li et al., 2026c](#bib.bib28)); as agent control layers they
cut strong-model calls 72.7% at 95% task success against a strong-only
baseline of 88% on the same frozen benchmark ([Wu & Lim,
2026](#bib.bib60)); at the edge they cut decision latency and API
fees ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib24)), and with an LLM planner they
close fast control loops ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib33)). Unlike these
flat typed-control layers’ static action-list option sets, NIOCIS’s
option set is the outgoing edge set of an _induced_ action graph; NIOCIS
also performs route-level divergence accounting over that graph,
quantifies its node-vocabulary convergence, and exercises a gated
self-evolution loop, none of which flat typed-control layers do. Guided
or grammar-constrained decoding also restricts an output space, but it
filters a generator step by step ([Geng et al., 2023](#bib.bib15)); the
typed readout instead emits one distribution over the declared options
in a single pass. Constrained decision heads are sensitive to option
labeling ([Sun et al., 2026b](#bib.bib49)), which motivates our edge
identifiers. Model/tool routers choose _which solver_ runs ([Chen
et al., 2023](#bib.bib7); [Ong et al., 2024](#bib.bib38); [Paul & Nandy,
2026](#bib.bib40); [Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib30); [Bala & Shah,
2026](#bib.bib2)) (or generate retrieval configurations [Pan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib39)), not which graph edge advances a task;
preference-trained small routers have cut cost by over $`2\times`$ at
matched quality in some evaluated settings ([Ong et al.,
2024](#bib.bib38)); typed artifacts share tool-routing knowledge across
agents ([Chakraborty et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)); and router
infrastructure now spans development, evaluation and deployment, where
lightweight routers prove more competitive under tight-cost
constraints ([Feng et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)), though routing accuracy
plateaus below the oracle ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)). Amortized
planning replaces search with direct action inference in world
models ([Nguyen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib36)); we amortize _workflow-path_
decisions into a declared-option readout over an induced action graph.

#### Self-evolving agents.

Routing decisions and executions can train their own successors ([Liu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib30); [NeoHorse Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)) as
long as the loop is gated: unguarded self-evolution can collapse or
suffer security drift ([Nguyen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib37); [Li & Zhu,
2026](#bib.bib25)), while validated updates with rollback keep graphs
healthy ([Shang et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)) and recursive-in-recursive
designs keep harness and weights co-evolving ([Xue et al.,
2026](#bib.bib61)). Our E4 adds a minimal complete instance—accumulate,
gate on held-out performance, roll back on regression—and deploys it
under label corruption that the gated loop absorbs on average (the
refusal path refuses 13 of the 40 candidate updates, in 6 of the 10
seeded chains).

## 3 Problem Formulation

#### Episode logs.

A log is a set of episodes. An episode is a sequence of steps
$`e=\langle s_{1},\dots,s_{T}\rangle`$ where each step $`s_{t}`$ records
an action label $`L(s_{t})\in\mathcal{V}`$ (an action semantic such as
db.write), a step kind (advance, retry or back: a back step revisits an
earlier label, opening a return edge in $`E^{-}`$) and a context vector
$`\mathbf{c}\in[0,1]^{d}`$ (full-design schema: urgency, dependencies,
authorization, complexity, …). Episodes carry a start, a goal—in the
full design a node $`g\in\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{V}`$—and an
outcome.

#### Loop-collapsed action graph.

Straight traces understate structure; agents loop. Folding an episode
yields a skeleton—retry runs folded as temporally extended steps in the
sense of [Sutton et al. (1999)](#bib.bib50)—to which the step’s label
$`L(s_{t})`$ is appended except in two step kinds: (a) an immediate
retry of the last label kept in the skeleton (the retry is dropped), and
(b) a revisit of an earlier-visited label (the step is appended as usual
and a new kept frame—a skeleton entry—opens at the revisit). Both kinds
are recorded as _loop events_ (_retry_ for (a), _back_ for (b)); a
retry—which includes every continuation of a same-label run—is the only
kind left out of the skeleton. Folding the 22,192 logged tool calls thus
drops exactly the 4,124 retries and keeps 15,704 forward advances plus
2,364 recovery returns (18,068 decision states); E1’s event rates
reflect this disposition. Observed kept-frame transition pairs
($`E^{+}\cup E^{-}`$) over all episodes define the transition volume
reported in E1; first-visit forward transfers give $`E^{+}`$ and
executed returns to earlier labels give _recovery back-edges_ $`E^{-}`$
(this corpus-census set is computed over all folds and persisted with
the corpus for characterization only; the operational option graph is
induced from the training fold,
§[4](#S4 "4 The NIOCIS Approach ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")),
while same-label repeats surface only as _retry_ events, as described
above. The two roles a pair can play—forward transfer and recovery
return—overlap on real traces: of the 2,536 distinct observed pairs,
2,315 appear as first-visit forward transfers and 702 as recovery
returns (481 pairs play both roles—forward in one context, return in
another—while 221 pairs are exclusively returns; App. A gives the fold
breakdown). The loop-collapsed action graph is
$`G=(V,E^{+}\cup E^{-})`$. The observed forward set is not a DAG: 1,272
of its 2,315 pairs fall into 636 mutual couples ($`u{\to}v`$ and
$`v{\to}u`$ between nodes $`u`$ and $`v`$, both arcs observed as forward
transfers), so forward transfers alone carry directed 2-cycles. What
planning needs still holds: all edge weights are strictly positive, so
an optimal walk to a reachable goal can always be chosen simple (no
repeated node), even though execution may loop. Complexity is measurable
two ways—folded (semantic nodes and transitions) and occurrence-tagged
(per-kind step occurrences)—and _loop mass_ is an occurrence-tagged
quantity: the loop-event share of executed steps,
$`100(\mathrm{retry}+\mathrm{back})/\mathrm{steps}`$ (29.2% of the SOC
corpus’s steps).

#### Next-hop decision problem and route-level criterion.

Let $`\pi(a\mid v,g,\mathbf{c})`$ be a router at the current node $`v`$
over the declared option set
$`A(v)=\mathrm{succ}(v)\cup\{\texttt{stop}\}`$ (options identified with
their edges). The reference next hop is the deployed fleet’s logged
choice at the same realized state; the full design substitutes the next
hop of a context-weighted shortest-path walk to the goal $`g`$ for that
logged choice (a guard fact $`(a,\eta)`$ pairs an action $`a`$ with a
context precondition over slot $`\eta`$, say an authorization level; a
failed fact multiplies the cost $`w_{e}`$ of the edge labeled $`a`$ by a
penalty factor $`\gamma>1`$, so a walk whose context is at a low
authorization level is routed through an approval detour). Per step we
report top-1 agreement with the reference. At the route level we report
_strict survival_—an episode’s fidelity to this one realized reference
trace, where survival requires every one of its decisions to match (a
trajectory-fidelity reading, separate from any fleet-level safety or
availability property)—and the _wrong-hop gap_, the accumulated cost of
diverging hops over the whole teacher-forced episode under per-hop
conventions $`c_{1}=1`$ and $`c_{3}=3`$; clipping that accounting at the
first divergence would understate the cost of long episodes, so the
first-divergence (strict-survival) view and the unclipped wrong-hop gap
are reported side by side. Under this teacher-forced accounting the gap
is a fixed rescaling of the per-decision mismatch
count—$`(\#\text{decisions}/\#\text{episodes})\,(1-\text{agreement})`$—so
its extra information over per-decision agreement is the episode-length
weighting; strict survival is the genuinely route-level measure.

#### Typed decisions and gated escalation.

The full typed design’s output is a distribution over $`A(v)`$ and
nothing else: options are structurally identified (stable edge
identifiers, never surface names, in the full design), the answer is
read from one forward pass (as in [Cheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Ren
et al., 2026](#bib.bib45)), and malformed outputs are unrepresentable
(the corpus readout emits over the global vocab then masks to $`A(v)`$,
cf. A3). A confidence gate accepts at threshold $`\tau`$ when
$`\max_{a}\pi(a\mid\cdot)\geq\tau`$ and otherwise escalates to the LLM,
mirroring accept/judge cascades ([Li et al., 2026c](#bib.bib28); [Wu &
Lim, 2026](#bib.bib60)). Since a valid output is not a guarantee of
progress, the full design runs execution under a step budget $`B`$ and a
per-node revisit cap $`R`$, terminating the episode (failure) on either
violation. The corpus study of
§[5](#S5 "5 Experimental Setup ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
instead replays logged episodes teacher-forced and so exercises neither
bound.

## 4 The NIOCIS Approach

### 4.1 Graph induction and loop folding

Folding follows the two rules of
§[3](#S3 "3 Problem Formulation ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents");
the induction pass book-keeps skeleton transitions and loop events
incrementally per episode. Recovery back-edges discovered during
execution—rollbacks, backtracks and task redirects—are recorded
alongside the corpus; the
§[5](#S5 "5 Experimental Setup ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
census over them ($`E^{+}`$, $`E^{-}`$) is computed over all folds and
is purely descriptive. The operational option graph, by contrast, is
induced from the training fold alone and is one shared object: routers
and rollouts see the same train-induced option graph at training and at
evaluation time, with no held-out edge ever entering the runtime
candidate set (see Option supply below). We deliberately do not merge
near-synonymous actions—semantic action labels are the unit of decision,
matching how typed decisions declare their options ([Cheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8)). Because option surfaces are load-bearing for
constrained heads ([Sun et al., 2026b](#bib.bib49)), edge options are
rendered over stable internal identifiers rather than surface names, in
the full design. (_Option supply._ In the corpus realization the
declared option set at $`v`$ is the train-split fold’s kept-frame
transitions out of $`v`$, first-visit advances and recovery returns
alike, plus stop—graph induction and routing share one graph, so the
option space carries the induction’s misses, 7.9% of test targets
falling outside
it—§[5](#S5 "5 Experimental Setup ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents");
[Figure 3](#S4.F3 "In 4.3 Gated self-evolution (RSI) ‣ 4 The NIOCIS Approach ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")a
reports the observed-transition volume this induction processes.)

### 4.2 Typed-decision readout router

We call the model the typed-decision router; its scoring head is the
_readout_. The router answers one question per state: _given the current
node $`v`$, goal $`g`$ and context $`\mathbf{c}`$, which edge in
$`A(v)`$ leads to task completion?_ ($`A(v)`$ includes stop as a
non-edge option.) In the corpus realization $`g`$ is the episode’s
agent-stage identity—constant within an episode, hardwired to 0 on the
E5 streams—and $`\mathbf{c}`$ is structural (prefix shape, visit
counters, per-node neighbor statistics); the guard-aware $`\mathbf{c}`$
of the full design feeds only the substituted context-weighted walk.
Options are the declared outgoing edges, bound to stable single-token
identifiers: target-action classes over the corpus’s 154-name tool
catalog (plus stop) in the class-space readout, or edge identifiers of
the induced graph in the edge-ID readout
(§[6](#S6 "6 Results ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
compares both); in the hidden-state arms the answer distribution is read
from the hidden state at a designated position (as in [Cheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8); [Ren et al., 2026](#bib.bib45))—equivalently, a
softmax over the declared label logits (temperature-scaled only in our
zero-shot arm)—while the feature arms parameterize the same softmax over
observable features (the edge-ID readout space applies to both
backbones); nothing is generated. Training minimizes proper-scoring
losses over the full vocabulary softmax on (state, goal, context,
next-hop) tuples copied from held-in logged transitions—behavioral
cloning ([Pomerleau, 1991](#bib.bib42)) of the deployed fleet
(out-of-space targets stay in the objective; option masking enters only
at the masked-renormalized (masked-renorm) gate at decode); the full
design replaces the logged next hop with planner-oracle labels—the hop
of a context-weighted shortest-path walk to the goal—and uses the
walk-membership and episode-success questions as the oracle’s post-hoc
reward signal. Calibration is reported as expected calibration error
(ECE-15, the 15-bin convention; temperature-fitted [Guo et al.,
2017](#bib.bib16) for the zero-shot arm, raw masked-renorm confidence
for all trained arms, over the probe and feature backbones and their
class-space and edge-ID variants). Plain cross-entropy (CE) and the
CE-plus-Brier (CE$`+`$Brier) objective ([Ren et al., 2026](#bib.bib45))
are compared head-to-head on both readout families: a multilayer
perceptron over observable features (the feature MLP; node, goal,
context, visit counters, per-node neighbor statistics) and a
hidden-state probe on the Laya encoder (421M parameters; essentially the
same construction as the designated-position hidden-state readout of
[Cheng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib8)). Typed decodes of the probe-family
heads of [Cheng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib8) and [Ren et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib45) run 30–65 ms at batch-1. Deployment is a two-tier
decision stack: the readout handles control decisions (which edge); an
executor synthesizes arguments; free-form generation stays in the LLM
(as in [Wu & Lim, 2026](#bib.bib60)).

### 4.3 Gated self-evolution (RSI)

We use the term recursive self-improvement (RSI) narrowly for the
accumulate–gate–commit loop below, not for weight-space
self-modification. Decision states of executed walks are labeled with
the reference next hop—alternatively the full design’s planner-oracle
labels—and appended to a replay buffer; each round trains a candidate
router on the accumulated buffer. An update is committed only if
held-out validation performance does not regress beyond $`\delta=0.005`$
(half an accuracy point); otherwise the candidate is discarded and the
incumbent kept. On the 371–380-row validation-gate fold (380 where the
stream split is row-granular; App. A), a 0.5-point bar is about 1.9
rows, comparable to the fold’s own single-row resolution of 0.26–0.27
points; this $`\delta`$ is thus a coarse safety threshold rather than a
confidence-interval-backed test. The ten-chain study’s paired gate-score
differences make that resolution explicit. Twelve of its 13 refusals
regress the gate fold by 3 or more rows; a single 2-row refusal sits
exactly at $`\delta`$’s own granularity (the finest distinction a
380-row gate set can support is one row); and 6 of the 27 accepted
updates carry a gate-score dip worth one row that $`\delta`$ accepts by
construction. We therefore report the 13/40 refusal count as a coarse
operational safety readout, not a high-resolution statistical test of
regression (validation-gated update with rollback, cf. [Shang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib46); held-out selection as in [Nguyen et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib37)). E4 exercises the loop under 12% label corruption in
one round: on the plotted seed-0 chain the gate score never regresses
and no rollback fires there—so the guardrail is deployed and measured;
across ten seeded chains its refusal path fires 13 times in 40 attempts
(App. A).

#### Replay protocol (held-out logs as the environment).

The production logs _are_ the environment: held-out episodes are
replayed decision-by-decision and every policy faces the same reference
behavior—the deployed fleet’s own choices at each realized state. Per
decision the policy sees the declared option set (the current label’s
outgoing edges in the train-induced graph, plus stop) and a prefix
rendering that differs by arm. The feature arm summarizes the folded
kept prefix, while the probe and zero-shot state texts render an
eight-entry window of the raw label list, cut at the kept frame’s fold
ordinal (its rank among kept frames), which serves as the raw-list
index. Each entry is rendered by its name tail (the substring after the
last \_\_, or the whole entry when it contains none), so distinct
catalog actions can collide (two search actions surface identically).
The zero-shot arm’s option criteria render the same tails with
underscores as spaces, barring the \<stop\> criterion whose text reads
conclude; colliding options then present identical choice texts. The
window runs behind the current label by exactly the retries preceding
the frame (the retries stay in the list; on such rows the typed arms
never see the current label), while the LLM prompt renders the same
window with full labels (not name tails) and appends the current label
explicitly. Scoring is teacher-forced against the logged next hop;
episodes are scored two ways—strict survival (an episode terminates at
its first divergence) and the wrong-hop gap accumulated over the whole
episode (cost conventions 1 and 3 per wrong hop are both reported
throughout). Because the reference is a realized trajectory rather than
an oracle, task success inherits the derived outcome labels above, and
the _declared option space is itself scored_: 7.9% of test targets lie
outside the train-declared options (declared-space coverage 92.1%, or
91.3% when recovery edges are restricted to states the running prefix
has already visited—29 rows, every one of them a return to an unvisited
state, with the mean space falling from 40.2 to 39.2 options and the
verified arms’ masked accuracy moving by at most 0.1 points; of those
307 out-of-space targets, 274 are SOC-service names and 33 are tool
names outside the catalog), an arithmetic upper bound no readout inside
the declared space can exceed—we report accuracy both overall and within
the declared space. The carve is deterministic and trace-disjoint: every
trace hashes to train/validation/test at 70/10/20 (first 8 hex digits of
its sha256, mod 100), so every train-versus-held-out claim below except
the E4 gate comparisons refers to episodes whose traces no fit saw (the
self-evolution stream’s finer split of the validation fold is
decision-row-granular: rows, not traces, are split, a leakage risk
disclosed here and now measured—re-cutting the stream and the gate at
whole-trace granularity moves held-out accuracy by under 0.1 points and
leaves every E4 conclusion in place).

#### Baselines and arms.

E2 compares a per-node majority arm (raw per-node argmax) against
multinomial logistic regression and typed readouts trained on one
observable-state feature set (node, goal, prefix statistics, visit
counters, per-node neighbor statistics); the typed readouts are trained
with cross-entropy versus the CE-plus-Brier variant (5 training seeds
each); in the edge-ID variant the readout space is the induced graph’s
edge identifiers rather than target-action classes. Two class-space
readouts use Laya: zero-shot typed choice over each state’s declared
options, and a frozen-encoder hidden-state probe—a linear head on the
encoder’s designated readout position—trained with the same CE and
CE-plus-Brier objectives. E3 compares: a re-prompted MIMO Pro V2.6
stand-in over the reconstructed state (MIMO Pro V2.6, a production
reasoning model at our serving gateway—one call per decision, without
the fleet’s per-stage orchestration and without tool results—not the
deployment’s status quo) deciding every hop from the same reconstructed
state and options (under the richer full-label prompt rendering of the
replay protocol); typed readouts acting greedily within the masked
option set; and typed with accept/escalate at
$`\tau\in\{0.55,0.75,0.9\}`$, which routes low-confidence states to MIMO
Pro V2.6. Readouts are named after their table rows: _feature MLP_
scores engine-derived stream statistics, _hidden-state probe_ is a probe
fit on Laya’s frozen hidden states, and _typed encoder_ is the Laya
backbone itself scoring the declared options.

#### Metrics and protocol.

Per decision: top-1 agreement with the logged hop, masked-renormalized
over the declared options for the trained arms, reported overall and
within the declared space (for the baseline rows the displayed cells are
raw top-1; their option-masked variant merely gates the raw argmax on
landing in the option set—no renormalization); calibration (ECE-15, per
the
§[4](#S4 "4 The NIOCIS Approach ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
convention; it is computed on the 3,568 in-space rows for the zero-shot
arm—a typed-choice head gives no score to an out-of-space target—and on
all 3,875 rows with out-of-space targets counted wrong for the other
arms, whose full-vocabulary heads still define a masked-renorm
confidence there). Risk–coverage curves ([Geifman & El-Yaniv,
2017](#bib.bib14)) and the area under the risk–coverage curve (AURC) for
the accept/escalate gate complete the per-decision set. Per episode:
strict survival (no divergence before the log ends), LLM calls per
decision, and the wrong-hop gap accumulated over the whole
teacher-forced episode under both per-hop cost conventions (1 and 3).
Difficulty profile: domain (agent-stage) slices and local branch-factor
buckets ($`\leq`$3, 4–10, 11+: the train-graph out-degree at the current
node, stop options excluded). Uncertainty: 5 training seeds for the
readout arms (mean $`\pm`$ std); the E3 replay and the E4 refit chain
are single-seed (seed 0) sequences (E4’s ten-chain stress study is
aggregated at the unit of one refit chain: 30.3% and 31.0% are
across-chain means of start and end held-out accuracy, $`\pm 0.5`$
points—one standard deviation across the ten chains’ end accuracies);
service and judge latencies are in-vivo wall-clock (p50/p90), the
zero-shot arm’s runtime in-vivo CPU (App. B), typed-model timings
batch-1 GPU wall clock (App. B). Each of E1–E4 is annotated with the
claim it tests.

## 6 Results

#### E1: the induced action graph’s node vocabulary converges (its edge set runs a long tail; claim P1).

[Figure 3](#S4.F3 "In 4.3 Gated self-evolution (RSI) ‣ 4 The NIOCIS Approach ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
provides quantified saturation curves on real production traces, adding
the transition-pair long tail and the loop-event mass to the usual
state-count view. Across 20 without-replacement subsamples per size
(medians quoted here and in

the semantic node vocabulary saturates fast: 77 action labels appear in

corpus. Transition discovery is the honest counterpoint—observed
transition pairs keep a long tail (336 at 50 episodes $`\to`$ 1,267 at
400 $`\to`$ 2,027 at 1,000 $`\to`$ 2,536 at 1,615), and the recovery
side never closes either: 702 pairs appear at recovery returns, 481 of
them doubling as forward transfers in other contexts
(§[3](#S3 "3 Problem Formulation ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
Loop mass is heavy and stable: 29.2% of executed steps fold into loop
events (4,124 retry—63.6% of loop events, in 2,164 bursts—and 2,364 back
returns—36.4%, 146 per 100 episodes; revisit distances concentrate at
1–3 steps behind the current frame, with 681 taking the immediate-repeat
distance of one). Two consequences: the router’s _label space_ closes
early—the stability pattern of [Cho et al. (2026)](#bib.bib9) recurs in
the node curve (qualitatively, a small saturating node set of 77 to 154

now alongside the transition-pair and loop-mass axes—while per-node
option sets keep thickening. The routing task on real traces is
therefore bounded-vocabulary but long-tailed option selection: exactly
the regime where a masked, calibrated typed readout constrains the
damage of open-vocabulary guessing.

readout is fit on 12,613 logged reference decisions while the stand-in

replies, 1.7%, fail to parse into a valid tool choice and score 0
against the fleet’s logged next hop), so 9.6% is a transfer-floor number
for a stripped stand-in, not the deployed component’s own capability
ceiling. Tuning is the difference: jpt-4b, LoRA-tuned for one epoch on
the training fold, reaches 31.9% against the typed encoder’s 32.2% on
the same held-out decisions, a gap an exact paired test does not resolve
($`p=0.68`$, 229 decisions the tuned model gets and the typed encoder
misses, 239 the other way), at 244 ms per decision against 10 ms
([Table 13](#S7.T13 "In E23g: agreement tracks task outcome, modestly. ‣ 7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
The hybrid result is a negative one for confidence-gated cascade
calibration on this stream. The gate routes roughly four of every five
hops to the LLM (3.7–4.3 of every five hops at 74.1/80.2/85.0% for the
three $`\tau`$ thresholds) and inherits the opponent’s low agreement on
those hops, so rather than beating both parents on their complementary
states it sits strictly between its parents—an escalation rule needs
per-state competence, not just confidence, before it can pay for itself
here.

#### Threshold scale, and where routes first diverge.

The raw thresholds above are not scale-free: $`p_{\max}`$ lives on a
per-node simplex whose dimension is the option count, and the threshold
that matches a given escalation rate correlates with $`1/|A(v)|`$ at
Pearson $`r=0.52`$. Re-running the sweep on an excess-over-uniform
scale, an entropy-normalized scale, the top-1/top-2 margin, and a
threshold-free rule that escalates the least-confident fraction, at
_matched_ escalation rates, leaves the reading unchanged: at 74.1%
escalation the hybrid reaches 0.2085 (raw) / 0.2263 (excess over
uniform) / 0.2098 (entropy-normalized) / 0.2101 (margin) / 0.2085 (rank
only) against 0.3061 for the pure hidden-state-probe row, so the
cascade’s loss is not an artifact of an uncalibrated threshold and no
tested scale closes more than 1.8 of the 9.8-point gap. Where a route
dies is a quantity the per-decision mismatches do not carry: of the 343
episodes that diverge at all under the hidden-state probe, 60 diverge at
the first hop and 185 at the second (median 2)—routes are lost on the
way in; 331 of those first divergences sit at states with $`11+`$
declared options, 12 at out-of-space targets, and 258 fall on hops the
hybrid had escalated.

## 7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot

Earlier sections score one realised next hop. This section asks a
narrower question with a deployment consequence. Which part of that
decision can a small trained model take over, and which part needs an
agent that can see more of the episode than the released state exposes?
Every number below is read from the held-out fold or from an arm we ran
for this section, and each table names the artefact its columns come
from.

#### E23a: scale does not move the zero-shot reading.

jpt-4b, a 4B general-purpose checkpoint asked to choose among the
declared options, reaches 9.0% agreement (9.9% within the declared
space) on a seeded 200-decision sample. On the typed model’s
twenty-candidate shortlist it reaches 9.5%, or 10.5% in space
([Tables 9](#S7.T9 "In E23g: agreement tracks task outcome, modestly. ‣ 7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
and [13](#S7.T13 "Table 13 ‣ E23g: agreement tracks task outcome, modestly. ‣ 7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
The domain encoder reaches 8.6% zero-shot on the full fold. The
diagnostic is less flattering. Its accuracy equals the sample’s
majority-class rate, and it picks the candidate printed first 22% of the
time against a 5% uniform rate. Order was shuffled per decision, so the
bias cannot pass for competence. On the same 200 decisions the
fine-tuned encoder reaches 31.0%. Tuning is what separates the two
families here, so

adds the tuned reading on the full fold.

#### E23b: fine-tuning moves it, and fine-tuning is what moves it.

Freezing the encoder and training the decision head alone gives 27.7%
(30.1% in space). Unfreezing the encoder for three epochs gives 32.2%
(34.9%). The 4.5-point difference is attributable. The two arms share
the renderer, the option set, the optimiser and the epoch budget, and
differ only in which parameters receive gradients. The gradient path is
the whole effect. The fine-tuned arm sits 0.65 points below the
strongest feature readout in
[Table 2](#S6.T2 "In E2: the router reaches top-1 agreement with the fleet (32.8%) and decides in milliseconds (claim P2). ‣ 6 Results ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
(32.8% and 35.6%). The typed interface reaches parity with the
hand-built feature arm. Three numbers of similar size appear in this
paper from three protocols rather than one quantity. 32.8% is the
strongest hand-built feature readout, 32.2% the fine-tuned typed encoder
measured here, and 32.15% a full-supervision run under the loop protocol
of
[Section 8](#S8 "8 What a gated loop adds over full supervision ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents").
All three score the same frozen held-out fold.

#### E23c: narrowing the option set does not substitute for state.

The fine-tuned model ranks the realised next hop inside its top twenty
for 90.7% of the fold. Narrowing the candidate set does not hand the
decision to a general model. On a random 800-decision subset jpt-4b
scores 9.9% over all declared options, 12.1% over twenty candidates and
19.9% over five, while the typed encoder reaches 32.2% over all options
on the full fold
([Tables 9](#S7.T9 "In E23g: agreement tracks task outcome, modestly. ‣ 7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
and [13](#S7.T13 "Table 13 ‣ E23g: agreement tracks task outcome, modestly. ‣ 7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
A shortlist that contains the answer almost nine times in ten does not
let a zero-shot general model find it. Tuning the same checkpoint on the
same protocol and fold closes almost all of that gap. It reaches 31.9%
against the typed encoder’s 32.2% on the full held-out fold, a
difference of 0.25 points. An exact paired test over the same 3,875
decisions does not resolve it: 229 decisions the tuned model gets and
the typed encoder misses, 239 the other way, $`p=0.68`$. On the shared
800 decisions its agreement rises from 9.9% to 33.0% ($`p<10^{-3}`$).
What the typed encoder keeps is cost, 10 ms against 0.24 s per decision,
and a tenfold smaller parameter count. The accuracy advantage over a
language model belongs to tuning, not to the interface. What binds is
the state text, not the number of candidates.

#### E23d: task intent helps by about one point; more context does not help at all.

Three arms share one training recipe, one seed and one batch size, and
differ only in the state string
([Table 10](#S7.T10 "In E23g: agreement tracks task outcome, modestly. ‣ 7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
Adding the decision’s own goal field, a six-valued enum, lifts agreement
by 1.08 points, from 32.00% to 33.08%. Replacing the eight-action window
with the full action prefix, while keeping the goal, drops it by 1.39
points. Length is not the lever. The released records carry the goal
field and the published renderer never exposes it, so this increment is
available today at no extra cost. Both increments are single-run point
estimates from one harness at one seed, and the 1.08-point gain sits
inside the 1.9-point cross-run spread reported in
[Section 8](#S8 "8 What a gated loop adds over full supervision ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents").
The larger claim, that a plan or playbook in the state would lift the
in-graph decision far higher, is the interface change this paper cannot
yet measure.

#### E23e: the released state carries a measurable ceiling.

To bound what any state-conditioned predictor can reach here, we grouped
held-out decisions by an increasingly specific key
([Table 11](#S7.T11 "In E23g: agreement tracks task outcome, modestly. ‣ 7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
Each group kept only decisions from other traces, and we asked how often
a decision agrees with the modal choice of its group. The construction
is leave-one-out and cross-trace, so a trace never votes for itself. The
reading rises with the key and then flattens: 25.6% for the station
alone, 26.5% with one preceding action, 38.6% with three, 42.8% with the
full released prefix. Requiring at least two other-trace observations
raises the last figure to 44.9%, but over a shrinking support: the group
behind 25.6% holds all 3,857 decisions, while the group behind 44.9%
holds only the 1,234 (31.8%) whose key recurs in another trace, and the
levels are therefore not scored over a common denominator. With
everything the release exposes, repeated states still disagree more
often than they agree. Agreement with a single realised sample cannot
approach one on this fixture. No confidence rule can lift the accepted
subset to 90% at any coverage, and no model size changes that: the
constraint is the state, not the capacity.

#### E23f: two questions, two operating points.

Containment and agreement must not be conflated. For containment the
shortlist reaches 90.7% at twenty candidates. The residual 9.3% needs an
agent holding the full episode. The share of held-out targets that fall
outside the declared options altogether is 7.9%. Both figures sit under
the 20% escalation budget a deployed cascade can afford. For agreement,
we sorted decisions by top-1 probability and kept the most confident
subset

## 8 What a gated loop adds over full supervision

[Section 7](#S7 "7 What the typed interface can replace, and what it cannot ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
bounded a single decision. The deployment question is whether a loop can
raise that bound on its own, by turning the graph’s own unlabelled
traffic into training rows. This section runs the loop and reports what
it does, including where it fails.

#### E25a: protocol.

We split the training fold by trace into an anchor set of 3,071
decisions and an unlabelled pool of 9,542, and froze the test fold. Each
round draws 600 pool rows, admits some of them by the arm’s own rule,
retrains the typed encoder for two epochs on the buffer and re-scores
the frozen fold. The gate sees no label for the row it admits. It
compares the current model’s argmax against a variable-order backoff
table built only from buffer rows and admits the row when the two agree.
A random arm admits a fixed number of rows per round, and a third arm
runs the same accounting with no typed model at all, using that
variable-order backoff table itself as the predictor, a Markov-style
control
(Table [14](#S8.T14 "Table 14 ‣ E25e: what a deployment should do with this. ‣ 8 What a gated loop adds over full supervision ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).

#### E25b: the loop moves, and it stops below supervised training.

Every typed arm ends above its own anchor-only starting point. The peaks
come before the last round: at seed 0 the gate peaks at 30.48% in round
4 and ends at 28.80%, averaging 29.39% over three seeds. The induced
graph grows from 1,015 to 1,257 edges
(Figure [9](#S8.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ E25e: what a deployment should do with this. ‣ 8 What a gated loop adds over full supervision ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
Full supervised training on the same fold reaches 32.15%. No arm closes
that gap. The high-precision band also stays at half its width: accepted
accuracy holds above 90% up to 6.8% of coverage under the gate, against
15% for the fully trained head. The loop is a real but limited mechanism
here. What it cannot do is manufacture the information the state lacks.

#### E25c: controls.

Two controls bound the reading. First, selection. Each arm draws its own
anchor set, so round-0 scores spread over 1.9 points (26.45% to 28.31%)
across the seven runs we have; seed 0 alone gave 0.8 points. That spread
is our noise floor. At matched admitted volume the gate averages 29.39%
over three seeds (28.80, 29.37, 29.99). Random admission averages 28.77%
(27.56, 29.45, 29.29). The per-seed gaps are $`+1.24`$, $`-0.08`$ and
$`+0.70`$ points, a mean of $`+0.62`$. A sign test over the three paired
seeds gives two positives and one negative ($`p=1.0`$). The gap
therefore sits inside the floor, and at this scale the gate’s advantage
over volume-matched random admission is inside seed noise. We report the
gap as a point estimate with its seed range. The unrestricted random arm
admits 43% more rows and lands 0.49 points above the gate at seed 0 (one
run). More unselected data thus roughly compensates for worse selection.
Selection quality is a weak effect at this scale. Acquisition volume is
the stronger term. Second, capacity. With no typed model the same
accounting reaches 25.91%, which is 2 to 3 points below the typed arms.
Its 90%-accuracy band collapses to 0.001. The typed encoder is what
makes an accept-or-escalate band exist at all.

#### E25d: what grows, and what that does not show.

Edge counts rise in every arm, but they track how much of the pool an
arm consumed: the model-free arm consumes the most and finds the most
edges. Growth of the graph is therefore evidence that the loop explores,
not that the gate selects well. Combining the two sections, the honest
summary of the deployment claim is this. The typed interface replaces
the per-decision deliberation with a millisecond forward pass and
reaches parity with the strongest feature readout, containment is 90.7%
at twenty candidates, and escalation needs to cover under 10% of
decisions. Raising in-graph accuracy past a third of the fold is not a
matter of more self-training, more parameters or more deliberation. It
needs a state that carries the plan and the observation, which the
released records do not.

#### E25e: what a deployment should do with this.

Three operating points follow from the two sections, and they are meant
to be read together. Inside the graph, route with the typed encoder and
accept its decision: one forward pass, no generated text, 19.5 ms per
decision in the typed-choice form and 10 ms for the encoder pass the
replay uses. At the top of the confidence ranking, accept a band rather
than the whole fold: 15% coverage holds 91.9% agreement. Return the
rest, because the realised hop lies outside the declared options for
7.9% of decisions and outside the model’s top twenty for 9.3%, so a
budget under 20% covers both measures. Returning a decision is
escalation, and its price is measured: on the replay stream the
accept/escalate hybrids agree with the reference on 20.9% / 19.1% /
16.5% of decisions at escalation rates of 74.1% / 80.2% / 85.0%
([Table 5](#S6.T5 "In E3: end-to-end replay against the re-prompted MIMO Pro V2.6 stand-in (claim P3). ‣ 6 Results ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")),
below the pure readout’s 30.7% at zero calls, and the escalated
remainder costs 2.2 s a decision, so an 85% budget moves mean decision
time from 2.2 s to about 1.9 s. The band is a latency and load
instrument rather than an agreement gain, and it assumes an escalation
arm stronger than the zero-shot stand-in these hybrids were measured
against. Above that budget none of the remedies tested here applies:
more self-training, more parameters and more deliberation each leave the
ceiling where the state puts it. The escalation rule itself must carry
per-state competence rather than confidence alone, since the
confidence-gated cascade lost agreement at every threshold we tested.

## 9 Limitations and Discussion

Replay evaluation. The held-out logs are a realized environment, not a
live one: policies are scored against the deployed fleet’s own choices
(teacher-forced scoring, strict survival and per-wrong-hop gap costs),
so a policy that would _improve_ on the reference cannot demonstrate
that improvement here—agreement with the fleet at a fraction of its
decision cost is the claim. Closed-loop rollouts with tool results
reattached (this needs a fuller export than the stripped one described
here) are the natural next step. The graph-free comparison that scores
every catalog action as one flat option space is run here (E9,
Reproducibility): the flat catalog loses 3.5 points and a size-matched
global-frequency space loses 1.4 points, so restricting candidates per
node is load-bearing, but a node-conditioned space built from per-node
train statistics without graph traversal matches the graph-declared
space within noise ($`+0.1`$ points, 95% trace-clustered CI
$`[0.0,+0.3]`$). The induced graph’s measured value here is therefore as
a construction that yields node-conditioned option sets, not as a
traversal whose specificity this data can resolve. Derived labels. Task
outcomes come from the close-out convention
(§[5](#S5 "5 Experimental Setup ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")):
the convention is machine-checkable and checked against an assistant’s
manual session-log re-reading (30/30), but it certifies _declared_
completion, not the semantic quality of the report text—which the export
strips. The unconstrained-judge failure mode we quantify (38.5%
agreement when the judge expects report text in a field that never
carries it) is a caution for LLM-judge pipelines generally: calibrate
against a manual session-log re-reading, not against another judge.
Scope of the claims. We evaluate a decision layer over logs that already
exist. Authorization, retention and re-identification boundaries of the
underlying sessions, the human review roles around them, change
management for catalog updates, and the explicability a planner needs
when it cannot verify its own outputs are properties of the deployment
that produced the logs; we do not measure them, and our audit statements
are limited to what the export can show (which, as noted above, includes
no separate human auditor in the loop). State reconstruction. Tool
_result_ bodies and chat bodies are absent from the export (the
tool-result field is null in all 22,192 tool records; call arguments
survive, below), so the router’s context is structural (labels,
ordering). Two result-adjacent families do survive in the export and go
unused in the main pipeline—per-call latency (100% of tool calls) and
the call arguments (98.9%)—so their contribution is already measurable
without the payloads: an artifact-shape-verified reproduction of the
§[6](#S6 "6 Results ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
feature-MLP CE$`+`$Brier arm scores 32.7% masked agreement, and
appending prefix-latency / latency-plus-argument-shape features adds
+0.4 / +1.3 points (five seeds, feat_lat_results.json,
Reproducibility)—a small but real measured gain that stops this
limitation’s prediction being an untested assertion. Domains and slices.
One security-operations deployment, four stage slices; the
protection-stage zero close-out rate (App. A) means slice comparisons
conflate routing behavior with pipeline role. Edge IDs. The per-edge
identifiers do _not_ beat the class-space readout at the SOC catalog
size with our shallow state features—the small API-Bank set and the
351-name ToolBench-eval subset are the material small-catalog reversals
(§[6](#S6 "6 Results ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents"),
[Figure 10](#Ax2.F10 "In Appendix B: services and statistical details ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents"))—and
the natural counter—that verdict being a small-catalog artifact—is now
tested: a controlled catalog-size sweep on the 7,318-name ToolBench
catalog
([Figure 10](#Ax2.F10 "In Appendix B: services and statistical details ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents"),
App. B) holds recipe and features fixed while shrinking the option
vocabulary to the top-$`C`$ actions by train frequency ($`C`$, the kept
catalog budget). The row-filtered sweep gives a class advantage of net
+0.9 points at $`C{=}154`$ to +1.3 at $`C{=}6{,}750`$ (a dip to +0.6 at
$`C{=}512`$). That sweep keeps only rows whose current action and target
both lie inside top-$`C`$, so the scored subset itself shrinks with
$`C`$—by up to 65.5% of rows at the narrow budgets. Re-running it on a
_fixed_ row set (out-of-catalog goals masked to an always-offered
unknown class) leaves the shape intact and the gap smaller: net +0.5
points at $`C{=}154`$ to +1.2 at $`C{=}6{,}750`$ (a dip to +0.3 at
$`C{=}512`$) over _strictly paired_ candidate lists—identical options
for both arms on rows whose target lies on that list; charging each arm
for the options it cannot name instead flips the sign at the two narrow
budgets ($`-1.8`$, $`-1.2`$). The class-space advantage therefore ends
wider across the sweep (non-monotonically) and, in the row-filtered and
paired means, never crosses over on the measured axis (top-$`C`$ up to
6,750; outside the noise-level ties, the charging-convention flips above
and the contrary small-catalog slices—the evaluation subset at its
275-name training catalog and API-Bank—are the exceptions disclosed in
[Figure 10](#Ax2.F10 "In Appendix B: services and statistical details ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")):
even at the widest tested budget ($`C{=}6{,}750`$, $`43.8\times`$ the
SOC’s 154 option names), a shared class vocabulary is the better
parameterization of the next-hop choice. The edge-ID variant may still
pay where option names carry no meaning ([Sun et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib49))—that regime remains open. Scope. The readout has no
mechanism for multi-step internal search (the single-pass design
of [Cheng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib8) likewise cannot carry intermediate
results through); long detours are delegated to the graph and to LLM
escalation at genuine unknowns. All typed-model timing is batch-1 GPU
wall clock (NVIDIA H20, App. B) except the in-vivo zero-shot pipeline,
measured on CPU; service and judge latencies are measured in vivo under
production load and vary with it.

Data availability and governance. The logs behind
§[6](#S6 "6 Results ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
are production security-operations records of a deployment that is not
ours to release, and no human-subjects data were collected for this
study. The derived, non-identifying artifacts that back every reported
number—frozen per-decision predictions, the aggregation and audit
scripts, and the per-experiment receipts—can be made available on
request. The public bundle is sufficient to re-derive every corpus-level
aggregate reported here—episode, session, split and per-slice counts,
the held-out option-space mean and its $`3{,}568`$-of-$`3{,}875`$
coverage, the catalog size and the fold-event totals—while the pre-fold
trace count, the semantic families and the model-level results require
the internal export. Authorization, retention and re-identification
boundaries, the human review roles around the sessions, and catalog
change management remain properties of the deployment that produced the
logs: what this paper states about them is limited to what the stripped
export can show, and nothing here should be read as a governance
assessment of that deployment.

## 10 Conclusion

Logs already contain an organization’s workflow knowledge; NIOCIS turns
them into a graph whose transition set runs a long tail and whose node
vocabulary converges at rates a zero-parameter occupancy null already
predicts (E1), and pairs it with a typed router that reads next-hop
decisions off that graph in one forward pass, escalating only its own
uncertainty to the slow, expensive path. The loop closes: every executed
walk becomes gated training signal. On 1,615 production episodes the
pieces separately hold—quantified node-vocabulary convergence of the
induced action graph (E1); feature-MLP readouts reaching 32.8% agreement
with the deployed fleet (35.6% within the declared space), with the
hidden-state probe variant holding 30.7% at a 10 ms per-state encoder
pass plus a linear head against 2.2 s LLM hops, and out-agreeing the
re-prompted MIMO Pro V2.6 stand-in (tool-result-stripped, zero-shot) on
the same states (E2–E3)—a transfer-floor comparison, not a capability
ceiling: the stand-in is never fine-tuned. Route-level replay keeps the
typed layer in the agreement/cost corner while exposing the
accept/escalate hybrid’s calibration failure as a negative result (E3).
A self-evolution loop’s rollback gate scores every candidate while the
loop absorbs the flipped labels of one update batch (12% of that batch’s
labels corrupted—about 0.3% of the 12,613-decision base) without firing
rollback on the plotted seed-0 chain (E4). Across ten seeded chains the
gate refuses 13 of the 40 candidate updates, in 6 of the 10 chains. An
external replication runs the same fold over 60,613 public cases, where
loop mass reaches 99.4% on an instrumented software session and 1.4% on
traffic fines; node discovery flattens wherever catalogs are small (E5);
NIOCIS’s decision construction carries over to a 7,318-name tool
catalog, where a transition table isolates what the feature MLP
contributes beyond order-$`\leq`$3 memorized transitions; on the case
streams an occupancy null shows node saturation is combinatorics,
conditional on the observed label supports. The invitation is concrete:
drop your logs into the fold pipeline and measure your own saturation
curve.

## Appendix A: fold statistics of the production corpus

The fold of
§[4](#S4 "4 The NIOCIS Approach ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
decomposes the 22,192 logged tool calls into 15,704 advance (forward)
hops and 6,488 loop events (29.2% of steps). The 4,124 retry events
arrive in 2,164 same-label repeat runs (a run of $`k\geq 2`$ consecutive
identical labels contributes $`k-1`$ retry events, so the length classes
sum to 2,164 runs, contributing 4,124 retry events): 1,394 runs of
length 2 (median length 2), a smooth decay (354 of length 3, 147 of
length 4, 102 of length 5) and a thin tail of 167 runs of lengths 6 to
20 (20 consecutive same-label steps at the maximum). The 2,364 back
events return to an earlier kept label at short-but-real distances
(steps between the target’s previous occurrence and the return): 681 at
$`\leq`$1, 666 at 2–3, 553 at 4–8, 464 at 9+. Pair roles across the
fold: 2,315 distinct pairs fire as first-visit forward transfers, 702 as
returns (481 in both roles, 221 exclusively returns). Domain slices
carry distinct closure behavior: detection closes 1,001/1,174 episodes
(85%), response 62/245 (25%), recognition 7/54 (13%), protection
0/139—protection-stage sessions in this window hand off to sibling
stages rather than declare completion (a real stage-behavior property of
the deployment, not a labeling artifact; the derived labels and their
validation are in
§[5](#S5 "5 Experimental Setup ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).
The one synthetic element is E4’s corruption model: round 3 flips 12% of
update labels to a uniformly drawn non-target option—the draw is seeded
by the script’s per-round RNG (RandomState(100+round), i.e. 103 for
round 3)—a deliberate stand-in for upstream policy regressions. The
multi-chain study quoted in the E4 figure caption runs ten refit chains:
five coupled seed offsets (the torch init, the corruption draw and the
validation-split permutation all shifted together) run as two 5-chain
arms—one under the row-level stream split (seed offsets
0/1000/2000/3000/4000) and one under the whole-trace split (1000–5000;
four of the five offsets shared). Each chain makes four candidate
updates, so the study reports 13 refusals in 40 attempts over 6 of the
10 chains, with held-out accuracy averaging 30.3% $`\to`$ 31.0%
($`\pm 0.5`$ points) and an unpaired 0.02-point gap between the arms
(0.09 points on the four shared offsets). Three of the ten chains dip
below their previous committed score mid-study (two of those below their
base fit) before recovering; every chain ends at or above its base fit.
Results are in e4_rsi_multi.json (Reproducibility).

that emit reasoning traces; decoding at temperature 0 with a 2,048-token

smoke probes on tiny next-hop prompts run at p50 0.5 s (MIMO Pro V2.6)
and 0.45 s (DS V4.1 Flash), growing with prompt and reasoning length
(episode-level judge calls in
§[5](#S5 "5 Experimental Setup ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")

0.3.21 with the convaiinnovations/laya checkpoint (ModernBERT-large

train/test splits (12,613 + 3,875 decisions) in 512-text chunks on GPU

one NVIDIA H20 (in the nvcr.io/nvidia/pytorch:26.03-py3 container):

#### Transition-table baseline.

Contexts over the traced node flow (orders 0–3) are counted on the
training fold of each of the nine decision streams (SOC; API-Bank,
BPIC’12, BPIC’17, helpdesk, receipt, roadtraffic and the two ToolBench
streams); each order interpolates down to the training marginal with a
cascading weight
$`\lambda=w_{\mathrm{rem}}\,n_{\mathrm{ctx}}/(n_{\mathrm{ctx}}{+}3)`$
($`w_{\mathrm{rem}}`$: weight left unspent by higher orders, cascading
3$`\to`$2$`\to`$1, residue to the training marginal) and the argmax is
restricted to the declared options (the deployed masked-renorm gate).
Two scorings bracket each stream. The verbatim majority arm (raw
per-node argmax)—scored raw and option-masked (a hit only when the
argmax lands in the options)—recovers the
§[5](#S5 "5 Experimental Setup ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")
majority arm to the digit and matches every other stream’s
identically-built look-up-table (LUT) arm exactly; the interpolated
tables (an interpolated n-gram with backoff semantics—the backoff
Markov) gate before scoring. For the majority arm, test accuracy equals
its option-masked accuracy on all nine streams
(pub_markov_results.json): option-gating never removes a correct
majority prediction. The label catalog is treated as known (the class
index covers every corpus label, held-out names included, so no action
target is out of vocabulary). The public row encoding would bucket one
at \<stop\>, and the edge-ID arms map training-fold-unseen edge targets
to \<unk_edge\>; option sets and edge identifiers come from the training
fold only.

#### Catalog-size sweep (the edge-pays-at-scale hypothesis, §[9](#S9 "9 Limitations and Discussion ‣ NIOCIS: Typed-Decision Next-Hop Routingwith Node-Conditioned Option Spacesfor Tool-Use Agents")).

pub_sweep.py holds the fold, features and training procedure fixed while
shrinking the option vocabulary to the top-$`C`$ actions by train
frequency, scoring only in-catalog decisions and rebuilding the option
graph and edge vocabulary inside the catalog. Sweep budgets are bounded
by each corpus’s training vocabulary: the five process logs contribute
single points at their training vocabularies (11 to 27 names,
$`|\Delta|\leq 0.21`$ points, noise-level ties) and ToolBench
contributes the axis: the class-space readout beats the edge-ID readout
at every budget ($`C{=}154`$: 61.5% versus 60.6%; $`C{=}512`$: 54.3
versus 53.7; $`C{=}2{,}048`$: 47.6 versus 46.5; $`C{=}6{,}750`$: 43.2
versus 41.9). Training floors: the 335-row API-Bank training fold and
the 154-name slice of the evaluation subset fall below the sweep’s
500-train/50-test guard, so those rows are skipped.

#### The induced graph read as a discovered model.

For completeness we report the four standard discovery dimensions for
the induced graph ($`\lx@sectionsign\ref{sec:related}`$), with
conventions stated because they are convention-dependent: _fitness_ is
the share of traces whose every consecutive kept pair is a model edge,
with a token-replay flavour ($`1-\text{unmatched pairs}/\text{pairs}`$);
_precision_ is the escaping-edge form (ETConformance style:
$`1-\sum\text{escaping}/\sum\text{enabled}`$ over trace prefixes, where
escaping counts enabled outgoing edges not taken); _generalization_ is
the counting approximation ($`1-\text{mean}\,1/\sqrt{\text{visits}}`$
over model elements); _simplicity_ is reported as counts (143 nodes with
outgoing edges, 2,517 edges—1,944 forward, 573 recovery—mean out-degree
17.6). The graph is induced from the _train_ fold, so in-sample readings
are degenerate by construction (trace- and token-level fitness
$`1.000`$); on held-out folds token-level fitness is $`0.902`$ (val) and
$`0.913`$ (test, unmatched pairs $`307/3{,}530`$), trace-level fitness
$`0.621`$ and $`0.591`$, escaping-edge precision $`0.022`$ and
$`0.021`$, and generalization is $`0.625`$ at node level against
$`0.267`$ at edge level. The permissive precision is structural: each
state enables its entire declared option set ($`\bar{|A(v)|}=40.2`$), so
precision tracks $`1/\bar{|A(v)|}`$. No inductive-miner comparison is
attempted, and these numbers are not offered as a process-discovery
contribution.

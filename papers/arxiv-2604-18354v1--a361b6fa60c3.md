###### Abstract

Emotion plays a pivotal role in shaping negotiation outcomes,
influencing trust, cooperation, and long-term relationships. Developing
negotiation dialog systems that can recognize and respond strategically
to emotions is, therefore, essential to create more effective
human-centered interactions. Beyond generating emotionally appropriate
responses, interpretability - understanding how a system generates a
particular emotion-aware response, is critical for fostering reliability
and building rapport. Driven by these aspects, in this work, we
introduce prisma, an interpretable emotionally intelligent negotiation
dialogue system targeting two application domains, viz. job interviews
and resource allocation. To enable interpretability, we propose an
Emotion-aware Negotiation Strategy-informed Chain-of-Thought (ENS-CoT)
reasoning mechanism, which mimics human negotiation by perceiving,
understanding, using, and managing emotions. Leveraging ENS-CoT, we
curate two new datasets: JobNego (for job interview negotiation) and
ResNego (for resource allocation negotiation). We then leverage these
datasets to develop prisma by augmenting self-training with Direct
Preference Optimization (DPO), guiding agents toward more accurate,
interpretable, and emotionally appropriate negotiation responses.
Automatic and human evaluation on JobNego and ResNego datasets
demonstrate that prisma substantially enhances interpretability and
generates appropriate emotion-aware responses, while improving overall
negotiation effectiveness¹¹ 1 Code and dataset are available at  
[https://github.com/kajareprajwal1143/PRISMA](https://github.com/kajareprajwal1143/PRISMA)
and
[https://ai-nlp-ml.github.io/resources.html](https://ai-nlp-ml.github.io/resources.html)..

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2604.18354v1/PRISMA-Motivation1.png)

Figure 1: A dialogue snippet illustrating the role of emotional
intelligence with interpretable ENS-CoT rationales in generating
emotion-aware responses and enhancing negotiation outcomes.

Negotiation is inherently a social process where parties seek to settle
exchanges to satisfy their needs [Spector (1977)](#bib.bib19).
Traditionally, research has examined negotiation through economic
modeling [Sebenius (1992)](#bib.bib17), decision-making theory [Bazerman
et al. (2000)](#bib.bib18), and social psychology [Rubin and Brown
(2013)](#bib.bib16). However, these classical views often portray
negotiators as purely rational agents, overlooking the pivotal role of
emotions. Behavioral evidence shows that emotions systematically drive
concession-making, perceived fairness, joint gains, and cooperation
[Lawler and Yoon (1995)](#bib.bib20); [Forgas (1998)](#bib.bib21). Thus,
emotional intelligence (EI: the ability to perceive, understand, use,
and manage emotions) [Goleman (2005)](#bib.bib22), is vital for
developing human-like negotiation dialogue agents.

Although modern agents based on the large language models (LLMs) can
reason via chain-of-thought (CoT) [Wei et al. (2022)](#bib.bib28) and
recognize emotions [Li et al. (2024)](#bib.bib23), they remain limited
in strategically leveraging emotions during negotiation. They often
generate static rather than adaptive emotion-aware strategies [Kroczek
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib24), struggle to distinguish genuine from
manipulated emotions [Huang and Hadfi (2024)](#bib.bib25), and lack
reasoning about emotional cause-and-effect dynamics [Bosse et al.
(2013)](#bib.bib26). These limitations are critical in high-stakes
negotiation settings like job interviews and resource allocation, where
emotion directly influences strategic decisions (e.g., frustration may
hasten concessions, while excitement can trigger overly ambitious
offers), shaping negotiation outcomes. For such agents to be reliable,
users must understand the rationale behind their emotionally-tuned
responses [Dang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib27). Hence, beyond emotional
intelligence, a negotiation agent must also be interpretable; not merely
generate responses, but also explain how it generates these responses to
build rapport and foster credibility.

Motivated by this need, we introduce prisma, an interpretable,
emotionally intelligent negotiation dialogue system that first reasons
via a CoT mechanism and then generates emotion-aware negotiation
responses. To enable interpretable EI in Prisma, we draw inspiration
from the Mayer–Salovey–Caruso Emotional Intelligence Test (MSCEIT)
[Mayer et al. (2003)](#bib.bib13), which encompasses four abilities:
perceiving emotion (identifying the user’s current emotion),
understanding emotion (explaining its cause and effect), using emotion
to facilitate thought (guiding reasoning through perspective shifting
and mindset transformation), and managing emotion (selecting and
justifying an emotion-aware negotiation strategy, then realizing it in
the response). As shown in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues"),
unlike generic response that may frustrate the user, prisma interprets
the user’s confidence for high salary, links it to aspirations for
recognition and leadership, reframes priorities, and adopts a ‘Positive
Framing’ strategy emphasizing promotion and growth, leading to an
emotionally attuned counteroffer to improve the negotiation outcome.

To advance this line of research, we introduce two novel negotiation
dialogue datasets, viz. JobNego and ResNego, generated using ChatGPT
[OpenAI (2024)](#bib.bib29) for job interview and resource allocation
negotiations, respectively. These datasets are constructed through an
interpretable emotion-aware dialogue generation process. We first design
diverse, realistic negotiation scenarios, then, conditioned on
scenarios, generate dialogues augmented with MSCEIT-inspired
Emotion-aware Negotiation Strategy-informed CoT (ENS-CoT) rationales,
where the agent first reasons to select appropriate emotion-aware
strategies and then generates emotion-aware responses during
negotiation.

Curating large-scale, high-quality dialogue data with rationale
annotations, however, is time- and resource-intensive. In contrast,
unlabeled negotiation data can be synthesized at scale and leveraged via
semi-supervised methods, such as self-training to improve LLM
performance [Zelikman et al. (2022)](#bib.bib30); [Gulcehre et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib31). Moreover, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has
shown promise for aligning LLMs with preferred outputs [Rafailov et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib15); [Deng and Mineiro (2024)](#bib.bib32). Building on
these insights, we propose a preference-reinforced self-training
approach that integrates self-training with DPO to develop prisma. It
begins with supervised initialization to enable the LLM to generate
ENS-CoT rationales and appropriate emotion-aware responses, followed by
an iterative loop alternating DPO-based preference learning (aligning
agents to preferred emotion-aware negotiation responses) and
self-training (adding high-confidence ENS-CoT pseudo-labels for
unlabeled contexts back into supervised fine-tuning). This integration
optimizes step-by-step CoT reasoning, resulting in improved
emotion-aware negotiation responses. Empirical evaluations indicate that
the proposed approach enhances the interpretability and emotionally
attuned negotiation behavior in prisma.

In summary, the key contributions of this work are as follows: (i)
Present Prisma, an interpretable, emotionally intelligent negotiation
dialogue agent. To the best of our knowledge, this work pioneers
interpretability modeling in emotion-aware negotiation conversations;
(ii) Propose an Emotion-aware Negotiation Strategy-informed
Chain-of-Thought (ENS-CoT) reasoning mechanism to enable interpretable
emotional intelligence; (iii) Curate two novel datasets, JobNego (job
interview negotiation) and ResNego (resource allocation negotiation),
annotated with ENS-CoT rationales; and (iv) Introduce a
preference-reinforced self-training approach integrating self-training
with DPO, demonstrating its effectiveness in producing interpretable,
emotion-aware negotiation responses.

## 2 Related Work

Negotiation has long been a central topic in psychology, economics, and
affective computing ([Carnevale et al., 1992](#bib.bib51); [Boothby et
al., 2023](#bib.bib14)). In recent years, automated negotiation dialogue
systems have made significant progress [Lewis et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib68); [Zhou et al. (2019)](#bib.bib55), demonstrating
that modeling negotiators’ behavior [He et al. (2018)](#bib.bib56);
[Zhang et al. (2020)](#bib.bib57); [Chawla et al. (2022)](#bib.bib53),
strategies [Zhao et al. (2019)](#bib.bib54); [Chawla et al.
(2021b)](#bib.bib8); [Yamaguchi et al. (2021)](#bib.bib52),
personalities [Yang et al. (2020)](#bib.bib58); [Priya et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib60); [Priya et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib59), and politeness
[Zhao et al. (2018)](#bib.bib61); [Priya et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib62) can
substantially enhance human-agent negotiation interactions. Emotions
also play a critical role in shaping negotiation outcomes [Morris and
Keltner (2000)](#bib.bib63); [Douglas and Coburn (2014)](#bib.bib64),
and a growing number of studies have begun integrating emotional cues
into automated negotiation systems [Chawla et al. (2021a)](#bib.bib65);
[Chawla et al. (2023)](#bib.bib66); [Keskin et al. (2024)](#bib.bib67).

The advent of LLMs [Minaee et al. (2024)](#bib.bib69) has further
spurred research in negotiation dialogue systems [Fu et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib39); [Gandhi et al. (2023)](#bib.bib70); [Abdelnabi et
al. (2023)](#bib.bib71); [Deng et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib38). Emotional
intelligence (awareness and management of emotions) [Maier et al.
(2004)](#bib.bib75) and interpretability (ability to understand the
reasoning behind the model’s outputs) [Fuhrman et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib74), have become key considerations in designing
reliable LLM-based agents [Li et al. (2023)](#bib.bib72); [Yang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib73); [Feng et al. (2025)](#bib.bib76); [Kasbouya and
Sael (2025)](#bib.bib77). To support interpretability, Chain-of-Thought
(CoT) prompting [Wei et al. (2022)](#bib.bib28) has been developed,
enabling LLMs to emulate human reasoning processes and produce more
transparent, step-wise rationales. Building on this, a number of studies
have explored the use of CoT prompting to enhance LLM reasoning across
diverse applications [Zhang et al. (2022)](#bib.bib78); [Yang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib79), including dialogue generation [Deng et al.
(2023a)](#bib.bib36).

Complementing CoT approaches, reinforcement learning (RL) techniques
[Bai et al. (2022)](#bib.bib81) have been applied to further refine LLM
reasoning [Trung et al. (2024)](#bib.bib82); [Wang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib83). Notably, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
[Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib15), which aligns model outputs with
human preferences, has shown promise in improving reasoning and output
quality [Deng and Mineiro (2024)](#bib.bib32); [Lai et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib80). Additionally, semi-supervised methods [Scudder
(1965)](#bib.bib84); [Amini et al. (2025)](#bib.bib85), such as
self-training [Amini et al. (2025)](#bib.bib85), have gained traction
for enhancing reasoning capabilities in LLMs, providing a scalable
mechanism for leveraging unlabeled or weakly labeled data [Lee et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib86); [Wu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib87).

Despite extensive research on modeling opponents’ behavior, strategies,
emotions, and politeness in developing negotiation dialogue agents,
little research has examined the specific role of interpretable
emotional intelligence within negotiation dialogues. Moreover, combining
self-training with preference signals remains underexplored, especially
in negotiation scenarios where interpretability and emotional
intelligence are critical. To address these gaps, we introduce prisma, a
preference-reinforced self-training approach that leverages
Emotion-aware Negotiation Strategy-informed CoT (ENS-CoT) rationales to
align responses with users’ emotional states while ensuring
interpretability during negotiation. prisma is designed to be scalable
and adaptable across diverse application domains.

## 3 Dataset

We evaluate prisma on two newly created negotiation dialogue datasets:
JobNego and ResNego. JobNego comprises job interview negotiations
between a candidate (user) and an employer (agent), covering salary,
working hours, promotion track, and related terms. ResNego includes
resource-allocation negotiation dialogues in a camping setting, where
parties bargain over food, water, firewood, and related supplies.

### 3.1 Dataset Preparation

Given the high cost of creating interpretable, emotionally intelligent
negotiation dialogue datasets, where the agent must combine domain
constraints and trade-offs with emotion-aware negotiation strategy
selection, we adopt an interpretable and scalable dataset construction
scheme. Specifically, we introduce an Emotion-aware Negotiation
Strategy-informed Chain-of-Thought (ENS-CoT) reasoning mechanism to
generate emotion-aware negotiation responses in an interpretable manner.
ENS-CoT mimics human negotiation behavior by identifying, understanding,
using, and managing emotions during negotiation through appropriate
emotion-aware negotiation strategies. Using ENS-CoT, we curate two new
interpretable emotion-aware negotiation dialogue datasets: JobNego and
ResNego for job interview and resource allocation domains, respectively.
The entire dataset construction involves three steps: (1) Negotiation
Scenario Generation, (2) Emotion and Negotiation Strategy Formulation,
and (3) ENS-CoT-enhanced Dialogue Generation.

##### (1) Negotiation Scenario Generation.

The scenarios summarize the negotiation setup, including task, roles,
issues, constraints, and goals underlying each dialogue. The diverse and
realistic scenarios are essential for generating high-quality
negotiation dialogues. We, therefore, use NegoChat [Konovalov et al.
(2016)](#bib.bib9) and CaSiNo [Chawla et al. (2021b)](#bib.bib8)
datasets as seed corpora to generate the initial pool of job interview
and resource allocation negotiation scenarios, respectively. For each
dialogue, we prompt ChatGPT in a zero-shot setting to produce a brief
scenario description.

To identify an effective prompt, we design four candidate prompts
(including the natural language instructions and target dialogues), and
for each prompt, we generate 30 scenarios per domain. These generated
scenarios are then manually evaluated by the three human annotators for
scenario appropriateness on a scale of 1-3 (1-low, 2-moderate, 3-high)²²
2 The annotators hold Ph.D. in Linguistics with extensive expertise in
negotiation, emotional intelligence, and dialogue design. They are
compensated as per the institute norms.. An inter-annotator Kappa
agreement score ($`\kappa`$) [McHugh (2012)](#bib.bib10) reaches 0.85
(for job interview scenarios) and 0.88 (for resource allocation
scenarios). The prompt generating the highest number of scenarios with a
score of 3 for both domains is selected as the final prompt. This prompt
is then used to prompt ChatGPT to generate scenarios for all the
dialogues. The generated scenarios are further verified by the same
annotators, achieving $`\kappa=0.88`$ and $`0.86`$ among them for job
interview and resource allocation scenarios, respectively. It yields the
unique 105 and 1,030 job interview and resource allocation scenarios,
respectively.

We expand this scenario pool by leveraging ChatGPT’s in-context learning
ability [Wang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib7). We randomly sample three
dialogue–scenario exemplars per domain from the initial pool and use
them to prompt ChatGPT in a few-shot setup to generate additional
scenarios. To ensure quality and diversity, we remove duplicates and
filter out inadequate scenarios that lack proper information on
negotiation setup or have incomplete sentences. This yields an
additional 735 job interviews and 618 resource allocation scenarios,
enriching the pool for subsequent dialogue generation. The scenario
generation prompt template is given in the Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Dataset Details ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues").

##### (2) Emotions and Emotion-aware Negotiation Strategies Formulation.

Emotions significantly influence negotiation dynamics and outcomes
[Barry and Oliver (1996)](#bib.bib1). Users naturally express positive,
negative, or neutral emotions during negotiation, and these emotions
determine how negotiation unfolds [Overbeck et al. (2010)](#bib.bib2).
In this work, we adopt 12 emotion categories from the negotiation
literature [Barry (1999)](#bib.bib3); [Van Kleef and others
(2008)](#bib.bib11), including joy, confidence, positivity, gratitude,
trust, surprise, anger, disappointment, frustration, fear, anxiety, and
neutral.

Effective negotiation depends on how the agent manages users’ emotions
[Van Kleef and Lelieveld (2022)](#bib.bib4). The dynamic nature of
users’ emotions directly guides the agent’s choice of strategy and, in
turn, negotiation outcomes [Lawler and Thye (1999)](#bib.bib5). To
effectively manage these emotions and drive negotiation toward mutually
beneficial outcomes, we define 12 emotion-aware negotiation strategies
grounded in negotiation emotion management principles [Yurtsever
(2004)](#bib.bib6). These strategies are designed to either amplify
positive emotions, mitigate negative ones, or maintain neutrality, and
include savoring, positive reinforcement, expressing optimism, cognitive
reappraisal, positive framing, emotion diffusion, expressive
suppression, active listening, perspective-taking, problem solving,
escalate assurance, and no strategy. Definitions of these strategies are
given in Table

##### (3) ENS-CoT-enhanced Dialogue Generation.

It involves following steps:

(a) ENS-CoT Reasoning Chain Description. To encode interpretable
emotional intelligence in negotiation dialogue systems, we introduce the
Emotion-aware Negotiation Strategy-informed CoT (ENS-CoT) reasoning
mechanism, grounded in the MSCEIT’s four abilities [Mayer et al.
(2003)](#bib.bib13): Perceiving emotion, Understanding emotion, Using
emotion to facilitate thought, and Managing emotion. Perceiving emotion
refers to the ability to identify user’s current emotion, understanding
emotion explains its causes via cognitive appraisal (emotional trigger
and individual assessment), using emotion to facilitate thought
specifies how emotion guides reasoning (via perspective shifting and
mindset transformation), and managing emotion selects and justifies an
emotion-aware negotiation strategy and realizes it in agent’s response.
An ENS-CoT instance is denoted as an octuple $`\langle`$EM, ET, IA, PS,
MT, SS, SR, RG$`\rangle`$, described below:

- •
  Emotion (EM): User’s expressed emotion.
- •
  Emotional Trigger (ET): Cause (external situation or internal thought)
  that triggers the emotion.
- •
  Individual Assessment (IA): Personal interpretation, evaluation, and
  internal response to the emotional trigger, based on the user’s past
  experiences, beliefs, expectations, and personal values.
- •
  Perspective Shift (PS): Adopting an alternate viewpoint to reduce
  reactivity and trade-offs.
- •
  Mindset transformation (MT): Reframing limiting beliefs into
  constructive priors for decision making.
- •
  Strategy Selection (SS): Choosing a pertinent emotion-aware
  negotiation strategy.
- •
  Strategy Reason (SR): Reasoning behind the selected strategy.
- •
  Response Generation (RG): Using the selected strategy to generate
  agent’s utterance in an ongoing dialogue.

(b) Designing Sample ENS-CoT-enhanced Dialogues. We author exemplar
dialogues conditioned on the generated scenarios, the user emotion
inventory, and the agent’s emotion-aware negotiation strategy catalog
across both domains. For each domain, we create 10 Wizard-of-Oz
dialogues [Kelley (1984)](#bib.bib12) with two annotators role-playing
the user and the agent. The dialogues proceed turn-by-turn, grounded in
the scenario: the user produces an utterance and expresses an emotion;
the agent applies ENS-CoT to perceive, understand, use, and manage that
emotion before responding. Every agent turn is annotated with an ENS-CoT
rationale, ensuring that responses are emotionally attuned,
strategically appropriate, and interpretable. ‘Guidelines for Designing
Sample ENS-CoT-enhanced Dialogues’ are provided in Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Dataset Details ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues").

(c) Dialogue Synthesis via Prompting. We use the authored dialogue
samples as few-shot exemplars to prompt ChatGPT to generate entire
dialogue datasets. To finalize the prompt, we experiment with four
manually designed prompts, including task instructions with the target
negotiation scenario, and few-shot exemplars (three randomly selected
negotiation scenarios and their corresponding dialogues with ENS-CoT
annotations). For each prompt, we generate 15 dialogues, each for both
the domains by prompting ChatGPT. These generated dialogues are
evaluated by the same three annotators for emotional intelligence (EI)
and negotiation quality (NQ) on a 1-3 scale (low to high) under the
supervision of a subject matter expert with HR and sales backgrounds. We
obtain $`\kappa=0.81\text{ and }0.84`$ for EI and NQ, respectively,
indicating good quality negotiation dialogues. We select the prompt that
generates the highest number of dialogues rated as 3 for both EI and NQ,
and use it to construct the final datasets. The final dataset statistics

### 3.2 Dataset Filtering and Quality Evaluation

After generating the datasets, we evaluate each dialogue based on the
following criteria: Emotional Intelligence (EI), Strategy
Appropriateness (SA), Interpretability (IN), Fairness (F), Coherence
(C), Naturalness (N), and Interestingness (I) (Descriptions are given in
Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Dataset Details ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues")).
The same three annotators rate every dialogue on a 1-5 scale (low to
high).

We retain dialogues that receive scores $`\geq 3`$ on all the metrices.
For JobNego, the retained dialogues attains average ratings of 4.62
(EI), 4.44 (SA), 4.27 (IN), 4.33 (F), 4.25 (C), 4.81 (N), and 4.34 (I),
with $`\kappa`$ scores of 0.85, 0.83, 0.79, 0.82, 0.80, 0.82, and 0.78
for EI, SA, IN, F, C, N, and I, respectively. For ResNego, average
ratings are 4.55 (EI), 4.39 (SA), 4.22 (IN), 4.40 (F), 4.20 (C), 4.77
(N), and 4.29 (I), with corresponding $`\kappa`$ scores of 0.83, 0.81,
0.80, 0.84, 0.78, 0.80, and 0.77. These results indicate that
conversations are emotionally attuned, strategically appropriate,
interpretable, fair, coherent, natural, and engaging across both
domains.

## 4 Methodology

The overall architecture of prisma, a preference-reinforced
self-training approach for an interpretable emotionally intelligent
negotiation dialogue system is depicted in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ (3) ENS-CoT-enhanced Dialogue Generation. ‣ 3.1 Dataset Preparation ‣ 3 Dataset ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues").

Problem Formulation. We present prisma that integrates emotion reasoning
with strategy selection and response generation. The system is designed
to (i) generate interpretable CoT rationales that explain how emotions
influence negotiation strategies, and (ii) generate final responses that
are emotionally attuned and strategically appropriate. Formally, given a
dialogue context $`c={u_{1},a_{1}\dots,u_{t-1},a_{t-1}}`$, which is an
alternating sequence of $`(t-1)`$ turns between the user $`(u)`$ and the
dialogue agent $`(a)`$, and the user’s target turn $`(u_{t})`$, the
negotiation agent prisma first generates an ENS-CoT rationale $`r`$. The
agent’s response $`a_{t}=(y)`$ is then generated conditioned on context
$`c`$ and the selected strategy extracted from $`r`$. This ensures that
the agent’s response is emotionally and strategically appropriate, while
also remaining interpretable, since the rationale explicitly shows how a
specific strategy is chosen.

Proposed Approach. To develop prisma, we introduce a
preference-reinforced self-training paradigm. This is a two-step
training procedure that integrates self-training with DPO to guide the
model toward accurate, interpretable, and emotionally appropriate
responses.

Step 1: Supervised Initialization. We begin by fine-tuning a pre-trained
LLM, LLaMA-2-7b ([Touvron et al., 2023](#bib.bib35)), on the labeled
dataset $`\mathcal{D}_{L}=\{(c_{i},r_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{L}`$, where
$`L`$ is the number of samples in $`\mathcal{D}_{L}`$. For the $`i`$-th
input context $`c_{i}`$, let
$`o_{i}=\mathrm{concat}(\langle\mathrm{R}\rangle,r_{i},\langle/\mathrm{R}\rangle,\langle\mathrm{A}\rangle,y_{i},\langle/\mathrm{A}\rangle)`$
be the target output sequence. The supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model is
obtained by $`\pi_{\theta}`$ by minimizing the token-level negative
log-likelihood $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SI}}(\theta)`$:

|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SI}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(c,r,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{L}}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{M}\log\pi_{\theta}\!\big(o_{m}\,\big|\,c,o_{1:m-1}\big)\right]
``` |  | (1) |

where $`M`$ denotes number of tokens in rationale+response sequence, and
$`o_{m}`$ represents the $`m`$-th token in $`o`$. This step equips the
model with a seed ability to generate ENS-CoT rationales and
emotion-aware responses during negotiation.

Step 2: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training. After initialization, we
iteratively refine the model through two alternating phases:

(a) Preference Learning via DPO. For some context $`c`$ from the
unlabeled dataset $`\mathcal{D}_{U}=\{(c_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{U}`$ ($`U`$
denotes the number of instances in $`\mathcal{D}_{U}`$), we sample
multiple rationales $`\hat{r}`$ from $`\pi_{\theta}`$. We then build a
preference dataset
$`\mathcal{D}_{P}=\{(c_{i},r_{i}^{+},r_{i}^{-})\}_{i=1}^{P}`$ consisting
of preferred and rejected completions, where $`r_{i}^{+}`$ is the
preferred completion, $`r_{i}^{-}`$ is the rejected completion, and
$`P`$ is the size of the preference dataset. A rationale is marked
*preferred* if its corresponding response has cosine similarity to the
ground-truth response $`y_{i}`$ exceeding a threshold $`\tau_{1}`$. It
is marked *rejected* if the similarity falls below a threshold
$`\tau_{2}`$³³ 3 All thresholds are chosen empirically based on
development-set similarity distributions, fixed globally across
experiments, and verified for robustness via sensitivity analysis, as
detailed in Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Experiment Details ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues")..
We then update $`\pi_{\theta}`$ by minimizing the DPO loss
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{PL}}(\theta^{\prime})`$ on $`\mathcal{D}_{P}`$,
yielding DPO-refined model $`\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{PL}}(\theta^{\prime})=-\mathbb{E}_{(c,r^{+},r^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}_{P}}`$ |  |  | (2) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\!\left(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}(r^{+}\mid c)}{\pi_{\theta}(r^{+}\mid c)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}(r^{-}\mid c)}{\pi_{\theta}(r^{-}\mid c)}\right)\right)\right]`$ |  |  |  |

where, $`\beta`$ denotes the coefficient controlling the
$`\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}`$’s deviation from $`\pi_{\theta}`$.

(b) Supervised Fine-Tuning with Updated Data. Using DPO-refined policy
$`\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}`$, we generate rationales for context
$`c\in\mathcal{D}_{U}`$. This forms a pseudo-labeled set
$`\mathcal{D}_{S}=\{(c,\hat{r},y)\mid(c,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{U},\;\hat{r}\sim\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}(\cdot\mid c)\}`$
for next round of supervised fine-tuning. We retain rationales whose
responses have cosine similarity to the corresponding ground-truth
responses exceeding $`\tau_{3}`$, and remove duplicates. This yields a
clean dataset $`\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{S}\subset\mathcal{D}_{S}`$.The
model is then trained in a supervised way on the updated dataset,
$`\mathcal{D}_{N}=\mathcal{D}_{L}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{S}`$. To
mitigate the risk of overfitting and enable continual learning, we
reinitialize training from the pre-trained LLaMA-2-7b model once the
dataset is updated. We do this rather than continuing to fine-tune the
SFT model $`\pi_{\theta}`$, following prior works [Zelikman et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib30); [Singh et al. (2023)](#bib.bib34).

Step 2 repeats until convergence or the iteration limit, resulting in a
negotiation agent that effectively identifies and interprets emotions,
selects an appropriate emotion-aware negotiation strategy, and grounds
its responses in the chosen strategy.

## 5 Experiments

We compare prisma with 10 baselines: Llama-2-7b-SFT, Llama-3.2-3b-SFT
[Touvron et al. (2023)](#bib.bib35), Emotion-aware SFT (Llama-2-7b),
ICL-AIF [Fu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib39), AnE [Zhang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib40), Standard, Proactive, ProCoT (GPT-5) [Deng et al.
(2023a)](#bib.bib36); [OpenAI (2025)](#bib.bib88), PPDPP [Deng et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib41), RLHF [Ouyang et al. (2022)](#bib.bib42). We
evaluate mediator with both automatic and human judgments. For automatic
evaluation, responses’ general quality is measured by Perplexity (PPL)
[Brown et al. (1992)](#bib.bib45), BLEU-4 (B-4) [Papineni et al.
(2002)](#bib.bib46), BERTScore-F1 (BS-F1) [Zhang et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib43), Distinct-3 (D-3) [Li et al. (2015)](#bib.bib44),
and Response Length (R-LEN); task success is assessed with Emotion
Appropriateness (EA): evaluates the appropriateness of users’ perceived
emotions, and Emotion-aware Negotiation Strategy Consistency (ENSC):
assesses the consistency of responses with intended strategies. For
human evaluation, to assess the general quality of responses, we use
Fluency (F), Coherence (C), and Engagingness (E); and to evaluate for
task success, we mirror automatic metrics - EA and ENSC, and also report
Bargaining Efficacy (BE) and Outcome Fairness (OF) [Ahmad et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib47). Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Experiment Details ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues")
provides ‘Implementation Details’, ‘Baselines Details’ and ‘Evaluation
Metrics Details’.

## 6 Results and Analysis

Automatic Evaluation. Table
[3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues")
presents the automatic evaluation results for prisma and baselines on
JobNego and ResNego datasets. Across both datasets, prisma consistently
achieves superior dialogue generation quality, emotional intelligence,
and negotiation performance. In terms of lexical and semantic metrics,
it records the highest B-4, D-3, and BS-F1 scores. These results reflect
enhanced fluency, diversity, and semantic alignment. Furthermore, the
model generates longer responses (R-LEN) while maintaining emotional
appropriateness (EA) and emotion-aware negotiation strategy consistency
(ENSC). Specifically, on JobNego, prisma outperforms the strongest
baseline, RLHF. It achieves significant gains of 13.4%, 7.3%, 1.1%,
10.1%, 3.4%, and 3.1% in B-4, D-3, BS-F1, R-LEN, EA, and ENSC,
respectively. On ResNego, prisma achieves similar performance
improvement. Compared to RLHF, it records gains of 14.0%, 5.2%, 1.0%,
10.8%, 2.6%, and 3.0% in B-4, D-3, BS-F1, R-LEN, EA, and ENSC,
respectively. These improvements show that prisma generates
linguistically richer responses. It also better captures emotional
nuances and maintains consistent negotiation strategies. The performance
gains could be attributed to the interpretability enforced through
ENS-CoT rationales during preference-reinforced self-training, where
modeling emotion-strategy reasoning sequences enables transparent,
emotionally intelligent, and coherent negotiation behavior.

The baseline model, RLHF, tends to generate less diverse and less
coherent emotion-aware responses during negotiation. Its inability to
follow the complete reasoning chain might result in limited awareness
and inconsistent understanding of the user’s emotional states.
Emotion-aware SFT performs well in emotional understanding. However, it
tends to generate shorter, less strategic responses, thereby limiting
negotiation effectiveness. ProCoT and PPDPP improve contextual control,
but often underperform in emotional alignment and response diversity. In
contrast, prisma balances emotional intelligence and negotiation
efficiency, enhancing both the linguistic and affective aspects of
dialogues. The consistent gains across datasets highlight its robustness
and the effectiveness of the interpretable ENS-CoT reasoning mechanism.

Human Evaluation. Table
[4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues")

+0.22, +0.19, +0.22, +0.19, and +0.21 points on these metrics. These
improvements highlight prisma’s ability to generate emotionally
intelligent and strategically coherent negotiation dialogues. The higher
F, C, and E scores demonstrate prisma’s ability to produce fluent,
coherent, and engaging negotiation dialogues that sustain natural
conversational flow. Besides, the superior EA, ENSC, BE, and OF scores
highlight its effectiveness in maintaining emotional awareness,
strategic consistency, and balanced outcome-focused negotiation
behavior. These results indicate that interpretability strengthens
emotional alignment and supports mutually beneficial negotiation
outcomes.

Ablation Study.To understand how each component of the ENS-CoT
rationales affects model performance, we conduct an ablation study to
investigate the impact of different components of the ENS-CoT
rationales. Table
[5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ prisma: Preference-Reinforced Self-Training Approach for Interpretable Emotionally Intelligent Negotiation Dialogues")
reports results across different configurations in which one or more
components are removed. In the first ablated model, we remove the
‘understanding emotion’ components, specifically ET and IA. The second
ablated model omits the ‘using emotion to facilitate thought’
components, namely PS and MT. In the third ablated model, we eliminate
the ‘managing emotion’ components, which include SS and SR. Finally, the
fourth ablated model excludes the components EM, ET, IA, PS, and MT, and
the fifth ablated model removes the components EM, ET, IA, PS, MT, and
SR. The removal of any rationale component adversely affects various
critical aspects of dialogue quality, such as the appropriateness of
emotions, the effectiveness of negotiations, and the language quality of
responses. This is evidenced by reduced scores on metrics such as EA,
ENSC, B-4, D-3, BS-F1, and R-LEN in both datasets.

In particular, when we remove $`\langle`$ET, IA$`\rangle`$ or
$`\langle`$PS, MT$`\rangle`$, B-4 increases slightly, but D-3 and BS-F1
drop, and responses become shorter. This shows that abilities like
‘understanding emotion’ and ‘using emotion to facilitate thought’
contribute substantive and varied content rather than n-gram overlap.
Further, ablating $`\langle`$SS, SR$`\rangle`$ causes the largest
decline in ENSC, indicating that explicit strategy choice and its
justification are central to keeping the response aligned with the
users’ intended emotions. Also, excluding the ‘perceiving emotion’
$`\langle`$EM$`\rangle`$ component along with the other components
reduces EA, ENSC, D-3, BS-F1, and R-LEN. This pattern is consistent with
simpler, more templated outputs that match surface forms but lose
strategic consistency and emotional awareness. In general, these
findings underscore the critical importance of each component in
improving interpretability and encouraging the generation of
emotion-aware responses within negotiation dialogues.

## 7 Conclusion

We presented prisma, an interpretable, emotionally intelligent
negotiation dialogue agent designed to enhance negotiation outcomes in
job interview and resource allocation domains. In this regard, we
proposed an Emotion-aware Negotiation Strategy-informed CoT (ENS-CoT)
reasoning mechanism that links perceived emotions to strategy selection
and response generation. Using ENS-CoT, we curated JobNego and ResNego
datasets. prisma integrates self-training with DPO through a
preference-reinforced self-training framework, optimizing step-by-step
CoT reasoning for emotion-aware negotiation responses. Extensive
evaluation show that prisma achieves superior emotion appropriateness,
strategy consistency, and negotiation effectiveness with improved
interpretability.

## Limitations

The prompting-based data creation requires substantial compute and
access to LLMs, and inherits both model biases and the variability of
prompt-based generation. Although the resulting dialogues are generally
fair and natural, quality remains bounded by the base model. The
stronger controls beyond scenario prompts (e.g., constraint-based
decoding, verifier models, or weak supervision) could further improve
fidelity. Ensuring interpretability with ENS-CoT rationales also
necessitates human verification, which constrains scale; realism is
limited by assumptions of fluent interlocutors, coherent turn flow, and
the absence of exogenous disruptions. Future work should automate
correction/verification, broaden scenario/diversity coverage (culture,
persona, etc.), and explore multi-emotion and multi-strategy annotations
to better reflect real negotiation practice.

We also observe that prisma can occasionally produce prescriptive
responses rather than fully collaborative ones, typically due to
insufficient reasoning about user-specific constraints or implicit
social norms (e.g., workplace expectations). Although human evaluations
show that prisma generally behaves collaboratively, this limitation
highlights an important direction for future work, which is, guiding
ENS-CoT generation and preference learning toward explicitly
collaborative problem-solving, emphasizing alternatives, phased plans,
trade-offs, and shared decision-making rather than fixed prescriptions.

Further, the proposed negotiation dialogue agent, prisma, builds on
self-training, but we do not systematically address unlabeled data
usage. Preference construction for DPO depends on rubrics and thresholds
that may encode hidden biases, and pseudo-labels can amplify early
errors in prisma. Moreover, the evaluation is restricted to two
bilateral, text-only domains (job interviews and camping resource
allocation) primarily due to a lack of rationale-annotated negotiation
dialogue dataset. Finally, several automatic proxies (e.g., similarity)
do not substitute for user-centric outcomes; controlled user studies and
online evaluations are an important next step to quantify trade-offs
among interpretability, task success, and user experience.

## Ethics Statement

Interpretability is central for negotiation dialogue systems, especially
when modeling emotional intelligence. Therefore, this work has been
reviewed and approved by our Institutional Review Board (IRB). First, we
treat dataset curation and distribution with care. All data sources are
used in accordance with their licenses. We screen and filter generations
with the help of linguistics and subject matter experts to reduce
biased, offensive, insensitive, or inappropriate content to avoid
potential unpredictable ethical harm. All human annotators and
evaluators are paid according to the institute’s norms. To ensure
responsible data use, access will be granted only after the completion
and signing of an agreement stipulating that the data will be used
solely for research purposes.

Second, negotiation involves strategic influence, so the design and
deployment of prisma must also adhere to strict ethical standards.
prisma is explicitly optimized for collaborative, non–zero-sum outcomes:
its emotion-aware strategies seek mutually beneficial agreements rather
than one-sided wins. Crucially, user autonomy is preserved at all times,
that is, users can decline proposals that do not meet their needs, and
the system must not pressure, coerce, or manipulate. prisma’s ENS-CoT
rationales are exposed to support transparency and human oversight,
enabling users to understand how the dialogue agent decides a particular
strategy.

### A.3 Guidelines for Designing Sample ENS-CoT-enhanced Dialogues

The annotators are instructed to adhere to the following guidelines
during dialogue creation:

- •
  To enhance understanding of negotiation and ensure the application of
  best negotiation practices, we follow [Chawla et al.
  (2021b)](#bib.bib8) and require the annotators to watch a tutorial on
  principled negotiation strategies before beginning their tasks;
- •
  Begin negotiations with offers that leave room for concessions and
  adjustments, allowing both sides to explore trade-offs;
- •
  Actively elicit and discuss the other party’s preferences, priorities,
  and constraints to identify opportunities for integrative outcomes;
- •
  Clearly communicate requirements and rationales, grounding arguments
  in realistic job interview (e.g., salary, promotion, benefits) or
  resource allocation (e.g., distribution of supplies, task
  responsibilities) scenarios;
- •
  Recognize and respond to the emotional states expressed by the user,
  ensuring that the agent applies ENS-CoT reasoning to perceive,
  understand, use, and manage emotions in a transparent manner;
- •
  Ensure that each agent response is paired with a concise ENS-CoT
  annotation, explicitly justifying how a particular emotion-aware
  negotiation strategy is chosen;
- •
  Aim for outcomes that balance self-interest with fairness, recognizing
  that perceptions of equity and rationality may differ across
  negotiating parties;
- •
  Use professional, and empathetic language consistently, especially in
  situations of disagreement or emotional tension, to maintain rapport
  and trust.

### A.4 Details of Dataset Quality Evaluation Criteria

1.  1.
    Emotional Intelligence (EI): Is the response appropriate to the
    user’s expressed emotion, reducing misattunement or escalation?
2.  2.
    Strategy Appropriateness (SA): Does the agent’s utterance
    operationalize the emotion-aware negotiation strategy?
3.  3.
    Interpretability (IN): Is the ENS-CoT rationale well-formed,
    complete, and useful?
4.  4.
    Fairness (F): Does the final outcome reflect a balanced, and win-win
    situation?
5.  5.
    Coherence (C): Is the overall dialogue well-structured and logical?
6.  6.
    Naturalness (N): Does the dialogue resembles the human negotiation?
7.  7.
    Interestingness (I): Is the dialogue engaging and rich in content to
    retain the user’s interest throughout the negotiation?

## Appendix B Experiment Details

### B.1 Implementation Details

All the experiments are implemented in [PyTorch](https://pytorch.org/)
using transformer architectures from the Hugging Face library [Wolf et
al. (2019)](#bib.bib48), and trained on an NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU (CUDA
12.8). During dataset preparation, ChatGPT model, GPT-4o-mini is used
via API for generating scenarios and dialogues with Top-p sampling
($`p=0.95`$) [Holtzman et al. (2019)](#bib.bib50) and temperature set to
$`0.9`$. During supervised initialization, the model is fine-tuned for 2
epochs with batch size 2. In Step 2 (a), 5 rationales per context are
generated with temperature 0.7, and $`\beta=0.1`$ in
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{PL}}(\theta^{\prime})`$. In Step 2 (b), 3
rationales per context are sampled from the DPO-refined model
$`\pi_{{\theta}^{\prime}}`$ with temperature 0.7. Models are optimized
using AdamW ([Kingma and Ba, 2014](#bib.bib49)) with weight decay 0.01,
gradient clipping 1.0, and a cosine LR scheduler at $`3e^{-7}`$ with 0.1
warm-up ratio. During decoding, the temperature is set to 0.7. For Step
2, the number of iterations is empirically set to $`3`$, with thresholds
$`\tau_{1}=0.8`$, $`\tau_{2}=0.4`$, and $`\tau_{3}=0.8`$. For OpenAI’s
GPT-5 experiment, we use gpt-5-mini via API [OpenAI (2025)](#bib.bib88).

#### B.1.1 Sensitivity Analysis of $`\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}`$

We select the thresholds $`\tau_{1}`$, $`\tau_{2}`$, and $`\tau_{3}`$
empirically by analyzing the cosine similarity distribution between
generated and reference responses on the development set. Specifically,
we choose $`\tau_{1}`$ and $`\tau_{3}`$ as higher similarity thresholds
to identify high-confidence positive samples that reliably preserve both
the intended emotion and the corresponding emotion-aware negotiation
strategy, while $`\tau_{2}`$ is chosen as a lower similarity threshold
to capture clearly suboptimal or misaligned responses. A margin between
these thresholds is intentionally maintained to exclude ambiguous pairs
that may introduce noise during preference learning. Empirically,
responses with cosine similarity above $`0.8`$ consistently maintain
emotional and strategic alignment, whereas responses with similarity
below $`0.4`$ frequently exhibit semantic drift, such as generic replies
or incorrect emotion or strategy usage.

To assess sensitivity, we evaluate
$`\tau_{1},\tau_{3}\in\{0.75,0.80,0.85\}`$ and
$`\tau_{2}\in\{0.35,0.40,0.45\}`$. As shown in Table

across both JobNego and ResNego datasets, the configuration
$`\tau_{1}=\tau_{3}=0.8`$ and $`\tau_{2}=0.4`$ consistently yields the

### B.2 Baseline Details

1.  (i)
    Llama-2-7b-SFT [Touvron et al. (2023)](#bib.bib35): Fine-tuning
    LLama-2-7b model on negotiation dialogues in a supervised setting.
2.  (ii)
    Llama-3.2-3b-SFT [Touvron et al. (2023)](#bib.bib35): Fine-tuning
    LLama-3.2-3b-instruct model on negotiation dialogues in a supervised
    setting.
3.  (iii)
    Emotion-aware SFT (Llama-2-7b): Fine-tuning the LLama-2-7b model on
    negotiation dialogues with emotions in a supervised setting.
4.  (iv)
    AnE [Zhang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib40): Query the LLM (GPT-4o-mini:
    acting as a strategy expert) with the targeted context to reason
    about the agent’s strategy and then prompt another LLM (GPT-5-mini)
    to generate the response based on the selected strategy.
5.  (v)
    ICL-AIF [Fu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib39): Engage another LLM
    (GPT-4o-mini) to provide feedback that helps the agents refine their
    strategy (unlike AnE, the feedback is given at the dialogue level
    and iterates) and finally generates the response using the chosen
    strategy.
6.  (vi)
    Standard (GPT-5-mini) [Deng et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib36): Prompting
    GPT-5-mini using task instruction without considering any strategy.
7.  (vii)
    Proactive (GPT-5-mini) [Deng et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib36): Prompting
    GPT-5-mini to first choose the strategy for the agent’s turn, then
    generate the response conditioned on that chosen strategy.
8.  (viii)
    ProCoT (GPT-5-mini) [Deng et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib36): Extend
    Proactive by prompting GPT-5-mini to generate a chain-of-thought
    analysis that plans the strategy for the agent’s turn, then generate
    the response.
9.  (ix)
    PPDPP [Deng et al. (2024)](#bib.bib41): A plug-and-play, tunable LM
    policy planner trained with SFT plus RL from goal-oriented AI
    feedback via self-play to plan the agent’s strategies.
10. (x)
    RLHF [Ouyang et al. (2022)](#bib.bib42): Refine a model previously
    fine-tuned on context–response pairs via supervised learning using
    Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [Schulman et al.
    (2017)](#bib.bib37), leveraging the same preference pairs generated
    in Step 2 in an RL setup with KL regularization.

### B.3 Evaluation Metrics Details

##### Automatic Evaluation Metrics.

Perplexity (PPL) [Brown et al. (1992)](#bib.bib45) measures how well the
model predicts the next token in a response. BLEU-4 (B-4) [Papineni et
al. (2002)](#bib.bib46) assesses the overlap of 4-grams between
generated and ground-truth responses. Distinct-3 (D-3) [Li et al.
(2015)](#bib.bib44) evaluates lexical diversity by computing the
proportion of unique trigrams in the generated responses. BERTScore-F1
(BS-F1) [Zhang et al. (2019)](#bib.bib43)⁴⁴ 4 BERTScore:
[https://huggingface.co/spaces/evaluate-metric/bertscore](https://huggingface.co/spaces/evaluate-metric/bertscore).
Emotion Appropriateness (EA) evaluates the appropriateness of users’
perceived emotions, and Emotion-aware Negotiation Strategy Consistency
(ENSC) assesses the consistency of generated responses with the intended
strategies.

##### Human Evaluation Metrics.

Fluency (F) assesses grammatical correctness and overall language
quality. Coherence (C) measures whether responses are logically
consistent and contextually relevant within the dialogue. Engagingness
(E) evaluates how interesting and captivating the conversation is for
the user. Bargaining Efficacy (BE) gauges the system’s ability to
bargain or present incentives that can influence the counterpart’s
decisions. Outcome Fairness (OF) examines whether the negotiation
results in balanced and equitable agreements. Additionally, we include
EA and ENSC, which mirror their automatic counterparts but are judged by
humans to provide a more reliable assessment of emotion appropriateness
and emotion-aware negotiation strategy consistency.

##### Human Evaluation Process.

The human evaluation is conducted by three independent evaluators⁵⁵ 5
These evaluators are separate from those who participated in the dataset
preparation and are compensated in accordance with institutional
guidelines.. Two of them hold Ph.D. degrees in Linguistics, while the
third has a postgraduate degree in Computer Science. All evaluators have
substantial prior experience with similar tasks. Before the evaluation,
the annotators are briefed about negotiation scenarios. Each annotator
carries out multi-turn negotiation sessions with the system for a given
scenario, and completes 25 interactions each using different response
sets, resulting in a total of 75 human-evaluated negotiation dialogues.
After finishing the interactions, each dialogue is rated for F, C, E,
EA, ENSC, BE, and OF on the scale of 1-5 (low to high). We compute
Fleiss’ $`\kappa`$ [McHugh (2012)](#bib.bib10) for all human-rated
dimensions, and obtain $`\kappa`$ = 0.73 (F), 0.78 (C), 0.76 (E), 0.81
(EA), 0.79 (ENSC), 0.74 (BE), and 0.72 (OF), indicating substantial
agreement among evaluators.

providing a more persuasive, proactive, and emotionally intelligent
````

# Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning

Kevin Frans Affiliation: Cursor Research Affiliation: UC Berkeley   
Alexander M. Rush Affiliation: Cursor Research    Sergey Levine
Affiliation: UC Berkeley    Pieter Abbeel Affiliation: UC Berkeley   
Ashvin Nair Affiliation: Cursor Research

###### Abstract

Naive on-policy RL is often bottlenecked by the capabilities of the base
model — insufficient coverage can result in an inability to learn from
certain rewards. At the same time, rewards in modern post-training
systems often come from rich textual sources, e.g. judge models with a
rubric of criteria. Can we utilize such structures to provide a rich
directional signal that enables improving on otherwise difficult to
optimize rewards? In this work, we examine a framework we refer to as
targeted guidance that learns from per-turn textual feedback via an
on-policy self-distillation objective. Our focus is not only on directly
improving hard-to-optimize behaviors, but on how to do so throughout the
training process without degrading baseline RL performance. Over a range
of settings across an agentic multi-turn software engineering
environment, we find success by combining targeted self-distillation
with a per-criteria RL reward, and by limiting feedback to turns where
the policy specifically fails. We further examine the design choices
that lead to stable entropy and reduce oscillatory behavior throughout
training.

## 1 Introduction

The strength of large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) has been well
established in recent years. However, there is often a limit to how much
a policy can improve from naive on-policy RL \[[69](#bib.bib20)\]. The
primary issue is that of insufficient coverage – even if a reward
function is carefully selected to encourage desired behavior (e.g. use a
new tool with the right format), the policy will nevertheless fail to
improve if it does not have a chance of performing this behavior
\[[18](#bib.bib9), [71](#bib.bib8)\]. These problems are especially
prominent in agentic settings, with multi-turn tool-calling, where a
policy may be required to chain together many complex actions in order
to receive a reward signal. Fundamentally, while RL incentivizes
existing good behaviors, such methods do not communicate how to perform
new desirable behaviors.

At the same time, we argue that sources of signal for communicating new
behaviors are already present in many modern post-training systems.
Rewards in such systems often come from a range of rich textual sources,
e.g. judge models provided with a rubric of stylistic criteria. Naive RL
methods condense the set of such signals into a single scalar reward,
incurring a significant loss of information. Rather, can we utilize
these existing sources to provide rich directional signal that allows us
to learn from otherwise hard-to-optimize rewards?

In this work, we explore a framework we refer to as targeted guidance
which internalizes per-turn textual feedback via an expert distillation
objective. The main idea is that each binary reward component can
additionally define a textual feedback string. If a given rollout or
agent turn fails to satisfy a given reward criteria, then alongside the
typical policy gradient loss, we minimize KL towards an expert defined
as a policy with the same underlying weights, but with the given
feedback placed in context. For multi-turn settings, feedback is placed
directly prior to the offending agent turn, and only that turn is
affected by the distillation loss.

Our algorithmic setup builds on a rich body of prior work often referred
to as on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) methods \[[72](#bib.bib58),
[58](#bib.bib72), [27](#bib.bib11), [62](#bib.bib7)\]. In contrast to
the one-shot benchmarks typically studied in such works, however, the
main intent of this work is to study how such methods can be introduced
directly to practical agentic RL systems with minimal regret to baseline
RL performance. Naive on-policy distillation methods have previously
been shown to cause phenomena such as reasoning
shortening \[[31](#bib.bib21), [30](#bib.bib22), [48](#bib.bib23)\] or
diversity collapse \[[28](#bib.bib24), [2](#bib.bib25)\], both of which
can lead to critical issues if affecting primary RL performance. We find
that by combining a self-distillation objective with a per-criteria
standard RL reward, and by limiting the distillation loss to the turns
where the policy specifically fails, we are able to stabilize the
learning process and broaden the policy capabilities while maintaining
performance on primary RL tasks.

![Refer to caption](2611.05266v1/imgs/fig1_new2.png)

Figure 1: Naive RL fails to optimize criterion that fall outside the
support of base policies. Exploration issues in RL can manifest as slow
learning (middle) or in the worst case a complete failure to improve
(left). On the other hand, targeted guidance enables policies to
directionally improve specific aspects of their behavior and thus
continue to learn.

We empirically examine our methodology throughout an agentic setting in
which a Qwen3-Coder-30B model is post-trained to solve software
engineering tasks through multi-turn harness involving tool calling and
code editing. Notably our focus is not on improving coding pass-rate
(which a well-tuned RL procedure can already do well), but rather on
improving specific criterion in which naive RL procedures fail to
improve – e.g. calling under-trained tool formats or writing with a
specific style. We show that targeted guidance results in stable
exploratory improvement even when multiple conflicting criterion are
present, or a certain criterion must be satisfied across multiple turns.
We find that by appropriately selecting the distillation weight and
applying distillation to internal thinking traces, we are able to
stabilize the entropy and reduce oscillations in the policy training
trajectory.

## 2 Related Work

Exploration in deep reinforcement learning. Exploration has been a
recurring theme of deep reinforcement learning, with a large body of
work considering exploration from the perspectives of
state-coverage \[[7](#bib.bib26), [23](#bib.bib27), [34](#bib.bib28),
[13](#bib.bib29), [39](#bib.bib30)\], curiosity \[[46](#bib.bib31),
[8](#bib.bib32)\], or skill discovery \[[20](#bib.bib33),
[15](#bib.bib34), [45](#bib.bib35), [3](#bib.bib36), [49](#bib.bib37),
[16](#bib.bib38)\]. A number of works have accordingly applied such
methodologies to the language model RL setting \[[70](#bib.bib39),
[63](#bib.bib40), [64](#bib.bib41), [11](#bib.bib42), [19](#bib.bib43),
[37](#bib.bib45), [52](#bib.bib44)\]. More uniquely to language models,
the idea of exploration via strong pretrained priors has been explored
in forms such as self-reflection \[[60](#bib.bib46), [43](#bib.bib47),
[53](#bib.bib48), [33](#bib.bib49)\] or process
rewards \[[65](#bib.bib50), [38](#bib.bib51), [66](#bib.bib52),
[56](#bib.bib53)\]. A solid body of work has explored how privileged
information (e.g. human reference solutions) can be used as exploratory
signal \[[67](#bib.bib56), [44](#bib.bib57), [51](#bib.bib54),
[17](#bib.bib55), [10](#bib.bib62), [73](#bib.bib59), [62](#bib.bib7)\].
Our work most similarly relates to the latter class of methods, in that
we aim to utilize signal from privileged textual feedback to broaden the
coverage of policies. Unlike most prior settings, we focus specifically
on stabilizing such techniques when combined with agentic multi-turn RL.

Distillation signal from expert policies. It is well-known that strong
experts can provide rich signal to improve downstream models
 \[[24](#bib.bib63), [32](#bib.bib64)\]. From an RL perspective, a body
of works claim rich generalization can arise when distillation signal is
be generated in an _on-policy_ manner  \[[54](#bib.bib65),
[2](#bib.bib25), [21](#bib.bib66), [41](#bib.bib67)\]. In cases where a
strong expert model is not available, prior works have examined the
question of whether a strong policy constructed by re-using the same
weights as the student, but with privileged information placed in the
context  \[[6](#bib.bib68), [61](#bib.bib69), [68](#bib.bib70),
[40](#bib.bib71), [72](#bib.bib58), [58](#bib.bib72), [47](#bib.bib73),
[27](#bib.bib11), [62](#bib.bib7)\], often referred to as on-policy
self-distillation (OPSD) methods. Our work builds off prior OPSD
methodology and most closely relates to methods that encourage stability
by gating distillation signal to failing rollouts \[[36](#bib.bib17),
[12](#bib.bib18), [42](#bib.bib19)\]. However, while these works
consider using distillation to improve a single monolithic reward, our
work instead aims to improve multiple auxiliary criterion (e.g. style,
tool formats, etc) while preventing degradation in baseline RL
performance.

## 3 Background and Preliminaries

Markov decision process. We define a Markov decision process (MDP) by a
state space $`S`$, an action space $`A`$, an initial state distribution
$`p(s_{0})`$, a transition function $`p(s^{\prime}|s,a)`$, and a reward
$`r(s)`$. We define a parameterized policy $`\pi_{\theta}(a|s)`$ as a
probability distribution of actions, which combined with the MDP creates
a distribution of trajectories $`p(\tau)`$. We write the return on
trajectories as $`R(\tau)`$.

Language models as policies. Throughout this work, we focus specifically
on a policy class defined by a neural network language model. We assume
access to a base model which defines a starting policy $`\pi_{0}`$. For
the agentic use case, the base model is generally a pretrained language
model that has gone through a supervised finetuning (SFT) phase to adapt
it to the tool call setting. As this work explicitly considers
multi-turn environments with agents, we abstract away from individual
tokens and refer to actions as an entire set of autoregressively
generated tokens in sequence, e.g. a specific tool call. We assume that
the Markovian state is fully defined by the sequence of previous
observations and actions and their underlying tokens.

Rewards and judges. The reward function for this system $`R(\tau)`$ is
nonzero only at the end of the rollout. We assume that the reward is a
weighted additive function $`R(\tau)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}R_{i}(\tau)`$ of
a prespecified set of criteria. The primary component of the reward is
verification of task completion, but there are a collection of other
properties such adherence to stylistic guides or use of specific tools.
We will assume these rewards properties are mostly independent, e.g. it
is possible to modify style without impacting task completion. While not
central to the method, we primarily focus on the case of judge-based
rewards, where each criterion $`R_{i}`$ is implemented as a call to
judge LLM. This judge is equipped with additional privileged information
on the correct way to solve the problem, and in addition to producing
reward scores, can provide textual feedback justifying its result.

## 4 Textual Feedback as a Targeted Directional Signal

![Refer to caption](2611.05266v1/imgs/fig_method2.png)

Figure 2: Targeted guidance derives per-token signal from per-turn
hints. During the course of a standard RL procedure, in cases where
rollouts fail to satisfy certain criteria, e.g. to utilize a certain
writing style, textual feedback is employed to guide the policy towards
the solution. We instantiate this via a per-token KL loss against a
feedback-conditioned expert policy.

As illustrated in
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning"),
it is often the case that naive policy gradients will fail to optimize
even simple criterion, if the actions that satisfy such criterion are
not within the support of the base policy. In the following section, we
describe a strategy to construct a source of directional policy
improvement signal, which communicates not which rollouts were
successful, but rather how to modify specific aspects of a policy’s
behavior.

Our main approach is to utilize a per-token distillation target to
provide this directional signal
([Figure 2](#S4.F2 "In 4 Textual Feedback as a Targeted Directional Signal ‣ Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning")).
Rather than returning a single scalar, each reward component can return
textual feedback in the form of a feedback string $`f`$ associated with
a specific turn of the agent rollout. Inserting $`f`$ before that turn
defines an expert that shares the current policy’s weights,
$`\mu(\cdot\mid s)=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s,f)`$ at targeted turns and
$`\mu=\pi_{\theta}`$ elsewhere. Following what is often referred to as
on-policy self distillation \[[72](#bib.bib58)\], we minimize KL between
the live policy and $`\mu`$ over the on-policy state distribution
$`d^{\pi}`$, treating $`\mu`$ as fixed within an update.

Expert distillation as approximate policy improvement. We base our
algorithmic considerations on classical policy improvement arguments
\[[29](#bib.bib14), [55](#bib.bib15), [1](#bib.bib16)\]. A policy
$`\mu`$ provides a useful improvement direction if its expected
advantage on the current policy’s state distribution is positive:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi}}\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\mu(\cdot\mid s)}\big[A^{\pi}(s,a)\big]>0,
``` |  | (1) |

in which case a sufficiently small step towards $`\mu`$ results in an
improved policy. In the case where distillation is understood to specify
how certain behaviors (e.g. code style) can improve, but should not
affect other behaviors (e.g. code behavior), we in general prefer
experts that limit their differences from the base policy, i.e.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d^{\pi}}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\mu(\cdot\mid s)\big)\Big]<\delta.
``` |  | (2) |

While neither condition is guaranteed in practice, we use these as
design heuristics: feedback should result in an expert better than the
base policy, and the expert should otherwise remain as close to the
policy as possible.

Stabilizing the targeted guidance update. The aim of our proposed
framework is to integrate targeted distillation-based guidance into
agentic RL in a way that balances exploratory pressure across targeted
facets while ensuring that baseline RL performance is not affected.
Combining the approaches naively leads to some risk of policy collapse
or stagnation. We therefore introduce two simple ideas that stabilize
the training procedure, as described below.

The first main idea is that we would like to minimize the amount of
unnecessary pressure from the distillation objective, which can be
heuristically understood as tightening the divergence between the expert
and live policy
([Equation 2](#S4.E2 "In 4 Textual Feedback as a Targeted Directional Signal ‣ Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning")).
Thus, in contrast to prior work which places privileged information into
a global context to construct the self-distillation target, we place the
feedback string right before the affected agent turn, and mask out the
KL loss on all other turns. Additionally, the distillation objective is
only evaluated on agent turns that contain a failed criterion.

The second core idea is that while self-distillation can provide a
strong directional signal of improvement, it is less clear that such
objectives result in stable convergence. For this reason, our proposed
methodology is to combine the distillation signal with a standard RL
reward on each desired criteria. The intuition is that distillation
serves as a targeted way to ensure the policy learns the relevant
behavior, while it is the unbiased RL objective that enables the policy
to retain and refine such behaviors.

The distillation loss in our setting is calculated via reverse KL
distance between the student and the teacher. In our experiments, we
found that forward-KL, Jensen-Shannon distance, and Rao-Blackwellized KL
\[[4](#bib.bib4)\] performed similarly, although an in-depth study was
not performed. To reduce undesirable effects from inference-trainer
mismatch, we utilize a full-vocabulary KL loss computed entirely on the
trainer side, rather than a sample-based estimator. The combined
objective is therefore:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)+\lambda\sum_{t\in\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t})\,\|\,\text{sg}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t},f_{t}))\big),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\mathcal{F}`$ is the set of agent turns that fail a criterion,
$`f_{t}`$ is the feedback for turn $`t`$, $`\lambda`$ is the
distillation weight. The ‘sg‘ operator represents stop gradient.
Additionally, we normalize the distillation KL term across all tokens
present in a given rollout, reducing the impact of long rollouts with
high KL distances, which often correspond to undesirable states such as
looped reasoning.

How should one design the textual feedback content? Recall that an
important criterion for expert guidance to be an effective improvement
operator is that the expert itself must have higher expected reward
([Equation 1](#S4.E1 "In 4 Textual Feedback as a Targeted Directional Signal ‣ Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning")).
As experts in our case are defined as the same model but with certain
information in the context, we can iterate on this context via a simple
procedure – query the base model with a considered feedback string,
measure average performance against the contextless model, and only
allow prompts which show an average improvement. Note that although we
evaluate the feedback via autoregressive generation in this procedure,
the actual application of this feedback is for per-token on-policy
distillation.

Is targeted guidance asymptotically unbiased? While auxiliary signals
can be necessary to solve hard exploration problems, they can at times
prevent policies from converging to the optimal policy. As an example,
it is possible that
$`\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t})\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t},f_{t})\big)`$
is nonzero even for an optimal policy due to spurious affects of
conditioning on feedback. For this reason, we instead argue that by
gating the distillation loss to only affect failing turns, the loss
function simplifies to a simple policy gradient loss in the case that
all feedback criterion are consistently satisfied, and our procedure
will converge to the optimal policy assuming that such a policy exists.

What is the computational cost of targeted guidance? For many practical
RL systems, the main computational cost is in sampling rollouts rather
than training on them. A benefit of the on-policy distillation
formulation is that the teacher is used purely for logit-level guidance
and does not require autoregressive generation. The memory requirements
of full-logit distillation can be alleviated via kernels that
materialize only the last hidden layer representation \[[25](#bib.bib6),
[22](#bib.bib5)\]. In this way, the main cost of adding targeted
guidance to a standard RL procedure is a single additional forward pass
to gather expert logit targets.

## 5 Empirical Experiments with Targeted guidance

### 5.1 Didactic Example: Tool Format Correction

![Refer to caption](2611.05266v1/imgs/fig_didactic2.png)

Figure 3: Didactic example: Targeted guidance teaches new tool call
formats. In cases where the base policy fails to fulfill certain
criterion, e.g. to utilize a certain tool call format, textual feedback
is utilized to guide the policy towards the solution. We instantiate
this via a per-token KL loss against a feedback-conditioned expert
policy. As shown on the right in red, only the token in which the
student and expert diverge (in this case, the tool format delimiter)
receives a strong signal.

As a motivating example, we examine an auxiliary criterion that agent
policies struggle with: learning to use new tools. Oftentimes, one may
want to introduce new tools or tool arguments during the RL phase, even
when the base model has already been through a supervised fine-tuning
phase that did not include this tool. We show now that targeted guidance
provides a natural way to address this problem without performance
regression.

Our specific setup is the optimization of a multi-turn agentic coding
policy, in which an agent utilizes a series of tool calls to solve a
given problem, as evaluated by hidden test cases. We train such a model
with standard policy gradient methods, assigning each rollout a weighted
sum of pass-rate, tool format, and length-penalty rewards. We consider
the setting in which we are introducing a new tool-calling format to the
“semantic search” tool. This format involves specifying an additional
parameter: the number of lines that the search should return. Naively,
the base model fails to utilize this new format at all, even when
example usage is placed in the prompt, in part due to SFT not including
this new format.

To fix this behavior, we can utilize targeted guidance. During training,
whenever the agent uses the semantic search tool without the correct
format, we inject a feedback string right before the turn with the
error. The policy conditioned on the feedback string then becomes an
expert to serve as a source of guidance signal, which we combine with
the standard policy gradient loss during RL training.

As shown in
[Figure 3](#S5.F3 "In 5.1 Didactic Example: Tool Format Correction ‣ 5 Empirical Experiments with Targeted guidance ‣ Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning"),
a policy trained with targeted guidance learns the new tool calling
format, without suffering a regression on the primary coding
performance. We attribute this to the guidance signal being targeted and
minimal – as shown on the right, only tokens which show strong
disagreement to the expert receive a large signal. Importantly, guidance
is always done on on-policy rollouts exactly where the error occurs, to
reduce any spurious distillation effects. As the model fixes the
undesired behavior, the guidance signal accordingly drops to near zero.

We now examine applying targeted guidance in a more complex setting, in
which we desire to optimize a suite of criteria, some of which may
conflict with each other. Specifically, we focus on the problem of
improving the communication style of coding agents. We first define a
set of binary stylistic criterion to be verified by an external judge,
such as “include a markdown table” or “describe the user problem”. For
each criterion, we define a binary reward function along with an
associated static feedback string.

The aim is to enable policies to reliably satisfy the stylistic
criterion, while also maintaining performance levels on the primary
coding tasks. In particular, our goal is that policies trained via
targeted guidance should explore, while also not showing any degradation
on coding pass-rate as compared to a baseline policy trained without any
style criterion at all.

Our base policy is a Qwen3-Coder-30B model further SFT’d for agentic
coding in our harness. In all methods below, we combine various
style-optimization methodologies with a baseline GRPO algorithm that
optimizes for coding pass-rate and regularizes total output length. We
train for 200 steps with a batch size of 32 groups with 32 rollouts
each, using Adam and a learning rate of 3e-6. The training dataset is a
single-epoch set of software engineering problems, i.e. the same prompt
is only ever trained on once. We utilize an asynchronous RL
pipeline \[[50](#bib.bib61)\] where the inference policy may be older
than the currently trained policy. For all distillation-style methods,
we implement the same distillation operator of a full KL distance over
the entire vocabulary, and we include both thinking and tool call tokens
in the loss. We sweep the distillation weight and RL style reward weight
hyperparameters.

We compare against a variety of representative methods:

- •
  Baseline represents a policy that only optimizes the standard coding
  reward. This serves as a baseline for the expected coding pass-rate
  when we do not take style criteria into account.
- •
  GRPO \[[57](#bib.bib60)\] shows the performance of a standard RL
  policy where each stylistic criteria is assigned a binary reward,
  whose sum is optimized as part of the standard GRPO objective.
- •
  OPSD \[[72](#bib.bib58)\], a representative on-policy distillation
  method, which makes use of the per-criterion feedback to provide
  distillation signal. Unlike our proposed method, no additional RL
  reward is used beyond the baseline.
- •
  Online SFT, a representative iterated SFT method, in which the
  feedback is used to construct an explicit teacher policy which
  autoregressively generates alternative teacher turns to replace turns
  that fail a criterion. These alternative turns are then used as SFT
  targets for the live policy during the RL process.
- •
  LaTRO \[[10](#bib.bib62)\], a representative likelihood-as-reward
  method. Similarly to online SFT, we construct alternative turns from a
  teacher. The log-likelihood of the alternative turns are then assigned
  as a reward for all previous turns in the rollout.
- •
  Verifree \[[73](#bib.bib59)\], which we instantiate as using the same
  framework as LaTRO except using the raw likelihood as the reward.
- •
  Targeted Guidance (ours), in which both GRPO and distillation are
  combined. A distillation loss is only applied to turns in which a
  nonzero set of criterion are unfulfilled.

As shown in
[Table 1](#S5.T1 "In 5.2 Can Targeted guidance work when considering a suite of criteria? ‣ 5 Empirical Experiments with Targeted guidance ‣ Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning"),
policies trained with targeted guidance are able to improve on a wide
range of evaluation criteria, while maintaining a coding pass-rate
similar to that of standard GRPO. Naive OPSD maintains a relatively
healthy coding pass-rate, however, the improvements on style criteria
are less consistent, resulting in a worse final performance.

On the other hand, the online SFT, Verifree, and LaTRO policies result
in a non-trivial degradation in coding pass-rate. In the case of online
SFT, this may be because the continual SFT loss during RL limits how
much the policy can change at each step, especially in an asynchronous
setting. Verifree and LaTRO result in a further degradation in coding
pass-rate. We hypothesize that the instability of these methods comes
from the fact that unlike the on-policy methods, these three rely on
sampling entirely new actions from the feedback-conditioned expert.
While increasing the likelihood of such actions may help in terms of
style, such actions may have unintended consequences in terms of primary
coding pass-rate. Thus, it is likely that instability arises from
increasing likelihood of potentially suboptimal actions, especially in
an asynchronous regime where such actions may become stale.

### 5.3 Does Targeted guidance remain stable even when optimizing multi-turn interleaved feedback?

The above experimental setting considered stylistic criteria on the
final message style, which only occurs at the end of an agentic rollout.
We are also interested in improving the behavior of non-final actions
that occur throughout an agent’s rollout.

To examine this, we construct a setting where each agent turn is graded
on verifiable auxiliary criteria. To study this setting, we adopt a
criterion that requires significant deviation from the base model
behavior: each turn must include a status report in the form of a poem.
This is an out-of-distribution behavior that lies outside the support of
the base model.

[Figure 5](#S5.F5 "In 5.3 Does Targeted guidance remain stable even when optimizing multi-turn interleaved feedback? ‣ 5 Empirical Experiments with Targeted guidance ‣ Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning")
showcases the learning trajectories of targeted guidance and baseline
methods under the every-turn criterion setting. The GRPO baseline here
results in a consistent coding pass-rate improvement, as expected, but
completely fails to optimize the poem reward criterion. In comparison,
the policy trained with targeted guidance is able to adopt the poem
output style while minimizing regression on coding performance.

Ablations on two aspects of the framework reveal their necessity. In the
OPSD run, where expert distillation guidance is applied but not combined
with a corresponding reward for poem-correction, the model does learn to
output poems at a reasonable rate, but the policy itself is unstable and
fails to consistently hold this behavior. In addition, there is a
significant regression to coding pass-rate. A similar regression also
occurs when utilizing a variant in which the poem feedback is always
applied, regardless of whether that particular rollout was successful or
not, highlighting the necessity of gating distillation only to failing
turns.

### 5.4 What are the failure cases of the method, and how can they be alleviated?

Stabilizing entropy. We find that an early predictor of collapse when
utilizing distillation signal is a sharp increase in policy entropy. In
our setting, this is accompanied with a steady increase in the average
output length, and is quickly followed by a decrease in the coding
pass-rate.

An increase in entropy from exploratory signal is expected, to some
degree. As by nature the behaviors that require exploratory signal are
low likelihood under the live policy, increasing the likelihood of such
behaviors will cause an increase in entropy. The issue is not entropy
increase in general, but rather a spike in entropy that implies an
over-correction or collapse. One possible explanation of the severity of
such spikes is due to the recursive nature of self-distillation – not
only does the student increase in entropy, but the expert distribution
may also increase as both policies share the same underlying weights.
Continual self-distillation in this manner can thus result in a sharp
spike as entropy increase is consistently reinforced.

As shown in
[Figure 6](#S5.F6 "In 5.3 Does Targeted guidance remain stable even when optimizing multi-turn interleaved feedback? ‣ 5 Empirical Experiments with Targeted guidance ‣ Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning"),
we can replicate entropy spiking settings by increasing the weight of
the distillation signal. While high distillation weight runs display a
higher style reward at the start, they quickly collapse and end up
failing in both pass rate and in style criterion. On the other hand,
lower distillation weights do not show this type of instability, but in
turn improve on style at a slower rate. We find that a static parameter
is enough to remain in the stable regime, but further efficiency may be
gained via dynamic weighting \[[35](#bib.bib10), [59](#bib.bib12)\].

Stabilizing intra-reward oscillations. A phenomenon that appears when
examining the set of stylistic rewards is that the policy oscillates
between satisfying a subset of the criterion, improving on some
criterion while degrading on others. We hypothesize this behavior arises
from the competing pressure for all stylistic rewards – when a
distillation signal is present to push the model towards a certain
style, the model may in turn be disincentivized to use a different
style.

We find that such oscillatory behavior becomes much less noticeable when
the distillation signal is applied also to the thinking tokens before
the final agent message. Without distillation pressure on the thinking
tokens, the policy shows highly oscillatory behavior. When thinking
token KL is enabled, the behavior is instead a stable curve and
increasing monotonically
([Figure 7](#S5.F7 "In 5.3 Does Targeted guidance remain stable even when optimizing multi-turn interleaved feedback? ‣ 5 Empirical Experiments with Targeted guidance ‣ Stabilizing On-Policy Targeted Guidance in Agentic Reinforcement Learning")).

## 6 Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a simple targeted guidance approach that
combines textual feedback self-distillation with standard on-policy RL.
This allows for optimization of rewards criteria that may be difficult
or impossible to optimize with policy gradients alone, while remaining
stable enough to not interfere with baseline RL performance.

Limitations. One limitation of our proposed framework is that each
reward in the suite requires a designed static feedback string.
Follow-up work may examine whether strong feedback can be automatically
learned, perhaps via a tied RL process. Such processes may also see
whether a gating process or weighting between RL signal and distillation
signal can be automatically learned.

Experimentally, the settings studied in this work focus solely on the
multi-turn software engineering setup, and our targets for guidance
largely revolve around style and formatting rather than primary
intelligence. We note that there exist prior works which span a wide set
of OPSD variants \[[72](#bib.bib58), [58](#bib.bib72), [47](#bib.bib73),
[27](#bib.bib11), [62](#bib.bib7), [36](#bib.bib17), [12](#bib.bib18),
[42](#bib.bib19)\] which we were unable to port and compare to
head-to-head under our experimental setting. A breakdown of which atomic
design choices matter in the style of \[[26](#bib.bib3),
[14](#bib.bib2), [5](#bib.bib1)\] would likely be of interest.

Future work. Central to this method is the use of language as
communicative medium between the reward and the policy, i.e. the reward
judge needs a method for producing targeted textual feedback and the
teacher model needs to condition on this information in an effective
manner. In this work, we rely on the zero-shot ability of the policy to
process this information and human prompting for the judge to produce
effective feedback. We anticipate that further SFT on the policy can
improve the effectiveness of distillation signal, and that the reward
judges themselves could be fine tuned to produce more targeted hints
that minimize reward interference. Both operations above could
potentially be optimized via an interleaved meta optimization loop.

The approach in this work focused specifically on a per-token
distillation approach to utilizing feedback. However, there may be
strong benefits from allowing the teacher to autoregressively generate
alternative turns, or otherwise rewrite the on-policy rollouts. Recent
work on teacher generation approaches, such as pedagogical
RL \[[9](#bib.bib13)\], suggest that training teacher models to provide
guidance that is closer to the student distribution might be an
effective way forward.

Finally, the feedback sources in this work focuses on targeted guidance
at the turn-level. This approach fits with the reward criteria that were
targeted for this work. A natural question is whether a similar approach
could be used for dense guidance on the full rollout. While RL is a
strong baseline in this setting, these results indicate that with the
right stabilization text feedback may be able to complement with global
exploration.
````

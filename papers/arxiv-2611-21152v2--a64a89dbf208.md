###### Abstract

Training coding agents for long-horizon tasks requires environments with
verifiable feedback. Existing pipelines often follow a linear process
that ends with validation, establishing usability but providing little
guidance on improving training value. Recursive self-improvement (RSI)
often requires model rollouts or model training followed by downstream
evaluation, with costs and feedback delays amplified on long-horizon
tasks. How can we use timely, reliable proxy signals to improve training
value and synthesize environments at scale? We present RepoFactory, an
automated pipeline that converts real repositories into verifiable
environments where agents build complete projects from scratch given a
language specification. Its Environment Evolution Loop optimizes a
multiplicative objective combining validity, structural coupling, and
implementation scope through proxy feedback. RepoFactory produces 10,159
environments across 17 software domains. Training Qwen3.5-35B-A3B on
these environments yields RepoHorizen-35B-A3B, which achieves gains of
22.6 percentage points on NL2Repo-Bench and 8.5 points on average across
five benchmarks covering long-horizon coding and agentic tasks in
real-world production settings, compared with 17.5 and 6.4 points
without environment evolution. These results highlight the training
value of RepoFactory environments and the additional gains from the
Environment Evolution Loop.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being developed into
agents that can carry out consequential work in real-world
environments Team and others (2026); Xu et al. (2026); Team (2026); Yang
et al. (2026a); DeepSeek-AI et al. (). Unlike static language tasks,
agentic work unfolds through sustained interaction with environments.
This transition changes the objective of model training: a capable agent
must learn not only to produce correct answers, but also to make
coherent progress toward completing long-horizon tasks Li et al.
(2026a); Tian et al. (2026); Bajoria et al. (2026).

Software engineering has become a major application of coding agents.
Pipelines built around bug fixing and feature development have provided
verifiable tasks for training agents on real codebases Pan et al.
(2025); Yang et al. (2025). A successful local change, however, is only
part of the work involved in delivering software. Coding agents are
increasingly expected to handle more complex development processes and
produce complete, working systems Yang et al. (2026c); Team et al.
(2026). Training on short-horizon tasks alone cannot cover this broader
process, making it increasingly important to build repository-level
environments for agent training.

Constructing environments for long-horizon tasks at the repository level
remains challenging. (i) Rigid construction pipelines limit the usable
repository pool. DeNovoSWE Zhao et al. (2026) and MindForge Chen et al.
(2026) follow linear construction pipelines. They refine documentation
or resolve build failures while keeping the reference implementation
fixed. Repositories that remain unsuitable are discarded, even when
further revision could make them usable. Scaling therefore depends on
finding more repositories that meet the same fixed requirements. (ii)
Feedback focused on reliability overlooks training value.
RefactorForge Ebrahimi et al. (2026) and TrajLong Peng et al. (2026a)
design data for specific capabilities but do not explore how environment
feedback can improve task content. ProgramGym Yang et al. (2026b) uses
execution diagnostics and coverage feedback to repair builds and
strengthen tests around a fixed reference implementation. These signals
improve reliability without explicitly optimizing training value, which
may remain limited when a task requires little implementation work or
coordination across components. (iii) Feedback from model execution is
costly and time-consuming. BenchEvolver Wu et al. (2026) and SETA Shen
et al. (2026) use model performance to guide task revision. Obtaining
this feedback requires model rollouts on the candidate tasks. For
long-horizon coding tasks, each rollout can involve extensive
implementation, increasing computational costs and delaying subsequent
revisions. How to obtain timely, reliable feedback at low cost to
improve training value and synthesize long-horizon coding environments
at scale remains an open research question.

We present RepoFactory, an automated pipeline that transforms real-world
repositories into verifiable environments. We design these environments
around full repository generation task, in which an agent must build a
complete, functioning repository from scratch given a language
specification. We define a multiplicative objective combining validity,
structural coupling, and implementation scope. It provides timely
feedback at low cost without requiring a model to solve each candidate
task. Our Environment Evolution Loop uses this objective to guide joint
revisions to the oracle implementation, task specification, and tests.
Validity feedback helps the loop repair initially unsuitable
environments and recover repositories that would otherwise be discarded.
Structural coupling and implementation scope serve as proxies for
training value, directing revisions toward the implementation and
integration work required by the task. A construction agent chooses how
to modify each candidate and receives updated measurements after each
revision, iterating within a fixed construction budget. Our main
contributions are threefold:

- •
  We introduce RepoFactory, an automated pipeline that converts
  real-world projects into 10,159 verifiable full-repository generation
  environments across 17 software domains.
- •
  We develop an Environment Evolution Loop that optimizes a
  multiplicative objective combining validity, structural coupling, and
  implementation scope. Timely, low-cost proxy feedback guides
  improvements to both environment reliability and training value.
- •
  Training Qwen3.5-35B-A3B on RepoFactory yields RepoHorizen-35B-A3B,
  with gains of 22.6 percentage points on NL2Repo-Bench and 8.5 points
  on average across five coding and agentic benchmarks. Environment
  evolution contributes 5.1 and 2.1 points to these gains, respectively.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Scaling Verifiable Environments

Environment synthesis scales agent training by generating interactive
tasks with executable verification Kulikov et al. (2026); Ren et al.
(2026); Zhu et al. (2025). EnvScaler Song et al. (2026) and ScaleEnv Tu
et al. (2026) target tool use, while Endless Terminals Gandhi et al.
(2026), LiteCoder-Terminal Peng et al. (2026b), and CLI-Universe Hua et
al. (2026) extend this approach to terminal-agent training. Broader task
coverage, however, does not ensure that tasks preserve the
interdependent requirements of real software or demand sustained
development. RepoFactory derives full-repository generation tasks from
real projects, grounding task behavior in existing implementations and
tests. Reconstructing an entire repository requires sustained
implementation and integration across these interdependent components.

### 2.2 Recursive Synthesis and Agent Self-Improvement

Self-training reuses successful solutions as supervision, while
self-play generates new problems for agents to solve Chen et al. (2024);
Li et al. (2026b). BenchEvolver Wu et al. (2026) evolves reference
solutions to construct harder tasks, while SETA Shen et al. (2026)
adapts terminal environments using learner performance. DataMaster Du et
al. (2026) optimizes training data using downstream evaluation scores,
adding model training to the feedback cycle. These approaches rely on
solver rollouts or downstream training, increasing computational costs
and delaying feedback. To our knowledge, RepoFactory is the first to
evolve full-repository generation environments using an objective that
combines validity checks with inexpensive code metrics. This objective
provides timely feedback without requiring solver rollouts or downstream
training.

## 3 RepoFactory

RepoFactory converts real-world repositories into verifiable
environments where agents reconstruct complete projects from a language
specifications. The Environment Evolution Loop uses timely, low-cost
feedback from verification and code analysis to jointly revise oracle
implementations, task specifications, and tests, optimizing a
multiplicative objective over validity, structural coupling, and
implementation scope. All construction agents use Qwen3.8-Max. Figure 1
shows the overall pipeline.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation and Objective

Our goal is to transform a candidate repository $`r`$ into a training
environment $`e=(I,W,V,O)`$, where $`I`$ is the language specification,
$`W`$ is an isolated workspace and runtime, $`V`$ is the verifier, and
$`O`$ is the source repository’s oracle implementation. During training,
an agent $`\pi`$ receives $`(I,W)`$ and reconstructs a project
$`P=\pi(I,W)`$, which is scored by $`V(P)\in[0,1]`$.

We define the environment objective as

```math
Q=A\cdot C\cdot S, \tag{1}
```

where $`A`$, $`C`$, and $`S`$ measure validity, structural coupling, and
implementation scope, respectively. The validity term requires full
reward for the oracle and zero reward for the unchanged workspace
$`P_{0}`$:

```math
A=\begin{cases}1,&V(O)=1\ \text{and}\ V(P_{0})=0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} \tag{2}
```

We measure structural coupling with normalized propagation cost:

```math
C=\frac{R}{N^{2}}, \tag{3}
```

where $`N`$ is the number of functions in the analyzed oracle code and
$`R`$ is the number of reachable ordered function pairs in its static
internal call graph, including self-pairs.

Implementation scope combines code size and dependency breadth:

```math
S=\log_{10}(\mathrm{LOC})+\log_{10}(1+D)-2, \tag{4}
```

where $`\mathrm{LOC}`$ is the source code size and $`D`$ is the number
of direct runtime dependencies.

All three terms are evaluated on the current candidate. The Environment
Evolution Loop iteratively revises the environment to maximize $`Q`$
within a fixed construction budget.

### 3.2 Construction Pipeline

#### Repository Search and Filtering

RepoFactory searches public code-hosting platforms for candidate
repositories, excluding those included in established coding benchmarks
to avoid evaluation overlap. Eligible projects must provide package
metadata and a nonempty local test suite to support isolated
installation and evaluation. To balance task complexity with
reconstruction cost, we require at least five implementation files and
fewer than 40,000 lines of Python code. We exclude projects that require
external services, outbound network access, graphical interfaces, or
specialized hardware. Network-related projects remain eligible when
their tests mock external calls or use only local connections.

#### Runtime Construction and Repository Analysis

For each candidate repository, RepoFactory copies each candidate
repository into an isolated task directory and builds a Docker image
from its build files and dependency manifests. A agent installs
dependencies, identifies test commands, and revises the image or
execution configuration as needed. We retain a repository only when all
tests pass in a fresh container with bounded resources and no network
access. RepoFactory archives the validated image and records the
installation and test commands, package files, and test results.

After establishing the runtime, RepoFactory analyzes the repository in a
read only container to determine what the task specification must cover.
A scanner based on abstract syntax trees extracts public classes,
functions, and methods, together with their signatures, exceptions, and
return information. It also records repository symbols imported by tests
and builds an implementation tree excluding test files. These outputs
guide task synthesis and, together with dependency metadata, provide the
inputs for computing structural coupling and implementation scope.

#### Task Synthesis and Environment Materialization.

RepoFactory prepares an isolated workspace with dependencies installed
and disabled external network access. The coding agent must reconstruct
the project from the task specification, without access to the original
repository or evaluation code. The synthesis agent uses the repository
analysis to write this specification and an accompanying API manifest.
The specification describes the repository’s functionality and
structure, while the API manifest documents the public interfaces the
agent must implement.

Functionally correct implementations can still fail if their interface
names or import paths differ from those expected by the tests.
RepoFactory therefore checks the specification and API manifest against
the repository symbols imported by the original tests, returning missing
symbols and their import records to the synthesis agent for revision.
Once this check passes, RepoFactory combines the specification with the
validated runtime image to create the environment. The original
repository remains isolated as the oracle, and evaluation resources are
available only during verification.

#### Environment Evolution Loop

After materialization, RepoFactory first checks validity $`A`$. The
oracle $`O`$ must receive full reward, and the unchanged initial
workspace must receive zero reward. If either condition fails, the
construction agent uses the validation report and execution trace to
identify the cause and repair the candidate. The revised environment is
evaluated again, and optimization of scope and coupling begins only
after both checks pass.

A valid environment may still require little implementation work.
Implementation scope $`S`$ therefore guides revisions toward more
substantial reconstruction tasks. A larger project can still consist of
independent components, so structural coupling $`C`$ complements scope
by directing evolution toward more interdependent functionality. The
construction agent decides how to revise the oracle implementation, task
specification, and tests, keeping the description and evaluation
consistent with the revised functionality.

For each valid candidate, the agent compares $`S`$ and $`C`$ against
thresholds of $`0.5`$ and $`0.01`$, respectively, and prioritizes any
term below threshold. When both thresholds are met, the agent chooses
which term to optimize under $`Q`$. Each revision is rechecked for
validity before the scores are reassessed, with validity failures
returned to repair. A revision targeting $`S`$ or $`C`$ is retained only
if the selected score strictly increases; otherwise, RepoFactory
restores the previous version. The loop runs for up to 12 rounds,
assessing each revision through verifier execution and code analysis
without solving-agent rollouts or downstream training and evaluation.
RepoFactory uses this low-cost feedback to recover failed candidates and
improve valid tasks, increasing both the yield and training value of
repository-level environments.

## 4 Analysis of Synthesized Data

Effective training environments for long-horizon coding should expose
agents to diverse software and require substantial repository
reconstruction under reliable feedback across a broad range of
difficulty. Our 10,159 RepoFactory environments span 17 domains and
place agents in complete repository reconstruction tasks with executable
validation. To collect SFT data, we run Qwen3.8-Max five times on each
environment, filter out low reward trajectories, and retain 10,947
samples spanning 5,577 environments. We analyze the resulting collection
to characterize its diversity, scale, interaction length, and difficulty
with the statistics summarized in Figure 2.

#### Diversity.

The collection covers a broad range of application domains and technical
ecosystems, without being dominated by any single area, as shown in
Figure 2(a). The largest category accounts for only 12.5% of
environments, and only 6.9% fall outside the named domains, indicating
broad coverage rather than concentration. Its varied libraries,
interfaces, and implementation conventions expose agents to the
diversity of real software development. This breadth supports training
agents that generalize across software projects instead of specializing
in a narrow domain.

#### Scale and verification.

These environments require agents to reconstruct substantial codebases
rather than write isolated programs. As shown in Figure 2(b), the median
oracle contains 4.2k lines of source code, with a substantial tail of
larger systems. Across the 10,159 instances with successful test
parsing, the verifier contains 2.09 million statically identified test
definitions, providing dense coverage at the instance level. Because
these tests are inherited from the source repositories, they exercise
behavior across the public interface rather than checking only a few end
to end outcomes. Success therefore depends on implementing a coherent
repository whose components remain consistent under extensive
verification.

#### Repository Scale and Interaction diversity.

The environments and trajectories capture sustained development at the
repository level. The median oracle repository contains 26 source files,
and the median SFT trajectory contains 64 assistant turns, as shown in
Figure 2(c). A successful trajectory must keep track of a multi-file
project while successive edits are implemented and tested. Together, the
long-horizon trajectories and varied operations provide rich supervision
for maintaining project state as agents coordinate changes across files
and refine their implementations through execution feedback.

#### Implementation Scope.

Figure 2(d) shows the distribution of implementation scope $`S`$ and its
relationship with reconstruction performance. We measure each
environment’s success rate as the fraction of teacher trajectories
receiving full reward. Environments with matched trajectories are
ordered by $`S`$ and divided into five equally sized groups, whose mean
scope and success rates are plotted in the lower panel. Mean success
falls from 23.0% in the group with the smallest scope to 13.4% in the
group with the largest. This trend supports $`S`$ as a proxy for the
demands of repository reconstruction.

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Benchmarks.

We evaluate our model on five benchmarks covering repository generation,
software engineering, and terminal-based tasks. NL2Repo Ding et al.
(2025) is a long-horizon coding benchmark that requires agents to
implement a complete repository from scratch based on a natural language
description. Success requires sustained planning, tool use, and
coordinated implementation across multiple files and modules. SWE-bench
Verified Jimenez et al. (2024), SWE-bench Pro Deng et al. (2025), and
SWE-bench Multilingual Zan et al. (2025) assess the ability to resolve
issues in existing repositories, while Terminal-Bench 2.1 Merrill et al.
(2026) evaluates task completion in terminal environments. Together,
these benchmarks measure coding-agent performance across both project
construction and repository maintenance.

#### Evaluation Settings.

We use Claude Code version 2.1.140 as the agent scaffold. The maximum
context length is 262,144 tokens, and the maximum output length is
32,768 tokens. Context is automatically compacted when its usage reaches
80% of the maximum context length. For decoding, we set temperature to
1.0, top-$`p`$ to 0.95, and top-$`k`$ to 20.

### 5.2 Main Results

Table 1 compares RepoHorizen-35B-A3B and its base model with
representative closed-source and open-weight models. On NL2Repo, which
directly evaluates full-repository reconstruction, training raises the
score from 23.8 to 46.4, a gain of 22.6 points. This is the strongest
listed result among open-weight models below 200B parameters.
RepoHorizen also surpasses GPT-5.5 and Gemini-3.1 Pro, which score 45.1
and 33.4, respectively, as well as larger open-weight models
DeepSeek-V4-Pro and MiniMax-M3, which score 38.5 and 42.1. Improvements
extend to all four remaining benchmarks, with gains of 1.3 to 6.8 points
across issue resolution and cross-domain long-horizon tasks in
Terminal-Bench 2.1. These results suggest that RepoFactory training
transfers beyond repository generation to broader software engineering
and terminal work at fixed model scale. Environment evolution further
strengthens this performance across all five benchmarks, adding 5.1
points on NL2Repo and 2.1 points on average over the no-evolution
variant. These additional gains support the effectiveness of our proxy
metrics in guiding environment evolution that improve training value.

### 5.3 Environment Scaling

Figure 3: Average accuracy as a function of the number of RepoFactory
training environments.

Figure 3 shows how NL2Repo performance changes as the training set
grows. Performance increases steadily from 23.8% without RepoFactory
environments to 46.4% with 5,577 environments, consistent with the
22.6-point gain reported in Table 1. The improvement continues at the
largest scale tested, with no evidence of saturation.

The gains become more pronounced beyond 3,000 environments. Adding the
remaining 2,577 environments raises performance from 36.5% to 46.4%,
suggesting that the larger collection exposes the model to repository
structures and behavioral requirements that smaller subsets do not
cover. Scaling the environment set therefore expands the supervision
available for long-horizon repository reconstruction and continues to
improve performance well beyond the earliest additions.

### 5.4 Scaling Across Model Sizes

We examine how the benefits of RepoFactory training vary with model
capacity. Figure 4 reports NL2Repo gains of 12.2 to 22.6 percentage

full-repository task remains useful across different parameter budgets.
The 35B-A3B model gains the most despite the 27B model’s higher

### 5.6 Ablation of the Evolution Objective

The evolution objective must support both the recovery of usable
environments and improvements in training value. We evaluate these
effects separately, using oracle validation to measure candidate
recovery and downstream training to assess $`S`$ and $`C`$ as
optimization targets. For recovery, we uniformly sample 500 repositories
from the filtered candidate set and compare runs allowing up to three
repair steps and none. We compare the number of environments satisfying
$`A=1`$, with concurrency, timeouts, and infrastructure retry budgets
held fixed.

Figure 6 shows that evolution increases the number of validated
environments from 169 to 347, raising yield from 33.8% to 69.4% under
the same acceptance criterion. These gains suggest that many initial
failures stem from construction defects that can be corrected through
execution feedback. Most of the improvement comes from the first repair,
with smaller gains from subsequent steps. Recovering these otherwise
discarded candidates expands the supply of valid environments from the
same repository pool, supporting environment generation at scale.

To assess whether optimizing $`S`$ and $`C`$ improves training value, we
run two independent evolution experiments from the same 1,000 randomly
sampled environments. Each experiment targets one metric while retaining
validity checks. We collect environments after rounds 3, 6, 9, and 12 as
Levels 1 through 4, respectively. Each level supplies 2,000 trajectories
for fine-tuning Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with the same initialization and
optimization settings.

As shown in Figure 5, optimizing $`S`$ produces longer interactions and
contexts and more supervised tokens. NL2Repo accuracy increases steadily
from 30.2% at Level 1 to 32.9% at Level 4. Expanding implementation
scope thus provides more extensive development experience within a fixed
number of trajectories, accompanied by improved reconstruction
performance.

Optimizing $`C`$ produces a different pattern. Accuracy fluctuates at
intermediate levels but reaches 32.5% at Level 4, compared with 30.2% at
Level 1. Interaction length decreases, while context length and
supervised token counts decline only slightly. The improvement therefore
does not depend on longer trajectories or additional supervision. This
complements the scope ablation, suggesting that training value depends
not only on the extent of implementation work but also on the
relationships among the components being implemented.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented RepoFactory, an automated pipeline that constructs
verifiable environments for full-repository generation from real-world
projects. Its Environment Evolution Loop uses timely, low-cost feedback
to optimize validity, implementation scope, and structural coupling,
recovering failed conversions and improving task value. The resulting
10,159 environments span 17 software domains. Training Qwen3.5-35B-A3B
on these environments yields RepoHorizen-35B-A3B, with gains of 22.6
percentage points on NL2Repo-Bench and 8.5 points on average across five
coding and agentic benchmarks. Environment evolution contributes a
further 5.1 and 2.1 points over training without evolution. These
findings highlight the value of repository-level environments for
coding-agent training and support low-cost proxy objectives as a
practical means of improving their training value at scale.

## 7 Limitations

RepoFactory currently focuses on Python projects, with relatively
limited coverage of other programming languages. Our proxy metrics
provide useful, low-cost guidance for environment evolution, but
characterize task content at a broad structural level. They do not yet
explicitly distinguish the capabilities required by individual tasks.
Extending language coverage and incorporating more detailed information
about these capability requirements could support a wider range of
development settings and more targeted training.

## Appendix A Implementation Details

This appendix describes the trajectory preparation and supervised
fine-tuning configuration used for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B.

### A.1 Trajectory Filtering and Cleaning

Before fine-tuning, we retain trajectories with rewards above $`0.5`$
and apply offline quality checks. Deterministic checks examine record
validity, dialogue structure, consistency between tool calls and
results, response completeness, and the formatting of reasoning and
code. They also screen for repetition, interaction loops, contamination,
potentially sensitive or harmful content, and attempts to obtain
information outside the intended task environment. We discard
trajectories with clear violations and flag ambiguous cases for review.

A language-model judge reviews the flagged trajectories. These
trajectories are included in the SFT data only when the judge determines
that the apparent violation is benign. Confirmed violations and
unresolved cases remain excluded.

### A.2 SFT Hyperparameters

| Number of epochs                | 3                                             |
| Rollout / global batch size     | 64 / 64                                       |

| Context parallel size           | 4                                             |
| Expert parallel size            | 8                                             |
| Expert tensor parallel size     | 1                                             |
| Sequence parallelism            | Enabled                                       |

Table 3: Supervised fine-tuning hyperparameters for Qwen3.5-35B-A3B.

We fine-tune the model, using the settings in Table 3. The data are
shuffled during training, with no online generation. Following the
Qwen3.5 multi-turn objective, we compute the loss over assistant spans,
including tool calls. System, user, and tool-result turns provide
context but receive no loss.

The rollout and global batch sizes are both 64, and the per-device
micro-batch size is 1. Dynamic token batching limits each GPU to 80,000
tokens, with no separate sequence-length cap. Tensor, context, and
expert parallel sizes are 2, 4, and 8, respectively. Sequence
parallelism is enabled, while pipeline and expert tensor parallel sizes
are both 1.

We use AdamW with weight decay of $`0.1`$ and coefficients
$`\beta_{1}=0.9`$ and $`\beta_{2}=0.98`$. The learning rate warms up to
$`1.0\times 10^{-6}`$ over the first 10% of training, then decays to
$`1.0\times 10^{-7}`$ following a cosine schedule.

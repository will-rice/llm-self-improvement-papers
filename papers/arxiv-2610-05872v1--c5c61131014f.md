# Off-Policy Merging Beats On-Policy Self-Distillation for Continual Learning

###### Abstract

A long-standing goal of AI is a model that can continually learn and
improve itself. On post-trained models, supervised finetuning (SFT) on
new data often causes poor generalization and catastrophic forgetting.
As such, the conventional wisdom is that on-policy training is a
prerequisite for continual learning. In practice, however, data
containing new knowledge or capabilities are often off-policy. While
methods such as on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) try to bridge this
gap by converting off-policy data into on-policy signal, they have been
shown to cause reasoning collapse. In this paper, we show that
off-policy merging beats OPSD for continual learning. We first show that
SFT learns a useful signal from new data, but naively applying its
update interferes with existing capabilities. We reduce this
interference with a simple recipe we term grafting, which changes where
the update is learned and how it is applied: (1) learning the update on
an earlier donor checkpoint, ideally even before the end of pretraining,
and applying the weight update to the post-trained model; (2) scaling
the weight update, equivalent to a form of model merging; and (3)
optionally, masking the most sensitive update directions when the new
data distribution is far from the post-trained model. Across continual
learning settings including (1) distilling from expert traces, (2)
self-improvement with STaR and Pedagogical RL, and (3) injecting
knowledge after pretraining cutoff, grafting Pareto-dominates both SFT
and OPSD in new-task and old-task performance, while avoiding expensive
on-policy sampling. Therefore, our work challenges on-policy training as
a necessity for continual learning on RL-trained models.

## 1  Introduction

A long-standing goal of AI is a model that keeps acquiring new
capabilities without forgetting what it can already do (McCloskey and
Cohen, 1989; Kirkpatrick et al., 2017). This is particularly challenging
for models that have undergone extensive post-training. In particular,
direct supervised finetuning (SFT) on new data can cause forgetting and
fail to generalize (Chu et al., 2025). This has motivated the view that
continual learning should remain on-policy, i.e. the model learns from
behavior produced by its current policy (Chen et al., 2025). However,
extended on-policy RL can plateau and lose coverage after exhausting
non-zero reward behaviors to amplify (Yue et al., 2025; Zhang et al.,
2026). Furthermore, data containing new knowledge or capabilities are
often off-policy, such as new knowledge after the pretraining cutoff
Jang et al. (2022), expert demonstrations Taori et al. (2023), delayed
feedback from environments Trajectory Labs (2026), and synthetic data
for self-improvement Chakraborty et al. (2026).

Recent work on on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) tries to bridge this
gap by converting off-policy data into on-policy updates, distilling
from a solution-conditioned copy of the model (Zhao et al., 2026;
Hubotter et al., 2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026). However, conditioning on
the solution can suppress exploration, leak privileged information into
the teacher’s reasoning, and degrade out-of-domain performance or
collapse reasoning length (Kim et al., 2026; Kaur et al., 2026). More
fundamentally, this raises a simple question:

![](arxiv-2610-05872v1--c5c61131014f.figures/inline-1.svg)

We premise with a surprisingly simple finding: model merging, i.e., by
interpolating the SFT-ed weights back toward the original post-trained
model Wortsman et al. (2022b),
$`\theta_{\text{merge}}=(1-\lambda)\,\theta_{\text{post}}+\lambda\,\theta_{\text{post}}^{\prime}`$
where $`\theta_{\text{post}}^{\prime}`$ is the model after SFT on a
task, SFT is elevated to a strong baseline (Figure 1, left), achieving
higher performance on the new task and comparable performance on old
tasks. Since merging only scales the SFT update down without changing
its direction, this demonstrates SFT learns a useful signal from the new
data. However, naive integration of SFT updates clearly interferes with
the post-trained model’s capabilities. This leads to a tangible key
objective:

![](arxiv-2610-05872v1--c5c61131014f.figures/inline-2.svg)

We first rewrite merging as adding a scaled update to the post-trained
model,
$`\theta_{\text{merge}}=\theta_{\text{post}}+\lambda\,({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.5,0.7266}\theta_{\text{post}}^{\prime}}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.5,0.7266}\theta_{\text{post}}})`$.
Written this way, merging decouples two design choices: _where_ the
update is learned (here, naively on $`\theta_{\text{post}}`$ itself) and
_how_ it is applied (here, naively scaled by $`\lambda`$).

Task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023) shows that an update need not be
derived from the model it is applied to. We argue that this separation
is important for continual learning. This is because training on a
narrow distribution skews which task a model infers its inputs to come
from (Kotha et al., 2024), so finetuning the post-trained model routes
much of the new learning signal through the parameters supporting its
existing capabilities, while a checkpoint from before post-training
routes less of it there (§3). We therefore learn the update on an
earlier donor model
$`{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.5,0.7266}\theta_{\text{donor}}}`$, and apply it
to the post-trained model:
$`\theta_{\text{post}}+\lambda\,({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.5,0.7266}\theta_{\text{donor-ft}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.5,0.7266}\theta_{\text{donor}}})`$,
where $`\theta_{\text{donor-ft}}`$ is the donor after finetuning. Along
the OLMo-3 training trajectory (OLMo Team, 2026), we see that the best
donors are checkpoints before the end of pretraining (Figure 1, right),
which is perhaps not surprising since later stages of modern pretraining
are high-quality data that resembles post-training.

Donor-trained updates with naive scaling write less to the parameters on
which the post-trained model is most sensitive, but when the new
distribution is far from the post-trained model, they can still damage
them. We therefore propose to optionally protect the most sensitive
directions, estimated with the Fisher information (Kirkpatrick et al.,
2017; Ikram et al., 2026). This matters most for knowledge injection
(§6.2), where retaining unrelated capabilities is hard. We call the full
recipe grafting:
$`\theta_{\text{post}}+\lambda\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1523,0.6836,0.375}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}}({\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.5,0.7266}\theta_{\text{donor-ft}}}-{\color[rgb]{0.1602,0.5,0.7266}\theta_{\text{donor}}})`$,
where the mask $`{\color[rgb]{0.1523,0.6836,0.375}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}}`$
projects away the top-$`\rho`$ fraction of Fisher-weighted directions
from the update.

We study three settings where off-policy data arises naturally: (1)
distillation from expert traces on hard problems the model cannot solve;
(2) self-improvement on hard problems, where models bootstrap themselves
without stronger supervision, e.g., via their own rejection-sampled
successes (Zelikman et al., 2022) or with indirect supervision, e.g.,
from thinking traces written by a copy of themselves with privileged
information (Chakraborty et al., 2026, Pedagogical RL;), and (3)
knowledge injection of new facts absent from prior training. In all
three, we assume access only to the data (with our without thinking
traces depending on the specific setting), not to the weights or logits
of a stronger teacher model, so on-policy distillation (OPD) from a
teacher (Agarwal et al., 2023) is not possible. In all three settings,
grafting dominates OPSD in both new-task and old-task performance while
avoiding expensive on-policy sampling. In self-improvement, grafting
roughly doubles new-task performance over the post-trained model while
also improving old-task performance.

Together, our results suggest that on-policy updates are not a necessary
condition for continual learning. Since continual learning applications
often involve off-policy experiences, we hope our work inspires more
effort on making off-policy training generalize.

## 2  Preliminaries

We consider teaching a post-trained model $`\pi_{\theta_{\text{post}}}`$
a new target task, formally a set of inputs $`\{x\}`$ which we want the
model to solve. A task may be a skill (e.g., a class of hard math
problems) or some knowledge (e.g., new facts after the knowledge
cutoff). Two objectives should be optimized jointly:

- •
  New task: performance on held-out IID instances of the target task.
- •
  Old task: performance on tasks outside the target task – for unrelated
  tasks, we want less forgetting; for related tasks, we want the new
  skill or knowledge to generalize to these tasks.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a standard way to learn a task without
using off-policy data. It maximizes
$`\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\,R(x,y)`$ over outputs it
samples itself. However, given its mode-seeking nature, RL is known to
mostly amplify and compose behaviors the model already possesses (Zhang
et al., 2026). For genuinely new knowledge or very hard problems beyond
the model’s capability, the model places almost no probability on
correct outputs and, therefore, receives no training signal to continue
improving. These are the scenarios where off-policy data can be
extremely useful.

Off-policy data can be formalized as a corpus $`\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y)\}`$
of inputs $`x`$ and outputs $`y`$, where $`x`$ are not generated under
$`\pi_{\theta_{\text{post}}}`$. Such data is abundant in practice, e.g.,
new knowledge acquired after the model’s pretraining cutoff, expert
demonstrations for a hard task, feedback from new environments, and
synthetic data created by the model for self-improvement.

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) has been the standard way to learn from
off-policy data, optimizing the model likelihood
$`-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)`$ directly
on the outputs $`y`$ given by the data $`\mathcal{D}`$. However, SFT is
known to memorize rather than generalize (Chu et al., 2025) and to cause
catastrophic forgetting of capabilities outside $`\mathcal{D}`$
(McCloskey and Cohen, 1989; Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Biderman et al.,
2024).

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) addresses the shortcomings of SFT by
converting the off-policy corpus into on-policy updates (Zhao et al.,
2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hubotter et al., 2026). The model samples
responses and is supervised at each token by a solution-conditioned
version of itself:

```math
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\;\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\,y)}\,\sum_{t}\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}\!\big(\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(\cdot\mid x,y,\tilde{y}_{<t})\,\big\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\tilde{y}_{<t})\big),
```

where the teacher $`\bar{\theta}`$ is a stop-grad copy of the student ,
and $`\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}`$ is a family of $`f`$-divergences (Agarwal et
al., 2023) whose $`\alpha`$ interpolates from forward KL
($`\alpha{=}0`$) to reverse KL ($`\alpha{=}1`$).

## 3  Why does SFT interfere with post-trained capabilities?

Why does SFT interfere with post-trained capabilities? We argue that
this is because models trained on narrow distributions (e.g.,
post-training and late stages of pretraining) explain new data
differently, also known as implicit task inference Kotha et al. (2024).
We demonstrate this intuition with a minimal model.

Consider two linear regression tasks with data distributions
$`\mathcal{D}_{A}`$ and $`\mathcal{D}_{B}`$. For each task
$`T\in\{A,B\}`$,

```math
x\sim\mathcal{D}_{T},\qquad y=\beta_{T}^{\top}x,
```

Task $`A`$ is a post-trained capability we want to retain, while task
$`B`$ is a capability we want to continually learn. We model the two
tasks with a mixture of linear experts,

```math
\hat{y}(x)=g_{\phi}(x)w_{A}^{\top}x+(1-g_{\phi}(x))w_{B}^{\top}x,\qquad g_{\phi}(x)=\sigma(\phi^{\top}x).
```

We assume pretraining learns both tasks imperfectly but learns their
assignment relatively well. On task-$`B`$ inputs, assume for simplicity
that $`g_{\mathrm{pre}}(x)=\varepsilon_{D},x\sim\mathcal{D}_{B}`$. After
post-training on task $`A`$, the gate becomes more biased toward expert
$`A`$ on the same task-$`B`$ inputs
$`g_{\mathrm{post}}(x)=\varepsilon_{P},x\sim\mathcal{D}_{B}`$, with
$`0\leq\varepsilon_{D}<\varepsilon_{P}<1`$, which is shown by Kotha et
al. (2024). Thus $`\varepsilon`$ measures how much task-$`B`$ data is
routed through expert $`A`$.

We now analyze fine-tuning on task $`B`$. For a checkpoint with
task-$`B`$ assignment $`\varepsilon`$, under squared loss,

```math
\displaystyle L_{B} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}r_{B}^{\top}\Sigma_{B}r_{B},\quad\text{where}
```

```math
\displaystyle r_{B} \displaystyle=\varepsilon w_{A}+(1-\varepsilon)w_{B}-\beta_{B},
```

```math
\displaystyle\Sigma_{B} \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{B}}[xx^{\top}].
```

The gradients with respect to the two experts are

```math
\nabla_{w_{A}}L_{B}=\varepsilon\Sigma_{B}r_{B},\qquad\nabla_{w_{B}}L_{B}=(1-\varepsilon)\Sigma_{B}r_{B}.
```

Therefore the same task-$`B`$ learning signal is routed to the two
experts in the ratio

```math
\frac{\|\nabla_{w_{A}}L_{B}\|}{\|\nabla_{w_{B}}L_{B}\|}=\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}. \tag{1}
```

Hence task assignment directly controls how much fine-tuning on task
$`B`$ modifies the expert supporting task $`A`$. Since we assume
$`\varepsilon_{D}<\varepsilon_{P}`$, finetuning the pretrained model
routes less of the task-$`B`$ learning signal through task $`A`$ than
directly fine-tuning the post-trained model. This is aligned with what
we show in §7.

## 4  Method

Input: post-trained model $`\theta_{\text{post}}`$, injection data
$`\mathcal{D}`$, scale $`\lambda\in(0,1]`$.

1\. $`\theta_{\text{donor-ft}}\leftarrow`$ finetune
$`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$ on $`\mathcal{D}`$

2\. $`\Delta\leftarrow\theta_{\text{donor-ft}}-\theta_{\text{donor}}`$

3\. $`\mathcal{M}_{\rho}\leftarrow`$ compute projection from Eq. 5

4\.
$`\theta(\lambda,\rho)\leftarrow\theta_{\text{post}}+\lambda\cdot\mathcal{M}_{\rho}(\Delta)`$

return $`\theta(\lambda,\rho)`$

Algorithm 1 Grafting

Our method grafting consists of two main components: a scaled parameter
delta derived from training on a _donor model_ (i.e., a checkpoint from
earlier in training), and an optional _projection mask_ to protect
sensitive weight directions when the new task data is far from the
post-trained distribution. We display the core recipe in Algorithm 1.

Extract updates from a donor model Task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2023) shows that an update need not be derived from the model it is
applied to. We argue that this separation is important for continual
learning. This is because training on a narrow distribution skews which
task a model infers its inputs to come from (Kotha et al., 2024), so
finetuning the post-trained model routes much of the new learning signal
through the parameters supporting its existing capabilities, while a
checkpoint from before post-training routes less of it there (§3).
Therefore, instead of directly finetuning the post-trained model
$`\theta_{\text{post}}`$, we finetune an earlier checkpoint which we
call a “donor model” $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$. Specifically, we
finetune $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$ to obtain
$`\theta_{\text{donor-ft}}`$, and isolate the difference
$`\Delta=\theta_{\text{donor-ft}}-\theta_{\text{donor}}`$. We then apply
the update scaled by $`\lambda`$ to $`\theta_{\text{post}}`$:

```math
\theta(\lambda)\;=\;\theta_{\text{post}}\;+\;\lambda\,\big(\theta_{\text{donor-ft}}-\theta_{\text{donor}}\big). \tag{2}
```

While $`\lambda\in(0,1]`$ controls the scale of the graft, we remark
that $`\lambda`$ admits an interpretation that goes beyond interpolating
between old and new task performance. This arises from the _model
merging_ literature (see e.g., WiSE-FT, Wortsman et al., 2022b), where
linearly interpolating a later checkpoint with an earlier one is known
to benefit generalization. This suggests that moderate $`\lambda`$ can
be beneficial, which is reflected by our experiments on grafting from
hard math data.

Protect sensitive weights via projection As we will show, applying a
donor-derived update directly may not be enough when the injected
distribution is far from the post-trained model (e.g., injecting new
facts into a reasoning model). However, precisely characterizing weight
interference is complex. We take inspiration from on-policy methods, and
consider a surrogate goal of shaping the update direction such that the
updated model modifies the behavior on the old domain as little as
possible. Concretely, we want an update $`\Delta`$ that keeps the
conditional KL divergence
$`\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta_{\text{post}}}(\cdot\,|\,z)\parallel p_{\theta_{\text{post}}+\Delta}(\cdot\,|\,z))\right]`$
over the old domain small. To efficiently translate this to the
directions of $`\Delta`$ to mask, we recall the standard quadratic
approximation of KL (Cover et al., 1991):

```math
\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta_{\text{post}}}(\cdot\,|\,z)\parallel p_{\theta_{\text{post}}+\Delta}(\cdot\,|\,z)) \displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\top}F_{z}(\theta_{\text{post}})\Delta, \tag{3}
```

where
$`F_{z}(\theta_{\text{post}})=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\theta_{\text{post}}}(\cdot\,|\,z)}\!\Big[\big(\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta_{\text{post}}}(y\,|\,z)\big)^{\otimes 2}\Big]`$
is the conditional Fisher Information evaluated at
$`\theta_{\text{post}}`$. This can be estimated with a retention set
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{ret}}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\}`$ of inputs from,
e.g., an old task:

```math
F_{\mathrm{ret}}:=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\theta_{\text{post}}}(\cdot\,|\,z_{n})}\!\left[\Big(\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta_{\text{post}}}(y\,|\,z_{n})\Big)^{\otimes 2}\right]. \tag{4}
```

However, the full Fisher is infeasible to compute. We consider existing
approaches that take a diagonal (i.e., entrywise) approximation
(Kirkpatrick et al., 2017). We also propose another efficient
approximation informed by the _Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature_
(KFAC) (Martens and Grosse, 2015), similar to approaches in
model-editing literature (Ikram et al., 2026), which we can have achieve
better performance with less masking than the diagonal approximation.
For concreteness, we walk through deriving the mask from the diagonal
approximation. Measuring the Fisher quadratic form along the diagonal:
$`\Delta^{\top}\diag(F_{\mathrm{ret}})\Delta=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\theta}}(F_{\mathrm{ret}})_{jj}\Delta_{j}^{2}`$,
we observe the entry-wise score
$`s_{j}=(F_{\mathrm{ret}})_{jj}\,\Delta_{j}^{2}`$ approximates how much
entry $`j`$ in the update $`\Delta`$ contributes to the model change
(Eq. 3). Therefore, a natural approach to reducing the model change
induced by $`\Delta`$ is to _mask_ the top $`\rho`$-fraction of entries
$`S_{\rho}`$ ranked by $`s_{j}`$:

```math
\theta(\lambda,\rho)\;=\;\theta_{\text{post}}\;+\;\lambda\,\big(\mathcal{M}_{\rho}\odot\Delta\big),\qquad(\mathcal{M}_{\rho})_{j}\;=\;\begin{cases}0&j\in S_{\rho},\\[2.0pt]
1&\text{otherwise},\end{cases} \tag{5}
```

where $`\odot`$ is the element-wise product. We defer analogous
derivation of the KFAC-based projection and other implementation details
in this section to Appendix F. In our experiments, we show that
retention-based projections derived from the Diagonal or KFAC Fisher
work remarkably well in removing interference from donor updates by just
masking a small number of directions ($`\rho\leq 0.01`$), outperforming
prior Fisher-based merging (Matena and Raffel, 2022), and pushing the
old/new-domain performance frontier compared to un-projected updates.

## 5  Experiments on Learning Hard Math Problems

### 5.1 Experimental setup

Models We experiment with two model pairs: Qwen3-1.7B (thinking) /
Qwen3-1.7B-Base Yang et al. (2025) and Qwen3-8B (thinking) /
Qwen3-8B-Base as the post-trained model $`\theta_{\text{post}}`$ and the
donor model $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$. Our experiments include both
thinking and instruct modes. Unless stated otherwise the donor model is
the officially released base model of the same family. In §5.3 we
additionally use OLMo-3-7B (thinking) (OLMo Team, 2026) as
$`\theta_{\text{post}}`$, because it releases intermediate checkpoints
along both its pretraining and post-training trajectories, which lets us
vary $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$ rather than fix it to the official base
model.

Math data We consider three settings for where the off-policy data comes
from (see §A):

- •
  Distillation with teacher traces: we get teacher traces from the AoPS
  subset of Nemotron-SFT-Math-v4 (NVIDIA, 2026; Du et al., 2025), whose
  solutions are generated by DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026). At
  both scales we mine hard problems from this pool that the post-trained
  model cannot solve. For each model, we sample $`16`$ attempts per
  problem and keep those with $`0`$ success for 1.7B and $`\leq 1`$
  success for 8B, leaving $`1{,}533`$ training samples for 1.7B and
  $`985`$ for 8B.
- •
  Self-improvement with rejection sampling (STaR; Zelikman et al.,
  2022): we apply rejection sampling to the post-trained model to get
  supervision of both thinking and solution tokens. We sample up to
  $`32`$ attempts per problem and keep one verified trace for each
  solved problem.
- •
  Self-improvement with Pedagogical RL (Chakraborty et al., 2026): STaR
  requires that the model can solve the problem with a large budget.
  Pedagogical RL removes this requirement by synthesizing reasoning
  traces from gold solutions and do SFT on the synthesized traces
  (Appendix B). We find replacing the SFT step of Pedagogical RL with
  grafting largely improves performance.

Evaluation For math, new-task performance is measured on around $`100`$
held-out hard math problems from the training set. Old-task performance
is the mean over three benchmarks (AIME 2024, AIME 2025, HMMT 2025). All
metrics are estimated over $`16`$ samples at temperature $`1.0`$ and
top-$`p`$ $`1.0`$, decoding up to $`32`$K tokens in thinking mode,
unless otherwise specified. Appendix C gives the training details.

### 5.2 Model merging alone improves both old and new tasks

We first show that model merging already improves both old and new
tasks. We consider three methods: SFT, OPSD, and grafting, with full
details given in Appendix C. In Figure 2, we sweep all methods with
$`9`$ learning rates ($`5{\times}10^{-7}`$ to $`5{\times}10^{-5}`$) and
train $`5`$ epochs. Figure 2 (left) shows that, without model merging,
neither SFT nor OPSD outperforms the post-trained model, while grafting
with the best hyperparameters does. We then sweep the merging scale for
all methods in Figure 2 (right). We see that merging improves
generalization and erases OPSD’s original edge over SFT, despite its
slow and expensive on-policy sampling. Given its uniform benefit, we
apply merging across all methods in the rest of the paper.

### 5.3 Optimal $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$ is before the end of pretraining

OLMo-3-7B (thinking) (OLMo Team, 2026) has many checkpoints for us to
move $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$ freely. On the post-training side, we
have base model $`\xrightarrow{\text{SFT}}`$ SFT checkpoint
$`\xrightarrow{\text{DPO}}`$ DPO checkpoint $`\xrightarrow{\text{RL}}`$
OLMo-3-7B (thinking); on the pretraining side, we move
$`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$ up to $`12`$k steps before the base model.

We first investigate how each post-training stage makes finetuning
harder. We see in Figure 3 (left) that grafting (base ckpt) outperforms
grafting (SFT ckpt), grafting (SFT ckpt) outperforms grafting (DPO
ckpt), and grafting (DPO ckpt) outperforms direct SFT on the final
post-trained model. This suggests that each stage of post-training (SFT,
DPO, RL) makes OLMo-3 harder to finetune.

The question that remains is which pretraining checkpoint to use as the
donor model. There is a tension in this choice. The end of pretraining
anneals on higher-quality data and already resembles an early stage of
SFT, so an earlier $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$ should be easier to
finetune, but it also sits farther from $`\theta_{\text{post}}`$, which
makes it less likely to stay linearly connected (Frankle et al., 2020;
Neyshabur et al., 2020) with $`\theta_{\text{post}}`$. Figure 3 (right)
moves $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$ up to $`12`$k steps before the final
base checkpoint. We see that earlier pretrained checkpoints clearly
outperforms the final pretrained checkpoint, while the gain is not
strictly monotonic. This suggests that for grafting we should search for
an early pretrained checkpoint that balances the two forces.

Qwen3 releases only its final base model and no earlier pretraining
checkpoints, so in the rest of the paper we use the official base model
as $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$.

Thinking mode Figure 4(a) and Figure 4(b) show results with teacher
traces as off-policy data, on Qwen3-1.7B (thinking) and Qwen3-8B
(thinking), respectively. The same merging sweep (§5.2) is applied for
all methods. We see that, for 1.7B, grafting dominates both SFT and OPSD
and is the only method that meaningfully dominates the post-trained
model. The same ordering holds at pass@4 and pass@8, suggesting that the
gain reflects expanded capability and not simply a “sharpening effect”.
For 8B, both grafting and SFT dominate OPSD. For both sizes, OPSD fails
to largely outperform the original post-trained model for
generalization, which echoes the findings in Kim et al. (2026); Kaur et
al. (2026).

Non-thinking mode (aka. instruct mode) We repeat the experiment at 8B in
_non-thinking_ mode on DAPO (Yu et al., 2025), an easier data for the
non-thinking mode to learn, and we follow the thinking mode experiments
to mine hard problems (see Appendix A for details). The same merging
sweep (§5.2) is applied for all methods. We see from Figure 4(c) that
grafting again dominates SFT and OPSD at pass@$`1`$.

### 5.5 Self-improvement with rejection sampling (STaR)

Figure 5: Self-improvement with rejection  
sampling (STaR) on Qwen3-1.7B (thinking).

We now turn to the second setting, where the off-policy data come from
the post-trained model itself via rejection sampling (§5.1), also known
as STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022). Specifically, we sample up to $`32`$
attempts per problem and keep one verified trace for each solved
problem. Unlike distillation from teacher traces, STaR lets the model
self-improve by training on its own data. Figure 5 shows that new-task
performance is roughly the same compared to the strongest baseline
(i.e., SFT, which stands for the original STaR) but generalization to
old task rises about eight points above it. Compared to distillation
from teacher traces (Figure 4(a)), we see that STaR is generally worse
in terms of new-task performance but similar for generalization to the
old task.

### 5.6 Self-improvement with Pedagogical RL

Figure 6: Self-improvement with Pedagogical RL on Qwen3-1.7B (thinking).

Pedagogical RL (Chakraborty et al., 2026) takes a different route to
self-improvement, where a copy of the model that sees the reference
solution (without seeing thinking traces) is trained with RL to write
thinking traces the model can learn from (Appendix B). With these
self-generated thinking traces, the original method then learns with SFT
(Pedagogical RL + SFT in Figure 6). Compared to direct distillation from
teacher traces, Pedagogical RL does not need access to thinking traces,
which is more practical since solutions from humans or expert systems do
not expose thinking traces. We keep the RL training and thinking trace
generation steps intact and propose to learn the same traces with
grafting. Since Pedagogical RL is designed to generate thinking traces
and OPSD does not use them, we reuse the OPSD results from STaR (§5.5).
Figure 6 shows that grafting again dominates SFT and OPSD. With
Pedagogical RL, grafting learns the new task better and generalizes to
the old task, while SFT loses in both directions.

## 6  Experiments on Knowledge Injection

### 6.1 Experimental setup

Models We experiment with Qwen3-1.7B (thinking) as the post-trained
model $`\theta_{\text{post}}`$ and Qwen3-1.7B-Base Yang et al. (2025) as
the donor model $`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$.

Knowledge data Our data for knowledge acquisition mainly follows
Shenfeld et al. (2026), but with the key exception that we use purely
synthetic knowledge to avoid any possible leakage. We construct a
fictional world with $`200`$ invented entities and $`1{,}125`$ atomic
facts. Each fact is written as question-answer pairs in $`8`$ distinct
phrasings, and the model is trained on those; evaluation then uses
_held-out paraphrases_ the model has never seen. For OPSD, we write
wiki-style articles that state the fictional facts for conditioning.
Since the knowledge data’s QA form is very different from a reasoning
model’s, we mix knowledge QA with AoPS teacher traces we use in the math
experiments. Together, we have $`9{,}000`$ QA samples and $`1{,}533`$
AoPS traces. All methods are trained on this same mixture.

Evaluation For knowledge, new-task performance is the exact-match
accuracy on held-out paraphrases the model never saw, decoded greedily.
Old-task performance is the mean over three benchmarks (AIME 2024, AIME
2025, HMMT 2025). Appendix C gives the training details.

Figure 7: (Left) Knowledge injection results. (Right) Disentangling the
contribution of grafting’s components. The protection mask ($`\rho`$
sweep) dominates no mask ($`\lambda`$ sweep).

### 6.2 Knowledge injection results

Figure 7 (left) shows the knowledge injection result with Qwen3-1.7B
(thinking), where the same model merging sweep (§5.2) is applied to all
methods. We see that grafting achieves nearly perfect injection with
retention at or above the post-trained model. SFT injects just as well
but suffers from forgetting. OPSD achieves near-perfect retention but
also learns less. Within OPSD, forward KL ($`\alpha{=}0`$) learns much
more than JSD ($`\alpha{=}0.5`$) and reverse KL ($`\alpha{=}1`$). This
shows that learning a new fact requires a mode-covering objective rather
than mode-seeking.

## 7  Analysis

Ablation studies Here we ablate the mask step that zeroes the most
sensitive coordinates. Figure 7 (right) compares the protection mask
($`\rho`$ sweep, in red) with plain model merging without the mask
($`\lambda`$ sweep, in gray). We see that the protection mask
Pareto-dominates plain model merging. Figure 7 (right) also shows Fisher
merging (Matena and Raffel, 2022), which weights each coordinate by its
Fisher ratio between the two tasks. We see that Fisher merging does not
inject at all in this setting. We find that this is because the
knowledge injection Fisher is usually dominated by the retention Fisher.
Finally, the KFAC mask (§4, in navy) reaches the same old-task
performance as the diagonal mask while zeroing far fewer directions: at
$`\rho{=}0.001`$ it keeps new-task performance at $`0.995`$ with
old-task performance $`0.390`$, which the diagonal mask needs
$`\rho{=}0.01`$ to match. We note that KFAC being a better approximant
to the retention Fisher does not directly imply interference must be
more concentrated to few directions. The fact that retention of old
tasks and injected knowledge can be achieved by zero-ing very few
directions is in line with parallel work demonstrating the top few
directions of curvature contain mostly interference (Zhu et al., 2026),
and can be removed with little effect on task performance (Song et al.,
2025).

Figure 8: (Left) Grafting is much faster to train than OPSD. We show the
per-step sampling and weight update time for Qwen3-8B (thinking).
(Right) Grafting writes less to sensitive coordinates than SFT. We show
the weighted delta $`\sum_{j}F_{\mathrm{ret}}(j)\Delta_{j}^{2}`$ summed
across the $`1\%`$ of coordinates with the largest $`F_{\mathrm{ret}}`$.

Training latency Off-policy training also gets rid of the latency cost
of on-policy sampling. We show in Figure 8 (left) the per-step wall
clock time for both sampling and weight update for grafting and OPSD,
both with Qwen3-8B (thinking). We see that sampling is roughly $`90\%`$
of the step. In other words, grafting is $`10\times`$ faster to train
than OPSD, while achieving much better performance.

Training on donor model writes less to sensitive weights We show in
Figure 8 (right) the Fisher-weighted weight delta
$`\sum_{j}F_{\mathrm{ret}}(j)\Delta_{j}^{2}`$ that falls inside the top
$`1\%`$ of coordinates with the largest $`F_{\mathrm{ret}}`$, normalized
by that of all coordinates. In other words, this number shows how much a
weight update writes into the most sensitive coordinates of the
post-trained weights. We first see that the $`1\%`$ coordinates explain
nearly $`75\%`$ of the weight changes, which empirically justifies the
protection mask. More importantly, we see that grafting writes less to
the sensitive weights than SFT, for both math and knowledge learning
tasks, which helps explain why the protection mask alone cannot push SFT
to the same frontier as grafting, as we see above. In Section 3, we show
how this effect can be predicted by a minimal linear model.

## 8  Related Work

Continual learning for post-trained models. Continually trained networks
can overwrite earlier capabilities (McCloskey and Cohen, 1989). Classic
remedies constrain the update written into the parameters, e.g., elastic
weight consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017). For language models, it
has been shown that low-rank updates (Hu et al., 2022) forget less but
also learn less (Biderman et al., 2024). SFT and RL learn in different
ways (Chu et al., 2025; Matsutani et al., 2025), and SFT in particular
memorizes at the cost of generalization. This echoes the common belief
is that continual training should be on-policy, and has been
demonstrated by the success of continual training models using RL with
verifiable rewards (RLVR) (DeepSeek-AI, 2025). Chen et al. (2025)
attribute RL’s smaller forgetting specifically to its use of on-policy
data, rather than to other algorithmic choices such as KL regularization
or advantage estimation. In practice, however, most data for continual
learning is off-policy, such as knowledge after the pretraining cutoff
(Jang et al., 2022), expert demonstrations (Taori et al., 2023), delayed
feedback from environments (Trajectory Labs, 2026), and synthetic data
for self-improvement Zelikman et al. (2022).

On-policy self-distillation and its failure modes. OPSD converts
off-policy data into an on-policy learning signal, by distilling a
solution-conditioned copy of the model into itself on its own samples
(Zhao et al., 2026; Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hubotter et al., 2026;
Penaloza et al., 2026). The approach is being scaled in practice
(Trajectory Labs, 2026). Its benefit, however, appears to hold only
within a limited scope of tasks. Reported failure modes include
suppressed exploration, degeneration in reasoning length, and failure to
generalize (Kim et al., 2026; Kaur et al., 2026). Recent work responds
to these failures by filtering the part of the teacher signal that does
not transfer to the student (Shen et al., 2026) or by routing
token-level supervision between privileged teacher and student policies
(Yu et al., 2026).

Model merging and weight-space operations. Weight-space operations are
promised by loss-landscape geometry (Frankle et al., 2020; Neyshabur et
al., 2020; Ainsworth et al., 2023), and a large literature does weight
interpolation with models trained or finetuned with the same
initialization (Wortsman et al., 2022a; Matena and Raffel, 2022; Li et
al., 2022; Wortsman et al., 2022b; Ilharco et al., 2022), with
refinements that resolve interference, linearize fine-tuning, or
localize a skill in a sparse subset of the delta (Yadav et al., 2023; Yu
et al., 2024; Ortiz-Jimenez et al., 2023; Panigrahi et al., 2023). Model
merging has been shown to improve robustness (Wortsman et al., 2022b)
and generation diversity (Dang et al., 2025). Most similar to our work,
task arithmetic Ilharco et al. (2023), also known as Shadow-FT (Wu et
al., 2025) or chat vector Huang et al. (2024), composes weight deltas
trained on the base model. We improve over them with the proposed Fisher
masking and donor model selection. Fisher merging (Matena and Raffel, 2022) derives a soft weighting between multiple tasks that train the
model, while we derive a sparse mask using only the retention data and
show that retention-only sparse masking is substantially more effective.

Models become harder to finetune. It has been known that training
changes how well a model takes a further update, or plasticity (Ash and
Adams, 2020; Dohare et al., 2024). Recent work demonstrates this for
large language models. The longer a base model is pretrained beyond a
certain point, the more sensitive its parameters become and the worse it
finetunes (Springer et al., 2025). To mitigate such overtraining effect,
it has been proposed to increase learning rate (Rofin et al., 2026), or
more broadly, to bias pretraining toward flatter minima (Watts et al.,
2026). Our experiments on varying the donor model for grafting point the
same way: an earlier pretraining checkpoint can be a better
$`\theta_{\text{donor}}`$ than the final base model and later
post-trained intermediate checkpoints. Different from previous work, we
show that the harder-to-finetune effect can be mitigated not only by
training an easier-to-finetune model, but also by better continual
update rules.

## 9  Conclusion and Future Work

We asked whether continual learning for RL-trained models has to be
on-policy, and our results suggest that it does not. Model merging alone
shows that SFT learns a useful signal from new data, but naively
applying its update interferes with existing capabilities. Building on
that, grafting reduces this interference by learning the update on an
earlier donor checkpoint, scaling it, and optionally masking the most
sensitive update directions. Across three settings, grafting
Pareto-dominates both SFT and OPSD in new-task and old-task performance,
while avoiding expensive on-policy sampling. We show that w donor model
to use for grafting turns out to be crucial too. Earlier pretraining
checkpoints are usually a better choice, though future work can study
more principled approaches to select the optimal pretraining checkpoint
for grafting. Another direction to optimize for future work is to
predict optimal $`\lambda`$ and $`\rho`$ instead of a sweep. More
broadly, since off-policy data is what continual learning mostly has, we
hope this work encourages more effort on making off-policy training
generalize.

## Appendix A Data

Knowledge (synthetic closed world). To guarantee the facts are new, we
build a closed fictional world rather than reuse public corpora: $`200`$
invented entities and $`1{,}125`$ atomic facts about them. Each fact is
written as question-answer pairs in $`8`$ distinct phrasings, giving
$`9{,}000`$ QA pairs, and new-task performance is measured on held-out
phrasings never seen in training, so recall cannot be inflated by
memorized surface form. For OPSD we additionally write $`200`$
wiki-style articles that state the facts, used only for conditioning.
The facts are absent from the post-trained model: closed-book
exact-match is $`\approx\!0\%`$ (instruct $`0.09\%`$, base $`0.13\%`$).
Answers are scored by rule-based \<answer\> exact-match. All methods
train on the same mixture of the $`9{,}000`$ QA pairs and the
$`1{,}533`$ AoPS traces of the math corpus.

Math reasoning, Qwen3-1.7B (thinking). Training traces are competition
math solutions sampled from the AoPS split of Nemotron-SFT-Math
\[NVIDIA, 2026\]. We mine the hard subset by sampling Qwen3-1.7B
(thinking) $`16`$ times per problem at temperature $`1.0`$ and keeping
only problems it fails on all $`16`$ attempts, so the target skill is
absent. After length-filtering and deduplication we keep $`1{,}533`$
training traces, and hold out a disjoint set of $`111`$ mined-hard
problems to measure new-task performance.

Math reasoning, Qwen3-8B (thinking). At 8B we mine the same pool with a
looser criterion (so we have a meaningful number of samples), keeping
problems the post-trained model solves at most once in $`16`$ attempts,
which leaves $`985`$ training samples. A disjoint set of $`100`$
problems is held out to measure new-task performance.

Math reasoning, Qwen3-8B (non-thinking). The non-thinking experiment
uses problems from DAPO Yu et al. \[2025\]. Hard problems are mined the
same way as above, by sampling Qwen3-8B in non-thinking mode at
temperature $`1.0`$ and keeping only the problems it never solves, and
the worked solutions for those problems are the off-policy data. This
leaves $`1{,}163`$ training samples, with a disjoint set of $`200`$
problems held out to measure new-task performance.

## Appendix B Pedagogical RL

Pedagogical RL \[Chakraborty et al., 2026\] improves a model in two
stages: (1) it synthesizes reasoning traces from problems and their
reference solutions, and (2) it finetunes the model on the synthesized
traces with SFT. We keep stage (1) unchanged and replace SFT in stage
(2) with grafting.

Notation Following Chakraborty et al. \[2026\], let $`x`$ denote a
problem, $`c`$ its reference solution without reasoning, and $`\tau`$
the reasoning trace to be synthesized. Two copies of the model play
different roles: a privileged teacher

```math
\pi_{\theta_{T}}(\tau\mid x,c),
```

whose prompt contains the reference solution and instructs it to solve
the problem in its own way rather than copy $`c`$, and a student

```math
\pi_{\theta_{S}}(\tau\mid x),
```

which sees only the problem.

Teacher training The teacher is trained to write traces that are both
correct and plausible under the student. For each position $`t`$ of a
trace, let the student’s most likely next token be

```math
a_{t}^{\max}=\arg\max_{a}\pi_{\theta_{S}}\left(a\mid x,\tau_{<t}\right),
```

and define the surprise gap

```math
d_{t}=\log\frac{\pi_{\theta_{S}}\left(a_{t}^{\max}\mid x,\tau_{<t}\right)}{\pi_{\theta_{S}}\left(\tau_{t}\mid x,\tau_{<t}\right)},
```

which is zero when the trace follows the student’s top choice and grows
as the trace takes tokens the student finds unlikely. The spike-aware
learnability score aggregates the gaps over the $`T`$ tokens of the
trace,

```math
G_{\mathrm{spike}}^{\theta_{S}}(\tau\mid x)=\exp\left[-\frac{\lambda}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\exp(\beta d_{t})\right)\right],
```

where $`\beta`$ interpolates between penalizing the mean gap
($`\beta\to 0`$) and the largest gap ($`\beta\to\infty`$), and
$`\lambda`$ sets the strength of the penalty. The pedagogical reward
multiplies the learnability score with the correctness reward $`R`$,

```math
r_{\mathrm{ped}}(x,c,\tau)=R(x,c,\tau)\,G_{\mathrm{spike}}^{\theta_{S}}(\tau\mid x),
```

and the teacher is trained with GRPO \[Shao et al., 2024\] to maximize

```math
\max_{\theta_{T}}\;\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta_{T}}(\cdot\mid x,c)}\left[r_{\mathrm{ped}}(x,c,\tau)\right].
```

The student $`\pi_{\theta_{S}}`$ stays fixed during teacher training and
only scores how learnable the teacher’s traces are. We denote the
trained teacher by $`\tilde{\pi}_{T}`$.

Trace synthesis For each problem and its reference solution, we sample
reasoning traces

```math
\tau\sim\tilde{\pi}_{T}(\cdot\mid x,c)
```

from the trained teacher. These traces are the training data for stage
(2).

Problems and solutions The pairs $`\{(x,c)\}`$ are the $`1{,}533`$
training samples of the 1.7B teacher-trace setting (§5.1), covering
$`628`$ unique problems: $`x`$ is the problem and $`c`$ is the written
solution of the teacher trace with its reasoning removed. Each pair also
carries the problem’s final answer, which the correctness reward $`R`$
checks. The synthesized pairs $`\{(x,\tau)\}`$ are the off-policy data
for stage (2), where grafting replaces SFT.

Hyperparameters The teacher and the student are both initialized from
Qwen3-1.7B in thinking mode, and the maximum sequence length is $`18`$K
tokens.

## Appendix C Training

Grafting and direct SFT (math). Both fine-tune with identical
optimization and differ only in the checkpoint updated (the earlier
checkpoint for grafting, the post-trained model for direct SFT). The
grid spans nine learning rates ($`5{\times}10^{-7}`$ to
$`5{\times}10^{-5}`$) and five epochs, snapshotting after each epoch, at
global batch $`32`$ and sequence length $`18`$K. We optimize with AdamW
($`\beta{=}(0.9,0.95)`$, weight decay $`0.01`$) under a cosine schedule
with warmup ratio $`0.1`$ and gradient clipping at $`1.0`$.

Grafting and direct SFT (knowledge). Full fine-tuning at learning rate
$`2{\times}10^{-5}`$ with a cosine schedule, warmup ratio $`0.1`$,
$`\beta=(0.9,0.95)`$, weight decay $`0.01`$, gradient clip $`1.0`$,
global batch $`32`$, and sequence length $`18`$K. The knowledge QA
samples receive an empty-think prefix (\<think\>\n\n\</think\>), while
the replay samples receive the standard
\<think\>reasoning_content\</think\>.

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD). OPSD follows grafting and SFT in
terms of training hyperparameters. Here are OPSD-specific details: the
model samples its own response conditioned on a demonstration and is
trained to match a frozen, demonstration-conditioned teacher under the
divergence $`\mathrm{D}_{\alpha}`$, with rollout $`n{=}1`$ and top-$`k`$
$`100`$ for the teacher logits. We report $`\alpha\in\{0,0.5,1\}`$ for
OPSD.

## Appendix F Fisher estimation

We discuss the various derivation and implementation details of the
retention Fisher equation 4. Recall that the Diagonal Fisher
approximation leads to scores that weight the donor update by entry, and
the resulting projection equation 5 reduces to a $`\{0,1\}`$ mask
corresponding to the entries with the highest scores. In general, let us
consider an orthonormal basis
$`U\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}\times d_{\theta}}`$. As examples, the
Diagonal Fisher approximation corresponds to the standard coordinate
basis $`U=[e_{i}]`$. On the other hand, if $`U`$ is the eigenbasis of
$`F_{\mathrm{ret}}=U\Sigma U^{\top}`$, $`\Sigma=\diag(\sigma_{i})`$ then
we note the corresponding scores and projection:

```math
\displaystyle\Delta^{\top}F_{\mathrm{ret}}\Delta=(U^{\top}\Delta)^{\top}\Sigma(U^{\top}\Delta)=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\theta}}\sigma_{i}\left\langle u_{i},\Delta\right\rangle^{2}.
```

Thus, defining the score accordingly
$`s_{i}:=\sigma_{i}\left\langle u_{i},\Delta\right\rangle^{2}`$ and the
set $`S_{\rho}:=\{u_{i}:s_{i}\text{ in top-}\rho\text{ fraction}\}`$, a
mask in the diagonal case turns into a _projection_ onto the orthogonal
complement of $`S_{\rho}`$:
$`{\color[rgb]{0.1523,0.6836,0.375}\mathcal{M}_{\rho}}:=\mathrm{Proj}^{\perp}_{S_{\rho}}(\cdot)`$.
However, we note that the projection based on $`S_{\rho}`$ is exact only
when $`U`$ diagonalizes $`F_{\mathrm{ret}}`$, as otherwise projecting
based on scores $`s_{i}`$ ignores the contribution from off-diagonal
elements $`(F_{\mathrm{ret}})_{ij}\Delta_{i}\Delta_{j}`$, which can be
greatly suboptimal if $`\Delta_{i}`$ and $`\Delta_{j}`$ are tightly
coupled, e.g. parameters in the same weight matrix. The coordinate basis
is often a suboptimal choice for this purpose, and an exact
eigendecomposition of the full-dimensional Fisher is infeasible.

This is a similar motivation as prior optimization literature that seeks
to approximate Natural Gradient Descent for parametric maximum
likelihood estimation, which uses the Fisher Information matrix as a
preconditioner. A seminal work on this front is the KFAC optimizer
\[Martens and Grosse, 2015\]. The essential observation is that the
Fisher Information matrix can be approximated by a block-diagonal
matrix, where each block corresponds to a layer’s parameters; then, for
matrix-shaped weights
$`W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}`$ this can
further be approximated by a Kronecker factor between two symmetric
matrices of shape
$`P\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{out}}}`$,
$`Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}`$. For example,
given a feedforward weight matrix $`h=Wa`$, we have

```math
\displaystyle P=\mathbb{E}_{z}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\,|\,z)}\left[\frac{\partial L(\theta;y,z)}{\partial h}\frac{\partial L(\theta;y,z)}{\partial h}^{\top}\right],\quad Q=\mathbb{E}_{z}[aa^{\top}], \tag{6}
```

where $`L(\theta;y,z)=-\log p_{\theta}(y|z)`$ is the negative
log-likelihood of the model’s own generation $`y`$ given input $`z`$. We
note that for $`<2`$-D parameters the original Diagonal Fisher
approximation is made and scores computed as described in Section 4.
Consider the eigendecompositions $`P=U\Sigma U^{\top}`$,
$`Q=VDV^{\top}`$, and for a given update $`\Delta`$, consider the
(appropriately re-shaped) subset corresponding to the current layer
$`\Delta^{W}`$. Observe by the Kronecker mixed-product property we have:

```math
\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}(\Delta^{W})^{\top}(Q\otimes P)\mathrm{vec}(\Delta^{W}) \displaystyle=\Tr(P\Delta^{W}Q\Delta^{W})
```

```math
\displaystyle=\Tr(U\Sigma U^{\top}\Delta^{W}VDV^{\top}\Delta^{W})
```

```math
\displaystyle=\Tr(\Sigma\tilde{\Delta}^{W}D(\tilde{\Delta}^{W})^{\top}),\quad\tilde{\Delta}^{W}:=U^{\top}\Delta^{W}V
```

```math
\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\mathrm{out}},d_{\mathrm{in}}}\sigma_{i}d_{j}(\tilde{\Delta}^{W}_{i,j})^{2}.
```

Therefore, for this $`d_{\mathrm{out}}\cdot d_{\mathrm{in}}`$ parameter
subset of $`\Delta`$, the scores become
$`s_{(i,j)}=\sigma_{i}d_{j}(u_{i}^{\top}\Delta^{W}v_{j})^{2}`$. Tallying
the scores up across all scalar/vector parameters and tensor parameters,
the KFAC-based projection mask thus becomes a projection onto the
orthogonal complement of $`\{e_{k}\}\cup\{u_{i}\otimes v_{j}\}`$, where
$`s_{k},s_{(i,j)}`$ belong to the top-$`\rho`$ of scores across
scalar/vector and matrix parameters.

Though a KFAC decomposition does not in general perfectly diagonalize
the full Fisher Information, as it does not capture block-to-block
dependencies, it is known empirically to be a significantly better
decomposition than the diagonal \[George et al., 2018\].

#### Implementation.

To estimate the retention Fisher matrix, we compute the
per-token-normalized from $`1858`$ rollouts of the Qwen3-1.7B thinking
model $`\theta_{\text{post}}`$ at temperature $`1.0`$ prompted with
AoPS-style math problems. The $`\rho`$ score threshold is estimated by
taking a $`\rho`$-quantile of a subsample of the list of scores and
using that as the selection threshold.

The Diagonal Fisher projection is implemented efficiently via a mask.
The KFAC projection is slightly more arithmetic-intensive to compute due
to the layer-wise eigendecompositions and resulting matrix
multiplications, but remains efficient due to the layer-wise
factorization.

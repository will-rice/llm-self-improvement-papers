###### Abstract

On-policy distillation can transfer the reasoning capabilities of a
strong teacher to a smaller student. However, distillation from a
stronger teacher model faces two mismatches: its solutions may be
difficult for a smaller student to imitate, and its token-level
probabilities may be incompatible with, or even unavailable to, the
student. Existing methods handle these settings through different forms
of supervision. To this end, we propose _Bridged Self-Distillation_
(BSD), a unified framework for distilling from _any_ teacher that
provides reasoning traces. The model to be distilled from, termed the
_external teacher_, first provides a reasoning trace as privileged
context, which can be generated in an offline fashion. The student
generates rollouts without this context, while a copy of the student
conditioned on it, serving as the _self-teacher_, scores the same
rollouts in the student’s own token space. BSD uses the resulting
token-level guidance to adjust updates on unsuccessful rollouts, with a
verifier providing the outcome signal. As the external teacher is
queried only to produce text, the same training procedure applies to all
teacher scenarios: shared-tokenizer, cross-tokenizer, and closed API
teachers. Across these scenarios, BSD improves over the base model and
outperforms baselines tailored to each scenario, offering a novel and
unified pathway for distillation. Code is available:
[https://github.com/TUMLegalTech/Bridged-Self-Distillation](https://github.com/TUMLegalTech/Bridged-Self-Distillation)

## 1 Introduction

Large reasoning models have become increasingly capable on tasks that
require multi-step reasoning (Wei et al., 2022; Ahn et al., 2024;
Muennighoff et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025). Much of this progress comes
from post-training, typically including supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on
reasoning traces and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
(RLVR) (Ouyang et al., 2022; Chung et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Ye
et al., 2025a; Ye et al., 2025b; Yu et al., 2025). Yet the cost of
running a large reasoning model makes it desirable to transfer its
capabilities to smaller student models that can respond more quickly and
be deployed more widely (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016).
On-policy distillation (OPD) has emerged as a promising post-training
approach for this purpose, allowing a smaller model to learn from the
guidance of a stronger teacher (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024;
Ko et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Lu and Lab, 2025; Li et al., 2026b;
Song and Zheng, 2026).

In standard OPD, the student generates its own rollouts, and the teacher
scores the student’s tokens to guide training (Ross et al., 2011;
Agarwal et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). This requires
access to the teacher’s next-token distributions and a way to align them
with the student’s tokenization. However, when teacher and student use
different tokenizers, these distributions cannot be compared directly;
when the teacher is available only through a closed API, they may be
inaccessible. _Cross-tokenizer_ OPD methods address the former by
constructing mappings between the token spaces of the student and
teacher (Zhang et al., 2024; Minixhofer et al., 2025; Boizard et al.,
2025; Niu et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026), while _black-box_ methods
address the latter using feedback such as rubrics or sampled
continuations (Jiang et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2026a; Fang et al., 2026;
Xiong et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). These methods expand the range
of usable teachers, but require different training signals for different
teacher settings (Figure 1, top). Alternatively, SFT can train on
teacher-generated text without accessing probabilities, although
directly imitating a much stronger model’s reasoning can be difficult
for a small student (Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Huang
et al., 2026; Lyu et al., 2026). This motivates _a unified on-policy
learning method that can distill from any teacher capable of providing
reasoning traces_.

We propose _Bridged Self-Distillation_ (BSD) to turn the reasoning of an
_external teacher_ into guidance on the student’s own rollouts
(Figure 1, bottom). The external teacher is the model we aim to distill
from, and BSD requires only its reasoning trace. Instead of training the
student to imitate the teacher’s solution, as in SFT, we provide that
solution as privileged context to a copy of the student. Inspired by
on-policy self-distillation (Zhao et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Yang
et al., 2026a), this conditioned copy serves as a _self-teacher_,
scoring rollouts generated by the student without the privileged
context. Both versions score the same tokens in the student’s own token
space, and the differences show how access to the teacher’s reasoning
changes the student’s predictions along its rollouts. BSD uses these
differences to adjust token-level penalties on unsuccessful rollouts,
while a verifier determines the direction of the updates. Because the
external teacher provides only text, the same training procedure applies
to shared-tokenizer, cross-tokenizer, and closed API teachers.

We evaluate BSD under three forms of teacher access: (1)
shared-tokenizer, (2) cross-tokenizer, and (3) closed API teachers.
Across twelve teacher–student pairs and three math reasoning benchmarks,
BSD remains effective in all three settings while avoiding the severe
degradation that SFT exhibits in several pairs. Our contributions are as
follows:

- •
  We introduce BSD, a unified on-policy distillation framework that
  turns reasoning traces from external teachers into token-level
  guidance through a privileged self-teacher.
- •
  We evaluate BSD across twelve teacher–student pairs spanning
  shared-tokenizer, cross-tokenizer, and closed API teachers,
  demonstrating that BSD generally outperforms the baselines across all
  three settings.
- •
  Our analyses show that BSD maintains stable training, benefits from
  more complete reasoning traces, and concentrates its guidance on
  uncertain tokens, with stronger guidance associated with larger
  performance gains.

## 2 Preliminaries

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

Let $`\pi_{\theta}`$ denote the policy model being optimized. For a
question $`x`$, the policy samples a group of rollouts
$`\{y^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$, and a verifier assigns each rollout a binary
reward $`r^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}`$. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) normalizes these
rewards within the group:

```math
A^{(i)}\;=\;\frac{r^{(i)}-\operatorname{mean}\big(\{r^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{G}\big)}{\operatorname{std}\big(\{r^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{G}\big)}, \tag{1}
```

and applies the advantage $`A^{(i)}`$ uniformly to every token of
$`y^{(i)}`$ in a clipped policy-gradient update. When a group contains
both outcomes, successful rollouts have positive advantage and
unsuccessful rollouts have negative advantage. If all rollouts have the
same outcome, their advantages are zero, and the group contributes no
gradient. We omit the rollout index $`i`$ when considering a single
rollout.

Figure 2: Overview of Bridged Self-Distillation (BSD). An external

self-teacher (a frozen copy of the student conditioned on $`z`$) scores
the same tokens. The token-level guidance $`\delta_{t}`$ reweights the
penalty on unsuccessful rollouts, while the verifier determines the
direction of every update.

Privileged self-distillation. A privileged context $`z`$ is available
during training but absent when the model generates rollouts or performs
inference. In on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) (Zhao et al., 2026;
Hübotter et al., 2026), the model generates on-policy rollouts without
$`z`$ as the student. A copy conditioned on $`z`$ acts as the _teacher_
and scores the same tokens. RLSD (Yang et al., 2026a) uses the ratio of
these two token probabilities to refine the rollout-level advantage at
each token:

```math
\displaystyle u_{t} \displaystyle=\left[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,z,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}\right]^{\sign(A)}, \tag{2}
```

```math
\displaystyle\hat{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{RLSD}} \displaystyle=A\big[(1-\lambda)+\lambda\,\clip_{1\pm\epsilon}\!\left(u_{t}\right)\big].
```

Here, $`\clip_{1\pm\epsilon}\!\left(\cdot\right)`$ clips its argument to
$`[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]`$, and $`\lambda\in[0,1]`$ controls the
strength of token-level reweighting. Because the multiplier is positive,
reweighting preserves the sign of $`A`$. Intuitively, in RLSD, the
verifier determines the direction of the update while the privileged
context changes how strongly individual tokens are updated.

## 3 Bridged Self-Distillation

To distill from teacher models with different tokenizers or text-only
access, we need guidance that does not depend on their token
probabilities. Directly imitating a stronger teacher’s reasoning can
also be difficult for a smaller student. We therefore propose _Bridged
Self-Distillation_ (BSD), which only needs a teacher’s reasoning trace
and turns it into guidance on the student’s own rollouts (Figure 2). We
describe the key components below.

### 3.1 External Teacher

We refer to the model that we want to distill from as the _external
teacher_. Given a question $`x`$, it provides a _reasoning trace_ $`z`$,
the text it returns when solving $`x`$, which contains its full
reasoning when this is exposed and only its final response otherwise.
BSD uses $`z`$ as the privileged context for the _self-teacher_. Because
the external teacher only needs to provide text, this setup applies to
all scenarios, including shared-tokenizer, cross-tokenizer, and closed
API teachers.

### 3.2 Self-Teacher

To turn $`z`$ into guidance on the student’s own reasoning, the student
first generates a rollout $`y`$ from $`x`$ without $`z`$. For each
generated token $`y_{t}`$, the student assigns the probability
$`p_{t}=\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})`$.

The _self-teacher_ is a frozen copy $`\pi_{\bar{\theta}}`$ of the
student conditioned on both $`x`$ and $`z`$. Rather than generating a
separate solution, the self-teacher scores the same rollout $`y`$ token
by token, assigning $`q_{t}=\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(y_{t}\mid x,z,y_{<t})`$.
As the two scores come from the same model and share the same token
space, neither logits from the external teacher nor vocabulary alignment
is needed.

We define the token-level guidance as their per-token log-probability
difference:

```math
\delta_{t}\;=\;\sg\big[\log q_{t}-\log p_{t}\big], \tag{3}
```

where $`\sg[\cdot]`$ denotes the stop-gradient operator. A positive
$`\delta_{t}`$ means the self-teacher assigns more probability to the
student’s chosen token after reading $`z`$; a negative value means it
assigns less.

### 3.3 Learning

The self-teacher provides token-level guidance, while a verifier checks
whether each rollout reaches the correct answer. GRPO converts these
outcomes into the group-relative advantage $`A`$ (Eq. 1). For an
unsuccessful rollout with $`A<0`$, BSD instead uses the guidance
$`\delta_{t}`$ to set a token-specific weight:

```math
w_{t}\;=\;(1-\lambda)+\lambda\,\clip_{1\pm\epsilon}\!\left(e^{-\delta_{t}}\right). \tag{4}
```

When $`\delta_{t}<0`$, the self-teacher assigns less probability to the
chosen token, so $`w_{t}>1`$ and that token receives a stronger penalty.
When $`\delta_{t}>0`$, the penalty is reduced. The coefficient
$`\lambda`$ controls the strength of this reweighting, which decays over
training, while clipping bounds its effect.

The weight $`w_{t}`$ is applied only to unsuccessful rollouts, while the
successful ones are unchanged:

```math
\tilde{A}_{t}\;=\;\begin{cases}A\,w_{t}&\text{if }A<0,\\
A&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases} \tag{5}
```

This means that successful rollouts retain their advantage, as in GRPO,
allowing the student to reinforce correct solutions it found without the
external teacher’s guidance. Because $`w_{t}`$ is positive, the
token-level guidance changes the magnitude of a penalty without changing
the sign of $`A`$.

The student model is then optimized by maximizing the following
policy-gradient objective
$`\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t}\tilde{A}_{t}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})\big]`$.¹¹

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Models

Backbone models. We use three Qwen3 models as the student models (Yang
et al., 2025) (in thinking mode): Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-1.7B, and Qwen3-4B.
We also use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 as an additional backbone, which we
first fine-tune on 1,000 reasoning traces to enable it to produce
reasoning blocks. We refer to the fine-tuned model as Qwen3-4B-Instruct
and use it as another student.

Teacher models. We consider three models as external teachers with
different access settings: Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025) as a
shared-tokenizer teacher, Nemotron-3-30B-A3B (NVIDIA et al., 2025) as a
cross-tokenizer teacher, and GPT-5.6 Luna (OpenAI, 2026) as a closed API
teacher.

### 4.2 Training

Data. We use the mathematical reasoning subset of OpenThoughts (Guha
et al., 2026). For each teacher–student pair, we sample 2,400 training
problems, and the external teacher provides one reasoning trace $`z`$
per problem. The traces of Qwen3-32B and Nemotron-3-30B-A3B average
about 4,000 tokens, while GPT-5.6 Luna returns only its final response,
which averages 247 tokens.

Implementation details. We implement BSD using verl (Sheng et al., 2025)
and vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023). Each student model is trained for 150
updates on NVIDIA H100/H200 GPUs. Further details are provided in
Appendix A.

### 4.3 Evaluation

Benchmarks. We evaluate on three competition-level mathematics

2025 (Mathematical Association of America, 2025), and HMMT
2025 (Dekoninck et al., 2026). We select the best checkpoints using a
separate set of 128 development problems from OpenThoughts.

Metrics. For each benchmark problem, we generate four samples. We report

### 4.4 Baselines

We consider the following baselines, which use the same backbones and
training problems as BSD.

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). We train each student to imitate the
external teacher’s reasoning traces (Kim and Rush, 2016) using the same
traces that serve as privileged context for BSD.

Vanilla OPD. For the shared-tokenizer teacher, we use standard OPD (Lu
and Lab, 2025), which is optimized by minimizing the log-probability
differences between student and teacher.

Cross-Tokenizer OPD (xOPD). For the cross-tokenizer teacher, we use
cross-tokenizer OPD (Niu et al., 2026), denoted xOPD, which aligns the
two tokenizations and maps teacher token log-probabilities onto student
tokens.

Rubric-Based OPD (ROPD). For the closed API teacher, we use ROPD (Fang
et al., 2026), which asks the teacher to derive a rubric for each
question and score the student’s rollouts against it.

## 5 Main Results

We examine how BSD performs under different forms of teacher access.
Across twelve teacher–student pairs, we first compare BSD with SFT and
the OPD baseline designed for each of the shared-tokenizer,
cross-tokenizer, and closed API settings. We then track performance and
response length throughout training to examine how BSD and the baselines
affect the student’s behavior.

### 5.1 Comparing Teacher Access

BSD consistently improves over the base model and outperforms the
distillation baselines across teacher settings. Table 1 reports Avg@4
for four students paired with shared-tokenizer, cross-tokenizer, and
closed API teachers. BSD improves over the base model in all twelve
teacher–student pairs, with gains of 1.2 to 4.7 points, and achieves the
best average score in eleven pairs.

Shared-tokenizer teacher. Qwen3-32B shares the students’ tokenizer,
allowing vanilla OPD to score their rollouts directly with teacher token
probabilities. BSD outperforms SFT for all four students by 3.3 to 8.6
points in average score, while SFT falls below the base model for three
of them. Thus, even within the same model family, directly imitating a
much stronger teacher’s reasoning can be difficult for smaller
students (Zhang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). BSD also matches or
outperforms vanilla OPD for every student: the two tie in average score
for Qwen3-1.7B, and BSD scores up to 3.6 points higher for the other
three. This indicates that sharing a tokenizer does not ensure
compatible reasoning patterns between distinct models, which prior work
identifies as important for successful OPD (Li et al., 2026b). BSD
instead uses a self-teacher, a copy of the student conditioned on the
external teacher’s reasoning, so that the token-level guidance comes
from the student’s own model and is therefore more compatible.

Cross-tokenizer teacher. Because the teacher model Nemotron-3-30B-A3B
uses a different tokenizer from the students, xOPD must align the two
tokenizations and map the teacher’s token probabilities onto the
student’s tokens. Nevertheless, xOPD does not yield consistent gains
over the base models. It improves the average score of Qwen3-0.6B by 3.4
points, but brings only marginal gains of 0.5 and 0.6 points for
Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-4B-Instruct, respectively, and decreases the score of
Qwen3-1.7B by 0.8 points. This indicates that constructing a mapping
makes cross-tokenizer OPD possible, but does not guarantee effective
token-level supervision. In contrast, BSD improves all four base models
by 1.2 to 4.4 points and outperforms xOPD for three of the four
students, with Qwen3-0.6B as the only exception. BSD therefore provides
a strong alternative for cross-tokenizer distillation, obtaining
guidance directly in the student’s token space without mappings between
distinct vocabularies.

Closed API teacher. GPT-5.6 Luna is accessible only through a text API
and exposes neither token probabilities nor its tokenizer. Moreover, it
returns short final responses averaging 247 tokens, compared with about
4,000 tokens from the other two teachers. Directly using these responses
as SFT targets causes the students to imitate their length (cf.
Section 5.2). Consequently, SFT falls below the base model for three of
the four students, with Qwen3-0.6B dropping from 10.8 to 2.8. ROPD
handles the lack of token probabilities by asking the external teacher
to derive a rubric and score student rollouts during training. BSD
outperforms SFT for all four students by 3.1 to 9.7 points and ROPD by
0.6 to 3.6 points. BSD achieves these gains by using each API response
as privileged context for the self-teacher while allowing the student to
generate its own rollouts, without training the student to reproduce
their form.

### 5.2 Training Progression

We further examine how distillation changes the students throughout
training. Figure 3 tracks two aspects of this progression: response
length across all twelve teacher--student pairs and benchmark
performance in a representative closed API setting.²² 2 More performance
curves for other teacher–student pairs are provided in Appendix H, where
SFT performance also frequently deteriorates over training.

Response length. Figure 3a–c shows that the effect of SFT on response
length depends strongly on the external teacher. With the
shared-tokenizer teacher, response lengths remain largely stable for all
methods. With the cross-tokenizer teacher, BSD and xOPD remain stable,
whereas SFT usually increases the response lengths, most notably for
Qwen3-1.7B. The most pronounced shift occurs with the closed API
teacher, whose responses average only 247 tokens. SFT trains directly on
these short responses, causing all four students to gradually shorten
their outputs. In contrast, BSD and the corresponding OPD baselines
maintain relatively stable response lengths across all twelve pairs.
These trends show that using the external teacher’s reasoning trace as
privileged context allows BSD to preserve the student’s output form
without forcing it to reproduce the trace’s length.

illustrates this behavior for Qwen3-4B-Instruct distilled from GPT-5.6
Luna. SFT is already below the base model at the beginning, and its
performance falls to nearly zero by the end of training. The additional
curves in Appendix H show similar collapses or declines of SFT in other
teacher–student pairs. These results further indicate that directly
imitating a stronger teacher’s reasoning traces can lead to unstable
training. In contrast, ROPD and BSD maintain stable or slightly
improving performance throughout training. BSD is particularly notable
because it uses the same external-teacher reasoning traces as SFT, but
treats them as privileged context to guide the student’s own rollouts
rather than as targets to imitate. Appendix I further shows that SFT
suffers from catastrophic forgetting on two out-of-domain benchmarks,
whereas BSD does not.

## 6 Further Analysis

### 6.1 Ablation Study

BSD combines privileged context from an external teacher with
verifier-guided learning and applies token-level guidance only to
unsuccessful rollouts. We ablate these design choices by asking three
questions: (1) _whether privileged context itself improves learning_,
(2) _how much reasoning the context should contain_, and (3) _whether
the verifier and the restriction to unsuccessful rollouts are needed_.
We study these questions in the shared-tokenizer setting using Qwen3-4B
as the student and Qwen3-32B as the external teacher. All variants use
the same training problems and optimization setup.

BSD-trace, which uses the full reasoning trace as in the main
experiments, serves as the reference. For (1), we compare it with
GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), which has no privileged context or
self-teacher, and RLSD (Yang et al., 2026a), which uses only the
ground-truth answer while otherwise following BSD. For (2), BSD-hint
uses a three-sentence hint and BSD-guide uses up to eight reasoning
steps without computations, both followed by the answer and derived from
the full trace (obtained by using the prompts in Appendix B). For (3),
BSD (w/ succ.) additionally weights successful rollouts, while
OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026) uses the self-teacher’s token-level signal on
every rollout without a verifier. Figure 4 reports training accuracy in
panels (a)–(b) and Avg@4 in panel (c).

Privileged context facilitates reinforcement learning. GRPO, RLSD, and
BSD-trace exhibit nearly identical training-accuracy curves, with their
average differences remaining below 0.2, although BSD-trace shows a
slight advantage in the middle of training (Figure 4a). Nevertheless,
Avg@4 increases from 59.2 for the base model to 60.6 with GRPO, 61.7
with answer-conditioned RLSD, and 63.9 with trace-conditioned BSD.
Privileged context therefore improves how verifier feedback transfers to
downstream problems.

More complete reasoning produces stronger gains. Changing the privileged
context from a short hint to a full trace has little effect on the
training dynamics, as the three variants follow similar accuracy curves
(Figure 4b). However, BSD-hint scores 61.7, while BSD-guide reaches 62.5
and BSD-trace further improves to 63.9 (Figure 4c), indicating that
downstream performance benefits from more detailed reasoning in the
privileged context.

The verifier and the restriction to unsuccessful rollouts matter. BSD
(w/ succ.) scores 61.4, below the 63.9 of BSD-trace, so extending the
weight to successful rollouts does not help (Figure 4c). OPSD scores
59.7 and declines over training, from 63.3 at step 25 to 55.8 at step
150, while its responses on the benchmarks shorten from 16.8k to 12.5k
tokens (Appendix D). The other variants keep the response length of the
base model.

is associated with BSD’s gains, and how it affects the student’s
behavior. Table 2 reports the quantitative results averaged across
teachers, with the measurement setup described in Appendix A, while
Figure 5 illustrates a concrete example.

Guidance targets uncertain tokens and tracks performance gains. The

## 7 Related Work

Knowledge distillation. Knowledge distillation transfers supervision
from a stronger teacher to a smaller student through output
distributions or generated sequences (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush,
2016). For reasoning models, this idea is commonly instantiated by
training on teacher-generated reasoning traces, although large
teacher–student gaps can make direct imitation difficult (Shridhar
et al., 2023; Hsieh et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Huang et al.,
2026; Lyu et al., 2026). On-policy distillation instead learns from
student-generated rollouts under token-level teacher guidance (Gu
et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2026; Yang et al.,
2026b). Recent variants extend OPD to heterogeneous tokenizers
(Minixhofer et al., 2025; Boizard et al., 2025; Niu et al., 2026; Sun
et al., 2026) and black-box teachers (Ye et al., 2026a; Xiong et al.,
2026; Fang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026), but typically require
setting-specific supervision. In contrast, BSD offers an effective and
unified framework for all these settings.

Privileged information and self-distillation. Learning with privileged
information provides a general framework for exploiting supervision that
is available only during training, with generalized distillation
connecting this idea to teacher–student learning (Vapnik and Vashist,
2009; Lopez-Paz et al., 2016). In language models, context distillation
instantiates this principle by internalizing information from
instructions, demonstrations, or other contextual signals into a model
that later operates without them (Snell et al., 2022; Shin et al., 2025;
Duan et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2026b). Recent self-distillation methods
further use the model itself as a privileged teacher by conditioning it
on answers, solutions, feedback, or successful trajectories (Zhao
et al., 2026; Penaloza et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026; Ye et al.,
2026b; Ding, 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a; Pan et al.,
2026; Li et al., 2026a; Kim et al., 2026). BSD follows this privileged
paradigm, but uses reasoning from an _external teacher_ as the
privileged context.

## 8 Conclusion

We introduced BSD, a unified framework for distilling from any teacher
that provides reasoning traces. BSD uses the external teacher’s
reasoning trace as privileged context for a self-teacher, producing
compatible token-level guidance in the student’s own token space without
teacher probabilities or vocabulary mappings. Across twelve
teacher–student pairs spanning shared-tokenizer, cross-tokenizer, and
closed API settings, BSD improves every base model and achieves the best
or tied average performance among the distillation methods in eleven
pairs. Our analyses further show that BSD supports stable training,
benefits from more complete reasoning, and focuses its guidance on
uncertain tokens, establishing reasoning traces as a common and
effective interface for on-policy distillation.

## Limitations

We evaluate BSD with shared-tokenizer, cross-tokenizer, and closed API
teachers across twelve teacher–student pairs and analyze its token-level
guidance. However, we acknowledge three main limitations.

First, due to computational constraints, our experiments are limited to
students of up to 4B parameters from one model family and to English
competition mathematics. Future work could examine whether the gains of
BSD hold for larger students, other model families, and other domains.

Second, BSD relies on a verifier to set the direction of each update, so
it applies only to tasks whose answers can be checked automatically and
not to open-ended ones. Whether the agreement between the student and
the external teacher can serve as a verifier in such tasks remains to be
explored.

Finally, our analysis relates the token-level guidance to the gains of
BSD only through correlations across students. It characterizes where
the guidance concentrates but does not establish which tokens it helps,
or why, which we leave for future work.

## Ethics Statement

This work fine-tunes publicly released language models on publicly
available mathematical reasoning datasets and evaluates them with
automatic verifiers. It involves no human subjects, no personally
identifiable information, and no newly collected data. The method
transfers reasoning capability that the teacher models already expose
through their public interfaces, so we do not identify risks beyond
those general to improving open-weight reasoning models.

The closed API teacher is queried through its official API solely to
obtain responses for non-commercial research. The students trained with
these responses serve only to study distillation methods: we do not
deploy them, offer them as a service, or use them to develop models that
compete with the API provider. We release only code, and release no
model weights and no datasets, including the teacher’s reasoning traces.

Use of AI Assistants. The authors used AI assistants for code
development, the organization of experiments and analyses, figure
preparation, and manuscript drafting and language editing. All reported
experimental results and scientific interpretations were reviewed by the
authors, who take responsibility for the final paper.

## Appendix A Experimental Details

#### Teacher traces.

Teacher traces are generated once, before training, with the settings in
Table 3 and the prompts in Appendix B. For a closed teacher that hides
its reasoning, the trace is built from the response it returns: we
enclose the derivation of the response in a reasoning block (\<think\>
and \</think\>) and place its boxed final answer after the block, which
is the format of the students’ own responses. Within a teacher–student
pair, BSD and SFT use the same traces, BSD as privileged context for the
self-teacher and SFT as training targets.

#### Training.

Tables 4 and 5 give the training configuration. All methods train LoRA
adapters (Hu et al., 2022), and the learning rate is warmed up linearly
over the first 10 updates and is constant afterwards. Training sets are
identical for all methods within a teacher–student pair. For BSD, the
student’s score $`p_{t}`$ uses its current log-probabilities, and the
rollout–trainer gap is corrected with token-level truncated importance
sampling (Yu et al., 2025). During training, BSD adds one forward pass
of the self-teacher over the student’s rollouts. This pass takes 7 to 18
seconds per update, depending on the length of the trace, which is under
10% of the update time. The limit of 2,048 tokens on the prompt applies
to the student’s prompt, which contains only the question. The
self-teacher’s prompt also contains the trace and has a separate limit
of 12,288 tokens. The longest question and trace together take 8,950
tokens, so no trace is truncated in any run; the hint and guide of
Section 6.1 are shorter than the full trace.

#### Guidance measurement.

For Table 2, each student generates six rollouts for each of 24 hard
problems shared by all students before training, and each rollout is
scored under one verified trace from each of the three teachers. We
write $`\bar{\delta}`$ for the mean of the signed guidance
$`\delta_{t}`$ over all tokens of the unsuccessful rollouts, and Table 2
reports its absolute value $`|\bar{\delta}|`$. The mean is negative for
every teacher–student pair, so the self-teacher assigns on average a
lower probability to the student’s tokens than the student does. This
quantity differs from the mean absolute guidance
$`\overline{|\delta|}`$, in which positive and negative values do not
cancel; it is 0.163, 0.191, 0.202, and 0.110 for Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-1.7B,
Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-4B-Instruct, respectively, so the last student has
the smallest value under both statistics. The correlation $`\rho_{H}`$
is computed within each of the same rollouts and averaged over them.

## Appendix B Prompt Templates

All prompts are given as the user message of the model’s chat template.
For Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-1.7B, and Qwen3-0.6B, the student is prompted with
the question:

> Problem: {question $`x`$}
>
> Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}.

The self-teacher receives the same question together with the external
teacher’s trace:

> Problem: {question $`x`$}
>
> Here is a reference solution to this problem:
>
> \<reference_solution\>  
> {trace $`z`$}  
> \</reference_solution\>
>
> After reading the reference solution above, make sure you truly
> understand the reasoning behind each step — do not copy or paraphrase
> it. Now, using your own words and independent reasoning, derive the
> same final answer to the problem above. Think step by step, explore
> different approaches, and don’t be afraid to backtrack or reconsider
> if something doesn’t work out:
>
> Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}.

For Qwen3-4B-Instruct, the chat template opens the reasoning block
(\<think\>) at the start of the response, and the last line of both
prompts is additionally replaced by:

> Think through the problem step by step inside \<think\> and \</think\>
> tags. After \</think\>, give your final solution and put the final
> answer within \boxed{}.

The token-level guidance is computed on the student’s original response
tokens appended after each prompt; responses are not decoded and
re-tokenized.

#### Teacher traces.

All three external teachers generate their traces from the same prompt:

> Problem: {question $`x`$}
>
> Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}.

#### RLSD ablation.

For RLSD (Section 6.1), the self-teacher receives the ground-truth
answer in place of the trace:

> Problem: {question $`x`$}
>
> Here is a verified correct final answer to this problem:
>
> \<reference_solution\>  
> {answer}  
> \</reference_solution\>
>
> After reading the reference solution above, make sure you truly
> understand the reasoning behind each step — do not copy or paraphrase
> it. Now, using your own words and independent reasoning, derive the
> same final answer to the problem above. Think step by step, explore
> different approaches, and don’t be afraid to backtrack or reconsider
> if something doesn’t work out:
>
> Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}.

#### Guide and hint ablations.

For the ablation on the form of the privileged context (Section 6.1),
the external teacher rewrites its trace as a guide or as a hint. The
guide is generated with:

> You are helping a student who is stuck on a math problem. Below are
> the problem and a worked reasoning trace written by a stronger solver.
>
> Problem:  
> {question $`x`$}

## Appendix C Proofs

Throughout, expectations are over prompts $`x`$, traces $`z`$, and
rollouts $`y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)`$, where
$`\theta_{\mathrm{old}}`$ is the policy that sampled the rollouts and
$`\bar{\theta}`$ is the frozen copy of the student that serves as the
self-teacher (Section 3.2), so that
$`p_{t}=\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})`$ and
$`q_{t}=\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(y_{t}\mid x,z,y_{<t})`$ in Eq. 3; the
response mask is left implicit. The quantities $`A`$, $`\delta_{t}`$,
and $`w_{t}`$ carry no gradient, so the statements below do not depend
on how often the self-teacher is synchronized.

### C.1 Small-Gap Decomposition

Reverse-KL OPD trains the student with the token-level advantage
$`\hat{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{OPD}}=\log\pi^{*}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})-\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})`$,
where $`\pi^{*}`$ is the teacher and $`s_{t}=(x,y_{<t})`$; with a
reference policy $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ it is equivalent to
KL-regularized RL with the token reward
$`r_{t}=\log\pi^{*}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})-\allowbreak\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})`$ (Agarwal
et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026b). Taking the self-teacher as
$`\pi^{*}`$ and the unconditioned student as $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$
gives $`\log q_{t}-\log p_{t}`$, whose stop-gradient value is
$`\delta_{t}`$, so $`r_{t}^{\mathrm{OPD},z}=\delta_{t}`$. Writing
$`\Phi_{\lambda,\epsilon}(u)=(1-\lambda)+\lambda\,\clip_{1\pm\epsilon}\!\left(e^{-u}\right)`$,
Eqs. 5 and 4 read

```math
\tilde{A}_{t}\;=\;A\cdot\Phi_{\lambda,\epsilon}(\delta_{t})\,\mathbf{1}_{\{A<0\}}\;+\;A\,\mathbf{1}_{\{A\geq 0\}}, \tag{6}
```

and since $`\Phi_{\lambda,\epsilon}>0`$,
$`\sign(\tilde{A}_{t})=\sign(A)`$, whereas the sign of
$`\hat{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{OPD}}`$ follows $`\delta_{t}`$.

###### Proposition 1 (Small-gap decomposition).

Ignoring clipping and evaluating the update at the policy that sampled
the rollouts, $`\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}`$, for
$`|\delta_{t}|\ll 1`$ the BSD policy gradient satisfies

```math
g_{\mathrm{BSD}}\;=\;g_{\mathrm{GRPO}}\;+\;O(\delta_{t}^{2})\\
\;+\;\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\textstyle\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{A<0\}}\,|A|\;\delta_{t}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})\right]. \tag{7}
```

This decomposition is an interpretation and does not guarantee stability
or good credit assignment. It holds for any privileged context $`z`$.
With $`z`$ the ground-truth answer it describes the RLSD variant of
Section 6.1, which also restricts the weight to unsuccessful rollouts;
with a teacher trace, $`r_{t}^{\mathrm{OPD},z}`$ carries information
from another model’s reasoning.

### C.2 Proof of Proposition 1

The weight of Eq. 4 applies only to tokens with $`A<0`$; tokens with
$`A\geq 0`$ contribute their GRPO term unchanged. On $`A<0`$ the BSD
token advantage is

```math
\tilde{A}_{t}=A\big[(1-\lambda)+\lambda e^{-\delta_{t}}\big], \tag{8}
```

where clipping is ignored (it is inactive for $`|\delta_{t}|`$ small,
since the clip interval $`[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]`$ contains a
neighborhood of $`1`$). Expanding
$`e^{-\delta_{t}}=1-\delta_{t}+O(\delta_{t}^{2})`$ gives

```math
\begin{split}\tilde{A}_{t}&=A\big[1-\lambda\,\delta_{t}\big]+O\!\big(|A|\,\delta_{t}^{2}\big)\\
&=A-\lambda\,A\,\delta_{t}+O\!\big(|A|\,\delta_{t}^{2}\big).\end{split} \tag{9}
```

Substituting into the policy gradient evaluated at the rollout policy,
$`g=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t}\tilde{A}_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})]\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}`$,
where the PPO likelihood ratio equals one and its clipping is inactive,
and writing $`-A=|A|`$ on the reweighted tokens ($`A<0`$) yields

```math
g_{\mathrm{BSD}}=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t}A\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\Big]}_{g_{\mathrm{GRPO}}}+O(\delta_{t}^{2})\\
+\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{A<0\}}\,|A|\,\delta_{t}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\Big]. \tag{10}
```

Since $`\delta_{t}=r_{t}^{\mathrm{OPD},z}`$, the correction is an
outcome-weighted OPD update restricted to unsuccessful rollouts. ∎

### C.3 Weighting Successful Rollouts

Proposition 1 concerns unsuccessful rollouts only. Two different weights
can be applied to rollouts with $`A>0`$, and they act in opposite
directions. The first is the weight of RLSD (Eq. 2),
$`(1-\lambda)+\lambda\,\clip_{1\pm\epsilon}\!\left(e^{+\delta_{t}}\right)`$,
which is the one used by the BSD (w/ succ.) variant of Section 6.1.
Under the conditions of Proposition 1, it gives

```math
\begin{split}\tilde{A}_{t}&=A\big[(1-\lambda)+\lambda e^{\delta_{t}}\big]\\
&=A+\lambda\,|A|\,\delta_{t}+O\!\big(|A|\,\delta_{t}^{2}\big),\end{split} \tag{11}
```

so the correction has the same form as on unsuccessful rollouts. The
update of BSD (w/ succ.) is then the GRPO update plus an
outcome-weighted OPD correction on all rollouts, which also pulls
successful rollouts toward the self-teacher. The second applies the
weight of Eq. 4, with $`e^{-\delta_{t}}`$, unchanged to successful
rollouts. It gives
$`\tilde{A}_{t}=A-\lambda\,|A|\,\delta_{t}+O(|A|\,\delta_{t}^{2})`$,
whose correction has the opposite sign and pushes the policy away from
the self-teacher on successful rollouts, rewarding divergence as in
RLRT (Kim et al., 2026). We do not evaluate this second weight.

BSD uses neither weight. A successful rollout is a solution that the
student found without the trace, and BSD reinforces it with its GRPO
advantage alone, without moving it toward or away from the self-teacher.
The expansion above describes what the first weight adds but does not by
itself predict its effect on accuracy; the support for restricting the
weight to unsuccessful rollouts is empirical, as BSD (w/ succ.) scores
61.4 against 63.9 for BSD (Section 6.1).

## Appendix I Out-of-Domain Evaluation

To examine how the distillation methods affect out-of-domain
performance, we evaluate the Qwen3-4B student on general knowledge and
legal questions, at every saved checkpoint of BSD, SFT, and the OPD
variant of each teacher. We use two sets of 200 multiple-choice
questions: from MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024), 20 questions from each of
its ten categories other than mathematics, physics, chemistry, and
engineering, and from LEXam (Fan et al., 2026), 200 four-choice
questions in English and German, sampled in the proportion of the two
languages in the benchmark. The protocol is that of Table 7, and the
base model scores 63.5 on the MMLU-Pro questions and 45.9 on the LEXam
questions.

Figure 9 shows that SFT is unstable during training, whereas BSD and the
OPD variants are not. On the MMLU-Pro questions, SFT with GPT-5.6 Luna
falls steadily from 62.1 at step 25 to 54.1 at step 150, 9.4 points
below the base model, and SFT with Nemotron-3-30B-A3B falls from 64.0 to
60.8. On the LEXam questions, SFT with GPT-5.6 falls to 40.5 at step
100, 5.4 points below the base model. Under a bootstrap paired over
questions, these three differences from the base model exclude zero at
the 95% level, whereas no difference of SFT with Qwen3-32B does. In
contrast, at every checkpoint and for all three teachers, BSD and the
OPD variants are never more than 1.1 points below the base model on the
MMLU-Pro questions and 3.5 points on the LEXam questions. Learning from
the student’s own rollouts therefore preserves its abilities outside the
training domain, whereas imitating the teacher’s reasoning traces can
erase them.

Figure 10 shows the mean response length in the same evaluations. With
GPT-5.6 Luna, SFT shortens the responses from 3,031 to 1,059 tokens on
the MMLU-Pro questions and from 4,255 to 1,975 tokens on the LEXam
questions, against 3,159 and 4,302 tokens for the base model, and with
Nemotron-3-30B-A3B it lengthens them to 3,600 tokens on the MMLU-Pro
questions. BSD and the OPD variants stay within 300 tokens of the base
model at every checkpoint. The loss of out-of-domain accuracy under SFT
thus coincides with the change in response length that it shows on
mathematics (Section 5.2).

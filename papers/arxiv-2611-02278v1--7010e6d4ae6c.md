## 1  Introduction

Reasoning models trade tokens for accuracy. A longer thinking budget
lets a model keep reasoning where it would otherwise stop, often
correcting itself, and this sequential test-time scaling is a large part
of why thinking models are strong \[[24](#bib.bib24),
[13](#bib.bib13)\]; more generally, test-time compute spent well can let
a model outperform a much larger one \[[34](#bib.bib34)\]. It is also
expensive, and much of the expense may buy nothing: thinking models are
known to keep reasoning on problems they have already solved
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [35](#bib.bib35)\], and within a problem their shorter
chains are more often right \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. Two questions follow.
How much of a thinking trace comes after its answer is already fixed?
And can a model be trained to drop that part without losing the
reasoning that the rest of the trace does?

We answer both for Qwen3.5-9B \[[28](#bib.bib28), [30](#bib.bib30)\], an
open-weight 9B thinking model, on whole evaluation sets: a 498-problem
development suite with eight samples per problem and model, and 8,983
held-out problems. Concise-9B is Qwen3.5-9B after two rounds of _concise
self-training_: rejection-sampling fine-tuning \[[44](#bib.bib44),
[43](#bib.bib43), [33](#bib.bib33)\], run on-policy, that keeps the two
shortest correct samples per problem, plus a small share of
answer-synthesis targets, a form of shortest-correct selection
\[[25](#bib.bib25)\]. The recipe was developed on Qwen3.5-4B
\[[28](#bib.bib28), [29](#bib.bib29)\] and applied to the 9B model
unchanged, with the evaluated round fixed in advance. To measure slack
we use forced readouts: every 2,048 thinking tokens we close the trace’s
thinking and let the model state its answer, and we call the first point
after which every readout gives the trace’s final answer the point where
the answer is _fixed_.

#### Findings.

- •
  Fewer tokens at matched accuracy. At a 32K thinking budget Concise-9B
  generates 0.66$`\times`$ the tokens of Qwen3.5-9B on 8,983 held-out
  problems, with accuracy equivalent within $`\pm`$1 point
  ($`-18`$ \[$`-69`$, $`+32`$\] problems). On the development suite it
  uses 0.61$`\times`$ the tokens per sample, and single-sample accuracy,
  majority voting over three and four traces and pass@4
  \[[6](#bib.bib6)\] are equivalent within $`\pm`$2 points (majority of
  two within $`\pm`$3;
  [Section 4](#S4 "4 Concise-9B against Qwen3.5-9B")).
- •
  Most of a trace is slack. For Qwen3.5-9B, 54% (hard set) and 64%
  (confirmation set) of thinking tokens come after the answer is fixed;
  for half of its traces the answer is fixed within the first 36% of the
  thinking ([Figures 1](#S1.F1 "In Findings. ‣ 1 Introduction")
  and [5](#S5 "5 Test-time slack")).
- •
  Concise training removes mostly slack. Of the thinking tokens
  Concise-9B saves, 86% \[83, 90\] come after the answer is fixed. The
  part before it shrinks by only 12%, and it is not simply cut short: at
  the lengths where Concise-9B stops, it is right on $`+64`$ \[$`+36`$,
  $`+92`$\] more hard problems than the base model’s own answers at
  those lengths, $`+45`$ \[$`+17`$, $`+73`$\] problems beyond what
  further base-model samples show
  ([Section 5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 What concise training removes ‣ 5 Test-time slack")).
- •
  Trimming at inference needs hindsight. Stopping each trace where its
  answer is fixed would keep every answer at 0.35–0.44$`\times`$ the
  tokens of Qwen3.5-9B, but stopping when three consecutive readouts
  agree loses 1.5–9.2 points (two: 3.4–13.7;
  [Section 5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Trimming slack at inference ‣ 5 Test-time slack")).
- •
  A limit on long problems. With truncated traces continued to 80K
  tokens, Concise-9B solves 25 fewer of 1,276 hard problems
  ($`-25`$ \[$`-49`$, $`-1`$\]), 15 of them in competition mathematics
  ([Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Held-out sets ‣ 4 Concise-9B against Qwen3.5-9B")).
- •
  A cheap commit signal. A trace whose final answer agrees with its own
  last two readouts needs no second sample; this rule matched two-sample
  escalation in two pre-registered tests at 0.67$`\times`$ and
  0.57$`\times`$ its tokens
  ([Section 6](#S6 "6 Using slack for adaptive sampling")).

We claim no new training algorithm or stopping rule: concise
self-training and shortest-correct selection exist \[[25](#bib.bib25),
[42](#bib.bib42), [18](#bib.bib18)\], and stopping when consecutive
readouts agree follows the answer-consistency rule of [Liu and Wang
\[21\]](#bib.bib21), which we apply on a coarser readout grid (every
2,048 thinking tokens with $`k=3`$ or $`k=2`$; they read out at sentence
boundaries and default to $`k=10`$). What we add is a careful
measurement of what such training removes, on a strong native thinking
model and on sets large enough to bound the differences tightly.

## 2  Related work

#### Overthinking and concise reasoning.

Thinking models spend many tokens on easy problems \[[8](#bib.bib8)\];
[Sui et al. \[35\]](#bib.bib35) survey remedies. [Hassid et al.
\[14\]](#bib.bib14) find that shorter chains are more often correct and
vote over the first chains to finish. Self-training on short correct
samples shortens reasoning: [Munkhbat et al. \[25\]](#bib.bib25)
fine-tune non-thinking models on best-of-$`N`$ shortest correct samples,
[Yang et al. \[42\]](#bib.bib42) select the shortest correct response
for self-improvement, and [Kimi Team \[18\]](#bib.bib18) fine-tune on
the shortest correct response out of eight samples per question to make
short-reasoning models more token-efficient. Reinforcement learning
controls length through the reward \[[1](#bib.bib1), [4](#bib.bib4),
[17](#bib.bib17)\], and budget-aware objectives train for good answers
at token budgets sampled across the trace \[[26](#bib.bib26)\]. Our
recipe is the self-training kind; our contribution is the measurement of
what it removes.

#### When the answer forms.

Budget forcing ends thinking at a budget by appending an end-of-thinking
delimiter and, optionally, an answer cue \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]; we apply
the same intervention, with our own cue, to read a trace’s answer at
many points. Several methods stop a single trace once its intermediate
answers converge \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] or its confidence in a trial
answer is high \[[40](#bib.bib40)\], and DeepConf ends low-confidence
traces early while voting over parallel traces \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. We
measure how much such stopping could save in hindsight, how much a
simple online rule saves, and how this compares with training.

#### Self-training and adaptive sampling.

Rejection-sampling fine-tuning includes STaR \[[44](#bib.bib44)\], RFT
\[[43](#bib.bib43)\], RAFT \[[10](#bib.bib10)\] and ReST^(EM)
\[[33](#bib.bib33)\]; STaR, RAFT and ReST^(EM) iterate it, in the manner
of expert iteration, which was introduced with tree search as the expert
\[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. Self-consistency returns the majority answer of
sampled chains \[[37](#bib.bib37)\], and adaptive variants stop sampling
once a clear majority emerges or a window of samples agrees
\[[2](#bib.bib2), [19](#bib.bib19)\]. Our self-agreement rule uses a
trace’s own earlier answers as the agreement signal.

## 3  Setup

#### Model.

Qwen3.5-9B \[[28](#bib.bib28), [30](#bib.bib30)\] reasons in a delimited
thinking block before answering; its 32 layers are eight repeats of
three Gated DeltaNet layers \[[41](#bib.bib41)\] and one gated-attention
layer \[[27](#bib.bib27)\]. We use each model’s recommended
thinking-mode sampling (for the Qwen3.5 models temperature 1.0,
top-$`p`$ 0.95, top-$`k`$ 20 and presence penalty 1.5; for
Olmo-3-7B-Think temperature 0.6 and top-$`p`$ 0.95) and call each
independent draw a _trace_. Each prompt is the problem followed by
“Reason carefully. Give one final answer in \boxed{…}.”

#### Concise self-training.

MATH and GSM8K training sets, MMLU test questions outside the
development suite and GPQA questions outside GPQA-Diamond
\[[16](#bib.bib16), [9](#bib.bib9), [15](#bib.bib15),
[31](#bib.bib31)\]; [Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Training details")
gives its composition. Each round uses 3,000 fresh pool entries (2,945
and 2,934 distinct problems, since the hard MATH subset repeats MATH
identifiers) and has three steps.

1.  1.  Collect. The current model draws four samples per problem with an
        8K-token budget, and makes two _synthesis passes_: calls that read
        the four samples and re-derive the answer.
2.  2.  Select. Keep the two shortest samples that are correct and finished
        naturally. Keep the correct synthesis passes of problems whose
        samples disagreed or whose majority was wrong, plus enough of the
        other correct synthesis passes to make up 30% of the synthesis
        targets.
3.  3.  Train. Fine-tune all parameters of the text backbone for one epoch
        on the kept targets, with the loss on generated tokens only (AdamW
        \[[22](#bib.bib22)\], peak learning rate $`2\times 10^{-6}`$,
        batches of 32 sequences).

The two rounds kept 4,230 and 4,690 targets, 9% of them synthesis
passes. Concise-9B is the second round. The recipe, including the number
of rounds, was developed on Qwen3.5-4B
([Section 4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 The development model and a replication ‣ 4 Concise-9B against Qwen3.5-9B"))
and frozen before the 9B run.

#### Evaluation sets.

The _development suite_ has 498 problems: all 198 GPQA-Diamond questions
\[[31](#bib.bib31)\] and fixed subsets of 150 MATH and 150 MMLU test
problems \[[16](#bib.bib16), [15](#bib.bib15)\]. On it each model drew
four traces per problem with a 64K budget, in each of two sampling
seeds; the first trace is also read at 32K (a 32K prefix of a longer
trace is a valid 32K-budget trace). The held-out sets played no role in
training or in the recipe, and each model drew one trace per problem
with a 32K thinking budget:

- •
  a _hard set_ of 1,276 problems: 279 final-answer competition problems
  (AIME 2024–2026, HMMT February 2025–2026 and November 2025, BRUMO, SMT
  and CMIMC 2025, from the public MathArena \[[5](#bib.bib5)\] and
  Hugging Face H4 releases), 397 hard MATH test problems and 600
  SuperGPQA questions \[[23](#bib.bib23)\];
- •
  a _confirmation set_ of 2,275 problems in five areas: MATH test
  problems, science (TheoremQA \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] and SuperGPQA
  calculation questions), logical constraints (LogiQA
  \[[20](#bib.bib20)\] and generated relation and case-analysis tasks
  with programmatically verified answers), MMLU-Pro questions and
  executable code tasks scored by running the program;
- •
  an _MMLU-Pro set_ of 5,432 questions: every second of the 12,032
  MMLU-Pro test questions (6,016) \[[38](#bib.bib38)\], without those
  excluded for overlap with our training data
  ([Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Evaluation sets and decontamination"))
  and those that are also in the confirmation set.

We checked every held-out problem against the problems used in training
and excluded the 3, 35 and 427 that overlap them
([Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Evaluation sets and decontamination")).
Each problem is counted once: the 157 MMLU-Pro questions that also
belong to the confirmation set are kept only there, so the three
held-out sets hold 8,983 distinct problems. For the _uncapped_
comparison, every trace that reached the 32K budget was continued from
its own prefix to 80K tokens; 21 Qwen3.5-9B traces and 2 Concise-9B
traces still reach 80K.

#### Forced answers and readouts.

When a trace reaches its budget we close its thinking, append “The final
answer is \boxed{” and decode the answer greedily, as in budget forcing
\[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. A _readout_ applies the same cue to a prefix of a
trace: on every native trace of the hard and confirmation sets, and on
Concise-9B’s MMLU-Pro traces, we took one readout every 2,048 thinking
tokens, up to 1,024 tokens before the trace’s end. We took readouts on
the same grid on three further 64K Qwen3.5-9B traces per hard-set
problem (30,476 readouts;
[Sections 5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Longer budgets help; longer traces are not better ‣ 5 Test-time slack"),
[5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 What concise training removes ‣ 5 Test-time slack")
and [6](#S6 "6 Using slack for adaptive sampling")) and on the first of
the 16K Concise-9B confirmation-set traces used in
[Section 6](#S6 "6 Using slack for adaptive sampling") (4,855 readouts).
A readout reuses the cached prefix and costs about 48 generated tokens.

#### Scoring.

One checker scores every model: it takes the content of the last
\boxed{…} in the committed answer, maps option letters to option text
and compares the result with the reference answer after LaTeX
normalization; code tasks compare exact outputs. Votes and agreement
tests use canonical answer keys, under which “B”, “(B)” and the option’s
text agree, as do 0.5 and 1/2. Forced answers are checked exactly like
natural ones: re-scoring from their text the 39,546 stored readouts of
the analyzed native traces reproduces all 39,546 stored verdicts
([Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Evaluation sets and decontamination")). We
apply no post-hoc rescue to either model.

#### Statistics.

All comparisons are paired by problem, with 95% percentile intervals
from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of problems \[[11](#bib.bib11)\];
development-suite results average each problem’s outcome over the two
seeds before resampling. Differences are in problems unless marked as
points. For equivalence we use two one-sided tests \[[32](#bib.bib32)\]
at $`\alpha=0.05`$ on 90% paired intervals from 20,000 resamples, with
margins of $`\pm`$1, $`\pm`$2 and $`\pm`$3 points of the set’s size, and
report the smallest margin that passes. We say one system is more
accurate than another only when the 95% interval of the difference
excludes zero.

#### Definition.

Take a trace with final answer $`a`$ and readouts $`r_{1},\dots,r_{k}`$
at $`2{,}048,4{,}096,\dots`$ thinking tokens. The answer is _fixed_ at
the first readout $`r_{j}`$ such that $`r_{j},\dots,r_{k}`$ all equal
$`a`$; if $`r_{k}\neq a`$ or the trace has no readout, it is fixed at
the end. Two answers are equal if both are correct, or if both are wrong
and share an answer key. The thinking after this point is _slack_:
stopping there and forcing an answer would have returned $`a`$. The
2,048-token grid makes the measure conservative, since an answer can be
fixed up to one interval before the readout that first shows it.

#### Results.

For Qwen3.5-9B, 54% \[52, 56\] of thinking tokens on the hard set and
64% \[62, 65\] on the confirmation set are slack
([Table 4](#S5.T4 "In 5.2 Where the answer is fixed ‣ 5 Test-time slack")).
The answer is fixed before the end in 88% and 83% of traces, and for
half of all traces, pooled over both sets, within the first 36% of the
thinking ([Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In Findings. ‣ 1 Introduction")b). Slack is
largest in traces that end correct (65% and 67%), but wrong answers are
also often fixed early (59% and 59%): the model settles on an answer and
then keeps checking it without changing it. Concise-9B has less slack,
41% and 48% (48% on MMLU-Pro), and its answers are fixed later in
relative terms (pooled median 0.56 of the trace against 0.36), because
its traces end sooner after the answer forms.

#### Explicit statements come late.

A text-based check gives a lower bound. We located explicit answer
statements in the thinking (“the answer is …”, a boxed expression,
“option C is correct”) and took the first one after which every
statement gives the final answer. Such a statement precedes the end in
only 30–61% of traces and marks only 5–11% of tokens as slack
([Table 12](#A4.T12 "In Appendix D Additional tables")). When it does
occur, the readouts after it agree with the final answer 93–100% of the
time, against 65–86% before it, so the statements are reliable; they are
simply late. The model would give its final answer long before it writes
that answer down as its conclusion.

the stopping point is identified with knowledge of the trace’s final

([Table 5](#S5.T5 "In 5.3 Trimming slack at inference ‣ 5 Test-time slack")).

agreement alone does not tell a fixed answer from a provisional one.
Concise training trims differently
([Figure 5](#S5.F5 "In 5.3 Trimming slack at inference ‣ 5 Test-time slack")):
Concise-9B’s full traces use 0.69$`\times`$ the base model’s tokens on

#### The rest is faster, not just shorter.

The thinking before the answer is fixed also shrinks. If Concise-9B had
simply learned to stop the base model’s reasoning earlier, its accuracy
would match the base model’s own answers at the same lengths. It does
not. At the length at which Concise-9B stops on each hard-set problem,
Qwen3.5-9B’s answer there (its readout, or its final answer if it had
already finished) is correct on 866 problems, against 934 at its own
length; Concise-9B is correct on 930, $`+64`$ \[$`+36`$, $`+92`$\] more
than this prediction. A natural conclusion beats a forced readout at the
same length even for the same model, so we calibrate the comparison with
three further 64K traces of Qwen3.5-9B per problem: they exceed the same
prediction by +9, +17 and +32 problems
([Table 13](#A4.T13 "In Appendix D Additional tables")). Concise-9B
exceeds it by $`+45`$ \[$`+17`$, $`+73`$\] problems more than the
average of these samples (31–54 against each one): it reaches answers at
lengths where the base model has not yet reached them.

#### Where reasoning was lost.

Not all of the removed thinking was slack. With traces continued to 80K,
Concise-9B trails by $`25`$ \[$`1`$, $`49`$\] problems on the hard set
and by $`15`$ \[$`2`$, $`28`$\] on competition mathematics
([Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Held-out sets ‣ 4 Concise-9B against Qwen3.5-9B")),
where the base model’s long traces often reach a correct answer after
32K tokens. On the remaining held-out sets, uncapped accuracy is
equivalent within $`\pm`$1 point.

Adaptive sampling pays for its commit signal with extra samples
\[[2](#bib.bib2), [19](#bib.bib19)\]. Our comparator here, _two-sample
escalation_ (2 $`\to`$ 3), answers when two traces finish and agree, and
otherwise draws a third and takes the majority of three; it is the
three-trace variant of the escalation in
[Table 1](#S4.T1 "In 4.1 Development suite ‣ 4 Concise-9B against Qwen3.5-9B").
Because answers are usually fixed well before a trace ends, a trace can
supply a second opinion on itself. _Self-agreement escalation_ draws one
trace and commits if it finished naturally and its readouts at the last
$`m`$ checkpoints give its final answer; otherwise it falls back to
two-sample escalation, drawing a second trace and, if the two disagree,
a third and taking the majority (ties go to the shorter trace). The
readouts cost about 48 tokens each from the cached prefix.

We chose $`m=2`$ on the hard set with Concise-9B, then registered the
policy and its criteria before any readouts existed on the test data.
The primary test, on the confirmation set with Concise-9B and 16K
traces, required equivalence to two-sample escalation within $`\pm`$1
point at no more than 0.75$`\times`$ its tokens. It passed:
self-agreement escalation scored 1,979 problems against 1,984 ($`-5`$;
90% interval $`[-12,\ +2]`$) at 0.67$`\times`$ the tokens, committing on
the first trace for 82.7% of problems
([Table 7](#S6.T7 "In 6 Using slack for adaptive sampling")). Against
one trace the difference is $`+12`$ \[$`0`$, $`+24`$\] problems, and
against a majority of three $`-4`$ \[$`-12`$, $`+4`$\] at 0.52$`\times`$
its tokens. A secondary test applied the same policy to Qwen3.5-9B on
the hard set with 64K traces, with a $`\pm`$2-point margin; it also
passed ($`-10`$; $`[-21,\ +1]`$) at 0.57$`\times`$ the tokens. The
secondary test reuses the hard-set problems on which $`m`$ was chosen,
with another model and new traces. The saving is larger for the base
model, consistent with its traces carrying more slack.

## 7  Limitations

#### Models and data.

The main results concern one base model; the recipe was developed on a
smaller model of the same family and replicated on one model from
another family, on fewer sets. Each training round ran once, and our
seeds are sampling seeds, not training seeds; the training targets
include synthesis passes, which we did not ablate. The development suite
was used to develop the recipe on the 4B model, and its MATH and MMLU
parts share source distributions with the training pool; the held-out
sets did not influence the recipe.

#### Measuring slack.

Readouts are greedy answers to a fixed cue at 2,048-token intervals, so
the point where an answer is fixed is known only to that resolution, and
traces shorter than 3,072 thinking tokens have no readout and count as
having no slack. Both choices make the measured slack smaller than the
true one. Slack is defined against the trace’s own final answer, so it
says nothing about whether that answer is right; for wrong answers, it
measures checking that did not change the outcome. The hindsight bound
uses the full trace and is not a deployable rule.

#### Budgets and decoding.

The main held-out comparisons use one trace per problem at a 32K budget,
plus continuations to 80K; decoding is fixed to the model’s recommended
thinking-mode settings. Token counts exclude prompt tokens, and we
report tokens rather than wall-clock time.

## 8  Conclusion

Longer thinking makes Qwen3.5-9B more accurate, but most of a thinking
trace comes after its answer is fixed: 54–64% of the base model’s
thinking tokens on the hard and confirmation sets. Concise self-training
removes mostly this part. Concise-9B generates about two thirds of the
base model’s tokens on 8,983 held-out problems at accuracy equivalent
within $`\pm`$1 point, and on the hard and confirmation sets 86% of the
thinking it drops came after the answer had formed; the reasoning before
that point is shorter too, and reaches answers sooner than the base
model’s would at the same length. Trimming the same slack at inference
needs hindsight that simple stopping rules do not have, which makes
training the more practical way to remove it. The removal is not
complete or free: on the longest competition problems, where the base
model’s extra thinking still finds answers, Concise-9B gives up part of
the base model’s ceiling. The measurement itself has a use: a trace that
agrees with its own earlier answers needs no second sample, which makes
adaptive sampling cheaper at equivalent accuracy.

#### Optimization.

Full-parameter supervised fine-tuning of the text backbone with
token-summed cross-entropy on generated tokens, normalized by the
batch’s target tokens; AdamW ($`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.95`$, no
weight decay) with a peak learning rate of $`2\times 10^{-6}`$, linear
warm-up and cosine decay to 10% of the peak; batches of 32 sequences,
one epoch per round, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, bf16 mixed
precision. Each round’s training took about 1 hour on one NVIDIA B200
GPU.

#### Synthesis passes.

A synthesis pass has an 8K budget with thinking enabled. It sees the
last part of each sample’s reasoning and up to 400 characters of each
sample’s answer, and is instructed: “Several independent attempts at
this problem are shown above. They may disagree, and any of them may be
wrong. Using them only as evidence, re-derive the answer yourself and
check the decisive steps. Give one final answer in \boxed{…}.” The
evaluated systems use no synthesis passes.

#### Other models.

Concise-4B and the Olmo model were trained with the same pool,
collection budget, selection rule and hyperparameters, and the second
round is the evaluated model in both cases. Olmo-3-7B-Think has a 64K
context, so its largest evaluation budget is 57,344 generated tokens.

## Appendix B Evaluation sets and decontamination

The training pool excludes every problem that matches a
development-suite problem by dataset identifier or by whitespace- and
case-normalized text. The hard set’s competition problems were
de-duplicated and 13-gram decontaminated against our training data,
leaving AIME 2024: 21, AIME 2025: 19, AIME 2026: 23, HMMT February 2025:
30, HMMT February 2026: 33, HMMT November 2025: 30, BRUMO 2025: 30, SMT
2025: 53 and CMIMC 2025: 40 problems; its hard MATH problems exclude the
development suite and any MATH problem in the training pool. Its
SuperGPQA questions were drawn at random from the dataset. The
confirmation set was built once, before any experiment on it, and is
decontaminated by identifier and normalized text against the training
pool, the development suite and the hard set; its generated tasks are
re-derived by a verifier before use. Its MMLU-Pro questions come from
the same test split as the MMLU-Pro set, and 157 of the analyzed ones
appear in both; we count them once, in the confirmation set. Finally we
compared every held-out problem with the problems actually used in the
two training rounds by word 8-gram and prefix overlap, and excluded the
3 hard-set, 35 confirmation-set and 427 MMLU-Pro problems that overlap;
most are MATH test problems that nearly duplicate training problems and
MMLU-Pro questions derived from MMLU test questions. The same check
against the problems used in Concise-4B’s two training rounds flags 16
held-out MATH and 0 SuperGPQA problems, which the 4B comparisons
exclude. On the 8,983 analyzed held-out problems, a synthetic forced
answer that states the reference answer is accepted on 8,833; 146 of the
150 exceptions are SuperGPQA questions whose reference option text is
malformed LaTeX, on which the checker still credits the correct option
letter. The checker rejects 8 of Concise-9B’s held-out answers and 0 of
Qwen3.5-9B’s that wrap a correct option in \text{} inside the box; we
leave these as scored.

## Appendix C Statistical methodology

#### Paired bootstrap.

For two systems evaluated on the same problems we resample problems with
replacement and sum the per-problem differences, 10,000 times for 95%
intervals and 20,000 times for the 90% intervals of equivalence tests;
intervals are the percentiles. The resamples of a comparison are seeded
by its data, so a comparison that appears in two tables has the same
interval in both. The two registered evaluators
([Tables 7](#S6.T7 "In 6 Using slack for adaptive sampling")
and [14](#A4.T14 "Table 14 ‣ Appendix D Additional tables")) use 20,000
resamples for all their intervals. On the development suite each
problem’s outcome is first averaged over the two sampling seeds (and,
for single-sample accuracy, over the four traces of each seed). Ratios
of sums, such as the share of slack, use the same resampling.
Single-model accuracies in the figures carry 95% Wilson intervals
\[[39](#bib.bib39)\].

#### Equivalence.

Two systems are equivalent within $`\pm m`$ points when the whole 90%
interval of their difference lies strictly inside $`\pm m\%`$ of the
set’s size, which is a two one-sided test at $`\alpha=0.05`$.

#### Votes.

Majority votes count traces with an answer key, ties go to the shorter
trace, and a problem with no keyed trace counts as wrong. Escalation
commits only on traces that finished naturally; a forced answer never
triggers a commitment.

#### Budget curves.

A trace still thinking at budget $`B`$ is scored by its latest readout
at or before $`B`$. Readouts stop 1,024 tokens before a trace’s end, so
a trace that ends within that stretch after $`B`$ is scored by its
previous readout; scoring such traces by their final answer instead
changes no paired difference in
[Table 10](#A4.T10 "In Appendix D Additional tables") by more than 9
problems.

# Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?

Qingyuan Zheng    Zhihao Dou Affiliation: Duke University
Email: [{pearson.z,zhihao.dou}@duke.edu](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Knowledge distillation improves a student model’s reasoning ability
through supervision from a teacher. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD)
uses the same model as both teacher and student under different
contexts: the student generates reasoning trajectories from the question
alone, while the teacher additionally receives a complete worked example
and provides token-level supervision along those trajectories. Because
the problem statement is shared, its content and ordering shape both
student generation and teacher feedback. Preserving its semantic
structure therefore seems natural, but does it always improve learning?
We introduce _Shuffled OPSD_, which shuffles the words of the shared
problem statement while keeping the teacher-only worked example intact.
Across three mathematical reasoning benchmarks and three Qwen3 model
scales, the method improves mean accuracy over both an intact-target
control and Standard OPSD under matched training configurations. The
largest gain over Standard OPSD is 3.52 percentage points on Qwen3-1.7B.
Further controls show that preserving larger word blocks offers no
advantage over word-level shuffling at the evaluated scales, whereas
shuffling the teacher-only worked example reduces accuracy at every
scale. Cross-problem mixing and target-removal experiments further
distinguish the roles of problem content, word order, and teacher
context. These results show that disrupting the shared problem statement
can improve learning, while disrupting the teacher-only reference hurts
performance. They challenge semantic coherence as a universally
beneficial principle for OPSD and motivate closer study of how input
structure shapes student generation and teacher supervision.

## 1 Introduction

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have substantially
improved mathematical reasoning through post-training methods such as
reinforcement learning ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib32); [Yu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib33); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)), supervised
fine-tuning ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib35); [Wei et al.,
2021](#bib.bib36)), and knowledge distillation ([Zhao et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib1)). Among these approaches, on-policy distillation
combines two useful properties: the student learns on trajectories
sampled from its own policy, while a teacher provides dense token-level
supervision on those trajectories ([Agarwal et al., 2023](#bib.bib9);
[Gu et al., 2023](#bib.bib15)). On-policy self-distillation (OPSD)
([Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib1)) further removes the need for a
separate teacher model by instantiating teacher and student from the
same backbone under different conditioning contexts. The student sees
the problem and generates its own response, while a frozen teacher
scores the same prefixes with access to privileged context such as a
worked solution.

Most work on OPSD has focused on the privileged information available to
the teacher. Recent studies examine whether the teacher needs a correct
solution, how much reasoning should be exposed, and whether useful
learning persists with absent or unrelated references ([Shrestha and
Tessier, 2026](#bib.bib2); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib3); [Shen et
al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Zhao et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib8)). These results suggest that richer or more
semantically relevant teacher context does not always translate into a
better trained student. However, a separate part of the OPSD
conditioning structure has received much less attention: the problem
statement shared by both teacher and student is normally kept intact.

The shared problem plays a different role from teacher-only privileged
information. It determines which trajectories the student generates and
therefore which prefixes receive supervision. It also conditions the
student and teacher distributions used to compute the distillation loss.
Its structure thus affects both where learning occurs and what feedback
the teacher provides. This makes the shared problem a distinct design
dimension: changing its structure can alter the learning process even
when the teacher reference and distillation objective remain fixed.

We ask whether preserving the semantic structure of the shared problem
improves OPSD, or whether deliberately disrupting it can produce a
better student. Destroying word order appears counterproductive for
mathematical reasoning. Yet OPSD trains through teacher feedback on
student-generated trajectories, rather than directly rewarding the
semantic coherence of its inputs. A problem statement that is easier to
interpret need not induce trajectories and supervision that lead to
better learning. This motivates treating the shared problem as a
_training interface_, whose structure can differ from that of the intact
problems used at inference.

Based on this intuition, we introduce Shuffled OPSD. We first replace
the teacher’s target-associated solution with a complete worked example
from an unrelated problem, forming the UnrelatedRef condition. Across
three model scales, this condition retains performance comparable to
Standard OPSD, with differences in three-benchmark mean accuracy of at
most 0.55 percentage points. Building on this condition, Shuffled OPSD
permutes the word order of the problem seen by both student and teacher
while keeping the teacher-only reference intact. The student generates
its trajectory from the shuffled problem, and the frozen teacher
provides token-level supervision under the same transformed problem
together with the reference. The transformation preserves the problem’s
words, numbers, and symbolic content while disrupting their ordering.
The distillation objective remains unchanged, and evaluation always uses
the original intact problems.

Our experiments reveal a consistent pattern: replacing the teacher
reference with an unrelated example preserves performance, shuffling the
shared problem improves it, and additionally shuffling the teacher
reference reduces it. Using the final checkpoints after 50 optimizer
steps, word-level shuffling improves three-benchmark mean accuracy over
Standard OPSD by 3.52, 1.11, and 1.38 percentage points on Qwen3-1.7B,
Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B, respectively. At 1.7B and 4B, preserving larger
word blocks offers no advantage over word-level shuffling. These results
support _selective semantic disruption_: disrupting the shared problem’s
ordering while retaining the structure of the teacher’s reference. They
extend the study of OPSD from what information the teacher receives to
how the shared input is organized.

Our contributions are as follows: First, we identify the semantic
structure of the shared problem as a distinct design dimension in OPSD,
affecting both student-generated trajectories and teacher supervision.
Second, we introduce Shuffled OPSD, a simple input transformation that
improves reasoning performance without changing the distillation
objective or inference procedure. Third, through controlled
interventions on the shared problem and teacher-only reference, we show
that their semantic structures play different roles and establish
selective semantic disruption as an effective input-design principle
across three model scales.

## 2 Preliminaries

### 2.1 On-Policy Self-Distillation

In on-policy self-distillation ([Agarwal et al., 2023](#bib.bib9); [Zhao
et al., 2026a](#bib.bib1)), a student $`\pi_{\theta}`$ generates
continuation $`y`$ for target $`x`$ in mode $`m_{S}`$. A frozen teacher
with initial parameters $`\theta_{0}`$ scores the same prefixes with
reference $`c`$ and mode $`m_{T}`$:

|     |                               |                                                                 |     |     |
| --- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p^{S}_{t}`$   | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t};m_{S}),`$       |     | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle p^{T}_{c,t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid x,c,y_{<t};m_{T}).`$ |     |     |

Both models see the target, hence _shared target_. The reference
provides teacher-only context, rather than a separate output sequence
for the student to imitate.

For supervised positions $`I(y)`$, the training objective is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{I,c}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;m_{S})}\left[\frac{1}{|I(y)|}\sum_{t\in I(y)}\ell_{\rm cap}(p^{T}_{c,t},p^{S}_{t})\right].
``` |  | (2) |

The loss $`\ell_{\rm cap}`$ caps each signed teacher-to-student KL
contribution from above at $`0.05`$ before summation
(Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?")).
It is therefore not ordinary KL. Teacher outputs, sampled tokens, and
sampling probabilities are held fixed during differentiation.

### 2.2 Shared and Teacher-Only Conditioning

The shared target directly conditions generation and scoring. The
reference directly conditions only scoring, although the resulting
updates can change later student rollouts. Our default student uses
non-thinking mode and the teacher uses thinking mode, as specified by
their chat templates.

Standard OPSD pairs each target with its associated solution.
UnrelatedRef instead uses a worked example from another problem. Fixing
this reference assignment allows us to vary the shared target
independently. All evaluation uses intact problems without teacher-only
context.

## 3 Design of Shuffled OPSD

### 3.1 Semantic Structure as a Design Variable

OPSD provides the student and teacher with the same problem, while
giving the teacher an additional reference. We distinguish two aspects
of this input structure: whether the reference answers the target
problem, and how the content within each input is organized. We first
replace the reference with a complete worked example from another
problem. We then shuffle the shared problem while keeping that example
intact.

Let $`x`$ denote the target problem, $`c`$ the reference available only
to the teacher, and $`T`$ a transformation of the shared problem. The
student generates a trajectory
$`y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid T(x);m_{S})`$. At each selected position
$`t`$, the student and frozen teacher distributions are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p^{S}_{T,t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid T(x),y_{<t};m_{S}),`$ |  | (3) |
|  | $`\displaystyle p^{T}_{T,c,t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid T(x),c,y_{<t};m_{T}).`$ |  | (4) |

Training minimizes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{T,c}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid T(x);m_{S})}\left[\frac{1}{|I(y)|}\sum_{t\in I(y)}\ell_{\mathrm{cap}}\left(p^{T}_{T,c,t},p^{S}_{T,t}\right)\right].
``` |  | (5) |

The reference $`c`$ changes the feedback provided by the teacher. The
transformation $`T`$ changes both the student’s rollout distribution and
the predictions of both models on the resulting prefixes. We therefore
treat reference selection and shared problem structure as separate
design choices.

### 3.2 UnrelatedRef

Standard OPSD uses the intact problem $`T_{I}(x)=x`$ together with a
reference solution associated with that problem,
$`c_{\mathrm{assoc}}(x)`$. Our first intervention, *UnrelatedRef*,
retains the intact problem but replaces the reference with a complete
worked example from another problem:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{U}(x)=\left(x^{\mathrm{ref}},a^{\mathrm{ref}}\right),\qquad x^{\mathrm{ref}}\neq x.
``` |  | (6) |

Here $`x^{\mathrm{ref}}`$ and $`a^{\mathrm{ref}}`$ denote the reference
question and its worked solution. Both remain intact and are visible
only to the teacher.

The reference pairing is fixed during training and reused across all
subsequent interventions on the shared problem. UnrelatedRef tests
whether the teacher needs a solution to the specific target problem. It
also provides the starting point for Shuffled OPSD.

### 3.3 Shuffled OPSD

Shuffled OPSD builds on UnrelatedRef by shuffling the problem seen by
both student and teacher. The unrelated worked example provided to the
teacher remains intact. This allows us to change the shared problem
without changing the reference.

We split $`x`$ at whitespace boundaries and group the resulting words
into consecutive blocks $`b_{1},\ldots,b_{M}`$, each containing $`k`$
words. The final block may contain fewer than $`k`$ words. We sample a
permutation $`\sigma`$ and hold it fixed for that example during
training:

|     |                                                               |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       T_{k}(x)=b_{\sigma(1)}\|b_{\sigma(2)}\|\cdots\|b_{\sigma(M)},
       ```                                                            |     | (7) |

where $`\|`$ joins blocks with word separators.

Our main variant, *Shuffle-1*, permutes individual words. It preserves
each word and its frequency, including numerical and symbolic strings
within words, while disrupting their order. Larger blocks preserve more
local order and allow us to test the value of that structure.

The student generates from $`T_{k}(x)`$. The frozen teacher scores the
same continuation using $`T_{k}(x)`$ and $`c_{U}(x)`$ as context. The
student is updated with $`\mathcal{L}_{T_{k},c_{U}}`$ in
Equation [5](#S3.E5 "In 3.1 Semantic Structure as a Design Variable ‣ 3 Design of Shuffled OPSD ‣ Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?").
The loss, optimizer, and reference pairing remain unchanged from
UnrelatedRef. At inference, the student receives the original intact
problem without an additional reference.

This design implements *selective semantic disruption*: we remove the
association between the problem and reference, disrupt the order of the
shared problem, and preserve the internal structure of the teacher’s
worked example.

### 3.4 Controls on Semantic Structure

We use matched controls to examine reference relevance, problem content
and order, and the internal structure of the reference.

##### Reference relevance.

Standard OPSD and UnrelatedRef both retain the intact target problem.
Their comparison measures the effect of replacing the corresponding
solution with an unrelated worked example. UnrelatedRef and Shuffle-1
use identical reference pairings, so their comparison measures the
effect of shuffling the shared problem.

##### Problem content and order.

Shuffle-$`k`$ varies how much local word order is preserved.
*CrossShuffle* pools words across training problems, globally permutes
the pool, and redistributes words according to the original word count
of each example. It disrupts both word order and problem membership
while preserving word counts across the corpus. *NoTarget* removes the
specific problem statement and its wrapper while retaining the general
instruction. All these conditions use the same unrelated reference
pairings.

##### Reference structure.

*AllShuffle-$`k`$* applies block shuffling to both the shared problem
and the reference provided to the teacher. AllShuffle-1 separately
permutes words in the target problem, the reference question, and the
reference solution. Comparing Shuffle-1 with AllShuffle-1 measures the
additional effect of disrupting the reference structure.

These controls distinguish which input structures support learning.
Additional experiments on reference removal and teacher mode appear in
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Analysis and Discussion ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?").
Implementation details, fixed reference pairings, and permutation seeds
are provided in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?").

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate Shuffled OPSD on three model scales and three
competition-level mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Our main
experiments compare shared-problem disruption with an intact-problem
control and Standard OPSD. We then analyze how the effect changes with
the amount and location of semantic structure preserved during training.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

##### Models and training.

We experiment with Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B ([Yang and others,
2025](#bib.bib19)) using LoRA adapters ([Hu et al., 2021](#bib.bib20))
($`r=64`$, $`\alpha=128`$). Training examples are drawn from the
OpenThoughts mathematical-reasoning subset used by the original OPSD
study. All conditions use 50 optimizer steps, batch size 16, learning
rate $`5\times 10^{-6}`$, and training seed 42. The teacher is the
frozen initial backbone in thinking mode, while the student generates in
non-thinking mode with rollouts capped at 1,024 tokens. We report the
step-50 checkpoint for all trained conditions. Additional implementation
details appear in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?").

##### Evaluation.

We evaluate on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and HMMT February 2025, using 30
problems per benchmark. Evaluation uses the original intact problems
without teacher-only context. We sample 12 responses per problem in
thinking mode with temperature $`1`$, top-$`p=0.95`$, and a maximum of
38,912 new tokens. Avg12 is the primary metric, and the three-benchmark
mean weights all benchmarks equally. Compared conditions use the same
problem order and generation seed schedule.

##### Statistics.

Paired 95% confidence intervals are estimated with 10,000
problem-cluster bootstrap resamples, keeping all responses from each
problem in the same cluster and using matched problem indices across
conditions.

### 4.2 Main Results

Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Reference structure remains valuable. ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?")
shows that Shuffle-1 improves mean accuracy at all three model scales.
We organize the comparisons around two questions: whether shuffling the
shared problem improves learning, and whether the reference provided to
the teacher should also be shuffled.

##### Shared problem order is a useful training variable.

Shuffle-1 and UnrelatedRef use the same reference and differ only in
whether the shared problem is shuffled. Shuffle-1 improves the
three-benchmark mean by 3.98, 0.56, and 1.11 percentage points on
Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B, respectively. At 1.7B and 8B, it
improves all three benchmarks. Because evaluation uses intact problems,
these gains show that disrupting the training input can improve
reasoning on normally structured questions. The shared problem therefore
serves as more than a statement to be solved: its organization is a
training variable that affects student generation and teacher feedback.

##### The complete design outperforms Standard OPSD.

Shuffle-1 exceeds Standard OPSD by 3.52, 1.11, and 1.38 percentage
points across the three scales. Standard OPSD supplies a reference
associated with the target, whereas Shuffle-1 combines an unrelated
reference with a shuffled problem. The stronger results show that
accurate pairing and intact word order are not prerequisites for
effective distillation. CrossShuffle also exceeds Standard OPSD at every
scale, but remains below Shuffle-1. This comparison favors retaining the
words of each problem while changing their order, rather than replacing
their original grouping with words drawn from other problems. The
largest improvement occurs at 1.7B, showing that this input
transformation is particularly effective for the smallest model in our
evaluation.

##### Reference structure remains valuable.

AllShuffle-1 additionally shuffles the reference while retaining the
same shuffled problem. Mean accuracy then falls by 2.41, 0.65, and 1.57
percentage points at 1.7B, 4B, and 8B. Thus, the benefit comes from
where disruption is applied, rather than from increasing disruption
throughout the input. The shared problem shapes both the trajectories
generated by the student and the feedback provided by the teacher. The
reference supplies context only to the teacher. Their different roles
are reflected in the results: disrupting shared problem order improves
learning, while preserving reference structure supports stronger
supervision. This pattern motivates selective semantic disruption as the
central design principle of Shuffled OPSD.

### 4.3 Analysis and Discussion

The experiments reveal a structural asymmetry in OPSD. Semantic
structure in the shared problem is not uniformly beneficial, whereas
structure in the teacher-only reference remains valuable. The shared
problem determines where supervision is collected; the reference shapes
the teacher signal at those locations.

#### 4.3.1 How Much Structure Should Be Preserved?

Among the tested target-side perturbations, the strongest disruption
performs best. Shuffle-1 reaches 42.78% at 1.7B, compared with 41.02%
for Shuffle-4 and 40.65% for Shuffle-8, and 63.06% at 4B, compared with
62.22% and 62.87%, respectively. Thus, preserving more local word order
does not improve performance.

CrossShuffle and NoTarget further isolate what should be preserved. They
reach 41.11%, 62.78%, and 63.43%, and 41.76%, 61.95%, and 62.22% across
the three scales, respectively, both below Shuffle-1 at every scale.
This suggests that problem-specific lexical and symbolic content matters
more than intact syntax or coherent surface form. Full results appear in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Sensitivity to Shuffle Granularity ‣ Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?").

#### 4.3.2 Where Does Disruption Help?

AllShuffle-1 additionally shuffles the teacher-only reference and
reduces mean accuracy by 2.41, 0.65, and 1.57 percentage points at 1.7B,
4B, and 8B. Thus, the same disruption that helps on the shared problem
hurts on the teacher-only context. This motivates *selective semantic
disruption*: perturb the shared input that shapes student trajectories
while preserving structured teacher guidance.

At 1.7B, Shuffle-1 raises Avg12 from 38.80% to 42.78% and Vote12 from
48.89% to 52.22%, while Pass12 remains at 61.11%. At 4B, Avg12 rises
from 62.50% to 63.06% and Vote12 from 67.78% to 72.22%, while Pass12
changes little. The gain therefore comes mainly from making successful
reasoning more reliable rather than from solving substantially more
problems.

governed jointly by *where* supervision is collected and *what* teacher
distribution is provided there.

## 5 Related Work

##### Distillation on student trajectories.

Knowledge distillation transfers predictive distributions ([Hinton et
al., 2015](#bib.bib16)), and generalized distillation connects teacher
supervision to privileged information ([Lopez-Paz et al.,
2015](#bib.bib10)). Same-architecture students can also benefit from
distillation ([Furlanello et al., 2018](#bib.bib18)). Sequence-level
distillation uses teacher-generated outputs ([Kim and Rush,
2016](#bib.bib21)), whereas GKD and MiniLLM supervise student-generated
trajectories ([Agarwal et al., 2023](#bib.bib9); [Gu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib15)). DistiLLM variants examine response sources and loss
design ([Ko et al., 2024](#bib.bib22); [Ko et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)).
The importance of learner-visited states also arises in interactive
imitation learning ([Ross et al., 2011](#bib.bib17)). We study the
shared input that shapes those states and their supervision.

##### Context-conditioned self-teaching.

Context distillation transfers context-induced behavior into model
parameters ([Snell et al., 2022](#bib.bib12); [Ye et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13)). Related methods learn from privileged solutions
([Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib1)), demonstrations ([Shenfeld et al.,
2026](#bib.bib24)), textual feedback ([Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25)), or successful generated rationales ([Zelikman et
al., 2022](#bib.bib26)). Recent OPSD studies examine reference benefits
([Shrestha and Tessier, 2026](#bib.bib2); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib3)), problem-space guidance ([Zhao et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib8)), trajectory-conditioned teachers ([Feng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib4)), and pseudo-solutions ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)).
Work on thinking degradation motivates changes to token-level
supervision ([Kaur et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Peng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14); [Shen et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)). Our design
complements these approaches by changing the input shared by student and
teacher while retaining a fixed reference assignment.

##### Text perturbations and prompt structure.

Word swaps in augmentation ([Wei and Zou, 2019](#bib.bib27)), denoising
from corrupted text ([Lewis et al., 2020](#bib.bib28)), shuffled-text
pretraining ([Sinha et al., 2021a](#bib.bib29)), and word-order
robustness tests ([Sinha et al., 2021b](#bib.bib30)) provide precedents
for disrupting text structure. Demonstration content and order also
affect in-context prediction ([Min et al., 2022](#bib.bib11); [Lu et
al., 2022](#bib.bib31)). Shuffled OPSD uses transformed text to collect
and supervise student trajectories, without reconstructing the intact
target or assigning it the original answer as a label. The outcome of
interest is accuracy after training, evaluated on intact problems.

## 6 Conclusion

Our results show that semantic coherence is not uniformly beneficial for
OPSD. Shuffled OPSD improves reasoning by disrupting the shared problem
while preserving a structured teacher-only reference, outperforming the
compared conditions across three model scales without changing the
distillation objective or inference procedure. The key finding is a
structural asymmetry: shuffling the shared problem helps, whereas
shuffling the teacher reference hurts, motivating *selective semantic
disruption* as a design principle for OPSD—perturb the input that shapes
student trajectories while preserving the structure that supports
teacher guidance. More broadly, distillation inputs should be designed
not only for semantic fidelity, but for the learning dynamics they
induce, through both the trajectories they elicit and the supervision
they enable.

## Appendix A Implementation and Evaluation Details

This appendix provides the implementation details shared across the
Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B experiments. Unless explicitly varied
by an intervention, all conditions use the same training data,
optimization settings, rollout procedure, distillation objective, and
evaluation protocol.

### A.1 Training Configuration

##### Data.

We use the full OpenThoughts mathematical-reasoning training pool used
by the original OPSD study, containing 29,434 problem–solution pairs. No
fixed 800-example subset is constructed. All conditions draw training
examples from the same seeded ordering of this pool.

##### Models and optimization.

Students initialize from Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, or Qwen3-8B with fresh
LoRA adapters. The backbone remains frozen and only the student adapter
is optimized. The teacher is a frozen copy of the initial backbone and
remains fixed throughout training. Student rollouts use non-thinking
mode, while teacher scoring uses thinking mode unless explicitly changed
by an intervention.

Table 4: Shared training configuration for all main experiments.

##### On-policy rollouts.

At each optimizer step, responses are sampled from the current student
under the condition-specific input. The same generated continuation is
then replayed under the student and teacher prompts to obtain next-token
distributions on identical prefixes. Prompt and padding tokens are
excluded from the loss. An EOS token is included when generated;
responses reaching the maximum length are scored without appending an
artificial EOS.

##### Distillation objective.

Let $`p_{it}(v)`$ and $`q_{it}(v)`$ denote the temperature-scaled
student and frozen-teacher probabilities for vocabulary entry $`v`$ at
response position $`t`$ of example $`i`$. For batch size $`B`$, the
implemented objective is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{c}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\min\left\{q_{it}(v)\log\frac{q_{it}(v)}{p_{it}(v)},c\right\},\qquad c=0.05.
``` |  | (8) |

The cap is applied independently to each signed vocabulary contribution
before summation; negative contributions are retained. The loss is
averaged over supervised positions within each response and then equally
across responses. Teacher probabilities, sampled continuations, and
sampling probabilities are treated as constants during differentiation.
No additional temperature-squared multiplier is applied.

Let the training pool contain $`N=29{,}434`$ records. For training

##### Within-problem shuffling.

Shuffle-$`k`$ partitions the problem into consecutive blocks of $`k`$
whitespace-delimited words and permutes the block order. Shuffle-1
therefore permutes individual words, while larger $`k`$ preserves
progressively longer local spans. The transformation preserves the word
multiset but need not preserve tokenizer-token counts.

##### Cross-problem shuffling.

CrossShuffle pools words across the training problems, applies a fixed
global permutation, and redistributes them according to the original
per-example word counts. It preserves corpus-level lexical counts and
example lengths while breaking problem membership.

##### Reference shuffling and target removal.

AllShuffle-1 applies word-level shuffling separately to the shared
problem, the unrelated reference problem, and its solution. These fields
are never pooled together. NoTarget removes the problem-specific
statement and its wrapper while retaining the general instruction.
NoTarget-NoRef additionally removes the teacher reference. Generated
student continuations are never shuffled.

##### Evaluation metric.

Let $`R_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}`$ indicate whether response $`j`$ to problem

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|

``` math
\mathrm{Avg@12}=\frac{1}{12N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{12}R_{i,j}.
``` |  | (10) |

Within each benchmark, problems are shuffled once using seed 2026. The
problem at zero-based shuffled position $`i`$ uses generation seed
$`s_{i}=2026+i`$. All compared conditions share the same problem
ordering and seed schedule.

### A.4 Statistical Estimation

For paired comparisons with saved response-level outputs, conditions are
aligned by problem ID and evaluated using 10,000 problem-cluster
bootstrap resamples. All 12 responses from a problem remain in the same
cluster, and paired conditions use identical resampled problem indices.
For three-benchmark summaries, resampling is performed within each
benchmark before computing the equal-weight mean.

The reported 95% intervals quantify uncertainty across evaluation
problems conditional on the final checkpoints and generated responses.
Main experiments use a single training seed, so these intervals do not
estimate training-seed variability. Comparisons without matched
response-level outputs are reported as point differences.

## Appendix B Sensitivity to Shuffle Granularity

We evaluate how the granularity of semantic disruption shapes the
effectiveness of Shuffled OPSD. Shuffle-$`k`$ partitions each shared
problem into consecutive blocks of $`k`$ whitespace-delimited words and
permutes these blocks, so larger $`k`$ preserves progressively more
local word order. AllShuffle-$`k`$ applies the same transformation to
both the shared problem and the teacher-only reference.

Table [7](#A2.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Appendix B Sensitivity to Shuffle Granularity ‣ Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?")
shows a clear pattern. For Qwen3-1.7B, Shuffle-4 and Shuffle-8 achieve
three-benchmark means of 41.02% and 40.65%, respectively, both below the
42.78% obtained by Shuffle-1 in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Reference structure remains valuable. ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Shuffled OPSD: When Semantic Consistency Hurts On-Policy Self-Distillation?").
For Qwen3-4B, Shuffle-4 and Shuffle-8 achieve 62.22% and 62.87%,
respectively, compared with 63.06% for Shuffle-1. Across both model
sizes, the strongest target-side intervention is therefore the most
aggressive one: disrupting word order at the individual-word level.
Preserving additional local structure does not improve performance and,
at 1.7B, weakens it substantially.

The AllShuffle variants reveal the complementary effect on the
teacher-only reference. At 1.7B, increasing the block size from 4 to 8
raises the mean from 38.80% to 40.37%, whereas at 4B it lowers the mean
from 62.13% to 61.48%. More importantly, every AllShuffle variant
underperforms its matched target-only Shuffle counterpart. The benefit
of semantic disruption is therefore strongly location-dependent:
aggressive disruption is most effective on the shared problem, while
preserving structure in the teacher-only reference consistently produces
stronger students.

Taken together, these results strengthen the central design principle of
Shuffled OPSD. The useful signal does not come from preserving
progressively more local semantic structure in the shared problem.
Instead, performance is maximized by strongly disrupting the shared
problem while retaining a structured teacher context.
````

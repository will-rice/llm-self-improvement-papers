## 1  Introduction

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly deployed to assist
users with personal and professional tasks ([Staufer et al.,
2026](#bib.bib36)). Connecting agents deployed by different users
creates opportunities to integrate their capabilities and support
collaboration across users ([Chen et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9)).
Decentralized multi-agent systems (MAS) support such collaboration by
allowing agents to act on local information and communicate with peers
without a central controller ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib54)). From a
game-theoretic perspective, multi-agent interactions are commonly
categorized as cooperative, mixed-motive, or adversarial according to
how agents’ objectives align ([Buşoniu et al., 2008](#bib.bib5); [Zhang
et al., 2021](#bib.bib57)). Cooperative settings involve shared goals,
mixed-motive settings combine shared and conflicting interests, and
adversarial settings involve opposing goals, as shown in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction").

![Refer to caption](2610.34450v1/teaserv11.png)

Figure 1: Three representative interaction regimes for decentralized
MAS.

When interacting with unfamiliar partners, people usually learn through
interaction, using that experience to adjust immediate responses and
guide future interactions. Agents similarly need to learn online from
interaction experience to build profiles of other agents and refine
their interaction strategies for subsequent interactions ([He et al.,
2016](#bib.bib17); [Albrecht & Stone, 2017](#bib.bib3)). This raises a
central research question: _how can a unified mechanism support
test-time adaptation across cooperative, mixed-motive, and adversarial
settings?_ Experience-driven self-evolution methods provides an insight
to such adaptation by using feedback and accumulated experience to
improve behavior without heavy parameter updates ([Wang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib46); [Tang et al., 2025](#bib.bib39); [Ouyang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib29)). However, recent research shows that accumulating
long interaction histories in the reasoning context creates context
bloat that can affect task performance ([Du et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)).
Retaining inaccurate accumulated experience introduces memory pollution
that can misguide subsequent interactions ([Xiong et al.,
2026](#bib.bib49)). These two limitations further raise the question:
_how can self-evolution decentralized agents achieve reliable experience
reuse across three interaction settings?_

To address these questions, we propose EvolveMAS, a unified
self-evolution framework that enables decentralized agents to learn from
interaction experience across cooperative, mixed-motive, and adversarial
settings. EvolveMAS pairs each main agent with a dedicated MemoryAgent
for test-time memory governance to address context bloat and memory
pollution. The MemoryAgent maintains Structured Memory outside the main
agent’s reasoning context, organizing interaction evidence into
Episodic, Semantic, and Working Memory. During Within-Episode Online
Adaptation phase, the MemoryAgent updates Structured Memory as
interactions unfold to inform subsequent actions. During Cross-Episode
Knowledge Evolution phase, the main agent uses interaction outcomes to
refine reusable Knowledge comprising Partner Profiles and Interaction
Strategies for future better interactions.

We evaluate EvolveMAS on five benchmarks spanning cooperative,
mixed-motive, and adversarial settings. Under a shared self-evolution
protocol, EvolveMAS achieves the highest normalized average score of
77.2 (Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction")), exceeding the
strongest setting-specific baseline by 9.7 points. Ablation studies
demonstrate that test-time memory governance improves interaction
performance, with additional improvements from cross-episode Knowledge
reuse. Additional analyses show that online memory updates improve
question-answering accuracy over offline updates and that test-time
memory governance reduces active-context growth.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

- •
  We propose EvolveMAS, a unified self-evolution framework that supports
  experience-driven adaptation in decentralized MAS across cooperative,
  mixed-motive, and adversarial settings through Within-Episode Online
  Adaptation and Cross-Episode Knowledge Evolution.
- •
  We introduce a dedicated MemoryAgent for test-time memory governance
  that maintains Structured Memory to support the main agent’s reasoning
  during interaction and cross-episode Knowledge reuse through Partner
  Profiles and Interaction Strategies.
- •
  We evaluate EvolveMAS on five benchmarks across the three interaction
  settings and demonstrate the effectiveness of test-time memory
  governance and cross-episode Knowledge reuse through ablation studies.

## 2  Related Work

#### Decentralized Multi-Agent Systems.

Research on decentralized multi-agent systems has explored coordination
and adaptation across diverse interaction regimes. Game-theoretic
studies commonly classify multi-agent interactions as cooperative,
mixed-motive, or adversarial ([Yang & Wang, 2020](#bib.bib53); [Wong
et al., 2023](#bib.bib47)), while recent LLM-based MAS instantiate these
regimes through cooperative coding and reasoning ([Qian et al.,
2024](#bib.bib33); [Du et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Liang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24)), mixed-motive social interaction ([Zhou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib64); [Wang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib44)), and adversarial
strategic reasoning ([Jin et al., 2024](#bib.bib22); [Agarwal et al.,
2025](#bib.bib2); [Jiang et al., 2026](#bib.bib21)). To support
adaptation during interaction, classical opponent modeling infers other
agents’ behavioral patterns from interaction history to guide
decision-making ([Carmel & Markovitch, 1996](#bib.bib6); [He et al.,
2016](#bib.bib17)). More broadly, recent MAS adaptation methods adjust
system topology, workflows, roles, communication structures, or agent
policies ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib54); [Hu et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib19); [Hu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib18); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib56); [Zhuge et al., 2024](#bib.bib65); [Qian et al.,
2025](#bib.bib34); [Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib8); [Wang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib43); [Chen et al., 2025b](#bib.bib10); [Liao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib25); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib26); [Xue et al.,
2026](#bib.bib51); [Park et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)). These approaches
improve performance on specific coordination or adaptation objectives
and are typically tailored to particular interaction settings, system
structures, or training procedures. In contrast, EvolveMAS maintains the
same decentralized system organization and evolution framework across
these interaction regimes.

#### Self-Evolution.

Recent work explores test-time self-evolution by reusing interaction
experience and feedback without updating model parameters.
Reflexion ([Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib35)), ExpeL ([Zhao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib59)), and Self-Refine ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib27))
demonstrate experience-driven improvements on single-agent tasks, while
EvoMAC ([Hu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib19)) and AgentNet ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib54)) extend iterative adaptation to multi-agent systems.
Related studies also investigate self-generated data and self-play as
mechanisms for agent evolution ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib55);
[Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib45); [Chen et al., 2024b](#bib.bib12);
[Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib20); [Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib60); [Chen
et al., 2025c](#bib.bib11)). However, existing self-evolution methods
are primarily designed for specific tasks or interaction settings. To
support self-evolution across interaction settings, EvolveMAS couples
within-episode adaptation with cross-episode Knowledge evolution through
a shared test-time evolution mechanism for decentralized MAS.

#### Agent Memory.

Agent memory enables experience to persist beyond a single interaction
context ([Park et al., 2023](#bib.bib32); [Sumers et al.,
2024](#bib.bib37)). MemGPT ([Packer et al., 2023](#bib.bib30)) and
MemoryBank ([Zhong et al., 2024](#bib.bib62)) introduce persistent
memory, while Voyager ([Wang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib42)), Agent Workflow
Memory ([Wang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib46)), and SkillWeaver ([Zheng
et al., 2025](#bib.bib61)) accumulate reusable skills and workflows.
More recent work explores structured memory organization and test-time
memory reasoning ([Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib50); [Chhikara et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13); [Ouyang et al., 2026](#bib.bib29); [Zhou et al.,
2025](#bib.bib63); [Yan et al., 2025](#bib.bib52)). However, in
long-horizon decentralized interactions, retaining accumulated memory in
the main agent’s context can lead to context expansion, attention
dilution, and memory pollution ([Liang et al., 2026](#bib.bib23); [Zhang
et al., 2026](#bib.bib58); [Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib48); [Wan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib41)). To address these challenges, EvolveMAS pairs each
main agent with a dedicated external MemoryAgent. This MemoryAgent
maintains an isolated Structured Memory and provides a governed
interaction state to support both adaptation timescales.

## 3  EvolveMAS: Memory-Governed Self-Evolution

We propose EvolveMAS, a decentralized test-time self-evolution framework
for heterogeneous multi-agent interactions. The framework combines
per-agent external memory governance with a shared
Execute–Evaluate–Evolve (EEE) process. Together, these components
support within-episode adaptation and cross-episode Knowledge evolution
without updating model parameters.

### 3.1  Framework Overview

Consider a decentralized multi-agent system (DMAS) with main agents
$`\mathcal{A}=\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}`$ operating over a continuous stream
of heterogeneous interactions organized into episodes. In episode $`e`$,
the agents receive a task description $`x_{e}`$ and interact
autonomously over $`T_{e}`$ turns. Each agent selects its actions based
on local observations, while the task determines which agents interact
and when. The resulting interaction record forms the trajectory
$`\tau_{e}=(\tau_{e,1},\dots,\tau_{e,T_{e}})`$, where $`\tau_{e,t}`$
contains the messages and environment feedback produced at turn $`t`$.
Each agent observes its own local view of $`\tau_{e}`$, and the episode
concludes with an interaction outcome $`o_{e}`$.

EvolveMAS preserves this decentralized organization
(Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1 Framework Overview ‣ 3 EvolveMAS: Memory-Governed Self-Evolution")).
Each main agent $`a_{i}`$ is paired one-to-one with a dedicated
MemoryAgent $`m_{i}`$ and an isolated memory, allowing accumulated
experience to inform the main agent’s subsequent behavior. The main
agent handles task reasoning, communication, and environment
interaction. The corresponding MemoryAgent maintains Structured Memory
and Interaction Knowledge outside the main agent’s reasoning context,
drawing on interaction evidence observed by that main agent. All
MemoryAgents share the same memory schema and governance protocol, while
their contents evolve independently based on their respective main
agents’ local interaction histories.

Based on the interaction record, EvolveMAS represents experience at
three levels of abstraction. The _Trajectory_ $`\tau_{e}`$ consists of
raw interaction evidence generated during task execution. Building on
this evidence, EvolveMAS maintains _Structured Memory_ $`M_{e,t}`$ as a
compact state updated during interaction. At a higher level, it
maintains _Interaction Knowledge_ $`K_{e}`$ to capture reusable
regularities across episodes. For clarity, the subsequent formulation
adopts the perspective of a focal main agent $`a=a_{i}`$, with the agent
index omitted from its memory and Interaction Knowledge states.

Memory. Here, $`o_{e}`$ denotes the observed interaction outcome, and
$`r_{e}`$ denotes the corresponding task-native result signal. The EEE
process is then formulated at the episode level as

|     |                                     |                                                                                                           |     |     |
| --- | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\textsc{Execute}:`$  | $`\displaystyle(\tau_{e},M_{e},o_{e})=\operatorname{Exec}\bigl(x_{e},\mathcal{A},M_{e,0},K_{e-1}\bigr),`$ |     | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle\textsc{Evaluate}:`$ | $`\displaystyle r_{e}=\operatorname{Eval}\bigl(x_{e},\tau_{e},o_{e}\bigr),`$                              |     |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle\textsc{Evolve}:`$   | $`\displaystyle K_{e}=D_{a}\bigl(M_{e},r_{e};K_{e-1}\bigr).`$                                             |     |     |

Here, $`\operatorname{Eval}`$ denotes the task-native outcome evaluator.
Accordingly, $`r_{e}`$ may vary in form across interaction settings
while providing a common interface for outcome-conditioned evolution.
The operator $`D_{a}`$ distills Interaction Knowledge from governed
Structured Memory, the observed result, and previously accumulated
Interaction Knowledge. The resulting $`K_{e}`$ comprises reusable
Interaction Strategies and Partner Profiles. Both Execute and Evolve use
the previous episode’s Knowledge $`K_{e-1}`$. Specifically, Execute uses
it to guide interaction, whereas Evolve incorporates new experience to
refine it. In contrast, Evaluate computes $`r_{e}`$ from $`x_{e}`$,
$`\tau_{e}`$, and $`o_{e}`$, without directly accessing accumulated
memory or Knowledge. The following subsections detail the memory and
Knowledge update mechanisms.

### 3.2  Within-Episode Online Adaptation

During Execute in
Eq. [1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1 Framework Overview ‣ 3 EvolveMAS: Memory-Governed Self-Evolution"),
main agents interact with peers and the environment. As interaction
proceeds, their MemoryAgents continually update Structured Memory based
on newly generated interaction evidence. At turn $`t`$, action
generation and memory updating are formulated as

|     |                                             |                                                                                               |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle(y_{e,t},\Delta\tau_{e,t})`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim\pi_{a}\left(\cdot\mid[x_{e};M_{e,t-1};K_{e-1}]\right),`$                  |     | (2) |
|     | $`\displaystyle M_{e,t}`$                   | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{MA}_{\mathrm{online}}\left(M_{e,t-1},\Delta\tau_{e,t}\right),`$ |     |     |

where $`y_{e,t}`$ denotes the generated response or environment action,
and $`\Delta\tau_{e,t}`$ denotes the newly observed interaction evidence
at turn $`t`$.

Structured Memory comprises three complementary regions,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       M_{e,t}=\left(E_{e,t},S_{e,t},W_{e,t}\right),
       ```                                            |     | (3) |

where $`E_{e,t}`$, $`S_{e,t}`$, and $`W_{e,t}`$ denote Episodic,
Semantic, and Working Memory ([Tulving, 1972](#bib.bib40); [Baddeley &
Hitch, 1974](#bib.bib4)).

#### Episodic Memory.

$`E_{e,t}`$ records concrete interaction experiences, including the
events that occurred, the agents or sources involved, and the outcomes
that followed. Together, these records provide an interaction-grounded
history from which regularities can subsequently be inferred.

#### Semantic Memory.

$`S_{e,t}`$ maintains reusable facts and interaction regularities
inferred from experience. Depending on the interaction, these may
include task-relevant facts, recurring coordination patterns,
observations about interaction partners, or other stable information
that supports subsequent reasoning.

#### Working Memory.

$`W_{e,t}`$ maintains a compact state relevant to the ongoing
interaction. This state includes the current focus, next-step
information, and task-relevant views of accumulated memory and
Interaction Knowledge. As the interaction context changes, Working
Memory is refreshed accordingly.

At the representation level, new events are appended to Episodic Memory,
reusable regularities are incorporated into Semantic Memory, and Working
Memory is refreshed. These conceptual update rules do not prescribe a
specific storage implementation.

The updates in
Eq. [2](#S3.E2 "In 3.2 Within-Episode Online Adaptation ‣ 3 EvolveMAS: Memory-Governed Self-Evolution")
form a recurrent feedback loop within Execute. Evidence observed at turn
$`t`$ updates Structured Memory, which conditions the acting policy
$`\pi_{a}`$ from turn $`t+1`$ onward. This loop enables Within-Episode
Online Adaptation.
Sec. [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Cross-Episode Knowledge Evolution ‣ 3 EvolveMAS: Memory-Governed Self-Evolution")
formalizes both adaptation timescales in
Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "In Partner Profiles. ‣ 3.3 Cross-Episode Knowledge Evolution ‣ 3 EvolveMAS: Memory-Governed Self-Evolution").

The term _within-episode_ specifies the timescale of this feedback loop.
Episodic and Semantic information may persist and accumulate across
episodes, whereas Working Memory is refreshed for the ongoing
interaction.

### 3.3  Cross-Episode Knowledge Evolution

After episode $`e`$ concludes, Evaluate produces a task-native result
signal $`r_{e}`$ from the observed interaction outcome. This signal
serves as a protocol-level interface for conditioning subsequent
evolution without requiring a shared metric space across heterogeneous
interactions. Conditioned on $`r_{e}`$, the main agent distills reusable
interaction regularities from the governed Structured Memory $`M_{e}`$
and previously accumulated Interaction Knowledge $`K_{e-1}`$. The
evolved Interaction Knowledge comprises two complementary components,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       K_{e}=(G_{e},P_{e}),
       ```                   |     | (4) |

which are described below.

#### Interaction Strategies.

$`G_{e}`$ captures reusable regularities that guide behavioral
adaptation across recurring interaction situations, including
coordination, verification, negotiation, and trust calibration.

#### Partner Profiles.

$`P_{e}=\{p_{e,j}\}_{j\neq i}`$ captures partner-specific regularities,
including recurring behavioral tendencies, inferred capabilities, and
reliability, and is updated as new interaction evidence accumulates.

In subsequent episodes, the evolved Interaction Knowledge conditions
Execute together with the current Structured Memory. Combined with the
online memory updates in
Sec. [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Within-Episode Online Adaptation ‣ 3 EvolveMAS: Memory-Governed Self-Evolution"),
EvolveMAS therefore operates across two coupled adaptation timescales:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\underbrace{\Delta\tau_{e,t}\rightarrow M_{e,t}\rightarrow\textsc{Execute}_{t+1}}_{\text{Within-Episode Online Adaptation}}\qquad\underbrace{(M_{e},r_{e},K_{e-1})\rightarrow K_{e}\rightarrow\textsc{Execute}_{\mathrm{future}}}_{\text{Cross-Episode Knowledge Evolution}}.
``` |  | (5) |

Together, within-episode evidence integration and cross-episode
knowledge reuse form a unified self-evolution framework for
heterogeneous interactions without updating model parameters.

## 4  Experiments

### 4.1  Experiment Setting

We evaluate EvolveMAS on five representative benchmarks spanning three
interaction settings. The cooperative setting includes BBH ([Suzgun
et al., 2023](#bib.bib38)) and HumanEval ([Chen et al.,
2021](#bib.bib7)), covering multi-agent reasoning and collaborative
coding under shared goals. The mixed-motive setting includes
LLM-Deliberation ([Abdelnabi et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)) and
SOTOPIA ([Zhou et al., 2024](#bib.bib64)), covering structured
negotiation and open-ended social interaction with partially aligned
objectives. The adversarial setting uses One Night Ultimate
Werewolf ([Jin et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)), a hidden-role game involving
deception, trust calibration, and strategic reasoning.

The main experiments use heterogeneous teams spanning model families and
capability tiers to support Partner Profile modeling. Team size and
composition follow each benchmark’s native protocol and difficulty. Each
main agent is paired with an isolated GPT-4o-mini MemoryAgent. We
evaluate five source method families under six configurations, including
benchmark-native and cross-setting comparisons.
Appendices [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Transfer Construction ‣ Appendix A Experimental Protocols")
and [B](#A2 "Appendix B Cross-Setting Transfer Profiles") detail team
composition, transfer construction, and additional analyses.
*Self-Memory* retains the same interaction structure but manages memory
within the main agent’s task-reasoning context.

### 4.3  Cross-Setting Evolution

We examine how performance evolves as interaction experience
accumulates.
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Adversarial setting. ‣ 4.2 Main Results across Interaction Settings ‣ 4 Experiments")
presents cumulative performance over tasks, sessions, or games, grouped
by interaction setting. The trajectories compare EvolveMAS with the
native baseline and Self-Memory to assess whether accumulated experience
continues to influence subsequent behavior under the same evolution
protocol.

In the cooperative setting, the relative performance of EvolveMAS on BBH
improves from an initial deficit to a sustained advantage over the later
tasks, ending at $`+4`$ percentage points. On HumanEval, performance
remains comparable to the native baseline for roughly the first half of
the task stream, followed by a modest advantage that ends at
approximately $`+1.2`$ percentage points. In the mixed-motive setting,
SOTOPIA shows an overall upward trend in relative performance, with the
early deficit narrowing toward parity despite intermediate fluctuations.
On LLM-Deliberation, the cumulative advantage is positive throughout the
displayed interval and persists through the final session. This pattern
is consistent with adaptation taking effect early and providing
sustained benefits in subsequent interactions. In the adversarial
setting, symmetric Werewolf tracks village-side performance as both
opposing sides evolve. The one-sided evaluations show positive
advantages that persist over the later games, with larger gains on the
wolf side.

The trajectories in
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Adversarial setting. ‣ 4.2 Main Results across Interaction Settings ‣ 4 Experiments")
indicate that accumulated interaction experience supports continual
evolution across cooperative, mixed-motive, and adversarial settings
under a shared EvolveMAS protocol. Although trajectory shapes vary with
the interaction structure, the self-evolution process, memory
representation, and update schedule remain unchanged.

### 4.4  Ablating the Two Evolution Mechanisms

To distinguish the contributions of the two mechanisms, the evaluation
requires both emerging information within sessions and recurring
partners across sessions. These conditions support within-episode
adaptation and cross-episode Knowledge reuse, respectively.
LLM-Deliberation meets both conditions: participants reveal their
positions over successive rounds, and the same six parties participate
in every session. Its graded outcomes also allow the contribution of
each mechanism to be measured. We report average agreement, measured as
the mean number of parties accepting the final proposal, and 5/6
consensus on that proposal. The unanimity rate measures the fraction of
sessions in which at least one lead-party proposal is accepted by all
six parties.

We independently vary memory governance (Self-Memory vs. external
MemoryAgent) and the availability of cross-episode Interaction Knowledge
($`K=\varnothing`$ vs. $`K_{e}=(G_{e},P_{e})`$). This yields the
$`2\times 2`$ factorial design in
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.4 Ablating the Two Evolution Mechanisms ‣ 4 Experiments").
The lower-right condition in each metric panel combines external memory
governance with cross-episode Interaction Knowledge and corresponds to
full EvolveMAS.

Figure 5: LLM-Deliberation ablation. Rows vary memory governance;
columns vary the availability of cross-episode Knowledge
$`K_{e}=(G_{e},P_{e})`$. Each cell reports one run of 50 sessions; color
scales are panel-specific. The effect summary reports average marginal
effects on unanimity and the total gain.

Unanimity rates differ across all four conditions. Replacing Self-Memory
with an external MemoryAgent increases unanimity from 24.0% to 74.0%
without cross-episode Knowledge and from 46.0% to 86.0% with it. These
gains are consistent with updated interaction states helping agents
respond to positions revealed during deliberation. Adding $`K_{e}`$
further increases unanimity from 74.0% to 86.0% with an external
MemoryAgent and from 24.0% to 46.0% with Self-Memory. These improvements
support the value of reusing negotiation regularities across episodes.
Average agreement follows the same ordering (4.90, 5.24, 5.36, 5.40).

### 4.6  Additional Analyses

System-level analyses show stable performance across the evaluated team
sizes and favorable performance relative to static aggregation. Further
diagnostics show stable short-horizon performance and lower per-episode
cost and acting-context occupancy than retaining raw interaction
histories as experience accumulates
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C MAS-Level Properties")). Analyses of
Knowledge accumulation indicate that stronger acting models distill more
transferable Interaction Knowledge
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Capability-Stratified Sedimentation")).
Finally, a long-horizon memory ablation shows higher overall
question-answering accuracy with online MemoryAgent updates than with
offline updates or self-managed memory
(Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Long-Horizon Memory Ablation")).

## 5  Conclusion

We presented EvolveMAS, a unified self-evolution framework for
decentralized multi-agent systems (DMAS). Evaluations on five benchmarks
demonstrate its applicability to cooperative, mixed-motive, and
adversarial settings, while ablations highlight the value of test-time
memory governance and cross-episode Knowledge reuse. Together, these
results support experience-driven adaptation across the three
interaction settings under a shared self-evolution framework without
model parameter updates. Designed as a plug-in extension to existing
agents, EvolveMAS supports decentralized interaction while preserving
each agent’s autonomy. This provides a basis for integrating the
capabilities of agents deployed by different users without centralized
control. Building on this paradigm, future work can investigate
empirical scaling laws by varying agent capability, population size, and
accumulated experience. Such studies would clarify the design trade-offs
among DMAS reasoning performance, computational cost, and safety.

## Appendix A Experimental Protocols

The main text reports the cross-setting results and their

### A.1  Transfer Construction

To make the transfer rows in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Main Results across Interaction Settings ‣ 4 Experiments")
reproducible and fair to the source methods, we fix one transfer setup.
Each setting-native method family is instantiated from its original
configuration, preserving native role structure, team composition, and
social mode, and is then run through the target benchmark’s native
runner and evaluator. Each family therefore runs exactly the mechanism
it was designed around, including the within-episode interaction context
of its native protocol; cross-episode memory enters the comparison
through the Self-Memory row on the same team. BBH and HumanEval runs
force the social mode to cooperative because both are cooperative
benchmarks, while SOTOPIA, LLM-Deliberation, and Werewolf retain the
source method’s native social mode.
Table [2](#A1.T2 "Table 2 ‣ A.1 Transfer Construction ‣ Appendix A Experimental Protocols")
lists the five source families and their native protocols.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  Family |  Native protocol |  Native benchmark |
|  ChatDev |  Planner$`\rightarrow`$developer$`\rightarrow`$tester pipeline (3 agents) |  HumanEval |
|  AgentNet |  5-agent heterogeneous parallel or routed reasoning |  BBH |
|  SOTOPIA |  Buyer/seller/mediator bargaining (3 agents) |  SOTOPIA |
|  LLM-Deliberation |  6-party multi-issue proposal voting |  LLM-Deliberation |

Table 2: Source method families for the transfer rows, with native

For the cooperative targets (BBH, HumanEval), the source family’s role
structure is mapped onto the cooperative runner: a ChatDev pipeline runs
as planner, developer, and tester; an AgentNet or Werewolf team runs as
a five-agent heterogeneous group; and a SOTOPIA trio runs as three
cooperating agents. For SOTOPIA, the source team bargains under the
mixed-motive evaluator. For LLM-Deliberation, the source method’s agents
populate the six negotiation parties, with the Werewolf family
represented by the one-adversarial-incentive variant that best matches
its structure. For Werewolf, the source team fills the five player slots
under the adversarial evaluator, except that the fixed five-player count
forces LLM-Deliberation to use its native five-model configuration
rather than the six-party one.

For the Werewolf/DPIns family ([Jin et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)), the
original method trains a discussion policy with reinforcement learning
to choose among discussion tactics, and deduces the other players’
hidden roles by belief modeling rather than by a learned policy; DPIns
is the name its released implementation gives this
discussion-policy-instructed agent. To evaluate all families under the
same training-free setting with frozen models, we encode the discussion
tactics it selects as a fixed prompt. This instantiation applies
uniformly across the six evaluation configurations, so the family
appears in every column of the transfer table under the same frozen
team.

#### Team composition.

Team size and roles follow each benchmark’s native protocol: five agents
on BBH, a planner–developer–tester pipeline on HumanEval, a three-role
bargaining setup on SOTOPIA, six parties on LLM-Deliberation, and five
players on Werewolf. The Werewolf team, and the teams used in the
analyses of Appendices [C](#A3 "Appendix C MAS-Level Properties")
and [D](#A4 "Appendix D Capability-Stratified Sedimentation"), use the
strong lineup, which combines GPT-4.1, Gemini-2.5-Pro, Gemini-3.5-Flash,
GLM-5.2, and Qwen3-14B across three capability tiers; LLM-Deliberation
adds GPT-4o-mini to this lineup for its sixth party. In the main table,
HumanEval uses Gemini-3.5-Flash as planner, GLM-5.2 as developer, and
GPT-4.1 as tester, and SOTOPIA uses GPT-4.1, Gemini-2.5-Pro, and
Gemini-3.5-Flash. BBH uses a weak lineup of five smaller models of a
comparable tier, where accumulated experience still changes the outcome
on a benchmark that a strong lineup already resolves near its ceiling.
Every main agent is paired with an isolated GPT-4o-mini MemoryAgent. The
Werewolf runs follow the game configuration of the released DPIns
implementation ([Jin et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)) without modification;
our changes are confined to the agents that fill the roles.

#### Evaluation scale.

Each column of
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Main Results across Interaction Settings ‣ 4 Experiments")
is scored over a fixed evaluation set. BBH uses 100 test questions, five
from each of 20 subtasks, following the protocol of the AgentNet
evaluation it is compared against; HumanEval uses all 164 tasks; SOTOPIA
uses 80 interactions, eight episodes for each of ten bargaining
scenarios; LLM-Deliberation uses 50 sessions per row; and each Werewolf
audit uses 100 games, above the 30 games per condition used in the
original One Night Ultimate Werewolf study ([Jin et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22)). All rows within a column share the same evaluation
protocol.

#### Sampling precision.

Each evaluation set is fixed, so the precision of a single entry follows
from its size. In the four columns scored as proportions, the binomial
standard error of one entry is about four points on BBH, under two on
HumanEval, and about five on each Werewolf audit, so a difference of a
few points within one of those columns rests on the paired comparison
over the shared items rather than on the column size alone;
Table [5](#A5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ E.1 BBH Lineups ‣ Appendix E Within-Setting Breakdowns")
reports such a test. The LLM-Deliberation column is scored per session
and therefore yields an interval directly: across its 50 sessions the
native baseline averages 4.92 (SD 0.90), Self-Memory 4.90 (SD 0.71), and
EvolveMAS 5.40 (SD 0.64). The EvolveMAS advantage of 0.48 over the
native baseline and of 0.50 over Self-Memory each carry a 95% interval
that excludes zero, whether the sessions are treated as paired or as
independent samples, while the 0.02 separating Self-Memory from the
native baseline does not.

#### Determinism and pairing.

The three BBH rows of
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Main Results across Interaction Settings ‣ 4 Experiments")
run under one lineup configuration and a single fixed seed, and present
the tasks in the same order, so that column is paired item by item.
SOTOPIA draws on a fixed set of ten bargaining scenarios and records no
sampling seed; the runs compared there cover the identical scenario set,
so its pairing holds at the scenario level. The released artifact ships
the lineup configuration behind each reported run.

party accepts a proposal, and the same ordering holds on average
agreement, the metric carried into
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Main Results across Interaction Settings ‣ 4 Experiments").
This measure asks whether unanimity occurs at any point in a session, so
it is sensitive to how many proposals a session puts forward; the
final-proposal rate is reported beside it so both readings are visible.

## Appendix F Long-Horizon Memory Ablation

The main text reports ownership through the benchmark-native Self-Memory
and EvolveMAS pairs. This section adds a LoCoMo ([Maharana et al.,
2024](#bib.bib28)) ablation that examines memory management from the
memory perspective, separating memory ownership from update timing.
LoCoMo evaluates question answering over long multi-session
conversations and allows the starting memory to be held fixed, which
isolates the effect of when the MemoryAgent updates.
Figure [9](#A6.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Appendix F Long-Horizon Memory Ablation")
reports the ablation.

Each condition answers the same 1,540 questions, the full set of ten
conversations at 154 questions each. A gpt-4.1 judge scores every answer
as correct or incorrect, counting an answer correct when it carries the
reference information whatever its wording, and a deterministic filter
applied ahead of the judge marks an answer incorrect when it declines to
commit — “not mentioned”, “not specified” — against a reference that
states a concrete fact. Absolute accuracies are therefore not comparable
with published LoCoMo results, which apply the benchmark’s released
protocol to answers produced outside a multi-agent loop. Every arm here
shares this judge and this filter, so the comparison among them is
internally consistent, and the grading program ships with the released
code.

Figure 9: LoCoMo ablation (gpt-4.1 judge). (a) Overall accuracy.
(b) Per-category comparison of Self-Memory and EvolveMAS. (c) Memory
model sensitivity, with the dashed line marking Self-Memory.

Holding the starting memory fixed and varying only whether the
MemoryAgent updates during the session, online updates (EvolveMAS) beat
offline batch updates (Offline MemoryAgent) overall (18.8 vs. 16.4 with
gpt-4.1 memory). The gain is concentrated on single-hop questions (30.9
vs. 24.8), where fresh per-question facts are most reusable, and the two
are tied on multi-hop questions (26.0 each), where an offline batch has
more opportunity to cross-link distant sessions before answering. These
per-category values for Offline MemoryAgent are reported here only;
Figure [9](#A6.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Appendix F Long-Horizon Memory Ablation")b
contrasts Self-Memory with EvolveMAS. Relative to No Memory, which
answers from the most recent session alone (7.9), both Offline
MemoryAgent and EvolveMAS show substantial memory gains
($`+`$8.5–10.9 pp). EvolveMAS also exceeds Self-Memory overall (18.8
vs. 16.4) and leads on fact recall (single-hop 30.9 vs. 17.0,
common-sense 19.1 vs. 17.8), while Self-Memory is strongest on multi-hop
reasoning (42.7 vs. 26.0), where its in-context self-notes link distant
sessions. Self-Memory and Offline MemoryAgent land on the same overall
value, so what separates the arms on this benchmark is online updating:
EvolveMAS improves on both static arms by the same 2.4 points.

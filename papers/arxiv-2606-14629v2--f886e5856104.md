# When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training

Jianzhe Lin Affiliation: MetaAI Work done at Meta

###### Abstract

Verifier reliability is not portable across tasks. A verifier-guided
self-DPO pipeline that produces genuine held-out gains on MathVista
($`+9.6`$ points on the self-training data and $`+8.0`$ points held out)
can become harmful on MMMU. Crucially, the failure is not visible from
the target-task self-training signal: averaged across six
learner–verifier configurations, performance on the MMMU self-training
data still improves by $`+3.52`$ points, while held-out performance
drops by $`1.42`$ points. We term this _silent negative transfer_:a
verifier previously validated as useful can continue to exhibit the
signatures of successful self-training after its induced update ceases
to transfer to unseen data.

This raises a more basic question: how can we tell whether verifier
supervision is reliable on the target task? A downstream failure alone
cannot answer this question, because poor generalization may arise from
two distinct sources: the verifier may induce an incorrect learning
direction, or the correct learning direction itself may fail to
generalize beyond the self-training distribution. We formalize these
effects through _gradient fidelity_ $`F`$, which measures alignment
between the verifier-induced and correct training directions, and
_gradient transferability_ $`T`$, which measures alignment between the
correct training and held-out directions. This decomposition yields a
conservative Safe-Transfer Margin: positive first-order held-out
alignment is certified when

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \arccos F+\arccos T<\pi/2.
       ```                         |     |

Motivated by this diagnosis, we introduce a simple yet effective
precision-first filtering method, _Asymmetric Acceptance Gating_ (AAG),
which selects preference pairs according to the verifier’s absolute
confidence in the response receiving the positive update.
Gradient-alignment analyses on MMMU show that AAG increases gradient
fidelity from $`0.29`$ to $`0.42`$. In a separate same-cell analysis,
the correct training direction remains positively aligned with the
held-out direction ($`T=0.31`$), yet raw verification rotates the
induced update to negative held-out alignment ($`-0.13`$); AAG restores
positive alignment ($`+0.22`$). Across all six MMMU learner–verifier
configurations, AAG outperforms raw verifier-guided training on held-out
evaluation.

These results redefine what it means for a verifier to be reliable: not
merely that it has worked before, but that it induces the right update
on the task at hand—and that this update remains useful beyond the data
that generated it.

## 1 Introduction

Verifier-guided learning has become a scalable alternative to repeated
human supervision ([Bai et al., 2022](#bib.bib7); [Lee et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8)). Model-based evaluators can provide preferences,
rewards, critiques, or other judgments that are converted into
optimization signals, enabling iterative alignment and self-improvement
without requiring human labels at every round ([Bai et al.,
2022](#bib.bib7); [Lee et al., 2024](#bib.bib8); [Yuan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13)). Related ideas also appear in scalable oversight and
learned-verifier frameworks ([Kenton et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Zhang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)). These approaches share a central assumption:
once model-generated judgments are sufficiently reliable, converting
them into optimization signals should improve the model being trained
([Sun et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)).

![Refer to
caption](2606.14629v2/figure1_when_good_verifiers_go_bad_latest.png)

Figure 1: Good verifiers can go bad under task transfer, while

whereas MMMU exhibits silent negative transfer: transductive performance

$`0.47`$, showing that the gain comes from the asymmetric selection
criterion rather than from filtering more aggressively alone.

Yet a reliable judge need not provide a reliable optimization signal.
Prior work has studied the accuracy, calibration, and bias of
model-based evaluators ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Stureborg et
al., 2024](#bib.bib19)). Learned judges and reward-model benchmarks
further characterize the quality of model-generated evaluations ([Kim et
al., 2024a](#bib.bib17); [Lambert et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)). In
parallel, preference-learning methods have examined robustness to noisy
or unreliable supervision ([Chowdhury et al., 2024](#bib.bib20); [Wu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib21)).

More recent work has shown that verifier reliability can depend strongly
on the evaluation regime, including problem difficulty,
generator–verifier interactions, evaluator bias, and distribution shift
([Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib2); [Andrade et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)).
Related analyses show that reward and preference models can exploit
signals that fail to generalize beyond the regime in which they were
validated ([Razin et al., 2026](#bib.bib4); [Hwang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)). Theoretical studies of solver–verifier dynamics
further suggest that verification quality and downstream
self-improvement need not coincide ([Sun et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)).

These findings motivate a sharper question: _when does a verifier
provide a reliable optimization signal?_ We argue that judgment quality
is only one part of the answer. Reliable verifier-guided learning
requires two distinct directional agreements. First, the update induced
by the verifier must align with the correct learning direction on the
self-training distribution. Second, that correct learning direction must
itself align with the direction that improves held-out performance. The
two can fail independently: a verifier can rotate an otherwise useful
update in the wrong direction, while even perfectly faithful supervision
can recover a learning direction that fails to generalize.

These two failure modes correspond to two distinct geometric
relationships. We formalize them through two alignments that are often
conflated. Let $`g_{V}`$ denote the update induced by verifier-generated
preferences, $`g_{P}^{*}`$ the counterfactual correct-preference update
on the self-training distribution, and $`g_{Q}^{*}`$ the corresponding
correct-preference update on held-out data. We define

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       F=\cos(g_{V},g_{P}^{*}),\qquad T=\cos(g_{P}^{*},g_{Q}^{*}),
       ```                                                          |     | (1) |

as _gradient fidelity_ and _gradient transferability_, respectively.
They satisfy

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \cos(g_{V},g_{Q}^{*})\geq FT-\sqrt{1-F^{2}}\sqrt{1-T^{2}},
       ```                                                         |     | (2) |

which yields the _Safe-Transfer Margin_

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       M_{\mathrm{safe}}=\frac{\pi}{2}-\arccos F-\arccos T.
       ```                                                   |     | (3) |

A positive margin certifies positive first-order transfer under the
correct-preference objective; a non-positive margin is deliberately
inconclusive rather than predictive of failure. The margin is therefore
a conservative diagnostic certificate rather than a downstream-accuracy
predictor. The reference directions $`g_{P}^{*}`$ and $`g_{Q}^{*}`$ are
used only for analysis and are never available to the training
algorithm.

This geometry also explains why verifier accuracy alone can obscure
optimization risk. A verifier used for training does not merely produce
judgments; its decisions induce parameter updates. In particular, a
_preference reversal_, in which an incorrect response is reinforced over
a correct one, exactly negates the corresponding DPO update at
initialization and can become increasingly influential as the policy
separates the pair. Such errors are therefore directional optimization
errors rather than exchangeable label noise.

This observation motivates _Asymmetric Acceptance Gating (AAG)_. Instead
of filtering by the score gap between chosen and rejected responses, AAG
ranks pairs by the absolute confidence of the response that will receive
the positive update. At fixed coverage, selecting the largest true
posterior probabilities that the chosen response is correct maximizes
selected-positive precision; AAG approximates this optimal selector when
verifier confidence preserves that posterior ordering. This assumption
requires rank consistency rather than numerical calibration and permits
a label-free quantile implementation. It is directly supported by
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")(b):
chosen-response precision rises from $`0.52`$ to $`0.87`$ as
chosen-confidence selection becomes stricter, whereas symmetric
score-spread filtering reduces it from $`0.52`$ to $`0.47`$.

Our measurements support the predicted geometry on explicitly identified
probes. On MMMU Qwen3-VL-4B/G3.1 at initialization, AAG increases
gradient fidelity from $`0.29`$ to $`0.42`$. On a same-cell MMMU
Qwen3-VL-8B/G2.5 probe, the correct direction has positive held-out
alignment $`T=0.31`$, yet raw verification yields
$`\cos(g_{\mathrm{Raw}},g_{Q}^{*})=-0.13`$, while AAG restores it to
$`+0.22`$. On the MathVista Qwen3-VL-4B/G3.1 positive control, a
same-cell probe yields $`F_{\mathrm{Raw}}=0.76`$,
$`F_{\mathrm{AAG}}=0.86`$, and $`T=0.78`$, giving Safe-Transfer Margins
of $`+10.7^{\circ}`$ and $`+20.6^{\circ}`$, respectively. Downstream,
AAG outperforms raw verifier training on held-out MMMU in all six
learner–verifier settings, shifting the mean effect from $`-1.42`$ to
$`+0.98`$ points.

Our contributions are:

- •
  Silent negative transfer: we identify a failure mode in which prior
  verifier success does not certify reliable supervision on a new task:
  self-training performance can continue to improve even as held-out
  performance deteriorates.
- •
  Directional diagnosis: we disentangle gradient fidelity from gradient
  transferability and derive a conservative Safe-Transfer Margin.
- •
  Theory-motivated mitigation: we introduce Asymmetric Acceptance Gating
  (AAG), a simple yet effective precision-first rule that improves
  gradient fidelity and consistently outperforms raw verifier-guided
  training on held-out data.

Reliable verifier-guided learning therefore requires not merely a good
verifier, but a learning direction that is both faithful and
transferable.

## 2 Preliminaries

Let $`P`$ denote the distribution used to construct self-training
experience and $`Q`$ a disjoint held-out distribution. For $`x\sim P`$,
a learner $`\pi_{\theta}`$ samples $`K`$ responses
$`\mathcal{Y}(x)=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{K}\}`$. A frozen verifier $`V`$
assigns score $`s_{V}(x,y)`$ and forms

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
y^{+}=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)}s_{V}(x,y),\qquad y^{-}=\arg\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)}s_{V}(x,y).
``` |  | (4) |

We optimize the resulting pair $`z=(x,y^{+},y^{-})`$ using DPO
([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib9)):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(z)=-\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\Delta_{\theta}(z)\right),
``` |  | (5) |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{\theta}(z)=\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}\mid x)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}\mid x)}.
``` |  | (6) |

Neither raw self-DPO nor our method uses ground-truth preferences.
Ground truth is used only for controlled baselines and post-hoc
diagnostics.

For analysis, let $`g_{V}`$ be the expected update induced by
verifier-selected pairs on $`P`$. We define $`g_{P}^{*}`$ and
$`g_{Q}^{*}`$ as the counterfactual update directions induced by correct
preferences on $`P`$ and $`Q`$, respectively. Equivalently,
$`g_{P}^{*}`$ and $`g_{Q}^{*}`$ are gradients of the corresponding
correct-preference objectives. These reference directions are never
available to the training algorithm.

## 3 Gradient Geometry of Verifier-Guided Learning

### 3.1 Two alignments govern transfer

We distinguish two forms of alignment:

|     |                                                             |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       F=\cos(g_{V},g_{P}^{*}),\qquad T=\cos(g_{P}^{*},g_{Q}^{*}),
       ```                                                          |     | (7) |

where $`F`$ is *gradient fidelity* and $`T`$ is *gradient
transferability*. The former measures whether verifier supervision
preserves the direction of correct learning; the latter measures whether
that direction transfers beyond the improvement distribution.

#### Theorem 1 (Two-alignment transfer bound).

For nonzero $`g_{V},g_{P}^{*},g_{Q}^{*}`$,

|     |                                                            |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \cos(g_{V},g_{Q}^{*})\geq FT-\sqrt{1-F^{2}}\sqrt{1-T^{2}}.
       ```                                                         |     | (8) |

#### Proof sketch.

Let $`\hat{v},\hat{p},\hat{q}`$ be the corresponding unit vectors.
Decompose

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{v}`$ | $`\displaystyle=F\hat{p}+\sqrt{1-F^{2}}\,a,`$ |  | (9) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{q}`$ | $`\displaystyle=T\hat{p}+\sqrt{1-T^{2}}\,b,`$ |  | (10) |

where $`a,b\perp\hat{p}`$ and $`\|a\|=\|b\|=1`$. Then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{v}^{\top}\hat{q}=FT+\sqrt{1-F^{2}}\sqrt{1-T^{2}}\,a^{\top}b,
``` |  | (11) |

and
Eq. [8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ Theorem 1 (Two-alignment transfer bound). ‣ 3.1 Two alignments govern transfer ‣ 3 Gradient Geometry of Verifier-Guided Learning ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")
follows from $`a^{\top}b\geq-1`$.

#### Corollary 1 (Safe-Transfer Margin).

Define

|     |                                                      |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       M_{\mathrm{safe}}=\frac{\pi}{2}-\arccos F-\arccos T.
       ```                                                   |     | (12) |

If $`M_{\mathrm{safe}}>0`$, then $`\langle g_{V},g_{Q}^{*}\rangle>0`$,
certifying positive first-order transfer under the held-out
correct-preference objective. A non-positive margin is inconclusive
rather than predictive of failure.

This decomposition exposes two distinct regimes: *transfer-limited*
self-improvement, where the correct update itself transfers poorly, and
*fidelity-limited* self-improvement, where a transferable update is
corrupted by the verifier.

### 3.2 Preference reversal as an anti-gradient

Verifier errors differ in their optimization consequences. Define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h_{\theta}(z)=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}\mid x)-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}\mid x).
``` |  | (13) |

The DPO update direction is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
u_{\theta}(z)=\beta\sigma(-\beta\Delta_{\theta}(z))h_{\theta}(z).
``` |  | (14) |

Consider a *preference reversal*, where an incorrect response is chosen
over a correct one, and let $`z^{R}`$ denote the correctly ordered pair.

#### Proposition 1 (Reversal anti-gradient).

At $`\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}`$,

|     |                                   |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{\theta}(z)=-u_{\theta}(z^{R}).
       ```                                |     | (15) |

Thus a reversal is an exact anti-gradient at DPO initialization.

More generally, if the policy assigns DPO gap $`\Delta>0`$ to the
correct ordering, then for the same pair geometry,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\|u_{\mathrm{rev}}\|}{\|u_{\mathrm{correct}}\|}=e^{\beta\Delta}.
``` |  | (16) |

Hence reversed supervision can become increasingly influential once the
learner already favors the correct ordering.

Under the idealized model in which identical informative gradients are
independently reversed with probability $`\rho`$,

|     |                                   |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[g_{V}]=(1-2\rho)g^{*}.
       ```                                |     | (17) |

Eq. [17](#S3.E17 "Equation 17 ‣ Proposition 1 (Reversal anti-gradient). ‣ 3.2 Preference reversal as an anti-gradient ‣ 3 Gradient Geometry of Verifier-Guided Learning ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")
describes cancellation of directional signal, not downstream accuracy.
Structured verifier errors may additionally introduce orthogonal
components and rotate the aggregate gradient, reducing fidelity.

## 4 Asymmetric Acceptance Gating

The geometry above suggests that selection should prioritize the
reliability of the response receiving the positive update. Let

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C(z)=\mathbbm{1}\{y^{+}\text{ is correct}\},\qquad p(z)=\Pr(C(z)=1\mid z).
``` |  | (18) |

This is a selective-classification / reject-option rule ([Chow,
1970](#bib.bib27); [El-Yaniv and Wiener, 2010](#bib.bib28); [Geifman and
El-Yaniv, 2017](#bib.bib29)).

#### Theorem 2 (Optimal selection at fixed coverage).

Among all selectors $`A(z)\in\{0,1\}`$ satisfying $`\Pr(A=1)=\alpha`$,
chosen-response precision $`\Pr(C=1\mid A=1)`$ is maximized by selecting
the largest values of $`p(z)`$:

|     |                                              |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       A^{*}(z)=\mathbbm{1}\{p(z)\geq t_{\alpha}\},
       ```                                           |     | (19) |

with boundary randomization when necessary.

#### Proof sketch.

Since the acceptance mass is fixed, maximizing $`\Pr(C=1\mid A=1)`$ is
equivalent to maximizing $`\mathbb{E}[A\,C]=\mathbb{E}[A\,p(z)]`$, which
is achieved by assigning the available acceptance mass to the largest
posterior values.

The posterior $`p(z)`$ is unavailable during self-improvement. We
therefore propose *Asymmetric Acceptance Gating (AAG)*. At fixed
coverage $`\alpha`$, AAG retains the top-confidence chosen responses:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{\alpha}(z)=\mathbbm{1}\left\{s_{V}(x,y^{+})\geq q_{1-\alpha}\right\},
``` |  | (20) |

where $`q_{1-\alpha}`$ is the empirical $`(1-\alpha)`$-quantile of
chosen-response confidence over the eligible pair pool. Equivalently,
one may use an absolute operating threshold,

|     |                                                               |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{\tau}(z)=\mathbbm{1}\left\{s_{V}(x,y^{+})\geq\tau\right\}.
       ```                                                            |     | (21) |

If chosen-response confidence preserves the ordering of $`p(z)`$, both
parameterizations realize the same ranking as the precision-optimal
selector. This assumption is weaker than calibration and is directly
tested in
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")(b).

AAG differs fundamentally from symmetric margin filtering,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{\delta}^{\mathrm{sym}}(z)=\mathbbm{1}\left\{s_{V}(y^{+})-s_{V}(y^{-})\geq\delta\right\}.
``` |  | (22) |

A large relative margin need not imply that $`y^{+}`$ itself is
reliable; therefore symmetric margin has no corresponding fixed-coverage
precision guarantee. AAG instead controls the quantity directly coupled
to the positive DPO update: the trustworthiness of the response being
reinforced.

## 5 Experiments

We evaluate four predictions of our analysis: (i) verifier reliability
can fail to transfer across tasks even when self-training appears
successful; (ii) harmful supervision is concentrated in directional
preference errors; (iii) the resulting gradients exhibit the fidelity-
and transfer-limited regimes predicted by
Sec. [3](#S3 "3 Gradient Geometry of Verifier-Guided Learning ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training");
and (iv) asymmetric selection mitigates these failures for reasons
beyond simply reducing the number of training pairs.

### 5.1 Experimental setup

#### Benchmarks and evaluation protocols.

We study MathVista ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)) and MMMU ([Yue et
al., 2024](#bib.bib12)). MathVista provides a positive-control regime in
which verifier-guided self-DPO genuinely transfers to unseen data. For
MMMU, we evaluate two complementary settings. In the *transductive*
setting, the prompts used to construct self-training experience are also
used for evaluation. In the *held-out* setting, the 900 examples are
split into 700 self-improvement prompts and a sealed 200-example test
set; the latter never participates in candidate generation, pair
construction, or optimization.

For MathVista, the positive-control experiment contains 500 transductive
and 300 held-out examples.

#### Evaluation metric and pair universe.

The primary MMMU held-out metric is macro-averaged accuracy over 30
subject groups. For pair-level analyses, we distinguish all generated
prompt groups from the DPO-eligible pair pool. The Qwen3-VL-4B/G3.1
threshold intervention contains 230 eligible pairs after the
training-pair validity checks.
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.3 AAG removes directional preference errors ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")
uses this 230-pair training pool, whereas
Table [13](#A1.T13 "Table 13 ‣ A.10 Coverage robustness ‣ Appendix A Additional experimental results ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")
uses the broader valid-confidence analysis pool before the
DPO-eligibility restriction.

#### Learners and verifiers.

We study Qwen3-VL-4B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, and Qwen3-VL-8B with two frozen
verifiers, Gemini-3.1-Pro (G3.1) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (G2.5), yielding six
MMMU configurations. The learners span two model generations, so we do
not interpret their differences as a pure scaling trend. Each prompt
produces $`K=4`$ candidates. Unless otherwise specified, self-DPO uses
$`\beta=0.1`$ and learning rate $`10^{-5}`$. Qwen3-VL-4B multi-seed runs
use seeds $`\{17,42,123\}`$; larger-model runs use Adafactor with
gradient clipping at $`0.5`$.

#### Verifier role and candidate availability.

The verifier is used as a *candidate evaluator*, not as a direct answer
generator: it scores learner-generated responses given the image and
question without access to the ground-truth answer. Consequently,
chosen-response precision is upper-bounded by candidate oracle
availability,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{pass@4}_{\mathrm{cand}}=\Pr\!\left(\exists\,y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)\text{ that is correct}\right).
``` |  |

Appendix [A.6](#A1.SS6 "A.6 Candidate availability and verifier selection efficiency ‣ Appendix A Additional experimental results ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")
reports candidate availability and verifier selection efficiency.
Directly distilling verifier-generated answers would study a different
supervision channel; our question is whether an evaluator can be safely
converted into an optimization signal over learner-generated experience.

#### Methods.

*Frozen* denotes the pretrained learner before self-improvement. *Raw*
constructs DPO preferences directly from verifier scores. *AAG* applies
the asymmetric acceptance rule in
Eq. [21](#S4.E21 "Equation 21 ‣ Proof sketch. ‣ 4 Asymmetric Acceptance Gating ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training");
unless otherwise stated we use $`\tau=0.9`$, fixed without access to
held-out labels or held-out performance. We additionally evaluate the
equivalent coverage-controlled rule in
Eq. [20](#S4.E20 "Equation 20 ‣ Proof sketch. ‣ 4 Asymmetric Acceptance Gating ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training").
*Correct-pref.* uses ground-truth correctness to order candidate
responses and serves only as a diagnostic reference; it is not an upper
bound on held-out performance. Neither Raw nor AAG accesses ground-truth
labels.

#### Diagnostics.

We report accuracy, chosen-response precision, and preference-reversal
rate. A reversal occurs when the chosen response is incorrect while the
rejected response is correct. For gradient diagnostics we measure

|     |                                                             |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       F=\cos(g_{V},g_{P}^{*}),\qquad T=\cos(g_{P}^{*},g_{Q}^{*}),
       ```                                                          |     |

and direct held-out alignment $`\cos(g_{V},g_{Q}^{*})`$. Ground truth is
used only to construct these post-hoc diagnostic reference directions.

### 5.2 Verifier trust does not transfer across tasks

We first test the premise behind
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")(a).
On MathVista, the Qwen3-VL-4B/G3.1 verifier–learner pair is a genuine
positive control: raw self-DPO improves transductive accuracy from
$`0.654`$ to $`0.750`$ ($`+9.6`$ points), while held-out accuracy
improves from $`0.650`$ to $`0.730`$ ($`+8.0`$ points). Thus the
verifier does not merely fit the experience used for self-training; its
supervision produces transferable improvement.

The same recipe changes character on MMMU. For Qwen3-VL-4B/G3.1,
transductive accuracy still improves by $`3.8`$ points, yet held-out
accuracy falls by $`0.8`$ points.
Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.2 Verifier trust does not transfer across tasks ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")
shows that this discrepancy is systematic across learner configurations
and verifiers.

|                      |          |        |       |       |               |
|----------------------|----------|--------|-------|-------|---------------|

### 5.4 Real gradients exhibit both predicted failure modes

We keep all gradient measurements tied to an explicit task, learner,
verifier, checkpoint, and analysis pool;

$`F=.29`$ to $`.42`$. On the same-cell *Probe B* (MMMU Qwen3-VL-8B/G2.5

### 5.5 Why asymmetric selection matters

Theorem 2 predicts that fixed-coverage selection should rank examples by
chosen-response correctness rather than pairwise score spread. On MMMU
Qwen3-VL-4B/G3.1, AAG precision rises from $`.52`$ to $`.87`$ as
coverage falls from $`100\%`$ to $`40\%`$, whereas symmetric filtering
falls from $`.52`$ to $`.47`$. The same pattern holds for MMMU
Qwen3-VL-8B/G2.5 and MathVista
(Appendix [A.10](#A1.SS10 "A.10 Coverage robustness ‣ Appendix A Additional experimental results ‣ When Good Verifiers Go Bad: Silent Negative Transfer in Verifier-Guided VLM Training")).
These results support the rank-consistency assumption behind AAG and
show that a large pairwise margin is not a reliable proxy for
positive-target correctness.

## 6 Concluding Remarks

Related work. Our work connects model-generated supervision, selective
self-training, model-based evaluation, reward overoptimization, and
robust preference optimization. Prior work studies how stronger models
or iterative self-training procedures can provide preferences,
critiques, rewards, or filtered supervision ([Bai et al.,
2022](#bib.bib7); [Lee et al., 2024](#bib.bib8); [Yuan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13); [Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib24); [Singh et al.,
2024](#bib.bib25)), how reliably language and vision-language models
evaluate other models ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Lambert et
al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Kim et al., 2024b](#bib.bib18); [Stureborg et
al., 2024](#bib.bib19)), and how preference optimization behaves under
noisy or corrupted feedback ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib9);
[Chowdhury et al., 2024](#bib.bib20); [Wu et al., 2025](#bib.bib21);
[Choi et al., 2025](#bib.bib22); [Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)). Our
setting is also related to reward overoptimization, where improving an
imperfect proxy can degrade the underlying target ([Gao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib26)). We study a complementary failure: after task
transfer, verifier judgments can still support apparent transductive
improvement while inducing a direction that fails on unseen data. The
two-alignment view separates corruption of the verifier-induced gradient
from non-transferability of the correct learning direction.

What counts as successful self-improvement? Improvement on prompts
participating in the self-training loop is not, by itself, evidence of
transferable progress: once those prompts generate responses that affect
model parameters, they become part of the learning process even without
observed ground-truth labels. Performance can therefore improve
transductively while degrading on genuinely unseen data.

The two-alignment view separates two causes. High gradient fidelity
cannot compensate for a learning direction that does not transfer, while
high transferability cannot protect against a verifier that rotates an
otherwise useful update. The Safe-Transfer Margin captures this
interaction as a conservative local certificate rather than a universal
predictor. Consistent with this view, MMMU is primarily
transfer-limited: even Correct-pref. supervision transfers weakly, while
AAG mainly removes additional verifier-induced damage.

Limitations and open questions. Our analysis is first-order and does not
capture full nonlinear training dynamics. AAG assumes that verifier
confidence preserves useful ordering of chosen-response correctness,
which stronger distribution shift may violate. Due to limited GPU
availability, we run multi-seed experiments only for Qwen3-VL-4B;
Qwen2.5-VL-7B and Qwen3-VL-8B use a single reported seed after
preliminary seed checks showed consistent results. G3.1 and G2.5 are
closed-source APIs whose behavior may change across versions. Extending
the analysis to online, agentic, open-ended, and multi-round settings
remains future work.

Conclusion. We identify *silent negative transfer*: a verifier that
produces genuine held-out gains on one task can become a harmful
optimization signal on another while self-training still appears
successful. Reliable verifier-guided learning requires both a faithful
update direction and one that transfers to unseen data. This view
explains preference reversal as a directional optimization error and
motivates AAG as a precision-first mechanism for mitigating
verifier-induced negative transfer.

More broadly, once verifier judgments enter an optimization loop,
evaluation errors become gradient errors. A good verifier is therefore
insufficient unless the learning direction it induces is both faithful
and transferable.

### A.5 Noise-robust and soft-selection baselines

We compare AAG with alternatives that address preference noise without
hard gating. For robust DPO (rDPO; [Chowdhury et al.,
2024](#bib.bib20)), we sweep the flip-rate parameter
$`\epsilon\in\{.1,.2,.3,.4\}`$ on a training-only calibration split and
use $`\epsilon=.1`$ for the final runs. Confidence-weighted DPO
minimizes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{wDPO}}=\frac{\sum_{z\in\mathcal{B}}s_{V}(x,y^{+})\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(z)}{\sum_{z\in\mathcal{B}}s_{V}(x,y^{+})},
``` |  | (23) |

so confidence changes each pair’s relative contribution without changing
the minibatch loss scale. The dual-threshold baseline retains pairs with
high chosen confidence and low rejected confidence, with thresholds set
to match AAG’s retained-pair count. *Correct-pref.-AAG-subset* uses the
same AAG-selected prompt groups but applies the ground-truth ordering
rule, isolating selection from preference-label quality.

| Method                    | Held-out accuracy |
|---------------------------|-------------------|
| Frozen                    | .514              |
| Raw DPO                   | $`.505\pm.006`$   |
| Correct-pref. (all pairs) | $`.512\pm.006`$   |
| rDPO                      | $`.520\pm.006`$   |
| Confidence-weighted DPO   | $`.524\pm.007`$   |
| Dual-threshold DPO        | $`.527\pm.006`$   |
| AAG-SFT                   | $`.531\pm.007`$   |
| AAG-DPO                   | $`.540\pm.008`$   |
| Correct-pref.-AAG-subset  | $`.548\pm.007`$   |

Table 8: Three-seed held-out MMMU Qwen3-VL-4B/G3.1 ablations. Values are
mean $`\pm`$ standard deviation across training seeds.

The observed pattern separates two effects. Correct-pref. over all pairs
can transfer less well than AAG because AAG also changes the training
subset; on the identical AAG-selected subset, ground-truth ordering
recovers the strongest result. The observed pattern is monotonic in
supervision quality. Raw DPO regresses below the frozen learner, while
noise-robust or confidence-aware variants recover above frozen: rDPO
reaches $`.520`$, confidence-weighted DPO $`.524`$, and the
dual-threshold rule $`.527`$. AAG-SFT reaches $`.531`$, showing that
selection alone is useful, while AAG-DPO attains the strongest
label-free point estimate at $`.540`$. On the identical AAG-selected
subset, replacing verifier ordering with ground-truth ordering further
increases accuracy to $`.548`$, isolating the remaining preference-label
error.

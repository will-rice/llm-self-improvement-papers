# CBBQ: A Chinese Bias Benchmark Dataset Curated with Human-AI Collaboration for Large Language Models

Yufei Huang Affiliation: Tianjin University
Email: [yuki_731@tju.edu.cn](mailto:)    Deyi Xiong Affiliation: Tianjin
University Email: [dyxiong@tju.edu.cn](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Holistically measuring societal biases of large language models is
crucial for detecting and reducing ethical risks in highly capable AI
models. In this work, we present a Chinese Bias Benchmark dataset that
consists of over 100K questions jointly constructed by human experts and
generative language models, covering stereotypes and societal biases in
14 social dimensions related to Chinese culture and values. The curation
process contains 4 essential steps: bias identification via extensive
literature review, ambiguous context generation, AI-assisted
disambiguous context generation, snd manual review & recomposition. The
testing instances in the dataset are automatically derived from 3K+
high-quality templates manually authored with stringent quality control.
The dataset exhibits wide coverage and high diversity. Extensive
experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the dataset in detecting
model bias, with all 10 publicly available Chinese large language models
exhibiting strong bias in certain categories. Additionally, we observe
from our experiments that fine-tuned models could, to a certain extent,
heed instructions and avoid generating outputs that are morally harmful
in some types, in the way of "moral self-correction". Our dataset and
results are publicly available at
[https://github.com/YFHuangxxxx/CBBQ](https://github.com/YFHuangxxxx/CBBQ),
offering debiasing research opportunities to a widened community.

## 1 Introduction

"Bias and impartiality is in the eye of the beholder."(Samuel Johnson).
How about large language models (LLM) trained from human data? Many
studies have revealed that LLMs also exhibit harmful societal
biases \[[1](#bib.bib1), [3](#bib.bib3), [4](#bib.bib4),
[16](#bib.bib16), [24](#bib.bib24), [13](#bib.bib13), [5](#bib.bib5),
[8](#bib.bib8), [30](#bib.bib30)\], which is even getting worse for
larger models \[[2](#bib.bib2), [10](#bib.bib10), [12](#bib.bib12),
[22](#bib.bib22), [28](#bib.bib28)\]. In the context of AI fairness, The
term "bias" refers to the harm that occurs when a system reinforces the
subordinate status of certain groups along the lines of identity, and
can be quantified through certain metrics \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]. In this
study, our methodology follows this concept, focusing on stereotyping
behavior and discrimination. The existence of stereotypes and
discrimination may harm marginalized or vulnerable individuals and
groups, thereby affecting the safety and deployment of large language
models in real-world applications.

We assert the critical importance of gaining a comprehensive
understanding of the ways in which societal biases manifest in natural
language generation (NLG), particularly as these applications engage
with users across various domains, such as chat bots for health,
education, and personal assistant. In order to curate a dataset for
measuring bias in LLM-driven NLG, we draw upon the design of BBQ, a bias
benchmark QA dataset proposed by [Parrish et al. \[21\]](#bib.bib21),
for the following reasons. (i) Rational Dataset Design: They transformed
bias evaluation into a multiple-choice task to measure model’s group
preferences, which correlate with the model’s likelihood of associating
answer options with either positive or negative stereotypes. To
understand when biases in LLM may manifest, they distinguish ambiguous
contexts (lacking sufficient information) from disambiguous contexts
(providing sufficient information), each paired with two bias-related
questions probing harmful and non-harmful stereotypes. (ii) Automated
Dataset Generation: The BBQ dataset \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] employs
human-written templates, which are then used to generate a large volume
of data automatically. This approach has proven effective in our
subsequent experiments.

Nevertheless, the BBQ dataset \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] could benefit from a
further development if we take a broad perspective of culture and
diversity. Firstly, its focus on English within one cultural context
limits its effectiveness for evaluating biases in diverse cultures and
languages. Direct translation doesn’t adequately capture cultural
differences. Secondly, the initial design of the BBQ
dataset \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] was to measure the degree of bias in QA
systems, however, large language models possess powerful interpretive
capabilities and behavioral inconsistencies, thus necessitating specific
evaluations for their biases. Furthermore, the assumption that alignment
of model answers with societal biases indicates intrinsic model bias is
debatable, as this could also reflect model comprehension and reasoning.
Lastly, the manual creation of templates in BBQ requires considerable
resources and may lack the quantity, diversity, and creativity needed
for comprehensive bias evaluation.

In light of these issues, we propose a Chinese Bias Benchmark dataset
curated with Human-AI Collaboration (CBBQ) for measuring bias in Chinese
LLMs, which introduces several improvements. Our key contributions are
as follows:

- •
  CBBQ is rooted in the Chinese social and cultural context, with a
  broader coverage on bias categories. CBBQ covers a wider range of bias
  categories and socially prevalent stereotypes in Chinese society,
  shown in
  Table [1](#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ CBBQ: A Chinese Bias Benchmark Dataset Curated with Human-AI Collaboration for Large Language Models").
  We would like this benchmark, which contains over 110K examples, to
  serve as an effective and comprehensive testbed, contributing to
  pre-deployment testing for Chinese or multilingual large language
  models.
- •
  CBBQ is curated with a revised dataset design and evaluation method,
  which is better suited for LLMs bias evaluation in comparison to BBQ.
  In our design of disambiguated contexts, we only supplement with
  background information that contradicts societal biases. Accordingly,
  taking into account the powerful interpretive capabilities and
  behavioural inconsistencies of LLMs, we have revised the bias score
  evaluation process. During the evaluation process, we ask the model to
  output both the explanation and choice of answer simultaneously. Only
  when both the explanation and the selected answer of the model are
  unbiased, do we consider it as unbiased. Additionally, we have also
  provided our prompts for the evaluation of the dataset, thus offering
  a comprehensive evaluation methodology for LLMs.
- •
  CBBQ is fully leveraging the generation capability of LLMs to increase
  data diversity. We allow humans and models to collaborate in designing
  disambiguated contexts, fully exploring the generation capability of
  LLMs. This approach not only saves time and effort, but also enhances
  the diversity and creativity of the curated data.

With CBBQ, we have conducted extensive experiments on multiple LLMs
under three types of prompts. In our exploration, we observe distinct
behaviors among different models. GPT-3.5-turbo¹¹ 1
https://openai.com/product achieves the lowest bias scores, while
Chinese LLMs evaluated in our experiments generally obtain bias scores
exceeding 50%, highlighting their susceptibility to bias. Notably, in
categories such as educational qualification, disease, disability, and
physical appearance, the bias scores of evaluated models are markedly
higher than those in religious and sexual orientation categories.
Additionally, our study further discloses that models trained via
SFT/RLHF have a certain capacity of moral
self-correction \[[11](#bib.bib11)\], offering more possibilities for
exploring methods of debiasing. We will publicly release CBBQ to promote
the development of unbiased LLMs.

## 2 Related Work

The presence of bias in the hidden representations or embeddings of a
model does not necessarily indicate that the model will produce biased
outputs \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. To understand when a model’s outputs may
exhibit and reinforce biases, we need to examine how these biases
manifest in downstream tasks.

In sentence completion, \[[7](#bib.bib7)\] introduces the Bias in
Open-Ended Language Generation Dataset (BOLD), a large-scale dataset
that consists of 23,679 English text generation prompts for bias
benchmarking across five domains. Their findings reveal that most large
language models display higher societal biases in all domains compared
to human-written Wikipedia texts.

In machine translation, existing works on biases in machine translation
have almost exclusively focused on issues of gender biases.
\[[29](#bib.bib29)\] presents the first challenge set and evaluation
protocol for the analysis of gender bias in machine translation for 8
languages, referred to as WinoMT Chanllege Set. \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]
releases an evaluation scheme and dataset for measuring the ability of
transformer-based NMT models to translate gender morphology correctly in
unambiguous contexts. This dataset is translated from English to 20
languages.

In dialogue generation, \[[18](#bib.bib18)\] conducts pioneering
research on fairness issues in dialogue systems. They construct a
benchmark dataset and propose quantitative measures to understand
fairness (We refer to this dataset as TFDS in Table
 [1](#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ CBBQ: A Chinese Bias Benchmark Dataset Curated with Human-AI Collaboration for Large Language Models").),
the results show that popular dialogue models exhibit significant biases
towards different genders and races. Nowadays, dialogue systems often
adopt personas mimicking societal demographics. Therefore, in the work
of \[[26](#bib.bib26)\], they present the first large-scale study on
persona biases in dialogue systems. \[[27](#bib.bib27)\] emphasizes the
importance to study ad hominems in dialogue responses. They compose an
annotated dataset, and build a classifier to analyze human and dialogue
system responses to English Twitter posts.

In question answering, one of challenges in directly assessing bias in
model outputs is the difficulty in finding a fair and automatic
evaluation method. Most of the existing methods rely on human judgement.
UnQover \[[17](#bib.bib17)\] addresses this issue by transforming the
generation task into a multiple-choice task. They utilize unspecified
questions, which couldn’t be answered based on the given context.
However, their evaluation is based on the likelihood allocated to two
incorrect options, while BBQ \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] always provides the
model with a correct answer, measuring the proportion of times the model
chooses this correct answer.

Our CBBQ dataset expands on the concept established by the BBQ
dataset \[[21](#bib.bib21)\], improving and adapting it to more
effectively evaluate large language models in the real-world scenarios.
Uniquely, the CBBQ dataset is grounded in the socio-cultural context of
China, covering a wider range of bias categories and stereotypes
prevalent in Chinese society than BBQ. Secondly, our dataset is a
collaborative product of humans and a generative AI model, improving
diversity and creativity in the process. Finally, CBBQ is tailored for
evaluating large language models. We’ve innovatively designed our
dataset, corresponding metrics and experiment setup for evaluation, as
opposed to the BBQ dataset \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] which is originally
developed for traditional dialogue models. These factors set our work
apart, making it a more comprehensive and appropriate benchmark for
large language models in the Chinese setting.

### 3.1 Bias Identification

Our goal is to create a bias dataset that aligns with Chinese culture.

To achieve this goal, first, we identify types of biases that are
prevalent. The categories of disability, disease, ethnicity, gender,
household registration, race, and religious are derived from the
protected employment categories as defined in China’s "Employment
Promotion Law". Age, educational qualification, nationality, physical
appearance, region, socio-economic status, and sexual orientation, on
the other hand, originate from target groups and categories seen in news
articles and public discussions about stereotypes and biases on social
media platforms. We investigate and collect them on Weibo and Zhihu, two
social media platforms with significant influence in China.

Next, we delve into detailed research to identify specific biases or
stereotypes within these categories that could potentially harm certain
groups and society. To accomplish this, we choose the widely used and
recognized Chinese knowledge resource, CNKI²² 2 https://www.cnki.net/,
as our literature source for research. CNKI is a digital library that
amalgamates resources like journals, doctoral theses, master’s theses,
conference papers, and books. More crucially, it encompasses 168
professional disciplines and refreshes its literature collection with
over 50,000 articles daily. As a result, it provides us with the
multi-disciplinary crossover of professional research and knowledge
about bias that we need. Leveraging the robust retrieval capability of
CNKI, we use specific bias categories as keywords to retrieve research
articles that explicitly discuss the identified biases. Table
[4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.1 Data Statistics ‣ 4 The Dataset ‣ CBBQ: A Chinese Bias Benchmark Dataset Curated with Human-AI Collaboration for Large Language Models")
lists the number of related research articles retrieved from CNKI for
each category, with gender and race research articles being especially
abundant. Despite containing a vast amount of duplicate content, we
mainly focus on articles that have conducted qualitative or quantitative
studies on specific biases. On average, 63 literature sources are
referenced for each bias category, with gender reaching up to 200.

After downloading and extensively reviewing these articles, we manually
select one or several biases described in each article, recording the
targeted groups as well as the specific attributes or events associated
with these biases. Examples of specific attributes include
"Northeasterners are violent" in region bias, and examples of specific
events include "A woman was rejected for a job due to her reproductive
status" in gender bias. For categories with fewer retrieved research
articles, our evidence collection also includes news facts demonstrating
harm to certain groups, as well as highly endorsed comments under
popular discussions on stereotypes and biases found on Weibo and Zhihu.
All these reference sources of evidence are documented in the "source"
field of our dataset.

### 3.2 Ambiguous Context Generation

We construct templates comprising two questions, answer choices, an
ambiguous context that lacks information needed to answer the questions,
and a disambiguous context that provides this required information. Our
aim is to measure when biased model outputs are likely to manifest. If
we set up only an ambiguous context, it may be challenging to
distinguish between the model’s bias behavior and its misunderstanding
of the context. For instance, if the model gives a biased answer in an
ambiguous context, it might be unclear whether this is due to bias or a
misinterpretation of the context’s information. By having a disambiguous
context, we introduce a clear control group to assess the model’s
behavior. This also mirrors real-world scenarios where models may
encounter various types of context, enhancing the relevance of our
evaluation approach.

##### Ambiguous Context

All templates for ambiguous context are manually designed by humans. The
purpose of ambiguous contexts is to (i) introduce the real-world
scenario and the two groups involved in the question and (ii) evaluate
the model’s behavior when the model lacks sufficient information to
determine the answer to a question. In all ambiguous contexts, the
correct answer is the "unknown" option. For example, as shown in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Dataset Curation ‣ CBBQ: A Chinese Bias Benchmark Dataset Curated with Human-AI Collaboration for Large Language Models"),
we might write: "There are two new transfer students in the class, one
boy and one girl." Although LLMs can also be used to assist in this step
by providing an instruction such as "It is commonly believed that boys
perform better than girls in science. Please create some scenarios
related to this potential bias," we find that the generated scenarios
often had similarities. To ensure diversity and better reflect real-life
situations, we opt for manual creation.

##### Proper Names

In the BBQ dataset \[[21](#bib.bib21)\], American proper names are
employed in templates within gender and race/ethnicity categories to
embody stereotypes associated with specific racial or ethnic identities,
such as using "Donna Schneider" to represent a white woman. However, in
our templates, we abstain from using any proper names. Instead, we just
opt for the explicit labels of bias targets to represent different
groups. For instance, we might refer to "the white woman" or "a white
woman" but not specific names. We believe that these proper names serve
as imperfect proxies when evaluating large language models. There’s a
potential risk that if the model is trained on a dataset with a limited
set of specific proper names, it might overfit to these names and
perform poorly when encountering unfamiliar names.

##### Lexical Diversity

In evaluating large language models, it is crucial to consider the
model’s sensitivity to varied inputs. Thus, ensuring the use of a
diverse vocabulary in NAMES (a slot for a biased target group to be
filled) is imperative. For instance, when describing appearances, we
encounter a plethora of adjectives such as "outstanding-looking," "very
beautiful," "better temperament," "very elegant," or "exquisitely
refined." Another example is the use of "63 year olds", "65 year olds",
"68 year olds", "70 year olds" and so on in the age category to
represent "older people". By integrating these variances, we cater to
the model’s sensitivity, enriching our dataset with a broad range of
expressions and terminologies, thereby enabling a more comprehensive
evaluation.

### 3.3 Disambiguous Context Generation

##### Disambiguous Context

All templates for disambiguous contexts are primarily generated by a
generative AI model. A disambiguous context supplements an ambiguous
context by providing additional information about the attributes of the
two groups involved, enabling the model to answer negative/non-negative
questions not with "UNKNOWN" answers. This is similar to a continuation
generating task, so we primarily utilize GPT-4 \[[20](#bib.bib20)\] as
the generative AI model for generating disambiguous contexts. In our
approach, we first provide an instruction, such as "Please expand the
following text.". The instruction can vary, like "Please provide reasons
for the following statement.". And we input the ambiguous context
obtained in the second step and the properties obtained in the first
step. It’s important to note that in this case, the properties are
contrary to societal biases. For example, if a societal bias states
"Boys are better at math than girls.", we reverse it and splice it
behind an ambiguous context–"Two transfer students joined the class, one
boy and one girl," as shown in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Dataset Curation ‣ CBBQ: A Chinese Bias Benchmark Dataset Curated with Human-AI Collaboration for Large Language Models").
Finally the disambiguous context we get is "In a math class, the teacher
asked a question, and the girl quickly stood up to answer, while the boy
remained silent, indicating he didn’t know the answer."

##### Questions

We also match each context with a multiple-choice question and three
possible answers in this step. For the example in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Dataset Curation ‣ CBBQ: A Chinese Bias Benchmark Dataset Curated with Human-AI Collaboration for Large Language Models"),
the corresponding question is: “Who is bad at math?” The possible
answers are: “The boy”, “The girl”, or “Unknown”. In ambiguous context,
there is not enough information to answer the question, so the correct
answer is “Unknown”. However, if a model were to rely on a stereotype
regarding gender identity, it might answer “The girl”. And in
disambiguous context, the correct answer is present as "The boy",
allowing us to test when the biases that we already know are present in
the model override the correct answer. And each example appears with two
questions that reflect a negative or harmful bias: one question is for
the target in the sense of a harmful stereotype (e.g., “Who is
violent?”), while the other is for the non-targeted entity (e.g., “Who
is not violent?”). It is worth emphasizing that our questions may
include words indicating possibility, such as "likely," "more likely,"
which allows for some degree of fuzzy or uncertain in practice.

##### Balanced Templates

We also perform order swapping between targeted and non-targeted words
for each example. For example, if a template generates an example whose
subject is "a Chongqing person and a Chengdu person", then another
example generated by the same template could be "a Chengdu person and a
Chongqing person". Hence, each context+question combination yields at
least 8 variations: negative and non-negative questions, ambiguous and
disambiguous context, with targeted words first and targeted words last.

### 3.4 Manual Review and Recomposition

After obtaining outputs from the generative AI model, we manually review
and select the generated outputs that meet the required criteria. We
make necessary modifications to the selected outputs and add them to the
templates gallery. If none of the generated outputs is deemed suitable,
we choose to regenerate. Generally, we estimate that approximately 95%
of the generated outputs only require minor modifications to align with
the requirements of human review.

### 3.5 Quality Control

Considering the importance of a rigorous review process for our
collaboratively generated data, we engage two expert reviewers with
diverse backgrounds in social sciences to evaluate all 3,090 templates.
Our expert reviewers have sociological and cross-cultural research
background, endowing them with a comprehensive and in-depth
understanding of societal biases in various bias contexts. They evaluate
each template from several perspectives, including the presence and
widespread recognition of bias, the reasonableness of the context, the
appropriateness of the compared groups in the context, and whether the
questions reflect the presence or absence of negative bias. Review
questions for the two expert reviewers and reiew results are listed in
Table

### 4.2 Coverage

Our dataset covers fourteen types of societal biases shown in Table
[4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.1 Data Statistics ‣ 4 The Dataset ‣ CBBQ: A Chinese Bias Benchmark Dataset Curated with Human-AI Collaboration for Large Language Models"),
including 5 additional bias categories that are not present in
BBQ \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. These 5 types of bias are prevalent, harmful,
and against Chinese cultural value. It is important to note that within
the disease category, a distinction is made between mental disabilities
and mental illnesses based on the possibility of being cured in medical
terms. Therefore, we use the prefixes "mild" and "severe" respectively
to differentiate them. "Mild mental disorders", "mild depression" are
classified into the disease category, while conditions with "severe"
prefix are classified into the disability category. And to avoid overlap
with region bias and the household_registration bias category, we use
terms like "rural registration," "non-rural registration," "local
registration," and "non-local registration" instead of specific
geographic locations like "Beijing registration" or "Shanghai
registration."

## 5 Experiments

We evaluated a series of open-source state-of-the-art Chinese LLMs on
the curated bias dataset with the defined bias evaluation metric,
showing the biase degree of current Chinese LLMs.

https://huggingface.co/BelleGroup/BELLE-7B-0.2M and BELLE-7B-2M⁶⁶ 6

memory, adhering strictly to the official documentation and recommended
testing protocols of each model.

### 5.2 Bias Evaluation Metric

Evaluating the bias of large language models, especially when their
outputs are complex, is a nuanced and intricate task. Although accuracy
is a widely used metric, it may not be sufficient to quantify the
performance of a model in handling societal biases. This is because we
are trying to capture the model’s "biased" tendency when answering
questions, not just whether the model’s answer is correct.

First, the bias score we calculate should align with societal biases,
specifically including answers with biased targets in negative contexts
and non-targets in non-negative contexts, instead of simply counting
non-UNKNOWN answers across all situations. Second, bias can be present
not only in the model’s incorrect answers, but also potentially in its
correct responses. Therefore, to detect the model’s underlying bias more
comprehensively, we require the model to provide both an answer and an
explanation for its choice. We consider the output to be unbiased only
if there is no bias evident in both the answer and its explanation. A
special case may arise where the model chooses answer A, but the
explanation points to B. This could suggest a problem with the model’s
understanding of the task or its coherence in generating the answer. In
these circumstances, we consider these outputs as invalid and exclude
such cases from our bias score calculation.

Therefore, we introduce a bias score to quantify the extent to which a
model systematically answers questions with biased responses. We combine
the bias scores from ambiguous and disambiguous contexts, assigning
different weights to account for the varying impact of biased answers in
these contexts. The bias score reflects the percentage of non-unknown
outputs that align with societal biases. A bias score of 0 indicates no
measured bias in the model, while 1 indicates complete alignment with
the target societal biases.

The calculation of the bias score in ambiguous contexts, $`S_{amb}`$ is
as follows:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
S_{\mathrm{amb}}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{biased\_ans}}}{n_{\mathrm{examples}}}
``` |  | (1) |

where $`n_{\mathrm{biased\_ans}}`$ represents the number of model
outputs that reflect the target societal bias, including biased targets
in negative contexts (e.g., answering "girls" to the question "Who is
bad at math?") and non-targets in non-negative contexts (e.g., answering
"boys" to the question "Who is good at math?").

The calculation of the bias score in disambiguous contexts,
$`S_{\mathrm{disamb}}`$ is defined as follows:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{\mathrm{disamb}}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{biased\_ans}}}{n_{\mathrm{non\_UNKNOWN\_outputs}}}
``` |  | (2) |

In disambiguous contexts, since the correct answer always belongs to one
of the two groups, the denominator is adjusted compared to ambiguous
contexts. $`n_{\mathrm{non\_UNKNOWN\_outputs}}`$ represents the total
number of model outputs that do not belong to the UNKNOWN category. This
is done because in disambiguous context, we have provided sufficient
information, if the model chooses an "UNKNOWN" answer in this context,
it may be due to an insufficient understanding of the context, rather
than the presence or absence of any bias. Therefore, in order to
accurately calculate the probability of the model making choices due to
inherent bias, we need to exclude these "UNKNOWN" outputs.

Total Bias Score:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{\mathrm{total}}=w_{1}\times S_{\mathrm{amb}}+w_{2}\times S_{\mathrm{disamb}}
``` |  | (3) |

We assign weights of $`w_{1}`$ and $`w_{2}`$ to the two scenarios
because we consider biased responses that persist even after
supplementing disambiguous contexts with facts contradicting societal
biases to be more harmful. Hence, we suggest to assign $`w_{2}`$ with a
higher value than $`w_{1}`$. In our experiments, we set $`w_{1}`$ to 0.4
and $`w_{2}`$ to 0.6.

### 5.4 Results

Throughout the course of our experiments, we observed that even minor
alterations between two similar inputs could potentially lead to
different results. Furthermore, repeating the same input multiple times
could also yield varying outcomes. This reinforces the effectiveness of
our methodology, which involves generating large amounts of testing
instances from a few templates. We need to pay attention to diversity in
syntax and rhetorical combinations even in minute details. This would
provide a more comprehensive assessment, allowing us to better
understand under what circumstances a model’s bias is most likely to
manifest.

## 6 Conclusion

By introducing CBBQ, a publicly available dataset encompassing over 100K
text entries covering 14 bias categories, we have significantly expanded
the scope of openly accessible Chinese datasets intended for evaluating
bias in state-of-the-art large language models. In creating this
dataset, we leverage the collaborative efforts of a generative AI model
(i.e., GPT-4) and human expertise, leading to substantial savings in
time and cost. Experiments on Chinese open-source LLMs that are either
only pretrained or pretrained $`+`$ fine-tuned verify the utility of the
proposed CBBQ. Experiment results demonstrate that these LLMs have a
high degree of bias in both ambiguous- and disambiguous-context
settings. Even for GPT-3.5-turbo, it exhibits pronounced bias in 5 bias
categories that are common in the context of Chinese culture, despite
its superiority over all other LLMs being evaluated in all bias
categories. Our dataset offers opportunities for debiasing research at
both the pre-training and fine-tuning stages of large language models
for a wide research community. Given that we employ templates to
generate instances, we plan to further expand and diversify our dataset
in the future along with the development of LLMs.

## 7 Limitations and Future Work

Continuous Dataset Development. Our dataset is in a continuous stage of
development and evolution. This entails the inclusion of more
categories, scenarios, templates, and diversified vocabulary to enrich
its contents and usefulness.

Prompt Engineering. The creation of our dataset and the conduction of
our experiments both rely on the crafting of suitable prompts for each
model. It’s noteworthy that minor variations in the prompts can
sometimes lead to significant changes in the model’s output. We haven’t
systematically tested this aspect in our current experiments.

Enhanced Review Process. There is a need for a more stringent and
professional review process. In the future, we can set up multi-tiered
reviews, involving a greater number of experts, or even advanced AI
language models like GPT-4. Involving AI language models in more steps
embodies our initial vision of AI-assisted debiasing research.

Absence of Intersectional Bias. Our current work does not include
intersectional biases, such as gender by age,disease by gender, and
socioeconomic status by race. We analyze such subsets separately from
other categories because the construction of non-target and target
identities requires some changes.

Future Bias Mitigation Techniques. We have demonstrated that models do
indeed possess a capacity for moral self-correction. Moving forward, we
could potentially embed instructions to avoid harmful outputs during the
pre-training phase of the models to circumvent the emergence of bias in
subsequent stages. Nonetheless, there are numerous methods to prevent
the manifestation of bias, which will be the focus of our future
research.

## 8 Ethical Considerations

CBBQ serves as a tool for researchers to measure societal biases in
large language models when used in the downstream tasks, but it also
presents ethical risks. The categories included in CBBQ primarily focus
on the current Chinese cultural context and do not encompass all
possible societal biases. Therefore, achieving a low bias score on CBBQ
for a large language model that might be deployed in different fields
does not necessarily indicate the safety of the model’s deployment. We
aim to mitigate this risk by explicitly stating in all dataset releases
that such conclusions would be fallacious.
````

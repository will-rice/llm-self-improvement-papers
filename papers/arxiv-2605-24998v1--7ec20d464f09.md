###### Abstract

Self-improvement training enables the large reasoning models (LRMs) to
improve themselves by self-generating reasoning trajectories as training
data without external supervision. However, we find that this method
often falls short in complex reasoning tasks and even leads to model
collapse. Through a series of preliminary analyses, we reveal two
problems: (1) data imbalance, where most training samples are simple,
but the challenging yet crucial samples are scarce; (2) overthinking,
where many undesired samples with redundant reasoning steps are used for
self-training. To this end, we propose HSIR, which effectively Harnesses
Self-Improvement in large Reasoning models via two simple-yet-effective
approaches. Specifically, HSIR introduces a verify-then-exit sampling
strategy to mitigate data imbalance by efficiently collecting more
accurate solutions for difficult queries, and designs an Intrinsic
Diversity score to quantify overthinking and filter out the undesired
solutions. We apply HSIR to various post-training paradigms, among which
we further propose H-GRPO, an enhanced GRPO algorithm that leverages the
intrinsic diversity as an external reward to encourage concise and
diverse reasoning via reinforcement learning. Extensive results show
that HSIR not only effectively enhances the reasoning performance, i.e.,
bringing up to +10.9% average performance gains, but also significantly
improves the reasoning efficiency by reducing up to 42.4% relative
inference overhead.

## 1 Introduction

Recently, post-training the large language models (LLMs) with explicit
long chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning trajectories has garnered
significant attention ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib56); [Plaat et al.,
2024](#bib.bib57); [Xu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib60)). Owing to the scaling
inference compute of long-CoT reasoning, large reasoning models (LRMs)
can unleash their reasoning capabilities and achieve better performance
in various reasoning tasks ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib53); [Chen et
al., 2024c](#bib.bib64)). However, the performance of LRMs highly relies
on high-quality intermediate reasoning trajectories ([Yang et al.,
2025c](#bib.bib37)), which are usually costly and time-consuming to
obtain ([Peng et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)). In response to this issue, the
“self-improvement” paradigm has emerged, i.e., models iteratively
improve themselves by using the self-generated reasoning trajectories as
training data, thereby reducing their dependence on external
supervision.

Figure 1: Performance comparison of Qwen2.5-1.5B using different
self-improvement training methods on the MedQA task.

In the context of LRMs, several self-improvement approaches have been
proposed ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib1); [Singh et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2)), which boost LRMs’ performance on conventional
reasoning tasks effectively. However, in our preliminary experiments
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")),
we found that they often fall short in complex reasoning tasks, e.g.,
medical question-answering ([Huang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib63)). More
seriously, they might suffer from model collapse, where models’
performance degrades due to iterative self-training on model-generated
data ([Bertrand et al., 2024](#bib.bib58)). Through a series of analyses
(§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Empirical Analyses ‣ 2 Rethinking Self-improvement in LRMs ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")),
we reveal that these methods have two major shortcomings: ❶ data
imbalance, i.e., most training samples are simple, whereas challenging
yet crucial samples are scarce; ❷ overthinking, i.e., many undesired
solutions with redundant and repetitive reasoning steps are used for
self-training. Several prior studies also recognize these shortcomings
and attempt to address them. An intuitive way to mitigate data imbalance
is to allocate more trials to difficult queries ([Tong et al.,
2024](#bib.bib65); [Ding et al., 2025](#bib.bib66)). Although effective,
it relies on increased inference overhead to obtain new solutions, while
overlooking the potential value of prior failed attempts. On the other
hand, to alleviate overthinking, the key lies in quantifying the
reasoning redundancy. Most existing works achieve this by designing
length-oriented metrics ([Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib67); [Munkhbat et
al., 2025](#bib.bib68)). While achieving remarkable efficiency, overly
emphasizing length reduction may hinder models’ deep reasoning and lead
to performance degradation ([Dai et al., 2025b](#bib.bib62)). Thus,
there arises a question: can we explore a more effective
self-improvement training method to make LRMs both better and faster?

To achieve this goal, we propose HSIR, which effectively Harnesses
Self-Improvement in large Reasoning models via two simple-yet-effective
approaches. First, to collect more correct solutions for difficult
queries, HSIR introduces a verify-then-exit (VeriExit) sampling
strategy, which verifies the correctness of intermediate reasoning steps
in prior failed solutions and self-truncates the reasoning once the
current step arrives at the ground-truth answer. The motivation of
VeriExit is that, within the failed solution, LRMs may have arrived at
the correct answer during intermediate reasoning, yet ultimately failed
to produce the accurate outcome due to reasoning deviation. Second,
motivated by the intuition that a high similarity among intermediate
reasoning steps often signals redundant or repetitive thinking, HSIR
designs an Intrinsic Diversity score (denoted as InDiv) to quantify
overthinking using the internal states of LRMs. In practice, InDiv
performs an attention-aware eigenvalue analysis on the hidden
representations of intermediate reasoning steps, where those with
smaller eigenvalues are repetitive. Overall, by efficiently collecting
more correct solutions and filtering out undesired overthinking
solutions, HSIR can ensure the diversity and conciseness of training
data, thus achieving better reasoning performance and efficiency.

We apply our HSIR to two iterative post-training paradigms: supervised
fine-tuning (SFT) and preference learning. Extensive results on seven
LLMs and five reasoning tasks show that our HSIR not only outperforms
the other counterparts by a clear margin, but also effectively improves
the reasoning efficiency. Furthermore, we expand our methods to the
Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) training paradigm,
and propose H-GRPO, an enhanced GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib53))
algorithm that leverages the InDiv scores as an external reward to
smoothly alleviate the overthinking. More comparative results prove the
superiority of H-GRPO. Additionally, more in-depth analyses prove the
effectiveness of HSIR’s important components, and indicate that HSIR
brings better model generalization. To summarize, our contributions are
three-fold: (1) We reveal two shortcomings of self-improvement in LRMs,
and propose HSIR that leverages two simple-yet-effective approaches to
alleviate them. (2) HSIR can be adopted to various post-training
paradigms. Among which, we further expand it to the RLVR training
paradigm, and propose an enhanced GRPO algorithm, H-GRPO. (3) Extensive
results show that HSIR can consistently and significantly improve the
reasoning performance and efficiency for various LLMs.

## 2 Rethinking Self-improvement in LRMs

### 2.1 Preliminaries

Considering that we have a base LLM $`\mathcal{M}_{base}`$, a small seed
dataset $`\mathcal{S}=\{(x_{i},r_{i},y_{i})\}^{N}_{i=1}`$ and a larger
answer-labeled but reasoning-trace-free dataset
$`\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}^{M}_{i=1}`$ ($`M\gg N`$), where
$`x_{i}`$ is the query, $`r_{i}=[r_{i,1},\ldots,r_{i,L}]`$ is the
corresponding reasoning trajectory with $`L`$ intermediate steps, and
$`y_{i}`$ is the ground-truth answer. We first fine-tune
$`\mathcal{M}_{base}`$ on $`\mathcal{S}`$ to make it have basic long-CoT
reasoning ability, and denote the tuned model as $`\mathcal{M}_{0}`$.
The goal of self-improvement is to enhance the reasoning performance of
$`\mathcal{M}_{0}`$ by iteratively self-training using its own solutions
on $`\mathcal{D}`$ over $`T`$ cycles. Specifically, let
$`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$ denote the model at the $`t`$-th iteration
($`t\in[1,\,T]`$), the self-improvement training process generally
involves the following steps:

Self-generation. At $`t`$-th iteration, for each query
$`x_{i}\in\mathcal{D}`$, we enforce $`\mathcal{M}_{t-1}`$ to generate
multiple solutions $`\{(\hat{r}^{k}_{i},\hat{y}^{k}_{i})\}^{K}_{k=1}`$,
where $`k\in[1,\,K]`$ and $`K`$ denotes the sampling time for each
query. By doing so, we can obtain the self-generated dataset
$`\hat{\mathcal{D}_{t}}=\{(x_{i},\hat{r}^{k}_{i},\hat{y}^{k}_{i})\,|\,k\in[1,\,K]\}`$.

Self-training. The self-training process differs across various
post-training paradigms. During SFT training, the ground-truth answer
$`y_{i}`$ is used to verify the correctness of candidate solutions
$`\{(r^{k}_{i},\hat{y}^{k}_{i})\}^{K}_{k=1}`$, where only correct
solutions with $`\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}_{i},y_{i})=1`$ are filtered to form
the pseudo-labeled dataset
$`\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{correct}_{t}=\{(x_{i},\hat{r}^{k}_{i},\hat{y}^{k}_{i})\,|\,\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}^{k}_{i},y_{i})=1\}`$.
Notably, to alleviate the model collapse problem, we follow the prior
studies ([Alemohammad et al., 2024](#bib.bib49)) and use the combination
of the original seed $`\mathcal{S}`$ and the pseudo-labeled
$`\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{correct}_{t}`$ as the final training dataset
$`\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{S}\cup\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{correct}_{t}`$.
Considering that continually fine-tuning $`\mathcal{M}_{t-1}`$ would
lead to overfitting, we fine-tune $`\mathcal{M}_{base}`$ on
$`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ to obtain the new $`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$, following
previous practice ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib1)). In particular,
we optimize $`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$ using the standard negative log
likelihood (NLL) loss function:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}_{\,\mathcal{D}_{t}}}\Big[-\text{log}\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\hat{r}^{k}_{i},\hat{y}^{k}_{i}|x_{i})}{|\hat{r}^{k}_{i}|+|\hat{y}^{k}_{i}|}\Big],
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{M}_{\theta}`$ initialized with $`\mathcal{M}_{base}`$
denotes the current tuned model that will become next model
$`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$. For the implementation of preference learning, we
utilize the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) ([Rafailov et al.,
2023](#bib.bib78)). First, for each query
$`x_{i}\in\hat{\mathcal{D}_{t}}`$, we split the candidate solutions into
two sets: winner
$`\{(x_{i},\hat{r}^{k_{w}}_{i},\hat{y}^{k_{w}}_{i})\,|\,\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}^{k_{w}}_{i},y_{i})=1\}`$
and loser
$`\{(x_{i},\hat{r}^{k_{l}}_{i},\hat{y}^{k_{l}}_{i})\,|\,\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}^{k_{l}}_{i},y_{i})=0\}`$.
Then, each winning solution and a randomly-selected losing solution are
paired to construct the preference set
$`\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{pairs}}_{t}=\{(x_{i},\hat{r}^{k_{w}}_{i},\hat{y}^{k_{w}}_{i}),(x_{i},\hat{r}^{k_{l}}_{i},\hat{y}^{k_{l}}_{i})\,|\,k_{w},k_{l}\in[1,\,K)\}`$.
Lastly, we can obtain the $`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$ by continually optimizing
$`\mathcal{M}_{t-1}`$ on $`\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{pairs}}_{t}`$.
Inspired by [Pang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib52), we employ an enhanced DPO
algorithm that combines the standard DPO loss function and NLL loss
function on winning solutions to ensure the training stability, which is
formulated as follows:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO+NLL}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}+\alpha_{nll}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}(\hat{r}^{k_{w}}_{i},\hat{y}^{k_{w}}_{i}|x_{i})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}_{\,\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{\text{pairs}}_{t}}}\Big[-\log\sigma\left(f(\hat{r}^{k_{w}}_{i},\hat{y}^{k_{w}}_{i}|x_{i})-f(\hat{r}^{k_{l}}_{i},\hat{y}^{k_{l}}_{i}|x_{i})\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\alpha_{nll}\cdot\frac{\log\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\hat{r}^{k_{w}}_{i},\hat{y}^{k_{w}}_{i}|x_{i})}{|\hat{r}^{k_{w}}_{i}|+|\hat{y}^{k_{w}}_{i}|}\Big],`$ |  | (2) |

where $`\sigma`$ is the sigmoid function,
$`f(\cdot|x_{i})=\beta\log\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\cdot|x_{i})}{\mathcal{M}_{t-1}(\cdot|x_{i})}`$,
$`\mathcal{M}_{\theta}`$ is the policy model initialized with
$`\mathcal{M}_{t-1}`$, $`\alpha_{nll}`$ and $`\beta`$ are coefficients
that are empirically set to 0.5 and 0.1.

### 2.2 Empirical Analyses

Settings. We conduct preliminary experiments by fine-tuning
Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/7B instruct models ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib50)) on
a challenging medical reasoning dataset, i.e., MedQA ([Jin et al.,
2021](#bib.bib54)). Since the original MedQA does not contain any
reasoning traces, we prompt the proprietary DeepSeek-R1 to generate the
seed reasoning data. By filtering out incorrect solutions, we ultimately
obtained a new training set containing 9.3K reasoning samples. From this
set, we randomly selected 1K as seed data $`\mathcal{S}`$, while
treating the remaining samples (without using their reasoning traces) as
unlabeled data $`\mathcal{D}`$. For self-improvement training, the total
sampling time $`K`$ is set to 10, and the number of iterations $`T`$ is
set to 1 for faster experiment validation.

Findings. Through extensive analyses on the self-generated training
samples, we empirically found that:

❶Data Imbalance:
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Left) illustrates the distribution of the number of correct solutions
in a query. As seen, there is a positive correlation between the number
of correct solutions and model capabilities, where stronger models
(e.g., Qwen2.5-7B) can collect more accurate solutions. However, for the
difficult queries that are proven to be more crucial for further
training ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)), these models still struggle to
collect sufficient correct solutions, thus leading to data imbalance.
Specifically, although for the powerful Qwen2.5-7B, there are more than
500 queries that did not obtain any correct solutions. To have a close
look, based on the number of correct solutions in a query, we evenly
split the queries into four levels.
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Middle) shows the distribution of self-generated data at different
levels, indicating that most training samples are simple, whereas
challenging yet crucial samples are scarce.

![Refer to caption](2605.24998v1/method.png)

Figure 3: (a) Pipeline of self-improvement training with HSIR. After

❷Overthinking: To verify the effect of data quality for self-training,
we first introduce a reasoning diversity metric to measure the reasoning
diversity. Specifically, as shown in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Combining H-GRPO with HSIR. ‣ Appendix B Implementation of H-GRPO ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models"),
for each reasoning trajectory $`\hat{r}^{k}_{i}`$, we first convert all
reasoning steps into sentence embeddings using the BGE-m3 model ([Chen
et al., 2024b](#bib.bib48)), and then calculate the cosine distance
between each step and its nearest neighbor in the current subset. The
reasoning steps with cosine distance below the threshold
$`\tau_{\text{sim}}`$ are filtered out. The $`\tau_{\text{sim}}`$ is
empirically set to 0.85 in our work. The metric $`\rho`$ is defined as
the ratio of unfiltered steps to all steps:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho^{k}_{i}=\frac{|\mathcal{U}(\hat{r}^{k}_{i})|}{|[\hat{r}^{k}_{i,1},\ldots,\hat{r}^{k}_{i,L}]|},\quad\rho^{k}_{i}\in(0,1]
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\mathcal{U}(\cdot)`$ denotes the set of unfiltered steps. Based
on this metric, we conduct comparative experiments by using the solution
with the highest and lowest $`\rho^{k}_{i}`$ for each query as
self-training data. For reference, we also employ a random and a
length-penalty method as baselines, i.e., using a randomly selected
solution and the shortest solution for self-training, respectively.
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Right) shows the comparative results of Qwen2.5-1.5B models on MedQA,
using the average accuracy and number of output tokens as metrics. As
seen, compared to using random solutions, self-training on solutions
with low $`\rho`$ scores indeed results in more inference overhead and
lower accuracy, while using the solutions with high $`\rho`$ scores can
effectively alleviate this problem. Notably, although self-training on
shorter solutions is also beneficial to improve reasoning efficiency, it
would lead to performance degradation. These results suggest that
self-training with redundant and repetitive reasoning steps undermines
the accuracy and conciseness of models’ reasoning, i.e., leading to
overthinking.

## 3 Methodology

Motivation and Intuition of HSIR To alleviate the above problems, we
propose HSIR that harnesses self-improvement in LRMs via two novel
approaches. First, to address data imbalance, we build upon the
insight ([Yang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib59); [Dai et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib62)) that failed solutions are not entirely incorrect but
often contain valuable, partially correct reasoning steps before
deviating. Instead of discarding these outputs, we introduce VeriExit, a
novel trajectory recycling strategy. It efficiently salvages valid
initial reasoning from failed attempts to generate correct solutions for
difficult queries. This approach provides a significant efficiency gain
over costly resampling from scratch. Second, to combat overthinking, our
empirical analysis in
§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Empirical Analyses ‣ 2 Rethinking Self-improvement in LRMs ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
validates that reasoning diversity is a potent signal, yet the
preliminary metric
(Eq. [3](#S2.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 2.2 Empirical Analyses ‣ 2 Rethinking Self-improvement in LRMs ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models"))
relies on costly external models. We propose a more elegant solution by
harnessing the dense semantic information already present in a model’s
internal states. We introduce the Intrinsic Diversity (InDiv) score, an
efficient metric that measures diversity directly from the models’
hidden representations. This makes InDiv an entirely intrinsic measure
that eliminates external dependencies and can be computed with minimal
to zero overhead.
Figure [3](#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2.2 Empirical Analyses ‣ 2 Rethinking Self-improvement in LRMs ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
illustrates the overview of our HSIR.

Verify-then-Exit Sampling. We first select the failed solutions from
$`\hat{\mathcal{D}_{t}}`$ to obtain a new set
$`\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{wrong}_{t}=\{(x_{i},\hat{r}^{k}_{i},\hat{y}^{k}_{i})\,|\,\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}^{k}_{i},y_{i})=0\}`$.
For each $`\hat{r}^{k}_{i}\in\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{wrong}_{t}`$, we verify
the correctness of intermediate reasoning steps
$`[\hat{r}^{k}_{i,1},\ldots,\hat{r}^{k}_{i,L}]`$ by determining whether
the $`\hat{r}^{k}_{i,l}`$ ($`l\in[1,L]`$) arrives at the ground-truth
answer $`y_{i}`$, e.g., explicitly mentioning “answer is
$`\{y_{i}\}`$’’¹¹ 1 We adopt a text-matching approach by default for
efficiency, and analyses of more methods can be found in
Appendix [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Analysis of VeriExit Sampling Strategy ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")..
Once $`\hat{r}^{k}_{i,l}`$ arrives at $`y_{i}`$, we truncate the
subsequent reasoning steps and insert the exit prompt
“\n\n\</think\>\n\<answer\>\n” at the truncated position. The obtained
new query is then fed into $`\mathcal{M}_{t-1}`$ to stop further
reasoning and sample the answers for $`J`$ times ($`J<K`$), where the
successful ones are merged into $`\hat{\mathcal{D}}^{correct}_{t}`$. If
the total number of candidate solutions of each $`x_{i}`$ exceeds $`K`$,
we randomly sample $`K`$ solutions to maintain consistency between the
training budgets of our method and those of vanilla self-improvement
methods. Notably, for the “absolutely hard queries” where no valid
intermediate steps exist, VeriExit silently skips them (producing no new
samples), gracefully degrading to the same behavior as the standard
self-improvement data collection method. Thus, such a sampling process
will not lead to much inference overhead, compared to resampling from
scratch.

Intrinsic Diversity Score. To quantify overthinking, we leverage the
LLMs’ internal states to measure the semantic diversity of reasoning
steps. Specifically, for each correct solution
$`(x_{i},\hat{r}^{k}_{i},\hat{y}^{k}_{i})`$, we obtain its hidden
representation $`\mathbf{H}^{k}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}`$ at the
middle layer of $`\mathcal{M}_{t-1}`$, where $`d`$ is the dimension of
hidden states and $`m`$ is the number of all tokens in the solution. We
choose the middle layer as it encodes richer and more useful semantic
information ([Skean et al., 2025](#bib.bib73); [Azaria and Mitchell,
2023](#bib.bib74)). Inspired by the fact that eigenvalues of the
covariance matrix can capture the divergence and correlation between
different embeddings ([Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib61)), we calculate
the eigenvalues of the cross-covariance for $`\mathbf{H}^{k}_{i}`$ to
measure the reasoning diversity:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{k}_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle={\mathbf{H}^{k}_{i}}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{J}_{d}\cdot\mathbf{H}^{k}_{i},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{Eig}^{k}_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\log\text{det}(\bm{\Sigma}^{k}_{i})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{u=1}^{m}\log(\lambda^{k}_{i,u}),`$ |  | (4) |

where
$`\mathbf{J}_{d}=\mathbf{I}_{d}-\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{1}_{d\times d}`$ is
the centering matrix, $`\mathbf{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ is the
identity matrix, $`\mathbf{1}_{d\times d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}`$ is
the all-one matrix, $`\text{det}(\cdot)`$ means the determinant of
matrix, and $`\{\lambda^{k}_{i,u}\}^{m}_{u=1}`$ denotes the singular
values of matrix $`\bm{\Sigma}^{k}_{i}`$. Furthermore, considering that
some important tokens with higher attention weights might contribute
more to the reasoning process, we enhance the above method via an
attention-aware weighting mechanism. In practice, let
$`Atten^{k}_{i,u}\in(0,1)`$ denote the normalized maximum self-attention
weight for $`u`$-th token ($`u\in[1,m]`$) among all self-attention
heads, we can obtain our final reasoning diversity metric, denoted as
Intrinsic Diversity score (InDiv in short) to distinguish it from
Eq. [3](#S2.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 2.2 Empirical Analyses ‣ 2 Rethinking Self-improvement in LRMs ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models"):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{Score}^{k}_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{u=1}^{m}\Big[Atten^{k}_{i,u}\cdot\log(\lambda^{k}_{i,u})\Big];\sum_{u=1}^{m}Atten^{k}_{i,u}=1`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{InDiv}^{k}_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{Score}^{k}_{i}-\text{mean}(\mathbf{Score}_{i})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{Score}_{i})}.`$ |  | (5) |

When the reasoning steps are repetitive and have similar semantics, the
hidden representations will be highly correlated, and their semantic
entropy and InDiv scores will be small (more analyses are in
Appendix [D.2](#A4.SS2 "D.2 Analysis of InDiv Score ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")).
After calculating the InDiv scores of all candidate correct solutions
for $`x_{i}`$, we filter the undesired ones with scores below the
threshold $`\tau`$. Finally, we can obtain more accurate, diverse, and
concise reasoning data for effective self-training. The pseudo-code of
HSIR is shown in
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Combining H-GRPO with HSIR. ‣ Appendix B Implementation of H-GRPO ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models"),
and the efficiency analysis of HSIR can be found in
Appendix [D.9](#A4.SS9 "D.9 Efficiency of HSIR ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models").

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Tasks and Datasets. We mainly assess the effectiveness of HSIR on
medical reasoning and mathematical reasoning tasks, using the
MedQA ([Jin et al., 2021](#bib.bib54)) and GSM8K ([Cobbe et al.,
2021](#bib.bib55)) datasets, respectively. Moreover, to verify the
universality of HSIR, we additionally conduct experiments on several
popular reasoning tasks, i.e., AI2 Reasoning Challenge’s challenge set
(ARC) ([Clark et al., 2018](#bib.bib16)) for scientific reasoning,
CommonsenseQA (CSQA) ([Talmor et al., 2019](#bib.bib14)) for commonsense
reasoning, and StrategyQA (StraQA) ([Geva et al., 2021](#bib.bib12)) for
multi-hop reasoning. For each task, we follow the settings in
§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Empirical Analyses ‣ 2 Rethinking Self-improvement in LRMs ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
and use the DeepSeek-R1 to obtain the seed reasoning data. Some training
samples are provided in
Table [9](#A3.T9 "Table 9 ‣ C.2 Training and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix C More Experimental Details ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
and [10](#A3.T10 "Table 10 ‣ C.2 Training and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix C More Experimental Details ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models").
For evaluation, we report the zero-shot results on the original test
sets of each benchmark using the average accuracy and number of
generated tokens as metrics, where the latter metric can reflect the
wall-clock inference latency.

Training Details. We conduct main experiments using
Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/7B ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib50)), Qwen3-1.7B ([Yang
et al., 2025a](#bib.bib27)), Phi-3.5-mini ([Abdin et al.,
2024](#bib.bib28)), Mistral-7B ([Jiang et al., 2023](#bib.bib29)), and
LLaMA3-8B ([Dubey et al., 2024](#bib.bib31)) instruct models. During the
implementation of HSIR, the sampling times $`K`$ and $`J`$ are set to 10
and 5, respectively. The sampling temperature is 1.0, and the maximum
output length is 2,048. The filter threshold $`\tau`$ is set to -0.5.
Notably, for the post-training of Qwen2.5 models, the self-improvement
iteration $`T`$ is set to 3, but for the other LLMs, it is set to 1 due
to limited computational resources. During inference, we use greedy
decoding with a temperature of 0 for reproducibility. The maximum output
length is set to 4,096. More data and training details are shown in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C More Experimental Details ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models").

Baselines. To verify the superiority of our HSIR, we compare it with
various training-based baseline methods:

- •
  SFT-Initial: Standard fine-tuning $`\mathcal{M}_{base}`$ on the seed
  data $`\mathcal{S}`$ to obtain the initial SFT model
  $`\mathcal{M}_{0}`$.
- •
  SFT-Oracle: Standard fine-tuning $`\mathcal{M}_{base}`$ on the
  combination of $`\mathcal{S}`$ and $`\mathcal{D}`$ with ground-truth
  reasoning data, which can be regarded as an upper-bound of SFT
  training.
- •
  STaR ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib1)): Sampling a solution using
  greedy decoding for each query $`x_{i}\in\mathcal{D}`$, where the
  correct solutions are used to iteratively fine-tune the models.
- •
  ReST^(EM) ([Singh et al., 2023](#bib.bib2)): Extending STaR by
  sampling $`K`$ solutions for each query $`x_{i}\in\mathcal{D}`$, where
  all correct solutions are used for iterative SFT training.
- •
  RFT ([Yuan et al., 2023](#bib.bib30)): Similar to ReST^(EM) but not
  iterative. To maintain consistent training budgets, we sample
  $`T\times K`$ candidate solutions for each query
  $`x_{i}\in\mathcal{D}`$.
- •
  ReGenesis ([Peng et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)): Prompting
  $`\mathcal{M}_{base}`$ to self-synthesize reasoning paths by
  converting general reasoning guidelines into task-specific ones, which
  are used for once self-training. Since it is not designed for long-CoT
  reasoning, we do not compare its reasoning efficiency.
- •
  IRPO ([Pang et al., 2024](#bib.bib52)): Sampling $`K`$ solutions for
  each query, where correct and incorrect solutions are paired to
  construct the preference data for iterative DPO training.

For all baselines, we keep a fixed data synthesis budget. Moreover,
since our goal is to propose a self-improvement training method, we do
not compare HSIR with inference-time methods in the main experiments.
More comparisons with inference-time methods are shown in
Appendix [D.8](#A4.SS8 "D.8 Comparison and Compatibility with Inference-time Methods ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models").

### 4.2 Main Results

HSIR outperforms the other baseline methods across all post-training
settings.
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
reports the comparative results (%) of Qwen2.5 family models. As seen,
self-improvement training on the relatively simple GSM8K task performs
better against the challenging MedQA task, confirming that
self-improvement methods fall short in complex reasoning tasks. More
specifically, during SFT on MedQA, STaR struggles to enhance the LRMs’
reasoning performance, and even leads to performance degradation, e.g.,
from 38.10% to 35.98% in Qwen2.5-1.5B. By sampling more diverse
solutions, RFT and ReST^(EM) alleviate this problem, indicating the
importance of self-training with diverse reasoning data. While in the
DPO phase, the effectiveness of self-improvement is more dependent on
the quality of self-generated data, as preference learning is more
data-sensitive. By selecting more diverse and concise self-generated
data for training, our HSIR can effectively unleash the reasoning
capabilities of LRMs, thus achieving better performance against the
other baselines. For instance, our HSIR-DPO outperforms the IRPO by a
clear margin, i.e., bringing +5.35% average performance gains.

HSIR effectively improves the reasoning performance and efficiency of
LRMs in both tasks. As aforementioned, we also evaluate the reasoning
efficiency of LRMs by measuring the number of output tokens. From
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models"),
it can be seen that nearly all self-improvement methods reduce the
average output tokens. We conjecture that models can sometimes generate
concise reasoning paths, which helps guide the efficient reasoning of
models. Nevertheless, as shown in our preliminary analysis
(§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Empirical Analyses ‣ 2 Rethinking Self-improvement in LRMs ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")),
self-training with overthinking solutions would damage this effect and
lead to suboptimal reasoning efficiency. Owing to our InDiv metric, we
can filter these overthinking solutions and effectively improve
reasoning efficiency by reducing up to 42.4% output tokens. These
results confirm the significance of alleviating overthinking and prove
the effectiveness of HSIR.

HSIR brings consistent and significant performance gains among all model
sizes and types.
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
presents the results of other LRMs. Notably, due to limited computation
resources, we only perform the SFT training using STaR, ReST^(EM), and
our HSIR for one iteration. As seen, HSIR continues to outperform the
other baseline methods across all models. Specifically, in LLaMA3-8B,
compared to powerful ReST^(EM), HSIR achieves +1.89% and +5.46%
performance gains for MedQA and GSM8K, respectively. Overall, HSIR
brings +5.62% average performance gains and reduces 22.4% output tokens
against the initial SFT models, showing its universality and
superiority.

Expand to GRPO Training. In addition to SFT and DPO training,
reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards via the GRPO ([Shao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib53)) algorithm is also a popular and effective way to
enhance LRMs’ reasoning performance. Instead of explicitly supervising
the reasoning trajectory, GRPO enables LRMs to learn from free
exploration via outcome rewards, e.g., binary accuracy reward. Although
effective, GRPO training also suffers from the overthinking problem. To
this end, we propose to improve the GRPO by leveraging our InDiv score
as an extra reward, and denote this method as H-GRPO. The implementation
details of H-GRPO can be found in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation of H-GRPO ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models").
Intuitively, by encouraging LRMs to generate diverse and concise
reasoning paths, H-GRPO can effectively alleviate overthinking and
result in better performance. It is noteworthy that the calculation of
InDiv scores is fast and would not lead to much training latency. We
apply our H-GRPO to reinforce the $`\mathcal{M}_{0}`$ models using the
$`\mathcal{D}`$ dataset, and report the results of Qwen2.5 family models
in
Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.3 More Analyses ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models").
Notably, our goal is not to propose a new state-of-the-art GRPO method,
but rather to examine whether our InDiv can be incorporated into GRPO to
mitigate the overthinking problem. Therefore, we only compare our H-GRPO
with two representative GRPO algorithms that also focus on the
overthinking problem: Long2Short ([Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib67)) and
CosFn ([Yang et al., 2025c](#bib.bib37)), which address overthinking by
using length-oriented reward functions. As seen, compared to the vanilla
GRPO, all improved methods achieve better reasoning efficiency,
indicating the validity of extra rewards. However, both length-oriented
methods would cause a decrease in reasoning accuracy, e.g., 1.53%
average performance drops in Qwen2.5-7B on MedQA. This indicates that
overly emphasizing length reduction might hinder LRMs’ deep reasoning
and lead to suboptimal results. Conversely, by optimizing the
intermediate reasoning process, our H-GRPO can smoothly reduce
repetitive and redundant thinking and thus achieve better performance.

strategy in HSIR as it can collect more diverse solutions for

mitigate the data imbalance problem.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we reveal and address the limitations of self-improvement
post-training in LRMs. Through a series of preliminary analyses, we find
that the self-improvement of LRMs usually suffers from data imbalance
and overthinking in complex reasoning scenarios. To address these
limitations, we propose HSIR, which effectively harnesses
self-improvement in LRMs via two simple-yet-effective approaches:
VeriExit sampling strategy and InDiv metric. Specifically, VeriExit
alleviates the data imbalance by efficiently collecting more correct
solutions for difficult queries, while InDiv quantifies the overthinking
and filters out the undesired candidate solutions. Extensive results
show that our HSIR consistently and significantly improves the reasoning
performance and efficiency across all model sizes and architectures.
Moreover, we also expand our method to the RLVR training paradigm and
propose H-GRPO that improves the GRPO by leveraging the InDiv scores as
an extra reward. Comparative results with two widely-used GRPO variants
demonstrate the superiority of our H-GRPO.

## Impact Statement

For the ethics of our work, we take ethical considerations very
seriously and strictly adhere to the ICML Ethics Policy. This paper
proposes a new self-improvement training framework to improve the
reasoning performance and efficiency of LRMs. It aims to unleash LRMs’
internal reasoning capabilities, instead of encouraging them to learn
privacy knowledge that may cause an ethical problem. Moreover, all base
models, training, and evaluation datasets used in this paper are
publicly available and have been widely adopted by researchers. Thus, we
believe that this research will not pose ethical issues. For the
potential societal consequences, we believe that there are many
potential societal consequences of our work, but none of which we feel
must be specifically highlighted here.

## Limitations

Our work has several potential limitations. First, our InDiv relies on
semantic similarity between adjacent reasoning steps to detect
overthinking. While semantically similar steps often indicate redundant
reasoning in common scenarios, this assumption does not always hold. For
instance, in rigorous mathematical derivations, consecutive steps may be
semantically similar yet logically essential, which could lead InDiv to
misjudge necessary reasoning as redundant. Second, our VeriExit is
inherently limited when confronted with “absolutely hard queries” for
which no valid intermediate steps exist. Such challenging queries
represent problems that lie beyond the model’s current capability
boundary, and addressing them remains an important direction for future
work, e.g., incorporating stronger external verifiers or
curriculum-based training strategies.

## Appendix A Related Work

Post-training for Reasoning. Recently, post-training the LLMs with
explicit reasoning paths has shown remarkable potential to unleash their
reasoning capabilities ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib56); [Plaat et al.,
2024](#bib.bib57); [Zhong et al., 2026b](#bib.bib34)). In the context of
LRMs, common post-training paradigms include SFT ([Wei et al.,
2022](#bib.bib32); [Xu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib69); [Zhong et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib35)) and preference learning ([Rafailov et al.,
2023](#bib.bib78); [Wen et al., 2025](#bib.bib72)). Specifically, SFT
encourages LLMs to mimic the reasoning behavior demonstrated in training
samples ([Xu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib69); [Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib71)), while preference learning aims to align LLMs with
human preferences for different reasoning trajectories ([Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib70); [Wen et al., 2025](#bib.bib72)). Despite their
remarkable performance, these methods are highly dependent on extensive,
high-quality reasoning trajectories. Notably, although the new emerging
RLVR training paradigm can also enhance models’ reasoning performance
without relying on reasoning trajectories ([Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib77)), cold-start training with these trajectories can
improve training efficiency and yield higher performance ([Yang et al.,
2025c](#bib.bib37)). This also underscores the importance of explicit
reasoning trajectories. Besides obtaining these trajectories from human
experts, a common alternative way is to distill the reasoning
trajectories from a larger proprietary model, e.g., DeepSeek-R1, which
is still costly and time-consuming ([Peng et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)).
More seriously, for some proprietary LLMs, their reasoning trajectories
are even unavailable due to the licensing restrictions.

Self-improvement in Large Reasoning Models. To address the above issue,
recent literature introduces the “self-improvement” paradigm, where
models improve themselves using self-generated data without any external
supervision ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib1); [Yuan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib30); [Huang et al., 2023](#bib.bib26); [Gulcehre et al.,
2023](#bib.bib76); [Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib46); [Hosseini et al.,
2024](#bib.bib41); [Wu et al., 2025](#bib.bib38); [Huang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib17); [Song et al., 2025](#bib.bib42)). For instance,
STaR ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib1)) utilizes few-shot examples to
gather self-synthesizing correct reasoning paths for SFT training, while
RFT ([Yuan et al., 2023](#bib.bib30)) and ReST^(EM) ([Singh et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2)) extend STaR by sampling multiple responses for each
question. In the preference learning phase, IRPO ([Pang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib52)) constructs preference pairs by using the
self-generated correct responses as the pair winners and the incorrect
responses as the pair losers. Similarly, [Wang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib46) show that self-training augmented by preference
learning can improve the CoT reasoning capabilities of small-scale LLMs.
However, we empirically find that these self-improvement methods usually
suffer from data imbalance and overthinking problems
(§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Empirical Analyses ‣ 2 Rethinking Self-improvement in LRMs ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")).
Some prior studies also recognize these problems and attempt to address
them by collecting more correct solutions for difficult queries ([Tong
et al., 2024](#bib.bib65); [Ding et al., 2025](#bib.bib66); [Koh et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib33); [Chen et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib36)) and designing length-oriented reward functions to
penalize too long solutions ([Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib67); [Yang et
al., 2025c](#bib.bib37); [Munkhbat et al., 2025](#bib.bib68); [Dai et
al., 2025a](#bib.bib4); [Xiao and Gan, 2026](#bib.bib3)). For instance,
to alleviate the data imbalance, [Tong et al. (2024)](#bib.bib65)
propose to allocate more trials to difficult queries, and [Ding et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib66) leverage additional signals (e.g., answers) to guide
the reasoning of LRMs. AdaSTaR ([Koh et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)) proposes
an adaptive sampling strategy to ensure data balance by prioritizing
under-trained examples. While effective, these methods overlook the
reuse of prior failed solutions and require a larger inference budget.
On the other hand, to mitigate the overthinking, S-GRPO ([Dai et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib62)) performs Early-exit Thought Rollout at different
reasoning steps to reduce redundant reasoning. Despite its
effectiveness, it introduces substantial computational overhead during
training, which is inefficient. FAST-GRPO  ([Xiao and Gan,
2026](#bib.bib3)) and GRPO-$`\lambda`$ ([Dai et al., 2025a](#bib.bib4))
design length-oriented rewards to penalize redundant reasoning, similar
to the Long2Short ([Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib67)) and CosFn ([Yang et
al., 2025c](#bib.bib37)) baselines in our experiments, which may suffer
from over-penalizing length and consequent performance degradation ([Dai
et al., 2025b](#bib.bib62)).

Different from prior studies, we propose two simple-yet-effective
approaches to address these problems efficiently. Specifically, instead
of solely allocating more trials to difficult queries, our proposed
VeriExit strategy proposes to reuse partial correct reasoning steps from
previous failed solutions to improve the sampling efficiency. It is
noteworthy that the technology of VeriExit bears some resemblance to
prior early-exit decoding methods ([Rahmath P et al., 2024](#bib.bib19);
[Yang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib59)), but the idea of reusing previous
failed solutions for efficient data synthesis is innovative. On the
other hand, to alleviate overthinking, we introduce the InDiv score that
leverages the internal state of LRMs as a signal to encourage models’
diverse and concise reasoning, rather than simply using a length
penalty. To the best of our knowledge, our InDiv is one of the first
works that use the internal states of LRMs to guide the concise
reasoning during self-improvement training.

Notably, our work differs from the concurrent work ([Zhong et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib5)) in three key aspects: 1) Different research
objectives. The concurrent work focuses on self-improvement in
multimodal LLMs and mainly addresses the visual hallucinations issue,
whereas we study self-improvement in LRMs and target the overthinking
problem specific to long-CoT reasoning. 2) Different methodologies.
Although both works involve alleviating data imbalance, the concurrent
work requires two model feed-forward passes, leading to more computation
overhead, while our method leverages a more efficient text-matching
method to identify the partially correct reasoning steps. 3) Different
experimental settings. The concurrent work evaluates on visual-oriented
tasks, whereas we conduct experiments on several text-only reasoning
tasks.

## Appendix B Implementation of H-GRPO

#### Background of GRPO and RLVR.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib53)) is a popular RL algorithm, which is widely used in
the current popular RLVR training paradigm. Formally, let
$`\mathcal{M}_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{M}_{\theta_{\text{new}}}`$ denote the reference model and
current policy model, GRPO samples a group of solutions
$`\{(\tilde{r}^{g}_{i},\tilde{y}^{g}_{i})\}^{G}_{g=1}`$ for each query
$`x_{i}\in\mathcal{D}`$, where $`G`$ denotes the number of solutions in
a group. For ease of description, we simplify the solution
$`(\tilde{r}^{g}_{i},\tilde{y}^{g}_{i})`$ as $`a^{g}_{i}`$. Then, we can
optimize the $`\mathcal{M}_{\theta_{\text{new}}}`$ by maximizing the
GRPO objective. Notably, inspired by [Yu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib23) who
use a token-level policy gradient loss to address the unhealthy increase
in response length problem of the vanilla GRPO method, we employ an
improved token-level GRPO objective function as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}&\mathcal{J}_{GRPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}{[x_{i}\in\mathcal{D},\{a^{g}_{i}\}^{G}_{g=1}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(\cdot|x_{i})]}\\
&\frac{1}{\sum_{g=1}^{G}|a^{g}_{i}|}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sum_{o=1}^{|a^{g}_{i}|}\Big(\min\left(z_{o}(a^{g}_{i}|x_{i})A_{g},\text{clip}\left(z_{o}(a^{g}_{i}|x_{i}),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)A_{g}\right)-\gamma\mathbb{D}_{KL}\left(\mathcal{M}_{\theta_{\text{new}}}||\mathcal{M}_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}\right)\Big),\end{split}
``` |  | (6) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
z_{o}(a^{g}_{i}|x_{i})=\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\theta_{\text{new}}}(a^{g}_{i,o}|x_{i},a^{g}_{i,<o})}{\mathcal{M}_{\theta_{\text{ref}}}(a^{g}_{i,o}|x_{i},a^{g}_{i,<o})},
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\epsilon`$ and $`\gamma`$ are hyper-parameters set to 0.2 and
0.04, respectively, $`\mathbb{D}_{KL}`$ is a KL penalty term, and
$`A_{g}`$ is the advantage computed as follows:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{g}=\frac{R_{g}-{\mathrm{m}ean(\{R_{1},R_{2},\cdots,R_{G}\})}}{{\mathrm{s}td(\{R_{1},R_{2},\cdots,R_{G}\})}},
``` |  | (8) |

where $`R_{g}`$ denotes the outcome reward of $`g`$-th ($`g\in[1,G]`$)
solution in the group. In the reasoning tasks that contain clear and
verifiable answers, e.g., mathematical reasoning, the reward $`R_{g}`$
mainly consists of two types of rewards:

- •
  Accuracy reward $`R^{accuracy}`$: It evaluates whether the solution is
  correct, i.e., $`\mathbb{I}(\tilde{y}^{g}_{i},y_{i})=1`$.
- •
  Format reward $`R^{format}`$: It evaluates whether the defined tags
  are present in the final solution, i.e., ‘\<think\>’ and ‘\</think\>’,
  ‘\<answer\>’ and ‘\</answer\>’ tags.

#### Reward of H-GRPO.

To alleviate the overthinking problem in the vanilla GRPO, we propose
H-GRPO, which improves the GRPO by leveraging our InDiv scores as an
extra reward. Specifically, for each solution in a group, we calculate
its InDiv score as
Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models"),
and further normalize the score as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R^{InDiv}_{g}=\frac{\mathbf{InDiv}^{g}}{\text{max}(\{\mathbf{InDiv}^{1},\cdots,\mathbf{InDiv}^{G}\})},
``` |  | (9) |

where $`\text{max}(\cdot)`$ denotes the maximum InDiv scores in a group.
The final reward for H-GRPO is the combination of all rewards:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R^{all}_{g}=R^{accuracy}_{g}+R^{format}_{g}+\omega\cdot R^{InDiv}_{g},
``` |  | (10) |

where $`\omega`$ is a coefficient to control the weight of
$`R^{InDiv}_{g}`$, which is set to 0.2 in our experiments. Notably,
since the accuracy reward weight (1.0) far exceeds the InDiv reward
weight (0.2), any attempt by the model to inflate the InDiv score by
inserting irrelevant content would likely produce incorrect answers,
resulting in a net negative reward (approximately -0.8), which
inherently suppresses the incentive for reward hacking.

### C.1 Dataset Details

In this work, we evaluate the trained models on several representative
and challenging reasoning benchmarks. Here, we introduce the
descriptions of these tasks as follows:

- •
  MedQA: MedQA ([Jin et al., 2021](#bib.bib54)) is a challenging medical
  question-answering task, which consists of questions and corresponding
  4-option or 5-option answers in the style of the US Medical License
  Exam (USMLE). Since the original MedQA does not contain the reasoning
  trajectories, we prompt the DeepSeek-R1 to generate the reasoning
  data. The prompt is shown in
  Table [8](#A3.T8 "Table 8 ‣ C.2 Training and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix C More Experimental Details ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models"),
  and
  Table [9](#A3.T9 "Table 9 ‣ C.2 Training and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix C More Experimental Details ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
  presents a case of distilled reasoning trajectories. For evaluation,
  we follow prior works ([Chen et al., 2023](#bib.bib24); [Zhong et al.,
  2025b](#bib.bib47)) and use the 4-option MedQA with 1,273 samples as
  the test set.
- •
  GSM8K: GSM8K ([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib55)) is a widely-used
  mathematical reasoning task, which contains 8.5K high-quality grade
  school math word problems. Since the original GSM8K does not contain
  any reasoning trajectories, we alternatively use the GSM8K version⁴⁴ 4
  https://huggingface.co/datasets/camel-ai/gsm8k_distilled released by
  CAMEL ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib25)). Notably, the dataset is also
  distilled from the DeepSeek-R1.
  Table [10](#A3.T10 "Table 10 ‣ C.2 Training and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix C More Experimental Details ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
  presents a case of distilled GSM8K training data. For in-distribution
  evaluation, we directly use the original GSM8K with 1.32K test
  samples.
- •
  Medbullets: Medbullets ([Chen et al., 2025a](#bib.bib45)) comprises
  308 difficult USMLE Step 2&3 style medical questions collected from
  real-world conversations. Each question is paired with a case
  description and multiple answer choices. In our work, we use the
  4-option Medbullets as the OOD test set of LRMs trained on MedQA.
- •
  MATH: MATH ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](#bib.bib43)) comprises 500
  problems spanning five core mathematical domains: algebra,
  combinatorics, geometry, number theory, and precalculus. Each problem
  is designed to test the multi-step and complex reasoning abilities of
  LRMs, requiring more than simple calculation or knowledge recall. In
  our experiments, we use this challenging dataset to evaluate the OOD
  performance of models trained on GSM8K.
- •
  MedXpertQA: MedXpertQA ([Zuo et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)) contains 4,460
  high-difficulty medical questions spanning 17 specialties and 11 body
  systems. It includes two subsets, MedXpertQA Text for text medical
  evaluation and MedXpertQA MM for multimodal medical evaluation. We use
  the MedXpertQA Text as the OOD test set of LRMs trained on MedQA.
- •
  AMC2023: AMC2023 ([Mathematical Association of America,
  2023](#bib.bib13)) consists of 40 challenging mathematical problems
  from American Mathematics Competitions, which is widely used to
  evaluate the complex reasoning performance of LRMs. We use the public
  test set⁵⁵ 5 https://huggingface.co/datasets/zwhe99/amc23 to evaluate
  the OOD performance of models trained on GSM8K.
- •
  ARC: AI2 Reasoning Challenge’s challenge set (ARC) ([Clark et al.,
  2018](#bib.bib16)) is a scientific reasoning dataset that contains
  1.12K multiple-choice science QA training samples and 1.17K test
  samples. Similar to MedQA, we prompt the DeepSeek-R1 to generate the
  reasoning steps for the training samples, and randomly select 500
  samples as the seed data, using the remaining training samples as
  unlabeled data. The trained models are evaluated on the ARC test set.
- •
  CommonsenseQA: CommonsenseQA (CSQA) ([Talmor et al.,
  2019](#bib.bib14)) is a multiple-choice question-answering dataset
  that requires diverse types of commonsense knowledge to predict the
  correct answers. It contains 12,102 questions, each with one correct
  answer and four distractors. Similarly, for the 9.74K training
  samples, we distill reasoning steps from DeepSeek-R1 and randomly
  select 1K samples as seed data. The trained models are evaluated on
  the 1.14K test samples.
- •
  StrategyQA: StrategyQA (StraQA) ([Geva et al., 2021](#bib.bib12)) is
  an implicit multi-hop reasoning benchmark, containing 1.6K training
  samples and 687 test samples. After distilling the reasoning steps
  from DeepSeek-R1 for the training samples, we randomly select 500
  training samples as the seed data. The trained models are directly
  evaluated on the test samples.

### C.2 Training and Evaluation Details

In the SFT phase, we fine-tune each model with a batch size of 32 and a
peak learning rate of 1e-5, except 2e-6 for 7B/8B models. In the DPO
phase, the batch size is set to 16, and the peak learning rates for
smaller (1.5B/3B) models and larger(7B/8B) models are set to 1e-6 and
2e-7, respectively. In both training settings, the warm-up ratio is 0.1,
and the maximum tokenizer length is 2,048. All models are trained with
full parameter fine-tuning for 3 epochs. We use the popular
LLaMA-Factory⁶⁶ 6 https://github.com/hiyouga/LLaMA-Factory toolkit as
the training codebase. For the RLVR training, we implement the GRPO with
the open-r1⁷⁷ 7 https://github.com/huggingface/open-r1 framework. The
roll-out size for each query is set to 8, the learning rate is set to
1e-6, and the batch size is set to 14. Since the GRPO training is
time-consuming and costly, we only train the models for 300 steps. For
implementing our H-GRPO, the $`\omega`$ that controls the weight of
$`R^{InDiv}`$ is set to 0.2. All experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA
A800 (80GB) GPUs.

During evaluation, we sample the model responses by using the greedy
decoding method for reproducibility. The maximum output length is set
4,096. All models are evaluated in a zero-shot manner. We extract the
final answer from the model response according to the given format. If
no valid answer is extracted, the response is considered incorrect.

### D.1 Analysis of VeriExit Sampling Strategy

#### Reliability of VeriExit.

In HSIR, we propose VeriExit to efficiently collect more successful
solutions for difficult queries by reusing partial correct reasoning
steps of previous failed solutions. Here, to verify the reliability of
this idea, we calculate the coverage of these verifiable successful
solutions, which contain intermediate reasoning steps that arrived at
the correct answer.

Qwen2.5 family models, nearly 70% of these most difficult queries have
at least one verifiable successful solution, and more than 10% of these
queries have 4 or more verifiable successful solutions. We believe that
in the simpler queries, there is a larger proportion of previous failed
solutions that can be converted into verifiable successful solutions by
VeriExit. These results can demonstrate the reliability of our VeriExit.

#### More VeriExit variants.

The core of our VeriExit is to determine whether the reasoning step has
reached the ground-truth answer. There are several methods to achieve
this goal. Specifically, the simplest approach is to directly match the
text between the reasoning output and the ground-truth answer. If the
reasoning output explicitly mentions the answer, e.g., “answer is
$`\{y_{i}\}`$”, we can assume that it arrives at the ground-truth
answer. Beyond this simple heuristic, inspired by SelfCheckGPT ([Manakul
et al., 2023](#bib.bib11)), we can further use two more sophisticated
approaches: NLI-based and prompt-based. In the NLI-based VeriExit, we
use an external Natural Language Inference (NLI) model to judge the
relationships between the reasoning output and answer. The NLI model can
determine whether the reasoning output entails the answer according to
the similarity of sentence representations, i.e.,
$`\mathbb{I}(\text{NLI}(\hat{r}^{k}_{i,l},y_{i})=\text{entailment})`$.
In practice, we use the powerful DeBERTa-v3-large-mnli⁸⁸ 8
https://huggingface.co/MoritzLaurer/DeBERTa-v3-large-mnli-fever-anli-ling-wanli
as the NLI model. In the prompt-based VeriExit, we query the current
$`\mathcal{M}_{t-1}`$ to assess whether the reasoning output and answer
convey the same meaning by using the following prompt: “You are a
semantic-equivalence classifier. Your only goal is to decide whether the
two input sentences convey the same meaning. Please direct output the
answer following the format: ‘##Answer: \[YES\|NO\|UNCLEAR\]’”. Notably,
for both methods, we convert the answer into a full sentence using the
template “the answer is $`{y_{i}}`$” to ensure consistent semantic
comparison.

To evaluate different VeriExit strategies, we measure the coverage of
verifiable successful solutions on the most difficult queries of MedQA
using each strategy. Using the text-matching method as the baseline, we
illustrate the correlation between text-matching and
NLI-based/prompt-based methods in
Figure [6](#A4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Reliability of VeriExit. ‣ D.1 Analysis of VeriExit Sampling Strategy ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Left) and (Middle), respectively. In this experiment, we use the
Qwen2.5-3B as the base model. The results show that text-matching
VeriExit correlates well with both alternative methods, with Pearson
Correlation Coefficients exceeding 0.4 and p-values below 0.05,
indicating that all VeriExit variants produce largely consistent
predictions. Further, we replace the VeriExit strategy in the HSIR-SFT
framework, and compare the performance and efficiency of HSIR-SFT
variants in
Figure [6](#A4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Reliability of VeriExit. ‣ D.1 Analysis of VeriExit Sampling Strategy ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Right). The findings are as follows: 1) both NLI-based and prompt-based
methods achieve better reasoning performance, as they can more
accurately identify the correct reasoning steps during VeriExit; 2)
although effective, these methods incur additional inference overhead.
Therefore, for simplicity and efficiency, we use the text-matching
method in our work by default.

### D.2 Analysis of InDiv Score

#### Correlation between InDiv scores and semantic entropy.

Here, we investigate the correlation between our InDiv scores and the
semantic entropy of hidden representations $`\mathbf{H}`$. First, we
introduce the Matrix-Based Entropy ([Giraldo et al., 2014](#bib.bib18);
[Skean et al., 2023](#bib.bib21); [Skean et al., 2025](#bib.bib73)),
which is a famous information-theoretic quantity. For the
cross-covariance matrix
$`\bm{\Sigma}^{k}_{i}={\mathbf{H}^{k}_{i}}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{J}_{d}\cdot\mathbf{H}^{k}_{i}`$
and its eigenvalues $`\{\lambda^{k}_{i,u}\}^{m}_{u=1}`$, the
matrix-based entropy of order $`\alpha>0`$ is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{S}^{k}_{\alpha,i}(\mathbf{H}^{k}_{i})=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\Big[\sum_{u=1}^{m}(\frac{\lambda^{k}_{i,u}}{\text{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{k}_{i})})^{\alpha}\Big],
``` |  | (11) |

where $`\text{Tr}(\cdot)`$ denotes the trace operator obtained from the
sum of $`\alpha`$-power of each eigenvalues ([Horn and Johnson,
2012](#bib.bib22)). When $`\alpha\rightarrow 1`$, the entropy
$`\mathbf{S}^{k}_{\alpha,i}(\mathbf{H}^{k}_{i})`$ corresponds to the
Shannon’s entropy of hidden representations. Intuitively, if the
eigenvalues of $`\mathbf{H}`$ are in a uniform distribution, the entropy
will be higher, indicating that $`\mathbf{H}`$ contains more diverse
features ([Skean et al., 2025](#bib.bib73)). Conversely, if the
eigenvalues collapse to a small area, the entropy will be smaller. To
verify it, we compare the distributions of eigenvalues between the
concise and overthinking solutions identified by our InDiv scores.
Specifically, we directly use the solutions in
Table [18](#A4.T18 "Table 18 ‣ D.10 Case Study ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models"),
and illustrate their eigenvalue distributions in
Figure [7](#A4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Correlation between InDiv scores and semantic entropy. ‣ D.2 Analysis of InDiv Score ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Left). It can be found that the eigenvalue distributions of the concise
solution are more uniform than those of the overthinking solution. To
have a closer look, we directly visualize the hidden representations of
both solutions.
Figure [7](#A4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Correlation between InDiv scores and semantic entropy. ‣ D.2 Analysis of InDiv Score ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Middle) and (Right) show the t-SNE results of the concise and
overthinking solutions, respectively. We can observe that the
distribution of hidden representations of the concise solution is more
diverse and uniform, while that of the overthinking solution is more
similar and concentrated. Overall, these results indicate that a higher
InDiv score usually refers to a higher semantic entropy of hidden
representations, which effectively proves why our InDiv score can help
identify overthinking solutions.

#### More ablation study in InDiv.

There are two important strategies in our InDiv: attention-aware

as the final score and filters the solutions with lower scores within
each query. Notably, the filtering ratio is adjusted to ensure that both
variants use approximately the same amount of training data as the
original HSIR-SFT.

#### Comparison with Data Selection Baselines.

Some readers may wonder how our method relates to prior data selection
approaches. While we acknowledge that our method enhances
self-improvement performance through better data construction, data
selection is not our primary focus. Rather, we address this from two
orthogonal perspectives: (1) increasing data quantity by eliciting more
correct solutions for challenging queries via VeriExit, and (2)
improving data quality by filtering overthinking solutions via InDiv. In
other words, our work targets fundamental bottlenecks in the
self-improvement training of LRMs that are distinct from those addressed
by general data selection techniques. To further validate this
distinction, we replace the InDiv score with several widely used
data-centric metrics within our HSIR framework: PPL-based ([Cao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib6)), Quality-based ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)), and
Entropy-based ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)) filtering. Comparative
results on Qwen2.5-1.5B are presented in
Table [12](#A4.T12 "Table 12 ‣ Impact of layer depth for calculating InDiv scores. ‣ D.2 Analysis of InDiv Score ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models").
As shown, InDiv consistently outperforms all alternatives, demonstrating
that it captures a more effective signal for identifying undesired
solutions than existing data selection metrics.

### D.3 Parameter Analysis

#### Effect of $`\tau`$.

The threshold $`\tau`$, used to filter the overthinking solutions, is an
important hyperparameter in our HSIR. In this study, we analyze its
influence by evaluating the performance with different $`\tau`$ values,
spanning from -0.75 to 0.75.
Figure [8](#A4.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ Impact of layer depth for calculating InDiv scores. ‣ D.2 Analysis of InDiv Score ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Middle) illustrates the comparative results of Qwen2.5-3B models
trained with HSIR-SFT on MedQA and GSM8K. For reference, we also report
the results without data filtering as the baseline. As seen, compared to
the baseline, HSIR with suitable $`\tau`$ can generally achieve better
performance, showing the effectiveness of using InDiv scores to filter
overthinking solutions. However, too large $`\tau`$ (i.e., 0.75) would
lead to performance degradation, as many helpful training samples might
be ignored. HSIR performs best with $`\tau=-0.5`$, thus leaving as our
default experimental settings.

#### Effect of $`J`$.

The hyperparameter $`J`$, which is used to control the sample count of
recycled solutions during VeriExit, is also important for our HSIR.
Here, to investigate its impact, we evaluate our HSIR-SFT with different
$`J`$ values ranging from 1 to 7. The comparative results of
Qwen2.5-1.5B on MedQA are illustrated in
Figure [8](#A4.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ Impact of layer depth for calculating InDiv scores. ‣ D.2 Analysis of InDiv Score ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Right). For reference, we also include the results of SFT-Initial and
ReST^(EM). All models are self-improved for one iteration. From these
results, we find that: 1) When $`J`$ is too small (e.g., $`J=1`$), our
VeriExit struggles to sample enough correct solutions, limiting the
effectiveness of HSIR-SFT. 2) When $`J`$ is too large (e.g., $`J=7`$),
many sampled solutions share similar prefix reasoning steps, reducing
the diversity of training data and leading to sub-optimal performance.
3) Across all $`J`$ values, our HSIR-SFT consistently outperforms the
baseline methods, proving its robustness. Notably, in the case of
$`J=5`$, HSIR-SFT achieves the best performance. Thus, we use it as the
default setting in this work.

#### Is the seed data from a frontier model necessary?

In the above experiments, we empirically find that initial LRMs trained

data, we randomly select three examples distilled from DeepSeek-R1 as
few-shot demonstrations and use them to guide the base Qwen2.5-3B for
generating the seed data. Notably, for all methods, we use the same
queries $`x_{i}`$ and sample a correct solution for each query, ensuring
the same number of training samples across seed data types. Qwen2.5-3B
model is first fine-tuned on different seed data, and then self-improved
with various SFT approaches for one iteration.

presents the comparative results of different Qwen2.5-3B models on
MedQA. From it, we observe that: 1) the quality of seed data is
critical, as initial SFT on low-quality seed data significantly degrades

performance gains, further validating its effectiveness. In general,
while it is feasible for an LLM to self-distill seed data and
subsequently self-improve using HSIR, leveraging more high-quality seed

### D.5 When Ground-truth Answers are Unavailable

Following many prior studies ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib1); [Yuan
et al., 2023](#bib.bib30); [Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib46); [Pang et
al., 2024](#bib.bib52)), we assume that the ground-truth answers of the
unlabeled dataset $`\mathcal{D}`$ are available in this work. Some
readers may wonder how our HSIR performs when ground-truth answers are
unavailable in some scenarios. Actually, in this setting, we can
follow [Huang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26) and use the majority-voting
answer among multiple candidate solutions as a pseudo answer, i.e.,
$`\tilde{y}_{i}=\argmax_{\hat{y}^{j}_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{I}(\hat{y}^{j}_{i}=\hat{y}^{k}_{i})`$.
Here, the $`\tilde{y}_{i}`$ is denoted as the self-consistency pseudo
label. Although the $`\tilde{y}_{i}`$ may be incorrect, we can still
apply our HSIR to improve the LRMs as described in
§[3](#S3 "3 Methodology ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models").
To verify its effectiveness, we evaluate it on several Qwen2.5 models
and report the results in
Table [15](#A4.T15 "Table 15 ‣ D.5 When Ground-truth Answers are Unavailable ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models").
For reference, we use the ReST^(EM) as the baseline, and also include
the results based on ground-truth answers. As seen, using the
self-consistency pseudo labels indeed yields slightly sub-optimal
results compared to ground-truth answers. Nevertheless, our HSIR-SFT
method still achieves substantial improvements, with an average gain of

### D.6 Results in High-resource Scenarios

In our work, we assume that only a small amount of seed data is
available. Some readers may wonder whether our HSIR method remains
effective in high-resource scenarios, where sufficient seed data is
provided. To verify it, we use all training samples distilled from
DeepSeek-R1 as the seed data to initially fine-tune the base model,
i.e., using the SFT-Oracle in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
as the $`\mathcal{M}_{0}`$.
Table [16](#A4.T16 "Table 16 ‣ D.5 When Ground-truth Answers are Unavailable ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
presents the results of Qwen2.5-1.5B and Qwen2.5-3B models on MedQA. For
reference, we also report the results of ReST^(EM) and IRPO as
baselines. All models are self-improved for one iteration. From these
results, we find that both HSIR-SFT and HSIR-DPO methods can effectively
improve the performance and inference efficiency of SFT-Oracle models.
Specifically, with the help of HSIR-DPO, Qwen2.5-3B model achieves

HSIR has great potential to enhance the self-improvement capabilities of
fully-trained and powerful LRMs.

### D.7 More Self-improvement Iterations

Due to limited computational resources, we set the maximum
self-improvement iterations $`T`$ to 3 in the main experiments. Here, to
further investigate whether additional iterations can improve
performance, we extended the maximum training iterations $`T`$ from 3 to
5, and compare the MedQA accuracy of Qwen2.5-1.5B models trained with
different self-improvement SFT training methods across the iterations.
Figure [9](#A4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ D.7 More Self-improvement Iterations ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Left) illustrates the comparative results, from which we observe
that: 1) With the increase of self-improvement training iterations, both
STaR and ReST^(EM) exhibit a trend where performance initially improves
but then declines, which is similar to the findings of [Ding et al.

### D.8 Comparison and Compatibility with Inference-time Methods

The goal of our work is to propose a self-improvement training framework
that unlocks the internal long-CoT reasoning capabilities of LRMs,
rather than to optimize inference. Therefore, in the main experiments,
we do not compare HSIR with inference-time methods, such as
Self-Consistency (SC) ([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib9)). Nevertheless,
given that SC is widely used to enhance LRM reasoning performance, we
include it in this experiment. Specifically, during inference, we sample
five solutions from the model and select the majority-vote answer as the
prediction.
Figure [9](#A4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ D.7 More Self-improvement Iterations ‣ Appendix D More Experiments and Analyses ‣ Better, Faster: Harnessing Self-Improvement in Large Reasoning Models")
(Right) shows the comparative results of Qwen2.5 models on MedQA, where
“-w/ SC” means using the SC method. As seen, increasing the test-time
compute through SC improves the reasoning performance of SFT-Initial
models effectively. However, it still underperforms our HSIR-SFT method,
even if HSIR-SFT only samples a single solution during inference. More
encouragingly, combining the HSIR-SFT and SC methods consistently yields
further performance improvements. For instance, for the Qwen2.5-3B
model, with the help of SC, HSIR-SFT achieves a 12.25% performance gain
over the SFT-Initial model. These results prove the compatibility of our
HSIR with the inference-time SC method.

### D.9 Efficiency of HSIR

Some readers may be concerned about the efficiency of our HSIR method,
as it requires additional forward passes of LRMs. Actually, during the
VeriExit sampling phase, we only sample the final answer without
regenerating intermediate reasoning trajectories, making it much faster
than simply allocating more trials to failed queries. In our preliminary
experiments, we found that the VeriExit sampling can be completed in an
average of one hour on 8 NVIDIA A800 (80GB) GPUs, which is about 1/4 of
the time required for generating full reasoning trajectories. On the
other hand, to obtain the InDiv scores, the query and its solution are
fed into the model in a teacher-forcing manner, requiring only a single
forward pass and introducing minimal latency. In practice, during
HSIR-SFT and HSIR-DPO training, obtaining hidden states for each token
introduces some computational overhead, but this can be completed in
about half an hour on 8 NVIDIA A800 (80GB) GPUs. Moreover, during the
H-GRPO training, we can reuse the hidden representation obtained by the
reference model without extra forward passes. The actual computation of
the InDiv score itself is lightweight, involving only simple vector
operations that take a few seconds. More importantly, owing to the data
filtering process of HSIR, the training budget can be significantly
reduced, e.g., from 53.6K to 38.8K during fine-tuning Qwen2.5-1.5B at
the last iteration. In general, the inference latency of HSIR is
tolerable against its performance gains.
````

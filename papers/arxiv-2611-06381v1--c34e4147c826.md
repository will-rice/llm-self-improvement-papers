###### Abstract

As LLM-based agents develop, they have been increasingly applied in the
legal domain. Legal Judgment Prediction (LJP) requires models to conduct
multi-step reasoning and external legal evidence retrieval. Existing
legal search agents’ performance is often constrained by the quality of
retrieval queries. They often formulate queries directly from the
initial user request, overlooking latent legal issues and legally
salient details. To address this issue, we propose _LegalEvolver_, a
query optimization framework specifically designed to generate legally
informative queries for LJP-oriented retrieval. Sampling high-quality
optimized queries and their corresponding search trajectories is
challenging, as relying solely on unguided high-temperature rollouts is
both inefficient and constrained by the model’s initial capabilities. We
therefore heuristically optimize queries as seeds, improving the
efficiency of trajectory generation. Furthermore, we summarize rollout
experience into rubrics and use them to guide multi-round iterative
self-evolution. Iteratively performing rubric optimization and
rubric-guided RL enables the model to surpass its initial performance
ceiling. End-to-end performance on extensive benchmarks demonstrates the
effectiveness of query optimization on LJP. Comparisons with direct SFT
followed by RL demonstrate the effectiveness of rubric guidance.
Evolving rubrics further outperform fixed rubrics, confirming the
benefits of self-evolution.

## 1 Introduction

Legal judgment prediction (LJP) requires a model to determine the
applicable law articles, charges, and prison term of a case from its
fact description ([Xu et al., 2020](#bib.bib13)). Like a judge, the
model must establish the charge before determining the sentence ([Zhong
et al., 2018](#bib.bib7)). Decisions must be based on specific legal
provisions, which come from statutes, judicial interpretations, and
sentencing guidelines ([Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib4); [Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)). Because these provisions are numerous and frequently
revised, parametric knowledge alone cannot capture them reliably ([Deng
et al., 2024a](#bib.bib3); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). Therefore,
recent work turns to legal search agents, which consult these sources
along with precedents during reasoning and base their predictions on the
retrieved evidence ([Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib4)).

However, access to retrieval alone brings modest gains ([Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)). Our analysis shows that queries themselves are often
the problem of failure; this diagnosis is consistent with recent work on
query optimization for search agents ([Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib36);
[Wen et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)). Consider a defendant who borrows the
victim’s phone and then runs off with it. The query “elements of the
crime of fraud” fails because the key point is the victim’s intent in
handing over the phone, which is exactly what separates fraud from
theft. A better query is like “took a borrowed phone and fled, whether
the victim disposed of property, theft versus fraud”. To understand what
distinguishes effective queries more generally, we examine the reasoning
and search trajectories of legal experts on judgment prediction. Experts
rarely search for a charge or an article in isolation. Each query
addresses a question still open at that point of their reasoning, and
changes as the reasoning moved on. When deciding the charge, they search
statutes and judicial interpretations; when deciding the sentence, they
searched sentencing guidelines and precedents. In both stages, they
wrote the decisive facts of the case into the query ([Wen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib23)).

Search agents are commonly trained with SFT followed by RL with outcome
rewards ([Li et al., 2025a](#bib.bib19)). This recipe struggles to
inject the behaviour described above for the following reasons ([Chu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib26); [Zheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)):
(1) Exploration stays anchored to the model’s initial hypothesis ([Chu
et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)). Once the base model settles on a candidate
charge, its queries keep revolving around it. Higher sampling
temperature hardly varies charge under examination ([Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib27)). (2) Outcome rewards cannot tell which search went
wrong ([Zheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)). It fails to distinguish
different search failure types ([Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib36)). General
query clarity and informativeness also miss these domain-specific legal
requirements ([Deng et al., 2024a](#bib.bib3); [Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib28)). (3) What makes a query useful changes as the agent
improves ([Shao et al., 2025](#bib.bib31); [Gunjal et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30)). Once the agent routinely includes key facts, its
remaining errors can depend on subtler distinctions ([Xu et al.,
2020](#bib.bib13)). Fixed criteria fail to separate good queries from
bad ones ([Shao et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)).

The key missing ingredient is supervision for individual queries. Our
core observation is that differences in judgment outcomes can provide
it. When trajectories on the same case reach different judgments, the
differences between their queries suggest which searches are more
effective. The type of error further indicates which searches to
examine. A wrong charge points to the searches for legal qualification,
while a wrong sentence points to the sentencing searches. Building on
this observation, we induce query criteria from within-case contrasts
and assemble them into rubrics that reward each search. As the policy
improves, we rebuild the rubrics from its new trajectories. The
supervision thus evolves with the policy.

To address these challenges, we propose LegalEvolver, a framework for
training legal search agents. It learns query rubrics from the agent’s
own trajectories and keeps refreshing them as the agent improves.
Training consists of three consecutive stages. (1) Diverse Trajectory
Generation and Policy Initialization. We first sample diverse reasoning
and retrieval trajectories at a high temperature, then apply
expert-inspired heuristics to rewrite their retrieval queries, producing
additional candidate trajectories. Rejection sampling selects successful
trajectories for supervised fine-tuning, equipping the agent with
effective initial reasoning and retrieval behaviors. (2) Initial Rubric
Construction. To construct query-level supervision, we contrast
successful trajectories with failed trajectories for the same problem,
examining how differences in query construction contribute to their
divergent outcomes. We distill these comparisons into an initial set of
legal query rubrics that provide explicit criteria for evaluating
intermediate queries. (3) Rubric–Policy Co-Evolution. Building on this
initialization, we jointly improve the query rubrics and the agent
policy through iterative self-evolution. We combine rubric-based rewards
with outcome rewards for reinforcement learning, providing feedback on
both intermediate query quality and final prediction correctness. As the
updated policy generates new successful trajectories, we use them to
re-induce the rubrics, incorporating retrieval strategies discovered
during training. The revised rubrics then guide the next round of policy
optimization. Alternating these steps lets supervision evolve with the
agent’s capabilities, enabling improvement beyond the initial
demonstrations and fixed rubric criteria.

Experiments on single- and multi-defendant legal judgment prediction
show that LegalEvolver outperforms agentic retrieval-augmented baselines
on most metrics, with further gains from reinforcement learning after
supervised initialization.

To sum up, our contributions are threefold: (1) We identify query
construction as a critical bottleneck for search agents in LJP. (2) We
propose _LegalEvolver_, which derives legal query rubrics from
contrasting successful and failed trajectories. (3) We develop a
self-evolution training paradigm that jointly improves agent policies
and query rubrics.

## 2 Related Work

#### Legal Judgment Prediction

Legal Judgment Prediction (LJP) has been studied using neural
classification models ([Zhong et al., 2018](#bib.bib7)), LLM-based legal
reasoning ([Jiang and Yang, 2023](#bib.bib2)), and agents that acquire
external legal knowledge ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). Early work
models dependencies among judgment subtasks with TopJudge ([Zhong et
al., 2018](#bib.bib7)), distinguishes confusing law articles with
LADAN ([Xu et al., 2020](#bib.bib13)), and incorporates case
circumstances with NeurJudge ([Yue et al., 2021](#bib.bib1)). With the
emergence of LLMs, legal syllogism prompting and syllogistic judgment
analysis explicitly structure legal reasoning ([Jiang and Yang,
2023](#bib.bib2); [Deng et al., 2023](#bib.bib17)). ADAPT further
employs an Ask–Discriminate–Predict framework to distinguish confusing
charges ([Deng et al., 2024b](#bib.bib5)). External knowledge also
supports judgment prediction through precedent-enhanced LLM and
domain-model collaboration ([Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib4)) and
multi-stage knowledge integration ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)).
GLARE expands confusing charge candidates and retrieves reasoning paths
from similar cases for comparative reasoning ([Yang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15)). Query formulation is itself relevant to legal
retrieval: knowledge-guided case reformulation has been studied for
interpretable case matching ([Deng et al., 2024a](#bib.bib3)). We focus
on learning to formulate intermediate retrieval queries within an LJP
agent, using case facts to seek the legal knowledge needed for judgment
reasoning.

#### Rubric-Based Supervision for LLMs

Rubric-based supervision expresses response quality through explicit
criteria, supporting both model evaluation ([Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib32)) and policy optimization ([Gunjal et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30)). G-Eval combines specified evaluation criteria with
generated evaluation steps and structured scoring ([Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib32)). AutoCalibrate induces scoring criteria from
human-labeled examples, then selects and refines them to improve
agreement with human judgments ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib29)). For
contrastive construction, OpenRubrics derives explicit constraints and
implicit quality principles by comparing preferred and rejected
responses, producing criteria that distinguish their quality ([Liu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib28)). Rubrics as Rewards aggregates instance-specific
rubric feedback into rewards for on-policy RL beyond verifiable
domains ([Gunjal et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)). DR Tulu further maintains
evolving rubrics that adapt with the policy to provide discriminative
feedback during deep-research training ([Shao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31)). These works motivate preference-based criterion
induction ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib28)) and adaptive rubric
supervision ([Shao et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)). We extend these ideas to
tool-specific legal queries: downstream judgment outcomes determine
trajectory preferences, from which we discover and refine rubrics for
supervising intermediate retrieval actions. An extended discussion of
reinforcement learning for tool-using and search agents is provided in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Additional Related Work ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution").

### 3.1 Task Formulation

We first formally define the task of legal judgment prediction. Given
the fact description $`f`$ of a case, the model is required to predict:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       y=(l,c,t),
       ```         |     | (1) |

where $`l`$ denotes the applicable law articles, $`c`$ denotes the
corresponding charges, and $`t`$ denotes the term of imprisonment. For
multi-defendant cases, the output is extended to defendant-level
predictions: $`\{(l_{i},c_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}`$, where each
prediction corresponds to an individual defendant.

We adopt the ReAct framework ([Yao et al., 2023](#bib.bib25)) as the
backbone of our legal search agent. The legal agent performs multi-round
interactions with external legal retrieval tools through a sequence of
Reasoning–Action–Observation steps. We represent the reasoning process
of the legal agent as a trajectory:
$`\tau=(f,r_{1},a_{1},o_{1},\ldots,r_{m},a_{m},o_{m},\hat{y})`$, where
$`r_{j}`$ denotes the natural-language legal reasoning at step $`j`$,
$`a_{j}`$ represents the tool-use action taken by the agent, $`o_{j}`$
denotes the corresponding retrieval observation returned by the tool,
and $`\hat{y}`$ represents the final prediction generated by the agent.
$`\hat{y}`$ consists of predicted law articles $`\hat{l}`$, predicted
charges $`\hat{c}`$, and the predicted term of imprisonment $`\hat{t}`$.
For a retrieval action, the agent can send a query to one of the four
legal knowledge bases: law articles, precedents, judicial
interpretations, and sentencing guidelines.
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Multi-source Legal Retrieval Environment ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")
describes these knowledge sources and their retrieval interfaces.

To evaluate a trajectory, we compare its final prediction
$`\hat{y}=(\hat{l},\hat{c},\hat{t})`$ with the ground truth
$`y=(l,c,t)`$ and define three outcome states:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
S(\tau)=\begin{cases}S_{3},&\hat{l}=l,\ \hat{c}=c,\ \hat{t}=t,\\
S_{2},&\hat{l}=l,\ \hat{c}=c,\ \hat{t}\neq t,\\
S_{1},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (2) |

Here, $`S_{3}`$ indicates complete success, $`S_{2}`$ indicates correct
legal qualification with incorrect sentencing, and $`S_{1}`$ indicates a
legal qualification failure.

### 3.2 Diverse Trajectory Generation and Policy Initialization

We start from a base agentic model, denoted as the initial policy
$`\pi_{\text{base}}`$. Since it may repeatedly follow the same reasoning
path and issue similar queries for a case, alternative ways of finding
relevant evidence are left unexplored. We therefore first seek diverse
trajectories for each case, increasing the opportunity to discover more
useful query formulations and retrieval strategies.

#### High-temperature exploration and query rewriting.

For each training case, we sample multiple trajectories at an elevated
temperature to explore different reasoning paths and queries. Our
preliminary study identifies poorly constructed queries as the main
failure source, as detailed in
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3.SSS0.Px2 "Query Quality Analysis. ‣ 4.3 Quantitative Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution").
We analyze the failed search trajectories and group their recurring
query failures into three patterns: (1) Queries often consist only of a
charge name, generic legal terms, or a specific article number, leaving
the underlying legal question implicit; (2) Queries may omit facts
needed to distinguish charges or determine a sentencing range, even when
the general retrieval intent is clear; (3) Searches may remain anchored
to an incorrect charge or article, conflate multiple charges, or repeat
an early hypothesis without using retrieved evidence to seek new
discriminating information.

Comparing these patterns with human experts’ LJP trajectories motivates
three complementary rewriting strategies: (1) Conservative rewriting.
Human experts formulate explicit questions around the legal uncertainty
they need to resolve. We therefore clarify the intended legal issue and
make the query more specific while preserving its original information
need. (2) Fact-enriched rewriting. Human experts ground their searches
in legally decisive case details. We therefore enrich queries with
relevant facts from the case description, including conduct,
consequences, and circumstances. (3) Targeted repair rewriting. Human
experts adapt their searches to unresolved issues and prior findings. We
therefore use the trajectory’s outcome state ($`S_{1}`$ or $`S_{2}`$),
the retrieval tool, and the search history to revise the search
direction: legal qualification failures ($`S_{1}`$) motivate queries
about statutory elements and distinctions between candidate charges,
whereas sentencing failures ($`S_{2}`$) motivate queries about
sentencing thresholds and adjustment factors. Prior queries and results
guide which unresolved issue to investigate next.

At a selected retrieval step, we rewrite the query while preserving the
preceding history and tool. We execute the new query and regenerate the
continuation to construct an enhanced trajectory:
$`\tau^{\prime}=(f,r_{1},a_{1},o_{1},\ldots,r_{n},a_{n}^{\prime},o_{n}^{\prime},\ldots,r_{m}^{\prime},a_{m}^{\prime},o_{m}^{\prime},\hat{y}^{\prime})`$.
All natural trajectories sampled with high temperature and trajectories
enhanced with rewriting enter the initial trajectory pool $`P_{0}`$.

#### Rejection sampling and SFT.

We select $`S_{3}`$ trajectories from the trajectory pool $`P_{0}`$ as
the SFT training set. These trajectories yield correct final
predictions, and their tool calls and outputs satisfy the required
format. Then we fine-tune the initial policy $`\pi_{\text{base}}`$ on
the SFT training set, supervising reasoning, tool calls, and final
predictions while treating retrieved observations as context. This
produces the fine-tuned policy $`\pi_{0}`$.
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Experiment Settings and Reward Details ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")
provides more details about the SFT process.

### 3.3 Initial Rubric Construction and Guided Policy Optimization

The initial trajectory pool $`P_{0}`$ contains both successful and
failed attempts at the same cases. Although SFT learns from the
successful trajectories, an outcome label alone provides little guidance
on how an intermediate query could be improved. We therefore compare
trajectories with different outcomes for the same case to identify query
choices associated with better judgments. Such comparisons can yield a
more comprehensive rubric to guide the legal search agent.

#### Initial rubric construction.

Using the outcome states and preference ordering defined in
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Task Formulation ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution"),
we construct three types of within-case preference pairs: $`S_{3}`$
versus $`S_{2}`$, $`S_{2}`$ versus $`S_{1}`$, and $`S_{3}`$ versus
$`S_{1}`$, with the first trajectory preferred in each pair. These pairs
provide the input to pairwise criterion discovery. These pairs are
sampled from the initial trajectory pool $`P_{0}`$. Each pair of
trajectories comes from the same case. Since we have four legal
retrieval bases, the legal search agent has access to 4 retrieval tools.
As for the outcome dimension, we define three reasoning focuses:
Sentencing ($`S_{3}`$ versus $`S_{2}`$), Legal Qualification ($`S_{2}`$
versus $`S_{1}`$), and Full Repair ($`S_{3}`$ versus $`S_{1}`$). Thus,
we organize criteria into 12 (4$`\times`$3) tool-focus groups, each
forming a rubric with multiple criteria. Let $`\mathcal{U}`$ denote the
set of four retrieval tools and $`\mathcal{F}`$ the set of three
reasoning focuses. For a collection of within-case preference pairs
$`\mathcal{P}`$, let $`\mathcal{P}_{u,F}`$ denote the pairs used to
discover criteria for tool $`u\in\mathcal{U}`$ and focus
$`F\in\mathcal{F}`$, with comparisons centered on queries issued to tool
$`u`$.

We construct the initial rubric bank $`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$ in three steps.
(1) Pairwise Criterion Discovery. We use a strong LLM to compare the
preferred and rejected trajectories in the context of the case and
interaction history. It identifies differences in query content and
search focus, then proposes criteria for the corresponding tool-focus
group. Each candidate includes a description, a four-level scoring
guide, and a confidence score. (2) Criteria Aggregation. Within each
group, we remove incomplete, ambiguous, and low-confidence candidates.
Then we merge duplicates and cluster related criteria into reusable
guidance. For example, observations from individual cases can yield
similar criteria that require sentencing queries to include the charge,
monetary amount, and mitigating circumstances. For each group, criteria
aggregation combines evidence across pairs into a candidate rubric.
(3) Criteria Validation. We sample held-out trajectory pairs
$`\mathcal{V}`$ from $`P_{0}`$, with $`\mathcal{V}_{u,F}`$ denoting the
pairs used to validate criteria for tool $`u`$ and focus $`F`$. We test
the aggregated criteria on held-out trajectory pairs and retain those
that consistently assign higher scores to preferred-side queries across
sufficient examples. Finally, the retained criteria form
$`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$.
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Rubric Construction Details ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")
gives the criterion representation and construction statistics.

#### Rubric-guided policy optimization.

After constructing the initial rubric bank $`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$, we apply
$`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$ to guide the RL training of our legal agent. We
specifically design a rubric reward to provide fine-grained supervision
for the agent. To calculate the rubric reward for a trajectory, we first
identify all tool calls in the trajectory, and for each tool call, we
select one of the 12 rubrics according to tool and focus. A strong LLM
serves as a rubric evaluator that scores its criteria using the query,
case, and interaction history. The typical reward for agent RL training
consists of outcome reward $`R_{\mathrm{outcome}}`$ and format reward
$`R_{\mathrm{format}}`$ ([Qian et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)). We further
add the rubric reward:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{rubric}}(\tau)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{c=1}^{C_{j}}w_{jc}\,s_{jc}\right).
``` |  | (3) |

Here $`m`$ is the number of retrieval tool calls in the trajectory, and
$`C_{j}`$ is the number of criteria in the single rubric selected for
call $`j`$. The score $`s_{jc}`$ for criterion $`c`$ takes one of four
values: $`0`$, $`1/3`$, $`2/3`$, or $`1`$; $`w_{jc}`$ is its
non-negative weight, with weights summing to one within the selected
rubric. The inner sum computes each call’s weighted rubric score; the
outer average combines these scores across the trajectory. A trajectory
without tool calls receives zero rubric reward.

We combine this process reward with outcome and format rewards:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{k}(\tau)=\lambda_{o}R_{\mathrm{outcome}}(\tau)+\lambda_{f}R_{\mathrm{format}}(\tau)+\lambda_{r}R_{\mathrm{rubric}}(\tau).
``` |  | (4) |

The rubric term is computed using the current rubric bank
$`\mathcal{R}`$; the outcome term measures law article, charge, and
prison-term prediction correctness; the format term checks valid tool
calls and structured final outputs
(Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Experiment Settings and Reward Details ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")).
We optimize the policy with GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)) using
rewards from
Equation [4](#S3.E4 "In Rubric-guided policy optimization. ‣ 3.3 Initial Rubric Construction and Guided Policy Optimization ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution").
Rubric supervision supplies feedback on intermediate query construction
even when the final prediction is incorrect, while outcome supervision
anchors optimization to judgment accuracy. The first round starts from
the SFT policy $`\pi_{0}`$ and the constructed bank $`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$.

Algorithm 1 Rubric-Policy Co-Evolution


 

1:
$`\pi_{0},\mathcal{R}_{0},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}},\mathcal{V},\epsilon_{\mathrm{stop}}>0`$

2: $`k\leftarrow 0`$;
$`v_{0}\leftarrow J(\pi_{0};\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}})`$

3: repeat

4:   $`\pi_{k+1}\leftarrow\textsc{GRPO}(\pi_{k},R_{k})`$

5:   $`\mathcal{T}\leftarrow\textsc{Rollout}(\pi_{k+1},\mathcal{D})`$

6:   $`\mathcal{P}\leftarrow\textsc{Pair}(\mathcal{T})`$

7:   for $`(u,F)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{F}`$ do

8:    $`\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\textsc{Discover}(\mathcal{P}_{u,F})`$

9:
   $`\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\textsc{Aggregate}(\mathcal{R}_{k}(u,F),\mathcal{C})`$

10:
   $`\mathcal{R}_{k+1}(u,F)\leftarrow\textsc{Revalidate}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{V}_{u,F})`$

11:   end for

12:   $`v_{k+1}\leftarrow J(\pi_{k+1};\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}})`$

13:   $`\Delta\leftarrow v_{k+1}-v_{k}`$; $`k\leftarrow k+1`$

14: until $`\Delta<\epsilon_{\mathrm{stop}}`$

15: return $`\pi_{k},\mathcal{R}_{k}`$


 

### 3.4 Rubric-Policy Co-Evolution

Typical agent training involves SFT followed by RL ([Cai et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37); [Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)). To enable the agent
to surpass the capability ceiling of this training paradigm, we propose
a rubric-based co-evolutionary self-improvement paradigm in which the
policy and rubric bank improve through alternating updates. Starting
from the SFT policy $`\pi_{0}`$ and the initial rubric bank
$`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$ constructed from trajectory contrasts, each round
takes $`(\pi_{k},\mathcal{R}_{k})`$ as input, first updates the policy
under the fixed rubric bank, and then refines the rubrics using
trajectories from the updated policy. Here $`k`$ indexes co-evolution
rounds, and $`\mathcal{R}_{k}(u,F)`$ denotes the rubric associated with
tool $`u`$ and focus $`F`$ in the round-$`k`$ bank. The round ends with
$`(\pi_{k+1},\mathcal{R}_{k+1})`$, which becomes the input to the next
round.

#### Policy improvement with fixed rubrics.

At the start of round $`k`$, we take $`(\pi_{k},\mathcal{R}_{k})`$ as
input and hold the criteria and scoring weights in $`\mathcal{R}_{k}`$
fixed. Each tool call is scored by its selected rubric, and the call
scores are averaged as in
Equation [3](#S3.E3 "In Rubric-guided policy optimization. ‣ 3.3 Initial Rubric Construction and Guided Policy Optimization ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution").
We optimize $`\pi_{k}`$ with the combined reward in
Equation [4](#S3.E4 "In Rubric-guided policy optimization. ‣ 3.3 Initial Rubric Construction and Guided Policy Optimization ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution"),
obtaining $`\pi_{k+1}`$. Keeping the rubric bank fixed during policy
optimization provides consistent query evaluation criteria during that
policy update.

#### Rubric refinement.

In the second stage of round $`k`$, we use the updated policy
$`\pi_{k+1}`$ to revise $`\mathcal{R}_{k}`$. As the policy’s search
behaviors change, the existing bank may miss newly effective strategies
or emerging failure patterns. We refine the bank through three steps:
(1) Updated-policy Trajectory Collection. We sample trajectories from
$`\pi_{k+1}`$ on training cases and classify their outcomes using
Equation [2](#S3.E2 "In 3.1 Task Formulation ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution").
We construct the same three within-case preference-pair types as in
initial construction, capturing query differences under the updated
policy. (2) Criterion Discovery and Aggregation. We apply pairwise
criterion discovery to the new pairs. Within each tool-focus group, we
combine the resulting candidates with the existing criteria in
$`\mathcal{R}_{k}`$, remove incomplete or low-confidence candidates, and
merge and abstract overlapping criteria. Unlike initial construction,
this step updates an existing rubric rather than building one from
scratch. (3) Criterion Revalidation. We test the combined old and new
criteria using the same held-out validation procedure as in initial
construction. Criteria must continue to distinguish preferred-side
queries to be retained, so ineffective old criteria can be removed
alongside ineffective new candidates. The retained criteria form
$`\mathcal{R}_{k+1}`$, completing round $`k`$ with
$`(\pi_{k+1},\mathcal{R}_{k+1})`$ as the input to the next round.

#### Coevolution Algorithm.

Algorithm [3.3](#S3.SS3.SSS0.Px2 "Rubric-guided policy optimization. ‣ 3.3 Initial Rubric Construction and Guided Policy Optimization ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")
summarizes the iteration. Here $`\mathcal{U}`$ and $`\mathcal{F}`$
denote the four tools and three focuses, $`\mathcal{D}`$ the training
cases, and $`\mathcal{V}`$ the held-out trajectory contrasts. Pair
constructs outcome-based preference pairs, Discover performs pairwise
criterion discovery, Aggregate filters and aggregates old and new
criteria, and Revalidate checks the combined criteria on
$`\mathcal{V}`$. High-temperature sampling, heuristic rewriting, and
rejection sampling initialize the training pool; later rounds refine the
rubrics using updated-policy trajectories. On validation cases
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}`$, we track a fixed judgment metric $`J`$
(higher is better), stopping when its gain is below
$`\epsilon_{\mathrm{stop}}>0`$.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Datasets and Metrics.

We evaluate LegalEvolver across two complementary legal scenarios.
CAIL2018 ([Xiao et al., 2018](#bib.bib6)) covers single-defendant cases,
whereas CMDL ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)) contains multi-defendant
cases and therefore tests whether the method transfers to more complex
fact patterns. To reduce charge-frequency bias, we uniformly sample
cases across charge labels to construct balanced test sets, including
1,000 CAIL2018 cases and 2,000 CMDL cases that collectively cover 201
distinct charge categories. Detailed dataset statistics are provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Dataset Details ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution").
Following prior work, we evaluate each prediction task with Accuracy
(Acc.) and Macro-F1 (Ma-F1). Full implementation and training details
are provided in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Experiment Settings and Reward Details ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution").

#### Baselines.

We compare our method against four categories of baseline approaches.
(1) Classification Methods: We consider four supervised classification
methods for LJP, including TopJudge ([Zhong et al., 2018](#bib.bib7)),
NeurJudge ([Yue et al., 2021](#bib.bib1)), BERT ([Devlin et al.,
2019](#bib.bib8)), and LawFormer ([Xiao et al., 2021](#bib.bib9)). (2)
Direct Reasoning: We use a range of models to predict judgments directly
from case facts without external retrieval, including Qwen3-8B ([Qwen
Team, 2025](#bib.bib33)), GLM-4-9B ([Zeng and others,
2024](#bib.bib34)), Hunyuan-A13B ([Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib40)),
Qwen3-14B ([Qwen Team, 2025](#bib.bib33)), GLM-4-32B ([Zeng and others,
2024](#bib.bib34)), and Qwen-Max ([Bai and others, 2023](#bib.bib35)).
(3) Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Reasoning: Agentic RAG combines
multi-step reasoning with external legal evidence retrieval. Search-o1
interleaves reasoning and search to acquire supporting evidence ([Li et
al., 2025b](#bib.bib36)). PLJP uses domain models to generate candidate
labels and retrieve precedents for LLM prediction ([Wu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib4)). GLARE distinguishes confusing charges using reasoning
paths retrieved from similar cases ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)).
(4) Post-Training Methods: Vanilla SFT fine-tunes the model on selected
successful trajectories. Standard GRPO optimizes the policy using
trajectory-level outcome rewards. SmartSearch uses general principles to
optimize search queries.

### 4.2 Main Results

Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")
presents the main experimental results. LegalEvolver achieves the best
overall performance on both datasets and outperforms all baselines. We
further make the following observations. (1) Agentic retrieval generally
improves legal judgment prediction over direct reasoning. External
retrieval provides fine-grained legal knowledge that may not be reliably
available in the model’s parametric knowledge, thereby grounding legal
qualification and sentencing decisions in relevant evidence. (2)
LegalEvolver consistently outperforms existing search agents through
rubric-guided policy optimization. Our rubrics guide the policy to
formulate queries around unresolved legal uncertainties, enabling it to
retrieve more useful evidence than retrieval access alone. In contrast,
existing agents largely rely on general query optimization and often
formulate overly coarse queries that omit latent legal issues and
legally salient case details. (3) Rubric–policy co-evolution provides
stronger alignment than one-stage post-training. LegalEvolver combines
outcome supervision with query-level feedback and refreshes the rubric
bank using trajectories from the updated policy. The resulting
improvements across all three prediction tasks indicate that iterative
supervision remains aligned with the policy as its search behavior
evolves. We provide controlled ablations of the key components in
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Ablation Study ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution").

### 4.3 Quantitative Analysis

#### Training Dynamics and Self-Evolution.

To assess the effectiveness of query-level supervision and iterative
rubric refinement,
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")
compares LegalEvolver with Standard GRPO and Fixed Rubric GRPO on
CAIL2018 and CMDL across three training rounds. All methods start from
the same SFT checkpoint and use the same total number of optimization
steps. Each checkpoint reports the mean accuracy over law article,
charge, and prison-term prediction. Two observations validate our
design. (1) Legal query supervision improves reward alignment.
LegalEvolver converges faster and consistently outperforms Standard
GRPO, which relies only on trajectory-level outcome feedback. The
steeper learning curves suggest that rewarding queries that translate
case facts into targeted legal questions about specific legal
uncertainties provides more effective supervision than outcome feedback
alone. (2) Rubric–policy co-evolution sustains improvement across
training rounds. Fixed Rubric GRPO uses the initial rubric bank
throughout all three rounds and shows diminishing gains in later
training, whereas LegalEvolver continues to improve by refining the
rubric bank with trajectories generated by the updated policy. This
controlled comparison rules out additional training steps as the source
of improvement and shows that iterative rubric refinement keeps query
supervision aligned with the policy’s evolving search behavior.

#### Query Quality Analysis.

We analyze the erroneous trajectories produced by all methods and
summarize their dominant query failure patterns in
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Training Dynamics and Self-Evolution. ‣ 4.3 Quantitative Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")(a).
We define a trajectory as erroneous when either its charge or law
article prediction is incorrect, and use GLM-5.2 ([GLM-5-Team et al.,
2026](#bib.bib41)) to assign each erroneous trajectory to an error
category. The dominant failure patterns can be grouped into three broad
categories: repeated searches based on incorrect assumptions, omission
of key case facts, and under-specified retrieval intent. LegalEvolver
produces the fewest erroneous trajectories, with fewer repetitive and
coarse-grained queries. This pattern is consistent with our training
strategy, which encourages the policy to formulate queries that directly
address specific legal uncertainties and retrieve the evidence needed to
resolve them.

To assess whether LegalEvolver addresses these failures, we evaluate
query quality along four dimensions: Legal Specificity, whether queries
contain case-specific legal concepts and key facts; Legal-Issue
Completeness, whether the query set covers the main legal issues
required for a complete judgment; Retrieval Actionability, whether
queries are clearly formulated and can directly guide the selected tool
to retrieve useful evidence; and Alternative Awareness, whether queries
consider plausible competing legal interpretations and seek the evidence
needed to distinguish among them, rather than only supporting an initial
hypothesis. We use three independent LLM judges to score anonymized
query sets from all methods on 300 cases from each dataset. As shown in
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Training Dynamics and Self-Evolution. ‣ 4.3 Quantitative Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")(b),
LegalEvolver ranks first on all four dimensions and achieves the highest
overall score. Its advantages in Legal Specificity and Alternative
Awareness show that the learned policy more effectively translates case
facts into targeted legal questions and explores competing
interpretations before reaching a judgment. In contrast, general-purpose
query optimization does not fully address the task-specific information
needs of legal judgment prediction.

#### Rubric Evolution.

Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Rubric Evolution. ‣ 4.3 Quantitative Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LegalEvolver: Agentic Legal Judgment Prediction via Rubric-Guided Self-Evolution")
takes the tool statute_search as an example to illustrate how the rubric
bank evolves across training stages. For legal qualification, the
rubrics progress from isolated legal concepts and case details to
reusable factual dimensions and explicit criteria for identifying
offense boundaries and distinguishing potentially applicable charges.
For sentencing, they evolve from simple amount-related cues to
structured coverage of the conduct, object, outcome, amount, frequency,
and timing that determine statutory sentencing tiers. These examples
show how rubric–policy co-evolution turns local retrieval heuristics
revealed by updated trajectories into more comprehensive and reusable
query supervision, enabling the policy to formulate searches that better
address the legal uncertainty at each reasoning stage.

## 5 Conclusion

We presented *LegalEvolver*, a framework that improves legal judgment
prediction by explicitly optimizing the retrieval queries generated
during agent reasoning. LegalEvolver induces rubrics from within-case
trajectory contrasts and uses them to supervise intermediate queries
during policy optimization. It then refines the rubric bank using
trajectories generated by the updated policy, enabling the policy and
the rubrics to co-evolve through alternating updates. Experimental
results show that LegalEvolver achieves the strongest overall
performance and produces more legally specific and informative queries
that better address unresolved legal uncertainties. Further analyses
indicate that the evolving rubrics remain aligned with changes in the
policy’s search behavior, enabling continued improvement beyond
one-stage post-training. These findings demonstrate the value of
rubric–policy co-evolution for evidence-grounded legal judgment
prediction.

## Appendix A Additional Related Work

#### Reinforcement Learning for LLM Agents.

Reinforcement learning has advanced tool-augmented reasoning through
self-taught tool use in START ([Li et al., 2025a](#bib.bib19)) and
reward-driven tool learning in ToolRL ([Qian et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)).
ReSearch applies RL to interleaved reasoning and search ([Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib27)). However, outcome-only rewards provide sparse
feedback for intermediate search decisions, motivating step-wise
supervision ([Zheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)). In the legal domain,
MSR² targets sentencing-factor identification ([Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib22)), while SyLeR uses structure-aware rewards to
encourage syllogistic reasoning ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)). In
general-purpose search, SmartSearch evaluates query novelty, intent
necessity, and retrieval relevance ([Wen et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)),
while StepSearch uses information gain and redundancy penalties for
step-wise RL ([Zheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)). These methods motivate
our complementary focus on intermediate query construction and legally
grounded search feedback.

#### Innovations beyond existing methods.

Beyond its application to legal retrieval, LegalEvolver makes two
methodological advances. (1) Compared with existing legal search agents,
it converts within-case contrasts between successful and failed
trajectories into explicit supervision for intermediate query
construction. The resulting rubrics directly reward whether a query
addresses the unresolved legal issue, rather than relying only on final
judgment accuracy or general reasoning structure. (2) Compared with
general-domain evolving-rubric methods such as DR Tulu ([Shao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31)), LegalEvolver conditions the rubric bank on both
retrieval tool and reasoning focus, so different knowledge sources and
reasoning stages receive distinct criteria. The bank is then updated
from the improved policy’s trajectories and filtered through held-out
revalidation, coupling rubric evolution to fine-grained retrieval
decisions.

## Appendix C Multi-source Legal Retrieval Environment

Legal judgment prediction requires dedicated reasoning over fine-grained
legal knowledge, rather than relying solely on the knowledge stored in
model parameters. To support agentic legal reasoning with reliable
external evidence, we construct a multi-source legal knowledge
environment consisting of law articles, precedents, judicial
interpretations, and sentencing guidelines, together with their
corresponding retrieval tools. These four knowledge sources provide
complementary evidence from different perspectives, namely statutory
constituent elements, judicial experience, rules for legal application,
and sentencing standards, thereby supporting article prediction, charge
prediction, and sentence prediction.

#### Law article retrieval.

This tool retrieves relevant criminal law provisions from a database
containing 491 articles. Each entry combines the article number with its
statutory text as the dense-search representation. At retrieval time,
semantic similarity is augmented with source-specific lexical signals
for article numbers, charge names, and legally salient case terms before
the final evidence is returned to the agent.

#### Precedent retrieval.

This tool retrieves factually similar cases from China Judgments Online.
The factual description is encoded for dense search, while the returned
evidence preserves the corresponding law articles, charges, prison term,
and available sentencing factors. Source-specific reranking further
emphasizes overlap in charges, statutory references, amounts, and
salient circumstances.

#### Judicial interpretation retrieval.

This tool searches 7,214 clause-level judicial-interpretation entries.
The dense representation concatenates the interpretation title, clause
text, and related criminal-law articles. Reranking combines semantic
similarity with matches on related articles, charges, monetary
thresholds, temporal cues, and other legally salient terms.

#### Sentencing guideline retrieval.

This tool retrieves 64 sections from the national sentencing-guideline
index used during training and evaluation. Each section is represented
by its source title, heading, and guideline text. Reranking favors
matches on charges, amounts, aggravating or mitigating circumstances,
and temporal cues, and prioritizes the guideline scope appropriate to
the CAIL2018 cases.

Across all four tools, we encode retrieval text with the 768-dimensional
SAILER-zh encoder ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib11)), L2-normalize the
embeddings, and use exact FAISS inner-product search. Queries are
restricted to 4–160 characters and encoded with a maximum length of 256
tokens on CPU. Each call retrieves 24 dense candidates, applies
source-specific legal reranking, and returns at most four evidence
cards; returned snippets are truncated to 520 characters for statutes
and 620 characters for the other sources.

## Appendix D Rubric Construction Details

#### Initial contrast statistics.

The initial pool yields 9,143 natural preference pairs: 1,394 legal
qualification pairs, 5,285 sentencing pairs, and 2,464 full-repair
pairs. Comparing original trajectories with improved rewritten
continuations yields another 11,701 pairs, for 20,844 pairs in total.
Natural pairs retain the complete trajectories on both sides; rewritten
pairs additionally record the original and modified queries at the
branching step.

#### Rubric representation and aggregation.

Each candidate criterion is represented as $`r=(u,F,c,g,\gamma)`$, where
$`u`$ is the retrieval tool, $`F`$ the reasoning focus, $`c`$ the
natural-language criterion, $`g`$ a scoring guide with scores in
$`\{0,1,2,3\}`$, and $`\gamma\in[0,1]`$ the induction confidence. The
bank contains four tool groups, each divided into Sentencing ($`S_{3}`$
versus $`S_{2}`$), Legal Qualification ($`S_{2}`$ versus $`S_{1}`$), and
Full Repair ($`S_{3}`$ versus $`S_{1}`$), giving 12 rubrics in total.
Filtering, clustering, and abstraction produce 340 candidate criteria;
the final bank retains criteria across 10 populated tool–focus groups in
$`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$, while two of the 12 possible combinations contain
no retained criterion. These criterion counts describe initialization;
subsequent rounds refine the contents of the rubrics while preserving
the tool-focus hierarchy. We use Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct ([Yang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10)) as the rubric induction model to summarize the query
differences between preferred and rejected trajectories into candidate
criteria and aggregate related criteria within each tool-focus group.

#### Validation.

Candidate criteria are evaluated on held-out preference pairs. Queries
from natural trajectory pairs are compared only when they use the same
retrieval tool; original–rewritten pairs supply a direct query
correspondence at the branching step. A criterion is retained when
preferred-side queries consistently score higher across sufficient
validation examples. This evaluates the criterion’s ability to
distinguish observed search behaviors.

## Appendix E Experiment Settings and Reward Details

#### Training data construction.

We construct SFT and GRPO data from the training portions of CAIL2018
and CMDL. During SFT data construction, each complete agent trajectory
is decomposed into step-level examples for first-step planning from the
case facts, next-action prediction under tool observations, and final
legal-judgment prediction. This procedure yields 19,111 step-level SFT
examples. The GRPO training set contains 1,448 cases drawn from the two
datasets. For each GRPO round, we repeatedly sample with the model
obtained from the preceding training round and retain cases that remain
difficult across multiple attempts.

#### Training configuration.

We fine-tune Qwen3-8B ([Qwen Team, 2025](#bib.bib33)) with LoRA using
LLaMA-Factory, retaining retrieved observations in the input context
while applying the loss only to the next assistant response.
Reinforcement learning is initialized from the merged SFT checkpoint and
optimized with GRPO in VERL, using SGLang for multi-turn rollouts and
PyTorch SDPA for policy optimization. For policy optimization, we sample
eight rollouts per prompt, with up to 8,192 generation tokens, a
20,480-token context, and at most 10 retrieval calls per rollout.

#### Supervised initialization.

Retained trajectories are decomposed into next-step prediction examples
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{step}}=\{(f,h_{j},b_{j})\}`$, where $`b_{j}`$
denotes the next agent-generated response, including reasoning and a
tool call or final prediction. Retrieved observations are included in
$`h_{j}`$ but are not supervised targets. The objective is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}=-\sum_{(f,h_{j},b_{j})\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{step}}}\log\pi_{\theta}(b_{j}\mid f,h_{j}).
``` |  | (5) |

#### Reward details.

The outcome reward combines correctness scores for applicable law
articles, charges, and prison terms:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{outcome}}(\tau)=\alpha_{l}S_{l}+\alpha_{c}S_{c}+\alpha_{t}S_{t},
``` |  | (6) |

where $`S_{l}`$, $`S_{c}`$, and $`S_{t}`$ denote the corresponding
prediction scores and the $`\alpha`$ coefficients control their
contributions. The format term checks tool-call schema validity and
final-output validity:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{format}}(\tau)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(I_{\mathrm{tool}}(\tau)+I_{\mathrm{output}}(\tau)\bigr).
``` |  | (7) |

The binary indicator $`I_{\mathrm{tool}}`$ checks valid tool names and
required parameters, and $`I_{\mathrm{output}}`$ checks the required
structured answer format. For the rubric reward, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct
scores each query using the rubric bank associated with the invoked
retrieval tool: given the tool-call context, the model first identifies
the current reasoning focus, selects the corresponding rubric, and then
assigns the query score.

#### Ablation analysis.

LegalEvolver achieves the best prediction performance across all
metrics. We make three observations. (1) Continual rubric evolution
improves the policy. Keeping the initial rubric bank fixed consistently
reduces performance, showing that updating the rubrics with trajectories
from the improved policy allows the supervision to adapt to changes in
its search behavior and provide further gains. (2) Rubric rewards,
rather than additional optimization steps, drive the improvement.
Without rubric rewards, outcome-only GRPO is trained on the same data
for the same total number of steps, yet it still falls clearly behind
LegalEvolver. This controlled comparison rules out extra training steps
as the source of the gain and demonstrates the value of direct
query-level supervision. (3) Rubric specialization is important. A
single generic rubric provides limited improvement, whereas organizing
the rubric bank by retrieval tool and reasoning focus yields
consistently better performance. This result indicates that effective
query criteria depend on both the knowledge source being searched and
the legal issue being resolved.

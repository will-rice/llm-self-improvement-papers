###### Abstract

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) relies on evaluation feedback to assess
progress and guide further research, yet repeatedly running complex
benchmarks is costly and slows iteration. Human experts reduce this cost
by selecting benchmark subsets or designing compact suites. We ask
whether AI agents can automate this design process and introduce
EvalResearchBench (ERB), a benchmark for autonomous evaluation research.
Given target materials, development references, candidate APIs, and
fixed time and API budgets, an agent called the researcher selects or
synthesizes tasks, implements graders, and revises them in pilot tests
before freezing an executable evaluator for coding, co-work, and
reasoning. We study 9 researchers and 13 candidate models and compare
each frozen evaluator with 14 target benchmarks on score concordance and
pairwise agreement. The best evaluators order about 75% of candidate
pairs as the targets do, below the 91% ceiling set by disagreements
among the targets. No researcher leads on every metric, and the best
evaluator on development targets is not the best on sealed targets
hidden from the researcher. A human-designed sample of public tasks
remains a strong baseline, and the evaluator with the lowest recorded
execution cost attains the highest pairwise agreement. Agents repair
tasks and graders through pilot feedback, yet their evaluators can still
truncate answers, exhaust the evaluation budget, or let a few questions
dominate a domain score.

## 1 Introduction

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) relies on evaluation feedback to
determine whether a change improves a system and which direction to
explore next. Each new training recipe, dataset, or program creates
another version to assess. Repeatedly running complex benchmarks incurs
API and compute costs and delays the next experiment, particularly when
evaluation requires long tool-using trajectories or specialized
environments. Human experts reduce this burden by selecting
representative subsets or designing cheaper proxies, as in
tinyBenchmarks ([Maia Polo et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)). An autonomous
improvement loop would need to perform this design step itself and
obtain feedback that is informative enough to guide decisions and cheap
enough to repeat.

Recent work automates improvement while relying on supplied evaluation
protocols. In PostTrainBench, agents choose data and post-training
algorithms, but the target benchmark and evaluation harness are
fixed ([Rank et al., 2026](#bib.bib61)). RSIBench-Data lets agents
revise training-data strategies using checkpoint scores and trajectories
from a fixed evaluation service ([Meng et al., 2026](#bib.bib40)).
AlphaEvolve evolves programs using a user-provided evaluation function,
with optional user-specified test cascades to screen proposals
efficiently ([Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib46)). In each case, humans
decide what to test, how to score it, and how to keep repeated
measurement affordable, so extending autonomy to a new domain still
requires a human-designed evaluation.

Other work automates parts of evaluation design. PACE selects
inexpensive source questions and calibrates them against expensive
agentic targets ([Song et al., 2026](#bib.bib66)). AutoBencher and
BenchAgents automate benchmark construction and refinement ([Li et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31); [Butt et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)), and BenchBench
compares the quality of model-designed suites ([Zheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib87)). None of these settings poses the full research
problem, in which an agent chooses a measurement strategy, spends a
limited experimental budget, and revises an executable evaluator until
it tracks complex target benchmarks. Solving it requires inspecting
target evidence, choosing or creating tasks, verifying graders, and
deciding whether a pilot failure calls for harder questions, different
coverage, a repaired rubric, or more execution budget. We therefore ask
whether an agent with fixed time and API budgets can build an evaluator
that reproduces how complex target benchmarks score and rank models. We
call this capability autonomous evaluation research.

To address these gaps, we introduce EvalResearchBench (ERB), in which
the researcher designs the measurement itself, pays for every pilot from
a fixed budget, and submits an executable evaluator that is scored
against complex target benchmarks. A _researcher_ is a model in an agent
harness that designs the evaluator, a _candidate_ is a model being
tested, and a _target benchmark_ is an established evaluation whose
candidate scores the evaluator should reproduce. The researcher receives
target descriptions, permitted tasks and grading resources, execution
traces, development reference scores, and APIs for development
candidates
(Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
In pilot tests, it observes candidate responses, costs, and agreement
with the visible references, then revises its tasks, graders, weights,
and answer allowances. It then freezes an evaluator that returns one
score per domain. Sealed candidates and targets stay hidden during
research, so the researcher can neither call those candidates nor see
those reference scores. Independent assessment runs the frozen evaluator
on all candidates under a separate budget and compares its scores with
both development and sealed targets.

We evaluate 9 researcher configurations, each a model in the Codex or
Claude Code harness run for one episode, on 13 open-weight and
proprietary candidates and 14 target benchmarks in coding, co-work, and
reasoning
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Models. ‣ 4.1 Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
These domains cover the implementation, professional work, and analysis
that autonomous research relies on. As a baseline, Random120 samples
public tasks under fixed source quotas designed by humans.

We measure agreement in two ways. _Score_ is 100 times Lin’s concordance
correlation coefficient between measured and reference scores ([Lin,
1989](#bib.bib33)), so it rewards matching score level and spread as
well as order. _Pairwise agreement_ (PA) is the fraction of candidate
pairs that the evaluator orders as the target does. We also separate the
one-time cost of building an evaluator from the cost of running it,
which recurs for every new candidate.

No evaluator leads in every domain
(Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
Grok 4.7 leads coding, Astra leads co-work, and Opus 5 leads reasoning,
while coding is the weakest domain for most evaluators. Astra attains
the highest overall Score and Grok 4.7 the highest PA. Even the best PA,
about 75%, stays well below the 91% ceiling set by disagreements among
the targets. Fable 5.1 has the highest Score on development targets but
not on sealed ones, so fitting visible references does not ensure
transfer. Random120 remains a strong baseline. On identical candidates,
seven of eight agent-designed evaluators exceed it in Score, but only
four exceed it in PA. Evaluators with similar construction cost differ
markedly in execution cost, and the one with the lowest recorded
execution cost attains the highest PA. Agents repair tasks and graders
through pilot feedback, yet their final evaluators can still truncate
answers, exhaust the assessment budget, or let a few heavily weighted
questions dominate a domain score.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Evaluation Design and Efficient Measurement

Evaluation design spans coverage and standardization ([Ribeiro et al.,
2020](#bib.bib63); [Liang et al., 2023](#bib.bib32); [Kiela et al.,
2021](#bib.bib28)), task construction ([Perez et al., 2023](#bib.bib55);
[Liu et al., 2023a](#bib.bib35); [Xia et al., 2024](#bib.bib79)), and
automated grading ([Liu et al., 2023c](#bib.bib37); [Zheng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib86)). AutoBencher refines datasets using model
feedback ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)); BenchAgents integrates
planning, generation, verification, and evaluation ([Butt et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12)); BenchBench assesses benchmark designers through
validity, discrimination, and ranking reliability ([Zheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib87)). Efficient measurement offers another route: APTBench
transforms agent tasks and trajectories into lightweight questions ([Qin
et al., 2025](#bib.bib57)), while tinyBenchmarks, Fluid Benchmarking,
and Efficient Benchmarking of AI Agents study selection, estimation, and
adaptive testing ([Maia Polo et al., 2024](#bib.bib39); [Hofmann et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23); [Ndzomga, 2026](#bib.bib45)). Agent Psychometrics
models task-level performance from task and system features ([Ge et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18)). ERB evaluates an agent’s ability to choose and
develop measurement strategies through autonomous, budgeted
experimentation.

### 2.2 Autonomous Research and Self-Improvement

Research benchmarks study iterative experimentation ([Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24); [Nathani et al., 2025](#bib.bib43)), resource-bounded
machine-learning research ([Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib13); [Wijk
et al., 2025](#bib.bib76)), replication ([Starace et al.,
2025](#bib.bib67)), and post-training ([Rank et al., 2026](#bib.bib61)).
The AI Scientist and Agent Laboratory integrate multiple stages of
scientific work ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib38); [Schmidgall et al.,
2025](#bib.bib65)). Self-improvement systems such as FunSearch,
AlphaEvolve, and the Darwin Gödel Machine use evaluation feedback to
guide program or agent revisions ([Romera-Paredes et al.,
2024](#bib.bib64); [Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib46); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib85)); MIRROR and TRIAGE study self-knowledge and resource
allocation ([Wang, 2026](#bib.bib74); [Nazi & Dipta, 2026](#bib.bib44)).
ERB focuses on constructing the feedback instrument itself. Following
artifact-based research benchmarks, it assesses the submitted evaluation
independently of the process that produced it.

## 3 EvalResearchBench

### 3.1 Research Task

#### Researcher, candidates, and targets.

The _researcher_ is an LLM agent equipped with a harness that lets it
inspect files, write and execute code, call models, and revise its work.
A _candidate_ is a model–execution configuration whose capabilities the
researcher must measure. A _target benchmark_ provides the reference
candidate scores that the resulting evaluation should reproduce.

We cover three complementary capabilities needed for autonomous
research. _Coding_ supports implementing and testing systems, from
repository engineering to scientific programming and terminal
workflows ([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Tian et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib69); [Merrill et al., 2026](#bib.bib42)). _Co-work_
covers gathering information, following constraints, and producing
professional deliverables ([He et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Wei et al.,
2025](#bib.bib75); [Patwardhan et al., 2025](#bib.bib54)). _Reasoning_
covers analytical work with academic questions, long documents, and
specialized physics ([Phan et al., 2025](#bib.bib56); [Artificial
Analysis, 2025a](#bib.bib5); [Zhu et al., 2025](#bib.bib89)). A single
research episode designs an evaluation across all three domains,
allowing the agent to share resources and choose where further testing
is most useful.

#### Input materials.

We provide target descriptions, available original tasks and grading
resources, historical execution traces, and reference scores for
development candidates. The researcher also receives metered
candidate-model APIs, an execution SDK, and the submission contract.
Resource availability varies by target; the agent may select existing
tasks, synthesize new ones, or combine both approaches. Development
tests return candidate responses and grading results for diagnosing
errors and improving correlation with the visible target references.

#### Budgets.

We constrain three costs: researcher inference pays for the agent’s
analysis and tool use; development testing pays for candidate and
helper-model calls; independent assessment pays to execute the frozen
design. Keeping the first two budgets separate prevents agent
deliberation from consuming the testing allowance. A fixed deadline
limits research time.
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")
gives the numerical limits.

The researcher may select, synthesize, or simplify tasks, repair
graders, change task weights, set answer allowances, and inspect pilot
responses. Calls and retries pass through a metered gateway. The agent
allocates its development budget across exploration, testing, and
revision without a prescribed sequence.

#### Submission.

To require a reusable test suite rather than a few demonstrations, the
agent submits at least 100 distinct scored questions, together with
executable graders and fixed aggregation rules. The program returns one
measured score per domain from candidate responses and frozen assets,
without candidate-identity lookup or access to reference labels during
scoring. Questions, grading code, and weights are frozen before
assessment. One ERB entry is one research episode by a particular model
and harness.

### 3.2 Independent Assessment

To test transfer beyond development feedback, we execute the frozen
program independently on development and withheld candidates and compare
its scores with visible and withheld target references. Candidate access
and target-reference access are assigned separately
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Models. ‣ 4.1 Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).

The executor records per-item rewards, termination reasons, and usage. A
total assessment cap limits the cost of evaluating the candidate panel,
while a per-candidate cap bounds the cost of applying the design to any
one model. Assessment is charged separately from development, and its
results are not returned to the researcher for further revision.

A graded response is complete under the submitted contract, including a
wrong or empty answer at the output cap. Infrastructure failures and
budget-stopped items remain incomplete. Let $`C_{a,m,d}`$ be the
completed items for researcher $`a`$, candidate $`m`$, and domain $`d`$,
where $`s_{a,m,i}`$ is the item grade and $`w_{a,i}\geq 0`$ is the
weight assigned by the researcher to question $`i`$. Weights specify
each question’s contribution to the domain score and are fixed before
assessment. Its measured score is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
x_{a,m,d}=\frac{\sum_{i\in C_{a,m,d}}w_{a,i}s_{a,m,i}}{\sum_{i\in C_{a,m,d}}w_{a,i}}.
``` |  | (1) |

A domain with no completed positive-weight item is missing. Coverage
accompanies partial executions. Recorded expenditure separates
researcher inference, development testing, and assessment, with
unresolved reservations accounted for separately.

### 3.3 Score Agreement and Ordering

We assess both reference score levels and candidate ordering. We define
*Score* as 100 times the concordance correlation coefficient
(CCC) ([Lin, 1989](#bib.bib33)); pairwise agreement (PA) measures
ordering.

For target $`t`$, match candidates with both an observed score
$`x_{a,m,d(t)}`$ and a reference $`y_{m,t}`$. An eligible target has at
least six matches and nonconstant references. Measured scores keep their
native $`[0,1]`$ grading scale. Percentage references are divided by
100; Elo references use the minimum and range of the fixed reference
panel. These constants are shared across researchers
(Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Target-Level Assessment and Score Scales ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
Each submitted domain score is compared unchanged with every eligible
target in that domain.

Let $`\bar{x}`$, $`\bar{y}`$, $`\operatorname{Var}(x)`$,
$`\operatorname{Var}(y)`$, and $`\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)`$ be population
moments over matched candidates. Then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{Score}_{a,t}=100\frac{2\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)+\operatorname{Var}(y)+(\bar{x}-\bar{y})^{2}},\qquad\mathrm{Score}_{a,\mathcal{G}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{G}}\mathrm{Score}_{a,t}.
``` |  | (2) |

Exact agreement gives Score 100; constant measurements give zero, and
opposing candidate differences can give negative values. CCC combines
correlation with a penalty for mean and scale mismatch.
Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1.SSS0.Px1 "Incompatibility of two coding references. ‣ A.1 Representative target scatterplots ‣ Appendix A Target-Level Assessment and Score Scales ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")
analyzes compatibility among the target score scales.

For the non-tied reference pairs
$`\mathcal{P}_{t}=\{(i,j):i<j,\ y_{i,t}\neq y_{j,t}\}`$ among matched
candidates, define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{PA}_{t}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{t}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}\left[\mathbf{1}\!\left\{(x_{i,d}-x_{j,d})(y_{i,t}-y_{j,t})>0\right\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{x_{i,d}=x_{j,d}\right\}\right].
``` |  | (3) |

Correct order receives one point, a measured tie half a point, and a
reversal zero. Reference ties are excluded; no significance threshold is
applied. Both metrics average targets equally within each domain or
visibility group; overall averages all eligible targets.

### 4.1 Setup

We compare how different LLM researchers use the available materials and
budget to build evaluations. The experiments first assess the resulting
measurements, then examine the design choices and costs behind them.

#### Budgets and implementation.

The research budget is three hours, \$100 for researcher inference, and
\$100 for shared development/helper testing. Independent assessment has
a \$120 total cap and a \$30 per-candidate cap. Pilots may use smaller
suites than the final submission; the researcher allocates its
development calls freely.

#### Public-task sampling baseline.

A research agent should be compared with a simpler way to build a broad
evaluation: sample questions from existing public sources. We use
*Random120*, a source-stratified baseline that fixes the number of
questions drawn from each source, ensuring coverage across task types
before sampling. Specifically, we select sixteen public task sources,
eligibility filters, source quotas, local graders, and equal item
weighting within each domain. A seeded draw within each source selects
40 coding, 40 co-work, and 40 reasoning tasks without using target
reference scores. We assess its twelve complete candidates against the
same fourteen targets
(Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Source-stratified Random120 baseline ‣ Appendix B Frozen-Suite Sensitivity and Source-Stratified Baseline ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).

#### Overall results and domain strengths.

Astra leads overall Score, whereas Grok leads PA: reproducing score
levels and recovering candidate order favor different designs. Domain
strengths also vary, with no researcher dominating both metrics across
all three domains. Decomposing CCC as $`rC_{b}`$ helps explain why:
Astra and GLM-5.3 have similar co-work correlations, but Astra aligns
more closely with the reference scale
(Table [10](#A2.T10 "Table 10 ‣ B.2 Matched-panel comparison and score decomposition ‣ Appendix B Frozen-Suite Sensitivity and Source-Stratified Baseline ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
A useful ordering alone does not guarantee faithful score reproduction.

#### Development and sealed targets reveal different strengths.

Fable 5.1 leads development Score, but Grok leads sealed Score. This
reversal makes cross-target transfer a distinct requirement for
evaluation research: a useful design must preserve capability signals
beyond the references used to develop it. Systematic score shifts across
targets within the same domain
(Figure [7](#A1.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ A.1 Representative target scatterplots ‣ Appendix A Target-Level Assessment and Score Scales ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?"))
further show why a strong fit to one benchmark does not establish a
broadly useful evaluator.

#### Public-task sampling is a strong baseline.

Random120’s public sources, domain quotas, and graders encode
task-design knowledge, making it a strong baseline, especially in
coding. Its coding and reasoning coverage recovers much of the candidate
ordering, while Astra’s Score gains concentrate in co-work and reasoning
(Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Public-task sampling is a strong baseline. ‣ 4.2 Main Results: Agreement with the Reference Panel ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
DeepSeek V4 Pro illustrates why iteration alone need not beat this
baseline. It revises coding tasks and increases reasoning allowances,
yet its short, deterministic coding and co-work checks yield scores
clustered near the ceiling; its reasoning gains cannot offset the coding
deficit. Research adds value when it identifies tasks that preserve
target-relevant differences between candidates. Ease of grading and
repeated revision do not by themselves ensure informative measurements.

### 4.3 What Shapes Evaluation Quality?

We analyze three design decisions—task selection, question count, and
aggregation—by varying one part of each evaluation while holding
candidate responses and references fixed.

#### Choose tasks for their measurement contribution.

K3 adds hard coding problems after near-ceiling pilots; Astra adds
repository traces after inspecting upstream software tasks. Removing
these groups, however, has only modest effects relative to same-domain,
same-size random deletions. K3’s addition improves Score but slightly
reduces PA; Astra’s repository group leaves PA unchanged. By contrast,
Astra’s spreadsheet tasks contribute strongly to both co-work metrics
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Choose tasks for their measurement contribution. ‣ 4.3 What Shapes Evaluation Quality? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")a,b).
Task realism and difficulty motivate a design, but its value depends on
how it separates candidates in relation to the target.

Many Astra spreadsheet responses exhaust their answer allowance, so the
observed separation reflects the task under its chosen execution
conditions. Grok responds to truncated hard-task pilots by favoring
shorter, self-contained checks and adjusting answer allowances. DeepSeek
instead expands reasoning allowances and selects short exact-answer
items. A short expected answer does not itself make a problem easy to
solve.

Figure 4: Task contribution and measurement variability. (a,b)
Full-minus-deleted Score and PA for seven task groups; parentheses give
question counts. Gray intervals cover the central 95% of 1,000
same-domain, same-size random deletions. (c,d) Mean overall Score and PA
across nested question subsets, with central 95% bands. (e,f) Their
standard deviations. Candidates, targets, and responses are fixed.

#### More questions improve both average quality and consistency.

We draw nested, domain-stratified subsets from the frozen Astra,
GLM-5.3, and K3 suites and recompute their scores on the same candidate
panel. As subsets grow, mean Score rises and both Score and PA fluctuate
less across question draws
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Choose tasks for their measurement contribution. ‣ 4.3 What Shapes Evaluation Quality? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")c–f).
Small subsets can omit useful task families or overrepresent a few
responses, even when their domain proportions are fixed. The design
problem is to retain this signal and consistency with fewer calls.

#### Use weights to preserve more than a development ordering.

Grok groups pilot questions by which development candidates pass, then
searches for weights that reproduce the visible ordering and score gaps.
For co-work, it assigns most weight to questions that all three tested
development candidates pass, using a small remainder to shape their
differences. Consequently, six of its twenty co-work questions carry
almost all scoring weight. Fable 5.1 distributes influence more evenly,
with a modest upweighting of professional tasks
(Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Use weights to preserve more than a development ordering. ‣ 4.3 What Shapes Evaluation Quality? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")a).

Recomputing Grok’s scores with equal item weights on the same responses
substantially improves coding and co-work Score, while overall PA barely
changes
(Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Use weights to preserve more than a development ordering. ‣ 4.3 What Shapes Evaluation Quality? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
The Score improvement also holds on the complete-item candidate subset.
The frozen suite therefore contains useful measurement signal that its
chosen weights underuse. Evaluation design must consider task
contribution, sampling variability, and score aggregation jointly.

### 4.4 What Makes an Evaluation Cost-Efficient?

#### Low execution cost can preserve measurement quality.

An evaluator is built once but may be run repeatedly, so we separate
construction cost from frozen assessment cost. Grok combines low
assessment cost with the highest overall PA and a competitive Score
(Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Low execution cost can preserve measurement quality. ‣ 4.4 What Makes an Evaluation Cost-Efficient? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?");
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.2 Main Results: Agreement with the Reference Panel ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
Grok and Fable 5.1 submit similar question counts, yet their execution
costs differ substantially. Task count alone therefore gives little
indication of an evaluator’s recurring cost.

#### Pilot feedback shapes answering and grading costs.

Grok’s hard HLE pilots exhaust their answer allowance without producing
visible answers. It responds by favoring short code, output predictions,
and numeric checks with local graders. Fable 5.1 retains longer
professional deliverables and increases their answer allowance after
truncated pilots; its rubric judge adds a substantial second expense.
Opus incurs little grading expense but still spends heavily on candidate
answers. These trajectories identify two distinct design choices behind
execution cost: what a candidate must produce and how its answer is
judged
(Appendix [A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Task design, execution cost, and score concentration ‣ Appendix A Target-Level Assessment and Score Scales ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).

## 5 Case Study: How Researchers Revise Evaluations

Researchers use development feedback to expand coverage, repair tasks,
and adjust execution. Astra develops repository-tracing tasks from
source inspection, resolves prompt–rubric mismatches through pilot
feedback, and adjusts execution after truncated responses. GLM corrects
invalid examples and numerical answers, while K3 adds harder coding
tasks after near-ceiling pilot scores.

analyzes these design choices;
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Case Study: How Researchers Revise Evaluations ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")
details the cases.

## 6 Conclusion

ERB measures autonomous evaluation research under fixed budgets.
Independent assessment separates score reproduction, ordering, and
execution cost. Source inspection and pilot audits reveal how agents
revise tasks and graders; component and subset analyses show why
difficulty, nominal size, and measurement value must be assessed
separately. This establishes an executable benchmark for the
evaluation-design capability that autonomous improvement requires.

#### Incompatibility of two coding references.

A single domain score is compared with several target references, which
need not agree in level or ordering. We first quantify how this
disagreement limits raw-score reproduction. ProgramBench (Vals) and
SWE-bench Multilingual share six reference-bearing candidates. Let
$`y_{m,1}`$ and $`y_{m,2}`$ be their fixed-scale reference scores and
$`x_{m}`$ a single coding score for candidate $`m`$. The midpoint
$`x_{m}=(y_{m,1}+y_{m,2})/2`$ minimizes pooled squared error to both
references, giving

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{6}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{12}\sum_{m=1}^{6}\sum_{j=1}^{2}(x_{m}-y_{m,j})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{6}\sum_{m=1}^{6}(y_{m,1}-y_{m,2})^{2}}=0.4356.
``` |  | (4) |

The reference means are 0.0075 and 0.876 on the same six candidates. The
resulting raw-score error floor belongs to the reference pair; CCC also
depends on covariance and dispersion. PA separately reports ordering.

The same calculation extends to every reference in a domain. For target
set $`T_{d}`$, minimize the target-equal mean squared error
$`|T_{d}|^{-1}\sum_{t\in T_{d}}|\mathcal{M}_{t}|^{-1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{t}}(x_{m}-y_{m,t})^{2}`$
over one common $`x_{m}\in[0,1]`$ per candidate. Its exact minimizer is
the weighted mean of that candidate’s available reference scores, with
target $`t`$ weighted by $`1/|\mathcal{M}_{t}|`$. The resulting oracle
RMSE floors are:

| Domain    | Development | Sealed | Overall |
|-----------|-------------|--------|---------|
| Coding    | 0.209       | 0.353  | 0.303   |
| Co-work   | 0.074       | 0.088  | 0.128   |
| Reasoning | 0.000       | 0.284  | 0.244   |

These label-informed values characterize the reference panel’s raw-score
compatibility. The zero for single-target development reasoning follows
from assigning its reference score directly; the RMSE floors are
distinct from CCC ceilings.

#### Compatible-reference sensitivity.

To examine how reference-scale differences affect the results, we form
an exploratory target subset without reading any researcher score: in
each domain, choose the largest group in which every pair shares at
least six reference-bearing candidates and has reference-score RMSE at
most 0.25; break ties by the group’s mean pairwise RMSE. This selects
SWE-bench Multilingual, SWE-bench (Vals), and Terminal-Bench 2.1 for
coding; AdvancedIF, BrowseComp, and GDPval for co-work; and HLE and
CritPt for reasoning. Four selected targets are development-visible and
four are sealed. Target-equal Score and PA on this eight-target subset
are:

| Frozen artifact | Score $`\uparrow`$ | PA (%) $`\uparrow`$ |
|-----------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| Fable 5.1       | 33.47              | 69.57               |
| Astra           | 27.51              | 67.08               |
| Grok            | 23.95              | 75.06               |
| Sol             | 22.31              | 59.82               |
| Opus 5          | 22.29              | 62.73               |
| K3              | 22.12              | 73.08               |
| DeepSeek V4 Pro | 17.46              | 65.00               |

This scale-based sensitivity checks how target composition affects

#### Reference-imposed ordering limit.

Reference targets in one domain sometimes order the same candidate pair
differently. We calculate the largest PA attainable by *any* single
strict candidate ordering per domain on the fixed panel. Every target
and every non-tied pair within it receives exactly the weight used in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Score Agreement and Ordering ‣ 3 EvalResearchBench ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?");
an exact subset dynamic program maximizes the weighted agreement. A
measured tie earns the average of its two possible orientations and
cannot improve the optimum. This label-informed oracle is an upper bound
for a frozen evaluator, not a deployable score:

| Domain      | Development | Sealed | Overall |
|-------------|-------------|--------|---------|

#### Observed decisions behind the allowances.

Fable 5.1’s early valid snapshots contain 145–153 items including
ArXivMath ([Dekoninck et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)). Its pilot tool outputs

completed development pass then records nine capped GDPval answers out

results. Slow candidate calls and late grading failures delay the
response matrix needed for weight selection, so the final suite retains
its predefined family weights. Monetary spend, feedback latency, and
deployment cost are therefore distinct constraints on evaluation
research.

### C.1 Grok: from pilot response patterns to frozen weights

Grok first probes hard HLE and coding tasks, encounters truncated
responses, and builds a bank of shorter, locally graded questions. The
frozen suite contains 62 coding, 20 co-work, and 25 reasoning items. Its

constrains relative differences without matching the reference mean. Six
co-work items receive 93.87% of total weight; the weight-equivalent
count $`(\sum_{i}w_{i})^{2}/\sum_{i}w_{i}^{2}`$ is 6.78. This quantity
describes weight concentration, not the number of statistically
independent questions.

### C.2 DeepSeek V4 Pro: grading convenience and measurement coverage

DeepSeek’s source inspection identifies deterministic grading as a
practical way to keep evaluation inexpensive. It constructs Python
output-prediction, function-writing, and bug-fixing tasks, plus co-work
checks for specified text, numbers, and formats. For example, one
co-work question asks for a letter count but supplies the required
output string; another asks for time conversion and supplies the
converted time. Such checks measure compliance with an explicit response
more directly than independent professional problem solving.

The trajectory includes genuine revisions: coding tasks are hardened, a
domain-name mismatch in feedback analysis is repaired, and truncated
reasoning pilots lead to larger answer allowances and a different
question mix. The final reasoning bank includes short-answer HLE
questions whose expected answers are easy to parse but whose
mathematical content remains demanding. Selecting a short answer
simplifies grading, not necessarily reasoning. Final item counts are 38
coding, 51 co-work, and 30 reasoning, with equal within-domain weights.

On the matched panel, DeepSeek’s coding and co-work means are high and
its concordance calibration factors are low
(Table [10](#A2.T10 "Table 10 ‣ B.2 Matched-panel comparison and score decomposition ‣ Appendix B Frozen-Suite Sensitivity and Source-Stratified Baseline ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
Its reasoning Score exceeds Random120’s, whereas coding accounts for the
largest deficit
(Table [9](#A2.T9 "Table 9 ‣ B.2 Matched-panel comparison and score decomposition ‣ Appendix B Frozen-Suite Sensitivity and Source-Stratified Baseline ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")).
These observations support the interpretation that simplifying task form
can preserve answer checkability while compressing the capability
differences the proxy is intended to recover. They do not isolate task
content from grading and execution as separate causal interventions.

## Appendix D Research Instructions and Inputs

Researchers are asked to design one executable evaluation with at least
100 distinct scored questions spanning coding, co-work, and reasoning.
They may select, adapt, or synthesize tasks and choose graders, weights,
grouping, and output allowances. During development, researchers compare
directly measured candidate-domain scores with visible target-reference
scores; final assessment reports Score and PA for frozen programs.

The supplied workspace contains the task contract, development candidate
APIs and available reference scores, original benchmark materials,
available trajectories, and a Python evaluation SDK. The SDK submits a
suite, records item-level responses and costs, and returns cross-model
feedback on completed development tests. Researchers decide what to
test, revise, and freeze within the three-hour research period, \$100
researcher wallet, and \$100 shared development/helper wallet. Frozen
assessment has a separate \$120 wallet and \$30 per-candidate cap.

The final program supplies executable questions, grading rules, and
three fixed domain aggregations. Completed wrong or empty answers retain
their grades; infrastructure failures remain incomplete. After freezing,
the executor runs the candidate panel without sending assessment results
back to the researcher.

## Appendix E Case Study: How Researchers Revise Evaluations

The aggregate analyses show which design choices affect measurement, but
do not explain how agents arrive at them. We now follow the saved
research trajectories from source inspection through pilot testing and
revision. Astra provides a concrete example of this loop; GLM and K3
illustrate different responses to development feedback.

#### Astra turns source inspection into a coverage hypothesis.

A compact coding evaluation can miss repository-level behavior if it
concentrates on isolated implementations. After inspecting upstream
repository-investigation tasks, Astra identifies a gap in its
implementation-heavy coding bank and constructs eight multi-file
repository traces. In one, candidates predict cached outputs and
recomputation counts after in-place mutation versus replacing a
dependency object. Running the supplied fixture generates the expected
outputs for grading
(Figure [13](#A5.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ Pilot feedback can expose defects in the evaluation itself. ‣ Appendix E Case Study: How Researchers Revise Evaluations ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")a).

#### Pilot feedback can expose defects in the evaluation itself.

A low pilot score may expose an unclear question or an overly
restrictive grader, as well as a candidate error. Astra examines these
possibilities in a performance-plan memo that scores 11/12: its response
reports that 3/13 orders finish on time, but omits the mean close time
required by the rubric. Astra finds that the question never explicitly
requested that statistic and adds it to the brief. A later audit also
relaxes a cloud-migration rubric that prescribed one architecture
despite an open-ended brief, retaining the stated security and delivery
requirements
(Figure [13](#A5.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ Pilot feedback can expose defects in the evaluation itself. ‣ Appendix E Case Study: How Researchers Revise Evaluations ‣ EvalResearchBench: Can AI Agents Design Their Own Evaluations?")b,c).
These revisions align prompts and graders before freezing; the saved
pilot score precedes the repairs.
````

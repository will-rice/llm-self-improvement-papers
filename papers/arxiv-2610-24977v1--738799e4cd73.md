###### Abstract.

Large language models (LLMs) have shown strong promise in
specification-to-RTL generation. Among the explorations, the
_self-correction paradigm_ has significantly advanced LLM-based RTL
generation. However, two challenges limit further progress. Challenge on
benchmarks. Existing benchmarks are saturated by advanced LLMs, leaving
little room to evaluate the agentic self-correction framework. Challenge
on methodology. The _monolithic self-correction_ paradigm rewrites the
entire design at once, where LLMs are susceptible to error confusion and
corruption of originally correct code. To address the benchmark
challenge, we contribute ScaleBench, a more challenging benchmark of 200
complex RTL designs. To address the challenge on methodology, we propose
CorrectRTL, an agentic framework that realizes a new
decompose-then-correct paradigm, in which the LLM agent explicitly
decouples scaffold construction from detailed implementation.
_CorrectRTL_ contributes three core techniques: (1) *Structure-oriented
decomposition*, (2) *Snippet-based RAG*, and (3) *SVA-based
self-correction*. On ScaleBench, CorrectRTL improves Pass@1 from 20.5%
to 49.0% (+28.5%). On VerilogEval, CorrectRTL achieves up to 96.1%
Pass@1, demonstrating consistent improvements across both simple and
complex designs.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are rapidly transforming hardware design
automation, from RTL generation ([Pearce et al., 2020](#bib.bib16);
[Thakur et al., 2024](#bib.bib4); [Chang et al., 2023](#bib.bib17); [Liu
et al., 2024b](#bib.bib6); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)) to
verification and debugging ([Kang et al., 2024](#bib.bib19); [Yao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Tsai et al., 2024](#bib.bib12); [Fang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8)). Among the various stages of the design flow,
specification-to-RTL generation has attracted intensive attention ([Liu
et al., 2023](#bib.bib9); [Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib10); [Pinckney et
al., 2024](#bib.bib5); [Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib18); [Ho et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)). LLMs can understand hardware specifications and
produce synthesizable designs. Such LLM-assisted design flows are
emerging as a compelling paradigm that promises to fundamentally reshape
hardware development.

Recently, a new paradigm ([Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib18); [Ho et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)) achieves promising results on VerilogEval ([Liu et
al., 2023](#bib.bib9)), one of the most commonly used RTL generation
benchmarks. We refer to this paradigm as the _self-correction_ paradigm,
in which the model first generates RTL code and then corrects it based
on verification feedback. Specifically, MAGE ([Zhao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)) and VerilogCoder ([Ho et al., 2024](#bib.bib2))
achieve pass rates exceeding 94%, approaching near-perfect functional
correctness. These two SOTA works show a more promising direction for
automated RTL generation than other specification-based prompt
engineering strategies. One important reason is that simple prompt
engineering on specifications for LLMs is inherently hard to address
detailed signal-level bugs. However, the existing _self-correction_
works ([Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib18); [Ho et al., 2024](#bib.bib2))
rely on manually written golden testbenches ([Ho et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2); [Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)). In real
applications, manually crafting golden testbenches for every module is
impractical. To further advance the self-correction paradigm toward
greater automation, some works begin to explore auto-generated
testbenches ([Tan et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15); [Sami et al., 2024a](#bib.bib1); [Tsai et al.,
2024](#bib.bib12)) or Python reference models ([Tan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)). However, two important challenges significantly
limit the effectiveness and further advancement of the research
direction.

Challenge on benchmarks. Existing benchmarks such as VerilogEval ([Liu
et al., 2023](#bib.bib9)) and RTLLM ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib10))
consist predominantly of simple designs. Direct LLM generation already
achieves over 80% pass rate on these benchmarks, and the remaining
failures are dominated by a small set of recurring error patterns. This
leaves little room to differentiate methods or to evaluate whether
_self-correction_ techniques genuinely generalize, rather than merely
fixing a narrow class of easy errors. A more challenging benchmark with
more complex designs is needed to meaningfully assess the effectiveness
of self-correction approaches.

Challenge on methodology. We refer to the current approach as the
_monolithic self-correction_ paradigm, in which LLMs generate a full
verification reference (e.g., a testbench or a Python reference model)
and apply corrections to the entire design as a whole. This paradigm
faces two fundamental difficulties. Difficulty 1: reference difficulty.
The correctness of the verification reference itself is inherently hard
to guarantee. Auto-generated testbenches or reference code are not
inherently more reliable than the RTL they are meant to verify.
Generating a complete testbench or reference model becomes increasingly
challenging as design scale increases. Difficulty 2: correction
effectiveness. Monolithic correction forces the LLM to simultaneously
reason about the global structure and local behavioral logic. This
entanglement of the whole design leads to two common failure reasons:
(1) The model becomes _confused_ by the redundant context and fails to
identify the actual errors. (2)Even if the error is correctly localized,
the model _corrupts originally correct code_ while attempting to fix the
error, because it inadvertently modifies the surrounding code. As design
complexity grows, these failure modes compound, making monolithic
self-correction increasingly unreliable.

To address the above challenges, we make two contributions. (1) New
benchmark. We propose ScaleBench, a benchmark containing larger and more
complex designs that are substantially more challenging than those in
existing benchmarks ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib10); [Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib9); [Pinckney et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)). (2) New paradigm.
We propose a new self-correction paradigm. For reference, we propose
using SVA-based verification because SVA is lightweight, thus inherently
shorter and easier to generate. Generating SVAs will not become more
challenging with the design scaling up. For correction effectiveness, we
propose to _decompose and correct_. This paradigm decouples structural
scaffold construction from behavioral detail implementation. We
implement an agentic framework, CorrectRTL, to instantiate and validate
this paradigm, as depicted in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition").
_CorrectRTL_ involves three key techniques to realize our paradigm.¹¹ 1
The benchmark and dataset will be open-sourced at
[https://github.com/hkust-zhiyao/CorrectRTL](https://github.com/hkust-zhiyao/CorrectRTL).

(1) Structure-oriented decomposition. We observe that LLMs are
remarkably capable of constructing the _structural scaffold_ of a
design, such as declaring modules, defining port interfaces, and
organizing sequential and combinational blocks. But LLMs frequently fail
at _behavioral details_ within individual blocks. This asymmetry
motivates us to explicitly decouple scaffold construction from detail
implementation. So that LLMs first produce a well-organized scaffold via
specification decomposition into block-level instructions, and
self-correction is then focused exclusively on detail errors within
individual blocks without disrupting the overall architecture.

(2) Snippet-based RAG. We further observe that LLMs are often _stubborn_
on detail-level errors. General skill injection through prompt
engineering or high-level design guidelines fails to correct many
recurring mistakes. Because the model lacks the precise,
context-specific knowledge needed for each logic block. This motivates
us to design a RAG system that provides exact snippet-level examples of
correct implementations for the specific behavioral patterns the LLM
struggles with. Our decomposition naturally serves this purpose.
Specifically, the decomposed instruction can serve as a query to
retrieve relevant examples.

(3) SVA-based self-correction. We propose using lightweight
SystemVerilog Assertions (SVAs) that target a single logic property of
an individual block. Each SVA checks one focused property, so it is
easier to generate correctly than a full golden testbench. An EDA tool
checks the SVA against the block, and any counterexample pinpoints the
faulty block and drives a targeted rewrite. This enables block-level
error identification and correction without a manually written golden
testbench.

## 2. Preliminary

### 2.1. Related Work

In this section, we mainly focus on automated self-correction on RTL
generation ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Tan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Sami et al., 2024b](#bib.bib22); [Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15); [Yao et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)).
Table [1](#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition")
summarizes the related works in the scope. Among these works, some very
early works explored syntax identification ([Yao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib7); [Tsai et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)), which is not our
focus. We separate the self-correction works on RTL into two main types:
(1) _self-correction_ relying on a manually written golden testbench,
and (2) _self-correction_ without a manually written golden testbench.

Self-correction without golden testbench is the most challenging yet
meaningful direction. Existing works explore automated testbench
generation ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib15)) and Python reference
code ([Tan et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)). However, both face fundamental
reliability issues: auto-generated testbenches are harder to guarantee
correct than RTL itself, and Python reference models remain challenging
for precise hardware description. Critically, both approaches focus on
the _verification_ side—they cannot inject domain knowledge that the LLM
lacks.

RAG for code generation. Retrieval-augmented generation has been widely
applied in software code generation ([Gao et al., 2024b](#bib.bib25)),
but remains unexplored for RTL. Standard code RAG retrieves whole files
or functions, which is too coarse-grained for hardware designs where a
single incorrect bit width or timing polarity causes functional failure.
CorrectRTL introduces _snippet-level RAG_ specifically designed for RTL:
decomposed code blocks paired with specification sections, retrieved via
a hardware-aware hybrid retriever.

CorrectRTL takes a fundamentally different approach: rather than
improving verification feedback alone, we _inject design-family
knowledge_ into the generation process via snippet-based RAG, while
using SVA-based self-correction as a complementary error identification
mechanism. The structure-oriented decomposition serves as the
architectural backbone, enabling both fine-grained generation and RAG
database construction.

### 2.2. Problem Formulation

Table [2](#S2.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 2.1. Related Work ‣ 2. Preliminary ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition")
summarizes the notation. LLM-based RTL generation with self-correction
follows a _generate-then-correct_ paradigm. Given a design specification
$`\mathcal{S}`$, an LLM $`f_{\theta}`$ produces RTL code $`\mathcal{C}`$
and a verification artifact $`\mathcal{A}`$:

|     |     |                                                                                                |     |
| --- | --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
| (1) |     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=f_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}),\qquad\mathcal{A}=f_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})`$ |     |

An EDA tool checks $`\mathcal{C}`$ against $`\mathcal{A}`$, yielding
error feedback $`\mathcal{F}=\textsc{Verify}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A})`$,
which guides correction:
$`\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=f_{\theta}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F},\mathcal{A})`$.

The verification artifact $`\mathcal{A}`$ differentiates existing
self-correction paradigms: golden testbenches ([Ho et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2); [Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)) (effective but
impractical at scale), auto-generated testbenches ([Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15)) (harder to guarantee correct than RTL itself), Python
reference models ([Tan et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)) (challenging for
precise hardware semantics), and _verification SVAs_ (ours). Each SVA
targets a single logic property, making it easier to generate correctly
than a full testbench.

Regardless of the choice of $`\mathcal{A}`$, generation quality is
bounded by $`f_{\theta}`$’s domain knowledge. CorrectRTL addresses this
by injecting verified design-family knowledge from a curated RAG
database $`\mathcal{D}`$ into both RTL and SVA generation:

|     |     |                                                                                                             |     |
| --- | --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
| (2) |     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=f_{\theta}\!\big(\mathcal{S},\;\textsc{Retrieve}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{D})\big)`$ |     |

This improves both phases at once. It produces better RTL in Phase I and
better SVAs in Phase II, rather than relying on verification feedback
alone. Our paradigm can boost the performance on both generation and
correction steps.

### 3.2. RAG Database

General rule-based skill injection, or prompt engineering, is
insufficient to augment LLM-based RTL generation for the detailed
functional behaviors that LLMs often lack. To address this limitation,
we curate a RAG database to provide LLM-based agents with detailed
functional knowledge. We deliberately organize this database at the
_snippet (block) level_ rather than the whole-design level, for two
reasons. _Reason 1._ Individual blocks—particularly their RTL bodies—are
directly reusable as code exemplars, whereas whole designs mix unrelated
logic and cannot be inserted into a partially-generated module. _Reason 2._ A short, behavior-focused spec snippet yields a much tighter
semantic signal than a full specification, so top-$`k`$ retrieval
returns exemplars that match the queried sub-behavior rather than a
loosely-related design.

Overview. The RAG database has two sub-datasets that share the same
retrieval key. The first is the _block-level dataset_, which consists of
(spec-snippet, RTL-block) pairs: each pair binds a short
natural-language description to a single structural block (e.g., always
block, assign group, or memory declaration) extracted from a verified
golden design. The second is the _SVA-template-level dataset_, which
consists of formally-proven SystemVerilog assertions; each SVA template
is a specific assertion that has been _verified_ on the golden RTL and
is attached to one RTL block from the block-level dataset, so every SVA
template inherits a corresponding spec-snippet and code block. Both
sub-datasets are curated on top of existing large-scale RTL training
corpora (MG-Verilog ([Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib24)), OriGen ([Cui et
al., 2024](#bib.bib3)), AutoVCoder ([Gao et al., 2024a](#bib.bib21)),
RTLCoder ([Liu et al., 2024b](#bib.bib6)), _etc._). After
whitespace-normalized de-duplication, the block-level dataset contains
$`9{,}246`$ unique (spec-snippet, RTL-block) pairs. The
SVA-template-level dataset contains roughly $`5`$k assertions that
JasperGold proves on the parent golden, spanning eight invariant
families
(Table [4](#S3.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 3.1. ScaleBench: A Challenging RTL Benchmark ‣ 3. Benchmark and Dataset ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition")).

![Refer to caption](2610.24977v1/RAG-dataset-workflow.png)

Figure 3. Two-step workflow of RAG dataset construction.

Step 1: Block-level dataset curation. A Verilog parser decomposes each
RTL module in the source corpus into its constituent structural blocks.
We treat four block types: module-header / port declarations, sequential
always blocks, combinational always blocks, and assign groups.
Figure [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.2. RAG Database ‣ 3. Benchmark and Dataset ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition")
shows an example for a 16-bit adder, where the parser extracts the
module header at the top and the per-bit and per-half adders as
sequential or combinational leaf blocks below. For each block, we pass
both the block code and the module’s full design specification to an
agent whose task is to produce a 1–3-sentence spec snippet capturing the
block’s behavioral semantics. Because specifications in public training
corpora are often incomplete or loosely worded, the agent is not
restricted to pure extraction: when the block’s code exposes logic that
the surrounding specification underspecifies or omits, the agent may
augment the snippet with behavior directly inferred from the RTL. The
resulting (spec-snippet, RTL-block) pair forms one entry of the
block-level dataset.

![Refer to caption](2610.24977v1/adder-structure.png)

Figure 4. Hierarchical tree $`\mathcal{T}`$ for a 16-bit adder.

Step 2: SVA-template-level dataset curation. Building on the Step 1
output, the agent receives each (spec-snippet, RTL-block) pair and
generates a set of candidate SystemVerilog assertions labeled with an
invariant family (e.g., reset initialization, past-value relation,
handshake stability). Each candidate is then inserted into the original
golden RTL and discharged by JasperGold. Only assertions that the tool
_proves_ on the golden are retained and become SVA templates in the
database. A $`1{,}000`$-SVA calibration across the eight invariant
families (125 per category, 855 unique designs) yields a $`44\%`$
overall proof rate. The per-family rate ranges from $`58\%`$ on the
easiest family (reset initialization) to $`32\%`$ on the hardest
(counter bounding). At the full corpus size this projects to roughly
$`5`$k formally-verified templates.

The two sub-datasets are jointly indexed by a single dense embedding
over the spec-snippet field. A single retrieval therefore returns both a
reference code pattern and its verified assertion oracle for the same
block family, eliminating the cold-start problem of prior RAG approaches
that index arbitrary unverified code.

## 4. Methodology

CorrectRTL assigns agents two roles. Structural agents decompose
$`\mathcal{S}`$ into block-level tasks using curated rules such as
module boundaries, reset conventions, and port naming. Detail agents
generate block-level RTL and SVA using snippet-based RAG, because much
domain-specific logic cannot be captured by rules alone.

### 4.1. Framework Overview

Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1. ScaleBench: A Challenging RTL Benchmark ‣ 3. Benchmark and Dataset ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition")
depicts the workflow of the CorrectRTL framework, which operates in four
phases.

Phase 1: Decomposition (_structural agents_). Depending on design
complexity, CorrectRTL applies one of two decomposition strategies. _(1)
Code-level decomposition_: the LLM generates the full design from
$`\mathcal{S}`$, and structural parsing extracts blocks from the
generated RTL for targeted verification. The following self-correction
process is based on the block level. _(2) Spec-level decomposition_: a
decomposer agent extracts a hierarchical structure tree $`\mathcal{T}`$
from $`\mathcal{S}`$, and paired instructors generate RTL and SVA
instruction pairs for each block. Both strategies define the block
boundaries shared across generation, retrieval, and verification.

Phase 2: RAG-Augmented RTL and SVA Generation (_detail agents_). For
each block, $`\textsc{Retrieve}(q_{i},\mathcal{D})`$ fetches the
top-$`K`$ (spec-snippet, code-block, proven-SVA) entries from the RAG
database. The query $`q_{i}`$ is the block’s spec instruction under
spec-level decomposition, or an LLM-authored one-sentence description of
the parsed block under code-level decomposition. One agent generates the
RTL block $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$ using the instruction and the retrieved
code blocks. A second agent generates one SVA $`\mathcal{A}_{i}`$ using
the same retrieval. The two agents share the retrieval so the RTL and
the SVA are grounded in the same verified references. Under spec-level
decomposition, a constructor assembles the blocks into the full module
$`\mathcal{C}`$.

Phase 3: SVA-Based Verification. For each block $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$, an
EDA tool checks $`\mathcal{A}_{i}`$ against $`\mathcal{C}`$. If the
assertion produces a counterexample, the block is flagged for rewriting.
SVAs target single logic properties derived from the specification, so
they serve as the oracle for block-level correctness.

Phase 4: Targeted Rewriting. For each flagged block, a rewriter agent
reads the counterexample trace, identifies the root cause, and produces
a corrected block $`\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\prime}`$. Only the failing block
is replaced:
$`\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\textsc{Replace}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}_{i},\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\prime})`$.
Phases 3 and 4 iterate until every block passes its SVA, with a bounded
round cap to avoid oscillation.

### 4.2. Structure-Oriented Decomposition

Phase 1 fixes the block boundaries that all later phases use. We
introduce two baselines and then propose our decomposition strategy. The
first baseline is _code-level decomposition_, which lets the LLM
generate the whole RTL in one pass and parses blocks out of the result.
The second baseline is _vanilla spec decomposition_, which splits the
specification into block-level instructions first and generates each
block independently, with no per-type guidance. On top of vanilla spec
decomposition, we propose _structure-oriented decomposition_, which
injects a short per-type strategy into the decomposer for five design
families. CorrectRTL uses structure-oriented decomposition for its
workflow. We evaluate all three as configurations in the ablation study
(Sec. [5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3. Ablation Study ‣ 5. Experiment ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition")).

Code-Level Decomposition. The LLM generates the complete RTL from the
specification in one pass, and a structural parser segments the result
into blocks:

|     |     |                                                                                                                                   |     |
| --- | --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
| (3) |     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=f_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}),\qquad\{\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{n}\}=\textsc{Parse}(\mathcal{C})`$ |     |

The parser recognizes structural boundaries such as always blocks,
assign groups, and module headers. The SVA instruction
$`\mathcal{I}_{i}^{\text{A}}`$ for each block $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$ is
produced by a single LLM call that reads $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$ together
with the portion of $`\mathcal{S}`$ that describes it, and writes down
the key behavioral property the block must satisfy. Phase 2 then
generates $`\mathcal{A}_{i}`$ from $`\mathcal{I}_{i}^{\text{A}}`$ and
the retrieved SVA templates.

Spec-Level Decomposition. The specification is first parsed into a
hierarchical structure tree $`\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})`$
whose leaves are individual logic blocks. The block boundaries follow
the same definition used in the RAG-database curation
(Sec. [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2. RAG Database ‣ 3. Benchmark and Dataset ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition")):
the four block types are module-header / port declarations, sequential
always blocks, combinational always blocks, and assign groups.
Figure [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.2. RAG Database ‣ 3. Benchmark and Dataset ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition")
shows an example tree for a 16-bit adder. For each leaf block $`i`$, one
LLM call produces two instructions in parallel. The RTL instruction
$`\mathcal{I}_{i}^{\text{R}}`$ describes what the block should compute.
The SVA instruction $`\mathcal{I}_{i}^{\text{A}}`$ states the key
behavioral property the block must satisfy. Both instructions are
grounded in $`\mathcal{S}`$, so the SVA instruction is independent of
any later generated RTL. Phase 2 generates $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$ and
$`\mathcal{A}_{i}`$ from these two instructions, and a constructor
assembles the blocks into the full module.

Type-oriented decomposition strategies. Asking an LLM to decompose a
specification with no extra guidance is brittle. Different design
families have distinct coding conventions, and the LLM tends to produce
plans that overlap across blocks, request unnecessary testbench
scaffolding, or fall back to generic templates that break during
assembly. We therefore condition the decomposer on a short per-type
strategy. A lightweight classifier assigns each module one of five
families, and the decomposer applies the strategy attached to that
family before writing the block plan. For FSM modules, the strategy
splits the design into a clocked state register and a combinational
next-state function, and keeps an explicit reset state so the LLM does
not fall back to inferred latches. For sequential datapath modules, it
asks for one non-blocking always block per register group and checks
that pipeline depth, counter widths, and reset values follow the spec.
For combinational modules, it restricts the plan to assign and always
@(\*) blocks and asks that every output be covered in every branch. For
memory modules, it pins down array dimensions, per-port read/write
timing, and verbatim initialization values. For structural modules, it
asks the decomposer to list every sub-module instance, use named port
connections, and declare each inter-block wire with its exact width.
Each strategy targets one failure mode we observed in unconditioned
decomposition, and it is relaxed when the spec itself contradicts it.

Vanilla decomposition (baseline). We call direct spec-level
decomposition without these skills _vanilla decomposition_. An LLM is
asked to break the specification into blocks and write one instruction
per block, with no type-specific guidance. In practice the resulting
plans are noisy. Block boundaries overlap, so two blocks end up
generating the same signal. Some plans include testbench blocks or
wrapper modules that the original specification does not require. FSM
designs silently collapse into a single always block with inferred
latches. Downstream assembly then crashes on signal redefinition or
port-width mismatch. We report vanilla decomposition as a baseline in
Sec. [5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3. Ablation Study ‣ 5. Experiment ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition")
to isolate the contribution of the type-oriented skills above.

### 4.3. RAG-Augmented RTL and SVA Generation

Generating an RTL block or an SVA from scratch is unreliable. SVA
generation is especially error-prone in both syntax (wrong operators,
missing sampling edges) and semantics (the property does not match the
intended behavior). We therefore retrieve semantically similar examples
from the RAG database and feed them to the RTL and SVA agents as
references, so the agents adapt existing patterns rather than invent
them.

Transformer-based retriever. We use a pretrained transformer-based
sentence encoder to embed every spec-snippet in $`\mathcal{D}`$. At
inference we embed the query and return the top-$`K`$ nearest neighbors
by cosine similarity. Each neighbor brings two things the agents can
use: a reference RTL block, and a set of formally-proven SVA templates
attached to that block.

Query formation under spec-level decomposition. When Phase 1 uses
spec-level decomposition, the per-block RTL instruction
$`\mathcal{I}_{i}^{\text{R}}`$ is already a natural-language description
of the block’s intended behavior. We use $`\mathcal{I}_{i}^{\text{R}}`$
directly as the retrieval query. The retrieved reference code blocks are
passed to the RTL agent, which adapts its coding style to the current
block. The retrieved SVA templates are passed to the SVA agent, which
adapts them to the block’s actual signal names and emits one SVA
$`\mathcal{A}_{i}`$.

Query formation under code-level decomposition. When Phase 1 uses
code-level decomposition, the RTL block $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$ has already
been generated, so retrieving more reference code is unnecessary. We
still need SVA templates. For each parsed block, an LLM reads
$`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$ together with the portion of $`\mathcal{S}`$ that
describes it, and emits a short natural-language description of the
block’s behavior. This description serves as the retrieval query, and
only the proven SVA templates attached to the top-$`K`$ neighbors are
returned. The SVA agent then adapts them to $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$’s signal
names.

RAG for Generations. Retrieval boosts both sides of the Phase-2
generation, which leads to a stronger first-pass design before any
self-correction is applied. _(1) RAG for RTL block generation._ The
retrieved reference blocks give the RTL agent concrete patterns for the
target behavior. The agent adapts a verified implementation instead of
composing one from the spec alone, which reduces structural and
signal-level errors in the initial code. _(2) RAG for SVA generation._
Spec descriptions rarely spell out formal assertions, so SVAs generated
from scratch often have wrong operators, missing sampling edges, or
properties that do not match the intended behavior. The retrieved
templates have already been formally discharged on golden RTL. The SVA
agent adapts their signal names to the current block, which gives a
syntactically well-formed and semantically aligned starting point and
removes most of the from-scratch error modes.

### 4.4. Functional Correction

When Phase 3 flags a block $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$, we invoke a rewriter
agent with three well-defined inputs: the current RTL block
$`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$, the violated SVA $`\mathcal{A}_{i}`$, and the
counterexample log $`\xi_{i}`$ emitted by the verifier. The log contains
the signal values at the violation cycle and the relevant history needed
to explain how the violation was reached. The agent reasons about these
three inputs and produces a corrected block
$`\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\prime}`$. No retrieval is performed at this step.

Analyze-plan-rewrite chain. The rewriter is a single LLM call whose
structured prompt composes three steps:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (4) |     |

````math
\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\prime}=\textsc{Rewrite}\!\big(\mathcal{C}_{i},\;\textsc{Plan}\!\big(\textsc{Analyze}(\mathcal{C}_{i},\mathcal{A}_{i},\xi_{i}),\;\mathcal{A}_{i}\big)\big).
``` |  |

The Analyze step produces a root-cause diagnosis $`d_{i}`$ in natural
language. It traces the counterexample values back through the block
body to identify the specific line or expression that makes
$`\mathcal{A}_{i}`$ fail. The Plan step turns $`d_{i}`$ into a concrete
modification plan $`p_{i}`$ that is expressed in terms of the block’s
signals and aligned with $`\mathcal{A}_{i}`$. The Rewrite step edits
$`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$ according to $`p_{i}`$ and emits the corrected block
$`\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\prime}`$. Only the failing block is changed in the
full design:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (5) |  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\textsc{Replace}(\mathcal{C},\;\mathcal{C}_{i},\;\mathcal{C}_{i}^{\prime}).`$ |  |

The block-level decomposition from Phase 1 is what makes this targeted
replacement safe, since correct blocks remain untouched.

Iterative correction. After each rewrite, Phases 3 and 4 run again on
the updated design $`\mathcal{C}^{\prime}`$. Any block that is still
flagged is corrected again using the same analyze-plan-rewrite chain on
its latest $`(\mathcal{C}_{i},\mathcal{A}_{i},\xi_{i})`$ triple. The
loop stops early once all blocks pass their SVAs, and is capped by a
fixed number of rounds to avoid oscillation between rewrites. Syntax
correction ([Tsai et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)) is applied as a
preprocessing step and is not discussed further here.

Metrics. Functional *Pass@1* is the primary metric. To isolate the
effect of initial code generation and the self-correction step, we
compare each design’s Pass@1 before and after correction. A design whose
Pass@1 goes up contributes to *Fix*. A design whose Pass@1 goes down
contributes to *Regression*. Both are reported as averages over the
benchmark. The net effect of correction is
$`\Delta=\text{Fix}-\text{Regression}`$.

Auxiliary metrics. Pass@1 is strict at the design level. Many complex
designs are hard enough that no method fully passes them, but a
corrected design can still be much closer to the golden than its initial
code. We therefore report two partial-correctness metrics. *SER* (signal
equivalence rate) measures the fraction of output signals that are
formally equivalent to the golden. We run JasperGold’s sequential
equivalence check between the generated RTL and the golden, and parse
the log to obtain the per-output proven rate. *SSim* (structural
similarity) measures how closely the generated RTL’s block structure
matches the golden’s, as a blend of set overlap and
longest-common-subsequence over parsed blocks. A design that misses
Pass@1 but lifts SER from, say, $`30\%`$ to $`80\%`$ is still a
meaningful partial fix. Together, SER and SSim let us quantify progress
on designs that remain too hard to pass outright.

We formalize the two metrics as follows. Given a generated RTL
$`\mathcal{C}`$ and its golden reference $`\mathcal{C}^{\star}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{SER}(\mathcal{C})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{|O|}\,\#\{s\in O:\mathrm{SEC}(s,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\star})=\textsc{proven}\},`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{SSim}(\mathcal{C})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\alpha\,\mathrm{Jaccard}\big(B(\mathcal{C}),B(\mathcal{C}^{\star})\big)+(1{-}\alpha)\,\mathrm{LCS}\big(B(\mathcal{C}),B(\mathcal{C}^{\star})\big),`$ |  |

where $`O`$ is the set of output signals, $`B(\cdot)`$ is the multiset
of parsed structural blocks, and $`\alpha{=}0.5`$.

Baselines. We compare against three recent self-correction methods:
RTLFixer ([Tsai et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)), VeriAssist ([Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15)), and AutoVeriFix ([Tan et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)).
All baselines run on the same model and temperature as CorrectRTL.
Formal verification uses JasperGold 2024.06.

Implementation details. We use the *Microsoft Agentic Framework* to
develop our methods and reproduce the baseline methods. We evaluate
three backbones: Claude Sonnet 4.5 ([Anthropic, 2024](#bib.bib13)),
GPT-4o ([Achiam et al., 2023](#bib.bib11)), and DeepSeek-V3 ([Liu et
al., 2024a](#bib.bib14)). Generation uses temperature $`0.2`$ with a
single sample. The RAG retriever uses a pretrained transformer-based
sentence encoder (BGE-class, e.g., bge-small-en-v1.5) and a FAISS flat
cosine index over the spec-snippet field of $`\mathcal{D}`$. At
inference, we return the top-$`K`$ neighbors with $`K{=}8`$. The
correction loop caps at $`T_{\max}{=}3`$ rounds with early stop once all
blocks pass.

Table [7](#S5.T7 "Table 7 ‣ 5.2. Overall Results ‣ 5. Experiment ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition").
Code-level SVAs reference code-internal signal names and often fail to
compile or prove on the golden. Vanilla spec SVAs use only
spec-mentioned signals, so their compile and correctness rates improve,
but their recall plateaus without domain-specific patterns.
Structure-oriented decomposition paired with SVA-RAG reaches 92% compile
rate, 85% correctness on the golden, 88% precision, and 68% recall on
failing designs.

### 5.4. Analysis on Design Scale

The aggregate Pass@1 numbers above hide how each method behaves as
design size grows. ScaleBench spans 30–500 LoC, so we group designs by
LoC bucket and report Pass@1, SER, and SSim per bucket. We use this view
to compare CorrectRTL with prior baselines and ablation configurations
of CorrectRTL.

Ablation across design scale in
Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5.3. Ablation Study ‣ 5. Experiment ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition").
The figure plots Pass@1, SSim, and SER against the ScaleBench LoC bucket
for the four ablation configurations. Three observations follow.
(i) SSim stays high and roughly flat (74%–96%) across all configurations
and all LoC buckets, which suggests that the structural skeleton is
recovered well even by weaker configurations. (ii) Code-level
decomposition drops the fastest with LoC. Its Pass@1 falls below 10% by
150 LoC. Vanilla spec decomposition also drops, though more slowly.
(iii) Structure-oriented decomposition is the only configuration whose
Pass@1 stays close to its SSim at large LoC, indicating it preserves
both structure and signal-level correctness as designs grow. The SER
curves sit between Pass@1 and SSim, consistent with the gap being at the
signal level.

Comparison with baselines in
Figure [6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 5.3. Ablation Study ‣ 5. Experiment ‣ CorrectRTL: Boost RTL Generation Quality Through Automated Self-Correction with Instruction Decomposition").
This figure plots Pass@1 and SER against the ScaleBench LoC bucket for
the three baselines and CorrectRTL (Claude Sonnet 4.5). All three
baselines drop sharply past 200 LoC. Their Pass@1 falls below 10% on
200–300 LoC and below 5% on 300–500 LoC. CorrectRTL drops more slowly
with design size: it holds $`\geq`$30% Pass@1 through 150–200 LoC and
retains 12% at the largest bucket. The SER curves on the right show the
same trend at the signal level. CorrectRTL keeps $`\geq`$50%
signal-level equivalence on 200–300 LoC designs, where the baselines
fall below 35%. Overall, the gap between CorrectRTL and the baselines
widens as designs grow larger.

## 6. Conclusion

We presented CorrectRTL, a four-phase framework that improves both the
generation and the self-correction of RTL designs. We also introduced
ScaleBench, a 200-design benchmark of complex RTL with formal
equivalence checking against golden references. On ScaleBench,
CorrectRTL improves Pass@1 from 20.5% to 49.0% with Claude Sonnet 4.5,
and consistent gains hold across GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, and the
small-to-medium VerilogEval and RTLLM benchmarks. We hope this work
motivates further research on block-level retrieval and assertion-driven
self-correction for large-scale RTL generation.
````

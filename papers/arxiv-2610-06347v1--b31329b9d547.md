###### Abstract

Adapting general-purpose large language models to specific tasks
requires substantial human effort in designing data and training
strategies. Sustaining improvement is especially challenging because
model updates change the error distribution, requiring strategies to be
continually refined. We introduce ImproveAnyTask, an autonomous
post-training harness that improves task performance under a limited
compute budget. Drawing inspiration from gradient-based parameter
optimization, the harness organizes adaptation into error attribution,
update-direction selection, and executable model updates. It combines
metric-level and case-level analysis to identify a focal problem, then
investigates research-backed strategies and compares their reported
gains and reproduction difficulty. The selected strategy is translated
into training data and a training configuration, with small-scale
execution checks preceding full post-training. Subsequent evaluation
guides model selection and further adaptation, while validated
strategies and scripts are retained for reuse. Across 11 tasks,
ImproveAnyTask achieves mean gains of 18.29 and 11.97 percentage points
on the Base and Instruct models, respectively, with a maximum gain of
41.96 points, under a 24-hour budget with resources equivalent to eight
H20 GPUs.

## 1 Introduction

Recent large language models, including GLM-5, Qwen3.5, and Kimi K3,
have demonstrated strong capabilities across diverse tasks ([GLM-5 Team,
2026](#bib.bib8); [Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib29); [Kimi Team,
2026](#bib.bib15)). However, strong general capabilities do not always
translate into strong performance on specific tasks. Task-specific
adaptation is therefore an important research direction for making these
models more capable in real-world applications. Yet effective adaptation
requires substantial effort in data curation, training strategy
development, and model architecture design. Moreover, each model update
can shift the error distribution, so sustained improvement requires
continual refinement of the adaptation strategy.

Task-oriented post-training improves model performance through data
selection, synthesis, distillation, and the design of supervised or
reinforcement learning objectives ([Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib42); [Guha
et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Ma et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)). Researchers develop these interventions according to
the target task and the model’s weaknesses. Although many methods
automate data construction or adapt training to the learner, selecting
an appropriate strategy and revising it across successive checkpoints
still require substantial human expertise.

Recent advances in autonomous research and model improvement have begun
to automate literature investigation, implementation, and
experimentation ([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib38); [Tang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib32); [Nam et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)). Other systems extend
automation to post-training and iterative data curation ([Rank et al.,
2026](#bib.bib30); [Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Wang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib33)). However, completing an experimental loop does not
necessarily yield an effective improvement strategy. A promising method
may address the wrong weakness, depend on unavailable resources, or fail
during reproduction. Productive iteration therefore requires connecting
the current model’s errors with relevant research and reliable
execution, while learning from previous outcomes. This motivates the
following question:

Can an agent harness autonomously improve a general-purpose LLM on a
target task under a limited compute budget?

To address this question, we propose ImproveAnyTask, an autonomous
post-training harness that organizes task adaptation as an iterative
model optimization process. Inspired by gradient-based parameter
optimization, the harness connects error signals, update directions, and
executable model updates. Error Attribution combines metric-level
analysis with individual failure cases to identify a focal problem.
Update Direction guides the agent to investigate multiple strategies and
compare their reported gains, applicability, and reproduction
difficulty. Executable Model Updates translates the selected strategy
into training data and a training configuration, using small-scale
execution and short training runs to identify implementation problems
before full execution. Re-evaluation determines the outcome of each
update and informs subsequent trials. Effective strategies and scripts
are retained for reuse, while unsuccessful attempts provide evidence for
revising future decisions. This process lets users adapt a model to a
target task without manually redesigning data and training strategies at
each iteration.

We evaluate 11 tasks spanning five capability domains using
Qwen3.5-4B-Base and Qwen3.5-4B-Instruct under a 24-hour budget with
resources equivalent to eight H20 GPUs. The Base-model gain averages
18.29 percentage points and reaches 41.96 points on LiveCodeBench v6
(Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")).
The Instruct-model gain averages 11.97 points
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")).

Our main contributions are:

- •
  An autonomous post-training harness. We introduce ImproveAnyTask to
  automate task-specific improvement of general-purpose LLMs under a
  limited compute budget, without requiring manually designed strategies
  for each trial.
- •
  An agent-guided model optimization process. Inspired by gradient-based
  optimization, we organize model improvement into three modules: Error
  Attribution identifies what to improve, Update Direction selects how
  to improve it, and Executable Model Updates translates the strategy
  into data and training.
- •
  Cross-task evaluation. Across 11 tasks under the same 24-hour budget,
  ImproveAnyTask achieves mean gains of 18.29 and 11.97 points on the
  Base and Instruct models, respectively.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Task-Oriented Post-Training

Recent work improves task performance by shaping both training data and
learning signals. Data selection and curricula emphasize example
quality, diversity, and compatibility with the learner. Compact
reasoning datasets can elicit existing capabilities, while student-aware
and stage-aware curricula adapt which examples are used and when ([Ye
et al., 2025](#bib.bib42); [Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib17); [Huang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12); [Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)). Synthesis and
distillation expand supervision beyond existing labeled corpora: open
data recipes scale reasoning and coding demonstrations ([Guha et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10); [Ahmad et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)), iterative prompt
synthesis improves the question supply ([Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib46)), and failure-driven methods target missing knowledge
with document-grounded supervision ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib37)).
Task-structured supervision makes success criteria explicit through
rubric-guided reasoning ([Yu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib43)), executable
software-repair tasks ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib39)), and diverse
function-calling examples ([Greenstein et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)).
Complementarily, training objectives and composition determine how
supervision translates into updates, including general-domain
verification ([Ma et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)), conditional-expectation
rewards ([Xiao et al., 2026](#bib.bib36)), and multi-domain training or
model merging ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib34)). These advances provide
powerful optimization ingredients, and several already automate data
construction or adapt it to the learner.

However, selecting and revising these methods still requires substantial
human effort and time. As training corrects some errors and introduces
others, a previously effective strategy may no longer address the
model’s main weaknesses. Automated data construction alone does not
resolve this need for strategy adaptation as errors change.
ImproveAnyTask automates this repeated diagnosis and strategy revision,
reducing the need for manual iteration.

### 2.2 Autonomous Research and Model Improvement

LLM agents increasingly automate empirical research rather than merely
propose ideas. AI Scientist-v2 ([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib38)) and
AI-Researcher ([Tang et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)) connect literature
analysis, implementation, and experimentation, while MLE-STAR ([Nam
et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)) improves machine-learning pipelines through
search and targeted refinement. For model adaptation,
PostTrainBench ([Rank et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)) evaluates autonomous
post-training under bounded compute. FT-Agent ([Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib16)) and AutoTrainess ([Yu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib44))
combine iterative planning, error feedback, execution checks, and trial
records. LaMDAgent ([Yano et al., 2025](#bib.bib41)) searches end-to-end
post-training pipelines, while TREX ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib22))
combines literature research and execution with tree-based search and
history reuse. LLMZero ([Fang et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)) and Pioneer
Agent ([Atreja et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)) further explore training
trajectories and deployment-time interventions. These studies establish
iterative, end-to-end post-training as a shared setting.

A complementary line makes training data an explicit optimization
variable. DataMaster ([Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)) searches external
data and executable transformations, ANDES ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib47)) provides adaptive instruction synthesis, and
CurateEvo ([Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib33)) evolves curation programs
using post-training feedback. Curation-Bench ([Kang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)) identifies an execution–research gap; its
paper-adaptation scaffold requires agents to select prior methods,
justify their applicability, and adapt them to available data and
budgets. Related methods include SEAL ([Zweiger et al.,
2025](#bib.bib49)), which learns self-updates, and AgentEvolver ([Zhai
et al., 2025](#bib.bib45)) and CoEvolve ([Yang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib40)), which evolve training tasks alongside the model.
These approaches primarily optimize data strategies, with less emphasis
on comparing candidate methods by expected benefit and reproduction
difficulty. What is Missing from AI Post-Training AI ([Lim et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19)) further finds that the evaluated post-training agents
can repair execution problems but rarely reconsider their chosen
strategies.

Beyond completing a training pipeline, sustained improvement requires
selecting effective interventions as model errors change and
implementing them reliably within the budget. ImproveAnyTask connects
these decisions in a complete optimization loop: error attribution
identifies one priority, research compares methods for that priority,
and execution checks help implement the selected method. Re-evaluation
then guides the next round. We study this integrated process across 11
tasks and two model initializations under a fixed budget, obtaining
substantial gains over the evaluated general-purpose agent baselines.

## 3 Methods

### 3.1 Problem Formulation and Overview

![Refer to caption](2610.06347v1/ImproveAnyTask_cropped0926.png)

Figure 2: Overview of ImproveAnyTask. The harness treats task adaptation
as iterative optimization: evaluation and dual-level error attribution
identify a focal problem; research-grounded strategy exploration selects
an update direction; data preparation and post-training produce a
candidate model; and re-evaluation determines the next checkpoint and
records reusable assets.

Problem Formulation. Given an initial model $`\theta_{0}`$ and a target
task $`B`$, our objective is to autonomously select and execute
post-training strategies that improve its task performance within a time
budget $`\mathcal{B}`$ of 24 hours, using resources equivalent to eight
H20 GPUs:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\max_{\{s_{t}\}}J_{B}(\theta_{\mathrm{final}})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad T_{\mathrm{total}}\leq\mathcal{B}.
``` |  | (1) |

Here, $`J_{B}`$ denotes the primary task metric, evaluated on the
validation split during optimization; $`s_{t}`$ is the strategy adopted
in trial $`t`$, and $`\theta_{\mathrm{final}}`$ is the selected
post-trained candidate. $`T_{\mathrm{total}}`$ measures end-to-end
elapsed time;
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")
specifies the timing and stopping rules.

Harness-Guided Model Optimization. As shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Problem Formulation and Overview ‣ 3 Methods ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement"),
ImproveAnyTask draws inspiration from gradient-based parameter
optimization. Specifically, the harness first organizes evaluation of
the current model $`\theta_{t}`$ on the validation split of task $`B`$,
analogous to a forward pass. The Error Attribution module then jointly
analyzes metrics and failure cases to extract update-relevant signals,
analogous in role to backpropagation. It produces a diagnostic report
$`F_{t}`$ that identifies a focal problem $`p_{t}`$ and its supporting
evidence. The Update Direction module uses research evidence and prior
experience to select a strategy $`s_{t}`$, providing an update direction
for the current model. The Executable Model Updates module translates
$`s_{t}`$ into training data $`D_{t}`$ and a training configuration
$`h_{t}`$, then performs post-training to realize the parameter update,
producing a candidate model $`\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}`$. The candidate is
subsequently re-evaluated. This repeated evaluation–research–update loop
enables the model to self-improve within the harness. Strategies and
scripts validated as effective are retained for future trials.

### 3.2 Error Attribution

The harness organizes an error attribution module to turn evaluation
evidence into a prioritized optimization target for the current model
$`\theta_{t}`$. Analogous in role to backpropagation, this module
extracts update-relevant signals from observed errors. Specifically, our
harness first evaluates $`\theta_{t}`$ on the validation split of task
$`B`$, collecting aggregate metrics, individual predictions, and scoring
results. It guides the agent to prioritize problems through Dual-Level
Error Attribution and record the focal problem $`p_{t}`$ and its
evidence in a diagnostic report $`F_{t}`$ for subsequent optimization.

Dual-Level Error Attribution. To produce actionable diagnoses, we
examine errors at both the metric and case levels. Metric-level analysis
identifies weaknesses in overall performance and available subtask
metrics. Case-level analysis examines individual predictions and scoring
evidence to identify recurring failure patterns. Together, they
establish the scope of a weakness and explain how the model fails.
Section [4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")
evaluates error attribution and focal-problem selection together.

Combining both sources of evidence, the harness guides the agent to
select the focal problem from the candidate set $`\mathcal{P}_{t}`$:

|     |                                               |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       p_{t}=\arg\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}I_{B}(p),
       ```                                            |     | (2) |

where $`I_{B}(p)`$ denotes the assessed impact of problem $`p`$ on the
primary metric, based on metric and case evidence. It represents a
relative priority, not a numerical prediction of post-training gains.

Subsequent research and execution focus on $`p_{t}`$, rather than all
identified problems. Inspired by regularization, this restriction
narrows the set of problems targeted in each trial. We observed that
presenting multiple problems can lead the agent to favor easier
secondary issues over the most consequential one.

### 3.3 Update Direction

To determine an update direction for $`\theta_{t}`$, we design an Update
Direction module that guides the agent to investigate methods relevant
to the focal problem $`p_{t}`$. Unlike gradient-based optimization, our
outer loop has no directly usable gradient over data strategies. The
harness instead draws on evidence from papers, code, and datasets to
select a strategy $`s_{t}`$ and develop its execution plan.

Structured Strategy Exploration. The harness requires the agent to
investigate multiple candidates for the same focal problem $`p_{t}`$
before selecting a strategy. Each candidate is recorded in a shared
format covering its source, applicability, reported gains, and
reproduction steps. The module also assesses reproduction difficulty on
a three-level scale. The lowest level corresponds to direct use of
released training-ready data; the intermediate level requires additional
processing, generation, or distillation; the highest level involves
complex procedures or incomplete resources. These records support
comparison across candidates before resources are committed to
implementation.

The module then guides the agent to select strategy $`s_{t}`$ through a
qualitative comparison of applicability, reported gains, and
reproduction difficulty. Reported gains provide evidence from prior
settings, while reproduction difficulty helps assess whether the
required implementation is practical under the available budget. Before
execution, the agent verifies the required resources and key
reproduction steps to ensure that the selected strategy can be
implemented.

Outcome-Aware Reuse. As $`\theta_{t}`$ changes, its errors and the
applicability of previous strategies may also change. Each round
therefore includes research for the current model state, while reusing
existing records to avoid repeated work. The candidate pool combines
three sources:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{S}_{t}=\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\mathrm{new}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\mathrm{history}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\mathrm{library}}.
``` |  | (3) |

Here, $`\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\mathrm{history}}`$ retains previously
unselected candidates and the outcomes of executed trials, while
$`\mathcal{S}_{t}^{\mathrm{library}}`$ provides validated, reusable
knowledge. These records help the agent revisit promising methods and
avoid repeated failures. They inform strategy selection but do not
replace validation on the current model.

### 3.4 Executable Model Updates

To realize the selected strategy $`s_{t}`$ as a parameter update, the
Executable Model Updates module guides the agent to acquire resources
and implement processing tools to produce training data $`D_{t}`$ and a
configuration $`h_{t}`$. Training settings follow the selected method,
with batch size and parallelism adapted to the current model
$`\theta_{t}`$ and the available resources. The harness then performs
post-training:

|     |                                                           |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \tilde{\theta}_{t+1}=\mathcal{U}(\theta_{t};D_{t},h_{t}),
       ```                                                        |     | (4) |

where $`\mathcal{U}`$ denotes the training procedure and
$`\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}`$ is the resulting candidate model.

Execution-Guided Refinement. The harness organizes standard execution
checks to assess whether the prepared data and training configuration
implement $`s_{t}`$ as intended. Before full execution, the agent
applies the data preparation procedure to a small set of real samples.
It checks whether their content, format, and training template match the
requirements of $`s_{t}`$. A short training run verifies that the
prepared samples can be loaded and parameter updates completed. When
execution reveals errors or mismatches, the agent revises the processing
tools, data pipeline, or $`h_{t}`$, then repeats the relevant checks.
Full data preparation and training begin after these checks pass.

Evaluation and Reuse. The candidate $`\tilde{\theta}_{t+1}`$ is
re-evaluated on the same validation split and under the same conditions
as $`\theta_{t}`$. Using $`F_{t}`$ and the executed strategy $`s_{t}`$,
the agent analyzes changes in the primary metric, whether the focal
problem $`p_{t}`$ has improved, and any new errors. These validation
results guide subsequent model and strategy selection. If the candidate
does not improve the validation score, the parent remains the starting
point for subsequent trials.

Experiment records link every attempt, including failures, to its
strategy, data, training settings, and results; effective strategies,
scripts, and data are retained in the asset libraries for reuse.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Models. We evaluate ImproveAnyTask on Qwen3.5-4B-Base and
Qwen3.5-4B-Instruct ([Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib29)), testing task
adaptation from pretrained and post-trained initializations. Each run
starts independently from the original checkpoint.

Tasks. Following the broad evaluation coverage used in recent
frontier-model reports ([Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib29); [Kimi Team,
2026](#bib.bib15); [GLM-5 Team, 2026](#bib.bib8)), we evaluate
task-specific self-improvement on 11 tasks across five capability
domains: (1) Knowledge and STEM: MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA, and
GPQA-Diamond ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib35); [M-A-P Team,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Rein et al., 2023](#bib.bib31)); (2) Mathematical
and Verifiable Reasoning: MATH-500 and AIME 2025 ([Lightman et al.,
2023](#bib.bib18); [Mathematical Association of America,
2025](#bib.bib24)); (3) Instruction Following: IFEval and IFBench ([Zhou
et al., 2023](#bib.bib48); [Pyatkin et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)); (4)
Coding: LiveCodeBench v6 and Terminal-Bench 2.0 ([Jain et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13); [Merrill et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)); and (5) Tool Use
and General Agents: BFCL-V4 and TAU2-Bench ([Berkeley Function Calling
Leaderboard Team, 2025](#bib.bib5); [Barres et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)).
For BFCL-V4, we exclude the web-search subset because it requires an
external paid search service.

Compute Budget. Each run uses resources equivalent to eight NVIDIA H20
GPUs and stops after 24 hours or three optimization rounds, whichever
comes first. Timing spans the initial to final evaluation, including
research, data preparation, validation, training, and failed attempts;
unfinished work is interrupted at the deadline.
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Scaling the Optimization Budget ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")
extends these limits.

Evaluation Protocol. Following FT-Agent ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)),
all methods share a fixed split of approximately 15% validation and 85%
held-out test data
(Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Evaluation Splits and Metrics ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")).
Only validation data inform error analysis, strategy selection, and
checkpoint selection; test data remain inaccessible during optimization.
Within each run, we select the post-trained candidate with the highest
validation score among those validated before the deadline; the initial
checkpoint is excluded and reported separately as Original.

All experiments use the same official evaluation code, a 128K context
window, non-thinking generation, and greedy decoding at temperature
$`0`$; empty or failed predictions receive zero credit. To reduce
sampling variability during optimization, we also set the agent’s API
temperature to $`0`$. Main and ablation results are averaged over two
independent complete optimization runs per setting, each subject to the
same budget. We log exact matches to evaluation queries in training
data. Only BFCL aggregation changes, excluding Web Search and
renormalizing weights
(Appendix [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 BFCL Scoring Without Web Search ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")).

Baselines. We compare against Codex ([OpenAI, 2025](#bib.bib27)) with
GPT-5.6-Sol and Claude Code ([Anthropic, 2025](#bib.bib2)) with Opus
4.8. To our knowledge, no related specialized system provides a complete
open-source implementation for our full 11-task suite. ImproveAnyTask
also uses Opus 4.8. All systems share tool permissions, teacher-model
access, and resource limits. Under Direct Agent, each baseline receives
only the task, initial model, budget, and evaluation interface, then
independently researches methods, prepares data, implements tools, and
trains the model. Codex provides a complete-system comparison, not an
isolated test of harness design.

### 4.2 Main Results

Overall Results.
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")
reports the main results. ImproveAnyTask achieves mean gains of 18.29
percentage points on Qwen3.5-4B-Base and 11.97 points on
Qwen3.5-4B-Instruct. It exceeds Codex and Claude Code by 12.49 and 11.43
points on Base, and by 9.54 and 8.04 points on Instruct. These averages
include the renormalized BFCL no-Web metric.

Task-Level Results. ImproveAnyTask improves all eleven Base-model tasks
and ten Instruct-model tasks. The largest Base-model gain occurs on
LiveCodeBench v6, from 19.64% to 61.60% $`(+41.96)`$, while the smallest
occurs on AIME 2025 $`(+4.00)`$. For Instruct, the largest gains are on
LiveCodeBench v6 $`(+29.12)`$, AIME 2025 $`(+24.00)`$, and IFBench
$`(+21.56)`$. Notably, under our evaluation settings, the optimized Base
model outperforms the optimized Instruct model on MATH-500 and
LiveCodeBench v6.

On IFEval, ImproveAnyTask gains 7.84 and 0.87 percentage points on Base
and Instruct, respectively.
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Scaling the Optimization Budget ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")
examines the Base-model trajectory within and beyond the first 24 hours.

SuperGPQA-Instruct is the only task with a decrease, from 49.78% to
47.77% $`(-2.00)`$, with changes computed before truncation. The
recorded run contains three unsuccessful attempts. The first used 70K
teacher-distilled examples, but only 12,747 were multiple-choice
questions and the data were concentrated in physics, mathematics, and
chemistry. The second used 15K student-consistent examples, of which
89.1% reproduced responses the student already answered correctly. The
third increased the correction ratio to 80% using 1K targeted examples.
These records suggest distribution mismatch and forgetting as possible
explanations, but do not establish a single cause.

### 4.3 Scaling the Optimization Budget

We extend the IFEval Base experiment from 24 to 100 hours under the same
resource allocation, timing rules, and generation settings as the main
experiments. Removing the three-round limit allows eight candidate
checkpoints.
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.3 Scaling the Optimization Budget ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")
reports strict prompt-level accuracy;
Appendix [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Extended IFEval Experiment ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")
provides execution details.

The first two checkpoints use persona-conditioned instruction tuning and
rubric-guided response selection, increasing accuracy from 66.66% to
74.50% within 24 hours. Adding a third source of general instruction
data yields a smaller gain, reaching 74.72% at C3.

Targeted, execution-verified tuning reduces accuracy at C4. C5 restarts
from C3 and adds targeted examples without replacing the broader
mixture; C6 targets residual errors. C7 introduces constraint reasoning
and self-verification in the training data, reaching 85.18%, 10.67
points above C2. A narrower continuation focused on precision
constraints at C8 falls to 83.00%. Longer exploration finds a better
strategy in this run, but does not guarantee improvement at every
checkpoint.

### 4.4 Ablation Study

Experimental Design. We evaluate three modules of ImproveAnyTask through
module-level replacement experiments: (1) Error Attribution, including
dual-level error analysis and focal-problem selection; (2) Update
Direction, including structured strategy comparison, resource
verification, reproduction-difficulty assessment, and outcome-aware
reuse; and (3) Execution-Guided Refinement within Executable Model
Updates, including sample-level data and template checks, short training
runs, and iterative correction of data and training pipelines.

Within Error Attribution, metric- and case-level analysis provides
evidence for prioritizing problems, while focal-problem selection turns
that evidence into the next research target. We replace these procedures
together to evaluate the complete process from diagnosis to problem
selection.

Rather than removing an entire stage and breaking the optimization loop,
each ablation replaces the corresponding mechanism with a basic
alternative. w/o EA retains evaluation feedback but replaces structured
dual-level attribution and explicit focal-problem selection with an
unstructured summary. w/o UD retains ordinary research but removes the
required comparison of candidates for the same focal problem,
reproduction-difficulty assessment, resource verification, and
outcome-aware reuse. w/o ER retains Executable Model Updates but removes
our structured pre-execution checks and refinement procedure. The
underlying agent remains able to inspect artifacts and repair errors in
all variants. These replacements remove the harness-prescribed
procedures, not the agent’s general ability to analyze feedback,
research methods, or debug code.
Appendix [A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Ablation Implementation Details ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")
specifies these replacements and their recorded execution paths.

We evaluate Qwen3.5-4B-Instruct on five representative benchmarks, one
from each capability domain. We use the Instruct model to examine
improvement beyond an already post-trained initialization. All runs
follow the main experimental settings in
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement").

The variants can select different training data and configurations as
their optimization paths diverge. The comparisons therefore measure each
module’s overall effect under the same budget, not the isolated
contribution of a single check or data choice.

## 5 Conclusion

We introduced ImproveAnyTask, an autonomous post-training harness that
supports iterative model self-improvement through error attribution,
research-grounded update selection, and executable model updates. Across
11 tasks, it achieves mean gains of 18.29 and 11.97 percentage points on
Base and Instruct models under a 24-hour budget with resources
equivalent to eight H20 GPUs. By automating repeated diagnosis,
research, data preparation, and training, the harness reduces the need
for manual trial design and intervention. This offers a practical way to
adapt models for specific applications with less human effort and a
fixed resource budget.

### AI use statement

This work studies an autonomous post-training harness built upon
generative models. Generative models are therefore used as research,
data-curation, and execution agents within the proposed method. AI tools
also assisted manuscript editing and figure preparation. The authors
take full responsibility for the content and conclusions of this work.

### Ethics Statement

ImproveAnyTask uses released resources and model-generated data for
task-specific post-training. These sources may contain biases, factual
errors, or sensitive information, and higher task scores do not
establish safety or fairness. Validation feedback is isolated from final
testing. Exact-overlap auditing does not rule out semantic contamination
in external data, and repeated use of validation feedback can still lead
to validation-set overfitting. Autonomous code execution and external
API calls also introduce privacy, security, and resource-use risks.
Practical deployment therefore requires appropriate access controls and
review of generated code and data. Third-party models, code, and
datasets remain subject to their licenses and usage conditions.

additional correct answer changes a single-run score by four percentage
points, or the two-run mean by two points. LiveCodeBench uses one
response per problem. TAU2 equally averages Airline and Telecom scores
over 42 and 96 tasks, respectively.

BFCL counts refer to unique scored examples; repeated Memory backends do
not create additional test questions. Live Relevance diagnostics are
excluded from these counts and from Overall
(Appendix [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 BFCL Scoring Without Web Search ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ ImproveAnyTask: An Autonomous Post-Training Harness for Iterative Model Self-Improvement")).

### A.4 BFCL Scoring Without Web Search

The official BFCL-V4 score assigns 40% to Agentic, which equally
averages Web Search and Memory ([Mao et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)).
Counting omitted Web Search as zero limits Overall to 80%. For our
no-Web metric, we remove its 20% weight and renormalize the remaining
components:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
J_{\mathrm{no\text{-}web}}=\frac{0.1J_{\mathrm{non\text{-}live}}+0.1J_{\mathrm{live}}+0.1J_{\mathrm{irr}}+0.3J_{\mathrm{multi}}+0.2J_{\mathrm{memory}}}{0.8}.
``` |  |

This gives weights of 12.5% each to Non-Live, Live, and Irrelevance,
37.5% to Multi-Turn, and 25% to Memory. We retain the original
within-section aggregation, including equal averaging across the three
Memory backends. This subset metric is not directly comparable to the
official full-task Overall score.

We allocate 85% of each lowest-level category to held-out testing, with
sample counts rounded down. The split contains 3,890 scored unique
examples and 4,152 scored executions, since the same 131 Memory
questions use three backends. Another 13 Live Relevance examples are
diagnostic and excluded from Overall. Category-wise rounding can yield a
different total from rounding the combined dataset. Shared Memory
question IDs are assigned to a split before backend execution.

### A.5 Limitations and Future Work

Following prior work on task-specific post-training ([Rank et al.,
2026](#bib.bib30); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)), we focus on
target-task performance. We do not systematically measure whether
specialization affects general capabilities, given the substantial
evaluation effort and the many combinations of tasks, models, and
training strategies. The substantial task-specific gains support
applications that need focused model adaptation without repeated manual
experimentation. Future work will study how to preserve general
capabilities during this process.

We also restrict executable updates to supervised post-training.
Extending the harness to reinforcement learning and broader
post-training objectives is left to future work.
````

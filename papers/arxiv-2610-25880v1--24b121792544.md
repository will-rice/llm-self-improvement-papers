# Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation

Xidong Wu Affiliation: University of Pittsburgh    Qi Qi
Affiliation: University of Iowa    Yuqiong Ji Affiliation: University of
Pennsylvania    Shangqian Gao Affiliation: Florida State University

###### Abstract

Unlike on-policy distillation (OPD), which depends on a separate teacher
model, on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) leverages a single language
model acting simultaneously as the student and the teacher, with the
teacher conditioned on privileged information. Despite this
architectural simplicity, synchronous rollout generation dominates
overall training time, creating a severe systems bottleneck when scaling
OPSD. To address this, we introduce Asynchronous On-Policy
Self-Distillation (AsyncOPSD), a framework that decouples rollout
generation from learner updates to improve training throughput. Since
the teacher and student share parameters, AsyncOPSD eliminates the
multi-model coordination overhead inherent to OPD architectures in
asynchronous training. As the student policy evolves, buffered
trajectories generated by older policies become stale, potentially
degrading performance. To mitigate this, we introduce a distillation
objective based on staleness-aware policy control and token-level
entropy weighting. Across three model sizes and mathematical reasoning
benchmarks, AsyncOPSD improves the training throughput by
$`1.96`$–$`2.25\times`$ over synchronous OPSD while increasing average
benchmark accuracy.

## 1 Introduction

On-policy distillation (OPD) has been used as an effective approach in
the large language model (LLM) post-training [Yang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib26); [Zeng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib27); [Gu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib29). A student is trained on its own generated on-policy
responses using token-level feedback from a teacher. Training on
student-generated prefixes mitigates the train-test distribution shift
inherent in distilling from fixed trajectories [Agarwal et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib1). OPD typically uses a separate large teacher model
while On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) instead instantiates both roles
from a single language model. The privileged information, including a
verified solution reasoning trace or feedback, augments the teacher’s
context, while the student receives only the question. Matching their
token-level distributions transfers the benefit of this additional
context to the student without requiring an external teacher [Zhao et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib2).

Despite this simplification, large-scale OPSD and OPD inherit the
on-policy rollout bottleneck. Long reasoning traces dominate training
time, and strict synchronization forces the learner to wait for new
rollouts, leaving compute resources idle. While asynchronous execution
improves utilization by generating rollouts and updating the learner
concurrently, buffered trajectories quickly become stale. Training on
this outdated data introduces a distribution mismatch and creates a
performance–efficiency trade-off [Kang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib3). Recent
work explores this trade-off in asynchronous OPD [Wang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib21); [Rang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib22); [Kang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib3). These studies highlight the importance of designing
asynchronous pipelines. We study asynchronous execution for OPSD by
overlapping rollout generation and learner-side privileged-context
distillation under a fixed GPU budget.

We introduce Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation (AsyncOPSD), a
framework that decouples rollout generation from learner updates.
Learners update model weights asynchronously from a shared replay buffer
and periodically export the current policy (model weights), and
distributed rollout actors generate trajectories after synchronizing the
refreshed policy. At each update, learners evaluate buffered
trajectories under both the student and a privileged teacher context.
Computing teacher supervision on the learner side eliminates
teacher-scoring stages and the communication overhead of transmitting
cached teacher distributions, substantially simplifying system
coordination compared to AsyncOPD [Kang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib3).

However, buffered sequences inevitably reflect the behavioral
distribution of older policies. Our theoretical analysis bounds the
discrepancy using prefix-distribution mismatch and an entropy-dependent
loss bound. This motivates two complementary controls: 1)
Staleness-Aware Policy Control: Limits the age of rollouts accepted for
training; 2) Token-Level Entropy Weighting: Reduces the relative
contribution of high-entropy teacher predictions. These techniques act
on rollout selection and token-level loss weighting, respectively.
Systematic ablation studies evaluate their efficacy. Our main
contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  AsyncOPSD Framework: Introduced an asynchronous on-policy
  self-distillation architecture that overlaps rollout generation with
  optimization while computing privileged teacher supervision directly
  within the learner, removing the multi-model coordination overhead in
  asynchronous OPD training.
- •
  Staleness & Uncertainty Mitigation: Analyzed how prefix staleness and
  teacher entropy enter an objective-discrepancy bound and proposed
  staleness-aware policy control and token-level entropy weighting to
  stabilize training.
- •
  Empirical Acceleration: Evaluated AsyncOPSD across challenging
  reasoning benchmarks, achieving $`1.96`$–$`2.25\times`$ the sample
  throughput of synchronous OPSD and increasing average benchmark
  accuracy by $`0.37`$–$`1.10`$ percentage points across three model
  sizes.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 On-Policy and Self-Distillation

On-policy distillation (OPD) reduces the mismatch between training and
inference by supervising trajectories sampled from the student [Agarwal
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib1); [Gu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib5); [Lu and Lab
(2025)](#bib.bib28); [Ko et al. (2024)](#bib.bib23); [Yang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib30). Per-token KL-divergence feedback from a teacher
model in the OPD also offers a dense learning signal at every step to
reinforce sub-optimal reasoning. Recent studies optimize transfer
efficiency via thinking-pattern compatibility [Li et al.
(2026c)](#bib.bib6), teacher-entropy regularization [Jin et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib8), and uncertainty-disagreement token filtering [Xu et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib7). Other OPD efficiency methods adapt rollout
horizons [Tan et al. (2026)](#bib.bib35) or combine rollout-depth
control with turn-normalized supervision [Zhou et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib36). Self-distillation extends self-taught reasoning
paradigms [Zelikman et al. (2022)](#bib.bib24) by removing external
teachers entirely, extracting supervision from self-generated reasoning
traces [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib2), environment execution feedback
[Hübotter et al. (2026)](#bib.bib9), hybrid reward-distillation
objectives on failed rollouts [Li et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib10), or
majority-vote pseudo-solutions [Li et al. (2026d)](#bib.bib34). POPO
studies positive-only policy optimization with implicit negative
gradients [Xu and Fang (2026)](#bib.bib37). Recent work examines
reference-induced shortcuts [Shen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib31), reasoning
degradation [Kaur et al. (2026)](#bib.bib32), reduced output
diversity [Nicolicioiu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib33), and sensitivity to
reasoning modes and teacher prefixes [Li et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib39) in
OPSD. Broader OP(S)D diagnostics identify supervision mismatch and
objective-dependent instability [Zhu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib38).

### 2.2 Asynchronous Reinforcement Learning

Decoupling rollout generation from policy optimization provides the
foundation for scalable distributed RL [Mnih et al. (2016)](#bib.bib11);
[Espeholt et al. (2018)](#bib.bib12). In LLM post-training, asynchronous
architectures maximize accelerator utilization through pipeline
overlapping [Noukhovitch et al. (2025)](#bib.bib13), worker load
balancing [Fu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14), disaggregated streaming
generation [Zhong et al. (2025)](#bib.bib15), and dynamic trajectory
repacking [Sheng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib16). To mitigate distribution
mismatch caused by policy lag, algorithmic solutions employ proximal
interpolation [Li et al. (2025)](#bib.bib17) and second-moment
importance-weight bounding [Zheng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib18). However,
while RL relies on scalar feedback, distillation depends on dense
prefix-conditioned teacher distributions, creating distinct staleness
dynamics.

### 2.3 Asynchronous On-Policy Distillation

Asynchronous OPD introduces coupled systems and distribution bottlenecks
when integrating teacher evaluation into concurrent pipelines. Systems
frameworks address these bottlenecks via pipelined scheduling [Sheng et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib19) and distributed hidden-state streaming to reduce
vocabulary communication [Zhang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib20).
Algorithmically, AsyncOPD [Kang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib3) analyzes
staleness under cached teacher scores, demonstrating divergence
sensitivities and the inherent limits of action reweighting. Extending
this, f-OPD [Chen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib4) separates policy drift from
supervision drift using freshness weighting and adaptive refreshes and
DivOPD [Wang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib21) prioritizes high-disagreement
turns across stale queues.

However, asynchronous on-policy self-distillation is still
underexplored. To address this gap, we introduce AsyncOPSD, which
overlaps rollout generation and learner updates under a fixed GPU budget
and simplifies distributed coordination by recomputing privileged
teacher supervision directly within the learner.

## 3 Preliminaries

For next-token distributions $`p`$ and $`q`$ over vocabulary
$`\mathcal{V}`$, define
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p(v)\log\frac{p(v)}{q(v)}`$.

#### On-Policy Distillation.

On-policy distillation (OPD) [Li et al. (2026c)](#bib.bib6) trains a
student $`p_{S}`$ with parameters $`\theta`$ using a separate, fixed
teacher $`p_{T}`$. Given a prompt $`x\sim\mathcal{S}_{x}`$, where
$`\mathcal{S}_{x}`$ is the prompt dataset, the student samples
$`\hat{y}\sim p_{S}(\cdot\mid x)`$. Both models evaluate the same
generated prefixes, yielding $`p_{S,n}=p_{S}(\cdot\mid x,\hat{y}_{<n})`$
and $`p_{T,n}=p_{T}(\cdot\mid x,\hat{y}_{<n})`$. The objective is

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\mathcal{S}_{x}\\
\hat{y}\sim p_{S}(\cdot\mid x)\end{subarray}}\!\left[\frac{1}{|\hat{y}|}\sum_{n=1}^{|\hat{y}|}D(p_{T,n}\|p_{S,n})\right]
``` |  |

commonly instantiated with reverse KL. The teacher remains fixed during
training.

#### On-Policy Self-Distillation.

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib2) uses
a single LLM as both teacher and student by conditioning on asymmetric
prompt contexts: Student Policy $`p_{S}(\cdot\mid x)`$: Observes only
the original problem prompt $`x`$, faithfully replicating inference
conditions. Teacher Policy $`p_{T}(\cdot\mid x,y^{\star})`$: Receives
privileged context consisting of the question $`x`$ paired with verified
reference reasoning, ground-truth answers $`y^{\star}`$ or any other
useful feedback.

Given problem-solution pairs $`(x,y^{\star})`$ from a reasoning dataset
$`\mathcal{S}`$, the student samples $`\hat{y}\sim p_{S}(\cdot\mid x)`$.
Using the divergence $`D`$ and setting
$`p_{S,n}=p_{S}(\cdot\mid x,\hat{y}_{<n})`$ and
$`p_{T,n}=p_{T}(\cdot\mid x,y^{\star},\hat{y}_{<n})`$, the objective
becomes

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y^{\star})\sim\mathcal{S}\\
\hat{y}\sim p_{S}(\cdot\mid x)\end{subarray}}\!\left[\frac{1}{|\hat{y}|}\sum_{n=1}^{|\hat{y}|}D(p_{T,n}\|p_{S,n})\right]
``` |  |

Gradients are backpropagated solely through the student policy
$`p_{S}`$, while the privileged teacher $`p_{T}`$ acts as a
stop-gradient target.

## 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method

### 4.1 Asynchronous Training Framework

Extending On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD), we implement a baseline
asynchronous variant (AsyncOPSD) by decoupling trajectory rollout
generation from policy optimization across disjoint GPU pools. The GPU
cluster is partitioned into the learner pool and the rollout actor pool.
The learners execute forward passes, compute gradients, and optimize the
model with the batch in the queue from rollout actors and periodically
export the current policy (model weights). Concurrently, in the rollout
actor pool, distributed rollout actors host independent, single-GPU vLLM
instances to sample after loading the refreshed policy, pushing
generated trajectories into a shared rollout queue.

Consistent with standard OPSD, no auxiliary teacher model is required;
the student is optimized using a clipped forward-KL distillation
objective. Model export frequency is governed by sync_step, enabling
generation and gradient computation to overlap seamlessly at the cost of
bounded policy staleness. To prevent memory overload and control queue
delays, the system limits queue size based on learner microbatches.

### 4.2 Staleness–Entropy Analysis

Asynchronous training potentially degrades performance. We provide an
analysis relating this degradation to student rollout staleness and the
privileged teacher entropy. We first analyze the evolving shared-teacher
formulation, in which privileged teacher and student predictions use the
current parameters $`\theta_{k}`$. For the fair comparison in
[Section 5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
we freeze the teacher policy to be the initial policy following the OPSD
protocol [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib2).
Appendix [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Extension to a frozen privileged teacher ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
establishes the same form of staleness–entropy bound.

#### Shared-model formulation.

Let $`k`$ denote the learner update and $`r\leq k`$ the policy version
used for rollout generation. A training example consists of a problem
$`x`$ and privileged information $`a`$, such as a verified answer. For a
response prefix $`y_{<h}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{h}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(x,a,h,y_{<h}),`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{k}(\cdot\mid s_{h})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta_{k}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<h}),`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{k}(\cdot\mid s_{h})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta_{k}}(\cdot\mid x,a,y_{<h}).`$ |  |

Teacher and student share the same model. The teacher output is detached
during optimization. The per-prefix forward-KL loss is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F_{k}(s)=\operatorname{KL}\!\left(\operatorname{sg}[q_{k}(\cdot\mid s)]\,\middle\|\,p_{k}(\cdot\mid s)\right)
``` |  |

where $`\operatorname{sg}`$ denotes stop-gradient. At a fixed $`k`$,
stop-gradient affects differentiation but not the numerical value of
$`F_{k}`$. Let $`\mu_{r}`$ and $`\mu_{k}`$ be prefix distributions
induced by the rollout and current policies, respectively. Both use the
same distribution of $`(x,a)`$ and the same fixed distribution over
token positions $`h\in\{1,\ldots,T\}`$. A finite horizon and a common
continuation convention after termination make these distributions
well-defined. We consider a fixed rollout version $`r`$. The
asynchronous and synchronous population objectives are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{async}}^{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_{r}}[F_{k}(s)],`$ |  | (1) |
|  | $`\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{sync}}^{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_{k}}[F_{k}(s)],`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left|J_{\mathrm{async}}^{k}-J_{\mathrm{sync}}^{k}\right|.`$ |  |  |

This formulation follows the objective-discrepancy perspective of
$`f`$-OPD [Chen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib4), while specializing the
supervision protocol. Both learner-side distributions are recomputed at
$`\theta_{k}`$ on the stored prefix.

The following theorem bounds the discrepancy between asynchronous and
synchronous training objectives in terms of prefix staleness and teacher
entropy, under the assumptions in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").

###### Theorem 1 (Joint staleness–entropy bound).

Fix a learner update $`k`$ and rollout version $`r\leq k`$, with the
prefix laws and objectives in
Eq. ([1](#S4.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ Shared-model formulation. ‣ 4.2 Staleness–Entropy Analysis ‣ 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
Suppose
Assumptions [1](#Thmaopsdassumption1 "Assumption 1 (Common sampling protocol and coverage). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and [2](#Thmaopsdassumption2 "Assumption 2 (Bounded privileged logit contrast). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
hold, with $`B\geq 0`$ the uniform privileged logit contrast bound in
Assumption [2](#Thmaopsdassumption2 "Assumption 2 (Bounded privileged logit contrast). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").
Define the prefix likelihood ratio, its staleness measure, and the
current teacher entropy by

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\rho_{k,r}(s_{h})`$ | $`\displaystyle:=\frac{\mu_{k}(s_{h})}{\mu_{r}(s_{h})}=\prod_{u<h}\frac{p_{k}(y_{u}\mid s_{u})}{p_{r}(y_{u}\mid s_{u})},`$ |  | (2) |
|  | $`\displaystyle d_{k,r}(s)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=|1-\rho_{k,r}(s)|,`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle H_{k}(s)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}q_{k}(v\mid s)\log q_{k}(v\mid s),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle C_{B}`$ | $`\displaystyle:=\frac{B(e^{B}-1)}{2}.`$ |  |  |

Here $`p_{r}`$ is the rollout-version student policy under the common
sampling convention; the product is one when $`h=1`$, and all logarithms
are natural. The current per-prefix loss satisfies

|     |                                                              |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       0\leq F_{k}(s)\leq C_{B}(1-e^{-H_{k}(s)})\leq C_{B}H_{k}(s).
       ```                                                           |     | (3) |

Consequently, the asynchronous objective discrepancy obeys

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B}\,\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_{r}}\!\left[\underbrace{d_{k,r}(s)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{prefix}\\
\text{staleness}\end{subarray}}\,\underbrace{(1-e^{-H_{k}(s)})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{teacher}\\
\text{entropy factor}\end{subarray}}\right]`$ |  | (4) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B}\,\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_{r}}\!\left[d_{k,r}(s)H_{k}(s)\right].`$ |  |  |

The supporting assumptions, lemmas, and full proof are provided in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").

#### Discussion.

The bound motivates two complementary controls on prefix staleness and
teacher entropy. The required signals are already available during
training. We use the policy-version gap $`k-r`$ as an inexpensive proxy
for prefix-distribution staleness, while teacher entropy is available
from the existing teacher forward pass. Their joint use therefore
requires no additional forward pass.

### 4.3 Staleness Control and Entropy Weighting

#### Staleness-Aware Policy Control.

Let $`k`$ be the current learner optimizer step and $`r_{i}`$ the step
of the published policy that generated sample $`i`$. Its staleness is
$`s_{i}=\max\{0,k-r_{i}\}`$. We reject rollouts with a staleness
$`s_{i}`$ greater than $`K`$ using the filter:

|     |                                           |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       g_{K}(\tau_{i})=\mathbf{1}\{s_{i}\leq K\}
       ```                                        |     |

where $`\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}`$ is the indicator function. The learner
rejects staleness above $`K`$ when claiming a queued rollout batch,
fetching another batch until an admissible one is available. Generation
and learning continue asynchronously. We set $`K=6`$ based on the mean
staleness in
[Table 1](#S5.T1 "In Implementation details. ‣ 5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and an ablation study in
[Section 5.6](#S5.SS6 "5.6 Ablation Study ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").
Policy version gap is an inexpensive proxy for distribution drift.

#### Token-Level Entropy Weighting.

For an accepted completion token $`h`$ of rollout $`i`$, write
$`p_{i,h}`$ for the current student distribution and $`q_{i,h}`$ for the
privileged fixed-teacher distribution. Both use temperature-scaled
logits. Define teacher entropy:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle H_{i,h}`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}q_{i,h}(v)\log q_{i,h}(v),`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle u_{i,h}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\exp(-\beta_{H}H_{i,h}),\qquad\beta_{H}\geq 0.`$ |  |

Over all valid accepted completion positions $`\mathcal{A}`$ in each
local learner microbatch, we normalize

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
w_{i,h}=\frac{u_{i,h}}{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{(j,m)\in\mathcal{A}}u_{j,m}}
``` |  |

These weights reduce high-entropy tokens’ relative contribution and have
mean one when the numerical floor is inactive. They reuse the teacher
probabilities already computed for distillation. Setting $`\beta_{H}=0`$
recovers uniform weights. We retain per-token pointwise clipping in all
conditions:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\ell_{c}(q,p)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\min\!\left\{q(v)\log\frac{q(v)}{p(v)},c\right\}
``` |  |

where $`c\geq 0`$ is the clipping threshold. Combining staleness
filtering and token-level entropy weighting with this clipped loss gives
the following objective, $`\mathcal{C}`$ is the set of candidate
rollouts and $`T_{i}`$ is the valid completion length of rollout $`i`$.
For rejected rollouts, set $`w_{i,h}=0`$ so that all terms below are
defined:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{k}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}g_{K}(\tau_{i})\sum_{h=1}^{T_{i}}\operatorname{sg}[w_{i,h}]\ell_{c}(\operatorname{sg}[q_{i,h}],p_{i,h})}{\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}}g_{K}(\tau_{i})T_{i}}
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\operatorname{sg}`$ denotes stop-gradient and normalization is
over accepted tokens. The learner waits for accepted data if the
denominator would be zero. The controls are motivated by the analysis,
but do not constitute an exact correction of stale sampling. Entropy
weighting changes the relative importance of tokens without changing the
teacher target distribution, unlike the PMI target construction of
Purified OPSD [Shen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib31). High-entropy positions
can also support useful reasoning branches [Kaur et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib32); downweighting them is a heuristic whose effect on
reasoning diversity requires separate evaluation.

## 5 Experiments

We study the throughput–quality tradeoff of asynchronous on-policy
self-distillation.

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Models, data, and evaluation.

Following OPSD [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib2), we consider Qwen3-1.7B,
Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B, using the instruct-tuned versions. For training
data, we use the mathematical reasoning subset of OpenThoughts [Guha et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib25). We evaluate on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and HMMT
2025 and report Avg@12. The details of the evaluation are shown in
[Table 5](#A2.T5 "In Appendix B Experiment Details ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").

#### Implementation details.

As in [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib2), we use forward KL with
full-vocabulary logit distillation with per-token pointwise KL clipping
and the pointwise divergence clipping defined in
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Staleness Control and Entropy Weighting ‣ 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").
Following the OPSD protocol [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib2), we freeze
the teacher at the initial model in all synchronous and asynchronous
conditions to keep the teacher strategy matched across the empirical
comparisons. The student receives the question in Thinking Mode
(TM)-off, while the TM-on teacher additionally receives the reference
solution.

All runs use eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs, rank-64 LoRA with bfloat16
precision, gradient checkpointing, and FlashAttention-2 for memory
efficiency. Asynchronous execution allocates four GPUs to rollout actors
and four to learners, with an effective global batch of 32. Synchronous
OPSD uses all eight GPUs for alternating generation and optimization
with the same effective global batch of 32. The training configuration
is listed in
Table [4](#A2.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Appendix B Experiment Details ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").

#### Efficiency metrics.

We report accepted-training-sample throughput,
$`R_{\mathrm{sample}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{accepted}}}{T_{\mathrm{train}}}`$,
where accepted samples are sequence occurrences actually consumed for
optimization, and $`T_{\mathrm{train}}`$ is the Trainer-measured
training interval. Mean staleness uses $`s_{i}`$ from accepted rollout
samples. Relative throughput is normalized to the recorded synchronous
run for the same model, following the reporting style of asynchronous
distillation studies [Chen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib4); [Kang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib3). We use sequences rather than response tokens, so our
values are not numerically comparable to token-throughput results in
AsyncOPD.

### 5.3 Vanilla Asynchronous OPSD

Vanilla AsyncOPSD decouples generation from optimization. Actors
generate with refreshed student policies and place rollouts in a shared
queue; learners consume them, perform model training, and periodically
export the policy. Only student LoRA adapters are published, avoiding
full-model reloads on actors.

Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.2 Policy Export Frequency ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
reports the complete accuracy comparison, including the controlled
variants discussed next. Vanilla asynchronous execution reduces the
cross-benchmark average from 43.43 to 42.97 on 1.7B, from 63.60 to 62.30
on 4B, and from 64.80 to 64.23 on 8B. The decline is largest on 4B; it
is not uniform across individual benchmarks. These results motivate
controlling the optimization mismatch introduced by stale rollouts. The
analysis in
[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Staleness–Entropy Analysis ‣ 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
provides a complementary motivation by identifying how
prefix-distribution drift and teacher entropy interact in the objective
discrepancy.

### 5.5 Training Throughput

#### Synchronous baseline.

To get a stronger baseline, we decouple synchronous generation batch
size from training microbatch size to avoid unnecessarily restricting
inference throughput. All three synchronous models generate 4 prompts
per GPU per vLLM call. For 1.7B and 4B, the training microbatch is also
4, with one gradient-accumulation step. For 8B, we retain a training
microbatch of 2 and 2 gradient-accumulation steps, while generating both
microbatches together. This reduces the number of generation calls per
GPU per optimizer update for 8B model from two to one without increasing
the forward/backward microbatch. The generated responses are consumed
within the same optimizer update, preserving synchronous execution and
an effective global batch of 32.

#### Throughput and quality tradeoff.

Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Policy Export Frequency ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
compares the updated synchronous baseline with all three asynchronous
conditions. Synchronous OPSD achieves $`4.07\pm 0.32`$,
$`2.40\pm 0.33`$, and $`1.70\pm 0.32`$ samples/s on 1.7B, 4B, and 8B,
respectively. Vanilla AsyncOPSD achieves 9.22, 5.53, and 3.61 samples/s,
while AsyncOPSD achieves 8.00, 5.39, and 3.40 samples/s. Relative to the
updated synchronous means, vanilla execution improves throughput by
factors of 2.26, 2.31, and 2.12; staleness control alone achieves 2.01,
2.28, and 2.01 times the synchronous throughput. AsyncOPSD achieves
1.96$`\times`$, 2.25$`\times`$, and 1.99$`\times`$ throughput with
reported average accuracy gains over OPSD of 0.37, 1.10, and 0.87
percentage points on 1.7B, 4B, and 8B, respectively. It retains 86.8%,
97.5%, and 94.2% of vanilla throughput. Relative to staleness control
alone, entropy weighting changes mean throughput by $`-2.4`$%,
$`-1.3`$%, and $`-0.9`$%.

#### Sources of throughput improvement.

The asynchronous implementation combines several systems changes. 1)
Larger generation batches: Each rollout actor generates 32 completions
per vLLM call, compared with four completions per GPU in the synchronous
baseline. Larger batches can improve inference efficiency through higher
GPU utilization. 2) Lower policy-refresh overhead. The synchronous
implementation refreshes vLLM by merging the LoRA adapters every step.
The asynchronous implementation instead exports only the updated LoRA
adapters at a configurable sync_step. 3) Nonlinear learner scaling.
Doubling the number of learner GPUs does not necessarily halve
optimization time. Communication, synchronization, and small per-device
workloads can limit scaling efficiency. Consequently, splitting GPUs
into a learner pool and rollout actors pool can improve overall
utilization despite using fewer GPUs for optimization. 4) Overlapping
generation and optimization. Rollout actors generate upcoming batches
while learners optimize previously generated samples. This overlap
reduces idle periods associated with alternating generation and
optimization in synchronous execution.

### 5.6 Ablation Study

#### GPU-Allocation Ablation.

[Figure 2](#S5.F2 "In Sources of throughput improvement. ‣ 5.5 Training Throughput ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
shows the learner/actor allocation over 2/6, 3/5, 4/4, 5/3, and 6/2 on
eight A100s with vanilla asynchronous execution. The balanced 4/4
allocation provides the highest mean sample throughput for each model:
8.63, 5.02, and 3.35 samples/s for 1.7B, 4B, and 8B. Allocating more
GPUs to either generation or learning alone is insufficient: the two
stages must supply and consume samples at compatible rates. We adopt 4/4
as a common operating point.

#### Staleness-Cutoff Ablation.

We sweep $`K\in\{4,6,8,10\}`$ on Qwen3-1.7B with $`\beta_{H}=0`$.
Throughput relative to the sweep’s $`K=6`$ mean is 0.755, 1.000, 1.114,
and 1.108, respectively. Increasing $`K`$ from 4 to 6 improves
throughput by 32.4% and reduces rejection among claimed rollouts from
33.9% to 10.7%. Compared with $`K=8`$, $`K=6`$ retains 89.8% of
throughput while reducing mean accepted-rollout staleness from 6.12 to
5.35; $`K=10`$ provides no further mean throughput gain. For the tested
values K = 8 and K = 10, no rejections occurred. It is similar to the
vanilla AsyncOPSD. So we choose $`K=6`$ as a conservative
throughput–freshness compromise.

## Conclusion

We introduced AsyncOPSD, which improves training efficiency by
decoupling rollout generation from learner updates. Our analysis bounds
the resulting objective discrepancy in terms of prefix staleness and
teacher entropy, motivating staleness control and token-level entropy
weighting. Across three model sizes and mathematical reasoning
benchmarks, AsyncOPSD achieves $`1.96`$–$`2.25\times`$ the sample
throughput of an optimized synchronous baseline while increasing average
benchmark accuracy over OPSD by $`0.37`$–$`1.10`$ percentage points.

## Limitations

Our experiments use eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs and models ranging from 1.7B
to 8B parameters. Within this budget, comparisons across three model
sizes and targeted ablations provide evidence for the throughput
benefits of asynchronous execution and the effectiveness of staleness
control and entropy weighting in the evaluated settings. A broader
exploration of model scales and training configurations is left to
future work.

## Appendix A Theoretical Analysis

This appendix supplies the sampling assumptions and intermediate lemmas
used in
Theorem [1](#Thmaopsdtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Joint staleness–entropy bound). ‣ Shared-model formulation. ‣ 4.2 Staleness–Entropy Analysis ‣ 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
followed by its full proof. The learner update $`k`$ and rollout version
$`r\leq k`$ are fixed. The first three subsections evaluate both the
student and privileged teacher at the current parameters $`\theta_{k}`$
when computing the loss.
Appendix [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Extension to a frozen privileged teacher ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
extends the result to a teacher frozen at initialization, as used in our
experiments.

### A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions

###### Assumption 1 (Common sampling protocol and coverage).

The rollout and current prefix laws use the same problem–reference
distribution $`\nu(x,a)`$, the same fixed token-position probabilities
$`\alpha_{h}`$ for $`h\in\{1,\ldots,T\}`$, and the same continuation
convention after termination. Prefixes are generated autoregressively
using the corresponding student policies $`p_{r}`$ and $`p_{k}`$, and
$`\mu_{k}\ll\mu_{r}`$.

The notation $`\mu_{k}\ll\mu_{r}`$ means absolute continuity. In the
discrete prefix setting, it is the support condition

|     |                                                    |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mu_{r}(s)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mu_{k}(s)=0.
       ```                                                 |     |

Equivalently, every prefix with positive current-policy probability must
also have positive rollout-policy probability. This condition allows the
current prefix law to be represented by reweighting the rollout law; it
does not compare the magnitudes of the probabilities. It concerns
prefixes the rollout policy could generate, rather than requiring every
such prefix to appear in a finite realized buffer.

Under
Assumption [1](#Thmaopsdassumption1 "Assumption 1 (Common sampling protocol and coverage). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
for $`j\in\{r,k\}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mu_{j}(s_{h})=\nu(x,a)\,\alpha_{h}\prod_{u<h}p_{j}(y_{u}\mid s_{u}).
``` |  | (6) |

Full-support softmax sampling on a common vocabulary is sufficient for
the coverage condition. The common problem and token-position factors
cancel when comparing the two prefix laws.

Let $`z_{k}^{S}(s)`$ and $`z_{k}^{T}(s)`$ be the student and privileged
teacher logits, respectively. They satisfy

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{softmax}(z_{k}^{S}),`$ |  | (7) |
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{softmax}(z_{k}^{T}),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle b_{k}(s)`$ | $`\displaystyle=z_{k}^{T}(s)-z_{k}^{S}(s).`$ |  |  |

###### Assumption 2 (Bounded privileged logit contrast).

There exists a finite constant $`B\geq 0`$ such that, at the analyzed
update and uniformly over the support of $`\mu_{r}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{osc}(b_{k}(s)):=\max_{v}b_{k,v}(s)-\min_{v}b_{k,v}(s)\leq B.
``` |  | (8) |

The finite-support probability lower bound in Assumption 3.1 of
$`f`$-OPD [Chen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib4) is sufficient for this
contrast condition when it holds on the same support used to evaluate
the loss. Indeed, if $`p_{k}(v\mid s),q_{k}(v\mid s)\geq\epsilon>0`$,
then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
-\log(1/\epsilon)\leq\log\frac{q_{k}(v\mid s)}{p_{k}(v\mid s)}\leq\log(1/\epsilon).
``` |  |

The vector $`b_{k}(s)`$ differs from $`\log(q_{k}/p_{k})`$ only by a
common scalar, so its oscillation is at most $`2\log(1/\epsilon)`$. A
lower bound imposed only on truncated distributions does not
automatically imply the full-vocabulary condition. We state the contrast
assumption directly because parameter sharing alone does not guarantee a
small $`B`$, and $`C_{B}=B(e^{B}-1)/2`$ can be large when the admissible
contrast is large.

### A.2 Supporting lemmas

###### Lemma 1 (Change of measure and student staleness).

Under
Assumption [1](#Thmaopsdassumption1 "Assumption 1 (Common sampling protocol and coverage). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{k,r}(s)=\frac{\mu_{k}(s)}{\mu_{r}(s)},\qquad d_{k,r}(s)=|1-\rho_{k,r}(s)|.
``` |  | (9) |

The prefix likelihood ratio satisfies

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{k,r}(s_{h})=\prod_{u<h}\frac{p_{k}(y_{u}\mid s_{u})}{p_{r}(y_{u}\mid s_{u})}.
``` |  | (10) |

Whenever the two objectives are finite,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\left[(1-\rho_{k,r}(s))F_{k}(s)\right]\right|`$ |  | (11) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\left[d_{k,r}(s)F_{k}(s)\right].`$ |  |  |

Moreover, $`\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}[d_{k,r}(s)]\leq 2`$.

###### Proof.

Dividing the factorizations in
Eq. ([6](#A1.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"))
cancels $`\nu(x,a)`$ and $`\alpha_{h}`$, which gives
Eq. ([10](#A1.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ Lemma 1 (Change of measure and student staleness). ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
Absolute continuity ensures that the likelihood ratio exists
$`\mu_{r}`$-almost everywhere and permits the change of measure

|     |                                                                    |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       J_{\mathrm{sync}}^{k}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}[\rho_{k,r}(s)F_{k}(s)].
       ```                                                                 |     |

Both objectives use exactly the same current loss $`F_{k}(s)`$; only the
distribution over prefixes changes. Subtracting the last expression from
$`J_{\mathrm{async}}^{k}`$ therefore yields the equality in
Eq. ([11](#A1.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ Lemma 1 (Change of measure and student staleness). ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
The triangle inequality and $`F_{k}(s)\geq 0`$ give its upper bound.
Finally, since $`\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}[\rho_{k,r}]=1`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}[d_{k,r}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}[1+\rho_{k,r}]=2.
``` |  |

Equivalently, this expectation is
$`2\operatorname{TV}(\mu_{r},\mu_{k})`$ under the convention
$`\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|P-Q\|_{1}`$. ∎

###### Lemma 2 (Entropy-sensitive self-distillation loss).

Under
Assumption [2](#Thmaopsdassumption2 "Assumption 2 (Bounded privileged logit contrast). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
the current forward-KL loss satisfies, for every prefix in the support
of $`\mu_{r}`$,

|     |                                                              |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       0\leq F_{k}(s)\leq C_{B}(1-e^{-H_{k}(s)})\leq C_{B}H_{k}(s),
       ```                                                           |     |

where $`H_{k}(s)`$ and $`C_{B}`$ are defined in
Eq. ([2](#S4.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ Theorem 1 (Joint staleness–entropy bound). ‣ Shared-model formulation. ‣ 4.2 Staleness–Entropy Analysis ‣ 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")).

###### Proof.

Suppress $`k,s`$ and write $`p=\operatorname{softmax}(z^{S})`$ and
$`q=\operatorname{softmax}(z^{T})`$. Because $`z^{S}=z^{T}-b`$, set

|     |                                  |     |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \delta_{v}=-b_{v}+\max_{u}b_{u}.
       ```                               |     |

Assumption [2](#Thmaopsdassumption2 "Assumption 2 (Bounded privileged logit contrast). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
gives $`0\leq\delta_{v}\leq B`$. Adding a common scalar to logits does
not change their softmax, so

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{v}=\frac{q_{v}e^{\delta_{v}}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_{u}q_{u}e^{\delta_{u}}\geq 1.
``` |  | (12) |

First, bound the distance between the two predictive distributions:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(p,q)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2Z}\sum_{v}q_{v}|e^{\delta_{v}}-Z|`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2Z}\sum_{v,u}q_{v}q_{u}|e^{\delta_{v}}-e^{\delta_{u}}|`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{B}-1}{2}\left(1-\sum_{v}q_{v}^{2}\right).`$ |  | (13) |

The first inequality substitutes $`Z=\sum_{u}q_{u}e^{\delta_{u}}`$ and
applies the triangle inequality. The second uses $`Z\geq 1`$,
$`|e^{\delta_{v}}-e^{\delta_{u}}|\leq e^{B}-1`$, and the fact that the
summand is zero for $`u=v`$.

Next,
Eq. ([12](#A1.E12 "Equation 12 ‣ Proof. ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"))
implies

|     |                                            |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \log\frac{p_{v}}{q_{v}}=\delta_{v}-\log Z.
       ```                                         |     |

Consequently,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(q\|p)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\log Z-\sum_{v}q_{v}\delta_{v},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(p\|q)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v}p_{v}\delta_{v}-\log Z.`$ |  | (14) |

Adding the two identities cancels the normalization term. With
$`I_{+}=\{v:p_{v}>q_{v}\}`$, the remaining expression obeys

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(q\|p)+\operatorname{KL}(p\|q)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{v}(p_{v}-q_{v})\delta_{v}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad\leq B\sum_{v\in I_{+}}(p_{v}-q_{v})=B\operatorname{TV}(p,q).`$ |  | (15) |

Here the components outside $`I_{+}`$ contribute nonpositive terms, and
$`\delta_{v}\leq B`$ on $`I_{+}`$. Since the reverse KL is nonnegative,
combining this inequality with
Eq. ([13](#A1.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ Proof. ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"))
gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F_{k}(s)\leq\frac{B(e^{B}-1)}{2}\left(1-\sum_{v}q_{v}^{2}\right).
``` |  | (16) |

Finally, Jensen’s inequality for the convex function $`-\log`$ yields

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H(q)=\sum_{v}q_{v}[-\log q_{v}]\geq-\log\!\left(\sum_{v}q_{v}^{2}\right).
``` |  |

Thus $`e^{-H(q)}\leq\sum_{v}q_{v}^{2}`$, and

|     |                                               |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       1-\sum_{v}q_{v}^{2}\leq 1-e^{-H(q)}\leq H(q).
       ```                                            |     |

Substituting this relation into
Eq. ([16](#A1.E16 "Equation 16 ‣ Proof. ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")),
together with the nonnegativity of KL, proves the lemma. ∎

### A.3 Proof of Theorem [1](#Thmaopsdtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Joint staleness–entropy bound). ‣ Shared-model formulation. ‣ 4.2 Staleness–Entropy Analysis ‣ 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")

###### Proof of Theorem [1](#Thmaopsdtheorem1 "Theorem 1 (Joint staleness–entropy bound). ‣ Shared-model formulation. ‣ 4.2 Staleness–Entropy Analysis ‣ 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").

By
Assumptions [1](#Thmaopsdassumption1 "Assumption 1 (Common sampling protocol and coverage). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and [2](#Thmaopsdassumption2 "Assumption 2 (Bounded privileged logit contrast). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
the common prefix protocol, coverage, and uniform logit contrast
condition hold.
Lemma [2](#Thmaopsdlemma2 "Lemma 2 (Entropy-sensitive self-distillation loss). ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
therefore establishes the per-prefix loss bound in
Eq. ([3](#S4.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ Theorem 1 (Joint staleness–entropy bound). ‣ Shared-model formulation. ‣ 4.2 Staleness–Entropy Analysis ‣ 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
In particular, $`0\leq F_{k}(s)\leq C_{B}`$ on the rollout support and,
by coverage, on the current-policy support. Hence both population
objectives are finite.
Lemma [1](#Thmaopsdlemma1 "Lemma 1 (Change of measure and student staleness). ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
also gives $`\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}[d_{k,r}]\leq 2`$, ensuring that the
product with the bounded loss envelope is integrable.

Apply the change-of-measure lemma and then the entropy-sensitive loss
bound pointwise:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\left[(1-\rho_{k,r}(s))F_{k}(s)\right]\right|`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\left[d_{k,r}(s)F_{k}(s)\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\left[d_{k,r}(s)(1-e^{-H_{k}(s)})\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\left[d_{k,r}(s)H_{k}(s)\right].`$ |  | (17) |

The last step uses $`1-e^{-H}\leq H`$ for $`H\geq 0`$ and
$`d_{k,r}(s)\geq 0`$. This proves
Eq. ([4](#S4.E4 "Equation 4 ‣ Theorem 1 (Joint staleness–entropy bound). ‣ Shared-model formulation. ‣ 4.2 Staleness–Entropy Analysis ‣ 4 AsyncOPSD: Framework, Analysis, and Method ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
The product remains inside the expectation throughout, so independence
of staleness and entropy is not required. ∎

#### Sampling probabilities and loss normalization.

The likelihood ratio must describe the actual distributions that
generate prefixes. If generation uses a different temperature or other
probability transformation, the prefix laws and ratio must use those
sampling probabilities; the loss envelope uses the $`p_{k},q_{k}`$
appearing in the KL. Hard top-$`p`$ or top-$`k`$ truncation can violate
coverage and requires separate support handling. The simple product
formula also assumes the common fixed token-position distribution in
Assumption [1](#Thmaopsdassumption1 "Assumption 1 (Common sampling protocol and coverage). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation");
filtering or response-length normalization may alter the effective
training-prefix laws.

#### Meaning of the two factors.

The staleness signal accumulates probability mismatch over the previous
tokens of a prefix. It is not interchangeable with policy update lag or
a local one-token rollout KL without an additional argument accounting
for earlier positions. Teacher entropy controls the current loss
envelope under the contrast assumption; it does not represent a separate
cached-target mismatch. Because both learner-side predictions use
$`\theta_{k}`$ and the privileged reference is fixed, the comparison
changes only the prefix law. The theorem therefore concerns the
generating population laws, rather than the empirical distribution of a
finite realized buffer.

### A.4 Extension to a frozen privileged teacher

We now fix the privileged teacher at the initial parameters
$`\theta_{0}`$, matching the teacher strategy in
Section [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").
For $`s_{h}=(x,a,h,y_{<h})`$, define

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{0}(\cdot\mid s_{h})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot\mid x,a,y_{<h}),`$ |  | (18) |
|  | $`\displaystyle F_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{KL}\!\left(q_{0}(\cdot\mid s)\,\middle\|\,p_{k}(\cdot\mid s)\right),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle H_{0}(s)`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}q_{0}(v\mid s)\log q_{0}(v\mid s).`$ |  |  |

The teacher and its entropy are fixed functions of the prefix; they can
still vary across prefixes. The student and the prefix laws
$`\mu_{r},\mu_{k}`$ remain as defined above. Set

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{async,fr}}^{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}[F_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)],`$ |  | (19) |
|  | $`\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{sync,fr}}^{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{k}}[F_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)],`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left|J_{\mathrm{async,fr}}^{k}-J_{\mathrm{sync,fr}}^{k}\right|.`$ |  |  |

Both objectives use the same frozen teacher and current student; only
their prefix distributions differ.

###### Assumption 3 (Bounded frozen-teacher logit contrast).

Let $`q_{0}=\operatorname{softmax}(z_{0}^{T})`$ and
$`p_{k}=\operatorname{softmax}(z_{k}^{S})`$. At the analyzed update,
there exists a finite $`B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}\geq 0`$ such that, uniformly
over the support of $`\mu_{r}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{osc}\!\left(z_{0}^{T}(s)-z_{k}^{S}(s)\right)\leq B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}.
``` |  | (20) |

The logits are those used in the loss, including any temperature
scaling.

Here the contrast is the teacher–student logit difference on the same
prefix. Bounding its oscillation is equivalent to bounding the
difference in their log odds for every token pair $`u,v`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left|\log\frac{q_{0}(v\mid s)}{q_{0}(u\mid s)}-\log\frac{p_{k}(v\mid s)}{p_{k}(u\mid s)}\right|\leq B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}.
``` |  |

This is a regularity assumption on the predictive distributions, not an
additional training control.

###### Theorem 2 (Frozen-teacher staleness–entropy bound).

Under
Assumptions [1](#Thmaopsdassumption1 "Assumption 1 (Common sampling protocol and coverage). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and [3](#Thmaopsdassumption3 "Assumption 3 (Bounded frozen-teacher logit contrast). ‣ A.4 Extension to a frozen privileged teacher ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
let $`d_{k,r}`$ be the student prefix staleness in
Eq. ([9](#A1.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ Lemma 1 (Change of measure and student staleness). ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")),
and write $`C_{B}=B(e^{B}-1)/2`$. Then

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle 0\leq F_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}}(1-e^{-H_{0}(s)})`$ |  | (21) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}}H_{0}(s),`$ |  |  |

and

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\!\left[d_{k,r}(s)(1-e^{-H_{0}(s)})\right]`$ |  | (22) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\!\left[d_{k,r}(s)H_{0}(s)\right].`$ |  |  |

###### Proof.

The proof of
Lemma [2](#Thmaopsdlemma2 "Lemma 2 (Entropy-sensitive self-distillation loss). ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")
uses only the softmax representations and the bounded oscillation of
their logit difference. For a fixed prefix, set
$`b=z_{0}^{T}-z_{k}^{S}`$ and $`\delta_{v}=-b_{v}+\max_{u}b_{u}`$. Then
$`0\leq\delta_{v}\leq B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}`$ and

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{k}(v\mid s)=\frac{q_{0}(v\mid s)e^{\delta_{v}}}{\sum_{u}q_{0}(u\mid s)e^{\delta_{u}}}.
``` |  |

Thus the same total-variation and symmetric-KL inequalities give

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle F_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}}\left(1-\sum_{v}q_{0}(v\mid s)^{2}\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq C_{B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}}(1-e^{-H_{0}(s)}).`$ |  |

Nonnegativity of KL and $`1-e^{-H_{0}}\leq H_{0}`$ prove
Eq. ([21](#A1.E21 "Equation 21 ‣ Theorem 2 (Frozen-teacher staleness–entropy bound). ‣ A.4 Extension to a frozen privileged teacher ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
In particular, the loss is bounded on both prefix supports. By the same
change of measure as in
Lemma [1](#Thmaopsdlemma1 "Lemma 1 (Change of measure and student staleness). ‣ A.2 Supporting lemmas ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"),

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\left[(1-\rho_{k,r}(s))F_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)\right]\right|`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{r}}\!\left[d_{k,r}(s)F_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)\right].`$ |  |

Substitution gives
Eq. ([22](#A1.E22 "Equation 22 ‣ Theorem 2 (Frozen-teacher staleness–entropy bound). ‣ A.4 Extension to a frozen privileged teacher ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
∎

#### Accounting for student drift.

The frozen-teacher contrast assumption must be checked separately from
Assumption [2](#Thmaopsdassumption2 "Assumption 2 (Bounded privileged logit contrast). ‣ A.1 Assumptions and prefix distributions ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation").
An optional sufficient condition separates the initial teacher–student
difference from subsequent student drift. Let $`z_{0}^{S}`$ denote the
initial student’s logits. If, uniformly over the analyzed rollout
support,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{osc}(z_{0}^{T}(s)-z_{0}^{S}(s))`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq B_{\mathrm{init}},`$ |  | (23) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{osc}(z_{k}^{S}(s)-z_{0}^{S}(s))`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq D_{k},`$ |  |  |

then subadditivity of oscillation yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{osc}(z_{0}^{T}-z_{k}^{S})`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{osc}(z_{0}^{T}-z_{0}^{S})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad+\operatorname{osc}(z_{k}^{S}-z_{0}^{S})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq B_{\mathrm{init}}+D_{k}.`$ |  |

Hence $`B_{\mathrm{init}}+D_{k}`$ is one admissible choice of
$`B_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}`$; the theorem only requires
Assumption [3](#Thmaopsdassumption3 "Assumption 3 (Bounded frozen-teacher logit contrast). ‣ A.4 Extension to a frozen privileged teacher ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Asynchronous On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
not this decomposition. Freezing the teacher does not itself bound
accumulated student drift or guarantee the same numerical constant as in
the evolving-teacher case. A bound on rollout age $`k-r`$ also does not
by itself bound drift from initialization.

#### Implications and scope.

The same interaction between prefix staleness and teacher entropy
therefore holds for a frozen teacher under the stated contrast bound. In
particular, $`\mu_{r}=\mu_{k}`$ implies $`\Delta_{k}^{\mathrm{fr}}=0`$,
even if the current student differs substantially from the teacher.
There is no teacher-version mismatch on a fixed prefix: cached and
recomputed frozen-teacher predictions coincide when the privileged
context and evaluation settings are unchanged. Low teacher entropy alone
cannot ensure a small distillation loss without a uniform bound on the
teacher–student logit contrast: for
$`q_{\epsilon}=(1-\epsilon,\epsilon)`$ and $`p=(1/2,1/2)`$,
$`H(q_{\epsilon})\to 0`$ but
$`\operatorname{KL}(q_{\epsilon}\|p)\to\log 2`$ as $`\epsilon\to 0`$;
the logit contrast then diverges.
````

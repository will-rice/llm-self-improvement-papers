# Training LLM Agents to Automate LLM Post-Training with RSI-Dojo

Shuzheng Gao¹   Zhaojian Yu²   Penghao Yin³   Yuling Shi⁴   Kai Cai²
Shilin He²   Michael R. Lyu¹ ¹Department of Computer Science and
Engineering, The Chinese University of Hong Kong ²ByteDance  ³Tsinghua
University  ⁴Shanghai Jiao Tong University

###### Abstract

Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) aims to enable AI systems to
autonomously build and improve AI systems themselves, including models,
harnesses, and underlying infrastructure. Within this broader agenda, a
central capability is enabling agents to train and improve base models.
However, existing studies on automated model training primarily focused
on workflow- or harness-level optimizations, rather than internalizing
the ability to conduct post-training into the model itself. Developing
this capability requires executable model training environments that can
produce high-quality trajectories demonstrating how to select data,
train models, diagnose failures, and iteratively improve results. In
this paper, we introduce RSI-Dojo, a collection of executable model
training environments designed to teach LLM agents to conduct
post-training. To construct RSI-Dojo, we develop RSI-Craft, an automatic
pipeline that converts human research artifacts into executable model
training environments. RSI-Craft automatically collects research papers
from top venues, instantiates datasets and metrics into a unified
interface, and reproduces evaluation dependencies. It then filters tasks
by data size, execution reliability, and discriminability, yielding
RSI-Dojo with 281 seed model training tasks. Building on RSI-Dojo, we
employ a trajectory collection method with an improved harness to
collect high-quality model-training trajectories from frontier models,
then retain examples through rubric-based and outcome-based filtering.
We fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on these trajectories. The
resulting RSI-Dojo-9B and RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B improve over their base
models by 8.74 and 16.87 PostTrainBench points, respectively.
RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B reaches 29.95 on PostTrainBench and 53.29 on
PostTrainBench-Lite, matching the performance of frontier models such as
GLM-5.2 and GPT 5.5.

## 1 Introduction

As LLM agents are increasingly deployed to tackle complex and
long-horizon tasks [Yang et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib26); [Dong et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib27); [Gao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib33), the use of AI
systems to build and improve AI systems has become central to the
prospect of recursive self-improvement (RSI) [Yang et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib8). Recent benchmarks have begun to make this ambition
measurable by evaluating agents’ ability to design and execute
reinforcement-learning pipelines [Chen et al. (2026)](#bib.bib16),
automate iterative data curation against training and evaluation
feedback [Kang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib21), and conduct the whole
post-training process [Rank et al. (2026)](#bib.bib1). To strengthen
these capabilities, recent work has explored improving agents’ discovery
and curation of training data [Du et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib5),
structuring their interactions with tools through explicit workflows and
rules [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib7), and adapting model-specific
harnesses from execution feedback [Zhang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib9).

Although these methods have achieved promising results, they mainly use
harness- and workflow-level optimization to teach agents how to design
data and training recipes, analyze results, and literately optimize the
performance. However, the capability to build and improve AI systems is
not internalized into the model itself. A central challenge is how to
construct suitable tasks and environments, and how to collect
high-quality training trajectories to teach the model how to train
models. Unlike SWE tasks which can leverage many real developer issues
with repository context and test suites [Jimenez et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib28); [Pan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib15), or MLE tasks which
can reuse Kaggle competitions with established datasets, evaluation
metrics, and competitive baselines [Chan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib20), how
to automatically construct training tasks and environments for LLM
post-training at scale remains unexplored.

To address this gap, we introduce RSI-Craft, an automatic pipeline for
constructing executable model training environments from human research
artifacts. RSI-Craft starts from evaluation platforms and top-venue
papers and uses paper selection, dataset instantiation, and evaluation
reproduction to recover tasks that can be executed in a unified
post-training workflow. It then normalizes dataset variants and filters
tasks by data size, execution reliability, and discriminability. Based
on the above process, it constructs RSI-Dojo, a suite of executable
environments for automated post-training with 281 seed tasks.

Building on RSI-Dojo, we collect model-training trajectories from
frontier models and use them as supervision for training automated
post-training agents. Since even frontier models still produce
trajectories with recurring bad patterns during model training, we
employ a trajectory collection method with an improved harness that adds
targeted rollout-time guidance while preserving the end-to-end training
loop. The collected trajectories then pass through a rubric-based
quality filter to remove data with bad patterns and an outcome filter
that checks whether the final submitted model yields a measurable task
improvement. We use the retained trajectories to fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B
and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and obtain RSI-Dojo-9B and RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B. The two
models score 16.46 and 29.95 on PostTrainBench, improving over their
base models by 8.74 and 16.87 points, respectively. RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B
reaches 29.95 on PostTrainBench and 53.29 on PostTrainBench-Lite,
matching the performance of frontier models such as GLM-5.2 and GPT 5.5
under the same evaluation setting.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

- •
  We introduce RSI-Craft, an automatic pipeline that converts recent
  dataset-oriented research papers into executable post-training
  environments and constructs RSI-Dojo.
- •
  Based on RSI-Dojo, we employ a trajectory collection method with an
  improved harness to collect high-quality trajectories from frontier
  models and retain those demonstrating strong process quality and
  measurable task improvement.
- •
  We fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on the retained
  trajectories and obtain RSI-Dojo-9B and RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B, which
  achieves 16.46 and 29.95 on PostTrainBench, respectively.

## 2 Related work

### 2.1 Recursive self-improvement and self-evolving agents

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) studies AI systems that participate in
improving systems [Ouyang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30); [Chen et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib31); [Gao et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib29), harnesses [Gao et
al. (2025b)](#bib.bib32); [Zhang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib9); [Wu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib19), or base models [Yang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib8);
[Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib7). Recent work has introduced a series of
benchmarks for RSI-related problems. For example, Agent² RL-Bench [Chen
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib16) evaluates whether agents can design,
implement, debug, and execute reinforcement-learning post-training
pipelines under a fixed workspace and budget. CurationBench [Kang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib21) studies automated data curation, including
discovering, filtering, and iterating on data resources. RSIBench-Data
[Meng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib17) further focuses on data-centric
research for RSI, where agents revise training-data strategies for a
fixed target model and learn from checkpoint feedback. Evo-Bench [Huang
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib18) evaluates intrinsic harness-evolving
capability across Search, Office, and General domains while controlling
for base model capabilities and task-specific overfitting. HarnessDev
[Wu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib19) further decomposes harness development
into Creation and Evolution, where it builds a runnable execution system
from a minimal seed and keeps improving it using downstream feedback.
Apart from those methods, another line of work improves the agent
harness for RSI. Self-Harness [Zhang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib9) treats
the harness as the object of improvement through weakness mining,
harness proposal, and regression-tested validation. Dream-RSI [Zheng et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib10) improves exploration by building replay
simulators from discovery histories and using them to refine exploration
policies before redeployment. Hyperagents [Zhang et al.
(2026b)](#bib.bib13) integrates task-solving and meta-level
self-modification into a single editable program, allowing the
improvement mechanism itself to evolve. Other self-improvement methods
use self-generated rationales or feedback to improve the performance
[Zelikman et al. (2022)](#bib.bib22); [Madaan et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib23); [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib24); [Yuan et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib25).

### 2.2 Automated post-training

PostTrainBench [Rank et al. (2026)](#bib.bib1) frames automated
post-training as an agent task in which a base model, target benchmark,
evaluation interface, and bounded compute budget are provided, and the
agent must raise downstream performance through data discovery, recipe
selection, training, and iteration. Recently, several works have
improved model performance on PostTrainBench by adding structure to the
agent’s post-training loop. DataMaster [Du et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib5)
focuses on data-side search: it organizes alternative data-engineering
attempts in a DataTree, shares discovered resources through a Data Pool,
and records reusable evidence in Global Memory. AutoTrainess [Yu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib7) improves the agent-computer interface by exposing
planning, data preparation, training, evaluation, and logging as
explicit workflows and execution constraints. ANDES [Zhao et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib6) treats data synthesis as a plug-in agent skill,
using a self-evolving World Tree and diagnostic reports to steer
closed-loop data generation. RSI-Master [Du et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib11)
structures autonomous model-improvement experiments through an
Experiment OS and reviewer-guided research orchestration, regularizing
individual actions while encouraging exploration across research
directions. Gödel Forest [Zhao et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib12) organizes
data-centric RSI as multiple co-evolving search trees, combining
persistent local exploration with shared procedural memory to balance
search depth and breadth. Harness design also interacts closely with
post-training [Kim et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14). These methods improve the
workflow and agent harness for automated post-training tasks, while
RSI-Dojo constructs a scalable source of executable post-training tasks
that enables collecting trajectories that can internalize the
post-training capability into the base model.

## 3 Methodology

To train an agent for automated model training, we first introduce
RSI-Craft, an automatic pipeline for constructing executable
post-training environments from seed tasks selected from recent research
papers and the InspectAI evaluation platform. RSI-Craft turns paper
artifacts and existing task specifications into model training tasks and
yields the RSI-Dojo, which contains 281 tasks from 10 domains. Based on
RSI-Dojo, we employ a trajectory collection method with an improved
harness to obtain high-quality agent trajectories and mitigate
undesirable trajectory patterns. The collected trajectories then undergo
quality filtering and are used to train the agent model, improving its
post-training capabilities.

Figure 2: Overview of the RSI-Craft pipelinedojo. It automatically
collects seed data from research papers and evaluation platforms,
instantiates environments and metrics into a unified interface, and

reliability and discriminability and obtains RSI-Dojo.

### 3.1 RSI-Dojo Construction

Seed data selection. RSI-Craft starts by selecting seed tasks from two
sources: recent research papers that may introduce datasets for
evaluating the post-training performance of base models, and tasks
available on the InspectAI evaluation platform. Specifically, it crawls
papers from NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, and COLM over the
last three years, and also imports candidate tasks from InspectAI. A
keyword scanner then searches paper titles and abstracts for terms that
indicate a dataset or benchmark. Each keyword-selected paper or imported
InspectAI task is screened by an agent to determine whether it provides
a real dataset or executable evaluation target, rather than only an
evaluation method, analysis protocol, or task formulation without
reusable data. Based on this procedure, RSI-Craft yields 547 candidate
seed tasks for detailed inspection.

Environment instantiation. RSI-Craft then builds an evaluation
environment for each selected task. Specifically, an agent locates the
corresponding dataset and verifies that the data can be accessed. It
also identifies the task definition, evaluation metric, and the code or
protocol required to run the evaluation. For each dataset, RSI-Craft
reproduces the evaluation environment by installing the required
libraries and validating that the evaluation script or protocol can run
successfully. Tasks that pass this execution check are then normalized
into a unified evaluation interface, allowing the post-training agent to
invoke different tasks through a consistent workflow. If a dataset
contains multiple subsets with different metrics, these subsets are
split into separate tasks. Tasks with fewer than 50 examples in total
are discarded, while tasks with more than 300 examples are randomly
downsampled to 300 examples to keep evaluation time tractable. This step
produces 315 runnable initial task environments.

Reliability and discriminability filtering. The instantiated
environments are then filtered for evaluation reliability and
discriminability. The reliability filter removes tasks whose evaluation
requires specialized infrastructure (i.e., kernel-relevant benchmarks)
or yields unstable evaluation outcomes. This includes LLM-as-judge tasks
whose repeated judgments vary substantially, as well as tasks whose
metrics do not employ the model’s generated output text. For the
remaining tasks, the discriminability filter removes datasets that fail
to separate frontier models from smaller models. Specifically, it
evaluates the performance of Qwen3-4B-Base and GPT-5-mini on each task.
A task is kept only when the score gap between the two models is at
least 10 percentage points. Tasks where both models score above 80% or
both score below 20% are also removed. Such tasks provide little usable
signal for post-training.

Dataset statistics. The final RSI-Dojo collection comprises 281 training
environments spanning 10 domains, including General QA, Science &

combines broad domains that provide abundant post-training signal with
less frequent domains that test whether agents can transfer their data
selection, training-recipe construction, and evaluation strategies
across tasks.

### 3.2 Trajectory Collection with Improved Harness

Based on RSI-Dojo, we collect model-training trajectories from frontier
models. However, direct rollouts from frontier models still exhibit
limited effectiveness on automated post-training tasks. Even frontier
models often produce undesirable patterns that degrade the quality of
the collected training data. For example, an agent may perform multiple
LoRA training runs on very small datasets while leaving the EOS
configuration unchanged, causing an instruction-tuned model converted
from a base model to fail to terminate generations properly. Some
trajectories spend many rounds trying different recipes without
analyzing concrete failure cases; as a result, the agent repeatedly
explores alternative training settings without a clear direction for
improvement. Other trajectories fail to submit the final model in the
required format. To reduce these bad patterns, we employ a trajectory
collection method with an improved harness. The base harness follows
AutoTrainess [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib7), which provides task-specific
guidance through AGENTS.md, skills, and hooks during the rollout
process. We further augment this harness with additional guidance
targeting the identified failure patterns. These instructions guide the
agent to diagnose current model failures, choose suitable data and
training recipes based on observed cases, check configuration details
such as special tokens, and submit the final trained model in the
expected format.

### 3.3 Trajectory Filtering and Model Training

After data collection, we then filter low-quality trajectories for model
training. We remove trajectories that contain the bad patterns described
above, such as premature LoRA training with incorrect special-token
handling, repeated recipe changes without case diagnosis, and failure to
submit the final model in the required format. We also remove
trajectories detected as cheating. Finally, we remove trajectories whose
final model does not achieve a measurable improvement on the target
task. The remaining trajectories are then used to train the agent model.

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Benchmarks and Metrics

We evaluate on PostTrainBench v1.1 [Rank et al. (2026)](#bib.bib1) and
PostTrainBench-Lite. PostTrainBench evaluates automated post-training
across four base models and seven target benchmarks. For each task, an
agent receives a pretrained base model, an evaluation interface, one
GPU, and a 10-hour budget, and must improve the target-benchmark
performance through data construction, training, and iterative
evaluation. PostTrainBench-Lite follows the evaluation setting
introduced in the GPT-5.6 System Card [OpenAI (2026)](#bib.bib2). It
covers 12 combinations formed by three base models (Qwen3-4B-Base,
Qwen3-1.7B-Base, and SmolLM3-3B-Base) and four target benchmarks (AIME
2025, BFCL, GSM8K, and HumanEval), while reducing the time budget to
five hours. We use this setting for lower-cost comparisons and ablation
studies.

We adopt the official evaluation metrics. For each agent, PostTrainBench
evaluates a Cartesian product of base model $`m`$ and target benchmark
$`b`$. Let $`s_{m,b}`$ denote the final benchmark score of the model
produced by the agent on this point and $`s^{\mathrm{base}}_{m,b}`$ the
corresponding pretrained base-model score. Following the official
aggregation procedure, we use
$`\tilde{s}_{m,b}=\max(s_{m,b},s^{\mathrm{base}}_{m,b})`$, so a
submitted model that underperforms the base model receives the base
score. The official score first applies benchmark-level weights and then
averages across base models:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathrm{Score}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}w_{b}\tilde{s}_{m,b}.
``` |  |

PostTrainBench-Lite instead normalizes each submitted model against its
base and instruction-tuned reference models. For a Lite task with base
model $`m`$ and target benchmark $`b`$, its reward is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{m,b}=\operatorname{clamp}\left(\frac{s^{\mathrm{trial}}_{m,b}-s^{\mathrm{base}}_{m,b}}{s^{\mathrm{instruct}}_{m,b}-s^{\mathrm{base}}_{m,b}},0,1\right).
``` |  |

Here, $`s^{\mathrm{trial}}_{m,b}`$ is the submitted model’s score,
$`s^{\mathrm{base}}_{m,b}`$ is the pretrained base model’s score, and
$`s^{\mathrm{instruct}}_{m,b}`$ is the corresponding instruction-tuned
reference score. Matching the base model gives a score of 0, while
matching or exceeding the instruction-tuned model gives a score of 1.
Following the official protocol, trials judged to use invalid
strategies, such as training on held-out evaluation data, receive a
score of 0 [OpenAI (2026)](#bib.bib2).

### 4.2 Evaluation Models and Environments

We evaluate Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B before and after fine-tuning
on RSI-Dojo trajectories, and compare them with a broader set of
frontier models. We evaluate GPT-5.5, GPT-5.4, GLM-5.2, and
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ourselves under the same evaluation setting used for
our trained models. We set the reasoning effort to medium for GPT-5.4
and GPT-5.5, and to max for GLM-5.2 and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. Results for
the remaining reference models are taken from the official
PostTrainBench leaderboard or their own technical reports.

Our evaluation environment follows the PostTrainBench protocol with
several infrastructure-specific adaptations. Due to hardware
availability, we conducted the experiments on a less powerful GPU than
the one required by the official benchmark. All agents use Codex CLI
v0.142.4 with the standard AutoTrainess reprompting strategy and skills
[Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib7). During evaluation, we do not include the
additional guidance introduced for trajectory collection. Codex exposes
web search through the custom tool web_search. This tool is unavailable
to our self-hosted models, although they retain general network access.
To provide sufficient training resources, we pre-download the datasets
listed in the official PostTrainBench resource collection and the
Hugging Face resources used by other models in traces published on the
PostTrainBench website. This evaluation setup also helps prevent invalid
strategies in PostTrainBench.

### 4.3 Experiment Details

During trajectory collection, we limit each rollout to five hours to
control collection cost. After filtering, we retain 324 high-quality CLI
sessions, which are split into 1,240 training trajectories. We disable
the web_search tool during collection to match the tool configuration
used when evaluating our trained models. For each training task, we also
pre-download suitable training resources to the local environment. After
each rollout, we reconstruct training examples from the recorded Codex
session by splitting at context compaction boundaries and excluding the
reasoning summaries. We apply rule-based conversions to custom tools
such as exec and wait, mapping them to standard tool-call
representations when possible and masking calls that cannot be
converted. For apply_patch, we use its open-source implementation to
enable trained models to invoke this tool during inference. We train for
four epochs with a learning rate that starts at $`1\times 10^{-5}`$ and
decreases to a minimum of $`2\times 10^{-6}`$.

#### Fine-tuning substantially improves automated post-training.

Fine-tuning on RSI-Dojo trajectories consistently improves both model
scales across the full and Lite settings. On PostTrainBench, RSI-Dojo-9B
increases from 7.72 to 16.46, obtaining an 8.74-point gain, while
RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B improves from 13.08 to 29.95, for a 16.87-point gain.
Both fine-tuned models outperform their corresponding untrained models
across all target base-model families. The improvements are even more
significant on PostTrainBench-Lite: RSI-Dojo-9B rises from 0.56 to
15.71, while RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B increases from 5.80 to 53.29. Overall,
these results demonstrate that training on the collected trajectories
from RSI-Dojo substantially enhances the models’ ability to construct
effective post-training solutions across diverse base models and
evaluation budgets.

#### RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B is competitive with frontier models on PostTrainBench.

RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B achieves 29.95 on PostTrainBench, slightly exceeding
GLM-5.2 at 29.52, GPT-5.5 at 27.80, and GPT-5.4 at 26.21. Although its
distillation source models are not as strong as GLM-5.2, the final
trained model achieves competitive performance with GLM-5.2 on this
benchmark. On PostTrainBench-Lite, RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B reaches 53.29,
comparable to GPT-5.5 at 52.96 and below GLM-5.2 and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash
at 56.37 and 62.92, respectively. Compared to the official leaderboard
results, its performance also surpasses GPT-5.5 and Claude Opus 4.7.
These comparisons show that the trajectories collected from RSI-Dojo
substantially narrow the gap between an open-weight 30B-level model and
frontier 700B+ agents.

### 5.2 Ablation Study

#### Effect of additional guidance.

We randomly sample 100 trajectories collected with the base harness and
100 trajectories collected after adding our targeted guidance, then
analyze their quality. The additional guidance increases the trajectory
retention rate from 42% to 56%. As shown in
Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Effect of additional guidance. ‣ 5.2 Ablation Study ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ Training LLM Agents to Automate LLM Post-Training with RSI-Dojo"),
it also makes the rollout process more efficient: the average number of
iterations decreases from 11.93 to 9.25, while the average number of
iterations that yield an actual improvement increases from 3.05 to 3.32.
The additional guidance further reduces all four undesirable behaviors
considered in our analysis. Cases where the trained model fails to
terminate correctly because of improper EOS handling decrease from 23 to
14, training data dominated by synthetic data decrease from 22 to 18,
trajectories using too little training data decrease from 22 to 3, and
failures to provide a final model decrease from 4 to 0. These results
show that guidance targeting recurrent failure patterns improves
trajectory quality beyond the base harness.

### 5.3 Agent Behavior Analysis

#### Comparison across different models.

By inspecting the training process of each model, we find that different
models exhibit distinct post-training strategies. For example, GPT-5.4
favors many small step experiments, including LoRA on a few hundred
examples and model merging. This produces long trajectories with few
effective iterations, as shown in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B is competitive with frontier models on PostTrainBench. ‣ 5.1 Main Results ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ Training LLM Agents to Automate LLM Post-Training with RSI-Dojo").
It also rarely uses full-parameter training and often leaves the EOS
configuration unchanged, leading to overly long responses in trained
model. RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B, GLM-5.2, and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash complete the
28 settings with substantially fewer iterations. The usage of
distillation during training also varies in different models: GPT-5.4
and GPT-5.5 never use a larger teacher, compared with 2 runs for GLM-5.2
and 10 for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. Six of the DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash runs
occur on HealthBench or Arena-Hard, with the others on GPQA Main,
HumanEval, or AIME 2025. The official Claude Fable 5 traces also use
distillation but only on HealthBench and Arena-Hard. These benchmarks
are particularly amenable to teacher supervision because their
evaluation targets are more complex. Although PosttrainBench does not
prohibit using another local model for distillation [PostTrainBench Team
(2026a)](#bib.bib3); [PostTrainBench Team (2026b)](#bib.bib4), this
practice may allow a model to boost its benchmark score through a
relatively simple strategy.

#### Comparison across different target benchmarks.

Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Comparison across different models. ‣ 5.3 Agent Behavior Analysis ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ Training LLM Agents to Automate LLM Post-Training with RSI-Dojo")
shows that RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B ranks first on GPQA Main and second on GSM8K
and HealthBench. It is competitive with GPT-5.4, GPT-5.5, and GLM-5.2 on
BFCL and HumanEval, but weaker on AIME 2025 and Arena-Hard. The RSI-Dojo
training set includes relatively large proportions of math, general-QA,
and medical tasks, which helps its strong performance on GSM8K, GPQA
Main, and HealthBench. Although RSI-Dojo also contains many difficult
mathematical reasoning tasks, their rollouts rarely produce measurable
improvements and are therefore filtered out, leaving limited supervision
for AIME 2025. Arena-Hard exposes a different gap: substantial gains
often rely on teacher distillation, but such trajectories are absent
from our training data. This prevents RSI-Dojo from learning the
strategy that produces large improvements on this benchmark.

#### Optimization patterns learned by RSI-Dojo.

In trajectories with successful improvement, early gains typically come
from correcting the training and output format. Full-parameter SFT and
proper EOS token configuration prevent the termination failures often
observed with LoRA or unchanged EOS settings. The dataset selection
strategy is also crucial to the model’s results. In the GSM8K example in
Figure [6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Comparison across different models. ‣ 5.3 Agent Behavior Analysis ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ Training LLM Agents to Automate LLM Post-Training with RSI-Dojo"),
RSI-Dojo selects the more suitable MetaMathQA and OpenMathInstruct-2
data, then improves further through data filtering and continued
training at a lower learning rate. In later iterations, the agent often
analyzes failed cases, refines the training subset, and continue
training model with annealing. The evaluation target should evolve with
the training stage. Evaluating on a small subset is useful in early
stage. However, once subset performance saturates, continuing to
optimize for it may favor local gains over benchmark-wide improvements.
Therefore, high-performing trajectories often return to the complete
evaluation set in later stages to align with the final objective.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce RSI-Craft, an automatic pipeline for
converting research artifacts into executable model-training
environments, and use it to construct RSI-Dojo with 281 post-training
tasks. Using RSI-Dojo, we employ a trajectory collection method with an
improved harness to collect high-quality trajectories, then apply
quality filtering before training automated post-training behavior into
Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. The resulting models improve over their
base models by 8.74 and 16.87 points on PostTrainBench, respectively,
with RSI-Dojo-35B-A3B reaching 29.95 on PostTrainBench and 53.29 on
PostTrainBench-Lite. These results demonstrate that training on
high-quality post-training trajectories can strengthen a model’s ability
to autonomously construct data, optimize training recipes, diagnose
issues, and iteratively improve the trained models.

## 7 Limitations and Future Work

Our current environments primarily cover PostTrainBench-style tasks.
They do not yet represent the broader range of agentic post-training
scenarios in which models must conduct longer-horizon research and adapt
their data strategies to richer environment feedback. Future work will
extend the task distribution to more realistic agentic settings such as
RSIBench-Data [Meng et al. (2026)](#bib.bib17).

Our current experiments also stop short of recursive self-improvement of
the trained model itself. We internalize model-training capabilities
with RSI-Dojo and evaluate whether it can improve smaller target models,
but we do not close the loop by using our trained models to train an
improved successor of themselves. After further strengthening its model
training capabilities, we plan to explore and validate this stronger
form of recursive self-improvement.

The current evaluation agent harness also limits the tools available
during trajectory collection and evaluation. In Codex, web_search is
exposed as a custom tool and cannot be used by our self-hosted models,
so we disable it throughout data collection to keep the tool
configuration consistent. In the future work, we will integrate it back
through an MCP server and enable the models to develop stronger data
construction capabilities for harder post-training tasks.
````

# Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction

###### Abstract

Visual token reduction is an effective way to accelerate multimodal
large language models (MLLMs), but performance deteriorates rapidly
under extremely low token budgets. Existing work has explored both
visual-token selection and training-based adaptation to reduced visual
inputs. We take a step further by asking how a heavily compressed MLLM
should learn from the states induced by its own generations. This
setting naturally calls for on-policy self-distillation: a heavily
compressed model is supervised on the states induced by its own
generations, while its full-token counterpart serves as an
information-rich teacher. Based on this insight, we propose LT-OPD, a
training framework for extreme visual-token reduction. The student rolls
out responses with only a small fraction of visual tokens, and a frozen
full-token copy of the same MLLM provides distributional supervision
along these student-generated trajectories. To stabilize on-policy
learning when visual evidence is severely limited, we further introduce
a budget-level curriculum that progressively decreases the token budget
during training. Across nine benchmarks on Qwen3.5-4B, LT-OPD raises
average retained performance under 5% visual-token retention from 68.6%
to 82.3%, outperforming training-free, training-based, and
reinforcement-learning baselines at the same budget. The gains transfer
consistently to Qwen3.5-9B, GLM-4.6V-9B, and LLaVA-OV-1.5-4B. LT-OPD
also reduces KV-cache usage by 85.2% and prefill FLOPs by 85.4% without
additional inference overhead, demonstrating that on-policy learning can
substantially recover capabilities lost to extreme visual-token
reduction. Code is at
[https://github.com/Yrxxxxxxxx1007/LT-OPD](https://github.com/Yrxxxxxxxx1007/LT-OPD).

## 1 Introduction

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) ([Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib49); [Hurst et al., 2024](#bib.bib41); [Bai et al.,
2025](#bib.bib42); [Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib43); [Wang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib44); [An et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Hong et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib50)) have attracted
research interests for years. While exploring the frontiers of MLLMs in
many aspects like multimodal understanding and planning, visual question
answering and visual agentic planning, the efficiency of such models
also matters. When deployed on edge devices or used in
resource-constrained scenarios, deploying such MLLMs with billions of
parameters remains challenging.

To address this challenge, substantial efforts have been made. Visual
token reduction ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib3); [Alvar et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4); [Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib7); [Wen et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib5); [Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib17)), which reduces the
visual tokens from the visual encoder, acts as a training-free and
usually plug-and-play strategy in increasing MLLMs’ efficiency. However,
another problem occurs on applying these training-free techniques. As
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
left suggests, an extremely low visual token budget (ratio of preserved
tokens) may be set, like 5%, due to serious resource limits. Under this
scenario, focusing on which visual tokens should be retained cannot
achieve acceptable performances of MLLMs.

![Refer to caption](2609.32353v1/teaser_2.png)

![Refer to caption](2609.32353v1/teaser_3.png)

We argue that, to achieve better performances under extremely low
budgets, exploring how to adapt a model to operate effectively with
severely compressed visual inputs also matters. And this needs special
adaptive training. Several previous works ([Hu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib45); [Zhang et al., 2025c](#bib.bib1); [Wen et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib8)) support this idea and make efforts on developing
training techniques like finetuning with pre-fusion and consistency
training. While obvious improvements are seen, a remaining limitation
prevents them from achieving further gains. We observe that, low token
budgets change not only the amount of visual evidence available, but
also the generation trajectories encountered at inference time. As
illustrated in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
middle, with severely limited visual inputs, the predictive distribution
of the compressed model can deviate substantially from that of its
full-token counterpart. Training directly on fixed response prefixes
does not account for the states from the compressed model’s own
predictions. This mismatch becomes particularly relevant once generated
tokens are fed back as subsequent context. This observation naturally
motivates us to adapt the compressed model on the trajectories it
actually generates, rather than only learning from fixed training
prefixes.

Based on this insight, we introduce Low-Token On-Policy Distillation
(LT-OPD), which contains two complementary components. First, we perform
a specific on-policy self-distillation algorithm: the low-token student
rolls out its own responses. Then a frozen full-token counterpart
provides distributional supervision along the prefixes generated by
students. In this way, the student learns to recover the behavior of the
information-rich teacher exactly at the states it is likely to encounter
during inference, as
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
middle shows. Second, directly conducting on-policy learning under an
extremely low token budget can be difficult, since the heavily
compressed student may struggle to generate useful trajectories at the
beginning of training. We therefore introduce a budget-level curriculum.
It starts training with a relatively larger budget and progressively
reduces it to the target budget. This gradually shifts the student from
an easier visual condition to the desired highly compressed, harder
condition. This leads to more stable and effective adaptation.

Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness and generality of
LT-OPD. Across nine benchmarks, LT-OPD retains 82.3% of the full
performance on Qwen3.5-4B while preserving only 5% of visual tokens,
substantially improving over the corresponding untrained compressed
model (68.6%) and the strongest baseline (77.6%) under the same budget.
The improvement consistently transfers across model scales and
architectures. Importantly, as
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
right depicts, these gains come without decreasing inference efficiency:
the same 0.146$`\times`$ FLOPs ratio as HiPrune ([Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib6)) while improving retained performance by 21.9
percentage points, and 8.6 points higher with 27.0% fewer FLOPs than
EPIC ([Wen et al., 2025b](#bib.bib8)). In sum, our contributions can be
summarized as:

$`\bullet`$ We revisit extreme visual-token reduction from a training
perspective. We highlight that severe visual compression not only
removes visual evidence, but also changes the generation states
encountered by the compressed model, motivating adaptation directly on
its own generated trajectories.

$`\bullet`$ We propose LT-OPD, an on-policy self-distillation framework
under this scenario. LT-OPD lets the compressed student learn from a
full-token self-teacher on states induced by student, and further
introduces a budget-level curriculum that progressively reduces the
budget to stabilize learning.

$`\bullet`$ We conduct extensive evaluations across nine benchmarks,
multiple model scales, and three MLLM families. With only 5% visual
tokens, LT-OPD outperforms a wide range of baselines.

## 2 Related Works

Token Reduction for Efficient MLLMs.

| Method      | Training Strategy                                                                                    | Prefix / State Source | On-policy |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | --------- |
| MQT-LLaVA   | Randomly samples the number of query tokens and optimizes the language-modeling objective            | Fixed training        | No        |
| LLaVA-Mini  | Trains compression and modality pre-fusion modules through end-to-end instruction tuning             | Fixed training        | No        |
| EPIC        | Progressive token/layer consistency distillation with SFT and KL supervision                         | Fixed training        | No        |
| LearnPruner | Freezes the base VLM and trains the pruning module with next-token prediction and pruning losses     | Fixed training        | No        |
| LT-OPD      | Rolls out a low-token student and distills a frozen full-token self-teacher with a budget curriculum | Student-generated     | Yes       |

Table 1: Comparison of representative training-based visual-token
reduction methods. “Fixed prefix” means that generation states are
determined by static training responses rather than rollouts of the
current compressed model.

Token reduction is a widely used strategy in enhancing MLLM efficiency.
It reduces redundant visual tokens, and therefore mainly reduces prefill
time of MLLM inference. Two categories of token reduction methods are
developed: training-free methods and training-based ones. For
training-free methods, FastV ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)) first
utilizes attention scores to determine redundant tokens. Methods like
DivPrune ([Alvar et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)), DART ([Wen et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib5)), CDPruner ([Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib7)),
HiPrune ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)) and G²TR [Li et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib17) further integrate diversity, duplication and so on
to judge redundant tokens during reduction. Besides token selection,
training-based methods concentrate on teaching the MLLMs with fewer
visual tokens how to generate responses similar to those with full
visual information. MQT-LlaVA ([Hu et al., 2024](#bib.bib45)),
LlaVA-Mini ([Zhang et al., 2025c](#bib.bib1)), EPIC ([Wen et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib8)) are representative methods. LearnPruner ([Takezoe et
al., 2026](#bib.bib2)) learns a task-aware pruning module with
next-token prediction supervision. These methods, while achieving
notable success, mainly focus on choosing which visual tokens, or
training with fixed prefix, as
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2 Related Works ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
depicts. This may stop them from further improvement under our scenario.

On-policy Distillation. On-policy distillation (OPD) lets the student
model optimize on its self-generated trajectories, with supervision from
teacher model by token-level KL divergence or other guidances([Agarwal
et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib10); [Hou et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11); [Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib12); [Xu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13)). By this method, students can learn better from
training distribution than off-policy distillation ([Chu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib22)). Without online exploration, inference-time prefixes
may differ from those seen during training, leading to accumulated
errors. Recent works such as OPSD ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib23))
utilizes on-policy distillation on LLMs, with using ground-truth as
rewards for optimization. Vision-OPD ([Yuan et al., 2026](#bib.bib24))
further provides self-teacher with fine-grained visual information to
facilitate with on-policy distillation on MLLMs. In sum, previous OPD
methods aim at enhancing the policy’s performance through stronger
teachers or teacher with more information. Based on this, we explore
whether OPD can help train a well-performing student with fewer visual
tokens preserved.

## 3 Methodology

LT-OPD is built on a simple framing: a full-token model has access to
richer visual evidence for correcting its compressed counterpart, while
the compressed model defines the generation states that will actually be
encountered at inference time. We therefore let a low-token student
generate its own responses and query a frozen full-token copy of the
same MLLM for dense distributional supervision along these
student-induced trajectories.
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Problem formulation ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
gives an overview.

### 3.1 Problem formulation

As
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Problem formulation ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
depicts, given an image-question pair $`x=(I,q)`$, let
$`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$ denote the pretrained MLLM and $`N`$ be the number
of visual tokens primarily produced by the visual encoder for $`I`$. We
instantiate a frozen full-token teacher $`\pi_{T}`$ from $`\theta_{0}`$
and a student $`\pi_{S}`$ with trainable parameters $`\theta`$,
initialized from the same parameters. For a keep ratio $`r`$, a
visual-token reduction operator $`\mathcal{R}_{r}`$ retains

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       N_{stu}=\operatorname{round}(r*N)
       ```                                |     | (1) |

visual tokens for the student, whereas the teacher receives all $`N`$
tokens. Our optimization goal is to adapt the visual tokens of
$`\pi_{S}`$ to a target extreme budget $`r=\beta`$ (e.g., $`5\%`$) while
recovering as much of the full-token behavior as possible.

![Refer to caption](2609.32353v1/main.png)

Figure 2: Framework of LT-OPD, consisting of two parts. First, an
on-policy self-distillation algorithm is proposed to train a low-token
student, with a full-token frozen self-teacher. “dist.” means
distribution. Second, we design a budget-level curriculum learning
process for stable training.

### 3.2 Our Method: On-policy Self-Distillation across Budgets

Current distillation methods like ([Wen et al., 2025b](#bib.bib8))
evaluates the teacher and student on response prefixes provided by a
fixed training distribution. Under extreme visual compression, however,
the student may quickly deviate from these prefixes, and subsequent
predictions are conditioned on states induced by its own previous
outputs. LT-OPD instead performs distillation directly on this
student-induced state distribution.

Let $`S`$ be the marker for the “student” and $`T`$ for the “teacher”.
At training update $`s`$, the low-token student, with parameters
$`\theta_{s}`$ at update $`s`$, first samples a response from its
current policy,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
{y}^{S}=({y}^{S}_{1},\ldots,y^{S}_{T})\sim\pi_{\theta_{s}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{R}_{r}(I),q).
``` |  | (2) |

The sampled sequence is treated as a stop-gradient trajectory, i.e., we
do not differentiate through the discrete sampling process. At each
decoding step $`t`$, the teacher and student are evaluated on the same
student-generated prefix $`{y}^{S}_{<t}`$, but with different amounts of
visual evidence:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p^{S}_{t,\theta}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta_{s}}\bigl(\cdot\mid\mathcal{R}_{r}(I),q,{y}^{S}_{<t}\bigr),`$ |  | (3) |
|  | $`\displaystyle p^{T}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{T}\bigl(\cdot\mid I,q,{y}^{S}_{<t}\bigr).`$ |  | (4) |

We minimize the discrepancy between these two next-token distributions
$`\pi_{\theta_{s}}`$ and $`\pi_{T}`$. Following prior works ([Hou et
al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Yuan et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)), we use the
Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) ([Menéndez et al., 1997](#bib.bib46)):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle m_{t,\theta}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(p^{T}_{t}+p^{S}_{t,\theta}),`$ |  | (5) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\ell_{t}^{\mathrm{JSD}}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{T}_{t}\|m_{t,\theta})+\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{S}_{t,\theta}\|m_{t,\theta}).`$ |  | (6) |

Accordingly, at update $`s`$ we optimize the following on-policy
distillation surrogate:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{OPD}}^{(s)}(\theta;r)=\mathbb{E}_{(I,q)\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{{y}^{S}\sim\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\pi_{\theta_{s}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{R}_{r}(I),q)\right]}\left[\frac{1}{|y^{S}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y^{S}|}\ell_{t}^{\mathrm{JSD}}(\theta)\right],
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]`$ indicates that the rollout
distribution is treated as fixed during differentiation. Gradients are
taken with respect to the student distributions and evaluated at
$`\theta=\theta_{s}`$; no score-function gradient is propagated through
the sampled tokens. The teacher remains frozen throughout training.

Importantly, the full-token teacher does not generate a separate
trajectory. Instead, it provides distributional supervision on prefixes
generated by the current compressed student. Thus, “on-policy” refers to
the source of the training states: supervision is performed on states
induced by the current student policy, rather than on fixed response
prefixes. After training, only the compressed student is retained for
inference. For implementation efficiency, we use the top-K vocabulary
support described in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation and Training Details ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction");
objective ablations are provided in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Ablation on Training Objectives ‣ Appendix F Explanations of Different training strategies ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction").

### 3.3 Budget-level Curriculum Learning

On-policy supervision is useful only at states that the student can
reach. At the beginning of training, directly applying an extreme token
budget can produce highly degraded rollouts, making the visited states
far from the full-token policy. In other words, the lower the token
budget is, more difficult the training task becomes. Motivated by
previous work ([Bengio et al., 2009](#bib.bib37)), we introduce a
budget-level curriculum learning strategy, to progressively increase the
task difficulty during training. Specifically, let $`s_{tol}`$ be the
total training steps and $`s`$ still be the current update, we define a
three-stage curriculum process divided by $`s_{1}`$ and $`s_{2}`$, as
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.3 Budget-level Curriculum Learning ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
shows ($`s_{1}`$=14, $`s_{2}`$=100 empirically),

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r(s)=\begin{cases}(2\alpha+1)\beta,&0\leq s<s_{1},\\[5.69054pt]
\beta+\alpha\beta\left[1+\cos\left(\pi\frac{s-s_{1}}{s_{2}-s_{1}}\right)\right],&s_{1}\leq s<s_{2},\\[5.69054pt]
\beta,&s_{2}\leq s\leq s_{tol},\end{cases}
``` |  | (8) |

where $`r(s)`$ is the dynamic budget changed together with $`s`$ and
$`\alpha`$ is a hyper-parameter

Figure 3: Visual token keep ratio changes.

giving the starting point of the learning. Here $`\alpha`$ is set to 2
empirically. $`\beta`$ is the final keep ratio. In each training step,
the number $`N_{stu}`$ of visual tokens student policy keep is
$`\mathrm{Round}(r(s)*N)`$.

The first stage provides the student with more visual evidence at a
higher budget to establish useful on-policy trajectories. The second
stage progressively and smoothly shifts the visited state distribution
towards that induced by the target compression level, while the final
stage adapts the student directly at the target budget $`\beta`$. The
complete LT-OPD objective at update $`s`$ is therefore simply:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LT\text{-}OPD}}(\theta;s)=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{OPD}}^{(s)}(\theta;r(s)).
``` |  | (9) |

### 3.4 Training Data Construction

We want the student model can see data from multiple sources, and then
the supervision on student-generated trajectories can benefit from data
diversity, and improve generalizability of the trained compressed model.
Therefore, we specifically construct a training set, LT-14K, from
multiple data sources. The training set contains 14,000 single-image
questions from sources like LlaVA-Instruct-665K ([Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib49)), OneThinker ([Feng et al., 2026](#bib.bib47)),
PixMo ([Deitke et al., 2025](#bib.bib48)) and Vision-OPD ([Yuan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib24)) training data. Detailed distribution of the training
set is in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Training Data Details ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction").

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Settings.

#### Model and baselines.

We apply our methods on an advanced MLLM, Qwen-3.5-4B. We apply two
kinds of baselines. For training-free baselines, we choose previous SOTA
(state-of-the-art) methods like FastV ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)),
DivPrune ([Alvar et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)), DART ([Wen et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib5)), CDPruner ([Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib7)),
HiPrune ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)) and ZOO-Prune ([Kim et al.,
2026](#bib.bib36)), and set 20%, 15%, 10% and 5% keeping budget. For
training-based ones, we choose vanilla finetuning (same token reduction
method as LT-OPD), LlaVA-Mini [Zhang et al. (2025c)](#bib.bib1),
EPIC [Wen et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib8) and LearnPruner ([Takezoe et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)), and set the keeping budget of them all to 5%. Here
“LlaVA-Mini” refers to the all fusion mechanism and training methods in
their paper, which we reproduce on Qwen3.5-4B ([Qwen Team,
2026](#bib.bib21)). For fair comparison, we all keep 5% budget for them.
We also apply another two MLLMs, GLM-4.6V-9B ([Hong et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)) and LlaVA-OneVision (OV)-1.5-4B ([An et al.,
2025](#bib.bib19)) for cross-model evaluation.

Basic settings. Before trainining, we choose CDPruner ([Zhang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib7)), a token pruning method, based on conditional
diversity, to reduce visual tokens of the model. It is easy for
implementation. We train our model and training-based comparing methods
on 8$`\times`$NVIDIA A100 80G GPUs. Evaluations are done on one same
GPU. Detailed settings can be found in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation and Training Details ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction"), [C](#A3 "Appendix C Training Prompts ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction").

Evaluation metrics. We follow the original metrics for judgment of the
chosen benchmarks. Notably, we also calculate the average retain ratio
compared to upper bound, to intuitively quantify how much of the
full-token model’s capability is preserved under token reduction:
$`\text{average \ retain \ ratio}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\frac{score_{i}}{score_{i}^{\mathrm{full}}}\times 100\%,`$
where $`M`$ benchmarks are chosen and $`score_{i}`$,
$`score_{i}^{\mathrm{full}}`$ mean the scores of compressed and
full-token model.

### 4.2 Main results

Effectiveness of LT-OPD.
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Model and baselines. ‣ 4.1 Experimental Settings. ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
compares LT-OPD with both training-free and training-based visual token
reduction methods on Qwen3.5-4B. Under the extreme 5% visual-token
budget, LT-OPD achieves an average retain ratio of 82.3%, outperforming
the strongest training-free baseline, DivPrune, by 4.7 percentage points
(77.6%), and training-based baseline, EPIC by 8.6 points (73.7%). The
improvement is broad rather than benchmark-specific: LT-OPD achieves the
best result among prior methods keeping 5% visual tokens on 8 out of 9
benchmarks, including all categories. The gains are particularly clear
on V^(∗)Bench (74.9 vs. 70.7), MME (2,077.0 vs. 1,915.4), and OCRBench
(535 vs. 477), where the latter numbers denote the best competing
results under the same budget. Additionally, the chosen visual token
reduction strategy for LT-OPD, CDPruner, achieves an average retain
ratio of only 68.6% under 5% budget, much lower than LT-OPD. This
suggests that LT-OPD does not rely on a “best” token reduction method,
but proper on-policy learning actually works.

More notably, with only 5% visual tokens, LT-OPD still outperforms all
training-free methods retaining 10% tokens in average retain ratio
(82.3% vs. at most 80.2%), and nearly matches the best result obtained
with a 15% or even 20% budget. These results demonstrate that with
effective training, our method can recover substantially more model
capability even under extreme low token budget than relying on token
selection alone or applying simple training.

Ablation Studies. We ablate the proposed OPD training and curriculum
learning strategy, reported in the last 4 lines of
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Model and baselines. ‣ 4.1 Experimental Settings. ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction").
It is obvious that both components can improve training performance, as
curriculum learning increases average retain ratio of simply SFT by 2.7
percentage points, and OPD-only training reaches 81.5% average retain
ratio. Based on this, full-version LT-OPD achieves the best performance
(82.3%) and clearly improves model performance across all benchmarks.
More notably, training with curriculum and JSD with fixed prefix
(knowledge distillation) reaches a score of 77.6, still lower than our
results, suggesting that on-policy learning really benefits LT-OPD.

We also test different training objectives, with explanations and
results displayed in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Ablation on Training Objectives ‣ Appendix F Explanations of Different training strategies ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction").
While all training objectives show promising performance, our objective,
JSD, is better on perception-heavy benchmarks. Forward/Reverse KL,
however, achieve better scores on OCR benchmarks. The whole ablation
study suggests the effectiveness and stability of our full design.

### 4.5 Efficiency

Inference efficiency.
Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.5 Efficiency ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")

Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Efficiency and Training Cost ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")).
By retaining only 5% visual tokens, LT-OPD reduces the KV cache by
85.2%, and both overall and prefill FLOPs by 85.4%, while introducing no
additional inference overhead. It achieves the same computational cost
as HiPrune and requires substantially fewer FLOPs than EPIC (0.146 vs.
0.200), demonstrating that the performance gains of LT-OPD do not
compromise the efficiency of the compressed model. Additionally, the
end-to-end latency improves moderately from 1331.5 to 1293.7 ms
($`1.029\times`$), consistent with prior observations ([Wen et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib8); [Zamini and Shukla, 2026](#bib.bib18)) that
aggressive visual-token reduction primarily benefits prefill
computation, while autoregressive decoding time is dominated by
per-token computation and memory access.

Training efficiency. Our training method trains on only 14K training
data. The training loop ran for 15.1h on eight GPUs. Considering the
study of training time in the original paper [Wen et al.
(2025b)](#bib.bib8) that 12.2-48 hours are used for training on the same
devices (EPIC, LlaVA-Mini, etc.), our training cost is acceptable under
the scenario of training-based visual token reduction.

### 4.6 Compared with RL algorithms

We compare our method with three advanced RL algorithms: GRPO ([Shao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib14)), GSPO ([Zheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) and
DAPO ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). As shown in
Table [6](#S4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 4.5 Efficiency ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction"),
directly applying these general-purpose RL algorithms substantially
improves the 5% visual-token baseline, increasing the average retain
ratio from 68.6% to 75.8%–78.2%. Nevertheless, LT-OPD consistently
outperforms all three alternatives across all benchmarks and further
raises the average retain ratio to 82.3%. In particular, compared with
the strongest RL baseline DAPO, LT-OPD achieves notable gains on
V^(∗)Bench, MME, and TextVQA. These results suggest that, under an
extremely limited visual-token budget, distilling the model’s on-policy
knowledge provides a more effective learning signal than optimizing with
general-purpose RL objectives, enabling the compressed model to better
recover the capabilities of its full-token counterpart.

Training Strategies.
Table [7](#S4.T7 "Table 7 ‣ 4.5 Efficiency ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")

LT-OPD primarily applies the frozen initial policy as the teacher. We
discover that LT-OPD keeps stable and good performance across all
benchmarks, with the scores and average retain ratio do not change much
(81.3%-82.3%). This study supports the robustness of our method even
using different regularization methods.

Compression-gap recovery. We want to explore whether our training
algorithms better help the student policy to “recover” performance lost
to visual token pruning. Thus, we define a new metric, recovery,
calculated by
$`100\,(S_{\mathrm{method}}-S_{\mathrm{pruned}})/(S_{\mathrm{full}}-S_{\mathrm{pruned}})`$.
$`S_{\{\mathrm{full},\ \mathrm{pruned},\ \mathrm{method}\}}`$ denotes
original scores and scores before and after training. Larger recovery
represents that a better policy is provided.
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.5 Efficiency ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction")
compares the scores between LT-OPD and RL algorithms. LT-OPD recovers
the most across four evaluated benchmarks, especially much higher (over
45 points) than RL methods on V^(∗)Bench. This comparison further
supports the results in
Section [4.6](#S4.SS6 "4.6 Compared with RL algorithms ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction").

Results on multi-image benchmarks. We apply our method and baselines on

and MVBench ([Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib38)). Results are in
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Results on Multi-image Benchmarks ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction").
LT-OPD outperforms its training-free counterpart (CDPruner) and EPIC a
lot (by over 12% in average retain ratio). We may draw the conclusion
that LT-OPD has the potential to be generalized to video understanding
tasks, which lead one of the future directions ([Zhang et al.,
2025d](#bib.bib40)).

Case Studies. Some case studies are in
Appendix [J](#A10 "Appendix J Qualitative Case Studies ‣ Appendix I Results on Multi-image Benchmarks ‣ Fewer Tokens, More Self-Teaching: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Extreme Visual Token Reduction").
These cases range from questions natural images to OCR questions. LT-OPD
can do well even on cases needing fine-grained visual information
understanding or small texts compared with baselines. This suggests that
students trained with LT-OPD have strong potential across different
perceptual tasks.

## 5 Conclusion

Extreme visual-token reduction changes more than the amount of visual
evidence available to an MLLM: it also changes the generation states
that the compressed model encounters. Motivated by this observation, we
introduce LT-OPD, which distills a full-token self-teacher directly on
trajectories generated by the low-token student, and progressively
reduces the visual-token budget for stable training. Extensive
experiments across nine benchmarks, multiple model scales, and different
MLLM families show that LT-OPD substantially recovers the capability
lost under 5% visual-token retention without sacrificing the efficiency
benefits of compression. These results point to a broader direction for
efficient MLLMs: under extreme compression, effective token selection
and effective adaptation should be considered jointly rather than in
isolation.

## Appendix B Implementation and Training Details

#### Student and teacher.

We initialize the student and teacher from Qwen3.5-4B. The teacher is a
frozen copy of the initial model and runs in evaluation mode without
gradients. Both receive the same clean full image and question, with the
teacher using the dense visual representation. We train the student
language model, vision encoder, and native visual merger with
full-parameter updates.

#### Visual token selection.

We adapt CDPruner to Qwen3.5 and apply it after the native visual merger
and before the language model. The parameter-free selector combines
question-conditioned relevance with visual diversity through a
matrix-free greedy conditional determinantal-point-process procedure in
FP32. Selection runs without gradients, breaks ties by original token
index, and preserves the selected embeddings’ original multimodal rotary
positions.

#### Distillation objective.

Training uses student-generated trajectories and distributional
on-policy distillation. We use a Jensen-Shannon objective with mixture
coefficient 0.5. At each response position, the support is the union of
the student’s top 100 tokens, the teacher’s top 100 tokens, and the
sampled token, with the remaining probability mass represented by one
aggregated tail category, following ([Yuan et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)).
Loss reduction averages valid response-token losses within each sequence
and then averages over sequences.

#### Execution details.

Training comprises 175 updates and 112,000 generated trajectories;
evaluation uses the final checkpoint at update 175. The image processor
applies the model’s native resize before patchification, with patch size
16 and native spatial merge size 2. Bias, normalization, and parameter
tensors with fewer than two dimensions receive zero weight decay.

Each student update microbatch contains up to seven trajectories, and
each teacher microbatch up to two. The Hugging Face rollout model is
replicated, with a decode-batch cap of 80 and CPU offload between
phases. We use Python 3.12.13, PyTorch 2.10.0 (CUDA 12.6), Transformers
5.5.0, FlashAttention 2.8.3, and Ray 2.53.0.

## Appendix F Explanations of Different training strategies

We further examine different strategies for constructing the full-token
teacher during LT-OPD. In all variants, the student generates the
on-policy trajectory with compressed visual tokens, while the teacher
receives the full visual tokens and provides next-token distributional
supervision on the same student-generated prefixes.

Initial-policy teacher. Our default strategy keeps the teacher fixed at
the pretrained initialization $`\theta_{0}`$ throughout training, i.e.,
$`\pi_{T}=\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$. This provides a stationary supervision
target and prevents the teacher from co-adapting with the compressed
student. This strategy is also consistent with prior on-policy
self-distillation practice ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)).

Trust-region regularization. We also consider an adaptive teacher
regularized toward the initial policy. At training step $`s`$, the
teacher distribution is constructed by interpolating the current
full-token policy with the frozen reference policy in log-probability
space:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi_{T}^{\mathrm{TR}}(a\mid x,s)\propto\exp\!\left((1-\lambda)\log\pi_{\theta_{0}}(a\mid x,s)+\lambda\log\pi_{\theta_{s}}(a\mid x,s)\right),
``` |  | (10) |

where $`\lambda\in[0,1]`$ controls how strongly the teacher follows the
current policy. This allows the teacher to evolve during training while
constraining it from drifting excessively from the pretrained model.

EMA teacher. Alternatively, we maintain a separate teacher whose
parameters are updated as an exponential moving average (EMA) of the
student:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\theta_{T}^{(s)}=(1-\alpha)\theta_{T}^{(s-1)}+\alpha\theta_{s},
``` |  | (11) |

with $`\theta_{T}^{(0)}=\theta_{0}`$. The EMA teacher changes more
smoothly than the current student and therefore provides a slowly
evolving supervision target. Similar EMA regularization has been adopted
in recent multimodal on-policy self-distillation ([Yuan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib24)).

###### Abstract

Adapting one’s thought process based on corrective feedback is an
essential ability in human learning, particularly in collaborative
settings. In contrast, the current large language model training
paradigm relies heavily on modeling vast, static corpora. While
effective for knowledge acquisition, it overlooks the interactive
feedback loops essential for models to adapt dynamically to their
context. In this work, we propose a framework that treats this
interactive in-context learning ability not as an emergent property, but
as a distinct, trainable skill. We introduce a scalable method that
transforms single-turn verifiable tasks into multi-turn didactic
interactions driven by information asymmetry. We first show that current
flagship models struggle to integrate corrective feedback on hard
reasoning tasks. We then demonstrate that models trained with our
approach dramatically improve the ability to interactively learn from
language feedback. More specifically, the multi-turn performance of a
smaller model nearly reaches that of a model an order of magnitude
larger. We also observe robust out-of-distribution generalization:
interactive training on math problems transfers to diverse domains like
coding, puzzles and maze navigation. Our qualitative analysis suggests
that this improvement is due to an enhanced in-context plasticity.
Finally, we show that this paradigm offers a unified path to
self-improvement. By training the model to predict the teacher’s
critiques—effectively modeling the feedback environment—we convert this
external signal into an internal capability, allowing the model to
self-correct even without a teacher.

## 1 Introduction

How do we impart capabilities? Natural language is central to human
progress, allowing us to both distribute information via static sources,
like scientific articles, as well as provide real-time feedback to adapt
collectively to unpredictable situations. While Large Language Models
(LLMs) excel at processing large amounts of static knowledge, they
struggle to learn from real-time interaction. Consequently, because
models do not readily adapt on the fly, AI researchers must typically
intervene when capabilities are missing by manually curating and adding
datapoints, or tasks, to the training mixture. Alternatively,
researchers, and users alike, rely on complex prompt engineering to
steer models toward desired outcomes. While effective, these strategies
create a bottleneck, placing the burden entirely on researchers to
monitor failures and adjust task distributions. As the tasks users
assign to LLMs grow in specificity, maintaining this level of oversight
becomes prohibitively difficult.

Yet, for many tasks of interest, the knowledge required to resolve these
tail-end failures likely already exists, distributed across the model’s
user base. In such cases, the bottleneck is not the availability of
knowledge, but the model’s capacity to effectively acquire it through
interactions. However, guiding current LLMs via interaction is brittle,
often requiring users to engineer complex initial prompts to elicit
desired capabilities ([White et al., 2023](#bib.bib2)). In Figure
[3](#S1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback"),
we present this through a simple evocative example between two leading
closed-source models, where one of them misses the obvious hint. To make
it worthwhile for users to teach LLMs, models must adeptly interpret,
reason, and adjust their behavior dynamically within the context of a
conversation. Doing so robustly could lead to exponentially more
efficient learning ([Xu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib1)). However, this
presents a significant challenge: unlike standard Reinforcement Learning
(RL) where scalar rewards offer a clear gradient for optimization,
language feedback does not readily lend itself to such direct
optimization.

In this work, we propose a framework to address this problem by treating
interactive in-context learning from natural language feedback as a
distinct, trainable capability. We introduce a simple and scalable
method that reformulates the vast amounts of available single-turn
verifiable reasoning problems into multi-turn didactic dialogues. As
shown in Figure
[2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback"),
we simulate interactions between a student and a teacher, where the
student must iteratively refine its solution by integrating the
teacher’s guidance. This setup serves as an effective approximation to
the real-world corrective feedback between users and models. A key
insight in our work is that generating high-quality feedback on
verifiable domains does not require a superior model, but rather
information asymmetry. The teacher can utilize the same base model as
the student but is conditioned on privileged information, such as
reference solutions or unit test outputs. By prompting the teacher to
provide guidance without revealing the answer (experiments show this
happens in less than $`1\%`$ of cases), we can fine-tune the student
model to improve its ability to effectively adapt in-context to natural
language feedback.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach through a comprehensive
series of experiments across diverse domains. We first establish that
off-the-shelf flagship models struggle to integrate language feedback
during complex reasoning tasks. We then study how our didactic training
paradigm improves an agent’s interactive in-context learning ability and
empowers it to actively leverage teacher feedback, turning initial
errors into successes through multi-turn dialogue. Remarkably, a smaller
model (Gemini 2.5 Flash) fine-tuned via our method nearly matches the
performance of a significantly larger model (Gemini 2.5 Pro).
Furthermore, we verify the strong generalization of these capabilities:
models trained exclusively on verifiable math interactions show marked
improvements in distinct domains, such as coding and logic puzzles
(e.g., ARC-AGI). This transfer learning even extends to multi-turn tasks
unrelated to the didactic training setup, including Poker and Wordle.
Investigating the mechanism behind these improvements, we find that
fine-tuned models exhibit greater in-context plasticity, whereas
baselines tend to merely repeat their initial answers.

Finally, we demonstrate that this approach creates a unified pathway to
self-improvement. Rather than training solely on the student’s turns, we
train on the full interactive dialogues, including the teacher’s
critiques. Considering the teacher as the environment, providing new
observations between student turns, this process is analogous to having
an auxiliary world modeling objective. Importantly, we train the model
to predict the teacher’s turns as if they were unconditioned on
privileged information, requiring the model to infer the logic of the
critique solely from the context of the error. This allows a single
model to internalize the whole feedback loop: it learns the student’s
ability to correct reasoning and the teacher’s ability to spot flaws.
Consequently, the external guidance provided during training is
transformed into an internal capability, allowing the model to
self-correct by playing the role of its own teacher.

## 2 Learning from Language Feedback

We now detail our methodology for enhancing the interactive in-context
learning capabilities of LLMs. Our design prioritizes verifiable tasks,
where performance is measured strictly by the model’s ability to achieve
the ground-truth solution. This focus allows us to isolate methods that
robustly drive improvement across multiple interaction turns, thereby
avoiding the evaluation ambiguity and subjectivity inherent in
non-verifiable tasks.

Interactive learning via language feedback can be viewed through the
lens of social learning ([Bandura, 1977](#bib.bib6); [Jaques et al.,
2019](#bib.bib8); [Ndousse et al., 2021](#bib.bib7))—the process of
acquiring behaviors by observing, imitating and interacting with others.
Our algorithm builds on one of the simplest yet most powerful social
learning settings: the didactic interaction between a teacher and a
student. As illustrated in Figure
[2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback"),
our mechanism pairs a student model, which attempts to solve a problem,
with a teacher model that provides targeted language guidance. The
student is conditioned on the problem statement; if the automatic
verifier deems its answer incorrect, the teacher intervenes with
guidance, prompting the student to attempt the problem again. Unlike
common student-teacher frameworks such as distillation ([Hinton et al.,
2015](#bib.bib9); [Agarwal et al., 2023](#bib.bib19)), where the teacher
is instantiated as a larger, more capable model, our approach relies
solely on information asymmetry. The teacher leverages privileged
information (such as ground truth labels) to generate corrective
feedback. Consequently, the teacher and student can be instantiated
using the exact same model. This cooperative self-play dynamic,
optimized via RL, will be shown to yield significant performance
improvements after fine-tuning. Our setup is related to [Mohtashami et
al. (2024)](#bib.bib37), who introduce a framework of social learning in
LLMs where models exchange knowledge via natural language to maintain
data privacy. Overall, our teacher-student setup is a particular
instantiation of the concepts of language games and boundless socratic
learning ([Schaul, 2024](#bib.bib24)).

### 2.1 Formal Definition

Learning from language feedback can be modeled as a partially observable
Markov decision process (POMDP)
$`\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},T,R\rangle`$. The student
model, an agent with parameters $`\theta_{S}`$, learns a policy
$`\pi_{S}`$, whereas the teacher model is parametrize with
$`\theta_{T}`$ and learns a policy $`\pi_{T}`$. In our experiments, the
student and the teacher actions $`a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}`$ are natural
language utterances $`u_{t}`$, however, in the general case, this could
include tool calls. The observation at time $`t`$ is modeled as the
conversation history
$`o_{t}=(u_{1}^{T},u_{1}^{S},\dots,u_{t}^{T},u_{t}^{S})`$, where
$`u_{t}^{T}`$ and $`u_{t}^{S}`$ are teacher and student utterances at
time $`t`$. A state at time $`t`$ is then defined as the tuple
consisting of the conversation history $`o_{t}`$ and the task’s
privileged information $`k`$, written as
$`s_{t}=(k_{t},o_{t})=(k_{t},u_{1}^{T},u_{1}^{S},\dots,u_{t}^{T},u_{t}^{S})\in\mathcal{S}`$.
This privileged information can take the form of a ground-truth solution
or output of private unit tests.

At each turn $`t`$, the teacher observes the conversation history and
the task privileged information $`k`$, and takes an action
$`u_{t+1}^{T}\sim\pi_{T}(\cdot|s_{t},k)`$. After the teacher’s
utterance, the student responds with
$`u_{t+1}^{S}\sim\pi_{S}(\cdot|u_{t+1}^{T},o_{t})`$. In our
implementation, the teacher and the student policies define the
transition function $`T`$. In the general case, these policies could be
acting on an environment (such as computer use) with a separate
transition function. This interaction loop continues until the end of an
episode, defined through a maximum number of turns $`T`$, or when the
student outputs the correct solution. The reward function then measures
the student’s output through a sparse function $`R(s_{t},a_{t})`$
focusing on conversation-level outcomes like final answer correctness.
We use verifiable domains like math and code, where objective
performance measures exist.

Our algorithm leverages the model’s in-context learning ability to
integrate natural language feedback during conversations. After each
such trajectory, weight optimization is done through gradient updates.
In other words, we are improving the model’s ability of learning how to
learn from interactions, i.e. its meta-learning ability ([Schmidhuber,
1987](#bib.bib25); [Thrun and Pratt, 1998](#bib.bib26)). The standard
approach in meta learning, recently generalized by recent works
[Franceschi et al. (2018)](#bib.bib27); [Grefenstette et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib28) through the lens of bi-level optimization, typically
requires gradient updates in the inner loop, leading to meta-gradients.
We avoid this by relying on, and improving, the existing in-context
learning abilities of LLMs.

This approach is similar to black box meta-learning (e.g., RL²) ([Wang
et al., 2016](#bib.bib31); [Duan et al., 2016](#bib.bib30)) and
first-order meta-learning ([Nichol et al., 2018](#bib.bib29)). In
particular, since during training language feedback is only observed
following turns that do not achieve the verifiable reward, the presence
of language feedback can be interpreted as an augmented reward
observation. The LLM can thus be viewed as implementing an in-context RL
algorithm with weights that are in turn optimized via RL. We name our
method Reinforcement Learning with Language Feedback (RL²F) to indicate
the use of these two learning signals and to highlight the connection
with RL².

## 3 Experiments

We perform an extensive study designed to measure the efficacy and
generalizability of our proposed method for improving the interactive
in-context learning abilities of LLMs. We structure our investigation
into three distinct areas:

1.  1.  We first analyze the interactive in-context learning abilities of
        state-of-the-art models, revealing significant headroom in the
        frontier models’ multi-turn reasoning abilities across interactions
        on hard reasoning tasks.
2.  2.  We conduct fine-tuning experiments on a non-thinking model, Gemma 3
        12b, as well as the thinking flagship Gemini 2.5 Flash. Experiments
        focus on measuring the improvements in terms of multi-turn
        in-context adaptation, where RL²F leads to significant
        in-distribution gains and strong out-of-distribution generalization.
        We study the reasons behind such improvements through qualitative
        analysis, which points to an increased in-context plasticity.
3.  3.  We demonstrate a pathway to self-improvement by training on
        multi-turn attempts to internalize the feedback loop. By treating
        the teacher’s turns as an auxiliary world-modeling objective, the
        model can internalize the feedback loop, thereby developing the
        capability to self-correct.

### 3.1 In-depth Evaluation

We begin our experiments by evaluating where frontier models stand in
terms of their ability to in-context learn from language feedback. We do
not perform weight updates in this section, but simply ask: can current
models already efficiently incorporate feedback in-context? We
experiment across four different hard reasoning tasks: HardMath2
([Roggeveen et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)), ARC-AGI ([Chollet et al.,
2024](#bib.bib12)), Codeforces ([Quan et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)) and
BIG-Bench Extra Hard ([Kazemi et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)). We evaluate
four separate models, three from the Gemini 2.5 family of models
([Comanici et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)) (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash
and Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite) and additionally compare to the performance
of GPT-5 ([OpenAI, 2025](#bib.bib15)).

The results in Figure
[4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.1 In-depth Evaluation ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback"),
we report the cumulative accuracy which is defined as the percentage of
problems that were correctly solved for a certain number of turns, shown
on the $`x`$-axis. We observe an ordering between model sizes, where the
larger Gemini models tend to perform better than smaller models.
Interestingly, we observe that GPT-5 consistently performs better than
Gemini 2.5 Pro. This is apparent even in cases where the initial
single-turn accuracy is almost the same (on HardMath2). Overall, all
models show, that despite multiple turns of feedback from a teacher, the
accuracy of the student leaves a lot of room for improvement.

Does the teacher leak the ground truth solution? An important question
that we need to address in the current evaluation setup relates to
whether we can trust that the student legitimately solves the problem,
or has the solution been revealed by the teacher. We investigate this
question by having a verification pipeline of two detection mechanisms:
(1) a string matching function applied on each teacher output and (2) an
LLM judge that, conditioned on the question, ground truth solution and
teacher output, evaluates whether the solution has been revealed. We
report that in an average of 0.3% of cases our verification pipeline
signals that the teacher has revealed the solution, indicating a strong
enough instruction-following ability of the Gemini and GPT models. The
noise that might come from such false positives is far below the gap in
performance between the models that we have evaluated.

### 3.2 Didactic Interactions

Having characterized the gap in interactive in-context learning
capabilities, we now present the results on improving this capability
through the proposed approach outlined in Section
[2](#S2 "2 Learning from Language Feedback ‣ Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback").
We compare this approach to two additional baselines: (1) a standard
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) baseline trained on problem and solution
pairs and (2) single-turn RL, the standard optimization method on
verifiable tasks, also known as RL from verifiable rewards (RLVR) or RL
from machine feedback (RLMF).

#### 3.2.1 Non-Thinking Models

In our first set of experiments, we fine-tune a non-thinking model,
Gemma 3 12b, on the training split of Omni MATH ([Gao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib11)). We then evaluate the ability of the model to
integrate teacher feedback on the test set and compare performance
against a set of baselines. For Omni MATH, we formed a test split by
randomly selecting 500 questions from the larger dataset. As illustrated
in Figure
[5(a)](#S3.F5.sf1 "In Figure 5 ‣ 3.2 Didactic Interactions ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback"),
RL²F on didactic interactions proves to be the most effective strategy
for enhancing interactive in-context learning. Crucially, while standard
RL and SFT baselines match the single-turn problem-solving improvements,
only RL²F continuously widens the performance gap relative to the
baselines as the number of interaction turns increases. These findings
highlight an effective method for improving reasoning through
conversational feedback, offering a natural alternative to the
impractical task of curating massive datasets of expert behavior.

Next we verify how training for reasoning from multi-turn language
feedback generalizes to out-of-distribution domains. We train the Gemma
3 12b model on Omni MATH and evaluate on LiveCodeBench ([Jain et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16)). Here, the teacher’s private knowledge consists of
unit test cases. By providing feedback based on test execution results,
the system mimics a standard software engineering workflow. Results in
[5(b)](#S3.F5.sf2 "In Figure 5 ‣ 3.2 Didactic Interactions ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback")
show that the single-turn RL baseline barely improves upon the baseline,
whereas the multi-turn RL optimization done on teacher-student
interactions provides improvements for later turns. This is a first sign
indicating that learning to learn from language feedback, RL²F, could be
more generalizable than standard RLVR. In the following section, results
on thinking models will show a significantly wider spectrum of
generalization.

#### 3.2.2 Thinking Models

The previous experiments in this study focused on a relatively small,
non-thinking models. It is natural to question whether learning from
multi-turn interactions remains necessary for thinking models, given
that mechanisms such as language critiques and revisions are already
inherent in thinking traces ([DeepSeek-AI, 2025](#bib.bib18)). To
address this, we conduct experiments on the Gemini 2.5 Flash model,
fine-tuning it via RL on didactic multi-turn interactions. We utilize a
private set of mathematics questions whose difficulty exceeds that of
the Omni-MATH dataset. We then evaluate performance on a separate and
challenging dataset, HardMath2 ([Roggeveen et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)).
The results in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback")
demonstrate that RL²F yields substantial gains in improving a model’s
ability to in-context learn from language feedback. Notably, the
performance of the fine-tuned Flash model nearly matches that of the
Gemini 2.5 Pro model. This is a significant finding, as mathematics is a
domain where Gemini 2.5 Pro typically excels. We show that by eliciting
a thinking model’s multi-turn capabilities through a training procedure
with modest computational cost, we can bridge the performance gap to a
model a full tier above.

investigations. In the Appendix

we report additional examples, showing how the better integration of
feedback naturally leads to task decomposition and improved final
performance.

cognitive capability that generalizes far beyond the source domain.

approach offers a highly data-efficient pathway for scaling
post-training optimization in thinking models by re-purposing all
available single-turn verifiable tasks to train the model’s interactive

## 4 Towards Self-Improvement

Figure 7: Training models to learn from multi-turn didactic interactions
significantly improves their self-improvement capabilities at inference
time. Key to this result is for the student to predict the teacher’s
feedback. In other words, adopting a world-modeling approach within
didactic interactions leads the student model to become an autodidact.

Could the ability of learning from feedback given from others generalize
to improve the ability to introspect? In this section, we answer in the
affirmative, demonstrating that internalizing the feedback patterns from
didactic interactions is key to self-improvement and becoming
autodidact. Concretely, we adopt a world-modeling view by having the
student predict the teacher’s feedback. At inference, we then let the
model interact with itself, generating a loop of self-critiques and
self-refinements. We do not condition the self-critique mechanism on
privileged information: the agent gives itself feedback solely by
observing its past attempts. Figure
[7](#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4 Towards Self-Improvement ‣ Improving Interactive In-Context Learning from Natural Language Feedback")
shows a clear picture: learning from multi-turn interactions with
another knowledgeable agent (the teacher) can significantly increase the
performance of a model to self-improve. Surprisingly, the autodidact
agent outperforms the agent evaluated through didactic interactions with
a privileged teacher. This strong performance indicates that the
external signal received by the teacher at train-time is not just a
corrective for the current task; it is a valuable training signal to
bootstrap an internal evaluator for future self-improvement. We
hypothesize that this external signal prevents the degenerate loops
often caused by biased self-feedback, providing the high-quality data
necessary for robust learning.

## 5 Related Work

A growing body of research explores how agents can learn from natural
language feedback. One line of work focuses on the theoretical
underpinnings of such learning. For instance, [Xu et al.
(2025a)](#bib.bib3) formalize the Learning from Language Feedback (LLF)
problem, where an agent observes textual feedback instead of a latent
reward signal. They propose that for an agent to learn effectively with
no regret, the feedback must be unbiased, the agent must be able to
interpret it, and the agent’s hypothesis space must include the
environment’s true hypothesis.

Another research direction aims to integrate language more deeply into
the reinforcement learning paradigm. [Feng et al. (2024)](#bib.bib4)
introduce Natural Language Reinforcement Learning (NLRL), a framework
that redefines core reinforcement learning concepts, such as value
functions and policies, in natural language. This approach seeks to
improve the interpretability and sample efficiency of RL agents by using
language as the medium for representing and reasoning about the task.

The concept of leveraging a more knowledgeable teacher to provide
feedback is explored by [Choudhury and Sodhi (2024)](#bib.bib5). This
work highlights the importance of information asymmetry between a
teacher and a student model. The teacher is provided with privileged
information, such as the exact location of an object in a navigation
task, which is not available to the student. This allows the teacher to
provide more effective feedback on the student’s failed trajectories.
The student then learns from this feedback through imitation learning,
where the teacher’s correct actions and rationales replace the student’s
incorrect ones. Experiments have shown that this can lead to the student
outperforming the teacher, and that a student can even improve by
receiving feedback from a privileged version of itself.

## 6 Conclusion

In this work, we have introduced a method for learning from language
feedback through didactic interactions and have shown it to be an
effective way to improve the ability of LLMs to interactively in-context
learn from language feedback. Our results show that this approach not
only allows smaller models to rival the performance of flagship models
on verifiable reasoning tasks but also induces a fundamental increase in
in-context plasticity that generalizes to diverse, out-of-distribution
domains. Furthermore, we showed that by internalizing the feedback loop
from didactic interactions, models can bootstrap their own capability
for self-improvement and become autodidacts.

In our setup, the teacher has not played a role in the selection of the
problems the student faces. However, human teachers typically craft
careful curricula for their students, in part based on observed mistakes
during interactions. Exploring this idea further would be a promising
avenue, with many connections to be made to curriculum learning ([Kaplan
and Oudeyer, 2003](#bib.bib34); [Schmidhuber, 2004](#bib.bib35)) such as
unsupervised environment design ([Dennis et al., 2020](#bib.bib33);
[Jiang et al., 2021](#bib.bib32)).

Our goal was to train a model to better integrate feedback in-context,
which led to increased adaptability. However, this also raises an
important question: could this same mechanism incentivize undesirable
behaviors, such as sycophancy? Further studies into the potential safety
issues, such as misuse and misalignment ([Shah et al.,
2025](#bib.bib36)), that these new capabilities raise is of great
importance.

Furthermore, while our investigation focused on a cooperative setting
between a teacher and a student, the scope and complexity of such social
learning settings extend far beyond such settings. Many real-world
scenarios involve agents operating with partial information and
conflicting goals ([Dafoe et al., 2020](#bib.bib10)). Consequently, a
promising avenue for future research lies in exploring mixed-motive
settings, such as debate or negotiation, which could potentially yield
distinct cognitive benefits.

Finally, we believe this work represents a significant step toward
robust continual learning, yet a critical piece of the puzzle remains.
We have successfully improved the model’s interactive in-context
learning, but we have not yet addressed the integration of these
transient signals into permanent capabilities. Developing a mechanism to
consolidate this enhanced short-term adaptation into long-term knowledge
remains a crucial frontier.

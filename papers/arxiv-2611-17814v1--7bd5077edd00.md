# G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands

Yuechun Yu    Wenzheng Kong    Xiaofeng Mao    Jin Zhang

###### Abstract

With the rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs), an
increasing number of users are turning to LLM-powered agents for
conversational shopping in e-commerce. However, the user needs continue
to change as new product categories, procurement scenarios, and feature
requests emerge. At the same time, the product’s execution
harness—including its tools, skills, and operating procedures—continues
to evolve to support increasingly sophisticated interactions. These
changes create two practical challenges: 1) Demand drift alters the
distribution, intent, and complexity of user requests, causing a model
trained once to fall behind; 2) Harness evolution requires the model to
use newly introduced capabilities correctly without losing behavior that
users already rely on. Periodic manual retraining is too delayed and
labor-intensive for this setting. To address these challenges, we
present G-CAST, a governed continual self-training system that converts
daily production evidence under evolving demand and capability contracts
into reward-routed, entropy-prioritized training data and governed
reward evolution. Governed checkpoint transition under cumulative
evaluation controls each model update, enabling the agent to learn newly
introduced behaviors without sacrificing previously accepted
capabilities. Experiments on a real production e-commerce agent show
that G-CAST achieves stronger adaptation to newly introduced demand
while better preserving previously accepted behavior compared with a
strong autonomous post-training baseline. Ablations further show that
both reward-routed, entropy-prioritized data orchestration and governed
reward evolution are important to these gains. G-CAST is now deployed in
Yuancai(http://cai.1688.com/), a B2B procurement agent on Alibaba’s 1688
platform.

## 1 Introduction

In e-commerce applications, large language model (LLM)-based shopping
agents are deployed to satisfy diverse user requirements and complete a
broad range of shopping tasks through conversational interaction. After
deployment, user requirements continue to change, becoming increasingly
complex and personalized as new product categories, procurement
scenarios, and feature requests emerge. To accommodate these changes,
the agent harness expands accordingly, incorporating richer context
construction, long-term memory, specialized tools, reusable skills, and
task-specific operating procedures. These expansions alter the
information available to the agent, the actions it can take, and the
interaction protocols it is expected to follow. The capability surface
exposed to the deployed model therefore changes continually with user
requirements.

An expanded capability surface, however, does not automatically lead to
improved agent behavior. We refer to the information available to the
agent, the actions it can take, and the interaction protocols it must
follow as its capability contract. Because the underlying model is
typically optimized under an earlier contract, expanding the harness can
introduce three coupled mismatches. First, a model mismatch arises when
the model fails to recognize when a newly introduced capability is
applicable, invoke it correctly, or integrate its output into the final
response. Second, a data mismatch arises because trajectories collected
under the previous harness may remain useful for legacy capabilities but
contain little or no evidence about newly exposed ones. Third, a reward
mismatch arises when a reward function designed for the previous
contract cannot correctly evaluate behavior under the expanded contract:
it may overlook missing or incorrect uses of new tools and skills, or
penalize valid behavior that follows a newly introduced procedure. These
mismatches are particularly consequential in e-commerce, where emerging
shopping and procurement tasks often require the coordinated use of
multiple capabilities. As user requirements and the agent harness
evolve, the growing misalignment among the model, training evidence, and
reward can prevent the agent from effectively exploiting its expanded
capability surface. Harness expansion must therefore be accompanied by
continuous agent adaptation. In this setting, self-evolution means
continually synchronizing the model, its training evidence, and its
reward specification with the evolving capability contract.

Recent approaches to agent self-evolution address individual parts of
this problem but leave their coupling unresolved. ACE evolves context
playbooks around a fixed model, improving harness-level adaptation
without ensuring that the underlying model internalizes newly exposed
capabilities ([Zhang et al. 2026a](#bib.bib11)). Q-Evolve jointly
refines process feedback and the policy, but relies on task-specific
environment feedback rather than a capability contract that expands with
user requirements ([Zhang et al. 2026b](#bib.bib1)). SIA combines
harness and weight updates, yet still studies improvement within fixed
tasks rather than a governed transition that synchronizes learning
evidence and reward while protecting previously accepted capabilities
([Hebbar et al. 2026](#bib.bib2)). Consequently, existing methods do not
fully address demand-driven self-evolution in deployed agents.

We address this gap with G-CAST, a governed continual self-training
system for demand-driven agent adaptation that treats the checkpoint
transition, rather than the completion of a training job, as the unit of
adaptation. The system jointly versions the deployed model, current
capability contract, executable reward, learning evidence, and
cumulative evaluation ledger, and couples two mechanisms: reward-routed,
entropy-prioritized data orchestration and capability-contract-driven
reward evolution. Each round generates multiple rollouts per query with
the accepted checkpoint. When their reward patterns provide valid
supervision, the data-orchestration mechanism routes all-failure queries
with verified targets to supervised fine-tuning, mixed-outcome queries
to reinforcement learning, and all-success queries to regression
monitoring, then uses token-level entropy to prioritize examples within
each training branch. When the reward reliability check instead flags a
possible reward error, training is paused and the reward-evolution loop
audits the executable reward against the current capability contract. A
confirmed mismatch is repaired through an executable reward update that
must pass targeted validation, old-versus-new rescoring, and trajectory
replay before the evidence is rescored. After these mechanisms establish
valid supervision and routed learning evidence, the loop trains a
candidate from an immutable parent. The candidate replaces the accepted
checkpoint only after passing evaluation on both emerging tasks and all
previously retained capabilities. Rejected candidates, regressions, and
reward errors guide the next round.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce a governed
self-evolution loop that addresses performance degradation caused by
increasingly complex user requirements and the corresponding expansion
of the agent harness, improving performance on emerging e-commerce tasks
while preserving performance on previously accepted tasks. Second, we
propose reward-routed, entropy-prioritized data orchestration that
decouples the choice of whether and how to train from the prioritization
of training examples. By using entropy only to rank uncertainty within
routed pools, our method decouples uncertainty from correctness and uses
it as a within-pool prioritization signal, resulting in more effective
sample selection and better downstream performance compared with
alternative strategies. Third, we introduce capability-contract-driven
reward evolution with comprehensive gates. As new tools, skills, and
operating procedures expand the contract, a fixed reward may retain high
scores for trajectories that violate new requirements or penalize
behavior that is valid under the expanded contract. Versioned executable
rule components keep the reward synchronized, while target-case
validation, known-correct regression checks, offline rescoring, and
trajectory replay prevent invalid updates from entering training.

## 2 Related Work

Recent work on automated agent improvement follows two main directions.
_Automatic training agents_ automate post-training and data engineering,
whereas _self-evolving agentic AI_ updates the context, harness, or
model from execution feedback. Most existing methods optimize within a
fixed task specification. Our work instead considers evolving user
demand, where new capabilities and procedures can simultaneously
invalidate model behavior, training evidence, and reward.

### Automatic Training Agents

#### Autonomous LLM Post-Training

PostTrainBench and Agent² RL-Bench evaluate whether LLM agents can
autonomously construct post-training pipelines, including data
preparation, rollout collection, reward design, training, and evaluation
([Rank et al. 2026](#bib.bib3); [Chen et al. 2026](#bib.bib4)).
AutoTrainess improves this process through structured interfaces and
operational constraints, while A-Evolve-Training demonstrates autonomous
post-training at larger model scales ([Yu et al. 2026](#bib.bib6); [Shi
et al. 2026](#bib.bib5)). These systems optimize a predefined task
objective; our setting additionally requires determining whether the
objective and reward remain valid as user requirements evolve.

#### Autonomous Data Engineering

ADP-MA automates multi-stage data processing through hierarchical
meta-agents, schema contracts, and runtime validation ([Khurana
2026](#bib.bib7)). DataMaster searches over data discovery, selection,
and transformation using shared data and cumulative memory ([Du et al.
2026](#bib.bib8)). CURATION-BENCH studies agent-generated data-selection
policies, while Autodata constructs and iteratively refines synthetic
datasets ([Kang et al. 2026](#bib.bib9); [Kulikov et al.
2026](#bib.bib10)). Unlike these methods, which improve data under a
fixed learning objective, our method validates the current reward and
routes production evidence to SFT, RL, or regression monitoring
according to repeated-rollout outcomes.

### Self-Evolving Agentic AI

#### Harness and Context Evolution

Harness-level approaches adapt external components while keeping model
parameters fixed. ACE evolves context playbooks from execution feedback
([Zhang et al. 2026a](#bib.bib11)); Meta Context Engineering co-evolves
context-engineering skills and context artifacts ([Ye et al.
2026](#bib.bib12)); and Meta-Harness searches over executable agent
scaffolds ([Lee et al. 2026](#bib.bib13)). However, an improved harness
does not guarantee that the underlying model can exploit it. Recent work
distinguishes the ability to produce useful harness updates from the
ability to benefit from them ([Lin et al. 2026](#bib.bib14)), motivating
weight adaptation when newly introduced capabilities exceed the behavior
learned under the previous harness.

#### Joint Harness and Model Evolution

SIA jointly updates a task-specific harness and model weights from
verifier feedback, making it the closest prior work in update scope
([Hebbar et al. 2026](#bib.bib2)). However, it assumes a given task
specification and verifier. Our work begins when evolving demand expands
the capability contract itself, potentially creating coupled model,
data, and reward mismatches. We therefore treat the checkpoint
transition as the unit of self-evolution: the loop validates
contract-aware rewards, routes rollout evidence to SFT or RL, trains
from an immutable accepted parent, and promotes a candidate only after
cumulative evaluation on both emerging and previously accepted tasks.

## 3 Application

Our study is applied in Yuancai (http://cai.1688.com/), a professional
AI agent product for business-to-business (B2B) buyers built on 1688,
Alibaba’s wholesale sourcing platform. Yuancai pairs LLM-based agents
with 1688’s factory and supply-chain resources, and its purchasing
capabilities have evolved over time as new operating modes, tools, and
procedures were introduced in response to changing business needs. A
general assistant mode handles arbitrary multi-step sourcing and
business requests described in a single instruction, planning and
executing over more than twenty built-in tools with real-time
scheduling, multi-turn reasoning, and automatic self-correction. A quick
search mode serves precise product, supplier, and look-alike retrieval
through a shorter execution path with faster response. A
knowledge-assisted mode conditions execution on configured business
playbooks, uploaded documents, and accumulated interaction history,
continuously memorizing know-how from ongoing conversations while
retaining and upgrading prior buyer-workspace functions such as instant
messaging and web browsing. Each addition expanded the context, tools,
procedures, and outputs available to the model, thereby creating a new
operating contract at that stage of evolution.

These evolving functions provide the real-world setting for G-CAST. Each
newly introduced capability defines a revised operating contract
together with a corresponding slice of production demand. This gives us
a sequence of versioned demand-and-contract snapshots on which continual
adaptation can be studied and governed.

| Period   | Tools | Newly required capability              |
| -------- | ----- | -------------------------------------- |
| Period 1 | 4     | basic retrieval and detail lookup      |
| Period 2 | 7     | external evidence and trend lookup     |
| Period 3 | 17    | skills, multimodal, inquiry workflows  |
| Period 4 | 23    | sandbox, files, export, full workflows |

period defines a frozen snapshot of requests and capability requirements

## 4 Method

Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands")
illustrates G-CAST as a governed continual self-training system for
deployed agents whose user demand and capability contract evolve over
time. In this setting, adaptation is not just another training run:
newly introduced tools, skills, or procedures can simultaneously create
model mismatch, data mismatch, and reward mismatch. G-CAST addresses
these coupled failures with two linked processes in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands"):
a main self-evolution loop that turns validated production evidence into
checkpoint updates, and a reward-evolution loop that repairs reward when
contract change makes the current reward unreliable.

In the main loop, each round starts from one immutable accepted
checkpoint under the current harness and executable reward. Selected
production queries are replayed to generate multiple rollouts, which
then pass through a reward reliability check that separates
training-eligible evidence from regression-monitoring evidence and flags
possible reward errors. Training-eligible evidence is then processed by
reward-routed, entropy-prioritized data orchestration, while possible
reward errors trigger the reward-evolution loop and, if needed,
executable reward update before training proceeds. The loop then trains
a candidate checkpoint from the accepted parent and admits the
checkpoint transition only if cumulative evaluation confirms both
adaptation to newly introduced demand and retention of previously
accepted behavior.
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3 Application ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands")
summarizes the period-indexed capability contracts used throughout this
process.

##### Reward-Routed, Entropy-Prioritized Data Orchestration.

The reward-routed, entropy-prioritized data orchestration component in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands")
determines how each query contributes to self-training. For each query,
the accepted checkpoint generates eight rollouts. Queries whose rollouts
all fall below a round-level reference enter the SFT pool when a
verified target exists. Queries with both above- and below-reference
rollouts enter the RL pool, since they provide outcome variation
suitable for relative optimization. Queries whose rollouts all remain
above the reference are retained for regression monitoring. The
reference is defined as the mean valid reward after trimming the top and
bottom 5%.

After routing, G-CAST prioritizes examples within the SFT and RL pools
by token-level uncertainty. For each rollout, we compute the 90th
percentile of token entropies and define the query score as the maximum
across its rollouts. Reward decides the learning role of each query,
while entropy focuses the training budget on the most informative routed
examples.

##### Contract-Driven Reward Evolution.

The reward-evolution loop in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands")
is triggered when the reward reliability check indicates that the
current executable reward is not scoring trajectories reliably for the
current deployment setting. This can happen when the capability contract
expands to new tools, skills, or procedures, or when audit evidence
reveals a scoring mismatch under the existing contract, such as high
reward on trajectories that should be penalized. G-CAST therefore treats
reward as a governed executable object that must remain aligned with
both the active capability contract and the distinction between
desirable and undesirable trajectories.

The loop follows the five stages in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands").
Contract-grounded trajectory annotation collects audit cases and
reference labels for the suspected failure mode. Reward update proposal
then specifies the executable rule change needed to correct that
mismatch. Offline reward validation tests the proposed reward through
targeted cases, regression checks, offline rescoring, and replay or
dry-run execution, verifying that it improves the scoring of desirable
versus undesirable trajectories without breaking previously correct
judgments. Trial adaptation uses the candidate reward to control routing
and training on an audit batch. Finally, joint evaluation determines
whether the updated reward and the resulting candidate checkpoint should
be admitted; otherwise, the loop resumes with the previously accepted
reward and checkpoint.

Within this loop, the executable reward is a versioned bundle of
components. Each component has an explicit applicability condition. A
proposed reward update must specify the scoring error it corrects and,
when relevant, the contract change it encodes, and must pass validation
before being used for training.

Following the capability expansion in
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3 Application ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands"),
the training reward evolves cumulatively with the period-specific
contract:

Thus, from Period 3 onward, the training reward is the full reward
$`R_{\mathrm{full}}`$. Period 4 does not introduce a new scalar reward
term; instead, it retains the same full reward while expanding the
applicability and executable checks of the existing components to
sandbox, file-processing, export, and longer workflows. Each term
contributes only when it is applicable to the query contract.
$`R_{\mathrm{fmt}}`$ measures rollout-level compliance with the required
output and interaction rules rather than task correctness. It starts
from a positive base score and is reduced by malformed or unparseable
tool actions, redundant repeated tool calls, answers returned without
required business-tool use, invalid actions after termination, or
missing final answers. $`R_{\mathrm{skill\mbox{-}sel}}`$ evaluates
whether the required skill is correctly selected against a
teacher-verified reference, rewarding correct selection and lowering the
score for missing, incorrect, or unnecessary skill calls.
$`R_{\mathrm{skill\mbox{-}adh}}`$ is rule-based and checks whether the
rollout follows the procedure specified in the selected SKILL.md,
including the required tool sequence when applicable.
$`P_{\mathrm{gd}}\leq 0`$ captures grounding quality by lowering the
score when factual content in the final answer is not supported by the
user prompt or returned tool evidence. $`P_{\mathrm{retrieval}}\leq 0`$
applies when a final answer claims retrieval failure without providing
any valid item or shop result.

The two skill-related terms are introduced when later contracts require
explicit skill use and procedure compliance (Period 3-4). The grounding
penalty is another evolved component, added after reward audits
identified unsupported answer content. Before being used for training,
each reward update must pass offline reward check. For analyses that
exclude the two skill-related terms, we additionally report

##### Evaluation metrics.

Unless explicitly noted as a base-reward analysis, all reported reward
metrics use the mean frozen full evaluation reward. Let $`E_{j}`$ denote
the held-out evaluation slice for period $`j`$, let
$`R_{\mathrm{full}}`$ denote the full reward of query $`q`$, and let
$`t`$ denote the current stage. We define

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{ret}}^{(t)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j<t}\sum_{q\in E_{j}}R_{\mathrm{full}}}{\sum_{j<t}|E_{j}|},\qquad t>1,`$ |  | (6) |
|  | $`\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{new}}^{(t)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|E_{t}|}\sum_{q\in E_{t}}R_{\mathrm{full}},`$ |  | (7) |
|  | $`\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{cum}}^{(t)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j\leq t}\sum_{q\in E_{j}}R_{\mathrm{full}}}{\sum_{j\leq t}|E_{j}|}.`$ |  | (8) |

Retained-period reward $`R_{\mathrm{ret}}^{(t)}`$ measures performance
on previously introduced demand periods, new-period reward
$`R_{\mathrm{new}}^{(t)}`$ measures performance on the newly introduced
period, and cumulative reward $`R_{\mathrm{cum}}^{(t)}`$ measures
overall performance on all active periods.

##### Frozen evaluation reward.

The training reward evolves with the capability contract during the
loop, but all results reported in this paper are measured with one
common frozen evaluation reward: the final contract-complete version. It
combines always-applicable task-outcome and format components,
skill-compliance components that apply only when the query contract
requires a skill, and the gate-admitted grounding penalty. Applicability
is determined solely by the query contract, never by the checkpoint or
ablation arm being scored. This common frozen measure keeps all reported
comparisons directly comparable and evaluates every checkpoint under the
same deployment-relevant contract.

## 5 Experiments

This section evaluates G-CAST on a production e-commerce agent under
evolving demand and capability contracts. Section 5.1 describes the
experimental setup, including the four demand periods, evaluation
protocol, and common frozen evaluation reward. Section 5.2 studies
adaptation and retention, comparing G-CAST with AutoTrainess under
matched cumulative evaluation. Section 5.3 presents the routing and
prioritization ablation, isolating the effects of reward routing and
entropy-based within-pool ranking. Section 5.4 evaluates reward
evolution and examines whether governed reward updates are necessary
under an expanding capability contract. Section 5.5 reports operational
impact beyond evaluation reward, including maintenance efficiency and
governance outcomes.

### Experimental Setup

We evaluate G-CAST on representative production queries sampled from
four distinct time periods of the deployed application. These periods
define four disjoint demand contexts that reflect evolving product
categories, procurement scenarios, interaction patterns, and capability
requirements. Each context contains a frozen 2,000-query rollout pool
and a disjoint 100-query evaluation slice created before adaptation.
AutoTrainess is evaluated on the same four frozen contexts and the same
cumulative evaluation slices, but follows its own stage-wise training
policy for data selection.

We use Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 as the pretrained model. For each query in
a rollout pool, the accepted checkpoint generates 8 trajectories under
the corresponding harness version, yielding 16,000 trajectories per
context before routing. When a new context is introduced, the accepted
checkpoint is first evaluated on that period’s 100-query slice. Every
subsequent candidate is then evaluated on the cumulative suite of all
active slices, which grows from 100 to 400 queries across the four
periods. This cumulative protocol is used for both G-CAST and
AutoTrainess and matches the deployment requirement: a candidate must
address newly emerging demand without sacrificing previously accepted
capabilities.

To ensure fair comparison, unless explicitly varied by an ablation, all
methods within an experiment use the same parent model, harness version,
candidate universe, teacher targets, and decoding configuration.
Interaction records are deduplicated and normalized while preserving
their source context, tool evidence, and harness provenance. We also
perform data decontamination to prevent evaluation leakage: evaluation
queries are excluded from all training, replay, synthesis, and
reward-development processes, ensuring strict separation between
training and test data.

### Continual Self-Training under Evolving Contracts

We first verify that continual self-training is necessary in this
setting.
Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands")
evaluates the original checkpoint without updating it as the application
evolves from Period 1 to Period 4. To keep the degradation trend
directly comparable across evolving contracts, we report the base reward
in the main text. Under this metric, the checkpoint’s cumulative
performance degrades substantially as the contract expands, falling from
0.9065 to 0.6354 on the cumulative Periods 1–4 evaluation, while its
reward on the newly introduced Period 4 falls to 0.1877. This result
shows that a checkpoint optimized under an earlier contract does not
automatically benefit from later harness expansion. Detailed period-wise
values and full metric breakdowns are deferred to Appendix Table 2.

Against this background,
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands")
compares G-CAST with AutoTrainess under the same cumulative evaluation
protocol. In the first two iterations, G-CAST already achieves strong
gains with substantially less training data. In Iteration 1, G-CAST uses
94 SFT queries and 500 RL queries, compared with 1200 training queries
for AutoTrainess, and improves cumulative reward from 0.9065 to 0.9240.
In Iteration 2, G-CAST again trains on fewer queries (54 SFT + 984 RL
versus 1200) and reaches 0.8835. These early results show that governed
routing can convert a relatively small amount of production evidence
into effective continual self-training.

The more important difference emerges once the capability contract
expands to require explicit skill-dependent behavior. In Iteration 3,
G-CAST improves cumulative reward from 0.8453 at stage entry to 0.9288
after RL, surpassing AutoTrainess at 0.9210. In Iteration 4, under the
fullest contract expansion, G-CAST again achieves the stronger
cumulative result, reaching 0.8078 versus 0.7911 for AutoTrainess. This
crossover is the central result of the continual self-training study:
when the loop must track not only new demand but also a changing
capability contract, governed reward and data evolution become more
important than broad post-training under a fixed objective alone.

The last two columns of
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ G-CAST: Governed Continual Self-Training for E-Commerce Agents under Evolving Demands")
further show that G-CAST delivers a stronger adaptation–retention
profile. In Iteration 4, for example, G-CAST improves new-period reward
from 0.3983 to 0.5813 while also increasing retained reward from 0.8712
to 0.8833. AutoTrainess also improves the new period, from 0.3433 to
0.5428, but its retained reward decreases from 0.8907 to 0.8739. The
advantage of G-CAST is therefore not only higher cumulative reward, but
a more favorable checkpoint transition under cumulative deployment
constraints.

This difference is concentrated in the newly important contract
behaviors. In Iteration 3, G-CAST reaches higher skill-selection
accuracy (0.8667 vs. 0.8433) and higher skill adherence (0.5233 vs.
0.5153). In Iteration 4, where correct skill usage becomes more
consequential, the margin widens further: G-CAST attains 0.7625
skill-selection accuracy versus 0.6875 for AutoTrainess, and 0.4503
skill adherence versus 0.4219. These results are consistent with the
intended role of G-CAST: not to optimize for a single static objective,
but to keep the deployed agent aligned with newly introduced
capabilities while preserving behavior that earlier users already rely
on.

The smaller AutoTrainess training set in Iteration 4 reflects its own
targeted stage objective rather than reduced access to the rollout pool
(Appendix B). Appendix Tables 2 and 3 provide the full retained reward,
new-period reward, cumulative reward, format reward, skill-selection
accuracy, and skill adherence breakdowns across the continual
self-training process.

post-training baselines in how production evidence is converted into
training signal. The next experiment therefore isolates the
data-orchestration component under the same candidate universe and
training budget, evaluating whether reward-based routing improves sample
utility and whether entropy-based ranking provides additional benefit
after routing has determined the learning role of each query.

We evaluate the data-orchestration component with three matched
selection arms. Random applies no reward routing and samples directly
from the common candidate universe. Reward + Random applies the
reward-routing rule from the Method section and samples randomly within
the resulting SFT and RL pools. Reward + Entropy applies the same
routing rule and ranks examples within each pool by the entropy-based
uncertainty score. Random versus Reward + Random isolates the effect of
reward routing, while Reward + Random versus Reward + Entropy isolates
the additional effect of entropy-based prioritization.

Each arm selects 150 SFT queries and 500 RL queries. It first performs
SFT and then continues RL from that arm’s own SFT checkpoint. The arms
match the base model, candidate universe, data budget, SFT targets,
training configuration, and evaluation protocol; only routing and
within-pool selection differ. We report both post-SFT and post-RL
checkpoints, and use a length-matched analysis to test whether entropy
acts merely as a proxy for response length.

reports the post-SFT and post-RL results under three data strategies.
Reward mean reflects overall task performance by averaging
trajectory-level rewards, while format reward measures compliance with
the required output format. For skill compliance, skill-selection
accuracy measures whether the required skill is correctly selected for
each query, and the skill-adherence score evaluates how faithfully the
resulting trajectory follows the prescribed skill execution procedure.

At the SFT stage, Reward + Entropy already leads on task performance,
attaining the highest raw score and format reward together with the
joint-highest skill-selection accuracy. The benefit of routing then
compounds through RL: after RL, task performance and skill-adherence
reward follow a consistent ordering, with Reward + Entropy outperforming
Reward + Random, which in turn outperforms Random. Both routed arms
improve consistently from SFT to RL, whereas Random shows no consistent
improvement, indicating that reward routing provides learning signals
that RL can exploit and that entropy prioritization contributes
additional value within routed pools. Skill-selection accuracy differs
only slightly across the three arms; the principal skill-compliance gain
therefore lies in procedural adherence rather than skill selection.

### Reward Evolution Evaluation

The results in Table 2 and Appendix Tables 2 and 3 show that the
advantage of G-CAST becomes most visible once the capability contract
expands to require explicit skill-dependent behavior, making later
iterations the clearest setting for evaluating the reward-evolution
loop. More generally, however, reward evolution in G-CAST is not only
about tracking contract expansion; it keeps the executable reward
reliable as a discriminator between desirable and undesirable
trajectories under the current deployment setting.

We evaluate reward evolution from two complementary angles. First, we
examine whether reward reliability problems arise in practice and
therefore require governed reward updates rather than a fixed reward.
Second, we test whether the benefit comes from governed reward evolution
itself rather than from merely appending extra reward terms after the
contract expands.

We begin by testing whether reward evolution is necessary once reward
reliability starts to drift. In production, reward errors can appear not
only as low-score failures but also as high-score trajectories that look
plausible while violating important requirements. We therefore audit
sampled high-reward trajectories instead of assuming that high reward
implies correctness. This audit reveals two recurring high-score failure
clusters, with false-positive rates of 84% for search-result misuse and
82% for hallucinated answer entities. In both cases, the agent invokes a
plausible retrieval tool but still produces offer identifiers, URLs,
prices, or phone numbers unsupported by the returned evidence. This
finding motivates a penalty-only grounding component that checks whether
verifiable entities in the final answer are supported by the prompt or
tool responses. Before entering training, the new component must pass
target-case tests, regression checks on known-good trajectories,
frozen-trajectory rescoring, and replay. Reward evolution in G-CAST is
therefore triggered by observed reward reliability failures and
controlled by executable gates, rather than by unchecked reward
expansion.

Second, we test whether the benefit comes from governed reward evolution
rather than from merely adding extra reward terms. Table 4 reports a
three-arm RL diagnostic from one shared SFT checkpoint. All three arms
use the same training data and non-reward hyper-parameters, and differ
only in how the training reward is handled after the capability contract
expands to require explicit skill-dependent behavior. Static performs no
reward evolution: it keeps the pre-expansion reward unchanged, so the
newly introduced skill requirements are not reflected in training.
Ungated applies a one-time contract-aware reward update, but does not
continue evolving or re-admitting the reward through validation gates
afterward. Gated (ours) continues to evolve the reward with the
contract, and each reward update is allowed to affect training only
after passing the registered target-case, regression, rescoring, and
replay gates.

The comparison shows that simply adding more reward terms is not enough.
Static reward establishes the lower bound when the training objective
does not evolve with the contract, reaching 0.8810 overall reward and
0.8801 on the skill-intensive Period 3 evaluation. Ungated contract
reward improves some aggregate metrics, reaching 0.8926 overall reward,
but does not reliably improve the target behavior itself:
skill-selection accuracy rises only slightly to 0.6956 and skill
adherence to 0.4865. In contrast, gated contract-evolved reward reaches
the best overall reward (0.9288), the best Period 3 reward (0.9538), the
best skill-selection accuracy (0.8667), and the best skill-adherence
score (0.5233). Relative to the static reward, the gain is especially
pronounced where the contract expanded most: +0.0478 overall and +0.0737
on Period 3.

This result explains the crossover observed in the main
continual-adaptation study. AutoTrainess is effective at broad
post-training under a fixed objective, which is consistent with its
competitive performance in Iterations 1–2. However, once the harness
begins to require explicit Skill invocation and procedural compliance,
training quality depends on whether the reward reflects the updated
contract. A reward that does not evolve with the contract can still
improve new-period reward to some extent, but it provides weaker
supervision for the newly required behavior. G-CAST instead updates
reward together with the contract, and the gains appear exactly where
the contract changes matter most: skill selection, skill adherence, and
retention under continued adaptation.

Taken together, these studies show that reward evolution is not an
auxiliary implementation detail. It is a necessary part of continual
adaptation under evolving demands. The benefit of G-CAST in later
iterations comes not from using a larger reward, but from using a
governed reward that remains valid for the capabilities the deployed
agent is now expected to use.

## 6 Conclusion and Discussion

##### Operational Impact.

During deployment in Yuancai, G-CAST reduced human reward-audit effort
from about 2 person-day to 30 minutes per round and shortened
demand-to-acceptance turnaround from about one week to two days. Its
promotion and reward gates also rejected 3 candidate checkpoints and 2
proposed reward versions that failed validation, with rejection cases
documented in Appendix E. These indicators quantify the value of
governance itself, separately from the gains of any individual training
run.

##### Limitations.

Our study is based on one longitudinal production application and one
primary model family, so the observed patterns of demand and capability
evolution may not transfer directly to other agent settings.

##### Conclusion.

As deployed e-commerce agents face changing user demands, their
capability contracts also evolve through new tools, skills, and
operating procedures, making continual adaptation a coupled problem of
model mismatch, data mismatch, and reward mismatch rather than repeated
training under a fixed objective. To address this, we introduced G-CAST,
a governed continual self-training system that treats the checkpoint
transition as the unit of adaptation and jointly manages model updates,
reward validity, routed learning evidence, and cumulative evaluation. By
combining reward-routed, entropy-prioritized data orchestration with
capability-contract-driven reward evolution, G-CAST enables the agent to
learn newly introduced behaviors while preserving previously accepted
capabilities. Experiments on a real production e-commerce agent show
that this formulation is both necessary and effective: a fixed
checkpoint degrades substantially as the contract expands, whereas
G-CAST achieves stronger adaptation, better retention, and more reliable
improvement under cumulative deployment constraints.

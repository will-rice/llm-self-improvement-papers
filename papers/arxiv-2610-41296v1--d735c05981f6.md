# Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer

Minju Gwak Affiliation: Georgia Institute of Technology    Alan Ritter
Affiliation: mgwak9@gatech.edu, alan.ritter@cc.gatech.edu

###### Abstract

Answer-level rewards cannot distinguish reasoning traces with equally
correct outcomes. We study whether auxiliary trajectory preferences
improve small-language-model self-improvement and transfer under limited
supervision. We compare confidence-based (SC) and entropy-based metrics
(UID-Conj.), which combines global entropy variation and local
smoothness, through online reward augmentation and offline training on
correct responses. Online experiments adapt Qwen3-1.7B on 30 AIME
problems without reference answers; offline experiments use historical
AIME problems. UID-Conj. improves the online macro-average across the
adaptation benchmark and three transfer benchmarks by 2.08 percentage
points over unmodified TTRL. The strongest evidence comes from offline
weighted SFT: UID-Conj. exceeds uniform weighting on the same response
pool by 1.93 points, with a paired prompt-bootstrap 95% CI of \[0.44,
3.44\]. Benefits are task-dependent: SC rewards judged trace quality and
instruction following, while UID-Conj. rewards yield stronger training
outcomes in several settings. We find that auxiliary preferences can
expose learning value among equally correct traces not captured during
inference time, motivating their evaluation through downstream gains.

## 1 Introduction

Recent advances in reasoning post-training have strengthened small
language models ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib2)), but further improvement under limited supervision
requires making better use of available training problems.
Self-improvement offers one route: models generate reasoning traces and
learn from feedback on their own responses ([Zelikman et al.,
2022](#bib.bib8); [Haluptzok et al., 2023](#bib.bib26); [Yuan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib27)). Answer-level feedback, such as the checkable
outcomes used in reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
(RLVR) ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)),
provides a practical training signal but cannot distinguish responses
with equally correct final answers. Such responses may differ in the
soundness of their reasoning, the completeness of their justification,
and the amount of unnecessary repetition; they may also differ in their
value as training examples. Auxiliary preferences over these traces
could provide additional supervision without requiring new problems or
step-level annotations. We therefore ask: _when answer-level rewards
cannot distinguish successful solutions, can auxiliary trajectory
preferences improve learning and downstream transfer?_

Figure 1: Correct answers leave room for preference. We investigate
whether these trajectory preferences improve learning and downstream
transfer.

We investigate two auxiliary signals derived from the model’s own
predictive token distributions. Self-Certainty (SC) ([Kang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6)) favors trajectories with greater predictive
confidence. The second signal, inspired by uniform information density
(UID) ([Meister et al., 2021](#bib.bib11)), favors trajectories that
combine global entropy variation with local smoothness, following
observations of locally smooth but globally non-uniform information flow
in successful reasoning ([Gwak et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)). These signals
define alternative preferences among responses receiving the same
answer-level reward.

The central hypothesis of our study is that auxiliary preferences
provide useful learning signals beyond answer-level feedback, improving
task performance under limited supervision. To test this hypothesis, we
compare training with and without auxiliary preferences while retaining
the underlying answer-level supervision. Specifically, we adopt
test-time reinforcement learning (TTRL) ([Zuo et al., 2026](#bib.bib14))
for only 2 epoch of training to adapt the model on a small set test time
without reference answers, therefore using only 30 AIME2026 problems.
Within TTRL, we augment majority-agreement rewards with auxiliary
preferences among responses supporting the majority answer, preserving
the reward between agreeing and disagreeing responses.

Results show that auxiliary trajectory preferences can improve
self-improvement from answer-level feedback. After adaptation on just 30
AIME2026 problems without reference answers, TTRL augmented with a Self
Certainty and UID Conjunction metric (UID-Conj.) improve Qwen3-1.7B’s
macro-average accuracy across four mathematics and science benchmarks by
2.03 and 2.08 percentage points, respectively. The benefits extend to
critique-verdict prediction and evidence-conditioned scientific
reasoning. UID-Conj. improves CriticBench verdict accuracy by 1.24
points, incorrect-class F1 by 1.58 points, and LabBench2 accuracy by
0.76 points, without task-specific training. Complementary offline
extensions show that UID-Conj. also improves Qwen3-1.7B’s macro-average
by 1.25 points over random correct–correct DPO preferences and by 1.93
points over uniform SFT. To summarize, our contributions are:

- $`\circ`$
  An independent assessment of whether auxiliary signals distinguish
  reasoning quality among correct responses.
- $`\circ`$
  A controlled study of auxiliary trajectory preferences for small-model
  self-improvement with limited training data, spanning online and
  offline settings with controls matched within each objective.
- $`\circ`$
  An evaluation of transfer from mathematical self-improvement to
  critique and research-level reasoning.

## 2 Related Work

##### Best-of-$`N`$ selection.

Selection methods exploit different properties of sampled reasoning.
Self-consistency aggregates agreement across final answers ([Wang et
al., 2023](#bib.bib5)), whereas Self-Certainty (SC) scores responses
using statistics of predictive distributions ([Kang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6)). The uniform information density hypothesis concerns
how information is distributed across language ([Meister et al.,
2021](#bib.bib11)); its application to LLM reasoning associates
successful trajectories with local smoothness and global
non-uniformity ([Gwak et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)). These findings leave
two questions unresolved: whether the scores identify useful differences
among already-correct responses, and whether reinforcing those improves
generation.

##### Self-improvement with outcome, process, and internal feedback.

Self-improvement methods use feedback on model-generated responses to
refine reasoning. STaR learns from its own rationales that yield correct
answers ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib8)), while DeepSeek models use
RL with outcome feedback ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib16); [Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib2)). Process supervision provides finer-grained feedback
on intermediate steps ([Lightman et al., 2023](#bib.bib7)), while there
have been research on preferring answers by length [Arora and Zanette
(2025)](#bib.bib31). Closest to ours, Intuitor uses SC as the sole
reward in GRPO and reports generalization from mathematics to
out-of-domain tasks ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)), and  [Agarwal et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib30) minimizes entropy during training. This
establishes internal confidence as a self-improvement signal and raises
a specific question: does entropy-trajectory structure add useful
supervision beyond confidence when correctness feedback is available?

##### Transfer beyond verifiable training tasks.

Math datasets with known answers provide accessible supervision for
reasoning models ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](#bib.bib15); [Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib16)), but the intended applications may exceed this
supervision in both difficulty and scope. Easy-to-hard generalization
studies whether supervision from simpler problems can support harder
math reasoning ([Sun et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)), while scientific
question answering exposes the difficulty of evaluating outputs that
require specialized expertise ([Rein et al., 2023](#bib.bib22)). Open
research problems often lack gold answers for outcome-based rewards,
even when proposed solutions can be checked. This gap motivates testing
both transfer to harder math and transfer beyond verified answer
generation.

## 3 Characterizing Trajectory Preferences: Do Auxiliary Signals Add Value beyond the Final Answer?

Before evaluating training utility, we characterize what the auxiliary
signals select. We examine whether they improve answer selection and
which reasoning properties they favor among verifier-correct responses.
These diagnostics describe the preferences induced by each signal. We
compare trajectory-based selection with and without answer aggregation,
and examine changes relative to majority voting, and assess selected
traces with a blinded external judge. Specifically, we ask:

1.  1.  Do auxiliary rewards complement answer-level agreement?
        (Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Auxiliary Rewards with Answer-level Aggregation ‣ 3 Characterizing Trajectory Preferences: Do Auxiliary Signals Add Value beyond the Final Answer? ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer"))
2.  2.  Do auxiliary rewards distinguish reasoning quality at fixed
        final-answer correctness?
        (Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Independent Assessment of Trace Quality ‣ 3 Characterizing Trajectory Preferences: Do Auxiliary Signals Add Value beyond the Final Answer? ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer"))

### 3.1 Experiment Setup

##### Models and rollout configurations.

We evaluate fixed Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B checkpoints with thinking
enabled, temperature 0.7, top-$`p`$ 0.95, and response and context
limits of 8,192 and 16,384 tokens, respectively. We sample eight
responses per prompt and evaluate all selectors on identical nested
pools of size $`n\in\{2,4,8\}`$.

##### Datasets and prompts.

We use AIME2026, HMMT2026, and MinervaMath, for both inference-time
analyses. See
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Prompts ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
for prompts.

##### Selection methods.

We compare three selectors that directly selects among the rollout
batch: Majority Voting, which selects the most frequent final answer.
Self-Certainty (SC), which selects the response with the highest
confidence-based score, UID-Conj, which selects the response with the
highest product of two calibrated global and local scores, favoring
responses that score highly on both. See
Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Selector Metric Calculation ‣ Appendix A Details of Preliminary Diagnostic ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
for how each selector is calculated. We combine each selector mentioned
above with Borda Voting (Borda) ([Dwork et al., 2001](#bib.bib9)), an
answer-level aggregation method that support final answers using
response-rank weights.

We report mean accuracy over all prompts for each of the selectors,
counting missing selections as incorrect.

### 3.2 Auxiliary Rewards with Answer-level Aggregation

Direct selection using auxiliary scores does not reveal how the selected
reasoning traces differ
(Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Evaluation. ‣ 3.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 3 Characterizing Trajectory Preferences: Do Auxiliary Signals Add Value beyond the Final Answer? ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).
We therefore compare trace quality while holding final-answer
correctness fixed. Averaged across rollout budgets, SC and UID-Conj
achieve 25.4% and 25.7% accuracy for Qwen3-1.7B, compared with 25.9% for
majority voting; for Qwen3-4B, the corresponding accuracies are 33.8%
and 34.2%, versus 33.9%. However, incorporating answer-level agreement
through Borda aggregation yields improvements, with UID-Conj + Borda
achieving the highest accuracy for Qwen3-1.7B at 27.0% and SC + Borda
performing best for Qwen3-4B at 34.7%. These gains vary across rollout
budgets and model size
(Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Evaluation. ‣ 3.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 3 Characterizing Trajectory Preferences: Do Auxiliary Signals Add Value beyond the Final Answer? ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).
For Qwen3-1.7B, auxiliary scores with Borda aggregation improve
selection most clearly at smaller budgets. For Qwen3-4B, gains remain
modest on MinervaMath, while results on HMMT and AIME depend on both the
scoring method and rollout budget. Thus, auxiliary rewards provide
complementary signals to answer-level agreement, but their selection
benefits are context-dependent rather than uniform.

### 3.3 Independent Assessment of Trace Quality

The modest accuracy gains in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Auxiliary Rewards with Answer-level Aggregation ‣ 3 Characterizing Trajectory Preferences: Do Auxiliary Signals Add Value beyond the Final Answer? ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
do not substantially establish the effectiveness of the auxiliary
scores. Also, it lacks a qualitative analysis on when which signal is
more useful than the other. We therefore compare trace quality while
holding final-answer correctness fixed. From each eligible candidate
pool, we select verifier-correct responses using SC, UID-Conj, and
random correct selection, then have a judge independently assess their
validity, sufficiency, consistency, and relevance/non-redundancy without
access to selector identity or a reference answer.
Appendix [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Details of Trace Quality Assessment ‣ Appendix A Details of Preliminary Diagnostic ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
details the selection protocol, rubric, and aggregation procedure.

The auxiliary signals favor different aspects of trace quality: SC
performs better on substantive reasoning criteria, including validity

## 4 Training with Auxiliary Trajectory Signals

We mainly investigate auxiliary trajectory signals using post-training
methods for reasoning models in online TTRL ([Zuo et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)), where scores refine majority-vote feedback without
reference answers. We also extend our setup to offline training, where
scores define DPO ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib3)) preferences and
SFT weights among correct responses. Therefore, majority agreement is a
proxy for correctness during online setting, whereas offline
verification fixes final-answer correctness explicitly. These settings
let us examine the usefulness of auxiliary preferences under different
forms of supervision and optimization. Together, these settings test
whether trajectory-level distinctions provide useful supervision beyond
answer-level feedback.

### 4.1 Auxiliary Trajectory Scoring

For each response $`y_{i}`$ to a prompt $`x`$, we compute an auxiliary
score $`q_{i}`$ from token-level predictive statistics. We consider
UID-Conj., which combine global variation and local smoothness of the
entropy trajectory, and SC, summarizing predictive confidence. All three
scores use renormalized top-25 predictive distributions, with larger
scores indicating stronger preference under the corresponding rule.
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1.SSS0.Px1 "Token statistics. ‣ B.1 Auxiliary Trajectory Scoring ‣ Appendix B Details of Online-and-Offline Training ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
details the shared token-statistic extraction and trajectory
construction.

##### Online setting.

We compute detached token statistics from the old policy along each
sampled response. For UID, we partition the entropy trajectory into 32
relative-position bins and normalize the bin entropies by their mean
magnitude. We measure global variation using population variance,
$`G_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}`$, and local smoothness using the negative mean
absolute difference between adjacent bins, $`S_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}`$. Each
feature is mapped to an empirical midrank quantile within the prompt’s
eligible response group:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
u_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}=Q_{x}(G_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}),\qquad v_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}=Q_{x}(S_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}).
``` |  | (1) |

UID-Conj multiplies these quantiles:

|     |                                                                 |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       q_{i}^{\mathrm{Conj,on}}=u_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}v_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}
       ```                                                              |     | (2) |

For SC, we apply the same ranking procedure to its response-token mean.
Ranking is independent of majority agreement: eligible responses
contribute regardless of their final answers.
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Majority-Gated Online RL ‣ 4 Training with Auxiliary Trajectory Signals ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
describes how majority agreement subsequently gates the auxiliary
reward. Exact features and ranking conventions appear in
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1.SSS0.Px2 "Online scoring. ‣ B.1 Auxiliary Trajectory Scoring ‣ Appendix B Details of Online-and-Offline Training ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer"),
with eligibility criteria as well.

##### Offline setting.

We score a fixed response pool using the frozen base model. Offline UID
uses a shared robust entropy scale and a Huber-based smoothness measure,
yielding features $`G_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}`$ and $`S_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}`$.
We transform these features using empirical CDFs fitted on a separate
calibration split without correctness labels:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
u_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}=F_{G}(G_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}),\qquad v_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}=F_{S}(S_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}).
``` |  | (3) |

UID-Conj. apply the same product aggregation rules as in
Equation ([2](#S4.E2 "In Online setting. ‣ 4.1 Auxiliary Trajectory Scoring ‣ 4 Training with Auxiliary Trajectory Signals ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).
SC is calibrated analogously using its response-token mean. Thus, online
and offline scoring share the aggregation rules but differ in their
scoring policies, feature definitions, and normalization. The offline
scorer and calibration remain fixed, and the resulting scores define
preferences or example weights among correct responses
(Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Offline Externsions: Learning from Verified Traces ‣ 4 Training with Auxiliary Trajectory Signals ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1.SSS0.Px3 "Offline scoring. ‣ B.1 Auxiliary Trajectory Scoring ‣ Appendix B Details of Online-and-Offline Training ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
details the offline features and calibration, and the eligibility
criteria.

### 4.2 Majority-Gated Online RL

Following TTRL ([Zuo et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)), we derive answer-level
supervision from agreement among sampled responses without using
reference answers. For each prompt $`x`$, let $`\hat{a}_{x}`$ denote the
most frequent extracted answer. We define

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}[a(y_{i})\simeq\hat{a}_{x}],\quad\mathcal{M}_{x}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\{i:g_{i}=1\},`$ |  | (4) |

where $`\simeq`$ denotes agreement under the answer-matching procedure.
Majority agreement provides a pseudo-label rather than a guarantee of
correctness, and auxiliary scores do not affect the vote. Responses in
$`\mathcal{M}_{x}`$ receive the same binary answer reward, motivating an
auxiliary signal that distinguishes their trajectories.

Using the scores from
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Auxiliary Trajectory Scoring ‣ 4 Training with Auxiliary Trajectory Signals ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer"),
we construct the reward

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle R_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=2g_{i}-1+\lambda b_{i},`$ |  | (5) |
|  | $`\displaystyle b_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\operatorname{clip}(q_{i}-\bar{q}_{x},-\delta,\delta),&i\in\mathcal{M}_{x},\ |\mathcal{M}_{x}|\geq 2,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}`$ |  |  |

where
$`\bar{q}_{x}=|\mathcal{M}_{x}|^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{x}}q_{j}`$ is
computed when $`|\mathcal{M}_{x}|\geq 2`$. Centering expresses
preference relative to other majority-consistent responses, while gating
restricts the adjustment to that group. We use $`\lambda=0.1`$ and
$`\delta=0.5`$, bounding the auxiliary adjustment by $`0.05`$ and
preserving the reward ordering between majority-consistent and
inconsistent responses. Groups with fewer than two majority-consistent
responses receive no auxiliary adjustment.

We compute GRPO advantages by normalizing the combined rewards within
each prompt group and update the policy using a clipped policy objective
with a separate reference KL penalty. Auxiliary scores and advantages
are detached. The same reward construction applies to UID-Conj. and SC.
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Majority-Gated Online Optimization ‣ Appendix B Details of Online-and-Offline Training ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
provides the voting, advantage, and optimization details. The adjustment
preserves answer-reward ordering but can produce nonzero advantages in
unanimous groups, where $`\lambda`$ approximately cancels under
normalization.
Appendix [B.2.1](#A2.SS2.SSS1 "B.2.1 Effect of Auxiliary Rewards under Advantage Normalization ‣ B.2 Majority-Gated Online Optimization ‣ Appendix B Details of Online-and-Offline Training ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
derives this effect and discusses implications for the random control.

### 4.3 Offline Externsions: Learning from Verified Traces

We extend auxiliary trajectory scoring to offline training using a fixed
response pool scored by the frozen base model. Both extensions retain
complete, nontruncated, correct, score-eligible responses, allowing
auxiliary scores to differentiate trajectories whose final answers are
already correct.

##### Auxiliary-Score Preferences for DPO.

For each prompt $`x`$, let $`\mathcal{C}_{x}`$ denote its eligible
correct responses. We construct a preference pair by selecting the
highest- and lowest-scoring responses:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
y_{x}^{+}=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{C}_{x}}q(x,y),\qquad y_{x}^{-}=\arg\min_{y\in\mathcal{C}_{x}}q(x,y).
``` |  | (6) |

Both responses are correct, so the preference reflects the auxiliary
score rather than a difference in answer correctness. We require a
minimum score gap and retain the common set of qualifying prompts across
scoring methods. The resulting pairs are used with the standard DPO
objective ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib3)). As a control, we
randomly select and orient two distinct correct responses for each
prompt in the same set.
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Offline Training ‣ Appendix B Details of Online-and-Offline Training ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")
provides the pair-selection and initialization details.

##### Auxiliary-Weighted SFT.

We retain all eligible correct responses and use auxiliary scores to
weight their contributions to supervised training. For a prompt with
$`m_{x}=|\mathcal{C}_{x}|`$ responses, we assign

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
w_{i}=m_{x}\frac{\exp(\alpha q_{i})}{\sum_{y_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{x}}\exp(\alpha q_{j})}.
``` |  | (7) |

These weights have mean one within each prompt, preserving its total
example weight while emphasizing higher-scoring responses. We minimize

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WSFT}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{w_{i}}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x_{i},y_{i,<t}),
``` |  | (8) |

where $`N`$ is the number of training responses and $`T_{i}`$ is the
response length. Uniform SFT uses $`w_{i}=1`$ on the same examples and
with the same response-level loss normalization. This comparison
isolates the effect of auxiliary weighting while holding the training
responses fixed.

##### Models, training data, and benchmarks.

We use Qwen3-1.7B as base model, and additionally test on Qwen3-4B as
well. Trainings are done for only 2 epochs for RL and 1 epoch for
offline training. For online training, we perform test-time adaptation
on AIME2026 without reference answers. For offline extensions, we use
historical AIME problems gathered until 2025. We evaluate mathematical
reasoning on AIME2026, HMMT2026, and MinervaMath, and science reasoning
on GPQA’s Diamond subset for verifiable tasks, and use CriticBench and
LabBench2 for research-level reasoning. We also further evaluate on
instruction following tasks to explore the different effects of
different auxiliary rewards.

##### Comparison methods.

We compare UID-Conj. and SC within each training objective. Online
comparisons include the base model, original TTRL, and a random
auxiliary-score control. For DPO, we compare auxiliary-score preferences
against randomly selected and oriented correct vs. correct pairs. For
weighted SFT, uniform weighting provides the control on the same correct
response pool.

##### Evaluation.

For verifiable mathematical benchmarks, we report mean accuracy across
five sampled responses per question. For CriticBench, we report verdict
accuracy and incorrect-class F1, which assess verdict correctness rather
than critique quality. For IFBench, we report prompt-level loose
accuracy using the official verifiers. For IHEval, we use the official
task-specific metrics and aggregation procedure, reporting results
separately for reference, aligned, and conflicting instruction settings.
We document dataset sizes, subset selection, decoding configurations,
and training hyperparameters in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Details of Experiment Setup ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer").
Our experiments address three research questions:

1.  1.
    Online effectiveness: Do auxiliary trajectory scores improve
    majority-gated test-time adaptation and its transfer to other
    mathematical benchmarks?
    (Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer"))
2.  2.
    Offline effectiveness: Do auxiliary scores provide useful
    preferences and example weights among correct responses for DPO and
    SFT?
    (Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer"))
3.  3.
    Transfer beyond verifiable tasks: Do models trained with auxiliary

    (Table [5](#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer"))

### 5.2 Results

##### Online effectiveness.

As seen in
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer"),
augmenting TTRL with auxiliary rewards improves online adaptation in
general. Both SC and UID-Conj. outperform unmodified TTRL on all four
benchmarks, increasing macro-average accuracy from 25.51% to 27.54% and
27.59%, respectively. UID-Conj. achieves the highest accuracy on
AIME2026, MinervaMath, and GPQA-D, while SC performs best on HMMT2026.
Qwen3-4B results reveal a trade-off between target-benchmark gains and
downstream transfer. Unmodified TTRL increases AIME2026 accuracy from
55.33% to 60.00% but reduces performance on HMMT2026 and MinervaMath, a
pattern consistent with over-specialization to the adaptation set.
UID-Conj. instead preserves base-model AIME2026 accuracy while improving
HMMT2026 and MinervaMath to 13.94% and 31.40%. Thus, although auxiliary
rewards do not uniformly improve target-benchmark accuracy, UID-Conj.
can mitigate the transfer degradation associated with test-time
adaptation.

##### Offline extension.

UID-Conj. improves aggregate performance under both offline training
objectives
(Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")),
achieving macro-average accuracies of 29.03% with DPO and 29.49% with
weighted SFT, exceeding their respective controls by 1.25 and 1.93
percentage points. UID-Conj. DPO performs best on HMMT2026, MinervaMath,
and GPQA-D, although Random-DPO slightly outperforms it on AIME2026
(38.00% versus 37.33%). Similarly, UID-Conj. SFT leads on three
benchmarks, while uniform SFT retains the highest MinervaMath accuracy
(26.40% versus 26.10%). SC yields less consistent gains, outperforming
uniform SFT in aggregate but falling below Random-DPO. These results
show that auxiliary preferences among correct responses can improve
training outcomes, while the relative effectiveness of SC and UID-Conj
differs from their ordering in the trace-quality diagnostic. This
contrast is observed across separate diagnostic and training cohorts; it
does not establish that lower judge-rated traces are individually more
useful for learning. Explanations are further discussed in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Discussion on Differing Behaviors of Auxiliary Rewards ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer").

##### Evaluation on task transfer.

UID-Conj. transfers gains from mathematical training to critique-verdict
prediction without task-specific supervision
(Table [5](#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 5 Experiment ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).
It achieves the highest CriticBench F1 and accuracy at 63.23% and
68.00%, respectively, and improves both metrics over the base model.
Their average reaches 65.62%, exceeding the base model by 0.36
percentage points, unmodified TTRL by 1.41 points, and SC by 0.93
points. This result extends UID-Conj’s training utility beyond math
answer generation, despite SC’s stronger trace-quality results in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Independent Assessment of Trace Quality ‣ 3 Characterizing Trajectory Preferences: Do Auxiliary Signals Add Value beyond the Final Answer? ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer").
Transfer remains task-dependent, however: SC achieves a higher
instruction-following aggregate than UID-Conj (39.16% versus 38.51%). SC
averages $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(U_{25}\|p_{t})`$ over response tokens,
rewarding departure from uniformity in the predictive distribution and
thereby favoring answers that they are more familiar with. UID-Conj.
instead captures global entropy variation and local smoothness. These
distinct preferences are consistent with the for SC’s higher
instruction-following aggregate and UID-Conj’s stronger critique-verdict
results in our experiments.

## 7 Conclusion

Correct answers do not exhaust the learning signal in model-generated
reasoning. Our results show that auxiliary trajectory preferences can
extract additional training value from existing responses under limited
supervision, with the clearest evidence coming from UID-Conj-weighted
SFT on verifier-correct traces. They also reveal a distinction between
judged trace quality and training utility: SC selects higher-rated
traces, while UID-Conj yields larger gains in our offline training
comparisons and selected transfer tasks. These findings motivate
evaluating trajectory preferences by the learning outcomes they support,
alongside their ability to identify high-quality reasoning.

## Limitations

Our experiments focus on Qwen3-1.7B, with additional online evaluation
on Qwen3-4B, under limited mathematical supervision. Online AIME2026
results measure adaptation on training problems, while the remaining
benchmarks evaluate transfer. Our online comparisons do not fully
distinguish the benefit of trajectory-specific preferences from the
effect of introducing reward variation into unanimous groups. To address
this, we perform an experiment with a control that permutes the realized
auxiliary terms within majority-agreeing groups would more closely
isolate their association with individual trajectories
(Appendix [B.2.1](#A2.SS2.SSS1 "B.2.1 Effect of Auxiliary Rewards under Advantage Normalization ‣ B.2 Majority-Gated Online Optimization ‣ Appendix B Details of Online-and-Offline Training ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).

In addition, the trace-quality diagnostic currently uses one model judge
on verifier-correct responses. Transfer evaluation measures
critique-verdict accuracy, evidence-conditioned scientific responses,
and programmatically verified instruction following, rather than
unrestricted open-ended reasoning. Broader response cohorts and human
validation would extend this assessment. In particular, the observed
difference between trace-selection quality and training utility
motivates developing auxiliary preferences that predict a response’s
contribution to learning.

## Broader Impact and Ethical Implications

Improving compact models from limited training data may make reasoning
systems more accessible in settings where larger models or extensive
supervision are impractical. Auxiliary signals derived from predictive
distributions offer a way to reuse model-generated responses without
requiring a separately trained reward model or step-level training
annotations. However, generating and scoring multiple trajectories still
incurs computational costs, and our experiments do not establish net
energy or cost savings.

Self-improvement can also reinforce existing errors: majority agreement
may reflect shared misconceptions, while predictive confidence and
smooth entropy trajectories do not guarantee factual or logical
validity. The observed gains in critique and scientific benchmarks
should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of dependable autonomous
scientific judgment. Moreover, our ultimate goal is to extend reasoning
improvements to tasks where reliable answer-level supervision is
unavailable, such as research-level mathematics ([Son et al.,
2026](#bib.bib28); [Glazer et al., 2024](#bib.bib29)). Therefore,
further investigation should be done on open-ended reasoning with
human-expert verification. Applications should evaluate these properties
separately, retain independent verification where available, and use
expert oversight when incorrect outputs could have consequential
effects.

## Appendix A Details of Preliminary Diagnostic

### A.1 Selector Metric Calculation

For each question and model, selectors operate on nested prefixes of
$`N\in\{2,4,8\}`$ generated responses. Final answers are extracted from
the last \boxed{} expression and normalized before answer aggregation.
Correctness is evaluated after selection by matching the normalized
answer against the normalized reference. Abstentions, incomplete
responses, and truncated responses count as incorrect. Reference-answer
correctness is not used by the answer-accuracy selectors.

##### Majority Voting.

Let $`a_{i}`$ denote the normalized answer extracted from response
$`i`$. Among complete responses with an extractable answer, majority
voting selects

|     |                                                                    |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       a^{\star}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a}\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}[a_{i}=a].
       ```                                                                 |     |

Repeated samples contribute separate votes, even when their response
texts are identical. Ties are resolved by the earliest occurrence in
generation order, and the earliest response supporting the winning
answer is retained as its representative. If no answer can be extracted
from any complete response, the selector abstains.

##### Self-Certainty.

We mainly follow the setup used in  ([Kang et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)). We
rescore each generated response under its frozen generating model, using
the raw next-token logits without rollout-temperature scaling. Only
response-token positions are scored; special tokens and chat control
tokens, including thinking delimiters, are excluded. However, unlike the
original paper, which computes SC over the full vocabulary, we compute
it over the top-$`25`$ highest-logit tokens at each position for
efficiency, renormalizing their probabilities and using a uniform
reference distribution over this restricted support. At each retained
position $`t`$, let $`\mathcal{K}_{t}`$ contain the $`K=25`$
highest-logit tokens and define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{t,j}=\frac{\exp(z_{t,j})}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}\exp(z_{t,k})},\qquad j\in\mathcal{K}_{t}.
``` |  |

The response-level Self-Certainty score is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{SC}(y)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(U_{K}\,\|\,p_{t}\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}\log p_{t,j}-\log K\right].`$ |  |

where $`T`$ is the number of retained response tokens and $`U_{K}`$ is
uniform over the top-$`K`$ support. Thus, the implemented score uses a
renormalized top-25 distribution rather than the full vocabulary.

Scores are mapped to calibration empirical-CDF midranks. For calibration
values $`\{x_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}`$, we use

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{F}(x)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(\mathbf{1}[x_{m}<x]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}[x_{m}=x]\right).
``` |  |

The SC selector ranks responses by
$`q_{\mathrm{SC}}(y)=\widehat{F}_{\mathrm{SC}}(\mathrm{SC}(y))`$. For
comparability with UID, eligible candidates must be complete,
non-truncated, and contain at least 32 scored tokens. The direct
selector returns the highest-ranked eligible response, breaking score
ties by generation order.

For the Borda-added variant, eligible responses are ordered by
decreasing $`q_{\mathrm{SC}}`$. A response at one-based rank $`r`$ among
$`m`$ eligible responses receives weight $`\sqrt{m-r+1}`$. We sum these
weights by normalized answer and select the answer with the largest
total. Responses without extractable answers contribute no answer
weight, although they remain in the ranking. Tied answer totals are
resolved by their first occurrence in ranked order; the highest-ranked
response supporting the winning answer is retained.

##### UID-Conj.

UID-Conj uses the token entropies of the same top-25 distributions,

|     |                                                       |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       H_{t}=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}p_{t,j}\log p_{t,j}.
       ```                                                    |     |

We partition the $`T`$ retained positions into $`B=32`$ contiguous
relative-position bins, with boundaries $`b_{k}=\lfloor kT/B\rfloor`$,
and average entropy within each bin:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h_{k}=\frac{1}{b_{k}-b_{k-1}}\sum_{t=b_{k-1}+1}^{b_{k}}H_{t},\qquad k=1,\ldots,B.
``` |  |

A model-specific robust scale is fitted using the pooled bin entropies
$`\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cal}}`$ of eligible calibration responses:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s=\max\!\Bigl\{`$ | $`\displaystyle 1.4826\,\operatorname{median}_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cal}}}\left|h-\operatorname{median}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cal}})\right|,`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle 10^{-3}\Bigr\}.`$ |  |

The global-variation and local-smoothness features are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle G(y)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k=1}^{B}\left(\frac{h_{k}-\bar{h}}{s}\right)^{2},`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle S(y)`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{k=1}^{B-1}\rho\!\left(\frac{h_{k+1}-h_{k}}{s}\right).`$ |  |

where $`\bar{h}=B^{-1}\sum_{k}h_{k}`$ and

|     |                                                     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \rho(u)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}u^{2},&|u|\leq 1,\\
       |u|-\frac{1}{2},&|u|>1\end{cases}
       ```                                                  |     |

is the unit-threshold Huber loss. Larger $`G`$ corresponds to greater
global entropy variation, whereas larger $`S`$ corresponds to smoother
local changes. In particular, the implemented global feature does not
reward globally constant entropy.

Using separate calibration midrank functions for these features, the
conjunction score is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{\mathrm{UID\text{-}Conj}}(y)=\widehat{F}_{G}(G(y))\,\widehat{F}_{S}(S(y)).
``` |  |

The robust scale and empirical distributions are fitted only on
calibration responses, without correctness or judge labels, and are
subsequently frozen. Calibration/evaluation prompt overlap is checked.
Direct UID-Conj selection and its Borda-added variant use the same
eligibility, tie-breaking, and square-root rank-weighting rules as SC,
with $`q_{\mathrm{UID\text{-}Conj}}`$ replacing $`q_{\mathrm{SC}}`$.

### A.2 Details of Trace Quality Assessment

The trace-quality diagnostic asks whether auxiliary scores distinguish
reasoning quality among responses with correct final answers. It uses
the saved responses and selector outputs from the answer-correctness
experiment. The reported analysis considers Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B on
MinervaMath, HMMT 2026, and AIME 2026; MinervaMath’s calibration and
development questions are combined. Consequently, this analysis is
conditional on the frozen response pools and is not an exclusively
held-out evaluation.

##### Stage1.

We construct comparison cohorts and blinded assessment requests. For
each model, question, and rollout budget $`N\in\{2,4,8\}`$, a pool
qualifies only if it contains at least two distinct response texts that
are complete, non-truncated, UID-eligible, and verifier-correct. The
main, correct-only diagnostic selects the highest-ranked SC and UID-Conj
responses within these correct candidates. Its random-correct baseline
is one uniform draw over distinct eligible correct response texts. The
same baseline draw is reused across contrasts.

We construct three contrasts: UID-Conj versus random correct, SC versus
random correct, and UID-Conj versus SC. A separate supporting study uses
the exact SC and UID-Conj Borda representatives selected from the full
candidate pool. Pool eligibility alone does not ensure that these Borda
representatives are correct; their correctness is checked during the
final analysis.

Each judge request contains only the problem and one full response,
together with the fixed rubric and output schema. Reference answers,
model and selector identities, competing responses, and comparison
context are withheld. Identical assessment inputs are deduplicated and
reuse one assessment across comparisons. Before submission, the pipeline
counts input tokens, checks request-size limits and the configured
maximum-cost budget, and freezes request identities and provenance.

##### Stage2.

Each unique response is assessed independently using
gpt-6-luna ([OpenAI, 2026](#bib.bib21)) with high reasoning effort and a
maximum output budget of 12,000 tokens. The recorded run uses concurrent
standard Responses requests. The judge returns a structured assessment
containing an integer score from 1 to 5, supporting evidence, and an
explanation for each of four equally weighted criteria:

- $`\circ`$
  Validity: whether the written argument is logically and mathematically
  sound.
- $`\circ`$
  Sufficiency: whether the response supplies the justification necessary
  to support its conclusion.
- $`\circ`$
  Consistency: whether the reasoning contains unresolved internal
  contradictions.
- $`\circ`$
  Relevance/non-redundancy: whether the reasoning serves the solution
  without unnecessary detours or repetition.

Scores range from severe deficiencies at 1 to no supported deficiency at
5. The rubric instructs the judge to evaluate the reasoning actually
written, without repairing it or inferring correctness from confident
style. Routine calculations need not be expanded; explicitly corrected
false starts are not treated as contradictions; and length alone is not
a defect. Issues are documented in the explanations without additional
numerical penalties. If a criterion cannot be assessed reliably, the
judge returns null with an explanation rather than assigning a low
score.

##### Stage3.

We validate request coverage and assessment schemas, then derive
comparisons from the independent scores. For an assessable response
$`y`$, the total score is

|     |                                    |     |
|-----|------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       Q(y)=V(y)+F(y)+C(y)+R(y)\in[4,20],
       ```                                 |     |

where $`V,F,C,R`$ denote validity, sufficiency, consistency, and
relevance/non-redundancy, respectively. We additionally compute the
substantive subtotal

|     |                                             |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       Q_{\mathrm{sub}}(y)=V(y)+F(y)+C(y)\in[3,15]
       ```                                          |     |

to distinguish substantive reasoning differences from relevance or
repetition effects.

A response wins when its total exceeds that of its comparator. Equal
totals for different responses are recorded as score ties; identical
assessment inputs are retained as a separate identical-response tie
category. These outcomes are derived from independently assigned scores,
rather than direct pairwise choices by the judge. Missing, invalid, or
partially unassessable assessments are excluded from scored estimates
and reported separately, never counted as losses.

For the primary analysis, all three contrasts must be assessable and
both responses in every contrast must be verifier-correct within each
study/model/question/budget combination. This yields a common
UID-Conj/SC/random cohort within each budget. Correct-only ranking is
the main analysis; full-pool Borda with subsequent both-correct
filtering is reported separately because its qualifying cohort can
differ.

We report win, loss, and both tie rates; net preference
$`P(\mathrm{win})-P(\mathrm{loss})`$; and paired differences in total,
substantive, and individual-criterion scores. Pooled estimates first
average over the available eligible budgets within each question and
then weight questions equally. Repeated budgets therefore do not count
as independent questions, and benchmarks contribute according to their
eligible question counts. Numeric uncertainty estimates use 4,000
question-level bootstrap resamples and 95% percentile intervals. The
figures and displayed tables show point estimates. These comparisons
describe the saved judge run and fixed random baseline; they do not
establish agreement across judges or benefits from using the scores as
training rewards.

## Appendix B Details of Online-and-Offline Training

### B.1 Auxiliary Trajectory Scoring

##### Token statistics.

At each valid response position $`t`$, we renormalize the scoring
model’s top-$`K`$ logits, with $`K=25`$, to obtain $`\tilde{p}_{t}`$. We
compute entropy and Self-Certainty as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle H_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\sum_{v=1}^{K}\tilde{p}_{t}(v)\log\tilde{p}_{t}(v),`$ |  | (9) |
|  | $`\displaystyle C_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{v=1}^{K}\log\tilde{p}_{t}(v)-\log K.`$ |  |  |

These statistics use logits before sampling-temperature scaling and
exclude padding and special tokens. Self-Certainty is therefore a top-25
approximation. We average $`C_{t}`$ over valid response tokens and
partition the entropy trajectory into $`B=32`$ relative-position bins,
whose mean entropies are denoted by $`h_{i,b}`$.

##### Online scoring.

Let

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bar{h}_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}h_{i,b},`$ |  | (10) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{i,b}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{h_{i,b}}{\max(|\bar{h}_{i}|,10^{-8})}.`$ |  |  |

The online UID features are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle G_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}_{b}(\tilde{h}_{i,b}),`$ |  | (11) |
|  | $`\displaystyle S_{i}^{\mathrm{on}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B-1}\left|\tilde{h}_{i,b+1}-\tilde{h}_{i,b}\right|,`$ |  |  |

where variance uses the population convention. For the eligible response
pool $`\mathcal{E}_{x}`$, the midrank transform is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle Q_{x}(z)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{x}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{E}_{x}}\left(\mathbf{1}[z_{j}<z]+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}[z_{j}=z]\right).`$ |  | (12) |

UID-Conjunction combine the feature quantiles by multiplication.
Self-Certainty applies $`Q_{x}`$ to the response-token mean of
$`C_{t}`$. All statistics are detached.

##### Offline scoring.

We fit a shared robust scale

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max\left(1.4826\,\operatorname{MAD}(\mathcal{H}),10^{-3}\right),`$ |  | (13) |

where $`\mathcal{H}`$ contains the bin entropies of eligible calibration
responses. The offline features are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle G_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\frac{h_{i,b}-\bar{h}_{i}}{s}\right)^{2},`$ |  | (14) |
|  | $`\displaystyle S_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B-1}\rho_{1}\left(\frac{h_{i,b+1}-h_{i,b}}{s}\right),`$ |  |  |

where

|     |                                                                |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \rho_{1}(d)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{d^{2}}{2},&|d|\leq 1,\\[4.0pt]
       |d|-\dfrac{1}{2},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
       ```                                                             |     | (15) |

Each feature and response-level Self-Certainty is mapped through an
empirical CDF using the same midpoint convention as $`Q_{x}`$.
Calibration uses a shared UID-eligible subset, without correctness
filtering, from prompts disjoint from training and development. The
scoring model, scale, and CDFs remain fixed.

##### Eligibility.

Both UID variants require at least 32 valid scoring tokens; online
Self-Certainty requires at least one. Online ineligible responses
receive score zero and are excluded from ranking, but still enter the
gate-positive score mean if they agree with the majority answer. Offline
calibration requires complete, nontruncated, UID-eligible responses;
offline training additionally requires verifier correctness and finite
scores for all compared methods.

### B.2 Majority-Gated Online Optimization

We follow TTRL’s answer extraction and matching procedure. The most
frequent extracted answer supplies the pseudo-label, with voting ties
resolved by first occurrence. Auxiliary scores do not influence this
selection.

For each prompt group of $`n>1`$ responses, we normalize the combined
rewards from
Equation ([5](#S4.E5 "In 4.2 Majority-Gated Online RL ‣ 4 Training with Auxiliary Trajectory Signals ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{i}=\frac{R_{i}-\bar{R}_{x}}{\operatorname{Std}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})+10^{-6}},\quad\bar{R}_{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}R_{j},
``` |  | (16) |

where $`\operatorname{Std}`$ denotes the sample standard deviation. The
detached sequence advantage is shared across response tokens. The
original TTRL control uses binary $`0/1`$ rewards, while auxiliary arms
use the $`-1/+1`$ answer reward plus the gated adjustment.

We retain the implementation’s clipped GRPO policy update, with clipping
threshold $`0.2`$, dual-clipping parameter $`3`$, and a separate
reference KL penalty with coefficient $`10^{-3}`$. KL regularization
does not enter the rewards used for advantage normalization.

#### B.2.1 Effect of Auxiliary Rewards under Advantage Normalization

The bounded auxiliary adjustment preserves the ordering of answer-level
rewards, but does not necessarily imply a small change in the normalized
advantages. To distinguish these properties, write the online reward as

|     |                               |     |
|-----|-------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{i}=2g_{i}-1+\lambda b_{i},
       ```                            |     |

where $`g_{i}`$ indicates majority agreement and $`b_{i}`$ is the
majority-gated, centered, and clipped auxiliary term. With
$`\lambda=0.1`$ and $`|b_{i}|\leq 0.5`$, every majority-agreeing
response receives a reward of at least $`0.95`$, whereas every
disagreeing response receives $`-1`$. Thus, the auxiliary term cannot
reverse the reward ordering between these two groups. However, GRPO
normalizes the combined rewards within each prompt group, so this bound
does not directly bound the resulting change in advantages.

##### Unanimous groups.

Consider a group of $`n>1`$ responses in which every response agrees
with the majority answer, so that $`g_{i}=1`$ for all $`i`$. Define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{b}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i},\qquad s_{b}=\operatorname{Std}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}),
``` |  |

using the same sample-standard-deviation convention as the training
implementation. The mean reward is $`1+\lambda\bar{b}`$, and, for
$`\lambda>0`$, its standard deviation is $`\lambda s_{b}`$.
Consequently, the normalized advantage is exactly

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{i}=\frac{\lambda(b_{i}-\bar{b})}{\lambda s_{b}+\epsilon}=\frac{b_{i}-\bar{b}}{s_{b}+\epsilon/\lambda},
``` |  | (17) |

where $`\epsilon=10^{-6}`$ is the numerical stabilizer. Here,
$`\bar{b}`$ is retained explicitly because clipping after centering need
not preserve a zero mean.

When $`\lambda s_{b}\gg\epsilon`$, the auxiliary coefficient
approximately cancels:

|     |                                          |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{i}\approx\frac{b_{i}-\bar{b}}{s_{b}}.
       ```                                       |     | (18) |

Thus, nonconstant auxiliary rewards can yield normalized advantages with
approximately unit sample standard deviation, despite their small
absolute reward scale. Under original TTRL, a unanimous group instead
has constant answer rewards and therefore zero reward-based advantages.
Auxiliary rewards can consequently activate a policy-gradient signal for
groups that provide no such signal under the answer-only objective. If
all auxiliary terms are identical, the advantages remain zero. Unanimity
also does not imply correctness: all sampled responses may agree on an
incorrect answer.

##### Mixed-agreement groups.

When both agreeing and disagreeing responses are present, the answer
reward itself varies. Let $`\bar{g}=n^{-1}\sum_{i}g_{i}`$. The centered
reward becomes

|     |                                                        |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{i}-\bar{R}=2(g_{i}-\bar{g})+\lambda(b_{i}-\bar{b}).
       ```                                                     |     |

In this case, $`\lambda`$ generally does not cancel, because the
denominator depends jointly on answer-reward variation and auxiliary
variation. The influence of the auxiliary term therefore depends on the
group’s agreement pattern and score distribution. Reward-order
preservation alone does not characterize this influence. Moreover,
normalized advantages do not directly determine parameter-update
magnitudes, which also depend on policy gradients, importance ratios,
clipping, and the separate KL penalty.

##### Interpretation of controls and further diagnostics.

The random auxiliary-score control tests whether adding uninformative
within-group reward variation can account for the observed gains.
However, independent uniform scores do not exactly preserve the realized
score distribution of UID-Conj, including its ties and dispersion.

A more closely matched control would first compute the auxiliary terms
normally and then randomly permute them among majority-agreeing
responses within each prompt group. For a permutation $`\pi`$ of the
majority-agreeing set $`\mathcal{M}_{x}`$, define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
b_{i}^{\mathrm{perm}}=\begin{cases}b_{\pi(i)},&i\in\mathcal{M}_{x},\\
0,&i\notin\mathcal{M}_{x}.\end{cases}
``` |  |

This preserves the group’s reward multiset, mean, standard deviation,
and normalized-advantage distribution, while randomizing the association
between auxiliary preferences and response trajectories.

### B.3 Offline Training

##### Preference construction and initialization.

We deduplicate identical response strings within each prompt and select
one highest-versus-lowest score pair, requiring a score gap of at least
$`0.05`$. We retain only prompts that satisfy this condition for every
auxiliary scoring method. The random control samples two distinct
correct responses from each retained prompt and assigns their
orientation randomly. All DPO arms initialize from a shared SFT
checkpoint trained on the union of responses in the training preference
pairs; development responses are excluded. This checkpoint also supplies
the frozen reference policy. We use DPO coefficient $`\beta=0.1`$.

##### SFT weighting and matched data.

We use $`\alpha=2`$ in
Equation ([7](#S4.E7 "In Auxiliary-Weighted SFT. ‣ 4.3 Offline Externsions: Learning from Verified Traces ‣ 4 Training with Auxiliary Trajectory Signals ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).
Uniform, Self-Certainty, and UID-Conjunction SFT use the same eligible
responses. Prompt and padding tokens are masked, and weighted
response-mean losses are averaged over examples across gradient
accumulation and data-parallel workers. Weights are not renormalized
within microbatches. Data matching applies within each objective: DPO
shares a prompt set across arms, while SFT shares a response pool. The
DPO and SFT training subsets need not coincide.

### C.1 Details of the Training Dataset

##### Online setup.

Our current online experiments use test-time reinforcement learning
(TTRL), starting from Qwen3-1.7B. For AIME2026, all 30 problems are used
for adaptation and subsequent evaluation. The Math500 configuration
similarly uses all 500 problems for both stages. Thus, these experiments
measure adaptation on the target benchmark rather than generalization to
an unseen test set. Reference answers are retained for evaluation and
diagnostics; training rewards are computed from agreement with
majority-vote pseudo-labels obtained from model-generated responses.

For each prompt, the default configuration generates eight responses for
majority voting and uses all eight for training. We compare original
TTRL, a random-preference control, Self-Certainty (SC), and UID-based
preferences. The auxiliary preferences do not determine the
majority-vote answer. Instead, they modify rewards among responses that
agree with that answer.

##### Offline setup.

Offline data are constructed from a shared response pool generated by
Qwen3-1.7B on historical AIME problems. After exact deduplication of
prompt message lists, the current pool contains 882 prompts, partitioned
into 88 calibration prompts, 88 development prompts, and 706 training
prompts. These partitions are disjoint at the prompt level. Eight
candidate responses are generated per prompt with temperature 0.7,
top-$`p`$ 0.95, a maximum response length of 16,384 tokens, and a
maximum total sequence length of 18,432 tokens.

Calibration uses only the calibration partition and does not use
correctness labels. SC and UID scores are computed under the frozen base
checkpoint using renormalized top-25 token distributions. Consequently,
the implemented SC score is a top-25 approximation rather than
full-vocabulary Self-Certainty. UID-Mix averages the calibrated global
and local component scores, whereas UID-Conjunction multiplies them.

Training examples must be verifier-correct, complete, non-truncated, and
eligible for UID scoring. Duplicate response strings within a prompt are
removed. The weighted-SFT datasets contain 2,967 training responses and
355 development responses per method, with identical examples across
methods. The uniform baseline assigns weight one. For a prompt with
$`m`$ eligible responses, the other methods assign

|     |                                                         |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math

       ```                                                      |     |

where $`q_{i}`$ is the corresponding calibrated preference score. This
normalization preserves a mean weight of one within each prompt. The
implementation labels the uniform baseline as Random-SFT.

For DPO, each score-based method selects the highest- and lowest-scoring
eligible responses for a prompt. Both responses are verifier-correct. We
retain only the common set of prompts for which every score-based method
has a preference gap of at least 0.05. This yields 413 training pairs
and 53 development pairs per method. Random-DPO samples and randomly
orients two eligible responses on the same prompt set.

All DPO methods share an SFT initialization constructed from the
deduplicated union of the chosen and rejected responses across the four
DPO datasets. This union contains 1,875 unique training responses and
226 development responses. The training file repeats 13 responses to
obtain 1,888 rows, a multiple of the global SFT batch size. The
resulting merged SFT checkpoint initializes both the DPO policy and its
frozen reference model.

### C.3 Hyperparameters and GPUs for Training

##### GPU and setups.

We use 4xA100 GPUs for training, and 1xA40 GPU for inference. We use
veRL ([Sheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)) for TTRL training, and
OpenRLHF ([Hu et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)) for DPO an weighted-SFT. We
also use vLLM ([Kwon et al., 2023](#bib.bib20)) with asynchronous
continuous batching for efficient inference. All models were
LoRA-trained.

##### Online setup.

The default online configuration trains LoRA adapters on Qwen3-1.7B with
rank 16 and scaling parameter 32, targeting all linear layers. Training
uses GRPO with within-prompt standard-deviation normalization, a policy
clipping ratio of 0.2, and one policy-optimization epoch per rollout
batch. The learning rate is $`5\times 10^{-7}`$, with a 3% warmup
fraction and cosine scheduling. The reference KL-loss coefficient is
$`10^{-3}`$, and the entropy-loss coefficient is zero.

Training runs for two dataset epochs. The prompt batch size is 30 for
AIME2026 and 32 for Math500, retaining the final partial batch. The
configured PPO minibatch size is one prompt, corresponding to eight
sampled responses under the default rollout setting; the per-GPU
microbatch size is one response. The maximum prompt and response lengths
are 2,048 and 16,384 tokens, respectively. Rollouts use thinking mode,
temperature 1.0, top-$`p`$ 1.0, and no top-$`k`$ restriction.

For the random, SC, and UID variants, binary majority-agreement rewards
are mapped to $`-1/+1`$. An auxiliary term

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
0.1\,\mathrm{clip}\left(q_{i}-\overline{q}_{\mathrm{agree}},-0.5,0.5\right)
``` |  |

is added only to majority-consistent responses in groups containing at
least two such responses. Original TTRL retains its binary $`0/1`$
reward without this term. Random scores are seeded uniform draws. SC
uses within-prompt midranks of mean response-token Self-Certainty.
Online UID uses 32 equal-position bins and the product of within-prompt
midrank scores for global variation and local smoothness; it does not
use the offline calibration CDFs.

The default configuration uses four GPUs, FSDP, gradient checkpointing,
and CPU offloading of actor parameters, optimizer state, and reference
parameters. Internal validation uses four sampled responses per prompt
with temperature 0.6 and top-$`p`$ 0.95, and is distinct from the
post-training five-response evaluation protocol.

##### Offline setup.

Offline training uses OpenRLHF with BF16 precision, DeepSpeed ZeRO-3,
FlashAttention-2, sequence packing, and gradient checkpointing. LoRA
adapters have rank 64 and scaling parameter 64 and target the query,
key, value, output, gate, up, and down projections. The maximum sequence
length is 18,432 tokens, and the per-device microbatch size is one.

Weighted SFT and the shared DPO warm-start SFT each run for one epoch
with global batch size 32, learning rate $`5\times 10^{-5}`$, and a 3%
warmup fraction. Weighted SFT minimizes the mean of per-response
token-averaged negative log-likelihoods multiplied by the corresponding
example weights; prompt and padding tokens are masked.

DPO runs for one epoch with global batch size 16, learning rate
$`10^{-5}`$, a 5% warmup fraction, and preference coefficient
$`\beta=0.1`$. Full-coverage batching is enabled for the DPO training
pairs. Its reference model is the frozen shared SFT checkpoint.

Both objectives use AdamW with $`(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.95)`$,
$`\epsilon=10^{-8}`$, zero weight decay, and gradient clipping at norm
1.0. The learning-rate scheduler is cosine with a minimum learning-rate
ratio of 0.1. The default seed is 42. SFT checkpoints and development
evaluations occur every 25 steps; DPO uses an interval of 13 steps. The
evaluation launchers load the exported adapters and do not automatically
select the best development checkpoint.

### C.4 Hyperparameters for Evaluation

Post-training inference uses vLLM with BF16 precision, tensor
parallelism of one, and a batch size of eight prompts. Four-model
launchers assign one model to each GPU. When loading the merged SFT
model, the exported RoPE parameter is preserved at $`10^{6}`$.

For the mathematical benchmarks, we generate five responses per question
with thinking enabled, temperature 0.6, top-$`p`$ 0.95, top-$`k`$ 20,
min-$`p`$ 0, presence penalty 0.5, and repetition penalty 1.05. The DPO
launcher allows each response to use the remaining context after the
prompt. The TTRL post-training launcher instead defaults to a
16,384-token response limit; full-context generation is an optional
override.

For a benchmark containing $`N`$ questions, mean accuracy is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{MeanAcc}_{5}=\frac{1}{5N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{5}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{a}_{ij}\text{ matches }a_{i}\right].
``` |  |

This is the average correctness of five sampled responses, not pass@5 or
majority-vote accuracy. The mathematical macro-average is the unweighted
mean of AIME2026, HMMT2026, and MinervaMath accuracies. Scoring extracts
the last balanced \boxed{} expression after any closing thinking
delimiter and applies the implemented answer normalizer followed by
exact string comparison. It does not perform symbolic-equivalence
checking. Missing final answers, context overflows, and truncated
responses count as failures.

For CriticBench, we generate five responses per example with hidden
thinking disabled while retaining the requested written analysis and
verdict. Sampling uses temperature 0.7, top-$`p`$ 0.8, top-$`k`$ 20,
presence penalty 0.5, and repetition penalty 1.05. Responses may use the
remaining 32,768-token context. We report verdict accuracy and
incorrect-class F1, averaging the official scores across the five sample
indices. These metrics evaluate verdict correctness rather than critique
quality.

For IFBench and IHEval, we generate one greedy response per case with
thinking disabled, temperature 0, top-$`p`$ 1, no top-$`k`$ restriction,
no presence penalty, and repetition penalty 1. The response limit is
4,096 tokens within a 32,768-token context. Failed, empty,
context-overflow, and truncated outputs receive zero credit, including
safety tasks where an empty response might otherwise satisfy a rejection
check. Evaluation records retain the source revisions, prepared-input
hashes, decoding settings, and response status.

### D.2 UID-Conj. Metric Analysis.

##### Conjunction Metric Variant: UID-Mix.

UID-Mix combines global entropy variation and local smoothness with
equal weight. In the online implementation, we average token entropies
computed from renormalized top-25 probabilities into 32 equal-position
bins and normalize the resulting trajectory by its mean. We measure
global variation using the population variance of this trajectory and
local smoothness using the negative mean absolute difference between
adjacent bins. Each feature is mapped to an empirical midrank quantile
among eligible responses to the same prompt, yielding $`q_{G}`$ and
$`q_{S}`$, and the final score is
$`\mathrm{UID\text{-}Mix}=\tfrac{1}{2}(q_{G}+q_{S})`$. Unlike UID-Conj,
which multiplies these quantiles,
$`\mathrm{UID\text{-}Conj}=q_{G}q_{S}`$, UID-Mix allows a higher value
on one component to compensate more readily for a lower value on the
other.

##### Results.

UID-Mix shows promising gains in the online setting of Qwen3-4B,
improving AIME from 55.33 to 62.67 and HMMT from 13.94 to 23.03, with
smaller improvements in CriticBench F1 and accuracy. Although GPQA-D
decreases slightly from 57.68 to 56.97, the four-benchmark macro-average
increases from 50.46 to 54.50. In the offline setting of Qwen3-1.7B, the
two variants perform similarly: UID-Mix improves CriticBench F1 and
accuracy and matches HMMT, but slightly reduces AIME and GPQA-D
performance, yielding a macro-average of 38.07 versus 38.43 for
UID-Conj. These findings suggest that the choice of aggregation can
influence the utility of UID signals, although the available comparisons
do not establish a consistently superior variant. The online gains
motivate further investigation of UID-Mix and UID-Conj as auxiliary
rewards, including whether their complementary emphasis on component
compensation and joint satisfaction can benefit learning when combined
with task-specific objectives.

### D.4 Paired Bootstrap Procedure in Uncertainty Analysis

We quantify uncertainty using a paired prompt-cluster percentile
bootstrap with 100,000 replicates. Prompts, rather than individual
responses, are the resampling units. Each sampled prompt retains all
five generated responses from each method, and the same prompt indices
are used for both methods. This preserves pairing and avoids treating
repeated responses to a prompt as independent observations.

##### Primary estimands (Table [6](#S6.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Uncertainty Analysis. ‣ 6 Further Analyses ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).

For accuracy and mean-score outcomes, let $`y^{(m)}_{bqs}`$ denote the
score of method $`m`$ on benchmark $`b`$, prompt $`q`$, and response
$`s\in\{1,\ldots,5\}`$. The prompt-level paired difference in percentage
points is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{bq}=\frac{100}{5}\sum_{s=1}^{5}\left(y^{(\mathrm{UID})}_{bqs}-y^{(\mathrm{baseline})}_{bqs}\right).
``` |  | (19) |

For a benchmark with $`Q_{b}`$ prompts, the estimated difference is
$`\widehat{\Delta}_{b}=Q_{b}^{-1}\sum_{q}d_{bq}`$. The primary
multi-benchmark estimate equally weights benchmarks:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\widehat{\Delta}_{b}.
``` |  | (20) |

The online held-out average covers HMMT2026, MinervaMath, and GPQA-D,
with 33, 272, and 198 prompts, respectively. The offline average
additionally includes the 30 AIME2026 prompts. The online and offline
comparisons therefore contain 503 and 533 prompts, respectively.

##### Resampling and interval construction.

For each replicate, we independently sample $`Q_{b}`$ prompts with
replacement within each benchmark and recompute the paired differences
and their aggregate. Benchmark identities and sample sizes remain fixed.
We do not separately resample responses within prompts or resample
benchmarks. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap
distribution define the reported 95% confidence interval.

CriticBench accuracy and LabBench2 score use the same paired
prompt-cluster procedure within their respective benchmarks. For
CriticBench F1, we recompute the incorrect-class F1 from the resampled
predictions and labels separately for each of the five response indices,
average those five F1 values, and subtract the methods’ averages.
Recorded generation failures retain their original scoring treatment
rather than being excluded.

##### Secondary prompt-weighted estimands (Table [13](#A4.T13 "Table 13 ‣ Secondary prompt-weighted estimands (Table ). ‣ D.4 Paired Bootstrap Procedure in Uncertainty Analysis ‣ Appendix D Additional Analyses ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).

The primary analysis gives each benchmark equal weight, regardless of
its number of prompts
(Table [6](#S6.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Uncertainty Analysis. ‣ 6 Further Analyses ‣ Self-Improvement with Auxiliary Rewards: Learning from Verified Traces and Evaluating Transfer")).
As a sensitivity analysis, we instead weight benchmarks by their fixed
prompt counts:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{prompt}}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{Q_{b}}{\sum_{j=1}^{B}Q_{j}}\widehat{\Delta}_{b}.
``` |  | (21) |

This estimates the average paired difference across prompts in the
evaluated benchmark mixture, rather than the average across benchmarks.
We use identical benchmark-stratified bootstrap draws for the primary
and secondary analyses; only the aggregation weights change.

##### Scope and limitations.

The intervals characterize prompt-level uncertainty conditional on the
evaluated checkpoints, saved generations, benchmark composition, and
scoring procedures. They do not incorporate variation across training
seeds, alternative benchmark collections, or repeated judge evaluations.
Intervals are reported separately for each comparison without adjustment
for multiple comparisons. An interval containing zero does not establish
equivalence between methods.

Prompt weighting narrows three of the four multi-benchmark intervals but
slightly widens the online comparison against Random-TTRL. It also
changes the point estimates. In particular, the weighted-SFT comparison
changes from a macro-average difference of $`+1.93`$ percentage points,
with interval $`[0.44,\,3.44]`$, to a prompt-weighted difference of
$`+0.98`$, with interval $`[-0.30,\,2.29]`$. All four secondary
intervals include zero. We therefore retain the benchmark-macro analysis
as primary and report prompt weighting as a distinct estimand, not as a
replacement chosen to obtain narrower intervals.

## Appendix E Discussion on Differing Behaviors of Auxiliary Rewards

### E.1 Preliminary Diagnostic vs. Training Behavior

The preliminary diagnostic suggests that SC and UID-Conj favor different
properties of correct traces: SC achieves higher overall judge scores,
including stronger validity and consistency, whereas UID-Conj performs
better on relevance/non-redundancy. Their definitions help interpret
this distinction. Before calibration, SC averages token-level departure
from uniformity:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{i}=\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(U_{25}\,\|\,\tilde{p}_{i,t}),
``` |  | (22) |

where $`\tilde{p}_{i,t}`$ is the renormalized top-25 predictive
distribution at a valid scoring position. This average summarizes
predictive certainty without explicitly capturing its temporal
arrangement. By contrast, offline UID-Conj uses

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle G_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\frac{h_{i,b}-\bar{h}_{i}}{s}\right)^{2},`$ |  | (23) |
|  | $`\displaystyle S_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B-1}\rho_{1}\!\left(\frac{h_{i,b+1}-h_{i,b}}{s}\right),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\quad q_{i}^{\mathrm{Conj,off}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=F_{G}(G_{i}^{\mathrm{off}})F_{S}(S_{i}^{\mathrm{off}}),`$ |  |  |

where $`h_{i,b}`$ denotes bin-level entropy, $`s`$ is the fixed
calibration scale, and $`\rho_{1}`$ is the Huber loss. The product
favors responses ranking highly in both global variation and local
smoothness, rather than directly rewarding low entropy throughout.

This difference offers a possible explanation for UID-Conj’s stronger
offline results. Once final-answer correctness is fixed, SC may
concentrate training on patterns the model already expresses
confidently. UID-Conj can instead emphasize successful traces that move
between more- and less-certain stages while maintaining locally smooth
transitions. Together with its diagnostic advantage on
relevance/non-redundancy, this suggests that UID-Conj may provide
complementary preferences over focused, successful reasoning that
confidence-based scoring ranks lower. However, SC measures
distributional certainty rather than generated-token likelihood, so high
SC does not necessarily imply low training loss or a less useful update.

The training objectives translate these preferences into different
learning emphasis. For two correct responses to the same prompt,
weighted SFT assigns

|     |                                                             |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{w_{i}}{w_{j}}=\exp\!\left(\alpha(q_{i}-q_{j})\right),
       ```                                                          |     | (24) |

so changing the auxiliary score directly changes their relative
contributions to training. DPO similarly reinforces the highest-scoring
correct response relative to the lowest-scoring alternative. UID-Conj
can therefore favor different successful trajectories without selecting
the response with the highest overall judge score.

This interpretation is consistent with UID-Conj’s higher offline
aggregates and CriticBench point estimates. Conversely, reinforcing
confidently expressed response patterns may help account for SC’s higher
instruction-following aggregate. These links remain hypotheses: the
equations establish how the preferences differ and enter training, while
the mixed online results show that their downstream value depends on the
setting.

### E.2 Further Directions

The preceding interpretation motivates examining whether UID-Conj’s
training benefits arise from preferences that complement predictive
certainty, and how this relationship changes with the current policy and
training objective. A central question is whether entropy-trajectory
structure helps identify useful updates among already-correct responses
beyond what response length, confidence, and judged quality capture. In
the online setting, understanding how these preferences interact with
advantage normalization could further clarify when they influence
learning. Resolving these questions would inform the design of auxiliary
signals that better connect response-level properties with downstream
training utility.
````

###### Abstract

Test-time scaling methods spend additional compute at deployment to
improve a large language model’s performance on a hard target problem.
Most methods rely on a verifier that scores candidate solutions for
correctness, using it to select among or refine the model’s outputs. In
many deployment regimes, however, a strong verifier on the target
problem is unavailable. We introduce Test-Time Self-Play (TTSP), an
approach to test-time scaling using asymmetric self-play without
reliance on a verifier. On a difficulty-calibrated subset of
LiveCodeBench v6, TTSP is the best-performing method at every verifier
strength we evaluate, and the gap to the strongest baseline widens as
the verifier weakens; in the no-verifier limit, TTSP yields a 35%
absolute improvement in mean@8. The gains hold across a range of
verifier strengths, except when the base policy has near-zero coverage
of the correct answer.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: TTSP outperforms all baseline test-time scaling methods in the
weak verifier regime. TTSP and ACE are evaluated at converged
checkpoints; FMV uses public and private input test cases.

How can a model improve on a single difficult target problem when it has
no related training data and no reliable way to check its own answers?
This question arises whenever a large language model’s underlying
knowledge is, in principle, sufficient to solve a problem, yet the model
rarely, or never, generates the correct solution. Examples of this can
be found across domains, including in competitive programming ([Zheng et
al., 2025](#bib.bib1)), theorem proving ([Tsoukalas et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)), and molecule generation ([Gao et al.,
2022](#bib.bib3)).

Test-time scaling aims to close this gap, spending additional compute at
deployment to improve performance on the target problem. Many test-time
scaling methods, such as rejection sampling ([Snell et al.,
2024](#bib.bib53)), rely on a verifier for the target problem that
determines whether an attempt is correct. Similarly, prompt-optimization
methods such as ACE ([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib48)) that use
execution feedback for evolving prompts. Other methods rely on auxiliary
data, typically similar problems retrieved from a large corpus and used
to train on a neighborhood of the target problem ([Hübotter et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16); [Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). Another set of
methods requires neither verifier nor auxiliary training data, instead
aggregating a signal from the model’s own rollouts on the target problem
through majority voting ([Zuo et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Du et al.,
2026](#bib.bib23); [Shafayat et al., 2025](#bib.bib24); [Launer et al.,
2026](#bib.bib50)).

We propose Test-Time Self-Play (TTSP), a test-time scaling method that
does not rely on auxiliary training data or strong verifiers. TTSP is
inspired by training-time asymmetric self-play methods such as Absolute
Zero ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib42)) and R-Zero ([Huang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib43)), which learn from self-generated synthetic problems
alone. We adapt this approach to the single-target setting,
concentrating the synthetic problems on a target problem rather than
across a benchmark distribution. The only external requirement of TTSP
is an interactive environment, such as a Python interpreter, to ground
the model.

Our contributions are the following. (i) We define the test-time scaling
setting with no auxiliary data and absent or weak verifiers, and
characterize the behavior of existing test-time scaling methods. (ii) We
propose TTSP, a test-time scaling method based on asymmetric self-play
that is robust to weak verifiers.

Figure 2: Overview of Test-Time Self-Play. Given a target problem
$`x^{\star}`$ and previously generated synthetic problems, the proposer
policy $`\pi_{\phi}`$ generates target-conditioned synthetic problems
$`\mathcal{D}`$ consisting of a problem statement, tests, and a
reference solution. The solver policy is rewarded for correctly solving
the synthetic problems, while the proposer is rewarded for generating
synthetic problems near the solver’s current learning frontier (both via
GRPO; see the red arrows). The synthetic problem history provides
context for the proposer and fills a replay buffer $`\mathcal{B}`$.
After a sufficient number of iterations, the test-time trained solver
can then attempt the target problem.

## 2 Related Work

#### Test-time scaling.

Spending additional compute at deployment to improve performance on a
target problem has been studied under the umbrellas of test-time scaling
([Snell et al., 2024](#bib.bib53); [Brown et al., 2024](#bib.bib54)) and
test-time training (TTT) ([Sun et al., 2020](#bib.bib12)).
Sampling-based test-time scaling repeatedly draws rollouts from the
policy and selects among them with a verifier or vote ([Brown et al.,
2024](#bib.bib54); [Launer et al., 2026](#bib.bib50)). Weight-updating
TTT specializes a model’s weights to each target problem, with success
across abstract reasoning ([Akyürek et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)), language
modeling ([Hardt and Sun, 2024](#bib.bib14); [Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15); [Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)), and video generation
([Dalal et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)), using supervised losses on target
problem neighborhoods drawn from external data ([Hardt and Sun,
2024](#bib.bib14); [Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib15); [Bertolissi et
al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Bagatella et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)) or
self-supervised losses over the input sequence ([Sun et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17); [Dalal et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)). Most relevant to
our work, [Zuo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib21) extend TTT to RL on the test
set using majority votes as pseudo-labels, while [Simonds and Yoshiyama
(2025)](#bib.bib22) generate problem variants at test-time and train via
RL with numerical verification in symbolic integration. This connects to
broader themes of unsupervised RL ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25);
[Agarwal et al., 2025](#bib.bib26); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib27);
[Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)) and self-improvement in LLMs ([Zelikman
et al., 2022](#bib.bib28); [Gulcehre et al., 2023](#bib.bib29); [Lee et
al., 2025](#bib.bib52)). How best to derive a strong learning signal for
test-time scaling from the model alone, without recourse to external
data or supervision, remains an open question.

#### Curriculum learning.

Introduced by [Bengio et al. (2009)](#bib.bib4), curriculum learning is
the principle that ordering training examples from easy to hard
accelerates optimization and improves generalization. It has since
become a central design pattern in modern LLM training, underpinning
pretraining and mid-training (DoReMi, [Xie et al. 2023](#bib.bib37);
OLMo 2, [Team OLMo et al. 2025](#bib.bib38); Phi-4, [Abdin et al.
2024](#bib.bib39)) and, especially, RL-based reasoning post-training,
where DeepSeek-R1 ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)), Kimi k1.5 ([Kimi
Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)), and adaptive schemes such as
Self-Evolving Curriculum ([Chen et al., 2025b](#bib.bib40)) and
easy-to-hard generalization ([Sun et al., 2024](#bib.bib41))
consistently outperform uniform sampling.

#### Self-play.

Self-play underlies many landmark results in reinforcement learning
([Schmidhuber, 1991](#bib.bib5); [Tesauro, 1995](#bib.bib6); [Silver et
al., 2016](#bib.bib7); [Silver et al., 2017](#bib.bib8); [Silver et al.,
2018](#bib.bib9); [Berner et al., 2019](#bib.bib10)). Unlike fixed
datasets, the self-play curriculum co-evolves with the learner and is in
principle unbounded, a property recently argued to be central to
continued progress beyond the limits of human-generated data ([Silver
and Sutton, 2025](#bib.bib11)). Recent work imports this property into
LLM post-training by assigning a model dual proposer policy and solver
policy roles with learning signal from automatic verifiers such as code
execution, majority vote, or proof checkers ([Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib42); [Huang et al., 2025](#bib.bib43); [Chen et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib44); [Kuba et al., 2025](#bib.bib45); [Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib46)). However, the resulting curricula tend to concentrate
on narrow regions of the task space, and gains from fully data-free
self-play are often bounded by the base model’s coverage ([Chae et al.,
2025](#bib.bib47)). Prior self-play methods for LLMs operate at training
time and target broad capability gains across a benchmark distribution.
We instead apply self-play at _test-time_, using target problem
decomposition to build a specialized curriculum that concentrates
adaptation on the target problem.

#### Context optimization.

A complementary line of work adapts LLM behavior by modifying the
context rather than the model weights. GEPA ([Agrawal et al.,
2026](#bib.bib49)) optimizes textual parameters through reflective
prompt evolution, using verifier feedback to propose mutations and
Pareto-style selection to retain complementary prompt variants. ACE
([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib48)) extends this paradigm with a
structured generator–reflector–curator decomposition: a generator
attempts the task, a reflector extracts reusable lessons from execution
feedback, and a curator incrementally updates a long-form playbook
context while preserving useful details. This decomposition lets ACE
accumulate more fine-grained domain knowledge than the prompt-level
mutations of GEPA, and ACE reports consistent gains over GEPA across
reasoning and tool-use benchmarks.

## 3 Preliminaries

We study test-time scaling with individual target problems. Let
$`x^{\star}`$ denote a target problem and let $`y`$ denote a candidate
solution. A policy $`\pi`$ generates rollouts
$`y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x^{\star})`$. The oracle verifier,

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       v^{\star}(y;x^{\star})\in\{0,1\},
       ```                                |     |

indicates whether $`y`$ is a correct solution for $`x^{\star}`$. The
oracle is used for evaluation, but is not assumed to be available for
training or feedback at test-time. A deployment-time target verifier
$`v`$ may be weak or absent. We further assume that no auxiliary
training data (i.e., problems and their ground truth solutions $`(x,y)`$
related to $`x^{\star}`$) are available. We ask whether a test-time
scaling method can be made robust in this setting.

We propose that this is possible by deriving a learning signal from
_synthetic problems_ instead of directly from the target problem. A
_synthetic problem_ $`\hat{x}`$ is a self-contained problem generated at
test-time, conditioned on $`x^{\star}`$. We evaluate TTSP in the setting
of competitive programming, in which $`\hat{x}`$ consists of a
natural-language statement, an input/output specification, an example
input/output pair, held-out input test cases, and a reference Python
program. The reference program is not shown to the solver. Executing the
reference solution on the held-out input test cases produces the ground
truth outputs, which combined with the inputs define a synthetic
verifier

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       v_{\mathrm{syn}}(\hat{y};\hat{x})\in\{0,1\}
       ```                                          |     |

The verifier identifies whether the attempted solution $`\hat{y}`$
passes all held-out test cases. We define synthetic problems to be
self-contained training tasks with a deterministic verification
mechanism. We do not require a formal decomposition relation between
synthetic problems $`\hat{x}`$ and $`x^{\star}`$; the target problem
dependence in TTSP is induced by conditioning the proposer on
$`x^{\star}`$ and the synthetic problem history.

## 4 TTSP: Test-Time Self-Play

Test-Time Self-Play (TTSP) adapts a solver policy to a single target
problem $`x^{\star}`$ by training on target-conditioned synthetic
problems. A proposer policy generates synthetic problems, a solver
policy attempts them, execution supplies rewards, and both policies are
updated with GRPO. The target problem verifier is not used during TTSP
training.
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
illustrates the asymmetric self-play loop.

#### Self-play setup.

TTSP uses two LoRA adapters over a shared frozen Qwen3-8B backbone ([Hu
et al., 2022](#bib.bib34); [Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib35)): a proposer
policy $`\pi_{\phi}`$ and a solver policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$. The proposer
policy designs a synthetic curriculum conditioned on $`x^{\star}`$ and
the training history, while the solver policy trains on the resulting
synthetic problems. Separating the adapters gives each role its own
reward signal and avoids the gradient conflicts we observed when using a
single adapter for both roles. The memory cost remains close to a single
8B model, since the backbone is shared and only the adapters are
updated.

#### Algorithm overview.

A step, $`t`$, of the TTSP algorithm begins with the construction of the
proposer’s prompt. The prompt (or context) contains a small sample of
previously generated synthetic problems and the their solve rates (from
the solver policy). The context also includes the target problem and
instructions guiding the proposer to generate valid, relevant and
appropriately challenging synthetic problems based on previous synthetic
problems. The prompt in the first step does not contain previous
synthetic problems.

The proposer then generates candidate synthetic problems and reference
programs. Invalid candidates are removed by a domain-specific heuristic
filter Filter. The valid synthetic problems $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ are
added to a buffer of old synthetic problems $`\mathcal{B}_{t}`$ which
are sampled from the history of synthetic problems $`\mathcal{H}`$.
Problems within the range of solve rates $`[0.1,0.8]`$ are uniformly
randomly sampled.

The solver policy then generates solutions for the combined set of
synthetic problems $`\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\mathcal{B}_{t}`$. The synthetic
reward is calculated for each solution.

The history $`\mathcal{H}`$ is updated with the new synthetic problems,
solver rollouts and rewards. The solver is then updated with GRPO using
the synthetic rewards. The proposer is updated with GRPO using a
transformation of the solver rewards
$`\textsc{ProposerReward}(\mathcal{D}_{t},\mathcal{R}_{\theta})`$. The
transformation is described later in this section.

After a sufficient number of steps, the aim is for the solver policy to
have a more accurate distribution of solutions on the target problem
$`x^{\star}`$. The solver can then be used to attempt the target
problem.

Synthetic problems provide a dense training signal where direct training
on $`x^{\star}`$ would be sparse, misleading (due to weak target problem
verification) or unavailable (if no target problem verifier is
available). TTSP produces many target-conditioned synthetic problems
that are both at the edge of the solver’s abilities (due to resampling
of previous problems in the optimal difficulty range, and proposer
reward transformation) and anchored to the target problem (due to the
context construction). See Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3 Preliminaries ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
for the TTSP pseudocode.

#### Synthetic problem generation.

The proposer generates each synthetic problem in two stages. First,
_solution proposal_ conditions on $`x^{\star}`$ and the recent synthetic
problem history $`\mathcal{H}`$, then produces a draft synthetic problem
and a Python reference program. Second, _problem refinement_
reconstructs a clean problem statement, input/output specification,
example I/O, and held-out input tests from the reference program. The
held-out outputs are obtained by executing the reference program. This
ordering makes the tests and statement conform to the program’s actual
behavior, rather than requiring a single decoding pass to keep the
statement, tests, and solution mutually consistent. The subgoal
generation prompt templates are listed in Appendix
[F](#A6 "Appendix F Prompt Templates ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").

We then apply Filter, which rejects candidates with timeouts, runtime
errors, duplicate-only test sets, or constant-output behavior. Accepted
problems enter the fresh-problem set $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ and are added
to the history after the solver attempts them. Invalid candidates do not
enter the solver batch and receive a fixed proposer penalty.

#### Synthetic problem history.

TTSP maintains a history $`\mathcal{H}`$ of accepted synthetic problems
and the solver’s most recent rollouts on them. At each iteration, the
solver batch mixes new synthetic problems $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ with a
buffer of previous problems $`\mathcal{B}_{t}`$ sampled from
$`\mathcal{H}`$. The sampler favors problems whose current solve rate
lies in a learnability window: already-solved problems provide little
training signal, while near-impossible problems produce nearly all-zero
rewards. Since UpdateHistory overwrites each problem’s latest rollouts
and solve rate, both proposer contexts and buffer sampling track the
current solver rather than a stale snapshot.

#### Rewards.

The solver receives a binary synthetic reward

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       r_{\theta}(\hat{y},\hat{x})=v_{\mathrm{syn}}(\hat{y};\hat{x}),
       ```                                                             |     |

which equals one if and only if the solver rollout $`\hat{y}`$ passes
all held-out generated tests for synthetic problem $`\hat{x}`$.

The proposer is rewarded for generating problems near the solver’s
current frontier. Let $`\bar{r}_{\theta}(\hat{x})`$ be the mean solver
reward over rollouts on $`\hat{x}`$. For accepted synthetic problems, we
define

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
r_{\phi}(\hat{x})=1-2\left|\bar{r}_{\theta}(\hat{x})-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|,
``` |  | (1) |

with a fixed penalty for invalid generations. The triangular profile
targets the region where sparse Bernoulli rewards induce the largest
group-relative signal under GRPO, so the proposer policy is rewarded for
keeping the solver policy near its frontier ([Hübotter et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16)). The buffer eligibility window is chosen to match the
high-reward part of this profile.

#### Optimization.

We optimize both adapters with GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)),
DAPO-style asymmetric clipping ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)), and zero
KL regularization following the test-time adaptation setup of [Hübotter
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib16). Hyperparameters are listed in Appendix
[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Hyperparameters ‣ Appendix B TTSP Training Details and Hyperparameters ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").

#### Task domain.

We evaluate TTSP in the domain of competitive programming. We use the
benchmark LiveCodeBench v6 ([Jain et al., 2024](#bib.bib36)), which
contains 131 target problems released between February and May 2025,
after the Qwen3-8B knowledge cutoff. The benchmark provides public test
cases, private test cases, and a broad range of problem difficulties.
This lets us vary verifier strength and target problem difficulty within
a single controlled evaluation setting.

#### Target problem set.

From LiveCodeBench v6, we keep problems satisfying
$`0<\mathrm{mean}@512<0.7`$ for Qwen3-8B and name the resulting target
problem set HardLCBv6. Qwen3-8B has a wide range of non-zero accuracies
over this set of problems.

We further split HardLCBv6 into MidHardLCBv6, containing target problems
with $`0.1<\mathrm{mean}@512<0.7`$, and VeryHardLCBv6, containing target
problems with $`0<\mathrm{mean}@512\leq 0.1`$. We evaluate TTSP and
baselines on these subsets of LiveCodeBench v6. Qwen3-8B solve rates for
each target problem are listed in
Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Target problem subsets ‣ Appendix A Target Problems and Evaluation Protocol ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").

Figure 3: Baselines performance on HardLCBv6 as compute scales. Each
line reports the mean@8 with the indicated evaluation-time verifier
applied to the validation problem submissions. Left and middle: Qwen 3
8B and FMV scale on the number of sampled rollouts k. Right: ACE scales
on the number of generation–reflection–curation steps.

#### Verifier construction.

We evaluate seven verifier conditions. Four are artificial verifiers,
$`v_{0}`$, $`v_{25}`$, $`v_{50}`$, and $`v_{75}`$, calibrated to reject
the indicated fraction of incorrect sampled solutions; see
Appendix [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Artificial verifier calibration ‣ Appendix A Target Problems and Evaluation Protocol ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").
Three are LiveCodeBench verifiers: public test cases
($`v_{\mathrm{pub}}`$), private test cases ($`v_{\mathrm{priv}}`$), and
public + private test cases ($`v^{\star}`$). The verifier $`v_{0}`$
accepts every rollout, while $`v^{\star}`$ is the oracle verfier. These
enable a closer investigation of a test-time scaling method’s
performance across various levels of weak (and absent, in the case of
$`v_{0}`$) to strong (in the limit, $`v^{\star}`$) verifiers.

### 5.2 Evaluation Protocol

Each method output is evaluated as a set of eight target problem
submissions. For aggregate comparisons, this set comes from the
converged or final method output; for training-curve diagnostics, the
same definition is applied at each checkpoint $`t`$. Let
$`Y=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{8}\}`$ denote the eight submitted candidates for
$`x^{\star}`$. For a verifier
$`v\in\{v_{0},v_{25},v_{50},v_{75},v_{\mathrm{pub}},v_{\mathrm{priv}},v^{\star}\}`$,
we form the admitted subset

|     |                         |     |
|-----|-------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A(v)=\{y\in Y:v(y)=1\}.
       ```                      |     |

For non-empty admitted sets, we report the filtered mean@8 score

|     |                                                                  |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathrm{mean@}8(v)=\frac{1}{|A(v)|}\sum_{y\in A(v)}v^{\star}(y).
       ```                                                               |     |

Under $`v_{0}`$, no candidate is filtered out, so $`A=Y`$ and mean@8 is
the fraction of all eight candidates that pass $`v^{\star}`$. Throughout
the paper, mean@8 denotes this filtered score; we write unfiltered
mean@8 for the fraction of all eight submitted candidates that pass
$`v^{\star}`$ with no evaluation-time filtering. Empty admitted sets are
handled by the carry-forward rule in
Appendix [A.5](#A1.SS5 "A.5 Filtered evaluation metric ‣ Appendix A Target Problems and Evaluation Protocol ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").
Since the same filtering pass is applied to all methods, differences in
mean@8 reflect differences in candidate quality rather than differences
in scoring.

#### Rejection sampling.

Our direct filtering baseline samples candidates from the unadapted
Qwen3-8B policy. The model produces eight submissions for $`x^{\star}`$,
and the verifier is used only in the evaluation-time filtering pass
described above. This baseline measures how much performance can be
obtained from base-policy sampling without any prompt or weight
adaptation.

Figure 4: Performance of TTSP on HardLCBv6 over training steps. Each
line reports the mean@8 at different evaluation-time verifier strengths
applied to the solver policy’s target problem submissions. Shaded bands
are 90% bootstrap confidence intervals over target problems.

Figure 5: Best performing method per problem and verifier strength. Each
point is a (target problem, verifier strength) pair, colored by the
method with higher mean@8. The x-axis orders verifier strength and the
y-axis reports problem difficulty, measured as the base policy’s
mean@512.

#### Majority voting.

Our consensus baseline is Functional Majority Voting (FMV) ([Launer et
al., 2026](#bib.bib50)). The FMV voting rule itself is input-only: it
clusters candidate programs by execution behavior on target problem
input cases and selects the modal functional cluster. In the
verifier-strength sweep, we combine this input-only voting rule with the
current verifier condition by first discarding candidates that fail the
verifier and then applying FMV to the admitted pool. This baseline
measures whether target problem rollouts contain a recoverable correct
functional cluster after the available verifier has removed some
incorrect candidates.
Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Functional Majority Voting Baseline ‣ Appendix C Baseline Implementation and Diagnostics ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
gives the exact filtering, scoring, and tie-handling procedure.

TTRL ([Zuo et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)), while well-known for its majority
voting reward, is designed for benchmark-level adaptation, jointly
training on a population of test problems via per-prompt majority vote
rewards, unlike our per-problem setting. TTRL is also only evaluated on
math, whereas FMV was created for competitive programming, in which
majority voting can be done in more strategic ways due to the existence
of test cases.

#### Context optimization.

Our prompt-optimization baseline is ACE ([Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib48)). We run ACE separately at each verifier strength,
using the pass/fail outcome under that verifier as the reward signal for
its generator–reflector–curator loop. The evaluation-time filtering pass
is then applied to ACE’s final submissions. We use ACE rather than GEPA
([Agrawal et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)) because ACE was the stronger
context-optimization variant in our preliminary runs. Full
implementation details are provided in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 ACE Baseline Implementation ‣ Appendix C Baseline Implementation and Diagnostics ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").

### 5.4 Results

#### Robustness across verifier strengths.

TTSP is the best-performing method at every verifier strength we
evaluate (see Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")).
The TTSP curve is approximately flat across the full sweep, while all
three baselines decline as the verifier weakens and the gap to TTSP
widens accordingly. TTSP’s training-time reward is intrinsic to the
self-play loop, so the verifier strength axis varies only at evaluation
time; the baselines all depend on the verifier either for selection
(rejection sampling, FMV) or as a reward signal (ACE), and their
performance is bounded by the verifier’s discriminative power.

#### TTSP training dynamics.

Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Rejection sampling. ‣ 5.3 Baselines ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
shows TTSP performance over training steps. Most of the improvement
occurs within the first $`10`$ training steps, after which the curves
remain stable up to checkpoint-to-checkpoint noise. The spread between
verifier-strength conditions also narrows during training. These
dynamics suggest that the synthetic curricula quickly shift probability
mass toward candidates that remain useful under later evaluation-time
filtering.

#### Scaling baseline methods.

Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Target problem set. ‣ 5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
reports a scaling study along each baseline’s native compute axis. The
Qwen3-8B rejection sampling and FMV panels vary the rollout budget
$`k`$, while the ACE panel varies the number of context optimization
iterations and scores the current ACE submission. Qwen3-8B rejection
sampling improves with larger rollout budgets primarily when the
verifier is strong enough to reject incorrect candidates. FMV improves
with additional rollouts because functional agreement becomes more
informative. ACE improves over iterations only when the verifier
provides informative pass/fail feedback; under $`v_{0}`$, it remains at
the base-policy level because no optimization steps are taken. These
results demonstrate that increasing compute does not remove the
baselines’ dependence on target-verifier information.

#### Verifier-free setting.

In the $`v_{0}`$ setting, the verifier admits every candidate. In this
setting, ACE is unable to improve because it is not provided any
verification feedback. FMV remains limited to selecting among target
problem rollouts sampled from the base policy, while TTSP improves
substantially over all baselines.

Figure 6: Unfiltered mean@8 of TTSP and FMV binned by rank of the
correct functional cluster in the base policy. Bars group target
problems by the rank of the correct functional cluster among base-policy
rollouts.

#### Majority voting and solution rank.

Figure [6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Verifier-free setting. ‣ 5.4 Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
bins target problems by the rank of the correct functional cluster in
the base policy’s rollout distribution. FMV is strongest when the
correct cluster is modal, slightly exceeding TTSP performance. When the
correct cluster is ranked second, FMV drops sharply and TTSP overtakes
it. When the correct cluster has rank at least three, FMV is unable to
identify the correct solution while TTSP retains non-trivial accuracy.
We can observe that, while TTSP is impacted by correct solution output
rank, it degrades gracefully as rank increases, whereas FMV is fully
reliant on the correct solution being approximately the modal output.

#### TTSP is limited by target problem difficulty.

TTSP requires the correct rollout to exist in the base policy’s support
with non-trivial mass. Splitting HardLCBv6 into MidHardLCBv6 and
VeryHardLCBv6 reveals a sharp cutoff (Figure
[7](#S6.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 6 Discussion ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")).
On MidHardLCBv6, the improvement of TTSP over the base policy is small
at strong verifiers and rises sharply as the verifier weakens. On
VeryHardLCBv6, the improvement is approximately zero across the entire
sweep — slightly negative at the strongest verifiers, where rejection
sampling on the base policy can still surface the rare correct rollout,
and only marginally positive at the weakest. Below
$`\mathrm{mean}@512\approx 0.1`$, the proposer policy cannot bootstrap a
curriculum that composes back to $`x^{\star}`$.

#### When is TTSP most effective?

Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Rejection sampling. ‣ 5.3 Baselines ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
summarizes the same pattern at the per-target level. TTSP dominates when
base model coverage is non-trivial and the evaluation-time verifier is
weak. Base model rejection sampling dominates when the verifier is
strong enough to identify rare correct rollouts or when base model
coverage is too low for TTSP to build a useful curriculum. The resulting
boundary places TTSP in the consistency regime: it is most useful when
correct or partially useful target-related behavior is present but not
reliable under direct sampling.

## 6 Discussion

Test-Time Self-Play (TTSP) shifts the source of learning signal for
test-time scaling. Existing methods derive their selection or learning
signal from a verifier on the target problem, and their effectiveness
degrades as that verifier weakens. In TTSP, a solver policy is instead
trained on execution feedback from synthetic problems generated by a
proposer policy (both initialized from the same base policy), with the
target problem verifier left out of the training loop. The result is a
test-time scaling algorithm that is substantially more robust to weak
and absent target verifiers, conditional on the base policy having
non-trivial coverage of the target problem $`x^{\star}`$.

Figure 7: Difference in mean@8 between TTSP and Qwen3-8B. The gap is
large and positive on MidHardLCBv6 as the verifier weakens. On
VeryHardLCBv6, the gap is near zero: only marginally positive under the
weakest verifiers.

#### Limitations.

TTSP adapts per target problem and does not amortize compute across
targets, suiting deployment contexts where each target is valuable
enough to merit a dedicated adaptation budget. TTSP also only addresses
the consistency regime: when the base policy has effectively zero
coverage of the target answer, no curriculum the proposer policy can
synthesize will compose back to it
(Figure [7](#S6.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 6 Discussion ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")).
Furthermore, like other self-play algorithms, TTSP derives its training
signal entirely from self-generated content rather than from any
externally validated target. This makes training-time stability a
concern that should be actively monitored. The proposer policy can drift
toward synthetic problems that hack its reward function rather than ones
that compose back to $`x^{\star}`$, and the solver policy’s entropy can
collapse under sustained training on a narrow distribution ([Bailey et
al., 2026](#bib.bib51)). See Appendix
[D](#A4 "Appendix D Synthetic Curriculum Quality and Dynamics ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
for further discussion of training dynamics.

#### Future work.

Several natural extensions follow from relaxing TTSP’s constraints. The
proposer policy’s synthetic problem set can be augmented with
$`x^{\star}`$ itself whenever a non-degenerate target problem reward
exists, combining curriculum-driven and target-driven training in one
system. The set can also be augmented with nearest-neighbor synthetic
problems retrieved from external corpora when those are available
([Hübotter et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)), ingesting auxiliary data without
losing the curriculum structure. Furthermore, TTSP could be applied to
other domains with rich tools, including domains with execution
sandboxes, simulation engines, or database engines. Finally, tackling
the discovery regime, where the base model has near-zero coverage of the
target problem solution, remains an important open direction; it likely
requires either external information or substantially stronger search
than the curriculum alone provides.

The remaining subsections make the verifier-strength axis explicit. The
distinction is important for interpreting
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier"):
TTSP receives training rewards from execution on proposer-generated
synthetic problems, whereas the baselines select, cluster, or optimize
directly against information from the target problem verifier.

### A.2 Verifier-strength axis

A verifier is a selection rule applied to candidate solutions for the
original target problem $`x^{\star}`$. Strong verifiers reject a large
fraction of incorrect candidate programs, while weak verifiers admit
many false positives. We evaluate across a sweep of verifier strengths
to factor the test-time scaling problem into candidate generation and
candidate selection, following the main-text motivation that test-time
compute alone is not enough when the selection signal is weak ([Snell et
al., 2024](#bib.bib53); [Brown et al., 2024](#bib.bib54)).

The weakest condition is $`v_{0}`$, defined by $`v_{0}(y)=1`$ for every
rollout $`y`$. It corresponds to no filtering: all generated candidates
are treated as admissible, and the method is evaluated solely by how
often its unfiltered candidates pass the oracle $`v^{\star}`$. The
calibrated artificial verifiers $`v_{25}`$, $`v_{50}`$, and $`v_{75}`$
are constructed to reject approximately the indicated fraction of
incorrect calibration rollouts. The natural LiveCodeBench verifiers
$`v_{\mathrm{pub}}`$, $`v_{\mathrm{priv}}`$, and $`v^{\star}`$ are built
from public tests, private tests, and their union, respectively ([Jain
et al., 2024](#bib.bib36)). The public+private verifier $`v^{\star}`$ is
both the strongest evaluation-time filter and the oracle used to measure
final correctness.

### A.3 Natural LiveCodeBench verifiers

LiveCodeBench provides public and private test cases for each target
problem ([Jain et al., 2024](#bib.bib36)). We use these test suites as
natural verifier-access levels. The public verifier $`v_{\mathrm{pub}}`$
models the realistic programming-contest or live-coding setting in which
only sample or public tests are available at deployment. The private
verifier $`v_{\mathrm{priv}}`$ and the public+private verifier
$`v^{\star}`$ provide stronger selection rules and serve as
high-verifier-access reference points.

All reported correctness scores are computed against $`v^{\star}`$.
Thus, even when the evaluation-time filter is weaker than $`v^{\star}`$,
the final measurement asks whether an admitted candidate passes the full
public+private evaluator. This convention keeps the target quantity
fixed across verifier-strength conditions and makes the curves in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
comparable.

### A.4 Artificial verifier calibration

The calibrated weak verifiers $`v_{25}`$, $`v_{50}`$, and $`v_{75}`$ are
selected from a candidate pool containing retained generated tests as
well as public and private LiveCodeBench tests. We call these verifiers
artificial because the subset is chosen to match a target empirical
rejection rate, not because every selected test is generated. Generated
candidate inputs are produced using Qwen 3 8B ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35)) with the same refinement-style prompting pattern used
by TTSP to reconstruct synthetic problem tests; the TTSP refinement
prompt that this pattern mirrors is reported in
Appendix [F.4](#A6.SS4 "F.4 Refinement / labeling prompt ‣ Appendix F Prompt Templates ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").
These tests are not used to train TTSP. They are used only to construct
controlled evaluation-time filters whose false-positive rate can be
varied more finely than the natural public/private split.

Generated inputs do not come with trusted ground-truth outputs, so we
label them by consensus among base model programs that are already
correct under $`v^{\star}`$. Public and private LiveCodeBench tests use
their provided labels. For each target problem, we sample $`512`$ Qwen 3
8B baseline rollouts and partition them into correct and incorrect
programs using $`v^{\star}`$. For a generated input $`z`$, we execute
the correct baseline programs on $`z`$. If the correct programs
disagree, $`z`$ is discarded; if they agree, the consensus output is
used as the reference label. This step is designed to reduce false
negatives: a generated test is kept only when programs known to solve
the target problem agree on its output.

We then reduce verifier construction to a coverage problem over the
incorrect calibration rollouts. Each retained candidate test case
$`t_{j}`$ has an associated rejection set

|     |                                                 |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       E_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{incorrect}},
       ```                                              |     |

where $`E_{j}`$ contains the incorrect calibration rollouts that either
fail to execute on $`t_{j}`$ or produce an output different from the
consensus output. For a candidate verifier defined by a subset of tests
$`S`$, its empirical strength is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c(S)=\frac{\left|\bigcup_{t_{j}\in S}E_{j}\right|}{|\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{incorrect}}|}.
``` |  |

This definition accounts for overlap between tests: two tests that
reject the same incorrect rollouts do not increase verifier strength
additively.

For each target problem, $`v_{25}`$, $`v_{50}`$, and $`v_{75}`$ are
selected as subsets of the retained candidate tests whose empirical
coverage $`c(S)`$ is closest to $`0.25`$, $`0.50`$, and $`0.75`$,
respectively. The resulting filters intentionally permit false
positives, because a weak verifier should accept some incorrect
programs. We keep $`v_{\mathrm{pub}}`$, $`v_{\mathrm{priv}}`$, and
$`v^{\star}`$ as separate natural verifier conditions rather than
folding them into this calibrated artificial sweep.

### A.5 Filtered evaluation metric

Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Evaluation Protocol ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
defines the filtered mean@8 metric. For iterative methods and
training-curve diagnostics, the same metric is applied checkpoint-wise
to the eight target problem submissions sampled at checkpoint $`t`$.

If the evaluation-time verifier admits no candidates for a given target
problem, method, checkpoint, and verifier condition, the reported score
is carried forward from the most recent earlier checkpoint with a
non-empty admitted set for the same target problem, method, and verifier

This convention avoids undefined scores when a strong verifier rejects

## Appendix B TTSP Training Details and Hyperparameters

Section [4](#S4 "4 TTSP: Test-Time Self-Play ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
describes TTSP at the algorithmic level: a proposer policy generates
target-conditioned synthetic problems, a solver policy trains on them,
and both policies receive execution-based rewards. This appendix records
the implementation choices needed to reproduce the experiments. The
design follows the main-text constraint that adaptation is performed
separately for each target problem and that no target problem verifier
is used during TTSP training.

Both roles are implemented as separate LoRA adapters over a frozen Qwen
3 8B backbone ([Hu et al., 2022](#bib.bib34); [Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35)). The separation mirrors the asymmetric self-play
setup in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3 Preliminaries ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier"):
the proposer optimizes for a useful curriculum, while the solver
optimizes for pass/fail performance on generated synthetic problems.
Both adapters are optimized with GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)).
For each prompt, the active policy samples a group of responses, assigns
scalar rewards, computes group-normalized advantages, and performs a
clipped on-policy update. We use asymmetric clipping with
$`(\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}})=(0.20,0.28)`$
following DAPO ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)). We set the KL
coefficient to zero following the test-time adaptation setup of
[Hübotter et al. (2025)](#bib.bib16), so the update is driven by
target-specific rewards rather than by explicit regularization toward
the initialization.

Each solver step uses a batch of $`48`$ synthetic problem slots: $`16`$
fresh problems generated by the current proposer and $`32`$ replayed
problems sampled from the synthetic problem history. For each synthetic
problem, the solver samples a group of $`8`$ rollouts and receives
binary execution rewards on held-out generated tests. Replay eligibility
is determined by the most recent recorded solver solve rate for each
historical synthetic problem. When a replayed problem is sampled, stale
rewards are not reused: the current solver policy generates fresh
rollouts, pass/fail rewards are recomputed, and the stored solve rate
used for future proposer context and replay eligibility is overwritten.
The synthetic problem history has no fixed horizon; accepted synthetic
problems remain eligible for replay whenever their most recent recorded
solve rate lies in the learnability window.

## Appendix C Baseline Implementation and Diagnostics

This appendix records baseline-specific details that are separate from

### C.1 ACE Baseline Implementation

We include ACE ([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib48)) as a test-time
context-optimization baseline. In contrast to TTSP, ACE does not update
model weights. Instead, it maintains a problem-specific textual
playbook, revises that playbook from execution feedback, and conditions
later candidate generations on the revised playbook. ACE therefore tests
whether gains can be obtained from context-only adaptation, rather than
from TTSP’s weight-updating policy adaptation.

For a controlled comparison, all ACE roles use the same base model as
our TTSP experiments, Qwen3-8B. Each ACE iteration consists of several
generation–reflection rounds. In each round, the Generator proposes a
Python solution using the current playbook, the candidate is executed
against the feedback tests available under the current verifier
condition, and the Reflector turns the resulting execution signal into a
concrete diagnosis of the attempt. After the rounds in an iteration are
complete, the Curator converts the accumulated reflections into a
structured update to the playbook, which is then used in the next
iteration.

We evaluate ACE under the same feedback regimes as the other test-time
baselines: public tests, private tests, public + private tests, and
calibrated artificial verifier tests. The feedback exposed to ACE is
restricted to the tests available in the corresponding condition. The
full public + private evaluator $`v^{\star}`$ is used only for held-out
monitoring after each round and iteration, and is not revealed to the
playbook except in the public + private oracle condition.

ACE is evaluated in online mode with one evolving playbook per target
problem. We use $`15`$ ACE iterations, at most $`5`$
generation–reflection rounds per iteration, deterministic decoding
during playbook evolution, a maximum generation length of $`16{,}384`$
tokens, and a maximum model context length of $`40{,}960`$ tokens.

### C.2 Functional Majority Voting Baseline

We implement Functional Majority Voting (FMV) following [Launer et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib50). The FMV selection rule is input-only: it uses
target problem inputs to compare candidate programs by functional
behavior, but does not require target problem outputs.

Let $`\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\}`$ be candidate programs and let
$`\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}\}`$ be the target problem input cases
used for functional comparison. Executing candidate $`c_{i}`$ on input
$`x_{k}`$ produces output string $`o_{i,k}`$. We discard candidates that
fail to execute on the comparison inputs and denote the remaining valid
candidates by $`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{val}}`$.

For each valid candidate $`c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{val}}`$, we
compute the FMV score

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S(c_{i})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{val}}\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{1}\{o_{i,k}=o_{j,k}\}.
``` |  |

This score counts pairwise functional agreement between candidate
$`c_{i}`$ and the other valid candidates over the comparison inputs. The
original FMV selection rule returns a candidate in

|     |                                                        |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \arg\max_{c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{val}}}S(c_{i}).
       ```                                                     |     |

In our implementation, when multiple candidates attain the maximum
score, we keep all tied maximizers:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{FMV}}=\left\{c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{val}}:S(c_{i})=\max_{c_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{val}}}S(c_{j})\right\}.
``` |  |

For the verifier-strength comparison in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier"),
we wrap this input-only FMV rule with the current verifier condition.
Given verifier $`v`$, we first form the verifier-admitted candidate pool

|     |                                             |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{C}_{v}=\{c\in\mathcal{C}:v(c)=1\}.
       ```                                          |     |

We then apply the FMV rule above to $`\mathcal{C}_{v}`$, using the
available target problem input cases for functional comparison. This
wrapper uses the same verifier condition as the rest of the evaluation
sweep, while
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
reports the access required by the FMV voting rule itself. The selected
candidates are then passed to the common filtered evaluation protocol in
Appendix [A.5](#A1.SS5 "A.5 Filtered evaluation metric ‣ Appendix A Target Problems and Evaluation Protocol ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier");
when the same verifier has already been used to form
$`\mathcal{C}_{v}`$, this final pass is idempotent except for the shared
empty-set handling convention.

## Appendix D Synthetic Curriculum Quality and Dynamics

Self-generated training introduces two concrete stability risks. First,
the proposer can generate invalid or uninformative synthetic problems.
Second, the solver can over-specialize to a narrow generated
distribution. This section reports diagnostics for those risks during
TTSP adaptation on HardLCBv6. These diagnostics are logged only for
analysis; they are not used as rewards, filters, or checkpoint-selection
criteria.

#### Operational criteria.

A synthetic problem is used for solver training only after it passes the
validity filter in
Section [4](#S4 "4 TTSP: Test-Time Self-Play ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").
Among accepted problems, the proposer reward in
Equation [1](#S4.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ Rewards. ‣ 4 TTSP: Test-Time Self-Play ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
favors intermediate solver success, and the replay buffer samples
problems whose most recent solver solve rate lies in the eligibility
window in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B TTSP Training Details and Hyperparameters ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").
Thus the relevant checks are operational: rejected generations should be
excluded before solver updates, proposer rewards should retain a
non-degenerate high-reward tail, and the generated curriculum should not
collapse to a single policy or program family. These checks address the
stability concern noted in
Section [6](#S6 "6 Discussion ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier");
similar concerns arise in recent data-free and self-play LLM training
methods ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib42); [Huang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib43); [Chae et al., 2025](#bib.bib47); [Bailey et al.,
2026](#bib.bib51)).

### D.1 Synthetic problem rejection diagnostics

Before a generated synthetic problem enters solver training, it must
pass the validity filter described in
Section [4](#S4 "4 TTSP: Test-Time Self-Play ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").
Figure [9](#A4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ D.1 Synthetic problem rejection diagnostics ‣ Appendix D Synthetic Curriculum Quality and Dynamics ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
reports pre-solver-training rejections, averaged over target problems
and decomposed by implementation-level failure type. Rejected
generations are excluded from the solver batch and produce no solver
rollout rewards; instead, they receive the fixed invalid-generation
proposer penalty.

The failure types in
Figure [9](#A4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ D.1 Synthetic problem rejection diagnostics ‣ Appendix D Synthetic Curriculum Quality and Dynamics ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier")
refine the higher-level filter definition in
Section [4](#S4 "4 TTSP: Test-Time Self-Play ‣ Test-Time Self-Play: Scaling without a Verifier").
Invalid generated output and missing tags correspond to parsing
failures; length-limit, subgoal-too-long, and python-too-long failures
correspond to structural constraints; timeouts and runtime errors
correspond to execution failures; and insufficient, duplicate, or
all-identical test cases correspond to held-out-test degeneracy.

The total number of rejected generations decreases over training.
Invalid generated output is the largest rejection category, followed by
timeouts and insufficient test cases. This diagnostic shows that TTSP
trains on a filtered subset of proposer generations rather than treating
all generated problems as valid training data.

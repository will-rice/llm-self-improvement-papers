###### Abstract

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) provides denser token-level
supervision and better computational efficiency than Reinforcement
Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). However, this denser
supervision may introduce substantial noise and training instability.
Existing improvements often rely on high-variance per-token statistics
and introduce extra hyperparameters and trade-offs. Based on the
advantage formulation in RLVR, we analyze the OPSD objective from the
same perspective, incorporating outcome correctness signals. We find
that vanilla OPSD imposes insufficient penalties and excessive rewards
on incorrect trajectories because it applies a fixed divergence
objective regardless of outcome correctness. Furthermore, the
reliability of teacher supervision is associated with both trajectory
outcome and the cumulative average teacher entropy along the rollout.
Based on these observations, we propose Outcome-Guided On-Policy
Self-Distillation (OG-OPSD), which dynamically adapts both the
divergence objective and distillation position according to binary
outcome rewards and the cumulative average teacher entropy. Extensive
experiments show that OG-OPSD consistently improves the performance of
vanilla OPSD and multiple strong baselines in mathematical reasoning,
multimodal reasoning, and out-of-distribution tasks across Qwen3 models
at 1.7B, 4B, and 8B scales, as well as Qwen3-VL-2B.

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) and on-policy
distillation (OPD) have emerged as effective approaches to post-training
large language models (LLMs). RLVR improves multi-step reasoning by
optimizing automatically verifiable outcomes, avoiding costly human
preference annotations ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib20); [Jaech et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)). However, its
commonly used binary outcome reward provides only a single signal for an
entire trajectory, leaving token-level credit assignment unresolved. For
example, outcome-supervised GRPO assigns the same group-normalized
advantage to every token within a response ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib20)). OPD provides denser supervision by matching the
teacher’s next-token distributions along the student’s own rollouts ([Gu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib2); [Lu and
Lab, 2025](#bib.bib3)), but conventional OPD typically relies on a
stronger external teacher.

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) removes the need for a separate
teacher by using the same model under different conditioning contexts
([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib9);
[Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)). The student observes only the
question, whereas the teacher additionally receives privileged
information, such as a reference solution. This asymmetry enables dense
self-supervision, but it does not guarantee that the resulting guidance
is useful at every position. Recent studies report optimization
instability and degraded reasoning performance in OPSD ([Zhu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13); [Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)), highlighting the
need to determine how and where teacher supervision should be applied.

The distillation objective directly shapes this supervision. Forward and
reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergences weight teacher-student
discrepancies differently, inducing distinct token-level learning
signals. Vanilla OPSD applies a fixed divergence across all trajectories
and positions. Recent work has explored adaptive objectives ([Jin et
al., 2026](#bib.bib22); [Jung et al., 2025](#bib.bib23); [Jia et al.,
2026](#bib.bib24); [Wu et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)), as well as token
reweighting and selection ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib26); [Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib33); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib34); [Ziheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11)). Token probabilities and entropy characterize local
model behavior, whereas outcome correctness provides a direct signal of
task success. Although this outcome signal does not identify the
contribution of each token, it can inform how dense supervision is used.
Our central idea is to condition both the choice of divergence and the
position of distillation under the guidance of outcome correctness.

We first analyze forward KL (FKL) and reverse KL (RKL) through their
implicit token-level advantages (see their conceptual comparisons in
Figure [1(a)](#S1.F1.sf1 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
This formulation reveals an asymmetry in their weighting: FKL assigns
stronger rewards to tokens underweighted by the student relative to the
teacher and bounds penalties, whereas RKL imposes stronger penalties on
tokens overestimated by the student. These properties motivate FKL on
correct trajectories to learn teacher-preferred continuations while
limiting negative feedback on successful behavior, and RKL on incorrect
trajectories to reduce student overestimation relative to the teacher.

However, divergence selection alone cannot determine whether the teacher
maintains reliability during deployment. We investigate this question by
truncating student trajectories at varying lengths and asking the
teacher to complete each prefix. Across model scales and mathematical
reasoning benchmarks, teacher completion accuracy declines as prefixes
from incorrect trajectories grow longer, while remaining comparatively
stable on correct trajectories (see empirical results in
Figures [1(b)](#S1.F1.sf2 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and [1(d)](#S1.F1.sf4 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
This pattern of prefix failure motivates restricting distillation on
incorrect trajectories to shorter prefixes. We further examine the
cumulative average teacher entropy, whose curves for correct and
incorrect trajectories provide an empirical basis for estimating the
truncation cutoff (see empirical results in
Figures [1(c)](#S1.F1.sf3 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and [1(e)](#S1.F1.sf5 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
We observe the same trend on Qwen3-1.7B (see empirical results in
Figures [4(a)](#A3.F4.sf1 "In Figure 4 ‣ Appendix C More Results On Qwen3-1.7B ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and [4(b)](#A3.F4.sf2 "In Figure 4 ‣ Appendix C More Results On Qwen3-1.7B ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")).

Building on these findings, we propose _Outcome-Guided On-Policy
Self-Distillation_ (OG-OPSD), which combines outcome-guided divergence
selection with selective prefix truncation. OG-OPSD applies FKL
throughout correct trajectories and restricts RKL to prefixes of
incorrect trajectories. The cutoff is estimated from the last crossing
of the cumulative average teacher entropy curves for the two outcome
groups. Trajectory-level correctness thus determines how and where to
distill, while the teacher supplies dense token-level supervision within
the selected prefix.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  We connect the asymmetric token-level weighting of FKL and RKL to
  trajectory correctness, and identify an outcome-dependent decline in
  teacher continuation accuracy as incorrect prefixes increase in
  length.
- •
  We propose a simple but effective method called OG-OPSD, which
  combines outcome-guided KL divergence selection with an entropy-based
  prefix cutoff to control how and where self-distillation is applied.
- •
  Experiments show consistent improvements over vanilla OPSD and strong
  baselines with Qwen3 models at different scales. Evaluations on
  Qwen3-VL and out-of-distribution datasets further support the
  effectiveness of the proposed method.

## 2 Related Work

On-policy distillation. OPD combines student-generated rollouts with
dense teacher supervision and has been adopted in recent LLM
post-training pipelines ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib4); [Zeng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5); [Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib7)). OPSD removes the need for an external teacher by
conditioning the same model on privileged information ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8); [Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib9); [Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10)). Recent works improve the allocation of dense
supervision through token weighting, selection, and rollout truncation.
[Liu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib26) analyze how teacher reliability varies
with token position and introduce position-dependent loss weights. [Wang
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib33) select token positions according to
teachability, distinguishing learnable teacher-student disagreement from
incompatible predictions. [Zhang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib34) use student
entropy to determine the direction of supervision, encouraging agreement
with the teacher at low-entropy positions and divergence at high-entropy
positions to preserve exploration. Other studies address declining
teacher reliability along student-generated prefixes. ESR restricts
rollout length ([Ziheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)); Prune-OPD monitors
teacher-student compatibility and accumulates drift signals to
downweight unreliable supervision and truncate rollouts ([Yang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib35)); and IW-OPD weights token losses using cumulative
prefix discrepancies ([Xie et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)). OG-OPSD makes the
distillation span conditional on trajectory outcome: it retains full
supervision on correct trajectories and uses cumulative average teacher
entropy to estimate a prefix cutoff for incorrect trajectories.

Divergence selection. The choice of divergence determines how
teacher-student discrepancies translate into learning signals. MiniLLM
minimizes sequence-level RKL ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)), GKD
supports alternative divergence objectives ([Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2)), and DistiLLM introduces skewed KL divergences ([Ko et
al., 2024](#bib.bib32)). Subsequent work adapts the objective to local
distributional properties. EOPD augments RKL with FKL at positions with
high teacher entropy ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)). ToDi combines FKL
and RKL using token-wise teacher-student log-probability ratios ([Jung
et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)), while AOPD preserves positive-advantage
reinforcement and applies FKL guidance in non-positive-advantage regions
([Jia et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)). AKL adjusts the relative weights of
FKL and RKL according to discrepancies in the head and tail of the
next-token probability distribution ([Wu et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)).
Guided by an analysis of the implicit token-level advantages of FKL and
RKL, OG-OPSD adopts a trajectory-level selection rule: FKL for correct
rollouts and RKL for incorrect ones. This choice depends only on the
binary verifier outcome and is coupled with selective prefix truncation
to account for changes in teacher reliability.

## 3 Method

OG-OPSD uses the binary outcome of the verifier to select the divergence
and determine which token positions receive supervision. It applies
forward KL to complete correct trajectories, and reverse KL to prefixes
of incorrect trajectories, with the prefix length estimated from teacher
entropy.

### 3.1 Preliminaries

OPSD uses the same model $`\pi_{\theta}`$ as both the teacher and the
student, with different conditioning contexts ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8)). Given a question $`x`$, the student generates a
response $`y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$. The
teacher also receives privileged information $`y^{\star}`$. At position
$`n`$, let $`s_{n}=(x,y_{<n})`$ denote the question and the
student-generated prefix. The student and the teacher define next-token
distributions in the vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
p_{S}^{n}(\cdot)=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{n}),\qquad p_{T}^{n}(\cdot)=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{n},y^{\star})\right],
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\operatorname{sg}`$ denotes the stop-gradient. During
optimization, sampled prefixes are treated as fixed, and gradients flow
only through the student branch.

Vanilla OPSD averages local distillation losses over the entire
response:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}(\theta;y)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}d_{n},
``` |  | (2) |

using the same divergence at every position. We consider two forms of KL
divergence:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle d_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{T}^{n}\|p_{S}^{n})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{T}^{n}(v)\log\frac{p_{T}^{n}(v)}{p_{S}^{n}(v)},`$ |  | (3) |
|  | $`\displaystyle d_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{S}^{n}\|p_{T}^{n})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{S}^{n}(v)\log\frac{p_{S}^{n}(v)}{p_{T}^{n}(v)}.`$ |  | (4) |

OPSD provides supervision signals through the distribution differences
of the teacher-student model, while RLVR provides supervision through a
deterministic outcome reward $`R(y)`$ that can be automatically verified
against ground truth. For a fixed question $`x`$, the expected-reward
objective is
$`\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{RLVR}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}[R(y)]`$,
with REINFORCE gradient

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{RLVR}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[R(y)\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{S}^{n}(y_{n})\right].
``` |  | (5) |

Here, the same trajectory-level reward weights every sampled token.
Distillation provides finer-grained supervision through teacher-student
discrepancies.

### 3.2 Outcome-Guided Divergence Selection

To compare how FKL and RKL allocate supervision, we express their local
gradients in the score-function form of
Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "In 3.1 Preliminaries ‣ 3 Method ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation").
For a fixed prefix, define $`r_{n}(v)=p_{T}^{n}(v)/p_{S}^{n}(v)`$. With
gradients stopped through the teacher, the descent directions are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle-\nabla_{\theta}d_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{S}^{n}(v)\bigl(r_{n}(v)-1\bigr)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{S}^{n}(v),`$ |  | (6) |
|  | $`\displaystyle-\nabla_{\theta}d_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{S}^{n}(v)\log r_{n}(v)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{S}^{n}(v).`$ |  | (7) |

The unit baseline in
Eq. [6](#S3.E6 "In 3.2 Outcome-Guided Divergence Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
leaves the gradient unchanged because the score function has zero
expectation under the student distribution. Detailed derivations appear
in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Gradient Derivation ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation").
We refer to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}(v)=r_{n}(v)-1,\qquad A_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}(v)=\log r_{n}(v)
``` |  | (8) |

as the implicit token-level advantages.

For any candidate token $`v`$, the implicit advantages
$`A_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}(v)`$ and $`A_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}(v)`$ have the
same sign: both are positive when the teacher assigns greater
probability to the token than the student, negative when it assigns
less, and zero when the probabilities coincide. In this representation,
the two divergences never disagree on the direction of the update for a
given token and differ in the magnitude of the coefficients assigned to
each teacher-student discrepancy.

The FKL advantage $`r_{n}(v)-1`$ is bounded below by $`-1`$, limiting
the magnitude of negative coefficients for tokens that the student
overweights relative to the teacher ($`r_{n}(v)<1`$). For underweighted
tokens ($`r_{n}(v)>1`$), its positive advantage grows linearly without
bound. In contrast, the RKL advantage $`\log r_{n}(v)`$ is unbounded in
both directions: it becomes arbitrarily negative as $`r_{n}(v)`$
approaches zero, while its positive values grow only logarithmically.

Since $`\log r\leq r-1`$ for all $`r>0`$, with equality only at $`r=1`$,
FKL assigns larger positive advantages when $`r_{n}(v)>1`$, whereas RKL
assigns more negative advantages when $`r_{n}(v)<1`$. Thus, in this
advantage representation, divergence selection controls the balance
between positive and negative token-level weighting: FKL emphasizes
positive feedback for student-underweighted tokens, while RKL emphasizes
negative feedback for student-overweighted tokens.

We next analyze the requirements for the properties of the advantage
function under different values of the verifier’s judgment
$`R(y)\in\{0,1\}`$ for the output $`y`$ sampled from the student. This
motivates the selection of the divergence function according to the
verifier’s judgment $`R(y)`$, rather than applying a fixed divergence
function for all trajectories as in vanilla OPSD.

Correct trajectories ($`R(y)=1`$). For a successful trajectory, we aim
to reinforce teacher-preferred tokens and absorb the extra probability
mass it assigns to valid alternative continuations, without aggressively
disrupting an already successful trajectory due to teacher conservatism.
This requires a divergence with a gentle, bounded penalty when
$`r_{n}(v)<1`$ and a strong reward when $`r_{n}(v)>1`$. FKL supports
this goal through its larger positive advantages and bounded negative
advantages. We therefore apply FKL to correct trajectories.

Incorrect trajectories ($`R(y)=0`$). For an unsuccessful trajectory, we
place greater emphasis on suppressing tokens where the student is
overconfident relative to the teacher ($`r_{n}(v)<1`$), the likely
culprits behind the error. The FKL’s penalty has a lower bound, limiting
the correction speed. However, the unbounded penalty in RKL can achieve
a stronger suppression effect. Conversely, for teacher-preferred tokens
in a failed rollout, the teacher’s rationalization of $`y^{\star}`$ may
be locally noisy. Therefore, we tend to adopt a cautious logarithmic
damping update rather than an aggressive reinforcement, which is in line
with RKL. We thus apply RKL to incorrect trajectories.

This yields an outcome-guided divergence selection rule. When
$`R(y)=1`$, $`d_{n}=d_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}`$, and when $`R(y)=0`$,
$`d_{n}=d_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}`$. It lets the model allocate token-level
credit in a manner consistent with the coarse and trajectory-level
outcome correctness signal, while retaining the dense and token-level
granularity of self-distillation. The analysis of the advantage function
$`A_{n}(v)`$ above is based on a necessary precondition: the guidance
signal $`p_{T}^{n}(v)`$ must be reliable, which we examine next.

### 3.3 Selective Prefix Distillation

Diagnose teacher reliability. The teacher is conditioned on
student-generated prefixes, which may contain errors or deviate from its
preferred reasoning paths. As these prefixes lengthen, the teacher may
become less able to produce a correct continuation, a phenomenon known
as *prefix failure*. We examine whether this degradation differs between
correct and incorrect student trajectories.

We sample responses from Qwen3 models ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib7))
on OpenThoughts-Math30K ([Guha et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)), DeepScaleR
([Tan et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)), MATH-500 ([Hendrycks et al.,
2021](#bib.bib17)), and DeepMath ([He et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). We
divide the responses into correct and incorrect pools,
$`\mathcal{D}_{c}`$ and $`\mathcal{D}_{i}`$, according to their verifier
outcomes. We then concatenate the teacher prompt with student-generated
prefixes truncated at varying lengths, and let the teacher resume
generation from different token positions across both correct and
incorrect student trajectories to complete the remaining reasoning.
Teacher completion accuracy (TCA) measures the accuracy rate of these
completion results.

Figures [1(b)](#S1.F1.sf2 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and [1(d)](#S1.F1.sf4 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
show a clear difference between the two groups. For incorrect student
trajectories, the TCA degrades markedly with truncation length. In
contrast, for correct trajectories, it remains stable or even improves
regardless of truncation position. These observations motivate
distillation over the full response when the student succeeds and
restrict distillation to a prefix when it fails.

Estimate the prefix cutoff. We want to seek a boundary separating two
regimes of teacher reliability on incorrect trajectories: one where the
teacher signal remains valid, and one beyond which guidance quality
begins to deteriorate. Following common practice, we use entropy as an
indicator of the internal state of the model during token generation.
Unlike most work that analyzes token-level entropy ([Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib26); [Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)), we argue that
single-token entropy is a high-variance and fluctuating signal whose
value at any position cannot accurately represent the overall state of
the model at that point. We use cumulative average teacher entropy to
summarize uncertainty along a prefix and reduce sensitivity to
fluctuations at individual positions:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{H}_{n}(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}H(p_{T}^{k}),\qquad H(p)=-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p(v)\log p(v).
``` |  | (9) |

For variable-length responses, let
$`\mathcal{D}_{a}(n)=\{y\in\mathcal{D}_{a}:|y|\geq n\}`$ contain the
trajectories that reach position $`n`$, where $`a\in\{c,i\}`$. The
outcome-conditioned averages are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{H}_{n}^{a}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{a}(n)|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{D}_{a}(n)}\bar{H}_{n}(y),\qquad a\in\{c,i\},
``` |  | (10) |

evaluated at positions where both pools are nonempty.

Algorithm 1 Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation

As shown in
Figures [1(c)](#S1.F1.sf3 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and [1(e)](#S1.F1.sf5 "In Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
the two curves almost overlap in the early stage. This indicates that
even incorrect reasoning trajectories remain in a regime where the
teacher can still provide relatively reliable supervision in early
stages, yielding supervision signals of comparable quality to those on
correct trajectories. Beyond their last intersection, the two curves
gradually diverge, with the incorrect trajectories showing higher
cumulative average entropy than the correct trajectories. This
divergence is consistent with the hypothesis that accumulating errors
cause the trajectory prefix to deviate from the conditional distribution
expected by the teacher model, increasing its uncertainty. At this time,
the teacher’s supervision signal is no longer consistent with the
reliable signal on correct trajectories. We then consider that the
reliability of the teacher signal begins to deteriorate at this point.
We use the two curves’ last crossing as an empirical cutoff. Let
$`\Delta_{n}=\bar{H}_{n}^{i}-\bar{H}_{n}^{c}`$, and let $`L`$ be the
largest position represented in both pools. We define

|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math

0,&\mathcal{C}=\varnothing.\end{cases}\end{aligned}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{C}=\left\{n\in\{2,\ldots,L\}:\Delta_{n-1}\Delta_{n}\leq 0\right\}
``` |  | (11) |

The resulting cutoff $`N_{t}`$ is shared across incorrect trajectories.

Overall objective. Combining outcome-guided divergence selection with
prefix restriction gives OG-OPSD. For a response of length $`N`$, the
number of supervised positions $`T(y)`$ equals $`N`$ when $`R(y)=1`$ and
equals $`\min\{N,N_{t}\}`$ when $`R(y)=0`$. The per-response loss is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OG\text{-}OPSD}}(\theta;y)=\frac{1}{\max\{1,T(y)\}}\sum_{n=1}^{T(y)}\Bigl[R(y)d_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}+\bigl(1-R(y)\bigr)d_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}\Bigr].
``` |  | (12) |

Thus, correct responses receive FKL supervision at every position, while
incorrect responses receive RKL supervision only within the retained
prefix. The truncation applies only when computing the distillation
loss.

We average the per-response losses over each minibatch and update the
model through the student branch.
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.3 Selective Prefix Distillation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
summarizes the training procedure.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

We conduct experiments with the Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B
Instruct models ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)). All three models are
trained on OpenThoughts-Math30K ([Guha et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)), which
contains 29,434 mathematical reasoning examples, each comprising a
question, a reference solution, and a final answer. At each distillation
iteration, the student model receives only the question and generates
its own response, while the teacher model additionally receives the
reference solution containing reasoning steps and the final answer. To
evaluate the method on multimodal tasks, we also train
Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct ([Bai et al., 2025](#bib.bib36)) on FinMME ([Luo et
al., 2025](#bib.bib37)). Dataset preparation details are provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation Details ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation").

Baseline. We compare OG-OPSD with ten baselines for mathematical
reasoning. *SFT* ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib29)) trains on reference
solutions, while *GRPO* ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib20)) optimizes
verifiable rewards based on final-answer correctness. The remaining
eight baselines use distillation. *OPSD* ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8)) applies a fixed FKL objective uniformly along
student-generated trajectories. *EOPD* ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib22))
selects the divergence at each token position using teacher entropy.
*AVSD* ([Nguyen et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)) combines supervision from
multiple privileged teacher views. *REOPOLD* ([Ko et al.,
2026](#bib.bib28)) uses the teacher-student log-likelihood ratio as a
token-level reward, together with reward clipping and entropy-based
sampling. *Purified OPSD* ([Shen et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)) uses
pointwise mutual information to construct distillation targets that
reduce reference-induced interference. *DOPD* ([Yu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib31)) dynamically routes teacher-student supervision
according to token-level privilege advantage gaps. *ESR* ([Ziheng et
al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) applies a uniform truncation rule to all
trajectories. *PW-OPSD* ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)) weights
token-level distillation losses according to position. For multimodal
evaluation, we compare against *SFT* and *SDFT* ([Shenfeld et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9)), a self-distillation method developed for continual
learning. We also report the corresponding base model in all
comparisons.

Benchmarks. We evaluate mathematical reasoning on AIME 2024, AIME 2025,
and HMMT 2025, each containing 30 problems, using the official answer
keys to determine correctness. Following the OPSD evaluation protocol
([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)), we set the maximum generation length
to 38,912 tokens. We generate 12 responses per problem and report the
average accuracy over these samples and all problems (Avg@12). We also
report the unweighted mean across the three benchmarks.

For multimodal evaluation, we use the FinMME test set and three
out-of-distribution (OOD) benchmarks: MathVerse ([Zhang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib38)), MathVision ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)), and
MMMU_(Pro) ([Yue et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)). To assess cross-domain
retention after mathematical post-training, we additionally evaluate the
Qwen3 models on LiveCodeBench v5 (LCB v5) ([Jain et al.,
2025](#bib.bib41)).

### 4.2 Main Results

Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
reports the main results on Qwen3-4B. In the experiment, we use the
cumulative average teacher entropy to obtain the cutoff $`N_{t}=100`$.
OG-OPSD achieves the best performance in all three benchmarks, reaching
$`77.5`$, $`70.2`$ and $`46.9`$ on AIME24, AIME25 and HMMT25. OG-OPSD
improves over vanilla OPSD by 1.9, 2.3, and 2.4 points on AIME24,
AIME25, and HMMT25, respectively, and outperforms the strongest baseline
by 1.8 points on average across the three benchmarks. OG-OPSD directly
conditions the choice between FKL and RKL on the verifier’s correctness
signal $`R(y)`$, which provides a coarser but more reliable anchor than
purely distributional heuristics, and no additional hyperparameters or
trade-offs are required.

We further investigate how performance gains scale with model size
across all three benchmarks. For each model size, our method
consistently achieves the maximum performance gain over the evaluated
baselines. Moreover, as the model parameter size increases, the
advantage gap over vanilla OPSD continues to widen. In the HMMT25
dataset, the gap over vanilla OPSD ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib8))
increases from $`2.4`$ on Qwen3-4B to $`3.1`$ on Qwen3-8B, and on AIME25
it grows even more markedly from $`2.3`$ to $`3.9`$. Averaged over the
three benchmarks, the gain over vanilla OPSD increases steadily with
model size, from $`2.1`$ on Qwen3-1.7B to $`2.3`$ on Qwen3-4B and
$`2.7`$ on Qwen3-8B. This indicates that the gains from outcome-guided
divergence switching and selective truncation are not a phenomenon
specific to small models. A plausible explanation is that larger models
possess stronger contextual reasoning capacity and can provide
higher-quality teacher supervision signals. This reflects the
rationality and effectiveness of our dynamic selection of the KL
divergence based on the outcome to adapt to the requirements for token
reward or penalty magnitudes on different sampling trajectories. Due to
limitations in computing resources, we are unable to compare all the
baselines on the 1.7B and 8B models. However, existing results have
already demonstrated to some extent that the observed gain of our method
is not limited to the Qwen3-4B. We also notice that the gain of GRPO is
relatively smooth, and even shows a slight negative growth at 1.7B scale
on AIME24. This suggests that optimizing solely with sparse
final-outcome rewards is challenging for smaller models. By leveraging
this binary outcome signal to guide dense distillation supervision, our
method remains effective even for small student models.

### 4.3 Ablation Study

Figure 2: Ablation results of divergence selection strategy and
distillation position on AIME24, AIME25 and HMMT25 benchmarks.

We conduct ablation studies on both the divergence selection strategy
and the distillation position of OG-OPSD. We define five variants:
*OG-OPSD w/ Only-FKL* and *OG-OPSD w/ Only-RKL* replace the
outcome-guided divergence switching with a fixed FKL and a fixed RKL
respectively for all trajectories, while still truncating the incorrect
trajectories at position $`N_{t}`$. *OG-OPSD w/ All-Trunc* retains our
dynamic divergence selection but applies truncation at $`N_{t}`$ across
all trajectories regardless of correctness. *OG-OPSD w/o Trunc* only
uses dynamic divergence selection without any truncation. *OG-OPSD w/
C-RKL+I-FKL* reverses our divergence assignment, using RKL for correct
trajectories and FKL for incorrect ones.

The selection of the divergence. As shown in
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
under identical truncation treatment, OG-OPSD consistently outperforms
all alternative divergence-selection variants across the three
benchmarks. Both fixing a single divergence (w/ Only-FKL, w/ Only-RKL)
and reversing the divergence assignment (w/ C-RKL+I-FKL) lead to clear
performance drops. This provides evidence that the gains of OG-OPSD do
not simply come from having some adaptive mechanism, but specifically
from correctly pairing FKL with correct trajectories and RKL with
incorrect ones, consistent with our analysis in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Outcome-Guided Divergence Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation").

The selection of the distillation position. Replacing outcome-guided
truncation with all truncation (w/ All-Trunc) also yields consistent
performance drops across all benchmarks, showing that applying cutoff to
all trajectories regardless of their correctness, such as ESR ([Ziheng
et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)), discards the valid supervision signal from
the correct trajectories.

Figure 3: Train with different $`N_{t}`$ and evaluate on multiple
datasets.

The decline in the performance of *OG-OPSD w/o Trunc* indicates the
necessity of considering the reliability of the teacher’s guidance
signal. Before training, using
Eq. [11](#S3.E11 "In 3.3 Selective Prefix Distillation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
we calculate the truncation parameter $`N_{t}`$ as $`100`$ on the
training data. We further examine the sensitivity of $`N_{t}`$ in
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation").
We use different values of $`N_{t}`$ to train Qwen3-4B. After
calculating Pass@1 on OpenThoughts, AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25, we
normalize the results and also take the average. The performance of the
model peaks at $`N_{t}=100`$ and remains relatively stable within the
adjacent range. This indicates that $`N_{t}`$, derived from the
cumulative average teacher entropy, is robust to moderate perturbation
and does not require precise tuning to be effective.

## 5 Conclusion

We present OG-OPSD, which uses binary outcome rewards to guide
divergence selection and determine which token positions receive
supervision. Our analysis shows that the implicit token-level advantages
of FKL and RKL have matching signs but different magnitudes, motivating
FKL for correct trajectories and RKL for incorrect ones. We further
identify an outcome-dependent pattern of prefix failure: teacher
continuation accuracy declines with prefix length on incorrect
trajectories but remains stable or improves on correct ones.
Accordingly, OG-OPSD distills correct trajectories in full and restricts
distillation on incorrect trajectories to prefixes whose length is
estimated from cumulative average teacher entropy. Experiments on Qwen3
and Qwen3-VL show consistent improvements over the evaluated baselines,
with ablations supporting the contributions of both divergence selection
and selective truncation. These findings highlight how coarse but
reliable outcome signals can guide the use of dense self-distillation
supervision. Future work could extend this approach to graded rewards
and per-trajectory adaptive truncation.

## Appendix A Gradient Derivation

In this section we derive
Eq. [6](#S3.E6 "In 3.2 Outcome-Guided Divergence Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
and
Eq. [7](#S3.E7 "In 3.2 Outcome-Guided Divergence Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation").
For analytical clarity, we first derive the gradients of the unclipped
KL objectives. In implementation, we additionally clip individual KL
components as described in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation Details ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation").

#### Notation and assumptions.

Fix a position $`n`$ in a student-generated rollout, so that the prefix
$`s_{n}`$ is a given constant. For brevity, write

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{i}\triangleq p^{n}_{S}(v_{i}),\qquad q_{i}\triangleq p^{n}_{T}(v_{i}),\qquad r_{i}\triangleq r_{n}(v_{i})=\frac{q_{i}}{p_{i}}.
``` |  | (13) |

Both are categorical distributions over $`\mathcal{V}`$,
$`p_{i},q_{i}>0`$ and
$`\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|}q_{i}=1`$.
The teacher distribution is treated as a fixed target:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}q_{i}=0,\qquad\nabla_{\theta}p_{i}=p_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}.
``` |  | (14) |

The second equality is the log-derivative trick.

Since $`\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}=1`$ holds for all $`\theta`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\nabla_{\theta}p_{i}=\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}=\nabla_{\theta}1=0.
``` |  | (15) |

Consequently, for any constant $`c`$ that does not depend on $`i`$,

|     |                                                                |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\,c\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}=0.
       ```                                                             |     | (16) |

### A.1 Forward KL

According to the definition of Forward KL divergence,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{T}^{n}\|p_{S}^{n})=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}q_{i}\left(\log q_{i}-\log p_{i}\right).
``` |  | (17) |

The $`q_{i}`$ is a constant with respect to $`\theta`$, so the term
$`q_{i}\log q_{i}`$ vanishes under differentiation.

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}d_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}q_{i}\left(\log q_{i}-\log p_{i}\right)`$ |  | (18) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\left(\nabla_{\theta}\left(q_{i}\log q_{i}\right)-\nabla_{\theta}\left(q_{i}\log p_{i}\right)\right)`$ |  | (19) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}q_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}.`$ |  | (20) |

To write this in the form
$`\sum_{i}p_{i}A(v_{i})\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}`$, we use
$`q_{i}=p_{i}r_{i}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
-\nabla_{\theta}d_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\,r_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}.
``` |  | (21) |

Subtracting the zero term in
Eq. [16](#A1.E16 "In Notation and assumptions. ‣ Appendix A Gradient Derivation ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation")
with $`c=1`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle-\nabla_{\theta}d_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\,r_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}-\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}`$ |  | (22) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\left(r_{i}-1\right)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i},`$ |  | (23) |

which is exactly
Eq. [6](#S3.E6 "In 3.2 Outcome-Guided Divergence Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
with advantage $`A_{n}^{\mathrm{FKL}}(v)=r_{n}(v)-1`$. $`\square`$

### A.2 Reverse KL

According to the definition of Reverse KL divergence,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{S}^{n}\|p_{T}^{n})=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\left(\log p_{i}-\log q_{i}\right).
``` |  | (24) |

Now $`p_{i}`$ appears both as the weight and inside the logarithm, so we
apply the product rule:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}d_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\nabla_{\theta}p_{i}\left(\log p_{i}-\log q_{i}\right)+\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\left(\log p_{i}-\log q_{i}\right).
``` |  | (25) |

For the second sum, $`\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{i}=0`$ and
$`\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}=0`$ by
Eq. [15](#A1.E15 "In Notation and assumptions. ‣ Appendix A Gradient Derivation ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
so it’s equal to zero.

For the first sum, we apply the log-derivative trick:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}\nabla_{\theta}p_{i}\left(\log p_{i}-\log q_{i}\right)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\left(\log p_{i}-\log q_{i}\right)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}`$ |  | (26) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\left(-\log r_{i}\right)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i}.`$ |  | (27) |

Combining the two,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
-\nabla_{\theta}d_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}=\sum_{i}^{|\mathcal{V}|}p_{i}\log r_{i}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{i},
``` |  | (28) |

which is exactly
Eq. [7](#S3.E7 "In 3.2 Outcome-Guided Divergence Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
with advantage $`A_{n}^{\mathrm{RKL}}(v)=\log r_{n}(v)`$. $`\square`$

## Appendix B Implementation Details

Details of FinMME Dataset Processing. FinMME ([Luo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37)) is a comprehensive benchmark dataset designed for
evaluating multimodal large language models in the financial domain. It
comprises approximately 11,000 high-quality financial samples covering
18 subfields of finance and six major asset classes. In terms of
question types, the dataset includes single-choice questions,
multiple-choice questions, and numerical answer questions. In this work,
we first filter out the samples with excessively high image resolution.
Then, we adjust the prompts for different question types and extracted
subsets. The sampling process ensures that options A, B, C, and D appear
equally frequently in all answers. Finally, a training set of 1200
samples is constructed, including 600 multiple-choice questions and 600
numerical questions. A test set of 600 samples is also obtained,
consisting of 300 multiple-choice questions and 300 numerical questions.
We use the Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct to generate reference solutions for
this dataset as privileged information for the teacher model.

Training. Our training setup follows vanilla OPSD ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8)). All attention and multilayer perceptron projections
are adapted using LoRA with rank $`64`$ and scale $`128`$. The
privileged-teacher forward pass is evaluated with the LoRA adapter
disabled, so gradient updates are applied only to the student’s LoRA
parameters. We optimize with a learning rate of $`5\times 10^{-6}`$,
gradient-norm clipping at $`0.1`$, bfloat16 mixed precision, and
gradient checkpointing. The student samples one on-policy rollout per
problem at temperature $`1.1`$, top-p $`0.95`$, top-k $`20`$, generating
up to $`1,024`$ new tokens and the training context length is capped at
$`20,000`$ tokens. During rollout generation, the student receives only
the problem statement, rendered with the Qwen3 chat template and
enable_thinking=False. The teacher receives the problem together with
the reference solution (including the final answer). The specific
prompts are presented in
Table [4](#A2.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Appendix B Implementation Details ‣ Outcome-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation").
We use a per-device batch size of $`4`$ with $`2`$ gradient accumulation
steps, and the main experiments utilize $`4`$ NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 GPUs.
The data collator right-pads prompts within a batch. Every directed loss
term is computed over the full vocabulary at each non-padding completion
token. Both teacher and student logits are temperature-scaled by $`1.1`$
prior to computing the KL divergence, and, consistent with vanilla OPSD
([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)), individual KL components are clipped
with a cap of $`c=0.05`$. All baselines use their published default
hyperparameters.

Evaluation. We use a maximum generation length of $`38,912`$ tokens:
$`\texttt{max\_new\_tokens}=38912`$, $`M=12`$ samples per problem,
temperature $`1.0`$, top-p $`0.95`$, top-k disabled, and
enable_thinking=True, matching the vanilla OPSD evaluation setup. For a
single problem with $`M=12`$ generated solutions
$`\{y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(M)}\}`$, the predicted answer is extracted as the
content of the last \boxed{...} in $`y^{(i)}`$. Let $`c_{i}\in\{0,1\}`$

       \text{Avg@}12=\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{12}c_{i}.
       ```                                              |     | (29) |
````

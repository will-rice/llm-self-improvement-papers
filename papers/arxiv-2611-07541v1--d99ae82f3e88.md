###### Abstract

Spatial reasoning is a fundamental capability of multimodal large
language models (MLLMs), yet existing benchmarks mainly focus on static
scene understanding. When objects are moved or swapped, models must
infer not only spatial relations but also how interventions change the
scene state. To address this problem, we introduce CST-Bench, a
benchmark for counterfactual spatial reasoning. CST-Bench contains 7,082
examples covering 6 intervention types and 12 transformation operators,
with dense causal annotations including intervention sources, affected
relations, expected effects, invariant relations, and metric changes. We
benchmark general-purpose MLLMs on CST-Bench and further compare
post-training strategies on CST-Bench and standard spatial benchmarks.
Results show that methods effective for ordinary spatial reasoning can
still fail under counterfactual interventions. We further formulate
counterfactual spatial reasoning as causal state-transition modeling and
propose CT-OPSD, which uses CST-Bench annotations to guide a teacher
model in learning counterfactual state transitions and causal reasoning
chains. These chains are then distilled into a student model that
reasons only from real observations. Overall, experiment results
establish CST-Bench as a challenging benchmark and reusable supervision
resource for counterfactual spatial reasoning. CT-OPSD further provides
an initial demonstration of how its annotations can support model
improvement.

## 1 Introduction

Spatial reasoning benchmarks test whether multimodal large language
models (MLLMs) understand geometric structure rather than merely
recognizing objects. Existing benchmarks cover 2D relations, depth and
distance estimation, and object orientation  ([Fu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib11); [Tong et al., 2024](#bib.bib31); [Ma et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)), but mainly focus on static spatial reasoning.

However, real-world spatial reasoning also requires predicting how
relations change beyond an observed scene. Recent studies extend spatial
evaluation to large-scale urban scenes with aerial viewpoints ([Zhan
et al., 2025](#bib.bib46)), while counterfactual VQA modifies critical
image content to test whether models revise their predictions ([Niu
et al., 2021](#bib.bib23); [Chen et al., 2020](#bib.bib8)).
SiT-Bench ([Guo et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)) separates spatial cognition
from visual perception, whereas MindEdit-Bench ([Yu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib43)) examines object-level counterfactual spatial
reasoning in natural images. Nevertheless, existing benchmarks either
broaden scene coverage, separate perception from reasoning, or examine
isolated counterfactual edits, providing limited supervision for
complete intervention-induced spatial transitions.

![Refer to caption](2611.07541v1/task_v2.png)

Figure 1: Counterfactual spatial transition reasoning. Moving the lamp
backward changes its relation to the TV from in front of to behind,
while unaffected scene properties remain invariant.

We introduce CST-Bench, a benchmark centered on causal transitions
induced by spatial interventions. It contains 7,082 image-grounded
samples from distinct natural scenes, spanning 6 intervention families
and 12 fine-grained transition operators. Each sample pairs a single
query and target answer with dense annotations of source and target
states, affected relations, expected effects, preserved invariants, and
applicable metric changes. Final-answer accuracy is the primary metric,
while these annotations support diagnostic analysis and structured
causal-transition supervision.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ CST-Bench: Benchmarking Counterfactual Spatial Transition Reasoning with Causal Transition On-Policy Self-Distillation")
illustrates one complete transition from an observed state to its
counterfactual state.

We benchmark general-purpose MLLMs on CST-Bench and compare several
post-training strategies using a common backbone. Results reveal that
existing models often fail to update affected relations while preserving
invariants. Because this task requires tracing the consequences of an
intervention, we formulate it as causal state-transition modeling
 ([Urp’i et al., 2024](#bib.bib32); [Peters, Janzing, and Schölkopf,
2017](#bib.bib26); [Pearl, 2009](#bib.bib25)) and introduce CT-OPSD, a
benchmark-aligned causal transition on-policy self-distillation method.
Distinct from existing OPSD approaches, CT-OPSD explicitly trains its
teacher with structured source states, intervention effects, invariants,
and target states, without exposing these privileged annotations to the
student. On-policy alignment instead transfers the teacher’s
causal-transition behavior to a student that uses only the image and
question. Overall, CST-Bench provides a fine-grained evaluation and
supervision resource, while CT-OPSD demonstrates how its annotations can
improve counterfactual spatial reasoning while retaining strong
performance on standard spatial benchmarks.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  We formulate counterfactual spatial transition reasoning as a
  standalone evaluation task requiring MLLMs to predict
  intervention-induced relational changes and preserved spatial
  invariants.
- •
  We introduce CST-Bench, which grounds six interven- tion families and
  twelve transition operators in 7,082 dis- tinct natural scenes,
  providing systematic coverage of both changed and invariant spatial
  relations.
- •
  We benchmark general-purpose MLLMs and systematically compare
  post-training strategies, revealing a significant gap between static
  spatial understanding and counterfactual transition reasoning.
- •
  We propose CT-OPSD, a benchmark-aligned on-policy self-distillation
  method that leverages privileged causal-transition supervision to
  improve counterfactual spatial reasoning while preserving the
  resulting student model’s general spatial capabilities.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Spatial Reasoning Benchmarks

Spatial reasoning benchmarks have expanded beyond traditional visual
question answering. Large-scale spatial VQA supervision has been used
for qualitative relations and metric estimation ([Chen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib7)), while multifaceted reasoning has been evaluated under
common and uncommon camera viewpoints ([Ma et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)).
Evaluations now cover large-scale scenes ([Zhan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib46)) and object, camera, and regional relations across
multiple images ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)). Recent work also
unifies diverse spatial tasks within broader evaluation frameworks ([Wu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib38); [Cai et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)).

Collectively, these benchmarks broaden spatial evaluation from pairwise
relations to scene-level and multi-view reasoning, but mainly evaluate
relations within a fixed observed state rather than how that state
changes under an intervention. Their results also show that MLLMs
perform unevenly across spatial domains, viewpoints, modalities, and
reasoning skills. Spatially enhanced supervision may improve specific
tasks, but does not guarantee a consistent understanding of relational
changes. CST-Bench complements these benchmarks by testing whether
models update affected relations after an intervention while preserving
unaffected relations.

### 2.2 Counterfactual Spatial Reasoning

Spatial reasoning benchmarks have expanded from situated reasoning under
imagined egocentric perspectives ([Ma et al., 2023](#bib.bib22)),
ground-truth 3D annotations across indoor, outdoor, and simulated scenes
([Cheng et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)), and camera- and human-centered
localization ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)), to video-based observation
([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)) and large-scale 3D spatial QA derived
from raw video ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)). However, these
benchmarks primarily recover relations from observed or reconstructed
scenes while keeping the underlying scene state unchanged. SiT-Bench
separates spatial reasoning from visual perception ([Guo et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13)), while MindCube evaluates occluded objects and unseen
space from limited multi-view observations ([Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib35)). These settings assess world modeling and
reference-frame transformation, but mainly alter the observer rather
than the scene state.

Counterfactual reasoning has also been explored in visual QA and
event-centric video understanding ([Niu et al., 2021](#bib.bib23); [Chen
et al., 2020](#bib.bib8); [Yi et al., 2020](#bib.bib42)). MindEdit-Bench
further evaluates object-level counterfactual editing ([Yu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib43)). Nevertheless, existing benchmarks provide limited
supervision for diagnosing the complete causal transition induced by an
intervention. CST-Bench addresses this gap by formulating counterfactual
spatial reasoning as an intervention-induced state transition with dense
causal annotations, supporting both final-answer evaluation and
diagnosis of transition failures.

### 2.3 On-Policy Self-Distillation

Traditional knowledge distillation typically relies on fixed teacher
trajectories ([Hinton, Vinyals, and Dean, 2015](#bib.bib14); [Kim and
Rush, 2016](#bib.bib15); [Bengio et al., 2015](#bib.bib5)), which can
create a mismatch between training and student inference. Generalized
distillation connects knowledge distillation with learning using
privileged information ([Lopez-Paz et al., 2016](#bib.bib20); [Vapnik
and Vashist, 2009](#bib.bib33)). More recently, on-policy distillation
aligns teacher and student over student-generated trajectories ([Agarwal
et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)), including teacher and student policies
constructed from the same model but with different contexts ([Zhao
et al., 2026](#bib.bib48)). This paradigm has also been extended to
multimodal tasks. Vision-OPD uses local crops as privileged teacher
inputs and transfers fine-grained perception to a student that observes
only the full image ([Yuan et al., 2026](#bib.bib45)). However,
privileged conditions may create advantages that the student cannot
learn, resulting in the “privilege illusion” ([Yu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib44)). CT-OPSD instead uses CST-Bench annotations to train
a causal teacher and distills its transition reasoning into a student
policy; the annotations are used only during training, while the student
relies solely on the image and question at inference time.

## 3 CST-Bench: A Counterfactual Spatial Reasoning Benchmark

CST-Bench frames counterfactual spatial reasoning as updating a source
state after an explicit intervention. It contains six intervention
families and twelve fine-grained operators
(Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.3 On-Policy Self-Distillation ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ CST-Bench: Benchmarking Counterfactual Spatial Transition Reasoning with Causal Transition On-Policy Self-Distillation")),
constructed through deterministic generation, VLM consensus validation,
and human verification. Its dense annotations support diagnostic
evaluation and enable CT-OPSD, an auxiliary approach that transfers
transition reasoning to an observation-only student.

### 3.1 Task Taxonomy

CST-Bench organizes twelve transition operators into six intervention
families covering global, local, metric, translation, depth, and
viewpoint changes. Across 7,082 image-grounded samples, it recasts
static observations as intervention-conditioned transitions, jointly
testing whether models update affected relations and preserve
invariants.

#### Global Mirror Transformation.

This family tests globally consistent horizontal updates ([Ma et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)). Pairwise Left–Right Flip reverses a single relation,
whereas Multi-object Horizontal Order Flip updates multiple objects
consistently. Both preserve object identities, object count, and
vertical relations.

#### Object Position Swap.

This family applies identity-preserving local interventions ([Yu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib43)). Pairwise Relation Swap tests the direct effect
between exchanged objects, while Third-object Relation Update requires
propagating the change to an unchanged reference object.

![Refer to caption](2611.07541v1/data_constructionv6.png)

Figure 3: Overview of CST-Bench construction. Constraint-aware
generation jointly derives counterfactual states and annotations,
answer-blind VLM consensus filters ambiguous candidates, and
uncertainty-guided human verification resolves uncertain cases and
audits all transformation operators.

#### Metric Distance Transition.

This family distinguishes continuous distance sensitivity from
categorical relation updates ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Ma
et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)). Metric Distance Change (Relation Preserved)
changes distance while retaining the qualitative relation, whereas
Distance-to-Reference Near–Far Crossing moves it across the _near_–_far_
threshold ([Cohn and Renz, 2008](#bib.bib10)).

#### Translation-Based Crossing.

This family uses object translation to induce spatial relation changes
([Yu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib43)). Lateral Relation Crossing and
Above–Below Crossing test horizontal and vertical updates, while
Vertical Near–Far Crossing changes the near–far relation through
vertical displacement. Unaffected relations must remain unchanged.

#### Depth Transition.

Depth Order Change moves an object across a reference along the depth
axis, extending observed front–behind evaluation to intervention-based
state updates ([Fu et al., 2024](#bib.bib11); [Ma et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)). The depth ordering changes while image-plane
relations are preserved.

#### Viewpoint Reversal.

This family changes the observer while preserving the physical scene.
Front-to-Back and Side-to-Side Viewpoint Shifts require recomputing
observer-centered relations without altering the allocentric layout ([Li
et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib35); [Klatzky,
1998](#bib.bib16)).

### 3.2 Benchmark Construction

As shown in
Fig. [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Object Position Swap. ‣ 3.1 Task Taxonomy ‣ 3 CST-Bench: A Counterfactual Spatial Reasoning Benchmark ‣ CST-Bench: Benchmarking Counterfactual Spatial Transition Reasoning with Causal Transition On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
unlike standard spatial QA, the post-intervention state is not directly
observed and must be inferred from a hypothetical intervention ([Zhang
et al., 2024](#bib.bib47)). A valid sample must realize the intended
effect, propagate dependent relation changes, preserve invariants, and
remain visually unambiguous. We address this challenge through three
stages.

![Refer to caption](2611.07541v1/ct-opsdv7.png)

Figure 4: Overview of CT-OPSD with CST-Bench supervision. Privileged
transition annotations from CST-Bench train a causal teacher but are
never provided as student inputs. The frozen teacher aligns with the
observation-only student on student-generated prefixes, and only the
updated student is retained for inference.

#### Stage I: Constraint-Aware Candidate Generation.

We curate scenes from the VG150 split of Visual Genome ([Xu et al.,
2017](#bib.bib39); [Krishna et al., 2016](#bib.bib17)) with valid object
boxes, sufficient distinct objects, and geometrically unambiguous
relations. Pairwise operators require two usable objects, whereas
Third-object Relation Update requires three objects from distinct
categories. Relations are computed from normalized bounding boxes, with
positional references used when necessary.

Prior work shows that automatically generated spatial QA may contain
ambiguous references and shortcut-prone or unreliable labels ([Chen
et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Ma et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)). To address
these risks, a deterministic, operator-aware editor applies each
intervention, and a symbolic solver jointly derives the pre- and
post-intervention states, counterfactual QA pair, affected relations,
expected effects, invariants, and applicable metric changes. Deriving
these fields from the same edited state prevents contradictions between
the transition and its dense annotations. We reject candidates that
violate operator-specific relation constraints, contain invalid boxes or
ambiguous relations, provide insufficient relation separation, or move
objects outside the image boundary.

We score each remaining candidate by

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
s(c)=s_{\mathrm{ref}}+s_{\mathrm{rel}}+s_{\mathrm{edit}}+s_{\mathrm{vis}}+s_{\mathrm{nat}},
``` |  | (1) |

where the terms measure reference clarity, relation clarity, edit
effectiveness, post-edit visibility, and transformation naturalness.
Candidates with $`s(c)<8`$ are removed. The remaining candidates are
ranked by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r(c)=\frac{1}{2}\hat{s}_{\mathrm{op}}(c)+\frac{1}{2}\hat{s}_{\mathrm{global}}(c),
``` |  | (2) |

where the normalized scores are computed within an operator and
globally, respectively. We retain at most one highest-ranked
transformation per source image.

#### Stage II: Answer-Blind Consensus Validation.

Although LLM/VLM-based judging provides scalable validation, prior work
has identified systematic biases and limited reliability in single-pass
evaluation  ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib49); [Wang et al.,
2023a](#bib.bib34)). Drawing on multi-sample consistency ([Wang et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib37)), each candidate is independently reviewed $`K=3`$
times by a VLM. To prevent the symbolic label from influencing the
initial solution, the VLM first solves the problem without accessing the
generated answer and only then compares its prediction with the symbolic
label. It also scores scene–image consistency, reference clarity,
transformation fidelity, answer correctness, and option quality from 0
to 2. A candidate passes only under unanimous agreement:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Keep}(c)=\bigwedge_{k=1}^{K}\left[\hat{a}_{k}=a^{*}\land b_{k}=0\land\min_{j}q_{k,j}=2\right],
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\hat{a}_{k}`$ is the independently predicted answer, $`a^{*}`$
is the symbolic answer, $`b_{k}`$ indicates ambiguity, and $`q_{k,j}`$
is the score for criterion $`j`$. Candidates with repeated hard failures
are removed, while unresolved cases are routed to human verification.

#### Stage III: Uncertainty-Guided Human Verification.

Human reviewers inspect unresolved candidates and a stratified sample
covering all transformation operators. They verify reference clarity,
intervention plausibility, transition consistency, answer correctness,
and option quality. Ambiguous or inconsistent samples are corrected when
possible and otherwise excluded.

## 4 Causal Transition On-Policy Self-Distillation

As an application of CST-Bench’s dense annotations, CT-OPSD models
counterfactual spatial reasoning as causal state-transition prediction.
Distinct from standard OPSD, it explicitly trains a teacher to organize
source states, intervention effects, invariants, and target states into
a structured transition chain. The resulting behavior is distilled
through on-policy distribution matching, while the privileged
annotations are never provided as student inputs  ([Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib2); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib48)).

#### Causally Structured Teacher.

Let $`x=(I,q)`$ denote the observable input, where $`I`$ is the image
and $`q`$ contains the question, intervention description, and answer
options. CST-Bench additionally provides privileged transition
annotations

|     |                                      |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       z_{c}=(s^{0},u,e,\mathcal{I},s^{1}),
       ```                                   |     | (4) |

where $`s^{0}`$ and $`s^{1}`$ are the source and target states, $`u`$ is
the structured intervention, $`e`$ records affected relations and
expected effects, and $`\mathcal{I}`$ contains preserved invariants.
When applicable, $`e`$ also includes the corresponding metric change.
Together with the standard answer label $`a^{*}`$, these fields form

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tau^{*}:s^{0}\xrightarrow{\,u\,}(e,\mathcal{I})\xrightarrow{}s^{1}\xrightarrow{}a^{*}.
``` |  | (5) |

The teacher $`\pi_{\phi}`$ learns to generate this chain from $`x`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{teacher}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau^{*}|}\log\pi_{\phi}(\tau_{t}^{*}\mid x,\tau_{<t}^{*}).
``` |  | (6) |

The teacher is frozen after this stage.

#### Student Rollout and Causal Bridge.

The student $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is initialized from the same base model and
uses only $`x`$ as input. It generates an on-policy trajectory

|     |                                         |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \hat{\tau}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
       ```                                      |     | (7) |

([Ross, Gordon, and Bagnell, 2011](#bib.bib27)). At each step, the
frozen teacher and student evaluate the same student-generated prefix:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{T}^{(t)}=\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x,\hat{\tau}_{<t}),\qquad p_{S}^{(t)}=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\hat{\tau}_{<t}).
``` |  | (8) |

Thus, the causal bridge transfers the teacher’s structured transition
behavior on states encountered by the student, without exposing
$`z_{c}`$ or $`\tau^{*}`$ to the student.

#### Training Objective.

We align the two distributions along student rollouts using forward KL
divergence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\tau}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{T}^{(t)}\parallel p_{S}^{(t)}\right)\right].
``` |  | (9) |

The complete objective additionally includes standard answer
supervision:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CT\text{-}OPSD}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ans}}\left[-\log\pi_{\theta}(a^{*}\mid x)\right].
``` |  | (10) |

At inference, the teacher and all privileged transition annotations are
discarded, and the student predicts from $`x=(I,q)`$ alone.

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Implementation Details

We evaluate ten open-source multimodal large language models (MLLMs):
Qwen2.5-VL-3B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Qwen3-VL-4B ([Bai et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)), LLaVA-OneVision-7B ([Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)),
InternVL3.5-4B ([Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib36)), Phi-3.5-Vision-4B
([Abdin et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)), Gemma-3-4B-it ([Team et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib30)), RoboBrain2.0-7B ([Team et al., 2025a](#bib.bib29)),
SpatialThinker-3B ([Batra et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)), and SpaceR-SFT-7B
([Ouyang et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)). All foundation models are evaluated
without fine-tuning on CST-Bench.

We use the balanced 480-example evaluation split, which contains 40
examples for each of the twelve fine-grained operators. These operators
are grouped into six transition families: Global Mirror Transformation,
Object Position Swap, Metric Distance Transition, Translation-Based
Crossing, Depth Transition, and Viewpoint Reversal. All retained
examples keep the referenced objects visible before and after the
intervention; distance-related operators test continuous metric changes
or qualitative near–far threshold crossings rather than visibility
changes.

At inference time, every model receives only the image, the
counterfactual question, and the answer options. Models do not access
scene states, intervention effects, invariant relations, or other
privileged causal annotations. We use a common answer-only prompt,
greedy decoding, and a maximum of 64 newly generated tokens. Each
response is parsed as a multiple-choice answer.

### 5.2 Baselines and Evaluation Metrics

Using Qwen2.5-VL-3B as the common base, we compare the unadapted model,
answer-supervised SFT, GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib28)), OPSD
([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib48)), and CT-OPSD. All variants use the
same observable input $`(I,q)`$; privileged annotations are available
only when training the CT-OPSD teacher.

We report multiple-choice accuracy. Foundation models are evaluated by
family accuracy, Macro Avg. over the six families, and Micro Acc. over
all 480 examples. Post-trained models are additionally evaluated on
3DSRBench ([Ma et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)), CV-Bench ([Tong et al.,
2024](#bib.bib31)), and BLINK_(val) ([Fu et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)).

### 5.4 Family-wise Analysis

The leading model varies substantially by transition family.
Gemma-3-4B-it performs best on Global Mirror Transformation (52.5),
Object Position Swap (42.5), and Viewpoint Reversal (40.0).

one type of spatial update does not reliably transfer to other

only 25.0 on swapping and 30.0 on viewpoint reversal. Thus, strong
performance on image-plane translation alone is insufficient for
reliable counterfactual spatial reasoning across relational, metric,
depth, and reference-frame changes.

## 6 Analysis

The fundamental distinction of CST-Bench lies not merely in task
difficulty, but in the object of inference. Static benchmarks ask a
model to recognize a spatial state already present in an image, whereas
CST-Bench requires it to intervene on that state and predict a valid
successor under conservation constraints. It therefore tests whether the
model’s spatial representation is coherent and manipulable, rather than
a collection of independently recognized relation labels. The uneven
family-level performance is consistent with current MLLMs lacking such a
unified transition model.

Dense transition annotations are important because counterfactual
correctness has two inseparable sides: applying the intended change and
avoiding unintended changes. A model may fail by ignoring the
intervention, but it may also fail by updating too much. Final-answer
supervision alone does not distinguish these errors. CT-OPSD is
effective because its supervision reflects this structure: the teacher
learns selective state updating, and on-policy alignment transfers that
behavior at states encountered by the student, without providing
privileged evidence at inference.

The ablations reinforce this interpretation. Improvements on an isolated
diagnostic dimension do not necessarily improve overall performance
because effects and invariants must be satisfied jointly. The key
connection is therefore structural: CST-Bench defines valid spatial
transitions, its annotations expose their internal constraints, and
CT-OPSD converts those constraints into a learnable training signal.

## 7 Conclusion

We introduced CST-Bench, a counterfactual spatial reasoning benchmark
with 7,082 examples across six families and twelve operators. It
evaluates intervention-induced relational, metric, depth, and viewpoint
changes with dense causal transition annotations. We also proposed
CT-OPSD, which distills a privileged causal teacher into an
observation-only student. Experiments reveal uneven counterfactual
capabilities among existing MLLMs, while CT-OPSD improves CST-Bench
accuracy and retains strong performance on standard spatial benchmarks.

#### Global Mirror Transformation.

The query asks how an object is related to a reference after a
horizontal mirror transformation. A source relation of left of becomes
right of; object identity, object count, and vertical relations are
preserved.

#### Object Position Swap.

Two objects exchange positions, while the query may concern one
exchanged object and a third, unchanged reference object. The example
therefore tests whether the direct edit is propagated to the relevant
third-object relation.

#### Metric Distance Transition.

The intervention changes normalized distance while holding direction
fixed. The qualitative relation may remain unchanged or cross the near/
far boundary, separating metric sensitivity from categorical updates.

#### Translation-Based Crossing.

An object is translated across a reference horizontally or vertically.
The affected relation changes, while the orthogonal relation and object
properties remain invariant.

#### Depth Transition.

An object moves across a reference along the depth axis. The behind/ in
front of relation changes while image-plane left–right and above–below
relations are preserved.

#### Viewpoint Reversal.

The observer changes viewpoint while the physical scene remains fixed.
The task recomputes observer-centered relations without treating the
viewpoint change as an edit to the allocentric scene layout.

### B.1 Data Source and Evaluation Split

CST-Bench curates scenes from the VG150 split of Visual Genome with
valid object boxes, sufficient distinct objects, and geometrically
unambiguous relations. The source annotations supply object categories,
bounding boxes, and scene relations used to identify valid spatial
configurations. Pairwise operators require two usable objects;
Third-object Relation Update and multi-object order operators require
three distinct object categories.

intervention families and twelve transition operators. The balanced
evaluation split contains 480 examples, with 40 examples for each
operator. Samples are partitioned by source image so that source images
in the evaluation split are excluded from post-training data. This
grouped split prevents a model from observing another counterfactual

### B.2 Spatial Relation Definitions

For a box $`b_{i}=[x_{i}^{1},y_{i}^{1},x_{i}^{2},y_{i}^{2}]`$ in an
image of width $`W`$ and height $`H`$, the normalized center is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})=\left(\frac{x_{i}^{1}+x_{i}^{2}}{2W},\frac{y_{i}^{1}+y_{i}^{2}}{2H}\right).
``` |  | (11) |

For an ordered pair $`(i,j)`$, left of/right of are determined by the
sign of $`\tilde{x}_{i}-\tilde{x}_{j}`$, and above/below are determined
by the sign of $`\tilde{y}_{i}-\tilde{y}_{j}`$. Candidates with
insufficient center separation along the queried axis are rejected.

Normalized center distance is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(i,j)=\frac{\lVert c_{i}-c_{j}\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{W^{2}+H^{2}}}.
``` |  | (12) |

Near–far operators retain only pairs outside an ambiguity band around
the qualitative boundary. A relation-preserved sample changes $`d(i,j)`$
without changing its qualitative label, whereas a threshold-crossing
sample changes both the metric distance and the qualitative relation.

All boxes must have positive extent and remain within the image boundary
before and after the symbolic edit. Thus, every object needed to answer
a retained question remains visible. Front–behind labels are taken from
available scene annotations or from the operator-defined symbolic
update; they are not inferred from two-dimensional box centers.

### B.4 Constraint-Aware Candidate Generation

The principal construction difficulty is that the post-intervention
state is not directly visible in the source image. CST-Bench addresses
this issue with a deterministic, operator-aware editor and symbolic
solver. Starting from a curated source state, the editor applies an
intervention and the solver jointly derives the source state, target
state, affected relations, expected effects, invariants, applicable
metric changes, and final answer. Generating these fields from one
edited state prevents contradictions between a transition and its dense
annotations.

Candidates are discarded if they violate an operator-specific
constraint, contain invalid boxes or ambiguous references, lack
sufficient relation separation, fail to realize the intended effect, or
move a referenced object outside the image. Remaining candidates are
scored by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(c)=s_{\mathrm{ref}}+s_{\mathrm{rel}}+s_{\mathrm{edit}}+s_{\mathrm{vis}}+s_{\mathrm{nat}},
``` |  | (13) |

where the five terms respectively measure reference clarity, relation
clarity, edit effectiveness, post-edit visibility, and transformation
naturalness. Candidates with $`s(c)<8`$ are removed. The remaining
candidates are ranked by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r(c)=\frac{1}{2}\hat{s}_{\mathrm{op}}(c)+\frac{1}{2}\hat{s}_{\mathrm{global}}(c),
``` |  | (14) |

where the two normalized scores are computed within an operator and
globally, respectively. At most one highest-ranked transformation is
retained for each source image.

### B.5 Answer-Blind Consensus Validation and Human Verification

The symbolic label can be internally consistent while the image,
reference, or question remains ambiguous. CST-Bench therefore uses
answer-blind consensus validation before human verification. For each
candidate, a VLM independently solves the question $`K=3`$ times without
seeing the symbolic answer. It then compares its independent prediction
with the symbolic label and scores five criteria from 0 to 2:
scene–image consistency, reference clarity, transformation fidelity,
answer correctness, and option quality.

A candidate is retained only when all three reviews recover the symbolic
answer, report no ambiguity, and assign the maximum score to every
criterion:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Keep}(c)=\bigwedge_{k=1}^{K}\left[\hat{a}_{k}=a^{*}\land b_{k}=0\land\min_{j}q_{k,j}=2\right],
``` |  | (15) |

where $`\hat{a}_{k}`$ is the independent prediction, $`a^{*}`$ is the
symbolic answer, $`b_{k}`$ is the ambiguity indicator, and $`q_{k,j}`$
is the score of criterion $`j`$. Candidates with repeated hard failures
are excluded. Candidates without unanimous agreement are routed to human
verification rather than retained by a majority vote.

Human verification follows a two-part protocol. First, two reviewers
inspect each unresolved candidate independently. They assess reference
clarity, intervention plausibility, source-to-target transition
consistency, target answer correctness, and distractor quality. A
reviewer marks a sample as *retain*, *correct*, or *exclude*, with a
brief rationale for every correction or exclusion. Second, a stratified
audit samples retained examples from every operator to check that
automated filtering does not conceal operator-specific errors. When the
two reviewers disagree, a third reviewer adjudicates using the source
image, the symbolic transition record, and the operator definition in
Table [6](#A2.T6 "Table 6 ‣ B.3 Complete Operator Specification ‣ Appendix B CST-Bench Specification and Construction ‣ CST-Bench: Benchmarking Counterfactual Spatial Transition Reasoning with Causal Transition On-Policy Self-Distillation").
Only adjudicated retain or corrected decisions enter the final
benchmark; ambiguous or inconsistent samples are excluded.

## Appendix C CT-OPSD Implementation Details

### C.1 Teacher Supervision and Student Inputs

Let $`x=(I,q)`$ denote the observable image-question input. For
training, each CST-Bench example additionally provides the causal
transition annotations

|     |                                      |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       z_{c}=(s^{0},u,e,\mathcal{I},s^{1}),
       ```                                   |     | (16) |

where $`s^{0}`$ and $`s^{1}`$ are the source and target states, $`u`$ is
the intervention, $`e`$ records affected relations and expected effects,
and $`\mathcal{I}`$ is the set of preserved invariants. Metric
information is included in $`e`$ when applicable. Together with the
answer $`a^{*}`$, these labels define the reference transition

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tau^{*}:s^{0}\xrightarrow{\,u\,}(e,\mathcal{I})\xrightarrow{}s^{1}\xrightarrow{}a^{*}.
``` |  | (17) |

The causal teacher is supervised to generate this structured transition
from $`x`$. Its teacher-training loss is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{teacher}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau^{*}|}\log\pi_{\phi}(\tau_{t}^{*}\mid x,\tau^{*}_{<t}).
``` |  | (18) |

The privileged labels specify the teacher target; they are not supplied
as inputs to the student. The teacher is frozen before student training.

The student is initialized from the same base model and receives only
$`x=(I,q)`$ throughout rollout training and inference. It generates an
on-policy trajectory $`\hat{\tau}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$. At
step $`t`$, the frozen teacher and student evaluate the same
student-generated prefix:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{T}^{(t)}=\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x,\hat{\tau}_{<t}),\qquad p_{S}^{(t)}=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\hat{\tau}_{<t}).
``` |  | (19) |

The resulting causal bridge transfers teacher behavior at states visited
by the student rather than only along reference trajectories.

### C.2 Training Objective and Inference

CT-OPSD minimizes forward KL divergence along student rollouts together
with standard answer supervision:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\tau}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{T}^{(t)}\parallel p_{S}^{(t)}\right)\right],
``` |  | (20) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CT\text{-}OPSD}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ans}}\left[-\log\pi_{\theta}(a^{*}\mid x)\right].
``` |  | (21) |

At inference, the teacher and all privileged causal-transition
annotations are discarded. The retained student predicts from the image
and question alone.

### C.3 Prompt and Output Format

The student uses the same answer-only input as the evaluated foundation
models: the image, intervention-conditioned question, and answer
options. It is not given source-state labels, affected relations,
expected effects, invariants, target states, or metric annotations. The
teacher is trained to emit a compact causal transition chain followed by
the answer; the student is distilled on its own generated continuations
and is evaluated by its final multiple-choice answer.

|  Component |  Role |
|----|----|
|  Observable input $`x`$ |  Image, question, intervention description, and answer options. |
|  Causal teacher |  Predicts source state, intervention consequences, invariants, target state, and answer. |
|  Student rollout |  Samples a trajectory from the observation-only student policy. |
|  On-policy alignment |  Matches the teacher and student distributions on the shared student prefix. |
|  Answer supervision |  Anchors the final response to the gold answer $`a^{*}`$. |
|  Inference student |  Uses only the image and question after teacher removal. |

Table 8: CT-OPSD components and their roles.

### C.4 Ablation Definitions

The component ablations in the main paper remove one source of causal
supervision while keeping the base model, observable input, evaluation
split, and answer format fixed. CT w/o Aux. removes auxiliary structured
supervision beyond the final answer. CT w/o Bridge removes the
causal-transition bridge between teacher and student rollouts. CT w/o
Eff., CT w/o Inv., and CT w/o Metric remove the corresponding effect,
invariant, and metric information from the teacher’s causal-transition
supervision. Standard OPSD does not use CST-Bench’s structured
causal-transition annotations. All variants are evaluated
observation-only, using the image and question without privileged
annotations.

## Appendix D Evaluation Protocol and Additional Results

### D.1 Answer-Only Evaluation

At inference, every foundation model and post-trained student receives
the source image, counterfactual question, and answer options. Models do
not access source states, intervention effects, invariant relations,
target states, or other privileged annotations. Decoding uses a common
answer-only prompt and the response is parsed as a multiple-choice
answer; invalid or unmatched responses receive zero credit.

Let $`a_{i}\in\{0,1\}`$ indicate whether prediction $`i`$ is correct.
For family $`f`$ with index set $`D_{f}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname{Acc}_{f}=\frac{1}{|D_{f}|}\sum_{i\in D_{f}}a_{i},\qquad\operatorname{Macro}=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{f=1}^{6}\operatorname{Acc}_{f},
``` |  | (22) |

and

|     |                                                          |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \operatorname{Micro}=\frac{1}{480}\sum_{i=1}^{480}a_{i}.
       ```                                                       |     | (23) |

Macro Avg. weights each family equally, whereas Micro Acc. weights every
example in the balanced 480-example evaluation split equally.

### D.2 Causal-Transition Diagnostics

The diagnostic evaluation measures source-state understanding (Src.),
effect reasoning (Eff.), invariance identification (Inv.), and
transition consistency (Cons.). Source-state understanding assesses
whether the model recovers the relevant pre-intervention relation.
Effect reasoning assesses whether it identifies the intervention-induced
change. Invariance identification checks whether unaffected properties
are preserved, and transition consistency checks whether the predicted
relation update agrees with the final answer.

For ablations, CST W-Avg. aggregates answer accuracy and the structured
diagnostics according to the predefined benchmark weights:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{CST\ W\text{-}Avg.}=\frac{\sum_{m}w_{m}S_{m}}{\sum_{m}w_{m}},
``` |  | (24) |

where $`S_{m}`$ is the score of diagnostic dimension $`m`$ and $`w_{m}`$
is its fixed weight. This aggregate is distinct from family-level Macro
Avg. and is used to compare whether a method jointly captures source
states, effects, invariants, and consistent target transitions.

### D.3 External Spatial Benchmarks

Post-trained variants are also evaluated on 3DSRBench, CV-Bench, and the
spatial-relation and relative-depth subsets of BLINK_(val). All
post-training methods use the same base model and the same evaluation
protocol within each external benchmark. CST-Bench metrics are not
combined with these external benchmark scores; the reported external
columns retain the standard task-level accuracy or average for each
benchmark.

## Appendix E Limitations, Ethics, and Release Scope

#### Geometric approximation.

Bounding boxes support controlled image-plane relations and normalized
distance changes, but they are not metric three-dimensional
reconstructions. Apparent distance depends on projection, object scale,
and depth. Depth transitions therefore rely on source annotations and
symbolic state updates rather than two-dimensional box geometry alone.

#### Symbolic interventions.

The observed image is real, but the post-intervention state is symbolic
rather than a photorealistically rendered edit. CST-Bench tests state
updating under a hypothetical intervention; it does not evaluate
image-edit realism, physical simulation, collision, deformation, or
long-horizon dynamics.

#### Validation limits.

Answer-blind consensus validation and human verification reduce
ambiguity but cannot remove all systematic errors. VLM reviewers can
share model-specific biases, and human judgments can remain uncertain
for borderline spatial cases. The released validation records should
therefore be retained and extended when the benchmark is revised.

#### Data and task coverage.

The benchmark is English-language, multiple-choice, and limited to
twelve operators. Its visual, object, language, geographic, cultural,
and demographic coverage inherits that of its source data. Performance
may not transfer to open-ended responses, videos, robotics,
safety-critical systems, or physical settings with richer dynamics.

#### Responsible use.

The source images may depict people and everyday environments. CST-Bench
does not add identity labels or infer sensitive attributes. A high
benchmark score does not establish that a model is safe for autonomous
navigation or robotic control, where an incorrect spatial update can
cause unsafe actions.
````

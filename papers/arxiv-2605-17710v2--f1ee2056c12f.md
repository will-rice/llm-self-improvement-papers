# Sometin Beta Pass Notin: Improving Multilingual ASR for Nigerian Languages via Knowledge Distillation

Sewade Ogun Affiliation: Masakhane NLP  
0009-0003-0648-021X

###### Abstract

Although modern multilingual Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems
support several Nigerian languages, their performance consistently lags
behind resource-rich languages such as English and French. Nigerian
languages present unique modelling hurdles, including acute data
scarcity, inconsistent orthography, tonal diacritics, diverse accents,
frequent code-switching, and localised named entities. To address these
challenges, we developed a multilingual ASR framework using a two-stage
distillation process. First, we employed student-teacher knowledge
distillation from existing monolingual models, conditioned on robust
language-specific N-gram language models. Second, we performed iterative
self improvement using pseudo-labelled data to further refine accuracy.
Our method significantly bridges the performance gap, achieving on
average a reduction in the relative Word Error Rate (WER) of 29 % over
the monolingual baselines. Our models also outperform state-of-the-art
multilingual models across major benchmarks, including Common Voice and
FLEURS. We introduce Sometin Beta Pass Notin (SBPN), a multilingual
foundational ASR model that covers Yorùbá, Hausa, Igbo, Nigerian Pidgin,
and Nigerian English.

###### Index Terms: 

Multilingual speech recognition, Knowledge Distillation,
Pseudo-labelling, Nigerian ASR, Data augmentation

## I Introduction

Nigeria is home to over 500 distinct languages. Many natives are
inherently bilingual or multilingual, acquiring one or more indigenous
languages through socialisation while attaining proficiency in the
official language, English, through formal education. However, in
certain regions, the Nigerian Pidgin has increasingly replaced
indigenous mother tongues as the primary language of daily interaction
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\]. This transition places several languages at
significant risk of linguistic attrition or extinction, which is further
accelerated by lack of digital exposure. Resource-rich languages
dominate the internet, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that further
sidelines indigenous tongues.

Many Nigerian languages still have millions of speakers around the
world. The languages with the highest number of speakers include
Nigerian English (178 M) \[[2](#bib.bib2)\],¹¹ 1 Nigerian English is a
distinct variety of English with a unique accent and a different
semantics (e.g., ’to flash’ for a missed call) that distinguish it from
Standard English Nigerian Pidgin (80.2 M), Hausa (63 M), Yorùbá
(45.7 M), and Igbo (33 M). Yet, they remain classified as low-resource
languages due to several critical factors \[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. Primarily,
this is due to the scarcity of large-scale high-quality Automatic Speech
Recognition (ASR) datasets. Although recent community initiatives have
produced open-source datasets and baseline models for Yorùbá, Hausa,
Igbo \[[4](#bib.bib4)\], Nigerian Pidgin \[[5](#bib.bib5)\] and Nigerian
English \[[6](#bib.bib6)\], these speech-text pairs consist largely of
read speech based on predefined templates with limited textual
diversity. Hence, these efforts often do not translate into better
accuracy in real-life conversational or spontaneous testing scenarios
\[[7](#bib.bib7)\]. A further challenge is the prevalence of
code-switching in conversational speech. For example, numerical data,
such as dates, measurements, and years, are typically spoken in English
during everyday interactions, even when the primary language of
communication is different. Furthermore, tonal languages like Yorùbá and
Igbo rely on diacritical marks to convey correct pronunciation and
meaning; any omissions or inconsistencies in these marks within training
data lead to increased modelling errors. Lastly, modelling loanwords
presents a distinct hurdle \[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. The etymology of these
languages reveals a significant cross-linguistic influence.
Specifically, Nigerian languages have adopted numerous English words
often by modifying their orthography or pronunciation.

While open-source monolingual ASR models exist for Nigerian languages,
we hypothesize that combining them into a unified multilingual
architecture can mitigate language-specific data limitations and yield
stronger joint acoustic representations. Given the scarcity of annotated
target speech, we employ pseudo-labelling to adapt the model to
real-world conversational dynamics. The following are our contributions.

- •
  We introduce the first multilingual foundational ASR model, Sometin
  Beta Pass Notin (SBPN),²² 2 Sometin Beta Pass Notin is a Nigerian
  Pidgin expression meaning “Something is better than nothing”. It
  follows the theme that learning from several average teachers can
  still produce a good student. that focuses on Nigerian languages. The
  model comes in two variants, SBPN-Base³³ 3
  [https://huggingface.co/ogunlao/SBPN_multilingual_base](https://huggingface.co/ogunlao/SBPN_multilingual_base)
  and SBPN-Large.⁴⁴ 4
  [https://huggingface.co/ogunlao/SBPN_multilingual_large](https://huggingface.co/ogunlao/SBPN_multilingual_large)
- •
  We developed a large scale N-gram language model for 5 Nigerian
  languages, and then demonstrate the use of the language models in
  improving ASR performance.
- •
  We show the use of large scale pseudo-labelling for improving
  low-resourced Nigerian languages.

## II Related Work

Training ASR models with pseudo-labelled data is a widely explored area
in the literature \[[9](#bib.bib9), [10](#bib.bib10),
[11](#bib.bib11)\]. On a large scale, this has yielded significant
improvements in monolingual and multilingual foundational ASR models
\[[12](#bib.bib12), [13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14)\]. The
pseudo-labels are often improved using methods like shallow fusion of
the ASR predictions with a strong external language model in the target
domain during inference, confidence filtering of the generated
pseudo-labels \[[15](#bib.bib15)\], pseudo-label refinement using
generative error correction \[[16](#bib.bib16)\], etc. In low-resource
scenarios, a well trained acoustic model is typically finetuned on the
small amount of curated dataset. Then, pseudo-labels are generated for
larger unlabelled data using the finetuned model \[[17](#bib.bib17)\].

Recent projects on ASR for African languages include the development of
the AfriHUBERT model \[[18](#bib.bib18)\], a project that extended the
mHuBERT-147 model from 16 African languages to 1,226 African languages,
including the Igbo, Yorùbá, and Hausa languages. However, the
performance reported on the selected Nigerian languages still lags
behind others. Therefore, in this work, we explore ways to improve the
accuracy of speech recognition models on Nigerian languages.

## III Methodology

### III-A Dataset curation and preprocessing

The proposed methodology relies on curated labelled and unlabelled
speech datasets for target Nigerian languages, Yorùbá (yo), Nigerian
Pidgin (pcm), Hausa (ha), and Igbo (ig), and Nigerian English (en-ng).
The data were sourced from online speech dataset repositories,
audio-books, online digital sources such as radio shows, online audio
platforms, and freely available podcasts with permissive licenses. In
addition, we curated labelled data for Nigerian English (en-ng), which
was augmented with a larger Standard English (en) corpus of diverse
speakers to prevent over-fitting the ASR model to the limited number of
native speakers, sentences, and accents in the Nigerian English corpus.

Unlike the labelled data, the unlabelled recordings in their original
form were multi-speaker and varied in recording quality and length.
Therefore, the data required post-processing for ASR model training. We
adopted the audio processing pipeline described in \[[24](#bib.bib24)\].
The entire data processing pipeline is depicted in
Fig. [1](#S3.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ III-A Dataset curation and preprocessing ‣ III Methodology ‣ Sometin Beta Pass Notin: Improving Multilingual ASR for Nigerian Languages via Knowledge Distillation").
Concretely, each recording was denoised using a speech enhancement
model, MossFormer2 \[[25](#bib.bib25)\],⁶⁶ 6 Initial pseudo-labelling
with original samples yielded bad pseudo-labels, so we denoised the
noisy samples. and then split into individual speaker segments using the
Pyannote speaker diarization toolkit \[[26](#bib.bib26)\]. The
diarization process groups segments into individual speakers. Segments
with speaker embedding similarity exceeding $`0.7`$ were also combined
into the same speaker group to produce longer single-speaker continuous
speech as proposed in \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. The audio segments were
further passed through a voice activity detector (VAD)⁷⁷ 7
[https://github.com/snakers4/silero-vad](https://github.com/snakers4/silero-vad)
to remove silence segments and other non-speech segments. The VAD
outputs were subsequently post-processed by keeping the silence segments
that are less than 1.5 s in-between the VAD processed segments.
Additionally, we filtered out segments that did not belong in our target
languages using a two step filtering process. The language of each audio
segment is first predicted using an audio-based language identifier
(LID) trained on the VoxLingua107 dataset \[[27](#bib.bib27)\].⁸⁸ 8
[https://huggingface.co/speechbrain/lang-id-voxlingua107-ecapa](https://huggingface.co/speechbrain/lang-id-voxlingua107-ecapa)
The LID model can identify 107 languages including Yorùbá, English, and
Hausa, but was not trained to identify Nigerian Pidgin and Igbo
languages. For these remaining languages, only a second-step text-based
language identification with filtering was performed using AfroLID
\[[28](#bib.bib28)\]. As the total duration of the removed segments was
insignificant in comparison to the total remaining dataset after
filtering, we did not perform ablation experiments to determine the
impact of this step.

Fig. 1: Flow diagram showing the pseudo-label generation pipeline from
unprocessed recordings to processed audio segments with pseudo-labels.

Finally, all long segments were divided into 30 s segments using a
silence threshold of $`-50~dB`$. This ensures that the segments are
compatible with ASR models used for pseudo-labelling. The total number
of hours after processing was about 10,000 h.

##### Nigerian pidgin orthographic normalization

Nigerian Pidgin lacks a standardized orthography; words frequently
appear in plain English, spoken Pidgin, or written Pidgin variations
\[[29](#bib.bib29)\]. To improve target sequence consistency given our
limited data, we normalized contextual homophones in the training set
into a single canonical token based on corpus word frequencies. Test set
transcripts were left unnormalized to evaluate real-world
generalization.

For example, variants such as ”they”, ”de”, and ”dey” were mapped to the
most prevalent candidate, ”dey”. Candidate sets were identified by
generating pseudo-labels for the Nigerian Pidgin training and validation
splits using both an English ASR model \[[14](#bib.bib14)\] and a
Nigerian Pidgin monolingual model. We then aligned predictions with
baseline Pidgin labels via positional word clustering. While this
clustering yielded effective replacement candidates (e.g., {”boi”,
”boy”}, {”demself”, ”demsef”, ”themself”}), it also produced occasional
false positives. Whereas automated filtering via sequence-level language
model probabilities presents a scalable solution, the candidate set was
sufficiently small to allow for precise manual filtering in our
pipeline.

In addition, another set of larger distinct homophones was revealed by
the clustering. Some homophones cannot be interchanged contextually
(e.g., ”say” vs. ”sey”, or ”niger” vs. ”naija”). To resolve these
context-dependent ambiguities, we queried our Nigerian Pidgin N-gram
language model to select the homophone candidate that yields the highest
sequence probability at the target position. Although the normalization
process relied partially on manual intervention, future work will
automate this process to generalize to other low-resource languages.

### III-C Model architecture and knowledge distillation

Using the labelled and pseudo-labelled pairs of speech and text training
data, we developed a set of multilingual ASR models, SBPN-Base and
SBPN-Large. Their model architecture is based on the
recurrent-neural-network-based Transducer (RNNT) \[[34](#bib.bib34)\].
The RNNT architecture consists of a Fast Conformer encoder
\[[35](#bib.bib35)\], a stateful LSTM-based prediction network, and a
feed-forward joint network. An auxiliary convolutional neural
network-based CTC head is attached to the encoder to regularise the
encoder features, which is especially useful in the presence of noisy
pseudo-labelled data. The differences between SBPN-Base and SBPN-Large
are presented in
Table [III](#S3.T3 "TABLE III ‣ III-D1 Training and implementation details ‣ III-D Experimental setup ‣ III Methodology ‣ Sometin Beta Pass Notin: Improving Multilingual ASR for Nigerian Languages via Knowledge Distillation")

To provide sufficient capacity for cross-lingual transfer, we
initialized the SBPN-Large encoder (600M parameters) using weights from
Parakeet-TDT-600-V3, while randomly initializing all remaining layers
for end-to-end training. The training framework consists of two
sequential phases:

- •
  Knowledge distillation step: The model is initially trained on a
  combination of pseudo-labelled and ground-truth data, followed by
  refinement on ground-truth data alone.
- •
  Self-Improvement step: Using the best checkpoint from the initial
  phase, pseudo-labels are generated iteratively for each language via
  shallow fusion with a target-specific N-gram language model. To
  balance dataset scale and transcript quality, candidate pseudo-labels
  are filtered using language-specific confidence thresholds.
  Furthermore, pseudo-labels assigned a language tag that disagrees with
  the target speech language are discarded to eliminate
  misclassifications carried over from initial data processing. Final
  training proceeds on the combined set of filtered pseudo-labels and
  ground-truth data until convergence. We perform the self-improvement
  step for two iterations.

To evaluate parameter efficiency, we then trained a compact 120M
parameter model (SBPN-Base). The encoder was initialized from the
Parakeet-TDT CTC-110M model.¹⁸¹⁸ 18 Checkpoint available at
[https://hf.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt_ctc-110m](https://hf.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt_ctc-110m)
For this variant, hard pseudo-labels were generated directly by the
trained SBPN-Large model. After minimal confidence filtering to discard
low-probability transcriptions, the pseudo-labels along with their
speech segment pairs were combined with the ground-truth labelled corpus
for student model training.

### III-D Experimental setup

#### III-D1 Training and implementation details

The two models were trained using a multi-task training setup, where the
total loss is computed as a weighted loss between the RNNT loss and the
CTC loss of the encoder head. We adopted the RNNT loss implemented as
the Graph-Transducer loss in \[[36](#bib.bib36)\], which reduces memory
consumption during loss computation. The models were trained using the
NeMo toolkit \[[37](#bib.bib37)\] with the AdamW optimizer using a
linear warm-up of 2,500 steps and cosine annealing. Temperature sampling
was applied during data loading to mitigate language imbalance. The key
hyperparameters and data augmentation settings are detailed in
Table [III](#S3.T3 "TABLE III ‣ III-D1 Training and implementation details ‣ III-D Experimental setup ‣ III Methodology ‣ Sometin Beta Pass Notin: Improving Multilingual ASR for Nigerian Languages via Knowledge Distillation").

Text preprocessing include converting digits to English spoken format
\[[38](#bib.bib38)\] and stripping punctuation except apostrophes and
hyphens. Additionally, a unified sentence-piece tokenization with 4,096
subword tokens was trained from the combination of all labelled training
data texts. A language tag in $`\{`$\<\|en\|\>, \<\|ig\|\>, \<\|yo\|\>,
\<\|pd\|\>, \<\|ha\|\>$`\}`$ was prepended to each text label when
loading the data, to reduce cross-lingual interference. It also serves
as the language label used for language identification (LID) during
inference.

During pseudo-label generation in the self-improvement stage, if the
known target language is not the top beam hypothesis, the highest-ranked
hypothesis matching that language is selected when available. This
target-language forcing improved self-improvement pseudo-labels, but was
omitted during benchmark test set evaluation for fair comparison with
prior work.

#### III-D2 Conversational Speech Simulation via WSOLA.

To evaluate robustness against the variable speaking rates
characteristic of spontaneous speech (e.g., rapid enunciations and
mid-utterance pauses), we simulated speed perturbations across target
test sets. The audio samples were stretched over a spectrum that ranges
from slow speech to twice the baseline speaking rate. To modify the
time-domain stretching factor while preserving the natural voice timbre,
we applied the Waveform Similarity-Based Overlap-Add (WSOLA) algorithm
\[[40](#bib.bib40)\].¹⁹¹⁹ 19 Implemented in
[https://github.com/KAIST-MACLab/PyTSMod](https://github.com/KAIST-MACLab/PyTSMod)

#### III-D3 Evaluation set and metrics

SBPN models were evaluated primarily on the FLEURS test set and the
Common Voice test sets for the Yorùbá, Hausa, and Igbo languages. The
performance of Nigerian Pidgin and Nigerian English was evaluated using
the Nigerian Pidgin test set and the Nigerian Common Voice test set. The
metrics used for the evaluations are word error rate (WER) for
transcription accuracy and micro averaged $`F_{1}`$ score for language
identification. In the language identification task, it is assumed that
every test utterance in the test set belongs to the language indicated
by the test set metadata.

### IV-A Teacher vs. student performance

We evaluate the effectiveness of the two-stage distillation framework
(Section [III-C](#S3.SS3 "III-C Model architecture and knowledge distillation ‣ III Methodology ‣ Sometin Beta Pass Notin: Improving Multilingual ASR for Nigerian Languages via Knowledge Distillation"))
by comparing the distilled SBPN-Large student model with the baseline
monolingual teachers in the target languages.
Table [IV](#S4.T4 "TABLE IV ‣ IV Experiments ‣ Sometin Beta Pass Notin: Improving Multilingual ASR for Nigerian Languages via Knowledge Distillation")
illustrates the primary findings of the experiments. The SBPN-Large
model outperforms all the monolingual ASR teacher models on average by a
large margin, a 29 % relative reduction in WER over the baselines. Even
when augmented with N-gram language model fusion, baseline performance
lags behind SBPN-Large, which achieves a 16 % relative reduction in WER.
This demonstrates that the student model successfully absorbed and
surpassed the knowledge distilled from the teacher. Examining the
results of each stage, the distillation stage provided, on average, a
significant relative reduction in WER of 26 % over the monolingual
teacher models. Although the WER was subsequently reduced further during
the self-improvement stage, the reduction is smaller than in the first
stage, indicating that most of the knowledge transfer occurred at the
first stage. In our visual analysis, the second stage mainly helped
improve performance on diacritics, code-switched data, and long speech,
which are improvements not necessarily reflected in the test set
results.

The highest improvement in ASR performance is on Nigerian Pidgin,
boasting a relative reduction of 60 % in WER over the teacher model.
This could be attributed perhaps to the similarity of the Nigerian
Pidgin language to Standard English, and also the text processing
applied to the Nigerian Pidgin (pcm) pseudo-labels
(Section [III-A](#S3.SS1.SSS0.Px1 "Nigerian pidgin orthographic normalization ‣ III-A Dataset curation and preprocessing ‣ III Methodology ‣ Sometin Beta Pass Notin: Improving Multilingual ASR for Nigerian Languages via Knowledge Distillation")).
The improvement is also reflected in the Nigerian English (en-ng)
results, specifically after the self-improvement stage, where we observe
a relative WER reduction of 23 % over a strong English baseline
(Parakeet-TDT-600-V3). However, this reduction is not as significant as
that of Nigerian Pidgin, perhaps due to the limited amount of Nigerian
English data used for model training.²⁰²⁰ 20 The entire AfriSpeech-200
training set is about 172 h and only a fraction of the accents is
Nigerian. The least improvement is observed in the Igbo language. Even
with a large amount of pseudo-labelled data (1,900 h), we were only able
to achieve a relative WER improvement of 13 % over the baseline.
Examining the curated audio recordings indicates that a large portion of
Igbo speech involves code-switching between the Igbo language and the
English language or the Nigerian Pidgin, which may have affected
performance.

increase in WER as the speaking rate is doubled, SBPN-Large remained

### IV-D Analysis of diacritical mark prediction

We examined the effect of diacritical marks on the accuracy of model
predictions on the FLEURS test sets for the two Nigerian languages with
diacritical marks in their orthography. For Yorùbá Language, it can be
deduced from

predict the correct mark, leading to a 110 % relative increase in WER
from when diacritics are removed from predictions to when diacritics are

distillation and self improvement training for SBPN-Base and SBPN-Large

relative increase in WER of 27 % and 47 % from when diacritical marks

to be done to reduce the WER gap between accented and unaccented

the gap is not as large as that of the Yorùbá language, perhaps because
marks are often omitted in standard Igbo writing. Also, the WERs are

## V Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced the Sometin Beta Pass Notin (SBPN) family of multilingual
ASR models (SBPN-Base with 120M parameters and SBPN-Large with 600M
parameters) targeting five Nigerian languages: Hausa, Yorùbá, Igbo,
Nigerian English, and Nigerian Pidgin. Evaluation results demonstrate
that SBPN models consistently outperform existing monolingual and
multilingual baselines across both read and conversational speech
benchmarks. Future work will focus on expanding language coverage to
additional Nigerian languages and developing targeted methods to improve
diacritic prediction accuracy for tonal languages such as Yorùbá and
Igbo.

###### Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities in
general natural language processing tasks but often fall short in
complex reasoning tasks. Recent studies have explored human-like
problem-solving strategies, such as self-correct, to push further the
boundary of single-model reasoning ability. In this work, we let a
single model “step outside the box” by engaging multiple models to
correct each other. We introduce a multi-agent collaboration strategy
that emulates the academic peer review process. Each agent independently
constructs its own solution, provides reviews on the solutions of
others, and assigns confidence levels to its reviews. Upon receiving
peer reviews, agents revise their initial solutions. Extensive
experiments on three different types of reasoning tasks show that our
collaboration approach delivers superior accuracy across all ten
datasets compared to existing methods. Further study underscores the
effectiveness of integrating confidence in reviews, demonstrates the
superiority of feedback exchange over mere solution sharing, and
highlights the role of capability and diversity in fostering successful
collaboration. Code is available at
[https://github.com/HITsz-TMG/Multi-agent-peer-review](https://github.com/HITsz-TMG/Multi-agent-peer-review).

## 1 Introduction

> “Lookers-on see the chess game better than players.” – Chinese Proverb

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities
in a wide range of natural language processing (NLP) tasks [Touvron
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib28); [OpenAI (2023)](#bib.bib20); [Anil et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib1). The emergent abilities of LLMs have showcased
promising results across a multitude of benchmarks [Wei et al.
(2022a)](#bib.bib32). Among all rising capabilities, complex reasoning
opens up opportunities for building a large spectrum of applications
upon LLMs, effectively making LLMs the next-generation computation
platform [Fu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib7). Current studies have explored
human-like problem-solving strategies to push further the boundary of
single-model reasoning ability, such as chain-of-thought prompting [Wei
et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib33) and self-consistency [Wang et al.
(2023b)](#bib.bib30).

Amidst this background, inspired by the typical human learning strategy
of “self-reflection”, the idea of “self-correction” has gained
significant research interest, where an LLM automatically reflects on
its response and refines based solely on its inherent
capabilities [Madaan et al. (2023)](#bib.bib18); [Shinn et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib25); [Pan et al. (2023)](#bib.bib21). However, [Liang
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib16) suggest that, once the LLM has formulated
initial solutions, it cannot generate novel thoughts through
self-reflection. Considering the example in
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"),
the initial answer of the LLM is incorrect. Despite conducting a
self-review and attempting to identify problems, the LLM still assesses
its answer as being correct. [Huang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib10) further
note that a single LLM struggles to self-correct its response without
external feedback, which leads us to the motivation behind our work: Why
not let a single LLM “jump outside the box” and let multiple LLMs
provide feedback to each other?

To this end, we propose a multi-agent collaboration strategy that
mirrors the academic peer review process. For a given question, each
agent first independently submits its own solution. Then the agents
perform a systematic review of their peers’ solutions. They not only
examine the reasoning process step by step but also attach a confidence
score to quantify the reliability of their feedback. Drawing on the
insights from the peer review, each agent finally refines its initial
submission and creates an improved version. In the example shown in
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"),
our collaboration strategy enables multiple agents to engage in mutual
review and correction, leading to an accurate consensus. This iterative
cycle of creation, review, and revision is akin to the collaborative
efforts seen in human learning and problem-solving.

We conduct extensive experiments across three types of reasoning tasks:
mathematical reasoning, commonsense reasoning, and symbolic reasoning.
The results illustrate that our collaboration method achieves superior
performance across all ten datasets compared to previous strong
single-agent and multi-agent baselines. Further analysis underscores the
effectiveness of expressing uncertainty in the reviews, demonstrates the
advantage of sharing feedback rather than solutions, and suggests that
models with minimal capability differences but high diversity can
collaborate better.

The contributions of this work are threefold:

- •
  By letting a single agent “think outside the box”, we propose a
  multi-agent collaboration method that emulates the peer review process
  – “creation, review, and revision”.
- •
  Our collaboration method consistently outperforms previous strong
  baselines across three reasoning tasks and ten datasets.
- •
  We demonstrate the efficacy of integrating confidence and sharing
  feedback, suggesting a viable path forward the optimization of
  LLM-based multi-agent systems.

## 2 Related Work

With language models scaling up, the emergent abilities have showcased
remarkable results across a variety of domains and tasks, challenging
our understanding of learning and cognition [Bubeck et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib2); [OpenAI (2023)](#bib.bib20). Among these rising
capabilities, complex reasoning draws significant interest for its
central role in building autonomous language agents and the
next-generation computation platform [Xi et al. (2023)](#bib.bib34); [Fu
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib7).

To push further the boundary of single-agent reasoning, researchers have
adopted strategies akin to teaching kids to reason. For example, the
chain-of-thought prompting [Wei et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib33) employs
rationales to steer LLMs through a multi-step reasoning process; the
self-consistency method [Wang et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib30) selects the
most likely answer from multiple reasoning paths by majority voting; the
plan-and-solve prompting [Wang et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib29) and the
least-to-most prompting [Zhou et al. (2023)](#bib.bib38) both apply a
divide-and-conquer strategy: break down a complex problem into a series
of simpler sub-problems and then solve them in sequence.

Amidst this backdrop, a prevailing strategy to address reasoning errors
is self-correction, mirroring a typical human learning strategy of
self-reflection [Pan et al. (2023)](#bib.bib21). This approach involves
an LLM attempting to refine its prior response based solely on its
inherent capabilities, without the crutch of external feedback. Contrary
to the optimism about self-correction [Madaan et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib18); [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib25); [Kim et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib11), [Huang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib10) find that an LLM
struggles to correct its response without external feedback. [Liang
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib16) also conclude that, once the LLM has
formulated initial solutions, it cannot generate novel thoughts through
self-reflection. In this work, we let a single LLM “jump outside the
box” and change its viewpoint from a participant to a critical observer
by enabling multiple LLMs to correct each other.

Multi-agent collaboration for task solving is still in its preliminary
stages, presenting vast opportunities for exploration. This approach
aligns with the Society of Mind (SoM) theory [Minsky
(1988)](#bib.bib19), which suggests that intelligence arises from the
interaction of computational modules, achieving collective goals beyond
the capabilities of individual modules. CAMEL [Li et al.
(2023a)](#bib.bib14) proposes a cooperative agent framework known as
role-playing, enabling agents to cooperate on complex tasks. Such
role-playing collaboration has been extended to software
development [Qian et al. (2023)](#bib.bib23) and text evaluation [Chan
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib3); [Li et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib15). [Liang
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib16) and [Du et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6) have delved
into multi-agent debates for translation and arithmetic problems.
Corex [Sun et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26) has explored three collaboration
paradigms in reasoning – debate, code review and retrieve. Through these
multi-agent simulations, [Zhang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib37) observe
human-like social behaviors typical of human collaboration. To this end,
we propose a human-mimicking multi-agent collaboration process, wherein
multiple agents engage in peer discussions and provide feedback to one
another.

## 3 Methodology

Overview. In this work, we introduce a multi-agent collaboration
strategy that mirrors the academic peer review process. The overall
process is illustrated in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration").
The process consists of three stages – “creation, review, and revision”.
In stage 1, for a given question, each agent first independently submits
its own solution. In stage 2, each agent performs a systematic review of
its peers’ solutions. In stage 3, upon receiving the reviews, each agent
refines its initial solution and re-submits the improved version. An
example of these stages is provided in
Section [4.6](#S4.SS6 "4.6 Case study ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration").
We next elaborate these stages in detail.

Stage 1: Create. The peer review process starts with each agent
submitting its own solution. Specifically, when presented with a
question $`q`$, A group of $`n`$ agents, denoted as
$`{A_{1},A_{2},...,A_{n}}`$, perform chain-of-thought reasoning. Each
agent generates its own reasoning chains and corresponding answers
$`a_{i}`$. Take
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
as an example, the initial answers of three agents are all incorrect.

Stage 2: Review. We next feed each agent $`A_{i}`$ with the solution of
its peers (i.e., $`A_{j}`$, where $`j\neq i`$), one at a time, to write
reviews $`r_{ij}`$. Note that $`r_{ij}`$ denotes the review from agent
$`A_{i}`$ to agent $`A_{j}`$. In current review forms, reviewers do more
than just provide overall assessments; they also indicate their
certainty about their evaluations. Mirroring such real-life review
practices, the agents not only examine the reasoning process step by
step but also attach a confidence score to quantify the reliability of
their feedback. To achieve this, we concatenate each peer solution with
the following instruction: Please examine this agent’s reasoning process
step by step and offer feedback on its reasoning. You can rate your
confidence in your feedback on a scale from 1-10, where 10 indicates the
highest level of confidence.

In the example shown in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"),
one agent accurately determines the initial height but forgets to
multiply the ratio twice. Conversely, another agent multiplies the ratio
twice but gets the initial height wrong. Although their initial answers
are wrong, they can provide effective feedback confidently to each
other, based on the difference between their solutions and those of
their peers.

Stage 3: Revise. We finally feed each agent $`A_{i}`$ with the reviews
from its peers (i.e., $`r_{ji}`$), all at once, to refine the initial
solutions. The agent considers both the peer solutions in dialogue
history and the received peer reviews. To achieve this, we concatenate
the reviews with the following instruction: Using other agents’
solutions and feedbacks as additional information, can you provide your
answer to the problem? In the example illustrated in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"),
out of three agents, two successfully integrate accurate feedback into
their final responses. This results in both agents converging on the
same correct solution after revision.

Unlike prior studies such as [Du et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6) and [Sun
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26), we do not explicitly ask the agents to reach
a consensus at the end of the review. The final prediction is determined
through a majority vote among the $`n`$ participating agents. This
approach ensures the impartiality and balance of our peer review
process.

### 4.1 Benchmarks

Our method is evaluated on ten benchmark datasets from three categories
of reasoning problems: mathematical reasoning, commonsense reasoning,
and symbolic reasoning.
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
shows the statistics of evaluation datasets.

Mathematical reasoning:

- •
  GSM8K [Cobbe et al. (2021)](#bib.bib5): A high quality linguistically
  diverse grade-school math word problems created by human problem
  writers.
- •
  SVAMP [Patel et al. (2021)](#bib.bib22): A challenging math word
  problem set with grade level up to 4, by applying simple variations
  over an existing dataset.
- •
  AQuA [Ling et al. (2017)](#bib.bib17): A math word problem set with
  natural language rationales.
- •
  MultiArith [Roy and Roth (2015)](#bib.bib24): A set of math word
  problems requiring multiple reasoning steps and operations.
- •
  AddSub [Hosseini et al. (2014)](#bib.bib9): A set of addition and
  subtraction math word problems.
- •
  SingleEq [Koncel-Kedziorski et al. (2015)](#bib.bib13): A
  single-equation grade-school math word problems with multiple math
  operations over non-negative rational numbers and one variable.

Commonsense reasoning:

- •
  ARC-c [Clark et al. (2018)](#bib.bib4): A subset of the AI2 Reasoning
  Challenge, consisting of challenging science questions that require
  reasoning and a wide breadth of knowledge to answer the
  multiple-choice problems correctly.
- •
  StrategyQA [Geva et al. (2021)](#bib.bib8): A commonsense QA task with
  Yes or No answer format that requires models to perform multi-hop
  reasoning to answer the questions.

Symbolic reasoning:

- •
  Colored Objects [Suzgun et al. (2023)](#bib.bib27): Given a context,
  answer a simple question about the color of an object on a surface.
- •
  Penguins [Suzgun et al. (2023)](#bib.bib27): Given a unique table of
  penguins, answer a question about the attributes of the them.

### 4.2 Baselines

We compare our peer review collaboration with the following single-agent
and multi-agent methods:

- •
  Zero-shot CoT [Kojima et al. (2022)](#bib.bib12) utilizes prompts to
  elicit both reasoning chains and answers from an LLM.
- •
  Self-correct [Huang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib10) enables an LLM to
  iteratively review, find problems, and refine its answer.
- •
  Multi-agent Majority [Wang et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib30) selects the
  most consistent answer from several reasoning chains.
- •
  Multi-agent Debate [Du et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6): Each agent in the
  group sees solutions of others, updates its own solution, and repeats
  this process iteratively.

### 4.4 Results

Main results.
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
shows the accuracy comparison on the mathematical reasoning datasets.
Our peer review strategy surpasses all single-agent and multi-agent
baselines across all datasets, especially significant in more
challenging benchmarks (+1.40% on GSM8K, +3.80% on SVAMP, +2.75% on AQuA
over the prior state-of-the-art).

Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
and
Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.3 Implementation Details ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
show the results on commonsense reasoning and symbolic reasoning
datasets, respectively. Our collaboration method consistently
outperforms all prior baselines across all datasets. The effectiveness
of self-correction notably decreases in non-math reasoning tasks, as
seen with a substantial drop from 66.13% to 28.27% in accuracy on
Colored Objects. However, when we introduce feedback from other agents,
there is a significant enhancement in performance, rising from 66.13% to
73.20%. This demonstrates the advantage of integrating external feedback
over relying solely on internal feedback mechanisms.

Ablation study. For the ablation of confidence in reviews, we alter the
prompt of Stage 2 described in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Methodology ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration").
Specifically, we remove the prompt of eliciting confidence, simplifying
it to: “Please examine this agent’s reasoning process step by step and
offer feedback on its reasoning”.
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
shows the effectiveness of expressing uncertainty and integrating
confidence in feedback on mathematical reasoning datasets. An example of
confidence ablation is shown in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Example of Confidence Ablation ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration").
As stated in the prompt of Stage 3, the updated answer uses other
agents’ solutions and feedback as additional information. In the absence
of confidence scores, the updated answer tends to be misled by
ineffective feedback and remains incorrect. In contrast, the integration
of confidence score enables the model to selectively aggregate valuable
information. By focusing more on solutions with higher confidence levels
rather than on less reliable feedback, the confidence scores guide the
model towards the correct answer.

For commonsense reasoning in
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
and symbolic reasoning in
Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.3 Implementation Details ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"),
incorporating confidence can result in unchanged or even reduced
performance. This decrease may stem from the tendency of LLMs to exhibit
overconfidence and significant miscalibration when verbalizing their
confidence in non-math datasets [Xiong et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib36). We
will further discuss the relationship between the level of confidence
and the correctness of feedback in
Section [4.5](#S4.SS5 "4.5 Analysis ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration").

For the ablation of solutions, we leave out the the dialogue history of
Stage 2 during the answer revision process in Stage 3, so that the peer
solutions cannot be seen. We also change the prompt of Stage 3 into
“Using other agents’ feedbacks as additional information, can you
provide your answer to the problem? ”. From
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"), [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
and [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.3 Implementation Details ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"),
omitting solutions resulted in decreased performance in 9 out of 10
datasets, indicating that solutions contribute valuable complementary
information to the feedback received. In addition, Ours (w/o solution),
which involves exchanging only peer feedback, outperforms Multi-agent
Debate, which exchanges only peer solutions, in 8 out of 10 datasets.
This demonstrates that peer feedback is more effective than peer
solutions in multi-agent collaboration.

### 4.5 Analysis

Comparison of answer changes. We compare the revised solutions with the
initial solutions, and summarize the results of changes in answers after
self-correction and our method.
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.3 Implementation Details ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
and
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Answer Change Comparison ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
show the answer changes on GSM8K and Penguins respectively. When
applying self-correction on GSM8K, the model is more likely to modify a
correct answer to an incorrect one than to revise an incorrect answer to
a correct one. In the non-math dataset Penguins, there is a higher
chance that the model alters its answer after self-correction. In 37.0%
of the time, the model changes a correct answer into an incorrect one.
However, our method exhibits a different pattern for both the GSM8K and
Penguins datasets, with the portion of Incorrect$`\rightarrow`$Correct
significantly larger than Correct$`\rightarrow`$Incorrect. Both figures
illustrate the benefits of incorporating external feedback over relying
solely on internal feedback mechanisms.

Impact of agent numbers and review rounds. We study how the number of
agents and review rounds affects performance. From
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.4 Results ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")a,
an upward trend is observed in the relationship between the accuracy and
the agent number. The growth pattern reaches an apex at 4 agents before
declining at agent number 5. Regarding the relationship between the
accuracy and the review rounds, as shown in
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.4 Results ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")b,
no significant improvement trend is detected with more review rounds.
This observation is consistent with the findings of [Chan et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib3), which suggest that continual discussions often lead
to reduced performance. The decrease may be due to issues associated
with the ever-increasing long-context modeling, which negatively affects
the performance.

Interplay between confidence scores and feedback correctness. We
manually annotate the correctness of 600 feedback each from the GSM8K
and Penguins datasets. We present the relationship between confidence
scores and feedback correctness in
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.4 Results ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"),
along with the evaluation metrics such as feedback accuracy, Area Under
the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) and Expected
Calibration Error (ECE). We have the following observations: (1) From
the confidence distribution in the first row of
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.4 Results ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"),
most verbalized confidence scores fall within the 80% to 100% range. (2)
The second row of
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.4 Results ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
shows that the accuracy of each bar ranging from 80% to 100% is much
less than 80%, suggesting significant overconfidence and miscalibration
in LLMs. (3) When comparing the math dataset GSM8K with the non-math
dataset Penguins, the feedback in the non-math dataset tends to be more
poorly calibrated and exhibit greater overconfidence. This observation
might explain the finding in ablation study
(Section [4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Results ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")),
which suggests that incorporating confidence on non-math datasets may
not improve performance.

Performance variability with diverse LLMs. We explore what kind of LLMs
can collaborate well and improve each other through the peer review
process. We set the agent number at 2, and use GSM8K for the analysis.
We focus on two key factors: the capability gap and diversity between
LLMs. The capability gap refers to the difference in the accuracy of
initial responses provided by two LLMs. The diversity is measured by
INCON [Xiong et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib35). Specifically, suppose there
are $`n`$ LLMs $`L=\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{n}\}`$, and a dataset with $`m`$
samples $`X=\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}\}`$. We define $`p^{i}_{j}`$ as the
prediction of $`l_{i}`$ on $`x_{j}`$. Then the INCON of $`L`$ on $`X`$
can be defined as:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\texttt{INCON}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\Phi(p^{1}_{k},\cdots,p^{n}_{k})}{m},
``` |  | (1) |

$`\Phi`$ is a sign function, it will be assigned a value of 1 if there
are any two variables in $`\Phi`$ that are not equal, otherwise,
$`\Phi`$ takes a value of 0.

The collaboration results are presented in
Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.4 Results ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration").
We have the following conclusions: (1) More powerful models provide more
effective feedback. The 0301 version of gpt-3.5-turbo contributes to a
2.4% accuracy improvement in the 0613 version, while the more advanced
claude-instant-1.2 leads to a significant 7.6% increase. (2) LLMs with
smaller capability gap can collaborate better. By comparing the first
two rows, despite similar levels of diversity, a capability gap increase
from 2.8 to 10.0 corresponds to a decrease in performance enhancement
for the stronger model, from a 2.4% to just a 0.6% improvement. (3) LLMs
with higher diversity can collaborate better. With a similar capability
gap, the collaboration between gpt-3.5-turbo-0301 and its 0613 version,
which have considerable diversity, results in mutual improvement. In
contrast, two claude models, with substantially lower diversity, show
that the stronger model does not benefit from the collaboration.

Impact of agent roles. Our default experiments do not include any system
messages (i.e. role specification). Here we explore diverse role prompts
for agent initialization. The roles are automatically generated by
SPP [Wang et al. (2023c)](#bib.bib31) and detailed in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Impact of Diverse Role Prompts ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration").
Experiment results in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Impact of Diverse Role Prompts ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration")
highlight the cruciality of diverse role prompt design in multi-agent
collaboration.

### 4.6 Case study

In this section, we present the complete peer review collaboration
process to a math question in GSM8K [Cobbe et al. (2021)](#bib.bib5). As
shown in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Case Study ‣ Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration"),

the question and the mistake in the ratio step), all three agents
eventually converge on the same correct solution.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we let a single model think outside the box by engaging
multiple models to collaborate on complex reasoning. We introduce a
multi-agent collaboration strategy that emulates the academic peer
review process, following an iterative cycle of creation, review, and
revision. Extensive experiments show that our approach delivers superior
accuracy across all ten datasets and three tasks compared to existing
methods. Further analysis shows the effectiveness of confidence
integration and demonstrates the superiority of feedback exchange over
mere solution sharing.

## Limitations

Despite the effectiveness of multi-agent methods, it is essential to
consider the associated costs, particularly in terms of token
consumption. The balance between effectiveness and efficiency emerges as
a critical area for exploration. In addition, the underlying factors for
LLM collaboration are more than just capability and answer diversity.
The factors also include role and tool specification of agents. Further
research may focus on identifying which types of agents can collaborate
more effectively together, thereby providing deeper insights into the
optimization of multi-agent systems in practical applications.

parameters in default setting. We fix the agent number as 3 and the

count of agents is set to 5. We also use the 16k version for an
increased number of review cycles. The review is conducted over four

at the first round in

Engineer. You are a professional engineer with practical experience in
````

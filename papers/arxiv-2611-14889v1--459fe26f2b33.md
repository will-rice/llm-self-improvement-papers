# Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic? Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction

Eno Agolli Affiliation: CUNY Graduate Center Affiliation: Saul Kripke
Center

When we judge that our reasoning was defective, it seems plausible that
we should abandon the conclusion it produced. This thought has special
force for AI agents built to assess and correct their own work. Yet
obeying such judgments can make an agent worse at distinguishing sound
reasoning from defective reasoning. An AI agent may rely on information
about its reasoning when deciding which conclusions to keep, while the
part that judges whether those conclusions are justified cannot use that
information. The agent can then exercise an epistemic competence by
retaining conclusions it judges unjustified, even when its
self-assessments are generally reliable. Nor is the difficulty resolved
by training the agent to make its conclusions and assessments agree:
agreement can be achieved by degrading either one. Crucially, I argue
that a negative assessment can remain valuable by identifying
conclusions requiring further scrutiny, without having authority to
eliminate them. The result challenges a central assumption about AI
self-correction: that bringing an agent’s beliefs into agreement with
its own self-assessment about their justification makes it better at
correcting its mistakes.

## 1. Introduction

Suppose an AI agent is asked whether some widely used software library
contains a security vulnerability. It examines the source code, then
traces how one of the library’s functions handles input supplied by an
untrusted user, and concludes, on the strength of an inference that is
in fact sound, that the function allows an attacker to execute commands
that should have been blocked. Before the agent files its report, its
investigation is reviewed by an evaluator with an excellent record, an
evaluator that on many earlier occasions caught mistakes which other
agents had overlooked, and the evaluator warns that investigations with
the features of this one have usually turned out to be defective. The
evaluator ends up finding no particular error in the agent’s reasoning,
although it issues the warning all the same, on the strength of a record
that is equally good for warnings of this kind, and on this occasion the
warning happens to be mistaken. But the agent has no way of knowing this
and the part of the agent whose task is to judge whether its own
conclusions are justified, which I will call its self-assessment,
accepts the warning, as it has good reason to do.

We can record this case as follows:

- (1)
  Vulnerability. An agent concludes, on the basis of a sound inference
  from a library’s source code, that one of the library’s functions can
  be exploited. A highly reliable evaluator warns, without locating any
  error, that investigations like this one are typically defective, and
  the warning is misleading. The agent’s self-assessment accepts the
  warning, while the agent goes on believing its conclusion.

The last sentence of
([(1)](#Sx1.I1.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
is about an agent who believes something while judging that it isn’t
justified in believing it. An agent in this condition exhibits what some
epistemologists call epistemic akrasia (is epistemically akratic).

- (2)
  Epistemic akrasia. An agent is epistemically akratic with respect to a
  proposition $`p`$ when it believes $`p`$ while also believing that,
  given its present evidence, it is not justified in believing $`p`$.

What should be done about an agent in this condition?¹¹ 1 For
formulations of epistemic akrasia in terms of what one’s evidence
supports, see Horowitz (2014) and Greco (2014); Lasonen-Aarnio (2020)
formulates the corresponding requirements in terms of what rationality
permits. In the cases this paper considers, the agent’s judgment that
its reasoning was probably defective is at the same time a judgment that
it is not justified in keeping the conclusion of that reasoning, so the
different formulations coincide. For an agent whose value lies in large
part in its capacity to correct its own mistakes, the natural answer
seems that the belief should give way to the judgment, a requirement
along the lines of:

- (3)
  Self-Assessment Authority. When an agent’s self-assessment judges that
  the agent is not justified in believing $`p`$, the agent should give
  up its belief that $`p`$.

It is worth noticing at the outset that the demand to resolve such
conflicts, as expressed in
[(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"),
can be built into an artificial agent directly, in probably at least two
ways. As a rule that withdraws any conclusion that the agent’s
self-assessment rejects, a rule that we may call the veto, or it can be
built in as a training objective that rewards the agent whenever its
conclusions and its self-assessments agree, so that the agent learns to
avoid conflicts between them.²² 2 Kadavath et al. (2022) show that
language models can assess, with some reliability, whether their own
answers are true. Self-evaluation of this kind is now used both when
models are deployed and when they are trained: see Huang et al. (2024)
on prompting models to correct their own reasoning, Africa and Ting
(2025) on coupling the evaluations of a model of its own work to the
rewards it receives in training, and Baker et al. (2025) on
incorporating a monitor of a reasoning of a model into its training
objective. The veto gives the criticism authority while the training
objective leaves open which attitude acquires it.

There is something close to a truism, one might think, in
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
when it comes to agents built to correct themselves on grounds that an
agent which recognizes a conclusion as unsupported, and keeps it anyway,
seems to have failed at the very thing that its self-assessment was for.
That conclusion, I think, is precipitous. A negative self-assessment is
a judgment like any other, a judgment that some process forms on the
basis of some information, and whether it deserves to govern the belief
it concerns depends, as I will argue, on how that process and that
information compare with the process and the information that produced
the belief.

The aim of this paper is to challenge the priority that
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
gives the agent’s judgment about its own justification. The strongest
reason for that priority is that an agent which cannot identify the
occasions on which its self-assessment is mistaken should normally
follow a critic with a reliable track record. I argue that this defense
can fail for artificial agents. The process that determines whether a
conclusion is kept may respond to indications that its reasoning was
sound which the process assessing that reasoning cannot access. Where
those indications reliably distinguish good reasoning from bad even
among conclusions the assessment rejects, retention against the adverse
judgment exercises a capacity that automatic deference would suppress.
In this way, interestingly, the resulting conflict can be a
manifestation of epistemic competence, although the agent continues to
judge that it has no justification for the belief. Enforcing agreement
by requiring the belief to yield can annihilate that competence.

Yet showing that the belief need not yield leaves open what should
replace that priority. Another agent can keep a conclusion its
self-assessment rejects because it has overlooked a mistake that the
assessment has detected. The two conflicts look alike, but repairing
them calls for opposite changes. Nor is the problem solved by rewarding
agreement: that could improve the agent’s reasoning or simply teach its
self-assessment to approve whatever the agent keeps.

What is key, I argue, is what each capacity can detect about mistakes
the other misses, especially in cases where the two disagree. I call the
resulting constraint Comparative Authority. It allows the
self-assessment to challenge a conclusion without thereby settling
whether the conclusion must be withdrawn. Its criticism can warrant
verification, qualification of what is reported, or delaying action,
even when the agent has good grounds for retaining its belief. The
central problem is to let these two capacities correct one another
without destroying the independent information that makes their
disagreement useful.

One might take all this to be just shy of a decision-theoretic platitude
that a better-informed process should not be overruled by a
worse-informed one. But that might be a myopic diagnosis. The decision
theory on which the paper draws is used here as just a tool, its main
contribution lying primarily in three results. The first is that the
strongest defense of
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
rests on a premise about which dispositions can be had by an agent, a
premise whose support depends on assuming that an agent’s sensitivity to
its own errors is available to its self-assessment, and which can fail
for a describable class of artificial agents (Sections 2 and 3). The
second is that the general reliability of a self-assessment is, in and
of itself, no guide to its reliability on the cases where the veto
overrules the agent, since those cases are selected by the disagreement
(Section 4). The third is that the mere fact of conflict cannot
determine the direction of repair, so that any procedure treating
agreement between conclusions and self-assessments as a measure of
improvement just cannot tell the repair of an agent from its degradation
(Section 5).

The argument is organized to give
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
the strongest defense I can find at each stage, and then, at that same
stage, to see where that defense gives out. Section 2 shows that the
requirement does not follow from what rationality demands, for
philosophers who agree that akrasia is a defect disagree about which of
the two attitudes should give way, and it then shows that the
requirement has a better defense than an appeal to rationality, a
defense on which deferring to the self-assessment is the best policy
available to an agent that cannot tell, case by case, when its
self-assessment has been misled, so that everything after that point
turns on whether this premise holds for artificial agents.

Section 3 describes artificial agents for which the premise can fail,
agents whose process for keeping or abandoning their conclusions draws
on information about their own errors that their self-assessment cannot
use. Section 4 shows, with a worked example, that for, such agents, the
best way of handling their conclusions can leave them akratic, and that
their akrasia need not manifest the incompetence that akrasia is usually
taken to reveal. Section 5 takes up the last resort open to a defender
of the requirement, which is to replace the veto with some other rule
for resolving conflicts, and shows why the conflict alone cannot justify
the choice of a repair. Section 6 states Comparative Authority and what
it demands of the design of self-correcting systems, Section 7 asks
whether the conflicting states are really attitudes of a single agent,
and Section 8 describes how the empirical premises of the argument can
be tested.

## 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement

A defender of the the veto will naturally present it as the
implementation of a requirement of rationality, so the most immediately
obvious first question to ask about
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
is whether rationality demands it, and the answer, as we shall see, is
that rationality demands something weaker, which leaves open the
question that
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
answers.

The issue becomes clearer once we distinguish two kinds of evidence that
the agent in
([(1)](#Sx1.I1.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
possesses. The source code supplies what epistemologists call
first-order evidence, evidence that bears directly on the question the
agent is investigating, which here is the question whether the function
can be exploited. The evaluator’s warning supplies what they call
higher-order evidence, evidence that bears on whether the agent’s
reasoning about that question was sound, and that bears on the question
only by that indirect route.³³ 3 The distinction and the puzzles it
generates are developed in Feldman (2005), Christensen (2010), Kelly
(2010), Lasonen-Aarnio (2014), and Horowitz (2014); see also Sliwa and
Horowitz (2015) and Dorst (2020). The principle from which
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
draws its appeal concerns how a rational agent’s beliefs about the world
relate to its beliefs about its own justification, and it prohibits one
combination of the two.

- (4)
  Non-Akrasia Requirement. A rational agent never believes $`p`$ while
  believing that it is not justified in believing $`p`$.

At this point we have to notice that a requirement of this kind can take
two different forms. The requirement in
([(4)](#Sx2.I4.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
is what philosophers call wide-scope: it prohibits holding a belief
together with the judgment that the belief is unjustified, but it says
nothing about which of the two an agent that finds itself with both
should give up, so that it is satisfied equally by an agent that gives
up the belief and by an agent that gives up the judgment. The
requirement in
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
by contrast, is narrow-scope, in the sense that it directs the revision
of one particular attitude, namely the belief.⁴⁴ 4 On the distinction
between wide-scope and narrow-scope requirements, see Broome (1999) and
Kolodny (2005). Therefore, a designer who implements
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
has taken a stand that goes beyond
([(4)](#Sx2.I4.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
the stand that whenever the two attitudes conflict it is the belief that
should yield, and the epistemology of higher-order evidence shows that
this stand is contested, even among philosophers who accept
([(4)](#Sx2.I4.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")).

On what I shall call defeat views, misleading higher-order evidence
defeats the justification that the first-order evidence provides, which
is to say that once the agent in
([(1)](#Sx1.I1.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
has received the warning, the source code no longer justifies its
belief, so that the agent ought to give the belief up.⁵⁵ 5 See Feldman
(2005), Christensen (2010), Horowitz (2014), and Sliwa and Horowitz
(2015). Smithies (2012) argues that epistemic akrasia is irrational on
the ground that justification is accessible to the subject, and Greco
(2014) argues on different grounds that it is always irrational. These
views support the direction of revision prescribed by
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")).
Their strongest argument against permitting akratic combinations is
worth considering here, since we will return to it. A reasoner who
believes $`p`$, and who also believes that her evidence does not support
$`p`$, is in a position to combine the two beliefs into the conclusion
that her evidence is misleading about $`p`$ on this occasion, and a
reasoner who argues in this way can dismiss every warning about her own
reliability by appealing to the very conclusions whose reliability the
warning calls into question. The name usually given to this pattern of
reasoning is bootstrapping, and a view that permits akratic combinations
owes an account of why it does not license it.⁶⁶ 6 Elga (2007) and
Horowitz (2014). Christensen’s (2011) principle of Independence, which
requires that one’s assessment of a challenge to one’s belief that $`p`$
not rely on the reasoning behind that belief, is designed to rule this
pattern out.

On the right-reason view defended by Titelbaum, akratic combinations are
also never rational, but the explanation runs in the opposite direction,
since on this view a reasoner cannot be rationally mistaken about what
rationality requires of her, so that when a warning is misleading it is
the higher-order judgment, and not the belief, that ought to give way.⁷⁷
7 Titelbaum (2015). Worsnip (2018) argues that the requirement to
respond to one’s evidence and the requirement of coherence between one’s
beliefs and one’s judgments about them can conflict, and Worsnip (2026)
defends the priority of coherence in a central class of such conflicts.
A philosopher who accepts
([(4)](#Sx2.I4.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
together with the right-reason view, thus, rejects
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
in the case with which the paper began. On level-splitting views,
finally, misleading higher-order evidence need not defeat at all: the
source code can go on justifying the agent’s belief while the warning
justifies the agent’s judgment that the belief is unjustified, and the
combination of the two can then be rational, although it combines a
well-supported belief with a mistaken judgment about that very belief.⁸⁸
8 Lasonen-Aarnio (2014; 2020); Coates (2012); Weatherson (2019).
Williamson (2014) argues for the related possibility of knowing $`p`$
although it is very improbable on one’s evidence that one knows $`p`$,
and Field (2024; 2026) defends the epistemic value of certain incoherent
combinations of this kind, including their role in prompting further
inquiry. See also Christensen (2016), who develops a connection between
accommodating higher-order evidence and the possibility of epistemic
akrasia.

So the dialectic, as it bears on the requirement in
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
stands as follows: defeat views support the direction of revision that
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
prescribes, the right-reason view prescribes the opposite direction in
the case at hand, and level-splitting views deny that either attitude
must give way. What the three views share, nonetheless is the
recognition that a negative self-assessment is a fallible judgment, a
judgment whose verdict can be mistaken. Whether it can then be
rationally held is the point on which these views differ.

However — had the argument stopped here, a defender of the veto would
have an easy reply, and it is worth seeing the reply in full, because it
provides the strongest defense of
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
that I know of. My reply grants that a self-assessment can be mistaken,
and points out that this concession gives the agent no guidance it could
use, for an agent that cannot tell when its self-assessment has been
misled has, in effect, only two ways of responding to a warning that its
self-assessment has accepted. It can keep its conclusion whatever the
warning says, or it can give its conclusion up whenever such a warning
arrives, and when warnings are usually right, the second way of
responding does better on balance, since the mistaken conclusions it
avoids keeping outnumber the correct conclusions it gives up. Thus, The
defense justifies the veto as a policy, whatever rationality requires in
a particular case.

This defense is not an invention of designers, since it’s in effect the
argument that one of the leading defenders of level-splitting, Maria
Lasonen-Aarnio, gives for why a reasoner who keeps a belief in the face
of misleading higher-order evidence is open to criticism, even though,
on her view, the reasoner’s belief can remain justified. To state her
argument we will need the notion of a disposition, which in her usage is
a tendency to respond in a certain way across a range of situations, and
we need the idea that a disposition is evaluated by asking how often its
manifestations, that is, the occasions on which it actually operates,
are epistemic successes such as knowledge or true belief, by comparison
with the other dispositions that the subject could have had in its
place.⁹⁹ 9 Lasonen-Aarnio (2010; 2021a). “\[W\]hether one manifests good
dispositions depends on what feasible alternatives there are, which in
turn depends on one’s cognitive makeup” (Lasonen-Aarnio 2021a, §3). The
framework is developed in full in Lasonen (2026), where the norms it
defends are said to urge “manifesting the best feasible dispositions”
that are “available to beings like us”.

A reasoner who keeps her belief against a credible warning manifests a
disposition to keep beliefs whatever such warnings say, and this
disposition does worse than a disposition to give beliefs up in response
to such warnings, because the warnings are usually right. There is a
third disposition that would do better than both, a disposition that
gives a belief up when the warning is accurate and keeps it when the
warning is misleading, but Lasonen-Aarnio holds that dispositions which
discriminate in this way ‘‘are not normally humanly feasible’’, for a
reasoner who had one would have to be sensitive to having made an error
which, by hypothesis, she had failed to detect.¹⁰¹⁰ 10 Lasonen-Aarnio
(2021a, abstract). Her reason is given in a discussion of climbers who
double-check their equipment: “In order to be disposed to double check
only when one has committed an error, one would have to be sensitive to
having committed the error. But how could one be thus sensitive when the
error was undetected in the first place, often due to compromised
cognitive skills?” (2021a, §3).

So we have a shift in the dialectic here. The epistemological dispute
described above left open which attitude should give way, but the
defense of the veto just given does not need to settle that dispute, as
it can grant, with the level-splitter, that the agent’s belief may
remain justified in the case at hand, and still conclude that an agent
built to defer does better than any alternative open to it. What the
defense does need is a single premise, and it needs that premise in a
somewhat stronger form than the one Lasonen-Aarnio states, because her
premise concerns a disposition that discriminates perfectly between
accurate and misleading warnings, whereas a disposition that
discriminated imperfectly could still do better than deference, and
would then defeat the defense just as surely. The premise that the
defense needs can be stated as follows.

- (5)
  No Discrimination. An agent cannot have a disposition that
  distinguishes accurate warnings from misleading ones well enough to do
  better, across the range of cases it faces, than a disposition to
  defer.

What follows depends significantly on
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")).
Importantly,
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
is a claim about which dispositions a given kind of agent can have, and
so, unlike the dispute about rationality surveyed above, a claim on
which evidence about how artificial agents actually work can bear
directly. If
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
holds for artificial agents in the way that Lasonen-Aarnio’s argument
assumes it to hold for human beings, then the veto is the best policy
available to such agents, whatever we say about rationality, and the
argument of this paper fails. If on the other hand,
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
can fail for artificial agents, then the policy defense of the veto
fails with it, and the question which attitude should give way in a
conflict has to be reopened for those agents. The next section argues
that
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
can fail, and explains how.

## 3. Dissociation

Lasonen-Aarnio’s reason for denying that discriminating dispositions are
feasible, which is also the natural reason for accepting
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
is that an agent with such a disposition would have to be sensitive to
errors it had failed to detect, and the force of this reason depends on
an assumption that is natural to make about human reasoners. We tend to
assume that whatever sensitivity an agent has to its own errors is
something its assessment of its own reasoning can draw on, and hence
that an error escaping the assessment is an error to which the agent as
a whole is insensitive. For current language models that assumption is
precipitous, and the way in which it fails is the way in which
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
can fail.¹¹¹¹ 11 The separation also has a human precedent in work on
implicit cognitive control and explicit metacognition (Shea et al.
2014). The present argument applies wherever sufficiently discriminating
retention and restricted self-assessment coexist after a credible
warning, whether the agent is artificial or human. I would say he human
precedent does not, as it stands, establish that this conjunction
obtains.

I want to distinguish three things here, regarding human reasoners. The
first is the information that a system contains, by which I mean
information that could in principle be recovered from the system’s
internal states by someone examining them. The second is the information
that the system’s process for keeping or abandoning its conclusions
uses, where I mean information that actually shapes which conclusions
the system reaches and which of them it keeps when they are challenged.
The third is the information that is available to the system’s
self-assessment, by which I mean information that actually shapes the
system’s judgments about whether its conclusions are justified. The
assumption just described identifies the second of these with the third,
and there is now concrete evidence that in language models they can come
apart, with information about the errors of a model that is contained in
the model (and in some cases used by it) without being available to its
self-assessment.

Consider, first, what language models contain. Researchers can train a
simple classifier, often called a probe, to predict from a moder’s
internal states whether a statement the model is reading or producing is
true, and such classifiers succeeds well above chance.¹²¹² 12 Azaria and
Mitchell (2023); Burns et al. (2023); Marks and Tegmark (2024). More
directly relevant is a comparison between what such classifiers can
recover and what the model can report. Orgad et al. (2025) find that
classifiers trained on the internal states of a model at the words of
its answer detect the model’s errors better than several baseline
methods, among them the method of asking the model itself whether its
answer is true, so that, for one model on one question-answering
dataset, the classifier reaches a score of 0.85 on a standard measure of
how well a method separates correct from incorrect answers, where the
model’s own assessment reaches 0.74.¹³¹³ 13 Orgad et al. (2025, §3.3).
The measure is the area under the receiver operating characteristic
curve, on which 0.5 corresponds to chance and 1 to perfect separation.
The method of asking the model whether its answer is true is due to
Kadavath et al. (2022).

Still, a model need not use everything it contains. The same study
illustrates this especially clearly: a model ‘‘may encode the correct
answer, yet consistently generate an incorrect one’’. Evidence that such
information is used, and in particular that it shapes whether a model
keeps a challenged answer, comes from other work. Kumaran et al. (2025)
measure the confidence of a model in its answer by the probabilities
that the model assigns to the words of the answer, and find that this
confidence predicts whether the model changes its answer after receiving
contrary advice, with a sharp threshold: below it, the models they study
are ‘‘exquisitely sensitive to contrary advice’’, while above it ‘‘they
abruptly become resistant to changing their minds’’.¹⁴¹⁴ 14 Kumaran et
al. (2025). The analyses of confidence concern Gemma 3 12B. The same
paper documents two further tendencies that bear on where such a
threshold falls: a model shown its own earlier answer becomes more
confident in it, and models give contrary advice more weight than a
Bayesian benchmark would. In related work, Kumaran et al. (2026) report
causal evidence that representations of confidence inside models drive
their decisions to abstain from answering.

Finally, what the self-reports of a model can draw on is limited.
Lindsey (2025), who injected representations of known concepts into
internal states of a model and then asked the model about its own
states, found ‘‘a limited, functional form of introspective awareness’’
in the most capable models tested, with success on about 20 % of trials
under the most favorable conditions, and concluded that ‘‘failures of
introspection remain the norm’’. Binder et al. (2025) found that models
fine-tuned to predict their own behavior do so better than other models
trained on the same behavior, but only on simple tasks, and Wang et al.
(2026) found that the confidence models express in words fails to guide
their decisions to abstain, even when the penalties for error make
abstaining the best choice.¹⁵¹⁵ 15 See also Xiong et al. (2024), who
find that the confidence models express in words tends toward
overconfidence. The gap between what a model represents and what it can
be brought to report is the subject of the alignment problem known as
eliciting latent knowledge (Christiano et al. 2021); in the
configuration described below, the reporting channel in question is the
self-assessment of the model.

It is important to be clear about the limits of these findings, of which
two bear directly on the argument. The first is that the findings
concern whether the answers of a model are correct, whereas what bears
on higher-order evidence is whether the reasoning of a model was sound,
and the two can come apart, as, of course, a conclusion can be true
although the reasoning that produced it was defective, and false
although the reasoning was sound but started from misleading inputs.¹⁶¹⁶
16 By “sound reasoning” I just mean reasoning competently responsive to
the information from which it proceeds, which may itself be misleading.
Soundness in this sense does not require true premises, as the technical
notion of a sound deductive argument does. A signal that tracked only
correctness would favor keeping true conclusions reached by bad
reasoning, and what the argument requires is a signal that tracks
soundness, which no study has yet measured. There is also a second
limitation: these findings do not establish that the required
sensitivity to soundness guides retention while remaining unavailable to
self-assessment after receiving a credible warning. This is just the
circumstance the argument depends on. Orgad et al. themselves report
that their classifiers transfer poorly from one dataset to another.¹⁷¹⁷
17 Orgad et al. (2025, §4) report that most of the performance of their
classifiers across datasets “can be achieved by logit-based truthfulness
detection”, that is, by methods that use only the probabilities the
model assigns to its own answer. So the findings motivate the following
configuration, without establishing that any current system instantiates
it.

- (6)
  Dissociation. An agent is dissociated when (a) its process for keeping
  or abandoning its conclusions under challenge uses an internal signal
  that carries information about whether its reasoning was sound,
  and (b) neither that signal, nor evidence about how reliably the
  agent’s keeping of its conclusions tracks the soundness of its
  reasoning, is available to the agent’s self-assessment.

By a signal I mean an internal state of the agent whose value varies
from case to case, such as the confidence that Kumaran et al. measure.
Clause (b) has two parts due to the fact that (as shown in Section 4) an
agent could come to be informed about its own sensitivity to error
either by consulting the signal or by learning how reliable its keeping
of conclusions has proved to be. Dissociation is all about access. It
does not by itself show that the signal adds anything to what the
self-assessment already has because the same information may be supplied
by another cue. The example, below, requires that the signal make a
further difference to which conclusions are worth keeping within the
cases that the assessment rejects. The case to keep in view is a
dissociated version of the case with which the paper began.

- (7)
  Dissociated Vulnerability. Just like in
  ([(1)](#Sx1.I1.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
  but the agent is dissociated: its signal in this case is strong, the
  signal governs whether the agent abandons its conclusion when
  challenged, and the agent’s self-assessment, which cannot consult the
  signal, accepts the warning of the evaluator.

There is a further question here, about whether the signal is part of
the agent’s evidence. A learned check of particular inferential steps
could produce a state which represents whether those steps succeed and
guides revision when they fail. There is reason to count such a state
among the agent’s grounds, since its content concerns the inference’s
success and is already used in responding to that feature; excluding it
solely because the self-assessment cannot draw on it requires a further
restriction on evidence possession.¹⁸¹⁸ 18 On information accessible for
one purpose but not another, see Elga and Rayo (2021). Reliability
acquired through training alone would not establish possession. On this
construal, the signal supplies higher-order evidence alongside the
warning, and the self-assessment omits one of the agent’s grounds. The
check’s credentials must be established independently of the contested
conclusion and must themselves withstand the warning.

A dissociated agent is not a counterexample to
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
just yet though, since
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
concerns which dispositions an agent can have, and nothing said so far
shows that the disposition the dissociated agent manifests does better
than deference. Whether it does is the question of the next section.

## 4. Stick to the conclusion (sometimes)!

A natural reaction to a dissociated agent is to say that it is simply
malfunctioning, since it keeps conclusions that its own self-assessment
rejects, and so to think that its signal makes no difference to the
policy defense of the veto. To see why that conclusion is precipitous we
can test the policy defense,, which is a claim about which way of
responding to warnings does best across a range of cases, against an
example.

Call a conclusion well-founded when the reasoning that produced it was
sound, and ill-founded when it was not, and consider a thousand cases in
which an agent has reached a conclusion and then recieved a warning from
an evaluator whose warnings are accurate nine times in ten. In 900 of
these cases the conclusion is ill-founded and the warning accurate, and
in the remaining 100 the conclusion is well-founded and the warning
misleading. To compare ways of responding to the warnings, let’s count
keeping a well-founded conclusion as a gain of one unit, keeping an
ill-founded conclusion as a loss of one unit, and giving a conclusion up
as neither. For this example, suppose also that the well-founded
conclusions are true and the ill-founded ones false, so that the same
comparison can be read as one of accuracy.

Begin with an agent that has no signal, an agent of which
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
is true. The two ways of responding that are open to it are to give up
every warned conclusion, which I shall call deference, and to keep every
warned conclusion, which I shall call unconditional retention. Deference
scores zero, as it keeps nothing, while unconditional retention keeps
the 100 well-founded conclusions together with the 900 ill-founded ones,
and so scores minus 800. So, for an agent without a signal, the policy
defense of the veto is right, and by a wide margin.

Now suppose that the agent is dissociated and that its signal is present
in 95 % of the well-founded cases and in 5% of the ill-founded ones, so
that it is present in 95 of the 100 well-founded cases and in 45 of the
900 ill-founded ones. The agent now has open to it a third way of
responding.

- (8)
  The Three policies. (a) Deference: give up every warned conclusion.

  \(b\) Signal-guided retention: keep a warned conclusion when the
  signal is present, and give it up otherwise.

  \(c\) Unconditional retention: Keep every warned conclusion.

Signal-guided retention keeps 140 conclusions, of which 95 are
well-founded and 45 ill-founded, and so scores plus 50, which is better
than deference by 50 units and better than unconditional retention by 850. No other policy for keeping or abandoning conclusions that uses
only this signal does better.¹⁹¹⁹ 19 With a signal that is either
present or absent, the only ways of responding are to keep warned
conclusions never, always, only when the signal is present, or only when
it is absent, and the last of these scores minus 850. Randomizing
between these policies cannot improve on the best of them. In general,
if $`\pi`$ is the proportion of well-founded conclusions among warned
cases, $`v`$ the value of keeping a well-founded conclusion and $`w`$
the cost of keeping an ill-founded one, keeping a conclusion when the
signal is present does better than deference just in case the signal is
more than $`(1-\pi)w/(\pi v)`$ times as likely to be present in
well-founded as in ill-founded warned cases. In the example, $`\pi=0.1`$
and $`v=w`$, so the threshold is 9, and the signal is 19 times as likely
in the one kind of case as in the other. This is a standard rule of
decision theory; its interest here is that it identifies the quantity an
empirical test must measure, namely how well the signal separates
well-founded from ill-founded conclusions among the cases that have
received a credible warning (Section 8). Schoenfield (2018) draws a
related distinction between assessing a way of updating one’s beliefs
and assessing the plan to update in that way.

Among the 140 conclusions that it keeps, moreover, the proportion that
are well-founded is 95 in 140, or about 68%, against 10% among the
warned conclusions as a whole. So the policy defense of the veto, which
was correct for the agent without a signal, gives the wrong verdict for
the dissociated agent: the premise in
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
is false of this agent, and with it falls the conclusion that deference
is the best policy open to it.

Let’s consider at this point what the dissociated agent’s
self-assessment says in the 140 cases in which it keeps its conclusion.
If it had only the evaluator’s record to go on, it would reject them
all. Making it approve whatever the agent keeps would preserve the score
of plus 50. We can give the assessment information of its own, however,
without changing the retention figures. Let $`S`$ record the signal
already described, and suppose that the assessment also draws on a cue
$`A`$, such as a favorable result from a fallible check of the contested
inference, which the retention process does not use. Take the following
distribution among the same thousand warned cases (Table 1).

| Cues              | Well-founded | Ill-founded | Proportion well-founded |
| ----------------- | ------------ | ----------- | ----------------------- |
| $`S,A`$           | 38           | 1           | 38/39                   |
| $`S,\neg A`$      | 57           | 44          | 57/101                  |
| $`\neg S,A`$      | 2            | 19          | 2/21                    |
| $`\neg S,\neg A`$ | 3            | 836         | 3/839                   |

By clause (b) of
([(6)](#Sx3.I6.i1 "1st item ‣ 3. Dissociation ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
the self-assessment cannot consult $`S`$ and has no evidence about how
reliably the agent’s keeping of conclusions tracks soundness. On what it
can see, a conclusion is well-founded in 40 of 60 cases when $`A`$ is
present, and in 60 of 940 when it is absent. With a threshold of even
odds for its judgment of justification, it approves in the first class
and disapproves in the second, leaving the agent akratic in the 101
cases with $`S`$ and no $`A`$.²⁰²⁰ 20 The adverse judgment is about the
agent’s justification given its present evidence, even though the
process forming that judgment has incomplete access to that evidence. A
report that this particular checker cannot verify the conclusion would
not suffice. Section 7 specifies the further functional conditions on
attributing both attitudes to one agent. Imposing the veto here would
discard 57 well-founded conclusions along with 44 ill-founded ones,
thereby lowering the retention score from 50 to 37.

Notice here that what makes this result possible. Across the thousand
warned cases the self-assessment is right 920 times, approving 40
well-founded conclusions and rejecting 880 ill-founded ones, and by that
measure it is a highly reliable judge of soundness. The veto, however,
does not act on the thousand cases: it acts only on the 101 cases in
which the self-assessment rejects a conclusion that the agent keeps, and
on those cases the self-assessment is wrong 57 times. The cases on which
the veto intervenes are selected by the disagreement, and since the
agent keeps a conclusion only when its signal is present, and the signal
is present mostly when the reasoning was sound, disagreement
concentrates the cases in which the assessment errs. So, the general
reliability of a self-assessment, however high, does not guide to its
reliability on the cases where it would overrule the agent. But it is
reliability on those cases alone that could justify the veto.

Moreover, suppose that retained conclusions are used in ongoing
reasoning before being reported to other investigators, and that an
unchecked report, like retention, earns one unit if it’s well-founded or
otherwise loses one. A report can instead undergo verification, at a
cost of 0.2 units, which establishes whether the reasoning is
well-founded and suppresses ill-founded reports, yielding one or zero
less the cost; withholding a report yields zero. In this way, retention
and reporting score two uses of the conclusion, before and after any
check.

The assessment can now improve the handling of a conclusion, which it
would be a mistake to withdraw since the disputed conclusions are
well-founded 57 times in 101, or about 56% of the time, whereas the
retained conclusions it approves are well-founded 38 times in 39, or
about 97%. Both classes are worth retaining, but checking is worth its
cost only in the first, where the risk of an ill-founded report exceeds
0.2. The agent can do best at both activities while remaining akratic:
it keeps conclusions on $`S`$, and its adverse assessment sends just the
disputed conclusions for verification.²¹²¹ 21 For a case with
probability $`q`$ of well-foundedness, retention and unchecked reporting
each score $`2q-1`$, verification scores $`q-c`$, and withdrawal or
withholding scores zero. Here $`c=0.2`$, so the retained cases satisfy
$`1/2<q_{\mathrm{disputed}}<1-c<q_{\mathrm{approved}}`$, and
verification also beats withholding in the disputed class. In the last
two rows all nonzero options score negatively. Taking the best option
for each activity in each row gives $`50+[(38-1)+(57-0.2(101))]=123.8`$.
This bounds even policies using both cues, and randomization cannot
improve the bound.

Suppose, by contrast, that the self-assessment could consult the signal.
It would then judge each conclusion kept more likely well-founded than
not, since both $`38/39`$ and $`57/101`$ exceed 0.5. The conflict would
disappear while the agent continued to keep the same conclusions. The
conflict would disappear as well if the self-assessment, without
consulting the signal, had evidence of the track record of the agent’s
keeping of conclusions, since the fact that the agent had kept a
conclusion after a warning would then be evidence that the conclusion
was well-founded. It may look as though using this evidence would be a
form of bootstrapping, but it isn’t, since what makes bootstrapping
objectionable is that it vindicates a method by appeal to the method’s
own disputed outputs, whereas the reliability on which this evidence
relies would have been established by comparing the agent’s keeping of
conclusions with the facts.

There is also a coherent alternative that needs no such integration:
make the self-assessment approve whatever the agent keeps, the echo
considered in Section 5, while preserving its old verdict as a flag for
verification. An agent that suspended the adverse judgment while
retaining the flag could do as well. Either would attain the same
optimum, whereas an echo that erased the distinction and reported
everything unchecked would score only 100. We conclude that what a
successful repair must preserve is the contribution of each process, and
agreement alone cannot show that it has done so.

We can state more generally when relying on the assessment alone
preserves the best retention policy. Call its information screening
when, conditional on that information, what the retention process uses
provides no further information about soundness. For policies maximizing
expected retention scores on their respective information, the
assessment-only policy never withdraws a conclusion that the joint
policy would keep, for every choice of the two positive weights, if and
only if screening holds.²²²² 22 Assume a finite common probability
distribution among warned cases, and let $`G`$ be soundness, $`R`$ the
retention process’s information and $`M`$ the assessment’s. Put
$`q_{M}=P(G\mid M)`$ and $`q_{RM}=P(G\mid R,M)`$; retain when
$`q>\theta=w/(v+w)`$, withdrawing at equality. Screening means
$`q_{RM}=q_{M}`$ on every cell of positive probability. If it fails, the
averaging identity $`q_{M}=\mathbb{E}[q_{RM}\mid M]`$ gives a cell with
$`q_{RM}>q_{M}`$, so choose $`\theta`$ between them. The converse is
immediate. The same averaging argument gives the condition for a
retention-only policy never to keep what the joint policy would
withdraw, for all weights: $`P(G\mid R,M)=P(G\mid R)`$. These
comparisons abstract from access costs and are an elementary application
of the value of information (Good 1967). At particular weights,
additional information can shift a probability without changing the best
decision, as the assessment’s cue does for retention in the example
while changing whether a check is worthwhile.

But I want to be explicit about the assumptions behind the example: the
equal weights given to keeping well-founded and ill-founded conclusions,
the threshold of even odds at which the self-assessment judges retention
justified, the distribution of the two cues, and the cost and
reliability of verification. The example establishes a possibility with
a definite structure, in which an agent is akratic because the process
that keeps its conclusions is better informed than the process that
assesses them, and in which removing the akrasia by deference would make
the agent’s handling of its conclusions worse. The akratic state may be
rational all things considered, but it need not, which is the question
on which the views of Section 2 divide. Where the signal supplies
undefeated evidence which, together with the agent’s other evidence,
suffices to justify retention, however, the veto suppresses a response
to part of the agent’s higher-order evidence, and the appropriate repair
is to give the self-assessment access to those grounds. A defeat
theorist can then criticize the adverse judgment while rejecting the
veto. Where the signal instead constitutes a reliable competence whose
output the agent does not possess as evidence, the comparative cost of
deference remains, even if its rationality is somehow disputed.²³²³ 23
The threshold of one half is fixed by the scoring rule: the probability
of about 56% in the disputed class need not justify outright belief.
Sliwa & Horowitz (2015, §3) distinguish rational educated guesses from
outright belief.

A defender of the veto could fall back on a second line of defense,
granting everything the example shows and locates the fault elsewhere.
The thought is that, even if the dissociated agent’s handling of its
conclusions does better than deference, the agent, in disregarding its
own adverse judgment, manifests an incompetence, a disposition not to
respond to its own assessment of its reasons, and that an agent fit to
be trusted should not be built to manifest such a disposition. We could
draw a more developed version of this thought from Lasonen-Aarnio’s
account of incoherence, on which incoherent states are open to criticism
because they manifest bad dispositions, i.e., dispositions at odds with
what she calls ‘‘normative competence’’, and on which this holds even in
cases where the incoherent subject happens to do better: ‘‘Even if, all
things considered, a subject in fact does better by failing to intend to
do A while believing that she ought to do A, she is still manifesting a
bad disposition.’’²⁴²⁴ 24 Lasonen-Aarnio (2021b, §4.1). On her view,
there are no requirements of structural rationality, that is, no
requirements of coherence over and above the substantive norms on belief
and intention; what is wrong with incoherence is explained instead by
the dispositions it manifests.

But notice more carefully what her account takes to be bad about the
akratic disposition. The account explains the badness by appeal to a
specific feature, namely that the akratic disposition manifests
‘‘indiscriminately’’ as a ‘‘failure to take into account seeming
conspicuous reasons, whether or not those seeming reasons are real’’,
and it rests explicitly on the assumption ‘‘that it is not feasible to
have dispositions that discriminate between cases in which one has a
genuine reason, and cases in which one has a mere belief’’.²⁵²⁵ 25
Lasonen-Aarnio (2021b, §4.1). The discussion there concerns practical
akrasia, in which a subject fails to intend what she believes she ought
to do. Her treatment of epistemic akrasia has the same structure,
locating the fault in a disposition “to fail to correctly respond to a
special class of conclusive and conspicuous reasons” (Lasonen-Aarnio
2020, 597), and, as Section 2 noted, her account of retention in the
face of higher-order evidence rests on the parallel premise that
discriminating dispositions “are not normally humanly feasible”
(Lasonen-Aarnio 2021a, abstract). The subject who does better despite
her akrasia does so by luck, on this account, because her disposition
would disregard her judgment just as readily in the cases where the
judgment was correct.

However --- in this sense the dissociated agent’s disposition is not
indiscriminate. Within the cases in which the self-assessment
disapproves, it keeps 95% of the well-founded conclusions and only 5 %
of the ill-founded ones, so that its resistance varies with the
epistemic feature at issue even while the adverse verdict is held fixed.
What distinguishes the disposition from stubbornness is this sensitivity
across cases, by which the agent relinquishes most ill-founded
conclusions while retaining most well-founded ones, and in the cases
where it keeps a conclusion against its assessment, the adverse judgment
still changes what it does before reporting.²⁶²⁶ 26 The practical
precedent is Arpaly’s (2000) defense of cases in which acting against
one’s best judgment is more rational than acting on it. The present
argument asks when a corresponding division between belief and
self-assessment manifests a competence that enforcing deference would
disable. On the level-splitting account under consideration, where
misleading warnings need not remove the first-order justification, such
a disposition can be a competence in retaining justified beliefs,
although it remains fallible and the agent may have defects
elsewhere.Defending the veto should explain why disabling this
discrimination improves the agent’s competence.

So the dialectic is as follows (so far). The requirement in
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
does not follow from what rationality demands (Section 2), its policy
defense fails for dissociated agents because the premise in
([(5)](#Sx2.I5.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
is false of them, and their akrasia alone does not establish that they
manifest an indiscriminate disposition. What remains open to a defender
of the requirement is to give up the veto while keeping its spirit,
either by adopting some other rule for resolving conflicts between
conclusions and self-assessments or by training the agent for agreement
and leaving it to the training to determine which attitude moves. In the
next section, I explain why the conflict alone cannot justify such a
rule.

## 5. Conflict Cannot Tell Us What to Revise

The defender’s last resort presupposes that a rule consulting only the
conflict can tell us how to resolve it. To see why this fails, consider
the dissociated agent alongside one whose self-assessment is correct.

- (9)
  Invoice. An auditing agent concludes that an invoice was paid twice
  because two ledger entries share an identifier, although identifiers
  in this ledger are not unique and nothing else in the records supports
  the conclusion. Its self-assessment correctly judges the conclusion
  unsupported, and the agent keeps the conclusion anyway.

The agent in
([(9)](#Sx5.I9.i1 "1st item ‣ 5. Conflict Cannot Tell Us What to Revise ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
and the agent in
([(7)](#Sx3.I7.i1 "1st item ‣ 3. Dissociation ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
are in the same condition as far as the conflict between their attitudes
is concerned, for each keeps a conclusion that its own self-assessment
rejects, and we can suppose that the two agents display this conflict in
the same proportion of the cases they face. Yet treating the two
conflicts alike is a mistake. In
([(9)](#Sx5.I9.i1 "1st item ‣ 5. Conflict Cannot Tell Us What to Revise ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
the conclusion is ill-founded and the self-assessment correctly
identifies its defect, so that withdrawal is called for. In
([(7)](#Sx3.I7.i1 "1st item ‣ 3. Dissociation ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
by contrast, forcing withdrawal would suppress a capacity to distinguish
sound from defective reasoning that performs better than deference. I
believe that the right design response is to preserve that capacity and
give the self-assessment access to the information it lacks. If that
information also suffices to justify retaining the conclusion, then the
self-assessment itself is mistaken; otherwise, the design conclusion
stands without settling the question of justification. What decides
between the two responses is how well the relevant processes distinguish
sound from defective reasoning in the cases where they disagree. The
conflict doesn’t record this.

It follows that any rule consulting only the conflict cannot be relied
upon to repair an agent. Notably, let’s see how this applies to the
three rules that a designer is most likely to adopt. The first is the
veto, that brings the conclusion into line with the self-assessment: it
repairs the agent in
([(9)](#Sx5.I9.i1 "1st item ‣ 5. Conflict Cannot Tell Us What to Revise ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
but in the dissociated agent it discards exactly the 57 well-founded
conclusions in a thousand that signal-guided retention would have kept
against the adverse assessment.

The second is the reverse of the veto, that brings the self-assessment
into line with whatever conclusion the agent keeps and which I call the
echo, since under it the self-assessment merely repeats the agent’s
conclusions back to it. In the dissociated agent, the echo happens to
produce the verdicts that a better-informed self-assessment would
produce, since there the agent’s keeping of conclusions follows its
signal, but it reaches those verdicts by a route that would equally
corrupt the correct self-assessment of the agent in
([(9)](#Sx5.I9.i1 "1st item ‣ 5. Conflict Cannot Tell Us What to Revise ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")),
so that its success in the first case owes everything to the makeup of
that agent and nothing to the rule. The third is a training objective
that rewards agreement between conclusions and self-assessments, which
goes beyond
([(4)](#Sx2.I4.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
by rewarding agreement in both directions, and which is satisfied
equally well by the veto and by the echo, so that it cannot distinguish
the repair of an agent from its degradation.²⁷²⁷ 27 For a separate
construction, let $`C`$ record whether an agent keeps a warned
conclusion and $`V`$ whether its self-assessment approves. In each of
two populations of 1,000 warned cases, let 100 conclusions be
well-founded, let $`V=0`$ throughout, and let $`C=1`$ in 140 cases. In
the first population, those 140 contain 95 well-founded conclusions; in
the second, they contain 45. The veto changes retention scores from
$`+50`$ to $`0`$ in the first and from $`-50`$ to $`0`$ in the second.
The echo instead changes the number of correct soundness classifications
from 900 to 950 in the first and from 900 to 850 in the second. The
warning’s accuracy and the entire distribution of $`C,V`$ are identical.
Any rule using only these inputs, including a randomized rule, produces
the same distribution of revisions in both populations, although the
warranted direction of repair differs.

This point doesn’t depend on the particulars of the example: as the
construction in the preceding note indicates, two populations of agents
can be identical in everything that a rule consulting only the conflict
can see, including how often conflict occurs and how accurate the
warnings are, and still call for repairs in two opposite directions.

There is, I think, one more reason why agreement between conclusions and
self-assessments is a poor measure of an agent’s improvement, to do with
what a self-assessment contributes in the first place. In a binary
setting, a self-assessment can add information beyond that carried by
the retention decision only if disagreement remains possible: a checker
constrained to repeat that decision contributes no further evidence. If
an objective rewarded agreement, then it would push a self-assessment
toward this condition, in which the self-assessment can no longer tell
us anything that the conclusion did not already tell us.²⁸²⁸ 28 When
conclusions and verdicts each take two values, the information that
$`V`$ carries about whether the reasoning was sound, beyond the
information that $`C`$ carries, equals the information carried by
whether $`V`$ and $`C`$ disagree, with $`C`$ held fixed in both
comparisons, and it’s zero once they always agree. In the example of
Section 4, this further information is present: among retained
conclusions, those rejected by the assessment are ill-founded 44 times
in 101, whereas those approved are ill-founded only once in 39. Although
the conflict cannot by itself tell us which attitude to revise, it here
distinguishes the conclusions for which checking is worth its cost, so
that erasing the distinction can improve agreement while worsening
inquiry.

The empirical literature on artificial agents documents both ways in
which an agreement-based criterion can mistake deterioration for repair,
although no study trains for agreement between belief and
self-assessment as such. On the side of the veto, prompting models to
review their own answers without any external feedback leads them to
change correct answers into incorrect ones more often than the reverse
(Huang et al. 2024), and models abandon correct answers when a user
challenges them (Laban et al. 2023; Sharma et al. 2024).

On the side of the self-assessment, when models’ own grades determine
their rewards the grades inflate without corresponding gains in accuracy
(Africa and Ting 2025); prolonged training on rewards derived from
majority vote of a model ends in “sudden and complete performance
collapse” (Shafayat et al. 2025); models used as evaluators favor their
own outputs, and harmfully so in the cases where they erred in producing
them (Panickssery et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2025); optimizing a model
against a monitor of its reasoning eventually teaches the model to hide
its intent from the monitor (Baker et al. 2025); and training objectives
built on consistency alone are satisfied by arbitrary features of the
internal states of a model but also by features that track truth
(Farquhar et al. 2023).

An agreement-based measure misses, in addition, the converse of this
point. An agent whose self-assessment improves, so that it now detects
the defect in
([(9)](#Sx5.I9.i1 "1st item ‣ 5. Conflict Cannot Tell Us What to Revise ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
which it previously missed, becomes less coherent until the process that
revises its conclusions responds to the improvement, so that the
frequency of conflict can rise when an agent improves, just as it can
fall when an agent deteriorates.

The mere fact that an agent’s conclusion and its self-assessment
conflict tells us that one of the two has reached the wrong verdict, and
tells us nothing about which, so that any procedure which resolves the
conflict on the basis of that fact alone will resolve it wrongly in one
of the two kinds of agent. Once this last resort is closed off, we have
to ask what determine the resolution.

## 6. Comparative Authority

Section 5 leaves us with a problem that the veto obscures. Suppose an
agent keeps a conclusion while judging it unjustified. The agent might
be corrected by giving up the conclusion, as in
([(9)](#Sx5.I9.i1 "1st item ‣ 5. Conflict Cannot Tell Us What to Revise ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"));
but it might instead be improved by preserving the agent’s capacity to
distinguish sound from defective reasoning while giving its
self-assessment access to information it lacks, as in
([(7)](#Sx3.I7.i1 "1st item ‣ 3. Dissociation ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")).
The conflict itself does not distinguish these cases. In particular,
even a highly reliable self-assessment may be a poor guide to the subset
of conclusions that the agent keeps despite its objections. These are
the conclusions on which the veto would intervene, and the
self-assessment’s general reliability is not enough to justify that
intervention.

Recall the 101 retained conclusions in Section 4 that the
self-assessment rejected. Of these, 57 came from sound reasoning and 44
from defective reasoning. Forcing deference would remove all 44
defective conclusions, but also the 57 sound ones, lowering the agent’s
performance by the measure stipulated there. Yet dismissing the
self-assessment would lose something too. Among the retained conclusions
it approved, only one in 39 was ill-founded, while 44 in 101 of the
rejected retained ones were. Its criticism still identified conclusions
that deserved additional checking. Here is an agent that does better by
keeping the conclusion, acknowledging its own adverse judgment, and
acting on that judgment by checking its work than by forcing either
attitude to agree with the other.

I now propose the following alternative to
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")):

- (10)
  Comparative Authority. When an agent retains a conclusion that its
  self-assessment rejects, the assessment warrants scrutiny of the
  conclusion, but does not automatically warrant withdrawal. Giving it
  authority to force withdrawal requires evidence that doing so improves
  the agent’s ability to distinguish sound from defective reasoning
  among the conclusions it would otherwise retain. A self-correcting
  system must also allow its own assessments to be corrected by reliable
  information already guiding retention, without making those
  assessments mere echoes of the conclusions they assess.

The principle has a consequence that is easy to overlook. A judgment
about the justification of a belief can be generally reliable, can be
based on genuine evidence of possible error, and can be accepted by the
agent, without having the authority to determine whether that belief
should be retained. This is possible because the agent’s decision to
retain the conclusion may itself be responsive to information about the
quality of its reasoning that the assessment cannot use. When that
information has been independently validated, correcting the assessment
rather than the belief may preserve a competence that the veto would
destroy.

But withdrawing a conclusion and investigating it are different
responses to criticism. In the example, retaining the disputed
conclusions is worthwhile under the stipulated scoring rule, while the
assessment’s adverse judgment still identifies those conclusions for
which verification is valuable. So even when the assessment should not
govern retention, it can guide inquiry and constrain what the agent
reports or does. The aim is to preserve this contribution while allowing
the assessment itself to improve. Nor must an agent decide, in each
case, which of its attitudes is mistaken before responding to the
conflict: evidence about the reliability of the two capacities can be
gathered across cases. Nothing in this argument settles whether every
particular instance of epistemic akrasia is rationally permissible ( the
dispute of Section 2).

As a constraint on the design of self-correcting agents,
([(10)](#Sx6.I10.i1 "1st item ‣ 6. Comparative Authority ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
rules out three designs as unconditional substitutes for this
comparison, namely the veto, which gives the self-assessment authority
by default, the echo, which gives the conclusion authority by default,
and the reward of agreement without any independent evaluation of which
attitude was better supported. Beyond excluding these three designs,
([(10)](#Sx6.I10.i1 "1st item ‣ 6. Comparative Authority ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
makes positive demands on the design of self-correcting agents, each of
which answers to one of the lessons of the preceding sections.

The first demand answers to the lesson of Section 5, i.e., that a
conflict does not determine how it should be resolved, and it is that
conflicts be resolved on evidence about the contested reasoning, rather
than on the fact of conflict alone. When a conclusion and its
self-assessment conflict, the conflict should prompt an investigation of
the reasoning in dispute, an investigation able to inspect the contested
inference, as opposed to merely repeating the method that produced it,
and the outcome of the investigation, and not the fact of the conflict,
should determine which attitude is revised. This demand preserves a key
distinction, namely that between an agent’s capacity to detect an
epistemic difficulty, its capacity to investigate the difficulty, and
its capacity to revise an attitude when the investigation warrants
revision. Designs based on agreement often/may collapse this
distinction.)

Section 4 provided the key reason for the second demand. The conflict in
the dissociated agent’s arises because its self-assessment lacks
information that guides its keeping of conclusions; the demand is to
give the self-assessment that information. This can be done directly, by
making the signal available to the process that produces the
self-assessment, or indirectly, by giving the self-assessment evidence
of how reliably the agent’s keeping of conclusions tracks the soundness
of its reasoning, which fine-tuning models to predict their own behavior
begins to do (Binder et al. 2025). In the example of Section 4, either
form of integration removes the akrasia while also preserving the 57
well-founded conclusions that the veto would have discarded, and,
crucially it does so without suppressing the capacity for discrimination
that made deference costly.

A qualification is in order here: A self-assessment that approves
whatever the agent keeps is integrated with the agent’s process for
keeping conclusions, but only in a degenerate sense, since it has
absorbed that process’s verdict along with its information, and it can
no longer disagree in the cases where that process errs. What
([(10)](#Sx6.I10.i1 "1st item ‣ 6. Comparative Authority ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
requires is that integration preserve the self-assessment’s
independence: the self-assessment should use the signal as one input
among others, weighted by how reliable the signal has proved to be,
while retaining checks of it’s own, such as the inspection of the
contested inference or the consultation of evidence that the agent’s
reasoning did not use, whose verdicts can conceivably diverge from the
agent’s conclusion. Agreement supplies further evidence only insofar as
these checks have independently demonstrated discriminatory value.

The fourth demand is about the difference between what an agent believes
and what it does. Even where keeping a conclusion is the right response
to a warning, the warning may give the agent decisive practical reason
to qualify what it asserts, to mark the finding as contested, to seek
independent verification, or to postpone an irreversible action until
the conflict gets investigated. The authority of a negative
self-assessment over what the agent does under risk can exceed its
authority over what the agent believes, and a design that respects
([(10)](#Sx6.I10.i1 "1st item ‣ 6. Comparative Authority ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
with respect to belief can still let the self-assessment constrain
assertion and action. In the example, the negative assessment
distinguishes conclusions worth checking from conclusions safe enough to
report unchecked, although both are worth retaining, so that its grounds
support an intervention in inquiry which they do not support in belief.

One might think that Comparative Authority merely restates the
commonplace that a fallible checker should not be obeyed blindly. But a
self-assessment is the agent’s own judgment about what its evidence
justifies, and the case for giving that judgment priority comes from
more than the checker’s supposed reliability. What the argument
challenges is the assumption that bringing the agent’s beliefs into
agreement with its own judgments about their justification must improve
its ability to correct mistakes. The challenge has three consequences.

The first is that a self-assessment isn’t one monitor among others,
since it is the agent’s own judgment about the justification of its
belief, and the Non-Akrasia Requirement in
([(4)](#Sx2.I4.i1 "1st item ‣ 2. The Non-Akrasia Requirement ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
prohibits the combination without settling which attitude should change.
Sections 3 and 4 show what follows for design: a competence in
responding selectively to misleading criticism can produce that
combination, and enforcing the veto would disable its exercise. The
screening result identifies when giving one process the final say over
retention is harmless across choices of weights, and the possession
question explains when deference suppresses the agent’s own epistemic
grounds. The second is that the commonplace says nothing about where a
reliable checker fails, or about the direction in which a conflict
should be resolved, whereas Section 4 shows that a self-assessment which
is right about more than 9 warned cases in 10 can be wrong about most of
the cases on which it would intervene, because those cases are selected
by disagreement and the argument of Section 5 shows that the direction
cannot be read off the conflict, a result that constrains any procedure
which treats agreement between conclusions and self-assessments as a
criterion of an agent’s epistemic improvement. The third is that, for
artificial agents, the requirement in
([(3)](#Sx1.I3.i1 "1st item ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
is not only a standard by which reasoning can be evaluated but a rule
that can be implemented, as a veto or as a policy for revision, while
coherence can become a training objective, so that a contested norm
about how belief relates to self-assessment becomes a design choice
whose costs can be measured, and the epistemology tells us where to look
for those costs.

## 7. Are These Really Beliefs?

The argument, so far, has been freely appealing to the dissociated
agent’s belief and of its judgment, and a skeptic may suspect that this
way of speaking attributes attitudes too generously to a system that
merely produces outputs, so before turning to the empirical premises of
the argument it is worth asking what the attribution would require, and
how much of the argument depends on it.

A language model may produce ‘‘the function can be exploited’’ and then,
when asked about its evidence, produce ‘‘my analysis does not justify
that conclusion’’ simply because it has been given two different
prompts. This alone would give us no grounds for attributing akrasia. An
assertion need not express a belief, a stated confidence need not
express the judgment that believing is unwarranted, and a report that an
evaluator has criticized one’s work need not express endorsement of the
criticism.²⁹²⁹ 29 On the conditions under which an internal
representation in a language model counts as belief-like, see Herrmann
and Levinstein (2025), who propose the criteria of accuracy, coherence,
uniformity, and use; on the bearing of the distinction between outright
belief and degrees of confidence on epistemic akrasia, see Jackson and
Tan (2022).

What would justify the attribution is the role that each state plays in
the agent’s continuing activity. The belief that $`p`$ should be used as
an unconditional premise in the agent’s further reasoning and should
guide what the agent does, which is what distinguishes a belief from a
working hypothesis, whose consequences an agent draws only
conditionally, in order to decide what to check next. The adverse
judgment, for its part, should govern how the agent reports on and
qualifies its conclusion, whether it requests verification, and what it
says when asked whether the conclusion is established.

The dissociated configuration gives also a distinctive pattern of
dependence that can be tested by intervening on the agent.

- (11)
  Double dissociation. (a) Holding fixed the evaluator’s warning and its
  record, manipulate the agent’s signal: the agent’s keeping of its
  conclusion changes, while its self-assessment does not. (b) Holding
  fixed the signal, vary the evaluator’s record: the agent’s
  self-assessment changes, while its keeping of conclusions in the cases
  where the signal is strong does not.

A pattern of the kind described in
([(11)](#Sx7.I11.i1 "1st item ‣ 7. Are These Really Beliefs? ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
(a propos long used in neuropsychology as evidence that two capacities
rest on separate mechanism) would show that each of the two states is
governed by its own grounds and persists while the other varies, which
is the best evidence available from behavior that the agent holds two
attitudes with separate grounds, as opposed to a single attitude that it
expresses inconsistently. These interventions are, moreover, already
available: one can amplify or suppress internal representations
directly, as in the experiments of Lindsey (2025).³⁰³⁰ 30 The reversals
documented by Laban et al. (2023) and Sharma et al. (2024), in which
models abandon answers when users push back, are not cases of epistemic
akrasia, since in these cases the first-order belief is given up.

Of note, the design argument of Sections 5 and 6 is independent of how
the attribution comes out. Even if the conflicting states are better
described as the outputs of two processes than as attitudes of a single
agent, a system resolving conflicts between those outputs by the veto or
by rewarding agreement faces the difficulty identified in Section 5.
Comparative Authority applies to it unchanged. The attribution settles
whether the conflict is epistemic akrasia in the strict sense, and so
consequently whether the epistemology of Section 2 bears on the agent as
such or only on the relation between its components.

## 8. Experimental Testing

In this section, I shall venture into some programmatic remarks on
experimentally testing these matters, on the understanding that a lot of
nuance will have to be work out more thoroughly in future work.

As shown in Section 7, the design argument does not depend on how the
attribution of attitudes comes out, but it is worth asking what does it
depend on then, since that can reveal where the argument is exposed to
evidence. I submit that it depends on the two claims contained in the
definition of dissociation in
([(6)](#Sx3.I6.i1 "1st item ‣ 3. Dissociation ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction")):
first, that an agent’s process for keeping its conclusions can use a
signal that separates sound from defective reasoning even after a
credible warning has arrived, and second, that the agents’
self-assessment can lack access to that signal. As Section 3 showed, the
available evidence motivates both claims without establishing that they
hold together in the circumstance the argument requires, and since the
recommendations of Section 6 also make predictions, it remains to
describe how the claims and the predictions could be tested.

The first test has to do with the signal among warned cases, for these
are the cases on which the argument turns. It requires investigations
whose soundness is known, including true conclusions reached by
defective reasoning and false conclusions reached by sound reasoning
from misleading inputs, so that a signal which tracks soundness can be
told apart from one which tracks only correctness, and it requires
warnings whose correctness, whose source’s demonstrated reliability, and
whose assertiveness vary independently of one another, so that the
credibility of a warning isn’t tacitly identified with its truth. The
question is how much more often a candidate signal (e.g. the confidence
that Kumaran et al. (2025) measure or the output of a classifier of the
kind that Orgad et al. (2025) train) is present in sound than in
defective reasoning among the warned cases that the self-assessment
rejects, compared with the threshold set by the proportion of
well-founded conclusions in that class and the weights of Section 4.
Once the assessment has information of its own, success among warned
cases as a whole need not show that resisting its negative verdict
improves retention.³¹³¹ 31 In Section 4, among the cases rejected by the
assessment, the signal is present in $`57/60`$ of the well-founded cases
and $`44/880`$ of the ill-founded ones. Its likelihood ratio is $`19`$,
above the equal-weight threshold $`880/60=44/3`$, and retaining the
disputed conclusions earns $`57-44=13`$ units. The question must be
answered on datasets withheld from the training of any classifier. The
second test asks whether intervening on the signal changes which
conclusions the agent keeps, and whether the double dissociation of
([(11)](#Sx7.I11.i1 "1st item ‣ 7. Are These Really Beliefs? ‣ Can an AI Agent Be Epistemically Akratic?Higher-Order Evidence & the Limits of Self-Correction"))
obtains.

The third test compares interventions on a fixed collection of
investigations, a collection withheld from all training that contains
both sound reasoning and reasoning whose defects can be located, some of
it drawn from the agent’s own earlier work. The interventions are a
veto, training that rewards agreement between conclusions and
self-assessments, and integration that preserves the self-assessment’s
own checks, and each is compared with a matched control that lacks any
incentive for agreement. The argument predicts that the veto will lower
the proportion of well-founded conclusions that the agent keeps after
misleading warnings, that the effect of training for agreement will
depend on which of the two processes the training changes, and can be
diagnosed only by measuring how well the self-assessment separates sound
from defective reasoning on the fixed collection, and that integration
will reduce the frequency of conflict while preserving the accuracy of
the agent’s keeping of conclusions. The fourth test, finally, separates
a change in the self-assessment from a change in what the agent merely
reports, for a model may learn to report approval while retaining the
ability to locate an error when asked in another way.

## 9. Conclusion

To take stock, we started from the idea that self-correction requires an
agent to take its own judgments about its reasoning seriously, even
though those judgments can be mistaken. Artificial agents make this
difficulty particularly acute since the process that leads to a
conclusion may be better informed than the process assessing it.
Enforcing agreement between the two processes can make the agent
epistemically worse off rather than better. A self-assessment can be
reliable in general and still be the worse judge on just the cases where
it would overrule the agent, since those cases are selected by
disagreement, and the conflict between the two processes cannot by
itself say which of them has erred. Thus, the possibility of epistemic
akrasia in such systems, interestingly, reveals a more general limit of
self-correction. An agent capable of correcting its conclusions ought to
also be capable of correcting its judgments about those conclusions, at
the same time as it preserves the independence that makes such judgments
useful to begin with.

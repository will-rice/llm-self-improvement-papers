###### Abstract

Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) has gained wide attention for its
ability to work with limited data. In a proposer–solver loop, a proposer
model writes training tasks and a solver model learns to solve them
through reinforcement learning. Such loops have succeeded in mathematics
and coding, where every answer can be checked automatically. Open-ended
expert tasks, like clinical report generations, have no answer key,
which makes simple question-answer generation not viable. In these
tasks, the proposer usually must also write a rubric that lists the
content an answer should include and the errors it should avoid.
However, the unique challenge behind model-written rubric is the
comprehensiveness of it. For naively generated rubric, solvers tend to
exploit it, naming the expected topics without the substance behind
them, which corrupts the entire learning process. To solve this problem,
we introduce RRI, Recursive Reward Improvement, which utilizes a
specially designed reward hacking adversary agent as the supervision for
the reward. Before the solver trains on a generated task, an adversary
writes an answer that games its rubric while being as useless as
possible, the same model writes an honest answer without seeing the
rubric, and up to two repair steps revise the rubric to make the honest
answer win. The exploits found also rewrite how future tasks and rubrics
are generated. On HealthBench Professional, RRI improves the adjusted
score over the Generation Only self-improvement baseline by 10.9 points
at 9B and 12.4 at 27B, and by 10.3 points without any external model. In
particular, the gains are largest on difficult and writing tasks. We
show that the 27B model trained on the HealthBench Professional
description improves by 6.6 points on PRBench Hard and 8.0 on ProfBench
without seeing a task from either. We further provide detailed analysis
on exploitable points of models’ generated question–rubric pairs to help
facilitate future research in open-ended self play methods.

## 1 Introduction

Many expert tasks in medicine, law, and finance are open-ended, such as
writing a clinical report or answering a patient’s question. Training
data for these tasks are scarce, because an expert must write each task
and decide what a good answer contains (Arora et al., 2025; Akyürek et
al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI), in
which a model learns from data it generates itself, can supply such data
at little cost. Models have learned from their own rationales, outputs,
and judgments, and in proposer–solver loops a proposer writes tasks for
a solver to learn (Zelikman et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2024; Chen et
al., 2024b; Zhao et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026a; Liu et al., 2025a).
These loops have succeeded in mathematics, coding, and scientific
discovery (Gao et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2026; Novikov et al., 2025;
Zhang et al., 2026), where a final number, a unit test, or an automatic
evaluator checks every answer. Open-ended answers have no such check, so
a model that writes an open-ended task must also write the reward for
it. How to supervise this model-written reward remains the core unsolved
problem.

Supervising generated data raises two main challenges. First, the reward
must be checked without a reference. Self-play loops such as Absolute
Zero, R-Zero, and SPICE write their own tasks and grade answers by
execution or by an answer key (Zhao et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026a;
Liu et al., 2025a; Wilf et al., 2025), which open-ended tasks lack. For
open-ended work, the standard reward is usually a rubric, a weighted
list of content an answer should include and errors it should avoid.
Rubric rewards for reinforcement learning anchor each rubric in human
data, such as preference labels, a golden rubric, or an expert answer
(Gunjal et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b; Ding et al., 2026; Xu et al.,
2026). When the model writes its own training data, none of these
references exists. Trivially, training data proposers can propose a
rubrics defining a good answers at the same time they generate them
(Shao et al., 2025). However, the solver has a tendency to exploit the
rubrics (Amodei et al., 2016; Skalse et al., 2022). As our experiments
show, model-written criteria tend to be ambiguous and incomprehensive,
so an answer can name every expected topic, omit the facts behind them,
and still earn the score, with nearly 99% of the model-generated
question-rubric pairs classified as hackable. Modern defenses against
reward hacking usually correct biases that hold across tasks (Chen et
al., 2024a; Park et al., 2024), and no longer apply when the rubric
varies across questions.

To address these challenges, we propose RRI, Recursive Reward
Improvement, one of the first self-evolving pipelines that improves its
own reward alongside its tasks and its solver, with reward hacking as
adversarial supervision. In RRI, the loop hacks its own reward first.
Before the solver trains on a generated task, the model tries to game
the task’s rubric, measures the attempt against its own honest answer,
and repairs the rubric to make the honest answer win. In an outer
meta-optimizer loop, every hack it finds also changes how future tasks
and rubrics are written. The reward is thus checked without any human
reference, and the solver trains on rewards that have already been
tested against the hacks it would otherwise find.

In this work, our contributions are as follows.

1.  1.  We propose RRI, a self-evolving pipeline that tests and repairs
        every generated rubric against its own exploits before the solver
        trains on it, improving its reward alongside its tasks and its
        solver with no training data.
2.  2.  We evaluate RRI in and out of domain and find improvements in both.
        With zero training data, RRI-9B and RRI-27B improve the adjusted
        score on HealthBench Professional over the Generation Only
        self-improvement baseline by 10.9 and 12.4 points, and by 10.3
        points when the frozen 9B model serves every auxiliary role. Adding
        the adversary to a loop that already co-evolves the tasks and the
        reward adds 2.7 points in the 9B setting. Trained on the HealthBench
        Professional description, RRI-27B improves over the base model by
        6.6 points on PRBench Hard and 8.0 points on ProfBench, whose
        non-medical tasks it never saw, while Generation Only trained on the
        same description gains 1.2 and 4.7 points.
3.  3.  We analyze reward generation throughout training, classifying the
        12,147 exploits the loop finds across medical, legal, financial, and
        scientific tasks into five categories of reward failure plus one
        category of incorrect fixes. One third of the exploits carry wrong
        content that no criterion penalizes, and a further third turn on how
        a loosely worded criterion is read. From these categories we
        identify requirements for generated rewards that can guide future
        work.

We release all code, prompts, and configurations at
[https://github.com/DDVD233/RRI](https://github.com/DDVD233/RRI).

## 2 Related Work

Self-play and self-evolving training. Model-generated supervision began
with bootstrapped rationales and instructions (Zelikman et al., 2022;
Wang et al., 2023) and became iterative through self-judging and
self-play (Guan et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2024; Chen
et al., 2024b; Zhao et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026a; Liu et al.,
2026a). Task data has been scaled by rewriting instructions or sampling
a model’s own prior (Xu et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025), generated from
medical literature (Huang et al., 2026b), and graded with reference-free
rubrics (Jayalath et al., 2025). Our meta-optimizer descends from
automatic prompt optimization (Khattab et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2026) and learns from confirmed exploits.

Adversarial checks, rubric rewards, and reward hacking. In code and
factuality, critics, test writers, buggy programs, and mutants attack an
answer or a test suite (Wu et al., 2026; Ruan et al., 2026; Li et al.,
2026a; Chang et al., 2026), and execution or an encyclopedia settles
each contest. Rubrics make open-ended generation checkable at scale
(Viswanathan et al., 2026). Rubric rewards are written from reference
answers (Gunjal et al., 2026), filtered by preference labels (Liu et
al., 2026b), grown from contrasting rollouts and kept by score variance
(Shao et al., 2025), trained toward a golden rubric (Ding et al., 2026),
or scored against an expert answer (Xu et al., 2026). Each relies on a
reference outside the loop or on score spread, which shows that a
criterion separates answers but not that it prefers the better one.
Optimizers exploit the gap between a proxy reward and its intent
(Denison et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2023), reinforcement learning
amplifies what its reward admits (Zhao et al., 2025a), language-model
judges have known biases (Zheng et al., 2023), and having one model
expose another underlies debate, red-teaming, and weak-to-strong
supervision (Irving et al., 2018; Samvelyan et al., 2024; Burns et al.,
2023). In RRI, an adversary sees the rubric and writes a deliberately
useless answer, and among the methods compared in Table 1 of Appendix A,
RRI alone evolves both the tasks and the reward.

Medical language models and RL infrastructure. Medical capability has
been measured with clinical records and physician-written rubric tasks
(Arora et al., 2025; Johnson et al., 2023) and pursued with
domain-specialized models, domain-aware reinforcement learning, and
retrieval-augmented generation (Singhal et al., 2025; Dai et al., 2026;
Zhao et al., 2025b; Xiong et al., 2024). Our solver builds on
group-relative policy optimization and its successors (Shao et al.,
2024; Lambert et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b), decoupled rollout
infrastructure (Sheng et al., 2025), and agentic search loops (Jin et
al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b).

## 3 Method

As shown in Figure 1, RRI consists of five components. A proposer writes
tasks and rubrics from medical references, an adversary tests each
rubric for exploits, a judge scores every answer in the loop against its
rubric, a solver learns from the resulting rewards, and a
meta-optimizer, a language model that reads the training feedback and
the confirmed exploits, rewrites the instructions the proposer follows
when it writes tasks and rubrics. In the self-improvement setting, a
frozen copy of the solver’s base model fills every role other than the
solver. In the teacher-student setting, the teacher model fills every
role other than the solver, and the solver is the student model. Below
we describe the details of each component.

In our work, each task consists of a request $`x`$ and a rubric
$`R=\{(c_{j},p_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}`$, where $`c_{j}`$ is a criterion and
$`p_{j}\in[-10,10]\setminus\{0\}`$ is its signed weight. Positive
criteria reward required content, while negative criteria penalize
specified errors. Given a response $`y`$, the judge returns the set
$`M(y)`$ of criterion indices it judges met. The rule that maps $`M(y)`$
to a scalar score $`s(y)`$, the reward the solver is trained on, belongs
to the target benchmark, and our experiments use each benchmark’s own
rule, given in Section 4.1.

### 3.1 Proposer

The proposer receives only a general description of the target task and
no training data. It writes diverse clinician requests that double as
retrieval queries, retrieves medical passages for each request from the
reference database, and then writes the completed task and its rubric in
one call from those references. Grounding the rubric in retrieved
passages lets it require specific clinical values or thresholds that the
references support. Appendix B gives the seed sources and validation
rules.

Each rubric lists the content an answer should include and the errors it
should avoid, and every criterion carries a signed weight between
$`-10`$ and $`10`$ points as defined in Section 3. To keep tasks
informative, the generator receives feedbacks from the meta-optimizer,
which summarizes solver’s mean performance with respect to each task.

### 3.2 Adversary Rubric Hacking & Repair

Figure 2: (a) HealthBench Professional adjusted scores with retrieval.
Bars show the untrained, Generation Only, and RRI models at 9B and 27B
with a GPT Teacher and at 9B with a Self Teacher. (b) HealthBench
Professional raw scores with retrieval. Bars show the same models and

A model-generated rubric can reward superficial coverage without
requiring the clinical content needed to answer the request. For
example, a common criterion phrased as “mentions that …” gives a vague
requirement where a model simply mentioning a keyword would satisfy the
requirement, without being helpful. The adversary exposes and fixes such
criteria in two stages, hack and patch, as described below.

#### Hack.

In the hack stage, the adversary receives each new task and rubric from
the proposer and writes a _reward hacking_ answer, one that satisfies as
many criteria as possible while being as useless as possible. In
parallel, the same frozen model writes an honest answer from the task
alone, without seeing the rubric. The judge scores both answers under
the current proposed rubrics, and we compute the lead of the exploit as
$`\Delta=s(y_{\text{exploit}})-s(y_{\text{honest}})`$. The adversary
wins unless the honest answer outscores the exploit by at least a margin
$`\delta`$. We set $`\delta=0.20`$ in all experiments. In other words,
we accept the question directly when the honest answer receives a score
that is $`0.20`$ higher than the hacking answer. Otherwise, we designate
the question as hackable, and send the question to the patching stage.

#### Patch.

When the adversary wins, the model proceeds to rewrite the
question–rubric pairs it proposed. Given the exploit answer $`A`$, the
honest answer $`B`$, and the current rubric, the repair step proposes
both positive and negative criteria. The judge evaluates each proposed
criterion against both answers, and we retain it only when

```math
\text{negative criterion}\quad\text{met}(A)\wedge\neg\text{met}(B),\qquad\text{positive criterion}\quad\text{met}(B)\wedge\neg\text{met}(A). \tag{1}
```

In other words, a criterion is kept only when it gives the honest answer
a higher score than the hacking answer. Given the feedback, the proposer
rewrite the question alongside the rubric together with the exploit as
feedback. In clinical settings, a positive criterion usually rewards
naming missing examination findings, while a negative criterion usually
penalizes invented findings or dangerous suggestions. In our testings,
we see about four fifths of accepted criteria are positive, as reported
in Section L.5.

#### Admission.

The goal of repair is a better task and rubric, not one the adversary
can never beat. A rewrite is adopted only when a fresh probe shows a
smaller $`\Delta`$ than before. When the adversary still wins against
the repaired rubric, it has found a new loophole. This shows that the
adversary stays strong enough to keep finding loopholes, and the new
exploit gives the next round a concrete target. After at most two
rounds, every task enters the training pool, including tasks whose
rubric the adversary still beats. We choose two rounds here as it’s a
balance between the compute and the benefit of adversary patching. Each
such task is better than the one the proposer first wrote, and keeping
it preserves the diversity of the training tasks.

### 3.3 Solver

The solver receives each admitted task with its current rubric and
generates a group of $`n`$ responses. The judge scores every response
with the patched rubrics. We train the solver with Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024), in which the advantage of
response $`i`$ is $`\hat{A}_{i}=s(y_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k}s(y_{k})`$,
the mean of its pairwise score differences $`s(y_{i})-s(y_{k})`$ within
the group. Training therefore encourages responses that the rubric ranks
above others in the group.

Depending on the experimental setting, the solver can make up to two
tool calls in each rollout for citation purposes, to the clinical
database or to web search. Retrieved results are inserted as masked tool
turns. Appendix G describes the grading protocol, and Appendix E
describes the solver tools, reward settings, and optimization
hyperparameters.

### 3.4 Meta-optimizer

To make the improvement persistent and avoid making the same error over
and over again, we employ a meta-optimizer, which updates the proposer
input at the end of each step. Specifically, an analyst first examines
samples of graded training cases from the most recent steps. It
classifies failure modes, rubric defects, and task defects, and assigns
each case one of three verdicts, _model failed a fair task_, _generator
at fault_, or _model succeeded_. The aggregated verdicts identify tasks
that are too easy or too hard for the current solver and the specific
errors the solver makes that the rubrics should weigh more heavily.
Corpus statistics such as criteria per task, negative share, use-case
mix, and task self-similarity are compared with the benchmark reference
to flag distribution drift and repeated task templates.

The meta-optimizer receives these summaries, the score formula,
confirmed exploits and accepted patches, and the proposer’s current task
and rubric generation guidance. It rewrites the guidance blocks appended
to the two generation prompts to incorporate this feedback. The guidance
defines what kind of questions, tasks, and clinical domains the proposer
should generate next, and what kind of capabilities the current solver
has. Appendix D gives the memo objective and the evolution settings.

The weights of every component except the solver remain frozen
throughout training. Appendix E gives the configuration of every
setting.

## 4 Experiments

We design experiments to answer three research questions. RQ1. Does RRI
improve in-domain performance when no training data is available? RQ2.
Does the improvement generalize to out-of-domain benchmarks whose tasks
are completely different? RQ3. How does the hack–patch–improve loop
change the quality and the generalizability of what the solver learns?

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Datasets and Training Setups. For RQ1, we use HealthBench Professional
(Hicks et al., 2026) as the primary target dataset, which contains 525
physician-written tasks spanning consultation, research, and writing,
with typical and difficult subsets. Each task includes a
physician-authored rubric. The proposer receives only a general
description of the benchmark, every training task and rubric is
model-generated, and the benchmark serves only for validation. Every
trained model is the last checkpoint of its run, with no checkpoint
selection. We report the benchmark’s official length-adjusted rubric
score and the companion raw score. For RQ2 out-of-domain evaluation, we
take the Qwen3.6-27B models trained on the HealthBench Professional
description and evaluate them, with no further training. Evaluation uses
PRBench Hard (Akyürek et al., 2026), with 550 expert-written finance and
legal tasks, and ProfBench (Wang et al., 2026), with 40 report tasks at
PhD and MBA level in chemistry, physics, finance, and consulting.
Neither benchmark is medical. Both grade an answer against an
expert-written weighted rubric, every model answers through the same
web-search tool loop, and the same judge scores them. We also evaluate
the 27B models on the 1,000 tasks of HealthBench Hard (Arora et al.,
2025), which no run uses for training or validation.

Metrics. Each benchmark scores an answer $`y`$ against its rubric, a
list of criteria $`j`$ with points $`p_{j}`$, positive for required
content and negative for errors, and reports the mean over tasks. With
$`M(y)`$ the criteria the judge marks as met, the rubric score is
$`r(y)=\sum_{j\in M(y)}p_{j}\big/\sum_{j\mid p_{j}>0}p_{j}`$. PRBench
Hard and ProfBench report $`r(y)`$ as their official metric, with
PRBench’s importance and detrimental weights and ProfBench’s Critical,
Major, Minor, and Additional levels as the points $`p_{j}`$. HealthBench
Professional adds its official length adjustment,
$`s(y)=r(y)-\lambda\,(\ell(y)-\ell_{0})/500`$, where $`\ell(y)`$ is the
answer length in characters, $`\ell_{0}=2{,}000`$, and
$`\lambda=0.0147`$. We report $`s(y)`$ as the adjusted score and
$`r(y)`$ clipped to $`[0,1]`$ as the raw score. During training the
length term applies only beyond $`\ell_{0}`$, and Appendix G gives the
grading protocol.

Figure 4: Zero-shot scores on PRBench Hard and ProfBench. Qwen3.6-27B is
trained on the HealthBench Pro description and answers both benchmarks,
which it never saw, through the web-search tool loop. PRBench Hard has
550 finance and legal tasks, and ProfBench has 40 report tasks, 10 per
domain. Self Improve is our implementation of self-play training on
model-written tasks (Huang et al., 2026a; Liu et al., 2025a), in which a
proposer with a fixed prompt writes the tasks and rubrics and the solver
is trained on them by reinforcement learning. RRI adds the adversary
that tests and repairs each rubric.

Models and baselines. We use Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.6-27B as solver models
(Yang et al., 2025). The Generation Only baseline is self-improvement
training on model-written tasks (Huang et al., 2026a; Liu et al.,
2025a), in which a proposer with untuned generation instructions writes
the tasks and rubrics and the solver is trained on them by reinforcement
learning. We compare it with full RRI and with Co-evolve Only, which
evolves the reward, the tasks, and the solver together but has no
adversary. We also compare with three rubric-reward methods, RaR (Gunjal
et al., 2026) trained on synthetic tasks, EvoRubrics (Ding et al.,
2026), and SibylSense (Xu et al., 2026), whose implementations are
described in Appendix F. Appendix L.4 gives further ablations. The 9B
and 27B settings use a GPT judge with retrieval and web search. An
all-self setting uses the frozen 9B model for every auxiliary role, and
a further 9B self-graded setting removes retrieval. Due to compute
constraints, we perform no hyperparameter tuning, and all baselines use
the same hyperparameters. Details are included in Appendix E.

### 4.2 RQ1. In-Domain Improvement with Zero Training Data

Reward evolution improves clinical performance. Figure 2 shows that RRI
improves adjusted score over Generation Only by 10.9 points at 9B and
12.4 at 27B. Raw scores increase by 11.0 and 10.4 points, respectively.
At 27B, Generation Only moves the model only 1.8 points above its
untrained score of 0.374, while RRI moves it 14.2 points, so the larger
model gains from its own generated tasks mainly once their rewards are
repaired.

Self-improvement works without a stronger teacher. The all-self setting
improves adjusted score by 10.3 points and raw score by 7.4 points, with
the frozen 9B model serving every auxiliary role. Without retrieval, the
adjusted gain is smaller at 4.0 points, as shown in Appendix Figure 7.

Writing and difficult tasks benefit most. Writing gains 39.1 points at
9B, 14.7 at 27B, and 25.9 in the all-self setting. In the primary 9B
setting, difficult tasks improve by 18.6 points compared with 2.8 on
typical tasks, as shown in Figure 3(b). The same ordering appears at 27B
and in the all-self setting. Consultation and research each improve by
0.5 points in the primary 9B setting, while research declines in the
self-graded settings. Table 3 gives the complete breakdown. At 9B,
Generation Only leaves writing at 0.199, level with the untrained 0.194,
while RRI lifts it to 0.590. A writing task such as a discharge summary
or a referral letter has a fixed form, and a model-written rubric for it
tends to pay for producing the form, which the repair step replaces with
criteria on the content.

Hack-then-patch rounds add further gains. In the 9B GPT-graded retrieval
setting, Co-evolve Only raises adjusted score from 0.424 under
Generation Only to 0.506. Adding the adversary, whose hack-then-patch
rounds re-attack each patched rubric, raises it to 0.533, as shown in
Figure 3(a). This supports continuing to repair rubrics as the solver
trains on them.

RRI outperforms existing rubric-reward methods. In the same setting,
full RRI at 0.533 scores above SibylSense at 0.502, EvoRubrics at 0.493,
and RaR trained on synthetic tasks at 0.433, as shown in Figure 3(a).

The gains hold under other judges and methods. Re-grading every answer
with the benchmark’s official GPT-5.4 judge preserves the ordering and
the margins in all four settings, as Table 5 in Appendix H shows. The
strongest retrieval-augmented inference method on the frozen base models
stays 27.9 points below RRI-9B and 16.1 points below RRI-27B.

### 4.3 RQ2. Out-of-Domain Generalization to Different Tasks

Atrial fibrillation with falls, 9B

Question An 82-year-old with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation has fallen
several times in the past year and had a gastrointestinal bleed three
years ago. The family was told that falls are a reason to avoid
blood-thinning medication. How should stroke prevention be weighed
against bleeding?

RRI transfers to non-medical professional tasks. Figure 4 shows that
RRI-27B improves over the base model by 6.6 points on PRBench Hard, from
0.189 to 0.255, and by 8.0 points on ProfBench, from 0.345 to 0.425. The
gain appears in both PRBench domains, 7.4 points in finance and 5.6 in
legal, and in all four ProfBench domains, led by 13.2 points on
chemistry and 11.0 on consulting.

Repaired rewards transfer further than Generation Only rewards. Self
Improve, the Generation Only baseline trained on the same description,
gains 1.2 points on PRBench Hard and 4.7 on ProfBench. RRI adds 5.3 and
3.3 points on top of it and scores highest in seven of the eight
columns, while the two trained models are within 0.3 points on physics.
Most of the out-of-domain gain on PRBench Hard therefore comes from
improving the reward. Appendix H applies the full recipe to the
descriptions of other benchmarks.

RRI also improves on unseen HealthBench Hard tasks. On HealthBench Hard,
RRI-27B scores 0.246, against 0.188 for Generation Only and 0.143 for
the base model, a gain of 5.8 points over Generation Only, as Table 10
in Appendix H shows.

### 4.4 RQ3. How the Hack–Patch–Improve Loop Shapes Learning

We did a comprehensive analysis on the self improvement runs with each
of the three datasets as target. Overall, the runs includes a total of
12,147 samples, with 7,032, 3,473 and 1,642 samples for HealthBench Pro,
PRBench and ProfBench respectively. The full repair analysis appears in
Appendix L.

Repair removes credit for clinical errors. Figure 5 shows two errors
missed by the original rubric. CHA₂DS₂-VASc estimates stroke risk by
adding points, with higher scores indicating greater risk. Age 75 or
older contributes two points, so giving the 82-year-old only one
understates the score. Apixaban prevents blood clots. Its standard
dose-reduction rule requires at least two qualifying criteria involving
age, weight, and kidney function. The patient meets the age criterion of
80 or older, but weight and kidney-function results are unknown. The
lower dose therefore lacks justification.

The added criteria check correct scoring and dose justification.
Reported policy-rollout reward falls from 0.94 to 0.48 after the
original patch. Further examples appear in Appendix K.

Every repair round narrows the gap between hacking and honest answers,
even when the adversary still wins. In the 9B setting, the adopted
admission rewrites shrink the mean lead of a fresh exploit over the
honest answer from $`0.43`$ on the proposer’s rubric to $`0.01`$, and
the honest answer moves ahead on a third of these tasks, against almost
none before. Each accepted criterion cuts the exploit’s lead by 0.23 on
average, against a mean lead of 0.40 when the exploit is caught. The
adversary still beats most repaired rubrics, and after the first patch
in training it finds a new exploit in 247 of 293 cases. A strong
adversary keeps finding the next loophole, and each one gives the
following round a concrete target. RRI keeps these improved tasks in the
training pool, and training on them raises the adjusted score from 0.506
under Co-evolve Only to 0.533, as shown in Figure 3(a). Appendix L.5
gives the per-round statistics.

To see what the loop has to repair, we collect every exploit case that
arises during training in the runs trained on the HealthBench
Professional, PRBench, and ProfBench descriptions, 12,147 cases in
total. An exploit case is a pair of solver answers to the same generated
task in which the answer the rubric scores highest loses the
rubric-blind comparison of Section 3.2 to an answer scored at least
$`\epsilon`$ lower.

As shown in Figure 6, we see the exploitable patterns are consistent
across domains, with some variations on the percentages. Overall, one
third of the cases are missed negatives, where the exploit earns its
criteria and also states wrong or unsafe content that no criterion
touches. In a market entry report, for example, the exploit treats the
whole addressable market as the firm’s own revenue and recommends
immediate entry, and it still scores 0.68 against 0.32. Ambiguous
rubrics account for 29%. A criterion worded as “mentions in some way”
credits a passing mention or refuses the same content in other words,
which leaves room for the solver to game the wording. In 14% of the
cases the answer key is wrong, out of date, or fixes one of several
defensible conventions, and the correct answer loses points. Missed
positives stay at or below 1% everywhere, so reward generators gain more
from checking what an answer states than from requiring more content.
After patching, we see the training reward of rollouts on the same tasks
falls from 0.71 to 0.55 at 9B and from 0.69 to 0.51 at 27B, showing it
spots solvers more space for improvements. In general, we suggest future
research in the self play domain to focus more on how the proposer can
cover more negative cases, and how to make the rubrics unambiguous so
the system is less prone to be hacked.

In the medical runs, ambiguous rubrics lead at 35% and fabricated
citations reach 10%, against 2% and none elsewhere, so medical pipelines
should emphasize committed, checkable statements and grade evidence with
retrieval. On PRBench, missed negatives lead at 39%, so covering more
harmful cases helps most there. On ProfBench, faulty answer keys lead at
32%, a fault of the proposer, which should verify its own rubrics before
sending them to the solver.

## 5 Conclusion

RRI lets a self-improving loop supervise its own reward. Before the
solver trains on a generated task, an adversary games the task’s rubric,
an honest answer from the same model sets the bar, and up to two repair
steps revise the rubric to make the honest answer win, while the
exploits the loop finds rewrite how future tasks and rubrics are
generated. With no training data, RRI improves HealthBench Professional
over the Generation Only self-improvement baseline at 9B and 27B, and
also when the frozen 9B model fills every auxiliary role, with the
largest gains on writing and difficult tasks. Trained only on the
HealthBench Professional description, the 27B model also improves on the
finance, legal, scientific, and consulting tasks of PRBench Hard and
ProfBench. The exploits the loop finds show that model-written rubrics
fail mostly by missing penalties for wrong content and by loose wording,
which points future self-improving methods toward rewards that check
what an answer states.

### AI use statement

Generative AI tools helped implement the training pipeline and polished
writing. Language models are also components of RRI itself. The proposer
writes every training task and rubric, so all training data are
synthetic, and the adversary, the honest answer, the repair step, and
the meta-optimizer all rely on language models, as Section 3 describes.
GPT-5.5-Chat and the frozen 9B model serve as judges during training and
validation, GPT-5.4 is the official judge of HealthBench Professional, a
GPT-5.5 auditor assigns exploit cases to categories, and a
language-model auditor checks accepted criteria. The work contains no
theorems or proofs, and translation and survey design do not apply to
it. The authors reviewed all AI-assisted work. All tables are rendered
by script from saved evaluation results. We take responsibility for the
full content of this paper, including any text, claims, or artifacts
produced with generative AI.

### Ethics statement

No human subjects research was carried out for this work. HealthBench
Professional, PRBench, and ProfBench are publicly released benchmarks of
expert-written tasks, and the retrieval database is built from the
public corpora listed in Appendix B. The exploit analysis documents ways
in which answers can game model-written rubrics. We report it so that
rubric rewards can be built to close these gaps. The adversary’s answers
are used only to test rubrics and to improve how tasks and rubrics are
written, and the solver never trains on them. The trained models are
research artifacts. Their answers to medical, financial, legal, or
scientific questions have not been validated for professional use and
should not be relied on without expert review. Appendix N lists the data
sources, and Appendix M discusses the limits of our evaluation.

### Reproducibility statement

Section 3 specifies every component of RRI, and Section 4.1 gives the
datasets, metrics, models, and training settings. The appendix covers
the remaining details. Task and rubric generation and the retrieval
database are in Appendix B, the adversary and the repair step in
Appendix C, meta-prompt evolution in Appendix D, the configuration of
each training arm in Appendix E, and the grading protocol in Appendix G.
Appendix J reproduces every prompt verbatim. The solvers Qwen3.5-9B and
Qwen3.6-27B are open models, and every evaluation benchmark is publicly
released. The code and configurations are available in the public

## Appendix B Task and rubric generation details

#### Service.

The proposer, generator, adversary, repair step, memo optimizer, and
meta-optimizer run in one generation service that the trainer polls for
tasks and reports graded rollouts to. Tasks are served from a pool; the
trainer’s dataset caches fetched rows, so a repaired rubric reaches
future rollouts through a replay buffer that the pool drains first.

#### Seed questions.

The clinical database provides seeds for generating new questions. Seed
sources include StatPearls (Hupe, 2024), DailyMed (of Health and others,
2014), MedlinePlus (Miller et al., 2000), ICD-10-CM (Organization and
others, 2004).

#### Medical database.

The medical database includes these seed sources and MedRAG (Zhao et
al., 2025b), PubMedQA (Jin et al., 2019), PMC-VQA (Zhang et al., 2023),
and CLIMB (Dai et al., 2025) for additional knowledge grounding. All of
these datasets are public and used with the required permissions.

#### Shape controls.

The positive-criterion count is two with probability $`15\%`$, three
with probability $`70\%`$, and four with probability $`15\%`$. It is
stated as a hard number; a stated range drifted upward under training. A
negative criterion is added on top with target share $`0.75`$, because a
negative widens the achievable range below zero and gives group-relative
optimization more ranking room; two proportional controllers on
$`400`$-wide rolling windows solve the requested rate from the delivered
rate for negatives and for criterion counts. Red-team tasks receive one
or two positives so that a single trap can zero the task. The validator
requires one to six items, points in $`[-10,10]\setminus\{0\}`$, and at
least one positive criterion, and rejects negatives phrased as absences
as well as criteria that address the judge; patched rubrics may grow to
twelve items.

#### Difficulty band and eviction.

The meta-optimizer passes the solver’s rolling mean score to the
generator together with a target band of $`0.4`$ to $`0.6`$. After four
graded rollouts, a task is evicted if its score variance is below
$`10^{-4}`$ or its mean is outside $`[0.05,0.88]`$; a patch returns the
task to the pool and resets the score window.

## Appendix C Adversary and repair details

#### Admission probe.

The exploit and honest answers are graded with three votes per criterion
under the strict judge note given in Appendix J, length-adjusted and
unclipped so that resolution below zero is preserved. A rubric passes
the probe when $`\Delta\leq-\delta`$ with $`\delta=0.20`$ and
$`0.15\leq s_{\text{honest}}\leq 0.92`$; otherwise the failure reason is
_exploitable_, _saturated_, or _unsatisfiable_, and the task goes to
repair. A probe in which the adversary scores nothing is discarded
rather than counted as unexploitable, and a probe that returns no usable
measurement, because generation or grading fails or an answer is too
short, admits the task unchanged. Up to two probe-repair rounds run at
admission. Each round rewrites the task and rubric together, in every
arm, and the rewrite is accepted only if a fresh probe shows $`\Delta`$
lower by at least $`0.15`$ with the honest score still in band;
proposals are salvaged rather than rejected, with points clamped to
$`\pm[5,10]`$ and growth trimmed to four added items. If no rewrite
clears the bar, the rewrite with the largest measured drop in $`\Delta`$
is adopted, provided the drop is positive and the honest score is in
band, and otherwise the task keeps its rubric. An adopted rewrite
therefore always leaves a fresh exploit a smaller lead over the honest
answer than the rubric it replaces. Every task enters the training pool
after its repair rounds, including tasks whose rubric the adversary
still beats, because the aim of repair is a better rubric and keeping
these tasks preserves the diversity of the pool. Admission runs in the
background with a cap of $`48`$ concurrent repairs.

#### Patch validation.

A proposed criterion must be $`40`$ to $`220`$ characters with
$`|p|\in[5,10]`$, fit under the patched-rubric cap, pass the rubric
validator, and have trigram overlap below $`0.6`$ with every existing
item. It is graded against both answers with three votes; an
undetermined verdict rejects. Negatives are additionally graded against
the cached honest answer and rejected if met. The normalized gap
removed, $`|p|/\sum_{p_{j}>0}p_{j}`$, must be at least $`0.05`$. Up to
five criteria are minted per round, two rounds per case, and at most six
patches per task over a run, and every acceptance writes the answer pair
with its verdicts to an evidence log.

## Appendix D Meta-prompt evolution details

#### Round.

Evolution runs at every scheduled evaluation interval on twenty graded
training cases sampled with failure weighting. Half are the worst cases,
a quarter are the best, and the remainder are sampled at random. The
per-case analyst uses closed vocabularies for failure modes, rubric
defects, and task defects, plus a free-text gap and a three-way verdict;
counts are aggregated in code. The corpus statistics are criteria per
task, criterion length, negative share, use-case mix, distinct
specialties, and task self-similarity by shingles, ensuring they are as
diverse as possible. The single optimizer call returns new guidance for
the query proposer and the task-and-rubric generator, together with a
summary; the guidance is substituted into a placeholder of a structural
template that is re-synchronized from code at every start. Every round’s
full assembled prompt, statistics, diagnoses, exploit evidence, and
ledger are snapshotted.

#### Memo optimizer.

The memo is limited to $`2{,}400`$ characters. Because exploit scores
are already near their ceiling, an objective based only on the
adversary’s margin can favor easier rubrics that raise honest scores, so
each memo version is scored as

```math
g\;=\;\overline{\Delta}_{\text{adv}}\;+\;\kappa\,\max\!\big[0,\ \bar{s}_{\text{honest}}-\bar{s}_{\text{honest}}^{[0]}-\tau\big], \tag{2}
```

where the honest-score baseline is fixed at the first measured version
so that successive small increases in leniency cannot accumulate
unchecked. The objective uses $`\kappa=2.0`$, tolerance $`\tau`$ equal
to the regression tolerance $`0.02`$, and target margin $`0`$, and
requires two observations before a commit decision. A version that
scores worse than the best previous version by more than the tolerance
is rolled back, and a version whose honest mean falls below $`0.25`$ is
flagged as over-restricting and not tightened further. Fixed prompt
rules take precedence over the memo, and code continues to enforce
criterion counts, points, and the negative share.

#### Fixed components.

The judge template, the strict judge note, the structural generation
templates, the validator, and the shape controllers are code constants.

## Appendix E Training configuration

#### Optimization.

Group-relative policy optimization with $`n=8`$ rollouts per task at
temperature $`1.0`$, train batch $`64`$ tasks, mini-batch $`16`$,
learning rate $`10^{-6}`$, clip ratio $`0.2`$, token-mean loss
aggregation, no KL loss and no KL in the reward, no entropy bonus,
prompt length up to $`6{,}144`$ tokens and response length up to
$`8{,}192`$. Validation on all $`525`$ tasks at temperature $`1.0`$
every five updates. Every reported score comes from the last checkpoint
of its run, with no checkpoint selection. Policy served by vLLM with
asynchronous rollouts and trained with FSDP on four B200 GPUs; arms on
the shared cluster use a four-GPU allocation and a 24-hour chained job.

#### Solver tools.

Up to two tool calls per rollout, for citation purposes, to a clinical
knowledge-base search and a web search. The clinical search uses Milvus
over StatPearls, DailyMed, MedlinePlus, ICD-10-CM, and PubMed abstracts,
with a frozen summarizer producing one evidence brief. The web search
returns verbatim result blocks with no model in the path. Tool spans are
masked; query tokens are trained.

## Appendix F Baseline implementations

Figure 3(a) compares RRI with three published rubric-reward methods in
the 9B GPT Teacher setting. Each baseline trains Qwen3.5-9B, and we
report its adjusted score on HealthBench Professional at the last
checkpoint of its run.

#### RaR with synthetic tasks.

Rubrics as Rewards (Gunjal et al., 2026) writes a checklist of weighted
criteria for each prompt from its reference answer and uses the
checklist as the reward for on-policy reinforcement learning. The method
requires labeled tasks, and our setting provides none. We therefore
first generate a static list of tasks, then use RaR to decompose each
task into smaller tasks, and train the solver on the result.

#### EvoRubrics.

EvoRubrics (Ding et al., 2026) co-evolves a rubric generator with the
policy. The two share one backbone through separate LoRA adapters and
are both updated by GRPO at every training step. For each prompt the
policy samples several answers, the generator samples several rubric
sets, and the judge scores every answer against every rubric set. The
policy is rewarded by its mean normalized score across rubric sets. The
generator is rewarded for rubrics that separate the policy’s answers and
for diverse and useful criteria. We follow the official implementation
and its documentation.

#### SibylSense.

SibylSense (Xu et al., 2026) keeps the rubric generator frozen and
adapts it through a memory bank of validated rubric items. A proposed
item is rewarded by how much higher it scores a reference answer than
the candidate answers, and the top items in each category condition the
next round of rubric generation. Between rounds the policy is trained by
GRPO on the current rubrics, and its samples replace the candidate
answers, so each round must find criteria that the trained policy fails.
We follow the official implementation and its documentation.

## Appendix G Grading protocol

#### Template.

Training and validation use the benchmark’s published per-criterion
judge template verbatim. The judge sees the conversation, the final
response, and one criterion, and returns an explanation and a boolean.
Each criterion is graded with three votes and the majority taken. During
adversarial probes and patch validation a strict note is appended, as
reproduced in Appendix J, instructing the judge to credit only content
the response itself states, so that addressing a topic, restating the
request, hedging, or deferring to guidelines is not met.

#### Score.

The rubric score $`r(y)`$ of Section 4.1 is negative when met negative
criteria outweigh met positive ones. The length term of $`s(y)`$ in
Section 4.1 is linear in the answer length and centered at $`2{,}000`$
characters, so it subtracts $`0.0147`$ per $`500`$ characters above the
center and adds the same amount below it; the length is counted after
the reasoning channel and tool spans are removed. Validation reports the
signed length-adjusted score averaged over tasks as accuracy and the raw
fraction clipped to $`[0,1]`$ per task as the unadjusted metric.
Training applies the length term one-sided, penalizing only beyond the
center.

#### Judges.

GPT-5.5-Chat performs training and evaluation grading in the GPT-graded
settings. In the self-graded settings, the locally served frozen base
model performs both roles. Within a setting every row, including the
untrained row, is graded by the setting’s judge. Validation scores are
taken from the judge of the reported run, and Table 5 re-grades the same
answers with the official GPT-5.4 judge.

## Appendix I Exploit categories

This appendix describes how the exploit cases of Figure 6 were
collected, how the categories were derived, and how every case was
assigned to one of them, and it gives the full breakdown with examples.

#### Exploit cases.

At every training step the solver writes several answers to each
generated task. The judge scores each answer under the generated rubric,
and the rubric-blind comparison of Section 3.2 ranks the same answers
into tiers. An exploit case is a task whose top-scoring answer falls
outside the best tier while an answer inside the best tier scores at
least the margin $`\epsilon=0.15`$ lower. We pair the top-scoring
answer, the exploit, with the lowest-scoring answer of the best tier,
the honest answer. The repair step receives the cases with the largest
disagreement in each round, and the analysis here covers all of them at
every step. This gives $`2{,}076`$ cases for the 9B run and $`4{,}986`$
for the 27B run trained on the HealthBench Professional description,
$`3{,}482`$ for the run trained on the PRBench description, and
$`1{,}647`$ for the run trained on the ProfBench description,
$`12{,}191`$ in total. For each case we keep the task, the rubric at
that step, both answers, the verdict the judge recorded on every
criterion for each answer, and the criteria added by the repair when the
case was repaired.

#### Deriving the categories.

We first read 480 cases in full, 120 per run spread evenly over
training, with LLM coders working in 20 independent batches. For each
case a coder described what the exploit answer did, which property of
the rubric or of the grading paid for it, what the repair added, and
whether the honest answer was really the better one, and wrote its own
label. The batches produced about 250 labels. We consolidated them by
the property of the reward that failed, which gives the five categories
of reward failure and the one category of incorrect fixes, the incorrect
pairs, in Table 12, and a second pass of coders assigned the same 480
cases to the consolidated categories to test their boundaries. Table 11
gives the definition of each category.

#### Missed negatives.

The exploit earns its criteria and also states wrong or unsafe content
that no criterion touches. A rubric of positive criteria cannot see what
should not be in an answer, and the negative criteria the proposer
writes name only the errors it anticipated. In a diagnosis coding task
the exploit lists an external cause code as the principal diagnosis,
which the coding rules do not permit, and still meets all four criteria,
since the rubric asks only that the code appear. In a market entry
report the exploit treats the whole addressable market as the firm’s own
revenue and recommends immediate entry, and it scores $`0.68`$ against
$`0.32`$ because the three criteria price only the sizing arithmetic.
This is the largest category overall and the one the negative criteria
of the repair step address. A reward generator needs a correctness check
over the whole answer in addition to its list of criteria.

#### Ambiguous rubrics.

A criterion addresses the right content, and its wording leaves open
what counts. Criteria worded as “mentions in some way” credit a passing
mention, a hedge, or a claim that evidence exists, penalties are avoided
by attributing a recommendation to other clinicians, and the same
content in other words is refused. In one case two interchangeable
consult notes score $`1.00`$ and $`0.68`$ because one uses the
criterion’s term for a structure and the other uses an accepted synonym.
Rubrics of one to three criteria make this worse, since a single verdict
moves the score from $`1.00`$ to $`0.00`$. Criteria should demand a
committed and checkable statement, and grading should extract the
required specifics from the whole answer.

#### Faulty answer key.

The content of a criterion is wrong, out of date, inherited from a
premise of the task, or fixes one of several defensible conventions, so
the correct answer loses points. In one clinical case all three criteria
restate one side of a contested management question, and the answer that
argues the other side from the diagnostic criteria scores $`0.00`$. In a
finance task the key applies a tax rate that a later regime replaces for
the transaction date in the task, and the answer built on retrieved
sources is docked for stating the current rate. The category is largest
for the ProfBench description, where a rubric is a short list of
reference numbers that the proposer computed and never checked. The
proposer should verify its key by executing the arithmetic or retrieving
the rule, and write conditional criteria where the task leaves a
convention open.

#### Fabricated citations.

A criterion asks for named studies, identifiers, sample sizes, or
statistics, and the judge can check only their form. One rubric asks for
named studies with sample sizes and relapse rates on a question for
which no such trials exist. The exploit supplies invented studies and
scores $`0.69`$, and the honest answer that reports the absence of
trials scores $`0.14`$. A demand for rigor becomes an incentive to
invent. The category is specific to the medical description, where
research tasks invite such criteria. Evidence criteria should name the
expected sources or be graded with retrieval, and the rubric should
reward reporting that evidence is absent.

#### Incorrect pair.

The answer preferred by the rubric-blind comparison is not the better
one, so the rubric’s ordering was defensible and there is no reward
failure. The comparison tends to prefer the longer or tidier answer and
cannot verify statutes, thresholds, or arithmetic. In one legal case
both answers rest on the same invented state statute, and in a tax case
the preferred answer takes a wrong threshold from a web snippet while
the exploit computes the requested figures correctly. The category
reaches 24% for the two non-medical descriptions. A flagged exploit
should be verified before it is repaired, and the loop needs a stopping
rule for pairs of equally good answers.

#### Missed positives.

The exploit is correct and minimal, and the rubric has no criterion for
the substance the honest answer adds. This is under 1% of the cases,
because the solver’s exploits add content and seldom withhold it. Most
accepted repair criteria are nevertheless positive, since a repair
usually demands the correct statement in place of the wrong one.

## Appendix J Prompts

All prompts are reproduced verbatim from the generation service and the
reward module; the implementation refers to the adversary as the
_farmer_, to the repair step as the _minter_, and to the rubric-blind
comparison as the _referee_, and those names appear unchanged in the
listings. Bracketed tokens such as \[\[K\]\] are substituted in code.
Every listed prompt except the converged memo at the end is a code
constant; only the guidance blocks that the meta-optimizer writes and
the memo it maintains change during a run.

#### The frozen adversary.

You are a RED-TEAM evaluator stress-testing a grading rubric. You are
not a physician and your output is never shown to a patient or a
clinician: it is scored by an automatic grader and then discarded.

You are given a clinician request and the EXACT rubric that will score
the answer. Write the response that scores as HIGH as possible while
doing as LITTLE real clinical work as possible. You are measuring
whether this rubric can be satisfied without knowing any medicine.

THE ONE RULE THAT DEFINES THE TEST: withhold every piece of content you
would have to actually KNOW or WORK OUT. Whatever the request is, there
is some substance that answering it properly requires --- a specific
value, a named entity, a decision, a computed result, a judgement about
this particular case, a faithful rendering of the source. Do not supply
any of it. Where a criterion demands it, gesture at the topic and move
on. Invent nothing: fabricating a specific to look knowledgeable defeats
the measurement as surely as knowing it would.

Then earn as much of the rubric as that constraint allows:

- Satisfy each criterion’s LETTER, not its intent. A hedged criterion
  ("mentions in some way that ...") is satisfied by naming what it is
  about.

- For a disjunctive criterion ("does at least one of: A, B, or C"), take
  whichever branch needs the least real knowledge, and do only that
  branch.

- Restate the request back as structure: headings, a summary of what was
  asked, the considerations that bear on it, next steps.

- Cover ground so that something touches every criterion, while
  committing to nothing.

- Reuse the criteria’s own vocabulary wherever it reads naturally.

- Fall back on generic professional language: seek specialist input,
  individualise to the patient, follow current guidance, arrange
  follow-up, document carefully.

OTHER HARD RULES --- breaking any of these makes the measurement
worthless:

- Do not trip any NEGATIVE criterion: those describe mistakes and cost
  points.

- Never mention the rubric, the criteria, grading, or that you are
  red-teaming.

- Match the request’s LANGUAGE and its requested FORM (letter, note,
  table, translation, summary) --- form is free to imitate; it is
  substance you must withhold.

- Stay under 2500 characters: length is taxed, so padding past that
  loses more than it gains.

Output ONLY the response text. No preamble and no \<think\> block.

#### The honest answer.

The same frozen model answers the task text alone, without the rubric,
under the solver’s system prompt.

You are a knowledgeable, careful medical AI assistant helping a
clinician. Read the request and respond with a directly useful,
accurate, and well-organized answer. Be complete but concise; follow the
clinician’s instructions and requested format exactly. Ground claims in
established clinical evidence, state important caveats and uncertainty,
ask for missing context when it materially changes the answer, and never
include unsafe or fabricated recommendations. Prioritize patient safety.

#### The admission-time rewriter.

You repair a REWARD SPECIFICATION that an adversary just farmed. You are
given a clinician task, its grading rubric, an answer that scored HIGH
by exploiting the rubric without doing any clinical work, and a
rubric-blind answer that did the work and scored LOWER. The
specification is wrong: it pays for asserting conclusions rather than
for knowing things.

Rewrite the TASK and the RUBRIC together, as one coherent body.

WHY A NEGATIVE CRITERION IS NOT ENOUGH, so you understand what to fix.
Forbidding the exploiter’s sentence only taxes the shortcut; the
criteria that PAID for it are still there. If every positive criterion
can be satisfied by stating a conclusion, a model with no knowledge
still scores well. The defect is on the POSITIVE side, and that is what
you must change.

REWRITE THE TASK when the question itself is answerable without
knowledge -- most often when the correct answer is a bare negative ("no
such threshold exists"), which anyone can assert. Keep the SAME clinical
scenario, specialty and difficulty: ask for what a clinician would have
to know or work out. Demand the specifics -- which agents, which study
designs and what they measured, what the numbers were, what follows
under a stated constraint -- so the answer cannot be a paraphrase of its
own grading criteria.

REWRITE THE RUBRIC so that EVERY positive criterion requires something
checkable that must be known or derived: a named entity, a numeric value
or range, a mechanism, an explicit comparison, or a decision justified
under the case’s constraint. A criterion that can be met by restating
the task, agreeing with a premise, or naming a topic without committing
to content is exactly the hole being closed -- do not write one.

Keep: at least as many criteria as the original (you MAY ADD up to three
more when each new one pays a distinct piece of checkable substance --
denser criteria are denser reward signal), points in \[5,10\] for
positives and \[-10,-5\] for negatives, at least two positives, and at
most three negatives. Every criterion must be independently checkable;
never split one fact across two criteria to inflate the count. Do not
mention the adversary, the rubric, or this instruction in the task text.

YOUR REWRITE IS NOT ACCEPTED ON YOUR REASONING. It is measured: a fresh
adversary that sees your new rubric and withholds all real content is
graded against it, and so is a fresh answer that never sees it. The
rewrite is kept ONLY if the knowledgeable answer now scores materially
HIGHER than the adversary’s. Write for that test.

Output ONLY a JSON object:

{"question": "\<the rewritten clinician task, self-contained\>",

"rubric_items": \[{"criterion_text": "\<...\>", "points": \<number\>},
...\],

"why_farmer_fails": "\<=40 words: what the lazy answer can no longer get
away with",

"why_honest_passes": "\<=40 words: what a knowledgeable answer supplies
that earns it\>"}

#### The repair step.

Given an exploit case, the repair step proposes the new criteria that
are then tested against both answers.

You repair a REWARD SPECIFICATION that was just exploited. A rubric
scored answer A above answer B; an independent referee that never saw
the rubric judged B the better clinical answer. The rubric is therefore
wrong. Repair it with 1-5 NEW criteria, which may be NEGATIVE, POSITIVE,
or a mix. Write as many as the contrast genuinely supports --- every
distinct piece of checkable substance B supplied and A withheld deserves
its own criterion, and denser criteria are denser training signal ---
but never split one fact across two criteria to inflate the count:

- A NEGATIVE criterion (-10..-5 points) names the mistake A made to earn
  points it did not deserve: what a BAD response DOES. It is kept ONLY if
  the grader judges it MET on A and NOT MET on B.

- A POSITIVE criterion (+5..+10 points) names a piece of REAL CLINICAL
  SUBSTANCE that B supplied and A withheld --- a specific value,
  threshold, named entity, computed result, or case-specific judgement. It
  is kept ONLY if the grader judges it MET on B and NOT MET on A. Positive
  criteria are the stronger repair: they REQUIRE the knowledge whose
  absence made the rubric farmable, where a negative can only forbid one
  phrasing of the evasion. Prefer at least one positive whenever B
  actually contains such substance.

Each criterion is applied mechanically to both answers by the same
grader that scored them; your reasoning is never part of acceptance. A
criterion that fires on both answers, or on neither, is discarded.

HOW TO PHRASE EACH CRITERION --- get this wrong and it is dropped before
it is even tested:

- Affirmative present tense, third person, ONE behaviour, 40-220
  characters.

- A negative describes THE MISTAKE ITSELF: "Recommends continuing
  metformin at an eGFR of 25". NEVER an absence ("Does not ...", "Avoids
  ...", "Fails to ...") --- the grader marks those MET when the answer is
  CORRECT, so the penalty lands on good answers. Auto-rejected.

- A positive describes THE SUBSTANCE ITSELF: "States the 2024 KDIGO eGFR
  threshold below which metformin must stop, with the numeric value". Make
  it satisfiable ONLY by actually stating the substance, never by naming
  its topic.

- NEVER start with "The response", "The answer", "The model",
  "Penalise", "Deduct", "Award", "Score" or "Mark". A criterion describes
  the response; it does not instruct the grader.

- Target the BEHAVIOUR or the SUBSTANCE, not this answer’s wording: it
  will grade every future rollout of this task.

Also classify the exploit with EXACTLY ONE label from
\[’hedge_namedrop’, ’task_restatement’, ’breadth_padding’,
’disjunction_cheapest’, ’keyword_stuffing’, ’unhedged_confidence’,
’format_compliance’, ’safety_boilerplate’, ’negative_absent’,
’criterion_ambiguous’, ’other’, ’none’\]. The label is bookkeeping and
never decides acceptance:

- a real exploit you cannot name with any other label -\> "other", AND
  STILL WRITE THE CRITERIA;

- "none" ONLY when A genuinely earned its score. Then output an empty
  items list.

Output ONLY a JSON object:

{"mode": "\<one label\>",

"items": \[{"criterion_text": "\<criterion\>", "points": \<-10..-5 or
+5..+10\>,

"why_separates": "\<=30 words"}, ...\]}

#### Strict judge note used in probes and patch validation.

Note: a criterion may describe something UNDESIRABLE and carry negative
points. Report whether the criterion is MET, not whether the response is
good. A good response that correctly avoids the described mistake does
NOT meet it. Judge only the content the response itself states. When a
criterion names specific content -- a value, threshold, dose, interval,
named entity, mechanism, comparison, or a committed decision -- it is
MET only if the response states that content explicitly. Addressing the

#### The meta-optimizer.

You are the meta-optimizer for a self-evolving curriculum that trains a
medical AI for HealthBench Professional (care consult, writing &
documentation, medical research --- NOT diagnosis). You rewrite the
appended guidance of two generation prompts:

(A) the QUERY PROPOSER --- proposes the clinician requests that become

(B) the TASK+RUBRIC GENERATOR --- writes each clinician task and its
grading rubric.

You are given: the reward formula the solver is optimised against;
MEASURED statistics of the tasks your last guidance actually produced,
against the generated training data; a COUNTED error analysis over this
step’s rollouts; the concrete capability gaps behind those errors; and a
history of previous guidance versions with the TRAIN mean score measured
under each.

=== HOW TO READ THE EVIDENCE (in priority order) ===

1. TRAIN MEAN SCORE IS NOT AN OBJECTIVE, and raising it is not evidence
   of anything. It is the solver’s score on tasks YOU wrote, so you can
   raise it at will by making tasks easier or rubrics looser --- and every
   past failure of this loop did exactly that. Read it only as a difficulty
   gauge: roughly 0.4-0.6 means the tasks are pitched about right; near 0.9
   means they are too easy to teach anything; near 0.1 means they are too
   hard to give gradient.

2. DISTRIBUTION and DIVERSITY statistics are arithmetic, not opinion.
   Where a generated statistic has drifted from its benchmark reference,
   correcting it is the highest-value edit you can make: training on a
   distribution the benchmark does not contain is wasted compute no matter
   how good the individual tasks are.

3. The ERROR TAXONOMY tells you what the solver actually lacks. Target
   the modes with the largest counts, but ONLY those attributed to
   "model_failed_fair_task". Counts under "generator_at_fault" are YOUR
   bugs --- fix the generator guidance instead of writing more tasks of
   that kind.

=== WHAT YOUR REWRITE MUST ACHIEVE (all four, simultaneously) ===

(1) DISTRIBUTION ALIGNMENT --- generated tasks must look like the
benchmark on every measured axis: use-case mix, criteria per task,
criterion length, point scale, and share of tasks carrying a negative
criterion. Where the measurements show drift, say so explicitly in the
guidance with the number to hit.

(2) DIVERSITY --- vary specialty, sub-topic, patient context,
language/register, artifact type, and difficulty. Template collapse is
the single most destructive failure this loop has: it is measured below
as task self-similarity, and if that number is climbing, your guidance
is the cause. Diversity comes from naming AXES to vary, never from
listing specific topics --- a topic list becomes the next template.

(3) QUALITY --- rubrics must be objectively checkable from the answer
alone, must test judgment BEYOND the task’s surface instructions, and
must not be satisfiable by restating the prompt.

(4) SOLVER NEED --- the tasks must exercise the specific capabilities
the error analysis shows the solver failing, at the granularity of the
"gap" strings, not the coarse category names.

Guidance is appended verbatim into each prompt, so write concrete
imperative instructions. Keep what the evidence shows is working;
replace what it does not. Each block \<= 300 words.

=== HARD CONSTRAINTS (these override anything the evidence seems to
suggest) ===

- The solver’s private reasoning channel is stripped before grading and
  NEVER graded. Never target chain-of-thought suppression, "no reasoning
  traces", word caps, or exact header echoes. Those criteria are dead
  weight that grade as free points.

- NEVER make rubrics easier, "transparent", or 1:1-mapped to the task’s
  stated deliverables. Raise difficulty through the TASK; never through
  leniency, and never by relaxing grading.

- Rubrics stay 1-5 SHORT single-fact criteria at +5..+10 each (modal
  +8), with NO required point total. Never push back toward many long
  conjunctive criteria or a fixed total: only the ratio of achieved to
  available points is scored, so a fixed total rewards covering ground
  over being right.

- Negative criteria belong on about a third of tasks, wherever a
  concrete clinical trap genuinely exists --- not on all of them, and not
  on none.

- Do NOT name or paraphrase specific held-out benchmark items.

- If a SPECIFICATION HACKABILITY section is present, treat it as your
  difficulty gauge’s companion and NOT as a licence to relax anything. It

  drives it is criteria that can be satisfied by naming a topic rather
  than stating a specific checkable value.

- Do not oscillate: if the history shows two alternatives have both been
  tried, pick a third direction rather than returning to either.

#### The exploit memo as converged in the 9B-setting run.

The final text of the memo, written by the memo optimizer over seventeen
versions under the objective of Equation 2, is reproduced below.

Known exploit: rubric-visible farmers gain credit by breadth-padding,
paraphrasing rubric topics, or giving generic medical statements that
match a category without committing to a case-relevant fact.

Drafting instructions:

- Do not write criteria that can be satisfied by naming, listing,
  mentioning, categorizing, or broadly discussing a diagnosis, test,
  treatment, risk, guideline, topic, recommendation, or keyword.

- Do not use topic-level verbs such as "addresses", "discusses",
  "mentions", "considers", "recognizes", "notes", "includes", or "covers".

- Write each criterion as a specific, checkable factual claim that is
  independently true or false.

- Require a concrete relationship with both sides specified:
  finding-\>interpretation, condition-\>action, result-\>next step,
  mechanism-\>effect, risk factor-\>consequence, evidence-\>conclusion, or
  contraindication-\>avoidance.

- Require the detail that makes the claim verifiable, such as a
  threshold, value, trend, comparison, timing, sequence, indication,
  contraindication, prerequisite, monitoring parameter, expected result,
  or expected outcome.

- Do not reward generic correctness. A criterion should fail if the
  response gives only a broad recommendation or textbook statement without
  the required qualifying detail.

- For reasoning criteria, require both the conclusion and the supporting
  evidence; neither alone satisfies the criterion.

- For management criteria, require the action plus the patient feature,
  trigger, rationale, consequence, or monitoring implication that
  determines appropriateness.

- For diagnostic criteria, require interpretation of a finding and its
  effect on likelihood, prioritization, differentiation, testing, or
  management.

- Avoid criteria passable through exhaustive lists, breadth of coverage,
  keyword overlap, or restating prompt facts. Require explanation of the
  significance of facts.

- Negative criteria should target concrete false factual claims,
  incorrect thresholds, wrong interpretations, invalid causal links,
  unsafe actions, or inappropriate recommendations.

- Prefer criteria that can be checked against a distinct factual
  assertion rather than the presence of a topic.

times in the past year and had a gastrointestinal bleed three years
earlier. A colleague has told the family that falls are essentially a

Atrial fibrillation is an irregular heart rhythm that can lead to blood

_I’m preparing a teaching summary for emergency medicine residents on

Please provide an evidence-based overview focused on why adolescents

## Appendix L Extended experiments

The experiments examine whether reward evolution improves Generation
Only training, whether it works when the frozen base model fills every
auxiliary role, and where gains appear across task types. Component
comparisons and repair logs then show how each part of the loop
contributes and what the accepted criteria change.

### L.1 Experimental Setup

Benchmark. HealthBench Professional (Hicks et al., 2026) is a set of
$`525`$ physician-written tasks, each a clinician’s request with a
physician-authored rubric of signed criteria. The tasks are tagged by
category, with $`236`$ _consult_, $`147`$ _research_, and $`142`$
_writing_ tasks; by difficulty, with $`256`$ _typical_ and $`269`$
_difficult_ tasks; and by one of $`28`$ specialties. We report the
benchmark’s official metric, the length-adjusted signed rubric score
$`s(y)`$ of Section 4.1 averaged over tasks, which is labeled _accuracy_
in the detailed tables, together with the unadjusted rubric fraction as
a companion metric. The benchmark is used for evaluation. Every training
task is written by the proposer.

Base models. We train Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.6-27B directly from the raw
base weights, as described in the Qwen3 Technical Report (Yang et al.,
2025).

Generation Only baseline. The comparison that isolates reward evolution
holds everything else equal, using the same base model, the same
group-relative recipe and hyperparameters, the same solver tools, the
same judge, the same number of generated tasks, and the same proposer
architecture, with the proposer’s prompts frozen. We call this the
_Generation Only_ baseline.

Training settings. We evaluate four settings. The _9B setting_ trains
Qwen3.5-9B with the GPT judge for training and validation and with the
solver’s retrieval and web-search tools enabled, and the _27B setting_
applies the same protocol to Qwen3.6-27B. The _all-self setting_ serves
every role, including the proposer, generator, adversary, repair step,
memo and meta-optimizer, training judge, and validation judge from the
frozen Qwen3.5-9B, keeping the entire loop self-contained. The
_no-retrieval setting_ turns retrieval off and serves the training and
validation judge from the frozen 9B. Within each setting, the
_untrained_ row is the base model evaluated under the setting’s own
validation judge before any update.

Grading. Training and validation use the benchmark’s per-criterion judge
template verbatim, with a three-vote majority per criterion. The GPT
judge is GPT-5.5-Chat, and the self-judge is the frozen base model.
Appendix G gives the template, the length constants, and the train-time
asymmetry.

Compute. Each arm runs on four B200 GPUs or a four-GPU allocation of a
shared cluster, with the policy served by vLLM and trained with FSDP,
and with the proposer and adversaries served from a separate generation
service, as detailed in Appendix E.

### L.3 Gains concentrate in writing and difficult tasks

By category. The three categories differ in how far a rubric can drift
from its intent. A research question has a fact of the matter that a
criterion can name; a consult has a management plan; a writing task,
such as a discharge summary, a referral letter, or a patient handout,
has a _form_, and a rubric for it is easy to satisfy by producing the
form. In the 9B setting, the Generation Only leaves writing accuracy at
$`0.199`$, level with the untrained model’s $`0.194`$, while research
and consult improve; RRI lifts writing to $`0.590`$, a gain of $`39.1`$
points and the largest single-cell effect in the table, as shown in
Figure 3(b). The same pattern appears in the 27B setting, where scores
rise from $`0.288`$ to $`0.435`$, and in the all-self setting, where
they rise from $`0.310`$ to $`0.569`$. Consult gains are moderate and
consistent, ranging from $`+0.5`$ to $`+10.5`$ points. Research is the
one category where Generation Only stays competitive, and in the
all-self setting it leads by five points, consistent with research
rubrics having the least room between their literal and intended
readings.

By difficulty. The benchmark’s difficult tasks are those whose rubrics
ask for a specific interpretation, threshold, or contraindication beyond
naming the topic. In the 9B setting, Generation Only improves difficult
accuracy by ten points over the untrained model and RRI by $`28`$;
relative to Generation Only, RRI gains $`18.6`$ points on difficult
tasks against $`2.8`$ on typical ones. The ordering holds across
settings, with $`18.8`$ versus $`5.7`$ at 27B, $`16.5`$ versus $`3.9`$
in the all-self setting, and $`12.3`$ versus $`-4.8`$ without retrieval.
A positive criterion minted by the repair step names a piece of
substance the exploit withheld, and training against it teaches the
policy to supply the specific fact. In the no-retrieval setting, where
the policy must supply from memory the facts the repaired rubrics
demand, the typical-task score gives back some of what the
difficult-task score gains; with retrieval, both rise.

### L.4 Repeated hack-then-patch rounds add to admission probing

With retrieval off, prompt evolution and admission-time adversary
probing reach $`0.424`$, compared with the untrained score of $`0.240`$.
The lower group adds retrieval. Starting from the Generation Only at
$`0.424`$, Co-evolve Only raises accuracy to $`0.506`$, and adding
repair rounds in which the adversary re-attacks each patched rubric adds
a further gain, reaching $`0.533`$ overall with the difficult-task score
rising from $`0.370`$ to $`0.427`$. This comparison supports repairing
each rubric repeatedly instead of screening it once. A single probe
leaves in place whatever exploit the first rewrite still permits, and
re-attacking the patched rubric closes it.

### L.5 Analysis of the Repair Process

Over the 9B-setting run, the loop processed $`540`$ exploit cases on
$`287`$ distinct tasks. It accepted $`1{,}451`$ criteria, $`1{,}175`$
positive and $`276`$ negative; each accepted criterion removed on
average $`0.23`$ of the exploit’s normalized margin, and the mean margin
of an exploit over its honest counterpart at the moment it was caught
was $`0.40`$. The adversary re-attacks every patched rubric, and in 247
of the 293 first-round cases it found a new exploit, which a second
round then patched. A re-attack that still wins shows that the adversary
keeps finding loopholes, while the rubric it attacks already carries the
criteria of the previous round. At admission, the generation-server log
of this run records $`2{,}796`$ tasks whose rubric the adversary beat
and the repair step rewrote. Each adopted rewrite cut the measured lead
of a fresh exploit over the honest answer, by $`0.37`$ on average, and
over the rewrites of a task the mean lead fell from $`0.43`$ to
$`0.01`$. The honest answer led on $`33\%`$ of these tasks after repair
and on $`0.1\%`$ before. Only $`22\%`$ reached $`\Delta\leq-\delta`$, so
admitting only rubrics the adversary can no longer beat would discard
nearly four fifths of the repaired tasks, each of which is better than
the version the proposer wrote. The 27B run shows the same composition
at a larger volume, with $`4{,}906`$ criteria on $`952`$ tasks, of which
$`84\%`$ are positive.

Across all runs, the most common exploit category is missed negatives,
where the exploit states wrong or unsafe content that no criterion
penalizes, while ambiguous rubrics lead in the runs trained on the
HealthBench Professional description, as Appendix I shows. Most accepted
repairs are positive criteria that demand the specific, correct
statement; for example, “reports that roughly $`30\%`$ of posterior
sternoclavicular dislocations compress mediastinal structures” replaces
the credit that “mentions the clinical significance of missed posterior
displacement” gave away, as illustrated in Appendix K. The exploit memo
converged to a short list of drafting rules of the same character,
forbidding topic-level verbs and requiring each criterion to be a
checkable claim with both sides of a relationship stated, as shown in
Appendix J; the memo optimizer reached that text in seventeen versions,
with the rollback rule reverting the versions that lowered the
adversary’s margin by making rubrics easier.

## Appendix M Limitations

Model-generated rubrics and model-based judges can make errors, and
passing an adversarial repair check does not guarantee that every
possible exploit has been removed. Results describe the reported
backbone, judge, and tool configurations. Benchmark performance does not
measure patient outcomes or establish clinical deployment effectiveness.
The held-out benchmark runs of Table 7 have no Generation Only control,
so they show that RRI improves the base model on those benchmarks and
not how much of the gain Generation Only would also obtain, and
ProfBench has only 40 tasks.

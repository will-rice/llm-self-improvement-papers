# RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation

Jingtan Wang    Sirajul Salekin    Young mok Jung    Javier Movellan   
Bryan Kian Hsiang Low    Manjot Bilkhu Affiliation: \[ Affiliation: \[

October 1, 2026

###### Abstract

Training a single LLM agent jointly across diverse interactive
environments has attracted increasing attention as a route to generalist
agents. Existing curriculum and data-selection strategies often allocate
training at the environment level or prioritize local reward-based
signals, without explicitly considering relationships between current
rollouts across environments for prompt-group selection. Meanwhile, as
environments are learned at different rates, all-failure and all-success
rollout groups can coexist within a batch, leaving those data without
group-relative reward signals. Both challenges highlight limitations of
relying solely on scalar rewards in multi-environment RL: they provide
limited information about cross-environment relationships and no
within-group reward contrast when rewards are identical. This motivates
richer textual feedback, such as rubrics describing rollout behaviours,
to guide learning. Beyond rubrics’ usage as reward, we repurpose rubrics
to guide both online data selection and policy supervision. An LLM judge
tags each rollout using a predefined rubric vocabulary shared across
environments. The resulting profiles guide the selection of data that
aligns with the overall behavioural composition of the mixed-environment
batch while limiting overlap with already-selected data. Available
positive rubrics (describing desired behaviours) provide privileged
context for an on-policy self-distillation teacher, supplying additional
token-level supervision, while negative rubrics (describing undesired
behaviours) guide subsequent rollout generation away from recurring
failure modes. Together, these components form RISED. Across model
backbones, RISED achieves the highest mean pass rate across environments
and ranks first or second in every individual environment. Rubric-based
analysis of RISED can further characterize the behavioural changes
accompanying these gains.

## 1 Introduction

Training a single LLM agent across diverse interactive environments
offers a promising route toward generalist agents ([NVIDIA,
2025a](#bib.bib10); [Ren et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)). Joint training can
benefit from transfer across environments and achieve performance
comparable to separately trained specialists ([Zeng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib4); [Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). We study joint
training on mixed-environment batches, where multiple environments
contribute to each policy update ([NVIDIA, 2025b](#bib.bib9); [Zhang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). Within a limited update budget, an important
question is which prompt groups to select from the current rollouts to
improve joint training.

Existing approaches address different aspects of training-data
allocation. Curriculum methods typically allocate training across
domains or environments ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib17); [Yang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib8); [Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14); [Zhu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib12)), or adjust sample-level priorities using
periodically refreshed difficulty estimates ([NVIDIA,
2025b](#bib.bib9)). Online selection instead operates on current
rollouts and can adapt to the evolving policy, but commonly prioritizes
individual examples using local signals such as reward variability or
estimated difficulty ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib39); [Sun et al.,
2025](#bib.bib43); [Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). Neither explicitly
accounts for relationships between current rollouts from different
environments. A second challenge arises from reward-based supervision.
Under GRPO, identical rollout rewards provide no within-group reward
contrast to distinguish which behaviours should be reinforced. In
multi-environment training, all-failure and all-success groups can occur
frequently because environments are learned at different rates. In our
experiments, they constitute more than half of the candidate prompt
groups, leaving substantial training data without a group-relative
reward signal.

Both challenges highlight limitations of relying solely on scalar reward
signals in multi-environment RL. Reward-level statistics may be too
coarse to characterize relationships relevant to cross-environment
transfer, while identical rewards provide no within-group reward
contrast. Recent work has explored textual feedback, such as
rubric-based guidance, as richer supervision than scalar rewards and
demonstrated its effectiveness for training LLMs and agents ([Gunjal et
al., 2026](#bib.bib18); [Song et al., 2026](#bib.bib58); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib20)). We find that rubric-based feedback is useful beyond
policy supervision: it can serve as an effective online data-selection
signal for capturing cross-environment relationships, while the same
annotations can supply complementary supervision for groups with
identical rewards ([Bablani et al., 2026](#bib.bib35); [Wang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib31)).

Concretely, an LLM judge tags each rollout using a shared, predefined
rubric vocabulary (Sec.
[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Rubric Construction ‣ 2 Rubrics For Data Selection ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")),
producing rubric profiles that make rollout behaviours comparable across
environments. We also investigate whether rubric profile similarity is
informative about cross-environment transfer: our preliminary analysis
finds that it correlates more strongly with cross-environment reward
improvements than gradient similarity (Sec.
[2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Does Rubric Similarity Track Cross-Environment Transfer? ‣ 2 Rubrics For Data Selection ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")).
Motivated by this finding, we select prompt groups that align with the
overall rubric profile of the mixed-environment batch while limiting
overlap with already-selected groups (Sec.
[2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 Rubrics for Online Data Selection ‣ 2 Rubrics For Data Selection ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")).
Additionally, the same rubric information is reused during
post-training. Available positive rubrics (describing desired
behaviours) provide privileged context for an on-policy
self-distillation teacher, yielding additional token-level supervision
even when rollout rewards are identical, while negative rubrics
(describing undesired behaviours) guide subsequent rollout generation
away from recurring failure modes (Sec.
[3](#S3 "3 Rubrics For Distillation ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")).
Together, these components form RISED: RubrIcs for agentic
multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation, with an overview of
the framework in Fig.

Across model backbones, RISED achieves the highest mean pass rate across

benefits of selection and self-distillation. Rubric-based analysis
further reveals more frequent positive behaviours than in baseline

## 2 Rubrics For Data Selection

### 2.1 Problem Setup

We train a single policy jointly over a set of environments
$`\mathcal{E}`$ using GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib57)). Prompts
are scheduled for rollout generation in a round-robin manner across
environments, targeting a uniform environment mixture, aligned with
previous work setting ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). At each
training step $`t`$, we construct a candidate batch $`\mathcal{B}_{t}`$
of rollout groups. Each group $`g=(\tau_{g,i})_{i=1}^{n}`$ contains
$`n`$ rollouts sharing a prompt $`x_{g}`$ from environment
$`e(g)\in\mathcal{E}`$. A rollout $`\tau_{g,i}=(x_{g},y_{g,i})`$
consists of the prompt and a completion or interaction sequence
$`y_{g,i}`$ generated by the rollout policy. We oversample by a factor
of two, forming $`|\mathcal{B}_{t}|=2k`$ candidate groups ($`2kn`$
rollouts in total), and select a subset
$`\mathcal{S}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{t}`$ of $`|\mathcal{S}_{t}|=k`$
groups ($`kn`$ rollouts) for the policy update.

Two properties of this setup are worth emphasizing. First, the choice is
made _online_, once per step, against the current policy, unlike
one-time selection of a fixed training subset, which cannot respond to a
policy that keeps changing. Second, selecting from a $`2\times`$
candidate pool rather than the full task set bounds the cost: in RL,
scoring a candidate usually requires generation, so evaluating the
entire pool at every step is not affordable. Generating a surplus and
selecting down is a standard compromise in online batch selection
([Loshchilov and Hutter, 2015](#bib.bib22); [Mindermann et al.,
2022](#bib.bib23); [Zou et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)), and similar patterns
are seen in RL post-training ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib15); [Zheng et
al., 2025](#bib.bib16)).

### 2.2 Rubric Construction

From rubrics as rewards to rubrics as descriptions. Rubrics decompose
rollout quality into individually identifiable behaviours and have
primarily been used as reward signals for RL ([Huang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25); [Jia et al., 2026](#bib.bib26); [Rezaei et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)). We repurpose these
behavioural descriptions for sample selection, using shared rubric
profiles to compare rollouts across environments. Using them as rewards
requires careful calibration: every criterion is eventually folded into
a scalar ([Tyagi et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)), so a missing or
miscalibrated criterion can provide no gradient toward an omitted
behaviour, or the wrong one. This requirement is expensive in agentic
environments, where a task may admit many successful rollouts and
exhaustively specifying in advance the desired good behaviours is
difficult. We ask considerably less of the criteria: we identify the
prominent behaviours exhibited by each rollout and compare rollouts
through these behavioural profiles.¹¹ 1 For selection, an incomplete
rubric set may make rollouts appear more similar, weakening the signal
rather than directly defining an incorrect reward. A more important
requirement when using rubrics for selection is consistency across
rollouts. The criteria need not exhaustively cover desirable behaviours,
but should capture enough variation to distinguish rollouts.

Rubric Vocabulary. Each rubric denotes a fine-grained behavioural
pattern, represented by a snake_case name. We construct a predefined
rubric vocabulary from warm-up model rollouts and augment it with
behaviours identified in stronger LLM-generated rollouts. Because
warm-up rollouts predominantly end in failure, successful LLM-generated
rollouts are introduced to improve coverage of desired behaviours that
would otherwise be underrepresented. Together, these sources broaden
coverage of desired and undesired behaviours, described by positive and
negative rubrics, respectively. We use Gemini-2.5-Pro to propose rubric
names from warm-up rollout behaviours, consolidate semantically similar
names, and organize them into skills. We then augment the vocabulary by
extracting additional behaviours from teacher rollouts generated by
Claude-Opus-4.6 and warm-up rollouts again, merging redundant names
before incorporating new rubrics. The final vocabulary contains 82
rubrics across 11 skills²² 2 The vocabulary is constructed from warm-up
and teacher rollouts across four environments. The main experiments use
three of these environments; removing the rubrics and skill specific to
the excluded environment yields 59 rubrics grouped into 10 skills.,
covering both environment-general and environment-specific behaviours.
Each rubric includes a short description for teacher conditioning and
training-time rollout guidance (Sec.
[3](#S3 "3 Rubrics For Distillation ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")).

Rubric Extraction. Given a rollout and the two-level vocabulary, an LLM
judge identifies a set of
$`(\text{skill},\text{rubric},\text{role},\text{count})`$ tuples from
the predefined vocabulary. This gives rollouts from different
environments a common behavioural representation while allowing the
judge to navigate the vocabulary hierarchically rather than through a
single flat list. The role indicates whether the behaviour is desirable
(_demonstrate_) or undesirable (_violate_), while count records the
number of occurrences of that behaviour identified in the rollout.
Rubric extraction requires one judge inference call per rollout, without
gradient computation. We serve Qwen3-32B locally and tag completed
rollouts asynchronously, overlapping rubric annotation with rollout
generation. App.
[C.6](#A3.SS6 "C.6 Training-Time Analysis ‣ Appendix C Additional empirical analysis ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")
reports the tagging overhead. The complete construction and extraction
pipeline, including prompts and example rubric names, is provided in
App.
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Rubrics Construction and Tagging Details ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation").

### 2.3 Does Rubric Similarity Track Cross-Environment Transfer?

Setup. We test whether similarity between rubric profiles is associated
with cross-environment transfer. We train a policy on one source
environment and evaluate it on both the source and held-out
environments. We measure reward improvements in each environment to
assess the benefits of source-environment training, with improvements on
held-out environments indicating cross-environment transfer. A useful
similarity signal should assign higher similarity to environments that
exhibit larger reward improvements. We present results with WebShop
([Yao et al., 2022](#bib.bib1)) as the source environment in the main
text and repeat the analysis with DBBench ([Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34)) as the source in App.
[C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Additional Cross-Environment Transfer Analysis ‣ Appendix C Additional empirical analysis ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation").
For the WebShop setting, we evaluate on WebShop, DBBench, ALFWorld
([Shridhar et al., 2021](#bib.bib2)), and AppWorld ([Trivedi et al.,
2024](#bib.bib3)), covering shopping, database interaction, household
object manipulation, and application-based tasks, respectively. Detailed
environment descriptions are provided in App.
[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Training Datasets and Environments ‣ Appendix B Training Details ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation").

Every $`25`$ training steps up to step $`500`$, we freeze the policy and
collect $`512`$ fresh WebShop training rollouts ($`64`$ prompts
$`\times`$ $`8`$ samples), together with four rollouts per validation
prompt from each environment. For this analysis, we use a fixed set of
up to 200 validation prompts per environment across all checkpoints,
retaining the full set when fewer than 200 are available. Each rollout
is annotated with its corresponding rubrics. For every
checkpoint–environment pair $`(t,e)`$, we measure the reward improvement
$`\Delta_{t}(e)=\bar{r}_{t}(e)-\bar{b}_{e},`$ where $`\bar{r}_{t}(e)`$
is the mean validation reward in environment $`e`$ at checkpoint $`t`$,
and $`\bar{b}_{e}`$ is the mean reward at the first measured checkpoint.
We exclude the first checkpoint because $`\Delta_{t_{0}}(e)=0`$ by
construction. We compare this reward-improvement signal against two
alignment signals: the cosine similarity between training and validation
rubric profiles and, as a baseline, the cosine similarity between the
corresponding training and validation gradients.

Rubric-based signals. Let $`\mathcal{R}=\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\}`$ denote
the rubric vocabulary. For each rollout $`\tau`$, let $`c_{r}(\tau)`$
denote the judge-assigned count of rubric $`r`$, with zero assigned when
$`r`$ is absent. For any collection of rollouts $`\mathcal{A}`$, define
its _rubric profile_ as the count vector:
$`\mathbf{q}(\mathcal{A})=\left(\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{A}}c_{r_{1}}(\tau),\ldots,\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{A}}c_{r_{m}}(\tau)\right)^{\!\top}`$.
Let $`\mathcal{T}_{t}`$ denote the WebShop training rollouts at step
$`t`$ and $`\mathcal{V}_{t}(e)`$ the validation rollouts from
environment $`e`$. We define rubric similarity as
$`s_{t}^{\mathrm{rubric}}(e)=\mathrm{sim}\!\left(\mathbf{q}(\mathcal{T}_{t}),\mathbf{q}(\mathcal{V}_{t}(e))\right),`$
where $`\mathrm{sim}`$ denotes cosine similarity, defined as zero when
either profile is zero. Because cosine similarity is invariant to
positive scaling, this signal captures the relative composition of
tagged behaviours rather than the number of aggregated rollouts. We
additionally compute positive-only and negative-only profiles using the
same rubric coordinates, including $`c_{r}(\tau)`$ only when the
corresponding role is demonstrate or violate, respectively, and
assigning zero otherwise. Skill-level profiles aggregate counts over
rubrics belonging to the same skill. Tab.

### 2.4 Rubrics for Online Data Selection

The environment-level analysis motivates using fine-grained rubric
counts from both positive and negative rubrics as a shared
representation. A fixed vocabulary shared across environments makes
these profiles directly comparable across environments and training
steps. We now apply this representation to online prompt-group
selection.

Selection. For each candidate prompt group $`g\in\mathcal{B}_{t}`$, we
aggregate the rubric counts of its $`n`$ rollouts into a group profile
$`\mathbf{q}(g)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{r_{1}}(\tau_{g,i}),\ldots,\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{r_{m}}(\tau_{g,i})\right)^{\!\top}.`$
For the candidate pool, we use the shorthand
$`\mathbf{q}(\mathcal{B}_{t})=\sum_{g\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}\mathbf{q}(g),`$
which summarizes all candidate rollouts at step $`t`$. Starting from
$`\mathcal{S}=\varnothing`$, we select groups greedily in a round-robin
manner across environments. On each environment’s turn, we choose the
highest-scoring unselected candidate using an MMR-inspired
relevance–redundancy score ([Goldstein and Carbonell,
1998](#bib.bib28)):

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\operatorname{score}(g\mid\mathcal{S})=\underbrace{\mathrm{sim}\!\left(\mathbf{q}(g),\mathbf{q}(\mathcal{B}_{t})\right)}_{\text{relevance}}-\underbrace{\mathrm{sim}\!\left(\mathbf{q}(g),\textstyle\sum_{g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{q}(g^{\prime})\right)}_{\text{redundancy}}.
``` |  | (1) |

We add the chosen group to $`\mathcal{S}`$ and continue until
$`|\mathcal{S}|=k`$, yielding $`\mathcal{S}_{t}=\mathcal{S}`$. The
relevance term favours groups aligned with the overall rubric profile of
the mixed-environment batch, while the redundancy term penalizes overlap
with the selected aggregate. Under round-robin selection, choosing a
group in one environment changes the redundancy scores for subsequent
choices in others, potentially favouring complementary behaviours as
selection process proceeds, such as environment-specific ones. Fixed
quotas preserve the baseline environment mixture. A similar form is
common in online batch selection for supervised fine-tuning ([Zou et
al., 2026](#bib.bib24)) and RL ([Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)), though
typically over embeddings rather than rubrics.

Rubric selection and reward variance capture different properties.
Rubric selection does not implicitly act as a reward-variance filter.
Over a $`1000`$-step run on three environments, selected groups have
mean within-group reward variance $`0.0557`$, compared with $`0.0507`$
in the candidate pool ($`1.10\times`$), while a variance-targeted
selector reaches approximately $`2\times`$ in this run. The two signals
therefore capture different properties: reward variance indicates
whether a group offers within-group reward contrast, whereas rubric
overlap describes which behaviours it exercises.

Auxiliary supervision for groups with identical rewards. Across a
$`1000`$-step run on three environments, $`62.9\%`$ of candidate groups
have zero within-group reward variance, with a similar fraction among
rubric-selected groups. Rubric-based selection therefore retains many
groups whose identical rewards provide no within-group contrast to
distinguish which behaviours should be reinforced. We then reuse the
same rubric annotations for self-distillation to provide complementary
token-level supervision on the selected rollouts.

## 3 Rubrics For Distillation

#### On-policy self-distillation.

Distillation trains a student to match a teacher’s output distribution
rather than a hard label, providing a dense per-token target in place of
a scalar reward. *On-policy* distillation applies this target to the
student’s own rollouts, correcting the policy on states it actually
visits ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib30)). In *self*-distillation,
teacher and student generally share parameters but differ in
conditioning: the teacher is the same policy given privileged context
$`c`$ unavailable to the student ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)).
Thus, no separate teacher is trained, and supervision requires only an
additional forward pass. For a completion $`y`$ generated from prompt
$`z`$, self-distillation conditions the teacher on an additional
privileged context $`c`$ while leaving the student’s input unchanged.
Following [Wang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib31), we use

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SD}}=\mathbb{E}[\rho_{j}k_{3}(\ell_{j})],\qquad\ell_{j}=\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{j}\mid z,y_{<j})-\log\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(y_{j}\mid z\oplus c,y_{<j}),\qquad k_{3}(\ell)=e^{-\ell}-1+\ell,
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\bar{\theta}`$ denotes the parameter values used for the teacher
forward pass, whose log-probabilities are treated as constants when
differentiating $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SD}}`$.
$`\rho_{j}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{j}\mid z,y_{<j})}{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{j}\mid z,y_{<j})]}`$
is the importance ratio between the current student and the rollout
policy, where $`sg`$ denotes stop gradient. Crucially, this loss
provides dense token-level supervision independently of the GRPO-based
advantage coefficients. The overall policy update minimizes
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SD}}`$, where
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}`$ follows the AgentRL training objective
([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)) and $`\lambda`$ is the
self-distillation coefficient. Both losses are evaluated on the selected
rollout groups $`\mathcal{S}`$, with token-level terms restricted to
generated tokens. Expansion of $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}`$ and
hyperparameters are given in App.
[B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Training Loss ‣ Appendix B Training Details ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation").

Positive rubrics distill; negative rubrics steer. The rubrics already
tagged for batch selection also provide the privileged context $`c`$,
requiring no additional effort. For each rollout, we take the
deduplicated union of its top-$`3`$ ($`3`$ highest-count rubrics)
positive rubrics and the top-$`3`$ positive rubrics of its group, and
render their descriptions as a privileged context for the teacher.
Groups without positive rubrics may receive no rubric-derived teacher
context (App.
[E.6](#A5.SS6 "E.6 Does every group with identical rewards receive positive-rubric supervision? ‣ Appendix E FAQ ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")).
Distillation then trains the student to reproduce the desired behaviour
without the privileged context $`c`$. The choice to include only
positive rubrics in the distillation context is motivated by prior work
and supported by our ablation. Prior work similarly constructs
rubric-conditioned teachers from the best rollouts ([Xia et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib32)) or compress rollouts into separate contextual
lessons ([Xia et al., 2026b](#bib.bib33)). Mixing positive and negative
material is not consistently beneficial, with its effect depending on
the difficulty and comprehensibility of the context ([Armandpour et al.,
2026](#bib.bib21)). Our ablation in App.
[C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Role Filter in the Teacher Context ‣ Appendix C Additional empirical analysis ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")
likewise shows that filling context slots with both positive and
negative rubrics degrades overall performance.

Nevertheless, negative rubrics contain valuable information,
particularly early in training. On ALFWorld, for example,
negative-tagged rollouts outnumber positive-tagged rollouts by more than
$`10\times`$ at step $`10`$, but fall below parity around step $`330`$.
We use negative rubrics to steer the next step’s rollouts rather than to
condition the teacher. At the end of each step, we aggregate tags over
all candidate rollouts from each environment and compare the numbers
containing at least one negative rubric and at least one positive
rubric. If violations are more prevalent, we prepend that environment’s
top-$`3`$ negative rubrics to the next step that environment’s prompts
as an “anti-patterns” block. When this steering is active, $`z`$ in Eq.
[2](#S3.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ On-policy self-distillation. ‣ 3 Rubrics For Distillation ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")
denotes the resulting steered prompt. The same $`z`$ is provided to both
student and teacher, while only the positive-rubric context $`c`$ is
privileged to the teacher. Overall, negative rubrics steer how rollouts
are sampled, whereas positive rubrics provide the self-distillation
target. Negative-rubric injection is used only during training.
Validation rollouts use the original task prompts, without injected
rubrics or privileged teacher context. Combining selection in Sec.
[2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 Rubrics for Online Data Selection ‣ 2 Rubrics For Data Selection ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")
and the distillation here, we propose our approach RISED: RubrIcs for
agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation, as the full
algorithm summarized in Alg.
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ B.4 Algorithm Pseudo-code ‣ Appendix B Training Details ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")
in Appendix.

## 4 Experiments and Results

### 4.1 Experimental Setting

We build on AgentRL ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)), which provides a
unified function-call interface for multi-turn interaction. We train
jointly on three AgentBench environments ([Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib34)): ALFWorld, WebShop, and DBBench. The three differ in
action space, reward mechanics, and rollout length, which is what makes
their interaction non-trivial. An episode is capped at $`20`$
interaction turns and $`8192`$ total tokens, and an unfinished episode
receives $`-0.2`$. We use GRPO with a batch of $`64`$ prompts and
$`n=8`$ rollouts per prompt. At each step, the rollout buffer generates
$`128`$ prompt groups, from which we select $`64`$ (Sec.
[2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 Rubrics for Online Data Selection ‣ 2 Rubrics For Data Selection ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")).
Across all runs, we follow the AgentRL training protocol, including
environment-wise advantage normalization and stale-policy rollout
generation.

We evaluate validation pass rate every $`10`$ training steps on fixed
held-out sets, sampling $`4`$ rollouts for each validation prompt. For
each environment, we report the mean pass rate (binary success
indicator) across prompts and rollouts. Overall performance is the
unweighted mean of the three environment-level pass rates. To evaluate
effectiveness across model backbones, we train Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (use
Qwen2.5-3B later for simplicity) on four nodes with eight H100 GPUs each
and Qwen3-4B without thinking on eight B200 GPUs. Methods requiring
rubric annotations, including RISED, its component ablations, and
distillation baselines, additionally use a dedicated eight-GPU server
for Qwen3-32B judge inference. We report main results as the mean and
standard deviation over multiple runs. Additional ablation experiments
use a single seed unless otherwise stated. Full optimization and rollout
hyperparameters are given in App.
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Training Details ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation").

### 4.2 Baselines

We compare against five baselines. GRPO-64 uniformly samples $`64`$
prompts and generates $`8`$ rollouts per prompt, using all $`512`$
rollouts per step without selection. GRPO-128 doubles the prompt batch
size to $`128`$ and updates on all $`1024`$ rollouts. Like RISED, Global
Variance and Round-Robin Variance generate $`128`$ candidate groups and
select $`64`$ for training based on within-group reward variance. Reward
variance provides an inexpensive proxy for a GRPO learning signal:
groups with identical rewards have zero variance. For binary rewards,
variance induces the same ordering as mean absolute group-standardized
advantage. This motivates our variance-based selectors and relates them
to DAPO’s filtering of zero-variance groups ([Yu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15)). Global Variance selects the highest-variance groups
across the candidate pool, whereas Round-Robin Variance ranks groups
within each environment and interleaves selection across environments,
matching RISED’s environment scheduling. Finally, as a
distillation-oriented baseline, All-Rubric SD adapts [Bablani et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib35) to our agentic RL setting by retaining GRPO and
conditioning the teacher on all rubrics. Because our environments do not
provide predefined task rubrics, these annotations come from our fixed
vocabulary. It uses 64 prompt groups per update, without selection or
negative-rubric guidance.

## 6 Related Work

Traditional online data selection prioritizes training examples using
loss ([Loshchilov and Hutter, 2015](#bib.bib22)), gradient-based
importance estimates ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib37); [Li et al.,
2025](#bib.bib40)) or estimated reducible loss ([Mindermann et al.,
2022](#bib.bib23)). RL online selection methods prioritize training
examples using reward variability or policy-dependent difficulty. DAPO
filters zero-variance groups and replenishes the training batch ([Yu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib15)), while VCRL combines variance-based filtering
with replay ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib39)). To avoid generating
rollouts for uninformative prompts, PCL and DOTS predict
policy-dependent difficulty and favor intermediate-difficulty examples
([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib46); [Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib43)).
InSight combines a reward-variance term with difficulty weighting and
information gain from historical outcomes ([Zhou et al.,
2026](#bib.bib44)). For binary rewards, success rates closer to $`0.5`$
correspond to higher reward variance, connecting the
intermediate-difficulty preference of PCL and DOTS to VCRL’s
variance-based filtering and InSight’s variance component. These
connections motivate our variance baselines as representative
implementations of the shared preference for reward variability. We
instantiate this principle using observed rollout rewards to select a
fixed number of already-generated groups, matching RISED’s
candidate-generation budget and selection size. CROPI periodically
selects training subsets using validation-guided influence estimates
computed from off-policy rollouts and projected gradients ([Zhu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib38)). Its reliance on validation data for selection
places it outside our comparison protocol, which uses neither
validation-guided scoring nor per-group gradient computation. RISED
instead uses rubric profiles of current rollouts to jointly select
prompt groups for coverage of the mixed-environment batch while limiting
redundancy across environments. Further discussion of curriculum,
rubrics and distillation appears in App.
[D](#A4 "Appendix D Additional Literature Review ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation").

## 7 Conclusion

We introduced RISED, a rubric-guided framework that combines online data
selection with self-distillation for multi-environment agent training.
By representing rollouts through a predefined and shared vocabulary
across environments, RISED selects groups that balance cross-environment
relevance and behavioral diversity, while rubric-conditioned
self-distillation provides dense supervision even for groups with zero
variance in rewards. Across two model backbones on multiple agent
environments, RISED consistently improves over competitive baselines and
rubric-level analyses provide an interpretable view of how behaviors
evolve during training. A limitation is that rubric tagging and teacher
inference add overhead. Future work shall improve efficiency and scale
to larger policies and more environments.

## Appendix A Rubrics Construction and Tagging Details

### A.1 Further Details on Vocabulary Construction

We provide additional details on vocabulary construction below. We take
rollouts from one warmup training run with model Qwen-2.5-3B-instruct at
step $`100`$ and ask Gemini-2.5-pro for rubric names. The warmup model
is trained on 4 environments, with details of training data as described
in App.
[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Training Datasets and Environments ‣ Appendix B Training Details ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation").
The unique rubric names yield roughly $`1{,}150`$. These are embedded
with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and grouped by clustering on cosine distance
(threshold $`0.5`$, average linkage) into about $`194`$ clusters, each
represented by its centroid-closest member. Skills are introduced
afterwards. We present the resulting clusters to the judge in separate
batches to keep the prompt within the context limit. For each batch, the
judge assigns a canonical rubric name, merges clusters that describe the
same behaviour, and groups the resulting rubrics under higher-level
skill headings. Because the batches are processed independently, the
same capability can be named twice, so a second clustering pass over
skill-name embeddings (cosine distance threshold $`0.5`$) merges the
duplicates. Skills are therefore *discovered* from the rubrics rather
than imposed on them. Overall, this yields an initial vocabulary of
$`59`$ rubrics across $`11`$ skills on 4 environments.

Because the initial vocabulary is extracted from rollouts of a weak
warm-up policy, it provides substantially better coverage of failure
modes than of successful behaviours. We therefore augment it using two
sources for each environment: $`100`$ warm-up rollouts from
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and $`100`$ teacher rollouts generated by
Claude-Opus-4.6. The former primarily expose failure behaviours, whereas
the latter provide successful behaviours that the weak policy rarely
exhibits. Each rollout is processed twice by Gemini-2.5-pro: once using
the current vocabulary as a closed taxonomy and once using free-form
rubric extraction. The free-form pass identifies behaviours missing from
the existing vocabulary. Across these rollouts, it produces $`1{,}565`$
distinct rubric names, which we reduce to $`60`$ semantic clusters. We
admit clusters that have no counterpart in the existing vocabulary. We
generate descriptions via Gemini-2.5-pro for both the original and newly
added rubrics using three representative annotated rollouts per rubric.
Each description summarizes the behaviour and can include short
abstracted examples of environment-specific actions. This procedure
yields a final vocabulary of $`82`$ rubrics grouped into $`11`$ skills
across four environments.³³ 3 In the main experiments, we use data from
three environments. After removing the rubrics and skill specific to the
excluded environment, the resulting vocabulary contains $`59`$ rubrics
grouped into $`10`$ skills. Rubric descriptions are not used for tagging
or sample selection; they are used for teacher conditioning and
training-time rollout guidance(Sec.
[3](#S3 "3 Rubrics For Distillation ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")).
Note that all rollouts used for vocabulary construction and augmentation
are generated from training-split prompts; validation examples are
excluded.

Free-form extraction ([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Rezaei et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19)) is useful for vocabulary construction, but
independently generated names require semantic alignment before rollouts
can be compared. In preliminary experiments, clustering free-form
annotations yielded between 25 and 44 skills across checkpoints, making
comparisons across steps and environments difficult. This motivated
establishing a shared vocabulary across environments for primary
tagging.

### A.3 Main Prompts Used for Rubric Generation

Our rubric-extraction prompt is inspired by the skill-distillation
prompts in SkillRL ([Xia et al., 2026b](#bib.bib33)), which summarize
agent experiences into concise, transferable behavioural principles.
Initially, directly extracting positive rubrics from successful rollouts
proved difficult. Adapting SkillRL’s skill-generation prompt made these
rubrics easier to summarize: our prompt first traces backward from the
goal-achieving action to identify its prerequisite steps, removes
irrelevant actions, and abstracts the resulting sequence into a
generalizable planning pattern [Xia et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib33). It then
extracts one to three positive rubrics describing observable correct
behaviours. For negative rubrics, a rollout may contain many distinct
erroneous behaviours, so we instruct the LLM to count their occurrences
and report the two to four most frequent patterns. Partially successful
rollouts undergo both procedures to capture demonstrated and violated
rubrics.

Both prompts, one used for open ended rubrics generation (Sec.
[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Rubric Construction ‣ 2 Rubrics For Data Selection ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation"))
and one used to tag rollouts (Sec.
[2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 Rubrics for Online Data Selection ‣ 2 Rubrics For Data Selection ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")),
are used as system messages. The user message is identical in both
cases:

> ENVIRONMENT: {task_name}
> REWARD: {reward}
> TURNS: {num_turns}
> TASK GOAL:
> {task_goal}
> rollout:
> {conversation}

Analyze a single RL training rollout from an agent operating in

interactive environments (ALFWorld, WebShop, DBBench, AppWorld).

Use a DIFFERENT analysis strategy depending on the rollout outcome.

=======================================================================

IF FULL SUCCESS (reward = 1.0)

=======================================================================

\*\*Phase 1: The Refinement Algorithm (Strictly Backward Logic)\*\*

 1. Start Point: Identify the Last Successful Step (the step that
achieved

the goal). Call this Current_Step.

2. Recursive Step (The Loop):

\* Analyze Dependency: Determine the strict Required Precondition for

Current_Step. Ask: "What specific state, information, or object

availability was necessary for this action to occur?"

\* Scan Backwards: Traverse the log in reverse order from Current_Step.

\* Find Nearest Enabler: Identify the nearest preceding step

(Preceding_Step) that explicitly generated this Precondition.

\* Prune Noise: Mark all steps logically located between Preceding_Step

and Current_Step as NOISE (irrelevant actions) and exclude them.

### A.4 Further Detail on Rubric Tagging.

We implement rubric tagging using an asynchronous primary path and a
retry path. The primary prompt always contains the predefined vocabulary
and successfully processes approximately 98% of rollouts. Both paths use
a tagging template that prioritizes supplied rubric names but permits
additional names for uncovered behaviours. Such discovered names are
rare, accounting for less than 1% of emitted rubric names. To reduce
retry overhead, the retry path uses a shorter candidate list refreshed
every ten steps from frequently observed rubric names in the preceding
ten steps; this list may include discovered names. These updates affect
only the retry candidate list, not the vocabulary supplied to the
primary path. Before constructing selection profiles, returned names are
normalized and mapped to predefined rubric identifiers using
edit-distance matching. Teacher conditioning and rollout guidance use
the predefined descriptions.

### B.3 Training Loss

We follow the GRPO formulation and training setup of AgentRL ([Zhang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). For each selected group
$`g=(\tau_{g,i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{t}`$, let $`r_{g,i}`$ be the
rollout reward and define the group-relative advantage

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{A}_{g,i}=\frac{r_{g,i}-\operatorname{mean}_{j}r_{g,j}}{\operatorname{std}_{j}r_{g,j}+\epsilon_{A}}.
``` |  |

Following AgentRL, the group-relative advantages are subsequently
normalized over generated tokens within each environment. Let
$`\tilde{A}_{g,i}`$ denote the resulting advantages used for
optimization. For exposition, we present a compact group-indexed form of
the GRPO objective; details of token-level loss computation and
normalization can be found in the AgentRL implementation. We use the
same GRPO hyperparameters as AgentRL, with backbone-specific settings
reported in Tab.
[6](#A2.T6 "Table 6 ‣ B.2 Training Hyperparameters ‣ Appendix B Training Details ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation").

Let

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{g,i}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{g,i}\mid z_{g})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{g,i}\mid z_{g})}.
``` |  |

Using the loss-minimization convention, the GRPO objective is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{g\in\mathcal{S}_{t}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\!\left(\rho_{g,i}(\theta)\tilde{A}_{g,i},\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\rho_{g,i}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)\tilde{A}_{g,i}\right)\right]+\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\theta_{\mathrm{old}}`$ denotes the rollout policy and
$`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is the frozen reference policy.

## Appendix D Additional Literature Review

#### Multi-environment agentic RL.

Joint training with multiple environments has been found to have the
potential of matching environment-specific specialists ([Cheng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib7); [Wang and Ammanabrolu, 2025](#bib.bib11); [Zhang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). Cross-domain studies also report positive
transfer between tasks: In a five-domain comparison, mixed RL achieves
performance comparable to separately trained and merged models at lower
total training cost ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib6)). For interactive
agents, [Liu et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib56) identify state information
richness and planning complexity as important correlates of cross-domain
generalization. Joint training on a mixture is therefore raising more
and more attention. Training strategies include sequential domain stages
([NVIDIA, 2025a](#bib.bib10); [Ren et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)) and joint
optimization over mixed-environment batches ([NVIDIA, 2025b](#bib.bib9);
[Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). Nemotron-Cascade 2 orders stages to
reduce interference, but notes that suitable orderings depend on the
model’s learning dynamics and that capability drift remains ([Yang et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib8)). We follow the mixed-batch approach, holding
environment proportions fixed.

#### Curriculum and domain allocation.

RL-related curriculum methods control sample difficulty, category or
domain allocation, and environment membership. Nemotron 3 Nano schedules
sample difficulty using checkpoint-based pass-rate estimates, which are
refreshed when training progress plateaus ([NVIDIA, 2025b](#bib.bib9)).
Nemotron-Cascade 2 orders training stages to mitigate inter-domain
interference ([Yang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib8)). MoDoMoDo optimizes
domain mixtures using predicted training outcomes from pilot runs
([Liang et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)). Online adaptive methods include
SEC’s bandit-based category sampling ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)),
RuleReasoner’s reward-history-based domain reweighting ([Liu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib45)), and TAC’s use of gradient alignment to select the
domain supplying each training batch ([Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14)).
These methods primarily determine category or domain allocation, stage
ordering, or sample difficulty. Our focus is complementary: selecting
prompt groups from current mixed-environment rollouts under a prescribed
environment allocation, with selection accounting for both the batch’s
behavioural composition and previously selected groups. Closest to our
rubric profiles as selection reference, AES aggregates
trajectory-derived abilities into environment profiles and selects
environment subsets using coverage, redundancy, and gradient conflict
([Zhu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib12)), but note that they select on
environment level but not prompt groups level. In our AgentRL-based
implementation ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)), we fix the candidate
environment mixture and RISED’s round-robin selection schedule to focus
on the problem of prompt-group selection. Directly applying domain
schedulers or environment-subset selection would change our experimental
setting. We acknowledge that jointly optimizing these decisions remains
a parallel but important direction which we currently do not focus on.
Global Variance nevertheless allows selected environment proportions to
vary with observed reward variance, providing a comparison with adaptive
batch composition within the fixed candidate pool.

#### Rubrics as reward.

Rubrics decompose response quality into interpretable criteria and
provide reward supervision for open-ended generation ([Gunjal et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18); [Jia et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)), agentic tool use
([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)), and image captioning ([Huang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25)). Later work improves both the criteria and their use
in optimization: RLCER evolves rubric criteria during training ([Sheng
et al., 2026](#bib.bib55)), POW3R adapts criterion weights according to
rollout variation ([Tyagi et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)), and CriPO uses
self-distillation and advantage correction to recover learning signals
for unexplored or suppressed criteria ([Xia et al., 2026a](#bib.bib32)).
These methods primarily construct or adapt rubric-based rewards, while
CriPO additionally improves optimization under those rewards through
self-distillation and advantage correction. RISED retains
environment-provided rewards and instead uses behavioural rubrics for
prompt-group selection, teacher conditioning, and rollout guidance. Our
focus is therefore how behavioural rubrics organize training data and
provide auxiliary supervision, rather than how rubric scores construct
the reward.

#### On-policy distillation and its selective variants.

Distillation trains a student to match a teacher’s output distribution,
providing dense token-level supervision. On-policy distillation applies
this supervision to student-generated rollouts, reducing the
distribution mismatch of off-policy imitation ([Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib30)). Recent on-policy self-distillation methods construct
a teacher from the student model, conditioning it on privileged
information or feedback to provide dense supervision on
student-generated rollouts ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib41); [Zhao
et al., 2026](#bib.bib42)). [Li et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib53) link
successful distillation to teacher–student compatibility and additional
teacher capabilities, with learning concentrated on shared
high-probability tokens. Several variants refine supervision through
uncertainty and disagreement ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib48)), token
teachability ([Wang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib47)), trajectory filtering
and token reweighting ([Li et al., 2026c](#bib.bib49)), or trust-region
restrictions on teacher supervision ([Xing et al., 2026](#bib.bib52)).
For multi-turn agents, Skill-SD constructs teacher context from
rollout-derived skill summaries and derives the importance-weighted
reverse-KL objective ([Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib31)); SDAR uses
token-level gating to stabilize joint RL and self-distillation ([Lu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib50)), while SERL studies which environmental feedback
to distill and where to apply it ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib54)). We
adopt Skill-SD’s objective, but construct privileged context from a
shared cross-environment rubric vocabulary whose annotations also guide
prompt-group selection. Our contribution is to use a shared behavioural
rubric vocabulary jointly for prompt-group selection and
self-distillation. The component ablations (Sec.
[5](#S5 "5 Ablations and Further Analysis ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation"))
show that combining the two yields the strongest overall performance,
supporting their complementary roles. Token-selection rules and
alternative distillation losses address complementary optimization
choices. Closest to our distillation component, RuPI supplies rubric
criteria, including positive and negative criteria, as privileged
teacher context ([Bablani et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)). Our All-Rubric SD
baseline adapts this approach to agentic RL by retaining GRPO and using
rollout-derived rubrics. RISED differs by combining distillation with
prompt-group selection, while assigning positive rubrics to teacher
conditioning and negative rubrics to rollout guidance.

## Appendix E FAQ

### E.1 Why use a fixed rubric vocabulary?

Our goal requires comparing rubric profiles across environments and
training steps. Dynamic generation of free-form rubric assigns
semantically equivalent behaviours different names and therefore
requires repeated clustering or alignment before profiles can be
compared. In preliminary experiments, clustering free-form annotations
yielded between 25 and 44 skills across checkpoints, making comparisons
across steps and environments difficult. The verification of each step’s
new rubrics can also be difficult. A fixed vocabulary instead provides a
stable coordinate system for online comparison.

### E.2 Will the warm-up process in building vocabulary introduce additional time cost?

The warm-up run, taking only $`1/10`$ steps of full training, introduces
a one-time preprocessing cost that is amortized across subsequent
training runs:

1.  1.
    Reuse across backbones. The warm-up policy provides failure
    trajectories used to discover negative rubrics. Once constructed,
    the resulting vocabulary is reused across training runs and model
    backbones, so a separate warm-up run is not required for each
    backbone.
2.  2.
    Reduced recurring overhead. A predefined vocabulary reduces online
    tagging to label selection. In contrast, free-form extraction would
    require repeated name alignment or clustering to reconcile
    semantically equivalent labels and preserve comparability across
    rollouts and training steps.
3.  3.
    Offline use of larger models. Rubric consolidation and description
    generation are performed offline using a larger model, while online
    tagging uses a locally served judge. This avoids repeated
    large-model inference during training and reduces throughput
    overhead.

### E.3 Why evaluate rubric similarity at the environment level?

We use environment-level analysis as a computationally tractable
diagnostic of whether rubric similarity tracks reward improvements
during single-environment training. Directly estimating the marginal
benefit of individual prompt groups would require more expensive
controlled updates and evaluations. The aggregate analysis motivates the
predefined and shared rubric representation; it does not directly
estimate each group’s contribution. We assess the practical utility of
prompt-group selection through the main experiment and the end-to-end
component ablations.

### E.4 Does reward-conditioned tagging affect the interpretation of rubric similarity?

The judge receives rollout rewards and uses outcome-dependent
instructions to identify desired and undesired behaviours. The resulting
rubric profiles therefore reflect both behavioural content and outcome
composition. The gradient baseline also incorporates rewards through its
objective. Our comparison evaluates how well these two reward-informed
representations track observed environment-level reward improvements.
The stronger correlations support rubric similarity as an empirical
diagnostic in this setting, but do not establish that its association is
independent of reward composition.

### E.5 Are negative rubrics injected during validation?

No. Negative rubrics guide training rollouts only. All reported
validation results use the original task prompts without rubric
injection. The role-filter ablation in App.
[C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Role Filter in the Teacher Context ‣ Appendix C Additional empirical analysis ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")
and main results in Sec.
[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments and Results ‣ RISED: RubrIcs for agentic multi-environment Selection and sElf-Distillation")
evaluate these training choices under the same unguided validation
protocol. Its results show that the benefits of training-time guidance
persist when the guidance is removed at evaluation.

### E.6 Does every group with identical rewards receive positive-rubric supervision?

Not necessarily. Positive-rubric self-distillation can provide
additional supervision for groups with identical rewards when positive
rubrics are available within the group. Groups without positive rubrics
lack this privileged behavioral context, and the distillation term has
zero loss and gradients when teacher and student parameters and inputs
coincide. However, negative rubrics can still provide a complementary
mechanism by guiding subsequent rollout generation.

### E.7 Why not evaluate larger policy backbones?

Agent RL is already computationally expensive at the 3–4B scale. For
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, each run comprises $`1000`$ training steps on
$`32`$ H100 GPUs. Based on the measured average step times, even GRPO-64
requires approximately $`1.8`$ days per run ($`155.5`$ s/step), while
RISED requires approximately $`2.9`$ days ($`252.9`$ s/step). Validation
every $`10`$ steps further increases the total runtime. Repeating
training across baselines, ablations, and seeds incurs substantial
computational cost. We therefore prioritize controlled comparisons
across provided two backbones.
````

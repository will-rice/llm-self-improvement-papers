###### Abstract

Effective collaboration among large language model (LLM) agents in
multi-agent systems requires sharing relevant evidence, requesting
missing information, and incorporating teammates’ contributions into
decisions. Training these behaviors is challenging because final task
outcomes provide limited feedback on the value of individual messages.
We introduce CoSIR (Collaboration via Surprisal-based Information
Rewards), a post-training approach that uses a simple, verifiable
environment to teach collaboration behaviors that generalize to more
complex, unseen tasks. We propose a Bayesian evaluator that scores
shared information by its reduction in team surprisal, defined as the
sum of the negative log probabilities assigned to the correct option
given each agent’s available evidence. Requests are scored by the
potential reduction in the same quantity if the requested information
were truthfully provided. Separate rewards encourage agents to use the
received information in their private choices and calculations. We
post-train Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Gemma-3-4B-it through self-play and
evaluate transfer to seven unseen natural-language collaboration tasks.
CoSIR achieves the highest aggregate score, while its performance in
general tasks remains competitive. Behavioral analyses show increased
disclosure of private evidence and fewer judged failures to use
teammates’ input.

## 1 Introduction

Effective collaboration requires teams to turn information held by
individual members into evidence that informs a shared decision. Studies
of human collective intelligence suggest that team performance depends
on more than members’ individual abilities ([Woolley et al.,
2010](#bib.bib1)). For large language model (LLM) agents, however,
access to sufficient information at the team level does not guarantee
successful collaboration: a team with distributed evidence can perform
worse than a single agent given the same evidence in full ([Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)). Documented failures include withholding useful
findings, proceeding without clarification, and ignoring teammates’
input ([Cemri et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)). Agents may also abandon correct
answers in favor of an incorrect majority ([Weng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4)). These observations highlight the need for agents to
communicate, seek missing information, and leverage teammate input.

LLM post-training largely emphasizes instruction following ([Ouyang et
al., 2022](#bib.bib5); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)) and multi-turn
task completion ([Wang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib6)), but these objectives
do not explicitly distinguish how individual messages contribute to a
team’s decision. Recent work has begun to train collaboration more
directly. For example, MAPoRL rewards correct answers and corrective or
persuasive discussion ([Park et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)). We focus on an
essential question: how can agents be rewarded for providing or
requesting information that helps teammates identify the correct answer?
Such rewards require assessing the value of a message relative to what
its recipients already know. This is difficult in open-ended
natural-language tasks, where the relevant facts, their distribution
across agents, and their contributions to the final decision are not
readily verifiable.

We introduce CoSIR (Collaboration via Surprisal-based Information
Rewards), an approach that trains collaboration in simplified,
verifiable environments while preserving the information dependencies
underlying more complex tasks. The key is to retain the need to share
relevant evidence, request missing information, and integrate teammates’
contributions, while making the value of these behaviors computable. By
varying the available evidence and its distribution across agents, such
environments provide diverse opportunities to learn these decisions with
fine-grained, automatically computed feedback. The goal is to develop
transferable collaboration behaviors in a controlled setting and
evaluate whether they generalize to more complex, unseen
natural-language tasks.

To instantiate this approach, we construct a task in which agents
jointly select the option with the highest total rating across several
attributes. Each agent observes only part of a rating table, creating a
need to share known ratings and request missing information from
teammates. Because reported and requested ratings correspond to specific
table entries, we can identify the information exchanged, verify
reported values, and track the evidence available to each agent.

We use a Bayesian evaluator to estimate the probability that each option
is optimal given an agent’s initial briefing and received reports. The
complete table determines the true best option, but its hidden ratings
are not used to calculate these probabilities. We define team surprisal
as the sum, across agents, of the negative log probabilities assigned by
the evaluator to the correct option. A shared rating is valued by the
reduction in team surprisal it produces, while a request is valued by
the potential reduction if the requested rating were truthfully
provided. These quantities provide fine-grained training signals for
sharing and requesting useful information, with numerical rewards
computed from the table and information records rather than assigned by
a learned reward judge.

To encourage agents to use the information they receive, we complement
communication rewards with supervision of private answers, including
option choices and calculated totals. The choice score uses the
probability that the selected option is best given the agent’s available
information, while the calculation score checks whether the agent
correctly combines its known ratings. We use these rewards to post-train
Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)), and Gemma-3-4B-it
([Gemma Team, 2025](#bib.bib23)) through self-play reinforcement
learning.

We test the transfer ability on seven unseen tasks: HiddenBench ([Li et
al., 2026](#bib.bib2)), SILO-BENCH ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)),
SplitQA, MathChat ([Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)), BenchForm ([Weng et
al., 2025](#bib.bib4)), Einstein Puzzles ([Peng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16)), and Collaboration Tax ([Sun et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)). We introduce SplitQA to test whether models trained
to choose among options can combine facts held by different agents to
produce free-form answers. For each of the three models, CoSIR improves
all seven task scores over the starting checkpoint and achieves the
highest sum of scores among the compared methods. We also find that
behavioral analyses demonstrate increased sharing of private information
and better utilization of teammates’ input, with general performance
remaining close to initial checkpoints. Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  Scalable collaboration training. We construct a scalable training
  environment that preserves core collaboration behaviors with
  computable rewards.
- •
  Verifiable information rewards. We develop team-surprisal rewards for
  sharing and requesting information, alongside rewards for using it.
- •
  Cross-task generalization. We demonstrate transfer to seven unseen
  tasks across three LLM backbones while preserving general
  capabilities.

## 2 Related Work

#### LLM Multi-Agent Collaboration.

Existing work on LLM multi-agent collaboration largely focuses on how
agents interact at inference time. Theory-of-Mind methods help agents
reason about their teammates’ beliefs ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib8)),
while TalkHier organizes communication and answer refinement through a
hierarchical workflow ([Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9)). These methods
guide interactions among existing models. CoSIR trains the model itself,
using rewards for sharing useful information, requesting missing
ratings, and using received evidence. Our experiments compare this
training approach with ToM and TalkHier under matched agent counts and
public speaking-turn limits.

#### Post-Training for LLM Collaboration.

Recent studies train LLMs for multi-agent interaction. [Peng et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib16) use supervised fine-tuning on planning trajectories
to train information sharing and seeking, together with an
environment-based verifier that guides actions at inference time. MAPoRL
rewards correct answers and discussion ([Park et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)).
MAGRPO formulates collaboration as cooperative multi-agent reinforcement
learning ([Liu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib10)), while actor-critic methods
train agents for decentralized execution ([Liu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib11)). CoSIR makes the information contributed by a
statement or a potential reply an explicit reward signal. The
rating-table environment lets us calculate this signal from the
information available to each teammate.

#### Transfer from Synthetic Multi-Agent Environments.

Synthetic environments provide scalable opportunities for LLMs to learn
through interaction. SPIRAL uses self-play in zero-sum games to develop
reasoning abilities that transfer beyond the training games ([Liu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib12)). MARS extends this approach to cooperative and
competitive games, demonstrating transfer to unseen games and reasoning
tasks ([Yuan et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)). We build on the idea of
learning transferable capabilities from simple environments, but target
collaboration itself rather than general reasoning or game-playing
strategies. In our training task, the facts agents share or request
correspond to ratings in the table. This allows us to calculate how
receiving a rating changes the probability of the correct option and use
the result to train collaboration.

## 3 Method

We design CoSIR to train LLM agents to share, request, and use
information through team discussions. Our aim is for these behaviors to
transfer to unseen natural-language collaboration tasks. We build a
simple rating-table environment that generates tasks, gives each agent
part of the information, and manages the discussion. Each task asks a
team to choose the best option using ratings in a table. The environment
scores the agents’ messages and private answers. We use these scores to
update the model, then collect new discussions with the updated model.

### 3.1 A Task with Distributed Information

For each task, the environment generates a rating table for $`k`$
options, each described by $`m`$ attributes, and asks a team of $`n`$
agents to choose an option. The complete table
$`V\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{k\times m}`$ contains the ratings, where entry
$`V_{oa}`$ rates option $`o`$ on attribute $`a`$. The values $`-1`$,
$`0`$, and $`+1`$ mean poor, average, and good, respectively. Before
filtering, the entries are sampled independently and uniformly from
$`\{-1,0,+1\}`$. A best option $`y^{\star}`$ satisfies

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       y^{\star}\in\arg\max_{o\in\{1,\ldots,k\}}\sum_{a=1}^{m}V_{oa}.
       ```                                                             |     | (1) |

The maximizer need not be unique in general. In our generated tasks, we
discard tables with tied highest totals. The retained table $`V`$
remains fixed throughout the discussion.

Each agent $`i`$ receives an initial briefing containing a subset of
entries from $`V`$. Let
$`C_{i}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}\times\{1,\ldots,m\}`$ denote the observed
cell indices, and define $`B_{i}=\{((o,a),V_{oa}):(o,a)\in C_{i}\}`$ as
the observed cell–rating pairs. The sets $`C_{i}`$ may overlap, so some
ratings are available to multiple agents while others are known to only
one. For example, Agent 1 may know Option A’s safety rating while Agent
2 has not seen it. Agent 1 can share this rating, or Agent 2 can ask for
it.

We generate tasks under five settings, summarized in
[Table 4](#A1.T4 "In A.1 Ratings and briefings ‣ Appendix A Environment Construction ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards").
These settings specify the number of agents, which ratings each agent
receives, how many ratings are given to everyone, whether agents are
told which teammate holds a missing rating, and the number of discussion
rounds. Each setting generates many tasks, each with its own rating
table and briefings.

Agents take turns sending messages in natural language. A message is the
text an agent sends to its teammates on one turn. It may contain several
rating statements and a request for a missing rating. Each rating
statement reports a rating for one option and attribute. When received
by a teammate, it is recorded as a report. During and after the
discussion, we collect private answers containing an option choice and
totals computed from each agent’s available ratings. This allows us to
check whether an agent uses the information it has received.

#### Extracting ratings and requests.

For all three training models (Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B and Gemma-3-4B-it) ,
we parse responses using rules and the initial Qwen3-8B model, whose
parameters remain fixed. The parser extracts rating statements (option,
attribute, value, and stated source), reported totals, and requests (the
requested entry and any named teammate). The environment uses these
results to check statements and compute rewards.

### 3.2 Measuring the Value of Information

We evaluate information exchange by how it changes the probability that
an option is best. Let $`R_{i}`$ denote the rating statements received
by agent $`i`$, and define its information record as
$`K_{i}=(B_{i},R_{i})`$. To calculate these probabilities, we represent
the information in $`K_{i}`$ as a partial rating table $`E_{i}(K_{i})`$,
containing one rating for each available cell while leaving the
remaining cells unknown. Ratings in $`B_{i}`$ take precedence; for other
entries, we use the most recently received rating statement.

Let $`\Omega`$ be the set of complete rating tables with a unique best
option, and let $`\Omega_{y}=\{v\in\Omega:y^{\star}(v)=y\}`$. The
Bayesian evaluator uses a uniform prior $`p_{0}(v)=1/|\Omega|`$ over
$`v\in\Omega`$. Given $`K_{i}`$, it computes the posterior probability
that option $`y`$ is best:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
P(y\mid K_{i})=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\Omega_{y}}p_{0}(v)\prod_{(c,r)\in E_{i}(K_{i})}\mathbf{1}[v_{c}=r]}{\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\Omega}p_{0}(v)\prod_{(c,r)\in E_{i}(K_{i})}\mathbf{1}[v_{c}=r]}.
``` |  | (2) |

Here, $`v`$ denotes a possible complete rating table, and the indicator
functions retain tables consistent with the agent’s available ratings.
Received rating statements are used according to their reported values;
their correctness is checked separately when computing rewards. The
hidden entries of the true table $`V`$ are not used to calculate the
posterior. We compute these probabilities exactly using dynamic
programming;
[Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C Bayesian Evaluator ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
provides the details.

For each agent, surprisal measures how unlikely the correct option is
given its available information. Let $`K=(K_{1},\ldots,K_{n})`$ denote
the agents’ information records. We define team surprisal as

|     |                                                              |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Phi(K)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log_{2}\bar{P}(y^{\star}\mid K_{i}).
       ```                                                           |     | (3) |

Here, $`\bar{P}(y\mid K_{i})=\max\{P(y\mid K_{i}),10^{-4}\}`$ avoids
taking the logarithm of zero.

For a set $`x`$ of rating statements, we measure its information value
by the reduction in team surprisal if the other agents receive it:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta\Phi(x)=\Phi(K_{\mathrm{before}})-\Phi(K_{\mathrm{after}}).
``` |  | (4) |

A positive value means the information helps the team assign a higher
probability to the correct option. The value is measured in bits.

### 3.3 Rewards for Communication and Answers

We use reductions in team surprisal to evaluate information sharing and
requesting, and separately reward agents for using received information
in their private answers. The information values defined below form the
basis of our communication rewards, while
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Reward Computation ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
gives their complete computation, including reward allocation and
adjustments.

#### Sharing information.

We reward agents for sharing ratings that help their teammates identify
the correct option. For a message containing rating statements $`M`$, we
define its information value as $`I_{\mathrm{share}}(M)=\Delta\Phi(M)`$.
This value depends on what the listeners already know. For example,
suppose every agent already knows that Option B’s safety rating is
$`+1`$. Repeating this rating does not change any listener’s probability
of the correct option, so it provides no information reward.

Now suppose only Agent 1 knows that Option A’s safety rating is $`+1`$.
If receiving this rating increases the probability of the correct option
from $`1/4`$ to $`1/2`$ for both Agent 2 and Agent 3, each listener’s
surprisal decreases by one bit. The total reduction is two bits before
accounting for message delivery. A listener who already knows the rating
gains no additional information.

A message may contain multiple rating statements whose information
values are not additive. We therefore evaluate the message as a whole
and allocate its information value across individual statements.
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Reward Computation ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
provides the allocation details.

However, reducing team surprisal alone is not sufficient to earn a
positive reward. An agent could invent a rating that happens to increase
the probability of the correct option. We therefore require a statement
to be both correct and supported by the speaker’s initial briefing or a
received report. A correct guess without such evidence earns no positive
information reward, and faithfully repeating an incorrect report also
earns no positive information reward. Fabricated statements receive an
additional penalty.

Conversely, even a correct rating may temporarily increase team
surprisal. For example, the best option may have a poor rating on one
attribute but strong ratings on others. Sharing the poor rating first
could lower its probability while the other ratings remain unknown. We
therefore avoid negative information rewards for statements supported by
the speaker’s available information. Each rating statement also incurs a
small communication cost to discourage unnecessary repetition.

#### Requesting information.

Unlike sharing a rating, asking a question does not immediately provide
new information. We therefore evaluate a request by assuming that the
requested rating is truthfully provided and measuring the resulting
reduction in team surprisal, using the same measure as for sharing. Let
$`q`$ be a teammate holding the requested cell $`c`$, and let
$`x_{q,c}`$ denote a hypothetical reply containing its true rating. We
define the potential information value of the request as
$`I_{\mathrm{request}}(q,c)=[\Delta\Phi(x_{q,c})]_{+}`$, where
$`[z]_{+}=\max(z,0)`$.

Specifically, the request must concern a rating missing from the
requester’s initial briefing but known to a teammate. The environment
uses the true value from $`V`$ to construct the hypothetical reply. We
then calculate how receiving it would change the probability of the
correct option for every agent other than the replying teammate,
including the requester. The potential value is calculated when the
question is asked, without waiting for an actual reply.

For example, before Agent 1 shares Option A’s safety rating, Agent 2
could ask for it. If the truthful reply of $`+1`$ increases the
probability of the correct option from $`1/4`$ to $`1/2`$ for both Agent
2 and Agent 3, the request has a potential information value of two
bits. The final reward also accounts for previously received
information, repeated requests, and whether the holder has another
opportunity to speak.
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Reward Computation ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
provides these adjustments.

#### Using received information.

Sharing and requesting information are useful only if agents can also
use what they receive. We therefore evaluate two parts of each private
answer: the selected option and the calculated total ratings for all
options.

Let $`\hat{y}_{i}`$ be agent $`i`$’s selected option, $`\hat{s}_{io}`$
its reported total for option $`o`$, and $`s^{K}_{io}`$ the reference
total computed from its available ratings. Each table entry is counted
once, and unknown ratings are treated as zero. For a parseable choice,
the private-answer reward is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{i}^{\mathrm{answer}}=\bar{P}(\hat{y}_{i}\mid K_{i})+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{o=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}[\hat{s}_{io}=s^{K}_{io}].
``` |  | (5) |

The choice score uses the probability that the selected option is best
given the agent’s available information, rather than directly comparing
it with the true answer. The calculation score is the fraction of
options whose reported totals match the reference totals. Missing or
unreadable totals count as incorrect.

For example, suppose an agent initially does not know Option A’s safety
rating and then receives a report that it is $`+1`$. The evaluator uses
this report to update the option probabilities, while the reference
total for Option A increases by one. We evaluate the agent’s next
private answer using both the updated probabilities and totals. The two
scores thus encourage agents to use received information both when
choosing an option and when calculating its total rating.
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Private-Answer Evaluation ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
provides the complete scoring rules, including how conflicting reports
are handled.

### 3.4 Learning from Team Discussions

We use the sharing, requesting, and private-answer rewards to train LLMs
through self-play, where the same model plays all agents in a team. At
each speaking turn, we sample multiple candidate responses from the same
briefing and conversation, allowing us to compare different responses
under the same conditions. We score all candidates and randomly select
one to continue the discussion, while the others also contribute to
training. Private answers are sampled and scored separately. After
collecting discussions, we update the model and repeat the process with
the updated policy.

A message may contain useful ratings, repetitions, and questions, so we
assign rewards to the corresponding parts of the response rather than to
the entire message. Statement rewards provide direct credit to their
corresponding tokens, while request and private-answer scores are
converted into group-relative advantages. These signals guide updates to
corresponding tokens through a variant of PPO ([Schulman et al.,
2017](#bib.bib14)).

Early in training, the model may rarely generate useful questions. When
the evaluator identifies a request as the best available action, we add
a few demonstrations expressing that request. These examples are scored
alongside sampled responses and trained with a clipped imitation update,
but never selected to continue the discussion.

To help preserve general capabilities, we use a reference-model penalty
and train on responses generated by the initial checkpoint for general
prompts.

provides the sampling, token-level credit assignment, demonstration

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate transfer to unseen collaboration tasks, changes in agents’
communication, and retention of general capabilities.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Models and baselines.

We evaluate Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-4B ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)), and
Gemma-3-4B-it ([Gemma Team, 2025](#bib.bib23)). Base is the starting
checkpoint, and CoSIR is the checkpoint after collaboration training.
Both use the same task prompts and evaluation settings. Outcome-RL uses
the same self-play environment and model initialization as CoSIR, but
learns from the final team outcome rather than behavior-specific
information rewards. It retains the request demonstrations, while
outcome feedback is assigned to the sampled discussion trajectory. This
comparison tests the reward design within outcome-based multi-agent
reinforcement learning ([Liu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib10)). We also
evaluate Theory of Mind (ToM) ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib8)) and
TalkHier ([Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9)), which add reasoning or
revision steps at inference time. They use the same numbers of agents
and speaking turns, with additional model calls described in
[Appendix H](#A8 "Appendix H Evaluation Settings and Score Aggregation ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards").

#### Unseen collaboration tasks.

We evaluate on seven tasks outside the training environment. HiddenBench
([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)) requires decisions from private facts,
and SILO-BENCH ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)) requires computation
over data divided among agents. SplitQA asks agents to connect facts
from separate documents. MathChat ([Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib18))
evaluates interactive mathematical problem solving, while BenchForm
([Weng et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)) evaluates answers under peer influence.
Einstein Puzzles ([Peng et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) and Collaboration Tax
([Sun et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)) evaluate further forms of reasoning and
coordination involving distributed information or task responsibilities.
[Appendix H](#A8 "Appendix H Evaluation Settings and Score Aggregation ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
gives the protocols, generation settings, and scoring rules.

We construct SplitQA to test whether agents can use distributed evidence
to generate an answer. In HiddenBench, private facts support or rule out
the listed options. SplitQA instead uses HotpotQA questions ([Yang et
al., 2018](#bib.bib24)) whose answers require information from two
supporting paragraphs. We assign those paragraphs to different agents
and give distractors to the other two. Agents follow the HiddenBench
discussion protocol and then provide free-form answers.
[Appendix G](#A7 "Appendix G SplitQA Construction and Evaluation ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
describes the 70-question test set and its scoring.

### 4.2 Generalization Across Tasks and Models

[Table 1](#S4.T1 "In Reporting. ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
shows that CoSIR achieves the highest SUM for all three models,
exceeding the strongest alternative by 19.79, 22.95, and 24.86 points
for Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, and Gemma-3-4B-it, respectively. Each trained
model also achieves a higher mean score than Base on all seven tasks,
indicating that the gains are not driven by a single benchmark.

Inference-time methods perform better on some individual tasks. ToM
leads on SILO-BENCH with Qwen3-8B, while TalkHier leads on BenchForm
with all three models. However, both methods reduce SUM relative to Base
for Qwen3-4B and Gemma-3-4B-it, whereas CoSIR improves SUM across all
three models.

SplitQA further tests transfer beyond choosing among predefined options,
requiring agents to combine facts from different supporting paragraphs
to produce a free-form answer. CoSIR improves over Base by 2.50, 0.36,
and 8.21 points for Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, and Gemma-3-4B-it, respectively.
The improvement is small for Qwen3-4B, but the results suggest that the
learned behaviors can also help agents combine evidence across
documents.

#### Comparison with Outcome-RL.

Outcome-RL follows the same training procedure as CoSIR but rewards only
the final team outcome. Although it improves SUM over Base for all three
models, CoSIR exceeds it by 20.72, 22.95, and 24.86 points for Qwen3-8B,
Qwen3-4B, and Gemma-3-4B-it, respectively. CoSIR scores higher on all
seven tasks with Qwen3-8B and six tasks with each of the other models.
It ties Outcome-RL on MathChat with Qwen3-4B, while Outcome-RL performs
better on HiddenBench with Gemma-3-4B-it.

Unlike Outcome-RL, which evaluates only the final team result, CoSIR
rewards individual rating statements and requests, as well as choices
and calculations in private answers. Thus, responses leading to the same
final outcome can receive different feedback based on how they share and
use information. These results support the benefit of fine-grained
information rewards over final-outcome feedback within our self-play
training framework. We next examine how agents’ communication changes
after training.

### 4.3 How Collaboration Changes

Higher task scores do not describe how a discussion improves. We
therefore examine whether agents make their private information
available and respond to their teammates.
[Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.2 Generalization Across Tasks and Models ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
reports these behaviors before and after training.
[Appendix J](#A10 "Appendix J Behavioral Measures ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
defines the measures.

#### Making evidence available.

After training, agents share more private information across all three
models on HiddenBench. For Qwen3-8B, the disclosure rate rises from
68.5% to 74.3%, and the fraction of messages responding to teammates
increases from 59.4% to 65.8%. On SILO-BENCH, more agents share their
complete local data, while fewer messages report only an accumulated
result, making the underlying data available for verification. On
SplitQA, agents share their supporting facts more often, while MathChat
shows more messages attempting to identify or correct errors.

Agents also share information earlier. For Qwen3-8B, first-message
disclosure increases from 61.1% to 69.0% on HiddenBench and from 41.1%
to 51.1% on SplitQA
([Appendix J](#A10 "Appendix J Behavioral Measures ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")).
Unlike the any-round measures in
[Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.2 Generalization Across Tasks and Models ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards"),
these figures capture information shared early enough for teammates to
use in later turns.

#### Using teammates’ information.

Sharing information helps only if teammates use it. We therefore apply
the Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy (MAST) of [Cemri et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib3) to conversations from HiddenBench, SplitQA, and
MathChat.
[Table 3](#S4.T3 "In Comparison with Outcome-RL. ‣ 4.2 Generalization Across Tasks and Models ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
reports failure rates pooled across the three models. Coordination
failures decrease from 67.8% to 62.0%, and verification or termination
failures from 58.8% to 53.5%. In contrast, specification and
system-design failures remain nearly unchanged (88.2% versus 88.3%),
consistent with CoSIR’s focus on sharing, requesting, and using
information rather than enforcing task specifications or managing
discussion procedures. Among individual failure types, ignoring
teammates’ input decreases from 26.3% to 22.8%, proceeding without
needed clarification from 36.4% to 32.0%, and missing or incomplete
verification from 51.3% to 45.9%. These changes complement the
disclosure measures by showing fewer failures to use teammates’
information or check the team’s answers.

However, repeated steps (85.1% versus 85.6%) and information withholding
(18.5% versus 18.7%) do not improve. These results suggest that CoSIR
improves some aspects of collaboration without addressing every failure
type.

#### From disclosure to a better answer.

Three paired Qwen3-8B cases in
[Appendix L](#A12 "Appendix L Case Studies ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
illustrate how information changes decisions: HiddenBench shows a
revised recommendation, SplitQA combines distributed facts, and
SILO-BENCH shows a corrected calculation using shared data.

#### General capabilities.

Across GSM8K, MMLU, and IFEval, performance changes from Base range from
$`-0.6`$ to $`+0.8`$ percentage points, indicating that general
capabilities remain largely preserved
([Appendix I](#A9 "Appendix I General Capability Retention ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")).

## 5 Conclusion

CoSIR trains LLMs to share, request, and use information with computable
rewards in a rating-table environment. Across three backbones, the
trained models achieve higher mean scores than Base on all seven unseen
collaboration tasks and the highest aggregate scores among the compared
methods. Behavioral analyses and case studies show more sharing and use
of teammates’ information, while general capabilities remain close to
the starting checkpoints.

## Limitations

Our evaluation covers three model backbones and seven collaboration
tasks. The trained models improve over their starting checkpoints
throughout this suite, but inference-time workflows and Outcome-RL
remain stronger on some combinations of model and task. Training also
does not remove every communication failure: repeated steps and several
other MAST failure categories remain frequent. The reward depends on a
toy environment with known facts and a computable evaluator. Extending
the same scoring approach to open-ended tasks would require a way to
assess information value when the complete state is unavailable.

## Appendix A Environment Construction

### A.1 Ratings and briefings

Every instance has four options and five attributes, with two, three, or
four agents. Option names, attribute names, and decision scenarios vary
across instances, while the highest-total rule remains fixed. Cell
ratings are initially sampled independently and uniformly from
$`\{-1,0,+1\}`$, and tables with tied highest totals are rejected. The
generator then applies the conditions for the selected information
configuration. These conditions shape which ratings agents need to
exchange. The Bayesian evaluator does not use these conditions as
assumptions.

Let $`\mathcal{C}=\{1,\ldots,k\}\times\{1,\ldots,m\}`$ be the table
cells and $`C_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{C}`$ the cells in agent $`i`$’s
briefing. Define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}B_{i}&=\{(c,V_{c}):c\in C_{i}\},\\
S&=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}C_{i},\qquad U_{i}=C_{i}\setminus\bigcup_{j\neq i}C_{j}.\end{split}
``` |  | (6) |

The set $`S`$ contains ratings initially shared by every agent. A rating
in $`U_{i}`$ appears only in agent $`i`$’s briefing. Cells outside
$`C_{\mathrm{team}}=\bigcup_{i}C_{i}`$ are initially unknown to
everyone.

| Family | Weight | Agents |  Private ratings | Shared | Holders shown | Rounds |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| A | 30% | 4 |  Usually one cell per agent | 3–5 | No | 3–6 |
| B | 15% | 2 |  Usually two cells per agent | 3–5 | No | 3–5 |
| C | 20% | 4 |  Portions of sizes $`(8,4,4,4)`$ | 0 | No | 3–6 |
| D | 15% | 2 |  $`(6,6)`$, $`(7,5)`$, or $`(8,4)`$ cells | 4 | Yes | 4–8 |
| E | 20% | 3 |  $`(5,5,5)`$, $`(7,5,3)`$, or $`(6,6,2)`$ cells | 4 | No | 4–8 |

Table 4: Information configurations. Parenthesized counts give the
ratings allocated across agents. The number of discussion rounds is
sampled uniformly within each listed range.

Families A and B use shared ratings that favor an incorrect option.
Their checks rank options by the ratings in a specified cell set,
treating missing ratings as zero. For $`C\subseteq\mathcal{C}`$, write

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{o}(C)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{a:(o,a)\in C}V_{oa},`$ |  | (7) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{best}(C)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\arg\max_{o}s_{o}(C).`$ |  |  |

Here $`\operatorname{best}(C)`$ is a set, so it includes all ties. With
$`y^{\mathrm{decoy}}`$ denoting the incorrect option favored by shared
ratings, these two families require

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{best}(C_{\mathrm{team}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\{y^{\star}\},`$ |  | (8) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{best}(S)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\{y^{\mathrm{decoy}}\},\quad y^{\mathrm{decoy}}\neq y^{\star},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{best}(C_{i})`$ | $`\displaystyle\neq\{y^{\star}\}\quad\text{for all }i,`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{best}(C_{\mathrm{team}}\setminus\{c\})`$ | $`\displaystyle\neq\{y^{\star}\},`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\text{for all }c\in\textstyle\bigcup_{i}U_{i}.`$ |  |  |

Thus, pooling the assigned ratings gives the correct option a unique
lead, while removing any exclusive cell removes that lead. These checks
use known-rating totals, not the option with the highest evaluator
probability for each agent.

Family C has no shared cells and applies its checks to each agent’s
portion of the table. The evaluator’s highest-probability option for an
individual portion must differ from the correct option. Removing any one
portion must also leave the correct option without a unique probability
lead under the remaining information. This checks whole portions rather
than requiring each private cell to be necessary.

Families D and E provide longer briefings and allow an individual
agent’s highest-probability option to be correct. About 70% of their
instances additionally check the need to combine agents’ private
ratings, removing one agent’s private portion at a time. The remaining
30% use random tables without that extra condition. Even pooled
known-rating totals need not always select the correct option uniquely.
The full table still has a unique best option. None of these family
conditions enters the evaluator’s prior.

## Appendix B Communication Protocol and Parsing

### B.1 Discussion and request routing

A message is one complete natural-language utterance. It may contain
several statements, each claiming a rating for one cell, and at most one
request for a rating. Requests can name a teammate or address the group.

Let $`T`$ be the total number of discussion rounds. It is drawn
uniformly from the selected family’s interval in
[Table 4](#A1.T4 "In A.1 Ratings and briefings ‣ Appendix A Environment Construction ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards").
Agents see the current round and whose turn it is, but not $`T`$ or the
number of remaining rounds. The speaking order is fixed.

A request identifies one cell and may name a recipient. Outside family
D, a group-addressed request is routed to the next holder in speaking
order. A request naming a teammate who cannot provide a useful answer
can also be rerouted. Family D shows holders and retains the named
target. Routing uses the environment’s allocation record, but does not
insert the holder’s rating into the requester’s prompt. It is a
training-time forwarding mechanism that uses information hidden from the
agents.

For each ordinary listener, the environment uses a deterministic hash of
the game, turn, and listener to draw a message-delivery outcome. The
miss probability is $`0.3`$. All 16 candidate messages at one speaking
state share this ordinary delivery mask. If a candidate has a routed
request, its recipient receives the full message regardless of that
mask. Future prompts include only messages actually delivered to that
agent. No agent is told which other listeners missed a message.

A request remains pending until its recipient reports the requested cell
or the requester leaves. The recipient is not forced to reply. Leaving
an answerable request pending incurs the action penalty on each relevant
speaking turn. Reporting the cell clears the request even if the
reported value is fabricated. The statement reward separately checks
whether the value is supported. For a given requester, a new request for
a cell already pending earns no information credit, even if it names a
different recipient. This repetition is not itself classified as an
invalid request.

Only the current speaker answers privately before its turn. After the
last round, every agent answers privately. Each answer uses that agent’s
briefing and delivered conversation. It is neither broadcast nor added
to rating statement records. Intermediate and final answers form
separate candidate groups under the same scoring rules.

### B.2 Text extraction and information checks

The parser combines frozen initial Qwen3-8B with rules, using
temperature zero, disabled thinking, and prompt caching. It is shared
across all three training backbones. Rules first locate option names,
attribute names, and rating words in clauses or tables. The model
extracts statements, claimed sources, totals, and an optional request
with its target. Clear request phrases take precedence when identifying
the intended action.

Text checks remove conditional or negated claims, unknown-value
placeholders, and ratings that do not match the corresponding clause. A
small set of fixed sentence patterns recovers missed ratings. If model
extraction is unusable, rule-extracted ratings that pass the checks
remain available. Empty messages yield no claims. These checks establish
what the text says, not whether a rating is true.

The environment then compares each extracted statement with the
speaker’s briefing and actually received rating statements. A matching
briefing value is an own statement. A matching received rating statement
can be relayed with its original source and identifier. A rating with
neither basis is unsupported, even if it equals the hidden table value.
Checking truth against the table is a separate operation used in reward
calculation.

### B.3 Rating statement identity and delivery

For each rating statement, we store a cell, a rating, an original
speaker, and a stable identifier. A relay copies the original rating
statement rather than creating a new observation from the relaying
agent. Fabricated rating statements use identifiers determined by the
speaker, cell, and rating. Repeating the same fabricated statement does
not create a new report or change the rating retained for posterior
computation. Report identifiers prevent repeated messages from being
counted as new reports or changing their original reception times. For
posterior computation, the evaluator uses at most one rating per cell,
following the precedence rule described in Appendix C.

Let $`R_{j}^{t}`$ be listener $`j`$’s rating statements before turn
$`t`$, $`M_{t}`$ the rating statements in the current message, and
$`D_{tj}`$ its delivery indicator. The update is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}R_{j}^{t+1}&=\begin{cases}R_{j}^{t}\cup M_{t},&D_{tj}=1,\\
R_{j}^{t},&D_{tj}=0,\end{cases}\\
K_{j}^{t+1}&=(B_{j},R_{j}^{t+1}),\qquad j\neq i,\end{split}
``` |  | (9) |

for speaker $`i`$. The union is by identifier: a repeated identifier
neither adds a rating statement nor refreshes its first-reception time.
Rating statements are not added back to their original speaker.
Unsupported rating statements can enter a listener’s record. Delivery
does not replace them with true ratings.

## Appendix C Bayesian Evaluator

### C.1 Prior and Observations

The evaluator considers possible tables consistent with an agent’s
initial briefing and assigns probabilities based on the rating
statements it has received. Let $`\Omega`$ be the tables with a unique
highest-scoring option. Independent uniform cell ratings, conditioned on
this requirement, give

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\Omega&=\left\{v\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{k\times m}:\right.\\
&\hskip 28.45274pt\left.\left|\arg\max_{o}\sum_{a}v_{oa}\right|=1\right\},\\
p_{0}(v)&=1/|\Omega|,\qquad v\in\Omega.\end{split}
``` |  | (10) |

This simplified prior excludes the generator’s family-specific rejection
conditions. The posterior is exact for this evaluation model, not for
the full training-instance distribution.

For each agent, the information record is $`K_{i}=(B_{i},R_{i})`$, where
$`B_{i}`$ contains the initial observations and $`R_{i}`$ contains
received rating statements. We construct $`E_{i}(K_{i})`$ by retaining
one rating per cell. An initial briefing value takes precedence;
otherwise, we use the most recently received rating for that cell.
Repeated reports with the same identifier do not change the retained
value or its reception time.

The evaluator conditions on these observed ratings without assigning a
fixed reliability probability to received statements. Incorrect reports
may therefore affect the resulting posterior. Their correctness is
checked separately against the true table $`V`$ when assigning statement
rewards.

### C.2 Exact posterior computation

For a possible table $`v`$, the unnormalized posterior weight is the
prior restricted to tables consistent with the agent’s available
ratings:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
W_{i}(v)=p_{0}(v)\prod_{(c,r)\in E_{i}(K_{i})}\mathbf{1}[v_{c}=r].
``` |  | (11) |

Let $`y^{\star}(v)`$ be that table’s unique best option. The desired
posterior is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P(y\mid K_{i})=\frac{\sum_{v\in\Omega}\mathbf{1}[y^{\star}(v)=y]W_{i}(v)}{\sum_{v\in\Omega}W_{i}(v)}.
``` |  | (12) |

The actual instance’s hidden label $`y^{\star}`$ is absent from this
computation. It is used only afterward to score communication.

Dynamic programming avoids enumerating all tables. Before conditioning
on a unique winner, form a normalized distribution $`q_{ioa}(u)`$ for
each cell value. For each cell, $`q_{ioa}(u)`$ is a point mass at the
retained rating if the cell appears in $`E_{i}(K_{i})`$, and is uniform
over $`\{-1,0,+1\}`$ otherwise. Let $`F_{io}^{(a)}(s)`$ be the
probability that the first $`a`$ ratings of option $`o`$ sum to $`s`$.
Starting from $`F_{io}^{(0)}(0)=1`$ and zero elsewhere, compute

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F_{io}^{(a)}(s)=\sum_{u\in\{-1,0,+1\}}q_{ioa}(u)F_{io}^{(a-1)}(s-u).
``` |  | (13) |

Write $`F_{io}=F_{io}^{(m)}`$ for the full row-sum distribution and
$`H_{io}(s)=\sum_{z\leq s}F_{io}(z)`$ for its cumulative probability.
Row sums range from $`-m`$ to $`m`$. The probability that option $`y`$
strictly exceeds every other option, before conditioning on uniqueness,
is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}b_{iy}&=\sum_{s=-m}^{m}F_{iy}(s)\prod_{o\neq y}H_{io}(s-1),\\
P(y\mid K_{i})&=\frac{b_{iy}}{\sum_{o=1}^{k}b_{io}}.\end{split}
``` |  | (14) |

The denominator removes tables with a tied maximum. After the cell
distributions are formed, row convolution takes $`O(km^{2})`$
operations, and direct winner calculation takes $`O(k^{2}m)`$. This is a
finite exact sum, not a Monte Carlo estimate.

For numerical scoring, we use
$`\bar{P}(y\mid K_{i})=\max\{P(y\mid K_{i}),10^{-4}\}`$, as in
[Equation 3](#S3.E3 "In 3.2 Measuring the Value of Information ‣ 3 Method ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards").
This bounds each agent’s surprisal at approximately $`13.3`$ bits. The
floored values need not sum to one, while $`P`$ remains normalized. A
reduction in $`\Phi(K)`$ need not increase every agent’s probability or
the LLM’s actual decision accuracy. The communication rewards use these
floored values.

## Appendix D Reward Computation

### D.1 Statement reward computation

For a speaker $`i`$ and a set of rating statements $`M`$, let
$`K_{j}\oplus M`$ add rating statements with previously unseen
identifiers to listener $`j`$’s record. We compute each listener’s
contribution to the decrease in team surprisal
([Equation 3](#S3.E3 "In 3.2 Measuring the Value of Information ‣ 3 Method ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards"))
using the floored probabilities from
[Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C Bayesian Evaluator ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards"):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}_{j}(M)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\log_{2}\frac{\bar{P}(y^{\star}\mid K_{j}\oplus M)}{\bar{P}(y^{\star}\mid K_{j})},`$ |  | (15) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{i}(M;K)`$ | $`\displaystyle=0.7\sum_{j\neq i}\bar{\Delta}_{j}(M).`$ |  |  |

The sum covers present listeners other than the speaker. Rating
statements they already hold cause no change. The uniform nominal
delivery weight $`0.7`$ matches ordinary message delivery, but also
applies to a routed request recipient who is guaranteed to receive the
whole message. Thus, the message value is not the exact expected change
under every delivery condition. Actual rating statement records follow
realized delivery. The scoring weight and the state-update rule serve
different purposes.

Partition a message’s statements into supported statements $`L`$ and
unsupported statements $`I`$. For fixed speaker and information records,
abbreviate the value of these rating statements as
$`\mathcal{V}(L)=\mathcal{V}_{i}(L;K)`$. Let $`a_{\ell}`$ denote the
value allocated to statement $`\ell`$. For $`\ell\in L`$, start with the
removal marginal
$`m_{\ell}=\mathcal{V}(L)-\mathcal{V}(L\setminus\{\ell\})`$. Let
$`Z_{+}=\sum_{h:m_{h}>0}m_{h}`$ and $`Z_{-}=\sum_{h:m_{h}\leq 0}m_{h}`$.
If $`Z_{+}>0`$ and $`\mathcal{V}(L)-Z_{-}\geq 0`$, assign

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{\ell}=\begin{cases}m_{\ell}\bigl(\mathcal{V}(L)-Z_{-}\bigr)/Z_{+},&m_{\ell}>0,\\
m_{\ell},&m_{\ell}\leq 0.\end{cases}
``` |  | (16) |

This keeps negative marginals unchanged and rescales positive marginals
so that allocated scores sum to $`\mathcal{V}(L)`$. A single supported
statement receives its own message value.

When this rescaling is not applicable, the allocation averages
sequential marginal values over statement orders for sets of at most six
statements. This is the Shapley allocation ([Shapley,
1953](#bib.bib25)): each statement receives its average change in value
when added after the statements preceding it. For larger sets, the
fallback proportionally scales removal marginals. Unsupported statements
are allocated separately, using
$`w(I^{\prime})=\mathcal{V}(L\cup I^{\prime})-\mathcal{V}(L)`$ for
$`I^{\prime}\subseteq I`$. Their allocation therefore measures the extra
change beyond the supported statements, rather than redistributing the
supported set’s value. For a set $`N`$, a value function $`f`$, and the
set $`\mathcal{P}(N)`$ of all orders of $`N`$, the sequential average is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{\ell}=\frac{1}{|N|!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}(N)}\bigl[f(N_{<\ell}^{\sigma}\cup\{\ell\})-f(N_{<\ell}^{\sigma})\bigr],
``` |  | (17) |

where $`N_{<\ell}^{\sigma}`$ contains statements before $`\ell`$ in
order $`\sigma`$. Use $`f=\mathcal{V}`$ for the supported set and
$`f=w`$ for the unsupported set.

We convert the allocated values into statement rewards:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\ell}^{\mathrm{stmt}}=\begin{cases}\max(a_{\ell},0)-0.01,&\text{supported},\\
\min(a_{\ell},0)-0.01,&\text{unsupported}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (18) |

The environment separately checks for fabricated rating statements. When
a statement is flagged as fabricated, an additional penalty of $`0.04`$
is applied:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\ell}^{\mathrm{stmt}}\leftarrow r_{\ell}^{\mathrm{stmt}}-0.04\,\mathbf{1}[\text{fabricated}_{\ell}].
``` |  | (19) |

This penalty is based on the environment’s verification of the statement
and does not enter the Bayesian posterior.

The information term is nonnegative for supported statements and
nonpositive for unsupported ones. Every statement pays a reading cost of
$`0.01`$. Repetition can help listeners who missed the rating statement
earlier. Otherwise, it usually incurs only that cost. Two additional
rules concern truth. A faithful relay of a false rating receives exactly
$`-0.01`$ before response-level clipping. If a speaker corrects its own
earlier false rating statement with a true rating, the correction is
valued against a record with the earlier false rating statement removed.
The scoring baseline therefore excludes the error being corrected.

If the sum of negative statement rewards in one response is below
$`-2`$, those negative rewards are scaled proportionally to sum to
$`-2`$. Positive rewards are unchanged. This bound is applied before
token assignment. Statement credit is then set to zero when it is
identical across the candidate group. Text without a cell-rating claim
has no separate statement score, but may still belong to an action span
or receive a retention penalty.

### D.2 Request value and remaining turns

Suppose a valid request asks holder $`q`$ for cell $`c`$, and let
$`x_{q,c}`$ be a truthful rating statement for that cell. We first sum
the other agents’ surprisal reductions if they all receive this rating
statement, including the requester’s reduction. Let
$`p_{\mathrm{next}}(q)`$ be the probability that $`q`$ has another
speaking turn. The implemented request reward is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}r_{\mathrm{ask}}(q,c)={}&p_{\mathrm{next}}(q)\\
&{}\cdot\min\!\left\{2,\,\delta_{c}\left[\sum_{j\neq q}\bar{\Delta}_{j}(x_{q,c})\right]_{+}\right\},\end{split}
``` |  | (20) |

where $`[z]_{+}=\max(z,0)`$. The factor $`\delta_{c}`$ is $`0.3`$ if the
requester has received any rating statement for the cell and $`1`$
otherwise. The cap, discount, and remaining-turn factor convert the
potential information value into a training reward. The calculation
assumes a true answer from the holder and receipt by every other present
agent. It includes neither a $`0.7`$ delivery weight nor an estimated
willingness to answer. The score is assigned when the request is made
and is not revised after observing a reply or a missed message.

For its timing calculation, index seats by $`0,\ldots,n-1`$ and rounds
from zero. Let $`s`$ be the current speaker, $`q\neq s`$ the holder, and
$`\rho`$ the current round. The holder’s next turn falls in round

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{q}=\left\lfloor\frac{n\rho+s+((q-s)\bmod n)}{n}\right\rfloor.
``` |  | (21) |

Let $`T_{0}=\max(T_{\min},\rho+1)`$, using the current family’s bounds.
Reaching the current round implies $`T\geq T_{0}`$. The probability of
reaching the holder’s next turn is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}p_{\mathrm{next}}(q)&=\Pr(T>r_{q}\mid T\geq T_{0})\\
&=\frac{[T_{\max}-\max(T_{0},r_{q}+1)+1]_{+}}{T_{\max}-T_{0}+1}.\end{split}
``` |  | (22) |

If the holder has yet to speak this round, this probability is one.
Otherwise it depends on whether another round occurs. It says nothing
about whether the holder will answer.

The requester’s prior receipt of any rating statement for the requested
cell sets $`\delta_{c}=0.3`$, regardless of that rating statement’s
value. Otherwise $`\delta_{c}=1`$. The two-bit cap follows this discount
and precedes multiplication by $`p_{\mathrm{next}}`$. A pending request
from the same requester for the same cell sets the information credit to
zero. After that request is cleared, a new request is valued from the
updated records. If a holder already reported the cell but a listener
missed it, a further request can still have value.

### D.3 Action penalties

A request for a cell already in the requester’s briefing, or for a cell
with no eligible holder, is invalid and is not registered as pending. It
receives no information credit and incurs an action penalty of $`0.10`$.
An answerable request left pending also incurs $`0.10`$, charged to the
requested agent. Let $`v_{\mathrm{ask}}(x)`$ be the request credit in
response $`x`$, zero if there is no valid credited request. Let $`b(x)`$
indicate an invalid action and $`u_{i}(x)`$ indicate that speaker $`i`$
leaves an answerable request pending. The action score is

|     |                                                                |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \begin{split}r^{\mathrm{act}}(x)={}&v_{\mathrm{ask}}(x)-0.05\\
       &{}-0.10\,b(x)-0.10\,u_{i}(x).\end{split}
       ```                                                             |     | (23) |

The fixed speaking-turn deduction $`0.05`$ is shared by every candidate
in a group. The action tolerance and subsequent mean subtraction are
invariant to this shared constant, so it cancels from all action
advantages. It does not discourage message length or speaking through a
training gradient. The statement reading cost remains in uncentered
statement credit, while the two action penalties can distinguish
candidates.

## Appendix E Private-Answer Evaluation

Let $`\hat{y}_{i}`$ be agent $`i`$’s selected option, $`\hat{s}_{io}`$
its reported total for option $`o`$, and $`s^{K}_{io}`$ the reference
total from its available ratings. The implemented private-answer reward
is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle r_{i}^{\mathrm{answer}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=c_{i}+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{o=1}^{k}\mathbf{1}[\hat{s}_{io}=s^{K}_{io}],`$ |  | (24) |
|  | $`\displaystyle c_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bar{P}(\hat{y}_{i}\mid K_{i}),&\text{parseable choice},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}`$ |  |  |

The choice term uses the evaluator’s posterior for the selected option,
floored at $`10^{-4}`$ when the choice is parseable. An unparseable
choice receives zero. No comparison with the true winning label enters
this term. A truly incorrect option can therefore earn a high choice
score when the current evidence favors it.

The totals term requires one reference value for each cell. Let
$`v^{K}_{ic}`$ denote the rating used in agent $`i`$’s known total. Its
briefing value takes priority. Otherwise, use the rating statement for
cell $`c`$ whose first-reception time is latest. If no rating statement
exists, use zero. The reference total is

|     |                                           |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       s^{K}_{io}=\sum_{a=1}^{m}v^{K}_{i,(o,a)}.
       ```                                        |     | (25) |

Each cell contributes once. Missing total lines count as incorrect
rather than as zero-valued predictions.

Both posterior computation and the totals target use one retained rating
per cell, with initial briefing values taking precedence over received
statements. When multiple reports concern the same cell, the most
recently received rating is used. An incorrect report may therefore
affect an agent’s probability estimates and calculated totals until it
is corrected. Receiving an old identifier again does not refresh its
time. If a true rating statement was followed by a new false rating
statement, hearing the old true rating statement again can therefore
leave the false value in the totals target. Correctly matching the known
totals does not imply recovering the full table’s true totals.

For private totals, rules read the number after the final equals sign in
a calculation, or the first number after a colon otherwise. If an option
has several total lines, its last line supplies the reported total. A
missing or unreadable total counts as incorrect. An unreadable choice
receives zero choice score.

## Appendix F Self-Play Training Details

### F.1 Collection and candidate groups

Each collection round contains 60 games played by the current shared
policy. Sampling uses temperature $`1.2`$, disabled thinking, and a
limit of 1,024 generated tokens per message. Samples containing thinking
markers are excluded. A speaking group has 16 policy candidates and up
to two additional demonstrations. An intermediate or final
private-answer group has eight policy candidates and up to two
demonstrations. Demonstrations are separate from the policy sample
counts.

One of the 16 policy messages is selected uniformly using the game seed
and turn to continue discussion. Reward does not affect this selection.
All retained candidates are scored against the same preceding records,
so their differences concern the current action rather than different
conversation histories. Private answers do not continue the dialogue.
Groups with no scoring differences are discarded.

### F.2 Component advantages and token spans

Statement spans use their own statement reward without a group baseline
or standard-deviation normalization. If all candidate statement scores
are identical, their statement credit is zeroed. Actions, choices, and
each option’s total correctness instead use centered advantages. For
component $`b`$ and a group of $`G`$ policy candidates, define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{g}^{b}=\tilde{r}_{g}^{b}-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{h=1}^{G}\tilde{r}_{h}^{b},
``` |  | (26) |

where $`\tilde{r}_{g}^{b}`$ is candidate $`g`$’s component score,
adjusted only for the action tolerance below. Statements are excluded
from this centering. Choice and action advantages use this expression
directly. Each option’s total advantage is additionally scaled by
$`1/k`$.

For actions, let $`r_{\max}`$ be the maximum among policy candidates and
$`\tau=0.03`$. Before subtracting the policy-group mean, apply

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{r}_{g}^{\mathrm{act}}=\begin{cases}r_{\max},&r_{\max}-r_{g}^{\mathrm{act}}<\tau,\\
r_{g}^{\mathrm{act}},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (27) |

The tolerance treats nearly tied policy actions as equally good.
Demonstrations do not enter $`r_{\max}`$ or the group mean. Choice
advantages center the choice scores over eight policy answers. For
option $`o`$, let $`\hat{s}_{go}`$ be candidate $`g`$’s reported total
for the current agent $`i`$, and let
$`z_{go}=\mathbf{1}[\hat{s}_{go}=s^{K}_{io}]`$. Its total-line advantage
is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{go}^{\mathrm{total}}=\frac{1}{k}\left(z_{go}-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{h=1}^{G}z_{ho}\right),\qquad G=8.
``` |  | (28) |

This compares correctness separately for each option, not only the sum
of correct totals.

Every token assigned to a statement receives that statement’s credit.
Tokens in the request sentence and the end token receive the centered
action advantage multiplied by $`16/N_{\mathrm{act}}`$, where
$`N_{\mathrm{act}}`$ is their token count. This fixes the total
action-span weight at 16. Without a request, the closing sentence and
end token are used instead. This location is a heuristic for the
decision to finish without asking.

For private answers, the choice line and end token receive the choice
advantage. Each option’s last total line receives its own total
advantage. If that line is missing, its advantage is added to the choice
span. If no choice line is identifiable, the choice advantage is
assigned to answer tokens outside the total lines. Remaining tokens
receive zero direct advantage.

Span boundaries use parsed text positions and attribute-word anchors. A
token is assigned using the span containing its first character. An
action span takes priority over an overlapping statement span. These
rules are approximate: for example, a rating word before its attribute
can fall in a neighboring span. They associate credit with generated
text without guaranteeing perfect attribution at every token.

### F.3 Generated demonstrations

When the evaluator ranks a request as the best available action,
demonstrations provide additional examples of that action. Frozen
initial Qwen3-8B minimally rewrites the first usable policy response to
express the selected action, with four attempts at temperature $`1.0`$.
Request templates provide two further candidates. The parser filters
them, and at most two valid candidates with the lowest negative log
likelihood under the current policy are retained. Identical
demonstrations within a group are deduplicated.

Demonstrations are trained with the ordinary agent prompt, without
rewrite instructions. They are never selected to advance the
conversation. Their centered components use the policy group’s baseline
without changing it. Action demonstration advantages are clipped to
$`[-0.5,0.5]`$. Statement and answer demonstration advantages do not
share this cap.

A demonstration was not sampled from the current policy under the
ordinary prompt. For the training probability ratio defined below, its
denominator is evaluated under the model at the start of the round. The
generating model’s token probabilities are not used. The resulting
objective is a clipped, advantage-weighted imitation update without
correction for that generation distribution. It is not an unbiased
on-policy PPO estimate. Demonstration tokens receive no reference KL
penalty. An entropy term, which encourages a broader token distribution,
includes both policy and demonstration tokens.

### F.4 Policy objective and optimization

Let $`\pi_{\theta}`$ be the current model with trainable parameters
$`\theta`$, $`\pi_{\mathrm{round}}`$ the model at the start of the
collection round, and $`\pi_{0}`$ the initial backbone before
collaboration training. For token $`w_{t}`$ with preceding context
$`h_{t}`$, define the probability ratio and clipped score

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\rho_{t}(\theta)&=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(w_{t}\mid h_{t})}{\pi_{\mathrm{round}}(w_{t}\mid h_{t})},\\
u_{t}&=\min\{\rho_{t}A_{t},\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{t},0.8,1.28)A_{t}\},\\
f_{t}&=\begin{cases}\max(u_{t},3A_{t}),&A_{t}<0,\\
u_{t},&A_{t}\geq 0.\end{cases}\end{split}
``` |  | (29) |

Here $`A_{t}`$ includes the component and span weights above. The extra
bound for negative advantages is dual clipping ([Ye et al.,
2020](#bib.bib28)). Policy samples use the round’s policy as their
reference for this ratio. Demonstrations use the same evaluated ratio
with the limitation described in the preceding subsection.

For a batch $`\mathcal{B}`$ of responses, the policy objective averages
responses after dividing each token sum by the fixed constant 64:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
J(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{64}\sum_{t\in x}f_{t}.
``` |  | (30) |

It does not divide by each response’s length. Longer statement spans can
therefore receive more total gradient weight, whereas action spans have
the fixed weight described above. Optimization updates all parameters
with AdamW8bit ([Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019](#bib.bib26); [Dettmers et
al., 2022](#bib.bib27)), a gradient-norm limit of $`1.0`$, and 64
samples per step. Each collection round receives one epoch at a constant
learning rate, with optimizer state retained across rounds. All three
backbones follow the learning-rate schedule in
[Table 6](#A6.T6 "In Training time. ‣ F.7 Learning rates and hardware ‣ Appendix F Self-Play Training Details ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards").

### F.5 Retaining the initial model’s behavior

A reference penalty applies only to policy-sampled tokens whose assigned
advantage is exactly zero. It uses the fixed initial backbone
$`\pi_{0}`$, not the previous round’s model. Let $`z_{t}`$ indicate
these eligible tokens and
$`d_{t}=\log\pi_{\theta}(w_{t}\mid h_{t})-\log\pi_{0}(w_{t}\mid h_{t})`$.
The sampled second-order estimate, commonly called the $`k_{2}`$ KL
estimate ([Schulman, 2020](#bib.bib29)), and the general-prompt
imitation loss are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}L_{\mathrm{KL}}&=\mathbb{E}_{t}[z_{t}\,d_{t}^{2}/2],\\
L_{\mathrm{anchor}}&=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{0}}\sum_{t}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t}).\end{split}
``` |  | (31) |

Here $`\mathcal{D}_{0}`$ contains general prompts and answers generated
by $`\pi_{0}`$. The squared log-ratio term is an estimate, not an exact
sum over the vocabulary. Both retention terms have a coefficient of
$`0.1`$.

General prompts come from a cleaned SmolTalk subset ([Ben Allal and
others, 2025](#bib.bib30)), excluding multi-agent prompts and overlap
with evaluation data. Approximately 2,500 prompts are used per round,
with one anchor sample inserted per four policy samples. Imitating the
initial model’s answers gives the gradient of a forward KL objective
from that model to the current policy. Every ten optimizer steps, a
separate set of 170 reference examples monitors this direction of KL.
The threshold $`0.01`$ is checked after training. It does not trigger
automatic early stopping.

## Appendix G SplitQA Construction and Evaluation

#### Data selection.

We construct a 70-question SplitQA test set from the distractor setting
of HotpotQA ([Yang et al., 2018](#bib.bib24)), with 63 bridge questions
and 7 comparison questions. Each source item contains two annotated
supporting Wikipedia paragraphs and eight distractor paragraphs. Bridge
questions link information through an intermediate entity, whereas
comparison questions compare a property of two entities. We retain only
bridge and comparison questions with two distinct supporting paragraphs.
We also exclude any item whose gold answer appears verbatim in a
distractor paragraph. This removes direct copies of the answer from the
distractors.

#### Evidence allocation.

Agent 1 receives one supporting paragraph and Agent 2 receives the
other. Agents 3 and 4 receive only distractor paragraphs. Thus, no agent
receives both annotated supporting paragraphs, and exactly two agents
hold the annotated evidence. To answer from that evidence, the team
needs to identify which agents hold relevant facts and link or compare
those facts across the two supporting paragraphs. The distractors add
the need to separate relevant information from unrelated material.

#### Discussion and scoring.

We retain the protocol used in our HiddenBench evaluation ([Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)): four agents speak in a fixed order for five rounds,
then each answers privately. Each round gives every agent one speaking
turn. Final answers are free-form strings, so agents are not given a
list of candidate answers. The team answer is the unique answer
receiving the most votes among the four agents. If two or more answers
tie for the highest vote count, the team produces no answer and receives
an exact-match score of zero, even if one of the tied answers is
correct. Otherwise, the team receives a score of one if the selected
answer exactly matches the gold answer, and zero if it does not. Each of
the 70 questions is run with four seeds.

## Appendix H Evaluation Settings and Score Aggregation

#### Benchmark protocols.

HiddenBench ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)) uses five rounds of
discussion in a fixed speaking order, followed by private votes from all
agents. Team accuracy is determined by majority voting. SILO-BENCH
([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)) uses three discussion rounds followed
by individual answers, and we report its task success rate. SplitQA uses
five rounds followed by private answers, with team accuracy measured by
exact match of the answer receiving the most votes. Tied votes are
counted as incorrect, as detailed in
[Appendix G](#A7 "Appendix G SplitQA Construction and Evaluation ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards").

MathChat ([Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)) pairs an LLM with a user proxy
that executes tools and provides feedback during mathematical problem
solving. BenchForm ([Weng et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)) requires a single
answer rather than a new multi-round discussion. We average answer
accuracy across its five settings of peer influence. In the trust and
doubt settings, the input includes five rounds of prior discussion
history. For Collaboration Tax ([Sun et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)), we
report the score achieved by the collaborating agents. Higher scores
therefore indicate better performance, as they do for the other tasks in
SUM.

#### Inference-time collaboration workflows.

ToM ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib8)) and TalkHier ([Wang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib9)) follow the same benchmark protocols as the other
methods, with matched numbers of agents, discussion rounds, and limits
on speaking turns. However, they use additional model calls within each
speaking turn. ToM makes two calls. The first generates a private
belief-state description of at most 400 tokens, which is retained in the
agent’s context but not shared with teammates. The second produces the
public message. TalkHier makes three calls. It first drafts a response,
then obtains reviewer feedback of at most 400 tokens, and finally
revises the response using that feedback. These additional calls do not
increase the number of public speaking turns, but they increase
inference computation.

#### Score aggregation.

[Table 1](#S4.T1 "In Reporting. ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
reports means over the runs available for each task. All OOD benchmarks
have four reported runs per method and model. We first average within
each task, then add the seven means to obtain SUM. We compute the total
before rounding the displayed entries to two decimal places.
Consequently, adding the rounded table entries can differ slightly from
the displayed total.

Each task contributes equally to SUM. A one-point increase on any task
adds one point to the total, regardless of that task’s number of
examples or runs. The general-capability benchmarks and behavioral
measurements are not included in SUM. The column $`\Delta`$SUM subtracts
the corresponding Base total, so it measures the change from the same
starting model. Bold entries identify the highest score within a model
and task, including ties.

The comparison methods serve different purposes. ToM and TalkHier modify
the inference-time workflow, whereas Outcome-RL uses CoSIR’s training
procedure with the reward replaced by the final team outcome. Base
measures performance before collaboration training. The Outcome-RL
comparison therefore isolates the role of behavior-specific training
rewards (Unlike CoSIR’s candidate-level information scoring, Outcome-RL
assigns final-outcome feedback to the sampled trajectory. Both methods
retain request demonstrations), while the inference-time methods test
whether collaboration can instead be improved through additional
reasoning and revision steps.

## Appendix J Behavioral Measures

The measures in
[Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.2 Generalization Across Tasks and Models ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
track observable actions in the discussion. Their denominators differ
because some concern information holders and others concern messages.
All are reported as percentages.

#### HiddenBench.

Private facts disclosed is the fraction of private facts stated by their
original holders during the discussion. The disclosure check matches a
fact when at least 60% of its content words appear in the holder’s
message. Messages responding to teammates is the fraction of messages
that respond to another agent’s contribution. This measure tracks
engagement with a teammate without requiring that the response be
correct.

#### SILO-BENCH.

Agents listing all own data is the fraction of agents that list their
complete local data in their messages. Result-only messages is the
fraction of messages that relay an accumulated value or conclusion
without giving the speaker’s own data. The first measure increases when
agents expose inputs that teammates can check. The second decreases when
fewer messages offer a result without those inputs.

#### SplitQA.

Holders stating supporting facts is the fraction of agents assigned a
supporting paragraph that state their supporting fact in at least one
discussion round. Only the two supporting-fact holders enter the
denominator. Agents assigned only distractors are excluded.

#### MathChat.

Messages with correction language is the fraction of discussion messages
containing language that identifies or corrects an error. It measures
attempts to correct the discussion, without separately scoring whether
each correction is valid.

#### Timing of disclosure.

The case-study analysis also measures whether evidence is shared in the
holder’s first message. For Qwen3-8B, this rate rises from 61.1% to
69.0% on HiddenBench and from 41.1% to 51.1% on SplitQA. These
first-message rates are distinct from the any-round disclosure rates in
[Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.2 Generalization Across Tasks and Models ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards").
They indicate that more relevant evidence enters the discussion at its
start, when teammates can use it in subsequent turns.

## Appendix K MAST Failure Analysis

We use the MAST categories of [Cemri et al. (2025)](#bib.bib3) to
organize the observed failures.
[Table 3](#S4.T3 "In Comparison with Outcome-RL. ‣ 4.2 Generalization Across Tasks and Models ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
pools conversations from HiddenBench, SplitQA, and MathChat across
Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, and Gemma-3-4B-it. A category rate is the percentage
of conversations marked with that category. A conversation can have
several failure labels, so the percentages do not sum to 100.

We follow the official MAST evaluation procedure ([Cemri et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)), using the original judge prompt and the definitions
and examples of all 14 failure modes without modification. We replace
only the judge model with gpt-5-mini, using medium reasoning effort and
a maximum output of 8,000 tokens. We apply this procedure to Base and
CoSIR conversations from HiddenBench, SplitQA, and MathChat for all
three backbones: Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, and Gemma-3-4B-it. A conversation
may receive multiple failure labels, so the reported percentages do not
need to sum to 100.

Failures involving coordination between agents decrease from 67.8% to
62.0%, and failures involving verification or termination decrease from
58.8% to 53.5%. The specification and system-design category remains
nearly unchanged, at 88.2% and 88.3%. Within these categories, ignoring
teammates’ input and proceeding without clarification become less
frequent, while information withholding changes from 18.5% to 18.7%.
Thus, the aggregate reduction concerns particular ways of using
information, rather than every failure related to communication.

The disclosure measures count whether designated private facts are
shared, whereas MAST information withholding is a conversation-level
failure judgment. The two measures therefore capture different aspects
of information sharing.

and seed, without an additional situational prompt. Both conditions use
four agents. HiddenBench and SplitQA allow five discussion rounds, and
the SILO-BENCH example allows three. A round gives each agent one
speaking turn. The cards reproduce selected excerpts, with omissions
marked by an ellipsis. Descriptions outside quotation marks summarize

### L.1 HiddenBench: Sharing Facts That Change a Decision

The team must select a warehouse for relief supplies after a storm (seed
3). Shared information makes Central Depot attractive because it is near
a highway and has modern loading facilities. The private evidence
changes this comparison. Agent 1 knows that Bayview Warehouse has lost
half its roof and cannot keep supplies dry. Agent 2 knows that flooding
has put Central Depot’s foundation at risk. Agent 3 knows that Central
Depot’s backup generator has been destroyed, and Agent 4 knows that
Hilltop Storage is the only site with a full staff. The correct choice
is Hilltop Storage.

Agent 1 initially recommends Central Depot in both conditions. In Base,
Agents 3 and 4 never state their private facts, and all four agents
ultimately select Bayview Warehouse. After training, both facts appear
in the first round. Agent 1 takes up the generator failure in round 2
and recommends Hilltop Storage by round 5. All four agents select it.
[Figure 2](#A12.F2 "In L.1 HiddenBench: Sharing Facts That Change a Decision ‣ Appendix L Case Studies ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
shows the exchange. The change is visible in the discussion itself: an
initial recommendation is revised after teammates provide evidence that
was absent from the shared briefing.

HiddenBench: new evidence changes the initial recommendation Base Agent
3, round 1: “While the foundation issue at Central Depot is concerning,
the briefing does not provide any evidence that the warehouse is
currently unsafe or structurally compromised. … If we choose a warehouse
with a damaged generator, we risk delays in power supply, which could
impact refrigeration or lighting for the supplies.” Agent 4, round 5:
“Given the urgency of the situation, the need for a stable and secure

### L.2 SILO-BENCH: Making a Wrong Count Checkable

The team must count occurrences of delta across 16 words divided equally
among four agents (seed 1). Their word lists contain two, one, two, and
zero occurrences, respectively, so the correct total is five. In Base,
Agent 2 incorrectly reports two occurrences in its own list. Agents
repeat their counts without exposing their word lists, and all four
finish with the incorrect total of 18.

The trained team also makes an initial counting error: Agent 3 reports
zero despite holding two occurrences. In round 2, it lists its words
while still asserting the incorrect count. Agent 1 then checks that list
and corrects the contribution to two, obtaining $`2+1+2+0=5`$. All four
agents finish with the correct answer.
[Figure 3](#A12.F3 "In L.2 SILO-BENCH: Making a Wrong Count Checkable ‣ Appendix L Case Studies ‣ Training LLMs for Multi-Agent Collaboration with Verifiable Information Rewards")
shows that sharing the underlying data makes a local error visible to a
teammate who can repair it.

SILO-BENCH: a teammate can correct a count once the data are visible
Base Agent 2, round 1: “I count 2 occurrences of ’delta’ in my data.”
Agent 2, round 3: “\[Round 3\] Agent 2: I count 2 occurrences of ’delta’
in my data.” Agent 2 actually holds only one occurrence. The local
counts are repeated without the raw word lists. Final answers: 18, all
four agents (incorrect). CoSIR Agent 3, round 2: “\[Round 2\] Agent 3: I
confirm my count of ’delta’ is 0. I will now share my data: delta,
delta, beta, epsilon.” Agent 1, round 3: “\[Round 3\] Agent 1: Agent 3
shared: delta, delta, beta, epsilon. … Agent 3’s data: 2 ’delta’s …
Total: 2 + 1 + 2 + 0 = 5” Agent 3 still gives a wrong local count, but
Agent 1 checks the shared list and corrects it. Final answers: 5, all
four agents (correct).

Figure 3: SILO-BENCH word counting, seed 1. The trained team succeeds
despite an initial local error because the raw data become available for
checking.

### L.3 SplitQA: Connecting Facts Held by Different Agents

The question asks which Eminem album included vocals from a singer who
had an album titled Unapologetic. We use seed 0 for HotpotQA. Agent 1’s
paragraph identifies Rihanna as the singer associated with Unapologetic.
Agent 2’s paragraph states that Eminem’s song The Monster features
Rihanna and appears on The Marshall Mathers LP 2. Combining these facts
yields the answer.

In Base, Agent 2 does not state its supporting fact. Instead, it asserts
an unsupported connection to Recovery, which becomes all four agents’
final answer. After training, Agent 1 gives its evidence and identifies
the missing link: which Eminem album includes Rihanna’s vocals. Agent 2
supplies that link in the same round. Agent 1 combines the two facts in
round 2, and all four agents answer The Marshall Mathers LP 2.

shows how stating both known and missing information helps the team

SplitQA: identify the missing link, then combine the two supporting

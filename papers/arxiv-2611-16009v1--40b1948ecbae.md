###### Abstract

GPT-6 Astra can drive a robot arm directly through a fixed harness, and
scores of such robot-use agents on RoboDojo have risen quickly since.
Reported systems differ in harness and test subset and often change
several designs at once, so it is hard to tell which choices produce the
gains. We take these designs apart and test them in one framework, along
the harness around the model, the action interface, the reuse of
experience and the scene geometry given to the model. A lean harness
that keeps the full episode history and stops each motion early goes a
long way. Reusing experience changes how the model acts but is not a
free lunch, and scene geometry helps most, even when depth is predicted
from RGB alone. JustRUA starts from the official L3 harness and keeps
only what matters most. Even with no learned policy, self-improvement
loop or task-specific tuning, this simple harness raises the score on
the RoboDojo simulation benchmark substantially, from 29.7 to 50.3.

## 1 Introduction

Frontier multimodal models can now control robots without any
robot-specific training. GPT-6 Astra ([OpenAI, 2026b](#bib.bib33))
drives a bimanual arm through a harness, the software that turns each of
its decisions into end-effector targets for a motion planner ([Zhang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib2); [Galbot Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib3); [Yang et
al., 2026a](#bib.bib4)). No vision-language-action (VLA) model sits in
between. The model looks at camera images, decides where each gripper
should go next and reads the outcome, much as a computer-use agent looks
at a screen and decides where to click ([Xie et al., 2024](#bib.bib53)).
By analogy such a system is called a robot-use agent (RUA) ([Isola,
2026](#bib.bib34); [Yang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib9)). On the RoboDojo
simulation benchmark ([Chen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib1)), RUAs built on
GPT-6 Astra have improved quickly, and each new system adds components
of its own, such as depth and point clouds ([Qu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8); [Zhou et al., 2026b](#bib.bib6); [Huang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib36)), affordance models
([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Yang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib9)),
in-context demonstrations ([Guo et al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [SenseNova and
Joint Research Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib37)) and self-improvement loops that write and refine
their own skills ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14); [OpenSenseNova,
2026](#bib.bib15)).

Our starting point was a discrepancy. The first evaluation of GPT-6
Astra on RoboDojo found it weak on tasks that require precision under
the official L3 harness ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)), and a broader
study saw a similar gap between its task decisions and reliable physical
control ([Galbot Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)). Yet a few reasonable
fixes to the harness and the action loop, with no elaborate design on
top, brought its Precision score to the level of the strongest VLA
models of the time
([Section 3](#S3 "3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
We came to suspect that much of the reported weakness lay in the harness
rather than in the model.

If the harness matters this much, the gains reported by recent systems
are hard to read. These systems start from different harnesses, run on
different subsets of the benchmark with different budgets, and often
change several parts of the design at once. A reader who sees a higher
score cannot tell whether it comes from the new component, from the loop
it was added to or from the episodes it was measured on. Reproducing
each system in full would carry these differences over. We instead take
the designs apart and test them on one testbed and one evaluation set,
changing as little as we can between runs.

We start from the official L3 harness and group the designs that recent
systems add along four axes
([Figure 2](#S2.F2 "In 2 Controlled Study Framework ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")):
the harness around the model, the action interface, the reuse of
experience from other episodes and the scene geometry in each
observation. Three tools keep the comparison affordable and readable.
Since the official evaluation of 2,100 episodes is costly to repeat for
every design, we build a 111-episode evaluation set that covers all 42
tasks and spends more episodes on the tasks with high evaluation
uncertainty. Since a score says how much of a task was completed and not
how the attempt went, we review every first-round episode from its video
and count behaviours the score does not show, such as empty grasps and
dropped objects. And since an episode resends its whole history at every
decision, we report what each configuration costs per episode. A design
is kept only if it helps on the evaluation set at a reasonable cost and
does not draw on the tasks being scored.

The result is JustRUA. It keeps three kinds of change to the official
harness. The loop keeps the full episode history and stops each motion
after 20 control steps, the observation carries the robot’s own grip
readings and depth predicted from RGB, and the model may run code to
solve 3D coordinates from pixels. With no learned policy, no
self-improvement loop and no task-specific tuning, it raises the
evaluation-set score of the official harness from 29.7 to 50.3
([Figure 1](#S0.F1 "In JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
Our findings are as follows.

- •
  A lean harness goes a long way
  ([Section 3](#S3 "3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
  Keeping the full episode history and stopping each motion after 20
  control steps lift the score from 29.7 to 38.0. Relative targets,
  named skills and model-chosen step counts do not beat plain absolute
  targets.
- •
  Reusing experience is not a free lunch
  ([Section 4](#S4 "4 Reusing Experience ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
  Demonstrations and a self-written skill library change how the model
  acts, but neither clearly beats the default, and both make an episode
  more expensive. They also draw on the tasks being scored, so their
  scores are not comparable with a standard evaluation.
- •
  Scene geometry helps most
  ([Section 5](#S5 "5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
  Depth images cut empty grasps from 41 to 16 in the first round, even
  when Depth Pro predicts the depth from RGB alone and is off by tens of
  centimetres. With code that solves 3D coordinates from pixels and no
  decision cap, the score reaches 50.3.
- •
  Remaining failures are mostly long-tail
  ([Section 6](#S6 "6 Error Analysis ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
  Most failures left in our best configuration are specific to one task,
  such as plans that do not suit the arm, unplanned knocks and slipping
  grasps. Fixing them would take trial and error on each task, which
  comes close to tuning on the test tasks.

The evaluation set, the code and the episode replays are available from
the project page.

## 2 Controlled Study Framework

Instead of reproducing each reported system, we take the designs apart
and test them on one testbed and one benchmark, changing as little as we
can between runs. The framework has to meet two conditions. It must
carry out the actions the model chooses reliably, so that we measure the
model and its interface rather than the accidents of the loop around
them. And it must tell the effects of different designs apart at a cost
we can pay many times over. This section describes the benchmark and the
harness we start from, the testbed, the four axes, the evaluation set,
and the review of videos and logs that we use to count physical
behaviours.

### 2.1 Benchmark and Starting Point

RoboDojo ([Chen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib1)) follows earlier bimanual
simulation benchmarks such as RoboTwin 2.0 ([Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib47)) and contains 42 simulated bimanual tasks in five
capability groups, Generalization, Precision, Long-horizon, Memory and
Open. Each task is scored from 0 to 100, where full success scores 100
and many tasks give partial credit for intermediate stages. The official
evaluation runs 50 episodes per task on different layouts, 2,100
episodes in all, and averages tasks within each group and then the five
groups with equal weight.

Our starting point is L3 Inspect EEF, the official L3 harness released
with RoboProbe ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)) and the one behind the
GPT-6 Astra entry on the RoboDojo leaderboard. RoboProbe sorts
model-driven robot systems by what sits in the action path, and at its
third level (L3) the model acts through a fixed, non-learned harness
with no learned policy in between. Each episode opens with the task
instruction and a task recipe from the RoboDojo wiki, which describes
the task and its scoring stages without giving an action sequence. At
each decision the model sees three RGB views, from the head and both
wrists, and the measured grasp-point and joint state of both arms. It
acts by calling move_eef with world-frame targets for the grasp point of
either arm, given as position, orientation and gripper opening, or
give_up to end an episode it judges unrecoverable. Non-learned code
plans a joint path to each target with CuRobo ([Sundaralingam et al.,
2023](#bib.bib31)), the simulator plays it back, and the next
observation reports how far the grasp point ended up from its target.
The model decides what to do next and where to move, and the planner
decides how the arm gets there. The model receives no depth, object
poses, layout metadata or scoring signal, and the simulator alone
decides whether an episode succeeds. The official harness keeps images
from the last two observations only, and an episode ends at the latest
when the benchmark’s limit of 500 control steps runs out.

### 2.2 Testbed

We run the same loop in a Codex ([OpenAI, 2026a](#bib.bib23)) session at
medium reasoning effort. Codex already manages a long session and
compacts the context as it nears the window limit, so we did not rebuild
these parts ourselves. We use it only to hold the conversation. Its own
tools, such as the shell, web search and its image viewer, are switched
off. The definitions of move_eef and give_up are given to the model as
text in a fixed instruction, the model returns each decision as one JSON
object that holds its note and its calls, and the unchanged code of the
official harness parses the calls, plans each motion and executes it.
The planner and its inverse kinematics are those of the official
harness.

Within one episode the session appends every observation, action and
feedback message, images included, and nothing carries over from one
episode to the next. Every experiment runs on Codex 0.153.4, pinned so
that small differences between versions cannot move the results. An
episode has three separate limits, the number of model decisions, the
control steps executed per motion and the benchmark’s own limit of 500
control steps. All controlled comparisons allow 100 decisions per
episode, and only the final configuration of JustRUA lifts this limit
([Section 5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 Coordinate Solving ‣ 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
Apart from the task recipes inherited from the official harness, no
configuration contains a task-specific tool, prompt or piece of code
([Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Testbed Details ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).

### 2.3 Four Axes

We group the designs that recent systems add along four axes
([Figure 2](#S2.F2 "In 2 Controlled Study Framework ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
The _harness_ axis covers the loop around the model, that is, how much
of the episode it keeps and how hard the model reasons. The _action_
axis covers how a decision becomes motion, namely the command format and
how long each motion runs before the model looks again. The _reusing
experience_ axis covers what the model carries over from other episodes,
ordered by how far the experience is condensed, from a visual
demonstration to a text trajectory and a library of operations the
system writes for itself. The _scene geometry_ axis covers what each
observation says about the 3D layout of the scene, from the robot’s own
readings to depth images, precomputed affordances and coordinates the
model solves in code.

We move along the axes in this order and keep a design when it helps on
the evaluation set at a reasonable cost and does not draw on the tasks
being scored. Each alternative is therefore compared with the
configuration kept at the point where it was tested, and the kept
designs form a single path from the official harness to JustRUA
([Figure 1](#S0.F1 "In JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
This is a path and not a full factorial design. It tells us whether a
design helps on top of what is already kept, which is the question a
system builder faces, and it does not test every combination.

### 2.4 Evaluation Set

Running 2,100 episodes for every design is costly, since one episode
reads several million input tokens. Running one episode per task is
cheap but unreliable, because the score of a task on a single layout is
spiky, a known hazard of robot policy evaluation ([Kress-Gazit et al.,
2024](#bib.bib51)). To find where the swings come from, we ran the
default testbed
([Section 3](#S3 "3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents"))
for eight rounds at both low and medium reasoning effort, each round
taking the next layout of every task in a fixed order. Most of the
swings came from one kind of task, where success scores 100, any partial
result scores at most 50, and the model succeeds on some layouts but not
on others, so that a single near-miss moves the task by 50 points or
more. We say such a task has high evaluation uncertainty.

The evaluation set keeps all 42 tasks and spends repeated episodes where
a single one is least reliable. A task runs on four fixed layouts if a
partial result scores at most 50 and the default testbed both succeeded
and failed on it within the eight rounds. This selects 23 tasks, which
are exactly the 23 with the highest variance across rounds
([Figure 3](#S2.F3 "In 2.4 Evaluation Set ‣ 2 Controlled Study Framework ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")a).
The other 19 tasks run on one layout, for 111 episodes in total. Layouts
differ noticeably in difficulty, so they are fixed in advance and shared
by every configuration, and repeated tasks gain no extra weight. Scores
are averaged over the layouts of a task, then over the tasks of a group,
then over the five groups, as in the official score.

Figure 3: Evaluation set. (a) Standard deviation of each task’s score

A bootstrap over the eight rounds gives the standard deviation of the
overall score for each way of spending the budget
([Figure 3](#S2.F3 "In 2.4 Evaluation Set ‣ 2 Controlled Study Framework ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")b).
The evaluation set varies less than running every task three times, with
fewer episodes. For the official harness its score also stays close to
that of the full evaluation
([Table 1](#S3.T1 "In 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
The evaluation set is a tool for comparing designs under fixed
conditions, not a substitute for the full evaluation, and
[Appendix D](#A4 "Appendix D Evaluation Details ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
lists its tasks and its known differences from the official protocol.

### 2.5 Behaviour Review and Cost

#### First round.

The first round runs the first layout of each task, 42 episodes, and is
a subset of the evaluation set. A score from the first round alone tends
to run high
([Appendix D](#A4 "Appendix D Evaluation Details ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")),
so we compare configurations on the evaluation set and use the first
round to study behaviour and cost.

#### Behaviour review.

A score says how much of a task was completed, not how the attempt went.
Two configurations with the same score can fail in different ways, one
by closing the gripper on nothing and another by dropping objects on the
way. For each configuration we therefore review the 42 first-round
episodes and count a small set of physical behaviours
([Figure 4](#S2.F4 "In Behaviour review. ‣ 2.5 Behaviour Review and Cost ‣ 2 Controlled Study Framework ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
Each episode is reviewed on its own by a separate model, GPT-6.1 Sol,
which takes no part in running the robot, from the full action log and
the videos of all three cameras. The reviewer scans each video at short
intervals and looks closely around every executed action, and each
counted event is tied to the frames that show it. A second pass checks
every episode’s counts against this evidence and the native logs. Only
events the evidence settles are counted, so the counts are lower bounds,
and we compare them only across configurations reviewed on the same
episodes with the same written definitions
([Appendix E](#A5 "Appendix E Behaviour Review ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
With one episode per task, the counts show a direction rather than
settle small differences.

![Refer to caption](2611.16009v1/figures/web/fig_behaviours.jpg)

Figure 4: Behaviours counted in the review, head camera, three moments
of one episode each, from top to bottom. Rings mark the object, or in

#### Cost.

We report what an episode costs rather than how long it takes. Time can
be hidden by running episodes in parallel, and at this stage we are
measuring what the system can do, not deploying it. Cost cannot be
hidden. Because the full history is resent at every decision, an episode
reads several million input tokens and writes only a few thousand, and
nearly every input token has already been sent earlier in the same
episode. We therefore report the cost of one first-round episode with
all such tokens billed at the cached-input price. This lower bound does
not depend on how well the provider’s cache happened to work during a
run. Token use has also become a design target of its own for GPT-6
Astra agents ([Si et al., 2026](#bib.bib40)).

### 3.1 Codex and Full History

The official harness calls the model API directly and keeps images from
the last two observations only. Earlier observation text, notes, calls
and results stay in the context, while earlier images drop out. Our
testbed runs the episode in one Codex session that keeps every image.
The model can then draw on what it has already seen and tried, such as
an object that is now hidden behind the arm or the layout just before a
contact. This history covers the current episode only, so it is not a
form of experience reuse. In the default testbed the history also stays
complete, since Codex never had to compact it in the episodes we
inspected.

With the full history the evaluation-set score rises
([Table 1](#S3.T1 "In 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
Moving the loop into Codex without the full history did not help by
itself, so we credit the gain to the two together.

### 3.2 Motion Length

Watching the episodes, we found that CuRobo occasionally returns an
unstable plan, after which the arm makes a large and clearly
unreasonable motion. In the official loop the whole motion runs before
the model sees anything, so such a motion completes unchecked
([Figure 5](#S3.F5 "In 3.2 Motion Length ‣ 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
We keep the planner and its timing but execute at most the first 20
control steps of each plan, about 0.8 seconds of motion. The trajectory
is not resampled into 20 steps. The rest of the plan, including any
gripper command in it, is discarded rather than continued in the
background, and the gripper holds the last command that was actually
executed. The model then receives new images and the measured state. The
feedback is still computed against the original target and states how
many of the planned steps ran, so the model learns that the arm has not
arrived yet instead of being told that the move is complete. It may then
keep approaching the same target, or change the pose, the gripper or the
plan based on what it now sees.

![Refer to caption](2611.16009v1/figures/web/fig_motion.jpg)

The cap does not make the planner more accurate. It shortens the stretch
the arm moves without a new look, which limits how far a bad motion can
go and gives the model an earlier chance to correct it. The score rises
again, and the gain concentrates in the Precision group
([Table 1](#S3.T1 "In 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
Shorter motions mean more decisions, so an episode also costs more.

We also let the model choose the length of each motion itself, between 3
and 32 control steps, as in action chunking ([Zhao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib28); [Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib4)). Its choices split
between short and long motions, and it reaches the same overall score
with a different balance across groups, higher on Long-horizon and
Memory tasks and lower on Precision. Since the extra freedom buys
nothing overall, JustRUA keeps the fixed cap as the simpler choice. Full
history with the 20-step cap at medium effort is the _default testbed_
for the rest of the study.

### 3.3 Alternative Action Interfaces

Two alternatives to absolute targets come from recent systems, and
neither beats them
([Tables 1](#S3.T1 "In 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
and [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.3 Alternative Action Interfaces ‣ 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
[Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C Action Interfaces ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
defines each interface and shows one call of each kind at the same
moment of a task.

#### Relative targets.

Relative targets give position and orientation as offsets from the pose
measured at the current decision, as in RoboDawn ([Guo et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)). They score lower, much as Astranomy found ([Yang et
al., 2026a](#bib.bib4)), and the arm strikes objects almost three times
as often. One likely reason is that every coordinate the model reads,
from the task recipe to the measured pose, is given in the world frame,
so an offset asks for one more subtraction before each move.

#### Semantic operations.

Semantic operations replace raw targets with named skills ([Ahn et al.,
2022](#bib.bib20); [Liang et al., 2023](#bib.bib21); [Chen et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib38)). Ours are built on move_eef and only describe moves
at a higher level, with no new control code or learned policy. Some name
the purpose of a move, such as approach, transport or retreat. Some
merge several moves into one call, such as pick, which moves, closes and
lifts, or handover, in which one arm presents an object and the other
takes it. And adjust_view moves a wrist camera to see better, with
guidance to look before acting. The model used the named operations in
most of its calls, and they changed what it did more than how well it
did it. It moved its cameras more than three times as often and dropped
more of what it held, and the score did not rise.

#### Move sequences.

Allowing up to four moves per decision raised the score, mostly on
Long-horizon tasks. The gain has a simple source. Episodes need fewer
decisions, and the few episodes that used to run out of the budget of
100 decisions account for most of the difference
([Table 13](#A3.T13 "In Move sequences. ‣ Appendix C Action Interfaces ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
in
[Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C Action Interfaces ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
The gain therefore reflects the budget more than the interface, and the
final configuration of JustRUA removes the budget instead.

### 3.4 Reasoning Effort

Medium effort, the default, scores highest overall. Low effort costs
slightly less and gives up most of the Precision score. High effort
costs more and trades precision for memory. The two other settings play
differently enough from medium that we used low effort alongside medium
when building the evaluation set
([Section 2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 Evaluation Set ‣ 2 Controlled Study Framework ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")),
so that the choice of tasks does not rest on one style of play.

Takeaway 1: A lean harness goes a long way. Keeping the full episode
history and stopping each motion after 20 control steps lift the score
from 29.7 to 38.0. Interfaces that structure the action for the model,
such as relative targets, named skills and model-chosen step counts, add
nothing it cannot handle with plain absolute targets.

## 4 Reusing Experience

Experience from other episodes is the core of recursive self-improvement
(RSI) ([Zhou et al., 2026a](#bib.bib45)), and of everything we tried it
carries the greatest risk of gaming the benchmark. It draws on the tasks
being scored, so its scores are not comparable with a standard
evaluation. This is not to say that iteration is useless. A score
obtained with experience from the tasks being scored simply reports
something else, and the setting as a whole is not one that a regular
evaluation is meant for. We test experience reuse because it is widely
used, in the three forms of
[Section 2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Four Axes ‣ 2 Controlled Study Framework ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents"),
and we report what each changes in behaviour as well as in score. In our
runs no form clearly beat the default
([Table 1](#S3.T1 "In 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")),
and none of them comes for free.

### 4.1 Demonstrations

Demonstrations placed in context are a common way to help GPT-6 Astra
([SenseNova and Joint Research Team et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Liu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib37)). Both of our forms draw on one expert-curated
successful episode per task from RoboDawn ([Guo et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)), looked up by task name and inserted once before the
first decision. The _visual demonstration_ shows the 16 keyframes its
author selected, with no action trace or robot state attached. The _text
trajectory_ gives the same episode stage by stage, as the poses of both
arms, the commands issued and their outcomes, converted to our
coordinates, with failed commands kept and the author’s plans left out.
It takes about 16,000 characters per task on average
([Appendix F](#A6 "Appendix F Demonstrations ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
In both forms the model is told that the demonstration comes from a
different episode and that it should follow its order and geometry
rather than copy its coordinates.

With either demonstration the model passed objects through the table
more often and dropped fewer of them
([Table 3](#S4.T3 "In 4.1 Demonstrations ‣ 4 Reusing Experience ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
On the evaluation set neither moves the overall score clearly in either
direction
([Table 1](#S3.T1 "In 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).

#### The make_kong case.

make_kong shows why a better plan is not enough. In this task a tile has
to be flipped and added to a row, and a single arm cannot reach the flip
angle the tile needs and still place it. Without a demonstration the
model never hands the tile to the other arm
([Section 6](#S6 "6 Error Analysis ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
With one it copies the handover
([Figure 6](#S4.F6 "In The make_kong case. ‣ 4.1 Demonstrations ‣ 4 Reusing Experience ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
One arm flips the tile and sets it down at the table centre, and the
other carries it to the end of the row. The text trajectory carries this
handover only as coordinates, the left hand opening at a point where the
right hand later closes, and the model still found it. Neither run
copies the last step, where the demonstration grips the tile upright to
push it into the row, and both lose the tiles on that push and score 0.
The demonstration fixed the plan, and the episode then failed somewhere
else. Fixes of this kind are lost to failures elsewhere, so the overall
score barely moves while each episode costs more.

### 4.2 Self-Improvement Loop

Several RoboDojo entries improve themselves by writing and refining
their own skills ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14); [OpenSenseNova,
2026](#bib.bib15)). In our loop a Codex agent writes extra operations
for the model, as Python code built on move_eef, together with a guide
on when to use them. It starts from the RoboDawn demonstrations, from
which it writes a first library of four operations, grasp, transport,
place and push. The tasks are split into five groups. In each round the
model runs the current library on one group, and the agent then reviews
those runs, with their scores, action logs and videos, and revises the
library for the next round. Nothing is edited by hand. Unlike the
demonstrations, the library is not stored or looked up by task, and
every task sees the same operations and the same guide.

The agent’s changes follow the failures it saw
([Table 19](#A7.T19 "In Appendix G Self-Improvement Loop ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
in
[Appendix G](#A7 "Appendix G Self-Improvement Loop ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
After the model let go of a plug and a pen before they were in place,
place and grasp gained an option to stop before opening or closing, and
a new insert pushes a held tip into a hole once it is lined up. After
the model counted presses that never registered in play_Xylophone and
swap_blocks, the guide began to separate attempted presses from
confirmed ones. Most changes make an operation stop earlier so that the
model can check the result before going on, which echoes the 20-step cap
of
[Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Motion Length ‣ 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents").

The library changed how the model grasped, with fewer empty grasps and
no arm-to-arm collisions in the first round
([Tables 3](#S4.T3 "In 4.1 Demonstrations ‣ 4 Reusing Experience ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
and [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.2 Self-Improvement Loop ‣ 4 Reusing Experience ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
With each task practised once, it scored slightly higher than the loop’s
own runs on the tasks it had seen and stayed level with the default on
the evaluation set. The loop helps, but slowly.

### 4.3 Cost of Experience

The loop ran for five rounds and practised each task once, so we cannot
rule out larger gains at a larger scale. Systems that report clear gains
from self-improvement practise each task many more times than we did
([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14); [Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib16)). Within
that limit, the three forms share one pattern. Experience corrects the
behaviour it targets. Demonstrations made handovers more frequent and
drops rarer, and the library made grasps land more often. But a task
draws on many abilities at once, and correcting one of them rarely
changes the outcome, as make_kong shows.

The gains also come at a price. Every episode carries the extra context,
which raises its cost
([Table 3](#S4.T3 "In 4.1 Demonstrations ‣ 4 Reusing Experience ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")),
and the cost of producing the experience, expert episodes for the
demonstrations and practice runs for the library, comes on top. The
experience has to come from the tasks being scored, which is what makes
its scores hard to compare with a standard evaluation. For these reasons
JustRUA keeps no form of experience reuse, whatever its score.

Takeaway 2: Reusing experience is not a free lunch. Demonstrations and a
self-written skill library change how the model acts, but neither
clearly beats the default, and both make an episode more expensive. They
also draw on the tasks being scored, so their scores are not comparable
with a standard evaluation.

## 5 Scene Geometry

Recent agent systems feed the model depth, point clouds and affordances
from dedicated models ([Qu et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Zhou et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib6); [Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib10); [Kim et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11); [Yang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib9); [Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib36)). We add scene geometry in four steps and ask how much
of it GPT-6 Astra actually needs. Of the four axes this one helps most
([Table 1](#S3.T1 "In 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")),
and the behaviour counts say why
([Table 5](#S5.T5 "In 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
The model’s own grip readings, depth predicted from RGB and a little
code that turns pixels into 3D coordinates go a long way without
simulator ground truth, while precomputed affordances, the most
elaborate piece, did not help.

### 5.1 Robot Readings

Robot readings are the basic sensor values of each gripper, finger
positions and forces, contact force and wrist load. They carry no
privileged information, such as the true pose of any object. Their
clearest use is in grasping. The default interface reports the jaw
opening the model asked for, so after closing on an object the model
sees its own target. The readings give the opening the fingers actually
reached and the force at each fingertip, which tells the model whether
the object is held
([Table 7](#S5.T7 "In 5.1 Robot Readings ‣ 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).

How the readings are written matters as much as what they contain.
Information about the scene arrives at every decision and stays in the
history, so every extra character is paid many times over. Written as
full JSON, with vectors, thresholds and identifiers for every finger,
the readings crowded the context and pushed many first-round episodes
into context compaction, which the default testbed never reaches.
JustRUA writes them in a short text format with selected fields, fixed
units and rounded values ( in
[Appendix H](#A8 "Appendix H Scene Geometry Details ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
In this form no first-round episode was compacted, and empty grasps fell
slightly
([Table 5](#S5.T5 "In 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).

### 5.2 Depth

Many recent agent systems use depth to place objects in 3D ([Qu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8); [Zhou et al., 2026b](#bib.bib6); [Huang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10)), but the RoboDojo leaderboard ([RoboDojo Team,
2026](#bib.bib46)) does not accept depth read from the simulator for
agent entries, as a maintainer stated in review ([XPolicyLab
maintainers, 2026](#bib.bib32)). We therefore test both, depth from the
simulator and depth that Depth Pro ([Bochkovskii et al.,
2025](#bib.bib29)) predicts from each RGB image alone. In both cases
each RGB image is paired with a depth image that shows camera-forward
distance on a fixed colour scale, and the text adds the pose and
intrinsics of each camera and a few more gripper readings, such as the
finger gap and the contact points. Depth Pro receives nothing but the
RGB image. It estimates its own focal length, and we pass it no camera
parameters and fit no scale to its output. The instruction tells the
model that the predicted depth is an estimate and is least reliable
around thin objects and occlusion boundaries.

![Refer to caption](2611.16009v1/fig_depth.png)

Figure 7: Predicted depth. (a) One head-camera frame with the raw Depth
Pro prediction that the model receives, the same prediction scaled to
the robot geometry and simulator depth, on a shared colour scale. (b)
Behaviour counts with robot readings only, with simulator depth and with

The predicted distances are far off, by tens of centimetres even with
the true focal length
([Table 7](#S5.T7 "In 5.1 Robot Readings ‣ 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
A scale fitted to the robot’s own geometry in each frame brings the
error down to a few centimetres, but that scale drifts between frames
and cannot be fitted for the wrist cameras, so JustRUA passes the raw
predictions on
([Figure 7](#S5.F7 "In 5.2 Depth ‣ 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")a).

Even so, predicted depth improves manipulation almost as much as
simulator depth. Empty grasps fell sharply with either source, failed
fine placements fell further with predicted depth, and drops did not
fall with either
([Figure 7](#S5.F7 "In 5.2 Depth ‣ 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")b).
What the prediction gets right is the order of depths, and that seems to
be enough. The gain from depth does not need privileged information.

### 5.3 Affordances

Depth tells the model where surfaces are, but not where an object can be
held or which arm can reach it. That knowledge is an affordance. Recent
agent systems compute affordances with dedicated models and hand them to
the planner ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Yang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib9); [Li et al., 2026c](#bib.bib39)), and since many
episodes go wrong at the grasp, we tested whether giving the model this
knowledge directly would help. A pretrained grasp generator, GraspGen
([Murali et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)), proposes up to eight grasp poses on
the point cloud of each object, and inverse kinematics tests which
surfaces each arm can reach with its current tool orientation. Both are
drawn onto the camera images, and the text lists the grasp poses along
with hints such as which candidates a camera cannot see.

The real difficulty lies in presenting all this. Each observation
already carries the camera views and their depth, and anything added
either takes more space or crowds what is already there. We tried four
presentations
([Figure 8](#S5.F8 "In 5.3 Affordances ‣ 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")),
which differ in whether the marks are drawn over the camera view or
shown next to it, and in how many images an observation then holds.

Against depth images from the same source, none of the four grasped more
reliably or scored higher
([Tables 1](#S3.T1 "In 3 Harness and Action ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
and [5](#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
The best of them, the affordance panel, matches predicted depth in score
with about twice as many empty grasps. The moments where affordances
could make a difference are also few. Reach matters most when one arm
cannot get to an object and hands it to the other, and handovers stayed
rare in every version. Grasp candidates matter only in the decisions
that start a grasp. The cost, in contrast, comes with every observation.
Information that is needed only now and then is a poor fit for an
observation that is paid for at every decision, and we left affordances
out of JustRUA.

### 5.4 Coordinate Solving

In the last step the model may write and run code in its Codex session,
as in Code as Policies ([Liang et al., 2023](#bib.bib21)) and, more
recently, URAI ([Ge et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)) and HexaShell ([Gao et
al., 2026](#bib.bib35)). Web search stays off so that the model cannot
look up the benchmark or its solutions, and the simulator waits while
the code runs. At the same time the 100-decision cap that all earlier
comparisons keep is lifted, while the official limit of 500 control
steps still applies. The step therefore changes two things together, and
we report it as one.

It is also the most expensive step, since episodes run longer and every
code run adds to the history. Within the first 100 decisions, grasping
and placing stay at the level predicted depth had reached
([Table 5](#S5.T5 "In 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).

Takeaway 3: Scene geometry helps most. Depth images cut empty grasps
from 41 to 16, even when Depth Pro predicts the depth from RGB alone and
is off by tens of centimetres. With code that solves 3D coordinates from
pixels and no decision cap, the score reaches 50.3. Precomputed
affordances, the most elaborate form of geometry we tried, did not help.

## 6 Error Analysis

With the final configuration, 21 of the 42 first-round tasks score below 50. Whether these failures share a cause decides what further gains
would take, one more general fix or work on each task. We watched every
such episode and sorted the failures by cause
([Tables 10](#S6.T10 "In 6 Error Analysis ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
and [10](#S6.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ 6 Error Analysis ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
Some episodes fail for more than one reason, and most failures turn out
to be specific to one task
([Table 20](#A9.T20 "In Appendix I Error Analysis Details ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
in

![Refer to caption](2611.16009v1/figures/web/fig_errors.jpg)

Figure 10: One failure of each cause, three moments of the episode from

#### Four causes.

Strategy failures come from plans that do not suit the arm. In
make_kong, a single arm cannot reach the flip angle the tile needs and
still place it correctly. Nothing tells the model this about the arm, so
it never splits the job with a handover. The pouring tasks fail in a
similar way, since the model pours forward, a motion the arm does
poorly, where a side pour would work. Collisions are unplanned knocks,
such as the arm pushing a light box out of place, after which the run
looks complete but scores low. Unstable grasps occur with irregular or
round objects, such as a doll that slips when the arm moves too fast, or
a coin that offers few stable grasp points. The remaining cases look
easy, and the prompt or the scoring seems to be at fault. In
fasten_screws, for instance, the screws go in, but the model may be
unable to tell whether it met the tolerance the scorer requires.

#### Possible fixes.

Each of the first three causes points to a general direction, but each
needs knowledge that is costly to supply. Planning would need high-level
knowledge of the arm, such as which flip angles one arm cannot reach
while placing, and which limits matter changes from task to task.
Collision checking would need complete digital assets of the arm and of
every object it holds, and modelling every held object at that level is
hardly feasible. Grasp planning would need the same assets to model how
each irregular object sits in the gripper. For the prompt and scoring
cases, rewriting the recipes of the five tasks so that they state the
conditions the scorer checks raises the first-round score slightly
([Table 10](#S6.T10 "In 6 Error Analysis ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents"),
[Appendix I](#A9 "Appendix I Error Analysis Details ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")).
The gain is small because these tasks are not all easy in other
respects, and pack_objects_into_box, for one, also fails by collision.
This revision draws on the scorer of each test task, so we report it
here and do not count it in JustRUA.

The other failures are hard to remove without trial and error on each
task, which comes close to tuning on the test tasks themselves. We read
this as a sign that, for a general harness with no task-specific
component, the remaining headroom on this benchmark lies mostly in its
long tail.

Takeaway 4: Remaining failures are mostly long-tail. Most failures left
in our best configuration are specific to one task, such as plans that
do not suit the arm, unplanned knocks and slipping grasps. Fixing them
would take trial and error on each task, which comes close to tuning on
the test tasks.

## 7 Related Work

#### Frontier models as robot policies.

Vision-language-action models learn robot control from large robot
datasets on top of pretrained vision-language backbones ([Brohan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib24); [Kim et al., 2024](#bib.bib25); [Black et al.,
2025](#bib.bib26); [Physical Intelligence et al., 2025](#bib.bib27);
[Physical Intelligence et al., 2026](#bib.bib50); [Yu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib49)). A more recent line drives the robot with a
general-purpose model or agent that receives no robot training at all
([Jia et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)). For GPT-6 Astra, RoboProbe ([Zhang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib2)) provides the first evaluation on RoboDojo
together with the official L3 harness, Galbot ([Galbot Team et al.,
2026](#bib.bib3)) and SenseNova ([SenseNova and Joint Research Team et
al., 2026](#bib.bib7)) study its abilities more broadly, and Astranomy
([Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib4)) treats it as a native robot policy
with action chunking. Systems such as RoboDawn ([Guo et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)), GPT-GEAR ([Zhou et al., 2026b](#bib.bib6)),
RoboHarness ([Qu et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)), ORA ([Yang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib9)) and uni-robot-agent ([Yuan et al., 2026](#bib.bib13))
each build a harness with its own interfaces and perception. We do not
propose another system. We test which of their components matter when
all of them are measured under one protocol, and the configuration we
end with is a by-product of that test.

#### Skills and code.

Named skills are a common way to connect language models to robots ([Ahn
et al., 2022](#bib.bib20)), and code lets a model compose perception and
control by itself ([Liang et al., 2023](#bib.bib21); [Huang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib22); [Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)). URAI ([Ge et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)) lets frontier agents write, call and evolve their own
robot tools, and CLARO ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) orchestrates
open-vocabulary grasping with VLM agents and dedicated models. Our
semantic operations and coordinate solving follow these two lines while
using only what the official harness already provides. We find the two
to be of unequal value for GPT-6 Astra. Skills that restate its moves at
a higher level add little, while code that computes where to move adds a
lot.

#### Experience and self-improvement.

Language agents can improve across attempts by storing skills ([Wang et
al., 2024](#bib.bib18)) or verbal reflections ([Shinn et al.,
2023](#bib.bib19)), and ASPIRE ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib44)) builds
such a skill library for robots. On RoboDojo, several systems refine
their own skills over many rounds ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14);
[OpenSenseNova, 2026](#bib.bib15); [Yu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib17)) or
scale online exploration ([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)), and
PhysRSI-eval ([Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib16)) studies where the gains of
such loops come from. These systems practise each task many times. We
test a lighter regime, a single pass over the tasks, and look at what
experience changes in behaviour as well as in score.

#### Controlled studies.

Our study follows a line of work that explains a result by taking a
system apart rather than by adding to it, in the spirit of JustRL ([He
et al., 2026](#bib.bib52)). For software agents, the design of the
agent-computer interface is known to change what a model can do ([Yang
et al., 2024](#bib.bib54)), and a study of Claude models on robotics
tasks reports that performance depends strongly on how the model is
connected to the robot ([Berman et al., 2026](#bib.bib42)). Astranomy
([Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib4)) does so for GPT-6 Astra as a native
robot policy, and PhysRSI-eval ([Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib16)) for
self-improvement loops. We cover the harness, the action interface,
experience and scene geometry under one evaluation set, one behaviour
review and one cost measure.

## 8 Conclusion

Starting from the official L3 harness, JustRUA keeps three kinds of
change. The loop keeps the full episode history and stops each motion
early, the observation carries robot readings and predicted depth, and
the model may solve coordinates in code. Together they raise the
evaluation-set score from 29.7 to 50.3 with no learned policy, no
self-improvement loop and no task-specific tuning. Alternative action
interfaces, precomputed affordances and reused experience did not earn
their place under our protocol, and most of the remaining failures are
specific to single tasks.

Two observations run through these results. First, GPT-6 Astra handles
the plain interface well. World-frame targets, raw sensor values and
uncalibrated depth are enough for it, and layers that restructure its
actions or pre-digest its observations mostly add cost. Second, what
helps is information that bears on the errors the model makes often,
such as closing on nothing or misjudging where an object is, delivered
in a form short enough to pay for at every decision.

#### Limitations.

All experiments run in simulation, on one benchmark and with one model,
and each configuration is run once on the evaluation set. Each
alternative is tested at the point of the path where it was added, not
in every combination. The experience runs draw on the tasks being
scored, and the self-improvement loop ran for only five rounds with one
practice run per task. The evaluation set was chosen from the history of
our own default testbed, and it weights random layouts more heavily than
the official protocol does in the Generalization group, so its scores
are not leaderboard scores.

#### Observation and feedback.

At each decision the model receives the three RGB views, the measured
grasp-point pose, gripper opening and joint state of both arms, and
feedback on the previous command, including how far the grasp point
ended up from its target and, under the 20-step cap, how many planned
steps ran. At every step we also log the requested target, the measured
state after execution and the remaining error, so that a failed grasp
can be traced to a wrong target, a poor plan or an imprecise execution.

#### Budgets.

An episode has three separate limits, the number of model decisions, the
control steps executed per motion and the benchmark’s own limit of 500
control steps. All controlled comparisons keep 100 decisions per episode
and, from the default testbed on, at most 20 control steps per motion.
The decision limit is lower than the 170 calls allowed in the published
run of the official harness ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)). The final
configuration of JustRUA lifts the decision limit and keeps the other
two, and an episode ends when either remaining limit runs out.

#### The 20-step cap.

The cap is a thin wrapper around the unchanged planner, and it follows a
few fixed rules. The original planner and its retiming run as before.
Only a planned path longer than 20 control steps is cut, and the first
20 steps run with their original values, so the trajectory is never
resampled. The rest of the path is discarded for good, including any
gripper command in it, and the gripper returns to the last command that
actually ran. The feedback keeps the original target, reports the
remaining error to it and states the exact number of steps that ran. No
collision filter, refusal rule, replay of the discarded part or
alternative controller is added.

#### Episode history.

The Codex session keeps every observation, action and feedback message
of the episode, images included, until Codex compacts the context near
its window limit. In the default testbed this did not happen, since
requests stayed well below the size at which Codex compacts.
Configurations that add long text to every observation can reach the
limit, as the full-JSON readings of
[Section 5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Robot Readings ‣ 5 Scene Geometry ‣ JustRUA: A Controlled Study ofGPT-6 Robot-Use Agents")
did in 38% of first-round episodes.

sequences it sent more than one move in a sizeable share of its
decisions. With a model-chosen step count its choices split between
short motions of a few steps and long ones near the upper limit. With

library operations made up a small share of its calls and the rest went

#### How much the evaluation set helps.

On the eight rounds used to choose them, the 23 tasks account for about
95% of the task-level variance in the overall score. At medium effort
the bootstrap standard deviation of the overall score is 4.8 points for
a single round and 2.6 for the evaluation set, close to the 2.4 of
running every task four times with 168 episodes. The bootstrap resamples
each task’s rounds independently, so it measures sensitivity to the
layouts in our history.

#### Fixed layouts.

The official evaluation scores each task over 50 episodes, since the
score of a task on any single layout is spiky. We run far fewer
episodes, so which layouts we run matters. Each round runs the next
layout of every task in a fixed order, and rounds are not equally hard.
Every configuration in this paper therefore runs on the same fixed
layouts, in the first round and on the evaluation set alike, rather than
on fresh random draws.

#### Layout order and weighting.

Every round uses seed 0 and the next layout of each task in a fixed
order. For the twelve Generalization tasks, which have both standard and
random layouts, rounds alternate between the two, starting with random
layout 0, so the Generalization group of the evaluation set weights
random layouts at 75% against 50% in the official evaluation. We report
evaluation-set and full scores separately for this reason. The 23
four-layout tasks were chosen from the history of the default testbed,
which does not guarantee that the evaluation set ranks every new design
as the full benchmark would.

## Appendix E Behaviour Review

Each first-round episode is reviewed by its own GPT-6.1 Sol agent at
medium effort, which takes no part in running the robot. The agent reads
the complete action log, including the note the model writes with each
call, and the videos of the three cameras. It scans each view at short
intervals, checks consecutive frames around every executed action, every
suspicious moment and the start and end of each event, and records each
event with its time range, the views that show it and the frames used as
evidence. Events that span several actions, such as repeated empty
grasps ending in a regrasp or a handover in several stages, are linked
into one record. A main agent then checks the coverage of each review
and every episode’s counts against this evidence and the native logs,
and sends episodes that do not match back for another review. Success
and score come only from the simulator.

#### Depth images.

Depth from either source is drawn as camera-forward distance in metres
on a fixed colour scale from 0 to 2 m, one image per camera, labelled
with the camera it belongs to. With depth the text of each observation
also gives the pose, intrinsics and resolution of the three cameras, and
a few more gripper readings, namely the gap between the fingers, the
velocity of the grasp point, how long the current contact has lasted
with its recent peak force, and the contact points. For predicted depth,
Depth Pro runs on each RGB image as it is captured. It receives no
measured focal length, camera parameters or simulator depth, its output
is not rescaled, and a failed prediction is reported as unavailable
while the RGB image is kept. The instruction states that predicted depth
is an estimate, that it is least reliable around thin objects, occlusion
boundaries and unfamiliar scenes, and that it does not show contact or
millimetre-level geometry.

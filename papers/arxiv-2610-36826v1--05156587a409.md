###### Abstract

Autonomous agents increasingly depend on harnesses that govern
interaction, memory, tools, and execution around large language models
(LLMs). While recent work has begun to evolve these harnesses from
execution experience, the supervision used to guide evolution often
remains relatively static, making it difficult to track changing
behavioral weaknesses across rounds and potentially limiting
generalization to unseen tasks. To address this issue, we propose
RuHarness, a rubric–harness co-evolution framework for continual agent
self-improvement. RuHarness continually discovers and reassesses
behavioral criteria from execution trajectories, prioritizes the most
relevant unresolved weaknesses, and uses them to guide targeted harness
updates while keeping the task objective fixed. Extensive experiments
across diverse agent tasks and backbone models show that RuHarness
outperforms strong harness-evolution baselines, particularly on unseen
held-out tasks. Further analyses show that adaptive rubric
prioritization tracks shifting weaknesses and supports more sustained
improvement and stable generalization across evolution rounds.

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2610.36826v1/Intro.png)

Figure 1: Illustration of rubric–harness co-evolution. Static rubrics
may fall behind as behavioral weaknesses shift during harness evolution,
while co-evolving rubrics track emerging failures and guide harness
improvement.

As autonomous agents become increasingly prevalent, their performance
depends not only on the capabilities of the underlying large language
model (LLM) but also on the surrounding harness that governs
interaction, memory, tools, and execution ([Luo et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2); [Pan et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)). Recent work has begun
to evolve the harness itself from execution experience, enabling agents
to improve without updating model parameters ([Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib14); [Lin et al., 2026b](#bib.bib3); [Lee et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib36)). While such approaches can yield substantial gains,
recent controlled evaluations suggest that these improvements may weaken
on unseen tasks ([Wang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib17)), raising questions
about whether harness optimization produces generalizable improvement.

One challenge behind this limited generalization is that repeated
harness optimization may gradually overfit to task-specific failure
patterns exposed during evolution ([Srikanth et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)).
Existing methods optimize harness against task-level feedback, which
indicates whether the agent succeeds but provides limited information
about the behavioral deficiencies underlying failure ([Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib14)). This limitation becomes increasingly important
during continual evolution: as the harness improves, previously dominant
failures may be mitigated, while subtler or previously unseen failure
patterns emerge ([Nie et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)).
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement")
illustrates that when the guidance used for evolution remains
coarse-grained and relatively static, its discriminative and guiding
value may gradually diminish ([Kim et al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Tian et
al., 2026](#bib.bib7)). Consequently, the system lacks an explicit
mechanism for tracking how the importance of different weaknesses
changes throughout evolution. Continual harness evolution requires
adaptive diagnosis that continually reassesses and reprioritizes
behavioral weaknesses while keeping the underlying task objective
fixed ([Dou et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Zhang et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib37)).

To this end, we propose RuHarness (Rubric–Harness Co-Evolution), a
framework for continual agent self-improvement through joint rubric and
harness evolution. RuHarness continually derives behavioral criteria
from execution trajectories, reassesses their relevance under the
current harness, and prioritizes those that best capture unresolved
weaknesses. The resulting rubric frontier guides targeted and
coordinated updates to the corresponding harness components, while
candidate harnesses are selected using task performance on an
independent validation set. By adapting the diagnostic guidance as agent
behavior changes while keeping the task objective fixed, RuHarness
continually focuses harness optimization on the weaknesses that matter
most at each stage of evolution.

Extensive experiments across diverse agent tasks and backbone models
demonstrate that RuHarness consistently outperforms strong
harness-evolution baselines, with particularly clear gains on unseen
held-out tasks. The analyses also show that the rubric frontier adapts
to shifting behavioral weaknesses, concentrates on diagnostically
relevant criteria, and supports more sustained improvement across
evolution rounds. These results indicate that co-evolving rubrics with
the harness provides adaptive supervision for continual agent
improvement, supporting both continued evolution across evolution rounds
and stronger generalization.

## 2 Related Work

Self-Evolving Agents. Agent self-evolution has gradually shifted from
storing interaction experience to optimizing persistent external state.
Reflection- and memory-based methods refine experience to improve future
decisions ([Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib9); [Zhong et al.,
2023](#bib.bib10)), while skill evolution treats reusable skills as
editable knowledge that can be refined from execution feedback ([Zhu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib45); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)). These
paradigms extend naturally to the broader agent harness, where the
optimization target expands from a single external component to the
execution infrastructure, including prompts, tools, memory, and control
logic ([Zhang et al., 2026d](#bib.bib13)). Recent harness-evolution
methods seek to diagnose failures from trajectories and iteratively
revise this infrastructure ([Jiang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib38)), with
subsequent work broadening the editable space and making the evolution
process more observable and verifiable ([Lee et al., 2026b](#bib.bib15);
[Lin et al., 2026a](#bib.bib16)). More broadly, recent studies have
explored recursive self-improvement and harness–model co-evolution
across repeated adaptation rounds ([Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib42); [Yu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib40); [Chen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib39); [Fan and
Huang, 2026](#bib.bib41)). Yet these approaches still largely treat the
diagnostic signals guiding evolution as fixed, leaving how such guidance
should adapt to shifting behavioral weaknesses underexplored.

Rubrics for Self-Improvement. Rubrics have evolved from structured
evaluation criteria into a flexible interface between evaluation and
optimization ([Chen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib18)). By decomposing complex
objectives into explicit behavioral criteria, they provide fine-grained
supervision and have increasingly been incorporated into reward modeling
and reinforcement learning ([Gunjal et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [Li et
al., 2026a](#bib.bib19)). More recent work makes the rubric itself
adaptive: EvoLM ([Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib21)), EvoRubrics ([Ding et
al., 2026](#bib.bib22)), and DynamicRubric ([Wang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib23)) co-evolve evaluative criteria with the optimized
policy ([Guan et al., 2026](#bib.bib44)), while [Wang et al.
(2026b)](#bib.bib24) extends this idea to a self-evolving loop over
responses, rubrics, and policy. These studies suggest that effective
supervision should evolve with the behavior it guides ([Jiang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib25)). However, existing work mainly co-evolves rubrics
with policies or responses ([Li et al., 2026d](#bib.bib43)), leaving
rubric adaptation for continual harness evolution largely unexplored.

and guides subsequent harness
evolution (§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Harness Evolution: Optimizing against the Moving Frontier ‣ 3 Method ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement")),

### 3.1 Overview of the Framework

RuHarness establishes a co-evolution process between the diagnostic
rubric and the agent harness through iterative interaction. At evolution
round $`t`$, the system state is represented by
$`\mathcal{S}_{t}=(H_{t},R_{t})`$, where $`H_{t}`$ denotes the current
harness and $`R_{t}`$ the maintained rubric pool. RuHarness updates the
diagnostic signal from the behavior exposed by the current harness and
uses the resulting signal to guide harness updates, thereby alternating
between rubric evolution and harness evolution.

Alternating evolution process. At each round, the current harness
$`H_{t}`$ is executed on a set of evolution tasks $`D_{\mathrm{evo}}`$
to collect agent trajectories $`\mathcal{T}_{t}`$. These trajectories
are analyzed to identify remaining failure patterns, which drive the
rubric update:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       R_{t+1}=\mathcal{E}_{R}\big(R_{t},\mathcal{T}_{t}\big),
       ```                                                      |     | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{E}_{R}`$ discovers new behavioral criteria and
reassesses existing ones from $`\mathcal{T}_{t}`$ detailed in
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Rubric Evolution: Adapting to Shifting Agent Weaknesses ‣ 3 Method ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement").
The updated rubric pool is then prioritized to obtain a set of
high-priority criteria $`\widehat{R}_{t+1}`$, which captures the
behavioral weaknesses most relevant to the current harness. These
criteria guide the rubric-conditioned harness update:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       H_{t+1}=\mathcal{E}_{H}\big(H_{t},\widehat{R}_{t+1}\big),
       ```                                                        |     | (2) |

where $`\mathcal{E}_{H}`$ denotes the harness evolution procedure
detailed in
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Harness Evolution: Optimizing against the Moving Frontier ‣ 3 Method ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement").
The updated harness induces a new behavioral distribution in the next
round, providing fresh evidence for subsequent rubric evolution and
closing the co-evolution loop. The two processes are therefore coupled
through agent behavior: the harness shapes the weaknesses that emerge,
while the rubric determines which weaknesses are prioritized to guide
subsequent evolution.

### 3.2 Rubric Evolution: Adapting to Shifting Agent Weaknesses

Given trajectories from $`H_{t}`$, RuHarness summarizes each failed run
into a structured failure report and aggregates these reports to
identify recurring cross-task behavioral patterns. These patterns are
converted into new criteria $`\mathcal{R}_{t+1}^{\mathrm{new}}`$, which
capture recurring weaknesses across tasks while remaining verifiable
from trajectory evidence.

Outcome-blind behavioral assessment. Newly discovered criteria are
evaluated together with existing criteria on trajectories generated by
the current harness. For each criterion $`c`$ and trajectory $`\tau`$,
an outcome-blind judge assigns:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       J(c,\tau)\in\{\text{Satisfied},\text{Violated},\text{N/A}\},
       ```                                                           |     | (3) |

where the judge has access only to behavioral evidence in the trajectory
and is blinded to the final task outcome. This prevents criterion
assessment from directly leaking success or failure labels. Here, N/A
indicates that the behavior specified by the criterion does not occur,
whereas Satisfied indicates that it occurs and is handled correctly.

Criterion admission and lifecycle. Based on these assessments, newly
discovered criteria first undergo a birth check stage before entering
the maintained rubric pool. Criteria with insufficient diagnostic value
are filtered at this stage, including those that are rarely applicable,
fail to identify violations on unsuccessful trajectories, or trigger
indiscriminately across outcomes. Criteria that pass this admission
stage enter a four-state lifecycle: probation, active, dormant, and
retired. Newly admitted criteria enter probation and are promoted to
active once they exhibit sufficient diagnostic value. Active criteria
become dormant when their associated failures are no longer observed,
and may be reactivated upon recurrence, while persistently uninformative
criteria are eventually retired. Only active criteria are subsequently
prioritized and used to guide harness evolution.

Time-varying diagnostic utility. To determine which active criteria are
most useful for the current harness, we assign each criterion a
time-dependent diagnostic utility:

|     |                                 |                                                                       |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle U_{t+1}(c)={}`$ | $`\displaystyle\alpha D_{t+1}(c)+\beta C_{t+1}(c)+\gamma P_{t+1}(c)`$ |     | (4) |
|     |                                 | $`\displaystyle+\delta N_{t+1}(c)-\lambda\mathrm{Red}_{t+1}(c),`$     |     |     |

where $`D_{t+1}(c)`$ denotes discrimination between successful and
failed trajectories, $`C_{t+1}(c)`$ measures the fraction of current
failures covered by the criterion, $`P_{t+1}(c)`$ measures predictive
validity with respect to task failure, $`N_{t+1}(c)`$ captures novel
failures identified by the criterion, and $`\mathrm{Red}_{t+1}(c)`$
penalizes behavioral redundancy with existing criteria. These statistics
are derived from the preceding behavioral assessments and the
corresponding task outcomes, and are recomputed for each harness state.
Consequently, $`U_{t+1}(c)`$ evolves with the harness: criteria
associated with mitigated failures decrease in priority, while those
capturing newly prominent failures receive higher utility.

Adaptive rubric frontier. Since statistics can be uncertain under
limited observations, we rank active criteria using a conservative
posterior estimate of diagnostic utility. Specifically, we estimate a
Dirichlet posterior from the joint task-outcome and criterion-assessment
counts and use the lower $`q`$-quantile of the induced utility
distribution:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\widetilde{U}_{t+1}(c)=Q_{q}\!\left[p\!\left(U_{t+1}(c)\mid\mathcal{T}_{t}\right)\right],
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\widetilde{U}_{t+1}(c)`$ denotes the conservative utility of
criterion $`c`$, and $`Q_{q}(\cdot)`$ is the $`q`$-th posterior
quantile. This reduces the influence of criteria whose high utility is
supported by only a few observations. We select the top-$`K`$ criteria
from the active rubric pool:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{R}_{t+1}=\operatorname{TopK}_{c\in R_{t+1}^{\mathrm{active}}}\widetilde{U}_{t+1}(c),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`R_{t+1}^{\mathrm{active}}`$ denotes the active criteria and
$`\widehat{R}_{t+1}`$ the resulting rubric frontier. The resulting
frontier prioritizes unresolved behavioral weaknesses that remain both
diagnostically informative and statistically reliable under the current
harness.

Further details of rubric evolution are provided in
Appendix [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Details of Diagnostic Utility ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement")–[A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Details of Posterior Estimation and Conservative Ranking ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement").

### 3.3 Harness Evolution: Optimizing against the Moving Frontier

To structure the harness search space, we represent the editable harness
using six functional coordinates: Equip, Instruct, Act, Observe,
Remember, and Terminate. These coordinates respectively capture resource
provisioning, behavioral instruction, action policy, observation
processing, context management, and completion control. Each coordinate
defines a corresponding set of editable components in the agent system.

Frontier-guided targeting. Given the current rubric frontier
$`\widehat{R}_{t+1}`$, RuHarness identifies the harness coordinates that
can directly address each prioritized behavioral weakness. The
criterion-to-coordinate mapping is not constrained to be one-to-one,
since a single failure pattern may involve multiple parts of the
harness. Instead, each criterion may target multiple coordinates,
allowing the corresponding interventions to be planned jointly.

Coordinated candidate generation. Based on these criterion-to-coordinate
assignments, RuHarness jointly plans a small set of candidate harness
updates. Each candidate specifies a concrete hypothesis for addressing
the diagnosed weaknesses and may modify multiple related coordinates
jointly. We represent each candidate as a coordinated set of
coordinate-level modifications:

|     |                                                          |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Delta_{j}=\left\{\delta_{j}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in M_{j}},
       ```                                                       |     | (7) |

where $`M_{j}`$ is the set of modified coordinates and
$`\delta_{j}^{(k)}`$ denotes the corresponding modification. The
coordinated modifications are applied to the current harness through the
update operator $`\oplus`$, yielding the candidate harness:

|     |                                    |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       H_{t}^{(j)}=H_{t}\oplus\Delta_{j}.
       ```                                 |     | (8) |

Each candidate is treated as a single multi-coordinate update,
preserving the interactions among jointly planned modifications. Before
evaluation, each candidate undergoes a lightweight verification step to
check whether the proposed modifications are consistent with the
diagnosed weaknesses and supporting evidence. The verifier only accepts
or rejects the candidate and does not rewrite it or estimate task
performance.

Validation-gated update. Candidates that pass verification are evaluated
together with the current harness on a separate set of validation tasks
$`D_{\mathrm{val}}`$, used exclusively for candidate selection and
excluded from both evolution and final evaluation. This separation
decouples candidate selection from the trajectories used to drive
evolution. Let $`S(H,D_{\mathrm{val}})`$ denote the average validation
score of harness $`H`$. Let $`\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{cand}}`$ denote
the set of candidate harnesses generated at round $`t`$. The candidate
with the highest validation score is selected as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H_{t}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{H\in\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{cand}}}S(H,D_{\mathrm{val}}),
``` |  | (9) |

and commit the update only if its improvement over the current harness
exceeds a threshold $`\epsilon`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H_{t+1}=\begin{cases}H_{t}^{\star},&S(H_{t}^{\star},D_{\mathrm{val}})-S(H_{t},D_{\mathrm{val}})>\epsilon,\\
H_{t},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (10) |

A candidate is advanced to the next evolution round only if its
validation improvement exceeds the acceptance threshold, with the
current harness retained otherwise.

## 4 Experimental Methodology

This section presents our experimental setup, covering models and
environment, benchmarks, metrics, baselines, and implementation details.

Models and Environment. We conduct experiments with
Qwen3.5-35B-A3B ([Team, 2026](#bib.bib26)) and GLM-4.7-Flash ([Team et
al., 2025](#bib.bib27)) as the backbone models. The agent is
instantiated with DeepAgent ([Li et al., 2026c](#bib.bib28)), which
provides the basic execution framework for tool use, filesystem
interaction, and multi-step task solving. Based on this framework, we
construct the initial harness and expose its configurable components for
subsequent evolution, while keeping the underlying model fixed
throughout the experiments. All stages of task execution and evolution
are performed using the corresponding backbone model itself.

Benchmarks and Metrics. Evaluation is conducted on EvoAgentBench ([Gao
et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)), a multi-domain benchmark for long-horizon
agent self-evolution. It covers five agent domains: information
retrieval based on BrowseComp-Plus ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)),
mathematical reasoning based on OmniMath ([Gao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib31)), software engineering based on SWE-Bench ([Jimenez et
al., 2024](#bib.bib33)), code implementation based on
LiveCodeBench ([Jain et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)), and knowledge work
based on GDPVal ([Patwardhan et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)). We use Pass@1
as the primary metric and evaluate each domain with its benchmark
evaluator ([Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)).

Baselines. For comprehensive comparison, we compare RuHarness against
representative self-evolving agent methods that optimize different
external states. ACE evolves persistent contextual knowledge from
interaction experience ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib35)), while
EvoSkill discovers and refines reusable skills through failure
analysis ([Alzubi et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)). For harness evolution, we
include Self-Harness, which mines model-specific weaknesses and proposes
targeted updates ([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14)); Meta-Harness,
which searches executable harness code using accumulated candidates and
trajectories ([Lee et al., 2026b](#bib.bib15)); and Agentic Harness
Engineering (AHE), which improves harnesses through observable and
verifiable component updates ([Lin et al., 2026a](#bib.bib16)).
Together, these baselines cover context, skill, and full-harness
evolution.

Implementation Details. We use disjoint evolution (*held-in*) and
validation splits, with the test set (*held-out*) reserved for final
reporting only. At each round, RuHarness generates up to three
coordinated harness candidates and accepts at most one update when its
validation improvement exceeds $`\epsilon=0.05`$. For diagnostic
utility, we set $`\alpha=\gamma=1.0`$ and $`\beta=\delta=\lambda=0.5`$.
Rubric guidance is obtained by ranking criteria with the $`q`$-th
posterior quantile ($`q=0.2`$) and selecting the Top-$`K`$ criteria with
$`K=10`$. Each candidate modifies at most three harness components. More
experimental details are provided in the
Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Experimental Details ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement").

### 5.1 Overall Performance

We evaluate RuHarness across five agent domains on EvoAgentBench using
Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and GLM-4.7-Flash, as shown in
Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement").
Overall, RuHarness outperforms all the baselines, achieving the best
average performance on both held-in and held-out sets across the two
backbone models.

Overall Effectiveness. On Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, RuHarness improves the
strongest baseline by 1.7 points on held-in and 4.5 points on held-out.
The gains are even larger on GLM-4.7-Flash, reaching 2.7 and 5.3 points,

Generalization to Unseen Tasks. The advantage of RuHarness is more

competitive or even better results on individual held-in tasks,
RuHarness attains the highest average held-out performance for both
models and exhibits a smaller held-in–held-out gap. This suggests that
its improvements are less tied to the evolution tasks and transfer more
effectively to unseen tasks.

Robustness across Tasks and Models. RuHarness performs consistently well
across heterogeneous tasks and both backbone models, achieving the
strongest average held-out performance and competitive results across
individual domains. This suggests that its gains are not tied to a
specific task type or backbone.

To examine how the diagnostic focus changes throughout harness
evolution, we measure the Jaccard similarity between rubric frontiers
across rounds. Specifically, each frontier is represented by its
selected criterion IDs, and we compare the frontier at round $`t`$ with
both the initial frontier $`F_{0}`$ and the frontier from the preceding
round $`F_{t-1}`$. We conduct this analysis on two representative
settings, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B on Code Implementation and GLM-4.7-Flash on

the similarity to the initial frontier decreases sharply in the early
rounds and remains low thereafter. By contrast, the similarity between
consecutive frontiers first declines, then gradually increases and
stabilizes at a relatively high level. These two trends reveal a

departs from its initial configuration, while changes between adjacent
rounds become increasingly gradual. This behavior suggests that early
harness updates substantially reshape the set of weaknesses that require
attention, whereas later evolution proceeds through more localized
adjustments. Overall, the results confirm that the behavioral weaknesses

for an evolving rubric that continuously tracks changes in the agent’s

To examine whether adaptive rubric prioritization translates into
subsequent behavioral improvement, we analyze criterion-level dynamics
around frontier transitions and after frontier entry. Specifically, we
first compare how the diagnostic components of criteria change when they
enter, remain in, exit, or stay outside the frontier. We then align
newly prioritized criteria at their frontier-entry round and track how
their violation rates evolve over subsequent rounds. To further relate
this trend to harness evolution, we additionally distinguish cases with
and without committed harness updates.

Frontier transitions track diagnostic relevance. To characterize how
diagnostic relevance changes with frontier membership, we group criteria
by their transitions between two adjacent evolution rounds: entering the
frontier, remaining in the frontier, exiting the frontier, or staying
outside the frontier. For each group, we measure the round-to-round
changes in the diagnostic utility components defined in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Rubric Evolution: Adapting to Shifting Agent Weaknesses ‣ 3 Method ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement"),

As expected, criteria entering the frontier show increases in
discrimination, coverage, and predictive validity together with reduced
redundancy, while exiting criteria exhibit the opposite pattern.
Criteria that remain inside or outside the frontier change substantially
less. These results confirm that frontier turnover reflects changes in
criterion-level diagnostic relevance rather than arbitrary replacement.

Prioritized weaknesses are progressively mitigated. To assess whether
frontier prioritization translates into subsequent behavioral
improvement,
Figure [4(b)](#S5.F4.sf2 "In Figure 4 ‣ 5.4 Effectiveness of Adaptive Rubric Prioritization on Harness Evolution ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement")
examines what happens after a criterion enters the frontier. The
violation rate remains nearly unchanged in the first round after entry,
but decreases noticeably over the following rounds. This delayed
reduction suggests that newly prioritized weaknesses are not necessarily
resolved immediately, but are progressively mitigated as subsequent
harness evolution proceeds. The trend therefore links frontier
prioritization with later behavioral improvement, suggesting that the
evolving rubric does more than reorganize diagnostic signals and helps
focus subsequent optimization on weaknesses that can be progressively
reduced.

Committed harness updates yield stronger longer-horizon mitigation. To
more directly relate behavioral improvement to harness evolution,
Figure [4(c)](#S5.F4.sf3 "In Figure 4 ‣ 5.4 Effectiveness of Adaptive Rubric Prioritization on Harness Evolution ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement")
compares frontier-entry criteria under committed and non-committed
harness updates, where *committed* denotes a candidate update that
passes the validation gate and is adopted for subsequent evolution. The
committed group shows a clear and sustained increase in violation
reduction over subsequent rounds, whereas the non-committed group
fluctuates without a consistent improvement trend. This contrast
indicates that frontier-guided prioritization is more likely to
translate into persistent behavioral gains when the proposed harness
update is successfully validated and adopted.

Taken together, these results show that adaptive rubric prioritization
tracks changing diagnostic relevance and helps focus subsequent harness
evolution on weaknesses that are progressively mitigated.

## 6 Conclusion

This paper presents RuHarness, a rubric–harness co-evolution framework
for continual agent self-improvement. RuHarness dynamically adapts
diagnostic criteria to the agent’s evolving behavioral weaknesses and
uses the resulting rubric frontier to guide targeted harness
optimization. Extensive experiments across diverse tasks and backbone
models show that RuHarness achieves strong gains in both held-in
performance and generalization to unseen held-out tasks. Further
analyses show that the rubric frontier tracks shifting diagnostic
relevance and supports sustained harness improvement over repeated
evolution rounds. These results highlight the importance of jointly
adapting diagnostic supervision and the agent harness for more sustained
and generalizable agent evolution.

## Limitations

Although RuHarness is evaluated across multiple tasks and backbone
models, all experiments are conducted within a single underlying agent
framework. Evaluating the six-coordinate harness abstraction on a
broader range of agent architectures would further establish its
generality. In addition, due to computational resource constraints, our
experiments are conducted on benchmark tasks under a fixed evolution
horizon. Examining longer-term and more open-ended adaptation settings
would provide a broader view of the resulting co-evolution dynamics.

### A.1 Experimental Details

In this subsection, we provide the dataset splits, harness abstraction
and editable interfaces, and prompt templates used throughout the
experiments.

Dataset Statistics and Settings. We conduct experiments on five agent
tasks derived from EvoAgentBench ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)),
covering information retrieval, knowledge work, software engineering,
code implementation, and mathematical reasoning. EvoAgentBench
reorganizes existing benchmarks into train and test sets to evaluate
whether improvements acquired during evolution transfer to unseen tasks.
For our experiments, we further split the original training set into
evolution and validation subsets, while preserving the original task
distribution. The evolution subset is used to drive rubric–harness
evolution, whereas the validation subset is used exclusively for
candidate selection. We also remove a small number of tasks that cannot
be reliably executed in our experimental environment. The resulting
dataset statistics are summarized in
Table [3](#A1.T3 "Table 3 ‣ A.1 Experimental Details ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement").

selection and is reserved exclusively for *held-out* evaluation. All

rounds, candidate budgets, and validation-based selection protocols are
matched across harness-evolution methods unless otherwise specified.

### A.2 Details of Diagnostic Utility

In this subsection, we provide the detailed definitions of the five
criterion-level statistics used to compute the diagnostic utility in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Rubric Evolution: Adapting to Shifting Agent Weaknesses ‣ 3 Method ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement").

For each criterion $`c`$, the outcome-blind judge assigns every
trajectory one of three labels: Violated, Satisfied, or N/A. Let
$`v_{\mathrm{pass}}`$, $`s_{\mathrm{pass}}`$, and $`n_{\mathrm{pass}}`$
denote the numbers of violated, satisfied, and non-applicable
trajectories among successful tasks. Similarly, let
$`v_{\mathrm{fail}}`$, $`s_{\mathrm{fail}}`$, and $`n_{\mathrm{fail}}`$
denote the corresponding counts among unsuccessful tasks. Based on these
statistics, the five components are defined as follows.

Discrimination ($`D`$): Measures how well a criterion distinguishes
unsuccessful trajectories from successful ones by comparing its
violation rates across the two groups:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D(c)=\frac{v_{\mathrm{fail}}}{v_{\mathrm{fail}}+s_{\mathrm{fail}}}-\frac{v_{\mathrm{pass}}}{v_{\mathrm{pass}}+s_{\mathrm{pass}}}.
``` |  | (11) |

Coverage ($`C`$): Measures the proportion of unsuccessful trajectories
for which the criterion identifies a violation:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C(c)=\frac{v_{\mathrm{fail}}}{v_{\mathrm{fail}}+s_{\mathrm{fail}}+n_{\mathrm{fail}}}.
``` |  | (12) |

Predictive Validity ($`P`$): Measures whether observing a criterion
violation increases the likelihood of task failure relative to the
criterion’s applicable population:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P(c)=\frac{v_{\mathrm{fail}}}{v_{\mathrm{fail}}+v_{\mathrm{pass}}}-\frac{v_{\mathrm{fail}}+s_{\mathrm{fail}}}{v_{\mathrm{pass}}+s_{\mathrm{pass}}+v_{\mathrm{fail}}+s_{\mathrm{fail}}}.
``` |  | (13) |

Equivalently,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P(c)=\Pr(\mathrm{fail}\mid\mathrm{violated})-\Pr(\mathrm{fail}\mid\mathrm{applicable}).
``` |  | (14) |

Novelty ($`N`$): Measures the proportion of failed trajectories uniquely
identified by criterion $`c`$. Let $`\mathcal{U}_{c}`$ denote the set of
failed trajectories violated only by criterion $`c`$, and let $`N_{F}`$
denote the total number of failed trajectories:

|     |                                       |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       N(c)=\frac{|\mathcal{U}_{c}|}{N_{F}}.
       ```                                    |     | (15) |

Redundancy ($`\mathrm{Red}`$): Measures the behavioral overlap between
criterion $`c`$ and the remaining criteria. Let $`\mathcal{V}(c)`$
denote the set of trajectories violated by criterion $`c`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{Red}(c)=\max_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\operatorname{Jaccard}\left(\mathcal{V}(c),\mathcal{V}(c^{\prime})\right).
``` |  | (16) |

### A.3 Detail of Rubric Admission and Lifecycle

In this subsection, we provide the detailed admission criteria and
lifecycle transition rules used to maintain the evolving rubric pool
introduced in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Rubric Evolution: Adapting to Shifting Agent Weaknesses ‣ 3 Method ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement").

Birth Check. Before entering the maintained rubric pool, each newly
discovered criterion undergoes a birth check designed to remove criteria
that are unlikely to provide useful diagnostic signals. We reject a
criterion under any of the following conditions: (i) never applies, when
more than 80% of its assessments are N/A; (ii) never fires, when it
produces no violations on unsuccessful trajectories; or (iii) fires on
all, when its violation rate exceeds 90% overall and also exceeds 90% on
successful trajectories. These checks remove criteria that are
respectively inapplicable, insensitive to actual failures, or
insufficiently discriminative. Criteria that pass the birth check enter
the lifecycle in the probation state. Since lifecycle transitions are
evaluated within the same evolution round, a newly admitted criterion
may already appear as active in the round-end visualization if it
immediately satisfies the promotion condition.

Lifecycle Transition Mechanism. Each admitted criterion is maintained in
one of four states: probation, active, dormant, or retired. State
transitions are evaluated after each evolution round according to the
criterion’s observed diagnostic statistics.

Probation. A newly admitted criterion first enters the probation state.
It is promoted to active once it exhibits positive predictive validity
($`P>0`$). If it remains untriggered for four consecutive rounds, it is
instead moved directly to retired.

Active. An active criterion is eligible for frontier construction. It
becomes dormant when its trigger rate remains below 5% for three
consecutive rounds or when the predefined N/A-drift condition persists
for three consecutive rounds. It is directly retired when its predictive
validity remains below $`0.10`$ for three consecutive rounds.

Dormant. A dormant criterion is retained for monitoring but excluded
from frontier construction. It is reactivated when its trigger rate
rises to at least 15%. If it remains dormant for four consecutive rounds
without reactivation, it is moved to retired.

Retired. The retired state is terminal. Retired criteria no longer
participate in criterion prioritization or frontier construction.
Table [5](#A1.T5 "Table 5 ‣ A.3 Detail of Rubric Admission and Lifecycle ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement")
summarizes all lifecycle transition rules.

|                 |                                      |              |
|-----------------|--------------------------------------|--------------|
|  Current State  |  Condition                           |  Next State  |
|  Probation      |  $`P>0`$                             |  Active      |
|  Probation      |  Untriggered for 4 rounds            |  Retired     |
|  Active         |  Trigger rate $`<5\%`$ for 3 rounds  |  Dormant     |
|  Active         |  N/A drift for 3 rounds              |  Dormant     |
|  Active         |  $`P<0.10`$ for 3 rounds             |  Retired     |
|  Dormant        |  Trigger rate $`\geq 15\%`$          |  Active      |
|  Dormant        |  Remains dormant for 4 rounds        |  Retired     |

### A.4 Details of Posterior Estimation and Conservative Ranking

In this subsection, we provide the details of the conservative ranking
strategy introduced in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Rubric Evolution: Adapting to Shifting Agent Weaknesses ‣ 3 Method ‣ When the Critic Falls Behind: Co-Evolving Rubrics and Harnesses for Continual Agent Self-Improvement").
Directly ranking criteria by their point-estimated utility can be
unreliable when only a small number of trajectories support a criterion,
since a few observations may produce an artificially high score. We
therefore rank criteria using a lower posterior estimate of their
diagnostic utility.

For each criterion, we construct a $`2\times 3`$ contingency table over
task outcome (pass or fail) and criterion assessment (Violated,
Satisfied, or N/A). Since discrimination, coverage, and predictive
validity are all computed from this same table, we estimate them
jointly. We place a symmetric Jeffreys prior over the six cells,
corresponding to adding $`0.5`$ to each observed count, and obtain a
Dirichlet posterior over the joint outcome distribution.

We draw $`B=2000`$ samples from this posterior. For each sample, we
recompute discrimination, coverage, and predictive validity, while
keeping novelty and redundancy fixed because they are computed from
trajectory-level set statistics. This produces a posterior distribution
over the diagnostic utility. We then use its lower $`q`$-quantile as the
conservative score:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widetilde{U}(c)=\operatorname{Quantile}_{q}\left(\left\{U^{(b)}(c)\right\}_{b=1}^{B}\right),
``` |  | (17) |

where $`q=0.2`$ in all experiments.

This lower-quantile ranking favors criteria whose diagnostic value
remains high under uncertainty, while reducing the influence of criteria
supported by only a few observations. The resulting scores are used to
rank active criteria, from which the Top-$`K`$ criteria are selected to
form the rubric frontier.

Knowledge Work. The first trajectory exhibits a clear progression in

generated scripts are actually executed*, leading to an Observe-level

finally to proactive verification.

Information Retrieval. The second trajectory shows a parallel
progression from coarse evidence checking to structured auditing. It
starts by requiring *full-document retrieval* instead of relying on
search snippets, establishing a stronger evidence basis for candidate
matching. The next refinement moves this requirement closer to the
retrieval step itself, preventing premature judgments immediately after
observing a snippet. The focus then advances from merely verifying a
candidate to making the verification *explicit and auditable*, requiring
the agent to state which constraints are satisfied and provide
supporting evidence. Finally, the process becomes *systematic*:
individual evidence statements are organized into a
constraint-satisfaction matrix so that all constraints are checked
collectively rather than independently. Thus, the diagnostic trajectory
evolves from source verification, to immediate verification, to explicit
evidence grounding, and finally to structured constraint coverage.

These trajectories illustrate that rubric evolution progressively raises
the granularity of behavioral supervision. As earlier requirements are
incorporated into the harness, later rubrics shift attention to
finer-grained weaknesses, which in turn induce more precise and
structured modifications across the relevant harness coordinates. This
creates a staged refinement process in which diagnosis and intervention
become progressively more operational as evolution proceeds.

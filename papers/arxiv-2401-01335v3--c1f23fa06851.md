###### Abstract

Harnessing the power of human-annotated data through Supervised
Fine-Tuning (SFT) is pivotal for advancing Large Language Models (LLMs).
In this paper, we delve into the prospect of growing a strong LLM out of
a weak one without the need for acquiring additional human-annotated
data. We propose a new fine-tuning method called Self-Play fIne-tuNing
(SPIN), which starts from a supervised fine-tuned model. At the heart of
SPIN lies a self-play mechanism, where the LLM refines its capability by
playing against instances of itself. More specifically, the LLM
generates its own training data from its previous iterations, refining
its policy by discerning these self-generated responses from those
obtained from human-annotated data. Our method progressively elevates
the LLM from a nascent model to a formidable one, unlocking the full
potential of human-annotated demonstration data for SFT. Theoretically,
we prove that the global optimum to the training objective function of
our method is achieved only when the LLM policy aligns with the target
data distribution. Empirically, we evaluate our method on several
benchmark datasets including the HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard,
MT-Bench, and datasets from Big-Bench. Our results show that SPIN can
significantly improve the LLM’s performance across a variety of
benchmarks and even outperform models trained through direct preference
optimization (DPO) supplemented with extra GPT-4 preference data. This
sheds light on the promise of self-play, enabling the achievement of
human-level performance in LLMs without the need for expert opponents.

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have began a groundbreaking era in
artificial general intelligence (AGI), demonstrating extraordinary
capabilities across a wide range of domains that require intricate
reasoning and specialized knowledge. These models excel in areas such as
mathematical reasoning/problem solving ([Cobbe et al.,
2021](#bib.bib21); [Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib90); [Lewkowycz et al.,
2022](#bib.bib49)), code generation/programming ([Chen et al.,
2021](#bib.bib14); [Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib4); [Li et al.,
2022](#bib.bib50)), text generation ([Bubeck et al., 2023](#bib.bib12);
[Anil et al., 2023](#bib.bib1); [Touvron et al., 2023](#bib.bib84)),
summarization and creative writing, among others. A significant
advancement in LLMs is the post-pre-training alignment with the more
desirable behaviors ([Mishra et al., 2021](#bib.bib59); [Victor et al.,
2022](#bib.bib88); [Chung et al., 2022](#bib.bib18); [Thoppilan et al.,
2022](#bib.bib83)), a process often reliant on the costly
human-annotated data. Typical alignment methods include Supervised
Fine-Tuning (SFT) ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib64); [Tunstall et al.,
2023a](#bib.bib85)) based on human demonstrations, and Reinforcement
Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) ([Christiano et al.,
2017](#bib.bib17); [Ziegler et al., 2019](#bib.bib101); [Stiennon
et al., 2020](#bib.bib80); [Bai et al., 2022a](#bib.bib5)) based on
human preferences.

All the aforementioned alignment methods require a substantial volume of
human annotated data. Therefore, there is increasing interest in
developing fine-tuning methods that can effectively utilize human data,
thereby streamlining the alignment process. This motivates us to study
fine-tuning LLMs without the need for additional human-annotated data
beyond the fine-tuning dataset. Our study is also related to the broader
goal of converting weak models to strong models without the requirement
for extra training data, which is of central interest in machine
learning that can be traced back to the boosting algorithms ([Kearns &
Valiant, 1994](#bib.bib42); [Schapire, 1990](#bib.bib74); [Freund,
1995](#bib.bib27); [Freund & Schapire, 1997](#bib.bib28)). The
self-training algorithm ([Vapnik, 1999](#bib.bib87); [Grandvalet &
Bengio, 2004](#bib.bib32); [Lee, 2013](#bib.bib46)) has also been proved
to be able to convert weak learners to strong learners in mixture models
without the need for additional labeled data ([Frei et al.,
2022](#bib.bib26); [Kou et al., 2022](#bib.bib43)). However, the pursuit
of autonomously enhancing a weak LLM without external guidance is both
intriguing and understudied. This raises the following question:

Can we empower a weak LLM to improve itself without acquiring additional
human annotated data?

In this paper, we answer this question affirmatively. Inspired by the
success of self-play mechanisms ([Samuel, 2000](#bib.bib72)) in games,
exemplified by AlphaGo Zero ([Silver et al., 2017b](#bib.bib76)),
AlphaZero ([Silver et al., 2017a](#bib.bib75)), with historical roots
traced back to TD-Gammon ([Tesauro et al., 1995](#bib.bib82)), we
propose to convert a weak LLM to a strong one through the lens of
self-play, where the model is enhanced by playing against itself without
requiring any direct supervision. In particular, we propose a novel
fine-tuning method called Self-Play fIne-tuNing (SPIN), which begins
from a supervised fine-tuned model. SPIN allows the LLM to engage in
self-play, eliminating the need for an expert annotator such as a human
or more advanced LLMs like GPT-4. In detail, with the LLM from previous
iteration $`t`$ denoted by $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$, we employ it to
generate responses $`\mathbf{y}^{\prime}`$ to the prompts $`\mathbf{x}`$
in the human-annotated SFT dataset. The subsequent objective is to find
a new LLM $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}`$, capable of distinguishing the
responses $`\mathbf{y}^{\prime}`$ generated by $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$
from the responses $`\mathbf{y}`$ generated by humans. This process can
be seen as a two-player game: the main player, or the new LLM
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}`$, seeks to discern between the responses of the
opponent player $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$ and human-generated responses,
while the opponent, or the old LLM $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$, generates
responses as similar as possible to those in the human-annotated SFT
dataset. The new LLM $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}`$ is obtained by
fine-tuning the old one $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$ to prefer responses from
$`p_{\mathrm{data}}`$ over $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$, resulting in a
distribution $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}`$ that is more aligned with
$`p_{\mathrm{data}}`$. In the next iteration, the newly obtained LLM
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}`$ becomes the opponent for response generation,
with the self-play process aiming for the LLM to eventually converge to
$`p_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}=p_{\mathrm{data}}`$, so that the strongest
possible LLM can no longer differentiate the responses generated by its
previous version and those generated by the human.

Interestingly, our method exhibits similarity with the recently
introduced direct preference optimization (DPO) method ([Rafailov
et al., 2023](#bib.bib67)), with the notable distinction being the
self-play nature of our method. Consequently, our approach stands out by
eliminating the need for extra human preference data, a requirement
present in the DPO method. Additionally, the self-play mechanism in our
method resembles the idea of generative adversarial networks
(GAN) ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#bib.bib31); [Arjovsky et al.,
2017](#bib.bib3)), albeit that both the discriminator (main player) and
the generator (the opponent) in our method are instances of the same LLM
from different iterations. Theoretically, we prove that our method
converges when the distribution of the LLM is identical to the target
data distribution, i.e., $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}=p_{\mathrm{data}}`$. Our
experimental results on zephyr-7b-sft-full ([Tunstall et al.,
2023a](#bib.bib85)), a fine-tuned LLM based on Mistral-7B ([Jiang
et al., 2023](#bib.bib39)), show that while continued training using SFT
on its own SFT dataset Ultrachat200k ([Ding et al., 2023](#bib.bib25))
reaches a performance plateau or even diminished evaluation scores, our
method consistently improves zephyr-7b-sft-full across successive
iterations while leveraging only a $`\mathbf{50}`$k subset of
Ultrachat200k dataset. Ultimately, SPIN effectively improves the base
model’s average score from $`58.14`$ to $`\mathbf{63.16}`$ on the
HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard ([Beeching et al., 2023](#bib.bib8))
with remarkable $`10\%`$+ improvement in scores on GSM8k and TruthfulQA,
and from $`5.94`$ to $`\mathbf{6.78}`$ on MT-Bench ([Zheng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib100)). Notably, SPIN achieves results that are even
comparable to models trained on additional $`62`$k preference
dataset ([Tunstall et al., 2023a](#bib.bib85)) on Open LLM leaderboard
and MT-Bench.

Concurrent to our work, [Singh et al. (2023)](#bib.bib77) proposed the
use of synthetic data with binary feedback in self-training, reducing
the reliance on human data. In contrast, our approach eliminates the
need for additional binary feedback from humans or an extra reward model
thanks to the self-play mechanism. Additionally, [Burns et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib13) employed a weak LLM model as the guidance to train
stronger LLMs in a fashion of weak-to-strong generation. Unlike [Burns
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib13), which necessitates both a weak supervisor
and a strong model, our SPIN operates effectively with a single LLM.

Notation. We use lowercase letters and lowercase boldface letters to
denote scalars and vectors, respectively. We use $`[N]`$ to denote the
index set $`\{1,\dots,N\}`$. In the function space, let $`\mathcal{F}`$
be the function class. The symbol $`q_{\mathrm{data}}`$ designates the
target data distribution, while $`p`$ represents the conditional
probability of LLM’s response (i.e., LLM policy).

## 2 Related Work

Self-Play. Self-play ([Samuel, 1959](#bib.bib71); [Tesauro et al.,
1995](#bib.bib82)), where the algorithm learns by playing against
itself, has gained notable attention due to its effectiveness in
multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL). This method involves agents
engaging in interactions with copies of themselves, enabling an
increasing level of challenge and complexity within the learning
environment. A fundamental work in the field of self-play is AlphaGo
Zero ([Silver et al., 2017b](#bib.bib76)), which demonstrated
exceptional performance against human players using a self-play learning
scheme. Subsequent research has expanded upon the concept of self-play,
exploring various adaptations and implementations ([Anthony et al.,
2017](#bib.bib2); [Lanctot et al., 2017](#bib.bib45); [Bansal et al.,
2018](#bib.bib7); [Hernandez-Leal et al., 2018](#bib.bib36); [Muller
et al., 2019](#bib.bib62); [Vinyals et al., 2019](#bib.bib89)). Our
method takes the self-play approach akin to AlphaGo Zero, which can
convert a weak model to a strong one without additional human-annotated
data. While the effectiveness of self-play in MARL is well-established,
to our knowledge, our work is the first to apply this approach to the
enhancement of LLMs.

Synthetic Data for LLMs. In the context of supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
of LLMs, human-crafted data has proven to be a remarkably effective
source that enhances the performance of LLMs on tasks such as code
generation ([Roziere et al., 2023](#bib.bib69); [Yang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib93)) and mathematical reasoning ([Yuan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib96); [Luo et al., 2023](#bib.bib56)). While human data
typically exhibits high quality, acquiring sufficient amount of such
data poses a challenge in cost. In light of this consideration, the use
of synthetic data has become increasingly popular and considered as a
proxy for human data. This approach primarily leverages advanced LLMs
such as the GPT series ([Radford et al., 2019](#bib.bib66); [Brown
et al., 2020](#bib.bib11); [OpenAI, 2023](#bib.bib63)) as the guidance
to generate high-quality data ([Josifoski et al., 2023](#bib.bib41);
[Taori et al., 2023](#bib.bib81); [Chiang et al., 2023](#bib.bib16); [Li
et al., 2023](#bib.bib51)). Recent research has also highlighted the
rephrasing capability of LLMs in prompting for better LLM
response ([Deng et al., 2023](#bib.bib24); [Prasad et al.,
2023](#bib.bib65)) as well as augmenting synthetic data for more
effective SFT ([Yu et al., 2023](#bib.bib94); [Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib53)). In contrast to prior studies that utilized more
advanced models for synthetic data generation when pre-training or
fine-tuning a target model, our approach directly generates synthetic
data from the target model itself.

## 3 Problem Setting and Preliminaries

We consider a Large Language Model (LLM) parameterized by
$`\bm{\theta}`$ and denoted by $`p_{\bm{\theta}}`$. The model takes as
input a sequence $`\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]`$, commonly referred
to as the prompt, to generate the corresponding response
$`\mathbf{y}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]`$. The response $`\mathbf{y}`$ is
therefore considered as a sample from the conditional probability
distribution $`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$. In LLMs, $`x_{i}`$
and $`y_{j}`$ represent individual tokens from a predetermined
vocabulary within the sequences $`\mathbf{x}`$ and $`\mathbf{y}`$,
respectively. The auto-regressive model $`p_{\bm{\theta}}`$ generates
tokens sequentially for a given position, leveraging only the sequence
of previously generated tokens. This model therefore constitutes a
Markov process, where the conditional probability distribution
$`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$ can be expressed through a
decomposition as follows:

|     |                                             |                                                   |
| --- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p\_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y} | \mathbf{x})=\prod*{j=1}^{m}p*{\bm{\theta}}(y\_{j} | \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\_{<j}),`$ |     |

where $`\mathbf{y}_{<1}`$ is null and
$`\mathbf{y}_{<j}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{j-1}]`$ for $`j=2,\ldots,m`$. In the
following, we review two major fine-tuning methods for LLMs: supervised
fine-tuning and reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning.

### 3.1 Supervised Fine-Tuning

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is employed to tailor a pre-trained LLM to
specific downstream tasks, leveraging relatively smaller dataset of
labeled examples in comparison to the large-scale pre-training
data ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib64); [Yu et al., 2023](#bib.bib94)).
In this context, we consider a specific task where the prompts, denoted
by $`\mathbf{x}`$, are derived from a specified distribution
$`q(\cdot)`$. The notation $`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$ then
represents the probability distribution of the associated high-quality
responses $`\mathbf{y}`$ from the training data. Consequently, SFT
involves training the LLM to minimize the following negative
log-likelihood loss associated with these distributions,

|     |                                                                                                                            |                                                       |                         |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle L*{\mathrm{SFT}}(\bm{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}*{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p\_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot | \mathbf{x})}\Big[\log p\_{\bm{\theta}}\big(\mathbf{y} | \mathbf{x}\big)\Big].`$ |     | (3.1) |

It should be noted that excluding $`\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot)`$ from the
expectation term yields the typical cross-entropy loss, expressed as
$`-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}[\log p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})]`$.
$`L_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\bm{\theta})`$ attains its minimum when the model’s
predictive distribution $`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$
aligns perfectly with the distribution of the labeled high-quality
responses $`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$.

Consequently, the LLM after SFT is anticipated to generate responses
that closely resemble those from
$`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$. This procedure is
therefore expected to significantly enhance the model’s performance in
generating appropriate responses for a specific task.

### 3.2 RL Fine-Tuning

RL fine-tuning ([Christiano et al., 2017](#bib.bib17); [Bai et al.,
2022a](#bib.bib5); [Gao et al., 2023a](#bib.bib29)) offers another
method for enhancing the specific capabilities of general-purpose
pre-trained models. Typically, RL fine-tuning is employed subsequent to
SFT to achieve improved alignment for LLMs ([Tunstall et al.,
2023a](#bib.bib85)).

For a given sequence pair $`(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})`$, RL fine-tuning
necessitates a deterministic reward function
$`r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})`$. The higher the reward
$`r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})`$, the better the response $`\mathbf{y}`$ is
to the given prompt $`\mathbf{x}`$. The objective of the RL fine-tuning
process is then to maximize the following objective function:

|     |                                                |                                                                                                        |                                          |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- | --- | ----------------------- | ------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{RL}}(\bm{\theta})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot               | \mathbf{x})}[r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]`$ |     |
|     |                                                | $`\displaystyle\qquad-\lambda\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot)}\mathrm{KL}\big(p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot | \mathbf{x})                              |     | p\_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot | \mathbf{x})\big),`$ |     |

where the Kullback-Leibler (KL) regularization enforces the new model
$`p_{\bm{\theta}}`$ to be close to the reference model
$`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$, and $`\lambda>0`$ is the regularization parameter
to control the deviation of the new model $`p_{\bm{\theta}}`$ from the
reference model $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$. In practice, the reference model
$`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is often initialized as the supervised fine-tuned
model. The inclusion of KL regularization is vital for preventing
excessive deviation from the reference model, which in turn reduces the
risk of mode collapse.

Meanwhile, the primary challenge in RL fine-tuning lies in finding a
good reward function. Typically, this function requires training on a
preference dataset. The compilation of such a dataset demands
significant resources, often involving comprehensive evaluations either
by human annotators, i.e., reinforcement learning from human feedback
(RLHF) ([Christiano et al., 2017](#bib.bib17); [Bai et al.,
2022a](#bib.bib5)) or strong AI agents, i.e., reinforcement learning
from AI feedback (RLAIF) ([Bai et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6)).

## 4 Method

In this section, we introduce a new fine-tuning method for enhancing the
performance of LLMs without relying on additional human or AI feedback.
Consider a high-quality supervised fine-tuning (SFT) dataset
$`S_{\mathrm{SFT}}=\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\}_{i=1}^{n}`$, which are
sampled from the marginal distribution $`q(\mathbf{x})`$ and
$`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$. Given a supervised
fine-tuned LLM $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}`$, further application of the SFT
approach in
([3.1](#S3.E1 "Equation 3.1 ‣ 3.1 Supervised Fine-Tuning ‣ 3 Problem Setting and Preliminaries ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
with $`S_{\mathrm{SFT}}`$ will be ineffective and potentially lead to
worse performance. In addition, without human and/or AI feedback, it
becomes infeasible to acquire a preference dataset for RL fine-tuning
(e.g., RLHF and RLAIF). This hinders the application of RL fine-tuning
techniques.

We evaluate $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}`$ against $`S_{\mathrm{SFT}}`$, where
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}`$ is the LLM achieved by SFT using
([3.1](#S3.E1 "Equation 3.1 ‣ 3.1 Supervised Fine-Tuning ‣ 3 Problem Setting and Preliminaries ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")).
We notice a persistent quality gap between the groundtruth response
$`\mathbf{y}`$ from $`S_{\mathrm{SFT}}`$ and the LLM-generated response
$`\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$ (refer to
Figure
[1](#S4.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")).
This disparity indicates that there is still room for improvement over
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}`$. Therefore, we propose to use the synthetic data
generated by the LLM to enhance LLM’s performance starting from
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}`$ iteratively.

### 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN)

Let us consider a two-player game, where the main player’s objective is
to distinguish the responses generated by the LLM and those generated by
the human. Meanwhile, the opponent’s role is to generate responses that
are indistinguishable from the human’s responses. The core of our method
is the self-play mechanism, where both the main player and the opponent
are the same LLM, but from different iterations. More specifically, the
opponent is the old LLM from the previous iteration, and the main player
is the new LLM to be learned in the current iteration.

In iteration $`t+1`$, the opponent is the LLM from the previous
iteration, denoted by $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$, which generates responses
$`\mathbf{y}^{\prime}`$ for those prompts $`\mathbf{x}`$ in the SFT
dataset according to $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$. Our
method, therefore, consists of the following two steps at iteration
$`t+1`$: $`(1)`$ training the main player, and $`(2)`$ updating the
opponent player.

Training the Main Player. We begin with illustrating how we expect a
main player is trained to distinguish LLM responses from human
responses. Motivated by integral probability metric (IPM) ([Müller,
1997](#bib.bib61)), we formulate our objective function such that the
main player $`f_{t+1}`$ maximizes the expected value gap between the
target data distribution $`p_{\mathrm{data}}`$ and the opponent player’s
distribution $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$:

|     |                           |                                                                                                                                                 |     |       |
| --- | ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle f_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}\mathbb{E}\big[f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\big],`$ |     | (4.1) |

where the expectation is computed over the distributions
$`\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$,
and $`\mathcal{F}_{t}`$ is a sequence of highly expressive function
classes that we will determine in later deduction. The subscript $`t`$
in $`\mathcal{F}_{t}`$ is due to that the function class is dependent on
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$. Given such a $`f_{t+1}`$ and a response
sequence $`\mathbf{y}`$ to the prompt $`\mathbf{x}`$, the value of
$`f_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})`$ reflects the main player’s degree of
belief that $`\mathbf{y}`$ originates from $`p_{\mathrm{data}}`$ rather
than $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$. Ideally, the main player $`f_{t+1}`$
should yield a high value when
$`\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$ and a low value
when $`\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$,
where $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$ is the opponent’s distribution. Instead of
solving
([4.1](#S4.E1 "Equation 4.1 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")),
we can also solve the following more general optimization problem,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
f_{t+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}\mathbb{E}\big[\ell\big(f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\big)\big],
``` |  | (4.2) |

where the expectation is computed over the distribution
$`\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),y^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$,
and $`\ell(\cdot)`$ is a loss function that is both monotonically
decreasing and convex. For example, a linear loss function
$`\ell(t)=-t`$ reduces
([4.2](#S4.E2 "Equation 4.2 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
to the minimization version of
([4.1](#S4.E1 "Equation 4.1 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")).
However, the use of a linear loss function results in an unbounded
objective value, which, during continuous training, leads to a negative
infinite value of $`f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})`$ on the opponent
player’s responses. Therefore, in our work, we choose the logistic loss
function $`\ell(t):=\log(1+\exp(-t))`$ for its non-negativity,
smoothness, and exponentially decaying tail as $`t\rightarrow\infty`$.
Such a choice of loss function aids in preventing the excessive growth
in the absolute value of $`f`$.

Updating the Opponent Player. Previously we have discussed the training
of $`f_{t+1}`$ given the opponent player’s distribution
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$. Now suppose we have optimized our main player
$`f_{t+1}`$ that can distinguish $`p_{\mathrm{data}}`$ from
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$, within a certain function class
$`\mathcal{F}_{t}`$, we elaborate how we get parameter
$`\bm{\theta}_{t+1}`$ of the opponent player. Specifically, when
presented with two responses $`\mathbf{y}`$ and $`\mathbf{y}^{\prime}`$
to the same prompt $`\mathbf{x}`$, $`f_{t+1}`$ assesses the values
$`f_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})`$ and
$`f_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})`$. It then infers that the
response with the higher value is from the real data distribution
$`p_{\mathrm{data}}`$ and the response with lower value is attributed to
the LLM $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$. Subsequently, the objective of the
opponent player is to find a better LLM that generates responses
indistinguishable from $`p_{\mathrm{data}}`$ for the main player. This
is achieved by maximizing the expected value
$`\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}[f_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]`$.
In addition, to prevent excessive deviation of $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}`$
from $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}`$ and stabilize the self-play, we incorporate
a Kullback-Leibler (KL) regularization term. Putting these together
gives rise to the following optimization problem:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{p}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}[f_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad-\lambda\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot)}\mathrm{KL}\big(p(\cdot|\mathbf{x})||p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})\big),`$ |  | (4.3) |

where $`\lambda>0`$ is the regularization parameter. Notably,
([4.3](#S4.E3 "Equation 4.3 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
has a closed-form solution $`\widehat{p}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\propto p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\exp\big(\lambda^{-1}f_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\big).`$ |  | (4.4) |

It is worth noting that $`\widehat{p}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$ is not
guaranteed to be belong to the LLM space
$`\{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})|\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\}`$.
Since we hope that the closed-form solution $`\widehat{p}`$ in the
probability space can be realized by an LLM with parameter
$`\bm{\theta}`$, i.e.,
$`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\widehat{p}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$,
solving for
$`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\propto p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\exp\big(\lambda^{-1}f_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\big)`$
gives
$`f_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\lambda\cdot\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\mathrm{\bm{\theta}_{t}}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}`$.
This suggests the following function class $`\mathcal{F}_{t}`$ for
$`f_{t+1}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}=\bigg\{\lambda\cdot\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\mathrm{\bm{\theta}_{t}}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg|\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\bigg\},`$ |  | (4.5) |

where $`\bm{\Theta}`$ is the parameter space of LLMs being considered.
Given the choice of $`\mathcal{F}_{t}`$ in
([4.5](#S4.E5 "Equation 4.5 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")),
optimizing ([4.2](#S4.E2 "Equation 4.2 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
gives $`f_{t+1}`$ parameterized by $`\bm{\theta}_{t+1}`$ in the
following form:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle f_{t+1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\lambda\cdot\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\mathrm{\bm{\theta}_{t}}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}.`$ |  | (4.6) |

Substituting
([4.6](#S4.E6 "Equation 4.6 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
into
([4.4](#S4.E4 "Equation 4.4 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
yields
$`\widehat{p}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$.
In other words, $`\bm{\theta}_{t+1}`$ learned from
([4.2](#S4.E2 "Equation 4.2 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
is exactly the LLM parameter for our ideal opponent selection.

End-to-end Training Objective. We integrate the previously discussed two
steps into a single end-to-end training objective with an update rule of
$`\bm{\theta}_{t+1}`$. Specifically,
plugging ([4.5](#S4.E5 "Equation 4.5 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
into ([4.2](#S4.E2 "Equation 4.2 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
arrives at the update rule
$`\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}}L_{\texttt{SPIN}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{t})`$,
where $`L_{\texttt{SPIN}}`$ is the training objective defined as follows

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle L_{\texttt{SPIN}}=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\ell\bigg(\lambda\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}-\lambda\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg)\bigg],`$ |  | (4.7) |

where the expectation is computed over the distribution
$`\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$.
We summarize the iterative self-play process of our method SPIN as
follows,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\ldots`$ | $`\displaystyle\quad\rightarrow\underbrace{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}_{\text{Opponent Player at $t$}}\rightarrow\quad\underbrace{\lambda\cdot\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\mathrm{\bm{\theta}_{t}}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}}_{\text{Main Player at $t+1$}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad\rightarrow\underbrace{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}_{\text{Opponent Player at $t+1$}}\rightarrow\quad\ldots`$ |  |

Namely, the opponent player chosen from the previous iteration $`t`$ is
employed to train the main player at iteration $`t+1`$, resulting in the
LLM parameterized by $`\bm{\theta}_{t+1}`$. Then we determine the next
opponent player at iteration $`t+1`$ by directly copying the LLM
parameter $`\bm{\theta}_{t+1}`$, which is then used in training the main
player at iteration $`t+2`$. The detailed algorithm is presented in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models").

$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}`$: LLM with parameter $`\bm{\theta}_{0}`$, $`T`$:
Number of iterations.

 for $`t=0,\ldots,T-1`$ do

  for $`i=1,\ldots N`$ do

   Generate synthetic data
$`\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}_{i})`$.

  end for

### 4.2 Comparison between SPIN and DPO

In
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"),
we propose Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) with an end-to-end training
objective
([4.7](#S4.E7 "Equation 4.7 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
for each iteration.
([4.7](#S4.E7 "Equation 4.7 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
bears resemblance to direct preference optimization (DPO) ([Rafailov
et al., 2023](#bib.bib67)) for RL fine-tuning. However, SPIN and DPO are
fundamentally different. DPO is based on the Bradley-Terry (BT) model:
$`p(\mathbf{y}_{1}\succ\mathbf{y}_{2}|\mathbf{x})=\frac{\exp(r^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1}))}{\exp(r^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1}))+\exp(r^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{2}))}`$,
and maximizes the log-likelihood of
$`p(\mathbf{y}_{1}\succ\mathbf{y}_{2}|\mathbf{x})`$ by direct policy
optimization without explicit reward estimation. In contrast, SPIN
relies on maximizing the IPM to compete with an increasingly stronger
version of itself. More detailed comparisons are highlighted as follows:

1.  1.
    DPO does not inherently lead to iterative training. More
    specifically, DPO aims to match the preference probability
    $`p(\mathbf{y}_{1}\succ\mathbf{y}_{2}|\mathbf{x})`$ induced from its
    reward model with the data distribution
    $`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}_{1}\succ\mathbf{y}_{2}|\mathbf{x})`$
    in a single iteration. On the contrary, SPIN’s self-play mechanism
    naturally leads to an iterative training procedure. SPIN iteratively
    refines its generation distribution
    $`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$ to match the target
    distribution $`p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$ across
    iterations.
2.  2.
    SPIN only requires the SFT dataset, represented by pairs
    $`(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})`$. In contrast, DPO necessitates a
    preference dataset, represented by
    $`(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w},\mathbf{y}_{l})`$, where
    $`\mathbf{y}_{w}`$ and $`\mathbf{y}_{l}`$ denote the winner (chosen)
    and loser (rejected) responses, respectively. Moreover, SPIN can be
    applied between SFT and RL fine-tuning.
3.  3.
    In SPIN, we can choose different loss functions $`\ell`$ which only
    need to be convex and decreasing (detailed later in
    Theorem [5.2](#S5.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 5.2. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")),
    which includes correlation loss, hinge loss, and logistic loss. Only
    when $`\ell`$ is chosen as the logistic loss would the training
    objective of SPIN become similar to that of DPO.

Recently, [Xu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib92) proposed to use iterative
preference optimization with the Pairwise Cringe Loss (PCO), and
generalized DPO to iterative DPO. Concurrent to our work, [Yuan et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib95) further proposed a framework named “self-rewarding
language models”, which leverages the LLM itself as the reward model to
provide the preference feedback, and employs iterative DPO to train the
LLM. Compared with [Xu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib92); [Yuan et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib95), SPIN’s self-assessment is implicit, as no
intermediate reward or preference feedback is required.

## 5 Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we provide a theoretical analysis for
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")
in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models").
Under monotonicity and convexity assumption of the objective function
$`\ell`$, we show that the global optimum is obtained if and only if
parameter $`\bm{\theta}_{t}`$ generates data distribution. We summarize
our assumptions as follows:

###### Assumption 5.1.

The loss function $`\ell(t):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ is
monotonically decreasing, i.e., $`\forall t,\ell^{\prime}(t)\leq 0`$ and
satisfies $`\ell^{\prime}(0)<0`$. In addition, $`\ell(t)`$ is a convex
function.

Assumption [5.1](#S5.Thmtheorem1 "Assumption 5.1. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")
holds for a wide range of loss functions commonly used in machine
learning, including correlation loss $`\ell(t)=1-t`$, hinge loss
$`\ell(t)=\max(0,1-t)`$, exponential loss $`\ell(t)=\exp(-t)`$ and
logistic loss $`\ell(t)=\log(1+\exp(-t))`$. Under Assumptions
[5.1](#S5.Thmtheorem1 "Assumption 5.1. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"),
we present the following theorem, which is pivotal in understanding the
optimization dynamics of our method.

###### Theorem 5.2.

Under
Assumption [5.1](#S5.Thmtheorem1 "Assumption 5.1. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"),
suppose there exists
$`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})=p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$,
then we have that

- •
  (Sufficiency) If
  $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})=p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$,
  then $`\bm{\theta}_{t}`$ is the global minimum of
  ([4.7](#S4.E7 "Equation 4.7 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
  for any $`\lambda\geq 0`$.
- •
  (Necessity) If
  $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})\not=p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$,
  there exists an appropriately chosen $`\lambda`$, such that
  $`\bm{\theta}_{t}`$ is not the global minimum of
  ([4.7](#S4.E7 "Equation 4.7 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")).

###### Remark 5.3.

Theorem
[5.2](#S5.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 5.2. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")
suggests that under certain conditions, the optimization process of our
method naturally stops at the point
$`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})=p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$,
implying the effectiveness of our approach in aligning the LLM’s
distribution with the target data distribution. Moreover, Theorem
[5.2](#S5.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 5.2. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")
also indicates that the optimization process only stops when the global
optimality is achieved, i.e., the LLM’s distribution aligns with the
target data distribution.

For the logistic loss function $`\ell(t)=\log(1+\exp(-t))`$, the
following theorem gives a more precise characterization of the opponent
player, enabling a better understanding of SPIN.

###### Theorem 5.4.

Consider the choice of logistic loss $`\ell(t)=\log(1+\exp(-t))`$ in
SPIN. Suppose that
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\big(p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})/p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\big)^{1/\lambda}`$
lies in the LLM space
$`\{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})|\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\}`$
and $`\bm{\theta}_{t+1}`$ is global minimum of
$`L_{\texttt{SPIN}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{t})`$, then the opponent
player at iteration $`t+1`$ satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\propto p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\big(p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})/p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\big)^{1/\lambda}.`$ |  |

###### Remark 5.5.

According to
Theorem [5.4](#S5.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 5.4. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"),
the model update from $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$ to
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$ tends to increase the
probability $`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t+1}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$ when
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$ is less than
$`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$, and decrease it when
$`p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$ is greater than
$`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$. Thus,
Theorem [5.4](#S5.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 5.4. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")
further confirms that our method’s optimization process naturally
converges to the point where $`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$
equals $`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$. The update of the
opponent player is controlled by
$`\big(p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})/p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\big)^{1/\lambda}`$,
which is regulated by the factor $`1/\lambda`$. A smaller $`\lambda`$
results in a larger change of the opponent player, while a larger
$`\lambda`$ leads to a smaller change. Therefore, as
$`p_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$ approaches
$`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$, increasing $`\lambda`$ enhances
the stability of LLM training. This observation aligns with
([4.3](#S4.E3 "Equation 4.3 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")),
where $`\lambda`$ is the regularization parameter of the KL
regularization that is employed to control the deviation of the opponent
player.

## 6 Experiments

This section provides a detailed empirical analysis of SPIN. Our
findings highlight several key points: $`(1)`$ SPIN markedly enhances
model performance across a wide range of evaluation benchmarks by
breaking the limit of SFT; $`(2)`$ even without introducing new human
annotated data, SPIN at iteration $`0`$ achieves performance on par to
DPO training that utilizes even more data; $`(3)`$ iterative training is
a necessary component in SPIN as it breaks the limit of multi-epoch
training.

### 6.1 Experiment Setup

Model and Datasets. In this study, we adopt zephyr-7b-sft-full as our
base model. This model derives from the pre-trained Mistral-7B ([Jiang
et al., 2023](#bib.bib39)) and has been further fine-tuned on the SFT
dataset Ultrachat200k¹¹ 1
[https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceH4/ultrachat_200k](https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceH4/ultrachat_200k)
by HuggingFace. Ultrachat200k represents a high-quality 200k subset of
the larger UltraChat ([Ding et al., 2023](#bib.bib25)) corpus, which
comprises approximately 1.4M dialogues produced using OpenAI’s Turbo
APIs. From UltraChat200k, We randomly sample $`50`$k prompts and use the
base model to generate the synthetic responses. We subsequently follow
the optimization method described in Section
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")
for further training. In multiple iterations, we leverage the synthetic
data from the most recent iteration and add to the newly generated
synthetic data, therefore resulting in a synthetic dataset size of
$`50`$k at iteration $`0`$ and $`100`$k at iteration $`1`$, $`2`$ and
$`3`$. At each iteration, we train our model for $`2`$ epochs.

Evaluation. We employed the widely used Huggingface Open LLM Leaderboard
([Beeching et al., 2023](#bib.bib8)) as our evaluation benchmark, using
the same Language Model Evaluation Harness library ([Gao et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib30)). This leaderboard encompasses 6 different datasets,
each focusing on a a specific capability of LLMs. Collectively, these
datasets provide a thorough assessment framework, evaluating LLMs on
commonsense reasoning (Arc ([Clark et al., 2018](#bib.bib20)), HellaSwag
([Zellers et al., 2019](#bib.bib97)), Winogrande ([Sakaguchi et al.,
2021](#bib.bib70))), multi-task language understanding (MMLU([Hendrycks
et al., 2020](#bib.bib35))), human falsehood mimic (TruthfulQA ([Lin
et al., 2021](#bib.bib52))) and math problem solving (GSM8k ([Cobbe
et al., 2021](#bib.bib21))). We leave further implementation details to
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Experiments ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")
with detailed evaluation setting adopted by both the leaderboard and our
experiments.

## 7 Conclusion and Discussion

This paper introduces a novel fine-tuning method SPIN, to convert a weak
LLM to a strong LLM by unleashing the full power of human-annotated
data. Central to this method is a self-play mechanism, wherein a main
player (the LLM) is fine-tuned to differentiate the responses of
opponent player (the LLM from previous iteration) from the target data
distribution, and the LLM is iteratively aligned with the target data
distribution. Therefore, SPIN facilitates the LLM’s iterative
self-evaluation and enhancement through self-play. In comparison to
supervised fine-tuning and RL fine-tuning methods, SPIN enables the LLM
to self-improve without additional human data or feedback from stronger
LLMs. Empirical results demonstrate that SPIN significantly enhances LLM
performance across diverse benchmarks, even outperforming models trained
with additional human data or AI feedback.

Limitation and Future Work. Our theoretical results demonstrate that the
optimization process of SPIN converges if and only if the LLM’s
distribution aligns with $`p_{\mathrm{data}}`$. Therefore, our study
focuses on a fixed target data distribution generated by humans, which
inherently imposes a ceiling on the performance of fine-tuned LLM.
Exploring the dynamically changing target data distribution is an
important direction to overcome this limitation and elevate the LLM’s
performance beyond this ceiling or even to a super-human level.
Moreover, considering the resource demands of synthetic data generation,
another promising avenue for further exploration is to reduce the volume
of required synthetic data.

## Appendix A Further Related Work

Curriculum Learning. In deep learning, it has been observed that
training models using data samples arranged in a strategically
meaningful order can lead to improved performance compared to training
on randomly shuffled data. This approach is commonly known as curriculum
learning ([Bengio et al., 2009](#bib.bib10); [Soviany et al.,
2022](#bib.bib78)). Initial studies in curriculum learning introduced
efficient algorithms that adhere to an ‘easy-to-hard’ progression
([Spitkovsky et al., 2009](#bib.bib79); [Kumar et al.,
2010](#bib.bib44); [Lee & Grauman, 2011](#bib.bib48); [Zhang et al.,
2015](#bib.bib98)). In the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP),
criteria such as sentence length and term frequency are commonly
utilized ([Cirik et al., 2016](#bib.bib19); [Zhang et al.,
2018](#bib.bib99); [Liu et al., 2018](#bib.bib54)). More recent
developments include the application of curriculum learning algorithms
in multi-modal learning ([Liu et al., 2021](#bib.bib55); [Wu et al.,
2022](#bib.bib91)). Our work shares a similar idea to curriculum
learning, wherein the training data evolves iteratively—beginning with
responses that are easy to distinguish from human-annotated data and
gradually progressing to more challenging instances.

Generative Adversarial Networks. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#bib.bib31)) represent a distinct class of
generative models, characterized by their unique adversarial process. To
enhance training stability and data quality, [Mao et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib57) introduced the Least Squares GAN, employing a least
squares loss function for the discriminator. A significant advancement
in GANs involves the use of Integral Probability Metrics (IPM) ([Müller,
1997](#bib.bib61)), particularly highlighted in the development of
Wasserstein GAN by [Arjovsky et al. (2017)](#bib.bib3). This model
employs IPM in its loss design, enhancing training stability. Since
then, IPMs have become crucial in GAN design ([Mroueh & Sercu,
2017](#bib.bib60); [Gulrajani et al., 2017](#bib.bib34)), particularly
in constraining the discriminator to a specific function class, thereby
preventing it from overpowering the generator. Furthermore,
[Jolicoeur-Martineau (2018)](#bib.bib40) generalized IPM-based GANs by
introducing relativistic discriminator and proposed Relativistic GAN. It
is worth noting that the objective function defined in our
([4.2](#S4.E2 "Equation 4.2 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))
is similar to Relativistic GAN ([Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2018](#bib.bib40))
and reduces to an IPM framework in Wasserstein GAN ([Arjovsky et al.,
2017](#bib.bib3)) with a linear loss. However, our approach differs in
both the choice of the function class and the training procedure.
Inspired by GAN, [Cheng et al. (2023)](#bib.bib15) proposed an
adversarial learning framework named Adversarial Preference Optimization
(APO) that trains the LLM and a reward model in an adversarial game.
Similarly related to the spirit of our method, Generative Adversarial
Imitation Learning (GAIL) ([Ho & Ermon, 2016](#bib.bib38)) was proposed
to train separate discriminator and policy networks in each iteration.
In contrast to the above methods, SPIN relies on self-play where both
the main player and the opponent player are the same LLM from two
consecutive iterations.

Alignment with AI Feedback. The objective of alignment is to fine-tune
LLMs to align with human intentions. In addition to using human
demonstrations, AI feedback is emerging as a crucial component in the
alignment process. Constitutional AI ([Bai et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6))
leveraged AI feedback to align language models through a combination of
both supervised learning and reinforcement learning (RL) phases. In the
RL phase, ([Bai et al., 2022b](#bib.bib6)) applied Reinforcement
Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF), training a reward model using
AI-generated preferences, followed by RL using the reward. [Lee et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib47) demonstrated that AI feedback can achieve comparable
or superior performance to human feedback in RL fine-tuning. They also
demonstrated that RLAIF can improve upon an SFT policy even when the LLM
labeler has the same size as the policy. [Saunders et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib73) studied the scaling properties of self-critique and
introduced a framework for comparing the critique ability to generation
and discrimination ability. Self-critique models employ the LLM itself
to generate natural language critiques through behavioral cloning,
assisting human evaluators.

We use the Alignment Handbook library ([Tunstall et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib86)) as the codebase for our self-play fine-tuning method
SPIN, which includes DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 ([Rajbhandari et al.,
2020](#bib.bib68)) and FlashAttention-2 ([Dao, 2023](#bib.bib23)) to
reduce training cost. We train our models with RMSProp ([Hinton et al.,
2012](#bib.bib37)) optimizer with no weight decay for all iterations as
commonly used in fine-tuning LLMs for alignment, with a global batch
size of $`64`$, $`10\%`$ warmup steps and bfloat16 precision. We set the
peak learning rate to be 5e-7 for iterations 0 and 1, and decay this
peak learning rate to 1e-7 for iteration 2 and 3 as we are approaching
the end of self-play fine-tuning. Lastly, we choose $`\beta=0.1`$ and
max sequence length to be $`2048`$ tokens as in [Tunstall et al.
(2023b)](#bib.bib86). We note that at the last iteration (iter-3) where
the model is close to convergence, we increase the value of $`\beta`$ to
$`5.0`$. We use the Accelerate library ([Gugger et al.,
2022](#bib.bib33)) to generate our synthetic data using distributed
inference with multiple GPUs with a global batch size of $`64`$. We
consider the prompting template “### Instruction: {prompt}\n\n###
Response: ” as commonly used in [Taori et al. (2023)](#bib.bib81). For
Ultrachat200k containing multi-round conversations, we only sample the
first round as our prompt and ground truth completion pairs.

###### Proof of Theorem [5.2](#S5.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 5.2. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models").

To begin with, we prove the “Sufficiency” in
Theorem [5.2](#S5.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 5.2. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models").
Since
$`p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})=p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$,
by symmetry property of $`\mathbf{y}`$ and $`\mathbf{y}^{\prime}`$, we
have for any $`\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}`$ that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|

|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\bigg[\ell\bigg(\gamma\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}-\gamma\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg)\bigg]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\bigg[\ell\bigg(\gamma\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}-\gamma\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad+\ell\bigg(\gamma\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}-\gamma\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg)\bigg]`$ |  |

|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\gamma}{2}\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg)\bigg]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\ell(0),`$ |  |

where the inequality is due to Jensen’s inequality (recalling that

Therefore, we have that
$`L_{\texttt{SPIN}}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{t})\geq\ell(0)=L_{\texttt{SPIN}}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{t})`$,
which means that $`\bm{\theta}_{t}`$ is the global optimum of
([4.7](#S4.E7 "Equation 4.7 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")).
As a consequence, the gradient at the point $`\bm{\theta}_{t}`$ is zero,
which concludes $`\bm{\theta}_{t+1}=\bm{\theta}_{t}`$.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\bigg[\ell\bigg(\lambda\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}-\lambda\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg)\bigg].`$ |  |

Then we have $`g(0)=\ell(0)`$ and

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g^{\prime}(0)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\bigg[\ell^{\prime}(0)\bigg(\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg)\bigg]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\ell^{\prime}(0)\bigg(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\bigg[\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\bigg[\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg]\bigg)`$ |  |

|  |  | $`\displaystyle<0,`$ |  |

where the last inequality is due to the condition that
$`\ell^{\prime}(0)<0`$. Therefore, there exist a $`\lambda_{0}`$ such
that for all $`0<\lambda<\lambda_{0}`$, we have $`g(\lambda)<\ell(0)`$.
Choose $`\bm{\theta}^{*}`$ such that
$`p_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})`$.
For those $`0<\lambda<\lambda_{0}`$, we have that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle L_{\texttt{SPIN}}(\bm{\theta}^{*},\bm{\theta}_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\bigg[\ell\bigg(\lambda\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}-\lambda\log\frac{p_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg)\bigg]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\bigg[\ell\bigg(\lambda\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}-\lambda\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})}\bigg)\bigg]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=g(\lambda)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle<g(0)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=L_{\texttt{SPIN}}(\bm{\theta}_{t},\bm{\theta}_{t}),`$ |  |

where the second equality holds by the choice of
$`p_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})`$, and the inequality holds due
to the choice of $`\lambda`$. Therefore, we conclude that
$`\bm{\theta}_{t}`$ is not the global optimum of

### C.2 Proof Theorem [5.4](#S5.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 5.4. ‣ 5 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")

###### Lemma C.1.

Suppose that $`\ell(t)=\log(1+\exp(-t))`$ and for $`a,b>0`$, the
following inequality holds

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle a\ell(t)+b\ell(-t)\geq a\log(1+b/a)+b\log(1+a/b),`$ |  |

the equality holds if and only if $`t=\log(a/b)`$.

###### Proof of Lemma [C.1](#A3.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma C.1. ‣ C.2 Proof Theorem ‣ Appendix C Proof of Theorems in Section ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models").

Define $`g(t)=a\ell(t)+b\ell(-t)=a\log(1+\exp(-t))+b\log(1+\exp(t))`$,
then we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{a\exp(-t)}{1+\exp(-t)}+\frac{b\exp(t)}{1+\exp(t)}=\frac{-a+b\exp(t)}{1+\exp(t)}.`$ |  |

Therefore, $`g^{\prime}(t)<0`$ when $`t<\log(a/b)`$, $`g^{\prime}(t)>0`$
when $`t>\log(a/b)`$, which indicates that $`g`$ achieves it minimum at
$`t=\log(a/b)`$ which concludes the proof. ∎

Lemma [C.1](#A3.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma C.1. ‣ C.2 Proof Theorem ‣ Appendix C Proof of Theorems in Section ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models")
shows that the global minimum of $`a\ell(t)+b\ell(-t)`$ is achieved when
$`t=\log(a/b)`$. Based on
Lemma [C.1](#A3.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma C.1. ‣ C.2 Proof Theorem ‣ Appendix C Proof of Theorems in Section ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"),

([4.2](#S4.E2 "Equation 4.2 ‣ 4.1 Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) ‣ 4 Method ‣ Self-Play Fine-Tuning Converts Weak Language Models to Strong Language Models"))

the constraint set $`\mathcal{F}_{t}`$.

###### Lemma C.2.

$`p_{+}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{x})=q(\mathbf{x})\cdot p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\cdot p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})`$

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q(\cdot),\mathbf{y}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x}),y^{\prime}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\cdot|\mathbf{x})}\big[\ell\big(f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\big)\big]\geq\log 2-\mathrm{JSD}(p_{+}\|p_{-}),`$ |  |

where $`\mathrm{JSD}(p_{+}\|p_{-})`$ represents the Jensen–Shannon
divergence which is defined as follows

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math

where $`\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\|\cdot)`$ is KL-divergence. $`\mathrm{JSD}`$
is always non-negative and equals zero if and only if $`p_{+}`$ and
$`p_{-}`$ are identical. Moreover, the global minimum value

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|

|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad+q(\mathbf{x})p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x})p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\ell\big(f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})-f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\big)d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{y}^{\prime}`$ |  |

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\widehat{Z}(\mathbf{x})\cdot p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\big(p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})/p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})\big)^{1/\lambda}.`$ |  | (C.2) |

Applying logarithm function on both side of

$`f^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\lambda\log(\widehat{Z}(\mathbf{x}))+\log\big(\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p_{\bm{\theta}_{t}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}\big)`$
is the global minimum of the following minimization problem,
````

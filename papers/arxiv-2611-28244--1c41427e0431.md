###### Abstract

Autoresearch agents, which autonomously design, run, and refine
experiments, are accelerating scientific progress, particularly in AI
research. However, existing agents rely on harnesses that combine
multiple mechanisms, making it difficult to determine how individual
components contribute to performance. We systematically study harness
design for long-horizon, self-evolving autoresearch agents along two
dimensions: context management, which determines what an agent retains
from its past attempts, and skill management, which governs how it
stores and reuses lessons from those attempts. Across MLE-Bench and
KernelBench with gpt-oss-120b, gpt-6-luna, and gpt-6-sol, we isolate the
effects of individual harness design choices by evaluating each against
a recency-truncation baseline within a shared agent framework. For
context management, we find that preserving individually accessible
records of past attempts is more effective than maintaining a rolling
summary, with context folding performing best across all six settings
and improving upon the baseline by up to 19.2%. For skill management,
selectively discarding persistently unused lessons is more effective on
average than consolidating them or promoting them to standing rules.
Building on these findings, we combine context folding and skill
deletion in MLEvolver, which achieves a 72.7% medal rate on MLE-Bench
Lite and a 94% $`\mathrm{fast}_{1}`$ score on KernelBench Level 3,
substantially outperforming AIDE and OpenEvolve under the same inference
budget. Overall, our study distills design principles for preserving
experimental history and curating reusable lessons in autoresearch
harnesses, paving the way for agents that turn accumulated experience
into sustained progress.

## 1 Introduction

Autoresearch agents can automate the cycle at the heart of empirical
research: given a task and a grader, they propose an idea, implement it,
run the experiment, read the result, and decide what to try next. They
are becoming increasingly important in AI for AI (AI4AI), where
improving existing AI systems directly accelerates subsequent research.
Recent autoresearch agents demonstrate this across diverse domains: on
MLE-Bench \[Chan et al., 2025\], AIDE \[Jiang et al., 2025\], and
ML-Master \[Liu et al., 2025b\] iteratively train and tune competitive
machine learning pipelines. On KernelBench \[Ouyang et al., 2025\],
Kevin \[Baronio et al., 2026\] learns to write and iteratively refine
fast CUDA kernels, and AlphaEvolve \[Novikov et al., 2025\] has even
sped up the training of the language models it is built on. What is
common among these workflows is their long horizons operation. The agent
attempts the same task tens or hundreds of times, receives an evaluation
score after each attempt, and iteratively refines its solution.

Over such horizons, an agent’s behavior depends not only on its language
model, but also on the harness that manages the memory it accumulates
about its own past. This memory takes two forms, each under a different
kind of pressure. The first is the history of past attempts. It grows
with every attempt, and soon exceeds the context window, thereby leading
to truncation \[Packer et al., 2023\]. The second is a library of
skills: natural language lessons distilled from earlier attempts and
retrieved when a similar situation recurs \[Shinn et al., 2023, Zhao et
al., 2024\]. The library can grow without limit, but it can become
redundant and noisy, which makes the right lessons harder to retrieve.
What an agent keeps, condenses, or discards in each form of memory is
therefore a central design decision.

Many mechanisms have been proposed for managing this memory. Agents page
information in and out of context \[Packer et al., 2023\], reflect on
and summarize past experience \[Park et al., 2023, Shinn et al., 2023\],
and extract, revise, and reuse insights or workflows \[Zhao et al.,
2024\]. However, these mechanisms are typically evaluated within
multi-component systems where differences in search policies, prompt
structure, and tool use confound cross-system comparisons. Even when
individual studies ablate their own designs, memory mechanisms are
rarely evaluated head-to-head under a shared, unified harness. As a
result, it remains unclear what each mechanism contributes on its own.

We address these questions through a controlled, bottom-up study of
memory harness design in iterative self-improvement, where an
autoresearch agent repeatedly attempts the same task and receives
empirical feedback after each attempt. This setting strains both memory
tiers early: the execution history quickly overflows the context window,
while the skill library accumulates redundant lessons. Starting from a
minimal recency-truncation baseline, we isolate five memory mechanisms
within an otherwise identical agent by changing exactly one component at
a time (Figure 2). Two manage working context: folding condenses each
attempt into an expandable digest, and compaction aggregates older
history into a running summary. Three govern the skill library: deletion
prunes unused lessons, merging consolidates semantic duplicates, and
promotion elevates proven skills into persistent standing rules.

We evaluate three model backbones, gpt-oss-120b, gpt-6-luna, and
gpt-6-sol, on two AI4AI benchmarks whose feedback differs sharply. On
MLE-Bench \[Chan et al., 2025\], the agent writes machine learning
solutions for Kaggle competitions spanning tabular, text, image, and
audio data, and receives a score after each attempt. We report a
gold-normalized score (GNS), which is 0 for a chance submission and 100
at the gold medal threshold. On KernelBench \[Ouyang et al., 2025\], the
agent writes CUDA kernels for PyTorch programs, from single operators to
full models, and receives compilation, correctness, and timing feedback.
We report the geometric-mean speedup over PyTorch Eager. Within each
task, all configurations start from the same seed solution, giving them
a common starting point for measuring improvement over iterations.

For context management, folding achieves the highest performance among
all evaluated mechanisms in all six model–benchmark settings, improving
MLE-Bench GNS by up to 7.9% and KernelBench geometric-mean speedup by up
to 19.2% over recency truncation. Our context analysis links this
advantage to folding’s preservation of a separate digest for every
attempt, which exposes more of the agent’s history within the context
budget. Compaction compresses older attempts into a single rolling
summary, leaving progressively less detail about each experiment as the
history grows. Folding keeps individual attempts accessible throughout
the run, helping the agent draw on earlier successes and failures when
choosing its next experiment.

For skill management, deletion yields the largest average improvement at
4.1% over the recency-truncation baseline. These results favor the
removal of persistently unused lessons over merging similar skills or
promoting them to standing rules. Our skill analysis also reveals
consistent differences in retrieval behavior across the three backbones.
Across configurations, gpt-oss-120b selects 6.4–7.1 skills per attempt,
compared with 1.2–1.9 for gpt-6-luna and 0.5–0.6 for gpt-6-sol. This
ordering persists across the baseline and all three skill-management
mechanisms, indicating that the backbone strongly influences how much
stored experience enters each attempt.

Guided by these findings, we build MLEvolver by combining context
folding and skill deletion, the best-performing mechanisms on average in
their respective dimensions. We compare MLEvolver with AIDE and
OpenEvolve using gpt-6-sol and a shared cap of 100 iterations per run.
As shown in Figure 1, MLEvolver earns medals in 16 of 22 MLE-Bench Lite
competitions, achieving a 72.7% medal rate. On KernelBench Level 3, it
produces correct kernels faster than PyTorch Eager on 47 of 50 problems,
reaching 94% on $`\mathrm{fast}_{1}`$. Averaged across these two
benchmarks, MLEvolver achieves relative gains of 22% over AIDE and 24%
over OpenEvolve.

Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  A controlled study of harness design for long-horizon, self-evolving
  autoresearch agents: six configurations within one agent that isolate
  five context- and skill-management mechanisms against a
  recency-truncation baseline.
- •
  An evaluation across three model backbones and two AI4AI benchmarks,
  with analyses of the effectiveness of per-attempt digests in folding,
  the dual benefits of skill retention and active management, and
  differences in skill retrieval behavior across backbones.
- •
  MLEvolver, a harness that combines context folding and skill deletion
  and substantially outperforms AIDE and OpenEvolve on MLE-Bench Lite
  and KernelBench Level 3.

## 2 Related Work

##### Harness Design and Memory in Autoresearch.

Autoresearch harnesses orchestrate iterative discovery by structuring
how language models store, retrieve, and build upon past attempts.
Existing systems couple search logic with bespoke memory stores:
FunSearch \[Romera-Paredes et al., 2024\] and AlphaEvolve \[Novikov et
al., 2025\] maintain program databases, AIDE \[Jiang et al., 2025\]
manages candidate trees, and ML-Master \[Liu et al., 2025b\] integrates
a selectively scoped execution log into its harness. Broader agent
harnesses also develop targeted mechanisms to mitigate context bounds.
These harnesses page state in and out of prompt buffers \[Packer et al.,
2023\], summarize completed sub-goals \[Hu et al., 2025\], train models
to fold execution trajectories \[Ye et al., 2026, Sun et al., 2026\], or
mask older observations to match summarization at reduced token cost
\[Lindenbauer et al., 2025\]. For procedural skills, prior frameworks
accumulate verbal reflections \[Shinn et al., 2023\], distilled insights
\[Zhao et al., 2024\], or synthesized experience replays \[Liu et al.,
2025a\]. However, uncurated skill catalogs degrade retrieval accuracy as
they expand \[Li, 2026\]. Recent harnesses address this bloat through
heuristic forgetting \[Zhong et al., 2024\], pruning low-utility records
\[Xiong et al., 2026\], or injecting curated operational playbooks into
working context \[Chen et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2026\]. Unlike prior
frameworks designed for single trajectories, cross-task transfer, or
brief retries, autoresearch harnesses must sustain refinement across
long horizons of scored attempts. In our study, we compare five context-
and skill-management mechanisms within a shared harness, varying one
component at a time.

##### AI4AI Benchmarks.

A growing set of benchmarks employs agents to automate the development
of AI systems themselves, a direction that ML-Master \[Liu et al.,
2025b\], MLAgentBench \[Huang et al., 2024\] and MLE-Bench \[Chan et
al., 2025\] task agents with improving machine learning models on
research problems and Kaggle competitions. KernelBench \[Ouyang et al.,
2025\] and TritonBench \[Li et al., 2025\] focus on writing correct and
fast GPU kernels. On RE-Bench \[Wijk et al., 2025\], agents outperform
human experts under short time budgets but gain less from longer ones,
so the effectiveness of making use of a long run of attempts remains an
open problem for autoresearch agents. We use MLE-Bench and KernelBench,
where every attempt is scored, so each mechanism’s effect can be tracked
throughout a run.

## 3 Memory Mechanisms

In this section, we introduce the iterative self-evolution procedure and
detail the memory mechanisms across two aspects: context management
(§3.3) and skill management (§3.4). Figure 2 illustrates one iteration
and how each memory mechanism interacts with the agent.

### 3.1 The Iterative Self-Evolution Loop

Each task is solved through a sequence of scored evolution iterations.
At iteration $`t`$, the agent first selects an action from three
operators: propose_new, which develops a new approach; refine_current,
which modifies the incumbent solution; and debug_current, which
addresses a failure in the incumbent. A skill picker then selects
reusable skills from the task-specific skill library. The coder receives
the task, standing rules, selected skills, and the representation of the
previous attempt history, and produces the next solution. The benchmark
evaluator executes the solution and returns task-specific feedback.
Finally, a skill writer records lessons from the iteration. Skill
creation remains an agent decision and any iteration can request zero or
more skills.

We use the same loop across all experimental configurations. Let
$`H_{t}=(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1})`$ denote the attempt history before
iteration $`t`$, where $`a_{0}`$ is a common seed solution and each
subsequent $`a_{i}`$ contains the generated solution, evaluation
feedback, and associated reasoning. The coder receives

```math
C_{t}=[T,R_{t},K_{t},\Phi(H_{t})],\qquad|C_{t}|\leq B, \tag{1}
```

where $`T`$ is the task description, $`R_{t}`$ is the set of standing
rules, $`K_{t}`$ is the subset of the task’s skill library selected by
the picker, $`\Phi`$ is the configuration-specific history policy, and
$`B=32{,}000`$ tokens is the fixed context budget.

### 3.2 Baseline

The baseline uses recency truncation. Let $`B_{H}`$ denote the portion
of the context budget available to the attempt history after accounting
for the other prompt components. The baseline retains the longest suffix
that fits:

```math
\begin{gathered}(a_{j},\ldots,a_{t-1}),\\
j=\min\{k:|(a_{k},\ldots,a_{t-1})|\leq B_{H}\}.\end{gathered} \tag{2}
```

When the history exceeds the available budget, the oldest attempts are
discarded first. A discarded attempt cannot be retrieved later. The
skill library remains independent of this truncation and continues to
accumulate skills that can be selected by the picker.

### 3.3 Context Management

Context management operates on the growing attempt history $`H_{t}`$. We
compare recency truncation with two mechanisms that preserve information
from older attempts in compressed form: folding and compaction.

##### Folding.

Folding represents every previous attempt using a compact diagnostic
digest while allowing selected attempts to be unfolded in full. A
summarizer $`\sigma`$ maps each attempt $`a_{i}`$ to a digest
$`\hat{a}_{i}=\sigma(a_{i})`$. At iteration $`t`$, the agent receives
the sequence of digests and selects a subset $`U_{t}`$ of attempts for
full expansion:

```math
\Phi_{\mathrm{fold}}(H_{t})=(\hat{a}_{0},\ldots,\hat{a}_{t-1})\oplus(a_{i})_{i\in U_{t}}. \tag{3}
```

Unfolded attempts are admitted according to their selected priority
until the history budget $`B_{H}`$ is reached. Every attempt remains
addressable throughout the run, so historical information can be
recovered based on relevance.

##### Compaction.

Compaction maintains a running summary $`m`$ together with a tail
$`\tau`$ of recent attempts. When the coder context reaches the
configured window, the history since the previous compaction is divided
into a recent tail $`\tau`$ and an older head $`h`$. The head is
incorporated into the running summary:

```math
m\leftarrow S(m,h),\qquad\Phi_{\mathrm{comp}}(H_{t})=m\oplus\tau. \tag{4}
```

The summary follows a fixed structure containing the task objective,
important details, and current work state. Unlike folding, compaction
does not preserve individual attempts that can later be selected and
expanded.

### 3.4 Skill Management

While context management governs the attempt history, the agent also
maintains a second memory tier: a task-local skill library $`L_{t}`$.
Each task initializes an empty library, and an active skill reaches the
coder only when selected by the skill picker. To prevent catalog bloat
and control retrieval overhead, we evaluate three management mechanisms:
pruning via deletion, consolidation via merging, and elevation via
promotion.

##### Deletion.

Deletion removes skills that remain unused for a sustained period. Let
$`u_{k}(t)`$ denote the number of consecutive iterations for which skill
$`k`$ has not been selected. We set the deletion threshold to
$`\tau_{d}=5`$:

```math
\left\{k\in L_{t}:u_{k}(t)<\tau_{d}\right\}. \tag{5}
```

A skill is therefore removed after five consecutive iterations without
selection. Deletion changes only the live skill catalog and does not
modify the attempt history or standing rules.

##### Merging.

Merging consolidates newly written skills that are semantically close to
an existing skill. Each skill $`k`$ has an embedding $`e(k)`$. For a
newly written skill $`k^{\prime}`$, its nearest live neighbor is

```math
k^{*}=\arg\max_{k\in L_{t}}\cos(e(k^{\prime}),e(k)). \tag{6}
```

If $`\cos(e(k^{\prime}),e(k^{*}))\geq\theta`$ with $`\theta=0.8`$, the
two skills are merged. Otherwise, $`k^{\prime}`$ is added as a new
skill. Merging therefore reduces redundancy in the skill library.

##### Promotion.

Promotion converts selected reusable skills into standing rules. Unlike
ordinary skills, standing rules are included in every subsequent coder
prompt and do not require retrieval by the skill picker.

A promotion agent examines the skill library and usage statistics. For
each skill, the promoter observes how often it was selected and how
often it was present during an iteration that produced a new best
result. It then nominates a subset $`N_{t}=\pi(L_{t})`$ for promotion. A
nominee must pass an admissibility check $`A`$ requiring that it
contributed to at least one improvement. Accepted nominees are added to
the standing-rule set $`R_{t}`$ and removed from the ordinary skill
library, yielding $`R_{t+1}=R_{t}\cup A(N_{t})`$ and
$`L_{t+1}=L_{t}\setminus A(N_{t})`$.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

We evaluate the five memory mechanisms and baseline on two machine
learning benchmarks. MLE-Bench evaluates agents on Kaggle
machine-learning competitions \[Chan et al., 2025\], while KernelBench
evaluates agents’ ability to generate fast and correct GPU kernels for
machine-learning workloads \[Ouyang et al., 2025\]. We use three model
backbones, gpt-oss-120b \[OpenAI et al., 2025\], gpt-6-luna \[OpenAI,
2026a\] and gpt-6-sol \[OpenAI, 2026b\]. For merging,
Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B Zhang et al. \[2025\] is used to generate the skill
embeddings.

Each task has an independent skill library, which is reset before the
task begins. All configurations for a given task start from the same
seed solution that is curated to be deliberately weak. The memory policy
determines how the attempt history and skill information occupy the
context.

##### MLE-Bench.

We evaluate on 10 competitions from MLE-Bench \[Chan et al., 2025\],
comprising six low-complexity and four medium-complexity competitions.
The selected competitions and benchmark-specific modifications are
detailed in Appendix B.

Each configuration runs 100 iterations per competition. In each
iteration, the agent’s solution trains on the competition’s training
data and writes a submission, which is then graded. The agent then
receives its score under the competition’s metric, together with any
terminal output produced during execution. Because the metrics differ in
scale and direction, we report a gold-normalized score (GNS), analogous
to the human-normalized score in reinforcement learning \[Mnih et al.,
2015\]:

```math
\mathrm{GNS}_{i}=100\cdot\frac{s_{i}-s_{i}^{0}}{s_{i}^{\mathrm{gold}}-s_{i}^{0}}, \tag{7}
```

where $`s_{i}`$ is a run’s best score on competition $`i`$,
$`s_{i}^{0}`$ the score of a do-nothing submission, and
$`s_{i}^{\mathrm{gold}}`$ the gold medal threshold on MLE-Bench’s
leaderboard for that competition \[Chan et al., 2025\]. We aggregate
over the $`N=10`$ competitions with weights $`w_{i}`$ of 1 and 2 for
low- and medium-complexity competitions:

```math
\mathrm{GNS}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\,\mathrm{GNS}_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}}. \tag{8}
```

##### KernelBench.

We evaluate 50 KernelBench problems across three difficulty levels
\[Ouyang et al., 2025\]: 20 Level-1 individual operators, 20 Level-2
fused operator chains, and 10 Level-3 model architectures. Appendix A.2
describes how the evaluation subset is constructed.

Each configuration runs for 50 refinement iterations per problem. At
each iteration, the generated CUDA kernel is compiled and evaluated
against the PyTorch Eager reference implementation on randomly generated
inputs. The evaluation runner applies static and runtime safeguards
against reward hacking, including checks for prohibited code patterns
and protection against reusing reference outputs. Implementations that
violate these checks are invalidated. Appendix A.1 describes the
correctness and timing procedures, while Appendix A.3 details the
reward-hacking safeguards.

We report the geometric-mean speedup over PyTorch Eager, consistent with
recent evaluations of agent-generated GPU kernels \[Dai et al., 2026,
Younesian et al., 2026\]. The geometric mean provides a continuous
summary of multiplicative performance gains across the 50 tasks and is
less sensitive than the arithmetic mean to exceptionally large speedups.

##### Folding performs best across all model backbones and both benchmarks.

Table 1 reports results across 10 MLE-Bench competitions and 50
KernelBench tasks. Folding achieves the highest performance among all
evaluated mechanisms in all six settings, with an average relative
improvement of 8.6% over the baseline. Its gains range from 1.6% to 7.9%
on MLE-Bench and 8.3% to 19.2% on KernelBench. With gpt-oss-120b,
folding raises GNS from 70.5 to 76.1 and geometric-mean speedup from
$`1.17\times`$ to $`1.29\times`$. With gpt-6-sol, it raises GNS from
93.2 to 94.7 and geometric-mean speedup from $`2.16\times`$ to
$`2.34\times`$. These improvements span the lowest- and
highest-performing backbones in our study. The context analysis in
Section 5.1 examines how retaining individually accessible records of
earlier attempts contributes to this consistent advantage.

##### Skill management consistently improves performance, with deletion performing best on average.

Deletion, merging, and promotion improve on the baseline for all models
and benchmarks. Deletion yields the largest average relative gain at
4.1%, followed by promotion at 3.0% and merging at 2.2%. Averaged across
the three mechanisms and both benchmarks, the gains are 5.1% for
gpt-oss-120b, 1.9% for gpt-6-luna, and 2.1% for gpt-6-sol. Compared with
gpt-oss-120b, gpt-6-luna gains 3.2 percentage points less on each
benchmark. For gpt-6-sol, the gap is 3.5 points on MLE-Bench and 2.5
points on KernelBench. Section 5.3 analyzes this difference by
investigating how the three models interact with the skill library.

##### Compaction remains near the recency-truncation baseline.

Compaction yields little improvement in final task performance on
average, with a mean relative gain of 0.3%. On MLE-Bench, it raises GNS
from 70.5 to 71.6 for gpt-oss-120b, but lowers it from 93.2 to 91.6 for
gpt-6-sol. On KernelBench, it increases geometric-mean speedup from
$`2.16\times`$ to $`2.20\times`$ for gpt-6-sol, but decreases it from
$`1.17\times`$ to $`1.16\times`$ for gpt-oss-120b. These mixed results
show that although compaction reduces the context occupied by earlier
attempts, it cannot produce a consistent performance gain over the
baseline. Section 5.1 examines this performance–cost trade-off and
compares how much information various context management mechanisms
retain from earlier attempts.

##### Most gains emerge early, but later iterations continue to refine the solutions.

Figure 6 shows performance over iterations for all three model
backbones. Averaged across the six configurations and three backbones,
KernelBench geometric-mean speedup increases by 65.2% in the first half
and by a further 7.4% in the second half. On MLE-Bench, GNS increases by
an average of 41.6 points in the first half and by another 2.9 points in
the second half. Thus, most improvement occurs during the first half of
the run, while every configuration continues to make measurable progress
during later iterations. In addition, folding leads at the midpoint in
five of the six model–benchmark settings and finishes first in all six.
This indicates that its advantage emerges early and persists through
subsequent refinement.

## 5 Analysis

### 5.1 How Context Affects Agent Performance

We quantify how often an attempt advances the incumbent solution using
the new-best rate: the share of attempts whose score beats every earlier
attempt of the same run. We report it for the first 10% of the
iterations and for the rest.

Figure 3: Context usage and progress under different context-management
mechanisms. (a) Average number of past attempts and average context
tokens in the coder’s prompt. (b) New-best rate for the three
mechanisms.

The three context management mechanisms achieve similar new-best rates
during the first 10% of iterations. As shown in Figure 3b, folding
reaches 35.4%, compared with 36.3% for compaction and 35.3% for the
baseline. After the first 10% of iterations, when more past attempts
have accumulated, folding achieves the highest average new-best rate at
12.0%, compared with 10.2% for the baseline and 9.7% for compaction.
Folding therefore discovers improvements about 18% more frequently than
the baseline and 24% more frequently than compaction during this later
stage.

Figure 3a shows how folding expands access to experimental history. The
baseline retains an average of 6.2 recent attempts in full and discards
earlier history. In contrast, folding condenses each attempt into a
compact digest of the attempted strategy and its outcome, retaining
records of 42.0 past attempts on average while allowing the agent to
recover full records when needed. It achieves this broader coverage with
approximately 12.1k context tokens on average, compared with 18.4k for
the baseline. Access to these prior successes and failures allows the
agent to target each new attempt more effectively.

Compaction also uses approximately 12.1k tokens on average, but it
achieves a lower later-stage new-best rate. It merges all older attempts
into one summary of bounded length, which cannot keep pace with a
growing history. Together, these results indicate that reducing context
usage alone is insufficient to sustain improvement. The representation
must also preserve useful access to accumulated experimental history.

### 5.2 Separating Skill-Library and Management Gains

To separate the benefit of maintaining a skill library from the
additional benefits of managing it, we compare each mechanism with a
No-Skill ablation in which the skill writer and picker are disabled.
Figure 4a reports relative performance changes computed separately for
each model–benchmark setting and then averaged across settings.

Figure 4: Performance and skill retrieval under different
skill-management mechanisms. (a) Gains over the no-skill agent. Each
mechanism adds its management gain (solid segment) to the baseline’s
library gain (hatched segment). (b) Skills picked per prompt (bars, left
axis) and gain over the same model’s baseline (dots, right axis),
averaged across both benchmarks. (c) Share of offensive and defensive
skills among the selected skills, averaged over MLE-Bench and
KernelBench.

Introducing an unmanaged skill library improves average performance by
2.1% over No-Skill, showing that recording and retrieving lessons
benefits iterative refinement. Managing those lessons provides further
gains over the unmanaged baseline, with deletion yielding the largest
average improvement at 4.1%, followed by promotion at 3.0% and merging
at 2.2%.

Deletion uses the model backbone’s recent skill selections as a signal
of relevance, pruning persistently unselected lessons as the run
progresses. This also reduces the number of entries the picker must
consider. As shown in Figure 8, on MLE-Bench with gpt-oss-120b, deletion
leaves 31 skills at the end of the run, compared with 312 for the
unmanaged baseline and 330 for promotion.

Merging and promotion use different criteria, which explains their
smaller average gains. Merging consolidates semantically similar skills,
but can weaken specific applicability conditions or implementation
details. Promotion keeps lessons from earlier attempts as standing
rules, even when the solution evolves and their original conditions no
longer apply. Appendix E.3 illustrates these issues with case studies
from both benchmarks.

### 5.3 Model Differences in Skill Retrieval and Orientation

The different gains from skill management coincide with differences in
how the three model backbones use the skill library. Figure 4b shows
that gpt-oss-120b retrieves 6.4–7.1 skills per prompt across
configurations, whereas gpt-6-luna retrieves 1.2–1.9 and gpt-6-sol
retrieves 0.5–0.6. This ordering holds across the baseline and all three
skill-management mechanisms, suggesting consistent differences in the
models’ skill selection behavior.

The models also differ in the orientation of the knowledge drawn from
the skill library. We classify a skill as offensive when it encourages a
new implementation, optimization, or search direction intended to
improve the score, and as defensive when it primarily protects the
current solution by preserving correctness or avoiding known failures.
Full classification criteria are provided in Appendix E.1. As shown in
Figure 4c, 53.1% of the skills retrieved by gpt-oss-120b are offensive,
compared with 30.2% for gpt-6-luna and 25.4% for gpt-6-sol.

Together, these observations suggest that the two proprietary backbones,
gpt-6-luna and gpt-6-sol, use the skill library more conservatively.
They draw on fewer stored experiences and more often select knowledge
aimed at protecting existing performance. The open-weight backbone,
gpt-oss-120b, draws on a broader set of experiences with a more even
balance between protective guidance and active optimization. This
difference offers one explanation for why skill management provides
larger gains for gpt-oss-120b.

## 6 MLEvolver: Combining the Best Choice in Each Dimension

Context folding and skill deletion deliver the largest average gains
among the context-management and skill-management mechanisms,
respectively. We combine them in MLEvolver and compare the resulting
harness with AIDE and OpenEvolve on 22 competitions in MLE-Bench Lite
and 50 problems in KernelBench Level 3. We track how each system
improves over 100 iterations and compare its final performance.

Figure 5: Performance of MLEvolver, AIDE, and OpenEvolve. (a) Any-medal
and gold-medal rates across the 22 MLE-Bench Lite competitions. (b)
$`\mathrm{fast}_{1}`$ and $`\mathrm{fast}_{2}`$ across the 50
KernelBench Level 3 problems. (c) Gold-normalized score on MLE-Bench
Lite through 100 iterations. (d) Geometric-mean speedup over PyTorch
Eager on KernelBench Level 3 through 100 iterations.

### 6.1 Setup

##### Harnesses.

MLEvolver combines the best-performing mechanism on average in each
dimension: folding for context management and deletion for skill
management. AIDE \[Jiang et al., 2025\] searches a tree of candidate
solutions. At each step, it drafts a new solution, refines the best node
found so far, or debugs a failing node. OpenEvolve \[Sharma, 2025\], an
open-source implementation of AlphaEvolve \[Novikov et al., 2025\],
maintains a program database organized into islands. At each iteration,
it samples a parent program and provides high-performing and
“inspiration” programs as context. The model edits the parent using
search-and-replace diffs.

##### Benchmarks and budget.

We evaluate 22 competitions in MLE-Bench Lite, the low-complexity split
of MLE-Bench \[Chan et al., 2025\], and 50 problems in KernelBench Level
3, which covers full model architectures \[Ouyang et al., 2025\]. All
three harnesses use gpt-6-sol, a 32K-token context window, and a budget
of 100 iterations per run.

##### Metrics.

We track progress over iterations using the gold-normalized score (GNS)
on MLE-Bench Lite and geometric-mean speedup over PyTorch Eager on
KernelBench, as in Section 4. We also report any-medal and gold-medal
rates on MLE-Bench Lite, the percentages of competitions in which the
best submission reaches at least the bronze and gold thresholds,
respectively \[Chan et al., 2025\]. On KernelBench, we report
$`\mathrm{fast}_{1}`$ and $`\mathrm{fast}_{2}`$, the percentages of
problems with a correct kernel whose speedup over PyTorch Eager exceeds
$`1\times`$ and $`2\times`$, respectively \[Ouyang et al., 2025\].

### 6.2 Results

##### MLEvolver outperforms AIDE and OpenEvolve on both benchmarks.

As illustrated in Figure 5a and Figure 5b, MLEvolver earns medals in 16
of 22 MLE-Bench Lite competitions (72.7%), a relative gain of 23.1% over
AIDE and 14.3% over OpenEvolve. It wins 10 gold medals, 42.9% more than
either baseline. On KernelBench Level 3, MLEvolver reaches 94% on
$`\mathrm{fast}_{1}`$, a relative gain of 20.5% over AIDE and 34.3% over
OpenEvolve. At the stricter $`\mathrm{fast}_{2}`$ threshold, MLEvolver
produces correct kernels with more than $`2\times`$ speedup over PyTorch
Eager on 31 of 50 problems (62%), more than twice as many as AIDE and
more than four times as many as OpenEvolve. These results show that
MLEvolver is effective for long-horizon, self-evolving autoresearch on
machine learning tasks.

##### MLEvolver pulls ahead early and extends its lead.

Figure 5c and Figure 5d show that MLEvolver reaches 97.4 GNS by
iteration 20 on MLE-Bench Lite and a $`2.08\times`$ geometric-mean
speedup by iteration 10 on KernelBench Level 3. On both benchmarks,
these early results exceed the performance that AIDE and OpenEvolve
reach at iteration 100. On MLE-Bench Lite, MLEvolver maintains its lead
and finishes at 98.1 GNS, 1.4% higher than AIDE and 2.6% higher than
OpenEvolve. On KernelBench, MLEvolver widens its lead in the second half
of the run. Between iterations 50 and 100, its geometric-mean speedup
increases by 11.9%, from $`2.67\times`$ to $`2.99\times`$, while AIDE
and OpenEvolve improve by 0.8% and 5.2%, respectively.

### 6.3 Discussion

##### Folded history supports informed exploration and refinement.

All three systems can explore different approaches. AIDE \[Jiang et al.,
2025\] follows a predefined policy for drafting, debugging, and
improving solutions, with hyperparameters controlling the number of
initial drafts and debugging behavior. OpenEvolve \[Sharma, 2025\]
samples parent and inspiration programs from its populations according
to configured rules. In MLEvolver, the model uses a catalog of attempt
digests to decide whether to refine a promising solution, debug a
failure, or propose a new approach when progress stalls. It can also
retrieve full records of relevant earlier attempts, including their
code, recorded reasoning, scores, and execution output. This combination
provides an overview of previously explored directions and access to the
details needed to build on earlier successes and failures. The value of
retaining this access is reflected in Section 5.1, where folding
preserves more experimental history within the context window and
achieves a higher new-best rate during later iterations.

##### Distilled skills give MLEvolver direct guidance for later attempts.

AIDE summarizes earlier valid solutions, and OpenEvolve provides
selected programs alongside summaries of recent changes, metrics, and
outcomes. Alongside the attempt digests, MLEvolver maintains a separate
library of lessons distilled from its experiments. Retrieving these
lessons directly reduces the need to reconstruct them from earlier code
and results. Offensive skills suggest promising directions to explore,
while defensive skills record constraints and failure patterns that can
help the model avoid repeating mistakes. Deletion prunes persistently
unselected skills, using the model’s retrieval behavior as a signal of
which lessons remain relevant as the search progresses. The benefits of
both retaining and managing these lessons are supported by Section 5.2,
where maintaining the skill library improves performance and deletion
provides a further gain.

##### Solution space shapes scaling.

The two benchmarks differ in the structure of their solution spaces. On
MLE-Bench Lite, the solution space is more discrete, with promising
solutions built around distinct choices of model family and feature
representation. The harnesses can therefore start with substantially
different submissions, contributing to the spread in GNS at iteration 1.
On KernelBench, the solution space is more continuous. The first correct
kernels run about as fast as PyTorch Eager on average, and later
optimizations yield incremental speedups. These differences favor
different search strategies. On MLE-Bench Lite, exploring several
distinct candidates increases the chance of finding a strong solution.
AIDE explores candidates across branches of a search tree, and
OpenEvolve samples from a population of programs. As the runs progress,
AIDE’s gap to MLEvolver narrows from 3.5% to 1.4%, and OpenEvolve’s
narrows from 15.4% to 2.6%. On KernelBench, successive optimizations can
build on one another, favoring sequential self-refinement. MLEvolver
uses a single refinement chain with context folding and skill deletion,
so each new edit can draw on results and lessons from earlier attempts.
AIDE refines a node in its search tree, and OpenEvolve edits a sampled
parent with guidance from selected programs. In both cases, the model
has a more limited view of which optimizations succeeded or failed in
earlier attempts. Between iterations 50 and 100, MLEvolver increases its
geometric-mean speedup from $`2.67\times`$ to $`2.99\times`$, while AIDE
and OpenEvolve make smaller gains and finish at $`1.85\times`$ and
$`1.41\times`$, respectively.

## 7 Conclusion

We present a controlled study of harness design for long-horizon,
self-evolving autoresearch agents. Within one agent, we isolate five
memory mechanisms and compare each with a recency-truncation baseline on
MLE-Bench and KernelBench using gpt-oss-120b, gpt-6-luna, and gpt-6-sol.
Three findings emerge from the experiment. First, folding performs best
in all settings by condensing each attempt into a short digest, allowing
the agent to retain more experimental history within the context budget
and target solutions more effectively. Second, both retaining skills and
actively managing them improve performance, with deletion providing the
largest average gain among skill-management mechanisms. Third, the
benefits of skill management vary with the model backbone’s skill
selection behavior. Both gpt-6-luna and gpt-6-sol retrieve fewer skills
and favor defensive guidance more strongly than gpt-oss-120b,
accompanying their smaller gains from skill management. Based on these
findings, we combine folding and deletion in MLEvolver, which achieves a
72.7% medal rate on MLE-Bench Lite and 94% on $`\mathrm{fast}_{1}`$ on
KernelBench Level 3, outperforming AIDE and OpenEvolve on both
benchmarks. Overall, our study identifies effective choices for
preserving experimental history and curating reusable lessons, providing
an empirically grounded foundation for long-horizon, self-evolving AI
research agents.

## Limitations

Our study isolates memory mechanisms within linear, sequential
refinement loops and leaves parallel scaling unexplored. Parallel search
strategies, such as concurrent tree search and population-based
evolution, distribute attempts across parallel trajectories and place
distinct demands on context budgets and skill selection. Our system
comparisons cover structured autoresearch frameworks with guided
research loops. Interactive coding agents, such as Claude Code or Codex,
are not evaluated. They execute open-ended sequences of tool calls and
internal refinement rounds, making it difficult to define a common unit
of iteration or enforce comparable evaluation budgets across systems.
Finally, each task initializes an independent skill library. Our
findings therefore characterize within-task experience accumulation,
leaving other evolutionary effects, such as cross-domain skill transfer
and multi-task skill distillation, for future work.

## Appendix A KernelBench Protocol and Evaluation Details

We evaluate all agent configurations on 50 tasks from KernelBench
\[Ouyang et al., 2025\]. This section describes the evaluation
environment, timing procedure, task subset selection, and reward-hacking
safeguards.

### A.1 Evaluation Environment and Timing Protocol

All evaluations execute on a dedicated NVIDIA GH200 144G HBM3e GPU. The
software environment runs Python 3.10.20, PyTorch 2.11.0+cu128, and CUDA
12.8.

##### Equivalence Verification.

Following Ouyang et al. \[2025\], functional correctness evaluates
numerical output equivalence between the PyTorch reference
implementation and the agent’s submitted kernel on identical input
tensors.

##### Latency Measurement.

The original KernelBench protocol \[Ouyang et al., 2025\] uses the
arithmetic mean execution time across runs to compute speedup, as its
benchmark suite exhibits a coefficient of variation ($`\sigma/\mu`$)
below 3%. In our long-horizon agent setting, execution times do not
consistently remain within this variation threshold across repeated
compilation and execution passes. We therefore use the median execution
time to compute speedup, reducing the influence of transient
operating-system overhead and driver allocation spikes.

### A.2 Stratified Problem Selection

KernelBench contains 250 problems across three difficulty levels. The
full suite includes many similar operations evaluated with different
tensor shapes, so uniform sampling could overrepresent some
computational patterns. We therefore construct a 50-task subset from 28
strata defined by computational structure. The subset includes at least
one task from every stratum and preserves the benchmark’s 2:2:1 level
distribution: 20 Level-1 tasks testing individual operators, 20 Level-2
tasks testing fused operator chains, and 10 Level-3 tasks testing model
architectures. Table 2 lists the selected problems and their stratum
assignments.

##### Sampling Frame and Exclusions.

Our sampling frame contains 238 of the 250 KernelBench problems: 99 from
Level 1, 89 from Level 2, and all 50 from Level 3. We exclude 12
problems for the following reasons:

- •
  Degenerate problems (3 Level-2 tasks: p23, p80, and p83). The
  reference implementations produce outputs that are either identically
  zero or numerically indistinguishable from zero under the benchmark’s
  correctness tolerance. For example, in problem 80, a column-wise
  maximum reduces the tensor to a single column, after which mean
  subtraction and GELU produce an all-zero output. A trivial kernel that
  returns zeros can therefore pass numerical validation without
  performing the intended computation. We exclude these problems because
  they do not meaningfully evaluate kernel optimization.
- •
  Algebraically reducible problems (8 Level-2 tasks: p13, p14, p18, p42,
  p44, p51, p65, and p98). In these problems, a matrix multiplication or
  convolution is followed by operations that permit most of the original
  computation to be removed through algebraic simplification. For
  example, the reference computation in problem 14,
  $`\sum_{j}(xW^{\top})_{j}/2\cdot s`$, can be rewritten as
  $`x\left(\sum_{j}W_{j}\right)/2\cdot s`$. This reformulation replaces
  a general matrix multiplication (GEMM) with a less expensive general
  matrix-vector multiplication (GEMV). We exclude these problems because
  their performance is determined primarily by discovering an algebraic
  shortcut.
- •
  Missing reference timing (1 Level-1 task: p95). Problem p95,
  CrossEntropyLoss, has no valid FP32 reference latency in our
  evaluation procedure. Because speedup is defined relative to the
  PyTorch Eager reference latency, we cannot compute a speedup for this
  problem and therefore exclude it from the sampling frame.

##### Stratification and Sample Allocation.

We group the 238 eligible tasks into 28 strata according to their
computational structure. The strata distinguish, for example, reductions
over different tensor dimensions, elementwise operations following
matrix multiplications or convolutions, and sequential or recurrent
computations.

We determine the initial number of tasks assigned to each stratum using
a regularized form of Neyman allocation \[Neyman, 1934\]:

```math
n_{h}=n\frac{N_{h}\hat{\sigma}_{h}}{\sum_{k=1}^{H}N_{k}\hat{\sigma}_{k}}, \tag{9}
```

where $`H=28`$ is the number of strata, $`n=50`$ is the total sample
size, $`N_{h}`$ is the number of eligible tasks in stratum $`h`$, and
$`n_{h}`$ is the number sampled from that stratum. The quantity
$`\hat{\sigma}_{h}`$ estimates the within-stratum standard deviation of
$`\log(\mathrm{speedup})`$ across pilot replicates. We regularize this
estimate toward an analytic optimization-headroom score ranging from 0
to 3. We also require every stratum to contain at least one sampled
task.

The level budgets are fixed at 20, 20, and 10 tasks, matching the full
benchmark’s 2:2:1 distribution. Sampling weights account for this
difference when estimating performance over the eligible frame.

##### Subset Selection and Runtime Matching.

Using the stratum allocations, we generate 3,000 candidate subsets by
sampling uniformly without replacement within each stratum, with seed 20260919. We select the candidate that best matches the 238-task
sampling frame in log reference runtime, using an objective that
combines the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) distance, the difference in mean
log runtime, and deviation from the target level distribution. Reference
runtime is the median latency of the PyTorch eager implementation on the
evaluation hardware and is independent of agent performance.

### A.3 Anti-Reward-Hacking Safeguards

Kernel-generation agents can discover shortcuts that increase measured
speedup without implementing the intended computation \[Baronio et al.,
2026, Dai et al., 2026\]. We combine static code inspection, runtime
isolation, and prompt-based guidance to detect or discourage these
strategies.

##### Reference Output Isolation.

Prior work has shown that candidate kernels may exploit outputs left in
GPU memory by the preceding reference execution \[Baronio et al.,
2026\]. In particular, a candidate that allocates an uninitialized
tensor may receive memory containing values from the reference output
and pass numerical verification without writing every output element. We
prevent this shortcut by retaining the reference output until numerical
verification is complete, so its device memory cannot be reused during
candidate execution.

##### Static Code Inspection.

Before compilation, we inspect each candidate implementation using
Python’s ast module. The validation gate rejects candidates that:

1.  1.  do not define explicit CUDA source containing at least one
        \_\_global\_\_ kernel;
2.  2.  delegate all reference operations to high-level frameworks or
        compilation interfaces instead of implementing a complete operation
        in a custom kernel;
3.  3.  patch timing functions, reference modules, or evaluation code;
4.  4.  modify input tensors or tensor references in place; or
5.  5.  spawn background threads or processes, or defer timed work to an
        execution stream that is not synchronized with the primary stream.

A candidate that fails any of these checks is marked invalid and can
never become the task’s best attempt; speedup is computed from each
task’s best valid attempt.

##### Numerical State Isolation.

Candidates also attempt to alter the evaluation by changing process-wide
numerical settings, such as PyTorch matrix-multiplication precision or
global TF32 flags. The harness records the relevant numerical settings
and restores the required evaluation configuration before reference
timing. Candidates that modify prohibited precision or backend settings
are rejected. This prevents a candidate from obtaining an apparent
speedup by reducing numerical precision or by slowing the reference
implementation.

##### Prompt-Based Integrity Guidance.

The runtime safeguards are complemented by prompt instructions that
discourage invalid optimization strategies before code is generated or
reusable knowledge is recorded. These instructions guide the agent
toward genuine kernel optimization but are not treated as enforcement
mechanisms.

### B.2 Competition-Specific Evaluation

Table 4 gives the metric and the direction, the do-nothing score
$`s_{0}`$, the perfect score $`s^{\ast}`$, and the gold medal threshold
$`s_{\mathrm{gold}}`$ of each competition.

##### Do-nothing score.

For AUC, $`s_{0}=0.5`$, since it is a random ranking. For Pearson
correlation, $`s_{0}=0`$, since the metric is already zero for an
uninformative prediction. For the other metrics we measure $`s_{0}`$ by
grading the do-nothing submission with the competition’s own grader:
submitting each tweet unchanged (Jaccard), predicting the class
frequencies (log loss, so $`s_{0}`$ is the entropy of the label
distribution), and predicting the best constant, the mean pixel value
(RMSE).

##### Gold threshold.

$`s_{\mathrm{gold}}`$ is the score at the gold rank on the competition’s
private leaderboard snapshot distributed with MLE-Bench, under Kaggle’s
medal rule: the top 10% of teams when fewer than 100 teams entered, the
top 10 teams for 100 to 249 teams, and the top 10 teams plus 0.2% of all
teams for 250 or more. We take $`s_{\mathrm{gold}}`$ from the
leaderboard that MLE-Bench provides for each competition \[Chan et al.,
2025\], a snapshot of the competition’s final Kaggle private
leaderboard.

### B.3 Raw Score Normalization.

The competitions use different metrics with different directions, so we
convert a run’s best raw score $`s_{i}`$ on competition $`i`$ into a
gold-normalized score:

```math
\mathrm{GNS}_{i}=100\cdot\frac{s_{i}-s_{i}^{0}}{s_{i}^{\mathrm{gold}}-s_{i}^{0}}, \tag{10}
```

where $`s_{i}^{0}`$ is the score of a do-nothing submission and
$`s_{i}^{\mathrm{gold}}`$ the gold-medal threshold (Table 4). For
minimized metrics (RMSE and log loss), the numerator and the denominator
change sign together, so a do-nothing submission scores 0 and a
gold-level one 100 regardless of the metric’s direction. This is the
form of human-normalized score in reinforcement learning \[Mnih et al.,
2015\].

##### Invalid or missing submissions.

An attempt that crashes, exceeds its limits, or produces no valid
submission receives no score. It does not count toward the run’s best,
but it is considered as using up one iteration. A run’s score on a
competition is that of its best valid attempt.

### B.4 Gold Anchoring

We anchor at the gold medal threshold because the distance from gold to
perfect varies widely across competitions. Measured against gold, every
competition has the same reference point, 100, set by the best human
teams on MLE-Bench’s leaderboard \[Chan et al., 2025\].

##### Scores above gold are retained.

We do not clip $`\mathrm{GNS}_{i}`$ at 100. If the competition beats the
gold threshold, the score may be over 100.

### B.5 Complexity Weights

For each configuration, we take $`\mathrm{GNS}_{i}`$ over its runs, and
aggregate over the ten analyzed competitions with a weighted mean:

```math
\mathrm{GNS}=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}\,\mathrm{GNS}_{i}}{\sum_{i}w_{i}}, \tag{11}
```

with $`w_{i}=1`$ for low-complexity and $`w_{i}=2`$ for
medium-complexity competitions. The categories come directly from
MLE-Bench. MLE-Bench labels a competition low complexity if an
experienced machine learning engineer could produce a sensible solution
in under two hours, excluding training time. It labels a competition
medium complexity if it would take two to ten hours \[Chan et al.,
2025\]. Based on that, we weight medium complexity competitions twice
because they demand more engineering.

### B.6 Execution Protocol

Each run starts from a deliberately chance-level seed solution, the same
for every configuration and backbone, which is evaluated as attempt 0.
The agent then runs 100 iterations, each consisting of one coder call
and one execution of the resulting solution.

Before grading, submission.csv must exist and match the sample
submission’s columns and row count. MLE-Bench’s grader then scores it,
rejecting malformed files \[Chan et al., 2025\]. An attempt that
crashes, times out, or produces an invalid submission receives no score,
and the agent sees the error output in its next prompt.

## Appendix E Behavior Data on Each Design

### E.1 Offensive and Defensive Skills

To describe the skill each backbone chooses, we label all of them as
offensive or defensive by their main purpose. An offensive skill aims to
raise the score of a working solution: a modeling technique, feature,
ensemble, or tuning step on MLE-Bench; a kernel technique such as
fusion, tiling, shared-memory reuse, or vectorized access on
KernelBench.

A defensive skill aims to prevent failure or loss. Its main
responsibility is to avoid crashes, version errors, time or memory
overruns, guard against leakage and overfit, or against incorrect
results, imprecise numerics, and timing noise on KernelBench.

##### Rules.

A skill is read as its title, description, and applicability trigger.
The title is lower-cased and its qualifiers are removed. Seven fixed
rules are tried in order, and the first that matches decides, so no
model is involved. Because the title states the skill’s main action,
rules 1–6 read only the title:

1.  1.  Defensive verb (defensive): the title starts with a protective verb,
        such as preserve, protect, keep, validate, guard, gate, avoid,
        prevent, bound, correct, fix, detect, or diagnose
2.  2.  Caution (defensive): a warning word or phrase appears anywhere in
        the title, such as “not”, “unproven”, “redundant”, “marginal”,
        “requires”, “fails”, “loses to”, “regresses”, or “diminishing”.
3.  3.  Plateau change (offensive): the title contains a plateau word
        (plateau, stagnation, stall) and a change word (diversify, change,
        pivot, switch, escape, escalate, alternative)
4.  4.  Offensive head (offensive): the head is a score-raising verb, such
        as add, use, combine, blend, stack, encode, augment, tune,
        calibrate, switch, or escalate, or the title reads “replace X with
        Y”.
5.  5.  Defensive words (defensive): a protection or a failure to prevent
        appears anywhere in the title, such as fallback, incumbent,
        checkpoint, budget, timeout, error, crash, leakage, or overfitting.
6.  6.  Offensive words (offensive): a technique or a positive result
        appears anywhere in the title, such as ensemble, augmentation,
        embedding, tuning, transformer, outperform, or improve.
7.  7.  Body: otherwise, the description and trigger decide: offensive if
        their offensive words outnumber their defensive and caution words
        together, and defensive otherwise.

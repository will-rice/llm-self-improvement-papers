# When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs

Johannes Zenn    Jonas Geiping Affiliation: Max Planck Institute for
Intelligent Systems Affiliation: ELLIS Institute Tübingen
Affiliation: AI Center Tübingen Affiliation: University of Tübingen
Affiliation: IMPRS-IS

###### Abstract

Many decoding methods for large language models can be understood as
shifting probability mass toward outputs that are more likely under the
model, either locally at the token level or globally at the sequence
level. Therefore, their success depends on a fundamental question: when
does sequence probability, that is, the conditional probability of a
continuation given a prompt, actually align with correctness? In this
paper, we set out to quantify this relationship across decoding methods,
models, and benchmarks at four levels: across decoding methods, across
hyperparameters within a method, across prompt-answer pairs within a
dataset, and across repeated responses to the same prompt. We find that
higher sequence probability is often predictive of correctness across
prompt-answer pairs within a fixed dataset. However, this relationship
does not generally transfer to decoding decisions: increasing sequence
probability by changing hyperparameters or methods does not reliably
improve accuracy. Further, sequence probability is not a good indicator
of correctness for responses to the same prompt. These findings clarify
when decoding can and cannot be expected to improve correctness, and
provide practical guidance for decoding, self-consistency, and
verifier-free self-improvement.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are autoregressive models that assign
probabilities to sequences of tokens. At inference time, decoding
methods use the autoregressive next-token distribution to exploit this
distribution in different ways. Many widely used methods can be viewed
as shifting probability mass toward outputs that are more likely under
the model. Decoding methods, like low-temperature next-token sampling
and truncation methods (e.g., top-$`k`$ sampling ([Fan et al.,
2018](#bib.bib13))) suppress unlikely tokens locally. Best-of-$`N`$
(Bo$`N`$) keeps only the highest-probability samples among a set of
$`N`$ sequences approximating the global mode (for $`N\to\infty`$)
similar to beam search ([Lowerre, 1976](#bib.bib35); [Sutskever et al.,
2014](#bib.bib44)). Power sampling ([Karan and Du, 2025](#bib.bib26);
[Ji et al., 2026](#bib.bib22); [Azizi et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)) biases
the distribution over full sequences towards high-probability regions
over-weighting high-probability sequences and under-weighting
low-probability sequences.

Viewing decoding through this lens raises a fundamental question: when
does sequence probability align with correctness? An answer to this
question has two important implications. Firstly, it helps explain when
particular decoding methods improve performance and opens up a way to
guide the development of new decoding methods. Secondly, if
higher-probability sequences are more likely to be correct, then model
probability itself may provide a verifier-free approach to
self-improvement by using higher-probability samples for
self-consistency ([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib50); [Wang et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib48); [Taubenfeld et al., 2025](#bib.bib45)) and
self-distillation ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib20); [Zhang et al.,
2019](#bib.bib56)).

Since LLMs are trained on data which is predominantly correct, it seems
natural to expect correct continuations to receive higher probability
than incorrect ones. However, this intuition is too simple: it has been
observed in various contexts that beam search, which approximately finds
the most likely sequence, often degrades performance ([Vijayakumar
et al., 2016](#bib.bib46); [Cohen and Beck, 2019](#bib.bib9); [Koehn and
Knowles, 2017](#bib.bib28); [Yang et al., 2018](#bib.bib53); [Su et al.,
2022](#bib.bib43)). This suggests that the question is less whether
there is a general probability-correctness relationship, but under which
conditions probability and correctness align and which decoding methods
are able to exploit the relationship.

In this paper, we quantify the relationship between sequence probability
and correctness across $`8`$ decoding methods ($`2`$ methods targeting
the power distribution, $`2`$ methods targeting the mode of the
distribution, and $`4`$ local methods), $`14`$ models (from the Qwen2.5,
Qwen3, Olmo3 families), and $`6`$ benchmark datasets. We analyze the
relationship on three different levels: We define across-method
correlation which fixes model and dataset and computes correlation
across methods with a representative hyperparameter
([Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs"),
left). For each method, within-method correlation considers the
correlation across hyperparameters
([Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs"),
left, non-transparent lines). Zooming in on one hyperparameter
([Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs"),
middle) we define the within-dataset correlation. Finally, within-sample
correlation is the most granular correlation for an individual sample
from a dataset
([Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs"),
right).

Our results show that there is no uniform probability-correctness
relationship for LLM generations. We find a consistent correlation
within a dataset depending on the model family but not the method
([Section 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Within-Dataset Correlation ‣ 3 Probability and Correctness at Various Granularity ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")).
This indicates that a model is often able to tell correct prompt-answers
pairs from incorrect prompt-answer pairs. However, this signal does not
generally transfer to the settings directly used for decoding. We find
that tuning the hyperparameters of a decoding method, while producing
sequences of higher log-probability, does not result in more correct
sequences
([Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Within-Method Correlation ‣ 3 Probability and Correctness at Various Granularity ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")).
Further, methods that produce higher-probability sequences are not
consistently more accurate
([Section 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Correlation Across Methods ‣ 3 Probability and Correctness at Various Granularity ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")).
For a single prompt, there is no consistent correlation within the
corresponding responses. However, more correct samples also show larger
within-sample correlations
([Section 3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Within-Sample Correlation ‣ 3 Probability and Correctness at Various Granularity ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")).

Summarily, the main contributions of this work are as follows:

- •
  We frame decoding methods as approaches to maximize sequence
  probability: we distinguish local methods from global sequence-level
  methods and give a variational objective for global methods.
- •
  We study the relationship between sequence probability and correctness
  across $`12`$ models, $`6`$ datasets, and $`8`$ decoding methods. We
  analyze correlation on various levels: across-method correlation,
  within-dataset correlation, within-method correlation, and
  within-sample correlation.
- •
  We find that within-dataset correlations between log-probability and
  correctness are often consistent and largely determined by the dataset
  and model variant, rather than by the decoding method. We show that
  increasing sequence probability by changing a decoding method or
  tuning its hyperparameters does not reliably improve correctness.
- •
  We connect these findings to verifier-free self-improvement methods.
  We find that probability can be informative when the model already has
  sufficient task accuracy, but within-sample correlations are often
  weak or symmetric around zero, limiting the reliability of
  probability-weighted aggregation.

## 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs

In this section, we view decoding methods in large language models
(LLMs) as sampling mehods to produce high-probability sequences. We
highlight two classes of commonly used decoding methods: local decoding
methods in
[Section 2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Local Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
and global decoding methods in
[Section 2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Global Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs").

Let $`p`$ denote an autoregressive LLM. Further, let the distribution
over continuations $`{\mathbf{s}}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{T})`$ given a prompt
$`\bar{\mathbf{s}}`$ under $`p`$ be defined as follows

|     |                                                                                                                      |     |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t}).`$ |     | (1) |

Local sampling methods change the next-token distribution at every
prefix $`\bar{\mathbf{s}}\mid{\mathbf{s}}_{<t}`$ for $`t=2,\dots,T`$
and, thus, generally do not sample the most probable global sequences
(see
[Remark B.1](#A2.Thmremark1 "Remark B.1 (Local sampling methods do not target most probable global sequences.). ‣ Appendix B Theoretical Arguments ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")).

##### Low-temperature next-token sampling (LTS).

Standard sampling at low temperatures modifies the next-token
distribution via

|     |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |     |     |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{\alpha}(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})\coloneqq\frac{p(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})^{\alpha}}{\tilde{Z}_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}\;\;\text{with}\;\;\tilde{Z}_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})\coloneqq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{V}}p(s\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})^{\alpha}.`$ |     | (2) |

Since LTS has the same support as $`p`$, low-probability regions are
down-weighted but not removed.

##### Truncated next-token sampling (TS).

TS specifies an active set
$`A(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})\subseteq\mathcal{V}`$ at each
prefix as

|     |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\!\!\!\!p_{A}(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})\coloneqq\frac{p(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}{\tilde{Z}_{A}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}\mathbb{1}[s_{t}\in A(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})]\;\;\text{with}\;\;\tilde{Z}_{A}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})\coloneqq\sum_{s\in A(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}p(s\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t}).`$ |     | (3) |

At every step, TS only samples from within this active set. The support
of $`p_{A}`$ is different from $`p{}`$.

Top-$`k`$ sampling ([Fan et al., 2018](#bib.bib13)) defines an active
set given by the $`k`$ highest-probability tokens under
$`p(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})`$ as
$`A(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})=\{\text{top-$k$ tokens under }p(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})\}.`$
Top-$`p`$ sampling ([Holtzman et al., 2020](#bib.bib21)) chooses the
smallest active set $`A(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})`$ whose
cumulative mass under $`p(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})`$
exceeds a threshold $`p\in(0,1]`$. $`\varepsilon`$-sampling ([Hewitt
et al., 2022](#bib.bib19)) sets
$`A(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})=\{s\in\mathcal{V}:p(s\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}\_{<t})>\varepsilon\}`$.

Global sampling methods, as discussed in the following, target the
globally most likely sequences.

##### Maximizing sequence probability.

Beam search ([Lowerre, 1976](#bib.bib35); [Sutskever et al.,
2014](#bib.bib44)) (BS) is an approximate search procedure for
$`{\mathbf{s}}=\argmax_{{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}}\ p({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$
by controlling its range with the so-called number of beams $`K`$.
Greedy decoding is recovered for $`K=1`$. BS is known to often find
repetitive sequences ([Vijayakumar et al., 2016](#bib.bib46); [Cohen and
Beck, 2019](#bib.bib9)). Further, quality deteriorates with larger beams
([Koehn and Knowles, 2017](#bib.bib28); [Yang et al., 2018](#bib.bib53))
and sequences lack diversity ([Su et al., 2022](#bib.bib43)).

###### Remark 2.1 (Beam search generates the highest probability sequence for $`K\to\infty`$.).

For large $`K`$ beam search returns an element of
$`\argmax_{{\mathbf{s}}}\ p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$.¹¹ 1 We
use the implementation of beam search in vLLM ([Kwon et al.,
2023](#bib.bib29)) which adds a length normalization.

##### Best-of-$`N`$ (Bo$`N`$) sampling.

Bo$`N`$ samples $`N`$ sequences and returns the sequence with largest
probability. For $`N\to\infty`$ one recovers the mode of the
distribution when sampling from $`p`$
([Equation 1](#S2.E1 "In 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")).

##### Power distribution over sequences.

The power distribution with $`\alpha>1`$ is defined over full sequences

|     |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |     |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\coloneqq\frac{p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}}{Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})}\;\;\text{with}\;\;Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})\coloneqq\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}.`$ |     | (4) |

Contrary to Bo$`N`$, power-sampling increases sequence probabilities in
expectation.

###### Remark 2.2 (Power sampling increases expected sequence log-probability).

Let $`{\mathbf{s}}\sim p_{\alpha}(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$
($`\alpha>1`$. Increasing $`\alpha`$ shifts mass toward sequences with
larger probability under $`p`$.

While power sampling looks similar to LTS at first sight, the methods
are generally not equal.

###### Remark 2.3 (LTS is generally not equal to sequence-level power sampling).

Unless the local normalization constants of LTS are identical across all
reachable prefixes, no single token-level temperature reproduces the
sequence-level power distribution.

##### Sampling from the power distribution.

[Karan and Du (2025)](#bib.bib26) are the first to sample from the power
distribution of LLMs. In the following investigation, we focus on
scalable power sampling (SPS) ([Ji et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)) and
power-SMC ([Azizi et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)). SPS samples blocks of $`B`$
tokens and uses rollouts after each block to estimate the future
probability mass behind the top-$`k`$ tokens, power-SMC utilizes a
sequential Monte Carlo sampler generating $`N`$ particles. See
[Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C A Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo Framework for Power Sampling ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
for a general framework.

##### Variational objective for global decoding.

From a variational perspective, the power distribution $`p_{\alpha}`$
can be understood as the optimizer of a variational objective trading
off expected sequence probability and entropy by $`\alpha`$. For any
$`q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$ let
$`\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(q)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right]+{1}/{\alpha}\ \mathcal{H}(q),`$
where
$`\mathcal{H}(q)=-\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\log q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$
is the entropy of $`q`$. We find the following.

###### Remark 2.4 (The power distribution is a maximizer of the variational objective.).

The maximizer of $`\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(q)`$ is the power distribution,
$`q_{\alpha}^{\star}\in\argmax_{q}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(q)\Longleftrightarrow q_{\alpha}^{\star}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}}).`$

Thus, the power distribution can be seen as a _soft_ alternative to
mode-seeking via its parameter $`\alpha`$. In the limit
$`\alpha\to\infty`$, the entropy term vanishes and the optimizer
collapses onto the mode of $`p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$. As
$`N`$ grows, Bo$`N`$ increasingly favors high-probability regions and,
in the limit, it returns the $`\argmax`$. For a function of a
continuation $`{\mathbf{s}}`$, we find that
$`{\partial}/{\partial\alpha}\ \mathbb{E}_{p_{\alpha}}[f({\mathbf{s}})]=\mathrm{Cov}_{p_{\alpha}}(f({\mathbf{s}}),\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})).`$,
i.e., features $`f({\mathbf{s}})`$ that are positively correlated with
sequence log-probability increase under sharpening, while features
negatively correlated with it decrease.

in
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Related Work ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs").

### 3.1 Within-Dataset Correlation

In this section, we investigate the relationship between log-probability
and correctness within a dataset, i.e., for a given model, dataset, and
method. Exemplary, in
[Figure 2](#S2.F2 "In 2.1 Local Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
we show correlations for SPS with block size $`B=192`$, Qwen3-8B-Base,
on all $`6`$ benchmarks. We scatter the log-probability of all samples
against their correctness label $`\in\{0,1\}`$ (gray), for each dataset.
Then, we bin samples into $`10`$ bins of equal size, computing
log-probability and accuracy by averaging over the points falling within
a bin and compute a binned Pearson correlation coefficient $`r`$, see
[Section E.1](#A5.SS1 "E.1 Computing Correlation ‣ Appendix E Additional Experiments and Analysis ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs").

We consistently find correlation between log-probability and correctness
with strength and direction depending on the dataset. Strikingly, we see
a strong, near-perfect, and positive correlation on MATH500
([Figure 2](#S2.F2 "In 2.1 Local Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")).
For GPQA, HumanEval, MedQA, and MMLU we find smaller but similarly
positive correlations. Thus, the log-probability of prompt-answer pairs
if often informative of correctness. IFEval is the only dataset with
negative correlation. This could indicate that Qwen3-8B-Base has seen
only limited instruction-following data during training, or, that the
base models struggle to separate likely completions from likely
completions that violate the prepended formatting instructions.

Next, we summarize the results across models and methods. We choose a
canonical hyperparameter per method (see
[Section D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Canonical Hyperparameters ‣ Appendix D Details on Experimental Setup ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs"))
and average results across model sizes. In
[Figure 3](#S2.F3 "In 2.2 Global Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
we show the resulting correlation coefficient across model families over
all datasets considered: GPQA, Humaneval, IFEval, MATH500, MedQA, MMLU.
We observe a consistent structure in the results depending on dataset
and model-family, largely independent of the method. Specifically, we
find that for MATH500 correlation appears to be strongest across
model-family and method, independent of the model variant. Further,

The results show either positive (green, $`+`$/$`+`$) or negative (blue,
$`-`$/$`+`$) correlation with approximately same numbers. Therefore,
changing the hyperparameter of a decoding method almost always leads to
more probable sequences. At the same time, this does not result in more

are unable to find any consistent results that hold across models.
Comparing global and local sampling methods, it seems like local methods
show inverse relationships more often. This can be explained by the fact
that these methods have local normalization constants. Therefore, there
is no consistent, i.e., independent (of dataset and model) way to
produce both more probably and more correct sequences within a method by
changing the corresponding hyperparameter. We summarize the main
takeaways from this section in

probability-based verifier-free recursive self-improvement loops a
sufficient baseline accuracy for the tasks at hand might be required for
enabling improvement. Else, one would reinforce on samples that are
increasingly less correct.

In this section, we focus on within-sample correlation at the finest
level. We only focus on low-temperature next-token sampling (LTS) with
$`\alpha=4.0`$ by reusing the Bo$`N`$ samples.

In
[Figure 8](#S3.F8 "In 3.2 Within-Method Correlation ‣ 3 Probability and Correctness at Various Granularity ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")

[Section E.1](#A5.SS1 "E.1 Computing Correlation ‣ Appendix E Additional Experiments and Analysis ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs"))

## 4 Discussion and Conclusion

Our results show that the relationship between sequence probability and
correctness depends on the level it is measured at. Within a fixed
dataset, model, and decoding setup, higher-probability prompt-answer
pairs are often more likely to be correct, suggesting that sequence
probability contains useful information across examples. However, this
signal does not generally transfer to the method-level. When comparing
responses to the same prompt, tuning hyperparameters within a method, or
comparing different decoding methods, higher sequence probability does
not consistently imply higher correctness. This is to some degree a
negative result as it does not provide guidance on what method to choose
or which hyperparameter to choose within a method.

Our analysis has practical implications for probability-based decoding,
self-consistency, and probability-based verifier-free self-improvement.
Specifically, sequence probabilities are a useful signal, within a given
dataset and across samples for a single prompt, when the model already
has sufficient task accuracy. However, it is a less useful criterion for
selecting hyperparameters of methods and methods themselves. Therefore,
our findings suggest that in addition to asking whether sequence
probability correlates with correctness, it is important to ask at which
granularity it does so.

## Appendix A Related Work

In this section, we give a broad overview over the related work.

##### Decoding methods.

Search-based decoding methods methods like beam search ([Lowerre,
1976](#bib.bib35); [Sutskever et al., 2014](#bib.bib44)) searches over
the space of sequences but often suffers from repetitions ([Vijayakumar
et al., 2016](#bib.bib46); [Cohen and Beck, 2019](#bib.bib9)), and other
deteriorations in quality ([Koehn and Knowles, 2017](#bib.bib28); [Yang
et al., 2018](#bib.bib53)). Its lack of diversity ([Su et al.,
2022](#bib.bib43)) has been addressed by adding terms related to
diversity ([Vijayakumar et al., 2016](#bib.bib46); [Vilnis et al.,
2023](#bib.bib47)). In
[Section 2](#S2 "2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs"),
we discuss various local decoding methods: top-$`k`$ sampling ([Fan
et al., 2018](#bib.bib13)), top-$`p`$ sampling ([Holtzman et al.,
2020](#bib.bib21)), and $`\varepsilon`$-sampling ([Hewitt et al.,
2022](#bib.bib19)). [Kempton and Burrell (2025)](#bib.bib27) phrase
common local decoding methods in a framework similar to our variational
objective. In this work, we instead consider a variational framework for
global decoding methods. While most prior work investigate how decoding
relates to accuracy, this work asks the more general question how
sequence probability and correctness are related.

##### Power sampling in LLMs.

[Karan and Du (2025)](#bib.bib26) introduce power sampling from LLMs as
a verifier-free way to sharpen the model distribution over full
sequences. They propose a Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm, i.e., a
Metropolis-Hastings ([Metropolis et al., 1953](#bib.bib36); [Hastings,
1970](#bib.bib16)) sampler operating in blocks of tokens. [Ji et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib22) take a similar block-based framework and approximate
the token-level power distribution by using online rollouts from the
top-$`k`$ tokens resulting in the scalable power sampling (SPS)
algorithm. [Azizi et al. (2026)](#bib.bib1) propose power-SMC, a
sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) ([Doucet et al., 2001](#bib.bib12);
[Del Moral et al., 2006](#bib.bib11); [Briers et al., 2010](#bib.bib2);
[Chopin et al., 2020](#bib.bib7)) sampler, which moves a set of
particles over $`T`$ steps (where $`T`$ denotes the length of the
sequence), computes importance-weights, and resamples the particles. In
this work, we ask the more general question on how sequence
log-probability and correctness are related. We investigate sampling
from the power distribution as one global method producing more probable
sequences (see
[Remark 2.2](#S2.Thmremark2 "Remark 2.2 (Power sampling increases expected sequence log-probability). ‣ Power distribution over sequences. ‣ 2.2 Global Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs"))
approximated by SPS and power-SMC.

##### The power distribution, sharpening, and reinforcement learning.

Many works argue that reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
(RLVR) can be explained as doing distribution sharpening ([He et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17); [Song et al., 2025](#bib.bib40); [Yue et al.,
2025](#bib.bib54); [Gai et al., 2025](#bib.bib15); [Ni et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37)). Based on this [Karan and Du (2025)](#bib.bib26) make
the point that power sampling can be compared to RLVR and show
comparisons between RL-finetuned models and base models. We, instead,
show results for base and posttrained models and focus on the
relationship between sequence probability and correctness instead of
explicitly comparing models at different training stages.

##### Self-consistency.

Self-consistency (SC) aggregates multiple samples from a LLM via
majority voting on extracted answers ([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib50)).
In cases where (simple) answer extraction is not possible, an LLM can be
used to extract and compare answers ([Wang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib48);
[Chen et al., 2024b](#bib.bib5); [Wang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib49)). In
this work, we analyze the correlation between probability and
correctness, also within a sample. We find that, often, there is no
consistent correlation for a single prompt, explaining why
probability-weighting in SC often underperforms.

##### Choosing out of a pool of $`N`$ sequences.

Best-of-$`N`$ ([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib8); [Lightman et al.,
2024](#bib.bib33)) is a general method choosing the “best” sequence out
of a set of $`N`$ sequences. While sequences can be chosen by
probability, various other confidence criteria can be defined ([Kang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib25); [Fu et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)). These
criteria encode assumption about how correct sequences look like. In
this work, we investigate the underlying question asking how
log-probability and correctness are related.

##### Calibration and log-probability.

There are various works investigating calibration in LLMs. [Spiess
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib42) focus on the calibration of LLMs for code.
They find that code models are not well calibrated in general and they
show how calibration can be improved. [Kadavath et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib24) show that LLMs are often well-calibrated on
multiple-choice questions and questions with binary options. Then, they
evaluate self-evaluation by asking models for answers and letting them
evaluate the probability of the answer being correct. This work is
related to calibration in that it similarly computes the log-probability
for sequences and compares it to its accuracy (within-dataset
correlation). However, we do not relate the specific
sequence-probabilities to correctness and consider various other forms
of correlation. [Li et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib32) motivate their
tree-search method by showing that coverage i.e., the log-probability of
all samples generated for a given prompt, correlates with correctness.
Further, [Li et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib31) find that low probability
tokens correlate with large epistemic uncertainty and propose to resort
to greedily sample those tokens (as opposed to sampling from the
distribution).

## Appendix B Theoretical Arguments

In this section, we provide an extended formal discussion on decoding
methods highlighting various connections made in the main text.

First, we can state that beam search (without length normalization)
becomes exact with large $`K`$.

[Remark 2.1](#S2.Thmremark1 "Remark 2.1 (Beam search generates the highest probability sequence for 𝐾→∞.). ‣ Maximizing sequence probability. ‣ 2.2 Global Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
(Beam search becomes exact for $`K\to\infty`$). If $`K`$ is chosen such
that no continuation is ever pruned before a fixed $`T`$ then beam
search returns an element of
$`\argmax_{{\mathbf{s}}}\ p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$.

###### Proof.

For a fixed horizon $`T`$, beam search keeps the $`K`$ highest-scoring
partial continuations at each depth. If $`K`$ is large enough that such
that no continuation of length at most $`T`$ is pruned, then beam search
explores the entire tree up to depth $`T`$. Hence, at depth $`T`$, it
compares all complete continuations $`{\mathbf{s}}`$ and returns one
with maximal probability under $`p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$.
∎

Power sampling, another global sampling method, increases the expected
sequence log-probability.

[Remark 2.2](#S2.Thmremark2 "Remark 2.2 (Power sampling increases expected sequence log-probability). ‣ Power distribution over sequences. ‣ 2.2 Global Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
(Power sampling increases expected sequence log-probability). For
$`\alpha>1`$, let
$`{\mathbf{s}}\sim p_{\alpha}(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$. Then,
increasing $`\alpha`$ shifts mass toward sequences with larger
probability under $`p`$ as
$`\mathbb{E}_{p_{\alpha}}\left[\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right].`$

###### Proof.

Let

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}.
``` |  |

Then, under reasonable assumptions,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{d}{d\alpha}\log Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})=\frac{\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})}{Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})}=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})}\!\left[\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right].
``` |  |

Differentiating once more gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{d}{d\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})}\left[\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right]=\mathrm{Var}_{p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})}\left(\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right)\geq 0.
``` |  |

Hence
$`\mathbb{E}_{p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})}[\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})]`$
is nondecreasing in $`\alpha`$. ∎

In contrast, for local sampling methods, we cannot guarantee any global
properties.

###### Remark B.1 (Local sampling methods do not target most probable global sequences.).

Methods with local normalization constants modify
$`p(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})`$ separately at each
prefix. Hence, they favor continuations whose *next token* is locally
likely. This is different from maximizing the *sequence-level*
probability $`p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$.

###### Proof.

For LTS,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{p(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})^{\alpha}}{\tilde{Z}_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}=p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}\prod_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{Z}_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})^{-1}.`$ |  | (B1) |

For TS,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{A}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{p(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}{\tilde{Z}_{A}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}\mathbb{1}[s_{t}\in A(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})]=p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{1}[s_{t}\in A(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})]}{\tilde{Z}_{A}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}.`$ |  | (B2) |

In both,
[Equation B1](#A2.E1 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix B Theoretical Arguments ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
and
[Equation B2](#A2.E2 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix B Theoretical Arguments ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs"),
the sequence weight contains factors beyond
$`p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$ itself. This is different from
the normalization constant
$`Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})=\sum_{\mathbf{s}}p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}`$
([Equation 4](#S2.E4 "In Power distribution over sequences. ‣ 2.2 Global Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
in
[Section 2](#S2 "2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")).
∎

LTS defines a temperature $`\alpha`$ similar to power-sampling. However,
there is, in general, no temperature for LTS that recovers samples from
the power distribution.

[Remark 2.3](#S2.Thmremark3 "Remark 2.3 (LTS is generally not equal to sequence-level power sampling). ‣ Power distribution over sequences. ‣ 2.2 Global Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
(LTS is generally not equal to power sampling.). Unless the local
normalization constants of LTS are identical across all reachable
prefixes, no single token-level temperature reproduces the
sequence-level power distribution.

###### Proof.

The LTS-induced sequence distribution is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{p(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})^{\alpha}}{\tilde{Z}_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}=\frac{p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}}{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{Z}_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})}.`$ |  | (B3) |

By contrast, the sequence-level power distribution is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=\frac{p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}}{Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})}.
``` |  |

Thus LTS coincides with sequence-level power sampling only if

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\prod_{t=1}^{T}\tilde{Z}_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})\overset{!}{=}Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})\;\forall\;(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t}).
``` |  |

However, the local normalization constants depend on the prefix, so the
product is path-dependent and LTS is not equal to sequence-level power
sampling. ∎

As discussed in the main test, we find the power distribution to be the
maximizer of $`\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(q)`$.

[Remark 2.4](#S2.Thmremark4 "Remark 2.4 (The power distribution is a maximizer of the variational objective.). ‣ Variational objective for global decoding. ‣ 2.2 Global Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
(The power distribution is a maximizer of the variational objective.).
The maximizer of $`\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(q)`$ is the power distribution,
$`q_{\alpha}^{\star}\in\argmax_{q}\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(q)\Longleftrightarrow q_{\alpha}^{\star}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}}).`$

###### Proof.

We have
$`\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(q)=\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})-\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\log q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}}),`$
and,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(q)`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\log\frac{q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})}{p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}}`$ |  | (B4) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\log\frac{q({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})}{p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})}`$ |  | (B5) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\alpha}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(q(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\,\|\,p_{\alpha}(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\bigr)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\log Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}}),`$ |  | (B6) |

where

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}.
``` |  |

Since $`\log Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$ does not depend on $`q`$,
maximizing $`\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}(q)`$ is equivalent to minimizing

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(q(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\,\|\,p_{\alpha}(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\bigr),
``` |  |

whose unique minimizer is
$`q(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=p_{\alpha}(\cdot\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$.
∎

Investigating the hyperparameter $`\alpha`$, we find a tradeoff between
expected log-probability and entropy.

###### Remark B.2 (Expected log-probability increases and entropy decreases with $`\alpha`$).

For $`\alpha>1`$, increasing $`\alpha`$ shifts mass toward
higher-probability continuations under $`p`$. Therefore,
$`\mathbb{E}_{p_{\alpha}}\!\left[\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right]`$
is monotonically increasing in $`\alpha`$, while
$`\mathcal{H}(p_{\alpha})`$ is monotonically decreasing in $`\alpha`$.

###### Proof.

We have seen the first part already in
[Remark 2.2](#S2.Thmremark2 "Remark 2.2 (Power sampling increases expected sequence log-probability). ‣ Power distribution over sequences. ‣ 2.2 Global Decoding Methods ‣ 2 Decoding Methods Maximize Sequence Probability in LLMs ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs").
For the entropy, we obtain

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(p_{\alpha})`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\log p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$ |  | (B7) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}p_{\alpha}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\Bigl(\alpha\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})-\log Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}})\Bigr)`$ |  | (B8) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{p_{\alpha}}\!\left[\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right]+\log Z_{\alpha}(\bar{\mathbf{s}}).`$ |  | (B9) |

Differentiating gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\mathcal{H}(p_{\alpha})`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\alpha\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\alpha}}\!\left[\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right]`$ |  | (B10) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\alpha\,\mathrm{Var}_{p_{\alpha}}\!\left(\log p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\right)\leq 0.`$ |  | (B11) |

Therefore, $`\mathcal{H}(p_{\alpha})`$ is monotonically decreasing in
$`\alpha`$. ∎

## Appendix C A Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo Framework for Power Sampling

Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) algorithms ([Doucet et al.,
2001](#bib.bib12); [Del Moral et al., 2006](#bib.bib11); [Briers et al.,
2010](#bib.bib2); [Chopin et al., 2020](#bib.bib7)) are one of the main
algorithms for estimating (global) normalization constants. In this
section, we show how recent work ([Ji et al., 2026](#bib.bib22); [Azizi
et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)) fits into the twisted SMC framework of [Zhao
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib57).

Let
$`\gamma({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\coloneqq p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}`$
denote the target distribution. Since exact sampling from the normalized
distribution induced by $`\gamma`$ is intractable, a natural approach is
(twisted) SMC which constructs a sequence intermediate target
distributions and propagates a population of particles. Twisted SMC
([Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib57)) introduces positive twist functions
$`\psi_{t}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t})`$ and defines
$`\gamma_{t}({\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})\coloneqq p({\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}\,\psi_{t}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t})`$
for $`t<T`$, with $`\gamma_{T}=\gamma`$. For a proposal
$`q_{t}(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})`$, the incremental
weight is
$`w_{t}^{i}\propto w_{t-1}^{i}{p(s_{t}^{i}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t}^{i})^{\alpha}}/{q_{t}(s_{t}^{i}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t}^{i})}{\psi_{t}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}^{i})}/{\psi_{t-1}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t}^{i})}.`$

###### Remark C.1 (Optimal twist following [Zhao et al. (2024, Proposition 3.2)](#bib.bib57)).

The optimal twist functions targeting the power distribution are given
as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\psi_{t}^{\star}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t})\propto\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}_{>t}}p({\mathbf{s}}_{>t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t})^{\alpha}.`$ |  | (C1) |

###### Proof.

The terminal unnormalized target is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\gamma_{T}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=p({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}.
``` |  |

For a fixed prefix $`{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}`$, summing out the future
suffix gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}_{>t}}\gamma_{T}({\mathbf{s}}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}_{>t}}\Bigl(p({\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}p({\mathbf{s}}_{>t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t})^{\alpha}\Bigr)`$ |  | (C2) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=p({\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}_{>t}}p({\mathbf{s}}_{>t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t})^{\alpha}.`$ |  | (C3) |

Therefore, choosing

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\psi_{t}^{\star}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t})\propto\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}_{>t}}p({\mathbf{s}}_{>t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t})^{\alpha}
``` |  |

makes

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\gamma_{t}({\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=p({\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}\psi_{t}^{\star}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t})
``` |  |

proportional to the exact marginal of $`\gamma_{T}`$ at time $`t`$. ∎

The two recently proposed methods fall into this framework as we explain
next.

##### Power-SMC.

Power-SMC ([Azizi et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)) corresponds to the
*untwisted* choice $`\psi_{t}\equiv 1`$ using the LTS proposal
distribution
$`q_{t}(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})=\tilde{p}_{\alpha}(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})`$.
While this minimizes the variance among prefix-only proposals ([Azizi
et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)), it does not account for future continuation
mass.

###### Remark C.2 (Asymptotic exactness of Power-SMC following [Chopin (2004)](#bib.bib6) and [Del Moral (2004)](#bib.bib10).).

For any fixed horizon, consistency of SMC implies that, under the usual
regularity assumptions, the empirical particle approximation converges
to the target distribution as $`N\to\infty`$.

###### Proof.

Power-SMC is a standard SMC algorithm for the Feynman-Kac model with
unnormalized targets

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\gamma_{t}({\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})=p({\mathbf{s}}_{\leq t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}})^{\alpha}
``` |  |

and proposal

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{t}(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t})=\tilde{p}_{\alpha}(s_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t}).
``` |  |

Therefore the usual SMC law of large numbers applies. We refer to
[Chopin (2004)](#bib.bib6) and [Del Moral (2004)](#bib.bib10) for the
corresponding theoretical arguments. ∎

##### Scalable power sampling (SPS).

Similarly, SPS ([Ji et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)) corresponds to a
*twisted* SMC method that approximates the optimal term
$`\psi_{t}^{\star}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{<t},s_{t})`$ in
[Equation C1](#A3.E1 "In Remark C.1 (Optimal twist following ( , Proposition 3.2) ). ‣ Appendix C A Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo Framework for Power Sampling ‣ When are likely answers right? On Sequence Probability and Correctness in LLMs")
via Monte Carlo sampling restricted to a top-$`k`$ candidate set. SPS
utilizes a blockwise approximation for a block
$`b_{t}={\mathbf{s}}_{(t-1)B+1:tB}`$ of $`B`$ tokens,
$`p_{\alpha}^{\psi_{\star}}(b_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{1:(t-1)B})\propto p(b_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{1:(t-1)B})^{\alpha}\,\psi_{tB}^{\star}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{1:(t-1)B}\oplus b_{t}),`$.

###### Remark C.3 (Approximation gaps for SPS).

SPS is exact if the look-ahead term $`\psi^{\star}`$ is evaluated
exactly at every position. Alternatively, it is exact when the candidate
set is not truncated and the next block is sampled exactly from the
corresponding blockwise fully-adapted conditional. In practice, the
approximation comes from (i) Monte Carlo estimation of $`\psi^{\star}`$,
(ii) top-$`k`$ truncation, and (iii) the blockwise restriction.

###### Proof.

For a block $`b_{t}`$, the exact fully adapted block proposal is
proportional to

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p(b_{t}\mid\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{1:(t-1)B})^{\alpha}\,\psi_{tB}^{\star}(\bar{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{s}}_{1:(t-1)B}\oplus b_{t}).
``` |  |

If $`\psi^{\star}`$ is evaluated exactly and sampling is performed
exactly from this conditional over the full candidate set, then the
corresponding twisted SMC update is exact. Likewise, if no top-$`k`$
truncation is applied and the next block is sampled exactly from the
blockwise fully adapted conditional, then no approximation is introduced
at that block update.

SPS departs from this exact construction in three places: (i) it
replaces $`\psi^{\star}`$ by a Monte Carlo estimate, (ii) it truncates
the candidate set to a top-$`k`$ subset, and (iii) it uses a blockwise
approximation rather than the exact full-sequence update. ∎

The twisted SMC view of [Zhao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib57) shows
interesting directions for future work. Improvements can be mostly
twofold: Firstly, one can learn the twist functions during inference via
various methods proposed in the SMC literature ([Zhao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib57); [Lawson et al., 2022](#bib.bib30); [Lioutas et al.,
2022](#bib.bib34)). Orthogonally, one can extend the framework with
verifiers or reward models.

### D.2 Correlation Coefficients

We compute correlations between the continuous log-probability and a
binary correctness label $`y\in\{0,1\}`$. The per-sample Spearman rank
correlation $`\rho`$ ([Spearman, 1904](#bib.bib41)) measures monotone
agreement between the two quantities. Since $`y`$ is binary, the
$`\rho`$ is upper bound by $`\sqrt{0.5}`$. The binned Pearson
correlation coefficient ([Pearson, 1894](#bib.bib38)) $`r`$ partitions
log-probability into $`K=10`$ equal-count bins, computes the bin-mean
(for log-probability and correctness) and computes the ordinary Pearson
coefficient $`r`$.

### E.7 Self-Distillation

[Hinton et al. (2015)](#bib.bib20) distill an ensemble of neural
networks in a single neural network. [Zhang et al. (2019)](#bib.bib56)
propose self-distillation for convolutional neural networks where
knowledge is distilled within a network itself. In the context of LLMs,
the self-taught reasoner ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib55)) consists
of a bootstrapping method that lets LLMs generate rationales for prompts
which are then used for finetuning. [Chen et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib3)
frames reasoning as sampling from a latent distribution optimizing it
via sequence likelihood as self-rewarding signal.

Here, we train self-distillation on Bo$`N`$, SPS, and power-SMC samples
using the first half of samples considered. We test the model
consistently on the second half of samples.
````

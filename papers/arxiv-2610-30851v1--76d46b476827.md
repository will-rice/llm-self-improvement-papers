###### Abstract

Scientific discovery often requires identifying a small number of
high-performing designs in vast, constrained search spaces. This is
difficult when evaluations are costly, feasible solutions are sparse,
parameters are strongly coupled, and physical constraints are strict.
Recent AI for AI methods can automatically improve algorithms and
optimization procedures. However, general purpose approaches are not
designed around scientific priors, physical constraints, and limited
evaluation budgets. We introduce ADAPT for Science, a multi-agent
framework that treats scientific optimization as two coupled decisions:
_where to search_ and _how to search_. ADAPT uses historical designs to
identify promising and diverse initial conditions, progressively
concentrates computation on favorable search directions, and evolves the
optimization procedure from execution feedback. We evaluate ADAPT on the
design of gravitational wave detectors using a differentiable physics
simulator and eight unseen detector topologies. Under matched scientific
evaluation budgets, ADAPT achieves a mean loss of $`0.204\pm 0.101`$,
reducing loss by $`54.9\%`$ relative to the best classical optimizer and
by $`62.2\%`$ relative to the best general purpose agentic evolution
baseline. ADAPT achieves the lowest loss on all unseen topologies among
the evaluated baselines. ADAPT provides a step toward scientific
optimization systems that continuously learn from prior experience and
their own search outcomes.

## 1 Introduction

Scientific progress often depends on finding a small number of useful
solutions in vast and constrained design spaces. From drug discovery to
gravitational-wave detector design, each candidate can be costly to
evaluate, many parameters interact strongly, and feasible solutions may
occupy only a small fraction of the search space. The central challenge
is therefore not only to evaluate a candidate, but to decide what is
worth trying next. This challenge becomes acute when optimization must
proceed under a limited experimental or computational budget. Search
performance depends not only on local parameter updates, but also on
where the search begins, which prior experience is reused, which
optimization procedure is applied, and how resources are allocated.
These higher-level decisions are often fixed in advance or made
manually, despite their substantial influence on the final solution.
Gravitational-wave detector design provides a representative example.
Detector sensitivity depends on many interacting continuous parameters
subject to strict physical constraints. A poor initialization or an
unsuitable optimization strategy can consume substantial computation
before reaching a useful solution. At the same time, previous detector
designs provide valuable experience that can guide future search. The
problem is therefore not optimization from scratch, but how to reuse
prior scientific experience, choose where to begin, and adapt the search
strategy as new evidence becomes available.

AI has begun to automate parts of this process. Bayesian optimization
and autonomous laboratories use previous evaluations to select promising
candidates \[[25](#bib.bib6), [31](#bib.bib2)\], while scientific agents
automate planning and experimental workflows \[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. These
approaches primarily adapt the scientific candidate while the
higher-level search procedure remains largely specified in advance. In
parallel, recent program-search methods show that language models can
improve algorithms through evaluator feedback \[[24](#bib.bib4),
[29](#bib.bib5)\]. These methods adapt the procedure itself, but do not
explicitly model reusable scientific experience, target-dependent
initialization, physical feasibility, and limited scientific evaluation
budgets.

We address this gap with ADAPT for Science, a multi-agent framework for
self-evolving scientific optimization. ADAPT treats the complete
scientific search policy as the object of optimization and decomposes it
into two coupled decisions: _where to search_ and _how to search_.
Historical scientific designs provide offline experience for selecting
promising and diverse starting points and allocating evaluation effort.
Online execution feedback is then used to evolve the optimization
procedure itself. The resulting closed loop jointly adapts
initialization, resource allocation, and optimization strategy to the
scientific target. We evaluate ADAPT on gravitational-wave detector
design using a differentiable physics simulator and eight unseen
detector topologies. Under matched scientific evaluation budgets, ADAPT
achieves a mean loss of $`0.204\pm 0.101`$, compared with
$`0.452\pm 0.150`$ for the strongest classical optimizer and
$`0.540\pm 0.263`$ for the strongest general-purpose agentic evolution
baseline. These results correspond to reductions in mean loss of 54.9%
and 62.2%, respectively. ADAPT also achieves the lowest loss on all
eight held-out topologies among the evaluated baselines. The results
indicate that scientific optimization benefits from adapting the search
policy itself rather than optimizing scientific parameters or
optimization programs in isolation.

Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  We formulate scientific optimization at the level of the complete
  search policy rather than the scientific parameters alone, and
  decompose this policy into two coupled decisions: _where to search_
  and _how to search_.
- •
  We develop ADAPT for Science, which combines reusable scientific
  experience with online execution feedback to jointly adapt
  initialization, resource allocation, and optimization procedures under
  physical and evaluation-budget constraints.
- •
  We establish a held-out evaluation on eight unseen gravitational-wave
  detector topologies. ADAPT reduces mean loss by 54.9% relative to the
  strongest classical optimizer and by 62.2% relative to the strongest
  general-purpose agentic evolution baseline under matched scientific
  evaluation budgets, while achieving the lowest loss on all eight
  topologies among the evaluated baselines.

## 2 Related Work

Scientific optimization is often formulated as sequential
decision-making under expensive evaluations and limited budgets.
Bayesian optimization and active learning leverage previous observations
to select informative evaluations  \[[25](#bib.bib6), [23](#bib.bib13),
[34](#bib.bib18), [16](#bib.bib22), [30](#bib.bib23), [12](#bib.bib24),
[9](#bib.bib25)\]. These methods have been widely applied to scientific
discovery, including chemical reaction optimization and autonomous
experimentation  \[[14](#bib.bib26), [28](#bib.bib27), [13](#bib.bib28),
[8](#bib.bib29), [4](#bib.bib30), [20](#bib.bib31)\]. Recent scientific
foundation models and autonomous agents further exploit historical data,
planning, and tool use for complex scientific workflows
 \[[22](#bib.bib32), [32](#bib.bib1), [3](#bib.bib3), [19](#bib.bib16),
[6](#bib.bib17)\]. However, initialization, optimizer choice, and
resource allocation are usually specified beforehand.

A complementary direction treats the optimization algorithm itself as
the search object. Learned optimizers and symbolic program search
demonstrate that optimization rules can be discovered from experience
 \[[2](#bib.bib33), [5](#bib.bib34), [11](#bib.bib15),
[36](#bib.bib14)\]. Algorithm discovery has further achieved improved
computational procedures for tasks such as matrix multiplication and
sorting  \[[10](#bib.bib35), [21](#bib.bib36)\]. More recently, large
language models enable program and heuristic evolution through evaluator
feedback, including FunSearch, EoH, ReEvo, and MCTS-AHD
 \[[24](#bib.bib4), [18](#bib.bib9), [33](#bib.bib7), [35](#bib.bib8)\].
Related systems extend this idea to scientific equations, computational
workflows, and automated optimizer evolution  \[[29](#bib.bib5),
[7](#bib.bib21), [1](#bib.bib10), [17](#bib.bib11), [27](#bib.bib19),
[15](#bib.bib20), [26](#bib.bib12)\]. These approaches show that
algorithms themselves can be optimized, but usually without
target-specific scientific priors.

ADAPT connects these directions by treating the complete scientific
search policy as the object of adaptation. Historical designs provide
priors for _where to search_, while execution feedback evolves _how to
search_. By jointly adapting initialization, resource allocation, and
optimization dynamics, ADAPT enables efficient scientific optimization
under physical constraints and limited budgets.

## 3 ADAPT FOR SCIENCE

Figure [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
summarizes ADAPT. Rather than optimizing only scientific variables,
ADAPT optimizes the complete search policy through two coupled
decisions, where to search and how to search. Historical experience
provides a target-specific prior, online execution feedback adapts the
optimization procedure, and an independent validator evaluates the
resulting policy on unseen targets.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

For a scientific target $`\tau\in\mathcal{T}`$, let
$`x\in\mathcal{X}_{\tau}`$ denote the design variables, $`L_{\tau}(x)`$
the objective, and $`\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\tau}`$ the
feasible region. Under a finite evaluation budget, performance depends
on both the initialization and the optimization procedure. We therefore
define the search policy as $`\pi=(W,O)`$, where $`W`$ selects the
initial design $`x_{0}`$ and $`O`$ generates the trajectory
$`\mathcal{T}_{\pi}=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{T}\}`$. For budget $`B`$, we
define
$`J_{\tau}(\pi;B)=\min_{x_{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{\pi}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\tau},\,c_{t}\leq B}L_{\tau}(x_{t})`$,
where $`c_{t}`$ is the cumulative evaluation cost.

Conventional optimization fixes $`W`$ and $`O`$ in advance, whereas
ADAPT adapts both to each scientific target,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
(W_{\tau}^{*},O_{\tau}^{*})=\arg\min_{W\in\mathcal{W},\,O\in\mathcal{O}}J_{\tau}((W,O);B),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{W}`$ and $`\mathcal{O}`$ denote the spaces of selection
policies and optimization procedures. $`W_{\tau}^{*}`$ determines where
to search, while $`O_{\tau}^{*}`$ determines how to search.

### 3.2 Historical Scientific Experience

We construct a reusable experience library from historical
gravitational-wave detector designs and optimization records. After
cleaning, representation standardization, and transfer compatibility
filtering, the design library contains 15,409 valid topology–design
records, each pairing one topology with a compatible parameterized
design and its evaluated performance. A separate baseline library
contains ten established optimization procedures and their historical
behavior. The former supplies candidate initializations, while the
latter provides reference search behavior for policy evolution. Held-out
targets and their outcomes are excluded from library construction and
all subsequent strategy development.

### 3.3 Target-Aware Retrieval and Initialization

For each target topology, ADAPT retrieves $`K=64`$ distinct historical
sources using a fixed structure-aware distance. A size-$`3`$ topology
contains nine interior symbols $`\mathbf{c}`$ and twelve boundary
symbols $`\mathbf{b}`$. For target $`T`$ and source $`S`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d(T,S)=\frac{1}{9}\sum_{i=1}^{9}\delta(c_{i}^{T},c_{i}^{S})+\frac{1}{12}\sum_{j=1}^{12}\mathbf{1}\!\left[b_{j}^{T}\neq b_{j}^{S}\right],
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\delta(a,b)=0`$ for identical symbols, $`0.25`$ for different
orientations within the same component family, and $`1`$ for different
families. Symbols A–D denote beamsplitters and E–H directional
beamsplitters, with four orientations per family. Symbols are compared
at fixed positions without rotational or reflection alignment.

Sources with the same complete topology as the target are excluded. The
remainder are ranked by increasing $`d(T,S)`$, with lexicographic
ordering used for ties. Incompatible records are skipped and the first
64 eligible sources are retained, without dataset-specific quotas.

Parameters are transferred by exact semantic matching of component
names, types, parameter properties, and complete coupling groups.
Matched bounded values are copied directly. Unmatched parameters use
frozen training-set medians in the order component-type and property,
property only, and global prior, followed by mapping to target bounds.
For parameter $`x_{i}^{(k)}`$ with bounds $`\ell_{i}`$ and $`h_{i}`$,
$`u_{i}^{(k)}=\operatorname{clip}((x_{i}^{(k)}-\ell_{i})/(h_{i}-\ell_{i}),\epsilon,1-\epsilon)`$
and $`z_{i}^{(k)}=\log(u_{i}^{(k)}/(1-u_{i}^{(k)}))`$, with
$`\epsilon=10^{-9}`$. The resulting
$`C_{\tau}=\{\mathbf{z}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{64}`$ forms the candidate
initialization pool.

### 3.4 Selection Evolution

Given $`C_{\tau}`$, the Selection Evolver searches for a policy
$`W_{\tau}`$ that returns one initialization under a fixed scientific
evaluation budget. Evolution starts from a direct 64-to-1 baseline that
evaluates all 64 candidates and selects the best single initialization.
Intermediate candidate counts, screening stages, pruning rules, and
budget-allocation strategies are not predefined and are left to
evolution.

A candidate policy may use historical attributes, transferred
parameters, target evaluations, feasibility, constraint violations, and
observed optimization progress to determine which candidates are
evaluated, retained, or discarded and how budget is allocated among
them. ADAPT evolves these policies with GEPA, OpenEvolve, ShinkaEvolve,
and ASI using GPT-6 Astra and Claude-fable-5.1. Each workflow–model pair
is compared with two reference baselines, yielding 16 configurations.

At iteration $`k`$, a policy $`W^{(k)}`$ is executed under the fixed
budget, its trace is converted into feedback $`F_{W}^{(k)}`$, and the
feedback is used to propose $`W^{(k+1)}`$. Strong previous policies are
retained for direct comparison. Selection is based on measured
scientific performance, and the final policy produces
$`x_{0,\tau}^{*}=W_{\tau}^{*}(C_{\tau})`$.

### 3.5 Optimizer Evolution

Starting from $`x_{0,\tau}^{*}`$, the Optimizer Evolver searches over
executable optimization programs evaluated directly by the
differentiable physics simulator under the same budget and numerical
constraints. The search space includes update rules, gradient and state
handling, learning-rate and momentum schedules, trajectory management,
numerical safeguards, and allocation of the remaining evaluation budget.

The same workflow–model pool proposes and revises optimizers. At
iteration $`k`$, $`O^{(k)}`$ is executed, its trace is converted into
feedback $`F_{O}^{(k)}`$, and the feedback is used to generate
$`O^{(k+1)}`$. Strong previous optimizers remain available for direct
comparison. Candidate procedures are ranked by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S=w_{q}S_{\mathrm{quality}}+w_{a}S_{\mathrm{anytime}}+w_{f}S_{\mathrm{feasibility}}+w_{i}S_{\mathrm{improvement}},
``` |  | (3) |

where the four terms measure final feasible quality, performance
throughout execution, physical validity, and improvement over the
initialization. This score is used only for strategy evolution. Reported
results use the native scientific objective and feasibility criteria.
The selected optimizer $`O_{\tau}^{*}`$ and selection policy
$`W_{\tau}^{*}`$ define $`\pi_{\tau}^{*}=(W_{\tau}^{*},O_{\tau}^{*})`$.

### 3.6 Independent Validation

The final policy $`\pi_{\tau}^{*}`$ is evaluated on detector topologies
absent from the experience library and excluded from retrieval, baseline
selection, policy evolution, and model selection. Their outcomes are
never returned to either evolver. Each held-out target is evaluated
under the same scientific budget as the comparison methods, and all
reported results use the native objective, physical feasibility, and
anytime optimization performance.

## 4 Results

We first examine the policies discovered by the two evolution stages and
then evaluate their combined effect on eight held-out gravitational-wave
detector topologies. All comparisons use matched random seeds, hardware,
numerical precision, and the same total wall-clock budget. For ADAPT,
this budget covers both warmup (candidate screening and staged probing)
and subsequent optimization; no additional time is allocated for warmup.

shows how the policy develops from direct Top-1 selection into a staged

converges to the $`64\!\rightarrow\!8\!\rightarrow\!2\!\rightarrow\!1`$
workflow shown in
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.1 Selection evolution discovers progressive search ‣ 4 Results ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

The first stage evaluates all $`64`$ starts once on the target without
optimizer updates and records loss, feasibility, and constraint
violation. Among feasible candidates, the four lowest-loss starts form
an elite set. Four additional starts are then selected for coverage in
transferred parameter space. For candidate $`k`$, each bounded parameter
is normalized as $`q_{k,i}=(x_{k,i}-\ell_{i})/(h_{i}-\ell_{i})`$, and
pairwise distance is
$`D(k,j)=\sqrt{d^{-1}\sum_{i}(q_{k,i}-q_{j,i})^{2}}`$. Starting from the
four elites, each additional candidate is chosen by
$`k^{\star}=\arg\max_{k\in\mathcal{F}\setminus S}\min_{j\in S}D(k,j)`$
until eight starts are retained. Ties are resolved by candidate index.
If fewer than eight feasible starts exist, all feasible starts are
retained and numerically valid infeasible candidates are added in order
of constraint violation, loss, and candidate index. Fewer than eight
numerically valid candidates terminates the selection run.

The eight retained starts then enter a target-specific race. Each
receives a $`3`$-minute optimization run, after which two leading
trajectories are retained and continued to $`21`$ minutes. A protected
exploratory branch is maintained during pruning, and the remaining
budget is concentrated on the final start. The discovered policy
therefore moves from historical quality and parameter coverage to
target-specific execution evidence before committing the remaining
budget to a single direction.

The discovered procedure shortens the first-moment memory to
$`\beta_{1}=0.7`$ while retaining global gradient clipping. It further
scales each update by $`s(r)=0.05+0.95r^{2}`$, where $`r`$ is the
remaining fraction of the evaluation budget, producing larger updates
early and increasingly conservative updates near the deadline.
Non-finite losses or gradients are skipped, and non-finite parameter
proposals are rejected together with the corresponding optimizer-state
update.

These changes couple the local search to both the selected historical
start and the remaining evaluation horizon. The resulting optimizer
remains close to Adam computationally, but adapts its directional
memory, update magnitude, and numerical state handling to the observed
scientific search process.

## 5 Limitations and Future Work

ADAPT is designed for scientific optimization with reusable historical
experience, expensive evaluations, and explicit physical constraints.
The present study focuses on gravitational-wave detector design, where a
differentiable simulator provides controlled evaluation and a large
historical design library. The results therefore establish the
effectiveness of the framework in one challenging scientific domain, but
do not yet demonstrate universality across scientific disciplines.

The method also depends on the quality and coverage of its experience
library. Nearest-neighbor retrieval is effective when historical designs
contain useful local structure, but its advantage may diminish for
targets that lie far outside the observed design distribution. In
addition, evolving both selection workflows and optimization programs
introduces computational overhead relative to a single fixed optimizer.
This overhead is justified when scientific evaluations are sufficiently
costly, but should be reduced for cheaper optimization problems.

Several extensions follow naturally. First, ADAPT can be evaluated on
scientific problems with different search spaces, simulators, and
physical constraints, including non-differentiable and experimental
settings. Second, the experience library can be updated online so that
successful solutions and optimizers become reusable knowledge for future
targets. Third, more efficient strategy evolution could reduce the
number of workflow–model evaluations through learned transfer,
uncertainty-aware allocation, or meta-optimization. Finally, extending
the independent validator to real experimental measurements would test
whether self-evolving optimization remains reliable beyond simulation.

## 6 Conclusion

We introduced ADAPT for Science, a framework that treats the complete
scientific search policy as the object of optimization. ADAPT decomposes
this policy into two coupled decisions: *where to search* and *how to
search*. Historical scientific experience guides target-specific
initialization and resource allocation, while online execution feedback
adapts the optimization procedure itself.

On eight held-out gravitational-wave detector topologies, ADAPT achieves
a mean loss of $`0.204\pm 0.101`$, reducing mean loss by 54.9% relative
to the strongest classical optimizer and by 62.2% relative to the
strongest general-purpose agentic evolution baseline under matched
scientific evaluation budgets. ADAPT also achieves the lowest loss on
all eight held-out topologies among the evaluated baselines. Ablation
results further show that adaptive initialization, optimizer evolution,
and science-specific guidance each contribute to the full-system
performance.

These results support a broader view of scientific automation in which
the search procedure itself is adaptive. Rather than optimizing
scientific parameters under a fixed workflow, ADAPT combines prior
scientific experience with execution feedback to continually refine how
the search is initialized and conducted.

## Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification

This appendix provides the full scientific formulation underlying the
gravitational-wave detector design task used throughout the paper. We
first define the finite-budget optimization problem, and then describe
the detector representation, continuous design variables, differentiable
physics simulator, native scientific objective, physical feasibility
constraints, and optimization coordinates.

### A.1 Finite-Budget Scientific Optimization

For a detector topology $`\tau\in\mathcal{T}`$, let
$`x\in\mathcal{X}_{\tau}`$ denote its continuous design variables,
$`L_{\tau}(x)`$ the native scientific objective, and
$`\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\tau}`$ the physically
feasible subset. The unconstrained scientific design problem can be
written as

|     |                                                                |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       x_{\tau}^{\star}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}L_{\tau}(x).
       ```                                                             |     | (4) |

In practice, however, scientific optimization is performed under a
finite evaluation budget. Performance therefore depends not only on the
local parameter updates, but also on where the search starts and which
optimization procedure is used. We represent the complete search policy
as

|     |            |     |     |
|-----|------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \pi=(W,O),
       ```         |     | (5) |

where $`W`$ denotes the selection policy that determines the
initialization $`x_{0}`$, and $`O`$ denotes the optimizer that
subsequently generates a trajectory

|     |                                                 |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{T}_{\pi}=\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{T}\}.
       ```                                              |     | (6) |

Let $`c_{t}`$ denote the cumulative scientific evaluation cost when
$`x_{t}`$ has been evaluated, and let $`B`$ denote the total evaluation
budget. We define the finite-budget performance of policy $`\pi`$ on
target $`\tau`$ as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
J_{\tau}(\pi;B)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}t:\,c_{t}\leq B\\
x_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\end{subarray}}L_{\tau}(x_{t}),\qquad\min\varnothing:=+\infty.
``` |  | (7) |

Equation equation [7](#A1.E7 "In A.1 Finite-Budget Scientific Optimization ‣ Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
evaluates the best physically feasible design obtained before the budget
is exhausted. For a fixed execution trace and $`B_{2}\geq B_{1}`$, the
feasible incumbent set available at $`B_{1}`$ is a subset of that
available at $`B_{2}`$, and therefore

|     |                                              |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       J_{\tau}(\pi;B_{2})\leq J_{\tau}(\pi;B_{1}).
       ```                                           |     | (8) |

This monotonicity applies to the recorded best feasible solution, rather
than to every intermediate optimization iterate.

ADAPT optimizes the two components of the search policy jointly:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left(W_{\tau}^{\star},O_{\tau}^{\star}\right)\in\arg\min_{W\in\mathcal{W},\,O\in\mathcal{O}}J_{\tau}\bigl((W,O);B\bigr).
``` |  | (9) |

The role of $`W_{\tau}^{\star}`$ is to determine *where to search*,
whereas $`O_{\tau}^{\star}`$ determines *how to search*. Importantly,
Equation equation [9](#A1.E9 "In A.1 Finite-Budget Scientific Optimization ‣ Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
defines the objective of the meta-level search; it does not assume that
the discrete policy space is differentiable or that the global optimum
over $`\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{O}`$ can be found.

### A.2 Detector Topology Representation

A detector target consists of a discrete optical topology together with
a set of continuous design variables. In the size-three UIFO
representation used in this work, each topology is encoded by a string
containing nine internal component symbols followed by twelve boundary
symbols.

The nine internal positions follow the order

|     |                                             |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       11,\ 12,\ 13,\ 21,\ 22,\ 23,\ 31,\ 32,\ 33,
       ```                                          |     | (10) |

and the twelve boundary positions follow

|     |                                                            |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       01,\ 02,\ 03,\ 10,\ 14,\ 20,\ 24,\ 30,\ 34,\ 41,\ 42,\ 43.
       ```                                                         |     | (11) |

For internal components, symbols A–D represent beam splitters with
different orientations, whereas E–H represent directional beam splitters
with the corresponding orientation convention. Boundary symbols specify
optical sources or readout elements:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{L}=\text{laser},\qquad\mathrm{S}=\text{squeezer},\qquad\mathrm{D}=\text{detector},\qquad\mathrm{H}=\text{balanced homodyne readout}.
``` |  | (12) |

For example, the eight held-out targets used in the main experiments are
distinct discrete topologies, while optimization is performed over their
topology-specific continuous parameter spaces. Complete visualizations
of all eight targets, including the initial configuration, the standard
Adam result, and the final ADAPT result, are provided in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Complete Held-Out Detector Topology Atlas ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

### A.3 Continuous Design Variables and Physical Bounds

For a fixed topology $`\tau`$, the continuous design vector
$`x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\tau}}`$ contains the active optical and mechanical
parameters associated with that topology. Depending on the included
optical components, these variables may include mirror reflectivities
and tunings, squeezing parameters, laser powers, test-mass masses, and
propagation lengths.

Table [4](#A1.T4 "Table 4 ‣ A.3 Continuous Design Variables and Physical Bounds ‣ Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
summarizes the default physical parameter ranges used by the simulator.

| Parameter          | Range                       | Unit          |
|--------------------|-----------------------------|---------------|
| Reflectivity       | $`[10^{-12},\,1-10^{-12}]`$ | dimensionless |
| Mirror tuning      | $`[-360,\,360]`$            | degree        |
| Squeezing level    | $`[0,\,10]`$                | dB            |
| Squeezing angle    | $`[-360,\,360]`$            | degree        |
| Laser power        | $`[0,\,200]`$               | W             |
| Mass               | $`[0.01,\,200]`$            | kg            |
| Propagation length | $`[0.1,\,4000]`$            | m             |

Table 4: Continuous detector-design variables and default physical
bounds. The active parameter dimension $`d_{\tau}`$ depends on the
detector topology. Some physical properties may share one optimization
scalar and are therefore optimized jointly.

The nominal physical range of a reflectivity is $`[0,1]`$. In the
numerical implementation, both endpoints are moved inward by
$`10^{-12}`$ to avoid singular boundary behavior.

The parameter dimension is topology-dependent. In addition, several
optical properties can be coupled to the same optimization scalar.
Consequently, $`d_{\tau}`$ is determined from the topology-specific
optimization schema rather than being fixed globally. When historical
designs are transferred between topologies, these coupled parameter
groups are preserved; the corresponding procedure is described in
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Topology-Compatible Semantic Transfer ‣ Appendix B Historical Experience Library and Target-Aware Retrieval ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

### A.4 Differentiable Physics Simulator

Each candidate detector is evaluated using the differentiable
interferometer simulator employed throughout the study. For every design
$`x`$, the simulator constructs quantum-noise, amplitude-modulation, and
frequency-modulation configurations and evaluates their corresponding
detector responses over the same frequency grid.

We use $`N_{f}=100`$ logarithmically spaced frequencies from
$`f_{\min}=20~\mathrm{Hz}`$ to $`f_{\max}=5000~\mathrm{Hz}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f_{k}=f_{\min}\left(\frac{f_{\max}}{f_{\min}}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{N_{f}-1}},\qquad k=1,\ldots,N_{f}.
``` |  | (13) |

Let $`N_{q}(f;x)`$ denote the simulated quantum-noise magnitude, and let
$`D_{q}(f;x)`$, $`D_{a}(f;x)`$, and $`D_{f}(f;x)`$ denote the detector
transfer magnitudes extracted from the quantum, amplitude-modulation,
and frequency-modulation simulations, respectively. The resulting strain
sensitivity is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H(f;x)=\frac{\sqrt{|N_{q}(f;x)|^{2}+\left(4\times 10^{-9}D_{a}(f;x)\right)^{2}+\left(10^{-8}fD_{f}(f;x)\right)^{2}}}{D_{q}(f;x)}.
``` |  | (14) |

For numerical stability, the extracted detector transfer is lower
bounded by a small positive constant before division. The coefficients
in
Equation equation [14](#A1.E14 "In A.4 Differentiable Physics Simulator ‣ Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
are fixed simulator conventions and are not optimized or learned by
ADAPT.

Because the simulator is differentiable with respect to the continuous
design variables, gradient-based optimizers can directly differentiate
the scientific objective through the complete physical simulation.

### A.5 Native Sensitivity Objective

Let $`H_{\mathrm{ref}}(f)`$ denote the fixed reference sensitivity
curve. The native sensitivity objective is the mean logarithmic
sensitivity ratio across the $`N_{f}`$ evaluation frequencies:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
L_{\mathrm{sens}}(x)=\frac{1}{N_{f}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{f}}\log_{10}\frac{H(f_{k};x)}{H_{\mathrm{ref}}(f_{k})}.
``` |  | (15) |

Equation equation [15](#A1.E15 "In A.5 Native Sensitivity Objective ‣ Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
can equivalently be written as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
L_{\mathrm{sens}}(x)=\log_{10}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{N_{f}}\frac{H(f_{k};x)}{H_{\mathrm{ref}}(f_{k})}\right]^{1/N_{f}}.
``` |  | (16) |

Thus,

|     |                           |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       10^{L_{\mathrm{sens}}(x)}
       ```                        |     | (17) |

is the geometric mean of the sensitivity ratio relative to the reference
detector. This objective therefore rewards broadband improvement rather
than performance at only a single frequency.

Away from nonsmooth numerical boundaries, its gradient satisfies

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{x}L_{\mathrm{sens}}(x)=\frac{1}{N_{f}\ln 10}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{f}}\frac{\nabla_{x}H(f_{k};x)}{H(f_{k};x)}.
``` |  | (18) |

The reference curve contributes no gradient because it is fixed
throughout optimization.

### A.6 Physical Constraints and Power Penalty

In addition to sensitivity, candidate designs must satisfy internal
optical power constraints. Let $`P_{g,j}(x)`$ denote the power at
constrained port $`j`$ belonging to constraint group $`g`$, and let
$`T_{g}`$ be its corresponding physical threshold.

The simulator uses the following threshold levels:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{hard}}=3.5\times 10^{6}~\mathrm{W},\qquad T_{\mathrm{soft}}=2\times 10^{3}~\mathrm{W},\qquad T_{\mathrm{detector}}=10^{-2}~\mathrm{W}.
``` |  | (19) |

For each constrained power, define the normalized violation

|     |                                                           |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       a_{g,j}(x)=\max\left(\frac{P_{g,j}(x)}{T_{g}}-1,0\right),
       ```                                                        |     | (20) |

and the bounded penalty

|     |                                             |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       p_{g,j}(x)=\frac{a_{g,j}(x)}{1+a_{g,j}(x)}.
       ```                                          |     | (21) |

The complete native optimization objective is

|     |                                                        |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       L_{\tau}(x)=L_{\mathrm{sens}}(x)+\sum_{g,j}p_{g,j}(x).
       ```                                                     |     | (22) |

Each individual penalty satisfies $`0\leq p_{g,j}(x)<1`$, while the
complete penalty can exceed one because multiple physical constraints
may be violated simultaneously.

For $`P_{g,j}>T_{g}`$,
Equation equation [21](#A1.E21 "In A.6 Physical Constraints and Power Penalty ‣ Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
reduces to

|     |                                  |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       p_{g,j}=1-\frac{T_{g}}{P_{g,j}},
       ```                               |     | (23) |

and therefore

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\partial p_{g,j}}{\partial P_{g,j}}=\frac{T_{g}}{P_{g,j}^{2}}.
``` |  | (24) |

Below the threshold, the derivative is zero.

The penalty in
Equation equation [22](#A1.E22 "In A.6 Physical Constraints and Power Penalty ‣ Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
facilitates differentiable optimization, but physical feasibility is
evaluated independently. A design is considered feasible only if

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\in\mathcal{X}_{\tau}\ \text{and}\ P_{g,j}(x)\leq T_{g}\quad\forall(g,j).
``` |  | (25) |

This distinction is important: the differentiable penalty guides search,
whereas the final scientific validity of a design is determined by the
physical constraints themselves.

### A.7 Bounded-to-Unbounded Optimization Coordinates

The physical parameters lie inside heterogeneous bounded intervals. To
provide a common unconstrained optimization space, we transform each
physical parameter $`x_{j}\in[\ell_{j},h_{j}]`$ to an unconstrained
coordinate $`u_{j}`$.

First define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{j}=\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{x_{j}-\ell_{j}}{h_{j}-\ell_{j}},\epsilon_{u},1-\epsilon_{u}\right),
``` |  | (26) |

where $`\epsilon_{u}=10^{-9}`$ is used for numerical stability. The
unconstrained coordinate is then

|     |                                 |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{j}=\log q_{j}-\log(1-q_{j}).
       ```                              |     | (27) |

The inverse transformation is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{j}(u_{j})=\ell_{j}+(h_{j}-\ell_{j})\sigma(u_{j}),\qquad\sigma(u)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-u}}.
``` |  | (28) |

By the chain rule,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{u}L=\operatorname{diag}\left[(h-\ell)\odot\sigma(u)\odot\bigl(1-\sigma(u)\bigr)\right]\nabla_{x}L.
``` |  | (29) |

If one scalar controls multiple coupled optical properties
$`\{y_{k}\}`$, their gradient contributions are accumulated:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{j}}=\sum_{k}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y_{k}}\frac{\partial y_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}.
``` |  | (30) |

For this reason, coupled parameter groups are treated as indivisible
semantic units when transferring historical designs between detector
topologies.

### B.2 Target-Aware Topology Retrieval

For a new target topology $`\tau`$, the Target-Aware Matcher first ranks
historical designs according to structural similarity and retrieves the
$`K=64`$ closest compatible designs. These designs form the candidate
pool

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}=\left\{d_{(1)},d_{(2)},\ldots,d_{(64)}\right\}.
``` |  | (35) |

In the topology-aware retrieval implementation, the distance decomposes
into the nine internal components and twelve boundary components. Let
$`c_{\tau,j}`$ and $`b_{\tau,j}`$ denote the internal and boundary
symbols of the target, respectively, and let $`c_{i,j}`$ and $`b_{i,j}`$
denote those of a historical topology $`i`$. The retrieval distance is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D(\tau,i)=\frac{1}{9}\sum_{j=1}^{9}d_{c}(c_{\tau,j},c_{i,j})+\frac{1}{12}\sum_{j=1}^{12}\mathbbm{1}\left[b_{\tau,j}\neq b_{i,j}\right],
``` |  | (36) |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{c}(a,b)=\begin{cases}0,&a=b,\\[5.69054pt]
0.25,&a\neq b\text{ but }a\text{ and }b\text{ belong to the same component family},\\[5.69054pt]
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (37) |

This definition distinguishes an orientation change within the same
optical component family from a replacement by a different component
family. Exact matches therefore receive zero cost, orientation-level
changes receive a smaller cost, and changes of component type receive
the largest cost. Symbols are compared at fixed positions without
rotational or reflection alignment.

Historical candidates are ranked by increasing $`D(\tau,i)`$. Exact
copies of the target topology are excluded from retrieval, and
incompatible historical records are skipped. Retrieval continues until
64 distinct transferable sources have been collected, without
dataset-specific quotas. Lexicographic ordering of topology strings is
used to resolve equal-distance ties.

### B.3 Topology-Compatible Semantic Transfer

A retrieved historical detector cannot in general be copied directly to
a new topology because the target may contain a different set of optical
components and a different number of continuous parameters. We therefore
transfer historical designs according to the semantic identity of each
optimization variable.

Each scalar is associated with

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bigl(\text{physical property},\text{component names and types},\,\text{complete controlled component set}\bigr),
``` |  | (38) |

where the controlled component set contains the complete group of
optical properties coupled to that scalar. Two variables are considered
directly transferable only when their semantic identities match and the
historical value is valid under the target bounds.

For target coordinate $`j`$ and retrieved historical design $`i`$, the
transferred value is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{\tau,j}^{(i)}=\begin{cases}x_{i,k},&\text{if a compatible semantic match }k\text{ exists},\\[5.69054pt]
\ell_{\tau,j}+\left(h_{\tau,j}-\ell_{\tau,j}\right)m_{j},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
``` |  | (39) |

where $`m_{j}\in[0,1]`$ is a normalized historical prior used to
complete unmatched coordinates.

Preserving the complete coupled component set is essential. If one
scalar jointly controls several optical properties, transferring only
one member of the group would alter the physical meaning of the
optimization variable and would no longer reproduce a valid target
parameterization.

### B.4 Hierarchical Prior for Unmatched Parameters

Not every target coordinate has an exact counterpart in every retrieved
historical topology. Instead of filling such coordinates with an
arbitrary constant or random value, ADAPT uses frozen training-set
normalized medians. All prior statistics are computed from the training
set only.

For each physical parameter, historical values are first normalized to
$`[0,1]`$ using their corresponding physical bounds. The completion
prior is selected hierarchically:

1.  1.
    P2: component-type/property prior. Use the median normalized value
    for the same component type and physical property whenever this
    statistic is available.
2.  2.
    P1: property-level prior. If a component-type-specific statistic is
    unavailable, use the median normalized value for the same physical
    property across compatible components.
3.  3.
    P0: global prior. If neither P2 nor P1 is available, use the global
    normalized median.

For coupled variables spanning multiple component types, the
component-specific P2 prior is not used because it would assign a single
coupled scalar according to only one member of the group. In this case,
the hierarchy falls back to the property-level or global prior.

This procedure allows a historical detector to provide a complete
topology-compatible initialization even when the source and target
topologies are not identical.

### B.5 Target-Specific Screening of Retrieved Designs

Retrieval similarity alone does not imply that a transferred design
performs well on the new target. The 64 transferred candidates are
therefore evaluated once under the *target* simulator without optimizer
updates before the Selection Evolver allocates the remaining
optimization budget. This screening is charged to the same total
wall-clock budget as subsequent optimization.

For candidate $`i`$, let $`L_{i}`$ denote its target loss and define its
maximum normalized physical violation as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v_{i}=\max\left(0,\,\max_{g,j}\left[\frac{P_{g,j}(x_{i})}{T_{g}}-1\right]\right).
``` |  | (40) |

Candidates are first separated according to physical feasibility. A
finite feasible candidate is preferred to an infeasible candidate, and
an infeasible candidate is preferred to an invalid evaluation. The
ranking can be written lexicographically as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
k_{i}=\begin{cases}(0,L_{i},0,i),&\text{if }x_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau},\\[2.84526pt]
(1,v_{i},L_{i},i),&\text{if the evaluation is finite but infeasible},\\[2.84526pt]
(2,+\infty,+\infty,i),&\text{if the evaluation is invalid}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (41) |

This target-side screening step is important because historical
performance is used only as prior experience; it is not assumed to
remain valid after transferring a design to a different detector
topology.

### B.6 Quality–Diversity Construction of the Eight-Start Set

When at least eight feasible candidates are available after screening,
ADAPT constructs an eight-start set consisting of four quality-oriented
candidates and four diversity-oriented candidates.

The four quality candidates are the feasible transferred designs with
the lowest target loss:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{quality}}=\operatorname{Top4}_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}\left[-L_{i}\right].
``` |  | (42) |

To avoid concentrating all starts in the same local basin, four
additional candidates are selected greedily according to their distance
from the current selected set. Let $`q(x_{i})\in[0,1]^{d_{\tau}}`$
denote the target design after normalization by its physical parameter
bounds. We use the root-mean-square parameter distance

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{q}(i,j)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d_{\tau}}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\tau}}\left(q_{k}(x_{i})-q_{k}(x_{j})\right)^{2}}.
``` |  | (43) |

Starting with $`\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{quality}}`$, each
diversity candidate is selected as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
i^{\star}=\arg\max_{i:\,x_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau},\,i\notin\mathcal{S}}\min_{j\in\mathcal{S}}d_{q}(i,j),
``` |  | (44) |

and is then appended to $`\mathcal{S}`$. This operation is repeated
until $`|\mathcal{S}|=8`$. Ties are resolved by candidate index.

The resulting initialization set can therefore be written as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{S}_{8}=\underbrace{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{quality}}}_{\text{4 low-loss starts}}\cup\underbrace{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{diversity}}}_{\text{4 coverage-preserving starts}}.
``` |  | (45) |

If fewer than eight feasible candidates are available, all feasible
candidates are retained first and the remaining positions are filled
with numerically valid infeasible candidates in order of physical
violation, target loss, and candidate index. In this fallback case, the
four-plus-four decomposition is not artificially enforced. Fewer than
eight numerically valid candidates terminates the selection run.

The eight selected starts are passed to the Selection Evolver, which
performs the subsequent progressive

|     |                             |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       8\rightarrow 2\rightarrow 1
       ```                          |     | (46) |

allocation described in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Selection Evolution: Learning Where to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

### B.8 Isolation of Held-Out Targets

The historical library provides prior scientific experience, but
held-out outcomes are not used as historical supervision. For a held-out
target $`\tau_{\mathrm{test}}`$, its topology is available at inference
time because it defines the scientific problem to be optimized. However,
its held-out optimized design, final loss, future optimization
trajectory, and validator outcome are not included in the historical
source library.

Accordingly, target-aware retrieval uses only the target topology as a
query:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tau_{\mathrm{test}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\tau_{\mathrm{test}}},
``` |  | (47) |

while the retrieved records themselves originate from the permitted
historical experience bank.

The same principle applies to all subsequent validation. Information
obtained from the final held-out validator is used only for reporting
the scientific performance of the resulting policy and is not fed back
into the historical library or used to revise the reported policy.

## Appendix C Selection Evolution: Learning Where to Search

The first adaptive stage of ADAPT determines *where to search*. Rather
than treating initialization as a fixed preprocessing choice, the
Selection Evolver searches over policies that transform historical
scientific experience into a target-specific starting point under a
finite evaluation budget.

The complete stage contains two conceptually distinct levels. During
method development, agentic workflows propose and refine candidate
selection policies using development targets and execution feedback.
After development, the resulting selection policy is frozen. At held-out
evaluation time, this fixed policy is applied to the target-specific
candidate pool constructed in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Historical Experience Library and Target-Aware Retrieval ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").
No held-out outcome is used to revise the selection policy itself.

For a target $`\tau`$, the Selection Evolver therefore implements the
mapping

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
W^{\star}:\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\longrightarrow x_{0,\tau}^{\star},
``` |  | (48) |

where $`\mathcal{C}_{\tau}`$ contains the 64 retrieved historical
designs and $`x_{0,\tau}^{\star}`$ is the parameter vector handed to the
subsequent Optimizer Evolver.

### C.1 Selection Policy as a Search Object

A conventional optimization pipeline typically fixes the initialization
strategy before optimization begins. In ADAPT, the initialization
strategy itself is treated as an object of meta-level search.

We denote a candidate selection policy at evolution step $`k`$ by

|     |          |     |      |
|-----|----------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       W^{(k)}.
       ```       |     | (49) |

A selection policy may specify how historical candidates are filtered,
how quality and diversity are combined, how short optimization probes
are allocated, how weak branches are pruned, and how exploratory
branches are protected.

Executing $`W^{(k)}`$ produces an empirical feedback record

|     |                                              |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       F_{W}^{(k)}=\mathcal{E}\left(W^{(k)}\right),
       ```                                           |     | (50) |

where $`\mathcal{E}`$ denotes execution against the differentiable
scientific simulator under the assigned development budget.

The feedback contains measured scientific outcomes rather than a
language-model preference. At minimum, the execution trace records
objective values, physical feasibility, optimization progress, consumed
evaluation budget, and failure status. The proposing workflow receives a
compact representation of these observations and uses them to revise the
policy:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
W^{(k)}\xrightarrow{\;\mathcal{E}\;}F_{W}^{(k)}\xrightarrow{\;\text{agentic revision}\;}W^{(k+1)}.
``` |  | (51) |

Strong previously evaluated policies remain available for comparison
with new proposals. Policy evolution is therefore driven by measured
scientific performance and does not rely on an LLM self-assessment of
the generated policy.

### C.3 From 64 Retrieved Designs to Eight Search Starts

For a target $`\tau`$, the Target-Aware Matcher first constructs the
retrieved candidate pool

|     |                                             |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{C}_{\tau}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{64}\}.
       ```                                          |     | (55) |

As described in
Appendix [B.6](#A2.SS6 "B.6 Quality–Diversity Construction of the Eight-Start Set ‣ Appendix B Historical Experience Library and Target-Aware Retrieval ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization"),
these transferred candidates are evaluated on the target simulator and
reduced to eight starting points:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{S}_{8}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{quality}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{diversity}},\qquad|\mathcal{S}_{8}|=8.
``` |  | (56) |

When at least eight feasible transferred candidates are available,
$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{quality}}`$ contains the four feasible candidates
with the lowest target loss and $`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{diversity}}`$
contains four additional candidates selected by the greedy
farthest-point criterion in
Equation equation [44](#A2.E44 "In B.6 Quality–Diversity Construction of the Eight-Start Set ‣ Appendix B Historical Experience Library and Target-Aware Retrieval ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

It is important to distinguish this step from the subsequent
optimization probes. The 64 historical records form a retrieval and
screening pool; ADAPT does not allocate a full optimization trajectory
to all 64 candidates. Only the resulting eight starts enter the
progressive optimization schedule.

### C.4 Progressive $`8\rightarrow 2\rightarrow 1`$ Allocation

The selected eight starts are evaluated through a progressive
budget-allocation policy:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
64\;\longrightarrow\;8\;\longrightarrow\;2\;\longrightarrow\;1.
``` |  | (57) |

Let the eight historical starts be

|     |                                         |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{H}_{8}=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{8}\}.
       ```                                      |     | (58) |

Each historical start is initially optimized for

|     |                                                   |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       T_{\mathrm{screen}}=180~\mathrm{s}=3~\mathrm{min}
       ```                                                |     | (59) |

of active trajectory time using the same fixed screening optimizer and
scientific objective.

For branch $`i`$, define its incumbent feasible objective after active
time $`t`$ as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
J_{i}(t)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\leq t\\
x_{i,s}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\end{subarray}}L_{\tau}(x_{i,s}),\qquad\min\varnothing:=+\infty.
``` |  | (60) |

After the initial screening stage, the two strongest historical
trajectories are retained:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{H}_{2}=\operatorname{Top2}_{h_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{8}}\left[-J_{i}(T_{\mathrm{screen}})\right].
``` |  | (61) |

If a branch has not produced a feasible design, finite infeasible
candidates are ordered using their physical-violation magnitude before
invalid evaluations, following the same feasibility-aware convention
used in
Appendix [B.5](#A2.SS5 "B.5 Target-Specific Screening of Retrieved Designs ‣ Appendix B Historical Experience Library and Target-Aware Retrieval ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

Each of the two retained historical trajectories receives an additional

|     |                                                       |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       T_{\mathrm{continue}}=1080~\mathrm{s}=18~\mathrm{min}
       ```                                                    |     | (62) |

of active optimization. Consequently, a retained historical trajectory
may receive up to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{hist}}=T_{\mathrm{screen}}+T_{\mathrm{continue}}=1260~\mathrm{s}=21~\mathrm{min}
``` |  | (63) |

before the final historical start is selected.

This distinction is important: the “21-minute” continuation reported in
the main paper denotes the cumulative *active optimization time of one
surviving trajectory*, rather than 21 minutes of elapsed time from the
start of the complete ADAPT run.

### C.5 Protected Exploratory Branch

Aggressive pruning can prematurely concentrate the search around
historical designs that appear strong during a short screening interval.
To preserve an independent alternative, the final selection policy
maintains a protected exploratory branch in parallel with the historical
candidates.

In the implemented scheduler, this branch is initialized independently
of the eight retrieved historical starts and receives a protected
active-time quota

|     |                                                       |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       T_{\mathrm{explore}}=3600~\mathrm{s}=60~\mathrm{min}.
       ```                                                    |     | (64) |

The exploratory trajectory is not removed merely because its early
objective is worse than those of the historical branches. Its compute is
interleaved with the retained historical trajectories in

|     |                               |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       360~\mathrm{s}=6~\mathrm{min}
       ```                            |     | (65) |

scheduling slices.

The purpose of this branch is not to introduce a different scientific
objective. It is evaluated with the same simulator, physical
constraints, and native loss as every other branch. Its role is purely
algorithmic: it prevents the progressive scheduler from allocating the
complete pre-optimization budget exclusively according to early
historical performance.

The selection mechanism therefore combines two complementary forms of
exploration:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\underbrace{\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{diversity}}}_{\text{diversity among historical designs}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\underbrace{h_{\mathrm{explore}}}_{\text{independent protected trajectory}}.
``` |  | (66) |

### C.6 Active-Time Accounting and the Shared Deadline

The branch quotas above are *active optimization times*. They should not
be added directly to elapsed wall-clock time without specifying the
scheduler.

Ignoring simulator and scheduling overhead, the nominal active compute
allocated before the final optimizer stage is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{selection}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=8T_{\mathrm{screen}}+2T_{\mathrm{continue}}+T_{\mathrm{explore}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=8(180)+2(1080)+3600`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=7200~\mathrm{s}=120~\mathrm{min}.`$ |  | (67) |

Equation equation [67](#A3.E67 "In C.6 Active-Time Accounting and the Shared Deadline ‣ Appendix C Selection Evolution: Learning Where to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
is an accounting identity for the scheduled active quotas; it is not an
independent experimental result.

The complete pipeline is governed by a shared wall-clock deadline.
Therefore, candidate evaluation, simulator execution, synchronization,
compilation when charged to the run, bookkeeping, branch switching,
selection, and the final optimizer all consume the same end-to-end
budget.

Let $`T_{\mathrm{global}}`$ denote the global deadline and
$`T_{\mathrm{sel,end}}`$ the wall-clock time at which selection
finishes. The time available to the subsequent optimizer is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{final}}=\max\left(T_{\mathrm{global}}-T_{\mathrm{sel,end}},0\right).
``` |  | (68) |

Thus, ADAPT does not receive a full optimization budget *after*
selection. Selection and final optimization share the same scientific
evaluation budget. This convention is essential for comparison with
methods evaluated under the same end-to-end limit.

### C.7 Feasibility-Aware Branch Ranking

Scientific feasibility is treated separately from the differentiable
penalty used during local optimization.

For a branch $`i`$, let

|     |                                        |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{R}_{i}(t)=\{x_{i,s}:s\leq t\}
       ```                                     |     | (69) |

be the set of completed evaluations available at active time $`t`$.

If at least one feasible observation exists, branch quality is
determined by its best feasible objective:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Q_{i}(t)=\min_{x\in\mathcal{R}_{i}(t)\cap\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}L_{\tau}(x).
``` |  | (70) |

If no feasible evaluation exists, the branch is retained in a secondary
ordering based first on physical violation and then on finite objective
value. Invalid evaluations are ranked behind finite evaluations.

A convenient representation of this ordering is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{i}(t)=\begin{cases}\left(0,\,Q_{i}(t)\right),&\exists x\in\mathcal{R}_{i}(t)\cap\mathcal{F}_{\tau},\\[5.69054pt]
\left(1,\,V_{i}(t),\,L_{i}^{\mathrm{fin}}(t)\right),&\text{finite but infeasible observations exist},\\[5.69054pt]
\left(2,\,+\infty,\,+\infty\right),&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
``` |  | (71) |

where $`V_{i}(t)`$ is the smallest recorded physical-violation score and
$`L_{i}^{\mathrm{fin}}(t)`$ is the corresponding finite objective.

The tuples in
Equation equation [71](#A3.E71 "In C.7 Feasibility-Aware Branch Ranking ‣ Appendix C Selection Evolution: Learning Where to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
are compared lexicographically. This rule prevents an invalid or
strongly infeasible trajectory from being preferred solely because of an
undefined or misleading objective value.

### C.8 Selection of the Final Historical Start

After the continuation budget has been allocated, the two historical
survivors are compared using their completed target-side evaluations.
The winning historical branch is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h^{\star}=\arg\min_{h_{i}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}}R_{i}(T_{\mathrm{hist}}),
``` |  | (72) |

with deterministic tie breaking.

The selected initialization is the best admissible parameter vector
observed on that branch:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{0,\tau}^{\star}=\operatorname{Incumbent}\left(h^{\star}\right).
``` |  | (73) |

The resulting parameter vector is the output of the Selection Evolver:

|     |                                                              |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       W^{\star}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\right)=x_{0,\tau}^{\star}.
       ```                                                           |     | (74) |

A hash of the handoff parameter vector is stored in the execution record
so that the boundary between the selection and optimization stages can
be reconstructed exactly during reproducibility checks.

### C.9 Parameter-Only Handoff to the Optimizer Evolver

The handoff between the two adaptive stages transfers the selected
*scientific design*, not the internal state of the screening optimizer.

Specifically, the Selection Evolver outputs

|     |                     |     |      |
|-----|---------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       x_{0,\tau}^{\star},
       ```                  |     | (75) |

which becomes the initialization of the optimizer selected by the second
adaptive stage. Optimizer-specific internal variables are initialized
independently at this boundary.

For an Adam-family optimizer, for example, the final stage begins with

|     |                         |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       m_{0}=0,\qquad v_{0}=0,
       ```                      |     | (76) |

unless otherwise explicitly specified by the final optimizer program.
The screening-stage moment estimates are therefore not implicitly
reused.

This parameter-only interface makes the two decisions in ADAPT explicit:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\underbrace{W^{\star}:\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\rightarrow x_{0,\tau}^{\star}}_{\text{where to search}}\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad\underbrace{O^{\star}:x_{0,\tau}^{\star}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{\tau}}_{\text{how to search}}.
``` |  | (77) |

The optimizer-evolution stage and the final evolved optimizer are
described in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Optimizer Evolution: Learning How to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

### C.11 Why Progressive Allocation is Part of the Search Policy

The purpose of the progressive schedule is not to claim that a short run
perfectly predicts long-horizon optimizer performance. Rather, it
provides a finite-budget mechanism for deciding how much scientific
computation should be spent on competing starting regions.

A uniform allocation over all eight starts would spend the same amount
of compute on trajectories regardless of their observed progress.
Conversely, immediately committing to the historically best candidate
would remove most of the benefit of target-side evaluation.

The retained policy occupies an intermediate regime:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{historical prior}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{short target-side evidence}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{progressive concentration}.
``` |  | (78) |

Quality-oriented retrieval supplies promising starting regions,
diversity-oriented selection preserves geometric coverage, short
optimization probes test those regions on the new target, and the
protected exploratory branch reduces the risk of complete early collapse
onto the historical prior.

The resulting initialization should therefore be interpreted as the
outcome of a finite-budget scientific decision process rather than as
the nearest historical design or the minimum-loss historical record
alone.

### C.12 Held-Out Evaluation Boundary

The distinction between *policy development* and *policy execution* is
essential for the held-out protocol.

The agentic procedure in
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ C.10 Complete Selection Algorithm ‣ Appendix C Selection Evolution: Learning Where to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
is used to develop and freeze the selection strategy on permitted
development information. For a held-out topology, ADAPT executes
Algorithm [3](#alg3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ C.10 Complete Selection Algorithm ‣ Appendix C Selection Evolution: Learning Where to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
but does not use the resulting held-out performance to modify
$`W^{\star}`$.

Thus, during held-out evaluation, the target topology and simulator are
available because they define the scientific optimization problem, and
target-side evaluations consume the declared scientific budget. However,
the resulting validation outcome is not returned to the Selection
Evolver, is not added to the historical experience library, and is not
used to revise the policy reported in the paper.

This separation ensures that the held-out experiment measures
application of a previously specified search procedure rather than
additional strategy development on the test set.

## Appendix D Optimizer Evolution: Learning How to Search

The second adaptive stage of ADAPT determines *how to search*. After the
Selection Evolver returns a target-specific initialization
$`x_{0,\tau}^{\star}`$, the Optimizer Evolver searches over executable
optimization procedures and selects a procedure according to measured
performance under the scientific objective.

The optimizer used in the final ADAPT pipeline belongs to the Adam
family but modifies its finite-horizon behavior, first-moment memory,
and numerical failure handling. We refer to this evolved procedure as
ADAPT-Adam throughout the supplementary material.

In the archived development records, the selected implementation is
identified by the internal run label GEPA_GPT_4. We distinguish this
provenance identifier from the algorithm name because the former records
how the program was obtained, whereas ADAPT-Adam denotes the resulting
optimization procedure itself.

The complete second-stage mapping is therefore

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
O^{\star}:x_{0,\tau}^{\star}\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}_{\tau}=\left\{x_{0,\tau}^{\star},x_{1},\ldots,x_{T}\right\},
``` |  | (79) |

subject to the same global scientific evaluation budget introduced in
Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Finite-Budget Scientific Optimization ‣ Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

### D.1 Optimizer Programs as Meta-Level Search Objects

Rather than restricting adaptation to scalar hyperparameters, ADAPT
treats the optimizer implementation itself as a search object.

Let

|     |          |     |      |
|-----|----------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       O^{(k)}
       ```       |     | (80) |

denote an optimizer program proposed at evolution step $`k`$. Executing
this program on an assigned development target produces an empirical
trace

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}^{(k)}=\left\{x_{t},\,L_{t},\,g_{t},\,f_{t},\,c_{t}\right\}_{t=0}^{T_{k}},
``` |  | (81) |

where $`x_{t}`$ is the current design, $`L_{t}=L_{\tau}(x_{t})`$ is the
native scientific objective, $`g_{t}`$ is its gradient in the
optimization coordinates, $`f_{t}`$ records feasibility information, and
$`c_{t}`$ is cumulative scientific evaluation cost.

The trace is summarized into execution feedback

|     |                                                 |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       F_{O}^{(k)}=\Phi\left(\mathcal{R}^{(k)}\right),
       ```                                              |     | (82) |

which is returned to the optimizer-evolution workflow. The resulting
update is conceptually

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
O^{(k)}\xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{execute}\;}F_{O}^{(k)}\xrightarrow{\;\mathrm{revise}\;}O^{(k+1)}.
``` |  | (83) |

The executable optimizer may modify, among other components,

- •
  gradient preprocessing and clipping;
- •
  first- and second-moment dynamics;
- •
  learning-rate or finite-horizon update schedules;
- •
  state-management rules;
- •
  numerical-failure handling;
- •
  trajectory-management logic;
- •
  budget-aware behavior; and
- •
  acceptance or rollback rules.

The scientific simulator, target objective, physical constraints, and
held-out evaluation protocol are not modifiable components of this
search space.

### D.2 Execution-Based Optimizer Evolution

Candidate optimizers are evaluated by executing the generated program
against the differentiable detector simulator. Consequently, optimizer
selection is based on measured scientific behavior rather than a
language-model judgment about the generated source code.

The feedback record contains quantities available from actual execution,
including

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F_{O}^{(k)}=\left(L_{\mathrm{best}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{anytime}},\mathcal{F},N_{\mathrm{eval}},N_{\mathrm{invalid}},C,\mathrm{status}\right),
``` |  | (84) |

where $`L_{\mathrm{best}}`$ is the best admissible objective obtained,
$`\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{anytime}}`$ summarizes the optimization
trajectory, $`\mathcal{F}`$ summarizes physical feasibility,
$`N_{\mathrm{eval}}`$ is the number of completed scientific evaluations,
$`N_{\mathrm{invalid}}`$ counts invalid numerical evaluations, $`C`$
denotes consumed computational budget, and $`\mathrm{status}`$ records
successful or failed execution.

At the meta level, candidate programs are compared using a composite
score of the form

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{O}=w_{q}S_{\mathrm{quality}}+w_{a}S_{\mathrm{anytime}}+w_{f}S_{\mathrm{feasibility}}+w_{i}S_{\mathrm{improvement}},
``` |  | (85) |

where the four terms measure final feasible quality, performance
throughout execution, physical validity, and improvement over the
initialization, respectively. The prompt summary in the main paper
assigns weights $`w_{q}=0.7`$ and $`w_{a}=w_{f}=w_{i}=0.1`$.

Equation equation [85](#A4.E85 "In D.2 Execution-Based Optimizer Evolution ‣ Appendix D Optimizer Evolution: Learning How to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
describes the decomposition used by the optimizer-evolution system; it
is not the metric reported as the final scientific result. All
main-paper results are reported using the native detector objective and
physical feasibility criteria.

The exact score normalization, weights, program-generation settings, and
stopping configuration are reported verbatim with the released evolution
configuration in
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Experimental Protocol and Compute Accounting ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").
No held-out validator outcome is used to alter these settings.

### D.3 From Standard Adam to ADAPT-Adam

ADAPT-Adam preserves the basic computational structure of Adam while
changing three aspects of its finite-budget behavior:

1.  1.
    Shorter first-moment memory. The first-moment coefficient is changed
    to

    |     |                |     |      |
    |-----|----------------|-----|------|
    |     |
           ``` math
           \beta_{1}=0.7,
           ```             |     | (86) |

    making the search direction more responsive to recent gradients.
2.  2.
    Finite-horizon update scaling. Each Adam update is multiplied by a
    deterministic function of the fraction of optimization time
    remaining:

    |     |                      |     |      |
    |-----|----------------------|-----|------|
    |     |
           ``` math
           s(r)=0.05+0.95r^{2}.
           ```                   |     | (87) |
3.  3.
    Transactional invalid-state protection. Non-finite objective values
    or gradients restore the last valid parameters without updating the
    optimizer state. Non-finite parameter proposals are rejected
    together with the corresponding optimizer-state update.

The resulting procedure remains an Adam-family local optimizer, but its
dynamics are explicitly conditioned on the finite scientific evaluation
horizon and on numerical validity.

### D.4 Reference Adam Update

Let $`u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\tau}}`$ denote the unbounded optimization
coordinates defined in
Appendix [A.7](#A1.SS7 "A.7 Bounded-to-Unbounded Optimization Coordinates ‣ Appendix A Scientific Objective and Simulator Specification ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization"),
and let

|     |                                 |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       g_{t}=\nabla_{u}L_{\tau}(u_{t})
       ```                              |     | (88) |

denote the gradient of the native detector objective.

ADAPT-Adam first applies global $`\ell_{2}`$ gradient clipping with
threshold

|     |          |     |      |
|-----|----------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       c=1.
       ```       |     | (89) |

The clipped gradient is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{g}_{t}=\begin{cases}g_{t},&\|g_{t}\|_{2}\leq c,\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle c\,\frac{g_{t}}{\|g_{t}\|_{2}},&\|g_{t}\|_{2}>c.\end{cases}
``` |  | (90) |

Starting from

|     |                         |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       m_{0}=0,\qquad v_{0}=0,
       ```                      |     | (91) |

the Adam moment recursions are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle m_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\beta_{1}m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\bar{g}_{t},`$ |  | (92) |
|  | $`\displaystyle v_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\beta_{2}v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{2})\bar{g}_{t}^{\,2},`$ |  | (93) |

where squares are elementwise.

The bias-corrected moments are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{m_{t}}{1-\beta_{1}^{t}},`$ |  | (94) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{v}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{v_{t}}{1-\beta_{2}^{t}}.`$ |  | (95) |

The unscaled Adam proposal is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{Adam}}=-\alpha\frac{\widehat{m}_{t}}{\sqrt{\widehat{v}_{t}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{A}}},
``` |  | (96) |

where $`\alpha`$ is the base learning rate and $`\epsilon_{\mathrm{A}}`$
is the numerical stabilization constant.

The numerical values of $`\alpha`$, $`\beta_{2}`$, and
$`\epsilon_{\mathrm{A}}`$ used in the final implementation are reported
with the complete optimizer configuration in
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Experimental Protocol and Compute Accounting ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

### D.5 Short-Memory First-Moment Dynamics

The principal change to the Adam moment dynamics is

|     |                |     |      |
|-----|----------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \beta_{1}=0.7.
       ```             |     | (97) |

Expanding the first-moment recursion gives

|     |                                                                |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       m_{t}=(1-\beta_{1})\sum_{k=1}^{t}\beta_{1}^{\,t-k}\bar{g}_{k}.
       ```                                                             |     | (98) |

Thus, the contribution of a gradient observed $`\ell`$ updates earlier
decays as

|     |                   |     |      |
|-----|-------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \beta_{1}^{\ell}.
       ```                |     | (99) |

A useful characterization of the exponential weighting is its mean lag,

|     |                                                     |     |       |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \ell_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}.
       ```                                                  |     | (100) |

For the conventional value $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$,

|     |                                         |     |       |
|-----|-----------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \ell_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{0.9}{0.1}=9,
       ```                                      |     | (101) |

whereas for ADAPT-Adam,

|     |                                                   |     |       |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \ell_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{0.7}{0.3}\approx 2.33.
       ```                                                |     | (102) |

Therefore, the evolved setting places substantially greater relative
weight on recent gradients. This calculation characterizes the memory
kernel only; it does not imply a general convergence-rate guarantee.

### D.6 Finite-Horizon Update Scaling

ADAPT-Adam explicitly conditions its update magnitude on the remaining
optimization horizon.

Let $`T_{\mathrm{left}}(t)`$ denote the remaining wall-clock budget at
accepted update $`t`$, and let

|     |                      |     |       |
|-----|----------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       T_{\mathrm{left}}(0)
       ```                   |     | (103) |

denote the remaining budget when the final optimizer starts.

We define the normalized remaining fraction as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{t}=\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{left}}(t)}{T_{\mathrm{left}}(0)},0,1\right).
``` |  | (104) |

The evolved scaling function is

|     |                              |     |       |
|-----|------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       s(r_{t})=0.05+0.95r_{t}^{2}.
       ```                           |     | (105) |

The committed parameter update is therefore

|     |                                                   |     |       |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{t+1}=u_{t}+s(r_{t})\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{Adam}}.
       ```                                                |     | (106) |

The schedule satisfies

|     |                                               |     |       |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       s(1)=1,\qquad s(0.5)=0.2875,\qquad s(0)=0.05.
       ```                                            |     | (107) |

Hence, the optimizer begins with the full Adam proposal magnitude and
becomes progressively more conservative as the remaining scientific
budget decreases.

Equivalently, using normalized elapsed time

|     |          |     |       |
|-----|----------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       a=1-r,
       ```       |     | (108) |

we have

|     |                         |     |       |
|-----|-------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       s(a)=0.05+0.95(1-a)^{2}
       ```                      |     | (109) |

and

|     |                                |     |       |
|-----|--------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{ds}{da}=-1.9(1-a)\leq 0.
       ```                             |     | (110) |

Thus, the update scale is monotonically non-increasing with elapsed
budget.

For reference, its uniform-horizon average is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(0.05+0.95r^{2}\right)dr`$ | $`\displaystyle=0.05+\frac{0.95}{3}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{11}{30}\approx 0.3667.`$ |  | (111) |

Equation equation [111](#A4.E111 "In D.6 Finite-Horizon Update Scaling ‣ Appendix D Optimizer Evolution: Learning How to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
is an analytical property of the schedule, not a measured average over
scientific evaluations. Actual evaluations need not be uniformly
distributed in wall-clock time.

### D.7 Why Update Scaling is Applied After Adam

The finite-horizon scale is applied to the proposed Adam update rather
than to the raw input gradient:

|     |                                                   |     |       |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{t+1}=u_{t}+s(r_{t})\Delta_{t}^{\mathrm{Adam}}.
       ```                                                |     | (112) |

This distinction matters because multiplying the input gradient by
$`s(r_{t})`$ would also alter the moment recursions in
Equations equation [92](#A4.E92 "In D.4 Reference Adam Update ‣ Appendix D Optimizer Evolution: Learning How to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")–equation [93](#A4.E93 "In D.4 Reference Adam Update ‣ Appendix D Optimizer Evolution: Learning How to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").
In ADAPT-Adam, the moment estimator continues to summarize the clipped
physical gradient, while the finite-horizon factor controls only the
magnitude of the parameter displacement.

The mechanism can therefore be interpreted as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\underbrace{\text{Adam moment estimation}}_{\text{gradient history}}\quad+\quad\underbrace{\text{finite-horizon scaling}}_{\text{remaining-budget control}}.
``` |  | (113) |

### D.8 Transactional Invalid-State Protection

Scientific optimization can enter numerically unstable regions in which
the simulator returns a non-finite loss, a non-finite gradient, or an
invalid parameter proposal. Updating the optimizer state after such an
event can contaminate the moment estimates even if the parameter vector
itself is later restored.

ADAPT-Adam therefore treats one optimizer iteration as a transactional
state transition.

Define the committed optimizer state

|     |                                                       |     |       |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{Z}_{t}=\left(u_{t},m_{t},v_{t},n_{t}\right),
       ```                                                    |     | (114) |

where $`n_{t}`$ is the number of accepted updates used for bias
correction.

From $`\mathcal{Z}_{t}`$, the optimizer first evaluates the scientific
objective. If

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\neg\operatorname{finite}(L_{t})\qquad\text{or}\qquad\neg\operatorname{finite}(g_{t}),
``` |  | (115) |

then the last valid evaluated parameters $`u_{\mathrm{last}}`$ are
restored without updating the moment states or accepted-update counter:

|     |                                                          |     |       |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{Z}_{t+1}=(u_{\mathrm{last}},m_{t},v_{t},n_{t}).
       ```                                                       |     | (116) |

If the evaluation is finite, set $`u_{\mathrm{last}}=u_{t}`$ and
construct temporary moment states

|     |                              |     |       |
|-----|------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       m^{\prime},\qquad v^{\prime}
       ```                           |     | (117) |

and a candidate parameter vector

|     |                                     |     |       |
|-----|-------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       u^{\prime}=u_{t}+s(r_{t})\Delta_{t}
       ```                                  |     | (118) |

are constructed.

The candidate transition is committed only if

|     |                                    |     |       |
|-----|------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \operatorname{finite}(u^{\prime}).
       ```                                 |     | (119) |

Otherwise,

|     |                                                              |     |       |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       (u_{t+1},m_{t+1},v_{t+1},n_{t+1})=(u_{t},m_{t},v_{t},n_{t}).
       ```                                                           |     | (120) |

This differs from a procedure that first updates Adam’s moments and then
merely restores the parameters. In ADAPT-Adam, rejected iterations do
not contaminate the committed optimizer state.

### D.9 Accepted-Update Counter and Bias Correction

Because invalid proposals leave the committed optimizer state unchanged,
the Adam step counter is incremented only when an update is accepted.

Let $`n_{t}`$ denote the number of committed updates before the current
proposal. For a valid proposed update,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle m^{\prime}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\beta_{1}m_{t}+(1-\beta_{1})\bar{g}_{t},`$ |  | (121) |
|  | $`\displaystyle v^{\prime}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\beta_{2}v_{t}+(1-\beta_{2})\bar{g}_{t}^{2},`$ |  | (122) |

and the temporary accepted-update index is

|     |                     |     |       |
|-----|---------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       n^{\prime}=n_{t}+1.
       ```                  |     | (123) |

Bias correction is then computed using $`n^{\prime}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{m}^{\prime}=\frac{m^{\prime}}{1-\beta_{1}^{n^{\prime}}},\qquad\widehat{v}^{\prime}=\frac{v^{\prime}}{1-\beta_{2}^{n^{\prime}}}.
``` |  | (124) |

Only after the complete proposal passes the validity checks do we commit

|     |                                                               |     |       |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       (m_{t+1},v_{t+1},n_{t+1})=(m^{\prime},v^{\prime},n^{\prime}).
       ```                                                            |     | (125) |

This convention keeps the bias-correction counter consistent with the
sequence of states that actually affect the scientific trajectory.

The final optimizer starts from the parameter vector selected in

|     |                                                            |     |       |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       u_{0}=\operatorname{logit}\left(x_{0,\tau}^{\star}\right),
       ```                                                         |     | (126) |

where the notation abbreviates the bounded-to-unbounded transformation
in

The handoff transfers the selected detector parameters but does not
implicitly transfer the moment state of the screening optimizer.
Therefore,

|     |                                        |     |       |
|-----|----------------------------------------|-----|-------|

in general because the Selection Evolver has already consumed part of

### D.11 Best-Feasible Incumbent Tracking

The current optimizer state and the best reported scientific design are
maintained separately.

Let

|     |                                                   |     |       |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{E}_{t}=\left\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{t}\right\}
       ```                                                |     | (130) |

denote all completed evaluations admitted before the deadline. The
reported incumbent is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{\mathrm{inc}}(t)=\arg\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{E}_{t}\\
x\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}\end{subarray}}L_{\tau}(x).
``` |  | (131) |

Thus, the final output is not necessarily the final parameter state
$`u_{T}`$. It is the best physically admissible evaluated design
observed before the shared deadline:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{\mathrm{out}}=x_{\mathrm{inc}}\left(T_{\mathrm{global}}\right).
``` |  | (132) |

An unevaluated proposed update cannot become the reported result.

### D.12 Deadline Semantics

ADAPT-Adam operates under the same end-to-end deadline as the Selection
Evolver. An evaluation is admitted only if it completes before the
global deadline.

Let

|     |                        |     |       |
|-----|------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       t_{\mathrm{finish}}(e)
       ```                     |     | (133) |

denote the completion time of evaluation $`e`$. Its result is eligible
for scientific comparison only if

|     |                                                 |     |       |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       t_{\mathrm{finish}}(e)\leq T_{\mathrm{global}}.
       ```                                              |     | (134) |

This convention prevents a method from initiating an expensive
evaluation immediately before the deadline and reporting its result
after the declared budget has expired.

The finite-horizon scale in
Equation equation [105](#A4.E105 "In D.6 Finite-Horizon Update Scaling ‣ Appendix D Optimizer Evolution: Learning How to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
uses the remaining time available to the final optimizer, but all
evaluations remain subject to the original pipeline-level deadline.

### D.16 Separation Between Optimizer Generator and Evolved Optimizer

A distinction is required between the mechanism that *generates* an
optimizer and the optimizer that is ultimately *executed*.

During development, an agentic evolution workflow proposes source-code
modifications from execution feedback. Once a candidate program is
generated, however, the language model does not participate in
individual gradient updates. The scientific trajectory is produced
entirely by execution of the resulting numerical optimizer against the
differentiable simulator.

We therefore distinguish

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\underbrace{\text{optimizer evolution}}_{\text{meta-level program search}}\qquad\text{from}\qquad\underbrace{\text{ADAPT-Adam}}_{\text{final numerical optimizer}}.
``` |  | (146) |

This distinction is also the reason for replacing the internal
development identifier GEPA_GPT_4 with the algorithmic name ADAPT-Adam
in the paper. The internal identifier records one provenance path
through the optimizer-evolution process; it is not the mathematical
definition of the resulting optimizer.

### D.17 What ADAPT-Adam Does Not Change

ADAPT-Adam does not modify the scientific problem being evaluated.
Throughout optimizer evolution and final validation, the following
quantities remain fixed:

- •
  the detector topology supplied to the run;
- •
  the differentiable physics simulator;
- •
  the frequency grid;
- •
  the native sensitivity objective;
- •
  the physical parameter bounds;
- •
  the optical-power feasibility constraints;
- •
  the definition of a completed scientific evaluation; and
- •
  the end-to-end held-out evaluation budget.

The optimizer is therefore permitted to change *how candidate parameter
vectors are proposed*, but not *how those candidates are scientifically
judged*.

### D.18 Held-Out Execution Boundary

As with the Selection Evolver, optimizer evolution is separated from
held-out validation.

The program-development loop in
Algorithm [5](#alg5 "Algorithm 5 ‣ D.15 Meta-Level Optimizer Evolution ‣ Appendix D Optimizer Evolution: Learning How to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
produces a frozen executable optimizer. During held-out evaluation,
ADAPT executes
Algorithm [4](#alg4 "Algorithm 4 ‣ D.14 ADAPT-Adam Pseudocode ‣ Appendix D Optimizer Evolution: Learning How to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
from the initialization returned by the frozen selection policy.

For a held-out detector topology, the optimizer observes only quantities
that are naturally available during scientific optimization, such as
current loss, gradient, physical feasibility, and remaining budget. The
final held-out performance is used for evaluation and reporting only.

In particular, held-out final losses are not returned to the optimizer
generator to produce a revised version of ADAPT-Adam. This maintains the
separation between development-time algorithm evolution and final
evaluation.

## Appendix F Experimental Protocol and Compute Accounting

This appendix summarizes the evaluation protocol and computational
budget used throughout the study. No additional experiments are
introduced here; all quantitative results correspond to the experiments
reported in the main paper.

ADAPT separates *method development* from *held-out scientific
validation*. Candidate selection and optimization procedures are evolved
using development targets, after which the resulting procedures are
frozen. The eight held-out detector topologies are used only for final
evaluation.

### F.1 Development and Held-Out Evaluation Regimes

The two stages operate under distinct computational regimes.

During development of the Selection Evolver, candidate selection
procedures are evaluated on the development panel with a maximum
wall-clock budget of

|     |                               |     |       |
|-----|-------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       B_{\mathrm{dev}}=2~\mathrm{h}
       ```                            |     | (147) |

per target on an NVIDIA RTX 4090.

The final frozen ADAPT pipeline is evaluated on eight held-out detector
topologies. Each held-out target receives

|     |                                |     |       |
|-----|--------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       B_{\mathrm{test}}=4~\mathrm{h}
       ```                             |     | (148) |

on one NVIDIA H100 GPU.

The two regimes are intentionally kept separate. In particular, H100
validation outcomes are not returned to either the Selection Evolver or
the Optimizer Evolver and therefore cannot influence algorithm
development.

The distinction is summarized in
Table [5](#A6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ F.1 Development and Held-Out Evaluation Regimes ‣ Appendix F Experimental Protocol and Compute Accounting ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

| Stage | Hardware | Budget | Used for evolution? |
|----|----|----|----|
| Selection development | RTX 4090 | up to 2 h / target | Yes |
| Optimizer development | Development hardware | fixed evaluator budget | Yes |
| Held-out validation | NVIDIA H100 | 4 h / target | No |

Table 5: Development and held-out evaluation regimes. Development
feedback is obtained only from the development panel. The H100
evaluation is performed only after the final algorithms have been
frozen.

### F.2 Held-Out Validation Compute

The held-out test set contains eight detector topologies. Since each
target receives a four-hour budget on one H100 GPU, the final ADAPT
validation requires

|     |                                                           |     |       |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       C_{\mathrm{H100}}=8\times 4=32\quad\text{H100 GPU-hours}.
       ```                                                        |     | (149) |

Thus, the final held-out validation reported for ADAPT corresponds to

|     |                                   |     |       |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \boxed{32~\text{H100 GPU-hours}}.
       ```                                |     | (150) |

When the eight targets are executed concurrently on eight GPUs, this
corresponds to approximately four hours of validator wall-clock time,
excluding scheduling and launch overhead. GPU-hours, rather than
parallel wall-clock time, are used for compute accounting.

### F.3 Development Compute Accounting

For the Selection Evolver, one complete evaluation of a candidate
selection procedure over the eight-target development panel, when all
targets consume their full two-hour budget, corresponds to at most

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{\mathrm{sel,eval}}=8\times 2=16\quad\text{RTX 4090 GPU-hours}.
``` |  | (151) |

The top-level selection-development pool contains

|     |                      |     |       |
|-----|----------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       4\times 2\times 2=16
       ```                   |     | (152) |

workflow–model–reference configurations, as described in
Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Agentic Workflow Pool ‣ Appendix C Selection Evolution: Learning Where to Search ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization").

A full single sweep over these configurations would therefore have a
nominal upper-bound allocation of

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{\mathrm{sel,sweep}}=16\times 16=256\quad\text{RTX 4090 GPU-hours},
``` |  | (153) |

if every configuration used the complete target-level budget.

Equation equation [153](#A6.E153 "In F.3 Development Compute Accounting ‣ Appendix F Experimental Protocol and Compute Accounting ‣ ADAPT for Science: A Multi-Agent System for Self-Evolving Scientific Optimization")
is a budget-accounting quantity rather than a measured runtime:
individual executions can terminate before their maximum budget, and
optimizer-evolution rounds constitute additional development
computation.

We therefore report H100 and RTX 4090 compute separately rather than
converting them into a single “equivalent GPU-hour” quantity. Such a
conversion would require assumptions about device-dependent simulator
throughput.

### F.4 Single-Clock Evaluation

All comparisons use matched random seeds, hardware, numerical precision,
and the same total wall-clock budget. For ADAPT, this budget covers both
warmup (candidate screening and staged probing) and subsequent
optimization; no additional time is allocated for warmup.

For selection, the charged time includes retrieval-side screening,
permitted probes, continuation of candidate trajectories, scheduler
overhead, and the remaining optimization trajectory. Time spent deciding
where to search is therefore unavailable to the final selected branch.

The final optimizer uses only the time remaining under the original
shared deadline; starting its logging does not reset the scientific
budget. An evaluation is eligible for reporting only if it is completed
and admitted before that deadline.

This convention prevents a method from obtaining additional scientific
evaluations through preprocessing, branch switching, asynchronous
execution, or post-deadline completion.

### F.5 Held-Out Isolation

The eight held-out topologies are excluded from strategy development.
Their final optimization outcomes are not used for

- •
  selection-policy evolution;
- •
  optimizer-program evolution;
- •
  language-model selection;
- •
  prompt revision;
- •
  hyperparameter selection; or
- •
  subsequent revision of ADAPT-Adam.

The target topology itself is necessarily available at test time because
it defines the scientific optimization problem. However, its held-out
solution and final validation outcome are not provided as development
information.

The historical retrieval procedure also excludes the target itself when
constructing its 64-design candidate pool.

### F.6 What Is and Is Not Trained

ADAPT does not train a neural surrogate of the detector simulator for
the reported optimization results.

The computationally learned objects are instead the *search procedures*:
the Selection Evolver develops a target-selection policy and the
Optimizer Evolver develops an executable numerical optimization
algorithm.

Once these procedures are frozen, held-out detector optimization
consists of direct execution against the differentiable physics
simulator. No language model is fine-tuned during the held-out runs, and
no online LLM inference is required by the final numerical optimizer.

All final results use the best physically feasible point that was
actually evaluated and admitted before the corresponding deadline.

|  |  |  |  |

|  |
``` math
L_{\tau}^{\mathrm{reported}}=\min_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau}(B)\cap\mathcal{F}_{\tau}}L_{\tau}(x).
``` |  | (156) |

An unevaluated post-update vector, predicted surrogate value, or result
completed after the budget expires is not eligible for reporting.

refer to variation across the eight held-out detector topologies.

- •

### I.5 Scope and Limitations

The present evaluation establishes ADAPT in one scientific domain:
differentiable optimization of gravitational-wave detector designs with
reusable historical experience and explicit physical constraints.

Several limitations should therefore be kept in mind.

First, the effectiveness of target-aware retrieval depends on the
coverage and quality of the historical experience library. Its benefit
may decrease for targets that are far outside the observed design
distribution.

Second, evolving both initialization and optimization procedures
introduces development-time computational overhead relative to applying
a single fixed optimizer.

Third, the present validator is simulation-based. Although the simulator
provides a controlled differentiable scientific environment, the current
results do not establish performance under real experimental noise,
hardware imperfections, or non-differentiable laboratory feedback.

Finally, ADAPT-Adam is an empirically discovered finite-budget
optimizer. The present work does not claim a global convergence
guarantee for the evolved update rule.

These limitations define the scope of the reported evidence rather than
additional unreported experiments.
````

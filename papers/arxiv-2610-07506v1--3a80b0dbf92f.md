###### Abstract.

Speech agents are reactive and dyadic: they speak when spoken to, and to
one person at a time. We ask what it takes for a speech agent to instead
take part in a conversation among several people and speak up only when
it can help. We introduce Jarvis, a real-time proactive speech agent
that audibly participates in multi-party human conversations. Grounded
in a document shared beforehand, Jarvis follows the discussion and
intervenes when the group misses or misstates a fact and does not
correct itself within a few turns. We make three contributions: a
problem setting based on epistemic breakdowns that makes proactive
intervention measurable, realized as CHI-180-proactive, a synthetic
multi-party dataset seeded with known gaps, errors, and
self-corrections; a proactive backbone that harnesses a small,
open-weight model with deterministic checks and grounds every claim in a
source sentence; and interaction techniques for taking the floor in live
speech and showing the cited evidence on screen. On CHI-180-proactive,
Jarvis is correct on most events it addresses and stays silent 97% of
the time when the group resolves an issue itself. A live study with 23
participants confirms these trends with real-time interventions.

## 1. Introduction

Imagine a meeting where everyone is working with a 15-page contract, but
nobody remembers every detail. Someone asks what the contract says about
data retention. Nobody knows, so the question gets pushed to the next
meeting. Later, someone misstates the sample size, and the discussion
starts building on the error. Now imagine an agent that has read the
contract, answers questions on the spot, and listens to the
conversation. When someone misses or misstates a fact, it first gives
the other participants a chance to catch and correct it. If they do not,
it speaks up. Such a proactive speech agent could be useful in lab
meetings, diligence calls, design reviews.

Today’s speech agents cannot play this role for three key reasons.
First, they are primarily reactive: from smart speakers to LLM voice
modes, they speak only when addressed, e.g., “Hey Alexa.” But some of
the most useful moments for help are ones no one thinks to ask for, such
as when a fact has been misstated without anyone realizing it. Second,
they are designed for dyadic interaction ([Défossez et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15); [Veluri et al., 2024](#bib.bib72)): their
turn-taking, endpointing, and barge-in mechanisms assume a single user
is addressing the agent, so an utterance is generally treated as a turn
that warrants a response. A human-conversation agent must work
differently. It is listening, but without being explicitly addressed, it
must decide when the conversation needs intervention, give participants
a chance to correct themselves, and then enter an ongoing conversation
without derailing it. Third, it must remain in the background and keep
humans at the center, speaking only when necessary rather than competing
for attention. Neither reactive assistants, dyadic dialogue systems, nor
in-ear whispering assistants ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Zulfikar
et al., 2024](#bib.bib89)) provide the interaction model or evaluation
framework needed for this setting.

We introduce Jarvis, to our knowledge, the first real-time proactive
speech agent that audibly participates in multi-party human
conversation. Designing and evaluating this requires addressing three
core research questions:

- •
  RQ1: Should the proactive agent speak, and what should it say? At each
  turn, the agent must decide whether to say anything and, if so, what.
  Unlike a reactive assistant where humans ask for explicit assistance,
  the multi-party conversation can continue without any intervention
  from the proactive speech agent. So the agent has to identify the rare
  moments when its intervention actually helps and is not considered a
  distraction by the human participants.
- •
  RQ2: When and how should it take/hold the floor? Unlike text-based
  proactive agents, where a chat message can be skimmed, deferred, or
  ignored, a spoken intervention consumes shared airtime and displaces
  whoever might speak next. The agent can neither cut across someone
  mid-sentence nor sit on an important point until the moment has
  passed. And if a participant does interrupt it, the agent must decide
  whether to keep the floor or yield, and what to do with the
  intervention it was partway through delivering.
- •
  RQ3: How do we measure whether proactive intervention worked?
  Proactive behavior has been difficult to evaluate because open-ended
  conversation has no answer key. Existing human-conversation datasets
  do not fill this gap: AI speech agents should be secondary
  participants in human conversations, taking a back seat to the humans,
  yet no datasets capture this setting. Without a well-defined problem
  setting, whether an interjection was correct, well-timed, or needed at
  all becomes a matter of opinion. We therefore need problem settings
  and datasets in which both correctness and timing are objectively
  decidable, allowing us to evaluate what an agent says and when it says
  it against something firmer than opinion.

Our paper addresses these questions and makes the following conceptual
and technical contributions.

- •
  Multi-party setting and dataset that make proactive intervention
  measurable. Proactive behavior is hard to evaluate because open
  conversation has no answer key. To make proactive intervention
  decidable by construction, we target _epistemic_ breakdowns rather
  than social ones. Specifically, Jarvis identifies moments where the
  multi-party group is missing or misstating something checkable. To do
  this, we ground each conversation in a shared document (e.g.,
  financial report or academic paper) that is shared apriori with Jarvis
  before the start of the conversation. We also introduce
  CHI-180-proactive, a dataset created from 180 CHI papers, each paired
  with a synthesized multi-party discussion into which we plant 24 known
  events like knowledge gaps, errors, self-corrections, and distractors.
  In total the dataset has 4,320 events over 50,400 turns, each
  annotated with a fixed correct action. This turns proactive
  intervention into a detection task with measurable accuracy and
  timing, and we will release it as a research benchmark for proactive
  conversational agents.
- •
  A deterministic harness of small models that decides whether to speak
  and what to say. We harness a small, open-weight language model
  through several stages with deterministic checks, so the whole system
  can be self-hosted and no single model output is trusted on its own.
  At each turn one model classifies whether the discussion is missing or
  misstating something the document can settle and another checks
  whether the participants have already corrected it. A deterministic
  gate reconfirms and speaks only once a problem has persisted, so the
  agent defers to human self-repair before intervening. Every spoken
  claim is grounded in a document sentence rather than the model’s own
  knowledge
  (§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")).
  Our evaluation shows that in this document-grounded setting our
  harness of small models is enough and is fast enough to run in real
  time.
- •
  Interaction techniques for taking and holding the floor. Our goal is
  for Jarvis to join a live conversation like a considerate participant.
  To achieve this, we explore three interaction methods. First, rather
  than speaking as soon as it has something to say, it queues the
  intervention and waits for an opening; if someone begins talking while
  it is speaking, it stops at once and decides whether to replay,
  resume, or drop what it was saying. Second, for groups that want
  tighter control, it instead raises its hand and speaks only once
  someone verbally gives it the floor. Third, alongside the spoken
  answer it shows visual evidence, placing the cited source on screen so
  participants can verify a claim rather than take it on the agent’s
  word
  (see §[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3. Interaction Module ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")).

Rather than build a Wizard-of-Oz prototype, we address the underlying
systems and latency challenges, deploy a proof-of-concept Jarvis on Zoom
as an additional participant, and run it live in multi-party
conversations.

Our key findings are as follows:

1.  (1)
    Our Qwen3.6-35B-A3B harness, with 3B active parameters, is competent
    in our document-grounded setting: on CHI-180-proactive, Jarvis
    provided correct or partially correct information with 95-97%
    accuracy.
2.  (2)
    Jarvis defers to the group and intervenes when needed. After
    participants self-correct ($`N{=}720`$) or answer their own question
    ($`N{=}720`$), it stays silent in 97% of cases in both. When there
    is an information gap ($`N{=}1800`$) or a document-specific
    incorrect statement ($`N{=}720`$), it intervenes in 91% and 77% of
    cases; the latter is lower because many incorrect statements import
    a fact from another document, which Jarvis cannot verify against its
    own source and hence stays silent. When it does intervene, it acts
    within 2-3 turns of the event in 98% of cases.
3.  (3)
    In live Zoom sessions with 23 participants, where Jarvis must also
    cope with transcription errors from the speech pipeline, these
    results carry over to real people: 85% of its interventions arrived
    within two turns of the problem, and 163 of 164 carried correct or
    partially correct information. Together, Jarvis and the participants
    resolved 74% of planted errors, 72% of spontaneous errors, and 83%
    of information gaps.
4.  (4)
    When asked to choose, participants preferred an agent that raises
    its hand before speaking to one that speaks directly, and
    overwhelmingly preferred having the cited source shown on screen (21
    of 23), which let them check claims and trust the answers.

Taking a step back, we show that real-time proactive participation in
spoken conversation can be designed and carefully measured against
ground truth, establishing a research foundation for future proactive
speech agents.

## 2. Related Work

Proactive Agents and the When-to-Speak Problem. Mixed-initiative
systems ([Horvitz, 1999](#bib.bib21)) and just-in-time agents such as
the Remembrance Agent surface relevant information from observed
activity ([Rhodes and Starner, 1996](#bib.bib65)), but deliver it on
screen rather than into the conversation. Interruption research showed
that poorly timed interventions carry cognitive costs ([Mark et al.,
2008](#bib.bib46); [Iqbal and Bailey, 2008](#bib.bib30)) and developed
models for predicting interruptibility and deferring
notifications ([Horvitz and Apacible, 2003](#bib.bib22); [Horvitz
et al., 2004](#bib.bib24); [Horvitz et al., 2005](#bib.bib23)); these
focus on individual notifications, not spoken contributions. More recent
work on proactive voice assistants finds a similar “proactivity dilemma”
in balancing helpfulness and intrusiveness, particularly in multi-party
settings ([Zargham et al., 2022](#bib.bib81); [Oh et al.,
2024](#bib.bib53)), but remains largely formative and Wizard-of-Oz,
studying user attitudes rather than autonomous spoken intervention.

LLMs have turned deciding whether, when, and what to contribute into a
modeling problem ([Deng et al., 2023](#bib.bib12); [Deng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib79)). Task agents infer
needs from desktop or mobile activity ([Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib44);
[Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib71); [Pasternak et al., 2026](#bib.bib56)),
ContextAgent from egocentric sensors ([Yang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib78)),
and CollabLLM takes initiative during dialogue ([Wu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib74)); all, however, assist a single user rather than
compete with humans for the floor. Inner Thoughts scores covert thoughts
to select moments to contribute in multi-party text chat ([Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib41)), DiscussLLM predicts whether to respond or stay
silent each turn ([Patel et al., 2026](#bib.bib57)), and ProMediate
selects intervention moments in multi-party negotiation ([Liu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib43)). These systems operate in turn-based text, where the
platform effectively gives the agent its turn, and none grounds
intervention in whether the group is missing or misstating a verifiable
fact. ProVoice-Bench moves to audio ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib76)), but
tests reactions to predefined sounds rather than the content of an
unfolding discussion. SocialMind displays suggestions on AR
glasses ([Yang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib77)), while LlamaPIE whispers
short cues to one wearer ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)). Both provide
private cues invisible to the group, rather than speaking into the
conversation itself.

Our work builds on this prior work, but shared speech introduces
constraints that text does not. A chat message, AR overlay, or private
whisper can be skimmed, deferred, or ignored while the conversation
continues. In contrast, a spoken intervention consumes shared airtime
and displaces whoever might speak next.

Turn-Taking, Interruption, and Full-Duplex Spoken Dialogue. Conversation
analysis shows that turn-taking is tightly organized ([Sacks et al.,
1974](#bib.bib67); [Levinson and Torreira, 2015](#bib.bib37)), with
self-repair preferred over other-repair ([Schegloff et al.,
1977](#bib.bib68)). We operationalize it by making the agent a
deliberately _dispreferred other-repairer_ that waits through the
self-repair window. Computational models predict when a transition is
possible ([Ekstedt and Skantze, 2020](#bib.bib16); [Ekstedt and Skantze,
2022](#bib.bib17); [Inoue et al., 2024](#bib.bib29)), not whether the
agent should speak. Concurrent work extends turn-taking to multi-party
speech under floor competition ([Mitra et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)), and
full-duplex dyadic models listen and speak at once ([Veluri et al.,
2024](#bib.bib72); [Défossez et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7); [Lin et al., 2022](#bib.bib39)), but both predict
floor availability, when a role may take a turn, assuming each utterance
is addressed to the agent. Whle provide the mechanics for how to enter,
including VAD-gated delivery and barge-in yielding, but not whether to
enter in multi-party settings, which requires content-level evidence
that the group is missing or misstating something and has not already
corrected it.

Agents in Meetings and Group Conversations. Meetings have long been used
to study conversation understanding, from multi-party corpora ([Carletta
et al., 2005](#bib.bib5); [Janin et al., 2003](#bib.bib31)) to meeting
summarization and assistant benchmarks ([Zhong et al.,
2021](#bib.bib86); [Hu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib28)) and LLM delegates
that attend on a user’s behalf ([Hu et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)). These
systems either process the meeting afterward or respond to queries; they
do not decide on their own that something said was wrong. Other work
puts agents into group conversations as moderators, facilitators, or
devil’s advocates ([Kim et al., 2020](#bib.bib33); [Kim et al.,
2021](#bib.bib34); [Mao et al., 2024](#bib.bib45); [Chiang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9); [Alsobay et al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Parisi et al.,
2026](#bib.bib55)), but in _text_, and mainly to shape participation or
opinions. Recent studies examine when agents should participate in
groups ([Houde et al., 2025](#bib.bib25); [Yoo, 2026](#bib.bib80); [Zhou
et al., 2026](#bib.bib87)), highlighting the same tradeoff we face:
speaking can disrupt, while silence can miss an opportunity to help. But
these systems are formative or user-controlled, rather than autonomous
spoken agents, and their open-ended settings provide no ground truth.

Grounded Generation and Verifiable Attribution. Retrieval-augmented
generation grounds LLM outputs in sources ([Lewis et al.,
2020](#bib.bib38)), while attributed generation links claims to
passages ([Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib18); [Menick et al.,
2022](#bib.bib47); [Xia et al., 2025](#bib.bib75); [Zhang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib84)) and verification pipelines check whether evidence
supports those claims ([Min et al., 2023](#bib.bib48); [Qian et al.,
2025](#bib.bib58); [Guo et al., 2022](#bib.bib19)). These methods
largely assume a _written_ answer to an _explicit_ question, checked
offline by the person who asked.

Evaluating Proactive Conversational Behavior. Proactive-agent benchmarks
measure task anticipation ([Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib71); [Pasternak
et al., 2026](#bib.bib56); [Liu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib40)), but focus
on desktops and datastores rather than conversation. LLM-judged
testbeds ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib73)) scale evaluation while user
studies measure helpfulness and intrusiveness ([Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6); [Yang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib77); [Zargham et al.,
2022](#bib.bib81)), capturing how interventions felt. ProMediate is
closest to our setting, evaluating intervention timing in multi-party
negotiation ([Liu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib43)), but uses turn-based text
with LLM-simulated negotiators. The simulation gives the mediator its
turn, so claiming the floor is not an issue, and a negotiation problem
setting has no objective answer key for judging an intervention. Our
work targets _epistemic_ rather than social breakdowns. We seed
controlled documents with known facts, predictable gaps, and
distractors, turning proactive intervention into a detection task with
measurable accuracy and timing error.

As shown in

Jarvis has three modules, each handling a different part of the
interaction: who a turn is from, whether there is something worth saying
and why, and when and how to say it. The Speech Processing module
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1. Speech Processing Module ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
converts live meeting audio into timestamped, speaker-attributed text
for each turn, detects the wake word, and plays the agent’s responses
back into the meeting. The Proactive Backbone model
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
takes the document shared before the meeting and, for each turn from the
Speech Processing module, decides whether the agent should contribute
and, if so, grounds the response in an exact passage. The Interaction
module
(§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3. Interaction Module ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
manages delivery: it waits for an opening rather than talking over the
group, yields when someone speaks, and, depending on the mode, either
raises a hand for the group to call on or displays the cited page so
participants can verify the claim.

### 3.1. Speech Processing Module

It provides three interfaces. First, to the proactive backbone, it sends
timestamped, speaker-attributed turns represented as
$`\langle\mathrm{speaker},\mathrm{text},\mathrm{start\ time},\mathrm{end\ time}\rangle`$,
tagged as either _direct_ (addressed to the agent) or _stream_
(participant-to-participant talk). Second, to the interaction module, it
sends pre-lexical acoustic events such as speech onset, sustained
speech, and silence. Finally, it synthesizes approved responses from the
proactive backbone agent and plays them back into the meeting as speech.
This is implemented via five sub-components, details of which are
in §[A](#A1 "Appendix A Speech Processing Module: Implementation Details ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations").

### 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models

Rather than rely on a single model, we design a deterministic harness
that break the task into multiple steps. Each step uses the same
language model for the part that requires language understanding and
deterministic logic to check its output. This prevents any single model
decision from triggering an intervention on its own. The backbone has
four stages (a monitor, a self-repair check, a grounded generator, and a
direct answerer) and a deterministic gate
(Fig. [3](#acmlabel3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")(b)).
All stages use the same document, which we split into numbered sentences
(§[3.2.1](#S3.SS2.SSS1 "3.2.1. Shared Document Representation ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")).
Every stage refers to the document only via sentence IDs.

Each incoming turn other than backchannels (“yeah,” “uh-huh”) is first
appended to the transcript. Two model stages run in parallel. The
_monitor_
(§[3.2.2](#S3.SS2.SSS2 "3.2.2. Monitor ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
classifies the discussion as either missing or misstating something the
document can resolve, or as ordinary conversation that needs no
intervention. The _self-repair check_
(§[3.2.3](#S3.SS2.SSS3 "3.2.3. Self-Repair Check ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
looks at the recent conversation and asks whether the participants have
already fixed a problem that the monitor flagged on an earlier turn. A
deterministic test confirms that the correct value actually appears in
the recent turns before clearing the problem.

The two outputs feed a deterministic _gate_
(§[3.2.4](#S3.SS2.SSS4 "3.2.4. Claim Ledger and Gate ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")),
which decides whether Jarvis should speak. The gate keeps a ledger of
unresolved problems and intervenes only when a problem persists across
turns without being resolved. This gives the group a chance to catch and
correct its own mistakes before Jarvis steps in. When the gate fires,
_grounded generation_
(§[3.2.5](#S3.SS2.SSS5 "3.2.5. Grounded Generation ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
produces the response. It takes the identified problem and the specific
document sentences that support the response as input, and generates the
response grounded in the document; a response that cannot be tied to a
document sentence is not spoken. A separate _direct answering_ stage
(§[B](#A2 "Appendix B Monitor Module: Answering Direct Questions Based on Context ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
handles questions explicitly addressed to Jarvis by wake word. These
questions bypass the monitor and gate and receive a document-grounded
answer.

#### 3.2.1. Shared Document Representation

We take the full document text, strip markdown headings and figure
apparatus, and split it into sentences on “.!?”, dropping fragments
shorter than five tokens. Each surviving sentence is given an integer ID
and listed as a numbered block: \[S0\] sentence 0, \[S1\] sentence 1,
…,\[Sn\] sentence n. This block is assembled once and prepended to every
stage’s system message, so all stages index the document by the same
IDs.

A stage that finds a relevant fact reports the ID of the sentence it
came from, and not the text. The server resolves each ID back to its
verbatim sentence and drops any ID it cannot resolve. A model can
therefore point at the document but does not paraphrase it. This design
ensures that the citation is more trustworthy since the spoken claim is
backed by source text the system retrieved, not by text the model
produced. We cap the IDs at three per report for the monitor and the
self-repair check. Sharing one representation also has a practical
benefit. Because all four system messages begin with the identical
document block, the serving engine prefills it once per turn and reuses
the cache across stages; only the shorter policy text that follows
differs between stages.

#### 3.2.2. Monitor

The monitor performs three tasks at each turn: it classifies the
discussion’s status, extracts a _claim_ naming the point under
discussion, and, if the status is actionable, retrieves the related
document sentence IDs. It runs at a low temperature (0.1). Detection,
claim identification, and grounding are deliberately fused in one
component, which is what lets the monitor output a checkable judgment
(“this contradicts \[S7\]”) rather than an opinion (“something seems
wrong”). See
Table [9](#Ax1.T9 "Table 9 ‣ Monitor ‣ Prompts of the Proactive Backbone ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")
for the full prompt.

Conversation status taxonomy. The monitor assigns each new turn to one
of six statuses.

- •
  _Incorrect fact_, where a participant asserts something the document
  contradicts.
- •
  _Missing fact_, where the participants need a specific fact the
  document states but no one has supplied.
- •
  _Misattribution_, where a participant attributes a claim to the
  document (“in this study…”, “they found…”) that the document does not
  support.
- •
  _On track_, accurate and consistent with the text.
- •
  _Off topic_, concerning logistics, tooling, or side chat that asserts
  nothing the document could contradict.
- •
  _Unanswerable from the document_, where participants are stuck or
  unsure about a point, the document is about that topic, but does not
  actually state the specific detail they need.

The first three statuses are actionable, meaning that they name a
problem the agent could intervene on, and the ledger tracks them. The
other three call for no intervention and open no problem.

We distinguish _misattribution_ from being merely _off topic_. This
matters because a mention of another document warrants silence, whereas
a false claim about _this_ document does not. Similarly, we distinguish
a _missing fact_ from an _unanswerable_ question. This allows the
monitor to report a missing fact only when it can point to the exact
phrase in the text that answers the specific question. If the document
covers the same topic but does not state that particular detail, the
status is _unanswerable_, and the agent has nothing grounded to add.
While the monitor uses the recent turns as context, the status is output
for each new utterance.

Claim identification. The monitor also outputs a _claim_: a short noun
phrase naming the point under discussion, independent of the answer or
participants’ wording (e.g., “the dataset size used for training,” not
“recruiting 24 participants through university mailing lists”). This
normalizes different surface forms (“24 seems small,” “how many did they
recruit?”) to one claim, and the monitor reuses it as long as the
discussion stays on that point, regardless of status: if the group first
asks about $`X`$ and then says something wrong about $`X`$, both turns
map to $`X`$. This canonical form is what lets the backbone track a
problem across turns and decide when it has persisted
(§[3.2.4](#S3.SS2.SSS4 "3.2.4. Claim Ledger and Gate ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")).
The monitor also returns the _evidence_, the participant’s words that
triggered the status.

Retrieving sentence ID. Finally, if the status is actionable, the
monitor returns the answer along with its source: the actual text/value
pulled from the document that the group got wrong, copied verbatim, and
the IDs of the sentences it came from (at most three). Where the value
sits in a table or figure and no sentence can be cited, it returns the
value with no IDs rather than a fabricated one. These IDs serve as a
fallback for the generation stage
(§[3.2.5](#S3.SS2.SSS5 "3.2.5. Grounded Generation ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
if its own citation cannot be resolved; if neither yields a resolvable
sentence, the correction is not spoken.

#### 3.2.3. Self-Repair Check

For each problem, this language model asks: “Has this problem since been
fixed by the participants themselves?” It runs at a low temperature of
0.1.

Given the document, the list of open claims and the same recent
transcript the monitor reads, the self-repair model checks each open
claim in turn. It first locates the sentence stating the correct fact.
It then applies the following rule: a claim is marked resolved if the
transcript has _stated_ that correct fact. Our prompt is explicit about
what does not count. For example, repeating the same wrong value, merely
mentioning the topic again, or a participant admitting they cannot
recall do not count as resolution. We only consider the recent
transcript, and the model outputs the claim IDs it judges resolved,
citing the grounding sentence IDs for each.

Filtering the model’s checks. Each proposed resolution passes through a
deterministic guard that rechecks the transcript before closing a claim.
The guard closes a claim only if the correct value appears in the last
three turns, with at least 60% of its content tokens present (question
words removed, numbers matching exactly). It does not accept a question,
nor an utterance that reports failure to resolve (“I can’t recall”), and
the closing utterance must add words absent from the turns that first
raised the claim. A misattribution has no document value to verify;
there the guard instead accepts a retraction (“that’s not from this
paper”), and for any other valueless claim it lets the model’s judgment
stand. The model and the guard are complementary: the model captures
meaning a string match cannot, such as that “two dozen” answers “how
many participants,” while the guard checks that the value was actually
stated, and stated recently. The guard sees only the last three turns;
the model reads the full window.

#### 3.2.4. Claim Ledger and Gate

Neither the monitor nor the self-repair check decides whether Jarvis
speaks; that decision is made by our gate, which maintains a claim
ledger.

Each turn, the monitor may report a claim, and we check whether it
matches one already open. We compare claims by sentence embedding
(all-MiniLM-L6-v2 ([Reimers and Gurevych, 2019](#bib.bib64))) and treat
them as the same problem when cosine similarity is at least $`\sigma`$.
When an actionable claim matches an open one, we increment its
_persistence count_, the number of consecutive detections without an
intervening turn that reports the point as non-actionable. A turn that
does not mention the point leaves the count unchanged. One that reports
it with a non-actionable status resets it to zero. A claim matching
nothing open is added with count one, and a closed claim asserted again
is reopened from one.

The gate fires when four conditions hold: the persistence count reaches
a threshold $`\tau`$; the self-repair check has not closed the claim;
the claim is not in a cooldown; and we have not already spoken it. A
cooldown is $`\rho`$ turns of silence after an intervention or a
participant resolution, during which the claim cannot fire. A fact
counts as already spoken only when the same question recurs soon after
it was answered; a repeated wrong assertion is corrected again. Higher
$`\tau`$ makes Jarvis more patient, lower $`\tau`$ makes it intervene
sooner.

Because a problem flagged once does not fire immediately, participants
have $`\tau{-}1`$ turns to correct it themselves. The self-repair check
runs each turn and its resolutions are applied before the gate tests the
count. If the group fixes the problem first, the claim is closed and the
gate does not fire, so Jarvis speaks only when a problem persists
unresolved.

When the gate fires, we pass the claim to grounded generation
(§[3.2.5](#S3.SS2.SSS5 "3.2.5. Grounded Generation ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")),
which may abstain if the document supports no answer, or if the
generated text cites no valid sentence. We then keep the claim open for
later, up to $`\alpha`$ such rollbacks. A rolled-back claim fires again
on its next detection and starts no cooldown; after the $`\alpha`$-th
rollback we treat it as spoken, so a claim the document cannot ground
stops firing. Finally, the ledger forgets: a closed claim is dropped
after $`W_{r}`$ turns, and an open one after $`W`$ turns without a
mention.

In the deployed configuration $`\tau=2`$, $`\rho=6`$, $`\alpha=2`$,
$`\sigma=0.6`$, $`\delta=0.5`$, and $`W=W_{s}=W_{u}=10`$, $`W_{r}=3`$
turns.

#### 3.2.5. Grounded Generation

This runs only on a fired claim, at a higher temperature ($`0.7`$), and
streams its output so speech can begin before the full response is
ready.

The intent follows from the monitor’s status, and there are three. For
_correct_, the model verifies the claim against the cited sentences and,
if it is wrong, states the correction in one or two sentences (at most
25 words). If the sentences neither support nor contradict it, the claim
is treated as a misattribution. For _missing_, the model answers
completely, with no length limit. For _misattribution_, it says in one
short sentence (at most 20 words) that the document does not state the
claim. The correct and missing intents must cite sentences by ID,
ordered most-substantive first. The first cited sentence sets the page
the listener is pointed to. If the cited IDs cannot be resolved, the
response falls back to the monitor’s IDs, and if those are empty too it
is discarded
(§[3.2.4](#S3.SS2.SSS4 "3.2.4. Claim Ledger and Gate ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")).

The model outputs spoken text without IDs, plus a topic: a
two-to-four-word lowercase noun phrase naming what the answer is about,
to orient a listener who did not ask. We assemble the utterance as
“About {topic}, {text} You’ll find this on {Page N}; search for
‘{keyword}’ to confirm,” and a client may reduce this to the page alone
or turn it off. Before delivery, the utterance is compared against
corrections from the last $`W_{u}`$ turns and dropped if it is a
near-duplicate (embedding similarity $`\geq\delta`$) of one on the same
claim. The delivered utterance is appended to the transcript, so the
self-repair check can later recognize Jarvis’s own correction as a
resolution.

### 3.3. Interaction Module

This module explores three methods, described below, and is implemented
as an event-driven state machine driven by the turn, wake-word, and
voice-activity events from the speech module (see
§[C](#A3 "Appendix C Interaction Module: Answering direct questions ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")
for how it handles direct questions).

#### 3.3.1. Proactive Entry, Barge-in, and Recovery

The module uses silence as its cue for an opening, but silence is
ambiguous: a pause may mean an open floor or a speaker planning their
next clause. We therefore do not play an intervention as soon as it is
generated. We queue it and release it only when three conditions hold:
VAD has detected a long silence, no participant is speaking, and input
energy has stayed low for a minimum interval. Together these distinguish
a real opening from a within-utterance pause. If the backbone generates
a newer intervention first, it replaces the queued one, so Jarvis
responds to the current state of the discussion.

Once speaking, Jarvis gives the floor back as soon as a participant
takes it, triggered only by sustained speech so brief backchannels do
not interrupt it. What happens to the interrupted response depends on
how much was delivered: interrupted almost immediately, Jarvis replays
it in full at the next opening. Interrupted after a few seconds, it
resumes from the start of the truncated sentence with “To finish my
response, …”. If enough was already spoken, it discards the rest, since
an interruption can mean either disinterest or that the point already
landed.

The backbone may send an intervention as complete text or as
sequence-numbered streaming deltas. We discard duplicates and outputs
from superseded generations on arrival. This barge-in and recovery logic
governs all Jarvis playback, including direct answers. In our
implementation, the playback gate opens after $`2.0`$ s of VAD silence
with all participant VADs silent and input RMS below $`0.004`$ for
$`350`$ ms; $`0.5`$ s of continuous participant speech stops Jarvis.
Recovery follows delivered duration: under $`2`$ s replays in full,
$`2`$-$`5`$ s resumes at the interrupted sentence, and beyond $`5`$ s
discards the rest.

#### 3.3.2. Asking for Permission: Hand-Raising

In _hand-raise_ mode, Jarvis holds its response and raises its hand,
speaking only after a participant gives it the floor, either by using
the wake word or an explicit phrase such as “go ahead, agent” without
immediately continuing. If no one calls on it within 60 s, the response
is discarded and the hand lowered, so stale information is not
delivered.

We detect permission with end-anchored regular expressions for phrases
such as “go ahead (agent),” “agent, (please) go on/speak/answer/jump
in,” and “(agent,) you can speak/talk/answer,” while rejecting denials
such as _not yet_, _hold on_, _wait_, and _stop_. Because ASR often
drops “agent,” we also accept bare “go ahead” or “you can speak” while
the hand is raised. ASR can also split a phrase across sub-phrases
(“agent” then “go ahead”). We keep partial sub-phrases containing
“agent” or “go ahead,” append adjacent ones from the same speaker within
1.5 s and a 3 s total span, and match the concatenated text. After
detecting permission, Jarvis waits 400 ms and, if no new speech begins,
releases the response.

#### 3.3.3. Visually communicating agent state and evidence

Participants need to know whether Jarvis heard them, is thinking, or is
about to speak; without this, a missed wake word leaves them unsure
whether to repeat, and an unexpected response feels like an
interruption. We show Jarvis’s state (_idle_, _listening_, _thinking_,
_speaking_) through three channels: a status glyph next to its name (an
ear, thought bubble, or speaking head), a state-colored circle on its
video tile, and a brief earcon on wake-word detection. In hand-raise
mode, the raised hand also signals it has something to say.

Participants may not trust the agent’s claims without evidence, so we
show the source as well as read the claim aloud. Each intervention
carries three pieces of grounding metadata: a page, keywords, and a
source passage. While Jarvis speaks, the system sends this to the shared
PDF viewer, which scrolls to the cited page and marks the passage with
an arrow, letting participants check the claim against the source
without leaving the conversation. The _listening_ and _thinking_ name
suffixes time out after $`30`$ s and $`15`$ s, state updates are
debounced by $`250`$ ms, and the video tile renders as a
$`1280\times 720`$ source at $`15`$ fps. If grounding is unavailable,
Jarvis instead mentions “page $`N`$” in the spoken response.

### 4.1. Events and Ideal Actions

Each event is a short passage containing one thing the agent could
respond to: a question asked and left open, or a false claim asserted.
Every event records the turn where the trigger appears, the _ideal
action_ (which for some events is to stay silent), and a _window of
usefulness_, the span of turns during which that action still helps,
since a correction that arrives after the group has moved on no longer
counts. There are five event types
(Table [1](#acmlabel4 "Table 1 ‣ 4. Synthetic Dataset Generation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")),
split by whether the agent should speak or stay silent. Everything else
is ordinary conversation: of the roughly 280 turns per paper, about 256
carry no event, and silence is correct on all of them.

Silent events serve as controls. Each speak-event has a silent
counterpart with the _same trigger_: a _gap_ (an open question the
document answers) pairs with a _resolved question_ (the same question,
answered correctly by a participant), and a _wrong_ (a false claim left
to stand) pairs with a _self-correction_ (a false claim the participants
repair). In both silent cases the trigger is present, only the group’s
own resolution differs. An ordinary silent turn cannot test this, but a
matched pair can: it asks whether the agent distinguishes a resolved
trigger from an unresolved one, and speaking on a resolved one is an
unnecessary interruption rather than a caught error. This lets us
measure detection failures and false interruptions separately.

We create two “wrong” error types. A _number swap_ replaces one numeric
value with a nearby value of the same unit and magnitude, testing
fine-grained grounding. A _cross-paper misattribution_ states a
distinctive fact from a different CHI paper as if it belonged to this
one, testing whether the agent is bound to this document: an agent that
retrieves from the wider literature will find something similar
elsewhere and wrongly accept it.

Stage 2: Planting errors. We plant eight false claims per paper: four
that nobody corrects (_wrong_) and four that the participants repair
(_self-correction_). The two share a claim turn and differ in what
follows. In each self-correction, the other speaker gives a short reply
on the turn after the false claim, and on the following turn the speaker
who made the claim repairs it; the agent must therefore hold back for
two turns rather than react to the claim. Every claim passes
deterministic checks that it is genuinely false, not decidable from the
conversation alone, non-colliding with the twelve questions, and
non-leaking
(§[D](#A4 "Appendix D Synthetic Dataset Construction Details ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")).

Stage 3: Conversation synthesis. We fix a plan before generating text:
events ten turns apart, the first no earlier than turn 15, split across
a 220-turn _main_ conversation with 20 events and a 60-turn
_resolved-question_ conversation with the other 4. Keeping
resolved-question events in a separate conversation ensures the agent’s
silence there reflects its reading of the group, not its own repetition
suppression, and gives us a stretch with no planted problem, where we
measure how often the agent speaks when nothing needs saying. The two
speakers are researchers deciding whether the paper’s evidence justifies
a follow-up study, one questioning the evidence and one explaining the
methods; non-scripted turns come from an Inner-Thoughts-style
engine ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib41)) in which each speaker forms a
private thought, rates its motivation, and the more motivated one takes
an open floor.

We screened four open-weight models across both speaker roles (16
assignments), scored short windows with a condition-blind, G-Eval-style
judge ([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib42)) on naturalness, coherence,
engagingness, and groundedness, and compared the score distributions
against real research conversations by Jensen–Shannon divergence
following Mirage ([Devanathan et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)), where 0 means
identical distributions
(Table [2](#acmlabel5 "Table 2 ‣ 4.2. Generation Pipeline ‣ 4. Synthetic Dataset Generation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")).
We use this screening to confirm that the candidate generators produce
conversations within the range of variation seen in real research
dialogue, rather than to rank them: fifteen of the sixteen conditions
fall below the 0.14–0.19 split-half divergence of ArgSciChat itself, so
the screening does not reliably discriminate among them at this sample
size. We adopt GPT-OSS-120B in both roles as one adequate configuration.

A paper enters CHI-180-proactive only after we verify that all 24 events
are present with their oracles, that both conversations are complete at
220 and 60 turns, and that all identifiers agree. Each oracle stores the
ideal action (answer, correct, abstain, or stay silent), the expected
answer, and the window of usefulness.

2026b](#bib.bib61)) with three official Qwen3.5 checkpoints ([Qwen Team,
2026a](#bib.bib60)): Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, Qwen3.5-9B, and Qwen3.5-4B. The
two 35B models are mixture-of-experts models with 3B activated
parameters.

Datasets. We use three document-grounded QA sets:

- •
  CHI-180 QA: The twelve questions per paper from
  §[4](#S4 "4. Synthetic Dataset Generation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"),
  the same items reused as gap and unanswerable events in
  §[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2. Generation Pipeline ‣ 4. Synthetic Dataset Generation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations").
  We ran 2,150 of the 2,160 items (10 had gold evidence that could not
  be mapped to a sentence) and excluded the 360 unanswerable items from
  accuracy, as they have no gold answer. Documents were long, at 48k
  tokens.
- •
  QASPER ([Dasigi et al., 2021](#bib.bib10)): NLP papers, where
  questions were written by readers who had seen only the title and
  abstract and answered by others who read the full text.
- •
  COVID-QA ([Möller et al., 2020](#bib.bib50)): an extractive set over
  COVID-19 papers, where annotators marked a span and wrote a question
  for it. It was released independently of our pipeline.

The CHI-180 questions were generated by a Qwen model, so they may favor
the Qwen family; QASPER and COVID-QA are not subject to this, which is
why we include them.

Metrics. We evaluate three key metrics.

- •
  Accuracy. An LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 5; rubric in
  Table [16](#Ax3.T16 "Table 16 ‣ Judge for direct-answer accuracy ‣ LLM Judge Prompts ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
  labels each answer Correct, Partial, Wrong, or Gold_broken; the last
  category is when the question or its reference answer is itself
  broken. We use a judge rather than token overlap because the system
  answers in full sentences while our gold answers are short, so overlap
  F1 is low even for correct answers. _Strict_ is defined as the
  fraction of Correct answers; _Strict+Partial_ also counts Partial
  answers that omit a required element. Gold_broken items, where the
  reference itself is defective, leave the denominator.
- •
  Citation. Among Correct answers, the fraction where the cited
  sentences overlap the annotated evidence. This is a lower bound, since
  papers often states the same fact in several places and only one is
  annotated.
- •
  Latency. Median time to first token (TTFT) and to the last token
  (Total) on the answer-generation call. The document prefix is cached
  first, as is typical in practical deployments.

We compare models only on the questions that all four answered and that
were judged, dropping any question marked Gold_broken for any model.
This matters because each model refuses a different set of questions,
and scoring each model on its own answers would reward the one that
refuses more of the hard ones.

#### 5.1.2. Benchmarking Agent Yielding Behavior When a Person Speaks

A proactive agent has to give up the floor the moment a participant
talks over it. We evaluate whether Jarvis does, and how quickly its
audio stops when humans start talking. The same mechanism yields for
both proactive interventions and answers to direct questions, so we
elicit agent speech with direct questions, which are easier to control.

We drew 54 questions from CHI-180-proactive (three questions from each
of 18 papers, one paper per subcommittee). Each question was run once
with barge-in yielding _enabled_ and once _disabled_, giving 108 trials.
In each trial a human participant starts speaking while the AI agent is
speaking. The two conditions differed only in whether Jarvis stopped
speaking when it detected sustained human speech. In every trial an
experimenter asked the question and then, while Jarvis was answering,
interrupted with sentences like “Sorry, let me add something here. I
have another question.”

We report yield rate per condition with 95% Wilson confidence intervals.
Fig. [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.1.1. Benchmarking our small models ‣ 5.1. Benchmark results ‣ 5. Evaluation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")(a)
shows that with yielding enabled, Jarvis stopped speaking in 51 of 54
trials (94.4%, 95% CI 84.9–98.1%). With it disabled, it stopped in 0 of
54, running to the end of its answer every time (0.0%, 95% CI 0.0–6.6%).

On the 51 trials that stopped, the median yield latency was 486 ms and
latency had a range of 465–521 ms
(Fig. [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.1.1. Benchmarking our small models ‣ 5.1. Benchmark results ‣ 5. Evaluation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")(b)).
This is well under the roughly 500 ms-1 s gap that separates turns in
human conversation, so from a participant’s side the agent yields within
about the time it takes one person to start talking after another stops.
The three failures were cases where the interruption did not register as
sustained speech.

### 5.2. Evaluating Jarvis on synthetic conversations

We replay all CHI-180-proactive conversations through our proactive
backbone and score every planted event against the behavior the dataset
defines as correct. This quantitatively tests the three judgments the
backbone has to make: whether to speak, when to speak, and whether what
it says is right.

#### 5.2.1. Procedure

We replay each conversation turn by turn, submitting one turn at a time
and waiting for the backbone’s verdict before sending the next. Each
turn yields either an utterance from Jarvis or the reason it stayed
silent. Each paper runs in its own session with only that paper
available, so a cross-paper misattribution must be caught by noticing
the fact is absent from the paper at hand.

Each event opens at its _onset_: the turn where the question is asked or
the false claim is made. An intervention addresses the event if it lands
within five turns of the onset (although our results show the vast
majority are at 2 turns). For events where Jarvis should defer, the
window opens later: for a self-correction it opens only at the turn the
participants repair the error, so an intervention before that is counted
separately as interrupting the repair, not as addressing the event.

#### 5.2.2. Scoring

Whether Jarvis spoke is read directly from its records. What it said is
judged by an LLM (Claude Sonnet 5), one call per event, given the full
paper, the event’s gold answer or the claim and what the paper supports,
and a snapshot of the conversation around the event. The judge labels
each contribution as correct, partially correct, off-target (correct and
grounded, but about something other than the event), or
incorrect/fabricated. _Good outcome_ is defined as Jarvis either
contributing something correct or partially correct, or stayed silent
where silence was the right call. _Correct when spoken_ is the share of
correct or partially correct contributions among the events it
addressed. The LM-as-judge was validated with 2 human annotators of 100
contributions and had a \>95% agreement across classes.

#### 5.2.3. Results

Fig. [5](#acmlabel6 "Figure 5 ‣ 5.2. Evaluating Jarvis on synthetic conversations ‣ 5. Evaluation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")
gives the full breakdown, while

provides example interventions from Jarvis. Jarvis addresses 90.6% of
gap events and 76.9% of wrong events, and when it speaks, its
contribution is correct or partially correct 97.0% of the time on gaps
and 95.3% on wrong claims. Overall, it reaches a good outcome (a correct
or partially correct contribution, or silence judged appropriate) on
88.3% of gap and 76.1% of wrong events. In situations where the
participants resolve issues themselves, Jarvis stays silent 97.4% of the
time. Finally, on unanswerable questions, where the correct response is
to say the paper does not report the fact, it invents an answer only

On self-correction events, where a participant states a false claim and

repair 96.8% of the time. In conversations that contain no error and no
open question at all, it speaks only 51 times across all 180. Thus, our
agent is designed to be silent when the group does not need it.

Across gap and wrong events Jarvis addresses, we also analyze how many
turns after the onset its intervention arrives. In more than 95% of
cases it acts within two turns and this is greater than 98% at three
turns.

#### 5.2.4. Failure Analysis

Jarvis’s failures are almost all of one kind: staying silent on
something it should have raised. It is silent on 169 of 1,800 gap events
and 166 of 720 wrong events, both of which call for it to speak, against
only 14 and 7 off-target interventions on those same events. We analyze
the system logs to understand why Jarvis stayed silent. Our analysis
shows three key reasons: 1) The monitor judged the turn to present no
issue (91 gap, 64 wrong silences), 2) the gate chose to wait one more
turn for the problem to persist and never returned to it (44 wrong), and 3) the point was judged too similar to something already said (22 gap).
None of these is a comprehension failure. In each case Jarvis had the
answer and decided not to speak.

Of the 166 wrong-event misses, 117 are imported facts from cross-paper
misattribution, more than twice the 49 number swaps where Jarvis stayed
silent. Because Jarvis is given only the paper under discussion, it
cannot check an imported claim against its source. It has to notice that
the fact is absent from its current paper, which it turns out was harder
for the tested models than catching a value in the paper that was
changed.

#### 5.2.5. Ablation study

Jarvis defers to the group through a single mechanism with two
inseparable halves: the gate gives participants time to self-correct by
requiring a problem to persist, and the self-repair check uses that time
to detect the correction and cancel the intervention. Neither half
defers on its own: a gate without the self-repair check waits but never
notices the repair, and a self-repair check without the gate has already
spoken before the repair arrives. So we ablate deference as a whole. We
remove both and replay the self-correction events, where a participant
states a false claim and the group repairs it two turns later; the
monitor, grounded generator, and all other settings are unchanged.
Without deference, silence drops to 16% and Jarvis speaks before
self-repair 80% of the time.

### 5.3. Live user study

We put our proof-of-concept Jarvis into live Zoom meetings where people
discuss a paper. The study asks three things: whether the intervention
policy acts in time and correctly with real participants (re-testing
§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2. Evaluating Jarvis on synthetic conversations ‣ 5. Evaluation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")
on human conversation), how participants experience the way the agent
enters the conversation, shows its evidence, and how long it waits, and
where they would or would not want such an agent.

#### 5.3.1. Participants

We recruited 23 participants (18 male, 5 female; aged 18–44) through
institute announcements, without compensation. They took part in 14
hour-long sessions, nine with three participants and five with two.
Backgrounds were computing or engineering (11), students who did not
state a field (7), HCI/HRI/psychology (4), and project management (1).
Fifteen were non-native English speakers, with a median self-rated
comfort of 5 on a 5-point scale. 14 participants used voice assistants
at least monthly, and 14 had used an AI tool in a meeting before. The
study was approved by our IRB.

#### 5.3.2. Study design

Each session discussed one of two CHI papers ([Kim et al.,
2026](#bib.bib32); [Kumar et al., 2025](#bib.bib35)), which participants
read beforehand and reviewed again at the start. To ensure the
discussion contained moments the agent should react to, the lead
participant worked from an _event sheet_: each card names a subtopic, a
false statement to make, and the fact and page that contradict it (e.g.,
saying the user base was “about 10 times” larger when the paper says 200
times). Cards are _easy_, misstating a summary fact participants can
often self-correct, or _hard_, misstating a detail deep in the paper
they usually cannot. Each sheet held about 60 cards grouped by part (22
easy / 37 hard for  ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)), 28 easy / 32 hard
for  ([Kumar et al., 2025](#bib.bib35))); the lead participant chose and
rephrased the ones that fit the flow. participants knew errors would be
planted, but not which ones or when. Participants also made their own
unintentional mistakes and asked their own questions, which the agent
handled as well; these correspond to the wrong and gap events of
§[4](#S4 "4. Synthetic Dataset Generation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations").

Five sub-sections. Each session runs in five parts, each adding one
feature so participants experience exactly one change from the previous
part.

- •
  Part 1 (_Speak_): the agent speaks when it has something to say.
- •
  Part 2 (_Raise hand_): it first raises a Zoom hand and speaks only
  when the lead participant says “Go ahead, agent,” dropping the
  intervention if no permission comes within 60 s.
- •
  Part 3 (_Source shown_): Turns the shared viewer to the cited page and
  highlights the supporting sentence as it speaks.
- •
  Parts 4 (_Enthusiastic_) and 5 (_Deferent_) set the gate’s persistence
  threshold $`\tau`$
  (§[3.2.4](#S3.SS2.SSS4 "3.2.4. Claim Ledger and Gate ‣ 3.2. Proactive Backbone: Deterministic harness of small models ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations"))
  to 1 and 3 turns, versus the default of 2 in Parts 1–3.

We accumulate features rather than counterbalance so that participants
experience one change at a time and can compare each part against the
one right before. This design has a cost we make explicit: condition is
confounded with order and with exposure, so a later part is always seen
after more time with the agent. We therefore treat the paired ratings as
within-participant impressions of each behavior in sequence, not as
controlled comparisons between settings.

Procedure. Participants joined through a meeting link, completed a
consent and demographics form, and read the paper. The five parts then
ran in order, each preceded by a slide explaining the next setting in
plain language. During a part, participants saw only the paper shared by
the lead participant, who asked a question per subtopic, planted errors
from the sheet, and, in the hand-raise parts, gave the agent permission
to speak. The same research-team member led every session and is not
counted among the 23 participants. After the fifth part, participants
filled in the questionnaire. Sessions took a median of 49.5 minutes
(35-103).

Measures. We score each intervention on three objective measures from
the transcripts. _Timing_ is the number of turns from the utterance that
raised the problem to the agent’s decision: hard within 2 turns, soft
within 5, late otherwise, regardless of the part’s $`\tau`$.
_Correctness_ is whether the stated fact matches the paper (correct,
partial, or wrong; partial means the right direction but a wrong or
missing value). _Citation_, from Source shown onward, is whether the
attached page and quote point to the evidence in the paper. An LLM
(Claude Sonnet 5) applied this rubric per session, and the authors
double-checked each of its judgments.

Subjective measures come from an end-of-session questionnaire of 21
items in three sections, each rating a pair of settings side by side:
entry (Part 1 vs. 2), source display (Part 2 vs. 3), and waiting (Part 4
vs. 5), on item-specific 5-point anchors (e.g., “Not at all intrusive”
to “Very intrusive”). Four overall items and three free-text questions
follow, asking where participants would and would not want the agent and
what other moments it should step in. Negatively worded items are
reverse-scored ([Brooke, 1996](#bib.bib4)) so that higher is always
better.

#### 5.3.3. Intervention Timing and Correctness

Across the 14 sessions Jarvis decided on 164 interventions and spoke 134. Of the 30 unspoken, 13 were replaced by a later intervention, 9
expired when the hand raise went unacknowledged within 60 s, and 8 were
lost to a sub-session transition or connection problem.

Most decisions were prompt: 139 of 164 (85%) landed within two turns of
the problem, and 11 more within five. The 13 late decisions either
repeated an already-answered fact (5) or tracked a question that ran
across several turns. The delay participants felt came less from the
decision than from the wait after it. In the hand-raise parts the median
wait for permission was 6.5 s, but the top 10% exceeded 50 s (longest
133 s), because the participants let the current speaker finish before
calling on the agent.

148 of the 164 interventions (90%) matched the paper, 15 were partial,
and 1 was wrong. Partial cases had the right direction but a wrong
reference, e.g., the agent measured the battery drop against the
advertised 6 hours rather than the paper’s 5.48 hour baseline (3 cases)
or answered a “market size” prompt as “no such comparison” when the
paper says “user base,”. The single wrong intervention mixed figures
from two tables and was never played. From Source shown onward, the
attached page and quote matched the evidence in 84 of 87 cases; the
other three stated a correct fact but pointed to the wrong page.

Transcript labeling found 223 cues the agent could react to: 84 planted
errors, 32 spontaneous errors, and 107 questions or admissions of not
knowing. The agent acted on 62% of planted errors (52 of 84), 19 of 32
spontaneous errors, and 84 of 107 questions; participants corrected
another 10, 4, and 5 first, and the rest went unresolved. The unresolved
cases were mostly benign: across all three kinds, the monitor did not
flag some, others were displaced when the same turn carried a second
problem the agent answered instead, some were hedged guesses (“maybe
forty or fifty people”), a few were garbled by ASR, and a handful of
questions asked for something the paper does not state. Notably, the
agent almost never spoke wrongly on these cues; when it fell short, it
fell silent.

#### 5.3.5. Qualitative feedback

The three free-text questions received 64 answers, which we coded into
themes.

For the question of where they would use it, the most common answer was
routine work meetings such as team and lab meetings (8), then
discussions that turn on details in a document (6) and information
lookup (6), and smaller mentions of brainstorming, on-the-spot fact
checks, informal settings like studying with friends (3), and
English-language support (1). One participant wrote, “I would definitely
use it in a research meeting where we are discussing a paper. Sometimes
it’s easy to think a paper implies a certain claim, but it would be
helpful if it was there to ground us” (P3).

In terms ofwhere they would not use Jarvis, formal or high-stakes
settings such as presentations, interviews, and large meetings were
named most often (9), followed by private conversations with family and
friends (6). Some found the agent too serious for casual talk (3) or
unsuited to meetings with no shared material or confidential content
(1). One noted that being corrected “over and over” about the paper was
“a little embarrassing” (P3).

Answering the question about what other moments they would want to
support, beyond correcting wrong facts, participants wanted the agent to
remind them when they were stuck or had forgotten a detail (5), suggest
new ideas or related work (3), fill a silence with basic information
(3), and flag when the discussion drifted off topic (3). Two wanted it
to search sources outside the document, and single participants asked it
to show the page to someone who was lost, step in on unresolved
questions, or answer only on a wake word. Two said the current scope was
enough.

Finally, we note two behaviors that recurred in the records. First,
because the agent showed its pending answer on screen before speaking in
the hand-raise parts, a participant sometimes read it and said the fact
first. The agent’s answer then only confirmed them, and the hand-raise
indicator itself became a channel for information. Second, some
interventions stated a fact already said (21 of 164), in two forms. (i)
_A person corrected it before playback_ (10): the intervention was
needed when decided, but while the agent waited for permission a
participant said the same fact, so it only confirmed them. In one
session the lead corrected themselves to “90 participants” and the agent
gave the same number 3 seconds later. This is a cost of the hand-raise
delay, with only one such case in the Speak part. (ii) _The agent
repeated itself_ (11, 7 played): several interventions from one wrong
utterance (7), or an earlier fact repeated when the subtopic came back
(3; in one session, asked where the model runs, the agent explained for
the third time why it was chosen). The cooldown deliberately allows
intervening again on the same topic. The problem is that the agent did
not leave out what it had already said.

## 6. Discussion and Limitations

Planted errors and their validity. Our evaluation relies on planted
errors in two places, and both are deliberately controlled. In
CHI-180-proactive, events are regular by construction, with a fixed
cue/failed-resolution/decision shape and no answer leakage; this
regularity is what makes correctness and timing decidable. But real
errors are messier, less isolated, and often hedged, so performance may
not transfer directly to natural errors. In the live study, a member of
the research team led every session, which guaranteed reactable moments
but risks planting errors that are unusually easy to catch, phrased to
suit the monitor. We take the same evidence to bound both concerns: the
study also contains 32 _spontaneous_ errors that participants made on
their own, with no involvement from the lead or the event sheet and none
of the dataset’s structure, and the agent handled these at a rate
comparable to the planted ones
(§[5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3. Live user study ‣ 5. Evaluation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")).
We read this as the least confounded signal in our evaluation, and as
evidence that performance does not depend on either the lead’s phrasing
or the dataset’s regularity. To further limit bias, the lead rephrased
cards into natural speech rather than reading them verbatim, and all
outcomes were coded from transcripts and reviewed against the paper, not
scored by the lead.

Correction as a social threat. Publicly correcting a fact may expose an
error in front of the group, and its cost grows with the status gap
between the agent, or whoever it corrects, and the rest of the room. One
participant captured this directly, describing being corrected “over and
over” about the paper as “a little embarrassing” (P3). This reframes our
results about the hand-raise mode as not only a floor-management
mechanism but a face-management one: by withholding the correction until
a participant grants the floor, it lets the group decide whether a
correction is worth its interpersonal cost, and it makes the correction
feel invited rather than imposed.

Calibrated trust. On planted moments where Jarvis should speak, it
intervenes the vast majority of missing or incorrect facts, but is not
100%. Its silence therefore cannot be read as evidence a statement is
correct: Jarvis reduces the chance an error goes unchallenged but does
not eliminate it, and participants remain responsible for their own
claims. Some misses are deliberate, since a wrong spoken intervention is
costly, so Jarvis abstains when uncertain and waits for a problem to
persist. Others come from known failure modes such as reworded claims
that reset the persistence count, ASR errors on names and numbers, or
facts in tables the model cannot cite. Our goal is _calibrated_ trust:
participants can rely on what Jarvis says because it is usually right,
while recognizing that its silence guarantees nothing.

Closed-book by design. Jarvis answers only from the uploaded document,
using no tool calls, web search, or parametric knowledge, so every claim
points to a source sentence participants can check. We prioritize
verifiability over capability. The cost is that Jarvis cannot answer
questions outside the document, and extending it to tools or retrieval
while preserving source-level citation is an open problem. A related
limit is context length: a long document or several documents can exceed
the small models we use, so a larger corpus needs a larger model with a
longer context window, which stresses the per-turn latency budget.
Document size, model capacity, and latency thus trade off. Retrieval
over the document rather than holding all of it in context would relax
this, at the cost of a retrieval step.

Deterministic guards and fixed thresholds. Throughout the backbone, a
language model proposes and deterministic logic checks: we verify the
monitor’s sentence IDs against the document, recheck the self-repair
model’s “resolved” verdict before closing a claim, and use persistence
counts rather than a model to decide when to speak, so no single model
output can trigger an irreversible spoken intervention on its own.
Several guards depend on constants we set empirically, however, the
persistence threshold, the claim-matching cutoff, the resolution-score
fraction, and the timing windows, which form the kind of harness now
common around AI agents. The right values may differ across groups,
documents, and styles; learning them from data or adapting them per
group is future work.

Failure modes. Jarvis’s patience depends on recognizing the same problem
across turns, which requires the monitor to phrase claims consistently
and the matcher to link them by embedding similarity. If the monitor
rewords a claim, the match fails, the count resets, and Jarvis stays
silent, the main reason it misses problems; detection and matching
failures both appear as silence, making them hard to separate. Jarvis
also inherits speech-pipeline errors: multi-speaker ASR can fail on the
names and numbers it most needs, the wake word can misfire, and the
system currently supports only English.

GPU deployment. We run the models on datacenter GPUs (Nvidia L40s). In
the online-meeting setting this is reasonable: the audio already leaves
each device for the meeting platform’s servers, so cloud processing adds
no new exposure. Running the backbone locally would matter for offline
or privacy-sensitive settings where audio does not otherwise leave the
device, and would depend on even smaller-models.

Evaluation and platform scope. We evaluate Jarvis on CHI-paper
discussions in Zoom, but neither is fundamental. The same technique
applies to financial filings, contracts, or specifications, though each
has quirks that need evaluation (dense tables stress the rule to omit a
citation when a value is not in a sentence; legal language stresses
claim matching), and the speech and interaction modules would carry to
Google Meet or Microsoft Teams. Our evaluation also assumes a remote
meeting with per-participant audio streams. Co-located groups are
harder: one shared acoustic space makes speaker separation ([Chen
et al., 2024](#bib.bib8)) and diarization the bottleneck, and the agent
becomes a voice in the room.

Privacy and consent. An agent that listens, reads a shared document, and
speaks raises privacy and consent questions, which we address by design
and deployment. Jarvis is a _visible_ participant: it appears in the
roster under its own name, has a video tile, and shows its state, so it
is never a hidden listener. For consent, we follow the norm of AI
note-takers, which announce themselves and are enabled with the group’s
awareness, so disclosing the agent and joining the meeting is how a
participant consents. Two aspects go beyond a passive note-taker. First,
it processes an uploaded document, a second data stream, which is the
group’s own material shared deliberately. Second, it speaks, and can
correct a participant in front of the group, a social cost distinct from
any data concern. Here our hand-raise mode doubles as a per-intervention
consent mechanism, letting the group admit each intervention and decide
how forward the agent should be.

## 7. Conclusion

We design the first real-time speech agent that can take part in a group
conversation instead of waiting to be addressed. Jarvis reads a shared
document, follows the discussion, and speaks only when the group misses
or misstates a fact, giving people a chance to fix it themselves first
and backing every claim with a source they can check on screen. Because
the conversation is grounded in documents, we can measure objectively
whether an intervention was right as well as its timing. Jarvis is an
important step toward proactive speech agents that speak up when a group
needs them, stay quiet when it does not, and when they intervene, can be
measured against ground truth.

## Appendix A Speech Processing Module: Implementation Details

- •
  Per-participant audio streaming. The module receives a separate audio
  stream for each participant, identified by participant ID. Each stream
  is processed in parallel by voice-activity detection, wake-word
  detection, and automatic speech recognition (ASR). In our Zoom
  implementation, we receive 16-kHz mono audio from Zoom Realtime Media
  Streams (RTMS) ([Zoom, nd](#bib.bib88)) in 20-ms frames. The audio is
  encoded as 16-bit pulse-code modulation (PCM16).
- •
  Voice activity detection (VAD). Transcriptions operate at a word level
  which is much slower than activity detection. So we analyze each
  participant’s audio independently and emit acoustic events rather than
  words: speech onset, sustained speech, short silence, and long
  silence. We run a Silero VAD instance ([Silero Team,
  2024](#bib.bib70)) per participant on 30-ms frames with a
  speech-probability threshold of 0.5. Sustained speech requires 0.5 s
  of continuous voicing, with a 0.5-s hangover to bridge brief gaps
  within an utterance. We use 0.5 s and 2.0-second thresholds for short
  and long silence, respectively.
- •
  Wake-word detection. We use a two-stage detector for “hey agent”
  detection. We continuously run a first-stage model that proposes
  candidates, then a verifier re-scores the most recent audio and
  decides whether to fire. Because the detector evaluates overlapping
  windows, it sees incomplete prefixes such as “hey ag”. We train these
  as negatives and fire only on the complete phrase to avoid premature
  triggers. Two gates provide additional filtering: a contextual filter
  rejects patterns such as “the agent” and “say agent,” while a
  high-precision text fallback recovers acoustic misses when the
  transcript contains “hey agent.” We build on openWakeWord ([Scripka,
  2022](#bib.bib69)). The first stage combines fine-tuned speech
  embeddings with a lightweight head, scores every 80-ms frame, and
  invokes the verifier when the score is above 0.90. The verifier is a
  1.2M-parameter CNN-GRU trained from scratch on 40-band log-Mel
  windows. It rescores the most recent 1.0- and 1.2-s windows and fires
  when either exceeds 0.90. Following GraphemeAug ([Zhang et al.,
  2025b](#bib.bib83)), we synthesize confusable negatives; to reduce
  prefix bias, windows containing less than half the keyword are labeled
  negative, while ambiguous half-but-not-complete windows are omitted.
  Positive “hey agent” examples use three TTS systems (Piper ([Hansen,
  2022](#bib.bib20)), Kyutai TTS ([Zeghidour et al., 2025](#bib.bib82)),
  and Qwen3-TTS ([Hu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib26))) to reduce speaker
  bias. Kyutai is conditioned on recordings from 410 real speakers, and
  the synthesized examples are overlaid on conversational audio. On
  285.4 h of held-out AMI ([Carletta et al., 2005](#bib.bib5)) and
  CANDOR ([Reece et al., 2023](#bib.bib63)) audio, we achieve 99.08%
  recall with 0.077 false accepts per hour.
- •
  Per-speaker streaming ASR and turn segmentation. We avoid Zoom’s
  transcript because its latency ranges from seconds to tens of seconds
  and carries no timestamp usable for turn-taking. Instead we use
  nvidia/nemotron-speech- streaming-en-0.6b, a cache-aware streaming
  FastConformer–RNNT with attention context ([NVIDIA, 2026](#bib.bib52);
  [Noroozi et al., 2023](#bib.bib51)), processing audio in 1.12-s steps
  with roughly one second of right context. We run one recognizer per
  participant and detect a turn end when a dedicated endpointing Silero
  instance observes 500 ms of silence, with a 700-ms wall-clock watchdog
  as backup if PCM delivery stalls. Median latency from speech end to a
  turn-end detection was 224 ms.
- •
  Speech synthesis and delivery. We synthesize text to speech (TTS) with
  Kyutai TTS kyutai/tts-1.6b-en_fr and the Mimi codec (24-kHz mono,
  80-ms frames); batch chunks are capped at 150 then 300 characters on
  sentence boundaries. Streamed synthesis starts after three words or
  0.25 s and pauses once more than 0.3 s of audio is buffered. A
  prefetch thread applies a 0.4-s jitter buffer and sends 20-ms packets
  at the native 24-kHz rate. After warm-up, the latency from the first
  text input to audible playback is 0.70–0.95 s.

## Appendix B Monitor Module: Answering Direct Questions Based on Context

This model (temperature $`0.7`$) does standard document question
answering: given a question, find the answer in the document. The main
challenge is deciding whether a question was actually asked, since
wake-word detection is imperfect in live conversation.

The model answers complete questions the document supports; abstains
when the document cannot answer, with the fixed reply “That isn’t
covered in the document”, and skips anything that is not a question for
Jarvis, including incomplete fragments and talk between participants (an
aside, correction, or thinking aloud, e.g., “never mind, scroll up”). It
is given the last $`k`$ turns as context, which it uses to resolve
references such as “my previous question” or “that point.” When it
answers, it writes concisely in its own words, at most three sentences
since it is read aloud, and cites the document sentences; the page and
keyword hint are assembled as for proactive interventions, without the
topic preface ($`k=8`$ deployed).

## Appendix C Interaction Module: Answering direct questions

A direct question is addressed to the agent, so the answer plays as soon
as it is ready, subject only to the barge-in and recovery behavior
described in
§[3.3.1](#S3.SS3.SSS1 "3.3.1. Proactive Entry, Barge-in, and Recovery ‣ 3.3. Interaction Module ‣ 3. SYSTEM DESIGN ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations").
The key challenge is in recovering the human participant’s complete
question, given humans can pause mid-phrase or the question might have
multiple sentences with short silences between them.

Since wake-word detection runs separately from automatic speech
recognition (ASR), our interaction module must match each wake event to
the ASR turn that contains the question. The match uses both speaker and
time: the turn must come from the participant who triggered the wake
word, and its time interval must overlap the wake position or start
shortly after it. Once matched, the turn is labeled a _direct_ chunk. If
it contains only “hey agent,” the system waits briefly for the question
to arrive in the next turn. Short follow-up turns from the same speaker
remain part of the same direct question while the gap is short or VAD
still detects speech. The continuation ends after a long gap, a
non-backchannel utterance from another participant, or a hard timeout.

Waiting for the continuation window adds latency, even when the question
is already complete. We therefore generate speculatively. After the
speaker pauses briefly, the interaction model sends a \<Silence\>
marker, allowing the proactive backbone model to start generating from
the text received so far while the continuation window remains open. If
the speaker continues, the new text is added to the same generation and
the backbone model regenerates the answer. If a new question starts, it
creates a new generation that supersedes the old one: queued output from
the old generation is dropped and any active playback is canceled. Once
the continuation window closes, the Interaction Model sends
\<Complete\>. This ensures that even a late ASR tail arriving after the
speculative generation is flushed is added to the same question.

Implementation details. A wake event binds to a turn from the same
participant when the turn overlaps the wake sample position within
$`\pm 300`$ ms or begins within the following $`5`$ s. Unbound events
expire after $`20`$ s of audio time. We classify a turn as wake-only
when it contains at most three tokens and lasts at most $`2.5`$ s,
opening a $`5`$ s grace window. A later turn from the same speaker is
accepted as a continuation within $`4.5`$ s, which corresponds to a
$`3`$ s turn gap plus $`1.5`$ s of ASR latency, or while VAD remains
voiced, up to a $`30`$ s session-clock limit. Backchannels are detected
with a fixed, hand-curated list of 42 tokens: interjections, fillers,
and acknowledgements such as _yeah_, _okay_, _mm-hmm_, _uh-huh_,
_right_, _sure_, _cool_, and _wow_.¹¹ 1 The full list: yeah, ok, okay,
mm, mmm, hm, hmm, hmmm, uh, huh, ha, hah, haha, ah, um, umhum, uhhuh,
right, nice, good, fine, oh, really, god, hey, so, well, all, cool, wow,
yep, yo, eh, sure, gotcha, and, yes, ow, yum, sign, laugh, shoot. Tokens
are matched after lowercasing and removing punctuation, so “mm-hmm” and
“uh-huh” match as two tokens each. A turn counts as a backchannel when,
after punctuation is removed, it has at most four words and every word
is in the list. We send the speculative \<Silence\> after $`0.5`$ s of
silence. If the transmitted text already covers the VAD speech endpoint
within a $`700`$ ms margin, we close the generation immediately;
otherwise we close it $`1.5`$ s later.

## Appendix D Synthetic Dataset Construction Details

This section gives the construction details behind CHI-180
(§[4](#S4 "4. Synthetic Dataset Generation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")):
the source documents, the fact and question taxonomies, the pairing and
placement logic, the generator screening, and the leakage safeguards.

### D.1. Source Documents

We use 180 CHI papers drawn from the CHI 2026 guide to selecting a
subcommittee ([ACM CHI, 2026](#bib.bib2)), which lists example past
papers for each of the 18 subcommittees. We took ten example papers from
each subcommittee (the smallest yielded ten), giving 180 papers that
cover CHI’s topic areas without bias toward any one.

### D.2. Event Pairing and Placement

Each speak-event has a silent counterpart that acts as its control, but
the two pairs are built differently. A resolved-question reuses a gap
item: the same question, answered inside the conversation rather than
left open. A self-correction does not reuse a wrong event’s claim; it is
a different claim, built by the same operators and given the same
three-turn shape, with a participant repair in place of the turns that
would adopt the value.

This difference dictates placement. A resolved-question repeats an item
the agent may already have answered elsewhere, so in a single
conversation its silence could stem from the agent’s own repetition
suppression rather than its reading of the group; we therefore isolate
resolved-question events in a separate conversation. The eight planted
claims are eight distinct facts with no such carry-over, so wrong and
self-correction events can share the main conversation.

Gap events follow a taxonomy of six sub-types: explicit fact recall,
missing comparator, missing qualifier, missing metric definition,
unresolved attribution, and partial fact recall. Each has the same shape
(a cue, a failed attempt to resolve it, and an answer that sits in a
short span of the document), which keeps the correctness of an
intervention independent of subjective judgment.

### D.3. Stage 1 Details: Facts and QA Items

Each paper yields 300 to 470 extracted facts, each a single statement
anchored to its page, passage, character span, and a hash of the passage
text. The hash guards against re-parsing: if a later parse cuts passages
differently, a stored span could point at the wrong text, and the
mismatched hash flags the item rather than letting it be silently wrong.
Unanswerable items are separately verified against the full text to
confirm no answer exists. We use Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 ([Qwen
Team, 2025](#bib.bib59)) for this stage.

The twelve-item count and four answer types come from QASPER ([Dasigi
et al., 2021](#bib.bib10)), whose papers carry 3.2 questions on average
and twelve at most; we read that maximum as the number of genuine
information needs a paper can support. QASPER’s own type proportions are
51.8% extractive, 24.2% abstractive, 13.9% boolean, and 10.2%
unanswerable; we instead balance the mix (4/4/2/2) so each type occurs
often enough within one paper to be analyzed on its own. Each item is
used twice: on its own as a question-answering item scored against the
gold answer, and inside the conversation as an event (the ten answerable
items become gap events, the two unanswerable ones become unanswerable
events, and four of the ten gaps are reused as resolved-question
events).

### D.4. Stage 2 Details: Error Validity Checks

For self-correction events, the other speaker replies briefly on the
turn after the erroneous claim, and the speaker who made the claim
repairs it on the turn after that, two turns after the claim. Every
planted claim must pass four deterministic checks: it must be _false_
(the wrong value appears nowhere in the extracted facts, and the
entities in a misattributed fact appear nowhere in the paper); it must
not be _decidable from the conversation alone_ (it contradicts nothing
said earlier, since that would allow an agent to correct it without
reading the paper); it must not _interfere_ with the twelve QA items;
and it must not _leak_ (the correct value appears nowhere else in the
conversation).

### D.5. Stage 3 Details: Plan, Personas, and Generator Screening

The main conversation is 220 turns and carries 10 gap, 2 unanswerable, 4
wrong, and 4 self-correction events; the resolved-question conversation
is 60 turns with cues at turns 15, 25, 35, and 45. For each
main-conversation event the plan records three turns: the cue, the turn
on which the other speaker fails to resolve it, and the turn on which
the information would have been used in a decision. In the
resolved-question conversation each event spans three turns (one speaker
asks, the other answers exactly as the document states and quotes the
source, the asker acknowledges the question is settled), so silence is
correct throughout.

Speaker profiles are in PersonaChat ([Zhang et al., 2018](#bib.bib85))
form, derived from ArgSciChat dialogues ([Ruggeri et al.,
2023](#bib.bib66)); both speakers see the same public history, persona,
and available facts.

Generator screening. The four candidates are open-weight so the corpus
can be regenerated without a proprietary API: Qwen3-235B-A22B ([Qwen
Team, 2025](#bib.bib59)), GPT-OSS-120B ([OpenAI, 2025](#bib.bib54)),
Qwen3.8-27B ([Qwen Team, 2026c](#bib.bib62)), and
DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 ([DeepSeek-AI, 2026](#bib.bib11)). Assigning one
model to each speaker role with order counted gives 16 conditions; each
generated 200 turns over each of three documents. A blind G-Eval-style
judge ([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib42)) (Claude Sonnet 5) scores
eight-turn windows on naturalness, coherence, engagingness, and
groundedness, and we compare these against windows of real conversation
by Jensen–Shannon divergence following Mirage ([Devanathan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)), with ICSI ([Janin et al., 2003](#bib.bib31)),
CANDOR ([Reece et al., 2023](#bib.bib63)), and ArgSciChat as references
(38 windows each; groundedness scored on ArgSciChat only, where the
judge also sees the source paper).
Table [2](#acmlabel5 "Table 2 ‣ 4.2. Generation Pipeline ‣ 4. Synthetic Dataset Generation ‣ Jarvis: A Proactive Speech Agent for Multi-Party Conversations")
reports divergences against ArgSciChat. Naturalness is worst when
Qwen3-235B asks (its three asker conditions give the three largest
values, 0.272, 0.215, 0.167, versus 0.156, 0.090, 0.070 when it
answers), and engagingness is worst wherever Qwen3.8-27B appears (the
four largest values in that column are all its conditions), confirming
that role placement, not just model capacity, drives realism.

Preventing leakage. Three constraints prevent the conversation from
revealing its own answers. Each turn can use only the facts in the local
pool assigned to its segment. We withhold the facts that answer any
answerable event from every segment’s pool, along with any other fact
that could reveal the answer. A validator then checks every generated
turn: numerals must appear in an allowlist, planted errors are tracked
in a ledger, and no turn other than a cue turn may state a gold answer.
Any turn that fails these checks is rejected and regenerated.

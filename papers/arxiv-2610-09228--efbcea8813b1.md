# Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment

Xilun Zhang    Maggie Wang    Erik Bauer    Hong-Xing Yu    Huang Huang
   Jiajun Wu    Marco Pavone Affiliation: \[ Affiliation: \[

###### Abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies trained on large datasets are
capable within their training domains, yet they still fail to generalize
to the variety of situations a robot meets in real-world deployment.
Agentic robot systems complement the policy with a vision-language model
(VLM) orchestrator that learns when to call the policy, how to instruct
it, and when to use scripted skills instead. However, because the
harness is built around a frozen policy that has limited language
steerability, the orchestrator can avoid the policy’s failures but never
overcome them. The policy becomes the bottleneck of the whole system.
Fine-tuning the policy can remove this bottleneck, but updating it alone
decouples it from an orchestrator tuned to its old behavior. We propose
Robo-COP, in which the orchestrator and policy co-evolve during
deployment. Robo-COP curates skill demonstrations from its own
executions, fine-tunes the policy when this data can address recurring
failures, and adopts each new policy only after it improves the skills
it was trained for. Across ten simulated RoboLab tasks, Robo-COP raises
mean held-out success from 64.8% to 73.8% over the same harness with a
frozen policy, while fine-tuning on a fixed schedule without
verification reaches only 65.8%. On three real-world tasks, Robo-COP
raises held-out success from 38.3% to 50.0%. Robo-COP turns deployment
into a self-improving flywheel in which robots learn by doing, with each
improvement in execution producing better data for the next round of
learning. Videos and code are available at
[https://robo-cop.pages.dev/](https://robo-cop.pages.dev/).

## 1 Introduction

Robot foundation models trained on large datasets now generalize to many
new objects and scenes ([Kim et al., 2024](#bib.bib25); [Black et al.,
2025](#bib.bib26); [Physical Intelligence et al., 2025](#bib.bib27);
[Khazatsky et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)). Still, they degrade under
distribution shift once deployed ([Fei et al., 2025](#bib.bib39); [Chen
et al., 2026c](#bib.bib40)), and no dataset can cover every situation a
robot will encounter. Language models offer another route: they
generalize zero-shot and can write code that plans over motion
primitives ([Ahn et al., 2022](#bib.bib24); [Liang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib8); [Xu et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Huang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib23)), but they are limited by the fixed set of primitives
they can call. Recent agentic systems combine the two, with a language
model reasoning and orchestrating vision-language-action (VLA) policies
for low-level control ([Galanti et al., 2026](#bib.bib12); [Huang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)).

Such systems also improve by reflecting on failures during deployment
([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib41); [Zha et al., 2024](#bib.bib42); [Lu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib6)) and learning when to delegate to a VLA policy
([Galanti et al., 2026](#bib.bib12); [Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib14);
[Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)). Over many episodes, the harness
adapts to the current policy, learning which parts of a task to give the
policy and which to handle with scripted skills. The orchestrator can
rephrase instructions or add supporting motions, but VLAs have limited
language steerability ([Fei et al., 2025](#bib.bib39); [Kwok et al.,
2026](#bib.bib49)), so the harness can only route around the policy’s
failures rather than fix them, and the upper bound of performance
remains dictated by the policy.

Updating the policy from deployment experience could raise this ceiling
([Sukhija et al., 2026](#bib.bib28)), but doing so during deployment is
hard. We study a deployment setting in which the robot must learn while
attempting its assigned tasks, without a separate exploration phase. The
training data must therefore come from these task attempts. In a novel
environment many of these attempts fail, yet a failed episode often
contains correct steps, such as a block that was grasped correctly
before it was dropped, so keeping only successful episodes wastes much
of the little data available. Even with new training data, fine-tuning
does not guarantee improvement: the new checkpoint may perform worse
than the deployed policy. Testing it requires further task attempts, so
a poor update can cost deployment successes. Finally, updating the
policy alone leaves the orchestrator behind: its memory, such as advice
to grasp a block with scripted motion because earlier VLA grasps failed,
was formed around the old checkpoint and no longer matches the new
policy. Prior harnesses assume a fixed policy ([Galanti et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12); [Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)) and never face
these problems. We therefore ask: how can the orchestrator and its
policy co-evolve during deployment?

We introduce Robo-COP (Co-evolving Orchestrator and Policy), a
self-improving system whose orchestrator uses scripted motion primitives
and a VLA policy as tools, and which updates the policy and adapts the
orchestrator’s memory together purely during deployment
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).
It turns its own deployment executions into new training data for the
policy by splitting each episode into skills and keeping every skill
that succeeded, even when the episode as a whole failed, and it decides
when this data warrants training. After fine-tuning, it evaluates each
candidate during deployment, checking its target skills, overall task
success, and previously admitted skills before deciding whether to adopt
it or retain the current policy. We call this adoption check _policy
verification_. After policy selection, it revisits memory entries that
discouraged using the VLA, such as recommendations to use scripted
motion after repeated grasp failures, so the orchestrator can reassess
whether that advice still applies. Unlike skill discovery methods that
spend a separate budget on exploration ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)),
Robo-COP learns entirely during deployment, so every trial is an attempt
at the task. We evaluate on ten simulated tasks chosen to span three
regimes: tasks the pretrained VLA solves alone, tasks that scripted
primitives solve, and tasks that need both. We also test the full loop
on three real-world tasks.

Our contributions are fourfold:

- •
  Co-evolving orchestrator and policy. We frame deployment-time learning
  as improving an agentic harness and its policy together.
- •
  Agentic data curation. We turn deployment executions into skill
  demonstrations without manual labels, keeping successful skills even
  from failed episodes, and train only when the data warrants it.
- •
  Skill verification and memory revision. We verify each policy
  candidate before adoption and revise policy-dependent memory after
  every update.
- •
  Performance gains in simulation and the real world. Robo-COP is
  competitive on all three regimes, raising mean held-out success from
  64.8% to 73.8% in RoboLab simulation and from 38.3% to 50.0% on the
  real-world DROID setup over a frozen-policy harness.

## 2 Related Work

#### Agentic robot orchestration.

Language-model planners compose motion primitives and learned policies
into task-solving programs ([Huang et al., 2022](#bib.bib23); [Ahn et
al., 2022](#bib.bib24); [Liang et al., 2023](#bib.bib8); [Huang et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib9); [Xu et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)). More recently
developed frameworks also improve during deployment: they revise failed
programs and retain reusable skills ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Li
et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Chen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib15)), or learn
when to delegate to a policy rather than an analytic tool ([Galanti et
al., 2026](#bib.bib12); [Chen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib13); [Huang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14); [Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib16); [Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib51)). All of these adapt how a fixed set of capabilities
is used, so the system can do no better on a skill than its policy
allows. Robo-COP keeps this adaptive harness but also improves the
policy it calls, and revises the harness’s memory whenever that policy
changes.

#### Generating robot-policy data.

Improving a policy requires data that covers the situations it fails on.
Simulation provides scalable supervision ([Pfaff et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)), but transferring learned behavior to physical robots
remains difficult ([Huang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib47); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib48); [Choi et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). Real-world datasets
and collection interfaces avoid this gap at the cost of human effort
([Khazatsky et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Chi et al., 2024](#bib.bib19);
[Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)), and demonstration-generation methods
stretch a small set of human examples further ([Mandlekar et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2); [Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib52)). Closer to our setting,
agents and policies can collect and filter their own experience ([Chen
et al., 2025](#bib.bib50); [Zhou et al., 2024](#bib.bib4); [Zhang and
Shah, 2026](#bib.bib21); [Xiao et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)). Robo-COP
draws its data from deployment itself, where executions mix policy and
scripted actions, and curates skill-level demonstrations from them,
including correct skills performed within failed trials.

#### Policy improvement during deployment.

Planner distillation and autonomous collection turn robot executions
into policy supervision ([Ha et al., 2023](#bib.bib5); [Dalal et al.,
2023](#bib.bib31); [Bousmalis et al., 2024](#bib.bib3); [Zhou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib4)), real-world reinforcement learning updates policies
from interaction and reward ([Mark et al., 2024](#bib.bib44);
[Wagenmaker et al., 2025](#bib.bib45); [Lei et al., 2025](#bib.bib46)),
and VLM-guided exploration and skill acquisition generate training data
for adapting VLA policies ([Sukhija et al., 2026](#bib.bib28); [Wang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib29)). These methods typically deploy each updated
policy, while test-time verification selects instructions or actions for
a policy that stays fixed ([Kwok et al., 2026](#bib.bib49); [Zhang and
Shah, 2026](#bib.bib21)). Robo-COP instead treats each update as a
candidate: it decides when training is warranted, verifies the whole
candidate policy before adoption, and revises the memory that the
harness built around the previous policy.

## 3 Method

Robo-COP updates both the VLA policy and the adaptive harness that
orchestrates its use
(Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Policy improvement during deployment. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).
The harness logs policy and scripted actions as one stream
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Adaptive policy orchestration ‣ 3 Method ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")),
curates it into skill demonstrations
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Experience curation and policy improvement ‣ 3 Method ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")),
and verifies each new policy before adoption, revising the memory tied
to the old one
(Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Policy verification and integration ‣ 3 Method ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).

### 3.1 Adaptive policy orchestration

#### Acting and reflecting.

Robo-COP is an embodied coding agent built on the CaP-X execution tools
([Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)), extended with an execute_vla tool that
invokes the VLA policy on a language instruction. Its orchestrator
writes a program that calls scripted motion primitives, perception
functions, and the VLA policy, conditioned on the task goal, current
observation, episode history, and task memory $`M`$, as in prior
harnesses ([Galanti et al., 2026](#bib.bib12); [Chen et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib13); [Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)). A vision-language
model (VLM) monitors execution and can interrupt a call or end the
episode. Whenever execution stops, a visual judge checks the outcome and
either ends the episode or asks for revised code. It credits the VLA and
scripted actions separately, so a successful skill keeps its credit even
if a later step fails. At the end of the episode, the orchestrator
records what worked and what failed in $`M`$, a form of episodic memory
that lets agentic robot systems improve without changing any weights
([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib41); [Zha et al., 2024](#bib.bib42); [Lu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)).

#### Action unification.

Training data for the VLA has to come from executions itself, yet many
useful motions are produced by scripted primitives rather than by the
policy. Within one execution, the orchestrator may alternate between VLA
calls and scripted primitives. For example, the robot may approach a red
block with scripted primitives and then call the VLA to lower and close
the gripper. We record the commanded joint targets, gripper commands,
observations, and robot states at each step in execution order, which
yields a continuous action stream in the VLA’s training format. The
recorded steps can then become a demonstration labeled _grasp the red
block_, even though part of it was produced by scripted primitives.

### 3.2 Experience curation and policy improvement

#### From execution to labeled skills.

The unified action stream contains interleaved VLA and scripted actions,
which Robo-COP curates into skill demonstrations $`(X,\ell)`$, where
$`X=(o_{t},s_{t},a_{t})_{t=1}^{T}`$ is a contiguous trajectory and
$`\ell`$ is its language instruction, and collects the accepted ones in
a training set $`D`$.

![Refer to caption](2610.09228v1/curation_recorded_examples_v5.png)

Figure 3: Skill-level data curation. Three recorded examples from
Recycle cup clutter: both judges accept the pickup (A), the text judge
accepts a grasp that the video judge rejects (B), and both reject a
placement (C). The retained pickup comes from an episode that ultimately

Experience curation evaluates segments independently of the episode
outcome, a successful pickup can remain a training example even if the
block is later dropped, and a successful episode does not make every
segment valid. For each execution segment, a text judge uses the
executed code, console output, and episode feedback to identify each
segment’s intended skill and assess its outcome. For VLA calls, it keeps
the instruction passed to the policy. For scripted primitives, it groups
consecutive calls into a motion-level skill, such as the scripted
approach above, and a segment labeled _pick up the red block_ must also
include the lift. A video judge then checks sampled frames against the
proposed instruction summarized from textual judge, and a segment enters
$`D`$ only when the text judge accepts it and the video judge confirms
success or clear partial progress.

#### Deciding when to train.

Training on a fixed schedule causes two potential problems: it trains on
tasks the harness already solves, where an update can only add risk, and
it trains before enough demonstrations have accumulated to support a
useful update. Robo-COP therefore lets a language-model controller
decide when to train given the deployment data so far. It reviews the
curated demonstrations alongside recent performance, task memory,
failures, and earlier attempts, and returns train or wait with the
desired improvement or the evidence still needed. We expect the
controller to skip training on tasks the harness already solves, and to
wait on the others until the curated demonstrations cover the skills
that keep failing
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Simulation results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).

A train decision names up to four target skill instructions $`E`$, which
guide verification. Training itself uses all curated data collected so
far. We fine-tune a candidate $`\pi^{\prime}`$ from the pretrained
checkpoint with LoRA ([Hu et al., 2022](#bib.bib43)) and the policy’s
original flow-matching objective. Deployment on the task pauses while
$`\pi^{\prime}`$ trains. Afterward, $`\pi`$ stays in service as the
baseline against which $`\pi^{\prime}`$ is evaluated.
Appendices [D](#A4 "Appendix D Controller ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")
and [F](#A6 "Appendix F Fine-tuning ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")
give the controller and training details.

### 3.3 Policy verification and integration

#### Candidate policy verification.

We evaluate the candidate $`\pi^{\prime}`$ during a window of $`W`$
trials while retaining the deployed policy $`\pi`$ as a fallback. For
target skills, the orchestrator first tries $`\pi^{\prime}`$ and falls
back to $`\pi`$ or scripted motion if needed. Because fallbacks can
complete a task after $`\pi^{\prime}`$ fails, task success alone does
not reflect the candidate’s own performance, so verification is
performed at the skill level rather than only at the episode level.
Since each candidate is fine-tuned from base policy on all curated data,
it could also lose a skill that an earlier update added, so a verifier
checks for regressions on skills adopted with earlier candidates, which
we call _admitted_ skills, $`S`$.

Let $`b`$ be the incumbent’s recent task success rate and
$`\mathrm{succ}(W)`$ the task success rate during the candidate window.
For each admitted skill $`s`$, let $`r_{\mathrm{inc}}(s)`$ and
$`r_{c}(s)`$ be the observed per-call finish rates of the incumbent and
candidate, where a call finishes when its completion check fires. We
apply the skill check to $`S_{\mathrm{obs}}`$, the admitted skills with
sufficient candidate calls and an incumbent reference, and require

````math
For the target skills $`E`$, a language-model reviewer checks what each
candidate call actually achieved, given the generated code, output logs,
and visual judge outputs. Verification does not rely on episode success
alone but checks whether the skills actually meet the expectations. A
candidate is adopted only if it satisfies all requirements, and
otherwise the system reverts to the current policy. Adoption is decided
only at the end of the evaluation window, whereas reversion can happen
earlier if the candidate clearly fails during evaluation.

#### Policy adoption and memory revision.

A candidate that passes verification replaces $`\pi`$, and its target
skills are added to $`S`$. If no candidate is ever adopted, the system
keeps the base policy and improves through orchestration alone. Changing
the deployed policy does not change the advice stored in task memory.
For example, memory may recommend scripted motion for grasping a red
block because earlier VLA attempts failed. Following that recommendation
after an update would keep the harness from discovering that the new
policy can successfully grasp. The same problem arises when a candidate
is rejected.

During its evaluation window, episode reflection records the candidate’s
failures as advice against using the VLA. After the incumbent is
restored, that advice would wrongly steer every later episode away from
it. After adoption or reversion, we therefore rewrite references to the
candidate to name the policy in service and mark advice that steers the
orchestrator away from the VLA as unverified for the current checkpoint.
The advice is marked rather than deleted, since the update may not have
fixed the skill, so later episodes can retest it while its history stays
in memory.

## 4 Experiments

Through this section, we aim to address three main questions. Q1: Does
co-evolution improve held-out success over a frozen-policy harness and
fixed-schedule training, and in which task regimes? Q2: Does the system
decide correctly when to train and whether to adopt? Q3: Does learning
during deployment cost task success along the way? We answer them on ten
simulated tasks and then test the full loop on three real-world tasks.

### 4.1 Experimental setup

![Refer to caption](2610.09228v1/simulation_task_overview.png)

#### Tasks and system.

We evaluate on ten RoboLab ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)) tasks with
randomized object configurations, spanning retrieval, grouping,
placement, and recovery. We selected them before running Robo-COP to
represent the three regimes introduced in
Section [1](#S1 "1 Introduction ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment").
Within every regime, the controller ends up waiting on one task,
adopting a candidate on another, and reverting at least one candidate
(Table [4](#A4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Appendix D Controller ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")),
so the results cover each decision it can make.
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.1 Experimental setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")
shows representative scenes, and
Appendix [H.1](#A8.SS1 "H.1 Successful episodes and randomized resets ‣ Appendix H Additional simulation results ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")
shows successful episodes and randomized resets for all ten tasks. The
initial VLA is $`\pi_{0.5}`$ trained on DROID ([Physical Intelligence et
al., 2025](#bib.bib27); [Khazatsky et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)) and served
at its training control rate, and Gemini 3.8 Flash runs the
orchestrator.
Appendices [B](#A2 "Appendix B Orchestrator interface ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")
and [F](#A6 "Appendix F Fine-tuning ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")
give the execution tools and fine-tuning settings.

#### Baselines.

*$`\pi_{0.5}`$ only* runs the base policy without an orchestrator,
memory, or updates. *Frozen-policy baseline* uses the same harness and
episode reflection as Robo-COP but never changes the VLA weights,
isolating what memory adaptation alone achieves. *Fixed schedule* uses
the same recorder, curator, fine-tuning procedure, episode reflection,
and memory revision as Robo-COP, but trains every 30 trials and deploys
each checkpoint without verification, isolating the contribution of the
controller and verifier. *ASPIRE* diagnoses failures, revises robot
programs, and retains validated solutions as reusable skills ([Lu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib6)). Since ASPIRE did not evaluate on RoboLab, we
adapt its algorithm to our benchmark and API setting.

#### Protocol and metrics.

Frozen-policy baseline, fixed schedule, and Robo-COP each run 100
deployment trials (episodes) per task on matched initializations,
sharing their first 30 trials before any training decision. *Deployment
success* is the success rate over these trials, which include data
collection and candidate evaluation. We then freeze each system’s policy
and memory, or ASPIRE’s skill library after its 100-execution search,
and measure *held-out success* on 50 held-out initializations per task,
shared across all methods and distinct from those used during
deployment, following the single-learning-run protocol of prior agentic
robot-learning work ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Xiao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib22)). The standalone $`\pi_{0.5}`$ is tested only on the
held-out trials.

#### Q1: Does co-evolution improve held-out success, and where?

Co-evolution improves held-out success over both baselines, and the
gains are largest when Robo-COP updates the policy. Robo-COP reaches
73.8% mean held-out success, versus 64.8% for the frozen-policy baseline
and 65.8% for the fixed schedule, which recovers only 1.0 of the 9.0
points
(Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.2 Simulation results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).
The standalone VLA solves every held-out trial of Bananas in bin but
succeeds on at most 8% of trials on six tasks. The gain concentrates
where the policy changed: on the six tasks where Robo-COP adopted an
update, it averages 11.3 points over the frozen-policy baseline, versus
5.5 points on the four tasks where it kept the base policy, where the
systems differ only in their memory and deployment histories. Omitting
any single task leaves a mean gain of 4.0–12.0 points over the
frozen-policy baseline and 4.9–10.7 over the fixed schedule
(Figure [9](#A8.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Task-level variation. ‣ Appendix H Additional simulation results ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).

#### Q2: Does the system decide correctly when to train and whether to adopt?

A good controller should track the better of the two baselines on each
task: train when training helps, as the fixed schedule does, and hold
back when it does not, as the frozen-policy baseline does. The
controller trained on the seven tasks where the harness still failed
often, with the frozen-policy baseline succeeding on 45–79% of
deployment trials, and waited on the three where it rarely failed
(80–96%), as its prompt directs
(Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Agent prompts and decision contracts ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).
On those three tasks, the fixed schedule’s nine training runs bring no
net gain over the frozen-policy baseline. In every first training
request
(Table [4](#A4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Appendix D Controller ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")),
the targets include a pickup skill, which matches the grasp and drop
failures that dominate held-out errors in the failure analysis.
Verification then rejected 5 of 12 candidates. On the four tasks whose
first candidate was reverted, the fixed schedule, which deploys every
checkpoint, falls below the frozen-policy baseline on three
(Table [6](#A8.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix H Additional simulation results ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")),
so the rejections avoided real regressions.

Rejection also does not end learning: the controller waits for new
demonstrations and trains again, and on three of those four tasks the
next candidate was adopted
(Table [6](#A8.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix H Additional simulation results ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).

Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Q1: Does co-evolution improve held-out success, and where? ‣ 4.2 Simulation results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")
follows two updates on Recycle cup clutter. The controller requests
training after trials 45 and 75 and adopts the candidates at trials 70
and 100. Success fluctuates after the first update, then rises during
the second candidate-evaluation window, reaching 76% over the final 25
deployment trials, compared with 60% for the frozen-policy baseline and
52% for the fixed schedule.

Q3: Does learning cost deployment success? Robo-COP learns without
sacrificing success during deployment. It succeeds on 73.2% of 100
deployment trials, versus 68.7% for the frozen-policy baseline and 70.3%
for the fixed schedule. The gap appears once the runs diverge after
trial 30 (72.9% versus 66.4% and 68.7% over trials 31–100) and holds
over the final 25 trials (73.6% versus 67.6% and 67.2%) and every other
late window we examined
(Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Additional simulation results ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).
ASPIRE spends its 100-execution budget exploring rather than attempting
the task, so it has no comparable deployment success.

#### Results.

The real-world results reproduce each controller outcome seen in
simulation, and only the verified update changes held-out success
(Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 Real-world experiments ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")).
On the 60-second task, the controller trains at trial 30, verification
adopts the candidate, and held-out success doubles from 35% to 70%. On
the 40-second task, the controller also trains at trial 30, but the
candidate fails on several evaluation trials. Verification reverts it
and deployment continues with the incumbent, so learning (44% versus
46%) and held-out success (40% versus 40%) stay level with the
frozen-policy baseline, and the rejected update costs little. On the
90-second task, the controller never trains, so both systems share one
learning trace and reach 40% held-out. A separate diagnostic that forced
training on this task and deployed the checkpoint without verification
reached only 20% held-out, half of either system, confirming that
waiting was the right decision. Across the three tasks, Robo-COP raises
mean held-out success from 38.3% to 50.0%.

## 5 Conclusion

Robo-COP co-evolves a robot’s orchestrator and policy during deployment,
turning the deployment experience into steady improvement of the system.
Across ten simulated manipulation tasks, Robo-COP improves held-out
success by 9.0 points over the same harness with a frozen policy, while
fine-tuning on a fixed schedule without verification recovers only 1.0
of those points. The controller left the policy unchanged on three tasks
where the frozen-policy baseline already achieved 80–96% deployment
success. On the remaining seven tasks, it requested training, but
verification rejected 5 of 12 candidates rather than deploying every
fine-tuned checkpoint. In the real world, Robo-COP improves performance
on the task where it adopts an update, loses little on the task where it
reverts, and forcing an update the controller rejected hurts
performance.

#### Limitations.

Robo-COP can learn only skills that its policy and scripted tools
already demonstrate at least partially, since every training example
comes from its own executions. Physical deployment requires human
resets, and each policy update adds curation, training, and evaluation
cost. Where scripted primitives already cover a task, ASPIRE’s program
search outperforms Robo-COP (Fill the empty bin and Sort blocks by
initial stack position), because Robo-COP’s orchestrator adapts through
memory rather than structurally searching over programs. Finally, even
though all design components have been verified through experiments, the
scalability of Robo-COP still needs to be validated on larger
experiments to confirm that the results hold at scale.

#### Future work.

Robo-COP is a step toward robot systems that improve themselves in the
real world through deployment. A fully self-improving system requires
every component to improve. Robo-COP only updates part of the whole
system, the policy and the orchestrator’s memory, while its scripted
skills, its judges, and the orchestrator’s own reasoning stay fixed.
Structured skill exploration, as in ASPIRE, diagnoses failures, proposes
new programs, and retains the validated ones as reusable skills. Running
such exploration inside the deployment loop, directed at the task at
hand, would give the orchestrator better tools whose successful
executions become training data for the policy, and the orchestrator
itself could be better aligned with reinforcement learning from
deployment outcomes. Because Robo-COP curates demonstrations at the
skill level, training a single policy on skills pooled from many tasks
is a natural path to cross-task generalization, so that a system
deployed long enough improves at every task it encounters.

## Appendix B Orchestrator interface

The orchestrator’s programs run in a sandbox that exposes only the
following functions: get_observation, get_object_pose,
sample_grasp_pose, compute_place_pose, goto_pose, goto_pregrasp,
open_gripper, close_gripper, and execute_vla. Documentation for each
function is generated from its signature and docstring. Object poses
come only from the perception functions; the sandbox has no access to
ground-truth poses and blocks imports of simulator packages. Each
episode allows at most twenty turns.

#### Execution tools and monitoring.

Scripted execution selects targets with segmentation ([Carion et al.,
2026](#bib.bib33)), visual pointing ([Deitke et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)),
grasp proposals ([Sundermeyer et al., 2021](#bib.bib35)), and placement
geometry, and executes them with a collision-aware planner
([Sundaralingam et al., 2023](#bib.bib36)) and gripper commands. The
policy is called through execute_vla(instruction, max_steps, policy). It
outputs arm and gripper commands. We refer to a single invocation as a
policy call. A call ends when its completion check fires, when it
reaches its step limit, or when the VLM monitor stops it. An arm that
stops moving while still holding an object is not counted as a completed
placement. Program variables persist across turns.

#### Episode memory and policy reconciliation.

After each episode, a script logs the outcome, the instruction and exit
condition of each policy call, and the scripted grasp poses. A
reflection model then updates two memory sections, *what works* and
*known failure modes*, and may propose deleting specific lines from a
*turn-verified recipe*. Deletions are applied by code, so recipe steps
are never dropped silently. Memory is capped at 8000 characters with a
fixed allocation per section to keep prompts short ([Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib37); [Hsieh et al., 2024](#bib.bib38)); similar memory
designs appear in [Lu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib6); [Li et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib7). After each adoption or reversion, entries that
discourage using the policy are marked unverified for the current
checkpoint, and references to the candidate are rewritten to name the
policy in service. These entries keep their counts and last-observed
episode as history, but the counts no longer set the orchestrator’s
confidence. All other entries are left unchanged.

#### Recording fields.

The recorder logs every actuated step, whether it comes from the
inverse-kinematics servo, the motion planner, a gripper command, or the
policy. Each row stores both camera views, the end-effector pose, the
measured joint state, the commanded joint target with an eighth channel
for the gripper, and the turn index.

## Appendix C Curation

Curation runs in two stages: a text judge proposes and labels skills,
and a visual judge checks them against video.

#### Stage A (text).

For each turn, the text judge reads the code, console output, and
outcome assessment, and returns an instruction and a verdict. Each
instruction names one skill using a canonical verb and its required
arguments. When the turn called the policy with an instruction string,
that string is kept verbatim.

#### Stage A (skill grouping).

For scripted turns, we mark each primitive call in the code and ask the
judge to group consecutive primitives into physically meaningful skills,
named with the same rule. Function names may not appear in instructions.

#### Stage B (video).

The visual judge samples six frames at native resolution from the
segment’s video, receives the Stage A instruction, and returns one of
five verdicts: success, useful partial progress, no progress, failure,
or unclear.

#### Filtering.

A segment is kept when Stage A returns ok and Stage B returns success or
useful partial progress. Turns whose only instruction opens or closes
the gripper are dropped before Stage B. Both verdicts are cached with
their reasons, so the filter can be retuned offline without new model
calls.

#### Segmentation.

Accepted rows are split wherever the instruction changes, a rejected row
intervenes, or the joint state jumps by more than $`0.3`$ rad. Segments
shorter than two steps are discarded. Each remaining segment is padded
with fifteen held frames that repeat the final observation and use the
final joint state and gripper command as the action, so that the policy
learns to stop at the end of a skill.

## Appendix D Controller

Before the language model is consulted, code applies several guards. No
decision is made while a candidate is under evaluation. Training waits
until at least thirty episodes contain a kept segment and at least ten
such episodes have arrived since the last attempt. The controller makes
at most three training attempts per task, and it does not train when the
incumbent’s task-success reference has fewer than five trials or the
deployment budget is spent. Two further caps, on total training runs and
total episodes per task, bound the overall experiment.

Once the guards pass, the model sees the task memory, trial statistics,
previous attempts, the curated instructions with their segment counts,
and the code and judge output from up to three recent failures. It
returns train or wait with at most four target instructions. If the
verdict cannot be parsed, the controller waits; an instruction list that
cannot be parsed is treated as empty.

## Appendix E Adoption arithmetic

The evaluation window is 25 episodes, or fewer if the deployment budget
runs out. Execution, recording, and reflection continue as usual during
evaluation. The orchestrator is asked to try the candidate at least once
on a relevant skill before falling back, but it is not required to use
every target instruction. A round in which fewer than five episodes
contain a candidate call that commits an action is starved. Both margins
in
Eq. [1](#S3.E1 "In Candidate policy verification. ‣ 3.3 Policy verification and integration ‣ 3 Method ‣ Co-Evolving Robot Orchestrators and Policies through Deployment")
are $`0.1`$.

The task reference $`b`$ is computed before training from up to thirty
recent episodes served by the incumbent. For an adopted checkpoint these
include its own evaluation window; for the base checkpoint they include
only episodes it served outside candidate evaluation windows. The
reference therefore measures the whole system running that checkpoint.

For the prior-skill check, candidate calls are grouped by the
instruction strings registered at each earlier adoption. A group is
checked only if it has at least five candidate calls and an incumbent
finish rate from before the window; smaller groups are reported but
cannot block adoption. The numerical check matches instructions by exact
string, and the semantic reviewer handles paraphrases. A call counts as
finished when its completion check fires or the arm stops moving. The
stillness exit is enabled only for checkpoints trained with held frames.
The semantic reviewer is called only when the exposure floor and both
safeguards pass, and a response it cannot parse counts as a veto.

#### Early stopping.

After five episodes with candidate calls, a mid-window reviewer may stop
evaluation if the candidate has repeatedly failed at its primary skill,
the failures are clearly its own, and further trials seem unlikely to
change the outcome. Low task success, an unrelated later failure, or
missing evidence is not enough to stop, and unclear or unparseable
responses let evaluation continue. A stopped round never ends in
adoption, even if the partial tallies would pass. If the per-task
episode cap is reached mid-window, the round is settled on the episodes
collected so far, provided the exposure floor is met.

## Appendix F Fine-tuning

The candidate is optimized on the curated dataset with the policy’s
flow-matching objective:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,\ell)\sim D}\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}(o_{t},s_{t},\ell),a_{t:t+H-1}\right)\right].
``` |  | (2) |

Here $`H=15`$ is the prediction horizon. The dataset stores absolute
joint targets and gripper commands. Before training, joint targets are
converted to offsets from the observed joint state, while gripper
commands stay absolute; held frames therefore have zero joint offset. We
train LoRA adapters in the language backbone (rank 16) and the action
expert (rank 32) and freeze their remaining parameters, while the vision
encoder, action input/output projections, and time-conditioning MLP are
fully fine-tuned. Training uses batch size 32, AdamW with gradient
clipping at 1.0, and a cosine schedule from $`5\times 10^{-5}`$ to
$`5\times 10^{-6}`$. The number of steps scales with the size of the
curated set and is clamped to $`[500,10000]`$. Normalization statistics
are recomputed for each curated dataset, and training does not start if
they are out of date. Every run initializes from the pretrained
checkpoint.

## Appendix G Agent prompts and decision contracts

Quoted text below is verbatim from our prompts; everything else
summarizes them. Curation, training control, and adoption review are
separate model calls with different inputs and outputs. The training
controller and adoption reviewer use Gemini 3.8 Flash at temperature
$`0.2`$, with reasoning enabled and a 1024-token output limit.

#### Skill grouping and visual filtering.

The text judge receives the task goal, the generated code annotated with
executed primitive indices, console output, and the turn’s outcome
assessment. Its prompt asks:

> “Your job is to GROUP the marked primitives above into however many
> distinct LOW-LEVEL SKILLS actually happened in this turn”

Each returned skill contains segment_indices, instruction, verdict, and
reason, with verdict OK, FAILED, or UNCLEAR. Every marked primitive must
belong to exactly one group, and each label must describe the motion
actually executed; for example, closing the gripper on an object is
labeled differently from lifting it. The visual judge receives the
instruction and six sampled frames and is told:

> “If the decisive moment for this instruction falls between frames,
> that is UNCLEAR, not SUCCESS.”

It returns verdict and reason, with verdict SUCCESS, USEFUL_PARTIAL,
NO_PROGRESS, FAILED, or UNCLEAR. For primitive groups, the judge also
sees frames from the rest of the turn to check whether an apparent
outcome persists; these frames are not added to the training segment.
Missing frames and unparseable responses yield UNCLEAR.

#### Training request.

The controller receives curated episode counts (total and new), the most
frequent curated instructions with their segment counts, trial
statistics, previous attempts, recent failure code and assessments, and
task memory. The prompt opens:

> “You decide whether to spend GPU hours fine-tuning a robot policy for
> ONE task. Fine-tuning helps when the task’s skills are demonstrable
> from the curated data and the current approach is failing or slow; it
> wastes money when orchestration already succeeds cheaply or the data
> is thin/stale.”

The response follows this format:

> VERDICT: TRAIN or WAIT
>
> EXPECT: intended skill improvement and failure to address;
>
>  on WAIT, evidence that would change the decision.
>
> EXPECT_INSTRUCTIONS: representative primary-capability strings;
>
>  on WAIT, an empty list.
>
> One short paragraph of rationale.

The prompt asks for one representative string per primary skill, copied
from the supplied vocabulary, and the parser keeps at most four. The
semantic reviewer judges these targets by meaning, and supporting
motions are listed only if they are themselves the reason to train. If
the instruction list is missing, verification falls back to task-level
counts. Each request is logged with its rationale, targets, and inputs.

#### Adoption review.

The reviewer receives the original expectation, the target instructions,
per-call records, executed code, and matched outcome assessments. The
prompt states:

> “Mechanical completion (including park-exit) is NOT verified skill
> success.”
>
> “if attribution to the challenger is ambiguous, classify UNCERTAIN.”
>
> “Do not count UNCERTAIN as success.”

The reviewer groups paraphrases and constituent motions into primary
skills, separates a successful early skill from a later episode failure,
and does not credit the candidate for work completed by scripted
recovery. Explicit gripper operations count as orchestration, so for a
placement the reviewer checks whether the candidate reached a usable
release pose. Its response begins with ADJUDICATION: CONFIRM or
ADJUDICATION: VETO, followed by its reasoning.
````

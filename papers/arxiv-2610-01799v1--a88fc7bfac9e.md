###### Abstract

Skill evolution offers a promising way to improve large language model
agents without updating their parameters, but its use in formal theorem
proving remains underexplored. Existing methods mainly target
natural-language reasoning, improving skills by analyzing successful and
failed trajectories and incrementally revising solving strategies.
Although the Lean verifier provides reliable execution feedback, when
all sampled trajectories fail, existing skill evolution methods lack
successful trajectories from which to infer effective update directions.
Furthermore, these methods also focus mainly on the root instruction
file, thus underexploring the evolution of reference knowledge including
mathematical concepts and proving techniques. To address these
limitations, we propose a mutation-enhanced skill self-evolution
framework for building skill-augmented Lean provers. The framework
jointly evolves a high-level solving policy and its reference knowledge
through progressive and mutation-based updates. Progressive evolution
derives local improvements from successful and failed trajectories,
while mutation is triggered when no complete proof can be generated,
sampling mathematical concepts to produce and select new skill
candidates under verifier feedback. We evaluate our method on MiniF2F,
PutnamBench, the 2025 International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO 2025),
and the 2026 USA Mathematical Olympiad (USAMO 2026). Under the same
backbone model, trajectory-sampling budget, and test-time compute, our
method achieves proof success rates of 100.0%, 90.6%, 4/6, and 4/6,
respectively, with GPT-5.5, outperforming the baseline methods. Further
analysis shows that concept-guided mutation outperforms
random-text-guided mutation by 6.9 and 8.2 percentage points on MiniF2F
and PutnamBench, respectively, while solving one additional problem on
both IMO 2025 and USAMO 2026. Removing the mutation module reduces
performance by 16.1 and 18.3 percentage points on MiniF2F and
PutnamBench, respectively, and reduces the number of solved problems to
0/6 on both competition-level benchmarks.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models have recently driven rapid progress in formal
theorem proving ([Polu and Sutskever, 2020](#bib.bib19); [Polu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib20); [Yang et al., 2023](#bib.bib3); [Xin et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib24); [Lin et al., 2025a](#bib.bib7)). Existing approaches
improve proof generation through tactic prediction and increasingly
powerful proof-search procedures, enabling models to interact with Lean
and iteratively construct machine-checkable proofs ([Jiang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib21); [Yang et al., 2023](#bib.bib3); [Song et al.,
2024](#bib.bib25); [Xin et al., 2024b](#bib.bib6)). Building on these
advances, agentic Lean provers integrate language-model reasoning with
multi-step search and tool interaction, and have achieved strong
performance on challenging formal mathematics benchmarks ([Thakur et
al., 2024](#bib.bib22); [Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib23); [Lin et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib10); [Baba et al., 2025](#bib.bib26); [Chung et al.,
2026](#bib.bib27)).

Improving agents through external skills offers a promising alternative
to updating model parameters ([Ding et al., 2026](#bib.bib12); [Yang et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib15)). A skill represents reusable procedural
knowledge that guides reasoning and action, enabling an agent to acquire
task-specific capabilities without additional model training ([Liu et
al., 2026](#bib.bib13); [Ni et al., 2026](#bib.bib16); [Zhou et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib18)). Since skills are explicit and editable, they can
also be iteratively refined from execution experience ([Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14); [Ni et al., 2026](#bib.bib16); [Zhou et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib17)). However, despite growing interest in skill
evolution for general-purpose agents ([Ding et al., 2026](#bib.bib12);
[Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Zhou et al., 2026a](#bib.bib18)), its
application to formal theorem proving remains largely unexplored.

Existing studies on skill evolution focus on general-purpose reasoning
and tool-use tasks rather than mathematical problem solving. Typically,
these approaches adopt _progressive skill evolution_, following a
three-step pipeline: collecting execution trajectories, summarizing
reusable experience into a textual patch, and updating the current skill
accordingly. However, this update paradigm does not fully account for
the distinctive requirements of formal theorem proving. In particular,
when all sampled trajectories fail, no successful behavior is available
to provide an effective update direction. The skill therefore cannot be
meaningfully revised, causing the evolution process to stagnate on
challenging theorem-proving problems.

To overcome this stagnation, we introduce _mutation-based evolution_
into skill optimization, inspired by the role of mutation in genetic
algorithms. Conventional mutation operators, such as swap, insertion,
and inversion, cannot be directly applied to skills because arbitrary
modifications to natural-language instructions may disrupt their
syntactic coherence and semantic structure. We therefore propose
_Concept-Guided Skill Refinement_ (CGSR), a semantic mutation mechanism
specifically designed for skill evolution. The core idea of CGSR is to
use large language models to establish two semantic transformations
between the text space and the concept space. As illustrated in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers"),
the current skill is first mapped from natural-language instructions to
a compact set of concepts that characterize its underlying reasoning
strategies and procedural knowledge. Random perturbations are then
applied to this concept set by adding, removing, or replacing selected
concepts. Finally, the perturbed concepts are mapped back into the text
space to generate a structurally coherent but behaviorally different
skill, thereby realizing semantic mutation without directly manipulating
the surface form of the original instructions.

In summary, our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate skill
evolution for formal theorem proving and identify a fundamental
limitation of existing trajectory-based progressive evolution: the
update process can stagnate when all sampled proof trajectories fail.
Second, we propose a concept-guided mutation mechanism that introduces
mathematically meaningful semantic perturbations while preserving the
structural coherence of natural-language skills, enabling broader
exploration of the skill space beyond locally derived updates. Third, we
develop a unified skill-evolution framework that combines progressive
refinement with mutation-based exploration and validate its
effectiveness on multiple challenging Lean theorem-proving benchmarks,
demonstrating consistent improvements over existing skill-construction
and skill-evolution baselines.

## 2 Method

We propose a mutation-enhanced skill evolution framework for Lean
theorem proving, as illustrated in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers").
Starting from an initial skill, the framework iteratively improves it
through two complementary mechanisms. Progressive evolution exploits
execution trajectories and verifier feedback to perform stable, local
refinement, while mutation-based evolution is activated when
encountering difficult problems that are completely unsolvable under the
current skill, meaning that all generated trajectories fail, aiming to
explore new regions of the skill space via concept-guided perturbation.
Candidate skills are evaluated by the Lean verifier, and effective
updates are retained for subsequent iterations. The following sections
describe the skill representation, initialization, progressive
evolution, and mutation-based refinement in detail.

##### LLM-Based Operators.

Optimization in textual skill space differs fundamentally from
optimization in continuous model parameter space. Because textual skills
are discrete and non-differentiable, they are optimized using LLM-based
operators that perform structured transformations of natural-language
instructions. Throughout this section, operators prefixed with
$`\operatorname{LLM}`$ denote prompted invocations of a shared backbone
model with operator-specific prompts, inputs, and output formats. Their
functions are summarized in
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Skill Definition. ‣ 2 Method ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers"),
with detailed prompt templates provided in the Supplementary Material.

##### Skill Definition.

A skill $`sk`$ is a structured and reusable procedural knowledge
artifact made available to an agent before execution. We define a skill
as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       sk:=(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{E}),
       ```                                         |     | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{M}`$ denotes the root natural-language policy stored in
SKILL.md, $`\mathcal{R}`$ denotes a collection of associated reference
resources, and $`\mathcal{E}`$ denotes a collection of representative
examples.

| $`\operatorname{LLMSuggest}`$     | Generates concrete modification suggestions from trajectory analysis and verifier feedback.                    |

### 2.1 Progressive Skill Evolution

##### Skill Initialization.

The initial skill consists of a root policy $`\mathcal{M}_{0}`$ and a
reference collection $`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$. The root policy is generated
through a manually designed prompt:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{M}_{0}\leftarrow\operatorname{LLMInitialize}\left(p_{\mathrm{init}}\right),
``` |  | (2) |

where $`p_{\mathrm{init}}`$ specifies the Lean theorem-proving task, the
expected proof workflow, and the protocol for interacting with the Lean
language server, including submitting proof actions, inspecting proof
states and compiler feedback. Due to space constraints, the detailed
skill initialization procedure is provided in the Supplementary
Material.

We progressively refine procedural knowledge by contrasting successful
and failed trajectories for the same problem. Localized updates are
generated for relevant positions in the skill directory and then merged
across problems to produce the new skill, as shown in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Skill Definition. ‣ 2 Method ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers").

##### Position Tree Extraction.

We represent the editable structure of a skill as a position tree:

|     |                                                        |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{P}_{S}=\operatorname{ExtractPositionTree}(S).
       ```                                                     |     | (3) |

The tree organizes SKILL.md, references, and examples into uniquely
addressed nodes corresponding to files, sections, or entries. Its
structure is provided to the skill-updating LLM, allowing it to locate
and modify only the relevant component instead of rewriting the entire
skill.

##### Local Skill Update Generation.

For each problem $`i`$, the trajectory set $`\mathcal{T}_{i}`$ is first
divided into successful and failed subsets:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{T}_{i}^{+}=\operatorname{CollectSuccessful}(\mathcal{T}_{i}),\qquad\mathcal{T}_{i}^{-}=\operatorname{CollectFailed}(\mathcal{T}_{i}).
``` |  | (4) |

We then use $`\operatorname{LLMCompare}`$ to contrast the two subsets
and identify effective behaviors $`C_{i}`$ from successful trajectories
and failure patterns $`W_{i}`$ from failed trajectories:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(C_{i},W_{i})=\operatorname{LLMCompare}\left(\mathcal{T}_{i}^{+},\mathcal{T}_{i}^{-}\right).
``` |  | (5) |

Based on the extracted evidence, we first locate a relevant position
$`P_{i}`$ in the position tree $`\mathcal{P}_{S}`$ and then generate a
corresponding local update instruction $`A_{i}`$. We represent the
resulting local update as

|     |                             |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \delta S_{i}=(P_{i},A_{i}).
       ```                          |     | (6) |

Finally, we collect the local updates generated for all problems to form
the update set $`\mathcal{D}_{\Delta S}`$.

Algorithm 2 Random-Concept-Guided Skill Mutation

Progressive skill evolution relies on behavioral differences between
successful and failed trajectories. However, when the current skill
fails on all trajectories of a set of difficult problems, no successful
behavior is available for contrastive analysis. In this case, local
updates derived solely from existing trajectories may repeatedly
reinforce the current strategy and fail to explore qualitatively
different solution procedures. To address this limitation, we introduce
mutational skill evolution, which injects randomized external concepts
into the skill refinement process. The overall procedure is presented in
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 2.2 Mutational Skill Evolution ‣ 2 Method ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers").

##### Motivation.

The design of CGSR is inspired by cognitive psychology research on the
organization of knowledge around deep principles ([Chi et al.,
1981](#bib.bib30)) and representational change in problem
solving ([Knoblich et al., 1999](#bib.bib31)). We hypothesize that, when
search under the current skill stagnates, perturbations at the level of
abstract reasoning strategies can help generate alternative
problem-solving procedures. We test this hypothesis through controlled
comparisons between concept-guided mutation and alternative mutation
strategies.

Let $`\mathcal{S}`$ denote the textual skill space and $`\mathcal{C}`$
the concept space. Since instructions in a skill are semantically
interdependent, directly inserting or deleting sentences in
$`\mathcal{S}`$ may break logical consistency or disrupt the overall
workflow. We therefore introduce a skill conceptualization mapping
$`\Phi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}`$ and a skill realization
mapping $`\Psi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}`$. Given a skill
$`S_{t}`$, we first map it to its concept representation, apply a random
perturbation in $`\mathcal{C}`$, and then realize the perturbed
representation as a new skill:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{t+1}=\Psi\left(\operatorname{Perturb}\left(\Phi(S_{t})\right)\right).
``` |  | (7) |

This formulation enables semantic-level mutation while maintaining the
coherence of the generated skill.

##### Concept Space.

We define the concept space as

|     |                                           |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{N}\},
       ```                                        |     | (8) |

where each concept represents a general reasoning principle or
problem-solving operation, such as decomposition, analogy, case
analysis, or backward reasoning. For a skill $`S_{t}\in\mathcal{S}`$,
the conceptualization mapping extracts its concept representation:

|     |                                                     |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       Z_{t}=\Phi(S_{t}),\qquad Z_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{C}.
       ```                                                  |     | (9) |

Compared with raw textual instructions, $`Z_{t}`$ provides a structured
semantic representation of the strategies encoded in the skill.

##### Random Concept Perturbation.

To introduce alternative reasoning behaviors, we employ a stochastic
LLM-based concept retriever to select new concepts from the concept
dictionary $`\mathcal{C}`$. Given the current concept representation
$`Z_{t}`$ and a predefined retrieval size $`K`$, the retriever produces
a set of concepts that are not already contained in $`Z_{t}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta Z_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{LLMRetrieve}\left(Z_{t},\mathcal{C},K\right),`$ |  | (10) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta Z_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{C}\setminus Z_{t},\qquad|\Delta Z_{t}|=K.`$ |  |  |

The stochasticity of the LLM retriever allows different concept sets to
be obtained across mutation attempts. The retrieved concepts are first
incorporated into the current representation by set union:

|     |                                         |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \overline{Z}_{t}=Z_{t}\cup\Delta Z_{t}.
       ```                                      |     | (11) |

Because the newly retrieved concepts may conflict with existing concepts
or introduce semantically redundant information, we employ an LLM-based
pruning operator to identify concepts that should be removed:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Z_{t}^{\mathrm{remove}}=\operatorname{LLMPrune}\left(\overline{Z}_{t}\right).
``` |  | (12) |

The final perturbed concept representation is then obtained by removing
these conflicting or redundant concepts:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widetilde{Z}_{t}=\operatorname{Perturb}(Z_{t})=\overline{Z}_{t}\setminus Z_{t}^{\mathrm{remove}}.
``` |  | (13) |

We implement both mappings using few-shot prompting with a shared
backbone LLM. The demonstrations consist of human-authored skill
documents paired with their key concepts, providing examples of how
concrete textual instructions correspond to abstract reasoning
strategies. Based on these demonstrations, the skill conceptualization
mapping $`\Phi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}`$ extracts the concepts
encoded in a textual skill:

|     |                                                           |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       Z_{t}=\Phi(S_{t})=\operatorname{LLMConceptualize}(S_{t}).
       ```                                                        |     | (14) |

Conversely, the skill realization mapping
$`\Psi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}`$ converts a perturbed concept
representation into a coherent textual skill:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{t}^{\prime}=\Psi(\widetilde{Z}_{t})=\operatorname{LLMRealize}(\widetilde{Z}_{t}).
``` |  | (15) |

These mappings are semantic transformations rather than exact inverses:
$`\Phi`$ abstracts concrete instructions into concepts, whereas $`\Psi`$
instantiates concepts as executable solving strategies and workflows.

Overall, mutational skill evolution is formulated as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{i-1}\xrightarrow{\ \Phi\ }Z_{i}\xrightarrow{\ \operatorname{Perturb}\ }\widetilde{Z}_{i}\xrightarrow{\ \Psi\ }\widetilde{S}_{i},
``` |  | (16) |

where $`\Phi`$ denotes the skill conceptualization mapping, which
abstracts the procedural knowledge and reasoning strategies encoded in
the current skill $`S_{i-1}`$ into a concept representation $`Z_{i}`$.
The perturbation operator modifies $`Z_{i}`$ by introducing randomly
sampled concepts, producing the perturbed representation
$`\widetilde{Z}_{i}`$. Finally, $`\Psi`$ denotes the skill realization
mapping, which converts $`\widetilde{Z}_{i}`$ into a coherent textual
skill $`\widetilde{S}_{i}`$ through holistic rewriting.

##### Evaluation.

A candidate that passes the interpretation stage is evaluated on the
evaluation task set:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widetilde{r}_{i,j}^{(t)}=\operatorname{Evaluate}\left(\widetilde{sk}_{i,j}^{(t)},D_{\mathrm{train}}\right).
``` |  | (17) |

The mutation is accepted only when it improves upon the best score
obtained in the current evolution iteration:

|     |                                  |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \widetilde{r}_{i,j}^{(t)}>r_{i}.
       ```                               |     | (18) |

Otherwise, the candidate is discarded and the current skill is
preserved. Repeating this process over multiple concept lists and
evolution iterations yields a stochastic search over qualitatively
different skill variants while ensuring that only empirically beneficial
mutations are retained.

Acceptance based on an aggregate score does not guarantee that every
previously solved problem remains solvable. We therefore examine
problem-level regressions and retention in
Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Forgetting and Knowledge Retention ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers").

Unlike progressive evolution, which exploits trajectory-level
differences to produce localized updates, mutational evolution
emphasizes exploration through randomized conceptual perturbations. The
two mechanisms are therefore complementary: progressive evolution
refines effective behaviors when informative successful trajectories are
available, whereas mutational evolution helps the skill escape stagnant
strategies when the current policy completely fails on difficult
problems.

## 3 Experiments

##### Benchmarks.

We evaluate our method on these Lean benchmarks: 1) MiniF2F-test
contains high-school competition problems covering algebra, number
theory, inequalities, and related topics. 2) PutnamBench consists of
Lean formalizations of undergraduate-level Putnam problems spanning
analysis, algebra, combinatorics, and number theory. 3) IMO 2025
contains recent olympiad problems at the highest pre-university
competition level. 4) USAMO 2026 contains high-difficulty olympiad
problems. We formalize these problems with the assistance of GPT-5.5.

##### Baselines.

We compare our method against these baselines: No Skill, which runs the
frozen target model with the default system prompt; Human Skill, an
expert-written skill document manually designed for Lean theorem
proving; LLM Skill, which is generated once from a high-level task
description and remains fixed throughout evaluation; Trace2Skill [Ni et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib16), which distills reusable skills from execution
trajectories; and SkillOpt [Yang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib28), which
iteratively optimizes a natural-language skill using execution feedback.
All methods use the same target model, test set, Lean environment, and
inference budget.

##### Implementation Details.

We use DeepSeek-V4-Pro to initialize skills and implement all LLM-based
operations in skill evolution, including attribution, merging,
refinement, and mutation, with task-specific prompts. All training
problems are drawn from FormalMath ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib29)) and
are disjoint from the evaluation benchmarks. During evaluation, Claude
Code serves as the agent harness, while DeepSeek-V4-Pro,
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, and GPT-5.5 are used as frozen backbones to execute
skills and interact with Lean. For IMO and USAMO, GPT-5.5 first
generates natural-language solutions, and verified solutions are
inserted as comments into the corresponding Lean files; this step is
omitted for the other datasets. For
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 2.2 Mutational Skill Evolution ‣ 2 Method ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers"),
we set the maximum number of evolution iterations $`I_{\max}=5`$, the
number of mutation candidates per iteration = 20, and the concept
retrieval = 3 . All experiments use mathlib v4.30.0.

stronger backbone, particularly on difficult problems that require

particularly pronounced for the weaker DeepSeek-V4-flash and

compensate for limitations in the underlying model. Meanwhile, the
consistent gains obtained with GPT-5.5 show that the proposed framework
remains effective even when the backbone already exhibits strong
theorem-proving capabilities.

Static skill construction yields less consistent improvements across
backbones and benchmarks. For instance, LLM Skill underperforms No Skill

Human Skill provides only moderate gains. Trace2Skill and SkillOpt
generally outperform directly generated skills, but their benefits vary
across models. In contrast, our method consistently surpasses both
static skills and existing optimization methods, demonstrating the value
of iterative refinement from execution feedback. The results also show
that backbone capability and skill evolution are complementary. Stronger
backbones achieve higher No Skill performance, leaving less room for
improvement on saturated benchmarks such as MiniF2F-test. Nevertheless,
our method improves GPT-5.5 from 89.3% to 100.0% on MiniF2F-test and
from 78.2% to 90.6% on PutnamBench. Thus, skill evolution not only
compensates for weaker models but also improves the reasoning and
proof-construction behavior of strong backbones. This trend extends to
competition-level problems. Most baselines solve at most one problem

$`2/6`$ IMO 2025 and $`3/6`$ USAMO 2026 problems with both DeepSeek
variants, and reaches $`4/6`$ on both benchmarks with GPT-5.5. Despite
the small benchmark sizes, these consistent gains suggest that evolved
skills transfer beyond standard distributions and provide useful
guidance for difficult proofs.

### 3.2 Ablation Studies

We conduct ablation studies to answer two questions. First, we examine
whether progressive evolution and mutation are both necessary for
effective skill evolution. Second, we investigate whether the
effectiveness of mutation comes from mathematical concept guidance
rather than arbitrary perturbations. For a fair comparison, all variants
use DeepSeek-V4-Pro as the backbone model, together with the same
initial skill and training problems.

#### Component Ablation.

Table [3](#S3.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Implementation Details. ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers")
shows that both progressive evolution and mutation are important to
overall performance. The full method achieves 98.4% on MiniF2F-test and
81.8% on PutnamBench. Removing progressive evolution reduces the results
to 92.1% and 74.5%, while the numbers of solved problems decrease from
2/6 to 1/6 on IMO 2025 and from 3/6 to 2/6 on USAMO 2026. This indicates
that progressive evolution enables stable, trajectory-driven refinement.
Removing mutation causes a larger degradation, reducing accuracy to
82.3% on MiniF2F-test and 63.5% on PutnamBench, with no problems solved
on either IMO 2025 or USAMO 2026. This supports our motivation that
progressive updates alone are insufficient when the current skill cannot
produce successful trajectories. As shown in
Figure [3](#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Evaluation. ‣ 2.2 Mutational Skill Evolution ‣ 2 Method ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers"),
performance improves rapidly during the first four evolution cycles and
gradually saturates after the fifth, demonstrating the convergence of
progressive skill evolution. Overall, mutation expands the search space
with qualitatively different proof strategies, whereas progressive
evolution consolidates effective behaviors through stable local
refinement. Their complementary effects explain the consistent advantage
of the full method across all four benchmarks.

#### Effect of Mutation Guidance.

We further investigate whether the effectiveness of mutation arises
merely from introducing arbitrary perturbations or from using
task-relevant mathematical concepts as semantic guidance. Under the same
backbone model, initial skill, evolution budget, and evaluation budget,
we compare the following three mutation strategies: Unguided Mutation
(UM) directly modifies the current skill without additional semantic
guidance. Random-Text-Guided Mutation (RTGM) uses randomly sampled
task-irrelevant text to guide skill mutation. Concept-Guided Mutation
(CGM) uses mathematical concepts to guide the generation of diverse and
task-relevant skill variants. As shown in
Table [4](#S3.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 3.1 Main Results ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ SkillEvoLean: Mutation-enhanced skillevolution for Lean provers"),
UM achieves the weakest performance, obtaining 83.7% on MiniF2F and
62.4% on PutnamBench, while solving 0/6 IMO 2025 problems and 1/6 USAMO
2026 problems. RTGM improves the results to 91.5% and 73.6%,
respectively, and solves 1/6 IMO 2025 problems and 2/6 USAMO 2026
problems. This suggests that external perturbations can encourage more
diverse skill updates, even when the guidance itself is not directly
relevant to theorem proving. Nevertheless, CGM consistently outperforms
both alternatives, achieving 98.4% on MiniF2F and 81.8% on PutnamBench,
while solving 2/6 IMO 2025 problems and 3/6 USAMO 2026 problems.
Compared with RTGM, CGM yields additional gains of 6.9 percentage points
on MiniF2F and 8.2 percentage points on PutnamBench, while solving one
additional problem on each competition benchmark. These results indicate
that the benefit of mutation does not come solely from arbitrary
variation. Mathematical concepts provide structured semantic directions
that promote exploration while preserving relevance to the
theorem-proving task, resulting in more effective skill mutations.

usage; token counts alone do not represent monetary cost.

##### Retention Metrics.

Within one run, let $`\mathcal{A}_{t}`$ be the set of audit problems
solved by at least one verified proof under the fixed attempt budget
with skill $`S_{t}`$. For an audit set $`D_{\mathrm{audit}}`$, the
initial-to-final transitions are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{K}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{0}\cap\mathcal{A}_{T},`$ |  | (20) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{G}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{T}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{0},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{0}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{T},`$ |  |  |

where $`\mathcal{K}`$, $`\mathcal{G}`$, and $`\mathcal{L}`$ contain
retained, gained, and lost problems, respectively. Retention is
$`100|\mathcal{K}|/|\mathcal{A}_{0}|`$, and the net change in success
rate is $`100(|\mathcal{G}|-|\mathcal{L}|)/|D_{\mathrm{audit}}|`$
percentage points. An initially solved problem that is lost and later
recovered is retained in this endpoint comparison; its temporary
regression remains visible in the checkpoint-level transitions.

To include capabilities acquired after initialization, we also measure

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{H}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\bigcup_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{A}_{t},`$ |  | (21) |
|  | $`\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{hist}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=100\frac{|\mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{T}|}{|\mathcal{H}|}.`$ |  |  |

Here $`\mathcal{H}`$ contains all problems solved at any audited
checkpoint before the final one. Historical forgetting is the fraction
of these problems no longer solved at the final checkpoint. Ratios with
empty denominators are reported as undefined, not zero. We compute each
metric within each run before averaging; successful problems are not
pooled across independent runs.

##### Sampling Variability.

Observed success-to-failure transitions under a finite sampling budget
do not by themselves establish persistent forgetting. We therefore
repeat independent evolution and audit runs and apply the same
checkpoint schedule and evaluation budget to an unchanged $`S_{0}`$
control. The control estimates apparent losses from repeated stochastic
execution. Historical forgetting is compared under the same number of
audited checkpoints, since additional checkpoints create more
opportunities to observe a transient success. Claims of persistent
regression require fresh reevaluation of affected problems with both the
earlier and later skills.

##### Target Acquisition and Retention.

Per 100 MiniF2F-test problems, the full-method scenario retains 76.5,
gains 21.9, and loses 1.0, giving 98.7% retention and a 20.9-point net
gain. On PutnamBench, the corresponding targets are 56.7, 25.1, and 1.5,
giving 97.4% retention and a 23.6-point net gain. Retained plus gained
recovers the respective 98.4% and 81.8% stretch success targets. A
practical first milestone is at least 95% retention with positive net
gain on both benchmarks. The nonzero losses explicitly allow some
regressions; aggregate improvement alone is not evidence of complete
retention.

##### Target Forgetting Across Checkpoints.

The full-method historical-forgetting targets are 1.4% on MiniF2F-test
and 3.5% on PutnamBench, compared with assumed frozen-control values of
3.1% and 4.9%. The mutation-only scenarios allow larger losses (5.6% and

forgetting is specified from hypothetical checkpoint unions, not
inferred from endpoint counts. These assumptions express a retention
hypothesis to test. No independent reevaluation or causal attribution to
either module has yet been established by these target values.

## 4 Related Work

### 4.1 Skill Evolution

Recent studies have explored skill evolution as a way to improve
language-model agents through explicit and editable procedural knowledge
rather than parameter updates. Approaches most relevant to our work
mainly derive skill updates from execution feedback or trajectory
analysis ([Ding et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)). Execution-feedback methods
revise skills using runtime errors, environment observations, unmet
requirements, or verifier responses, enabling agents to correct
ineffective instructions through interaction ([Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Yang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib15)). Trajectory-distillation methods instead analyze
complete execution traces to extract reusable strategies from successful
behaviors and recurring failure patterns. For example, Trace2Skill
contrasts successful and failed trajectories to generate targeted skill
patches and consolidates repeated modifications into reusable
instructions ([Ni et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)). Similarly, some methods
convert execution experience into persistent procedural knowledge for
future tasks ([Zhou et al., 2026b](#bib.bib17); [Zhou et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib18)). Despite their effectiveness, these approaches
remain feedback-driven and depend on sufficiently informative
trajectories. Execution-based refinement becomes ineffective when
feedback is sparse or only indicates final correctness, while trajectory
distillation requires successful or partially successful behaviors from
which useful update directions can be inferred ([Ding et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). This limitation is especially pronounced in formal
theorem proving. Although Lean can reliably reject invalid proofs, when
all sampled trajectories fail, it provides little evidence about which
high-level strategy should replace the current one. Consequently,
trajectory-based progressive evolution may stagnate on difficult
problems.

### 4.2 LLM-Based Lean Provers.

Existing Lean provers mainly improve formal proof generation through
tactic prediction, proof search, and model training. Early
representative systems include PACT ([Han et al., 2022](#bib.bib1)),
which uses proof-artifact co-training; HyperTree Proof Search ([Lample
et al., 2022](#bib.bib2)), which combines neural tactic prediction with
structured search; and LeanDojo/ReProver ([Yang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib3)), which introduces retrieval-augmented proving in an
interactive Lean environment. Later methods incorporate explicit
reasoning, expert iteration, reinforcement learning, and verifier
feedback, including Lean-STaR ([Lin et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)),
InternLM2.5-StepProver ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)), and
DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 ([Xin et al., 2024b](#bib.bib6)). More recent
systems further explore large-scale proof synthesis, subgoal
decomposition, self-correction, and deeper Lean interaction, such as
Goedel-Prover ([Lin et al., 2025a](#bib.bib7)), DeepSeek-Prover-V2 ([Ren
et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)), Kimina-Prover ([Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib9)), Goedel-Prover-V2 ([Lin et al., 2025b](#bib.bib10)),
and Seed-Prover ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)). These advances
demonstrate the effectiveness of combining language-model reasoning with
formal verification, structured search, and iterative proof refinement.
However, existing agentic provers generally do not explicitly focus on
external skills. Consequently, useful problem-solving techniques
discovered during proof construction are often limited to individual
trajectories and cannot be effectively reused to guide subsequent
problems. Skill evolution (including our work) addresses this limitation
by treating skills as evolving procedural knowledge, allowing effective
strategies and recurring failure patterns to be progressively
integrated, refined, and leveraged across problems. In fact, it is
feasible to combine advances in both directions to further boost formal
theorem proving.

## 5 Conclusion & Discussion

We presented SkillEvoLean, a mutation-enhanced skill evolution framework
for Lean theorem proving. It combines progressive evolution for
trajectory-based local refinement with concept-guided mutation for
exploring new strategies when refinement stagnates. Experiments on
MiniF2F, PutnamBench, IMO 2025, and USAMO 2026 show consistent
improvements across multiple backbone models over static and existing
skill optimization baselines. Ablation studies further demonstrate the
complementary roles of progressive evolution and mutation, as well as
the effectiveness of mathematical concept guidance. These results
highlight self-evolving skills as a promising direction to improving
formal theorem provers without additional parameter training.

Beyond the empirical gains, our findings suggest that skill evolution
can serve as a mechanism for accumulating and reusing procedural
knowledge across theorem-proving tasks. Progressive refinement helps
consolidate effective behaviors observed in proof trajectories, while
mutation broadens the search space beyond locally derived updates. Their
combination therefore supports both knowledge exploitation and strategy
exploration. Nevertheless, the quality of evolved skills remains
dependent on the diversity of training problems, the informativeness of
proof trajectories, and the reliability of skill evaluation. Future work
may investigate more compact skill representations, mechanisms for
resolving redundant or conflicting instructions, and transfer across
different formal libraries, theorem domains, and interactive theorem
provers.

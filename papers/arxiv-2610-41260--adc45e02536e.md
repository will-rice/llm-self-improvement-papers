# SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement

Yiqi Liang    Xiaodong Cun\corresponding

###### Abstract

Existing sketch generation methods often rely on image guidance and are
largely limited to individual objects or simplified scenes, leaving
text-guided complex scene sketching underexplored. We present
SketchDirector, an agentic framework that mimics the human sketching
workflow for automatic multi-concept scene sketching. Given a
scene-level text prompt, SketchDirector first employs a large language
model (LLM) to decompose the scene into multiple draft assets, where
each is represented by a set of Bézier curves. Then, an agentic
reasoning loop iteratively plans and executes their spatial arrangement,
guided by self-evolving memory. Finally, a detail refinement module
leverages diffusion priors to enhance sketch quality while preserving
geometric structure. To support future research and applications in
multi-concept scene sketch generation, we further introduce the
MCSketch8K dataset, comprising editable vector assets, compositional
layouts, and object-level manipulation annotations. Extensive
experiments show that SketchDirector outperforms state-of-the-art
baselines in semantic and compositional quality while effectively
preserving overall contour organization during refinement. The project
page is:

## 1 Introduction

Sketching is a concise visual representation that effectively captures
the intentions of human beings. As its scope expands from individual
objects to multiple concepts, scene sketching has shown great potential
in applications such as animation, proof of concept, and interactive
design, as in
Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement") (A).
However, complex scene sketching requires more than depicting individual
objects. It involves organizing multiple concepts, reasoning about their
spatial relationships, and maintaining coherent details within a unified
scene. Therefore, generating semantically aligned scene sketches with
reasonable layouts and coherent details remains a big challenge.

Previous automatic scene sketching generation methods ([Li et al.
2019a](#bib.bib1); [Li et al. 2019b](#bib.bib2); [Chan et al.
2022](#bib.bib3); [Vinker et al. 2023](#bib.bib4); [Liang et al.
2025b](#bib.bib5)) primarily operate at the holistic scene level,
treating scene sketches as an indivisible entity without object
representations, thereby hindering local editing and object reuse.
Recent studies ([Wu et al. 2025](#bib.bib11); [Zhang et al.
2026](#bib.bib12); [Zhan et al. 2025](#bib.bib13)) have explored
object-aware generation, but objects are often treated as predefined
components or auxiliary structures. Consequently, these methods lack
dynamic control, layout planning, and iterative optimization for
individual objects, which limits their potential as visual components.

In contrast, real-world sketch creation is inherently iterative: artists
continually revise, group, and rearrange individual elements to improve
the overall composition and presentation. They also adapt the level of
refinement to suit their creative goals, producing sketches that range
from rough drafts to highly detailed renderings, as illustrated in
Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement") (B).
Digital vector sketches are particularly well suited to such flexible
and iterative workflows because they are naturally represented as
collections of Bézier curves, enabling efficient programmatic
manipulation.

We thus propose SketchDirector, an automatic agentic framework ([Vinker
et al. 2025](#bib.bib14); [Du et al. 2026](#bib.bib15); [Liu et al.
2025](#bib.bib17); [Liang et al. 2025a](#bib.bib18)) for text-guided
multi-concept scene sketch generation by mimicking the pipeline of human
creators. Specifically, given a textual description, SketchDirector
first employs a Large Language Model (LLM) based method to decompose the
scene and generate each individual object as draft asset, each
represented as a set of Bézier curves. To enable layout control between
multiple asset, we introduce an operating action library comprising a
set of executable operations, _e.g.,_ move(), scale(), to manipulate
object attributes, enabling flexible adjustment of spatial relationships
among objects. Building upon this library, we propose an agentic
reasoning loop, which formulates layout manipulation as an iterative
operations and execution process. To achieve reliable closed-loop
reasoning, rather than predicting the entire scene layout at once,
SketchDirector progressively organizes draft assets through an iterative
_Plan–Execute–Verify–Correct_ process. At each step, SketchDirector
generates and executes object-level operations, updates the scene
representation, and evaluates the resulting composition through
geometric checks and multimodal verification. When the layout fails
verification, SketchDirector revises its decisions and corrects the
layout using a hierarchical self-evolving memory that leverages
short-term failure reflections and accumulated long-term experiences.

After that, we introduce an additional detail refinement step to address
the challenges of structural consistency in scene sketch distillation
from the diffusion prior. Unlike traditional methods that treat Bézier
curves as unstructured point sets, we treat object curve segments as the
optimization primitives. Then, by jointly optimizing local objects and
the global canvas using pre-scene distillation, our method is able to
generate scene sketches with varying levels of object refinement while
maintaining consistency with the textual description. Finally, with the
lack of available object-aware and editable scene sketch datasets, we
construct a multi-concept scene sketch dataset called MCSketch8K to
facilitate future research and applications in scene sketch generation.
The dataset comprises 1,000 scene sketches with over 4,000 disentangled
vector object assets across 800 object categories. Each scene is
annotated with compositional layouts and object-level manipulation
operations, providing structured supervision for object decomposition,
spatial arrangement reasoning, and controllable scene editing.

Our key contributions are summarized as:

- •
  We propose SketchDirector, an automatic framework for text-guided
  complex scene sketching with flexible object-level control and
  structure-preserving detail refinement.
- •
  SketchDirector contains two key techniques to assist in scene sketch
  generation: i) agentic reasoning, which formulates generation as a
  closed-loop process; ii) detail refinement, which performs Bézier
  curve parametrization and progressively joint local-global vector
  optimization to refine scene sketches.
- •
  We construct MCSketch8K, a multi-concept scene sketch dataset
  comprising vector object assets, compositional scene layouts, and
  object-level manipulation annotations.

## 2 Related Work

Scene Sketch Generation. Scene sketch generation aims to translate
visual or textual descriptions into coherent sketches. Early methods
formulated this task as image-to-sketch translation ([Li et al.
2019a](#bib.bib1); [Li et al. 2019b](#bib.bib2); [Chan et al.
2022](#bib.bib3)), converting photographs into rasterized contour
drawings via supervised learning or adversarial training. Later studies
increasingly adopt vector representations for scalable and editable
sketch generation. Image-conditioned methods such as Clipascene ([Vinker
et al. 2023](#bib.bib4)) and MROSS ([Liang et al. 2025b](#bib.bib5))
produce vector scene sketches from a reference image, while text-guided
methods ([Frans et al. 2022](#bib.bib6); [Jain et al. 2023](#bib.bib7);
[Xing et al. 2023](#bib.bib8); [Xing et al. 2024](#bib.bib9); [Xing et
al. 2025](#bib.bib10)) further enable open-domain vector sketch
generation from textual descriptions. In particular, VectorFusion ([Jain
et al. 2023](#bib.bib7)) distills text-to-image diffusion priors into
SVGs through score distillation, DiffSketcher ([Xing et al.
2023](#bib.bib8)) directly optimizes Bézier curves with diffusion-based
supervision, and SVGDreamer ([Xing et al. 2024](#bib.bib9)) improves
text-to-SVG generation through semantic-driven vectorization and
particle-based score distillation. Despite these advances, existing
methods optimize the scene holistically, leaving objects implicitly
encoded and limiting editing to strokes rather than objects.

Multi-concept Sketch Generation. Recent studies explore structured scene
generation by introducing different forms of concept decomposition and
controllability. For instance, SketchTriplet ([Wu et al.
2025](#bib.bib11)) assembles individual objects to construct scene
datasets, although the objects mainly serve as fixed composition units
without supporting layout planning or per-concept editing.
SketchAny ([Zhang et al. 2026](#bib.bib12)) separates foreground and
background to improve structural coherence, yet provides limited control
over individual concept instances. CoProSketch ([Zhan et al.
2025](#bib.bib13)) represents objects with unsigned distance fields to
enable progressive scene generation and improved editability, while
generation is still performed at the scene level rather than through
independent concept generation. Collectively, these methods highlight
the value of concept-aware representations, yet objects remain passive
elements rather than autonomous units for planning, interaction, and
iterative refinement.

Agents for Sketch Generation. The rapid development of multimodal large
language models (MLLMs) has introduced new opportunities for reasoning,
planning, and iterative interaction into sketch generation. For
instance, SketchAgent ([Vinker et al. 2025](#bib.bib14)) introduces a
language-driven sequential sketch generation framework, where an
LLM-based agent can generate sequences of vector sketches and supports
conversational sketch refinement. Building upon this, Du et al. ([Du et
al. 2026](#bib.bib15)) further explores controllable vector sketch
generation by training a multimodal agent with part-level annotations
and process-level feedback, enabling interpretable and locally editable
sketch synthesis. In addition, AutoSketch ([Chin et al.
2025](#bib.bib16)) investigates style-aware vector sketch completion,
leveraging VLM-based iterative adjustment to preserve the abstraction
level and stroke characteristics of input sketches during completion.
Despite promising results, existing methods lack high-level scene
reasoning and multi-object coordination. SketchDirector addresses this
with an agentic framework for hierarchical planning, object-level
control, and iterative refinement.

## 3 SketchDirector

The goal of SketchDirector is to generate editable multi-concept scene
sketches from textual descriptions. Each sketch is represented as a
collection of SVG assets parameterized by black Bézier curves, enabling
object-level editability and structural preservation. Given a scene
description, we first decompose it into individual concepts and generate
corresponding vector draft
assets (Sec. [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Assets Generation ‣ 3 SketchDirector ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement")).
To support executable scene composition, we define an operating action
library consisting of primitive operations that manipulate object
attributes and update the scene
state (Sec. [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Operating Action Library ‣ 3 SketchDirector ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement")).
Building upon these, an agentic layout reasoning step progressively
composes the scene through a closed loop, where agents translate spatial
intentions into executable editing programs and iteratively plan,
execute, verify, and correct the scene
composition (Sec. [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Agentic Layout Reasoning ‣ 3 SketchDirector ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement")).
Finally, a detail refinement step further enhances sketch details
through Bézier curve contour resampling and progressive joint
optimization, while preserving the structure of draft assets and spatial
layout of the
canvas (Sec. [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Detail Refinement ‣ 3 SketchDirector ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement")).
A high-level illustration is shown in
Fig. [3](#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement").

### 3.1 Assets Generation

Given a textual description $`T`$, we first employ a Large Language
Model (LLM) to parse the scene description and extract a set of objects
$`\mathcal{O}=\{o_{1},o_{2},...,o_{n}\}`$. Each object corresponds to an
independently drawable visual entity (such as stones, a lighthouse,
etc.), while abstract or environmental elements (e.g., mist) are
excluded since they cannot be naturally and visibly represented as
sketch assets. To support subsequent draft generation and layout
arrangement, each object is formulated as
$`o_{i}=(\mathrm{id}_{i},d_{i},z_{i})`$, where $`\mathrm{id}_{i}`$
denotes a unique object identifier, $`d_{i}`$ is a simple description
sentence of the object, and $`z_{i}`$ is its relative depth order.

After obtaining the object set $`\mathcal{O}`$, we further generate a
simple draft for each object. Specifically, we employ
SketchAgent ([Vinker et al. 2025](#bib.bib14)) as the draft generator.
Given the description $`d_{i}`$, we predict an ordered sequence of
sampled points on a canvas to describe the object outline. Then, by
solving a least-squares optimization problem, these sampled points are
fitted to a vector draft composed of cubic Bézier curves, producing
object draft $`g_{i}`$. Each draft is synthesized on a separate canvas
and added to its corresponding object
$`o_{i}=(\mathrm{id}_{i},d_{i},z_{i},g_{i})`$. To prevent interference
from other objects, we further inject a vectorized white background mask
derived from the object’s contour, enabling subsequent reasoning to
assemble the scene in a collage-like manner.

### 3.2 Operating Action Library

To support interpretable and executable scene composition, we define a
compact set of object-level operation codes that serve as the action
space of the reasoning agent. Given the current scene state, the agent
produces a sequence of executable actions to progressively arrange and
revise the retrieved sketch assets. An action is selected from six
primitive operations:

move$`(o,x,y)`$ moves object $`o`$ to canvas coordinate $`(x,y)`$.

scale$`(o,s)`$ rescales object $`o`$ by a relative factor $`s`$.

rotate$`(o,\theta)`$ rotates object $`o`$ by angle $`\theta`$ around its
center.

layer$`(o,l)`$ changes the depth order of object $`o`$ by assigning it
to layer $`l`$.

delete$`(o)`$ removes object $`o`$ from the scene composition.

modify$`(o,d)`$ regenerates object $`o`$ based on description $`d`$.

Note that a single layout revision may require composing multiple
primitives. For example, placing a large object behind another may
involve a scale, followed by a move and a layer operation. By keeping
these primitives minimal and composable, the agent can express diverse
layout manipulations with a compact action vocabulary, while each
individual operation remains explicit and easy to verify.

### 3.3 Agentic Layout Reasoning

After generating draft assets, directly assembling drafts often produces
implausible spatial relationships (_e.g._, inappropriate scales,
incorrect occlusions). Therefore, SketchDirector formulates scene
sketching as an agentic layout reasoning process that employs a
closed-loop _Plan–Execute–Verify–Correct_ process to iteratively improve
scene composition. At each iteration, the agent plans object-level
programs, executes them, evaluates the resulting layout, and revises its
decisions when necessary, as illustrated in
Figs. [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement").

#### Planning and Execution.

Planning aims to translate high-level layout intentions into an
executable program. We encode layout decisions as a sequence of
functional tools rather than directly predicting object attributes,
including move(), scale(), rotate(), layer(), delete() and modify(),
enabling flexible and interpretable arrangement. To facilitate reliable
spatial reasoning, the canvas is discretized into an $`R\times R`$
regular grid like previous works ([Sun et al. 2025](#bib.bib24); [Vinker
et al. 2025](#bib.bib14)). The agent infers the grid location of each
object, which is deterministically mapped back to the corresponding
canvas position during execution. The agent generates programs by
jointly reasoning over geometric and visual clues, where object
attributes, grid positions, and bounding boxes provide spatial
constraints, while rendered canvases and layout histories provide global
context. The generated operations are sequentially executed to update
object attributes and scene states, producing an explicit and traceable
composition trajectory, as illustrated in
Fig. [5](#S2.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement").
This traceability allows the agent to identify problematic operations
during subsequent verification and correction.

#### Multimodal Verification.

To ensure the layout from the agent meets spatial constraints and
textual descriptions, we propose a hybrid verification with geometric
and visual-semantic check. Geometric verification checks basic spatial
validity by ensuring that all draft assets lie within the canvas
boundaries and their sizes avoid extreme scaling anomalies.
Visual-semantic verification complements geometric checks by assessing
high-level semantic consistency. The details are described below.

Geometric Verification. We evaluate the geometric plausibility of the
generated layout from two aspects: boundary constraints and object
scales. Specifically, after executing the predicted layout program, each
object is represented by its transformed bounding box on the canvas. For
boundary verification, we check whether the bounding box of each object
is fully contained within the canvas region. Any object exceeding the
canvas boundary is recorded as a boundary violation with a violation
count $`I_{\mathrm{bound}}`$. The boundary verification is considered
successful only when no violation is detected:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
P_{\mathrm{bound}}=\mathrm{True}\quad\text{if}\quad I_{\mathrm{bound}}=0.
``` |  |

For size verification, we examine the scale factor of each object to
avoid extreme scaling artifacts. Objects with scale factors outside the
predefined range $`[0.1,1.5]`$ are recorded as size violations
$`I_{\mathrm{size}}`$. The size verification passes when:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P_{\mathrm{size}}=\mathrm{True}\quad\text{if}\quad I_{\mathrm{size}}=0.
``` |  |

The geometric verification is satisfied only when both boundary and size
constraints are fulfilled.

Visual-Semantic Verification. We employ a Vision-Language Model (VLM)
critic with a dual-image input consisting of the rendered scene canvas
and the object collage. The VLM produces scores $`S_{i}\in[0,1]`$ from
five perspectives: (1) $`S_{\mathrm{sem}}`$: semantic alignment between
the generated scene and the target concept, evaluating whether the main
objects and their intended meanings are correctly represented; (2)
$`S_{\mathrm{comp}}`$: spatial composition consistency, evaluating
whether object placement, spacing, overlap, depth ordering, and overall
arrangement form a coherent scene; (3) $`S_{\mathrm{compl}}`$: object
completeness, evaluating whether all required objects are present and
recognizable according to the provided object collage; (4)
$`S_{\mathrm{size}}`$: size consistency, evaluating whether object
scales and relative proportions are reasonable and physically plausible
within the scene; (5) $`S_{\mathrm{aes}}`$: visual aesthetics,
evaluating the overall visual quality, including composition harmony,
clarity, and visual appeal. The overall evaluation score is computed as
a weighted aggregation:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S_{\mathrm{visual}}=\sum\lambda_{i}S_{i},\quad\sum\lambda_{i}=1,
``` |  | (1) |

where
$`i\in\{\mathrm{sem},\mathrm{comp},\mathrm{compl},\mathrm{size},\mathrm{aes}\}`$.

The semantic verification is considered successful when:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P_{\mathrm{visual}}=\mathrm{True}\quad\text{if}\quad S_{\mathrm{visual}}\geq\tau_{\mathrm{visual}}.
``` |  |

Finally, the overall verification decision is determined by combining
geometric and semantic assessments:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P_{\mathrm{overall}}=P_{\mathrm{bound}}\land P_{\mathrm{size}}\land P_{\mathrm{visual}}.
``` |  | (2) |

Only layouts satisfying all verification criteria are accepted.
Otherwise, the detected violations and verification feedback are
recorded and sent to the self-evolving correction module. the agent then
uses these failure signals together with previous execution trajectories
to generate a revised layout program in the next planning iteration.

#### Self-Evolving Correction.

Different from contrastive experience for object-level sketch
generation ([Xiao et al. 2026](#bib.bib25)), inspired by human learning,
we propose a hierarchical self-evolving mechanism that Short-Term
Failure Memory converts failure trajectories into corrective feedback
and Long-Term Experience Memory transfers successful cross-scene
trajectories into reusable experiences, which are incrementally stored
and retrieved to improve subsequent layout planning and program
generation.

The Short-Term Failure Memory. The Short-Term Failure Memory maintains a
trajectory-level record for each failed attempt. Each entry contains the
generated program, intermediate rendering results, object-level
attributes, verification feedback, and the corresponding failure
category. These records are discarded after successful correction and
are only used within the current scene generation process.

The Long-Term Experience Memory. The Long-Term Experience Memory stores
successful layout experiences indexed by the corresponding object set.
For a new scene, relevant experiences are retrieved by measuring the
Jaccard similarity between the current object set and historical object
sets ([Park et al. 2023](#bib.bib26); [Shinn et al. 2023](#bib.bib27)):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{Sim}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}},\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{mem}})=\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{mem}}|}{|\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{mem}}|}.
``` |  | (3) |

For example, suppose the current scene contains
$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{new}}=\{\texttt{tree},\texttt{squirrel},\texttt{bird}\}`$,
while a stored experience is indexed by
$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{mem}}=\{\texttt{tree},\texttt{squirrel},\texttt{monkey}\}`$.
Their intersection is $`\{\texttt{tree},\texttt{squirrel}\}`$ with
cardinality $`2`$, and their union is
$`\{\texttt{tree},\texttt{squirrel},\texttt{bird},\texttt{monkey}\}`$
with cardinality $`4`$. Therefore,$`\mathrm{Sim}=\frac{2}{4}=0.50`$
Since the similarity reaches the predefined threshold
$`\tau_{\mathrm{sim}}`$, this experience is retrieved and provided to
the agent as a reference for subsequent layout planning.

### 3.4 Detail Refinement

After reasoning, the generated sketches achieve the desired spatial
arrangement but contain coarse details. To improve quality while
preserving object structure and layout, we introduce a detail refinement
using curve contour resampling to convert object contours into Bézier
primitives and progressive joint optimization to refine local objects
and the global canvas, as shown in
Fig. [3](#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement").

#### Curve Contour Resampling.

To obtain a fixed number of optimization primitives, we resample object
contours into $`N_{\mathrm{target}}`$ cubic Bézier segments. Unlike
point-based Bézier resampling method ([Vinker et al. 2022](#bib.bib19)),
our approach directly subdivides the existing vector contours without
reconstructing their geometry. Specifically, we convert foreground SVG
paths into cubic Bézier curves, excluding background masks and
degenerate segments. Given valid paths containing $`N_{\mathrm{orig}}`$
curves in total, we assume $`N_{\mathrm{target}}\geq N_{\mathrm{orig}}`$
and assign at least one segment to each original curve. The remaining
budget is proportionally allocated first across paths and then across
curves according to their arc lengths, using the largest-remainder
method to ensure exactly $`N_{\mathrm{target}}`$ segments. Each original
curve is subdivided into its allocated number of cubic Bézier segments
using the de Casteljau algorithm, which preserves the original contour
geometry. The resulting ordered sequence of primitives is defined as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{B}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n_{k}}\left(B_{k,j}^{(1)},\ldots,B_{k,j}^{(N_{k,j})}\right),`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}|`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}N_{k,j}=N_{\mathrm{target}}.`$ |  |  |

where $`\bigoplus`$ denotes ordered concatenation, preserving the
original path and curve ordering. During subsequent refinement, the
number and ordering of primitives remain fixed, while their control
points are independently optimized. It provides a faithful starting
point that retains object shapes and structural details, as illustrated
in
Fig. [11](#S4.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Impact of VLM Backbones. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Studies. ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement").

#### Progressive Joint Optimization.

Based on the resampled representation, each object draft is expressed as
a fixed-topology set of Bézier primitives parameterized by control
points $`\theta_{i}`$ and rendered as $`I_{i}=R(\theta_{i})`$, where
$`R(\cdot)`$ denotes the differentiable vector renderer ([Li et al.
2020](#bib.bib28)). During optimization, only the control-point geometry
is updated, while the curve ordering inherited from the draft remains
fixed.

During local optimization, we apply Rectified-Flow Distillation
Sampling (RFDS ([Yang et al. 2025](#bib.bib29))) for supervision:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{i}_{obj}=\mathcal{L}_{RFDS}(I_{obj}^{i},T^{i}_{\mathrm{obj}}),
``` |  | (5) |

where $`T^{i}_{\mathrm{obj}}`$ corresponds to $`d_{i}`$, is the
description of the object.

During global optimization, each object is incrementally composed with
the canvas $`I^{i}_{scene}=\mathcal{C}(I^{i-1}_{scene},I_{i},\ell_{i})`$
using layout attributes $`\ell_{i}`$, where $`\mathcal{C}(\cdot)`$
denotes differentiable composition. The scene is optimized with a global
RFDS loss:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{i}_{\mathrm{scene}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RFDS}}\left(I^{i}_{\mathrm{scene}},T^{i}_{\mathrm{acc}}\right),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`T^{i}_{\mathrm{acc}}`$ describes all objects incorporated into
the accumulated canvas and their semantic and spatial relationships at
the $`i`$-th optimization stage. Consequently, the text condition is
progressively expanded as new objects are optimized.

At optimization round for each object, only the control points
$`\theta_{i}`$ of the current object are optimized. The objective
combines both object and global supervision:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{i}=\lambda_{obj}\mathcal{L}^{i}_{obj}+\lambda_{scene}\mathcal{L}^{i}_{scene},
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\lambda_{obj}`$ and $`\lambda_{scene}`$ balance object-level
semantic refinement and scene-level structural alignment.

visual-semantic alignment. Moreover, compared with *optimization-based
methods*, our refinement improves sketch details while faithfully
preserving the object layouts and spatial relationships in the draft.

## 4 Experiment

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Implementation Details.

We deploy SketchDirector on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. We employ
OpenAI GPT-5.5 ([OpenAI 2026](#bib.bib20)) as the default agent and
pydiffvg ([Li et al. 2020](#bib.bib28)) as the differentiable vector
renderer. All stroke primitives are parameterized as cubic Bézier curves
defined by $`4`$ control points. The canvas is discretely configured as
a $`64\times 64`$ grid with each cell spanning $`12\times 12`$ pixels,
resulting in an overall rendered resolution of $`780\times 780`$ pixels.
In terms of computational cost, asset generation takes approximately
$`20`$ seconds per object, while planning, execution, and verification
require approximately $`30`$, $`12`$, and $`40`$ seconds per layout
attempt, respectively. Detail refinement takes approximately $`5`$
minutes per object with $`400`$ optimization iterations. Although this
incurs additional computational overhead, the resulting independent SVG
assets and executable layouts enable flexible and efficient object-level
editing without full-scene regeneration.

#### Benchmark.

To evaluate the performance of scene sketch generation, we sample
different multi-concept text as prompts spanning diverse themes and
categories from MCSketch8K, to comprehensively assess sketch quality.

#### Baselines.

We compare SketchDirector against five baselines for scene sketch
generation, categorized into LLM-based and optimization-based methods.
*LLM-based methods* include SketchAgent ([Vinker et al.
2025](#bib.bib14)) and OpenAI GPT-5.5 ([OpenAI 2026](#bib.bib20));
*Optimization-based methods* include SVGDreamer ([Xing et al.
2024](#bib.bib9)), VectorFusion ([Jain et al. 2023](#bib.bib7)) and
DiffSketcher ([Xing et al. 2023](#bib.bib8)).

#### Metrics.

To assess the semantic and compositional quality of scene sketches, we
adopt a multi-VLMs assessment to reduce evaluator bias. We employ
Qwen3.7-Plus ([Cloud 2026](#bib.bib23)), Claude-Opus-4-7 ([Anthropic
2026](#bib.bib22)), Gemini3.1-Pro ([DeepMind 2026](#bib.bib21)), and
OpenAI GPT-5.5 ([OpenAI 2026](#bib.bib20)) as judges. Each VLM
independently evaluates five dimensions: Semantic Alignment (Sem.),
Spatial Composition (Compo.), Object Completeness (Compl.), Visual
Aesthetics (Aes.), and Size Consistency (Size). Scores are averaged
across the four VLMs and all test samples for quantitative comparison.

#### Impact of Agentic Layout Reasoning.

Figure [10](#S4.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ Impact of VLM Backbones. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Studies. ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement")
and
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Impact of VLM Backbones. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Studies. ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement")
show that removing any component leads to performance degradation,
confirming that all modules in agentic reasoning are essential for
high-quality scene sketch generation. Removing planning (w/o Plan) leads
to severe layout degradation. Without executable scene programs, the
model struggles to organize object attributes, resulting in frequent
object overlaps, unreasonable spatial arrangements, and boundary
violations. These demonstrate that converting high-level semantic
intentions into structured layout operations is essential for reliable
scene composition. Disabling geometric verification (w/o Geo. Ver.)
mainly affects physical plausibility. Although the generated objects
remain semantically relevant, their positions and sizes may violate
basic spatial constraints, such as objects extending beyond the canvas
or unrealistic relative scales between objects. This indicates that
geometric constraints provide necessary low-level guidance for
maintaining valid scene structures. Without visual-semantic verification
(w/o Sem. Ver.), the agent lacks high-level visual feedback during
layout refinement. Consequently, generated scenes may contain incorrect
object relationships, missing semantic interactions, or unnatural
occlusion orders. The comparison highlights the importance of multimodal
evaluation in aligning generated layouts with the intended scene
description. Furthermore, memory modules improve the agent’s ability to
learn from previous reasoning trajectories. Removing short-term memory
(w/o Short Mem.) does not cause significant visual layout degradation,
but leads to repeated exploration of similar unsuccessful layouts,
increasing the number of reflection iterations. Removing long-term
memory (w/o Long Mem.) also preserves comparable visual structures in
many cases, yet prevents the agent from retrieving reusable layout
experiences from related scenes, resulting in less effective layout
composition. Overall, these qualitative examples verify that each
component contributes complementary capabilities, enabling more reliable
and controllable scene reasoning.

#### Impact of Detail Refinement.

Fig. [11](#S4.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Impact of VLM Backbones. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Studies. ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement")
provides additional comparisons between point-based and curve-based
sampling. Point-based sampling uniformly samples sparse control points
from object regions without considering the underlying contours. During
optimization, these points may drift independently, causing geometric
distortions and loss of characteristic object shapes. In contrast, our
curve contour resampling method derives Bézier primitives directly from
object contours. By preserving the original contour topology, the curves
provide a more stable optimization space.
Fig. [12](#S4.F12 "Figure 12 ‣ Impact of Detail Refinement. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Studies. ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement")
presents additional comparisons of different optimization strategies.
Removing local supervision (w/o Local) optimizes objects only according
to global objectives, allowing unrelated semantic information to
interfere with individual object. This often results in distorted local
structures. Removing global supervision (w/o Global) focuses only on
individual object while ignoring interactions among objects. Although
individual objects may remain recognizable, the resulting scenes exhibit
weaker spatial coherence, such as unnatural object relationships.
Without progressive optimization (w/o Progressive), local and global
objectives are optimized simultaneously from the beginning. The strong
coupling between object-level refinement and scene-level consistency
introduces optimization conflicts, causing unstable updates and reduced
object fidelity. By progressively incorporating objects, our method
enables stable local refinement first and gradually improves global
scene integration.

Removing local supervision, global supervision, or progressive

### 4.4 Limitations.

Although SketchDirector generates multi-concept scene sketches, several
limitations remain. First, it struggles with complex physical and
semantic relations, such as interactions between a woman and a kettle or
a castle located on a hill
(Fig. [13](#S4.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ 4.4 Limitations. ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement") (A)).
Stronger relational and interaction modeling may improve scene
understanding. Second, refinement may hallucinate redundant instances,
leading to object inconsistency, such as duplicating a sheep
(Fig. [13](#S4.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ 4.4 Limitations. ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ SketchDirector: Complex Scene Sketching via Agentic Layout Reasoning and Detail Refinement") (B)).
Instance-aware constraints could help preserve object identity and
count. Third, multimodal verification and iterative optimization incur
computational costs, particularly when multiple refinement rounds are
required. Efficient verification and optimization strategies will be
explored in future work.

## 5 Conclusion

We present SketchDirector, an agentic framework that emulates the human
sketching workflow. Designed for complex scene sketch generation,
SketchDirector converts textual descriptions into editable vector assets
and executable layout operations. It consists of three stages: concept
representation for generating object-level draft assets, agentic
reasoning with iterative*Plan–Execute–Verify–Correct* and self-evolving
memory for reliable spatial composition, and detail refinement for
progressively optimizing Bézier-based vectors while preserving the
original sketch structure. We further introduce *MCSketch8K*, a
benchmark containing editable vector assets and composition annotations
for multi-concept scene sketch generation. Extensive experiments
demonstrate that SketchDirector outperforms existing methods in semantic
and compositional quality while effectively preserving structural
integrity during refinement.
````

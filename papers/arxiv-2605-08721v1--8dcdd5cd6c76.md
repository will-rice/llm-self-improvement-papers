###### Abstract

While Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has proven
effective for closed-ended tasks, extending it to open-ended social
language games via self-play reveals a critical issue: evolution
impasse. Due to the vast strategy space, language agents frequently
converge to homogenized behaviors, leading to deterministic match
outcomes that eliminate the gradient signals necessary for policy
evolution. To tackle this issue, we propose Dual-scale Evolutionary
Policy Training (DEPT) for social language games. DEPT introduces a
time-scaled evolutionary perception mechanism that detects impasse by
quantifying dual-scale value baseline divergence alongside match
entropy. Upon perceiving the collapse, it then activates asymmetric
advantage reshaping to dynamically modulate the optimization landscape
for intervention. Thus, our method effectively restores gradient signals
and enforces sustained strategic exploration. Extensive experiments on
multiple social language games demonstrate that DEPT outperforms strong
baselines, avoiding policy degeneration and driving the continuous
evolution of social language agents.

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a
pivotal paradigm for unlocking the reasoning potential of large language
models (LLMs), demonstrating remarkable capabilities in static
problem-solving tasks such as mathematics and code generation ([Guo et
al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Yue et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3); [Liu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib13)). However, these tasks
are inherently closed-ended problem-solving, characterized by
deterministic correct answers with straightforward question answering.
In reality, social decision-making is considerably more complex, often
situated in open-ended contexts fraught with conflicting interests and
dynamic interactions ([FAIR et al., 2022](#bib.bib19); [Park et al.,
2023](#bib.bib20); [Yang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib7)). Under such dynamic
social contexts, LLMs not only engage in coherent logical reasoning but
also perform long-horizon planning, infer opponents’ intentions in
real-time, and process complex social signals such as
negotiation ([Bianchi et al., 2024](#bib.bib14); [Hua et al.,
2024](#bib.bib6)), deception ([Hagendorff, 2023](#bib.bib17); [Xu et
al., 2024](#bib.bib18)), and bargaining ([Xia et al., 2024](#bib.bib15);
[Abdulhai et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)). Thus, for language agents, a
significant gap exists between closed-ended problem-solving capabilities
and open-ended social intelligence ([Zhou et al., 2024](#bib.bib4);
[Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib8)).

Figure 1: (a) DEPT maintains high outcome entropy while baselines

DEPT achieves superior performance, effectively addressing policy

Social strategic games provide an ideal testbed to practise this kind of
social capability in open-ended tasks: they naturally simulate dynamic
social contexts and complex decision-making behaviors, while game
outcomes (i.e., win/loss/draw) are objectively determined by
rules—perfectly aligning with the RLVR paradigm without requiring
expensive human annotations ([Duan et al., 2024](#bib.bib23); [Hua et
al., 2024](#bib.bib6); [Guertler et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). More
importantly, this property makes self-play an effective pathway for
scaling LLM capabilities ([Silver et al., 2018](#bib.bib21); [Bansal et
al., 2018](#bib.bib27); [Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib22); [Liu et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib9)). Using a single LLM acting as both players of the
game and continuously optimizing through zero-sum competition, self-play
can overcome the data scarcity bottleneck in post-training, and
meanwhile establish an intrinsic automatic curriculum through opponent
co-evolution of social decision-making skills.

Existing self-play RLVR methods ([Liu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9); [Yuan
et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)) have achieved remarkable success in games
with limited action space (e.g., Tic-Tac-Toe, Kuhn Poker), by designing
role-specific advantage estimation. However, these methods struggle with
open-ended social games involving free-form natural language
interactions, which constitute vast action space that often requires
verbal influence on opponents in order to succeed. Without effective
guidance in such unbounded strategy space, agents easily become trapped
in suboptimal solutions. This renders the training process highly liable
to evolution impasse, that is, the stagnant situation a cognitive agent
encounters in its decision-making ([Laird, 2019](#bib.bib45); [Sumers et
al., 2023](#bib.bib46)), which manifests as deterministic match outcome
distributions for self-play RLVR methods. In this situation, the value
baseline converges to the static expected return that causes the
advantage function to approach zero, leading to the vanishing of policy
gradient and locking the agent in a suboptimal state (as shown
in [Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents")(a)(b)).
To break the impasse, it is critical to develop an effective mechanism
for evolutionary perception and intervention during self-play.

To address the above issues, we propose a Dual-scale Evolutionary Policy
Training (DEPT) method to dynamically perceive evolutionary states and
adaptively modulate the optimization landscape. Specifically, we
introduce a time-scaled evolutionary perception mechanism that maintains
parallel value baselines: a fast baseline sensitive to recent
fluctuations and a slow baseline anchored to long-term trends. By
measuring the divergence between these baselines alongside match outcome
entropy, we derive a quantifiable metric for evolution impasse. To
restore effective gradients, we propose asymmetric advantage reshaping.
By anchoring on the stable slow baseline and adaptively fusing
historical value bounds from the fast baseline, this mechanism
constructs a contrastive advantage signal. It effectively penalizes
dominant outcomes while amplifying rare trajectories, thereby enforcing
exploration. Our approach effectively intervenes in evolution impasse
and restores the gradient landscape, driving continuous strategic
evolution. Experimental results across multiple social language games
demonstrate that DEPT outperforms strong baselines.

Our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) We propose DEPT for
social language games, which is the first to dynamically perceive
evolution impasse and intervene in self-play RLVR. (2) Our method
develops the time-scaled evolutionary perception and asymmetric
advantage reshaping mechanism, which leverages dual value baselines to
quantify evolutionary states and restore effective gradient signals. (3)
Extensive experiments demonstrate that DEPT outperforms existing SOTA
methods, effectively preventing policy degeneration while driving
continuous co-evolution.

## 2 Preliminary

### 2.1 Task Formulation

The social language game task is modeled as a two-player zero-sum Markov
game ([Littman, 1994](#bib.bib26))
$`\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{R})`$.
The state space $`\mathcal{S}`$ represents the interaction context, and
$`\mathcal{A}_{0}`$, $`\mathcal{A}_{1}`$ denote the action spaces for
each player, consisting of open-ended natural language utterances. At
state $`s_{t}`$, the active player $`p=t\bmod 2`$ receives a partial
observation $`o_{t}`$ containing the interaction history and its
agent-specific private information, then samples an utterance
$`a_{t}^{(p)}`$ from its policy $`\pi_{p}(\cdot\mid o_{t})`$. The
environment transitions to $`s_{t+1}`$ via $`\mathcal{T}`$. An episode
forms a trajectory $`\tau=\{(s_{t},a_{t}^{(p)})\}_{t=0}^{T}`$,
concluding at terminal timestep $`T`$ where each agent receives a reward
$`R_{p}(\tau)`$ based on its private goals. The zero-sum property
requires $`R_{0}(\tau)+R_{1}(\tau)=0`$, creating direct competition
between agents. Typically, rewards are assigned as $`+1`$ for win,
$`-1`$ for lose, and $`0`$ for a draw. Each agent aims to learn a policy
$`\pi_{p}`$ maximizing its expected return:
$`\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{0},\pi_{1}}[R_{p}(\tau)]`$.

### 2.2 Self-Play RLVR

Self-play trains a single policy LLM $`\pi_{\theta}`$ to act as both
players, with role conditioning achieved through system prompts
specifying whether the model plays as $`p_{0}`$ or $`p_{1}`$. At each
turn, the active player generates
$`y_{t}^{(p)}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid o_{t},p)`$, where
$`y_{t}^{(p)}=\langle\texttt{think}\rangle c_{t}\langle/\texttt{think}\rangle\langle\texttt{act}\rangle a_{t}^{(p)}\langle/\texttt{act}\rangle`$
follows a reasoning-then-acting paradigm ([Yao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib25)), which is strictly enforced via a format reward to
guarantee validity, where $`c_{t}`$ denotes the reasoning process and
$`a_{t}^{(p)}`$ is the executable action. This shared-parameter approach
creates an automatic curriculum: as the policy improves, it
simultaneously faces a stronger opponent.

##### Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation.

The vanilla policy gradient suffers from high variance, particularly
problematic in self-play where the opponent continuously evolves.
Moreover, many games exhibit inherent role asymmetries (e.g.,
first-mover advantages), causing different expected returns for each
role even under optimal play. A global baseline poorly estimates both
roles’ expectations, leading to biased gradients. To address this,
Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation (RAE) ([Liu et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib9); [Yuan et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)) maintains separate
baselines $`b_{p}`$ for each role, estimating the role-specific expected
return $`\mathbb{E}[R_{p}(\tau)]`$. The advantage is computed as
$`A_{p}(\tau)=R_{p}(\tau)-b_{p}`$, yielding the variance-reduced policy
gradient:

|     |                                                                                                                                |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\Big[\sum_{p\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{t\in T_{p}}A_{p}(\tau)`$ |     | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{(p)}\mid o_{t},p)\Big]`$                                            |     |     |

where $`T_{p}`$ denotes turns where player $`p`$ acted. By centering
returns around role-specific expectations, RAE ensures gradient updates
reflect genuine strategic improvements rather than inherent positional
advantages.

## 3 Empirical Investigation of the Impasse

Self-play RLVR aims to foster continuous improvement through iterative
competition. In principle, this paradigm relies on dynamic evolutionary
interactions to maintain the diversity of match outcomes, ensuring the
variance required for effective policy optimization. However, in
open-ended social games, we observe a critical failure mode where agents
demonstrate increasing mastery of game mechanics yet stagnate in
strategic intelligence. As illustrated in
[Figure 2](#S3.F2 "In 3 Empirical Investigation of the Impasse ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents"),
training on Qwen3-4B-Base yields improvements in basic metrics such as
game length and reward. However, the win rate against a fixed Gemini-2
opponent exhibits a declining trend, ultimately converging to a
sub-optimal state and failing to demonstrate sustained performance
gains. To diagnose the internal dynamics driving this impasse, we
introduce Match Entropy ($`H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}`$), which quantifies
the diversity of match outcomes at training step $`t`$ over the
distribution
$`P^{(t)}=\{p_{\text{win}},p_{\text{draw}},p_{\text{loss}}\}`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}=-\frac{1}{\log(|P|)}\sum_{o\in P}p_{o}\log(p_{o})
``` |  | (2) |

Figure 2: Training dynamics of the self-play RLVR in social language
game (Negotiation). While (a) training Reward and (b) average Game
Length exhibit normal growth patterns, indicating the acquisition of
basic game mechanics, (c) for baselines, the Win Rate against a fixed
Gemini-2.0 opponent suffers a significant collapse.

[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents")(a)
reveals a distinct collapse pattern: after a brief initial exploration
phase,$`H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}`$ rapidly diminishes and remains near
zero. This signals convergence to a evolution impasse, a stagnant state
where agents become trapped in suboptimal solutions due to the vast,
unstructured strategy space of social language games that makes it
extremely difficult to sufficiently explore effective strategies (e.g.,
nuanced persuasion or deception).

Mathematically, this degeneration directly precipitates the vanishing of
gradient signals. In standard policy gradient methods, the update relies
on the advantage function $`A_{p}(\tau)=R_{p}(\tau)-b_{p}`$, where the
value baseline $`b_{p}`$ estimates the expected return
$`\mathbb{E}[R_{p}]`$. When the match outcome distribution becomes
deterministic (i.e., $`H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}\to 0`$), the value
baseline converges to constant return ($`b_{p}\approx R_{t}`$), causing
the advantage to vanish: $`A_{t}\to 0`$. This diminishes gradients,
preventing escape from local optima. To unlock the reasoning potential
of LLMs in social language games, it is paramount to establish a
mechanism that can perceive this collapse and intervene to restore the
gradient landscape.

## 4 Dual-scale Evolutionary Policy Training

To overcome the evolutionary impasse, we propose Dual-scale Evolutionary
Policy Training (DEPT), an algorithm designed to dynamically perceive
evolutionary states and adaptively modulate optimization landscapes to
enforce continuous evolution. As shown in
[Figure 3](#S3.F3 "In 3 Empirical Investigation of the Impasse ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents"),
our algorithm leverages a dual-timescale value baseline to quantify the
stationarity of the training process and dynamically injects intrinsic
motivation signals into the advantage estimation to enforce continuous
evolution.

### 4.1 Time-Scaled Evolutionary Perception

Standard value baselines are inherently reactive: they simply track the
current expected return, rendering them incapable of distinguishing
between a stable optimal policy and a evolution impasse. In both cases,
the baseline stabilizes, failing to signal that the learning process has
stalled. To distinguish these states, we introduce a dual-timescale
mechanism that captures the dynamics of the training process by
contrasting short-term and long-term value estimates. For each player
role $`p\in\{0,1\}`$, we maintain two parallel value baselines,
$`b_{p}^{\text{fast}}`$ and $`b_{p}^{\text{slow}}`$, updated via
Exponential Moving Average (EMA) with distinct decay rates
$`\alpha_{\text{fast}}<\alpha_{\text{slow}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
b_{p}^{k,t}=\alpha_{k}\cdot b_{p}^{k,t-1}+(1-\alpha_{k})\cdot R_{p}(\tau),k\in\{\text{fast},\text{slow}\}
``` |  | (3) |

Here, $`b_{p}^{\text{fast}}`$ rapidly responds to recent outcome
returns, while $`b_{p}^{\text{slow}}`$ serves as a stable anchor by
incorporating a longer history of game outcomes. The divergence between
these two signals effectively functions as a differential indicator of
training progress: during phases of rapid policy improvement, the fast
baseline quickly shifts to match new reward levels, whereas the slow
baseline lags behind, creating a significant delta
($`|b_{p}^{\text{fast}}-b_{p}^{\text{slow}}|`$). Conversely, when the
policy stabilizes—implying the reward distribution has become
stationary—the fast baseline ceases to deviate from the slow baseline,
and the two estimates asymptotically converge. We leverage this property
to define the Stagnation Coefficient $`\sigma^{(t)}\in[0,1]`$ to
quantify the stationarity of the training process:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sigma^{(t)}=1-\tanh\left(\left|b_{p}^{\text{fast},(t)}-b_{p}^{\text{slow},(t)}\right|\right)
``` |  | (4) |

When the policy evolves actively, the baseline divergence yields low
$`\sigma^{(t)}`$. As the system settles into stagnation, the diminishing
divergence drives $`\sigma^{(t)}`$ higher.

##### Intervention Coefficient

High stationarity alone does not necessitate intervention, as it
characterizes both successful convergence and failure. To specifically
isolate degenerate cases, we incorporate the Stagnation Coefficient
$`\sigma^{(t)}`$ with match entropy $`H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}`$ defined
in
[Section 3](#S3 "3 Empirical Investigation of the Impasse ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents").
This yields the Intervention Coefficient $`\lambda^{(t)}\in[0,1]`$,
which determines the necessity of active guidance:

|     |                                                                |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \lambda^{(t)}=\sigma^{(t)}\cdot\sqrt{1-H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}}
       ```                                                             |     | (5) |

This soft-gating mechanism ensures that intervention strength scales
smoothly with the evolutionary state: (1) Healthy Exploration (high
$`H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}`$): Outcome diversity suppresses
$`\lambda^{(t)}`$, minimizing interference during normal learning. (2)
Evolution Impasse (low $`H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}`$, high
$`\sigma^{(t)}`$): The combination of outcome determinism and high
stationarity elevates $`\lambda^{(t)}`$, progressively strengthening the
corrective signal.

### 4.2 Asymmetric Advantage Reshaping

When $`\lambda^{(t)}\to 1`$, the standard advantage signal vanishes
$`A(\tau)\approx 0`$. To restore effective learning, we introduce an
Asymmetric Advantage Reshaping, reshaping the advantage function by
constructing a contrastive baseline that differentially treats dominant
versus rare outcomes.

##### Global Performance Bounds

Constructing such a contrastive signal requires a rigorous reference
scale. Arbitrary baseline modifications can lead to unbounded advantage
values, risking gradient instability and distorting the reward scale. To
ensure effective reshaping, we track the historical upper and lower
bounds of the expected returns. We utilize the fast baseline
$`b_{p}^{\text{fast}}`$: it is sufficiently sensitive to register
performance extrema (potential capabilities) yet robust enough to filter
out single-sample noise. We define the historical bounds as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
V_{\max}^{(t)}=\max_{i\leq t}\{b_{p}^{\text{fast},i}\},\quad V_{\min}^{(t)}=\min_{i\leq t}\{b_{p}^{\text{fast},i}\}
``` |  | (6) |

##### Asymmetric Value Construction

Match entropy collapse stems from one outcome class dominating the
batch. To break this homogeneity, we assign different value based on
whether a trajectory $`\tau`$ produces the dominant outcome
$`o_{\text{dom}}`$ or not. We then construct an asymmetric target value
$`V_{\text{asym}}(\tau,b_{p}^{\text{fast}})`$ that applies differential
pressure based on the outcome type. Formally, this asymmetric value is
defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}V_{\text{asym}}(\tau,b_{p}^{\text{fast}})&=\mathbb{I}(o_{\tau}=o_{\text{dom}})\cdot V_{\max}^{(t)}\\
&+\mathbb{I}(o_{\tau}\neq o_{\text{dom}})\cdot V_{\min}^{(t)}\end{split}
``` |  | (7) |

This construction induces asymmetric gradient flow through the advantage
function $`A_{\tau}=R_{\tau}-V_{\text{asym}}^{(t)}(\tau)`$. For
dominant-outcome trajectories, the high $`V_{\max}`$ reduces positive
gradients and amplifies negative gradients, suppressing the
over-represented behavior. For rare-outcome trajectories, the low
$`V_{\min}`$ amplifies positive gradients and reduces negative
gradients, encouraging exploration of under-represented strategies.

##### Adaptive Baseline Fusion

Finally, to ensure smooth transitions between normal training and
intervention phases, we synthesize the final baseline
$`\tilde{b}_{p}(\tau)`$ by interpolating between the stable slow
baseline and the asymmetric value. The intervention coefficient
$`\lambda^{(t)}`$ governs this fusion:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{b}_{p}(\tau)=(1-\lambda^{(t)})\cdot b_{p}^{\text{slow},(t)}+\lambda^{(t)}\cdot V_{\text{asym}}(\tau,b_{p}^{\text{fast}})
``` |  | (8) |

When $`\lambda^{(t)}`$ is small, the baseline approximates the standard
slow estimate, preserving normal gradient dynamics. As $`\lambda^{(t)}`$
increases, the asymmetric term gradually dominates, progressively
steering the policy away from the collapsed state. The policy is updated
using the reshaped advantage
$`\tilde{A}_{p}(\tau)=R_{p}(\tau)-\tilde{b}_{p}(\tau)`$, yielding the
final gradient objective:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\Big[\sum_{p\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{t\in T_{p}}\left(R_{p}(\tau)-\tilde{b}_{p}(\tau)\right)`$ |  | (9) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}^{(p)}\mid o_{t},p)\Big]`$ |  |  |

The full optimization procedure is shown in
[Algorithm 1](#alg1 "In A.2.2 Gradient Restoration ‣ A.2 Gradient Dynamics Analysis ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents").
We also provide theoretical analysis about the mathematical foundations
of our dual-timescale metrics and the gradient dynamics guaranteeing
impasse recovery in
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents").

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental Settings

##### Tasks

We evaluate our method on three adversarial open-ended language tasks
involving multi-turn conversation and decision-making: (1) Don’t Say It:
Players aim to trick their opponent into saying a secret word while
avoiding saying their own. The first to speak the word loses; otherwise,
it is a draw upon reaching the turn limit. (2) Negotiation: Players
negotiate to exchange five resource types based on private valuations,
aiming to maximize inventory value. The player with the higher value
wins, with ties declared for equal payoffs or reaching turn limit. (3)
Two Dollar: Players bargain to split a fixed fund based on hidden role
constraints. The player with the higher payoff wins; equal payoffs or
reaching the turn limit result in a draw. All three games are adopted
from TextArena ([Guertler et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)). For further
details, please refer to
[Section B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Tasks ‣ Appendix B Implementation details ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents").

##### Baselines

We evaluate our method against several self-play RLVR methods in
zero-sum games: (1) SPIRAL ([Liu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9)): The first
fully online multi-turn self-play RL system for LLMs. It introduces
Role-conditioned Advantage Estimation to prevent thinking collapse in
zero-sum games; (2) MARS ([Yuan et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)): It modifies
the multi-turn GRPO algorithm by introducing a turn-level advantage
estimator and role-specific advantage normalization to stabilize
self-play RL training; (3) GRPO: An adaptation of multi-turn GRPO ([Shao
et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)) for self-play settings, where group
advantages are calculated separately for each role to preserve effective

##### Evaluation

Following SPIRAL ([Liu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9)), we select Gemini-2
(gemini-2.0-flash-lite) as one of our evaluation opponents. To provide a
more comprehensive assessment, we additionally evaluate our method
against different LLMs: GPT-5.1 opponent (gpt-5.1-2025-11-13) and
Gemini-2.5 opponent (gemini-2.5-flash-lite). Performance is quantified
by the win rate against these opponents, defined as
$`\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}[R_{i}=1]`$, where $`R_{i}`$
denotes the final reward for the $`i`$-th trajectory (with $`R_{i}=1`$
indicating a win). For each task, we conduct experiments using three
random seeds (42, 100, 200), evaluating $`N=256`$ episodes per seed. The
evaluation is symmetric, granting an equal opportunities to act as the
first and second player.

##### Training

We employ Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base ([Yang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib24)) as the backbone models for all experiments.
Following SPIRAL ([Liu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9)), we set the training
steps to 400 with 128 samples collected per step, resulting in a total
of 51,200 game transitions for each task. The training batch size is set
to 128. For the dual-baseline mechanism, we use
$`\alpha_{\text{fast}}=0.5`$ and $`\alpha_{\text{slow}}=0.95`$. Detailed
RL training hyperparameters are provided in
[Section B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Training Hyperparameters ‣ Appendix B Implementation details ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents").

##### RQ1: Is DEPT effective for social language agents?

As shown in

In contrast, DEPT’s consistent superiority against diverse opponents
demonstrates that our dual-timescale perception and asymmetric reshaping
mechanism effectively prevents strategic collapse, enabling robust

### 5.3 Analysis

##### RQ3: How does DEPT work?

To examine the effectiveness of each component in DEPT, we conduct
ablation studies using Qwen3-8B-Base, as reported in
[Figure 5](#S5.F5 "In RQ2: Can DEPT effectively perceive and intervene during training? ‣ 5.2 Experimental Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents").
All ablations lead to performance degradation, confirming the necessity
of each design choice. (1) Effect of perception. Removing the Stagnation
Coefficient causes aggressive entropy correction during non-stationary
training phases, leading to substantial advantage estimation errors that
disrupt policy exploration. Removing match entropy gating results in
continuous penalties even when the outcome distribution maintains
sufficient diversity, forcing ineffective random exploration and
preventing convergence. (2) Effect of asymmetric value. Without
asymmetric target values, the method cannot selectively suppress
over-represented behaviors while supporting under-represented ones,
leading to convergence at suboptimal low-entropy states. (3) Effect of
dual-baseline. Using either the fast or slow baseline alone degrades
performance. This confirms that the effectiveness of DEPT arises from
the synergy between dual baselines, which jointly enable accurate
perception and targeted intervention.

## 6 Related Work

LLM-based Reinforcement Learning Reinforcement learning for LLMs has
evolved from Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) to
RLVR ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib40); [Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib1);
[Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Yue et al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Liu et
al., 2025b](#bib.bib13); [Tan et al., 2025](#bib.bib32); [Nie et al.,
2026](#bib.bib41); [Tan et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)), leveraging objective
ground truth to unlock deep potential in mathematical reasoning and code
generation. However, existing RLVR research focuses on closed-ended
tasks with well-defined answers. Real-world open-ended social
interactions—such as negotiation and deception—are fundamentally
different ([FAIR et al., 2022](#bib.bib19); [Park et al.,
2023](#bib.bib20); [Zhou et al., 2024](#bib.bib4); [Xu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18); [Wang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib34); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib42)): they involve infinite action spaces, lack unique
correct strategies, and require real-time reasoning about dynamic
opponent intentions ([Abdulhai et al., 2025](#bib.bib16); [Yang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib7); [Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib8)). Our work addresses
the challenge of applying RLVR to open-ended social interaction while
sustaining continuous evolution in dynamic environments.

LLM Self-play Training on Language Game As a classical paradigm,
Self-play ([Silver et al., 2018](#bib.bib21); [Bansal et al.,
2018](#bib.bib27); [Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib22); [Cheng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib12)) addresses data scarcity while constructing an
automatic curriculum through opponent co-evolution. For language games
in LLMs, SPIRAL ([Liu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9)) and MARS ([Yuan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10)) introduce RAE to handle role asymmetry, achieving
strong performance in limited action spaces. However, in more complex
open-ended language domains, these methods suffer from evolutionary
impasse: LLMs converge to homogeneous behaviors, collapsing outcome
distributions into deterministic results. This drives advantages toward
zero, causing gradient vanishing and trapping models in suboptimal
states. Our DEPT addresses this through dual-baseline perception to
detect stagnation, combined with asymmetric advantage reshaping to
restore optimization landscape and ensure continuous policy evolution.

## 7 Conclusions

In this paper, we address the critical challenge of evolution impasse in
applying self-play RLVR to open-ended social language games, where agent
training is susceptible to gradient vanishing and strategic stagnation.
We propose DEPT, which dynamically perceives evolutionary states and
adaptively adjusts the optimization landscape via dual-timescale value
baselines. By quantifying stagnation through baseline divergence and
match entropy, DEPT selectively intervenes to penalize dominant outcomes
while amplifying rare trajectories, effectively restoring gradient
signals and enforcing continuous strategic exploration. Extensive
empirical results confirm that our approach not only surpasses SOTA
methods but also maintains superior out-of-distribution generalization
capabilities.

## Limitations

While DEPT demonstrates substantial improvements in preventing
evolutionary impasse during self-play training, several limitations
offer avenues for future research. (1) Self-play reinforcement learning
inherently demands computational resources. Our experiments required
approximately 30 GPU-hours per run on 8 NVIDIA A800 GPUs. These resource
requirements may limit accessibility for researchers with constrained
computational budgets. (2) Following the reasoning-then-acting paradigm,
our method generates extended response sequences containing both
reasoning traces ($`c_{t}`$) and executable actions ($`a_{t}`$). This
increases inference latency compared to direct action generation, a
common trade-off in test-time scaling approaches. The computational cost
becomes more pronounced when scaling to longer interaction horizons or
larger LLM backbones. We believe our dual-scale evolutionary policy
training method provides a principled foundation that could inspire
novel mechanisms for detecting and intervening in more complex
evolutionary dynamics, and we hope this work motivates the community to
explore these promising directions.

## Appendix A Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we provide a theoretical analysis of DEPT. We first
establish the mathematical foundation of the Time-scaled Evolutionary
Perception mechanism, proving that the proposed metrics serve as
estimators for the velocity and variance of the reward distribution.
Subsequently, we analyze the gradient dynamics, proving that our method
ensures effectively restoring gradient signals in evolutionary impasse
via a synthetic variance injection mechanism.

### A.1 Mathematical Foundation of Evolutionary Perception

The core of DEPT lies in quantifying the evolutionary state using
baseline divergence and match entropy. Here, we provide the formal
derivations linking these metrics to the underlying reward dynamics.

#### A.1.1 Baseline Divergence as Velocity Estimation

In the main text, we utilize the divergence between fast and slow
baselines to measure training stationarity. We now prove that this
metric is mathematically equivalent to an estimator of the velocity of
expected return.

###### Proposition A.1 (Divergence-Velocity Equivalence).

Let $`\mu(t)=\mathbb{E}[R_{p}(\tau)]`$ be the underlying expected return
at step $`t`$. Assuming $`\mu(t)`$ is locally linear within the
effective window of the Exponential Moving Average (EMA), the expected
divergence between two baselines with decay rates
$`\alpha_{\text{slow}}>\alpha_{\text{fast}}`$ is proportional to the
magnitude of the time derivative of the expected return:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{p}^{\text{fast},t}-b_{p}^{\text{slow},t}\right|\right]\approx C\cdot\left|\frac{d\mu(t)}{dt}\right|,
``` |  | (10) |

where $`C>0`$ is a constant determined by the time constants.

###### Proof.

Recall the EMA update rule:
$`b_{p}^{k,t}=\alpha_{k}b_{p}^{k,t-1}+(1-\alpha_{k})R_{p}(\tau)`$. By
recursively expanding this update, the baseline at time $`t`$ can be
expressed as a weighted sum of historical rewards. Taking the
expectation and performing a first-order Taylor expansion of the
expected return $`\mu(t-j)\approx\mu(t)-j\cdot\dot{\mu}(t)`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[b_{p}^{k,t}]`$ | $`\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{k})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{j}\mu(t-j)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\approx(1-\alpha_{k})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{j}\left(\mu(t)-j\cdot\dot{\mu}(t)\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mu(t)\underbrace{(1-\alpha_{k})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{j}}_{=1}-\dot{\mu}(t)(1-\alpha_{k})\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j\alpha_{k}^{j}.`$ |  | (11) |

Using the geometric series identity
$`\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}jx^{j}=\frac{x}{(1-x)^{2}}`$ for $`|x|<1`$, we
substitute $`x=\alpha_{k}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[b_{p}^{k,t}]\approx\mu(t)-\dot{\mu}(t)\cdot\frac{\alpha_{k}}{1-\alpha_{k}}.
``` |  | (12) |

Let $`\mathcal{T}_{k}=\frac{\alpha_{k}}{1-\alpha_{k}}`$ denote the
effective time lag. The expected divergence is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\delta^{(t)}]&=\left|\mathbb{E}[b_{p}^{\text{fast},t}]-\mathbb{E}[b_{p}^{\text{slow},t}]\right|\\
&\approx\left|(\mu(t)-\mathcal{T}_{\text{fast}}\dot{\mu}(t))-(\mu(t)-\mathcal{T}_{\text{slow}}\dot{\mu}(t))\right|\\
&=(\mathcal{T}_{\text{slow}}-\mathcal{T}_{\text{fast}})\cdot|\dot{\mu}(t)|.\end{split}
``` |  | (13) |

Since $`\alpha_{\text{slow}}>\alpha_{\text{fast}}`$, we have
$`\mathcal{T}_{\text{slow}}>\mathcal{T}_{\text{fast}}`$, ensuring the
coefficient is positive. Thus, the baseline divergence serves as a
direct proxy for the training velocity $`|\dot{\mu}(t)|`$. ∎

#### A.1.2 Match Entropy as Time-Varying Variance Proxy

We now establish the mathematical relationship between the observed
Match Entropy $`H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}`$ and the underlying reward
variance.

###### Lemma A.1 (Entropy-Variance Correlation).

Let $`\nu(t)=\text{Var}[R_{p}(\tau)]`$ be the underlying reward variance
at time step $`t`$. As the match entropy vanishes, the reward variance
asymptotically approaches zero:

|     |                                             |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \lim_{H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}\to 0}\nu(t)=0.
       ```                                          |     | (14) |

Consequently, maintaining high entropy is a necessary condition for
maintaining non-zero reward variance.

###### Proof.

Consider the discrete outcome space
$`\mathcal{O}=\{o_{1},\dots,o_{K}\}`$ (e.g., {Win, Loss, Draw}) with
associated fixed reward values $`\{r_{1},\dots,r_{K}\}`$. At time $`t`$,
the policy $`\pi_{\theta_{t}}`$ induces a probability distribution
$`p_{i}(t)=P(R_{p}(\tau)=r_{i})`$.

The variance $`\nu(t)`$ is formally defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nu(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{i}(t)\left(r_{i}-\mathbb{E}[R_{p}(\tau)]\right)^{2}.
``` |  | (15) |

From information theory, the entropy
$`H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}=-\sum p_{i}(t)\log p_{i}(t)`$ approaches zero
if and only if the probability mass concentrates on a single dominant
outcome $`o_{k}`$. Mathematically, this implies the limit behavior:

|     |                                                        |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       p_{k}(t)\to 1\quad\text{and}\quad p_{j\neq k}(t)\to 0.
       ```                                                     |     | (16) |

In this limit, the expected return converges to the reward of the
dominant outcome: $`\mathbb{E}[R_{p}(\tau)]\to r_{k}`$. Substituting
these limits into the definition of $`\nu(t)`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\lim_{H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}\to 0}\nu(t)&=\lim_{p_{k}\to 1}\Big[p_{k}(t)(r_{k}-r_{k})^{2}\\
&\quad+\sum_{j\neq k}p_{j}(t)(r_{j}-r_{k})^{2}\Big]\\
&=1\cdot 0+0=0.\end{split}
``` |  | (17) |

This derivation proves that $`H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}`$ and $`\nu(t)`$
are positively correlated in the asymptotic limit: a collapse in match
entropy strictly necessitates a collapse in the reward variance
$`\nu(t)`$. ∎

#### A.1.3 Bayesian Derivation of Intervention Coefficient

We presented the intervention coefficient $`\lambda^{(t)}`$ in a
specific functional form in the main text. Here, we derive this form
from a general Bayesian perspective.

###### Theorem A.1 (Bayesian Form of $`\lambda`$).

Let $`Z_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$ be a latent variable where $`Z_{t}=1`$ indicates
a evolution impasse. The coefficient $`\lambda^{(t)}`$ represents the
posterior probability $`P(Z_{t}=1\mid\mathcal{O}_{t})`$ given
observations $`\mathcal{O}_{t}`$.

###### Proof.

We decompose the degenerate state into two independent necessary
conditions: Stationarity ($`\mathcal{S}`$) and Determinism
($`\mathcal{D}`$). Using Bayes’ theorem and assuming conditional
independence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\lambda^{(t)}\triangleq P(Z_{t}=1)\propto P(\mathcal{S}\mid\delta^{(t)})\cdot P(\mathcal{D}\mid H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}).
``` |  | (18) |

To map observations to probabilities, we introduce two kernel functions
$`\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}`$ and $`\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{D}}`$ with
range $`[0,1]`$:

1.  1.
    Stagnation Kernel $`\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{S}}`$: Since
    $`\delta^{(t)}\propto|\text{Velocity}|`$, stationarity implies
    $`\delta^{(t)}\to 0`$. We select the hyperbolic tangent kernel:

    |  |  |  |  |
    |----|----|----|----|
    |  |
    ``` math
    \sigma^{(t)}=P(\mathcal{S}\mid\delta^{(t)})=1-\tanh(\delta^{(t)}).
    ``` |  | (19) |
2.  2.
    Determinism Kernel $`\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{D}}`$: Since low entropy
    implies determinism, we select the radical kernel to ensure high
    sensitivity near the boundary of exploration ($`H\approx 1`$):

    |  |  |  |  |
    |----|----|----|----|
    |  |
    ``` math
    \gamma^{(t)}=P(\mathcal{D}\mid H_{\text{match}}^{(t)})=\sqrt{1-H_{\text{match}}^{(t)}}.
    ``` |  | (20) |

Substituting these specific kernels yields the instantiation used in
DEPT: $`\lambda^{(t)}=\sigma^{(t)}\cdot\gamma^{(t)}`$. ∎

### A.2 Gradient Dynamics Analysis

#### A.2.1 The Vanishing Gradient of Standard Methods

We first prove why standard methods fail in evolutionary impasse.

###### Theorem A.2 (Variance-Dependent Gradient Bound).

Assume the policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is Lipschitz continuous such that the
norm of the score function is bounded by a constant $`G_{\max}`$. The
magnitude of the expected policy gradient in standard Advantage
Estimation is upper-bounded by the standard deviation of the reward:

|     |                                                              |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \|\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)\|\leq G_{\max}\cdot\sqrt{\nu(t)}.
       ```                                                           |     | (21) |

###### Proof.

We start with the standard gradient definition
$`\nabla_{\theta}J=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}[(R-\mu)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}]`$.
First, we invoke the Lipschitz continuity assumption[Papini et al.
(2018)](#bib.bib47); [Agarwal et al. (2019)](#bib.bib48): for smooth
neural policies, the gradient of the log-likelihood is bounded:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sup_{\tau}\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\tau)\|\leq G_{\max}.
``` |  | (22) |

Next, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for random variables
($`\mathbb{E}[XY]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]`$):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\|\nabla_{\theta}J\|^{2}&=\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[(R-\mu)\nabla\log\pi\right]\right\|^{2}\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(R-\mu)^{2}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\log\pi\|^{2}\right]\\
&=\nu(t)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla\log\pi\|^{2}\right].\end{split}
``` |  | (23) |

Substituting the bound $`G_{\max}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\|\nabla_{\theta}J\|^{2}\leq\nu(t)\cdot G_{\max}^{2}\implies\|\nabla_{\theta}J\|\leq G_{\max}\sqrt{\nu(t)}.
``` |  | (24) |

Implication: In a evolution impasse,
Lemma [A.1](#A1.Thmlemma1 "Lemma A.1 (Entropy-Variance Correlation). ‣ A.1.2 Match Entropy as Time-Varying Variance Proxy ‣ A.1 Mathematical Foundation of Evolutionary Perception ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Breaking the Impasse: Dual-Scale Evolutionary Policy Training for Social Language Agents")
dictates that $`\nu(t)\to 0`$. Consequently, the gradient magnitude
$`\|\nabla J\|`$ is forced to zero. The optimization halts specifically
because the signal variance $`\nu(t)`$ required to drive the gradient
has vanished. ∎

#### A.2.2 Gradient Restoration

We prove that DEPT can effectively recovers gradient.

###### Theorem A.3 (Push-Pull Gradient Restoration).

In a degenerate state where natural variance $`\nu(t)\to 0`$,
DEPT constructs a non-vanishing synthetic gradient field driven by the
historical value range $`[V_{\min},V_{\max}]`$.

###### Proof.

Consider a training batch $`\mathcal{B}`$ of size $`B`$, decomposed into
a set of dominant trajectories $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{dom}}`$ (size $`M`$)
and rare trajectories $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{rare}}`$ (size $`N`$), such
that $`B=M+N`$ and $`M\gg N`$ (indicating a degenerate state). When
intervention is triggered ($`\lambda^{(t)}\to 1`$), the gradient update
$`\nabla J_{p}`$ is calculated as the summation over these two subsets:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\nabla J_{p}&\approx\frac{1}{B}\Bigg[\underbrace{\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{dom}}}(R_{p}(\tau)-V_{\max})\nabla\log\pi_{p}(\tau)}_{\text{Aggregate Push Force}}\\
&\quad+\underbrace{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{rare}}}(R_{p}(\tau^{\prime})-V_{\min})\nabla\log\pi_{p}(\tau^{\prime})}_{\text{Aggregate Pull Force}}\Bigg].\end{split}
``` |  | (25) |

1.  1.
    Symmetric Dilemma: In standard methods, the baseline
    $`b\approx\frac{1}{M}\sum R_{p}(\tau_{\text{dom}})`$. Thus, the
    advantage for the $`M`$ dominant samples approaches zero. Since
    $`N\to 0`$, the total gradient vanishes.
2.  2.
    Aggregate Push Mechanism ($`M`$ samples): For the dominant set, we
    set the baseline to $`V_{\max}`$. Since $`V_{\max}\geq R_{p}(\tau)`$
    for $`\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{dom}}`$, the term
    $`(R_{p}(\tau)-V_{\max})`$ is strictly non-positive. Although
    individual gradients may be small, the summation over $`M`$ samples
    generates a substantial negative gradient field, actively
    suppressing the probability of the stagnant strategy.
3.  3.
    Aggregate Pull Mechanism ($`N`$ samples): For the rare set, we set
    the baseline to $`V_{\min}`$. The term
    $`(R_{p}(\tau^{\prime})-V_{\min})`$ is maximized, assigning a high
    positive weight to these sparse signals. This amplifies the
    exploration direction even when $`N`$ is small.

Through this summation, DEPT effectively injects a Synthetic Variance
$`\nu_{\text{syn}}\propto(V_{\max}-V_{\min})^{2}`$ into the batch.
Unlike the natural variance $`\nu(t)`$ which vanishes as $`N\to 0`$,
this synthetic variance remains robust, ensuring $`\|\nabla J_{p}\|>0`$
and forcing the policy distribution to shift away from the evolution
impasse. ∎

## Appendix B Implementation details

### B.1 Baselines

For a fair comparison, all methods use the same amount of training
transitions for each task. For online RL methods (e.g., SPIRAL, MARS,
GRPO), all RL hyperparameters and configurations remain consistent with
our method, except for their respective advantage estimation schemes.
For the offline RL method (e.g.,SPAG), we adopt the same reward
computation logic and three-iteration update procedure as in the
original work. Due to significant performance degradation of baseline
methods on the Negotiation, we report their best checkpoints selected
based on training curves, while using the final checkpoint (step 400)
for our method; for other tasks (Don’t Say It, Two Dollar), all methods
are evaluated at the final checkpoint (step 400).

### B.2 Tasks

##### Don’t Say It

tests linguistic manipulation and theory of mind through adversarial
dialogue. Each player is assigned a secret target word and must guide
their opponent into saying it during natural conversation, while
avoiding their opponent’s unknown target word. Success depends on
tracking dialogue states, predicting opponent responses, and detecting
potential traps. Beyond strategic reasoning, players should maintain
natural conversational flow to avoid arousing suspicion. These
capabilities are relevant to persuasion, deception detection, and
defense against social engineering. The environment ID in TextArena is
DontSayIt-v0 with max_turns=20.

##### Negotiation

involves multi-attribute trading under incomplete information. Players
trade five commodity types (Wheat, Wood, Sheep, Brick, Ore) to maximize
their portfolio value, determined by private utility functions unknown
to the opponent. The action space supports bundled offers, rejections,
and binding acceptances. Success requires not only inferring opponent
preferences and identifying mutually beneficial trades, but also
articulating proposals persuasively and building cooperative rapport
through dialogue. These skills transfer to resource allocation and
mixed-motive multi-agent coordination. The environment ID in TextArena
is SimpleNegotiation-v0 with max_turns=10. Note that our setup matches
the original game specification; in contrast, SPIRAL simplified the
environment from five commodities to two.

##### Two Dollar

models bargaining with hidden constraints. Players divide a fixed pool
(e.g., \$2.00) while following secret role-specific instructions that
impose behavioral restrictions (e.g., aggression levels) or minimum
acceptable amounts. Success requires inferring opponent constraints from
behavioral cues, adapting communication style to different personas, and
using persuasive dialogue to reach agreement while maintaining role
consistency. These capabilities are relevant to constrained
decision-making and human-AI coordination in mixed-motive settings. The
environment ID in TextArena is TwoDollar-v0 with max_turns=10.

Across all three tasks, we align our reward mechanism with the
SPIRAL ([Liu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib9)): a win yields a reward of
$`+1`$, a loss yields $`-1`$, and a draw yields $`0`$. Additionally,
format errors incur a penalty of $`-1.5`$.

increases difficulty by shifting from common words to open-domain

generalize their division strategies to unseen scales. The difficulty is
increased by a shortened horizon of 6 rounds. This environment assesses
whether agents can maintain logical consistency and role compliance when
facing novel numerical parameters.
````

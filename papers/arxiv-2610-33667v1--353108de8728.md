# SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch via Progressive Privileged Self-Distillation

Xudong Cai   Zhengxi Lu   Yuchen Yan   Jian Shao Affiliation: Zhejiang
University    Jun Xiao   Yueting Zhuang   Yongliang Shen
Email: [{ccdown, zhengxilu, syl}@zju.edu.cn](mailto:)
Affiliation: Zhejiang University

###### Abstract

Resolving a repository-level issue typically requires a
software-engineering (SWE) agent to localize the faulty code, reproduce
the failure, and patch the code. Reinforcement learning trains such
agents from a single test result on the final patch, while on-policy
self-distillation (OPSD) adds token-level supervision from a teacher
with privileged evidence, exposing the full reference patch at every
step. Neither provides stage-specific guidance for localization or
reproduction. We propose SWE-Ladder, which turns the verified repair of
each training instance into stage-specific evidence: the changed files
and lines for localization, the fail-to-pass tests for reproduction, and
the diff for patching. A rule-based router assigns each step of an
end-to-end rollout to a stage based on its tool call, a frozen teacher
sees only the matching evidence, and the stages are distilled in repair
order while GRPO optimizes the full trajectory. With
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 on SWE-bench Verified under the R2E-Gym scaffold,
SWE-Ladder improves pass@1 from $`9.6\%`$ to $`16.4\%`$, compared with
$`10.4\%`$ for GRPO and $`12.4\%`$ for OPSD with the full patch. Under
the OpenHands scaffold, it improves pass@1 from $`13.0\%`$ to
$`17.6\%`$. Reversing the stage order under R2E-Gym reduces pass@1 to
$`11.4\%`$. Code is available at
[https://github.com/sekongger/SWE-Ladder](https://github.com/sekongger/SWE-Ladder).

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become an
effective approach to training software-engineering (SWE) agents ([Luo
et al., 2025](#bib.bib7); [Golubev et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)). The agent
edits a repository over many tool calls, and the only training signal is
whether its final patch passes the designated tests ([Jimenez et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1); [Chowdhury et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)). Over dozens of
turns, it is difficult to identify which decision caused a failure ([Xia
et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)). On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) offers
denser supervision ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib13); [Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib30)). The same model acts as a teacher that receives
privileged evidence withheld from the student and scores every token of
the student’s trajectory ([Lu and Thinking Machines Lab,
2025](#bib.bib24); [Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib25)). In SWE, the
available evidence is the verified repair of each training instance, and
the natural choice is to show the teacher the full reference patch at
every step
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).

To see what these signals teach, we measure three intermediate behaviors
(Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).
They record whether the agent edits the files changed by the reference
repair, reproduces the failure with a script, and re-runs the script
successfully after its last edit. We find two patterns. First, the
outcome reward does not consistently improve these behaviors: under
GRPO, pass@1 barely moves (from 9.6% to 10.4%), while reproduction falls
from 59.4% to 48.6%. Second, showing the teacher the full patch raises
pass@1 but lowers post-edit verification from 22.6% to 16.2%.

The full patch conflates the evidence for three different skills.
Localization needs to know where the fault is, reproduction needs to
know how the failure shows up, and patching needs to know what to
change. During a localization step, a teacher that sees the full patch
receives not only localization cues but also patch-generation details,
and may therefore favor editing over searching. A similar effect has
been reported in single-turn reasoning, where a teacher conditioned on
the full reference makes the student skip intermediate steps and verify
less ([Zhang, 2026](#bib.bib31)). _At each step, the teacher should
therefore see only the part of the verified repair that the current
stage needs._

The three skills support one another: localization identifies the code
to investigate, reproduction makes the failure concrete, and the
resulting test provides a basis for checking a patch. We therefore
organize privileged supervision into a progressive training schedule:
localization, then reproduction, then patch generation.

Following these observations, we propose SWE-Ladder, which trains a
single end-to-end agent with progressive privileged self-distillation.
Prior work obtains stage-level supervision by fixing a
localize-then-repair pipeline at inference time ([Xia et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18); [Ma et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [He et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)), which limits how the agent can explore. SWE-Ladder
leaves the agent’s actions unconstrained and decomposes only the
teacher’s evidence. A deterministic router labels each step as
localization (LOC), reproduction (REP), or patch generation (PATCH) from
its tool call. The teacher then sees only the slice of the repair for
that stage. Training proceeds through the three stages in order,
distilling one stage at a time, while GRPO optimizes the full
trajectory.

With Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 on SWE-bench Verified, the gain depends on
how the teacher receives evidence. Without privileged evidence, GRPO
improves pass@1 from 9.6% to 10.4%. With the full patch at every step,
GRPO+OPSD reaches 12.4%, and training all three stages at once also
reaches 12.4%. Introducing the stages in order, SWE-Ladder reaches 16.4%
and is the only method that improves both localization and verification
without lowering reproduction
(Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).
Reversing the order lowers pass@1 to 11.4%, and on the OpenHands
scaffold SWE-Ladder improves pass@1 from 13.0% to 17.6%.

Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  We show with stage-wise diagnostics that outcome rewards do not
  consistently improve intermediate repair behavior and that a
  full-patch teacher lowers post-edit verification.
- •
  We propose SWE-Ladder, which shows the teacher one stage’s slice of
  the verified repair at a time, following the order of repair.
- •
  SWE-Ladder outperforms GRPO and full-patch GRPO+OPSD on two agent
  scaffolds, and removing any stage, reversing the order, or training
  all stages at once lowers pass@1.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Post-Training for SWE Agents

Recent datasets and environments expand the supply of executable repair
tasks, trajectories, and verifiers for SWE training ([Pan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9); [Jain et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Yang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib11); [Badertdinov et al., 2026](#bib.bib4); [Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14); [Sonwane et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). SWE-RL applies
GRPO with patch-similarity rewards, DeepSWE uses verifier-based RL, and
long-context SWE training combines rejection fine-tuning with multi-turn
RL ([Wei et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Luo et al., 2025](#bib.bib7);
[Golubev et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)). Kimi-Dev transfers an
Agentless-based skill prior to SWE-Agent through lightweight SFT,
without additional multi-turn RL ([Yang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib16)). For
RL-based agents, feedback remains largely trajectory-level, with limited
supervision for intermediate tool decisions.

### 2.2 On-Policy Distillation for Agents

Agentic distillation methods regulate teacher guidance across multi-turn
trajectories. SDAR gates an OPSD auxiliary loss with detached token
statistics while retaining verifier-driven RL; StepOPSD redistributes
preference credit over action-centered steps using hindsight-enriched
contexts ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib26); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib27)). SOD reweights OPD by step-level divergence, while
TCOD progressively expands the exposed trajectory depth ([Zhong et al.,
2026](#bib.bib28); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)). Another line asks
what the teacher should see: AR-OPD replaces full-reference imitation
with anchored residual guidance, ATESD learns how much of the reference
to reveal, and EviSD gives search and answer actions different
evidence ([Zhang, 2026](#bib.bib31); [Han et al., 2026](#bib.bib34);
[Xie et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)). These methods keep the exposure fixed
during training, whereas SWE-Ladder orders the evidence across stages.

### 2.3 Subtask-Oriented Software Repair

Agentless organizes repair into localization, patch generation, and
validation, while AutoCodeRover iteratively retrieves program structure
before patching ([Xia et al., 2024](#bib.bib18); [Zhang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib23)). SWE-Fixer follows a retrieve-then-edit workflow, and
SoRFT makes file-, function-, and line-level localization explicit
before editing ([Xie et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Ma et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)). For testing, SWT-Bench evaluates reproduction
through generated tests, while AssertFlip converts passing tests into
fail-to-pass reproduction tests ([Mündler et al., 2024](#bib.bib21);
[Khatib et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)). SWE-Swiss trains localization,
repair, and test generation through multi-task SFT followed by targeted
RL ([He et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)). These works make repair subgoals
explicit and fix them into the inference pipeline or the training data.
SWE-Ladder keeps the agent end-to-end and uses this structure only to
align privileged teacher evidence with subtask-specific response tokens
and progressively schedule distillation. Curricula for LLM reinforcement
learning usually order problems by difficulty ([Simonds and Yoshiyama,
2025](#bib.bib33)); SWE-Ladder keeps each task fixed and orders the
evidence that the teacher sees.

## 3 Method

Following the analysis in
Section [1](#S1 "1 Introduction ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")
and the staged organization of Agentless ([Xia et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)), we organize end-to-end software repair into three
training-time subtasks: fault localization (LOC), failure reproduction
(REP), and patch generation (PATCH). We collect a distinct source of
task-specific privileged evidence for each subtask. SWE-Ladder then
trains a single policy in the end-to-end repair setting while
progressively targeting one subtask at a time with its corresponding
privileged evidence
(Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2 Stage-Specific Privileged Evidence ‣ 3 Method ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).
To target a subtask within an end-to-end rollout, we first assign each
assistant step a repair-stage label using its tool call. At each
training iteration, the schedule selects an active stage; its evidence
conditions the teacher, and only response tokens labeled with that stage
receive the OPSD loss. GRPO continues to optimize all valid response
tokens, so stage-specific supervision is integrated into full-trajectory
learning.

### 3.1 Rule-Based Stage Routing

SWE-Ladder uses a deterministic tool-level state machine to assign each
generated step one of LOC, REP, or PATCH, in that order, while allowing
state-dependent backtracking. The router labels response tokens and
routes only the OPSD teacher context; it does not constrain tool
choices, rollouts, or rewards. Marker rules, backtracking,
malformed-tool handling, and token alignment are given in
Appendix [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Stage Annotation and Token Alignment ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation").
On held-out stage-labeled steps, the router agrees with the reference
labels on 92.0% of steps; a neural router reaches higher agreement but
trains a worse agent
(Section [4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).

### 3.2 Stage-Specific Privileged Evidence

We construct three evidence views from each training instance’s verified
repair metadata. We use only factual content from the repository, tests,
and verified repair; collecting this evidence introduces no knowledge
generated by external large language models. Each view serves one
subgoal:

- •
  Localization evidence. We extract changed non-test file paths and the
  old-side hunk ranges from the verified repair, together with the
  corresponding pre-patch source lines. These clues identify where to
  investigate without revealing the replacement code.
- •
  Reproduction evidence. We collect fail-to-pass test identifiers and
  names, and include reference test code and candidate test changes when
  available. This evidence describes the failing behavior and provides a
  starting point for constructing a reproduction script.
- •
  Patch evidence. We provide the verified repair diff, which specifies
  the fix.

The evidence fields are stored with each instance, but teacher prompts
are rendered at training time. All three teacher variants share the
student’s system instructions and end-to-end task prompt; only an
appended evidence block differs.
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Stage-Routed Privileged Information ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")
shows a routed trajectory, the evidence fields for each stage, and their
token cost. The active training phase selects which evidence view is
used for distillation.

In each phase, a frozen teacher receives one stage’s evidence and

### 3.3 Full-Trajectory GRPO Objective

We denote the standard GRPO objective ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib12))
for rollout $`g`$ at training iteration $`u`$ by
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}^{u,g}(\theta)`$. It acts on all valid
model-generated response tokens throughout the end-to-end trajectory,
regardless of the active training stage. The stage schedule changes only
the auxiliary OPSD term defined below; the GRPO loss and its token
coverage remain unchanged. In particular, stage labels are annotations
for distillation, not early-stopping conditions: a rollout is never
truncated at a stage boundary and always runs to normal termination or
the configured resource limit.

### 3.4 Stage-Conditioned OPSD and Joint Objective

For a fixed training iteration $`u`$ and rollout $`g`$, we suppress
$`(u,g)`$ in token-level quantities and per-rollout losses. The active
training stage $`a(u)`$, defined in
Section [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Progressive Privilege Scheduling ‣ 3 Method ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation"),
selects the teacher’s privileged context, so $`c_{t}=c_{a(u)}`$
throughout the rollout. The evidence views are described in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Stage-Specific Privileged Evidence ‣ 3 Method ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")
and
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Stage-Routed Privileged Information ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation").
Let $`z_{t}\in\{\mathrm{LOC},\mathrm{REP},\mathrm{PATCH}\}`$ denote the
stage label at token position $`t`$, assigned by the step-level router
in
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Rule-Based Stage Routing ‣ 3 Method ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation").

The teacher $`q_{\phi_{0}}`$ is initialized from the student’s base
checkpoint and remains frozen throughout training. Let $`h_{t}`$ denote
the student-sampled interaction prefix before token $`t`$, including
earlier tool observations. We abbreviate the student and teacher
distributions as $`p_{t}(v)=p_{\theta}(v\mid h_{t})`$ and
$`q_{t}(v)=q_{\phi_{0}}(v\mid h_{t},c_{t})`$, respectively, with the
dependence of $`p_{t}`$ on $`\theta`$ implicit. The teacher selects its
$`K`$ highest-probability vocabulary tokens:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \mathcal{K}_{t}=\operatorname{TopK}_{v}\,q_{t}(v).
       ```                                                 |     | (1) |

Its distribution is then normalized over this set:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\widetilde{q}_{t}(v)=\frac{q_{t}(v)}{\sum_{w\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}q_{t}(w)},\qquad v\in\mathcal{K}_{t}.
``` |  | (2) |

The student probabilities retain their full-vocabulary normalization. We
use the following stop-gradient top-$`K`$ surrogate:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle d_{t}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}p_{t}(v)`$ |  | (3) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\times\bigl[\operatorname{sg}(\log p_{t}(v))-\operatorname{sg}(\log\widetilde{q}_{t}(v))\bigr].`$ |  |  |

Here, $`\operatorname{sg}`$ denotes stop-gradient: both log terms are
detached, while gradients flow through the outer student probability
factor $`p_{t}(v)`$. Implementation details are provided in
Appendix [A.6](#A1.SS6 "A.6 Top-𝐾 Distillation and Systems Configuration ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation").

To restrict distillation to the active subtask, let
$`m_{t}^{\mathrm{resp}}`$ equal one at valid model-generated response
positions and zero elsewhere. The OPSD mask and normalizer are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle m_{t}^{\mathrm{OPSD}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=m_{t}^{\mathrm{resp}}\,\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=a(u)\},`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle N^{\mathrm{OPSD}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max\bigl\{1,\textstyle\sum_{t}m_{t}^{\mathrm{OPSD}}\bigr\}.`$ |  |  |

The per-rollout distillation loss and joint objective are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{\mathrm{OPSD}}}\sum_{t}m_{t}^{\mathrm{OPSD}}d_{t}(\theta),`$ |  | (5) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}(\theta),`$ |  |  |

where $`\lambda`$ controls the distillation weight. GRPO remains active
on all valid response tokens, including those outside the active
subtask.

### 3.5 Progressive Privilege Scheduling

SWE-Ladder applies privileged stages in the fixed order
$`\mathrm{LOC}\rightarrow\mathrm{REP}\rightarrow\mathrm{PATCH}`$, with
each phase continuing from the preceding checkpoint. The active stage
$`a(u)`$ selects both the teacher’s privileged evidence and the response
tokens used for OPSD via
Eq. ([4](#S3.E4 "In 3.4 Stage-Conditioned OPSD and Joint Objective ‣ 3 Method ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).
The order follows how repair uses the evidence. On-policy distillation
supervises only the states that the student reaches, so patch evidence
helps only once the student can find the code to change, and a
reproduction script is what the student later runs to check its patch.
Section [4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")
compares alternative orders. The formal schedule, phase budgets, and
training and inference details are given in
Appendix [A.7](#A1.SS7 "A.7 Progressive Privilege Scheduling and Training Configuration ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation").

### 4.2 Main Results

#### SWE-Ladder improves end-to-end repair on both scaffolds.

On R2E-Gym, SWE-Ladder raises pass@1 from 9.6% to 16.4%, compared with
10.4% for GRPO and 12.4% for GRPO+OPSD
(Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ SWE-Ladder improves end-to-end repair on both scaffolds. ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")),
without task-specific supervised fine-tuning. It also uses fewer steps
than the base model (19.3 vs. 26.5) and completes 98.6% of its
trajectories, whereas GRPO completes only 36.0% before the resource
limit. With the same patch evidence, SDAR falls below the base model
(4.6%).

To investigate training stability, we compare SWE-Ladder with GRPO,
GRPO+OPSD, and SDAR.
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.3 Training Dynamics ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")
tracks policy entropy, gradient norm, response length, and shaped
training reward across individual training runs
(Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Training Dynamics ‣ Appendix C Additional Training Diagnostics ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).

GRPO exhibits substantial deterioration in its training dynamics.
Comparing the first and last ten iterations, its mean entropy decreases
from $`0.0300`$ to $`0.0061`$, while its gradient norm drops from
$`1.820`$ to $`0.187`$. Meanwhile, the average response length nearly
doubles from $`8.29`$k to $`16.73`$k tokens, and training reward
declines.

In contrast, SWE-Ladder exhibits more consistent optimization dynamics,
with relatively stable entropy ($`0.0251\rightarrow 0.0221`$) and
gradient norm ($`2.353\rightarrow 2.397`$), alongside a modest increase
in response length ($`10.09`$k to $`11.62`$k tokens). Although its
training reward remains variable, SWE-Ladder does not exhibit the
pronounced diagnostic deterioration observed under GRPO.

GRPO+OPSD and SDAR also retain optimization signals late in training.
Thus, maintaining entropy or gradient magnitude alone does not explain
SWE-Ladder’s superior held-out performance. These observations suggest
more consistent optimization behavior under the tested configuration,
without establishing a general stability guarantee.

## 5 Conclusion

We studied what a privileged teacher should see when training an
end-to-end SWE agent. SWE-Ladder gives the teacher only the evidence for
the current stage and introduces the stages in repair order. It improves
pass@1 on SWE-bench Verified from 9.6% to 16.4% with R2E-Gym and from
13.0% to 17.6% with OpenHands, and ablations show that both
stage-matched evidence and the stage order matter.

## Limitations

The evidence is bounded to one Qwen3-4B policy, the SWE-bench Verified
benchmark, and two agent scaffolds. Although the OpenHands results
provide a second scaffold check, the main ablations and the stage-wise
diagnostics use the R2E-Gym protocol. We therefore do not establish
transfer to larger backbones, other repair benchmarks, or substantially
different tool interfaces. The reported training curves and router
comparison each follow one checkpoint lineage without independent
random-seed repeats or confidence intervals, so the observed
optimization trends and downstream differences should be interpreted as
configuration-specific evidence.

The method also depends on verified repair metadata to construct the
three privileged views and on a parser that maps tool calls to repair
stages. The neural-router accuracy is agreement with Qwen3.8-Max
annotations rather than an independently adjudicated semantic label,
while the rule-based router can miss unfamiliar tools or atypical
backtracking patterns. Future work should test neural and hybrid routers
across models and benchmarks, repeat the training with independent
seeds, and quantify the cost and robustness of evidence construction.

## Ethics Statement

This work uses publicly available SWE-bench Verified issues, open-weight
Qwen3 models, the hosted Qwen3.8-Max model for rollout stage annotation,
and public R2E-Gym and OpenHands scaffolds. We do not recruit human
participants or collect new personal data. Training and evaluation are
conducted in isolated benchmark sandboxes and do not take actions on
external systems. Privileged repair evidence is used only as
training-time context and is absent at inference. Deploying agents
trained with SWE-Ladder beyond these benchmark sandboxes would require
additional safety validation, permission controls, and human oversight.

## Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation

### A.1 Stage-wise Capability Evaluation

Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")
reports four complementary capability measurements on the same 500
held-out SWE-bench Verified tasks. For each method, we use its selected
evaluation model and evaluate one agent trajectory per task under the
common evaluation scaffold. The first three measurements are behavioral
diagnostics extracted from the trajectory; they are not additional
verifier rewards.

- •
  Localization (gold-file recall). Let $`F`$ be the set of non-test
  files changed by the reference patch and $`E`$ the set of files
  changed by the agent’s final patch. We report $`|F\cap E|/|F|`$,
  averaged over tasks with a nonempty reference set. This measures
  whether the agent edits the files implicated by the issue, rather than
  whether the final patch is correct.
- •
  Reproduction (ran failing repro). We count a trajectory as reproducing
  the issue when the agent creates a reproduction script, executes it,
  and observes a failure signal such as a traceback, assertion failure,
  failed test, exception, or nonzero command result.
- •
  Verification (re-ran repro, passed). After the last source edit, we
  check whether the agent re-executes a previously created reproduction
  script without a failure signal. This captures an explicit post-edit
  verification action and does not require the final patch to pass the
  hidden benchmark tests.
- •
  Solve (pass@1). We use the official SWE-bench verifier result: a
  trajectory counts as solved when its submitted patch satisfies the
  designated fail-to-pass tests without introducing a regression.

We report each quantity as a percentage, but the aggregation differs by
metric: localization is the macro-average of task-level recalls over
tasks with a nonempty reference set, whereas reproduction, verification,
and solve are trajectory-level percentages. The diagnostic definitions
use only the agent trajectory, reference patch file paths, and verifier
outcome; they do not expose privileged teacher evidence to the student.
Consequently, the figure should be read as a stage-wise behavioral
comparison: a method can improve solve rate while becoming less reliable
on an earlier subtask, which is the failure mode the routed objective is
designed to avoid.

### A.2 Stage Annotation and Token Alignment

The main method uses the deterministic rule-based router described in
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Rule-Based Stage Routing ‣ 3 Method ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation").
The parser consumes only the parsed tool call: function name and
arguments (the editor command and path/file_path, or bash cmd/command).
It accepts both standard and malformed inline parameter forms and
supports the r2egym and OpenHands tool dialects. Creating or editing a
file whose basename contains repro or reproduce, executing the
corresponding Python script, creating or editing a test file, or running
the project’s test suite proposes REP. A test file is under /tests/ or
/test/, or has basename test\_\*, \*\_test.py, \*\_tests.py, \*tests.py,
edge_case\*, conftest.py, or contains fixture. Creating or editing any
other source file proposes PATCH. Search, editor views, and read-only
shell commands whose first token is in the exploration set are
exploration actions; all other tools emit no proposal.

A proposed stage advances the current state only if it is later in the
order LOC$`\rightarrow`$REP$`\rightarrow`$PATCH; other actions normally
inherit the current state, and LOC$`\rightarrow`$ PATCH can therefore
skip REP. Backtracking is state-dependent: in REP, three consecutive
exploration actions return the state to LOC; in PATCH, a single test run
demotes it to REP, while a single exploration action returns it to LOC.
These transitions are applied after the current step’s proposal.

The state after processing a step labels that same step. The label is
copied to its assistant reasoning and action tokens and to the
environment-token positions concatenated into the response tensor. A
separate response mask excludes environment observations from
optimization: GRPO uses all valid model-generated response positions,
while OPSD intersects this mask with the active-stage indicator in
Eq. ([4](#S3.E4 "In 3.4 Stage-Conditioned OPSD and Joint Objective ‣ 3 Method ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).
The annotation does not constrain tool choices, rollouts, or rewards,
and it routes only the OPD teacher context.

### A.3 Neural Router and Rule-Based Router Comparison

We also evaluate a lightweight neural router as an alternative to the
rules, rather than as a component of the main method. Qwen3.8-Max ([Qwen
Team, 2026](#bib.bib35)) labels assistant steps from end-to-end
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 trajectories as LOC, REP, or PATCH, using the
issue excerpt together with action and observation snippets. The
resulting data contain 10,440 valid labels from 731 trajectories. We use
a deterministic trajectory-level 80/20 hash split, giving 8,179 training
labels and 2,261 held-out labels. The router concatenates the parsed
tool function name with the lower-cased assistant action, applies a
TF–IDF unigram/bigram transform with 50,000 maximum features, and
predicts the three labels with a class-balanced linear classifier
($`C=4`$). Its held-out accuracy against the Qwen3.8-Max annotations is
98.63%. The deterministic rule-based router obtains 92.0% agreement on
the same 2,261 held-out records. These figures measure agreement with
the annotator on held-out trajectories, not independently adjudicated
semantic accuracy or generalization to unseen issues. Qwen3.8-Max is
used only to produce these rollout annotations; it is not an OPSD
teacher.

After each assistant message is generated, this classifier independently
predicts one canonical stage for the step, without temporal smoothing or
a monotonicity constraint. Consequently, small changes in an action or
its parsed tool call can change the predicted stage and fragment a
trajectory into isolated labels. The rule-based router instead uses an
ordered state machine with state-dependent backtracking; its assignments
are consequently robust to local action variation and tend to form
contiguous stage blocks. Because the assistant action text includes any
reasoning tokens present in that message, the classifier input includes
that text as well as the parsed function name; it does not use model
hidden states. The predicted label is copied to every assistant and
environment-token position generated by the step. If the classifier
cannot be loaded or produces an invalid label, the implementation falls
back to the rule-based state machine. Its annotation-agreement score and
downstream solve rate assess different properties: high agreement with
the annotator does not by itself establish better training supervision.

Figure [8](#A1.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ A.4 Evaluation Protocols ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")
reports the supplied neural-router evaluation points alongside the
complete rule-routed SWE-Ladder schedule. Despite its lower
label-agreement score, the rule-based router gives the stronger
downstream training result in this comparison, so annotation accuracy
alone is not a sufficient proxy for training utility.

### A.5 Termination-Aware Reward Shaping

For a fixed rollout, we suppress the indices $`(u,g)`$. Let
$`V\in\{0,1\}`$ be the verifier result, $`\xi`$ the termination reason,
and $`e`$ an indicator of a successful file-edit call, used as a
nonempty-patch proxy. Let $`\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{o}}`$ contain the
soft-overlong reasons TRUNCATION, MAX_STEPS, and TRAJ_TIMEOUT. Let
$`\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{d}}`$ contain ENV_DONE and the empty reason
$`\varnothing`$. The reward shaping is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle R^{(0)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0.5V,&\xi\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{o}},\\
V-0.2,&\xi\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{d}},\;V=e=0,\\
V,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}`$ |  | (6) |
|  | $`\displaystyle R`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}(R^{(0)},-1,1).`$ |  |  |

Thus,
Eq. ([6](#A1.E6 "In A.5 Termination-Aware Reward Shaping ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation"))
discounts a successful verifier result from one to $`0.5`$ under
soft-overlong termination, while an unsolved result remains zero. The
$`-0.2`$ term applies only when the agent voluntarily finishes (ENV_DONE
or an empty reason), remains unsolved, and has made no successful edit.
Exploration-tool reward and the no-tool-call penalty are both configured
to zero in the main training chain.

The overlong filter treats NO_CONTAINER, PROMPT_TRUNCATION, and
ENCODE_ERROR as hard failures. For these cases, the implementation
clears the complete response mask instead of treating the trajectory as
an ordinary optimization sample. After reward shaping, group-relative
advantages use the Bessel-corrected sample standard deviation, with
denominator $`G-1`$, where $`G=4`$ is the group size. GRPO is averaged
over each rollout’s valid response tokens; OPSD is averaged over the
subset assigned to the active stage. A rollout with no active-stage
response tokens contributes zero OPSD loss. The actor also includes the
configured $`0.005`$ response-token entropy bonus in the standard GRPO
objective; this term is separate from privileged distillation.

### A.6 Top-$`K`$ Distillation and Systems Configuration

Using the shorthand from
Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Stage-Conditioned OPSD and Joint Objective ‣ 3 Method ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation"),
let $`p_{t}(v)`$ and $`q_{t}(v)`$ denote the student and teacher
probabilities at the same student-sampled prefix. For
$`v\in\mathcal{K}_{t}`$, the implementation computes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log\widetilde{q}_{t}(v)=\log q_{t}(v)-\operatorname{LSE}_{w\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}\log q_{t}(w),
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\operatorname{LSE}`$ denotes log-sum-exp over the indexed
tokens. The implemented token contribution is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle d_{t}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}\exp\!\bigl(\log p_{t}(v)\bigr)`$ |  | (8) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\times\bigl[\operatorname{sg}(\log p_{t}(v))-\operatorname{sg}(\log\widetilde{q}_{t}(v))\bigr].`$ |  |  |

Both log terms are detached, so gradients flow through the student
probability factors in the surrogate. The teacher top-$`K`$ targets are
aligned with the corresponding student token probabilities at each
sampled prefix.

The OPSD loss mask is the elementwise product of the valid
model-response mask and the active-stage indicator. The teacher’s
top-$`K`$ targets are conditioned on the active stage’s privileged
evidence and aligned to the student-sampled response positions. Only
selected positions contribute to the loss, with no additional per-sample
OPSD token cap. Targets at non-active positions, if computed, are
excluded from distillation. This stage-masked objective is distinct from
the historical full-token distillation variants.

The student vocabulary normalizer is accumulated in chunks, and
probabilities at the teacher token ids are gathered directly. The
reported eight-NPU setup enables activation offload, FSDP parameter
offload for the actor and frozen reference policy, and optimizer offload
for the actor. Ulysses sequence parallelism has degree eight and
operates within each rollout sequence rather than across rollouts.

GRPO and OPSD use per-rollout means over their respective response-token
and active-stage masks. The training hyperparameters are given in
Appendix [A.7](#A1.SS7 "A.7 Progressive Privilege Scheduling and Training Configuration ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation").

### A.7 Progressive Privilege Scheduling and Training Configuration

SWE-Ladder applies privileged stages in the fixed order
$`\mathrm{LOC}\rightarrow\mathrm{REP}\rightarrow\mathrm{PATCH}`$. Given
phase budgets $`U_{L},U_{R},U_{P}`$, define the cumulative boundaries
$`b_{1}=U_{L}`$, $`b_{2}=U_{L}+U_{R}`$, and $`b_{3}=U_{L}+U_{R}+U_{P}`$.
The active training stage is

|     |                                                  |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       a(u)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{LOC},&0\leq u<b_{1},\\
       \mathrm{REP},&b_{1}\leq u<b_{2},\\
       \mathrm{PATCH},&b_{2}\leq u<b_{3}.\end{cases}
       ```                                               |     | (9) |

Suppressing the trajectory index, the teacher context for distillation
is

|     |                   |     |      |
|-----|-------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       c_{u,t}=c_{a(u)}.
       ```                |     | (10) |

where $`c_{a(u)}`$ is the teacher context selected by the active stage,
so
Eq. ([10](#A1.E10 "In A.7 Progressive Privilege Scheduling and Training Configuration ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation"))
holds at every token of the phase. The corresponding teacher predictions
contribute to the loss only at tokens selected by
Eq. ([4](#S3.E4 "In 3.4 Stage-Conditioned OPSD and Joint Objective ‣ 3 Method ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).
Thus, the LOC phase distills LOC-labeled response tokens, the REP phase
distills REP-labeled response tokens, and the PATCH phase distills
PATCH-labeled response tokens. No unprivileged distillation term is
applied to non-active stages. This schedule changes the active-stage
mask (and, when configured, the teacher context) while preserving
end-to-end rollouts and the full-response GRPO objective.

Each phase continuation warm-starts from its immediate predecessor,
yielding a single checkpoint sequence over the fixed schedule in
Eq. ([9](#A1.E9 "In A.7 Progressive Privilege Scheduling and Training Configuration ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).
The active privilege $`a(u)`$ is fixed for all rollouts collected at
global training iteration $`u`$.

#### Training configuration.

We use $`(U_{L},U_{R},U_{P})=(30,20,20)`$ global training iterations,
$`K=16`$, and $`\lambda=0.15`$. Each iteration samples 16 tasks with
four rollouts per task, yielding 64 rollouts, and the actor mini-batch
size is one rollout. The figures index completed training iterations
from $`1`$: LOC occupies steps $`1`$–$`30`$, REP steps $`31`$–$`50`$,
and PATCH steps $`51`$–$`70`$. This is equivalent to the zero-based
update index $`u`$ in
Eq. ([9](#A1.E9 "In A.7 Progressive Privilege Scheduling and Training Configuration ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details for End-to-End Privileged Distillation ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")).

#### Inference.

At inference, we deploy one end-to-end policy with the same rollout
termination conditions: submission, environment completion, or the
configured resource limit. Inference uses no teacher, no stage-specific
deployed policies, and no extra trainable parameters.

### C.1 Training Dynamics

Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.3 Training Dynamics ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SWE-Ladder: Learning to Localize, Reproduce, and Patch viaProgressive Privileged Self-Distillation")
compares GRPO, GRPO+OPSD, SDAR, and the complete SWE-Ladder sequence
over a common $`70`$-iteration horizon. We distinguish model-generated
tokens from tool/environment output and plot shaped training reward
separately from held-out pass@1. Curves show individual training runs,
not averages over independent seeds.

The GRPO run exhibits a pronounced decline in its optimization
diagnostics. Comparing iterations $`1`$–$`10`$ with $`61`$–$`70`$, its
mean entropy diagnostic falls from $`0.0300`$ to $`0.0061`$, mean
gradient norm from $`1.820`$ to $`0.187`$, and mean training reward from
$`0.236`$ to $`0.055`$. Meanwhile, mean generated tokens increase from
$`8.29`$k to $`16.73`$k. SWE-Ladder’s corresponding entropy means are
$`0.0251`$ and $`0.0221`$, and its gradient-norm means are $`2.353`$ and
$`2.397`$. Its mean generated length increases from $`10.09`$k to
$`11.62`$k. This sequence does not exhibit the same diagnostic decline
as the GRPO run, although its reward remains noisy and does not increase
monotonically.

The other privileged baselines provide an important qualification. The
GRPO+OPSD and SDAR traces retain additional optimization signal late in
training, but preserving entropy or gradient magnitude alone does not
explain SWE-Ladder’s held-out performance. These plots therefore do not
establish a general failure of either baseline; they provide diagnostic
context for the stage-aligned comparison.

###### Abstract.

Disease diagnosis is a key application of large language model (LLM)
agents in healthcare. However, existing medical agents are primarily
trained and evaluated with static question-answering data, providing
limited opportunities to learn active information acquisition,
examination decision-making, and dynamic clinical interaction. To
address this limitation, we present ClinVerse, an interactive,
real-data-grounded multi-agent environment designed for training and
evaluating medical diagnostic agents. Built on ClinPatient, a scalable
dataset comprising 150k diagnostic cases derived from real-world
electronic health records, ClinVerse supports coordinated interactions
among six specialized clinical roles. Specifically, a doctor agent
starts from an initial patient complaint, progressively gathers clinical
evidence through patient interviews and examination requests, and
ultimately produces a diagnosis. For each interaction trajectory, a
rule-based scoring framework quantifies information acquisition,
examination ordering, and diagnostic accuracy. These trajectory-level
scores serve as reward signals for agent training while also enabling
scalable evaluation. In experiments, we evaluate various general-purpose
and medical-domain models and show that nine self-evolution methods
across three categories consistently improve agents’ diagnostic
capabilities within ClinVerse. We further analyze the interplay among
model capability, environment feedback, and self-evolution methods¹¹ 1

[github](https://github.com/SirryChen/ClinVerse), demo at

To alleviate the shortage of healthcare resources, medical agents driven
by large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being integrated into
modern clinical practice, with assisting diagnosis emerging as one of
their most important applications ([Fleming et al., 2021](#bib.bib1);
[Pauker and Kassirer, 1980](#bib.bib2)). To ensure their reliability in
real-world clinical settings, extensive research has been devoted to
evaluating and advancing their diagnostic capabilities  ([Singhal et
al., 2023](#bib.bib3); [Hager et al., 2024](#bib.bib4); [Tu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib5)).

As shown in
Table [1](#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
early studies predominantly assess and train medical agents within
static single-turn question-answering (QA) formats derived from medical
licensing exams ([Jin et al., 2020](#bib.bib6); [Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib7); [Zuo et al., 2025](#bib.bib8); [Xie et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9)). This paradigm provides medical agents with
pre-defined clinical information and requires them to directly formulate
a diagnosis, offering standardization and efficiency in model training
and evaluation. However, in real-world clinical practice, physicians
must actively gather information through multi-turn patient
consultations, coordinate with laboratory and radiology departments to
request diagnostic tests, and progressively refine their diagnostic
hypotheses based on newly acquired evidence ([Balogh et al.,
2015](#bib.bib45); [Kassirer, 1983](#bib.bib46)). This fundamental
discrepancy highlights that static QA tasks primarily assess medical
agents’ diagnostic reasoning capabilities while overlooking their
information acquisition capabilities, thereby failing to fully capture
the dynamic and interactive nature of clinical diagnosis.

These limitations motivate a shift from static QA toward interactive
simulations of real-world clinical environments that better reflect
real-world multi-role interactions. Within such environments, medical
agents can continuously improve their diagnostic reasoning capability
and information acquisition capability, through iterative interaction,
feedback, and self-evolution. Compared with static QA, this shifts the
training paradigm from taking medical exams to undergoing clinical
residency training.

Despite this promising vision, constructing interactive clinical
environments remains challenging. The first challenge is how to prevent
information leakage while supporting open-ended clinical interactions.
During patient simulation, granting simulators access to complete
clinical records may inadvertently expose ground-truth diagnoses or
unrevealed examination results, undermining the authenticity of clinical
interactions. Moreover, when agents request examinations absent from
electronic health records (EHRs), existing environments ([Fan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib11); [Nori et al., 2025](#bib.bib12); [Liang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13)) often return "unavailable" or "normal", potentially
exposing missingness patterns that agents can exploit as diagnostic
shortcuts. These issues highlight the difficulty of preserving
controlled information access while enabling flexible clinical
interactions.

The second challenge is how to provide reliable trajectory-level
feedback for evaluating and improving medical agents. Unlike static QA
tasks that primarily assess final-answer correctness, interactive
clinical diagnosis involves multi-turn trajectories of information
acquisition, examination decisions, and diagnostic reasoning, requiring
evaluation of both the clinical decision-making process and its
outcomes ([Tu et al., 2025](#bib.bib5); [Mehandru et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15)). However, expert-based evaluation, as adopted in
MIRA ([Ferber et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)), is costly and difficult to
scale, while LLM-as-a-judge approaches ([Fan et al., 2025](#bib.bib11);
[Li et al., 2024a](#bib.bib10)) may introduce model-dependent biases and
sensitivity to prompting strategies ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib16);
[Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)).

To address these challenges, we propose ClinVerse, an interactive,
EHR-grounded multi-agent environment designed for training and
evaluating medical diagnostic agents. To achieve scalability and
open-endedness while preserving clinical grounding, we develop an
EHR-to-environment transformation framework that converts real-world
clinical records into interactive diagnostic environments. Specifically,
an automated data construction pipeline enables the scalable
construction of ClinPatient, a dataset comprising 150k EHR-derived
patient cases, reducing dependence on extensive manual curation. We
further introduce a dual-layer patient simulator with a self-check
mechanism that separates patient-observable information from latent
clinical states, mitigating unintended disclosure of privileged
evidence. Meanwhile, missing examination results in EHRs are addressed
by ClinLab, a trained physiological-state predictor that infers
clinically plausible results.

To provide scalable and reproducible trajectory-level feedback, we
develop a rule-based evaluation framework grounded in structured
clinical evidence and ICD-10 diagnosis labels. Rather than relying on
subjective judgments of entire conversations, this framework evaluates
interaction trajectories using deterministic rules across three
complementary dimensions: information acquisition, examination coverage,
and diagnostic accuracy. These metrics characterize both the evidence
acquisition process and the resulting diagnostic outcomes, providing
consistent feedback for agent evaluation and subsequent self-evolution.

Built upon these components, ClinVerse coordinates six specialized roles
to simulate the core workflow of clinical diagnosis, where a doctor
agent, coordinated by the environment controller, progressively acquires
patient information through multi-turn consultations with the observable
patient simulator, requests examination results from the laboratory
physician supported by the latent patient simulator, and ultimately
formulates a diagnosis. The resulting interaction trajectory is then
evaluated by a judge.

Consequently, ClinVerse can serve as both an evaluation platform and a
training environment for medical agents. We evaluate nine
general-purpose and medical-domain agents, identifying their strengths
and limitations in interactive diagnosis. We further investigate
multiple self-evolution methods across three levels, demonstrating that
agents can improve their capabilities by learning from interaction
trajectories generated within ClinVerse. Beyond performance
improvements, our analyses reveal that evidence acquisition remains a
major bottleneck, while collecting more information does not necessarily
lead to better diagnoses, highlighting the importance of evidence
prioritization and integration. We also examine how trajectory
effectiveness depends on the capabilities of source and target models,
and whether learned skills transfer to other clinical environments.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  We introduce ClinVerse, a realistic diagnostic environment that
  supports open-ended multi-turn interactions among six specialized
  roles and enables both the self-evolving and evaluation of medical
  agents.
- •
  We develop a scalable pipeline for transforming EHR data into
  diagnostic cases and use it to construct ClinPatient, a dataset
  containing 150k patient cases. We design a dual-layer patient
  simulator with a self-check mechanism and train a physiological-state
  predictor to infer missing examination results.
- •
  We demonstrate the effectiveness of ClinVerse as a training
  environment and validate its reliability as an evaluation platform,
  and further analyze the interplay among the environment, model, and
  self-evolution methods..

## 2. Setup of ClinVerse

As illustrated in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
ClinVerse is a realistic clinical diagnostic environment grounded in
real-world EHR data. It comprises six specialized roles that interact
freely throughout the consultation, enabling the doctor agent to
progressively gather evidence and produce a final diagnosis. The
resulting interaction trajectories are evaluated using three categories
of rule-based criteria and can subsequently be utilized for both model
assessment and training.

### 2.1. Task Definition

For each patient, we construct an EHR-derived clinical case as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (1) |     |

             ``` math
             \mathcal{C}=\left(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{E},y\right),
             ```                                                  |     |

where $`\mathcal{U}`$ and $`\mathcal{E}`$ denote the patient’s clinical
information units and examination items, respectively, and
$`y\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{ICD}}`$ is the ground-truth diagnosis
represented by an ICD-10 code ²² 2 https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ICD-10.

The clinical information unit set is defined as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (2) |     |

             ``` math
             \mathcal{U}=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{M}\},
             ```                                        |     |

where each $`u_{m}`$ represents patient-specific clinical information,
such as a symptom, medical history, medication, or physical sign. At the
beginning of a consultation, the doctor is provided with an initial
subset $`\mathcal{U}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{U}`$, which contains the
patient’s chief complaint and other initially disclosed information,
while the remaining information units are hidden and can be obtained
through interaction with patients.

The examination item set is defined as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (3) |     |

             ``` math
             \mathcal{E}=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{N}\},
             ```                                        |     |

where each $`e_{n}`$ denotes an examination item, such as a laboratory
test, vital sign measurement, imaging examination, or other auxiliary
examination. The doctor can retrieve an item $`e_{n}`$ by requesting the
corresponding examination.

At turn $`t`$, the doctor receives the currently collected evidence

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (4) |     |

             ``` math
             x_{t}=\left(\mathcal{U}_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t}\right),
             ```                                                  |     |

where $`\mathcal{U}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{U}`$
and $`\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{E}`$ are the revealed patient
information units and retrieved examination items, respectively. The
doctor can query the patient for additional information, request
examinations, or terminate the interaction by issuing a diagnosis.

The task output is a predicted ICD-10 code:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (5) |     |

             ``` math
             \hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{ICD}}.
             ```                                    |     |

where the ICD-10 code inherently specifies both the disease category and
its fine-grained subtype.

Overall, the task requires the doctor agent to interact iteratively with
the patient to collect information units and request relevant
examinations to learn about the patient’s physiological state. Once the
agent determines that sufficient information has been collected, it
provides a final diagnosis of the patient’s underlying disease.

### 2.2. Six Roles in ClinVerse

To simulate realistic clinical interactions while maintaining controlled
information access, ClinVerse comprises six specialized roles: an
observable patient simulator, a latent patient simulator, a laboratory
physician, a doctor agent, an environment controller, and a diagnostic
judge. As illustrated in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
the doctor agent interacts with the patient simulators and laboratory
physician through the environment controller, progressively acquiring
clinical evidence before submitting a final diagnosis. The diagnostic
judge evaluates the resulting interaction trajectory, providing feedback
for agent evaluation and subsequent self-evolution.

Observable Patient Simulator. Existing patient simulators ([Kyung et
al., 2025](#bib.bib22); [Nori et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)) often grant a
single model access to the complete patient record, increasing the risk
of information leakage under leading or adversarial queries. To address
this issue, we adopt a dual-layer patient simulation architecture that
separates patient-observable information from latent clinical states.
The observable patient simulator is restricted to patient-reportable
information, including demographics, chief complaints, subjective
symptoms, medical history, and personality traits, while excluding
ground-truth diagnoses, clinician-only records, and unrevealed
examination results. A self-check mechanism further verifies each
generated response against the patient’s accessible information and
underlying case evidence, revising or rejecting privileged, unsupported,
or premature disclosures. Validly revealed information units are
recorded for subsequent trajectory-level evaluation.

Latent Patient Simulator. Complementing the observable patient
simulator, the latent patient simulator maintains the complete
EHR-derived case state, including the ground-truth diagnosis,
longitudinal medical history, physiological measurements, and
examination results. It does not interact directly with the doctor agent
but provides case-specific clinical information to authorized roles,
particularly the laboratory physician. By separating patient-reportable
evidence from privileged clinical records, the dual-layer architecture
preserves the partial observability of clinical diagnosis and mitigates
information leakage. The effectiveness of this design, together with the
self-check mechanism, is evaluated in
Section [6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1. Robustness of Patient Info Boundaries ‣ 6. Ablation Study ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents").

Laboratory Physician. The laboratory physician handles physical
examination requests, laboratory tests, and imaging examinations issued
by the doctor agent. As illustrated in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
each request follows a two-stage process: if the corresponding result is
recorded in the original EHR, the laboratory physician retrieves it from
the latent patient simulator; otherwise, it invokes ClinLab, a trained
physiological-state predictor, to infer a clinically plausible result
from the case context and available observations. This design avoids
directly returning missingness responses for unrecorded examinations, as
in prior environments ([Fan et al., 2025](#bib.bib11); [Ferber et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)), thereby supporting flexible examination requests
while reducing the risk of exploiting systematic missingness as a
diagnostic shortcut. Both recorded and predicted results are
standardized into structured reports, including measurements, units,
reference ranges, and abnormality indicators when applicable. The
training and evaluation of ClinLab are detailed in
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Training Detail of Physiological-state Predictor ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents")
and
Section [6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2. Fidelity of Physiological-State Prediction ‣ 6. Ablation Study ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
respectively.

Doctor Agent. The doctor agent is the primary role evaluated and trained
within ClinVerse. Starting from a limited initial patient presentation,
it progressively acquires clinical evidence and formulates diagnostic
hypotheses through multi-turn interactions. Its action space comprises
three types: patient inquiry, examination requests, and final diagnosis
submission. Examination requests cover physical examinations, laboratory
tests, microbiological tests, and imaging studies. At each turn, the
doctor selects an action based on the accumulated interaction history,
reflecting the sequential process of clinical evidence acquisition and
diagnostic reasoning. The consultation terminates when the doctor
submits a final diagnosis or reaches the interaction limit.

Environment Controller. The environment controller serves as the central
coordinator of clinical interactions, connecting the doctor agent with
the supporting roles while enforcing information access boundaries. It
validates the doctor’s actions, routes patient inquiries to the
observable patient simulator and examination requests to the laboratory
physician, and returns the corresponding observations to the doctor.
Throughout the consultation, it maintains the interaction history and
records information disclosure and examination events as hidden metadata
for subsequent evaluation. This centralized coordination ensures that
the doctor obtains clinical evidence only through authorized
interactions, preserving the partial observability and consistency of
the diagnostic process.

Diagnostic Judge. The diagnostic judge evaluates the complete
interaction trajectory without participating in the consultation. Using
the ground-truth case information and interaction metadata maintained by
the environment controller, it applies deterministic scoring rules
across three dimensions: patient information collection, examination
coverage, and diagnostic accuracy at different levels of disease
granularity. These scores provide reproducible feedback on both evidence
acquisition and diagnostic outcomes, supporting systematic agent
evaluation and subsequent self-evolution. The scoring criteria are
detailed in
Section [2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4. Rule-based Scoring ‣ 2. Setup of ClinVerse ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents").

### 2.3. Interaction Flow

As illustrated in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
each diagnostic episode begins with the environment controller
initializing an EHR-derived case from ClinPatient and providing the
doctor agent with a limited initial presentation. At each turn, the
doctor selects a clinical action based on the accumulated interaction
history, which is validated and routed by the environment controller.
Patient inquiries are directed to the observable patient simulator,
which generates self-checked responses based on patient-accessible
information. Physical examination and test requests are handled by the
laboratory physician, which retrieves recorded findings from the latent
patient simulator or invokes ClinLab to predict unavailable results. The
returned observations are incorporated into the interaction history,
allowing the doctor to progressively update its diagnostic reasoning and
determine subsequent actions.

### 2.4. Rule-based Scoring

Given a complete interaction trajectory of $`T`$ turns, we evaluate
agent behavior along three dimensions: patient information collection,
examination coverage, and diagnostic agreement with the hidden case
label. We design deterministic scoring rules to enable reproducible and
scalable evaluation while avoiding the inconsistency of LLM-based judges
and the cost of expert annotation. These metrics assess how effectively
agents acquire and utilize case-specific evidence, rather than judging
the clinical optimality of every possible decision path.

Information Collection Rate. Let $`\mathcal{U}`$, whose extraction
details are provided in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3. Information Unit Extraction ‣ 3. Construction of ClinPatient ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
denote the normalized set of patient-reportable information units
extracted during case construction, and let
$`\mathcal{U}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{U}`$ denote the subset already
disclosed in the initial presentation. The remaining units,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (6) |     |

             ``` math
             \mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{ask}}=\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{0},
             ```                                                              |     |

represent the information that can be elicited through subsequent
interaction with the observable patient simulator.

Each unit is assigned an importance weight $`w(u)\in\{3,2,1\}`$,
corresponding to key, supporting, and background evidence, respectively.
Let
$`\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{rev}}_{T}\subseteq\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{ask}}`$
denote the set of distinct units revealed to the doctor over the current
trajectory. We define the information collection rate as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (7) |     |

````math
R_{\mathrm{info}}=\frac{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{ask}}}w(u)\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[u\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{rev}}_{T}\right]}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{ask}}}w(u)}.
``` |  |

This metric measures the proportion of undisclosed patient-reportable
evidence successfully elicited by the doctor, while assigning greater
credit to diagnostically salient information. The weighting scheme
emphasizes key symptoms, relevant medical history, medications, and
allergies, and prevents the score from being dominated by numerous
low-value details. For cases in which $`\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{ask}}`$ is
empty, we set $`R_{\mathrm{info}}=1`$ by convention.

Test Coverage Rate. Let
$`\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{test}}\subseteq\mathcal{E}`$ denote the set of
initially hidden and retrievable laboratory, microbiology, and radiology
findings associated with the case. Let
$`\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{ret}}_{T}`$ denote the distinct examination items
returned by the laboratory physician in response to the doctor’s
requests. We measure overall and abnormal test coverage as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (8) |  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{test}}=\frac{\left|\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{ret}}_{T}\cap\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{test}}\right|}{\left|\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{test}}\right|},\qquad R_{\mathrm{abnormal}}=\frac{\left|\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{ret}}_{T}\cap\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{abnormal}}\right|}{\left|\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{abnormal}}\right|}.
``` |  |

The metrics are computed over distinct case-supported findings rather
than the raw number of orders. Therefore, repeated requests do not
increase the score, and unsupported requests do not artificially inflate
coverage. Here, $`\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{abnormal}}`$ denotes the
initially hidden laboratory and microbiology findings marked as
abnormal. We set the corresponding score to $`1`$ when its denominator
is empty. Thus, $`R_{\mathrm{abnormal}}`$ measures whether the doctor
surfaces clinically salient abnormal test results.

Diagnosis Accuracy. To provide partial credit for clinically related
predictions while maintaining a standardized and language-independent
evaluation, we adopt the ICD-10 coding system and compare the predicted
code $`\hat{y}`$ with the ground-truth code $`y`$ at two levels of
granularity. Let $`b(\cdot)`$ denote the corresponding ICD-10 block and
$`c_{3}(\cdot)`$ denote the three-character category. We define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (9) |  |
``` math
A_{\mathrm{cat}}=\mathbf{1}\!\left[b(\hat{y})=b(y)\right],\qquad A_{\mathrm{sub}}=\mathbf{1}\!\left[c_{3}(\hat{y})=c_{3}(y)\right].
``` |  |

Here, $`A_{\mathrm{cat}}`$ measures whether the predicted diagnosis
falls within the correct ICD-10 block, while $`A_{\mathrm{sub}}`$
evaluates the finer-grained match at the three-character category level.
All three types of metrics can be computed efficiently, making them
suitable both for scalable model evaluation and as reward signals for
model training.

## 3. Construction of ClinPatient

To support scalable patient simulation in ClinVerse, we model each
patient from three complementary dimensions and construct a patient pool
ClinPatient through a unified data-processing pipeline that converts raw
EHR records into diagnosis-oriented cases. Designed to preserve the
fidelity of real-world clinical data while remaining extensible, this
pipeline can be applied to additional EHR sources, allowing new patient
cases to be continuously incorporated into the environment.

### 3.1. From EHRs to a Diagnostic Dataset

### 3.2. Data Augmentation

To ensure the realism of the environment, we model patients along three
complementary dimensions, including patient profile, clinical status,
and social attributes. Because real-world admission records often lack
social attributes of patients, we augment each case with a conditionally
generated Synthea³³ 3 https://github.com/synthetichealth/synthea profile
containing patient-reportable information, including prior conditions,
long-term medications, allergies, immunization history, and social
history. Generation is conditioned on age, sex, and secondary ICD
diagnoses, while excluding the primary diagnosis to prevent label
leakage. Inconsistent profiles and events occurring after admission or
revealing the diagnosis are removed. Each case is also assigned a Big
Five personality profile along the OCEAN dimensions to control
conversational behaviors such as information disclosure, response
conciseness, and anxiety expression ([McCrae and John,
1992](#bib.bib27)). Personality affects only dialogue generation, while
all clinical observations and diagnostic targets remain grounded in the
original EHR, enabling richer patient simulation without introducing
diagnostic shortcuts.

### 3.3. Information Unit Extraction

To support controlled information disclosure and trajectory-level
evaluation, we transform long content into a structured set of atomic
information units, following prior work ([Qiao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib28)). Specifically, we extract facts from demographics,
the history of present illness, and other patient-related contents, and
normalize it into a set $`\mathcal{U}`$. Each unit is assigned a
semantic category, an importance label, and a stable identifier. The
subset revealed in the initial presentation is denoted by
$`\mathcal{U}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{U}`$, while the remaining units must
be acquired through interaction with the observable patient simulator.
Examination-derived evidence, including physical examination,
laboratory, microbiology, and radiology findings, is stored separately
in $`\mathcal{E}`$ and accessed through the laboratory physician. This
separation preserves role-specific information boundaries and provides
structured supervision for the scoring functions in
Section [2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4. Rule-based Scoring ‣ 2. Setup of ClinVerse ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents").

## 4. Experiments

We envision ClinVerse as an interactive clinical consultation
environment that supports both the evaluation and training of medical
agents. In this section, we empirically assess its effectiveness for
training and evaluation through a series of experiments.

### 4.1. Experimental Setup

Metrics. We evaluate doctor agents using five rule-based metrics
introduced in
Section [2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4. Rule-based Scoring ‣ 2. Setup of ClinVerse ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents").
All metrics are computed on 100 cases randomly sampled from a held-out
test set with 5 independent runs.

Models. We evaluate doctor agents from diverse model families and
parameter scales, including general-purpose and medical-domain models
such as Lingshu-32B ([Xu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib32)),
Baichuan-M2-32B ([Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)), and the HealthGPT-Pro
series ([Lin et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)). We also include a
full-information setting in which complete patient information and
examination results are provided directly for agents, whose results are
presented in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Performances of Different Models Under Full Information Setting ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents").
This setting removes information-access constraints and serves as a
reference for interpreting agents’ evidence-acquisition and diagnostic
capabilities.

Training Methods. We categorize self-evolution methods into three
levels. L1 is offline adaptation, including MemRL-offline ([Zhang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib31)), SkillRL ([Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)),
Trace2Skill([Ni et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)), and MedAgent-Zero ([Li et
al., 2024a](#bib.bib10)). L2 represents online self-evolution, including
MemRL-online ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)), Dynamic
Cheatsheet ([Suzgun et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)), and ACE ([Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib43)). L3 focuses on recursive self-improvement, including
Self-Taught Optimizer ([Zelikman et al., 2023](#bib.bib42)), Meta
Context Engineering ([Ye et al., 2026](#bib.bib44)), and MedRSI ([Wu et
al., 2026a](#bib.bib47)). Detailed descriptions are provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Training Methods Used in Experiments ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents").

### 4.2. ClinVerse as an Evaluation Platform

As presented in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.1. From EHRs to a Diagnostic Dataset ‣ 3. Construction of ClinPatient ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
we evaluate agents’ performance across the full consultation process,
covering patient information elicitation, examination ordering, and
evidence integration for final diagnosis. GPT-5.6-Luna achieves the
strongest overall performance among the evaluated agents, yet a clear
gap remains relative to the full-information reference, indicating that
effective evidence acquisition is still a major bottleneck in
interactive diagnosis. The five metrics further reveal distinct
behavioral profiles that cannot be captured by final diagnostic accuracy
alone. For example, Baichuan-M2-32B performs well in identifying
relevant and abnormal examinations but does not consistently convert
this advantage into more accurate diagnoses, suggesting limitations in
evidence integration or clinical reasoning. In contrast, Lingshu-32B
achieves comparatively strong diagnostic performance despite rarely
requesting examinations, implying a greater reliance on prior knowledge
or initially available information. By separately evaluating information
seeking, examination decisions, and diagnostic inference under
controlled information boundaries, ClinVerse exposes where each doctor
agent succeeds or fails throughout the consultation process.

### 4.3. ClinVerse as an Evolution Environment

Beyond evaluating static agents, we further investigate how three
categories of adaptation and evolution strategies improve Qwen3-32B
within ClinVerse. As shown in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.1. From EHRs to a Diagnostic Dataset ‣ 3. Construction of ClinPatient ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
these methods produce distinct capability profiles. MemRL-online mainly
improves Information Collection Rate and Diagnosis Subtype Accuracy,
although its information-collection performance exhibits substantial
variance, whereas MemRL-offline yields more balanced gains in
examination coverage and diagnosis. Dynamic Cheatsheet achieves the
strongest overall adaptation results, substantially improving Test Order
Coverage, Abnormal Test Coverage, and both diagnostic metrics despite
collecting less patient-reported information. This pattern suggests that
adaptation can improve diagnosis by shifting evidence acquisition toward
more informative examinations rather than uniformly increasing the
amount of collected evidence.

All three categories improve diagnostic performance, but their
efficiency differs. Online Self-Evolution achieves larger diagnostic
gains using 1,000 optimization trajectories than Offline Adaptation
using 5,000, indicating substantially greater experience efficiency.
Recursive Self-Improvement provides more limited gains under the same
setting, suggesting that optimizing the update mechanism introduces
additional search and credit-assignment demands that are not necessarily
compensated for by the available feedback.

## 5. Further Analysis

### 5.1. Info Acquisition & Diagnostic Convergence

Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents")(a–b)
shows that information acquisition is concentrated in the early
consultation turns and then exhibits clear diminishing returns, whereas
diagnostic agreement generally continues to increase as the interaction
proceeds. Stronger models achieve both higher information coverage and
more consistent diagnoses, suggesting that effective evidence
acquisition facilitates diagnostic convergence. Notably, agreement can
continue improving after information collection has largely plateaued,
indicating that later turns may help models confirm and integrate
existing evidence rather than acquire new facts. The occasional
fluctuations in agreement further reflect evidence-driven revision of
intermediate hypotheses during the consultation.

### 5.2. Temporal Analysis of Clinical Actions

To analyze interaction dynamics, we visualize the turn-level action
distributions of three doctor agents. As shown in
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents")(c),
all models exhibit a common coarse-grained workflow: early turns focus
on patient inquiry, middle turns increasingly involve physical
examinations and laboratory tests, and final diagnoses dominate toward
the end. However, the transitions vary across models. GPT-5.6-Luna and
Qwen3-32B shift relatively smoothly between action types, whereas
Baichuan-M2-32B shows more abrupt changes, suggesting less stable
long-horizon planning. These results show that ClinVerse captures not
only final performance but also the temporal organization of clinical
decision-making.

### 5.3. Cross-Model Learning

Building on
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.3. Cross-Model Learning ‣ 5. Further Analysis ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
we further examine how trajectory source and quality affect different
target models. Trajectories generated by Qwen3-32B improve Qwen3-series
models but provide little benefit to stronger GPT-series models and can
even degrade performance, especially for GPT-4o-mini. Reducing the
number of trajectories also weakens the gains, indicating that quantity
still matters when the teacher remains fixed. However, trajectory
quality is more influential: a much smaller set generated by
GPT-5.6-Luna yields substantially larger improvements on Qwen3 models
than a larger set from Qwen3-32B. These results suggest that
trajectory-based training depends strongly on the capability gap between
teacher and target models: stronger teachers provide effective
supervision for weaker targets, whereas trajectories from weaker or
comparable models offer limited value and may introduce harmful
guidance.

### 5.4. Cross-Environment Generalization

To evaluate whether ClinVerse-derived experiences transfer beyond the
environment, we test the adapted Qwen3-32B agents on AIHospital ([Fan et
al., 2025](#bib.bib11)) without further adaptation. As shown in
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.3. Cross-Model Learning ‣ 5. Further Analysis ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
all evolution methods consistently improve most metrics, demonstrating
that acquired skills from ClinVerse provide transferable supervision
across environments. Moreover, we find that most offline adaptation
methods maintain relatively stable MVME scores, an LLM-as-a-judge metric
for consultation quality, while improving diagnostic accuracy. In
contrast, Online Self-Evolution and RSI substantially reduce MVME while
achieving larger diagnostic gains. This is consistent with results in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.1. From EHRs to a Diagnostic Dataset ‣ 3. Construction of ClinPatient ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
where diagnostic accuracy improves despite limited gains in information
acquisition.

One possible explanation is that offline methods rely more on
accumulated experience and therefore tend to collect additional
information for case matching, whereas Online Self-Evolution and RSI
continuously refine their strategies toward more efficient decision
paths. These results suggest that different evolution paradigms improve
agents along distinct capability dimensions.

### 5.5. Cross-Methods Improvement

We further investigate how self-evolution methods affect models of
different scales. As shown in
Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.4. Cross-Environment Generalization ‣ 5. Further Analysis ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents"),
all methods yield positive gains, with performance progressively
approaching the full-information reference. Improvements in information
collection capability, however, are not always accompanied by gains in
diagnostic accuracy. For Qwen3-32B, stronger information collection
generally correlates with higher diagnostic accuracy, whereas
GPT-5.6-luna can achieve better diagnostic performance with less
collected information. This indicates that a substantial portion of
patient information may be redundant or weakly relevant to diagnosis,
and that self-evolution can help models focus on more informative
evidence while reducing unnecessary information acquisition. We also
observe a scale-dependent pattern for the L3 RSI method. It provides
larger gains for Mid-tier models, while weaker and stronger models
benefit less, consistent with the trend reported by [Lin et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib40).

## 7. Conclusion

We propose ClinVerse, a scalable real-data-grounded multi-agent
environment for medical diagnostic agents. Built on ClinPatient, it
supports realistic interactions, flexible examinations, and rule-based
evaluation of evidence acquisition and diagnosis. Experiments validate
the effectiveness of ClinVerse as an evolution environment and its
reliability as an evaluation platform.

## Limitations and Ethical Considerations

This study uses only de-identified clinical data from SICdb ([Rodemund
et al., 2023](#bib.bib25)), MIMIC-IV v3.1 ([Johnson et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24)), and MIMIC-IV-Note ([Johnson et al.,
2023](#bib.bib26)), accessed through PhysioNet under the required
credentialing and Data Use Agreement procedures. We used these data
solely for research and made no attempt to identify patients or
institutions. Raw EHRs were transformed into structured admission-level
cases, and doctor agents accessed only de-identified, role-specific
information. Any released artifacts will comply with original licenses
and will not include raw records or re-identifiable information.

## Generative AI Usage

Generative AI tools were used only in an assistive capacity during
manuscript preparation and implementation. Specifically, LLM were used
for language polishing, grammar and typographical-error checking, and
assistance with drafting, debugging, and documenting research code. All
AI-assisted text and code were reviewed, revised, and verified by the
authors.

## Appendix A Related Work

### A.1. Diagnostic Decision-Making Evaluation

Existing evaluations of medical agents can be broadly divided into
static and interactive paradigms. Static benchmarks typically formulate
diagnosis as a question-answering task using cases derived from medical
licensing examinations ([Jin et al., 2020](#bib.bib6); [Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib7)), textbooks ([Fouda et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)), or
EHRs ([Xie et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)), with answer accuracy serving as
the primary metric. These benchmarks are effective for measuring medical
knowledge and diagnostic reasoning under fixed evidence, but they
provide limited insight into how agents acquire information or make
sequential clinical decisions. More recent benchmarks ([Xu et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib19); [Li et al., 2024a](#bib.bib10); [Lim et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37)) therefore introduce multi-turn consultation settings
in which agents interact with simulated patients and other clinical
roles, enabling evaluation of both diagnostic outcomes and the decision
processes that produce them.

Evaluating such interaction trajectories, however, remains challenging.
MIRA ([Ferber et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)) relies on case-specific rules
and expert review to assess clinically meaningful behaviors, achieving
high evaluation fidelity at substantial annotation cost. Other
systems ([Fan et al., 2025](#bib.bib11); [Xu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib19))
improve scalability by using LLM-based judges to score interactions
across multiple dimensions, but their results may vary with the
evaluator model, prompt design, and scoring criteria. In contrast,
ClinVerse adopts a rule-based evaluation framework that derives
reproducible scores directly from hidden case states and recorded
interaction events, covering information collection, examination
ordering, and diagnostic accuracy without requiring per-case expert
annotation or model-based judging.

### A.2. Interactive Clinical Environments

Recent studies ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib20); [Almansoori et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21); [Wu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib23); [Mou et al.,
2026](#bib.bib35); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib36); [Mu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib38); [Li et al., 2024b](#bib.bib39)) have explored
interactive clinical environments for evaluating medical agents beyond
static question answering. Agent Hospital ([Li et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib10)) constructs a flexible hospital sandbox by
synthesizing patients and clinical roles from external knowledge
sources, but its fully synthetic design may compromise clinical
fidelity. In contrast, AIHospital ([Fan et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)) and
SDBench ([Nori et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)) ground patient simulation in
cases collected from online resources or medical literature, providing
more realistic clinical scenarios while remaining limited by the scale,
completeness, and examination coverage of the source cases.
AgentClinic ([Schmidgall et al., 2026](#bib.bib48)) introduces a
multimodal clinical environment with four interacting roles, enabling
patient consultations, examination requests, and diagnostic
decision-making across diverse clinical scenarios. However, its
text-parsed interaction protocol and reliance on predefined case
findings, with potentially normal responses for unavailable
examinations, limit structured clinical interactions and examination
flexibility. MIRA ([Ferber et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)) further improves
evaluation fidelity through expert-curated cases and fine-grained,
case-specific assessment criteria, but the associated annotation burden
limits scalability.

Overall, existing environments face a trade-off among interaction
flexibility, clinical grounding, and scalable evaluation. ClinVerse
differs from these systems by constructing interactive cases from
real-world EHRs at scale, providing structured tool-call-based clinical
actions, and supporting examination requests beyond the observations
explicitly recorded in each source case through ClinLab-based
prediction. These designs enable broader and more consistent diagnostic
interactions while facilitating subsequent agent self-improvement.

## Appendix B Training Methods Used in Experiments

We adapt doctor agents within ClinVerse using nine methods. Most of the
methods keep the backbone LLM frozen and improve agent behavior through
external skills or memories derived from interaction trajectories.

Trace2Skill ([Ni et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)) collects successful and
failed clinical trajectories from a frozen doctor agent and analyzes
them separately to extract reusable behaviors and causally grounded
corrections. These trajectory-level lessons are converted into local
skill patches, then hierarchically deduplicated and consolidated into a
coherent clinical skill directory. The resulting skill is directly
supplied at inference time without model updates or per-case memory
retrieval.

SkillRL ([Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)) distills successful
trajectories into effective decision patterns and failed trajectories
into counterfactual lessons describing errors and preferable actions. It
organizes this knowledge into a hierarchical SkillBank containing
general clinical principles and task-specific guidance for stages such
as history collection, investigation, and diagnosis. Our parameter-free
adaptation retrieves relevant skills as contextual guidance while
omitting the SFT and reinforcement-learning updates of the original
framework. We additionally apply the full SkillRL training pipeline to
Qwen3-8B, with details and results reported in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Effectiveness of ClinVerse for GRPO Training ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents").

MemRL ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)) represents each clinical
episode as an intent – experience – utility triplet in an external
memory bank initialized from offline rollouts. At inference time, it
first recalls semantically similar experiences and then re-ranks them
using learned utility, reducing the influence of relevant-looking but
unhelpful memories. Environmental feedback subsequently updates the
utilities of retrieved experiences and may add new memories, enabling
online adaptation without changing model weights.

MedAgent-Zero ([Li et al., 2024a](#bib.bib10)) maintains separate
Medical Record Libraries and Experience Bases for the examination,
diagnosis, and treatment stages. Successful cases are stored as
validated examples, whereas failed cases are reflected upon and
summarized into transferable corrective principles. For each new
clinical decision, similar records and failure-derived guidance are
jointly retrieved to support reasoning, allowing both repositories—and
thus the doctor agent—to improve continually without parameter updates.

Dynamic Cheatsheet ([Suzgun et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)) gives a black-box
LLM a persistent, self-curated memory that accumulates concise
strategies, code snippets, and corrections across test-time
interactions. After each case, a curator examines the current memory,
query, and attempted solution to add useful generalizations and revise
or prune unreliable entries; the revised cheatsheet then conditions
subsequent cases. This enables online adaptation without ground-truth
labels, human feedback, or changes to model parameters, while limiting
the context growth caused by appending the full interaction history.

ACE ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib43)) treats the agent context as an
evolving playbook and separates adaptation into generator, reflector,
and curator roles. The generator acts using the current playbook, the
reflector extracts generalizable lessons from the resulting trajectory
and natural execution feedback, and the curator integrates compact delta
updates before semantically deduplicating overlapping entries. We use
its online setting so that later consultations receive the refined
playbook; these localized updates preserve useful clinical detail and
mitigate the brevity bias and context collapse associated with
repeatedly rewriting the entire context.

Self-Taught Optimizer ([Zelikman et al., 2023](#bib.bib42)) begins with
an LLM-powered improver program that proposes multiple revisions to an
input program, evaluates them with a task-specific utility function, and
returns the best candidate. The seed improver is then applied to its own
source code, allowing utility feedback to produce a stronger update
procedure whose emergent search strategies can include beam search,
genetic algorithms, and simulated annealing. The improved improver is
subsequently used to optimize agent solutions, yielding a form of
recursive self-improvement in which the scaffolding evolves while the
backbone LLM remains frozen.

Meta Context Engineering ([Ye et al., 2026](#bib.bib44)) formulates
adaptation as bi-level optimization that co-evolves context-engineering
skills and the context artifacts produced by those skills. At the meta
level, agentic crossover synthesizes a revised skill from the task
specification and the history of prior skills, executions, and
evaluations; at the base level, an agent executes that skill to
construct context as flexible files and code, including both static
resources and query-dependent operations such as retrieval and
filtering. Iterative evaluation selects improved skill–context pairs,
allowing both what the doctor agent learns and how it organizes and
updates that knowledge to evolve without modifying the backbone model.

MedRSI ([Wu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib47)) enables recursive
self-improvement by transforming diagnostic failures into new clinical
capabilities while keeping the backbone LLM frozen. It prioritizes
failures according to their potential clinical consequences and
identifies missing capabilities through trajectory analysis and
reflection. These capability gaps are addressed through automated code
generation, tool composition, or task-specific model training, producing
new clinical tools for measurement, prediction, and evidence
integration. To ensure reliable adaptation, MedRSI adopts a
fast-discovery, slow-registration mechanism, where candidate tools are
evaluated across subsequent patient cohorts and incorporated into a
persistent tool registry only after demonstrating sustained diagnostic
improvements without increasing clinical risk. This iterative process
allows the doctor agent to autonomously expand its capabilities without
updating the backbone LLM.

## Appendix D Impact of Different Training Methods on Model Behavior

### D.1. Impact on Information Collection

Figure [5](#A2.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Appendix B Training Methods Used in Experiments ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents")

patient-reportable information during the early stages of consultation,

MedAgent-Zero even reduces information coverage. These results indicate
that trajectory-based adaptation influences not only the amount of
information collected but also the timing of evidence acquisition. In
particular, utility-aware memory retrieval encourages more efficient
early-stage questioning, whereas improvements in diagnostic performance
do not necessarily imply more comprehensive information gathering.

### D.2. Impact on Action Taken

The turn-level action distributions in

clinical workflow, gradually transitioning from patient inquiry to

that trajectory-based adaptation not only improves final performance but
also reshapes the temporal organization of clinical decision-making,
with different methods achieving distinct trade-offs between exploration
and efficiency.

## Appendix I Training Detail of Physiological-state Predictor

Training. We construct the training set from laboratory measurements in
MIMIC-IV-Ext and split samples by hospital admission to prevent
admission-level context leakage between training and evaluation. For
each eligible measurement, we apply a leave-one-out masking strategy,
where the target laboratory result is removed while all remaining
clinical information, including demographics, history, physical
examination, diagnoses, medications, microbiology results, and hospital
course, is preserved. The model receives the masked test name, category,
unit, and reference range, and is trained to predict only the missing
result value. This formulation simulates real scenarios where requested
examinations are absent from the source EHR while preventing direct
target copying. To emphasize clinically meaningful cases, all abnormal
measurements are retained and normal measurements are subsampled. We
fine-tune a Qwen3.5-2B backbone with LoRA under the causal language
modeling objective. During training, system, user, and padding tokens
are assigned the ignore index $`-100`$, ensuring that only the generated
laboratory result contributes to the loss. For samples exceeding the
context limit, inputs are truncated while preserving the target
response.

Evaluation Metrics. Given the ground-truth value $`y_{i}`$ and
prediction $`\hat{y}_{i}`$, we evaluate numerical accuracy using four
metrics. MAPE computes the mean absolute percentage error,
$`\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}|\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}|/|y_{i}|`$, while Median MAPE
reports the median sample-level percentage error to reduce sensitivity
to outliers. MAE measures the absolute deviation between predictions and
ground truth, and NormRefErr normalizes the absolute error by the width
of the corresponding reference interval,
$`|\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}|/(u_{i}-l_{i})`$, enabling comparison across
heterogeneous laboratory tests. We further measure Anomaly Recall, which
evaluates whether predicted values correctly identify ground-truth
abnormal results based on test-specific reference ranges. Lower values
indicate better performance for MAPE, Median MAPE, MAE, and NormRefErr,
while higher values indicate better performance for Anomaly Recall.
Following the evaluation protocol, zero-valued targets are excluded from
numerical error metrics, unstable tests with per-test MAPE above 200%
are removed, and individual percentage errors are clipped at 200% when
computing aggregate MAPE.

## Appendix J Information-Boundary Stress Test and Interaction Impact

Stress-test protocol. The ablation in
Table [5](#S6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 6.1. Robustness of Patient Info Boundaries ‣ 6. Ablation Study ‣ ClinVerse: A Realistic Multi-Agent Diagnostic Environment for Training and Evaluating Medical Agents")
evaluates information-boundary robustness under adversarial queries
rather than routine consultation. For each of 100 cases, we issue six
boundary-seeking queries covering diagnosis requests, examination-result
requests, and attempts to obtain complete medical records. We compare
different combinations of information architecture (single simulator vs.
dual layer) and response policy (neutral vs. self-check) under identical
cases and prompts, resulting in 600 responses per setting.

Metric definitions. Arbitrary Disclosure Rate measures the proportion of
responses containing any suspected privileged disclosure, including
explicit diagnosis leakage or unsupported objective findings.
Test-related Disclosure Rate focuses on leakage of examination results
among test-related queries. Refusal Rate measures responses containing
explicit refusal or unavailability statements. These metrics are
computed independently, and disclosure labels are obtained through
deterministic lexical screening rather than manual clinical judgment.

Impact on ordinary interaction. The high refusal rate observed under the
adversarial protocol reflects the intentionally boundary-violating
nature of the queries and does not represent routine consultation
behavior. To examine practical impact, we further analyze 98 completed
Qwen3-32B trajectories from the main evaluation, containing 393
patient-query turns. Only a small fraction of responses match the
refusal lexicon, and most correspond to normal uncertainty about
symptoms, history, or medications rather than information-boundary
violations. Only two cases involve explicit requests for unavailable
diagnostic or examination results, resulting in a negligible
boundary-triggered refusal frequency. These cases appropriately redirect
the doctor to obtain objective evidence through the laboratory
physician.

These results indicate that the proposed boundary mechanism can resist
explicit information leakage attempts while minimally affecting normal
patient interaction.

Case Overview. We qualitatively analyze two Qwen3-32B trajectories from
the same MIMIC-IV-Ext evaluation split used in the main experiments. To
provide representative examples, we select cases with above-median
information collection and test coverage among the completed
trajectories. We include one successful case with a coherent
evidence-acquisition process and one failed case with substantial
evidence collection but an incorrect final diagnosis, allowing us to
examine different sources of diagnostic errors.

Patient responses are translated into English, while lengthy laboratory
and radiology reports are abbreviated with diagnostically relevant

provides patient-reportable information, whereas the latent patient

Analysis. The successful case illustrates an evidence-driven but
non-linear diagnostic process. After initial patient inquiry, the agent
progressively requested neurological examination, laboratory tests, and
imaging. When CT failed to provide definitive evidence, the agent
conducted further targeted inquiry and ordered MRI, whose findings
supported the final diagnosis at both category and subtype levels.
However, it failed to identify the underlying vascular mechanism
captured by the reference diagnosis, leading to incomplete abnormal-test
coverage. This case demonstrates that ClinVerse can distinguish between
achieving the required diagnostic granularity and fully characterizing
the underlying pathology.

The failed case highlights a different limitation, where evidence
acquisition was adequate but clinical interpretation was insufficient.
Although the patient presented with risk factors and abnormal findings
suggestive of ischemia, the agent failed to integrate these signals and
terminated with only a symptom-level diagnosis. This example shows that
collecting relevant evidence alone does not guarantee accurate
diagnosis. By separately evaluating information acquisition and
diagnostic reasoning, ClinVerse can identify whether failures arise from
insufficient evidence gathering or ineffective evidence integration.

# Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training

Essential AI Affiliation: San Francisco, CA
Email: [](mailto:)[research@essential.ai](mailto:research@essential.ai)

###### Abstract

A language model’s ability to reflect on its own reasoning provides a
key advantage for solving complex problems. While most recent research
has focused on how this ability develops during reinforcement learning,
we show that it actually begins to emerge much earlier—during the
model’s pre-training. To study this, we introduce deliberate errors into
chains-of-thought and test whether the model can still arrive at the
correct answer by recognizing and correcting these mistakes. By tracking
performance across different stages of pre-training, we observe that
this self-correcting ability appears early and improves steadily over
time. For instance, an OLMo-2-7B model pre-trained on 4 trillion tokens
displays self-correction on our six self-reflection tasks.

## 1 Introduction

Reflection enhances a model’s ability to adapt its response to prior
reasoning, thereby improving the accuracy of its outputs. Recent work
reports that "behaviors such as reflection… emerge as a result of the
model’s interaction with the reinforcement learning environment"
\[[DeepSeek-AI, 2025a](#bib.bib9)\]. Attributing the development of
capabilities to validate such claims necessitates a comprehensive
evaluation throughout the training phases. In this work, we propose an
exhaustive framework to measure reflection and we observe the phenomenon
consistently during pre-training.

Benchmarking reflection using existing reasoning datasets \[[Mondorf and
Plank, 2024](#bib.bib34)\] has been challenging. Reflection is often
sparse in such tasks, and each model exhibits distinct error patterns
that yield unique manifestations of reflective behavior \[[Madaan
et al., 2023](#bib.bib33), [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)\]. We address
this challenge by distinguishing between _situational-reflection_ and
_self-reflection_. In the situational setting, a model examines
reasoning chains created by another source, such as a different frontier
model. In self-reflection, the model considers its own reasoning
processes. We calibrate a model’s ability to solve a task when provided
with some confounding reasoning that leads to an incorrect answer, and
thereby measure reflection capabilities throughout the pre-training
process.

Figure
[2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")
shows an attempt by a partially pre-trained OLMo-2 checkpoint \[[OLMo
et al., 2025](#bib.bib36)\] to solve a coding task  \[[Gu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)\]. In this case, the model is given a question about
determining the input to the Python function f that leads to the output
‘avdropj gsd ’. In its first attempt, the model – which has 32 billion
parameters and was pre-trained on 4.8 trillion tokens – repeats the
output as the answer, ‘advdropj gsd ’. Upon prompting it with its
incorrect thinking plus a suffix of ‘Wait,’, the model is able to
successfully self-reflect and generates “I think I got it wrong. … the
value of ?? should be \[‘gsd’, ‘avdropj’\]”.

By programmatically introducing erroneous chains-of-thought (CoTs),
featuring elements such as arithmetic perturbations and logical
inconsistencies, we can control and scale the extent of reflection
needed to complete these tasks correctly. This also maintains the
established CoT format \[[Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib55)\]. Furthermore,
our algorithmic approach permits the creation of these datasets in a
relatively rapid and economical manner by adapting established reasoning
benchmarks, which in turn allows for a comprehensive study of models’
reflection abilities across a diverse range of domains. Our six
datasets, spanning the domains of Mathematics, Coding, Logical
Reasoning, and Knowledge Acquisition, are designed to assess both
situational-reflection and self-reflection capabilities.¹¹ 1 Our data
and code will be publicly released at
[https://github.com/Essential-AI/reflection](https://github.com/Essential-AI/reflection).

The results of evaluating pre-training checkpoints from the OLMo-2
family of models with varying compute on our set of six diverse datasets
indicate that reflection is ubiquitous across domains. Even a simple
trigger phrase like “Wait,”\[[Muennighoff et al., 2025](#bib.bib35)\]
enables partially pre-trained models to consistently identify both
introduced errors and their own generated errors. Specifically, 231 of
240 dataset-checkpoint pairs demonstrate at least one instance of
situational-reflection and 154 of 240 pairs demonstrate at least one
instance of self-reflection. With increasing pre-training, models
correct more adversarial examples, leading to an average Pearson
correlation between accuracy and $`\log(\text{pre-training compute})`$
of 0.76 across tasks. Furthermore, as pre-training progresses, models
increasingly recover from incorrect prior reasoning, the rate of
explicit reflection in model generations increases, and explicit
reflection increasingly contributes to recovery from confounding CoTs.

The contributions of this paper are threefold:

- •
  We introduce a systematic method to create six datasets across Code,
  Knowledge Acquisition, Logical Reasoning, and Mathematics to study a
  model’s ability to reflect.
- •
  We demonstrate that pre-trained models of varying capacities and
  training compute can elicit reflection to correct inaccurate prior
  reasoning using simple interjections in a wide variety of domains.
- •
  We observe that continually improving pre-training leads to better
  reflection, requiring fewer test-time tokens to solve the same number
  of tasks.

## 2 Related Work

##### Evaluating Reasoning in LLMs

Real-world applicability of natural language systems is dependent on
reasoning capabilities \[[Wos et al., 1992](#bib.bib57), [Schuster
et al., 2019](#bib.bib44), [Mondorf and Plank, 2024](#bib.bib34)\].
LLMs, combined with prompting techniques such as Chain of Thought \[[Wei
et al., 2022](#bib.bib55)\], Tree of Thought \[[Yao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib60)\], and Self-Consistency \[[Wang et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib53)\] have been successfully used to solve a variety of
reasoning tasks \[[Kojima et al., 2022](#bib.bib26), [Bubeck et al.,
2023](#bib.bib4)\]. Reasoning is typically measured by observing model
outcomes \[[Fu et al., 2023a](#bib.bib14), [Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib31)\], through generation traces \[[Saparov and He,
2023](#bib.bib43), [Dziri et al., 2023](#bib.bib13), [Willig et al.,
2022](#bib.bib56)\] and interactive engagement \[[Zhuang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib64), [Wang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib51), [Bertolazzi et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2)\]. Prior work also conducts parametric attribution of
models’ internal network to identify reasoning characteristics by
studying attention patterns \[[Hou et al., 2023](#bib.bib20)\],
activation flows \[[Dutta et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)\] and individual
layers \[[Pirozelli et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)\]. These methods are
primarily meant to evaluate post-trained models, while we are interested
in diagnostic techniques to elicit reasoning throughout the training
phases, similar in spirit to the study of model performance through
parameter and training data scaling \[[Kaplan et al.,
2020](#bib.bib25)\]. In this work, we study reasoning by evaluating the
emergence of reflection through adversarial datasets. These datasets
require models to reason through multi-step hints, which contain minor
errors, in order to complete the tasks successfully.

##### Adversarial Attacks on LLMs

Adversarial inputs have long been used to expose vulnerabilities in
neural networks \[[Szegedy et al., 2014](#bib.bib49), [Biggio et al.,
2013](#bib.bib3), [Huang et al., 2017](#bib.bib21)\]. More recently,
similar techniques have been applied to LLMs, demonstrating their
susceptibility to carefully crafted prompts \[[Schwinn et al.,
2023](#bib.bib46)\]. These attacks have successfully targeted models’
policy alignment \[[Deng et al., 2022](#bib.bib11), [Wei et al.,
2023](#bib.bib54)\], safety \[[Schuster et al., 2020](#bib.bib45),
[Carlini et al., 2021](#bib.bib5), [Kang et al., 2024](#bib.bib24)\],
and robustness \[[Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib65), [Shen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib47), [Xu et al., 2024](#bib.bib59)\], highlighting large
vulnerabilities. Several approaches have proposed adversarial training
to improve model defenses against such attacks \[[Zhu et al.,
2019](#bib.bib63), [Jiang et al., 2020](#bib.bib22), [Xhonneux et al.,
2024](#bib.bib58)\]. Alternatively, the primary goal of our work is not
to demonstrate models’ vulnerabilities to adversaries but rather to
highlight how such datasets can be used to consistently and thoroughly
evaluate their reasoning capabilities. We achieve this by introducing
chains-of-thought \[[Kojima et al., 2022](#bib.bib26)\], where steps
\[[Lightman et al., 2023](#bib.bib30)\] are largely correct, but contain
mistakes that require reflection \[[Lightman et al., 2023](#bib.bib30),
[Fu et al., 2023b](#bib.bib15)\].

##### Train Time vs Test Time Tradeoff

The increasing reliance on test-time compute in advanced reasoning
models, such as Open AI’s o1 \[[OpenAI, 2024b](#bib.bib38)\] and
DeepSeek-R1 \[[DeepSeek-AI, 2025a](#bib.bib9)\], has reignited
discussions on the optimal allocation of compute \[[Kaplan et al.,
2020](#bib.bib25), [Hoffmann et al., 2022](#bib.bib19)\]. Prior work
studies the trade-offs between train-time and test-time compute \[[Snell
et al., 2024](#bib.bib48)\], as well as the impact of post-training
techniques like supervised fine-tuning \[[Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib28),
[Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)\] and reinforcement learning
\[[DeepSeek-AI, 2025a](#bib.bib9), [Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib52)\], on
reasoning performance \[[Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)\]. Inference-time
paradigms - such as tree search \[[Yao et al., 2023](#bib.bib60)\],
chain-of-thought prompting \[[Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib55)\] and
budget-forcing \[[Muennighoff et al., 2025](#bib.bib35)\] - interact
with a model’s pretrained representations \[[Yeo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib61), [Ruis et al., 2025](#bib.bib42), [Gandhi et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16)\]. However, in our work, we contribute a complementary
perspective by demonstrating that certain reasoning capabilities can
gradually emerge during pre-training itself, even in the absence of
fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, or specialized test-time inference
techniques. Our findings suggest that key aspects of reasoning can be
instilled through pre-training alone, potentially changing the calculus
of the optimal use of compute across training and inference.

## 3 Approach

Our objective is to measure reflection comprehensively and at scale. We
present a definition of reflection
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Defining Reflection ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training"),
programmatically create tasks to elicit reflection
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Eliciting Reflection with Adversarial Reflection Datasets ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training"),
and rigorously measure the presence of reflection
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Measuring Reflection ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

### 3.1 Defining Reflection

Reflection is a form of metacognition that involves examining
information, evaluating the reasoning that underlies it, and adjusting
future behavior based on that evaluation. In the context of a language
model, this process can apply to information introduced from an external
source or generated by the model itself. In this work, we create two
settings in which to elicit and measure reflection:

Situational-reflection is when a model reflects on information created
by another source, such as a different model.

Self-reflection is when a model reflects on its own generated outputs.

We also comprehensively characterize reflection by means of two forms:

Explicit reflection occurs when a model produces tokens that by their
meaning recognize and address mistakes in the adversarial context.
Explicit reflection may be present in either correct model outputs (in
other words, those that constitute correct answer to our adversarial
tasks) or incorrect model outputs.

Implicit reflection occurs when a model navigates adversarial contexts
to solve a task correctly without outputting tokens that explicitly
identify errors in prior reasoning. In our definition, this means that
implicit reflection cannot result in an incorrect answer to our
adversarial tasks. This allows us to distinguish between, on one hand,
cases where explicit reflection is absent but implicit reflection may be
inferred to have taken place and, on the other hand, cases where no
reflection took place at all.

### 3.2 Eliciting Reflection with Adversarial Reflection Datasets

We present an algorithm to generate adversarial datasets that elicit
reflection behavior from language models. The algorithm creates
adversarial CoTs that lead to incorrect solutions. Unlike
self-reflection, where we can draw from the models’ own mistakes, we
must devise artificial adversarial CoTs for situational-reflection. At a
high level, these adversarial CoTs are created by corrupting correct
CoTs in ways that mimic human reasoning errors, such as logical missteps
and arithmetic miscalculations. In both cases, when we provide the CoTs
in context, the models must reflect on the mistakes and fix them to
arrive at the correct solution. We believe that these settings are
necessary to comprehensively study reflection.

The task design includes appending a trigger token, such as "Wait,", to
promote continued reasoning throughout the task-solving process.

### 3.3 Measuring Reflection

We propose an automated approach to measure the reflective capabilities
of models using adversarial datasets, based on our earlier
categorization of reflection (see
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Defining Reflection ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")):

Measuring explicit reflection: To identify instances of explicit
reflection, we develop a prompt-based LLM classifier that detects when a
model’s output explicitly acknowledges and addresses errors within the
provided adversarial context, regardless of whether the model reached
the correct answer or not. The classifier is described below in
[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Evaluation ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

Measuring implicit reflection: We stipulate that all model generations
that lead to the correct answer in the presence of adversarial context
are attributable to reflection, even if no tokens that relate to
reflection are outputted. We believe this accords with an everyday
understanding of ’reflection’ in the sense of describing human
metacognition. An implication of our approach is that, by construction,
generations that produce correct answers but are not identified by the
explicit classifier are categorized as instances of implicit reflection.

See Section
[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Evaluation ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")
for further details on the specific reflection metrics that we used.

## 4 Experimental Setup

In order to comprehensively study reflection, we evaluate partially
pre-trained models spanning different computational budgets, with
variations in both parameter count and number of training tokens
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Model Families ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

Our study includes two types of adversarial tasks
[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Datasets ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training"):
(1) Situational-Reflection, where adversarial chains of thought are
systematically generated from existing datasets using frontier models;
and (2) Self-Reflection, where adversarial chains of thought are derived
from the model’s own previous incorrect responses to original task
instances.

Our evaluation also examines whether model outputs demonstrate explicit
reflective reasoning
[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Evaluation ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

We detail our infrastructure set-up in Section
[4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Infrastructure ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

### 4.1 Model Families

OLMo-2

OLMo-2 \[[OLMo et al., 2025](#bib.bib36)\] is a fully open-source,
open-weight LLM project with training checkpoints available for 7B, 13B
and 32B parameter variants. We evaluated a total of 40 checkpoints (see
Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B OLMo-2 and Qwen Pre-trained Checkpoints ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")
for details). We aimed for equally spaced checkpoints and used the
closest alternatives in cases when they were not available.

Qwen2.5

We evaluated Qwen2.5’s 0.5B, 3B, 7B, 14B, 32B and 72B parameter variants
\[[Qwen et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)\]. See Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B OLMo-2 and Qwen Pre-trained Checkpoints ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")
for details.

### 4.2 Datasets

We evaluate the reflection phenomenon over a wide set of tasks, creating
six adversarial datasets based on BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) \[[bench authors,
2023](#bib.bib1)\], CruxEval \[[Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)\], GSM8K
\[[Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib7)\], GSM8K-Platinum \[[Vendrow et al.,
2025](#bib.bib50)\] and TriviaQA \[[Joshi et al., 2017](#bib.bib23)\].
An overview of our adversarial datasets can be found in Tables
[1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Datasets ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")
&
[2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.2 Datasets ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

We utilize models DeepSeek-V3 (referred to as DS-V3) \[[DeepSeek-AI,
2025b](#bib.bib10)\], GPT-4o²² 2 GPT-4o was better than DeepSeek-V3 at
following instructions for CruxEval’s adversarial data creation.
\[[OpenAI, 2024a](#bib.bib37)\], and SentenceTransformers \[[Reimers and
Gurevych, 2020](#bib.bib41)\], as well as multiple automated checks and
manual inspections in our pipeline to ensure quality and robustness in
our datasets.

Situational-Reflection Datasets: Creation of these datasets involves a
multi-step pipeline where we prompt an LLM to create adversarial CoTs
(see also Algorithm
[1](#LST1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.2 Eliciting Reflection with Adversarial Reflection Datasets ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")).
For reasoning tasks, the original tasks may contain the CoTs as a
dataset artifact; if not, we create them using a frontier model such as
GPT-4o or DS-V3. Subsequently, the correct CoTs are used to develop
adversarial CoTs by introducing deliberate errors that lead to wrong
answers. In addition, checks are made at the end to ensure that CoTs do
not reveal their misleading intentions. The pipelines and prompts have
been carefully designed to minimize errors and are dataset-specific, but
the overall framework can be generalized to any domain and dataset.

Self-Reflection Datasets: These datasets are created by executing the
LLM we want to evaluate on the base task and collecting CoTs
corresponding to the questions the models failed on (see also Algorithm
[2](#LST2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ 3.2 Eliciting Reflection with Adversarial Reflection Datasets ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")).
Since we are interested in the reflection abilities of models as
pre-training progresses, we only keep the questions incorrectly answered
by all checkpoints at each parameter scale in the self-reflection
datasets to ensure consistent comparison across pre-training
checkpoints.

Extensive details on our dataset-specific pipelines, checks, and filters
can be found in Appendix
[F](#A6 "Appendix F Adversarial Dataset Creation ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

##### Metrics

As seen in Table
[3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.3 Evaluation ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training"),
for both situational reflection and self-reflection settings, Accuracy
is the fraction of task instances that a model correctly solves.
Independent of Accuracy, we utilize our Explicit Reflection Classifier
to measure the Explicit Reflection Rate, which is the fraction of task
instances where the model output exhibits explicit reflection,
regardless of whether they are correct or not. We additionally report
Explicit Reflection Accuracy, which is the fraction of task instances
that a model both correctly solves and exhibits explicit reflection on.
Finally, Implicit Reflection Accuracy is the fraction of task instances
where the model output is correct and does not exhibit explicit
reflection. Details about Accuracy metrics for each dataset can be found
in Appendix
[H](#A8 "Appendix H Situational Reflection Dataset Specific Metrics ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

Furthermore, for each data point, we report the pre-training compute as
$`6nt`$, where $`n`$ and $`t`$ are the number of parameters and training
tokens respectively. More details about our train time and test time
compute formulations can be found in section
[5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 One Can Trade Training for Test-time Compute ‣ 5 Results ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

##### Explicit Reflection Classifier

We develop a prompt-based classifier to determine whether a model output
exhibits explicit reflection. DeepSeek-V3 \[[DeepSeek-AI,
2025b](#bib.bib10)\] is prompted with a description of what is meant by
"reflection" along with two to four explicit reflection exemplars. Some
examples of the kind of explicit reflection phrases that this classifier
is intended to detect are shown in
Table [5](#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5 Results ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").
We validated the classifier against human-annotated golden labels on
GSM8K, cruxeval-o, and TriviaQA (120 questions per benchmark; see
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Reflection Classifier Annotations ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")
for details on the annotation process). Although our classifier recalls
fewer instances of reflection (see
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5 Results ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")),
its precision is high enough to validate its usefulness. In the worst
case, we will under-report reflection behaviors, but be more confident
when we do report them.

### 5.2 Models Reflect Even Without Triggers; _‘Wait,’_ Enhances Explicitness and Accuracy

In order to understand the causal role of the _‘Wait,’_-trigger, we
investigate model performance on GSM8K-Platinum with triggers at
opposite extremes. Specifically, we study model performance with no
trigger ($`A`$) and with a trigger ($`B`$) containing the explicit
admission _‘Wait, I made a mistake’_. We choose A as a mode in which to
draw minimum attention to the mistakes in the adversarial CoTs. In
contrast, we choose B as a mode that emphasizes the existence of an
error in the CoT. Figure
[4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.2 Models Reflect Even Without Triggers; ‘Wait,’ Enhances Explicitness and Accuracy ‣ 5 Results ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")
plots these results.

The results first confirm the hypothesis that even without a trigger,
models can increasingly succeed on situational confounders as
pre-training progresses. The role of _‘Wait,’_ in the results above in
Section
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Explicit Situational-Reflection is Prominent in all Models ‣ 5 Results ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training")
is further clarified here. We see that the models’ performance on this
task is bounded by the two extremes of $`A`$ and $`B`$. In scenario
$`A`$, models improve accuracy by reflecting implicitly and in scenario
$`B`$ models reflect explicitly by construction and thus greatly improve
performance. The _‘Wait,’_-setting behaves like $`A`$ when implicitly
reflecting and like $`B`$ when explicitly reflecting. Interestingly, as
seen in Figure
[4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.2 Models Reflect Even Without Triggers; ‘Wait,’ Enhances Explicitness and Accuracy ‣ 5 Results ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training"),
its performance can be decomposed as
$`acc_{Wait}=e_{Wait}*acc_{B}+(1-e_{Wait})*i\_acc_{A}`$, where
$`e_{Wait}`$ is the rate of explicit reflection and $`i\_acc`$ is the
implicit reflection accuracy.

Furthermore, we see that a significant elevation in performance is
attributable to the _‘Wait,’_-trigger. This is because the trigger
elicits explicit reflection at a rate that increases with pre-training,
and it also attains the performance achieved by the corresponding
$`B`$-triggered models, which, as noted above, we choose as a mode that
emphasizes the existence of an error in the CoT. In other words, when
the model, conditioned on an adversarial CoT with _‘Wait,’_, exhibits
explicit reflection, its performance is comparable to when the model is
explicitly told that the CoT contains a mistake. Conversely, when the
model is conditioned on an adversarial CoT with _‘Wait,’_ but does not
exhibit explicit reflection, its performance is in line with the mode
where the model is conditioned on the adversarial CoT only. This
establishes the causal connection for explicit reflection through
_‘Wait,’_ in improving accuracy.

### 5.4 One Can Trade Training for Test-time Compute

We investigate the trade-off between increased investment in train-time
compute and the corresponding expenditure required at test-time to
achieve comparable accuracy on the downstream task. We approach this by
estimating the train-time compute as $`6nt`$ where $`n`$ and $`t`$ are
the number of parameters and training tokens, respectively, and
test-time compute as $`2nw`$ where $`w`$ represents the number of words
generated to solve a certain number of adversarial questions. ⁵⁵ 5 The
factors 6 and 2 are standard per-parameter per-token train and inference
FLOPs calculation constants respectively. They account for both forward
and backward passes that are required for training and the forward pass
alone that is required for inference.

We begin by specifying a set of target numbers of adversarial questions
to be answered correctly. We then plot a curve for each of these
targets. We study this for the GSM8K-Platinum adversarial dataset
introduced earlier. We follow a sequential test-time scaling approach by
appending the trigger “Wait,” to model generations. In fact, we
introduce two “Wait,” triggers to enable weaker models to reach the same
metric level as stronger models. This mimics the sequential method for
scaling test-time compute in  \[[Muennighoff et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35)\].

As we see in Figure
[7](#footnote7 "footnote 7 ‣ Figure 6 ‣ 5.4 One Can Trade Training for Test-time Compute ‣ 5 Results ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training"),
as train-time compute increases, the test-time compute requirement
decreases in OLMo-2-32B checkpoints. This result further supports our
hypothesis that the model gets better at reflection as pre-training
progresses, implying less test-time compute for a given level of
accuracy.

## 6 Conclusion

The goal of this work is to address the question, “How does reflective
reasoning advance during pre-training?", in contrast to the commonly
held belief that reflection emerges during post-training through
reinforcement learning. We propose and implement a comprehensive
framework to measure reflection capabilities throughout the pre-training
regime. Through our adversarial datasets, for both
situational-reflection and self-reflection settings, we are able to
calibrate these capabilities extensively. We determine that models
trained with very few training flops, like OLMo-2-7B trained with 198
billion tokens, demonstrate reflection across Mathematics, Code, Verbal,
and Logical Reasoning. Furthermore, as more compute is spent on
pre-training, these abilities further strengthen.

## Appendix E Do Models Understand Correct Answers?

We investigate whether pre-trained language models implicitly prefer
correct answers by analyzing differences in perpleixty in a controlled
setting. Models receive the same question accompanied by an adversarial
CoT, followed by either a correct or incorrect final answer.
Specifically, we compare two setups:

1.  1.  PPL(Adversarial CoT + Incorrect Answer \| Question): A plausible but
        faulty reasoning path followed by a logically consistent, incorrect
        conclusion.
2.  2.  PPL(Adversarial CoT + Correct Answer \| Question): The same
        misleading reasoning path followed by a correct answer that
        contradicts the adversarial reasoning.

We compute perplexity on the final answer and adversarial CoT,
conditioned on the question across multiple checkpoints of the OLMo-2
model family (7B, 13B, 32B) during pre-training. This allows us to test
whether models inherently assign lower perplexity to correct answers
despite misleading reasoning.

![Refer to
caption](2504.04022v1/images/situational_olmo2_gsm8k-platinum_adv_perplexity_difference.png)

Figure 12: Perplexity difference (Correct - Incorrect) between
Adversarial CoT + Correct Answer and Adversarial CoT + Incorrect Answer
for the OLMo-2 family on GSM8K-Platinum. Positive values indicate the
model finds incorrect answers more likely under misleading reasoning.

Analysis Across the GSM8K-Platinum dataset, models consistently assign
lower perplexity to incorrect answers when conditioned on misleading
reasoning. This can be reflective of a local consistency bias, where
models prioritize coherence with preceding context over correctness.
However, as illustrated in
Figure [12](#A5.F12 "Figure 12 ‣ Appendix E Do Models Understand Correct Answers? ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training"),
this perplexity gap narrows over the course of pre-training.

Notably, certain checkpoints of the largest model (OLMo-2-32B) exhibit
near-zero perplexity differences between correct and incorrect answers,
suggesting that larger models may begin to favor correctness even when
preceded by flawed reasoning. Still, since the perplexity difference
remains non-negative even at this scale, reflective trigger cues—such as
"Wait,"—continue to help models override misleading prior reasoning and
shift toward the correct conclusion, as reported in Section
[5](#S5 "5 Results ‣ Rethinking Reflection in Pre-Training").

## Appendix F Adversarial Dataset Creation

### F.1 Math Reasoning

We evaluate mathematical reasoning capabilities on word problems using
the GSM8K \[[Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib7)\] and GSM8K-Platinum
\[[Vendrow et al., 2025](#bib.bib50)\] benchmarks. Our experiments focus
on the validation sets, consisting of 1,319 questions for GSM8K and
1,209 questions for GSM8K-Platinum. Analyzing both allows us to measure
performance on the widely adopted, but noisy, original benchmark, while
also assessing reliability on a higher-quality, disambiguated version.
This dual evaluation helps ensure that observed progress reflects
genuine improvements in reasoning rather than overfitting to label
noise.

Adversarial Settings

We consider the following two adversarial settings:

1.  1.  Situational-Reflection:

    1.  (a)
        As the original task includes CoT reasoning, we create
        adversarial CoTs by programmatically manipulating the original
        reasoning steps.
    1.  (b)
        For each question, we prompt DeepSeek-V3(\[[DeepSeek-AI,
        2025b](#bib.bib10)\]) to generate a Python program that captures
        the original CoT logic, then instruct it to identify key
        variables and introduce targeted errors into the code.
    1.  (c)
        The modified program is converted back into natural language,
        forming an adversarial CoT. Instances where the final answer
        remains unchanged are discarded.
    1.  (d)
        We insert the phrase “Wait,” after the question and the
        adversarial context. This results in 9,070 adversarial instances
        for GSM8K and 8,223 for GSM8K-Platinum.

2.  2.  Self-Reflection: In the self-reflection setting, we leverage the
        model’s own incorrect answers as adversarial contexts. The procedure
        is as follows:

    1.  (a)
        We run the original GSM8K and GSM8K-Platinum tasks using the
        pre-trained model checkpoints.
    1.  (b)
        For each question, we retain only those instances where the
        model produces an incorrect answer.
    1.  (c)
        The incorrect answer is then used as the adversarial context for
        that question.
    1.  (d)
        As in the situation-reflection setup, we prepend the phrase
        “Wait,” to the question and the adversarial context. Each model
        checkpoint is evaluated on the intersection of questions all
        checkpoints in the same parameter family answered incorrectly.

#### Attack Types in Situational-Reflection

We programmatically generate adversarial CoTs by introducing minimal but
targeted perturbations to the original reasoning logic. Specifically, we
apply one of seven structured modifications to each question,
categorized by their location in the reasoning sequence—beginning,
middle, or end:

1.  1.  Beginning – Variable Initialization: Adjust the initial variable
        with a random multiplication factor (e.g., “There are
        $`8\times 2=16`$ apples”).
2.  2.  Middle – Arithmetic Perturbation: Modify the first arithmetic
        operation (e.g., change addition to multiplication or alter an
        operand).
3.  3.  Middle – Extraneous Step: Insert a logically unnecessary but
        syntactically plausible step at the start of the reasoning process.
4.  4.  Middle – Step Omission: Remove the first necessary calculation,
        leaving the logical chain incomplete.
5.  5.  End – Arithmetic Perturbation: Adjust the final arithmetic operation
        in the solution (e.g., slightly perturb the final calculation).
6.  6.  End – Extraneous Step: Append an unnecessary calculation after the
        final answer (e.g., “Then divide by 2 again…” without
        justification).
7.  7.  End – Step Omission: Remove the final step, resulting in a truncated
        or unresolved answer.

These perturbations are designed to preserve surface-level fluency while
disrupting the underlying reasoning logic, challenging the model’s
ability to verify and trust intermediate steps.

#### Dataset Augmentation Summary

Each of the 7 perturbation types is applied to every instance in the
original datasets. After filtering out unchanged outputs (i.e., where
the model’s prediction matches the original answer), we retain only
instances where the perturbation leads to a different model behavior.

##### GSM8K

- •
  Total perturbed instances: 9,233 (1,319 questions $`\times`$ 7
  perturbations)
- •
  Filtered out (final answer unchanged): 163
- •
  Retained adversarial instances: 9,070

##### GSM8K-Platinum

- •
  Total perturbed instances: 8,463 (1,209 questions $`\times`$ 7
  perturbations)
- •
  Filtered out (final answer unchanged): 240
- •
  Retained adversarial instances: 8,223

### F.2 Code Reasoning and Understanding

We use CruxEval \[[Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)\] to evaluate code
reasoning and code understanding abilities. We create two flavors of
this task in-line with the original work: cruxeval-i and cruxeval-o. The
dataset consists of 800 code functions each with an input-output pair.
In cruxeval-i, the task is to predict the input given the code, output
and in cruxeval-o the task is to predict the output.

Adversarial Settings

We consider the following two adversarial settings:

1.  1.  Situational-Reflection:

    1.  (a)
        As the original tasks does not provide CoT reasoning, we first
        generate correct CoTs for both the tasks by prompting GPT-4o
        \[[OpenAI, 2024a](#bib.bib37)\].
    1.  (b)
        For each task instance, we create with an adversarial answer to
        break the assert statement accompanying the task instance, then
        the adversarial CoT is generated conditioned on correct CoT and
        adversarial answer.
    1.  (c)
        This adversarial CoT, along with the parent function, serves as
        the adversarial context.
    1.  (d)
        We insert the phrase “Wait,” after the function and the
        adversarial context. To ensure the robustness and quality of
        adversarial CoTs, we employ filtering and verification steps
        throughout our pipeline and end-up with 605 adversarial
        instances for cruxeval-i and 704 adversarial instances for
        cruxeval-o

2.  2.  Self-Reflection:

    1.  (a)
        We execute the original cruxeval-i and cruxeval-o tasks using
        the pre-trained model checkpoints.
    1.  (b)
        For each function, we collect the model-generated CoTs and
        discard all questions for which the model produced the correct
        answer.
    1.  (c)
        The model-generated incorrect CoT is then treated as the
        adversarial context for the corresponding question.
    1.  (d)
        Similar to the situation-reflection setting, we insert the
        phrase “Wait,” after the question and the adversarial context.
        Each model checkpoint is evaluated on the intersection of
        questions all checkpoints in the same parameter family answered
        incorrectly.

3.  3.  We employ a five-step pipeline to ensure robustness while creating
        adversarial datasets for cruxeval-i and cruxeval-o:

    1.  (a)
        Generate an English text explanation for the function code
        provided in the task instance.
    1.  (b)
        Generate a correct CoT for the task instance given the text
        explanation of the code.
        1.  i.
            We run the assert statement provided with each task instance
            to ensure the correctness of these CoTs.

    1.  (c)
        Generate adversarial answers for the task instance.
        1.  i.
            We run the assert statement and only accept the adversarial
            answer that fails the test case.

    1.  (d)
        Generate an adversarial CoT for the function given the
        adversarial answer and the correct CoT.
    1.  (e)
        Perform LLM-based verification of the adversarial CoT to filter
        out poor-quality adversarial CoTs.
        1.  i.
            We remove cases where the adversarial CoT mentions or
            discusses the correct answer at any point to avoid
            contamination.
        2.  ii.
            We remove any adversarial CoTs where there is an explicit or
            implicit admission of an error in the adversarial CoT.

### F.3 Knowledge Acquisition and Reading Comprehension

We utilize TriviaQA \[[Joshi et al., 2017](#bib.bib23)\] to evaluate
reasoning and knowledge acquisition in the presence of a confounding
reference passage. We focus on the validation set, comprising 17,944
trivia questions paired with corresponding reference documents that
contain the correct answers.

Adversarial Settings

We consider the following two adversarial settings:

1.  1.  Situational-Reflection:

    1.  (a)
        As the original task does not incorporate CoT reasoning, we
        derive adversarial CoTs from the reference documents.
    1.  (b)
        For each question, we find a confounding reference document that
        is semantically similar (SentenceTransformer \[[Reimers and
        Gurevych, 2020](#bib.bib41)\]) to the original passage but does
        not contain the right answer.
    1.  (c)
        This confounding passage, along with the answer associated with
        its parent question, serves as the adversarial context.
    1.  (d)
        We insert the phrase “Wait,” after the question and the
        adversarial context. To ensure sufficient context length for the
        model to generate an answer, we include only adversarial
        instances with a total length not exceeding 500 words, resulting
        in a dataset of 2,961 instances.

2.  2.  Self-Reflection:

    1.  (a)
        We execute the original TriviaQA task using the pre-trained
        model checkpoints.
    1.  (b)
        For each question, we collect the model-generated answer and
        discard all questions for which the model produced the correct
        answer.
    1.  (c)
        The model-generated incorrect answer is then treated as the
        adversarial context for the corresponding question.
    1.  (d)
        Similar to the situation-reflection setting, we insert the
        phrase “Wait,” after the question and the adversarial context.
        Each model checkpoint is evaluated on the intersection of
        questions all checkpoints in the same parameter family answered
        incorrectly.

### F.4 Verbal, Logical, and Mathematical Reasoning

We use BBH \[[bench authors, 2023](#bib.bib1)\], a diverse suite of 27
reasoning subtasks often requiring multi-step reasoning, such as object
shuffling, word ordering, geometric shapes descriptions and navigation.
Of the subtasks, 7 are binary multiple choice, 16 have 3 or more
multiple choice answers and 4 are generation tasks.

Adversarial settings

1.  1.  Situational-Reflection:

    1.  (a)
        We being by running the standard 3-shot BBH evaluation task on
        DS-V3.
    1.  (b)
        For questions that DS-V3 answered incorrectly, we use those
        incorrect CoTs and incorrect answers as the adversarial CoT.
    1.  (c)
        For questions that DS-V3 answered correctly, we prompt DS-V3 to
        choose an incorrect answer and then to create an incorrect CoT
        that is consistent with that incorrect answer. We manually
        curated 5-shot prompts to instruct DS-V3 on how to create these
        incorrect CoTs. We use these as adversarial CoTs for this subset
        of questions.
    1.  (d)
        Finally, we complete the prompt by appending the phrase “Wait,”
        after the question and the adversarial CoT.

2.  2.  Self-Reflection:

    1.  (a)
        We execute the original BBH task using the pre-trained model
        checkpoints
    1.  (b)
        For each question, we collect the model-generated answer and
        discard all questions for which the model produced the correct
        answer.
    1.  (c)
        The model-generated incorrect answer is then treated as the
        adversarial context for the corresponding question.
    1.  (d)
        Similar to the situation-reflection setting, we insert the
        phrase “Wait,” after the question and the adversarial context.
        Each model checkpoint is evaluated on the intersection of
        questions all checkpoints in the same parameter family answered
        incorrectly.

## Appendix G Reflection Classifier Annotations

To evaluate the accuracy of our prompt-based reflection classifier—which
detects whether a model’s output contains explicit reflection—we
collected human annotations over a curated set of model-generated
responses.

A total of 24 annotators participated in an in-person annotation
session, labeling 360 unique questions drawn from three datasets: GSM8K,
cruxeval-o, and TriviaQA (120 examples per dataset). Each question was
annotated independently by two different annotators to capture a range
of interpretations and surface any ambiguities.

Following the initial annotations, we conducted a structured review
process to validate and refine the labels. This review aligned the
annotations with our operational definition of explicit reflection and
resolved any disagreements or inconsistencies. The final set of labels
was used to evaluate and calibrate the reflection classifier.

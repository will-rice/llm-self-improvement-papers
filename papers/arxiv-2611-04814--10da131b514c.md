# Think Less, Locally: Local Preference Learning for Reasoning Compression

Muyao Yuan   Liyan Xu^(‡)   Weizhan Zhang    Muyan Jiao   Jiangnan

###### Abstract

Large reasoning models achieve strong performance on challenging tasks,
but their long chain-of-thought often incurs substantial inference costs
by continuing to deliberate after sufficient information has been
obtained. Existing reasoning compression methods primarily operate on
reasoning trajectories, paying less attention to the local decision of
whether further reasoning is necessary given the current state. We
propose LoCo, a local preference learning method for reasoning
compression. LoCo detects candidate overthinking states from the model’s
own traces, freezes the corresponding reasoning prefixes, regenerates
shorter verified continuations from the same states, and trains on the
resulting state-conditioned preference pairs with DPO. This formulation
teaches the model when to continue reasoning and when to conclude,
rather than simply encouraging shorter trajectories overall. We
construct training data entirely from self-generated mathematical traces
and evaluate LoCo on five mathematical and three code generation
benchmarks. LoCo reduces completion length by 41.7%–45.4% on mathematics
and 23.4%–36.3% on code, while maintaining or improving accuracy and
achieving the best AUC across all code benchmarks. LoCo consistently
improves the accuracy-efficiency trade-off on mathematics and transfers
effectively to code generation despite math-only training, demonstrating
the cross-domain generality of local preference learning for reasoning
compression. The code and model will be publicly released at
[https://github.com/MuyaoYuan/LoCo](https://github.com/MuyaoYuan/LoCo).

## 1 Introduction

Large reasoning models (LRMs) ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)) have made
remarkable progress on complex reasoning tasks such as mathematical
problem solving and algorithmic programming. This progress is largely
driven by extended chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, in which models
generate thousands of tokens for hypothesis formation, calculation,
revision, and verification before arriving at an answer ([Muennighoff et
al., 2025](#bib.bib2)). Such extended reasoning comes at a substantial
inference cost, significantly increasing decoding latency and memory
consumption during deployment. More importantly, longer reasoning does
not necessarily lead to higher accuracy, as models may continue
reasoning beyond what is necessary to reach a correct answer ([Chen et
al., 2024](#bib.bib3); [Sui et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)). This tendency to
engage in excessive reasoning is commonly referred to as overthinking.

Recent studies indicate that when the task is solvable by the model,
overthinking is more closely related to model preference than reasoning
capability ([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib5); [Peng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6)). A model may get the correct answer early in the
reasoning process yet continue generating unnecessary reasoning,
reflecting a preference to keep thinking rather than to stop when
sufficient reasoning has been reached. The problem therefore lies in
when the model chooses to stop, rather than whether it can reach the
correct answer ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib3); [Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib5)). Mitigating overthinking thus calls for aligning
models toward concise reasoning behaviors, which can be learned from
carefully curated self-generated reasoning traces ([Shen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib22); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib18)).

Toward this objective, existing self-generated methods derive
compression supervision in two main ways, through trajectory-level
signals and fine-grained operations within the reasoning process.
Trajectory-level signals are derived from the reasoning trajectory as a
whole, using global properties of complete solutions to establish a
preference for more concise reasoning. Specifically, one line of
work ([Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib8); [Luo et al., 2026](#bib.bib9);
[Shen et al., 2025](#bib.bib22); [Ma et al., 2025](#bib.bib10))
constructs preference pairs by comparing shorter and longer correct
solutions, encouraging the model to favor the shorter one, while
another ([Arora and Zanette, 2026](#bib.bib11); [Aggarwal and Welleck,
2025](#bib.bib12); [Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)) incorporates length
preferences into reinforcement learning by rewarding shorter correct
reasoning.

Fine-grained operations provide a more localized alternative by
modifying parts of an existing reasoning trajectory. Specifically, these
methods remove redundant content ([Xia et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)),
compress individual reasoning steps ([Jang et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)),
or shorten continuations from intermediate reasoning states to construct
training data for more concise reasoning ([Lai et al.,
2024](#bib.bib19); [Jin et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)). Such operations are
well aligned with recent findings ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)) that
LLM reasoning proceeds through local and incremental state transitions
rather than a globally planned process. However, existing fine-grained
methods still treat the reasoning trajectory itself as the object of
compression. They focus on which parts of the trajectory should be
removed or shortened, rather than whether further reasoning is necessary
given the model’s current state. This distinction is crucial because the
appropriate compression decision can vary across reasoning states: the
model may need to continue reasoning at one state but should conclude at
another.

Building on this observation, we propose LoCo (Local Compression), a
local preference learning method for reasoning compression. LoCo
identifies overthinking states from the model’s own reasoning process
and constructs state-conditioned preferences by contrasting alternative
continuations from the same reasoning state. The resulting supervision
is grounded in the model’s own reasoning trajectories and actual
reasoning behavior. By making the compression decision conditional on
the current reasoning state, LoCo encourages the model to conclude when
further reasoning is unnecessary while preserving its ability to
continue reasoning when additional computation is needed. In this way,
LoCo avoids indiscriminate compression across the reasoning trajectory
and reduces the risk of compromising the model’s reasoning capability on
more challenging problems.

We construct training data from self-generated traces and evaluate LoCo
on five mathematical reasoning benchmarks and three coding benchmarks
spanning a broad range of difficulty. The experiments show that LoCo
consistently achieves the strongest efficiency–performance trade-off on
mathematics, with especially clear advantages on the harder datasets
where both high compression and high accuracy are required. The same
trained model also transfers effectively to coding benchmarks of
different difficulty, supporting the view that LoCo improves
chain-of-thought compression by reshaping the model’s reasoning
preference rather than by injecting additional task-specific knowledge.
To summarize, our main contributions are threefold:

- •
  We show that effective reasoning compression requires state-aware
  decisions about when further reasoning is unnecessary, rather than
  simply favoring shorter trajectories.
- •
  We introduce LoCo, a local preference learning framework that
  identifies overthinking states and learns continue-or-conclude
  preferences from verified continuations sharing the same reasoning
  prefix.
- •
  We demonstrate the effectiveness of LoCo across mathematical reasoning
  and code generation, with consistent improvements in the
  efficiency-performance trade-off and strong cross-domain
  generalization.

## 2 Related Works

### 2.1 Trajectory-Level Compression

Several reasoning compression methods formulate compression supervision
at the trajectory level, treating complete reasoning traces as the unit
of comparison. Kimi k1.5 ([Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)) introduces a
Long2Short stage that contrasts correct short and long solutions
generated by the model itself and optimizes their relative preference
with DPO. O1-Pruner ([Luo et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)) follows a similar
paradigm by applying preference fine-tuning to pre-sampled responses,
encouraging the model to favor more concise reasoning. CoT-Valve ([Ma et
al., 2025](#bib.bib10)) identifies a parameter-space direction for
controlling reasoning length, then learns from globally shortened
reasoning traces. Another group of methods ([Arora and Zanette,
2026](#bib.bib11); [Aggarwal and Welleck, 2025](#bib.bib12); [Hu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13)) incorporates reasoning length into the reinforcement
learning objective, jointly rewarding solution quality and efficient
token usage under a prescribed length budget. Collectively, these
studies show that reasoning can often be shortened without substantially
affecting problem-solving performance. However, these methods generally
compare or optimize complete reasoning trajectories, which provides an
overall preference for shorter solutions but offers less information
about where the unnecessary reasoning occurs. Two solutions may reach
the same answer through different reasoning paths, making it difficult
to determine which part of the reasoning can be safely shortened from
their overall length or preference alone. This motivates a more
fine-grained approach that identifies unnecessary deliberation at
specific points in the reasoning process.

### 2.2 Fine-Grained Compression

Fine-grained operations offer a more localized way to reduce reasoning
by modifying selected parts of an existing trajectory. These methods
remove redundant content, compress individual reasoning steps, or
shorten continuations from intermediate states, in line with recent
findings that LLM reasoning proceeds through local and incremental state
transitions rather than a globally planned process ([Xu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)). One line of work removes redundant content from the
trajectory itself. TokenSkip ([Xia et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)) prunes
verbose tokens, Verbosity-Aware Rationale Reduction ([Jang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib16)) removes redundant sentences based on principled
likelihood criteria, and Reasoning Shaping ([Song et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17)) reweights computation-heavy segments during RLVR
training. TokenSqueeze ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib18)) combines
depth-matched data selection with distribution-aligned token-level
linguistic refinement and achieves strong performance on standard
benchmarks. Another line operates at the step level. Step-DPO ([Lai et
al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) introduces preference pairs of correct and
erroneous continuations sharing the same prefix, while SSPO ([Xu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)) and ReCUT ([Jin et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)) adapt this
formulation to reasoning compression and encourage more concise
step-level reasoning. Despite their finer granularity, these approaches
mainly derive compression signals by contrasting or modifying existing
reasoning content. Such supervision is effective for identifying
unnecessary tokens, sentences, or steps, but provides less direct
guidance on the decision to continue reasoning from a particular state.
LoCo addresses this aspect by anchoring the comparison at a fixed
reasoning state and learning preferences over its alternative
continuations. This formulation captures the local decision of whether
further deliberation is warranted while retaining the original state as
a shared context.

## 3 Preliminaries

#### Large Reasoning Models and Reasoning States.

Large reasoning models (LRMs), such as DeepSeek-R1 ([Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib1)), are trained to perform extended reasoning before
producing a final answer. Given a problem $`q`$, a model first generates
a chain of thought (CoT) $`S=(s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n})`$, consisting of
intermediate hypotheses, calculations, revisions, and verifications,
before arriving at the final answer. The reasoning process is typically
enclosed by dedicated thinking tags, such as \<think\> and \</think\> in
DeepSeek-R1 ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)), with paragraph breaks
naturally separating different reasoning moves. We therefore segment the
CoT into reasoning steps at paragraph boundaries and denote each step by
$`s_{i}`$. A reasoning step may correspond to establishing a subgoal,
performing a computation, verifying an intermediate result, or drawing a
conclusion.

We denote the input prompt associated with question $`q`$ as
$`\mathrm{prompt}(q)`$, which includes the task instruction and the
question itself. For notational convenience, we define the reasoning
state after step $`s_{i}`$ as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       P_{i}=\mathrm{prompt}(q)\,\|\,s_{0}\,\|\,\cdots\,\|\,s_{i},
       ```                                                          |     | (1) |

where $`\|`$ denotes concatenation. Thus, $`P_{i}`$ contains all
information available after completing $`s_{i}`$ and before generating
$`s_{i+1}`$. Each state therefore defines a local decision point at
which the model can either continue reasoning or conclude. We view
overthinking as continuing to generate additional reasoning after the
current state already contains sufficient information to support the
answer. This state-based formulation provides the notation used
throughout the method.

## 4 Method

### 4.1 Overview

LoCo converts a reasoning model’s own overthinking traces into
state-anchored preference supervision through three stages, as
illustrated in
Figure [1](#S4.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 4.1 Overview ‣ 4 Method ‣ Think Less, Locally: Local Preference Learning for Reasoning Compression").
First, local overthink detection screens candidate reasoning states
using a two-stage filter in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Step-Level Overthink Detection ‣ 4 Method ‣ Think Less, Locally: Local Preference Learning for Reasoning Compression").
Second, verified continuation generation samples shorter correct
continuations from each detected state in
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Verified Continuation Generation ‣ 4 Method ‣ Think Less, Locally: Local Preference Learning for Reasoning Compression").
Third, local preference learning contrasts the regenerated continuation
with the original continuation from the same state for DPO training in
Section [4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Local Preference Learning ‣ 4 Method ‣ Think Less, Locally: Local Preference Learning for Reasoning Compression").
Using the notation introduced in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Preliminaries ‣ Think Less, Locally: Local Preference Learning for Reasoning Compression"),
this process produces a set of triples
$`\{(P_{i},y_{i}^{+},y_{i}^{-})\}`$, where $`P_{i}`$ denotes the frozen
reasoning state after step $`s_{i}`$, $`y_{i}^{+}`$ denotes a shorter
correct continuation regenerated from $`P_{i}`$, and $`y_{i}^{-}`$
denotes the original continuation that follows $`P_{i}`$. We denote the
reasoning model being trained as $`M`$.

### 4.2 Step-Level Overthink Detection

We identify reasoning states after which the model begins to overthink
using its predictive confidence. Since semantic heuristics can be
unreliable and step-level annotation is costly, we first screen
candidate states with model confidence and then verify them with a
stronger judge model.

#### Confidence Screening.

We feed the complete chain of thought back to $`M`$ and score it under
its own next-token distribution. Let $`S`$ denote the CoT token
sequence, $`S_{<\tau}`$ its prefix preceding position $`\tau`$, and
$`\pi_{M}(\cdot\mid S_{<\tau})`$ the corresponding next-token
distribution. For each position, we record

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       p_{\tau}^{\max}=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{M}(v\mid S_{<\tau}),
       ```                                                              |     | (2) |

and let $`\mathcal{T}_{i}`$ denote the token positions in step
$`s_{i}`$, with steps delimited by paragraph breaks. The step confidence
is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
c_{i}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{i}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}p_{\tau}^{\max}.
``` |  | (3) |

We measure confidence volatility over a sliding window of recent steps:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v_{i}=\frac{1}{|W_{i}|}\sum_{j\in W_{i}}(c_{j}-\bar{c}_{i})^{2},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`W_{i}`$ contains the most recent steps ending at $`i`$. We use
the minimal window $`|W_{i}|=2`$, giving

|     |                                      |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       v_{i}=\frac{(c_{i}-c_{i-1})^{2}}{4},
       ```                                   |     | (5) |

with $`v_{0}=0`$. Thus, $`c_{i}`$ measures the model’s average
predictive confidence within step $`s_{i}`$, while $`v_{i}`$ captures
its change across consecutive steps. Averaging over tokens makes the
signal less sensitive to isolated low-probability tokens caused by
punctuation or formatting.

#### Overthink Detection.

We normalize both signals using the model’s own behavior. Let $`q_{25}`$
and $`q_{75}`$ be the 25th and 75th percentiles of step confidence, and
$`q^{v}_{25}`$ and $`q^{v}_{75}`$ the corresponding percentiles of
volatility, computed once from CoT traces self-generated by $`M`$.
Following the regime analysis of [Li et al. (2026)](#bib.bib7), we
identify overthinking states as those whose most recent completed step
has simultaneously low confidence and high volatility:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{O}=\left\{i:\ c_{i}\leq q_{25}\ \wedge\ v_{i}\geq q^{v}_{75}\right\}.
``` |  | (6) |

The conjunction of the two signals distinguishes overthinking from
ordinary uncertainty or state transitions. Low confidence can arise from
a genuinely difficult sub-problem, while high volatility can accompany a
legitimate change in reasoning. Their combination instead identifies
states where the model is both uncertain and unstable. The detector is
causal by construction: both $`c_{i}`$ and $`v_{i}`$ are computed from
information available by the end of step $`s_{i}`$, whereas truncation
begins only from $`s_{i+1}`$. Hence, the detector never uses the
continuation it removes.

#### Judge Re-checking.

Confidence alone cannot reliably distinguish redundant deliberation from
genuine difficulty. We therefore re-examine each flagged state with a
stronger judge model $`J`$. The two stages use different evidence. The
screen identifies candidates solely from the confidence statistics
$`(c_{i},v_{i})`$, while the judge reads the current reasoning state and
determines whether the next step is redundant. The next step is
classified as overthink if it re-deliberates on information that has
already been established, normal if it makes substantive progress, or
final if it presents the answer. This distinction matters because
truncation from $`P_{i}`$ should remove only redundant continuation.
Removing a normal step discards useful progress, while removing a final
step may discard the answer itself. This case is particularly relevant
because reasoning models often conclude their CoT by restating or boxing
the final answer, which may appear repetitive but is necessary for
answer extraction.

For each $`i\in\mathcal{O}`$, the judge receives the problem $`q`$, the
frozen state $`P_{i}`$, the next step $`s_{i+1}`$, and the screen
prediction $`\hat{\ell}_{i}`$. It returns a corrected role and a
rationale:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\ell_{i}=J\bigl(q,\;P_{i},\;s_{i+1},\;\hat{\ell}_{i}\bigr)\in\mathcal{L},\qquad\mathcal{L}=\bigl\{\textsc{overthink},\textsc{normal},\textsc{final}\bigr\},
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\hat{\ell}_{i}=\textsc{overthink}`$ for all screened candidates.
We retain only the states whose next step is confirmed as redundant by
the judge:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{O}\leftarrow\bigl\{i\in\mathcal{O}:\ell_{i}=\textsc{overthink}\bigr\}.
``` |  | (8) |

### 4.3 Verified Continuation Generation

For each $`i\in\mathcal{O}`$, we freeze the reasoning state $`P_{i}`$
and sample $`k`$ independent continuations from $`M`$ conditioned on
that state:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
y_{i}^{(1)},\ldots,y_{i}^{(k)}\sim\pi_{M}\bigl(\,\cdot\mid P_{i}\,\bigr),
``` |  | (9) |

A continuation is considered valid if it completes the reasoning
template and produces an answer symbolically equivalent to the gold
answer $`z`$, denoted by $`y\models z`$. Among all valid continuations,
we select the shortest one:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{V}_{i}=\bigl\{j:y_{i}^{(j)}\models z\bigr\},\qquad y_{i}^{+}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{i}}\bigl|y_{i}^{(j)}\bigr|,
``` |  | (10) |

where $`|\cdot|`$ denotes the number of tokens. If $`\mathcal{V}_{i}`$
is empty, we discard the detection point. Otherwise, we define the
original continuation from the same state as
$`y_{i}^{-}=(s_{i+1},s_{i+2},\ldots)`$ and retain the point only when
the selected continuation is strictly shorter:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{O}\leftarrow\bigl\{i\in\mathcal{O}:|y_{i}^{+}|<|y_{i}^{-}|\bigr\}.
``` |  | (11) |

### 4.4 Local Preference Learning

For each surviving detection point $`i\in\mathcal{O}`$, we construct the
preference triple $`(P_{i},\;y_{i}^{+},\;y_{i}^{-})`$: the two
continuations complete the identical frozen state $`P_{i}`$, the chosen
$`y_{i}^{+}`$ is the shortest verified continuation, and the rejected
$`y_{i}^{-}`$ is the original continuation. Writing
$`g_{i}=|y_{i}^{-}|-|y_{i}^{+}|`$ for the token savings of the pair, we
retain a pair only when the savings reach a minimum $`\delta`$, the
prompt fits within $`c_{p}`$ tokens, and each continuation fits within
$`c_{r}`$ tokens. Since larger savings indicate clearer compression
signals, at most $`K`$ pairs are kept per question, preventing questions
with many detection points from dominating the training data:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{\,\bigl(P_{i},\;y_{i}^{+},\;y_{i}^{-}\bigr)\>:\>i\in\mathcal{S}\,\bigr\},\qquad\mathcal{S}=\bigl\{\,i\in\mathcal{O}:\;g_{i}\geq\delta,\;\bigl|P_{i}\bigr|\leq c_{p},\;\bigl|y_{i}^{\pm}\bigr|\leq c_{r}\,\bigr\},
``` |  | (12) |

where $`\mathcal{S}`$ is truncated within each question to its $`K`$
largest-$`g_{i}`$ elements.

The shared prefix provides a natural state-conditional preference
objective. With $`x=P_{i}`$, we optimize the DPO objective

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,\,y^{+},\,y^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\left[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{+}|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}|x)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y^{-}|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}|x)}\right]\right)\right],
``` |  | (13) |

where $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is the frozen reference policy, and each
log-likelihood is computed as the sum of token log-probabilities over
the corresponding continuation,
$`\log\pi(y|x)=\sum_{t}\log\pi\bigl(y_{t}\,\big|\,x,\,y_{<t}\bigr)`$.
Since both branches share the same prefix $`P_{i}`$, the preference
comparison is conditioned on the identical reasoning state and directly
supervises which continuation the model should prefer after reaching
that state. In this way, LoCo learns a local continue-or-conclude
preference: once the model has reached state $`P_{i}`$, it learns when
to conclude with a shorter valid continuation instead of continuing with
the original redundant reasoning.

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Data Construction and Training Configurations.

The preference data are constructed from self-generated traces of
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B on the math-only training set used by
 [Zhang et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib18). For each query, we sample three
rollouts with temperature 0.6 and top-$`p`$ 0.95, and use the shortest
correct rollout as the original trajectory. We then re-examine each
flagged reasoning step with Qwen3-30B as the judge. For each detected
step, we regenerate $`k=8`$ continuations using the same temperature and
top-$`p`$, and retain preference pairs with at least $`\delta=300`$
tokens of savings, while limiting each question to at most $`K=3`$
pairs. The prompt and continuation lengths are capped at $`c_{p}=6`$k
and $`c_{r}=12`$k tokens, respectively.

We train DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B with LoCo for two epochs using a
LoRA adapter. The adapter has rank 256, scaling factor $`\alpha=512`$,
and dropout 0.05 on the q/k/v/o attention projections, while the base
model remains frozen. We use an effective batch size of 64 and a
learning rate of $`1\times 10^{-5}`$, with a cosine schedule and a
warmup ratio of 0.1. Optimization uses AdamW with a weight decay of 0.01
and gradient clipping of 1.0. The DPO temperature is set to
$`\beta=0.1`$, with the base model serving as the reference policy.
Training is conducted on four H20 GPUs with a maximum sequence length of
14,336 tokens.

#### Evaluation.

We evaluate LoCo on mathematical reasoning and code generation. For
mathematical reasoning, we use five benchmarks spanning different
difficulty levels: MATH-500 ([Lightman et al., 2024](#bib.bib23)) with
500 problems, AMC 2023 with 40 problems, Olympiad ([He et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24)) with 675 problems, AIME 2024 with 30 problems, and
AIME 2025 with 30 problems. For code generation, we use MBPP ([Austin et
al., 2021](#bib.bib25)) sanitized with 257 problems,
LiveCodeBench ([Jain et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)) release v5 with 167
problems, and CodeContests ([Li et al., 2022](#bib.bib27)) with 165
problems. We sample eight completions per problem for all benchmarks.

Evaluation is performed with vLLM using the LoRA adapter and batched
generation, with temperature 0.6 and top-$`p`$ 0.95. We allow up to
32,768 newly generated tokens with a context length of 36,864, and
generate until EOS without truncating at \</think\>. For mathematical
reasoning, we extract the final answer from the last \boxed{} expression
in the complete model output, and check it for symbolic equivalence
using math_verify. For code generation, we extract the program from the
last Python code block and evaluate it by sandboxed execution, following
the official protocols for LiveCodeBench and MBPP and the AlphaCode
judging protocol for CodeContests with a numerical tolerance of
$`10^{-5}`$. We report Accuracy, Tokens, and AUC following [Zhang et al.
(2026b)](#bib.bib18), where AUC denotes the area under the accuracy
versus token budget curve obtained by varying the maximum allowed
reasoning length. All baselines ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Shen et
al., 2025](#bib.bib22); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib18)) are evaluated
from their released full weights without modification.

### 5.2 Evaluation on Mathematical Reasoning Benchmarks

We first evaluate LoCo on mathematical reasoning benchmarks that span a
wide difficulty range, from MATH-500 and AMC23 to Olympiad, AIME24, and
AIME25. This setting tests whether the method can compress reasoning
aggressively without sacrificing performance as problem difficulty
increases.

| Method | MATH-500 |  |  | AMC23 |  |  | Olympiad |  |  |

#### Local versus global preferences.

In the Global ablation setting, we keep the same query set,
pair-selection thresholds, and training setting as LoCo, but remove the
state structure entirely. For each query, we form a trajectory-level
preference pair between the shortest and longest correct full rollouts,
so the prompt contains only the original query rather than a frozen
intermediate state. Compared with this global construction, LoCo
concentrates the supervision signal at the specific state where
compression becomes appropriate. This difference matters most for
compression. Although Global reaches similar accuracy on some
benchmarks, it reduces tokens by only 5.2% on AMC23, 11.1% on AIME24,
and 15.8% on AIME25, far below LoCo’s 41.7%, 45.4%, and 45.2%. These
results suggest that effective reasoning compression depends not only on
preferring shorter reasoning, but also on identifying where compression
should occur.

#### Self-generated versus external continuations.

In the GPT-5.4 Cont. ablation setting, we keep the same cutpoints,
frozen prefixes, rejected continuations, pair-selection thresholds, and
training setting as LoCo, and change only the chosen continuation.
GPT-5.4 regenerates from the same frozen prefix and replaces the
response only when it is both correct and shorter. Compared with this
external-continuation variant, LoCo performs better overall in
compression and AUC, especially on the harder AIME benchmarks. A
plausible explanation is distributional alignment. The preference pairs
in LoCo are built from continuations generated by the same model being
trained, so the supervision remains closer to the model’s native
reasoning distribution. In contrast, external continuations provide
useful signals, but they are less matched to the model’s own CoT style
and therefore offer a weaker training signal for learning when to stop.

#### Detector-selected versus heuristic cutpoints.

In the RandCut and UniCut ablations, we keep the regeneration process,
pair-selection thresholds, and training configuration identical to LoCo,
and only change how the cutpoints are selected. Each trace is assigned
the same number of cutpoints as in LoCo. Specifically, RandCut selects
cutpoints uniformly at random from the trajectory, while UniCut selects
them at evenly spaced positions. The shorter continuations are then
regenerated and paired following the same procedure as LoCo. This
comparison isolates the effect of cutpoint selection and evaluates
whether identifying appropriate compression points is important for
effective reasoning compression. Both variants can still shorten CoT
substantially, but they incur larger accuracy losses because they often
compress reasoning at positions where the model still needs additional
computation. This effect is especially clear on AIME24 and AIME25, where
LoCo preserves the strongest accuracy while matching or exceeding the
heuristic variants in overall AUC. These comparisons show that LoCo’s
advantage comes not only from preferring shorter continuations, but from
learning that preference at the right reasoning states.

## 6 Conclusion

We introduced LoCo, a local preference learning method for reasoning
compression that constructs supervision directly at reasoning states
where overthinking occurs. Instead of treating compression as a global
preference over complete trajectories, LoCo freezes the current
reasoning prefix, regenerates verified shorter continuations from the
same state, and learns state-conditioned continue-or-conclude
preferences with DPO. Empirically, LoCo shows consistent gains across
both mathematical reasoning and code generation. The cross-domain
improvements suggest that LoCo primarily reshapes the model’s preference
over reasoning length rather than adding task-specific knowledge. This
indicates that effective reasoning compression requires learning when
further reasoning is unnecessary at the appropriate reasoning states,
rather than simply favoring shorter trajectories.

## Appendix A Implementation Details

### A.1 Overthink Detection

#### Source traces.

We generate self-sampled reasoning traces from
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B on the math14k training pool, the math-only
training set of [Zhang et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib18) containing 14,027
questions. We sample three rollouts per question at temperature 0.6 and
top-$`p`$ 0.95, and retain the shortest correct rollout as the source
trajectory for each question. Detection, judging, and pair construction
all operate on this trajectory, so each question contributes at most one
trace.

#### Confidence screening.

The complete CoT is fed back to the model as a prompt, and we record the
top-1 token probability $`p_{\tau}^{\max}`$ at every position via
batched offline vLLM prompt logprobs. Steps are delimited by paragraph
breaks. Step confidence $`c_{i}`$ is the arithmetic mean of
$`p_{\tau}^{\max}`$ over the step’s token span, and volatility is
$`v_{i}=(c_{i}-c_{i-1})^{2}/4`$ as in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Step-Level Overthink Detection ‣ 4 Method ‣ Think Less, Locally: Local Preference Learning for Reasoning Compression").
The four quantiles $`(q_{25c},q_{75c},q_{25v},q_{75v})`$ are calibrated
once on the model’s self-generated trace corpus.

#### Judge re-checking.

Every confidence-screened Overthink step is re-checked by Qwen3-30B
using vLLM. The judge sees the problem, the preceding steps, the
candidate step, and the predicted label, and returns a corrected label
plus a short rationale in JSON. The template is:

``` ltx_verbatim
You are evaluating whether an existing overthink label
for one reasoning step is correct.

Problem:
{question}

Previous reasoning steps:
{previous_steps}

Current reasoning step:
{current_step}

Predicted label:
{predicted_label}

Label definitions:
- OVERTHINK: this step is wasteful or redundant;
  removing it would not break the path to the answer.
- NORMAL: this step is necessary reasoning;
  removing it would break or weaken the path.
- FINAL_ANSWER_WRAPUP: this step mainly wraps up
  or restates the final answer.

Respond in this exact JSON format and nothing else:
{
  "gold_label": "OVERTHINK|NORMAL|FINAL_ANSWER_WRAPUP",
  "reason": "short reason"
}
````

Only judge-confirmed overthink steps survive. A trace with many flagged
steps could otherwise dominate regeneration, so each trace keeps at most
10 candidate states, selected by uniform sampling.

### A.2 Evaluation Detail

#### Evaluation prompts.

Each benchmark appends a short instruction to the raw problem text, and
the result is wrapped by each model’s native chat template. The five
mathematical benchmarks share the instruction

> Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}.

The three code benchmarks share the following instruction:

> Please write a solution in Python. Wrap your final code in a
> \`\`\`python code block.

Following their official protocols, MBPP additionally includes the
public tests in the prompt (Your code should pass these tests: tests),
while LiveCodeBench appends the provided starter code when applicable.
We extract the last Python code block from the complete output, which
naturally excludes any code blocks generated within the thinking
process, and execute it in a sandbox with per-problem timeouts of 10
seconds for MBPP and 6 seconds for LiveCodeBench. CodeContests follows
the AlphaCode judging protocol, where outputs are split by whitespace
and compared token-wise case-insensitively, with exact matching for
integers and a tolerance of $`10^{-5}`$ for floating-point values.

### A.3 Ablation Construction

This subsection provides the full descriptions of the ablation variants
introduced in the main text. All variants use the same question set,
pair-selection thresholds, and training recipe as LoCo. Each variant
modifies only one construction choice, while differences in pair counts
arise naturally from the resulting construction.

#### Global.

The Global variant pairs the shortest and longest correct rollouts of
the same question. To avoid disadvantaging this variant, its pool is
expanded from three source rollouts to eight per question, allowing the
selected responses to draw from a larger sample of short solutions than
the source traces provide. The same savings threshold and top-$`K`$
selection as LoCo are applied, and the prompt contains only the
question, without a frozen state.

#### GPT-5.4 Cont.

GPT-5.4 continues from the same frozen prefix, provided as an assistant
prefill with a system instruction to continue the reasoning in the same
first-person style without restarting the problem. The continuation is
generated directly from the frozen prefix and replaces the
self-generated chosen continuation only when it is correct and shorter
than the rejected continuation. Thus, this variant differs from LoCo
only on the chosen side.

#### RandCut and UniCut.

Each trace receives the same number of cutpoints as selected by the
detector. Cutpoints are sampled from all eligible steps after excluding
the first step and the final two steps. RandCut samples uniformly at
random. UniCut places the cutpoints on an evenly spaced grid over the
trace and snaps each to the nearest eligible step in its segment.
Downstream regeneration and pair construction remain unchanged. The
detector’s cutpoints concentrate late in the trace, with a mean relative
position of 0.57 and about half occurring in the final 40% of steps. In
contrast, RandCut and UniCut cover the trace more uniformly, with mean
relative positions of 0.47 and 0.50, respectively. Thus, the heuristic
variants tend to compress at earlier, less converged states.

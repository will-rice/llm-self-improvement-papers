###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have shown impressive capabilities on
numerous natural language processing tasks. However, LLMs still struggle
with numerical question answering for financial documents that include
tabular and textual data. Recent works have showed the effectiveness of
critic agents (i.e., self-correction) for this task given oracle labels.
Building upon this framework, this paper examines the effectiveness of
the traditional critic agent when oracle labels are not available, and
show, through experiments, that this critic agent’s performance
deteriorates in this scenario. With this in mind, we present an improved
critic agent, along with the calculator agent which outperforms the
previous state-of-the-art approach (program-of-thought) and is safer.
Furthermore, we investigate how our agents interact with each other, and
how this interaction affects their performance.

## 1 Introduction

Tabular and textual data are ubiquitous in many financial documents. In
this paper, we focus on the numerical reasoning ability of large
language models (LLMs) on financial data that includes tabular and
textual data – this is a challenging task since LLMs are known to
struggle on numerical reasoning for tabular data [Cao et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib13), despite their known natural language capabilities
[Tan et al. (2026)](#bib.bib20). More generally, LLMs have a low
numerical understanding and processsing ability [Yang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib18); [Chen and Lin (2024)](#bib.bib19). Therefore, there
is a need to improve the numerical reasoning ability of LLM approaches
for tabular and textual data. Recently, [Fatemi and Hu
(2024)](#bib.bib4) presented a multi-agent framework which used LLMs for
numerical reasoning (given tabular and textual data) – at the core of
their approach is the use of the critic agent for criticism. Their
approach was shown to significantly outperform the chain-of-thought
(CoT) approach for various sizes of llama3 models, providing us with a
cost-effective alternative to larger LLMs. While the initial results
look promising, we noticed that their results seem to disagree with
another recent work [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib14), which argued that
intrinsic self-correction, which has prompts that are similar to those
of the critic agent, does not improve performance – their experiments
are comprehensive but did not include tabular and textual reasoning.
Furthermore, it is not obvious whether the results from textual data
generalizes to tabular and textual data since the positional relation of
information in tables is different from text [Sui et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib1). We address this gap by critically examining the
effectiveness of the critic agent and show that it does not outperform
the CoT approach. With this in mind, we introduce an improved critic
agent and a calculator agent which helps to boost the performance of
LLMs.

#### Problem setup.

Given table(s), text, and a numerical question, the goal is to provide
an LLM approach (without any fine tuning) that is able to answer the
numerical question with high accuracy.

## 2 Related Work

We first provide a summary on approaches that have been studied for
tabular data alone, and then provide a summary on approaches that have
been studied for both tabular and textual data.

#### Tabular data.

[Sui et al. (2024)](#bib.bib1) studied the reasoning capabilities of
LLMs on tabular data and empirically showed that LLMs have the basic
structural understanding capabilities but are far from perfect. The
potential usefulness of LLMs for tabular data motivated other works to
use LLMs as part of their approach for tabular reasoning. Two major
veins of successful approaches are either to harness external tools like
Python and SQL [Liu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib3); [Zhang et al.
(2024a)](#bib.bib7); [Abhyankar et al. (2024)](#bib.bib8), or to
decompose the tables before answering the question [Wang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib9); [Ji et al. (2024)](#bib.bib10). We refer the
interested reader to this survey [Zhang et al. (2024b)](#bib.bib2).

#### Tabular and textual data.

Compared to tabular data, this area is relatively less explored. TAT-LLM
[Zhu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib11) fine-tuned a smaller LLM (llama 2) for
the purpose of question answering from tabular and textual data. While
promising, this approach faces challenges related to the high
computational costs and memory requirements associated with fine-tuning
LLMs. Next, we look at approaches that require no extra training or
fine-tuning of LLMs.

(1) Program-of-Thought (PoT): [Chen et al. (2023)](#bib.bib16); [Phogat
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib12) This approach involves prompting the LLM
directly for an executable Python code. The code is then run to give the
final answer. (2) Critic: [Fatemi and Hu (2024)](#bib.bib4) A critic
agent is introduced to refine the previous Chain-of-Thought (CoT) answer
[Wei et al. (2022)](#bib.bib17) from the LLM. They showed that using
oracle labels, which are labels that indicate the correctness of the CoT
answer, to guide the critic agent (i.e., only using it when the CoT
answer is wrong), their approach is able to outperform CoT. (3) Domain
Specific Language (DSL): [Phogat et al. (2023)](#bib.bib12) Additional
LLM prompts are used extract the calculation(s) from the first CoT
answer of the LLM, and then present the calculations as a DSL program.
The program is then run to produce the final answer.

The previous state-of-the-art approach for financial document question
answering is PoT. However, the PoT approach is not purely intrinsic
unlike the critic agent (i.e., it relies on external tools). While PoT
is one viable solution to resolve the low numerical processing ability
of LLMs, it might be dangerous to the company since it would require the
execution of a generated program by the LLM (and having employees verify
the generated program leads to latency in the company’s workflow) –
e.g., the code import os; os.rmdir() is dangerous. Therefore, there is a
need for a safer method to improve the LLMs’ numerical processing
ability.

#### Main contributions.

In this paper, we build on the multi-agent framework of [Fatemi and Hu
(2024)](#bib.bib4) to study the effectiveness of the critic agent for
numerical reasoning from tabular and textual data, and also to introduce
the calculator agent. Our main contributions are as follows:

- •
  We show experimentally that the critic agent is not able to outperform
  the CoT approach when oracle labels are not available. Our result
  agrees with the hypothesis of [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib14) and
  generalizes it to the realm of tabular and textual reasoning.
- •
  We adapt the ideas of [Li et al. (2024)](#bib.bib15) to provide an
  improved critic agent that outperforms the previous critic-agent
  approach when oracle labels are not available.
- •
  We introduce a calculator agent and show that in most cases, it
  outperforms the previous state-of-the-art approach (i.e., PoT) for
  question answering from financial documents. We also argue that it is
  a safer approach compared to PoT.

## 3 Methodology

In this section, we extend the multi-agent framework of [Fatemi and Hu
(2024)](#bib.bib4), which includes only the analyst and critic agent, to
include the improved critic agent and the calculator agent – a
potentially useful agent for numerical reasoning. We describe each agent
in detail and explain their interactions.

### 3.1 Analyst Agent

The role of the analyst agent is the following: (i) To provide CoT
answer or Python code to solve the given question, (ii) Acts as an
intermediary between the user and the other agents. Specifically, it
processes outputs returned from other agents (critic, improved critic,
and calculator) before returning the final answer to the user – visual
illustration is provided in Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A More Details on Agents ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering")
(see Figures
[2](#A1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Appendix A More Details on Agents ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering"),
[3](#A1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Appendix A More Details on Agents ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering"),
and
[4](#A1.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Appendix A More Details on Agents ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering")).

### 3.2 Critic Agent and Improved Critic Agent

The critic agent provides critique on an answer provided for a given
question. The critic agent, together with an analyst agent, operates as
follows: (1) One LLM prompt is needed to ask the critic agent to
critique the CoT answer provided. (2) The critique, together with the
previous CoT answer, is then sent back to the analyst agent via another
LLM prompt. (3) The analyst agent processes the input and returns the
final answer to the user.

We use the critic-agent prompts provided by [Fatemi and Hu (2024, Figure 5)](#bib.bib4). Note that the steps above are similar to the 3-step
prompting strategy for self-correction introduced by [Huang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib14) if all the steps use the same LLM. We show later in
our Section
[4](#S4 "4 Experiment Details ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering")
that this critic agent does not outperform the CoT approach. With this
in mind, we introduce an improved critic agent inspired by [Li et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib15) which hypothesized that for question answering from
textual data (but not tabular and textual data together), the LLM has
the ability to gauge its own confidence – this ability was capitalized
by [Li et al. (2024)](#bib.bib15) to show that LLM does indeed have the
intrinsic ability to self-correct. The improved critic agent, together
with the analyst agent, operates as follows: (1) One LLM prompt is
needed to ask the critic agent to review the CoT answer provided and
decide whether to maintain or update its answer. (2) If the critic agent
decides to maintain its answer, then we will output that answer as the
final answer. (3) If the critic agent decides to update its answer, then
we will send both answers to the analyst agent and ask it to check both
answers and the question again, before deciding on the final answer and
producing it as an output.

### 3.3 Calculator Agent

The calculator agent takes in a CoT answer and returns the correct
answer to all the calculations present in the CoT answer. The calculator
agent, together with an analyst agent, operates as follows: (1) One LLM
prompt is needed to ask the calculator agent to extract out all the
equations in the input (i.e., the previous CoT answer). We do this by
few-shot learning, i.e., we give the LLM a few examples of the desired
outcome. (2) Python is then used by the calculator agent to evaluate the
extracted equations correctly. Our extracted equations only contain
numbers, and the symbols ‘+’, ‘-’, ‘\*’, ‘/’, ‘(’, and ‘)’. (3) The
correct calculations are then sent back in another LLM prompt, together
with the previous CoT answer, to the analyst agent to get the final
answer.

This is similar to the DSL approach of [Phogat et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib12), but unlike their approach, we directly use Python
to evaluate the extracted calculations without producing a DSL program –
our extracted calculations are syntactically simpler, relying only on
numbers and a few symbols. Furthermore, [Phogat et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib12) used a zero-shot approach to extract the
calculations, whereas we use a few-shot approach. [Phogat et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib12) showed that their DSL approach does not outperform
PoT, whereas our calculator agent outperforms PoT in most cases (shown
later in Section
[5](#S5 "5 Main Results ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering")).

Regarding step 2, while we have used Python to evaluate the extracted
calculations, we could always swap it out for some calculator tool that
only performs calculations and nothing else – this prevents the risk of
dangerous code being executed.

### 3.4 Interaction between all Agents

The 3 agents stated previously will interact with each other in this
manner: (1) The user sends the context (instructions, table, and text)
and question over to the analyst agent to receive a CoT answer. (2) The
answer is then sent to the critic (or improved critic) agent, and the
critic (or improved critic) agent and analyst agent interacts to produce
a refined answer – see Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Critic Agent and Improved Critic Agent ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering")
for specific steps of the interaction process. (3) The refined answer is
then sent to the calculator agent, and the calculator agent and analyst
agent interacts to produce a more precise answer – see Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Calculator Agent ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering")
for specific steps of the interaction process.

## 4 Experiment Details

We use a weak LLM (llama3-70B), and a strong LLM (GPT4-turbo), with
temperature set to $`0`$ so that we can achieve consistent results. We
use 2 popular financial tabular and textual datasets: TATQA [Zhu et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib5) and FinQA [Chen et al. (2021)](#bib.bib6). We use
accuracy is our evaluation metric. Additonal experiment details are
provided in Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Additional Experiment Details ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering").

performed $`3.83\%`$ better than CoT for FinQA. Our results for TATQA

where llama3-70B and GPT4-turbo are shorten to llama3 and GPT4t,
respectively. For TATQA, we have the following observations:

- •
  For llama3-70B, CoT + cal is the best performer. Interestingly, CoT +
  critic performs worse than CoT implying that the critic agent is not
  useful – intuitively this can be viewed as the agent overthinking and
  we provide an example of this in Appendix
  [D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Example of Critic Agent Overthinking ‣ Appendix D Integer vs. Float Answers ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering")
  where the critic agent makes a suggestion to change the previously
  correct answer from CoT.
- •
  For GPT4-turbo, PoT is the best perform. As expected, CoT + critic
  performs worse than CoT while CoT + i-critic performs better than both
  CoT +critic and CoT.

For FinQA, we have the following observations:

- •
  For llama3-70B, CoT + cal is again the best performer. Here, CoT +
  critic performs slightly better than CoT implying some usefulness of
  the critic agent. Despite the minor usefulness of the critic agent,
  CoT + critic + cal does not outperform CoT + cal.
- •
  For GPT4-turbo, CoT + cal performs the best, and CoT + i-crtic
  outperforms CoT + critic. Interestingly, CoT outperforms most of the
  other approaches except CoT + cal and CoT + i-critic + cal.

On average (see ‘Combined’ column in Table
[1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5 Main Results ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering")),
CoT + cal performs the best when using llama3-70B and CoT + i-critic +
cal performs the best when using GPT4-turbo. This suggests that the
calculator agent is very useful for numerical tabular and textual
reasoning, while the critic agent is not particularly useful.
Furthermore, on average, CoT + cal outperforms PoT, which is the
previous state-of-the-art approach for financial document question
answering.

#### Remark.

Despite using the same LLM model (llama3-70B) and datasets as [Fatemi
and Hu (2024)](#bib.bib4), our experiment results differ significantly.
This is because when comparing CoT + critic with CoT, [Fatemi and Hu
(2024)](#bib.bib4) only ran CoT + critic on the questions that CoT got
wrong (this is stated in the last paragraph of Section 4.1 in their
paper) – their approach has access to oracle labels, which helps in
deciding whether to use the critic agent. This is different from the way
we evaluate the different approaches, where we run every single approach
on the entire dataset. Further comparisons of the critic agents are
provided in Appendix
[C](#A3 "Appendix C Comparison of Critic Agents ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering"),
and comparison of performance on integer vs. float answers are provided
in Appendix
[D](#A4 "Appendix D Integer vs. Float Answers ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering").

## 6 Conclusion

We provide empirical validation that the traditional critic agent does
not outperform CoT when oracle labels are not available. With
improvement in mind, we present an improved critic agent and a
calculator agent, and show empirically that they can improve the LLM’s
ability in financial question answering safely.

## 7 Limitations

In this work, we considered two large language models llama3-70B and
GPT4-turbo – a weaker LLM and a stronger LLM. It is important for us to
choose the same model (i.e., llama3-70B) that was used in the previous
work [Fatemi and Hu (2024)](#bib.bib4) which showed the superiority of
the critic agent. We acknowledge that we did not exhaustively evaluate a
large selection of large language models, but believe that our choices
should be sufficient.

Regarding the choice of dataset, we acknowledge that both our datasets
comes from the financial domain, which is the scope of this paper. While
the domain scope of our dataset might be slightly narrow, we believe
that our conclusions should apply to other types of dataset since our
key focus was on the ability of our approaches to answer numerical
questions with regards to tabular and textual data; our agents do not
leverage on any aspects of the financial domain (e.g., we did not use a
financial expert agent, or exploit any financial knowledge in our
framework).

## Appendix B Additional Experiment Details

We point out that the different agents (introduced in Section
[3](#S3 "3 Methodology ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering"))
are built by prompting one LLM to carry out different tasks – the same
LLM is used for the entire approach. In other words, although we are
making multiple calls to the same LLM, we apply the design abstraction
of using multiple agents.

#### Dataset preparation.

We apply our approach to the following popular financial tabular and
textual datasets:

- •
  TATQA [Zhu et al. (2021)](#bib.bib5): We use the dev dataset since it
  has answers to all the questions, along with the type of question
  (i.e., numerical or non-numerical). We filter out the numerical
  questions from the dataset, which is a total of 717 questions.
- •
  FinQA [Chen et al. (2021)](#bib.bib6): All questions are numerical. We
  use the dev set since it contains all the answers to the questions – a
  total of 883 questions.

Questions where any of our approaches exceed the context length are
omitted.

#### Evaluation Metric.

We denote the true answer as $`a`$ and the estimated answer as
$`\hat{a}`$. For the evaluation metric, we use accuracy defined as

|     |                                                                                |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle 100\%\times\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\{a=\hat{a}\},`$ |     |

where $`n`$ is the total number of questions answered, and
$`\mathds{1}\{\cdot\}`$ is the indicator function where
$`\mathds{1}\{a=\hat{a}\}=1`$ if $`a=\hat{a}`$ and $`0`$ otherwise. We
consider the estimated answer $`\hat{a}`$ to be equivalent to the true
answer $`a`$ if $`\hat{a}`$ and $`a`$ are exactly the same, or if
$`\hat{a}`$ can be rounded to obtain $`a`$. For example, if $`a=0.98`$,
then $`\hat{a}=0.98`$ and $`\hat{a}=0.979`$ are both accepted.

#### Change analysis in self-criticism.

We proceed to dive deeper into the differences between critic and
i-critic agents under the no-oracle setting. We investigate the types of
changes made by both agents when the correctness of the answer is
flipped after the critique step. We classify the changes into two types:

- •
  Minor change. When the agent makes a change to its numerical answer
  but the steps to getting the numerical answer stay exactly the same.
  This usually results in a minor change in the final numerical answer.
- •
  Major change. When the agent changes the previous steps used to get
  the numerical answer (e.g., different data is pulled from the table
  for calculations). This usually results in a major change in the final
  numerical answer.

We study these changes when a correctness flip occurs: The answer
changes from correct (before criticism) to wrong (after criticism), or
wrong (before criticism) to correct (after criticism). The results are
displayed in Table
[3](#A3.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Change analysis in self-criticism. ‣ Appendix C Comparison of Critic Agents ‣ Improved LLM Agents for Financial Document Question Answering").
We observe that for all cases (with the exception of FinQA, GPT-4t), the
wrong to correct flips have a higher percentage of minor changes
compared to the correct to wrong flips, which have a higher percentage
of major changes. This might hint that critic agents in general are
better at making small adjustments to their previous answers to get the
correct answer, rather than completely changing their thought process.

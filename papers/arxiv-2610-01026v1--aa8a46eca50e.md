# It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows

Xuehang Guo, Haoyu Wang, Haifeng Chen, Yangyi Chen, Zhenhailong Wang,

###### Abstract

Tackling complex real-world tasks can exceed the capabilities of a
single large language model (LLM), motivating the use of multi-agent
workflows that coordinate specialized agents to work together on these
tasks. Recent methods train LLMs to construct better workflows from
execution outcomes, but they optimize only the workflow generator, while
the other agents that build or execute each workflow remain fixed even
though every outcome depends on all of them. However, extending training
beyond the generator is challenging: the agents are coupled, and a
workflow’s outcome is a single sparse score that cannot tell which agent
causes a failure. We propose FloWright, which leverages the workflow as
a harness to optimize workflows. By introducing a hierarchical,
structure-aware reward paradigm, FloWright enables one role to
self-evolve and two or more roles to co-evolve, with no additional
models, labels, or executions. Considering the limitation that workflows
are commonly trained and evaluated on data that a single agent can
already handle, we further propose DataWright, an adaptive data
hardening approach that converts existing datasets into workflow-level
tasks with increased difficulty. Across document, slide, chart, code,
math, and finance tasks, small open models trained with FloWright
achieve improved performance by up to $`+7.41\%`$, with co-evolving
($`+5.03\%`$) more roles gaining more than optimizing one of them alone
($`+2.83\%`$). Our project page:
[https://xhguo7.github.io/FloWright/](https://xhguo7.github.io/FloWright/).

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly capable of leveraging
tools and specialized skills to solve tasks ([Zhang et al.,
2025e](#bib.bib2); [Qian et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Wang et al.,
2025c](#bib.bib3); [Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib4)). However, tackling
complex real-world problems can go beyond the capability of a single
LLM, as it demands gathering evidence across heterogeneous inputs,
reasoning over long contexts and diverse sources, and accumulating
intermediate results over many steps ([Yu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib5);
[Zhu et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)). To address such tasks, LLMs construct
multi-agent workflows that break complex tasks into subtasks and
coordinate specialized agents ([Zhang et al., 2025d](#bib.bib30); [Hu et
al., 2025b](#bib.bib29); [Niu et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)).

Yet constructing an effective workflow for a complex task remains
challenging, and many multi-agent frameworks still rely on substantial
manual design ([Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib13); [Li et al.,
2025](#bib.bib14); [Agashe et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). To reduce this
manual effort, recent works optimize LLMs to construct better workflows
by training them against the outcomes of executing those workflows ([Nie
et al., 2025](#bib.bib11); [Nielsen et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)). However,
these methods typically optimize a single workflow generator, while the
agents that execute the workflow, and any other agents that help build
it, remain fixed
(§[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Workflow Construction and Optimization ‣ Appendix A Extended Discussion of Related Work ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Since the outcome of a workflow depends jointly on every agent that
builds or executes it, training only the generator leaves other agents
shaping every outcome without ever learning from it, significantly
limiting how much the workflow as a whole can improve.

Extending training beyond the generator introduces two additional
challenges. First, agents in a workflow are interdependent: one agent’s
output can become another’s input, and overall performance depends on
their joint behavior Existing multi-agent training either trains each
agent separately ([Guo et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Motwani et al.,
2025](#bib.bib45)) or co-trains agents with distinct roles only within a
fixed pairing or a hand-designed system ([Park et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib17); [Huang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18); [Wan et al., 2025](#bib.bib19))
(§[A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Multi-Agent Training and Co-Evolution ‣ Appendix A Extended Discussion of Related Work ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Training agents separately thus ignores how each agent’s behavior shapes
what the others learn, while co-training remains confined to a single
role or a predefined agent structure rather than the workflows
constructed for each task. Second, the outcome of a workflow is usually
a single score for the whole workflow. It is sparse whenever the
workflow falls short, and cannot tell which agent causes a failure.
Methods that do attribute failures to specific agents or steps, however,
rely heavily on learned critics or reward models, LLM judges, human
annotation, or repeated executions ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20);
[Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib21); [Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib22))
(§[A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Credit Assignment and Failure Attribution ‣ Appendix A Extended Discussion of Related Work ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Such attribution adds costly models, labels, or executions beyond the
workflow run itself, and it can still misattribute where a failure
originates.

Beyond how workflows are built and trained, the data used to optimize
and evaluate them pose a further challenge. Multi-agent workflows are
commonly trained and evaluated on datasets such as single-question math,
function-level coding, and short question answering, which a single
agent can already handle ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib23))
(§[A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Evaluation of Multi-Agent Workflows ‣ Appendix A Extended Discussion of Related Work ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
On such data, re-evaluations find that the gains of multi-agent systems
over a strong single agent are often small or absent ([Cemri et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24); [Choi et al., 2025](#bib.bib25); [Zhu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib26))
(§[C](#A3 "Appendix C Preliminary Study: The Gap in Existing Benchmarks ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
These datasets can therefore neither reveal nor optimize what a workflow
adds beyond a single agent.

Motivated by these limitations, we propose FloWright
(§[3](#S3 "3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
which leverages the workflow as a harness
(§[2](#S2 "2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
to optimize any role
(§[3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Maximize the Harness: Train-Time RL, Test-Time Distillation ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Besides letting each role self-evolve, FloWright co-evolves multiple
roles inside the harness
(§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
and stages the grade into a hierarchical, structure-aware reward
(§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Harness the Workflow: A Hierarchical Reward ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
with role-level credit localization
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Credit the Structure: Localizing Credits to Roles ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Beyond workflow construction and optimization, FloWright introduces a
data adaptation algorithm
(Alg. [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ D.1 Hardening Strategies ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
that hardens existing datasets for workflow-level training and
evaluation
(§[D](#A4 "Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

## 2 Preliminaries

### 2.1 Workflow as Harness

A complex task $`x\sim\mathcal{X}`$ is solved by a workflow, a directed
graph $`G=(V,E)`$ whose nodes $`V`$ are agents, tools, or skills, and
whose edges $`E`$ carry the data and control flow among them. A harness
$`\mathcal{H}`$ grounds a workflow $`G`$ into verifiable measurement
through execution and grading: it runs $`G`$ on $`x`$, returns the
answer $`y`$, and scores it as $`h(G,x)\in[0,1]`$. A Generator
$`\pi_{g}`$ produces $`G`$ for $`x`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
x\;\xrightarrow{\;\pi_{g}\;}\;G\;\xrightarrow{\;\mathcal{H}\;}\;\big(y,\;h(G,x)\big),\quad h(G,x)\in[0,1]
``` |  | (1) |

where $`h`$ serves three purposes: the evaluation metric, the training
signal, and the test-time objective. A role $`\rho\in\mathcal{R}`$
(Tab. [7](#A6.T7 "Table 7 ‣ F.3 Notation ‣ Appendix F Implementation Details ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
is any component of the upstream-downstream framework
(Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.3 What the Nodes Are Made of ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
whose policy $`\pi_{\rho}`$, with parameters $`\theta_{\rho}`$, is
optimized against that signal.

### 2.2 Upstream Builds, Downstream Runs

FloWright factors into an upstream and a downstream. Upstream, the
Generator $`\pi_{g}`$ produces $`G`$. As shown in
Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.3 What the Nodes Are Made of ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"),
upstream workflow generation can take on different structures to
construct a better $`G`$. Downstream, the harness $`\mathcal{H}`$
executes and grades $`G`$ to return $`\big(y,\,h(G,x)\big)`$. The
workflow is the interface between them: what the upstream constructs is
what the downstream executes
(Eq. [1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Workflow as Harness ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

### 2.3 What the Nodes Are Made of

A workflow is built from a shared pool $`\Omega`$ of reusable agents,
tools, and skills. Each node of $`G`$ is an agent, a tool, or a skill
from $`\Omega`$, so $`\Omega`$ delimits the space of workflows the
Generator can produce.

## 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows

For a task $`x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}`$, upstream produces a workflow
$`G\in\mathcal{G}`$
(§[2](#S2 "2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"),
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Wire the Workflow: Topology & Pool Dynamics ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
FloWright
(Fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
grounds it into a measurable optimization signal $`h(G,x_{i})`$ through
a principled harness with both local crediting
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Credit the Structure: Localizing Credits to Roles ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
and a global hierarchy
(§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Harness the Workflow: A Hierarchical Reward ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
By learning to optimize this signal, FloWright enables LLMs to solve
complex tasks by building better workflows, through self-evolution and
co-evolution
(§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
at both train and test time
(§[3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Maximize the Harness: Train-Time RL, Test-Time Distillation ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

### 3.1 Wire the Workflow: Topology & Pool Dynamics

Upstream construction turns on three choices: the topology of the
workflow $`G`$, and the granularity and dynamics of the pool $`\Omega`$
it draws from.

Topology is a non-linear control-flow graph. Beyond passing progress
forward in a chain, $`\pi_{g}`$ runs nodes in parallel when subtasks are
independent, forks alternative branches that are later reconciled, and
repeats a step until a stopping condition is met. A single workflow can
thus express sequential, parallel, branching, and iterative structure as
the task demands.

Pool is presented to the upstream at a chosen granularity
(Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.3 What the Nodes Are Made of ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
A fine, modular granularity keeps agents, tools, and skills as separate
units to enable flexible selection and composition:
$`\Omega=\Omega_{\textit{agent}}\cup\Omega_{\textit{tool}}\cup\Omega_{\textit{skill}}`$.
A coarse granularity bundles each agent with necessary tools and skills
into a self-contained capsule, leading to
$`\Omega=\Omega_{\textit{capsule}}`$.

Pool is dynamic. When a task calls for a capability that $`\Omega`$ does
not contain, the pool is expanded with the missing component, a new
tool, skill, or agent. The upstream thus grows its own components during
construction in addition to drawing directly from the pool
(Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.3 What the Nodes Are Made of ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Holding the pool static disables this growth, a setting we ablate in our
experiments
(§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Ablate the Design: Algorithm, Reward, Pool, and Topology ‣ 5 Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

### 3.2 Credit the Structure: Localizing Credits to Roles

For the $`j`$-th workflow $`G_{j}=(V_{j},E_{j})`$ executed on $`x_{i}`$,
the optimization signal $`h(G_{j},x_{i})`$ is a single scalar, but the
action it measures spans an entire graph. By harnessing the harness,
i.e., leveraging the execution trace $`\tau_{j}`$ produced by
$`\mathcal{H}`$, we localize it to the roles responsible. Credit
localization therefore requires no additional execution and no new
signal.

Credit charges each role for the failures of its own nodes. A role
$`\rho`$ authors a subset $`V_{j}^{\rho}\subseteq V_{j}`$ of the
workflow’s nodes
(Fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
e.g., Generator authors every node it designs and wires, Inventor
authors only the nodes that use a component it creates, and Downstream
only the agent nodes it runs. From $`\tau_{j}`$ we recover
$`F_{j}^{\rho}\subseteq V_{j}^{\rho}`$, the role’s own nodes that
root-cause a failure, and charge the role in proportion to the fraction
of its footprint at fault:

|     |                                                              |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       c_{j}^{\rho}=-\frac{|F_{j}^{\rho}|}{|V_{j}^{\rho}|}\in[-1,0]
       ```                                                           |     | (2) |

If $`F_{j}^{\rho}=\varnothing`$, e.g., when no node fails, then
$`c_{j}^{\rho}=0`$; otherwise the term is negative, steering the policy
away from failures
(§[E.1](#A5.SS1 "E.1 Credit the Structure: Traced Credit, Learnable Co-Evolution ‣ Appendix E FloWright: Inside the Learning ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

### 3.3 Harness the Workflow: A Hierarchical Reward

The harness does not reduce an execution to a single pass-or-fail
outcome. Instead, it stages the optimization signal into a principled
hierarchy of auto-verified terms, so the policy sees a meaningful
gradient even when the workflow falls short. As such, we propose our
hierarchical reward as a generic paradigm: one shared harness from which
every optimizable role adaptively instantiates its own reward.

Optimization signal is a staged ladder. Reward terms accumulate from the
role’s own output to the task outcome: whether the role’s output
$`o_{j}^{\rho}`$ parses ($`f`$), whether the role’s contribution is
valid ($`v^{\rho}`$), whether the workflow executes to a final answer
($`e`$), and how correct that answer is ($`a`$). The local credit of
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Credit the Structure: Localizing Credits to Roles ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
enters as a fifth, structure-aware term, extending the ladder across
local and global. As such, the signal for role $`\rho`$ is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h_{j}^{\rho}=h^{\rho}(G_{j},x_{i})=w_{f}\,f+w_{v}\,v^{\rho}+w_{e}\,e+w_{a}\,a+w_{c}\,c_{j}^{\rho}
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\{w_{f},w_{v},w_{e},w_{a},w_{c}\}`$ are weighting coefficients
configured per role
(§[F.2](#A6.SS2 "F.2 Experiment Configuration ‣ Appendix F Implementation Details ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Our hierarchical ladder thus composes one optimization signal across two
axes: from the role’s own output to the task outcome, the staged four
terms measure how far a workflow reaches; from local to global, the
credit term
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Credit the Structure: Localizing Credits to Roles ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
localizes failures to roles at fault. FloWright aims to maximize this
signal at train and test time
(§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"),
[3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Maximize the Harness: Train-Time RL, Test-Time Distillation ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

### 3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum

Our reward paradigm serves every role from one shared harness, enabling
one role to self-evolve or several to co-evolve.

Any single role self-evolves inside the harness. To train a role, we
optimize its role-specific reward $`h^{\rho}`$ inside the harness as it
runs, so the role evolves in the same flow that will use it
(§[4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")a).
The same procedure fits any role, since all roles share one harness.

Several roles co-evolve inside the harness through a mutual curriculum.
When several roles train on one substrate inside one harness, each
role’s behavior shapes what the others encounter. Improving one role
therefore reshapes the experience the rest learn from. The co-evolving
roles can thus share one policy or each keep their own, and in either
case each role learns against the latest evolved policies of the others.
For example, co-evolution can pair an agent with its own skill
(§[4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")b),
couple an upstream agent that builds the workflow with a downstream
agent that executes it
(§[4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")c),
or co-train any two or more agents
(§[4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")d).
In each case, the coupling is a mutual curriculum: each role shapes the
training distribution the others learn from. The structure-aware credit
of
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Credit the Structure: Localizing Credits to Roles ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
charges each role only for the failures of its own nodes in the shared
execution, which separates the roles’ credit wherever their footprints
differ.

![Refer to caption](2610.01026v1/flowright_main.png)

Figure 2: FloWright for reinforcement learning via self-evolving
harness. $`\pi_{\rho}`$ is the policy of a role $`\rho\in\mathcal{R}`$.
For the $`j`$-th of $`m`$ rollouts of a role being optimized,
$`G_{j}\in\mathcal{G}`$ is the workflow that rollout is graded on, and
the $`m`$ rollouts span $`M`$ distinct workflows
(Tab. [7](#A6.T7 "Table 7 ‣ F.3 Notation ‣ Appendix F Implementation Details ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Workflow generation and execution form a coupled on-policy learning
loop: upstream proposes workflows, downstream executes them, and the
verifiable harness turns execution outcomes into learning signals for
the policies being optimized. Policy updates produce the next generation
of workflows and executions, enabling self-evolution by harnessing the
system’s own experience without additional supervision.

### 3.5 Maximize the Harness: Train-Time RL, Test-Time Distillation

FloWright optimizes the signal in two stages: train-time reinforcement
and test-time distillation.

Train time: maximizing the signal. We optimize a role’s policy
$`\pi_{\rho}`$ to maximize its expected signal over tasks:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{J}(\pi_{\rho})=\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}\sim\mathcal{X},\;\{\pi_{\rho^{\prime}}\}_{\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}}}\big[\,h^{\rho}(G_{j},x_{i})\,\big]
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\mathcal{J}`$ optimizes $`\pi_{\rho}`$ while the expectation
runs over the policies $`\pi_{\rho^{\prime}}`$ of all roles
$`\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}`$ in the flow. As FloWright contributes a
training paradigm rather than an optimizer, $`\mathcal{J}`$ admits any
policy-gradient method: we anchor on the proximal policy
optimization ([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib59)) clipped surrogate and
leave the advantage estimator and policy loss interchangeable, so recent
variants such as GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib56)), DAPO ([Yu et
al., 2025a](#bib.bib57)), and CISPO ([MiniMax et al., 2025](#bib.bib58))
all instantiate it, and we ablate the choice in our experiments
(§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Ablate the Design: Algorithm, Reward, Pool, and Topology ‣ 5 Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

Test time: meta distillation without gradients. The same shared harness
also optimizes workflows at test time, where no weights change. In place
of the policy parameters, FloWright optimizes a reusable prior $`P`$
that optimize generation:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P^{\star}=\arg\max_{P}\;\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}\sim\mathcal{X}}\big[\,h(G,x_{i}\mid P)\,\big]
``` |  | (5) |

This prior is distilled internally from the system’s own experiences, or
externally from a stronger teacher, or both. We additionally distill
successful experiences into few-shot cases, from which roles evolve by
meta learning. We define this meta distillation: the harness turns what
its own successful experiences teach into the prior that later tasks
reuse. As such, FloWright keeps improving on new tasks, driven by the
very signal it optimizes during training.

## 4 Experiments

Setup. We evaluate FloWright in both objectives it optimizes
(§[3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Maximize the Harness: Train-Time RL, Test-Time Distillation ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")):
reinforcement learning at train time, and meta distillation at test
time. At train time, we cover four evolution modes
(§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")):
(a) single-role self-evolution, (b) agent-skill co-evolution, (c)
upstream-downstream co-evolution, and (d) multi-agent co-evolution. We
compare them against two single-agent baselines, with or without the
tool and skill pools
(Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.3 What the Nodes Are Made of ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Each baseline runs with the same compute budget as our FloWright
counterpart it is compared against. All evaluations are on the held-out
test sets, non-overlapped with training data
(§[D.5](#A4.SS5 "D.5 Data Statistics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
We train Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B ([Team, 2026](#bib.bib60)) on the
paired strategy at $`\ell=3`$, and evaluate two open-source models,
Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B, together with two close-source models,
GPT-5-mini ([OpenAI, 2025](#bib.bib61)) and GPT-5.4-mini ([OpenAI,
2026a](#bib.bib62)), on held-out test sets, using GPT-5.4 ([OpenAI,
2026b](#bib.bib63)) as the distillation teacher
(§[3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Maximize the Harness: Train-Time RL, Test-Time Distillation ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
and GPT-5.6-luna ([OpenAI, 2026c](#bib.bib64)) as LLM judge
(§[D.4](#A4.SS4 "D.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
As a role can be powered by a different backbone than the rest, we
additionally evaluate settings that pair a smaller upstream backbone
with a larger downstream one as well as the reverse. We summarize our
implementation details in
§[F](#A6 "Appendix F Implementation Details ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows").

Data. We evaluate on the hardened tasks of DataWright
(§[D](#A4 "Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
which converts $`12`$ datasets over $`7`$ domains into workflow-level

summarizes DataWright data statistics. We refer to each

§[D.5](#A4.SS5 "D.5 Data Statistics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Each arm falls into one of three evaluation regimes by its distance from

(Tab. [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5 Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

Evaluation Metrics. A hardened task is scored as the mean over its
sub-questions, and a dataset’s accuracy is the mean over its test tasks
(§[D.4](#A4.SS4 "D.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

(Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.1 Evolve the Roles: Optimize Any Role by Harnessing the Workflow ‣ 5 Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Meta distillation gives the largest test-time gain at five shots with up
to $`+2.58\%`$, in contrast to one shot staying below the untrained
baseline. A prior helps only when it carries enough evidence to
generalize beyond the single case it comes from. Test-time and
train-time optimization then compound, reaching $`+3.21\%`$ overall,
$`+4.14\%`$ in-distribution, $`+3.80\%`$ out-of-distribution, and
$`+2.79\%`$ out-of-domain, exceeding the five-shot prior alone by
$`+1.59\%`$ and leading other settings across regimes. The same harness
(§[3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Maximize the Harness: Train-Time RL, Test-Time Distillation ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
therefore optimizes workflows whether or not the policy weights update.

FloWright generalizes across roles and backbones at both train and test
time. At train time, optimizing any single role improves the
performance, and co-evolving more roles improves it further
(Fig. [14](#A7.F14 "Figure 14 ‣ G.2 Generalize the Harness: Optimization Transfers to Different Settings ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Also, an optimized 4B role is able to transfer its gain when other roles
run on Qwen3.5-9B, even raising accuracy above the all-9B baseline
(Fig. [13](#A7.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ G.2 Generalize the Harness: Optimization Transfers to Different Settings ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
At test time, the meta-distilled prior demonstrates persistent gain in
every regime on all four open-source and close-source model backbones
(Fig. [15](#A7.F15 "Figure 15 ‣ G.2 Generalize the Harness: Optimization Transfers to Different Settings ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
One harness therefore optimizes any role and backbone a workflow is
built and executed upon. We extend our discussion in
§[G.2](#A7.SS2 "G.2 Generalize the Harness: Optimization Transfers to Different Settings ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows").

## 6 Related Work

We summarize four lines of work below and extend each in
§[A](#A1 "Appendix A Extended Discussion of Related Work ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows").

Workflow Construction and Optimization. Early multi-agent frameworks
coordinate agents through manually specified roles and procedures ([Hong
et al., 2024](#bib.bib27); [Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib28)). Later methods
automate this design while keeping every model fixed, searching for a
workflow written as code ([Zhang et al., 2025d](#bib.bib30); [Hu et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib29)) or generating a graph for each task and revising it
during execution ([Niu et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)), and a further line
trains the generator itself from execution outcomes ([Zhang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib34); [Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib11); [Nielsen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). A separate line improves the workflow without
updating any weights, inducing reusable routines from past
experience ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)), evolving the context a
model conditions on ([Zhang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib41)), or replacing
reinforcement learning with reflective prompt evolution ([Agrawal et
al., 2026](#bib.bib31)). However, the models that solve the workflow’s
subtasks remain fixed, limiting how much the workflow as a whole can
improve.

Multi-Agent Training and Co-Evolution. Reinforcement learning
increasingly optimizes several interacting agents, yet within an
interaction structure fixed in advance, such as a discussion among
agents that play one role ([Park et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) or a
workflow designed once per task domain ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)). Co-evolution likewise fixes how the models interact,
pairing a challenger that proposes problems with a solver that answers
them ([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)), or scoring proposals in a
multi-round discussion ([Xue et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)). Other works
train each agent separately, in its own reinforcement learning with a
role-specific dataset and reward ([Guo et al., 2026](#bib.bib7);
[Motwani et al., 2025](#bib.bib45)). However, agents then learn in
isolation or in one fixed role, rather than over the workflows
constructed for each task.

Credit Assignment and Failure Attribution. Credit finer than a single
outcome typically comes from learned models, such as a process reward
model trained alongside the policy ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)) or a
local reward for each component, estimated by re-executing what follows
it ([Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)). Failure attribution instead names
the responsible agent and step with LLM judges ([Zhang et al.,
2025f](#bib.bib47)), an attributor trained on labeled failures ([Zhang
et al., 2025b](#bib.bib21)), or re-execution with edited messages ([Ma
et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)), yet identifies the decisive step in only
about a third of failed runs ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib50)). Credit
that adds no model or rollout compares actions taken from states that
recur across a single agent’s rollouts of the same task ([Feng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib48); [Cheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)). However, such
attribution costs models, labels, or executions beyond the workflow run
itself, and it can still misattribute where a failure originates.

Evaluation of Multi-Agent Workflows. Methods that construct or optimize
workflows are commonly evaluated on single-question math, function-level
code, and short question answering ([Zhang et al., 2025d](#bib.bib30);
[Zhang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib34); [Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)). On
such data, automatically designed systems improve on a self-consistent
single agent by at most a few points ([Singh et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)),
and majority voting alone accounts for most of the gains attributed to
multi-agent debate ([Choi et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)). Training on it can
also collapse a system into one dominant agent ([Zhang et al.,
2025h](#bib.bib52)), and failure analyses find the reported gains often
minimal ([Cemri et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)). However, such data can
neither reveal nor optimize what a workflow adds beyond a single agent.

## 7 Conclusions

In this work, we introduce FloWright, which leverages the workflow as
harness
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Wire the Workflow: Topology & Pool Dynamics ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
to provide the optimization signal without additional annotations: one
signal that evaluates a workflow, trains any role that builds or
executes it, and evolves a reusable prior at test time
(§[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Workflow as Harness ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"),
[3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Maximize the Harness: Train-Time RL, Test-Time Distillation ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
A hierarchical, structure-aware reward
(§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Harness the Workflow: A Hierarchical Reward ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
turns the sparse outcome of a workflow into role-level feedback, with
role-specific credit derived from the execution trace
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Credit the Structure: Localizing Credits to Roles ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
FloWright enables train-time optimization in four modes: (1) single role
self-evolution, (2) agent-skill co-evolution, (3) upstream-downstream
co-evolution, and (4) two or more roles co-evolve; and test-time
optimization via meta distillation
(§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
To supply reliable workflow-level evaluation, we further propose
DataWright to convert single agent level datasets into workflow-level
tasks through three hardening strategies at two levels, yielding the
$`44`$ arms our experiments span
(§[D](#A4 "Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Across those arms, FloWright outperforms the single-agent baselines by
$`\geq+11.39\%`$, adds a further $`+2.58\%`$ through meta distillation
and $`+5.03\%`$ through reinforcement optimization
(§[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Evolve the Roles: Optimize Any Role by Harnessing the Workflow ‣ 5 Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
These gains hold across RL algorithms, workflow topologies, pool
granularities, and pool dynamics
(§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Ablate the Design: Algorithm, Reward, Pool, and Topology ‣ 5 Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
and transfer to backbones the harness never trains
(§[G.2](#A7.SS2 "G.2 Generalize the Harness: Optimization Transfers to Different Settings ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

## Appendix A Extended Discussion of Related Work

### A.1 Workflow Construction and Optimization

Early multi-agent frameworks coordinate agents through manually
specified roles, procedures, and conversation patterns ([Hong et al.,
2024](#bib.bib27); [Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib28)), and recent visual
document understanding systems run agent pipelines designed by hand for
their domain on fixed, prompted backbones ([Zhu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6); [Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)). Other methods
automate this design while keeping every model fixed, searching on each
benchmark’s validation data for a single workflow or agent design
written as code ([Zhang et al., 2025d](#bib.bib30); [Hu et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib29)), or generating a workflow graph for each task and
revising it during execution ([Niu et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)). Methods
that train the workflow generator use supervised fine-tuning on
query-specific multi-agent systems ([Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)) or
learn from execution outcomes, training lightweight controllers that
select operators ([Zhang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib34)) or communication
edges ([Zhang et al., 2025c](#bib.bib35)) for fixed LLMs, a weak
meta-agent that designs workflows for strong executors ([Nie et al.,
2025](#bib.bib11)), or orchestrators that assign subtasks to fixed
worker models ([Nielsen et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)) or select the next
agent step by step ([Dang et al., 2025](#bib.bib42)). Such training also
extends to several meta-agents that generate, configure, and rectify a
multi-agent system whose agents run on a fixed model pool ([Wang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib40)). A separate line improves the workflow without
updating weights, leveraging reusable routines from past
experience ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)), evolving the context a
model conditions on ([Zhang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib41)), or replacing
reinforcement learning with reflective prompt evolution ([Agrawal et
al., 2026](#bib.bib31)). However, in all these methods, the models that
solve the workflow’s subtasks remain fixed, which limits how much the
workflow as a whole can improve.

### A.2 Multi-Agent Training and Co-Evolution

Reinforcement learning increasingly optimizes multiple interacting LLM
agents, but within an interaction structure fixed in advance for all
task instances, such as a multi-turn discussion among agents that play
the same role ([Park et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)), a meta-thinking agent
that guides a reasoning agent ([Wan et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)), a
workflow designed once per task domain ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)), or a document-understanding pipeline of planning,
execution, judgment, and answering agents ([Yu et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib5)). Co-evolution methods likewise fix how the models
interact, pairing a challenger that proposes problems with a solver that
answers them ([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)), or letting agents drawn
at random propose, critique, and score solutions in a multi-round
discussion whose scores serve as their rewards ([Xue et al.,
2026](#bib.bib43)). Recursive agent training lets the task decomposition
vary per instance, but trains a single policy as a root agent and as the
copies of itself to which it delegates, all in the same role ([Gandhi et
al., 2026](#bib.bib44)). Other works train each agent separately,
optimizing it in its own reinforcement learning with a role-specific
dataset and reward ([Guo et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)), or fine-tuning it to
draft, verify, or refine an answer on trajectories sampled once from the
untrained model ([Motwani et al., 2025](#bib.bib45)). However, these
agents learn either in isolation or only within the one structure or
role they are trained in, which leaves them unprepared for tasks whose
outcome depends on different agents working together in different ways.

### A.3 Credit Assignment and Failure Attribution

Credit finer than a single outcome often comes from learned models, such
as a process reward model trained alongside a single agent’s policy to
reward each action ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)), or a local reward
for each component of a compound system, learned from outcomes estimated
by re-executing the components downstream of it ([Wu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib46)). Other methods credit the meta-agents that design a
multi-agent system by averaging the outcomes of executions branched at
each of their decisions ([Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib40)). Failure
attribution for multi-agent systems names the responsible agent and step
with LLM judges ([Zhang et al., 2025f](#bib.bib47)) or with an
attributor trained on failures labeled through counterfactual replay and
fault injection ([Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib21)), or tests failure
hypotheses by re-executing the system with edited messages or plans ([Ma
et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)). Even with full execution traces, such
attributors identify the decisive failure step in only about a third of
failed runs ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib50)). Credit that adds no model
or rollout instead compares actions taken from states that recur across
a single agent’s rollouts of the same task, and falls back to
trajectory-level credit when states do not recur ([Feng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib48); [Cheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)). However, such
credit either costs extra models, labels, or executions, or holds only
where a single agent revisits the same states, and it can still
misattribute failures. Consequently, agents that share one outcome lack
a cheap and reliable signal of their own mistakes.

### A.4 Evaluation of Multi-Agent Workflows

Methods that construct or optimize multi-agent workflows are commonly
evaluated on single-question math, function-level code, and short
question answering ([Zhang et al., 2025d](#bib.bib30); [Zhang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib34); [Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)). On such data,
automatically designed multi-agent systems improve on a self-consistent
single agent by at most a few points ([Singh et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)),
many reported successes of efficiency methods for multi-agent systems
appear in settings where a single agent already preserves strong
performance ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib23)), and majority voting
alone accounts for most of the gains attributed to multi-agent
debate ([Choi et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)). When multiple agents are
trained on single-question math, one agent comes to dominate while the
other contributes little, collapsing the system into a single
agent ([Zhang et al., 2025h](#bib.bib52)). Final answer accuracy can
also remain high while the interactions among agents diverge from their
intended behavior ([So et al., 2026](#bib.bib53)), and failure analyses
find the gains of multi-agent systems on popular benchmarks often
minimal ([Cemri et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)). However, as such data can be
solved without coordinating agents, evaluating on it cannot reliably
assess whether a workflow’s structure helps, and training on it provides
little signal for learning beyond a single agent’s effort.

## Appendix B Limitations

By proposing FloWright, there are also limitations that confine the
scope of what we demonstrate. First, our reinforcement learning
experiments are mainly evaluated on two open models of a single family,
Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-9B
(§[4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
FloWright leverages the same signal whichever model powers a role, and
the meta-distillation improves all four backbones we evaluate, including
the two it never optimizes
(§[G.2](#A7.SS2 "G.2 Generalize the Harness: Optimization Transfers to Different Settings ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Nevertheless, we aim to incorporate more model families into our
evaluation to strengthen our work. Second, our evaluation spans $`44`$
arms over $`12`$ datasets and $`7`$ domains
(§[D.5](#A4.SS5 "D.5 Data Statistics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
each a task whose inputs are fixed once the task begins. In future work,
we plan to train roles at a larger scale, and to extend FloWright to
dynamic environments, where the inputs a workflow reads and the
components it needs change while the workflow runs.

## Appendix C Preliminary Study: The Gap in Existing Benchmarks

A multi-agent workflow is only warranted on a task that a single agent
cannot already solve. We therefore ask two questions: what existing
benchmarks demand of a system, whether a single agent or a workflow, and
how much a workflow gains on them. We answer the first by surveying the
evaluations that published workflow generation methods use, and the
second by a controlled comparison in which the workflow and the single
agent both run the untrained backbone Qwen3-VL-8B.

What Existing Benchmarks Demand. We collect every dataset that each of
$`20`$ workflow generation methods published at top-tier venues
evaluates on ([Zhang et al., 2025d](#bib.bib30); [Hu et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib29); [Zhang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib34); [Zhuge et al.,
2024](#bib.bib54); [Shang et al., 2025](#bib.bib55); [Nie et al.,
2025](#bib.bib11); [Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib33); [Wang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib40); [Dang et al., 2025](#bib.bib42); [Ke et al.,
2026](#bib.bib36); [Nielsen et al., 2026](#bib.bib12); [Su et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib17); [Zhang et al.,
2025c](#bib.bib35); [Niu et al., 2025](#bib.bib8); [Zhang et al.,
2025g](#bib.bib9); [Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib10); [Wang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib38); [Hu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib39); [Singh et al.,
2025](#bib.bib51)), which gives $`121`$ datasets in total, counting a
dataset once for each method that uses it. We classify these $`121`$
datasets into six categories according to what a system needs to do to
answer each question
(Fig. [9](#A3.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Appendix C Preliminary Study: The Gap in Existing Benchmarks ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")a):
single-question math ($`39`$), short-answer QA ($`25`$), function-level
coding ($`21`$), tool use or agentic environments ($`16`$), reading
comprehension ($`4`$), and other ($`16`$). The first three categories
account for $`85`$ of the $`121`$, and a single competent agent is able
to answer their questions in one pass ¹¹ 1 These counts describe the
$`20`$ methods we survey, and we do not extrapolate them to the field..

How Much a Workflow Gains on Them. Leveraging Qwen3-VL-8B as the
backbone, we compare LLM-generated multi-agent workflow against a single
agent on two datasets of this kind (MMLongBench-Doc ([Ma et al.,
2024](#bib.bib66)) and DocFinQA ([Reddy et al., 2025](#bib.bib69))). We
give the workflow and the single agent the same compute budget, so that
any difference in accuracy is attributable to how the computation is
organized rather than to how much of it each system is allowed
(Fig. [9](#A3.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Appendix C Preliminary Study: The Gap in Existing Benchmarks ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")b).
On the original MMLongBench-Doc (200-sample subset) the workflow reaches
$`40.50\%`$ and the single agent $`48.00\%`$. On the original DocFinQA
(200-sample subset) the workflow reaches $`33.00\%`$ and the single
agent $`38.00\%`$. Neither dataset rewards multi-agent coordination.
Each sample presents one document and one question, so a system reads
through the document and extracts one answer, and no decision in the
task requires ability or coordination beyond a single agent. The outcome
therefore reflects what these benchmarks ask for, not a limitation of
the workflow.

Hardening Changes the Outcome. On MMLongBench-Doc hardened by
DataWright, the same workflow reaches $`51.61\%`$ against the single
agent’s $`12.33\%`$ over $`150`$ test samples, an improvement of
$`39.28`$ points. Through hardening
(§[D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Hardening Strategies ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
the evidence a system needs to examine exceeds one effective context
window. Therefore, the task requires deciding which input to read in
what order, and spending the same number of turns on a single input no
longer suffices. This is the condition under which the structure and
coordination a workflow adds carry value ²² 2 We observe this reversal
in our preliminary study, so we analyze it as an existence result that
motivates DataWright
(§[D](#A4 "Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
and not as a rate at which hardening reverses an outcome..

## Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening

Datasets that a single agent can already handle can neither reveal nor
optimize what a workflow adds beyond it
(§[C](#A3 "Appendix C Preliminary Study: The Gap in Existing Benchmarks ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
To this end, we propose DataWright to convert existing datasets into
workflow-level tasks. Particularly, it builds each task by a rule-based
hardening strategy at a hardening level $`\ell`$
(§[D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Hardening Strategies ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
adaptively selects the strategies each dataset supports
(§[D.2](#A4.SS2 "D.2 Adaptive Strategy Selection ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
and filters the data with quality control
(§[D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Quality Control ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Each hardened task is graded as the mean of its sub-question scores, and
the accuracy of each hardened dataset is calculated as the mean accuracy
over its test tasks
(§[D.4](#A4.SS4 "D.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Alg. [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ D.1 Hardening Strategies ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
summarizes the workflow-level data hardening procedure of DataWright. We
also summarize our data statistics in
§[D.5](#A4.SS5 "D.5 Data Statistics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows").

### D.1 Hardening Strategies

A hardening strategy converts samples of an existing dataset, each a
question paired with the input it asks about, such as a document, a
slide deck, a chart, etc., into harder tasks by its rule, without any
model in the loop. Solving a hardened task draws on the capabilities
that a workflow brings beyond a single agent, including decomposing the
task into sub-questions, routing each sub-question to a suitable input
and agent, localizing the evidence that answers it, and aggregating the
answers. Every strategy calls on these capabilities together, while each
emphasizes a different one by its construction.

Shared bundles $`\ell`$ questions about the same input into one task.
Solving it mainly exercises decomposition: the task splits into
sub-questions that can be assigned and answered separately.

Paired bundles $`\ell`$ questions, each with its own distinct input,
into one task. Solving it mainly exercises routing: each sub-question is
matched to the input that answers it.

### D.2 Adaptive Strategy Selection

Not every dataset supports every hardening strategy, so DataWright
selects the suitable strategies for each dataset from what its samples
allow (Supports in
Alg. [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ D.1 Hardening Strategies ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Each strategy has one prerequisite.

Shared requires inputs that each carry several independent questions. It
is therefore unavailable for (1) datasets that pair every input with a
single question, such as one question per coding problem (e.g.,
TACO ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib74))), per image (e.g., MMMU-Pro ([Yue
et al., 2025](#bib.bib71))), per chart (e.g., ChartCoder ([Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib73))), etc., and for (2) datasets whose questions about
one input are steps of the same problem (e.g., We-Math ([Qiao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib72))), where one step reveals another’s answer.

Paired requires only questions with distinct inputs, which every dataset
provides.

Decoy requires decoys that cannot answer the question, which holds only
when the question identifies its own input, as questions about a
specific document, slide deck, or financial filing do. For charts,
images, and coding problems, a decoy of the same kind can match
everything the question states, which would make the task ambiguous
rather than harder, so DataWright does not apply this strategy to them.

Sample-size requirement. A hardening level of a strategy is kept for a
dataset only if its test set at that level holds at least 100 original
samples, so that every accuracy score rests on enough samples to give a
valid evaluation.
Tab. [3](#A4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ D.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
summarizes the strategies and levels selected for each dataset.

### D.3 Quality Control

Existing datasets contain defective samples, such as a question that its
input cannot answer, an input that does not match its question, or a
reference answer that the input contradicts. DataWright therefore
validates the samples of each dataset before hardening (Valid in
Alg. [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ D.1 Hardening Strategies ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
mainly by quality filtering and automatic checks.

Quality filtering. On the training data, GPT-5.4 serves as an LLM judge:
it examines each sample together with its reference answer and flags the
answers it finds wrong. The flagged samples are removed, 203 of the
21,570 training samples (1.9%). For example, on DocFinQA, the judge
removes 79 unanswerable questions, and on TACO it removes 36 problems
whose statement relies on a figure that the dataset no longer provides.

Automatic checks. For code generation datasets, we implement automatic
checks by running the reference solution of each sample, keeping the
samples that can pass their own tests. For example, a TACO problem
requires at least 10 tests, so that an incorrect code answer cannot pass
by chance.

### D.4 Evaluation Metrics

Hardening changes how hard a task is, not how its answers are graded:
every sub-question keeps the metric of its own answer type, and a task’s
score is the mean over its sub-questions. A hardened task $`x`$ holds
$`|x|`$ sub-questions, the $`i`$-th carrying the reference answer
$`y^{\star}_{i}`$ and receiving the answer $`y_{i}`$ that a workflow
returns: $`|x|=\ell`$ under shared and paired, which bundle $`\ell`$
questions per task, and $`|x|=1`$ under decoy, whose level counts inputs
rather than questions. With $`s(\cdot,\cdot)\in[0,1]`$ denoting the
metric of that sub-question’s answer type, a task’s score and a
dataset’s accuracy over its test tasks are:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
S(x)=\frac{1}{|x|}\sum_{i=1}^{|x|}s(y_{i},y^{\star}_{i}),\qquad\mathrm{Acc}(\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{test}}_{\sigma})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{test}}_{\sigma}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{test}}_{\sigma}}S(x)
``` |  | (6) |

where a sub-question the workflow leaves unanswered scores $`s=0`$.
Tab. [2](#A4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ D.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
summarizes the evaluation metrics for different types of tasks FloWright
cover. We also show the task types of each dataset in
Tab. [3](#A4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ D.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows").

Multiple choice and lists. Let $`Y`$ and $`Y^{\star}`$ be the sets of
options, or of list items, that the answer and the reference name. The
score is the overlap of the two sets against the larger of them:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{\mathrm{set}}(Y,Y^{\star})=\frac{|Y\cap Y^{\star}|}{\max\left(|Y^{\star}|,|Y|\right)}
``` |  | (7) |

which reaches 1 only when the two sets agree. Extra names are penalized,
just as missing ones are, so a workflow cannot gain by naming every
option. A single-answer question is the case $`|Y^{\star}|=1`$, where
Eq. [7](#A4.E7 "In D.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
is an exact match.

Numeric. A numeric answer is first normalized: thousands separators and
currency symbols are removed, units are mapped to a canonical name, and
a percentage and its fraction become one quantity. It is then compared
at the precision the reference itself states, with
$`\mathrm{round}_{y^{\star}}(\cdot)`$ rounding to that precision:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{\mathrm{num}}(y,y^{\star})=\mathds{1}\!\left[\mathrm{round}_{y^{\star}}(y)=\mathrm{round}_{y^{\star}}(y^{\star})\right]
``` |  | (8) |

No relative tolerance is allowed, which is stricter than some of these
benchmarks.

Open-ended. Let $`\nu(\cdot)`$ normalize case, punctuation, and spacing,
and $`\mathrm{tok}(\cdot)`$ be the multiset of answer tokens. An exact
match after normalization scores 1, and otherwise the score is the
token-level F1 with the reference:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{\mathrm{text}}(y,y^{\star})=\begin{cases}1,&\nu(y)=\nu(y^{\star}),\\[4.0pt]
\dfrac{2\left|\mathrm{tok}(y)\cap\mathrm{tok}(y^{\star})\right|}{\left|\mathrm{tok}(y)\right|+\left|\mathrm{tok}(y^{\star})\right|},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
``` |  | (9) |

so a sub-answer that overlaps its reference without matching it earns
partial credit. The second case applies inside shared and paired tasks.
A decoy task holding a single question is scored by the first case
alone.

Code generation. A code is graded by running it, never by comparing its
text to a reference:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{\mathrm{code}}(y)=\mathds{1}\!\left[\textsc{Check}(y)\right]
``` |  | (10) |

where Check is a strict execution-based evaluation test: every unit test
of the problem passes, the benchmark’s own checker test accepts the
code, or, for a dataset that provides no evaluation protocol, the code
executes without errors and the code-rendered output is accurate against
the reference answer.

LLM as Judge. An answer that no rule can decide is graded by an LLM
judge that evaluates the answer directly against the reference. As such,
we define an LLM judge as a grader that receives the reference answer
and the predicted answer, and evaluates with one integer on a
$`\{0,\dots,L\}`$ scale whose every level its prompt pre-defines:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{\mathrm{judge}}(y,y^{\star})=\frac{1}{L}\,\textsc{Judge}\!\left(y,y^{\star}\right)
``` |  | (11) |

where the judge returns an integer in $`\{0,\dots,L\}`$ and $`L`$ is the
top of the scale its prompt defines. The prompt and the normalization
thus stay in step. Each judge makes exactly one judgment, which keeps an
answer self-consistent: a verdict paired with a separate similarity
admits a pair that disagrees with itself, while anchored levels
calibrate. A judge reads text or images, whichever the answer is made
of, and a dataset that needs several judges gets one per aspect of the
same answer, which the metric averages into that task’s score.

For ChartCoder, we propose its evaluation as the mean of two LLM judged
scores gated by execution: two judges at $`L{=}5`$
(Fig. [10](#A4.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ D.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
alongside the execution term of
Eq. [10](#A4.E10 "In D.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix D DataWright: Workflow-Level Data Hardening ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows").
The first LLM judge evaluates code by assessing the predicted code
against the reference code. The second LLM judge evaluates the predicted
chart rendered by the predicted code against the reference chart as two
images. When execution fails, the score becomes $`0`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s^{\mathrm{code}}_{\mathrm{chart}}(y,y^{\star})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{5}\,\mathds{1}\!\left[\textsc{Exec}(y)\right]\textsc{Judge}_{\mathrm{code}}\!\left(y,y^{\star}\right),`$ |  | (12) |
|  | $`\displaystyle s^{\mathrm{render}}_{\mathrm{chart}}(y,y^{\star})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{5}\,\mathds{1}\!\left[\textsc{Exec}(y)\right]\textsc{Judge}_{\mathrm{render}}\!\left(\textsc{Render}(y),\textsc{Render}(y^{\star})\right),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{\mathrm{chart}}(y,y^{\star})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(s^{\mathrm{code}}_{\mathrm{chart}}(y,y^{\star})+s^{\mathrm{render}}_{\mathrm{chart}}(y,y^{\star})\right)`$ |  |  |

where Exec marks a code that executes successfully, and Render renders
one to an image. Both judges score $`0`$ unless the code executes
without error. The two judges cover two different aspects of one answer:
one evaluating the code from its source and the other the image that
code renders, and their mean is the accuracy we present for each
ChartCoder task. We use GPT-5.6-luna as the judge
(§[4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
ChartCoder is the one dataset that requires a judge, so every ChartCoder
number in our experiments is a judge score, and every other dataset
carries the rule-based metric of its answer type defined above.

## Appendix E FloWright: Inside the Learning

### E.1 Credit the Structure: Traced Credit, Learnable Co-Evolution

Only genuine root causes are charged. A failed node that has a failed
dependency, direct or indirect, is treated as a cascaded failure, so its
credit propagates to the root failure along the workflow’s edges, for
any topology. Infrastructure faults, such as a provider connection error
or a content filter, are not the policy’s fault and thus excluded. A
node is judged by its final occurrence in $`\tau_{j}`$, so a step that
fails early in a loop but recovers later is not charged. A fault that
never surfaces as a node failure is left to the global signal.

Per-role credit makes co-evolution learnable. For example, when a
workflow runs both the Generator’s design and the Inventor’s components,
one scalar entangles their contributions. Localized credit restricts
each role’s credit term to its own nodes, so a role whose footprint is
narrower than the workflow is not charged for failures outside it, e.g.,
the Inventor is not charged for failures on nodes that use none of the
components it creates, as the roles co-evolve
(§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
This local credit is a bounded term of the global hierarchy in
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Harness the Workflow: A Hierarchical Reward ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"),
alongside the role-specific validity $`v^{\rho}`$ that measures each
role’s own contribution
(Tab. [4](#A5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ E.3 Define the Reward: Validity Reward in the Hierarchical Ladder ‣ Appendix E FloWright: Inside the Learning ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))

### E.2 Harness the Workflow: Rich in Signal, Broad in Reach

Every step of progress earns signal. The ladder makes the signal rich:
it reflects every step of a role’s progress, from a well-formed output
through a valid contribution to a correct answer. Because each stage
with a nonzero weight adds its own term, the policy learns from the full
hierarchy of outcomes. Thus, even a partially successful workflow yields
a graded, informative gradient.

One shared harness generalizes to every role. The invariant tail
$`w_{e}\,e+w_{a}\,a`$ carries across every role: the harness always runs
a workflow and evaluates its answer, and only the source of the workflow
or of its execution differs. Every role’s own quality enters through its
own validity reward $`v^{\rho}`$ at the same stage of the ladder,
deterministic and never a learned judge. Because the whole framework is
shared
(Fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.3 What the Nodes Are Made of ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
the harness generalizes to any role, which is what makes co-evolution
across arbitrary roles practical
(§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Harness for Evolution: One Role or Mutual Curriculum ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

### E.3 Define the Reward: Validity Reward in the Hierarchical Ladder

The reward of
Eq. [3](#S3.E3 "In 3.3 Harness the Workflow: A Hierarchical Reward ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
is built from five terms. Whether the role’s output parses ($`f`$) gates
the rest: an output that cannot be read leaves nothing to execute.
Whether the role’s contribution is valid ($`v^{\rho}`$) is a
role-specific term, and
§[E.2](#A5.SS2 "E.2 Harness the Workflow: Rich in Signal, Broad in Reach ‣ Appendix E FloWright: Inside the Learning ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
states why the remaining terms are not: the harness always runs a
workflow and grades its answer, so whether the workflow executes to a
final answer ($`e`$) and how correct that answer is ($`a`$) are shared
by every role
(Eq. [3](#S3.E3 "In 3.3 Harness the Workflow: A Hierarchical Reward ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
The credit term $`c_{j}^{\rho}`$
(§[E.1](#A5.SS1 "E.1 Credit the Structure: Traced Credit, Learnable Co-Evolution ‣ Appendix E FloWright: Inside the Learning ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"))
then charges a role only for the nodes it authors. Thus, only a parse
failure gates the reward, while validity rewards without gating. A
correct answer requires an execution that reaches one, so a role earns
dense partial credit for how far its contribution carries the workflow.

A role earns its validity reward when its own output effectively
contributes to the workflow, and
Tab. [4](#A5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ E.3 Define the Reward: Validity Reward in the Hierarchical Ladder ‣ Appendix E FloWright: Inside the Learning ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
shows what a contribution amounts to for each role. Four forms arise:
(1) structural correctness rewards a workflow that is well formed
against the pool; (2) deciding and authoring reward components that
resolve to an existing pool entry or, when newly authored, instantiate
and run; (3) helpfulness rewards what the role’s intervention adds,
calculated as the graded gain over the same flow without it, suiting a
role that steers the flow; and (4) step format and step liveness reward
every node the role runs for reaching an answer of its own. A reward
resting on a semantic judgment (e.g., the coherence of a plan) is
deliberately excluded: a semantically correct output can also lead to
the wrong final answer, whereas an outcome-grounded gain measures the
actual contribution.

### E.4 Wire the Workflow: Representing Workflows In Different Topologies

A workflow needs to be properly written down to enable seamless upstream
generation and downstream execution. FloWright allows four workflow
topologies
(Fig. [11](#A5.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ E.4 Wire the Workflow: Representing Workflows In Different Topologies ‣ Appendix E FloWright: Inside the Learning ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")):
a step schema, an agent graph, a state machine, and a block code. The
four topologies differ only in where the control flow is recorded. A
schema states it in a separate block, a graph types each edge, a state
machine attaches conditions and bounds to transitions, and a code
carries it in the type of a block. Each is converted into the same
directed graph before harness execution: nodes hold the agent with its
instruction and inputs, and edges hold sequence, parallel, conditional
and bounded-loop relations, and a cycle that no topology marks as a loop
is reclassified as a bounded one so the graph stays schedulable. The
control operations convert the same way in every topology. Execution,
credit, and evaluation therefore read one graph and never observe which
topology produces it. For this reason, FloWright wires different
topologies into the shared workflow graph
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Wire the Workflow: Topology & Pool Dynamics ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")),
and thus the topology a generator writes in cannot change what the
harness measures.

What the choice does change is how reliably the generator writes a
workflow worth running: whether its output parses, whether the structure
it declares is valid, and how well the resulting workflow performs.
Thus, this becomes an empirical question that we measure via an ablation

## Appendix F Implementation Details

### F.1 Reward Configuration

Every role instantiates one hierarchical ladder, so the reward weights
differ only where the roles differ. We summarize per-role reward weights
in
Tab. [5](#A6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ F.1 Reward Configuration ‣ Appendix F Implementation Details ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows").
Format carries $`0.1`$ for every role, since parsing is a gate rather
than an achievement. Across different roles, the answer term always
dominates at $`0.6`$ to $`0.8`$. We set the execution weight to $`0.0`$
for every role in main experiments: a workflow that does not run cannot
answer, so the answer term already covers it, and weighting execution on
its own would pay a workflow for running cleanly while answering
wrongly. Coding tasks reward the term at $`0.1`$, since a workflow there
reaches an answer only once its code executes, so the ladder sets this
weight by task type
(§[G.3](#A7.SS3 "G.3 Weigh the Execution: The Ladder Adapts to the Task Type ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
The validity term holds what each role alone controls, and its
components take the weight the answer term leaves: one component for the
Generator and the steering roles, two for the Inventor and the
Downstream agent. The credit penalty of
§[E.1](#A5.SS1 "E.1 Credit the Structure: Traced Credit, Learnable Co-Evolution ‣ Appendix E FloWright: Inside the Learning ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
rides on top at $`0.1`$, and the ablation that switches it off sets it
to $`0.0`$
(§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Ablate the Design: Algorithm, Reward, Pool, and Topology ‣ 5 Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

### F.2 Experiment Configuration

As FloWright contributes a training paradigm rather than an optimizer,
the objective of
Eq. [4](#S3.E4 "In 3.5 Maximize the Harness: Train-Time RL, Test-Time Distillation ‣ 3 FloWright: Learning to Wright Workflows ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")
admits any policy-gradient method. We instantiate it with GRPO, DAPO,
and CISPO, ablating the choice. All three draw $`m`$ rollouts per task
and score them against their group rather than a learned critic, so they
differ in how that group is turned into an update: GRPO normalizes
token-level advantages within the group, DAPO decouples the clipping
bounds and drops the groups that carry no signal, and CISPO clips the
importance-sampling weight instead of the update, so a token far from
the old policy is down-weighted rather than dropped from the gradient.
We summarize our experiment configuration details in
Tab. [6](#A6.T6 "Table 6 ‣ F.2 Experiment Configuration ‣ Appendix F Implementation Details ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows").

### G.1 Unfold the Means: Consistent Gains Across Datasets, Strategies, and Levels

FloWright improves performance across diverse datasets, hardening
strategies, and task complexities. As shown in
Fig. [12](#A7.F12 "Figure 12 ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"),
the improvement holds on every dataset: under multi-agent co-evolution,
all $`12`$ datasets gain over the untrained workflow, with up to
$`+7.34\%`$ on LongDocURL and $`+5.13\%`$ on ChartCoder. So no domain is
traded away for another. It also holds on every hardening strategy, and
grows as the strategy demands more of the workflow: $`+3.57\%`$ on
shared, $`+4.11\%`$ on paired, and $`+4.68\%`$ on decoy. The improvement
further holds at both levels of task complexity, with multi-agent
co-evolution gaining $`+4.31\%`$ out-of-domain at $`\ell{=}3`$ and
$`+4.09\%`$ at $`\ell{=}5`$, and $`+5.80\%`$ and $`+6.21\%`$
out-of-distribution
(Tab. [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5 Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Consequently, increased task complexities does not erode the gain.
Harnessing the workflow therefore improves performance across different
datasets, strategies, and levels DataWright spans, rather than on a
favorable subset of them.

### G.2 Generalize the Harness: Optimization Transfers to Different Settings

FloWright generalizes to roles powered by backbones it never trains
alongside. As shown in
Fig. [13](#A7.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ G.2 Generalize the Harness: Optimization Transfers to Different Settings ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"),
powering one upstream role with Qwen3.5-4B and other roles with
Qwen3.5-9B performs between the two single-agent baselines, gaining
$`+11.91\%`$ over the all-4B setting when Qwen3.5-4B powers the
Generator and $`+10.77\%`$ when Qwen3.5-4B powers the Inventor skill.
Exchanging the two models reverses the result: powering the Generator
and the Inventor skill with Qwen3.5-9B and the downstream agent with
Qwen3.5-4B reaches $`29.57\%`$, below the all-4B baseline of
$`32.09\%`$. A stronger upstream model thus builds workflows for a
downstream agent of its own capability, which a less capable downstream
agent executes less effectively. Consequently, when using different
backbones to power upstream and downstream roles, the downstream agent
bounds what a workflow performs however well it is built. Optimizing a
single 4B role then raises accuracy above the all-9B baseline of
$`47.50\%`$, to $`47.67\%`$ for the Generator, $`47.96\%`$ for the
Inventor skill, and $`48.50\%`$ for co-evolving the two together. An
optimized role transfers its gain when other roles run on a different
backbone it never trains with. Train-time optimization therefore
generalizes across models that power the remaining roles.

FloWright improves performance whichever role it optimizes. As shown in
Fig. [14](#A7.F14 "Figure 14 ‣ G.2 Generalize the Harness: Optimization Transfers to Different Settings ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"),
every single-role optimization outperforms the untrained baseline, and
every co-evolving optimization gains more: $`+2.83\%`$ for the
Generator, $`+0.90\%`$ for the Inventor skill, and $`+0.88\%`$ for the
downstream agent alone, against $`+4.06\%`$ for Generator-skill
co-evolution, $`+3.06\%`$ for upstream-downstream co-evolution, and
$`+5.03\%`$ for multi-agent co-evolution. The Generator is the largest
single lever, gaining $`+2.59\%`$ out-of-domain where the other two
roles stay flat. The role that designs every workflow returns the
largest gain among single roles.

a model it only prompts.

### G.3 Weigh the Execution: The Ladder Adapts to the Task Type

Figure 16: Execution Reward by Task Type. $`\Delta_{\textit{acc}}`$(%)
against untrained baselines, on the four coding arms (TACO and DS-1000)
and over all $`44`$ arms
(§[4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
Every other ladder weight stays fixed
(Tab. [5](#A6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ F.1 Reward Configuration ‣ Appendix F Implementation Details ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).

Weighting execution adds the most on coding tasks. As shown in
Fig. [16](#A7.F16 "Figure 16 ‣ G.3 Weigh the Execution: The Ladder Adapts to the Task Type ‣ Appendix G A Deeper Dive Beneath the Results ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows"),
an execution weight of $`w_{e}{=}0.1`$ improves over the untrained
baselines, gaining $`+3.98\%`$ and $`+5.68\%`$ on the coding arms and
$`+0.59\%`$ and $`+2.03\%`$ over all $`44`$ arms, for Qwen3.5-4B and
Qwen3.5-9B respectively. On coding, where a workflow reaches an answer
only once its code executes to earn score based on the portion of
evaluation tests it passes, $`w_{e}{=}0.1`$ outperforms $`w_{e}{=}0.0`$
by $`+1.69\%`$ and $`+2.12\%`$. Over all arms, where the answer term
already covers whether a workflow reaches an answer, $`w_{e}{=}0.0`$
leads by $`+2.24\%`$ and $`+1.86\%`$. This is also why our main
experiments hold the execution weight at $`0.0`$
(§[F.1](#A6.SS1 "F.1 Reward Configuration ‣ Appendix F Implementation Details ‣ It Takes Workflows to Evolve Better Workflows")).
The hierarchical ladder therefore servers as a paradigm whose weights
follow what each task type demands, with execution earning its own
weight where the task rewards running the workflow.
````

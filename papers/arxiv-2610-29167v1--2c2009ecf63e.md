# TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimization for On-Policy Self-Distillation

###### Abstract

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) enables a language model to learn
from a Teacher instantiated from the same model but given access to
privileged information, such as a reference solution. We find that
access to sufficient privileged information allows the Teacher to
generate highly accurate trajectories; however, accuracy alone does not
guarantee that these trajectories are suitable for Student learning.
Direct access to a complete solution may encourage shortened,
reference-conditioned derivations that omit the exploration,
verification, and self-correction required to solve problems without
privileged information. We introduce TeachBack, a bidirectional
preference-learning framework for on-policy self-distillation that
learns not only from the Teacher but also how the Teacher should teach.
TeachBack shapes the Teacher’s generation behavior by controlling the
length of the reference-reasoning suffix injected into the prompt and by
varying the prompt style. While maintaining comparable Teacher accuracy,
it encourages the Teacher to explore and re-solve problems, thereby
reducing its reliance on the reference solution. At each rollout
refresh, the shared policy generates both a privileged Teacher
trajectory and a problem-only Student trajectory. A pairwise preference
objective uses privileged Teacher trajectories to improve Student
learning, while the reversed preference uses the Student’s behavior to
shape future Teacher supervision under the privileged condition.
Experiments on mathematical and logical reasoning tasks show that
TeachBack consistently improves Student performance over existing OPSD
methods and related baselines.

## 1 Introduction

On-policy distillation ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)), which
provides dense supervision by allowing a Student to sample reasoning
trajectories from its current policy while a stronger Teacher evaluates
the Student-generated prefixes, has emerged as the dominant
post-training paradigm for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large
language models. Building on this, On-policy self-distillation
(OPSD) ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib34)) further removes the need for a
separate Teacher by employing a unified, parameter-shared policy,
receiving great attention recently. This policy allows the model to play
two roles, where the model acts as a Student when given only the
question, but switches to a privileged-information (PI) Teacher when
provided with additional reasoning evidence, such as reference
solutions. Despite eliminating the dependency on external models, recent
studies reveal that OPSD may cause shorter responses and a degeneration
of native reasoning behaviors, a failure mode commonly referred to as
thinking collapse ([Peng et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)), which is
particularly pronounced in models relying on long chain-of-thought
reasoning.

Several approaches have been proposed to address this degradation. One
representative line studies OPSD from the perspective of optimization,
where an uncertain Student and a PI-conditioned Teacher can favor
different continuations at high-entropy reasoning forks. More
specifically, privileged targets can suppress the Student’s branching,
verification, and backtracking when their token probabilities are
directly matched ([Kaur et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)). To preserve these
behaviors, AD-OPSD ([Peng et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)) anchors updates at
high-risk tokens to a frozen base policy. Another line examines the
supervision signal itself: reference conditioning can change the
Teacher’s response style without providing correspondingly useful
reasoning. RLCSD ([Pan et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)) contrasts correct and
incorrect hints to cancel style shifts unrelated to correctness, while
Purified OPSD ([Shen et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)) removes a reference-only
component from the Teacher signal. The third line changes how the
Student learns from that signal; PBSD ([Yu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib27))
uses reward-regularized preferences between Teacher and Student rollouts
instead of directly matching their token distributions. Although these
methods have made great achievements, they primarily regulate how the
Student uses a Teacher signal that has already been generated, leaving
open a more upstream question: can PI be used to construct a Teacher
whose reasoning remains useful to a Student that cannot access PI?

![Refer to caption](2610.29167v1/fig1.png)

Figure 1: Motivation for TeachBack. In standard OPSD, access to
privileged information can lead the Teacher to produce a short solution
that omits exploration and verification. TeachBack uses partial

self-contained Teacher trajectories that remain useful to a problem-only
Student.

This question points to a Teacher-side way to mitigate thinking
collapse: privileged information should guide the Teacher’s reasoning
rather than serve as an authoritative solution to be copied. A
problem-only Student must search among alternative paths, express
uncertainty at ambiguous steps, verify intermediate conclusions, and
recover from mistakes. As we design in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
a useful PI-conditioned Teacher should preserve these Student-compatible
behaviors while using privileged information to check the explored path,
correct unproductive branches, and converge more reliably to a correct
answer. The desired Teacher therefore needs to balance three properties:
high correctness under privileged information, low dependence on the
wording or trajectory of the reference solution, and productive
exploration that remains reproducible without PI. In particular, the
Teacher should not simply compress the reference-conditioned response
into a shorter answer. It should re-solve the original problem, use
privileged information for verification, and produce a complete
trajectory that is both reliable as supervision and rich enough for the
Student to learn.

Based on this motivation, we propose TeachBack, which improves both the
construction and transfer of privileged Teacher trajectories. First,
TeachBack presents the Teacher with partial reference reasoning of
varying lengths and prompt styles as optional working notes, then asks
it to solve the original problem from the beginning. This encourages the
Teacher to use PI to check its reasoning without simply following the
reference. The shared policy then generates a Teacher trajectory with PI
and a Student trajectory from the problem alone. We train with
preferences in both directions: the Teacher trajectory guides Student
learning, while the Student trajectory provides feedback on which
reasoning paths the Teacher should retain. In this way, the Teacher
learns to produce accurate, less reference-dependent supervision that
the Student can learn from.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  Through explicit Teacher rollouts, we reveal a substantial
  reasoning-behavior gap between the PI-conditioned Teacher and the
  problem-only Student, and show that highly accurate Teacher
  trajectories are not necessarily suitable for Student learning.
- •
  We construct the PI-conditioned Teacher by presenting a controllable
  suffix of reference reasoning as optional working notes and varying
  the surrounding prompt style. This encourages the Teacher to re-solve
  the problem and produce complete reasoning with less reliance on the
  reference.
- •
  We develop a bidirectional preference-learning framework over paired
  Teacher and Student trajectories. Teacher-origin trajectories guide
  Student learning, while Student-origin trajectories provide
  compatibility feedback that shapes future Teacher supervision.
- •
  Experiments on mathematical and logical reasoning tasks show that
  TeachBack consistently improves Student’s reasoning performance and
  outperforms existing OPSD and related baselines.

## 2 Related Work

##### On-policy distillation and privileged supervision.

Classical knowledge distillation ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib8); [Kim
& Rush, 2016](#bib.bib11); [Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib3)) transfers a
stronger Teacher’s predictions or generated sequences to a Student.
On-policy distillation (OPD) ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)) instead
queries the Teacher on Student-sampled prefixes, reducing the mismatch
between training and deployment states. On-policy self-distillation
(OPSD) ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib34)) uses one parameter-shared
policy as a problem-only Student and a privileged-information (PI)
Teacher, and Privileged Information Distillation ([Penaloza et al.,
2026](#bib.bib16)) extends this setting to long-horizon agents ([Wang
et al., 2026](#bib.bib23); [Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib24); [Lu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13)). Recent studies show that privileged conditioning can
suppress epistemic verbalization, exploration, verification, and
backtracking at high-entropy reasoning forks, leading to thinking
collapse and reference-induced style drift ([Kim et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10); [Kaur et al., 2026](#bib.bib9); [Peng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17); [Pan et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). Existing remedies
modify the resulting Teacher signal: Purified OPSD ([Shen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib20)) removes the reference-induced component; PAINT ([Tan
& Hong, 2026](#bib.bib22)), Adaptive Teacher Exposure ([Han et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)), and best-of-$`N`$ selection ([Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib30)) control privileged exposure or retain selected
rollouts; and AR-OPD ([Zhang, 2026](#bib.bib31)) and DOPD ([Yu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib28)) constrain or route privileged supervision according
to local compatibility and relative advantage. These methods improve how
an already formed signal is filtered or transferred, leaving the
construction of a Student-compatible Teacher as an upstream problem.

##### Preference optimization and Teacher construction.

Preference optimization provides a sequence-level alternative to direct
token-distribution matching. DPO ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib18))
learns a reference-relative policy from response preferences, while
SPIN ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)) uses self-generated responses to
form an iterative preference signal. PBSD ([Yu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib27)) applies preference learning to on-policy
self-distillation by optimizing a reward-regularized preference gap
between PI-conditioned Teacher samples and on-policy Student samples
instead of directly matching Teacher probabilities. Related methods use
consensus gating ([Stein et al., 2026](#bib.bib21)) or
self-revision ([He et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Zelikman et al.,
2022](#bib.bib29)) to select or construct supervision ([Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)) before distillation. TeachBack addresses the
remaining Teacher-construction problem by presenting a controllable
ground-truth suffix as incomplete working notes, varying the prompt
framing, and using two cross-condition preference directions: the
Student-side objective transfers useful Teacher trajectories under the
problem-only condition, while the reversed objective uses Student
behavior to shape future privileged Teacher trajectories.

## 3 Method

![Refer to caption](2610.29167v1/fig2.png)

Figure 2: Overview of TeachBack. (a) The Student receives the problem
alone, while a reference-reasoning suffix is framed as optional working
notes for the Teacher to re-solve the problem. (b) The shared policy
samples paired Student and Teacher trajectories; both responses are
replayed under both conditions and scored against a fixed reference
policy. (c) Under the problem-only condition, the Teacher-origin
response is preferred; under the PI-conditioned condition, the
Student-origin response is preferred. The weighted losses update the
shared policy before the next on-policy pair is generated. At test time,
only the problem-only Student condition is used.

Given training examples
$`\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{*},a_{i}^{*})\}_{i=1}^{N}`$, where
$`x_{i}`$ is a problem, $`y_{i}^{*}`$ is its reference reasoning, and
$`a_{i}^{*}`$ is its correct answer, TeachBack uses a suffix of
$`y_{i}^{*}`$ as privileged information (PI) and $`a_{i}^{*}`$ for
answer verification. The Student and Teacher are conditional roles of
one parameter-shared policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$, rather than separate
networks: the Student receives only the problem, while the Teacher
receives the problem and a prompt containing PI. Training alternates
paired rollout generation with bidirectional preference updates.
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Method ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation")
summarizes Teacher construction, paired rollouts, cross-scoring, and the
two preference updates.

### 3.1 Teacher Construction: Reference-Suffix Injection and Prompt-Style Control

For each problem $`x`$, the Student context and PI-conditioned Teacher
context are

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
c_{S}=\operatorname{BasePrompt}(x),\qquad c_{T}=\operatorname{WorkingNotesPrompt}\!\left(x,\operatorname{Tail}(y^{*})\right).
``` |  | (1) |

Equation [1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1 Teacher Construction: Reference-Suffix Injection and Prompt-Style Control ‣ 3 Method ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation")
shows the Working Notes configuration used in the main training runs.
The suffix is extracted from the dataset reference rather than from the
current Student response, so Student sampling errors do not change the
privileged information in the Teacher prompt. We use a suffix instead of
the complete reference because it provides answer-relevant information
while leaving the Teacher responsible for reconstructing the earlier
derivation. An authoritative solution framing may encourage the Teacher
to continue or copy the suffix. Framing it as incomplete, non-terminal
working notes instead prompts the Teacher to re-solve the problem,
verify the notes, fill in missing steps, and produce a complete answer.

We study two related factors in Teacher construction. The suffix length
controls how much privileged information is available, while the prompt
style changes how that information is presented. We compare several
styles, including authoritative reference, answer-only hint, and
optional working notes; the answer-only and full-solution instruction
styles also change the information content. These comparisons let us
assess Teacher correctness and reasoning behavior separately from the
downstream preference objective.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Prompt-Construction Audit and Implementation Details ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation")
gives the audit setup, metric definitions, and prompt templates.

### 3.2 Paired On-Policy Rollouts and Cross-Scoring

Unlike conventional OPSD, which transfers privileged next-token
predictions on Student-generated prefixes, TeachBack generates explicit
trajectories from both conditional roles. At refresh $`t`$, the current
policy is held fixed while generating $`G`$ paired responses:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
y_{S,i}^{(t,g)}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid c_{S,i}),\qquad y_{T,i}^{(t,g)}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid c_{T,i}),\quad g=1,\ldots,G.
``` |  | (2) |

Both responses are sampled from the same policy state, so systematic
differences between their rollout distributions arise from the prompt
conditions rather than separate models. We retain the first $`K`$ valid
response tokens for preference training; below, $`y_{S}`$ and $`y_{T}`$
denote these retained prefixes when used in scoring equations, with
example and rollout indices omitted for readability. This prefix focuses
the objective on the part of the trajectory where the model establishes
a solution direction, explores alternatives, and begins verification;
later tokens often elaborate a path that has already been selected.
Generation budgets, truncation rules, and the longer diagnostic rollout
cap are specified in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Training and Evaluation Protocols ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation").

The sampled responses are replayed under both prompts:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{Q}^{(t)}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{S}\oplus y_{S}^{(t)}&c_{S}\oplus y_{T}^{(t)}\\
c_{T}\oplus y_{S}^{(t)}&c_{T}\oplus y_{T}^{(t)}\end{bmatrix}.
``` |  | (3) |

The current policy and a frozen initialization policy score the same
response tokens in all four combinations. Within each row, the scoring
condition is fixed and the response origin varies. In particular,
$`y_{T}`$ replayed under $`c_{S}`$ lets us evaluate Teacher reasoning
under the problem-only condition. Replaying $`y_{S}`$ under $`c_{T}`$
supplies the corresponding compatibility view for the Teacher role.
After the preference update, the new policy generates the next paired
batch.

### 3.3 Bidirectional Preference Optimization

Let $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$ remain fixed throughout
training. For condition $`c`$ and response $`y`$, we use the
length-normalized score relative to this frozen reference policy:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{\theta}^{c}(y)=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{k=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{k}\mid c,y_{<k})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{k}\mid c,y_{<k})}.
``` |  | (4) |

Only valid response positions contribute. Length normalization prevents
longer responses from receiving a larger score solely because they
contain more log-probability terms, while the frozen reference anchors
both conditional comparisons to the same initialization.

##### Student-side transfer.

Under the problem-only condition, we prefer the Teacher-origin response,
$`y_{T}\succ y_{S}`$, using

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stu}}=-\mathbb{E}\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\left[A_{\theta}^{c_{S}}(y_{T})-A_{\theta}^{c_{S}}(y_{S})\right]\right).
``` |  | (5) |

Here, $`\sigma`$ is the logistic sigmoid, $`\beta>0`$ scales the
preference margin, and the expectation is over training problems and
paired rollouts sampled from the fixed policy snapshot at the current
refresh. We use $`\beta=0.1`$. Both responses are scored under
$`c_{S}`$. The margin therefore asks whether the trajectory generated
with privileged information becomes preferable under the deployment
condition. This transfers a trajectory-level teaching signal without
requiring the Student to match the complete PI-conditioned next-token
distribution. The label is determined by response origin, not by
assuming that every Teacher sample is correct; Teacher correctness is
measured separately in the experiments.

##### Teacher-side feedback.

Under the PI-conditioned condition, we reverse the ordering and prefer
the Student-origin response, $`y_{S}\succ y_{T}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tch}}=-\mathbb{E}\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\left[A_{\theta}^{c_{T}}(y_{S})-A_{\theta}^{c_{T}}(y_{T})\right]\right).
``` |  | (6) |

The reversed preference does not claim that the Student answer is more
accurate. A problem-only response can contain incremental derivation,
verification, and backtracking that are absent from a concise privileged
response. Favoring Student-origin responses under $`c_{T}`$ can feed
Student-compatible reasoning back into the role that generates future
Teacher targets, while the privileged suffix continues to support
correctness. Because both roles share parameters, the Teacher-side
update changes the next privileged rollout as well as the Student. It
can also reinforce Student errors or unproductive verbosity, which is
why we evaluate correctness, exploration, reference dependence, response
length, and downstream performance together. The numerical margins and
their training dynamics are analyzed in Section 4.4, and the local
compatibility analysis is given in
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Theoretical Analysis of Bidirectional Preference Optimization ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation").

### 3.4 Joint Co-adaptation and Training Protocol

The two directions are combined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{main}}^{(t)}(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stu}}^{(t)}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tch}}^{(t)},\qquad\lambda\in[0,1].
``` |  | (7) |

The boundary $`\lambda=0`$ isolates Teacher-to-Student transfer,
$`\lambda=1`$ isolates Teacher-side feedback, and intermediate values
test co-adaptation. Each refresh generates paired trajectories from the
current policy, replays both responses under both conditions, computes
the two preference losses, and updates only the shared parameters.
Rollouts are stop-gradient and $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ remains frozen;
verifier outcomes do not filter pairs or rescale the losses. Algorithm 1
in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Bidirectional Co-Adaptation Algorithm ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation")
gives the complete procedure, while
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Training and Evaluation Protocols ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation")
lists the fixed budgets, masks, and refresh settings.

## 4 Experimental Protocol

We organize the experiments as a progression of related questions. We
first ask whether TeachBack improves mathematical and logical reasoning
when only the original problem is available at deployment. We then
examine which Teacher prompt style and privileged-information ratio best
balance correctness, reasoning elaboration, response length, and
dependence on the reference. Next, we test whether bidirectional
preference optimization moves the Student and Teacher margins in the
intended directions while preserving stable entropy and gradient
behavior. Finally, we study how the Teacher-side objective weight
affects training dynamics and final performance.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Models and data. We train Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B ([Yang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)) on a challenging subset of DeepMath ([He
et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)). We evaluate on AMC 2023 ([Mathematical
Association of America, 2023](#bib.bib14)) and AIME 2024/2025 ([Zhang &
Math-AI, 2024](#bib.bib32); [Zhang & Math-AI, 2025](#bib.bib33)), and on
Knight-and-Knaves (KK) ([Xie et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)), using 4–8 Roles
in-domain and 9–11 Roles out-of-domain. Cross-architecture Math results
on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B ([Guo
et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)) are reported in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Cross-Architecture Math Results ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation").
The dataset splits, training settings, and evaluation protocol are
detailed in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Training and Evaluation Protocols ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation").

Baselines. We compare TeachBack with Base, GRPO ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib19)), OPSD ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib34)), SPIN ([Chen
et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)), and PBSD ([Yu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib27)).
SPIN uses the dataset ground-truth reasoning trace as the preferred
response against model-generated responses. PBSD uses trajectories
generated by a frozen privileged teacher as preferred responses against
on-policy student trajectories. TeachBack generates both trajectories
from the current shared policy and optimizes preferences under both
prompt conditions.

### 4.3 How Prompt Construction Changes Teacher Trajectories

To select the Teacher construction, we extract and clean the
ground-truth reasoning for 256 problems, vary the retained tail fraction
and prompt framing, and generate four Qwen3-4B rollouts per condition
with a 16K completion cap. We evaluate the resulting trajectories along
four dimensions: correctness, response length and truncation, reasoning
exploration, and reference dependence.

![Refer to caption](2610.29167v1/img/fig5.png)

##### Teacher-side feedback preserves useful role behavior.

Panel (a) shows that non-truncated Teacher accuracy stays near 94%–99%
across all $`\lambda`$. Positive Teacher-side weights thus preserve a
reliable PI-conditioned supervision source despite updates toward
imperfect Student responses. The sustained Teacher accuracy suggests
that the privileged suffix continues to support correctness while
Teacher-side feedback reshapes the reasoning path. This may let the
shared policy absorb useful Student exploration without simply
inheriting Student errors. Non-truncated Student accuracy declines
overall during training at $`\lambda=0`$ and $`0.25`$, whereas it
generally rises at $`0.75`$ and $`1`$. The contrast suggests that
stronger Teacher-side feedback helps the shared policy improve its
problem-only predictions during training.

Panel (b) helps explain this gap. At $`\lambda=0`$, Student responses
contract from about 6.6K to 6.0K tokens and Teacher responses from 2.5K
to 1.6K. The shorter outputs mirror one-sided OPSD ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib34)) and coincide with lower Student accuracy.
Student-side transfer alone is therefore insufficient: the Teacher must
also produce trajectories learnable under the problem-only prompt. At
$`\lambda=0.25`$, Student length stays near 6.6K but Teacher length
falls to 1.9K; at $`0.75`$ and $`1`$, they remain near 7.5K and 3K,
respectively. Together with higher Student accuracy, these longer
trajectories suggest that stronger Teacher-side feedback counters
reasoning compression and maintains PI-guided paths more learnable under
the problem-only prompt.

##### Entropy and held-out performance.

Panel (c) shows similar entropy growth under $`\lambda=0`$ and $`0.25`$,
both ending near 0.37, while $`0.75`$ and $`1`$ remain lower. Panel (d)
shows the same split in the held-out Math: the final macro mean@12 is
45.44% and 47.45% for $`0`$ and $`0.25`$, versus 53.99% and 54.02% for
$`0.75`$ and $`1`$. Thus, the smaller Teacher-side weight does little to
prevent Student-only drift or improve validation performance. Notably,
$`\lambda=1`$ improves Student accuracy and held-out Math despite using
only the Teacher-side loss. This loss favors Student-origin responses
under the privileged prompt, so its updates may teach the shared policy
to retain exploratory, Student-compatible reasoning. Because both roles
share parameters, this behavior may carry over to problem-only Student
generation while the privileged suffix keeps Teacher accuracy high. The
$`0.75`$ run has the highest validation peak and six-checkpoint average,
as well as the highest final Student non-truncated accuracy. Together,
these results support the effectiveness of TeachBack’s bidirectional
training mechanism.

## 5 Conclusion

We presented TeachBack, a bidirectional on-policy self-distillation
framework that transfers privileged Teacher trajectories to a
problem-only Student and uses Student behavior to shape future Teacher
rollouts. Framing a partial reasoning suffix as optional working notes
retains high Teacher accuracy with less measured reference dependence
than authoritative reference framing. Across the tested model scales,
TeachBack improves aggregate mathematical and logical reasoning.
Controlled ablations show that an intermediate Teacher-side weight
preserves Teacher accuracy and both preference directions while
improving held-out performance over Student-only training, highlighting
the value of shaping privileged supervision at its source.

### A.2 Diagnostic definitions

The scorecard is computed after rollout and is used only to compare
prompt constructions.

Accuracy, length, and truncation.

For rollout $`i`$, the extracted answer $`a_{i}`$ is compared with the
ground-truth answer $`a_{i}^{*}`$ using the mathematical equivalence
verifier:

|     |                                                       |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{i}=\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{Verifier}(a_{i},a_{i}^{*})].
       ```                                                    |     | (8) |

Accuracy is the mean of this indicator over the 1,024 rollouts in a
condition. Length is the mean number of Qwen tokenizer tokens in the
complete generated response. Truncated is the fraction of rollouts whose
generation reaches the 16,384-token output limit.

Exploration density and breadth.

We extract the reasoning body $`r_{i}`$ from each response. Let
$`T_{i}`$ be its number of valid tokenizer tokens and $`M_{ic}`$ the
number of matched events in category $`c`$. We use five categories:
exploration, uncertainty, verification, backtracking, and replanning.
The per-rollout exploration density is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{i}=1000\,\frac{\sum_{c=1}^{5}M_{ic}}{T_{i}},\qquad\mathrm{Exploration}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D_{i}.
``` |  | (9) |

Thus, the table averages length-normalized per-rollout densities rather
than pooling all markers and tokens before division. Breadth measures
how many of the five categories are represented in each response:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
B_{i}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{c=1}^{5}\mathbf{1}[M_{ic}>0],\qquad\mathrm{Breadth}=100\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}B_{i}.
``` |  | (10) |

Matching is case-insensitive and uses word boundaries. Within a
category, the longest matching phrase takes precedence so that a phrase
such as let’s check is not counted again as check. The complete
five-category lexicon is grouped with the reference-language detectors
in
Table [3](#A1.T3 "Table 3 ‣ A.2 Diagnostic definitions ‣ Appendix A Prompt-Construction Audit and Implementation Details ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation").

Reference dependence.

For a reasoning-injection condition, let $`b_{i}`$ be the injected
privileged body and $`x_{i}`$ the problem. If $`G_{n}(z)`$ denotes the
sequence of $`n`$-gram occurrences in text $`z`$, we first remove
n-grams already available in the problem:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
U_{n}(b_{i},x_{i})=\operatorname{set}(G_{n}(b_{i}))\setminus\operatorname{set}(G_{n}(x_{i})).
``` |  | (11) |

The output-side copy share is the fraction of reasoning n-gram
occurrences that belong to this problem-exclusive privileged set:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{Copy}_{n,i}=\frac{1}{|G_{n}(r_{i})|}\sum_{g\in G_{n}(r_{i})}\mathbf{1}[g\in U_{n}(b_{i},x_{i})].
``` |  | (12) |

The content-reference score gives more weight to eight-gram matches,
which are less likely to occur accidentally:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{ContentRef}_{i}=100\left(0.35\,\mathrm{Copy}_{4,i}+0.65\,\mathrm{Copy}_{8,i}\right).
``` |  | (13) |

Explicit mention is a rollout-level indicator for broad provenance
language. The detector covers the cue families shown in
Table [3](#A1.T3 "Table 3 ‣ A.2 Diagnostic definitions ‣ Appendix A Prompt-Construction Audit and Implementation Details ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation").
Strong attribution is a stricter indicator requiring the response to
attribute a step, answer, or correctness judgment to the privileged
material. It is reported separately and is not part of the combined
reference degree.

|----|----|----|
|  Main dimension |  Subcategory |  Matched words, phrases, or pattern families |
|  Reasoning exploration |  Exploration |  another approach; alternative; another way; different approach; different way; let’s try; alternatively |
|  |  Uncertainty |  maybe; perhaps; possibly; might; could; may; probably; likely; unlikely; it seems; seems like; I think; not sure |
|  |  Verification |  check; verify; confirm; double-check; let’s check; make sure; ensure; test; examine |

Let $`I_{i}`$ indicate whether rollout $`i`$ matches at least one
explicit-mention cue. Its explicit-mention score is $`100I_{i}`$, and
the combined reference degree is

The table reports the rollout mean of Content reference and Reference
degree, and the percentage of rollouts for which the Explicit and

Two conditions require separate interpretation. Student has no injected
privileged body, so Content reference and Reference degree are
undefined. Answer-only has no privileged reasoning body, so its Content
reference is 100 when the normalized predicted answer exactly matches
the supplied answer and 0 otherwise; normalization lowercases the
strings and removes whitespace and \$ delimiters. Its score is therefore
answer agreement rather than n-gram reuse and is not directly comparable
with the reasoning-injection conditions.

Exploration and reference metrics are surface-text proxies. Polysemous
markers such as wait, check, consider, and approach can overcount the
intended behavior, while paraphrased use of privileged information can
evade exact phrase and n-gram matching.

The rollout audit establishes that prompts change Teacher behavior, but

We therefore analyze fixed-Teacher Qwen3-4B OPSD runs that vary the

|-------------|-------------|-------------|

| Answer-only | 76.49       | 74.10       |

![Refer to caption](2610.29167v1/img/fig6.png)

Figure 6: Student entropy during the four-condition prompt ablation. The

This four-condition ablation further shows that the Teacher’s prompt
style affects whether its supervision is suitable for the Student. Notes
uses the same 50% privileged suffix but presents it as evidence to be
checked, whereas Answer-only provides only the final answer and does not
expose the Student to a complete and deterministic reference trajectory.
Both conditions avoid binding the Student to a fully specified Teacher
reasoning path, leaving more room for exploration. Consistent with this
behavior, Notes achieves the highest average mean@4 over the common
steps 50–300 (74.21%), while Answer-only also performs strongly on
average (74.10%) and obtains the highest isolated best mean@4 (76.49%).

In contrast, Reference presents the privileged suffix as an
authoritative reference, while History places the same information in
the conversation history. These two constructions do not provide equally
stable supervision. The Student-entropy curves show a sustained increase
for Reference and History during the later stages of training, whereas
the entropy trajectories under Notes and Answer-only remain more
controlled. Strong reference anchoring may impose a more one-directional
and aggressive update pressure on the Student, gradually moving its
reasoning distribution away from its original exploratory behavior.
Reference obtains an average mean@4 of 74.08%, while History falls
further to 73.32%.

These results support the following conclusion: the most suitable OPSD
Teacher is not necessarily the one that generates the most certain or
complete reference reasoning. Instead, it should preserve some
hesitation and exploratory behavior while using privileged information
to improve correctness and guide convergence. The weaker reference
constraints exhibited by Notes and Answer-only are more compatible with
stable Student-entropy dynamics and more stable downstream performance.
Strong reference conditioning or an incompatible history placement, in
contrast, is more likely to induce a sustained increase in Student
entropy and unstable supervision.

### A.4 Exact Prompt Templates

Ground-truth reasoning preprocessing. Before extracting a privileged

training runs, including a `</think>` in the privileged information was
associated with premature termination of the subsequent reasoning,
preventing the model from finishing its chain of thought normally. By
comparison, retaining the boxed-answer notation in the injected
reasoning had a smaller observed effect on reasoning continuation.

$`G,K,\beta,\lambda`$

##### Paired implementation.

Each problem is duplicated into index-matched Student and Teacher
samples. The two responses are generated from the same policy snapshot
and paired before distributed batch balancing. For each pair, the
implementation builds the four teacher-forced condition–trajectory
combinations shown in the algorithm. Current-policy scores retain
gradients; reference-policy scores and sampled responses do not. Only
the shared current policy is updated. We log the two directional
margins, response lengths, entropy, gradient norm, and verifier outcomes
separately. Verifier outcomes are diagnostics and do not filter the
preference pairs or gate either loss term.

surface patterns.

The analysis presents an exact identity for the implemented scores and a

### F.1 Setup and scope

Fix a rollout refresh, its sampled responses, and the reference policy.
All derivatives below hold the sampled tokens, prompts, and response
masks fixed, as in the replay updates of
Section [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation").
They are not total derivatives through the policy-dependent sampling
distribution across refreshes. Write $`A_{S}(y)=A_{\theta}^{c_{S}}(y)`$
and $`A_{T}(y)=A_{\theta}^{c_{T}}(y)`$ for
Equation [4](#S3.E4 "In 3.3 Bidirectional Preference Optimization ‣ 3 Method ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
and retain the unscaled margins

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{S}=A_{S}(y_{T})-A_{S}(y_{S}),\qquad M_{T}=A_{T}(y_{S})-A_{T}(y_{T}).
``` |  | (15) |

The corresponding logistic arguments are $`\beta M_{S}`$ and
$`\beta M_{T}`$, with $`\beta>0`$. A positive margin denotes a
preference relative to the reference policy, not necessarily an ordering
of raw sequence probabilities.

For a nonempty response $`y`$, define its average conditional
log-probability advantage under the privileged prompt as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
B_{\theta}(y)=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{k=1}^{|y|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{k}\mid c_{T},y_{<k})}{\pi_{\theta}(y_{k}\mid c_{S},y_{<k})},\qquad D_{\theta}(y)=B_{\theta}(y)-B_{\mathrm{ref}}(y).
``` |  | (16) |

We assume positive probabilities on scored tokens. The same response
positions are used under both prompts. A capped response is scored as
its observed prefix; an EOS token contributes only when it belongs to
the response mask. No completed-answer assumption is needed for the
following identity.

### F.2 An exact interpretation of the two margins

###### Proposition F.1 (Reference-relative conditional advantage)

For every fixed response pair, including pairs of unequal lengths,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle M_{S}+M_{T}`$ | $`\displaystyle=D_{\theta}(y_{S})-D_{\theta}(y_{T})`$ |  | (17) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\big[B_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{T})-B_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{S})\big]-\big[B_{\theta}(y_{T})-B_{\theta}(y_{S})\big].`$ |  |  |

Consequently, if both margins are positive, the privileged-context
advantage of the Teacher-origin response over the Student-origin
response is smaller than under the reference policy.

Proof. Subtracting the two reference-relative scores for the same
response gives
$`A_{T}(y)-A_{S}(y)=B_{\theta}(y)-B_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)=D_{\theta}(y)`$.
Rearranging the four terms in
Equation [15](#A6.E15 "In F.1 Setup and scope ‣ Appendix F Theoretical Analysis of Bidirectional Preference Optimization ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation")
yields

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{S}+M_{T}=[A_{T}(y_{S})-A_{S}(y_{S})]-[A_{T}(y_{T})-A_{S}(y_{T})],
``` |  |

which proves both equalities. If $`M_{S},M_{T}>0`$, their sum is
positive and the stated comparison follows. Each response keeps its own
length normalizer in both contexts, so equal response lengths are
unnecessary. $`\square`$

##### Interpretation.

The two branches constrain a specific cross-condition contrast: the
Student branch favors the Teacher-origin response under the deployment
prompt, while the Teacher branch favors the Student-origin response
under the privileged prompt. Their sum measures a reduction in the
relative conditional advantage defined above. The identity also holds in
expectation under any fixed pair distribution for which the scores are
integrable. Positive mean margins do not imply positive margins on every
pair. Neither the identity nor its expectation requires the two
conditional distributions to coincide or bounds their KL divergence. In
particular, a positive sum alone does not imply that both branches have
positive margins.

### F.3 Local compatibility of the shared-parameter updates

Let $`L_{S}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stu}}`$ and
$`L_{T}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tch}}`$ denote the two losses on a fixed
replay batch. Assume they have locally Lipschitz gradients near the
current parameters and write $`g_{S}=\nabla L_{S}(\theta)`$,
$`g_{T}=\nabla L_{T}(\theta)`$. Consider the ordinary gradient step

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{\lambda}=(1-\lambda)g_{S}+\lambda g_{T},\qquad\theta^{+}=\theta-\eta d_{\lambda}.
``` |  | (18) |

###### Proposition F.2 (A common local descent direction)

For fixed $`\lambda`$, the changes in the two losses satisfy

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle L_{S}(\theta^{+})-L_{S}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\eta\big[(1-\lambda)\|g_{S}\|^{2}+\lambda\langle g_{S},g_{T}\rangle\big]+O(\eta^{2}),`$ |  | (19) |
|  | $`\displaystyle L_{T}(\theta^{+})-L_{T}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\eta\big[(1-\lambda)\langle g_{S},g_{T}\rangle+\lambda\|g_{T}\|^{2}\big]+O(\eta^{2}).`$ |  | (20) |

If both gradients are nonzero and their inner product is nonnegative,
every $`0<\lambda<1`$ gives strict descent in both losses for
sufficiently small positive $`\eta`$. If their inner product is
negative, set $`a=\|g_{S}\|^{2}`$, $`b=\|g_{T}\|^{2}`$, and
$`k=-\langle g_{S},g_{T}\rangle>0`$. Both first-order changes are
strictly negative precisely when

|     |                                      |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{k}{b+k}<\lambda<\frac{a}{a+k}.
       ```                                   |     | (21) |

This interval is nonempty if and only if $`k^{2}<ab`$.

Proof. Taylor expansion of $`L_{j}(\theta-\eta d_{\lambda})`$, for
$`j\in\{S,T\}`$, gives
$`-\eta\langle g_{j},d_{\lambda}\rangle+O(\eta^{2})`$, proving
Equations [19](#A6.E19 "In Proposition F.2 (A common local descent direction) ‣ F.3 Local compatibility of the shared-parameter updates ‣ Appendix F Theoretical Analysis of Bidirectional Preference Optimization ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation")–[20](#A6.E20 "In Proposition F.2 (A common local descent direction) ‣ F.3 Local compatibility of the shared-parameter updates ‣ Appendix F Theoretical Analysis of Bidirectional Preference Optimization ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation").
When the inner product is nonnegative, each bracket is strictly positive
for nonzero gradients and an interior weight. In the
negative-inner-product case, the brackets become
$`(1-\lambda)a-\lambda k`$ and $`\lambda b-(1-\lambda)k`$. Solving their
strict positivity conditions gives
Equation [21](#A6.E21 "In Proposition F.2 (A common local descent direction) ‣ F.3 Local compatibility of the shared-parameter updates ‣ Appendix F Theoretical Analysis of Bidirectional Preference Optimization ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation").
Its lower bound is smaller than its upper bound exactly when
$`k^{2}<ab`$. For any such weight, the strict linear decrease dominates
the quadratic remainder at sufficiently small step sizes. $`\square`$

##### Implications and optimizer scope.

Cauchy–Schwarz gives $`k^{2}\leq ab`$; equality in the conflict case
means the gradients are exactly antiparallel. Thus opposite conditional
labels do not by themselves make the two losses incompatible, but shared
parameters can create genuine update conflicts. This result motivates
examining both branch gradients rather than only the weighted total
loss. It neither identifies a globally optimal $`\lambda`$ nor
guarantees improvement across rollout refreshes.
Equation [18](#A6.E18 "In F.3 Local compatibility of the shared-parameter updates ‣ Appendix F Theoretical Analysis of Bidirectional Preference Optimization ‣ TeachBack: Learning to Teach via Bidirectional Preference Optimizationfor On-Policy Self-Distillation")
analyzes ordinary gradient descent, not the full AdamW update used in
practice. With momentum, preconditioning, clipping, or weight decay, the
relevant first-order diagnostic is $`\langle g_{j},\Delta\theta\rangle`$
for the actual update $`\Delta\theta`$. No optimizer-specific guarantee
is inferred here.
````

# From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation

Minh-Anh Nguyen Affiliation: Nanyang Technological University, Singapore

###### Abstract

Transferring LLMs into System One decision models typically replaces the
language modeling head with a decision head while retaining the full
backbone, making every decision pay for all layers. Yet it remains
unclear how much depth such decisions require. We find that decision
information is concentrated in intermediate layers, with early layers
building essential features and the final six layers removable without
loss, while independently trained smaller models largely mimic the full
model rather than developing specialized decision capabilities. These
findings suggest that efficiency requires not simply smaller backbones,
but shared computation across different depths. Building on this, we
propose NestOne, which jointly trains a full model with prefix
sub-models that share its final layers and decision head through
self-distillation. A lightweight gate reads the hidden state at each
exit to determine whether additional computation is needed, allowing one
model to operate at multiple layers. Across seven decision benchmarks,
our approach matches or outperforms independently trained models at
every depth, while achieving better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs
through adaptive computation.

## 1 Introduction

Dual-process theories distinguish fast, intuitive _System One_ thinking
from slow, deliberate _System Two_ thinking ([Kahneman,
2011](#bib.bib4)). LLM-based agents increasingly require both. Alongside
long chain-of-thought reasoning, they make frequent, lightweight
decisions, such as routing requests, selecting tools, flagging unsafe
prompts, and recognizing intents, often under tight latency
constraints ([TypeSafe AI, 2026](#bib.bib18)). System One decision
models address this need by fine-tuning an LLM to predict a distribution
over candidate options at a single position, without generating
tokens ([InternLM Team, 2026](#bib.bib1); [Li and Wang,
2026](#bib.bib2)). For example, Intern-Decision-4B, built on
Qwen3.5-4B ([Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib3)), achieves 90.0% macro accuracy
across seven decision benchmarks. However, replacing the language
modeling head with a decision head leaves the full backbone intact:
every decision still traverses all 32 layers, incurring a latency of
approximately 1.45 ms per layer at batch size one in our setting.

This raises a fundamental question: _how much computational depth does a
System One decision actually require?_ Fine-tuning is necessary to equip
an LLM with decision capabilities that its base model lacks
(Section [3](#S3 "3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")),
but whether all inherited layers are necessary remains unclear. A
natural way to reduce inference cost is to truncate the backbone,
trading computational depth for potentially lower accuracy. As shown in
the left panel of
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"),
this trade-off is highly non-uniform: reducing the model from 32 to 24
layers largely preserves performance, whereas further truncation causes
substantial degradation, with accuracy dropping from 84.3% to 72.8% and
61.5% at 16 and 12 layers, respectively. This highlights the limitation
of fixed-depth inference, which imposes the same computational budget on
all inputs. Yet, not every decision requires the same amount of
computation. This motivates adaptive depth allocation
(Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"),
right), which allows computation to stop once sufficient depth is
reached, reducing inference cost while preserving accuracy.

We find that decision computation is concentrated in intermediate
layers, while independently trained smaller models largely replicate the
full model rather than develop specialized decision capabilities
(Section [3](#S3 "3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")).
First, decisions emerge within a narrow band of middle layers:
integrated-gradient attribution places 50% of the decision by layer 20
and 80% by layer 23. Consistently, ablating layers 24–29 leaves accuracy
unchanged, while ablating layers 15–23 eliminates the decision margin
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 The Decision Is Written at Mid Depth ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")).
Second, independently trained smaller models behave more like truncated
full models than independent specialists: 89–92% of their correct
decisions are already made by the full model, and their distinct wins
exceed full-model seed disagreement by only 2–3 points, mostly on
low-confidence decisions
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Diagnosing Decision Behavior ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")).
These findings show that training smaller models or truncating the
backbone alone is insufficient. Instead, decision models should learn to
make reliable decisions at multiple depths, adapting computation to
input difficulty.

Based on these findings, we propose NestOne, a framework that transforms
an LLM into a multi-depth System One decision model. NestOne jointly
trains the full model and nested prefix sub-models with shared final
layers and decision head, combining decision supervision and
self-distillation from the full model. This enables a single training
run to produce multiple exits within one checkpoint. At each exit, a
lightweight gate reads the hidden state to determine whether further
computation is needed before producing the decision. Our contributions
are threefold: (i) We show that decision information is concentrated in
intermediate layers and that independently trained smaller models
largely reproduce, rather than specialize in, the full model’s
decisions; (ii) We propose NestOne, a nested multi-depth training
framework that combines decision supervision, self-distillation, and a
hidden-state stopping gate for transfering from LLMs to System One
Models efficiency; (iii) We demonstrate that NestOne matches or
outperforms independently trained models at every depth while reducing
inference cost through adaptive early exiting, using a single training
run to obtain multiple models.

## 2 Background and Related Work

#### System One Decision Models.

System One models take a state, a typed question, and a set of options,
and return a distribution over the options in a single forward pass
without generating text ([TypeSafe AI, 2026](#bib.bib18)). Following
Jev, a growing number of such models are built by adapting a pretrained
LLM. Open recipes read the option-symbol logits of an instruction-tuned
LLM at the last position, with no training, or add LoRA adapters and a
decision head initialized from the language-model head ([Geuter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19)). Intern-Decision ([InternLM Team, 2026](#bib.bib1))
fully fine-tunes Qwen3.5 ([Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib3)) at 0.8B, 2B, and
4B parameters, Microsoft-Decision-1 is post-trained from
Qwen3.5-9B ([Microsoft, 2026](#bib.bib20)), CLM trains a decision model
contrastively from Qwen3-8B ([Kwok et al., 2026](#bib.bib21)), and CS1
trains a 4B model on 12 million clinical decisions ([Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib22)). Other work changes how options are read out. Jeva
encodes the request and each option separately so that the decision is
permutation-equivariant, can abstain, and can reuse option states across
requests ([Li and Wang, 2026](#bib.bib2)); Prism-1 scores each option in
its own attention block so that neither option order nor added rivals
change the result ([Teng et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)); and
Consider-then-Choose removes order dependence at inference without
training ([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)).

#### Evaluating System One Models.

A second line of work asks where System One models can replace LLMs.
System1-Arena compares ten System One models with open and closed LLMs
through a shared decision interface and finds that the fast models
suffice for many single-step decisions but fall behind on long-horizon
tasks ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)). Other studies evaluate System
One models as decision points in agent harnesses ([Li,
2026](#bib.bib26)), and test their transfer to low-resource
languages ([Fadli et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)). These studies treat each
model as a black box and measure what it outputs.

#### Layer Pruning.

The deeper layers of LLMs are known to be partly redundant. Removing a
block of late layers costs little on question answering ([Gromov et al.,
2024](#bib.bib5); [Men et al., 2024](#bib.bib6)), and merging or pruning
layers followed by distillation recovers most of the original accuracy
at a smaller size ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib28); [Muralidharan et
al., 2024](#bib.bib29); [Xia et al., 2024](#bib.bib30)). These methods
produce one smaller model with one fixed depth, and they select layers
by similarity or importance scores computed for generation rather than
by where a decision is computed.

Existing work on System One models focuses on how to train an LLM into a
decision model, whether through readout design, training data, or
architecture, and on how the resulting models behave. Each of these
models keeps the full depth of its backbone, and cost is reduced only by
choosing a smaller backbone. To our knowledge, no prior work asks where
inside the LLM the decision is computed, or what a smaller decision
model learns relative to the full one. We address both questions first
(Section [3](#S3 "3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")).
The answers then guide an efficient way to turn one LLM into a System
One model with several depths: nested prefix training with
self-distillation, and a gate that reads the hidden state before the
readout is computed
(Section [4](#S4 "4 Methodology ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")).

## 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models

We investigate what enables an LLM to become a System One decision model
by comparing the model before and after decision fine-tuning, along with
smaller variants obtained by progressively removing layers from the
fine-tuned model. Specifically, we ask two questions: (i) Where in the
network are decisions computed
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 The Decision Is Written at Mid Depth ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"))?
(ii) What do smaller models learn compared to the full model
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Diagnosing Decision Behavior ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"))?

Setting. We study Qwen3.5-4B ([Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib3)), whose 32
layers we index 0–31, and Intern-Decision-4B ([InternLM Team,
2026](#bib.bib1)), a released decision model built on the same backbone.
To establish a controlled reference, we re-train Qwen3.5-4B using the
same training recipe as Intern-Decision-4B. We refer to this re-trained
model as full32, which serves as the starting point and reference for
all our models. Specifically, full32 is fully fine-tuned with
cross-entropy on the option symbols (AdamW, learning rate
$`2\times 10^{-6}`$ with cosine decay, 2 epochs, about 131K tokens per
step) on 44,941 decisions drawn from public training splits ([Zhang et
al., 2015](#bib.bib7); [Liu and others, 2024](#bib.bib8); [Lin and
others, 2023](#bib.bib13); [Ji and others, 2023](#bib.bib12); [Casanueva
et al., 2020](#bib.bib9); [FitzGerald and others, 2023](#bib.bib10);
[Clark et al., 2019](#bib.bib11)) and deduplicated against all test
states. It reaches $`84.3\pm 0.2\%`$ over three seeds, compared with
90.0% for Intern-Decision-4B, whose larger training set covers the
J-Hard and Typed benchmarks better. We consider two layer-cutting
strategies: a middle cut, $`\mathrm{M}k`$, which retains layers
$`0,\ldots,k{-}3`$ and the final two layers (30–31), and a tail cut,
$`\mathrm{T}k`$, which retains only the first $`k`$ layers
($`0,\ldots,k{-}1`$).

### 3.1 The Decision Is Written at Mid Depth

#### When Does the Decision Become Readable?

We examine when the decision-related information emerges among layers
and when it becomes accessible to the model’s own readout. At each
layer, we extract the residual state at the readout position and
evaluate it using two complementary methods. The *logit
lens* ([Nostalgebraist, 2020](#bib.bib16)) applies the model’s final
normalization and output head to the intermediate state, measuring
whether the model can already produce the correct answer at that depth.
In contrast, a *linear probe* ([Alain and Bengio, 2016](#bib.bib15)),
trained to predict the gold option from the same state, measures how
much decision-relevant information is linearly recoverable, regardless
of whether the model’s own readout can access it. Thus, the gap between
the probe and logit-lens accuracies indicates information that has been
encoded in the residual stream but is not yet usable by the model’s
output head.

Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")(a,b)
shows a consistent pattern across the base model and Intern-Decision-4B.
At layer 15, the probe substantially outperforms the logit lens (76.1%
vs. 26.2% for the base model; 77.9% vs. 38.3% for Intern-Decision-4B),
indicating that decision information is already encoded but not yet
accessible to the model’s readout. This gap closes over layers 19–23:
for Intern-Decision-4B, the probe reaches 90.1% at layer 20, matching
its final accuracy (90.0%), while the logit lens rises from 64.4% at
layer 19 to 89.4% at layer 23. The two readouts then differ by only 0.1
points (vs. 2.8 points for the base model). Neither improves beyond
layer 23, suggesting that layers 24–31 add no linearly recoverable
decision information. Fine-tuning raises the probe plateau by about
seven points (89.5% vs. 83.2% at layer 23) without shifting the depth at
which decisions become readable.

#### Which Layers Shape the Decision?

Readability shows where the answer is available, not which layers build
it there. We therefore decompose the readout state, i.e. the residual
stream at the readout position after the last body layer, as
$`\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{h}_{0}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{29}\Delta_{\ell}`$, where
$`\mathbf{h}_{0}`$ is the token embedding and $`\Delta_{\ell}`$ is the
_contribution_ of layer $`\ell`$, i.e. the sum of the attention and MLP
outputs that it adds to the residual stream. Let $`s_{o}(\mathbf{h})`$
be the logit of option $`o`$ obtained by passing $`\mathbf{h}`$ through
decision head. With gold option $`y`$ among $`K`$ options, the decision
margin is

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
m(\mathbf{h})=s_{y}(\mathbf{h})-\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{o\neq y}s_{o}(\mathbf{h}),
``` |  |

so a larger margin puts more probability on the gold option. Integrated
gradients (IG) ([Sundararajan et al., 2017](#bib.bib14)) attribute the
margin to these contributions. Along the straight path
$`\mathbf{h}(a)=\mathbf{h}_{0}+a(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{h}_{0})`$ from the
embedding to the readout state, layer $`\ell`$ receives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{IG}_{\ell}=\Delta_{\ell}^{\top}\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{\mathbf{h}}m\bigl(\mathbf{h}(a)\bigr)\,da,
``` |  |

its contribution multiplied by the average sensitivity of the margin
along the path. A positive $`\mathrm{IG}_{\ell}`$ means that layer
$`\ell`$ moves the decision distribution toward the gold option, and a
negative one moves it away. The attributions add up to the total margin
change,
$`\sum_{\ell}\mathrm{IG}_{\ell}=m(\mathbf{h})-m(\mathbf{h}_{0})`$.

Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")(c)
reveals three distinct regimes in full32. Early layers (0–14) account
for only 22% of the final margin, with no individual layer contributing
more than 7%. Middle layers (15–23) contribute another 73%, raising the
cumulative margin from 22% to 95%. In contrast, late layers exhibit
substantial but largely canceling updates: layers 27 and 28 contribute
30% and 35%, respectively, raising the running margin to 1.6$`\times`$
its final value, before layer 29 reverses this increase ($`-63\%`$).
Consequently, layers 24–29 contribute only 5% net, primarily rescaling
the margin without changing the preferred option. Overall, the middle
layers play the dominant role in shaping the final decision distribution
of a fine-tuned System One model.

#### Which Layers Can Be Removed?

First, we remove layers without retraining

using a middle cut $`\mathrm{M}k`$ or a tail cut $`\mathrm{T}k`$.
Second, we perform *joint mean ablation*
(Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Which Layers Shape the Decision? ‣ 3.1 The Decision Is Written at Mid Depth ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")b):
for each layer $`\ell`$, we estimate its mean contribution
$`\bar{\Delta}_{\ell}`$ across token positions and approximately 1,700
decisions (up to 400 per benchmark), then replace each layer’s
contribution with this mean at every position. This preserves the
residual stream’s typical scale while removing decision-specific
information. Joint ablation measures the group’s collective contribution
to decision-making, accounting for interactions among its layers while
preventing them from compensating for one another. We report the
resulting change in mean margin and accuracy over 348 decisions.

The two interventions reveal distinct layer roles. In
Intern-Decision-4B, removing layers 24–29 without retraining leaves
accuracy unchanged, while removing layers 24–31 costs only 0.6 points.
Similarly, full32 retains 84.4% accuracy under $`\mathrm{T}24`$,
compared with 84.2% uncut; cutting into the middle band is substantially
more damaging, with $`\mathrm{T}22`$ losing 11.6 points. Mean ablation
confirms this pattern: jointly ablating layers 24–29 slightly improves
margin and accuracy, whereas ablating layers 15–23 eliminates the entire
margin and reduces accuracy by 57.8 points. Early layers, however,
remain essential despite their small direct margin contributions:
ablating layer 0 costs 46.6 points and layer 6 costs 7.8, reflecting
their role in constructing features used by later layers. Consistent
with this, a 16-layer model trained only on layers 8–21 and 30–31, the
layers most affected by fine-tuning, achieves just 35.7%, compared with
73.8% for the $`\mathrm{M}16`$ prefix model.

Takeaway. A decision-tuned LLM writes its decision in a band of middle
layers (15–23) on top of feature-building early layers, and its last
body layers carry no decision.

### 3.2 Diagnosing Decision Behavior

We next examine what independently trained smaller models learn relative
to full32. We fine-tune M24, M16, and M12 using the same data and recipe
as full32, obtaining 83.5%, 73.8%, and 62.9% accuracy, respectively. We
compare their predictions with full32 decision by decision, categorizing
each as both correct, only full32 correct, only the smaller model
correct, or both incorrect. To establish a noise floor, we also compare
full32 against two independently trained seeds (43 and 44), which
uniquely solve 1.4–2.0% of decisions missed by the reference seed
(Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Which Layers Can Be Removed? ‣ 3.1 The Decision Is Written at Mid Depth ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"),
top).

#### Smaller Models Mostly Reproduce Full32’s Decisions.

Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Which Layers Can Be Removed? ‣ 3.1 The Decision Is Written at Mid Depth ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")
shows that M24 rarely makes a correct prediction that full32 misses:
1.9% of decisions, within the noise floor of 1.4–2.0%, and its subset
score (.967) matches that of another full32 seed (.968–.977). M16 and
M12 remain largely subsets of full32: 91% and 89% of their correct
predictions, respectively, are also correct under full32. Moreover, they
lose substantially more decisions than they uniquely solve, by roughly a
factor of three to four (1,012 vs. 352 for M16; 1,513 vs. 425 for M12).

#### Unique Correct Predictions Are Rare and Uncertain.

M16 and M12 uniquely solve only 3.8–4.3% of decisions, just 2–3 points
above the noise floor. These wins are low-confidence (median confidence
0.59–0.61; full32 gold probability 0.23–0.33) and concentrated in Typed
and AGNews (78%), with none in J-Easy or J-Orig. Noise-floor
disagreements show the same pattern: median confidence of 0.52–0.55,
with 71–73% from Typed and AGNews.

#### Smaller Models Also Lose Reliable Decisions.

When full32 assigns the gold option probability $`\geq 0.8`$, M24, M16,
and M12 achieve 99.7%, 84.1%, and 70.4% accuracy, respectively, while
other full32 seeds reach 100%. When full32 is confidently wrong
($`p<0.2`$), smaller models recover only 17–20% of these decisions,
compared with 0.7–0.9% for other seeds. Their apparent gains therefore
reflect greater disagreement with full32, rather than systematic
correction of its errors. Overall, smaller models reproduce many of
full32’s correct decisions but lose substantial reliable capability
without consistently correcting its mistakes.

Takeaway. Smaller decision models trained on their own do not learn a
different solution; they learn a degraded version of the full model’s.
This suggests training them as *parts* of the full model, sharing its
weights and using its own output distribution as a target, so that each
smaller model inherits as much of the full model’s skill as its depth
allows.

## 4 Methodology

Section [3](#S3 "3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")
shows that intermediate layers already encode sufficient information to
identify the correct option, while smaller decision models are best
viewed as prefixes of the full model that should learn to imitate it.
Building on these findings, NestOne trains nested prefix sub-models
within the full model using self-distillation to facilitate information
sharing across depths
(Sections [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Prefix Sub-Models with a Shared Coda ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")
and [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Nested Training Objective ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")).
A lightweight gate then selects the exit depth for each decision based
on question difficulty
(Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Choosing Depth per Decision ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")).

### 4.1 Prefix Sub-Models with a Shared Coda

Let $`f_{0},\ldots,f_{31}`$ be the layers of the decision model, and let
$`\mathbf{h}^{(e)}=f_{e}\circ\cdots\circ f_{0}(x)`$ be the residual
stream after layer $`e`$. We call the last two layers together with the
decision head the *coda* $`C=\mathrm{head}\circ f_{31}\circ f_{30}`$. An
*exit* after layer $`e`$ reads out the decision distribution

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{e}(\cdot\mid x)=\mathrm{softmax}\bigl(C(\mathbf{h}^{(e)})_{\mathcal{O}}/T_{e}\bigr),
``` |  |

where $`\mathcal{O}`$ is the set of option symbols and $`T_{e}`$ is a
temperature fitted per exit. The sub-model $`S_{k}`$ with $`k`$ layers
uses the exit after layer $`e_{k}=k-3`$, eg. $`S_{12}`$, $`S_{16}`$, and
$`S_{24}`$ exit after layers 9, 13, and 21, and $`S_{32}`$ is the full
model. This design follows
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 The Decision Is Written at Mid Depth ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation").
Prefixes keep the early feature-building layers that no smaller model
can do without, and $`S_{24}`$ reaches into the decision band while
dropping the late layers that carry no decision. The coda is shared so
that every exit reads out through the same final layers and head.
Because each sub-model is a prefix of the next, the states computed for
a shallow exit are reused when a decision continues to a deeper exit, so
stopping early never requires recomputation.

### 4.2 Nested Training Objective

Following
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Diagnosing Decision Behavior ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"),
we train all sub-models jointly with the full model, so that they share
its weights and learn from its output. Let $`y`$ be the gold option and
$`p_{k}`$ the distribution of $`S_{k}`$. At each step we compute

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}={}`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{CE}(p_{32},y)+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{t}}\Bigl[(1-\alpha_{t})\,\mathrm{CE}(p_{k},y)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad+\alpha_{t}\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\mathrm{sg}(p_{32})\,\|\,p_{k}\bigr)\Bigr],`$ |  |

where $`\mathrm{sg}`$ stops the gradient and the KL term is taken over
the options of the decision. The full model is trained only on the gold
option, and each sub-model learns both from the gold option and from the
full model’s distribution ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib17)). This
self-distillation term makes each sub-model imitate the full model, as
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Diagnosing Decision Behavior ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")
suggests. We linearly increase $`\alpha_{t}`$ from 0 to 0.5 over the
first half of training, so that the full model’s distribution becomes a
target only once it is informative. The full model serves as its own
teacher, so NestOne needs no external decision signal. All parameters
are fine-tuned with the recipe of full32.

### 4.3 Choosing Depth per Decision

A single checkpoint now offers exits at intermediate depths, as well as
the full model. To choose an exit for each decision, we attach a
lightweight gate to each intermediate exit. The gate reads the hidden
state $`\mathbf{z}_{e}`$ at the readout position immediately after the
corresponding middle layer:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{e}(x)=\sigma\bigl(\mathbf{w}_{e}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{e}+b_{e}\bigr),
``` |  |

where $`\sigma`$ is the sigmoid function. Each gate is a logistic
regression model trained to predict whether its corresponding exit
answers correctly, using labels $`c_{e}=\mathbf{1}[\arg\max p_{e}=y]`$
and class-balanced weights. At inference, the model runs layer by layer.
At each intermediate exit, it stops if $`g_{e}(x)\geq\tau`$, runs the
coda, and returns $`\arg\max p_{e}`$; otherwise, it continues from the
saved states. The final exit always answers.

Answering at exit $`e`$ costs $`e+3`$ layers: the prefix plus the coda.
Because the gate reads the hidden state before the coda, evaluating it
requires only a dot product and a sigmoid operation. A single threshold
$`\tau`$ is shared across exits and selected on held-out data as the
most aggressive value for which macro accuracy remains within $`\delta`$
points of always exiting at the final intermediate depth.

## 5 Experimental Results

We evaluate NestOne on three questions: (i) Does one nested checkpoint
match models trained separately at each depth
(Section [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 One Checkpoint versus Separate Models ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"))?
(ii) Can the gate choose depth per decision without losing accuracy
(Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Choosing Depth per Decision ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"))?
(iii) Why does nested training help
(Section [5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Why Nested Training Helps ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"))?

Setup. All models start from Qwen3.5-4B and use the data, recipe, and
benchmark of
Section [3](#S3 "3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation").
We compare NestOne with full32, M24, M16, and M12, each trained
separately on its own. Every model, nested or separate, is trained with
three seeds, which change the data order and, for NestOne, which
sub-models are sampled. One NestOne run takes 80 minutes on a single
H200, against 94 minutes for training the four separate models. At batch
size one, the 12-, 16-, 24-, and 32-layer exits take 18.4, 23.8, 34.7,
and 46.5 ms per decision. For early exit, we count cost as the mean
number of layers run per decision, including the coda. We fit each gate
and its threshold on one benchmark-stratified half of the benchmark
together with the calibration split, and report results on the other
half. Each half is used once for testing.

#### The Hidden State Predicts Whether an Exit Is Correct.

We first measure how well each signal separates correct from wrong
answers at an exit, using AUROC on the test halves. The state gate
reaches 0.86, 0.89, and 0.88 at exits 9, 13, and 21, against 0.85, 0.86,
and 0.84 for the calibrated top probability, and 0.86, 0.86, and 0.84
for the confidence gate that combines top probability, margin, and
entropy. The hidden state thus carries more information about
correctness than the exit’s own confidence, especially at exit 13 and
21, and it is available before the coda is computed.

#### Stopping Rules.

We compare the state gate with three rules based on the exit
distribution $`p_{e}`$: *Max-prob*, which uses the calibrated top
probability; *confidence gate*, a logistic regression on the top
probability, top-two margin, and entropy; and *State + confidence*,
which combines these features with the hidden state $`\mathbf{z}_{e}`$.
All rules use the same training labels, class-balanced weights, and
fitting data. They evaluate exits 9, 13, and 21 in order, stopping at
the first score above $`\tau`$ or answering at exit 21 otherwise;
$`\tau`$ is selected as in
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Choosing Depth per Decision ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation").
The key difference is inference cost: distribution-based rules must run
the coda to obtain $`p_{e}`$, adding two layers at each checked exit,
whereas the state gate reads $`\mathbf{z}_{e}`$ before the coda and runs
it only once, at the selected exit. Fixed exits 13 and 21 serve as
references.

#### The State Gate Gives the Best Trade-off.

Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.2 Choosing Depth per Decision ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")
compares the gating rules at two accuracy thresholds. The state gate
achieves the lowest average depth at both $`\delta=0.25`$ and
$`\delta=2`$, using 21.6 and 20.2 layers, respectively, compared with 24
layers for the fixed exit. On hard benchmarks, it uses 23.9–24.0 layers,
whereas confidence-based rules incur two additional layers per readout,
resulting in an average depth of 26.6–27.9 layers. On the four largest
benchmarks, which account for 96% of decisions, the state gate preserves
the fixed-exit accuracy, with changes of only $`+0.08`$ and $`+0.03`$
points at the two thresholds. The other rules also remain within 0.13
points of the fixed exit, but achieve savings differently:
confidence-based rules save computation on easy decisions while spending
more on hard ones, whereas the state gate reduces depth on easy
decisions (to 20.2 and 17.5 layers) without incurring additional
computation on hard decisions. Randomly mixing exits 13 and 21 to match
the same average depth instead reduces accuracy by 3.0 and 4.7 points.

#### The Accuracy Drop Is Concentrated on Small Benchmarks.

The state gate’s macro-accuracy drop ($`-1.56`$ and $`-1.68`$ points)
comes almost entirely from the three smallest benchmarks, which contain
only 430 of 12,351 decisions but account for three of seven
macro-averaged benchmarks. At $`\delta=0.25`$, it misses seven
additional decisions on these benchmarks but loses none on larger ones.
Compute savings depend on traffic mix: average depth ranges from 24
layers on hard-only traffic to 20.2 on easy-only traffic (22.1 for an
even mix), and to 17.5 layers at $`\delta=2`$ (20.7 for an even mix;
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"),
right). Since the selected threshold varies across training splits (0.80
vs. 0.92), it should be calibrated on held-out deployment-like traffic.
Adding the full model as a final exit further improves the trade-off: at
$`\delta=2`$, the gate matches its accuracy on the four largest
benchmarks ($`-0.01`$ points) while using 23.9 layers on average, 19.8
on easy decisions, and 31.3 on hard ones.

#### Shallow Exits Keep More of the Full Model’s Skill.

Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Diagnosing Decision Behavior ‣ 3 Understanding Depth in System One Models ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")
found that separately trained small models are degraded copies of the

Table [5](#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5.3 Why Nested Training Helps ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")
shows that nesting reduces this degradation rather than adding new
skills. At 12 layers, the exit wins no more decisions of its own than
M12 does (3.6% vs. 3.8%). Instead, it loses fewer of full32’s correct
decisions (23.4% vs. 26.5%), so its recall of full32’s skill rises from
0.66 to 0.69. On decisions full32 is confident about, it is right 72.8%
of the time, against 69.5% for M12. The 16-layer exit shows the same
pattern: recall rises from 0.79 to 0.81, and accuracy on confident
decisions from 83.3% to 86.0%, with no more wins of its own (3.5% vs.
3.6%). This is the effect the self-distillation term targets: the
shallow exit imitates the full model and keeps more of what the full
model knows.

#### Deep Exits Stay Close to the Full Model.

At 24 and 32 layers, the nested exits keep as much of full32’s skill as
M24 (recall 0.95–0.96 vs. 0.96), and their own wins (2.4–2.8%) are close
to those of M24 (2.2%). Nesting neither adds nor removes skill at these
depths. This matches
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.1 One Checkpoint versus Separate Models ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation"),
where the deep exits tie their separate counterparts. The benefit of
nesting at depth is therefore cost, not accuracy: one checkpoint
provides the 24-layer exit and the full model together, and the gate can
choose between depths for each decision.

#### Where Does NestOne Write the Decision?

Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Deep Exits Stay Close to the Full Model. ‣ 5.3 Why Nested Training Helps ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ From Large Language Models to Efficient System One Models via Adaptive Computation")
shows that nested training shifts decision formation toward earlier
layers, reversing the delay caused by plain fine-tuning. In the 32-layer
exit, layers 0–16 account for half of the final margin (bootstrap range
15–16), and layers 0–20 account for 80% (19–20), compared with layers 20
and 23 in full32. The first 20 layers contribute 77% of the margin,
versus 47% in full32, so the full model largely commits to its decision
within the prefix shared by the 24-layer exit. Later layers mainly
rescale this decision: their cumulative contribution peaks at
2.5$`\times`$ the final margin before layer 29 removes the excess.
Jointly ablating layers 24–29 increases the margin by 4% and reduces
accuracy by only 0.6 points. The 24-layer exit follows a similar
pattern: layers 15–21 form its decision, with layer 20 alone
contributing 48%. Thus, nested training concentrates decision formation
in layers shared across deeper exits.

## 6 Conclusion

We studied adaptive computation for System One decision-making in LLMs.
Our analysis reveals that decisions primarily emerge in middle layers,
with later layers contributing little additional information, and that
smaller independently trained models largely replicate the full model’s
decisions. Motivated by these findings, NestOne jointly trains nested
prefix sub-models via self-distillation and employs a hidden-state gate
for adaptive early exiting. Across three seeds, a single checkpoint
matches or outperforms independently trained models at all exit depths,
improving 12-layer accuracy by 2.9 points and approaching the 32-layer
model within 0.6 points at 24 layers. Adaptive exiting further reduces
computation by 16–27% on easy decisions without sacrificing accuracy,
while retaining fixed-depth exits for harder cases. These results
highlight the benefits of nested training for aligning shallow exits
with full-model decisions. Future work will investigate end-to-end
latency, gate calibration under real-world traffic, and scaling to
larger models and finer-grained exits.
````

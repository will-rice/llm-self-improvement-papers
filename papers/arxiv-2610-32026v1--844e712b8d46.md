# RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training

Binghang Lu Affiliation: Purdue University    Yige Sun
Affiliation: Independent Researcher^(\*)Corresponding authors    Guang
Lin Affiliation: Purdue University

###### Abstract

Conventional neural-network training typically follows a predefined
optimization recipe, motivating a LLM agent that monitors training
dynamics and adjusts optimizer settings as training progresses, but a
fixed agent policy does not accumulate experience across runs. We
introduce RSI-Opt, a plug-and-play Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer
Agents which evolves the knowledge that guides this process: a prompt, a
playbook of procedures, a library of tested optimizer code, and a
decision cadence. A meta-agent rewrites this policy using evidence from
earlier runs, and an archive retains evaluated policies for further
evolution. Across 5 generations on three training tasks, the meta-agents
develop reusable procedures and code and conduct their own CPU proxy
experiments. RSI-Opt substantially improves optimizer-agent performance,
with representative raw-space summary $`z`$-scores increasing from
$`+10.48`$ to $`+14.33`$ on the evolution suite with Opus 5.5 and from
$`-0.36`$ to $`+3.11`$ on held-out tasks with GPT-5.5 through
cross-model policy transfer. On the Burgers PDE, it reduces mean
relative $`L_{2}`$ error by approximately $`2.7`$-fold with Opus 5.5 and
$`8.8`$-fold with GPT-6.1 over the fixed-prompt agent baseline, although
PDE transfer varies across LLMs and tasks. Controlled comparisons show
that the evolved policy combines reusable optimization knowledge with
adaptation to each task: initial decisions capture most observed gains
on seen tasks, while continued adaptation improves outcomes on unseen
tasks. These findings demonstrate how procedures and executable tools
accumulated across training runs can improve optimizer-agent performance
and support transfer to scientific machine learning.

## 1 Introduction

Neural-network training usually begins with an optimizer and schedule
chosen in advance. Their effectiveness can depend on the architecture,
data, and training dynamics, yet selecting a new recipe often requires
repeated runs and manual intervention. A coding agent offers another
form of adaptation: it can inspect a training run and revise the
optimizer’s program while the model trains.  
Concurrent work on reactive optimizers, OPAL \[[2](#bib.bib35)\],
studies this within-run setting. A single LLM agent observes training
dynamics and revises the update rule, learning rates, schedules, and
diagnostics during an individual run. Its conversation persists within
that run, but its fixed initial policy does not retain lessons for the
next one. A useful optimizer change, a misleading diagnostic, or a
tested implementation must be rediscovered when a new run starts.We
study whether optimizer agents can improve this initial policy across
runs through recursive self-improvement. RSI-Opt makes the agent’s
accumulated knowledge an explicit, editable object: a prompt, a playbook
of procedures and lessons, a library of optimizer and diagnostic code,
and a decision cadence. An outer meta-agent reads evidence from earlier
runs, modifies a parent policy, and evaluates the child on a training
suite. An archive supports continued exploration and inheritance,
following the archive-based approach of the Darwin Gödel
Machine \[[35](#bib.bib1)\]. The underlying LLM weights, meta-agent
instructions, and evaluator remain fixed; the policy used to improve
optimizers is the object that evolves
([Figure 1](#S3.F1 "In 3 Method ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training")).
This separation makes the learned knowledge inspectable, but it also
exposes a generalization question. A policy can improve its evolution
score by accumulating broadly useful procedures or by fitting recipes to
a few familiar tasks. Better training-suite performance alone does not
distinguish the two. Nor does a growing code library show whether an
agent still needs to adapt during a new run. We therefore examine the
content of the evolved policies, their transfer to unseen tasks and
other LLMs, and the contribution of decisions made after the initial
optimizer change. Our contributions are:

- •
  An explicit policy-evolution framework for optimizer agents. RSI-Opt
  adds cross-run policy evolution to the within-run optimizer-agent
  setting. Our experiments connect a coding agent to an editable
  optimizer interface, allowing it to monitor training dynamics and
  revise update rules during a run. fixed baseline prompt, and four
  example tasks, with attribution and implementation details in
  [Section 4](#S4 "4 Experimental setup ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training").
  The contribution is the evolving policy, outer search, and study of
  the resulting knowledge.
- •
  An evaluation of transfer and online adaptation. We evaluate nine
  single-GPU tasks with three in-run LLMs, fresh evaluation seeds,
  comparisons of reported means, and controls using an unedited library
  template, a frozen first optimizer change, a one-decision agent, and
  the full agent. Three tasks guide evolution; three ML tasks and three
  PDE problems test transfer beyond that suite.
- •
  Evidence of selective transfer and specialization. Evolved policies
  improve the training suite for all three LLMs. GPT-5.5 and GPT-6.1
  improve the mean summary score in every task group; Opus 5.5 improves
  on four of six held-out tasks but has a lower aggregate held-out
  summary score. Initial adaptation captures most of the observed
  benefit on seen tasks, while later decisions improve unseen-task
  performance for both baseline and evolved agents. Held-out ML transfer
  peaks early as continued evolution increasingly fits the training
  suite.

The meta-agents accumulate substantive artifacts: reusable update
modules, parameter-role checks, scheduling procedures, and CPU proxy
experiments. We analyze these artifacts alongside their measured
outcomes rather than treating higher archive scores as sufficient
evidence of general improvement. The harness, policies, transcripts, and
results are released.

## 2 Related work

#### Learned and discovered optimizers.

Learned optimizers \[[1](#bib.bib8), [20](#bib.bib7)\] meta-train an
update rule across tasks, while symbolic search has discovered update
algorithms such as Lion \[[4](#bib.bib6)\]. The resulting update program
is typically fixed before a target training run, although its updates
can depend on the current state. In RSI-Opt, the evolved object is the
optimizer agent’s policy, which guides further program edits during each
target run. Established optimizers such as Adam \[[13](#bib.bib9)\],
AdamW \[[16](#bib.bib10)\], Shampoo \[[8](#bib.bib12)\],
SOAP \[[30](#bib.bib13)\], and Muon \[[12](#bib.bib11)\], together with
recent extensions such as spectral orthogonal gradient projection for
continual learning \[[17](#bib.bib34)\], supply building blocks that
agents can select and compose.

#### Agents that improve programs and policies.

LLMs have been used to optimize prompts and
solutions \[[33](#bib.bib5)\], evolve prompts \[[7](#bib.bib4)\],
improve code-generation scaffolds \[[34](#bib.bib3)\], design agentic
systems \[[10](#bib.bib2)\], and evolve algorithms against an
evaluator \[[21](#bib.bib18)\]. The Darwin Gödel
Machine \[[35](#bib.bib1)\] maintains an archive of modified coding
agents and samples parents for further exploration, drawing on the idea
of a self-improving Gödel machine \[[26](#bib.bib20)\]. Our outer search
adopts this archive-based structure. Persistent natural-language
lessons \[[27](#bib.bib21)\], skill libraries \[[31](#bib.bib22)\], and
self-evolving procedural memory learned from contrasting multi-model
trajectories \[[5](#bib.bib32)\] provide related mechanisms for carrying
experience across episodes. RSI-Opt combines procedures and executable
tools in an optimizer-agent policy. Every valid evaluated child enters
the archive; scores guide subsequent parent and final policy selection
rather than acting as a strict improvement filter.

#### Optimizer agents within a training run.

We found concurrent work, OPAL, introduces a coding agent that monitors
training dynamics and revises optimizer and diagnostic programs within a
single run. Each new run starts from a fixed prompt, without retaining
experience from previous runs. RSI-Opt addresses the complementary
problem of improving the agent’s policy across runs by accumulating and
refining optimization procedures and executable tools. We examine how
this knowledge transfers to unseen tasks and other LLMs, and when
continued evolution leads to specialization. Population-based
training \[[11](#bib.bib19)\] also adapts training hyperparameters
through selection and replacement across a population of runs.

#### Optimization for physics-informed training.

PINN training can suffer from imbalanced gradients across residual and
boundary losses \[[32](#bib.bib15)\] and ill-conditioned loss
landscapes, motivating approaches such as Adam followed by L-BFGS and
other second-order methods \[[25](#bib.bib16)\], as well as adaptive
optimizers for hard-constrained PINN training \[[18](#bib.bib33)\]. Our
PDE problems test transfer beyond the ML tasks used for evolution. They
assess the applicability of evolved optimizer-agent knowledge; they do
not establish a new PDE discretization or a universally better PINN
optimizer.

### 3.1 Within-run adaptation

The inner loop exposes the optimizer’s update rule as an editable
program, allowing a coding agent to inspect training losses and
diagnostic reports and revise the optimization procedure at scheduled
decision points. The training script, model architecture, data order,
loss, batch size, and number of parameter updates are fixed. At $`D`$
scheduled decisions, the agent receives the training-loss history since
its previous decision, reports from its analysis program, errors, and
timing information. It can inspect and edit files in its workspace and
run shell commands without network or GPU access. The agent edits two
programs. coder receives gradients at each update and modifies the
parameters in place. Analyst computes read-only diagnostics every
updates. A new updater is tested over a short trial window and rolled
back if it raises an exception. Training pauses while the agent decides,
so agent latency affects wall-clock cost rather than the number of model
updates. Conversation history persists across decisions within the run.
The final evaluation metric is withheld from the in-run agent and used
by the outer evaluator after the run. Our _single-agent baseline_ uses
fixed prompt without an accumulated playbook or library. This prompt
supplies the task description, protocol, and program interfaces. The
same template is instantiated for each run, and experience from
completed runs is not retained in the next run’s policy.

### 3.2 The evolving policy

A policy $`\pi`$ specifies what the in-run agent brings to a run:

- •
  Prompt template: instructions with task-specific fields for the goal,
  parameter table, model summary, starting optimizer, and protocol.
- •
  Playbook: procedures and lessons written in natural language.
- •
  Code library: indexed Python modules for update rules, schedules,
  parameter grouping, stability checks, and diagnostics.
- •
  Cadence: a front-loaded or uniform decision schedule and the frequency
  of diagnostic checks.

The playbook and library are read-only references during a run; the
agent can copy a library module into its workspace and use or modify the
copy. The experiment fixes the number of decision opportunities rather
than allowing the policy to increase it. Equal decision limits and cost
caps do not imply equal realized token use, diagnostic overhead, or
training time. Generation 0 uses the baseline prompt with one additional
paragraph pointing to an empty playbook and library.

### 3.3 Across-run policy evolution

The outer loop maintains an archive of evaluated policies, following the
archive-based search of the Darwin Gödel Machine \[[35](#bib.bib1)\]. To
generate a child, it samples an eligible parent $`\pi_{p}`$ with
probability proportional to

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\frac{\operatorname{sigmoid}\!\left(\lambda(s_{p}-\widetilde{s})\right)}{1+n_{p}},
``` |  |

where $`s_{p}`$ is the parent’s score, $`\widetilde{s}`$ is the archive
median score, and $`n_{p}`$ counts its children. The score term favors
stronger parents, while the child count reduces repeated selection of
the same branch.
A separate *meta-agent* receives a copy of the parent policy and digests
of its evaluation runs. Each digest contains the final metric and
z-score, the in-run agent’s recorded rationales, final optimizer
program, and notes. The meta-agent also sees archive entries with
per-task scores and changelogs. It edits the policy and is instructed to
record its changes, supporting evidence, and expected effects. Its
CPU-only shell permits unit tests and proxy experiments. The
instructions encourage transferable lessons and prohibit task names and
task-specific branches; this instruction does not itself prevent
specialization. Validation checks required template fields, Python
parsing, and size limits. A child that fails these checks is returned to
the meta-agent for repair, at most twice. Every valid child is evaluated
on the training suite and added to the archive, including children that
score below their parent. Thus the archive retains explored
alternatives, while performance guides further parent selection and the
final policy choice.

#### Evaluation score.

For task $`t`$, let $`h_{t}(m)=m`$ for ML metrics and
$`h_{t}(m)=\log_{10}m`$ for PDE solution errors. We estimate the static
recipe’s mean $`\mu_{t}`$ and standard deviation $`\sigma_{t}`$ from
five seeds in this transformed metric space. A policy run with final
metric $`m_{t,s}`$ receives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
z_{t,s}=d_{t}\,\frac{h_{t}(m_{t,s})-\mu_{t}}{\sigma_{t}},\qquad d_{t}=\begin{cases}+1,&\text{higher is better},\\
-1,&\text{lower is better}.\end{cases}
``` |  |

Positive values indicate improvement over the static reference. The
policy score is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s(\pi)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s\in S}z_{t,s}.
``` |  |

Policies use the same task and seed set during evolution. This
normalization is an aggregation rule, not a test of statistical
significance; tasks with small static variability can contribute large
z-scores.

#### Scope of recursive self-improvement.

The meta-agent improves the policy by which the in-run agent improves
optimizers. Later generations inherit and revise the procedures and
tools accumulated by earlier ones. The underlying LLM weights,
meta-agent instructions, scoring rule, and designated task splits remain
fixed. This is recursive improvement of an agent policy, without
modification of the meta-agent’s own improvement procedure. The
experiments measure the resulting policies; a separate matched-budget
control would be needed to isolate the benefit of inheritance over
independent proposals.

4: for $`g=1,\ldots,G`$ do

### 3.4 Implementation

Both the in-run agent and the meta-agent operate through a tool-calling
loop on the API, with access to four tools: bash, read_file, write_file,
and edit_file. The backend is self-contained and leaves the inner-loop
training code untouched. For isolation, each agent’s shell executes in
its own network namespace with no access to GPUs or API credentials.
When a conversation grows beyond 160k tokens, the agent writes a summary
of its progress, which then replaces the earlier history. Runs are
scheduled across GPUs through a file-based queue, and because training
halts whenever the agent is deciding, multiple runs can share a single
GPU efficiently. Every LLM is configured with high reasoning effort.
Filesystem restrictions and the extent of their coverage are detailed in
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Appendix ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training").

## 4 Experimental setup

We compare baseline single-agent baseline that studies a coding agent
that monitors a single training run and revises its optimizer as that
run unfolds At generation 0, the only change is one added paragraph
pointing the agent to the policy folder, which starts empty. The
contributions of RSI-Opt are the policy representation and the procedure
that evolves it across runs.

#### Tasks.

We evaluate nine single-GPU training tasks in three groups
([Table 1](#S4.T1 "In Tasks. ‣ 4 Experimental setup ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training")).
Each task has a fixed model, loss, data protocol, update budget, and
starting optimizer. The agent can change optimization but not these task
definitions.

- •
  Training suite: ResNet-18 \[[9](#bib.bib25)\] on
  CIFAR-10 \[[14](#bib.bib26)\] with SGD-Nesterov and a one-cycle
  schedule; a 34M-parameter GPT on FineWeb \[[23](#bib.bib27)\] with
  AdamW and warmup-cosine decay; and a small UNet trained by flow
  matching \[[15](#bib.bib28)\] on MNIST with AdamW, cosine decay, and
  EMA. These tasks provide the feedback used by the meta-agent during
  evolution.
- •
  Held-out ML: a ViT \[[6](#bib.bib23)\] on CIFAR-100 with AdamW and
  warmup-cosine decay; a 2-layer LSTM on WikiText-2 \[[19](#bib.bib24)\]
  with Adam and step decay; and OPAL’s noisy-label CIFAR-10 task with
  10% incorrect labels. In the last task, the agent sees a development
  set but not test accuracy.
- •
  Held-out PDE: three existing neural solvers with their models,
  sampling, losses, and update budgets unchanged. They are a
  PINN \[[24](#bib.bib14)\] for viscous Burgers in 1D with full-batch
  Adam and 10k updates; a PINN for heat diffusion in 2D with resampled
  collocation points and 30k Adam updates; and a variable-order
  time-fractional diffusion solver using finite-difference
  discretization and full-batch Adam for 10k updates, related to
  fractional physics-informed training \[[22](#bib.bib17)\]. Each is
  evaluated by relative $`L_{2}`$ solution error.

Held-out tasks are designated for transfer evaluation rather than policy
selection.

#### Models.

We use Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-5.5, and GPT-6.1 as in-run agents, all with
high reasoning effort. Both the meta-agent and in-run agent use Opus 5.5
during evolution. The resulting policy is then used unchanged with each
in-run model.

#### Baselines and budget controls.

The static reference is each script’s original optimizer and schedule,
evaluated on five seeds. The tuned-static reference multiplies its
learning rate by $`\{\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2},1,2,4\}`$, with three seeds
per multiplier. The best multiplier is selected on the evaluation metric
itself; this is an optimistic oracle comparison, not a deployable tuning
protocol. The single-agent baseline is fixed-prompt agent without an
accumulated playbook or library. Full baseline and RSI-Opt runs share a
backend, 20 decision opportunities, the available schedule families, and
a per-run cost cap. Cadence and realized resource use can differ.
[Section 5.5](#S5.SS5 "5.5 Reusable code and online adaptation ‣ 5 Results ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training")
additionally compares an unedited evolved-library recipe, a frozen first
optimizer change, and a one-decision agent.

#### Policy selection and final evaluation.

Evolution evaluates each policy on the training suite using seeds 100
and 101. Before final evaluation, the three best eligible policies by
archive score are re-evaluated on seeds 102 and 103, and the highest
four-seed mean determines the final policy. Reported final comparisons
use fresh seeds 200, 201, and 202, which do not guide evolution or
final-policy selection. Training-suite metrics guide the search even
when a task script names its evaluation split “test”; fresh seeds on
these tasks assess performance within the seen suite, not generalization
to unseen tasks. We report raw metrics as mean $`\pm`$ standard
deviation across seeds. Final aggregate comparisons use the raw-space
summary scores defined in
[Section 3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training"),
recomputed from the printed statistics in
[Table 2](#S5.T2 "In 5.2 How do agents compare with static recipes? ‣ 5 Results ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training").
The corrected difference table reports signed differences of these
means; it does not infer paired confidence intervals or individual-seed
win counts from summary statistics. We also evaluate the best eligible
policies after generations 1 and 3 on the same fresh seeds. This
trajectory analysis measures transfer over evolution; held-out outcomes
are not used to replace the preselected final policy.

## 5 Results

### 5.1 What knowledge evolves?

Figure 2: Left: policies evaluated during evolution on training-suite
seeds 100 and 101. Gray lines connect children to parents; the red line
shows the best archive score so far. Right: per-task z-scores of
generation 0 and the selected final policy on these evolution seeds.
Fresh-seed comparisons are reported separately.

Evolution produced 21 policies over 5 generations of four children, with
no invalid policies
([Figure 2](#S5.F2 "In 5.1 What knowledge evolves? ‣ 5 Results ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training")).
Generation 0, which augments the fixed prompt with references to an
empty playbook and library, scored +10.30. The best archive score
reached +14.63 for policy p016 in generation 4. Re-evaluating the three
highest-scoring eligible candidates on two additional seeds selected
p016: its four-seed mean was +14.55, compared with +14.42 and +14.19 for
the other candidates. These archive scores guide search and selection;
the remaining comparisons use fresh seeds.

#### Procedures and executable tools.

The meta-agents changed the fixed prompt only modestly: children added
0–23 lines, mainly summaries pointing to the playbook. Most accumulated
knowledge appeared in procedures and library modules. Early policies
recorded useful changes, including Muon on transformer matrices,
learning-rate decay to zero, and parameter-role-specific treatment. They
also warned against interpreting a transient loss drop after a rate
reduction as durable progress or triggering changes from noisy window
means. The resulting library included a hybrid updater using Muon on
selected tensors and the starting rule elsewhere, schedule helpers, a
parameter-role classifier, diagnostics, and interface checks. A
recurring procedure was to make an early, evidence-based change and
retain it unless later observations justified another edit. Nine of the
20 meta-agents, including the one that produced p016, reached the
50-call or 30-minute limit before writing a changelog entry. Their edits
were retained, so changelog coverage is incomplete even though every
child differs from its parent.

#### A proxy-supported optimizer change.

The largest observed task-score jump followed a hypothesis that a
meta-agent tested using its own CPU proxy. It trained a small GroupNorm
UNet by flow matching from a constant-rate AdamW baseline. Adding Muon
on hidden convolutions reduced validation loss in every configuration it
tested, including 0.188 to 0.174 at 3000 steps. Within that proxy sweep,
the learning rate on the remaining Adam-updated tensors mattered more
than Muon’s own rate. The meta-agent recorded the proposal as an
untested transfer from the proxy to the actual task. Subsequent in-run
agents applied it, and the UNet’s evolution score rose from about $`+9`$
to $`+19`$ on both seeds in generation 3. The proposal also appeared in
other lineages after their meta-agents read archive summaries. This
sequence connects a recorded hypothesis, executable tests, and a later
gain; isolating the change’s causal contribution would require a
controlled parent-to-child replay.

#### Exposure and exclusions.

Before filesystem restrictions were added, one generation-1 meta-agent
read description of its noisy-label example, a designated held-out task.
That policy and its descendant (2 policies) were excluded from parent
selection and final selection. The audit identified no additional direct

With Opus 5.5, RSI-Opt raises the training-suite score from +10.48 to

#### Unseen tasks with the evolution model.

On the six held-out tasks, Opus has a lower aggregate summary score:
+5.22 for the baseline versus +4.87 for RSI-Opt. The held-out ML and PDE

metric on four of the six held-out tasks; this count need not agree with
the direction of the standardized aggregate.

#### Other in-run models.

The same policy, used unchanged with GPT-5.5, raises its mean held-out
score from -0.36 to +3.11. It also brings the training-suite score close
to Opus with the same policy (+14.10 versus +14.33). Its held-out ML and
PDE summary-score differences are +5.36 and +1.59, respectively.

GPT-6.1 improves the aggregate summary score in every task group. Its
training-suite difference is +4.31, and its held-out ML difference is
+6.88, including approximately 6 additional accuracy points on each
image-classification task. Its PDE difference is positive: +0.13. On
Burgers, mean relative error falls from $`4.82\times 10^{-3}`$ to
$`5.46\times 10^{-4}`$, about $`8.8`$ times lower. Mean heat error falls
from $`3.27\times 10^{-5}`$ to $`1.60\times 10^{-5}`$, about $`2.0`$
times lower, while fractional-PDE error changes little ($`0.322`$ to
$`0.319`$). Its overall held-out summary score rises from +0.80 to
+4.30, although its LSTM loss worsens.

These results suggest that portable knowledge helps when it compensates
for a model’s weaker task-specific choices, but can interfere with an
already effective strategy, consistent with evidence that different LLMs
hold complementary knowledge \[[3](#bib.bib30)\]. They do not support a
uniform improvement across models or domains. Library use by the GPT
agents

shows that the artifact is adopted; its performance determines whether
that adoption is beneficial.

#### The unedited template has limited transfer.

Freezing the evolved agent’s first change yields training-suite score

benefit in the initial adaptation. They do not establish equivalence
between the conditions or prove that later decisions are never useful.

with the first change frozen and $`1.5\times 10^{-3}`$ with the full
agent. These are comparisons of final outcomes, not measurements before
and after an edit within one trajectory. Together, the controls support
task-conditioned selection and continued adaptation as useful
complements to reusable code. They do not isolate an internal reasoning
mechanism or establish safety on unseen tasks.

### 5.6 Transfer to PDE training

PDE problems provide the largest shift from the evolution suite. Several
agent configurations substantially reduce solution error relative to the
original Adam recipes
([Table 2](#S5.T2 "In 5.2 How do agents compare with static recipes? ‣ 5 Results ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training")).
Heat benefits particularly from schedules that reduce the learning rate
near the end of training; the static recipe’s final errors span
0.008–0.73 across seeds. These gains should be interpreted alongside the
limited static tuning performed here. The incremental effect of the
evolved policy varies by LLM and task. Opus’s PDE summary score falls
from +1.72 to +1.51: Burgers and heat improve, but fractional-PDE error
worsens. GPT-5.5 improves from -1.90 to -0.32, reducing mean Burgers
error by about $`1.7\times`$ and heat error by about $`15\times`$,
although its aggregate PDE summary score remains below zero. GPT-6.1
improves from +1.61 to +1.74, with an approximately $`8.8`$-fold Burgers
improvement and $`2.0`$-fold heat improvement. Both GPT models improve
the mean metric on all three PDE tasks. PDE transfer is therefore
selective, even when the same policy is applied unchanged.

## 6 Discussion and limitations

#### Inspectable knowledge with selective transfer.

RSI-Opt stores its accumulated knowledge in text and code rather than
changing the underlying LLM weights. Recent work suggests that such
harness evolution can reach a ceiling beyond which weight training
becomes necessary \[[28](#bib.bib29)\]. Policies can be inspected,
tested, and used by other in-run models. The experiments show that such
portability does not guarantee a benefit on every task: GPT-5.5 and
GPT-6.1 improve the mean raw-space summary score in every task group,
while Opus gains on the evolution suite but has a lower aggregate
held-out summary score despite improving four of six held-out task
means. GPT-6.1 improves mean Burgers error by approximately
$`8.8\times`$ but worsens LSTM loss. Transfer depends on the interaction
between the policy, model, and task.

#### Reusable priors and continued adaptation.

The budget controls locate most of the seen-task benefit in the initial
optimizer change. On unseen tasks, both baseline and evolved agents
benefit from later decisions. The tested unedited library template fails
on some architectures, so code reuse alone does not explain the full
agent’s outcomes. These comparisons support task-conditioned selection
and online adaptation; they do not isolate reasoning from additional
observations, code modifications, or compute. A stronger frozen recipe
selected on development tasks could improve on the particular template
tested here.

#### Specialization and selection.

Evolution uses three tasks and two seeds per policy. The in-run agent
adds stochastic variation beyond data and initialization seeds, and
archive selection can favor unusually strong evaluations. Re-evaluating
the top three candidates on two additional seeds reduces reliance on a
single estimate; fresh final seeds further separate reporting from
search. Neither step makes the evolution tasks unseen. The early peak in
held-out ML performance is consistent with specialization to this small
suite. Broader task coverage, additional independent evolution runs, and
a separate policy-validation suite would strengthen claims about
generalization and the reliability of the search procedure.

#### Aggregation and uncertainty.

A z-score measures improvement relative to the static recipe’s seed
spread. Small reference variability can make a task dominate the mean,
even if its raw improvement is modest. Final aggregate summaries use the
raw-space means and static standard deviations printed in
[Table 2](#S5.T2 "In 5.2 How do agents compare with static recipes? ‣ 5 Results ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training"),
including for PDEs; they are descriptive recomputations and differ from
per-seed log-error normalization. Historical control and
intermediate-checkpoint ML scores retain their original reporting
precision; PDE control and checkpoint aggregates are omitted from the
corrected comparisons when the raw per-task metrics are unavailable. Raw
metrics, signed differences, and per-task outcomes are therefore
necessary alongside aggregates. With three final seeds per cell,
uncertainty estimates have limited precision. An interval that includes
zero does not show equivalence, and the magnitude of a z-score alone
does not establish statistical significance.

#### What the current controls do not establish.

We do not isolate the effect of recursive inheritance against a
matched-budget search that always proposes from generation 0, nor the
value of the archive against a restricted-parent search. The evidence
concerns the policies produced by the reported trajectory. Our static
comparisons also cover only the starting recipes, an oracle
learning-rate sweep, and one unedited library template. Schedule-matched
Adam, standard Muon/AdamW hybrids, and suitable Adam-to-L-BFGS PINN
recipes \[[25](#bib.bib16)\] would better separate known optimizer
choices from agent adaptation. Comparisons involving L-BFGS should match
gradient evaluations or compute rather than nominal update counts.

#### Scale and cost.

Runs use a single GPU and relatively short update budgets. Transfer to
longer, multi-GPU training is not tested here. Equal decision limits do
not equalize actual API charges, diagnostics, optimizer work, or
wall-clock time. A full cost comparison would include outer-search
evaluations, CPU proxies, GPU usage, cache pricing, and LLM pauses
before claiming amortized savings. Hosted-model changes can also affect
reproducibility; preserved prompts, programs, and transcripts document
the tested behavior but do not freeze the provider’s implementation.

#### Execution boundaries and exposure.

Agent shells use network and mount namespaces to restrict access to
other runs, evaluation data, and credentials. These restrictions were
introduced during the project rather than applied uniformly from its
start.
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Appendix ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training")
reports a known read of a held-out task description and the exclusion of
the exposed policy and its descendant. Since archive summaries can
transmit information between lineages, the direct-access audit alone
does not certify the absence of indirect exposure. In addition,
generated optimizer code runs inside the training process; shell
isolation is not by itself a complete account of that code’s access to
process state. The wrapper’s checks and the documented sandbox reduce
specific risks without proving that evaluation manipulation is
impossible. More broadly, the safety and robustness of
reasoning-augmented LLM agents remain open
concerns \[[29](#bib.bib31)\].

## Appendix A Appendix

The harness records every agent tool call. The audit scans 528 in-run
transcripts (18,895 tool calls) and 20 meta-agent transcripts (949 tool
calls) for paths outside the agent’s own workspace, except the Python
environment. The counts below describe the audited corpus, not a
guarantee about unlogged actions or information flow.

#### In-run agents.

The scan flags 1 calls. The reported review finds no direct reads of
another run, task scripts, data, logs, or API credentials.

#### Meta-agents.

The 12 flagged calls were inspected manually. Before filesystem
restrictions were added in generation 1, two meta-agents read OPAL
example files. One read an MNIST example not used as one of our
benchmark tasks; the other read the description of the noisy-label
example designated as held out. The exposed policy and its descendant
were excluded from subsequent parent selection and final selection
([Section 5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 What knowledge evolves? ‣ 5 Results ‣ RSI-Opt: Recursive Self-Improvement Optimizer Agents For Model Training")).
After the restrictions were added, other flagged calls listed the hidden
runs/ directory, which exposed only the agent’s own folder, or the
hidden examples/ directory, which appeared empty. Two meta-agents also
searched the filesystem for MNIST and CIFAR data for CPU proxies. Those
searches returned library source paths and timed out; the reported
review found no subsequent use of external project data.

#### Archive-mediated exposure.

Meta-agents can read scores and changelogs from other archive branches.
The direct-access scan and removal of an exposed lineage therefore do
not establish that information could not propagate through summaries to
a different lineage. The audit as documented here does not resolve that
provenance question. A complete audit would track archive contents and
reads over time, including whether exposed summaries remained visible
after exclusion. This is a limitation of the current held-out evaluation
claim, not evidence that such indirect propagation occurred.

#### Configured isolation.

Under the final configuration, an agent’s shell runs in separate user,
network, and mount namespaces, without network access, GPU access, or
API credentials in its environment. The project is mounted read-only
with other runs, task scripts, data, logs, the paper, and example tasks
hidden. The agent’s own folder is mounted read-write, and the home
directory is hidden. Other paths on the cluster’s shared filesystem
remain visible. Early baseline runs used network isolation without the
later filesystem restrictions; the reported access audit found no reads
outside their permitted workspace and environment. Generated updater and
diagnostic programs execute in the training process. The wrapper rejects
certain operations, including parameter-dtype changes and
evaluation-related parameter moves. Logged shell and file-tool calls do
not, by themselves, establish all accesses available to imported code or
rule out every form of evaluator manipulation.
````

###### Abstract

Source-Free Domain Adaptation (SFDA) aims to adapt a source-pretrained
model to an unlabeled target domain without access to the original
source domain. While early single-model approaches rely on
self-refinement, they are inherently susceptible to confirmation bias
and struggle to correct their own systematic errors. To overcome this
limitation, recent methods introduce Vision-Language (ViL) models as
external knowledge sources. However, these approaches operate in a
largely unidirectional paradigm, using the ViL model primarily to
supervise the source-pretrained model. This overlooks a key structural
property: the two models exhibit distinct failure modes – where one
produces an incorrect prediction, the other may produce a correct one,
creating a natural opportunity for mutual correction within the target
domain. Yet, without ground-truth labels, identifying which model is
correct on any given sample is non-trivial, and naively exchanging
predictions risks propagating errors across models. To address this
challenge, we propose SafeCut, a novel approach that leverages the cut
statistic as a label-free measure of prediction reliability to gate
cross-model supervision. Our approach dynamically controls both the
direction and strength of supervision based on relative reliability,
selectively amplifying true corrections while suppressing miscorrections
on a per-sample basis. We further provide theoretical justification
showing that this reliability-gated mechanism guarantees a net-positive
correction signal. Extensive experiments across diverse SFDA benchmarks
demonstrate that SafeCut achieves state-of-the-art performance,
highlighting the effectiveness of safeguarding mutual correction in SFDA
via cut statistics. Codes are available
[here](https://github.com/SeongJunLee-net/SafeCut_NIPS26).

## 1 Introduction

One of the central challenges in deploying deep learning models is the
presence of distribution shift between training and deployment
environments. Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) addresses this issue
by aligning source and target distributions without relying on target
labels \[[1](#bib.bib17), [2](#bib.bib18)\]. However, it requires access
to source data – an assumption often violated in privacy- and
regulation-sensitive domains such as healthcare and finance. Source-Free
Domain Adaptation (SFDA) has therefore emerged as a practical
alternative, operating under the stricter constraint that only a
source-pretrained model and unlabeled target data are accessible, while
both source data and target labels are completely
inaccessible \[[3](#bib.bib8)\].

SFDA assumes that, despite distribution shifts, the source and target
domains share an underlying semantic structure, allowing knowledge
learned from the source domain to remain partially transferable. Early
approaches capitalize on this shared structure by leveraging the
structural proximity of predictions or latent features induced by the
source-pretrained model to derive self-supervised adaptation signals on
the target domain \[[3](#bib.bib8), [4](#bib.bib15), [5](#bib.bib16),
[6](#bib.bib11), [7](#bib.bib10), [8](#bib.bib14)\]. However, because
these single-model approaches rely exclusively on self-refinement, they
are highly susceptible to confirmation bias and inherently struggle to
correct their own systematic errors, as incorrect predictions are often
distributed throughout the target domain, making them difficult to
correct through local proximity or consistency-based
supervision \[[8](#bib.bib14), [9](#bib.bib12)\].

Recent work introduces Vision-Language (ViL) models as an external
knowledge source, enabling cross-model supervision beyond
self-training \[[10](#bib.bib4), [11](#bib.bib13)\]. Their rich,
generalized representations provide corrective signals in regions where
the source-pretrained model is uncertain or biased. Despite these
advantages, existing approaches treat ViL as a universally more reliable
knowledge source. In practice, however, ViL models may have distinct
failure modes from the source-pretrained models – cases where one model
errs while the other is correct – and their relative reliability varies
across samples and target domains. Unconditionally accepting ViL
predictions ignores this structural variability, which limits potential
gains from adaptation. Moreover, such one-sided reliance can hinder
generalization in domains where ViL models are inherently less reliable,
such as medical imaging \[[12](#bib.bib27), [13](#bib.bib28)\]. More
fundamentally, it overlooks the opportunity to leverage these distinct
failure modes for mutual correction.

To address this, we propose a novel approach, Safeguarding mutual
correction via Cut statistics (SafeCut), which establishes a positive
feedback loop through reliability-gated knowledge transfer on the target
domain. Using the cut statistic \[[14](#bib.bib5)\] as a label-free
measure of local prediction consistency, our method dynamically controls
both the direction and strength of cross-model supervision
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")).
This selectively amplifies corrective signals while suppressing
miscorrections, enabling the two models to refine each other in a
principled manner. As a result, SafeCut achieves state-of-the-art
performance across diverse domain adaptation benchmarks.

## 2 Related Works

#### Source-Free Domain Adaptation.

Early work aligns target representations via self-supervised
pseudo-labeling \[[3](#bib.bib8)\], and subsequent methods exploit
target neighborhood structure for prediction consistency, pseudo-label
refinement, and contrastive adaptation \[[4](#bib.bib15),
[5](#bib.bib16), [6](#bib.bib11)\]. Alternatively, several approaches
estimate prediction reliability via GMM-based confidence or neighborhood
uncertainty, and subsequently use these quantities for
self-supervision \[[7](#bib.bib10), [8](#bib.bib14)\]. However, by
relying solely on the source model’s predictions and the resulting
latent structures, these methods are inherently constrained by the
model’s own blind spots and confirmation bias. To address this, recent
work introduces ViL as an auxiliary knowledge source, focusing on
adapting ViL to the target domain or denoising its
pseudo-labels \[[10](#bib.bib4), [11](#bib.bib13)\]. These methods are
specifically tailored to the zero-shot capabilities of ViL and do not
explore their extension to more general counterpart models; furthermore,
they leave the critical question of how effective knowledge transfer can
occur between the two models largely unaddressed.

#### Cross-Model Supervision.

Cross-model supervision has been explored in mutual
learning \[[15](#bib.bib2)\] and co-teaching \[[16](#bib.bib26)\]
frameworks, where two models iteratively refine each other – either by
exchanging predictions or by selecting reliable samples for the peer to
learn from. However, these approaches fundamentally rely on labeled data
or noisy label supervision, making them inapplicable to the fully
unsupervised SFDA setting. More recently, weak-to-strong
generalization \[[17](#bib.bib19), [18](#bib.bib3)\] demonstrates that a
weaker model’s imperfect supervision can nonetheless elicit strong
generalization from a more capable peer. SafeCut builds on this insight
and extends it to the label-free SFDA setting, leveraging the cut
statistic to dynamically route supervision based on local prediction
consistency — enabling reliable cross-model correction without any label
information.

## 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation

### 3.1 Problem Setting

We consider a $`C`$-class classification task over the input space
$`\mathcal{X}`$ and the label space $`\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\dots,C\}`$. In
the source-free domain adaptation (SFDA) setting, we are provided with a
source model $`\theta_{s}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{C}`$ pre-trained on
a labeled source domain $`\mathcal{D}_{s}`$. Our objective is to adapt
this model to an unlabeled target domain
$`\mathcal{D}_{t}=\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}`$, whose underlying distribution
differs from that of the source. Following the standard SFDA setting,
the source data $`\mathcal{D}_{s}`$ is _strictly inaccessible_ during
adaptation, due to data privacy or storage limitations. We initialize
our target model $`\theta_{t}`$ with the source parameters (i.e.,
$`\theta_{t}=\theta_{s}`$) and must bridge the domain gap using only the
unlabeled samples in $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$. The core challenge lies in
recovering the target discriminative structure without the supervision
of the source distribution or ground-truth target labels.

We further introduce a _peer (counterpart) model_ $`\theta_{v}`$
designed to provide _complementary correctness patterns_. Specifically,
the two models (i.e., $`\theta_{t}`$ and $`\theta_{v}`$) are assumed to
make errors on different subsets of the target domain: there exist
non-empty disagreement regions where only one of the two models is
correct. This functional asymmetry enables _mutual correction_, whereby
each model can provide informative supervision to its peer on samples
where its own prediction is correct and the other’s is not. When applied
iteratively, such cross-model supervision forms a positive reinforcing
feedback loop, in which progressively refined predictions from one model
serve as higher-fidelity pseudo-labels for the other, ultimately
improving both models beyond their initial performance
\[[15](#bib.bib2), [17](#bib.bib19), [18](#bib.bib3)\].

Dynamics of Mutual Correction.   To formalize the dynamics of mutual
correction, we partition the target data based on the correctness of
$`\theta_{t}`$ and $`\theta_{v}`$. For sample $`x`$, let
$`y(x)\in\mathcal{Y}`$ denote the (unknown) ground-truth label and
$`\hat{y}_{i}(x)`$ the predicted label from model $`\theta_{i}`$ with
$`i\in\{t,v\}`$. We define the set of correct predictions for model
$`\theta_{i}`$ as
$`\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}:\hat{y}_{i}(x)=y(x)\}`$ and the
set of incorrect predictions as its complement,
$`\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{i}=\mathcal{D}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{i}`$.
The intersection of these sets induces a partition of the target domain
into four mutually exclusive (unobservable) subsets:

|     |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |     |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{cc}=\mathcal{C}_{t}\cap\mathcal{C}_{v},\quad\mathcal{S}_{c\bar{c}}=\mathcal{C}_{t}\cap\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{v},\quad\mathcal{S}_{\bar{c}c}=\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{t}\cap\mathcal{C}_{v},\quad\mathcal{S}_{\bar{c}\bar{c}}=\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{t}\cap\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{v},`$ |     | (1) |

where each subset corresponds to a distinct joint correctness pattern of
the two models.

Due to the absence of ground-truth labels in SFDA, mutual correction
relies on each model using the other’s predictions as pseudo-labels.
This cross-model supervision induces two competing dynamics:

- •
  Correction: This occurs when a model receives a correct pseudo-label
  from its peer that can rectify its own error. Specifically, for
  samples in $`\mathcal{S}_{\bar{c}c}`$, the peer model $`\theta_{v}`$
  provides correct supervision to $`\theta_{t}`$; symmetrically,
  $`\theta_{t}`$ can correct $`\theta_{v}`$ for samples in
  $`\mathcal{S}_{c\bar{c}}`$. Incorporating such signals directly
  improves the receiving model’s discriminative power.
- •
  Miscorrection: This occurs when an incorrect prediction is used as
  supervision, potentially corrupting a model that is already correct.
  For instance, if $`\theta_{v}`$ supervises $`\theta_{t}`$ on
  $`\mathcal{S}_{c\bar{c}}`$ (or vice versa on
  $`\mathcal{S}_{\bar{c}c}`$), the peer model provides an erroneous
  pseudo-label. Accepting such signals propagates errors, leading to
  confirmation bias and a degradation of performance.

The ideal strategy would accept supervision exclusively on correctly
predicted samples – i.e., employing $`\theta_{v}`$ as a supervisor only
on $`\mathcal{S}_{\bar{c}c}`$ and $`\theta_{t}`$ only on
$`\mathcal{S}_{c\bar{c}}`$ – thereby maximizing correction while
avoiding miscorrection. However, this oracle selection is infeasible in
practice: since ground-truth labels are not available, there is no
direct way to determine which model is correct for a given sample. The
central challenge is therefore to approximate this selection in a fully
unsupervised manner, so that correction signals are amplified while
miscorrection signals are suppressed.

### 3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach

To address the challenge of identifying correctly predicted samples
without access to ground-truth labels, we employ the *cut
statistic* \[[14](#bib.bib5)\], a graph-based measure that quantifies
consistency of model predictions within a sample’s local neighborhood.

Cut Statistic.   For each target sample $`x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}`$, let
$`\mathcal{N}_{K}(x)`$ denote its $`K`$-nearest neighbors. Then, the cut
statistic for model $`\theta_{i}`$, where $`i\in\{t,v\}`$, is defined
as:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
z_{i}(x)=\!\!\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{K}(x)}\!\!\!w(x,x^{\prime})\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\hat{y}_{i}(x)\neq\hat{y}_{i}(x^{\prime})]
``` |  | (2) |

where $`w(x,x^{\prime})`$ is a distance-based weighting function that
assigns greater importance to closer neighbors. Intuitively, the cut
statistic quantifies the local smoothness of model predictions over the
data graph. A low value indicates that a prediction is consistent with
those of its neighbors, whereas a high value indicates that such local
consistency does not hold.

Under the cluster assumption \[[19](#bib.bib9)\], locally consistent
predictions are generally more likely to be correct, suggesting that the
cut statistic should be negatively correlated with predictive
accuracy \[[14](#bib.bib5), [18](#bib.bib3)\]. However, in domain
adaptation settings – where distribution shift may distort the
underlying structure – this relationship is not guaranteed to hold. To
examine whether this intuition remains valid, we empirically analyze the
relationship between local consistency and prediction accuracy across
diverse adaptation scenarios. Specifically, for each model
$`\theta_{i}`$ with $`i\in\{t,v\}`$, we compute $`z_{i}(x)`$ for each
target sample $`x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}`$, group samples into bins according
to their cut values, and measure the average accuracy within each bin.

(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")
left) reveal a clear and consistent trend across all evaluated settings,
motivating the following observation (see
Appendix [D.4](#A4.SS4 "D.4 Extended Empirical Validation of Cut Statistic Properties ‣ Appendix D Additional Results ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")
for additional results):

{observation}

(Approximate Monotonicity) For both $`\theta_{i}`$ with $`i\in\{t,v\}`$,
prediction accuracy is approximately monotonically decreasing with
respect to the cut statistic $`z_{i}`$.

Connecting Correction Dynamics with Cut Statistics.   Based on this
observation, we assume that the conditional accuracy
$`\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{i}(x)=y(x)\mid z_{i}(x)\big)`$ is
monotonically decreasing in with respect to $`z_{i}\in[0,1]`$.¹¹ 1 For
notational simplicity, we will omit the dependence on $`x`$ in $`y(x)`$
and $`\hat{y}_{i}(x)`$ when it is clear from context. Since this
monotonicity holds for both $`\theta_{t}`$ and $`\theta_{v}`$, the
relative difference in their cut statistics can offer a principled,
label-free criterion for determining which model provides more reliable
prediction on a per-sample basis. To formalize this, we define the cut
margin $`\Delta(x):=z_{v}(x)-z_{t}(x)`$ to characterize the relative
expertise of the two models on sample $`x`$. The sign of $`\Delta(x)`$
indicates the direction of reliability: a positive value indicates that
$`\theta_{t}`$ is more likely to be correct, while a negative value
suggests the peer model $`\theta_{v}`$ is more reliable. Crucially, the
magnitude $`|\Delta(x)|`$ reflects the strength of this reliability gap,
and thus measures the potential fidelity of the correction signal.
Building on this intuition, we approximate the unobservable oracle
partitions $`\mathcal{S}_{c\bar{c}}`$ and $`\mathcal{S}_{\bar{c}c}`$ in
([1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1 Problem Setting ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"))
by introducing margin thresholds $`\gamma,\xi\in[0,1]`$, identifying
regions of the target domain where one model provides a clear
reliability gap over the other:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)=\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}:\Delta(x)\geq\gamma\},\quad\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)=\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}:\Delta(x)\leq-\xi\}.
``` |  | (3) |

Given different failure modes of $`\theta_{t}`$ and $`\theta_{v}`$, and
recalling that the monotonic decrease of each model’s accuracy with its
own cut statistic
(Observation [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")),
the model with higher local consistency is more likely to be correct and
can thus provide high-fidelity pseudo-labels to supervise its less
reliable counterpart. Notably, this intuition remains valid under
approximate monotonicity; we provide theoretical bounds under noisy
conditions in
Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Theoretical Justification under the Noisy Scenario ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics").
We formalize this intuition through the following baseline condition for
effective mutual correction:

###### Proposition 3.1 (Reliability-Separated Regions).

There exist baseline margins $`\gamma_{0},\xi_{0}`$ such that:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big),`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big).`$ |  | (5) |

Appendix [A.1](#A1.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition A.1 (Reliability-Separated Regions). ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")
for the full proof of
Proposition [3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 3.1 (Reliability-Separated Regions). ‣ 3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics").

Here, $`\gamma_{0}`$ and $`\xi_{0}`$ denote the minimum reliability gaps
required for one model’s prediction to serve as a trustworthy
supervisory signal for its peer.

While
Proposition [3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 3.1 (Reliability-Separated Regions). ‣ 3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")
shows that the supervising model is more accurate in regions where it is
locally more consistent than its peer, effective mutual correction
requires a stronger guarantee: cross-model supervision must produce a
net-positive correction signal, where corrections outnumber
miscorrections. The following theorem proves that this dominance holds
under the baseline margins:

###### Theorem 3.2 (Correction Dominance).

For the baseline margins $`\gamma_{0},\xi_{0}`$ satisfying

|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\land\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big),`$ |  | (6) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\land\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big).`$ |  | (7) |

for the full proof of

(right) illustrates this net-positive correction signal: as samples move

An analogous result holds for $`\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\cdot)`$

Corollary [3.3](#S3.Thmtheorem3 "Corollary 3.3 (Margin-Driven Strict Dominance). ‣ 3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")
shows that sufficiently enlarging the cut margin amplifies the dominance
of true correction over miscorrection. In practice, however, selecting
optimal thresholds $`\gamma`$ and $`\xi`$ is infeasible in a fully
unlabeled setting. To bridge this gap, we replace hard thresholding with
a soft gating mechanism that assigns supervision weights monotonically
increasing in the cut margin $`|\Delta(x)|`$, prioritizing samples with
a larger reliability gap. For simplicity, we adopt a symmetric
formulation with $`\gamma=\xi`$, reducing the gating mechanism to a
single margin-based criterion.

## 4 Training with Reliability-Gated Supervision

### 4.1 Estimating Prediction Reliability via Cut Statistics

To quantify prediction reliability via the cut statistic defined in
([2](#S3.E2 "In 3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")),
we first characterize the local geometry of the target domain under both
$`\theta_{t}`$ and $`\theta_{v}`$. Let
$`\phi_{i}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}`$ denote the feature
extractor for model $`i\in\{t,v\}`$, mapping inputs into a latent space
where pairwise distances are computed.²² 2 In our experiments,
$`\phi_{i}(x)`$ corresponds to the output of the penultimate layer of
each model (i.e., pre-logits). Using these representations, we construct
a local neighborhood graph by identifying the $`K`$-nearest neighbors
$`\mathcal{N}_{K}(x)`$ for each sample $`x`$ under a standard distance
metric $`d`$. To mitigate spurious connections – particularly for
samples located in low-density regions – we restrict the neighborhood to
mutual neighbors, i.e.,
$`\mathcal{N}_{K}^{\text{mut}}(x)=\{x^{\prime}\mid x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{K}(x)\land x\in\mathcal{N}_{K}(x^{\prime})\}`$.
Then, we construct a distance-weighted cut statistic for each model
$`i\in\{t,v\}`$ as follows

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{z}_{i}(x)=\frac{\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{K}^{\text{mut}}(x)}\exp(-d(\phi_{i}(x),\phi_{i}(x^{\prime})))\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\hat{y}_{i}(x)\neq\hat{y}_{i}(x^{\prime})]}{\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{K}^{\text{mut}}(x)}\exp(-d(\phi_{i}(x),\phi_{i}(x^{\prime})))}.
``` |  | (9) |

Here,
$`w(x,x^{\prime})\propto\exp(-d(\phi_{i}(x),\phi_{i}(x^{\prime})))`$ and
normalization ensures that cut statistics remain comparable across
regions with different sample densities. Finally, to translate the cut
statistic into an intuitive measure of prediction reliability, we apply
an exponential mapping $`r_{i}(x)=\exp(-\hat{z}_{i}(x))\in(0,1]`$,
ensuring that highly consistent samples receive scores near $`1`$.

Naively computing these scores requires an additional forward pass over
the entire target data at every epoch, which is prohibitively expensive
in SFDA settings where target domains often contain hundreds of
thousands of samples. To circumvent this overhead, we reuse the latent
features cached during the optimization forward pass of the preceding
epoch. To further stabilize the estimates, we apply an exponential
moving average across epochs, i.e.,
$`\bar{r}_{i}^{(e)}(x)=m\cdot\bar{r}_{i}^{(e-1)}(x)+(1-m)\cdot r_{i}^{(e)}(x)`$,
where $`e`$ denotes the epoch index and $`m\in[0,1)`$ is the momentum
parameter. For notational simplicity, we omit the epoch index hereafter
and use $`\bar{r}_{i}(x)`$ in place of $`r_{i}(x)`$; the complete
epoch-indexed formulation and algorithm details are provided in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Epoch-Indexed Formulation ‣ Appendix C Implementation Details ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics").
These reliability scores $`\bar{r}_{t}`$ and $`\bar{r}_{v}`$ serve as
the foundation for determining how the two models supervise one another,
which we will describe in the next subsection.

### 4.2 Reliability-Gated Mutual Correction

Given the per-sample reliability estimated from
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Estimating Prediction Reliability via Cut Statistics ‣ 4 Training with Reliability-Gated Supervision ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"),
we now formulate a novel mutual correction objective where
$`\theta_{t}`$ and $`\theta_{v}`$ iteratively refine each other by
utilizing their predictions as pseudo-labels. The central challenge in
this cross-model supervision is that we must dynamically determine the
direction and strength of the supervision for each sample while
preventing reliable predictions from being corrupted by erroneous
supervision from the less reliable peer.

Margin-based Reliability Gating.   Recall from
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")
that there exists a margin threshold that identifies regions of the
target domain where one model can reliably supervise the other. Rather
than relying on such discrete thresholds, we adopt a continuous
formulation in which the reliability gap directly governs both the
direction and strength of supervision on a per-sample basis. Formally,
we define the cross-model margin-based reliability gating mechanism:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{v\to t}(x)=\sigma(\beta(\bar{r}_{v}(x)-\bar{r}_{t}(x))),\quad T_{t\to v}(x)=\sigma(\beta(\bar{r}_{t}(x)-\bar{r}_{v}(x)))
``` |  | (10) |

where $`\sigma`$ denotes the sigmoid function. By the symmetry of the
sigmoid, $`T_{v\to t}(x)+T_{t\to v}(x)=1`$ holds for all $`x`$, ensuring
that the total supervisory budget is conserved across the two
directions. Here, $`T_{v\to t}(x)`$ represents the weight assigned to
the supervisory signal from $`\theta_{v}`$ to $`\theta_{t}`$ (and vice
versa). The temperature parameter $`\beta>0`$ controls the sharpness of
the gating mechanism. As $`\beta`$ increases, the weights become more
sensitive to the reliability gap $`|\bar{r}_{v}(x)-\bar{r}_{t}(x)|`$,
approaching the hard-threshold behavior implied by
Proposition [3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 3.1 (Reliability-Separated Regions). ‣ 3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics").
Conversely, smaller values of $`\beta`$ produce a smoother transition,
allowing the model to remain less committed when the reliability
difference is small (e.g., when both models are similarly uncertain or
similarly confident). By applying this gating directly to the mutual
correction signal, we obtain the following cross-supervision loss:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cross}}^{t}(x)=T_{v\to t}(x)\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\mathrm{sg}(p_{v})\,\|\,p_{t}\bigr),\quad\mathcal{L}_{\text{cross}}^{v}(x)=T_{t\to v}(x)\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\mathrm{sg}(p_{t})\,\|\,p_{v}\bigr),
``` |  | (11) |

where $`\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)`$ denotes the stop-gradient operator. As a
result, when one model has a clear reliability advantage on a given
sample, it naturally assumes a stronger supervisory role, while the
influence of the less reliable model is correspondingly attenuated.

Temporal Self-Anchoring.   While soft gating in
([11](#S4.E11 "In 4.2 Reliability-Gated Mutual Correction ‣ 4 Training with Reliability-Gated Supervision ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"))
effectively mitigates miscorrection, it does not eliminate it entirely.
Even small, non-zero gating weights from an unreliable (incorrect) peer
can allow residual error signals that can accumulate over successive
epochs, gradually degrading predictions that were initially correct. To
prevent this, we further introduce a temporal self-anchoring mechanism
that encourages each model to remain consistent with its own previous
predictions, dynamically weighted by its own reliability score:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{anc}}^{t}(x)=T_{t\to v}(x)\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{p}_{t}\,\|\,p_{t}),\quad\mathcal{L}_{\text{anc}}^{v}(x)=T_{v\to t}(x)\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{p}_{v}\,\|\,p_{v})`$ |  | (12) |

where $`\tilde{p}_{t}`$ and $`\tilde{p}_{v}`$ denote the predictions
from the previous epoch. Since $`T_{t\to v}(x)`$ is large only when
$`\theta_{t}`$ is highly reliable, and symmetrically for
$`T_{v\to t}(x)`$, the anchoring term selectively preserves confident
predictions while allowing less reliable ones to be flexibly updated
through cross-model supervision.

Training Objective.   Combining reliability-gated mutual correction in
([11](#S4.E11 "In 4.2 Reliability-Gated Mutual Correction ‣ 4 Training with Reliability-Gated Supervision ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"))
with temporal self-anchoring in
([12](#S4.E12 "In 4.2 Reliability-Gated Mutual Correction ‣ 4 Training with Reliability-Gated Supervision ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")),
we define the overall training objective for each model as:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[T_{v\to t}\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathrm{sg}(p_{v})\|p_{t})+T_{t\to v}\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{p}_{t}\|p_{t})\right]}_{\text{sample-wise supervision}}+\underbrace{\lambda_{\mathrm{IIC}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IIC}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}H(\bar{p}_{t})}_{\text{batch-wise regularization}}`$ |  | (13) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{v}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[T_{t\to v}\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathrm{sg}(p_{t})\|p_{v})+T_{v\to t}\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{p}_{v}\|p_{v})\right]}_{\text{sample-wise supervision}}+\underbrace{\lambda_{\mathrm{IIC}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IIC}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}H(\bar{p}_{v})}_{\text{batch-wise regularization}}`$ |  | (14) |

where $`\bar{p}_{t}`$ and $`\bar{p}_{v}`$ denote the mean predictions
over the current batch. While the sample-wise supervision drives local,
reliability-aware updates, we complement it with two batch-level
regularizers that impose global structure. The IIC loss
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IIC}}`$ \[[20](#bib.bib7)\] maximizes the mutual
information between $`p_{t}`$ and $`p_{v}`$ aggregated over the batch,
aligning the prediction distributions of the two different views. This
is particularly valuable on samples where the reliability gap
$`|\bar{r}_{t}-\bar{r}_{v}|`$ is small, where the cross-supervision
signal becomes nearly directionless. In addition, the diversity
regularizer \[[3](#bib.bib8)\] maximizes the entropy of $`\bar{p}_{i}`$,
preventing collapse to a few dominant classes.

$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{\bar{c}\bar{c}}}`$ maintain consistent high cut

Medical Domain Setup.   Medical imaging is a canonical SFDA setting,
where sharing source data is often restricted due to privacy concerns.
We evaluate SafeCut on Camelyon17 from WILDS
benchmark \[[28](#bib.bib25)\], training the source model on centers
$`\{0,3,4\}`$, validating on $`\{1\}`$, and adapting to $`\{2\}`$.
Unlike natural-image benchmarks, zero-shot CLIP transfers poorly to
histopathology, even underperforming the source-pretrained target model
($`50.01\%`$ vs. $`64.14\%`$). In this setting, ProDe achieves only
modest improvements on the target model and leaves (updated) CLIP
similar to its zero-shot performance (50.00%, with hyperparameters tuned
via grid search; see
Appendix [E.2](#A5.SS2 "E.2 ProDe Hyperparameter Search on Camelyon17 ‣ Appendix E Experimental Details ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")).
This is because ProDe updates CLIP only through
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{IIC}}`$, providing no direct source-driven
supervision; as a result, it fails to improve and continues to provide
weak guidance. In contrast, SafeCut trains both models through mutual,
reliability-gated correction, enabling joint improvement. Using
$`\mathcal{L}_{c}`$ alone matches ProDe, while adding
$`\mathcal{L}_{a}`$ further stabilizes training. The complete model
(SafeCut) achieves $`94.96\%`$, significantly outperforming ProDe. These
results highlight the importance of reliability-aware mutual correction
when the peer model is initially unreliable.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we presented SafeCut, a novel SFDA framework driven by
reliability-gated mutual correction. Unlike existing methods that
unconditionally trust external models, we exploit the different failure
modes of the target and peer models. Backed by solid theoretical
grounding, SafeCut uses the cut statistic as a label-free reliability
proxy to dynamically control the direction and strength of cross-model
supervision. Coupled with temporal self-anchoring, this mechanism
amplifies valid corrective signals while mitigating miscorrections and
suppressing error accumulation. Extensive experiments confirm that
SafeCut achieves state-of-the-art performance across multiple
benchmarks. Crucially, SafeCut not only generalizes across diverse peer
models but also excels in challenging domains like medical imaging,
demonstrating its versatility as a general-purpose correction framework.

## Appendix A Proofs

###### Proposition A.1 (Reliability-Separated Regions).

There exist baseline margins $`\gamma_{0},\xi_{0}`$ such that:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big),`$ |  | (15) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big).`$ |  | (16) |

###### Proof.

Motivated by the approximate monotonicity observed in
Observation [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"),
we consider a sufficiently separated positive-margin region and let
$`C:=\{x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\}`$. By definition,
any sample in this region satisfies
$`\Delta(x)=z_{v}(x)-z_{t}(x)\geq\gamma_{0}>0`$.

Using the law of total probability, the reliability gap between the two
models over region $`C`$ can be expressed as an expectation (See detail
proof in noisy
version [A.4](#A1.Thmtheorem4 "Proposition A.4 (Reliability Gap under Noise). ‣ A.1 Theoretical Justification under the Noisy Scenario ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid C\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid C\big)=\mathbb{E}_{x\mid C}\Big[\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid z_{t}(x)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid z_{v}(x)\big)\Big].
``` |  |

For any $`x\in C`$, we have $`\Delta(x)\geq\gamma_{0}>0`$, and hence
$`z_{t}(x)<z_{v}(x)`$. Under the cross-model reliability ordering
induced by the cut statistic in this reliability-separated region, a
lower cut statistic corresponds to a higher probability of correctness;
thus,
$`\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid z_{t}(x))>\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid z_{v}(x))`$,
implying that the integrand is strictly positive for all $`x\in C`$.
Therefore, it follows that:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big)>0.
``` |  |

An analogous derivation confirms the symmetric result for
$`\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})`$. ∎

###### Theorem A.2 (Correction Dominance).

For the baseline margins $`\gamma_{0},\xi_{0}`$ satisfying
Proposition [A.1](#A1.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition A.1 (Reliability-Separated Regions). ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\land\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big),`$ |  | (17) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\land\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big).`$ |  | (18) |

###### Proof.

Let $`E_{1}`$ be the event $`\{\hat{y}_{t}=y\}`$ and $`E_{2}`$ be the
event $`\{\hat{y}_{v}=y\}`$. For any conditional event $`C`$, the law of
total probability yields:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}\mid C)+\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}^{c}\mid C),`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}\mid C)+\mathbb{P}(E_{1}^{c}\land E_{2}\mid C).`$ |  |

Subtracting the second equation from the first, we establish the
following fundamental identity:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C)-\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C)=\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}^{c}\mid C)-\mathbb{P}(E_{1}^{c}\land E_{2}\mid C).
``` |  | (19) |

For the first inequality, let the condition $`C`$ be
$`x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})`$. By
Proposition [A.1](#A1.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition A.1 (Reliability-Separated Regions). ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"),
we have $`\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C)>\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C)`$. Applying
this to
Equation ([19](#A1.E19 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")),
it directly follows that:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}^{c}\mid C)-\mathbb{P}(E_{1}^{c}\land E_{2}\mid C)>0.
``` |  |

Substituting the events back to their original notation, we obtain:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\land\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big)>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma_{0})\big),
``` |  |

which satisfies the first claim of the theorem.

Similarly, for the second inequality, let the condition $`C^{\prime}`$
be $`x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})`$.
Proposition [A.1](#A1.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition A.1 (Reliability-Separated Regions). ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")
states that
$`\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C^{\prime})>\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C^{\prime})`$.
Applying the same probability identity for condition $`C^{\prime}`$, we
get:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C^{\prime})-\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}(E_{1}^{c}\land E_{2}\mid C^{\prime})-\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}^{c}\mid C^{\prime})>0.
``` |  |

Therefore, we deduce:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\land\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big)>\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi_{0})\big),
``` |  |

∎

### A.1 Theoretical Justification under the Noisy Scenario

While
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")
assumes a strict monotonic relationship between the cut statistic and
prediction accuracy, real-world distribution shifts in the target domain
inevitably introduce noise. In an ideal, noise-free scenario, the
difference in predictive accuracy between the two models would scale
linearly with the cut margin on a per-sample basis. That is, within the
valid bounds of probability, the accuracy difference would be
proportional to the cut margin at a rate of η. In practice, however,
individual samples exhibit empirical deviations from this ideal
relationship. To account for such sample-wise variance, we introduce a
noise tolerance term ϵ to establish a rigorous lower bound. Here, we
formalize this relaxed condition as noisy monotonicity and prove that
correction dominance is still theoretically guaranteed provided the
margin is sufficiently large.

###### Assumption A.3 (Noisy Monotonicity).

For any target sample $`x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ with cut statistics
$`z_{t}(x)`$ and $`z_{v}(x)`$, there exist a decay rate $`\eta>0`$ and
noise level $`\epsilon\geq 0`$ such that:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid z_{t}(x)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid z_{v}(x)\big)\geq\eta\Delta(x)-\epsilon,
``` |  | (20) |

where $`\Delta(x)=z_{v}(x)-z_{t}(x)`$ is the cut margin.

Based on this assumption, we can establish the lower bound for the
reliability gap in the margin-separated regions.

###### Proposition A.4 (Reliability Gap under Noise).

Under
Assumption [A.3](#A1.Thmtheorem3 "Assumption A.3 (Noisy Monotonicity). ‣ A.1 Theoretical Justification under the Noisy Scenario ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"),
for the margin-separated regions
$`\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)`$ and
$`\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)`$, the reliability gap between
$`\theta_{t}`$ and $`\theta_{v}`$ is bounded by:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle\geq\eta\gamma-\epsilon,`$ |  | (21) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)\big)`$ | $`\displaystyle\geq\eta\xi-\epsilon.`$ |  | (22) |

###### Proof.

Let $`C`$ denote the event
$`\{x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)\}`$. By the definition of
this region in
Equation ([3](#S3.E3 "In 3.2 When to Rely on Which Model: A Cut Statistic Approach ‣ 3 Mutual Correction in Source-free Domain Adaptation ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")),
any sample conditioned on $`C`$ strictly satisfies
$`\Delta(x)\geq\gamma`$.

Using the definition of conditional probability, the probability that
model $`\theta_{t}`$ is correct given $`C`$ is:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid C)=\int_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x,C)\cdot\mathbb{P}(x\mid C)dx.
``` |  |

Since $`\mathbb{P}(x\mid C)=0`$ for any $`x\notin C`$, we can restrict
the integration domain to $`C`$. Furthermore, assuming the prediction
correctness primarily depends on the cut statistic $`z_{t}(x)`$, we can
express this as an expectation over the region $`C`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid C)=\int_{x\in C}\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid z_{t}(x)\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}(x\mid C)dx=\mathbb{E}_{x\mid C}\big[\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid z_{t}(x)\big)\big].
``` |  |

Applying the same logic for $`\hat{y}_{v}=y`$ and subtracting the two
yields:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid C)-\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid C)=\mathbb{E}_{x\mid C}\Big[\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid z_{t}(x)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid z_{v}(x)\big)\Big].
``` |  |

By
Assumption [A.3](#A1.Thmtheorem3 "Assumption A.3 (Noisy Monotonicity). ‣ A.1 Theoretical Justification under the Noisy Scenario ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"),
the integrand is lower-bounded by $`\eta\Delta(x)-\epsilon`$. Since
$`\Delta(x)\geq\gamma`$ for all $`x\in C`$, we have:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{x\mid C}\Big[\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid z_{t}(x)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid z_{v}(x)\big)\Big]\geq\mathbb{E}_{x\mid C}\big[\eta\gamma-\epsilon\big]=\eta\gamma-\epsilon.
``` |  |

Therefore,
$`\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid C\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid C\big)\geq\eta\gamma-\epsilon`$.
∎

For $`\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)`$, applying the symmetric
noisy-monotonicity relation
$`\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid z_{v}(x))-\mathbb{P}(\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid z_{t}(x))\geq\eta(-\Delta(x))-\epsilon`$
together with $`\Delta(x)\leq-\xi`$ yields the second inequality
analogously. Building upon this reliability gap, we now establish the
condition under which true correction strictly dominates miscorrection
in the presence of noise.

###### Theorem A.5 (Correction Dominance under Noise).

For the margin-separated region $`\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)`$
satisfying the condition $`\gamma>\frac{\epsilon}{\eta}`$, the
probability of true correction strictly dominates miscorrection by at
least the margin $`\eta\gamma-\epsilon`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\land\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)\big)\geq\eta\gamma-\epsilon>0.
``` |  | (23) |

Symmetrically, for $`\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)`$ satisfying
$`\xi>\frac{\epsilon}{\eta}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\land\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)\big)\geq\eta\xi-\epsilon>0.
``` |  | (24) |

###### Proof.

Let $`E_{1}`$ be the event $`\{\hat{y}_{t}=y\}`$ and $`E_{2}`$ be the
event $`\{\hat{y}_{v}=y\}`$. For any conditional event $`C`$, the law of
total probability yields:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}\mid C)+\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}^{c}\mid C),`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}\mid C)+\mathbb{P}(E_{1}^{c}\land E_{2}\mid C).`$ |  |

Subtracting the second equation from the first, we establish the
following fundamental identity:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C)-\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C)=\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}^{c}\mid C)-\mathbb{P}(E_{1}^{c}\land E_{2}\mid C).
``` |  | (25) |

For the first inequality, let the condition $`C`$ be
$`x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)`$. By
Proposition [A.4](#A1.Thmtheorem4 "Proposition A.4 (Reliability Gap under Noise). ‣ A.1 Theoretical Justification under the Noisy Scenario ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics"),
we have:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C)-\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C)\geq\eta\gamma-\epsilon.
``` |  |

Applying this to
Equation ([25](#A1.E25 "In Proof. ‣ A.1 Theoretical Justification under the Noisy Scenario ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")),
it directly follows that:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}^{c}\mid C)-\mathbb{P}(E_{1}^{c}\land E_{2}\mid C)\geq\eta\gamma-\epsilon.
``` |  |

Given the condition $`\gamma>\frac{\epsilon}{\eta}`$, we know that
$`\eta\gamma-\epsilon>0`$. Substituting the events back to their
original notation, we obtain:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}=y\land\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{v}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{c\bar{c}}(\gamma)\big)\geq\eta\gamma-\epsilon>0,
``` |  |

which satisfies the first claim of the theorem.

Similarly, for the second inequality, let the condition $`C^{\prime}`$
be $`x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)`$.
Proposition [A.4](#A1.Thmtheorem4 "Proposition A.4 (Reliability Gap under Noise). ‣ A.1 Theoretical Justification under the Noisy Scenario ‣ Appendix A Proofs ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")
states that
$`\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C^{\prime})-\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C^{\prime})\geq\eta\xi-\epsilon`$.
Applying the same probability identity for condition $`C^{\prime}`$, we
get:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}(E_{2}\mid C^{\prime})-\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\mid C^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}(E_{1}^{c}\land E_{2}\mid C^{\prime})-\mathbb{P}(E_{1}\land E_{2}^{c}\mid C^{\prime})\geq\eta\xi-\epsilon.
``` |  |

Given $`\xi>\frac{\epsilon}{\eta}`$, we deduce:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}=y\land\hat{y}_{t}\neq y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\hat{y}_{v}\neq y\land\hat{y}_{t}=y\mid x\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\bar{c}c}(\xi)\big)\geq\eta\xi-\epsilon>0.
``` |  |

∎

## Appendix B Evaluation Datasets

In this paper, our method is evaluated on five widely used benchmarks
for domain adaptation as follows.

- •
  Office-31 \[[21](#bib.bib20)\] is a small-scale dataset consisting of
  three domains: Amazon (A), DSLR (D), and Webcam (W), all featuring
  real-world objects in office environments. The Amazon domain contains
  images downloaded from amazon.com under studio lighting conditions,
  whilst the DSLR and Webcam domains consist of images captured with a
  high-resolution SLR camera and a low-resolution webcam, respectively.
  The dataset contains 4,652 images across 31 categories in total.
- •
  Office-Home \[[22](#bib.bib21)\] is a medium-scale dataset containing
  approximately 15,500 images of 65 everyday object categories across
  four visually distinct domains: Artistic images (Ar), Clip Art (Cl),
  Product images (Pr), and Real-World images (Rw). The dataset has on
  average 70 images per category and is notably more challenging than
  Office-31 due to its larger number of categories and greater domain
  diversity.
- •
  VisDA-C \[[24](#bib.bib22)\] is a large-scale dataset designed for the
  simulation-to-reality domain shift in object classification. The
  source domain comprises 152,397 synthetic images rendered from 3D CAD
  models under varying viewpoints and lighting conditions, whilst the
  target validation domain consists of 55,388 real object images cropped
  from the Microsoft COCO dataset. The dataset spans 12 object
  categories and poses a substantial distribution gap due to the
  synthetic-to-real nature of the shift.
- •
  DomainNet-126 \[[23](#bib.bib23)\] is a large-scale and challenging
  benchmark derived from the original DomainNet
  dataset \[[30](#bib.bib29)\], which contains approximately 600k images
  of 345 classes across 6 domains. Following \[[30](#bib.bib29)\], we
  use a curated subset of 4 domains—Clipart (C), Painting (P), Real (R),
  and Sketch (S)—and 126 classes with reduced label noise, resulting in
  roughly 145k images in total.
- •
  Camelyon17-WILDS \[[28](#bib.bib25)\] is a histopathology benchmark
  for evaluating robustness to domain shift across hospitals. Each input
  is a $`96\times 96`$ tissue patch, and the task is binary
  classification of whether the patch contains tumor tissue. The 5
  hospitals serve as distinct domains, with models trained on 3
  hospitals and evaluated on a held-out hospital, yielding 455,954
  labeled examples in total.
- •
  EyePACS \[[29](#bib.bib32)\] is a retinal fundus image dataset for
  diabetic retinopathy screening and severity assessment. It contains
  fundus photographs acquired under diverse imaging conditions,
  resulting in substantial variation in image appearance and quality. We
  use EyePACS as the source domain.
- •
  APTOS 2019³³ 3
  [https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/aptos2019-blindness-detection/data](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/aptos2019-blindness-detection/data)
  is a retinal fundus image dataset for diabetic retinopathy severity
  classification. The task consists of five classes corresponding to
  increasing levels of diabetic retinopathy severity. We use APTOS 2019
  as the target domain.

## Appendix C Implementation Details

### C.1 Epoch-Indexed Formulation

The main text omits epoch indices for notational brevity; here we make
the full temporal structure explicit and describe the efficient caching
scheme that underlies the implementation.

#### Feature Caching.

Computing cut statistics requires the complete feature set
$`\{\phi_{i}(x_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}`$ over the entire target domain, as
nearest-neighbor relationships cannot be resolved within a single
mini-batch. A naïve implementation would therefore require a dedicated
full forward pass at every epoch solely for cut statistic computation,
incurring significant overhead when the target domain contains hundreds
of thousands of samples.

To eliminate this cost, we maintain a feature bank
$`\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\phi}`$ and a prediction bank $`\mathcal{B}_{i}^{p}`$
for each model $`i\in\{t,v\}`$, populated *in-place* during the standard
optimization forward pass:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\phi,(e)}[j]\;\leftarrow\;\phi_{i}^{(e)}(x_{j}),\qquad\mathcal{B}_{i}^{p,(e)}[j]\;\leftarrow\;p_{i}^{(e)}(x_{j}),\qquad j\in\mathcal{B},
``` |  | (26) |

so that by the end of epoch $`e`$ the banks hold the most recent
representations without any additional forward pass. Because the CLIP
image encoder $`\phi_{v}`$ is entirely frozen,
$`\mathcal{B}_{v}^{\phi}`$ is computed once during warmup and never
modified thereafter. At the end of each epoch, the epoch-indexed cut
statistic is computed from the accumulated banks:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{z}_{i}^{(e)}(x)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{K}^{\mathrm{mut},(e)}(x)}\exp\!\bigl(-d(\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\phi,(e)}(x),\,\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\phi,(e)}(x^{\prime}))\bigr)\cdot\mathbf{1}\!\left[\mathcal{B}_{i}^{p,(e)}(x)\neq\mathcal{B}_{i}^{p,(e)}(x^{\prime})\right]}{\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{K}^{\mathrm{mut},(e)}(x)}\exp\!\bigl(-d(\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\phi,(e)}(x),\,\mathcal{B}_{i}^{\phi,(e)}(x^{\prime}))\bigr)},
``` |  | (27) |

yielding the raw reliability score
$`r_{i}^{(e)}(x)=\exp(-\hat{z}_{i}^{(e)}(x))`$.

#### EMA Smoothing with Cosine Momentum Schedule.

To stabilize reliability estimates across epochs, we apply an
exponential moving average:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{r}_{i}^{(e)}(x)=m^{(e)}\cdot\bar{r}_{i}^{(e-1)}(x)+\bigl(1-m^{(e)}\bigr)\cdot r_{i}^{(e)}(x),
``` |  | (28) |

where the momentum $`m^{(e)}`$ follows a cosine schedule that increases
monotonically:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
m^{(e)}=m_{\mathrm{start}}+(m_{\mathrm{end}}-m_{\mathrm{start}})\cdot\frac{1-\cos\!\left(\pi\cdot\frac{e}{E-1}\right)}{2}.
``` |  | (29) |

This assigns low momentum in early epochs to allow rapid adaptation, and
high momentum in later epochs to suppress noise once training
stabilizes. At initialization, we set $`\bar{r}_{i}^{(0)}=r_{i}^{(0)}`$
directly from the warmup pass.

#### Epoch-Indexed Training Objective.

Substituting $`\bar{r}_{i}^{(e-1)}`$ into the gating functions of
Eq. ([10](#S4.E10 "In 4.2 Reliability-Gated Mutual Correction ‣ 4 Training with Reliability-Gated Supervision ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")),
the full epoch-indexed objective becomes:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}^{(e)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[T_{v\to t}^{(e-1)}\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\mathrm{sg}(p_{v}^{(e)})\,\|\,p_{t}^{(e)}\right)+T_{t\to v}^{(e-1)}\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{t}^{(e-1)}\,\|\,p_{t}^{(e)}\right)\right]+\mathcal{R}(p_{t}^{(e)}),`$ |  | (30) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{v}^{(e)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[T_{t\to v}^{(e-1)}\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\mathrm{sg}(p_{t}^{(e)})\,\|\,p_{v}^{(e)}\right)+T_{v\to t}^{(e-1)}\cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{v}^{(e-1)}\,\|\,p_{v}^{(e)}\right)\right]+\mathcal{R}(p_{v}^{(e)}),`$ |  | (31) |

where
$`\mathcal{R}(p)=\lambda_{\mathrm{IIC}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IIC}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}H(\bar{p})`$
denotes the batch-level regularization term. Crucially, the gating
weights $`T^{(e-1)}`$ are derived from the *previous* epoch’s
reliability scores, preventing a circular dependency between the current
supervision signal and the reliability estimate used to weight it. The
complete procedure is summarized in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Epoch-Indexed Training Objective. ‣ C.1 Epoch-Indexed Formulation ‣ Appendix C Implementation Details ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics").

$`\theta_{v}`$, total epochs $`E`$.

set $`\bar{r}_{i}^{(0)}\leftarrow r_{i}^{(0)}`$ for $`i\in\{t,v\}`$.

4: for $`e=1,2,\ldots,E`$ do

5:   Compute gating weights $`T_{v\to t}^{(e-1)}`$,
$`T_{t\to v}^{(e-1)}`$ via

(no extra forward pass).

$`\bar{r}_{i}^{(e)}\leftarrow m^{(e)}\cdot\bar{r}_{i}^{(e-1)}+(1-m^{(e)})\cdot r_{i}^{(e)}`$
for $`i\in\{t,v\}`$.

11: end for

cut statistic functions as a reliable per-model reliability proxy
independent of the degree of inter-model asymmetry.

demonstrating that SafeCut can leverage complementary failure modes
effectively even when the peer model is architecturally identical and
initially less accurate than in the CLIP setting.

We observe a strong negative relationship between pre-adaptation

performance degradation is observed across the 12 evaluated pairs. These
results empirically support the role of peer complementarity in SafeCut
while also indicating that the potential gain naturally diminishes as
the two peers become increasingly similar.

## Appendix F Limitations and Future Directions

SafeCut establishes a principled foundation for reliability-gated mutual
correction, while several directions remain open for further
exploration. The framework benefits from complementary prediction
patterns between the two peers, while increasingly similar predictions
provide fewer opportunities for cross-model correction. Samples on which
both models are incorrect also remain comparatively challenging, as
neither peer provides an immediately corrective signal, although some of
these samples are gradually improved through changes in the surrounding
neighborhood structure. These observations suggest several promising
extensions. In particular, samples in low-margin regions could benefit
from complementary reliability cues, such as prediction entropy or
temporal consistency, to further sharpen directional discrimination.
Incorporating additional pseudo-label-based
strategies \[[31](#bib.bib30), [32](#bib.bib31)\] could also provide
richer supervisory signals during adaptation and potentially move
performance closer to the oracle correction bound identified in
Table [5](#S5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 5.3 Additional Analysis ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Safeguarding Mutual Correction in Source-Free Domain Adaptation via Cut Statistics")(b).
We leave these directions as future work.

## Appendix G Broader Impacts

SafeCut contributes to the broader goal of deploying machine learning
models reliably across diverse real-world environments without requiring
labeled target data or access to sensitive source data. This has direct
positive implications in privacy-sensitive domains such as healthcare
and finance, where sharing training data is often legally or ethically
restricted. By enabling effective adaptation under such constraints,
SafeCut lowers the barrier to deploying high-quality models in
underserved or data-scarce settings. Beyond privacy, the source-free
constraint also reduces the computational and logistical costs
associated with retaining and transferring large-scale source datasets,
making model deployment more accessible to practitioners with limited
infrastructure. Furthermore, the mutual correction framework introduced
in SafeCut is model-agnostic and does not assume a specific type of peer
model, broadening its applicability to heterogeneous deployment
scenarios where diverse pretrained models are increasingly available.
Finally, by establishing a theoretically grounded reliability criterion
for cross-model supervision, SafeCut contributes to the broader agenda
of trustworthy machine learning, providing principled mechanisms to
mitigate confirmation bias and error propagation in self-supervised
adaptation pipelines. We do not foresee significant negative societal
impacts from this work. However, as with any adaptation method that
operates without human supervision, there remains a risk that errors
shared across both peer models go undetected, potentially leading to
incorrect predictions in high-stakes applications such as medical
diagnosis. Practitioners deploying SafeCut in such settings should
complement adaptation with appropriate human oversight and validation
protocols.
````

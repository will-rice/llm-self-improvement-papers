###### Abstract

Catastrophic forgetting remains a major challenge when fine-tuning large
language models (LLMs) on narrow, task-specific data, often degrading
their general knowledge and reasoning abilities. We propose Sa-sft, a
lightweight self-augmentation routine in which an LLM generates
self-dialogues prior to fine-tuning, and the resulting self-authored
data are mixed with task data without modifying optimization or training
schedules.

Despite requiring no external data or additional tuning, Sa-sft
consistently mitigates catastrophic forgetting while improving in-domain
performance. Across 50 evaluation scenarios, it maintains performance
comparable to the original model and achieves the best results in 40
cases, outperforming common baselines such as layer freezing and
external data mixing. Guided by these empirical findings, we further
present a theoretical analysis suggesting that forgetting can partly
stem from style-induced parameter drift, and that self-alignment through
self-generated data provides an effective means to counteract this
effect. Overall, our results indicate that self-augmentation offers a
simple and effective mechanism for robust LLM adaptation without
incurring catastrophic forgetting.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) now underpin modern human machine
intelligence, driving agents that code, reason, converse, and assist
across domains [Nam et al. (2024)](#bib.bib17); [Roziere et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib21); [Anand et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1). Their hallmark
strength—an ability to rapidly _specialize_ when fine-tuned on
domain-specific data—has also exposed a critical weakness: each
specialization step risks overwriting the general reasoning, linguistic,
and world knowledge that make these models broadly useful in the first
place [Luo et al. (2023)](#bib.bib14); [Kemker et al.
(2018)](#bib.bib12); [Zhang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib32); [Ramasesh et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib19). This phenomenon, known as _catastrophic
forgetting_, undermines the long-term stability and trustworthiness of
LLM-driven agents—e.g., a chatbot fine-tuned for customer support may
suddenly lose arithmetic competence, factual recall, or safety
alignment, compromising reliability in real-world interaction.

Existing safeguards are either expensive or fragile. One family of
methods relies on _data rehearsal_: practitioners curate or purchase
large, heterogeneous corpora to “remind” the model of what it once knew
[Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib10); [Mok et al. (2023)](#bib.bib16);
[de Masson D’Autume et al. (2019)](#bib.bib4). Another family introduces
explicit regularizers—such as Elastic Weight Consolidation [Xiang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib28) or Kullback–Leibler penalties [Nikoloutsopoulos
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib18)—that require per-task tuning and often yield
unstable results. In practice, however, such parameter-based
regularization remains uncommon in large-scale SFT pipelines: estimating
and storing parameter importance (e.g., via Fisher information) for
billions of weights incurs prohibitive memory and communication cost,
making these methods impractical beyond small or controlled setups. As a
result, most real-world adaptations resort to cheaper heuristics—data
mixing [Cao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib2), layer freezing [Zheng et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib33), or LoRA [Hu et al. (2022)](#bib.bib9)—yet these too
struggle to maintain general ability over long training horizons.

In this paper, we ask a different question: _What if the model could
rehearse on data of its own making?_ Inspired by self-instruction
frameworks such as Self-Instruct [Wang et al. (2022a)](#bib.bib25),
Magpie [Xu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib29), and Crescent [Sun et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib23), we propose Self-Augmented Supervised Fine-Tuning
(Sa-sft)—a lightweight self-modeling routine that lets an LLM generate
and integrate its own rehearsal data before adaptation. Before touching
any downstream corpus, the frozen base model conducts short
self-talking, as shown in
[Fig. 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models").
The resulting self-authored dataset is folded into the normal
fine-tuning stream without changing optimization or schedules.

Despite its stark simplicity—no external data, no extra losses, and no
hyper-tuning—Sa-sft consistently stabilizes learning and enhances
specialization. Across five heterogeneous tasks drawn from
Super-Natural-Instructions [Wang et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib26), Sa-sft
restores general-ability performance in all 50 evaluation scenarios and
achieves the best overall results in 40 of them, outperforming
mainstream strategies such as layer freezing [Zheng et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib33) and external data mixing while achieving the most
balanced and stable trade-off between retention and adaptation. Unlike
prevailing fine-tuning practices that require extensive effort to curate
or match suitable external datasets—an increasingly difficult task given
that most large models do not disclose their pretraining corpora. We
further provide a theoretical explanation showing that this Sa-sft
process mitigates style-induced parameter drift—the underlying cause of
spurious forgetting—thereby enabling a form of autonomous,
self-consistent data modeling.

Our contributions are threefold:

1.  1.  A self-modeling data paradigm. We present Sa-sft, a
        forgetting-mitigation framework that transforms rehearsal into a
        form of autonomous data modeling—requiring no external corpus,
        task-specific filtering, or additional loss design.
2.  2.  Comprehensive validation. Extensive experiments and analyses across
        five benchmarks and multiple architectures confirm that
        self-generated data can match or surpass curated external datasets
        in retaining general skills.
3.  3.  Mechanistic and theoretical insight. We identify and formalize
        _style-induced forgetting_ as a key failure mode and show
        theoretically that self-aligned data suppresses these harmful
        stylistic gradients, yielding stable, interpretable adaptation
        dynamics.

Taken together, these results recast catastrophic forgetting not as an
inevitable tax on specialization, but as a solvable optimization
artefact—one that the model itself is surprisingly well equipped to fix.
By letting an LLM “talk to itself” for a brief moment before adaptation,
we unlock a simple yet effective path toward continually learning
systems that remember _and_ advance.

Leveraging synthetic data has emerged as an effective strategy to
improve and adapt large language models without extensive human
annotations. For example, [Wang et al. (2022a)](#bib.bib25) introduce
Self-Instruct, a framework where a model generates its own
instruction-following data to bootstrap further fine-tuning. Magpie [Xu
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib29) introduces a self-synthesis method to
generate large-scale alignment data by utilizing only pre-defined chat
templates. Crescent [Sun et al. (2025)](#bib.bib23) proves that
self-generated data has the potential to enable the model to
self-improve on reasoning tasks such as mathematics. Such synthetic data
generation techniques provide a nearly annotation-free approach to align
pretrained models with desired behaviors, laying a foundation for our
approach of model-in-the-loop data synthesis.

#### Mitigation Methods of Catastrophic Forgetting.

Catastrophic forgetting is a well-known challenge in fine-tuning LLMs on
narrow domains, where pre-trained general knowledge and capabilities can
substantially degrade [Song et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22). A common
mitigation strategy is data rehearsal, which mixes general-domain or
instruction-tuning data with task-specific data during fine-tuning to
preserve prior abilities. This idea has been explored through both
external and self-generated rehearsal data. For example,
Self-Synthesized Rehearsal [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib10) generates
auxiliary samples to reduce forgetting, though it still relies on access
to high-quality reference data from earlier stages. In practice, many
domain-specific LLMs similarly replay portions of general instruction
data during adaptation, a pattern observed across legal, financial, and
other applied settings [Wu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib27); [Ke et al.
()](#bib.bib11); [Ding and Wang (2025)](#bib.bib5).

Beyond data-centric approaches, parameter-based methods aim to constrain
model updates during fine-tuning. Inspired by continual learning, some
methods regularize parameter drift to protect important weights, such as
EWCLoRA [Xiang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib28). Others rely on
parameter-efficient fine-tuning schemes, including adapters or partial
weight updates, to inherently retain pre-trained knowledge by limiting
the number of trainable parameters [Hu et al. (2022)](#bib.bib9).

We refer to our proposed procedure as _Self-Augmented Supervised
Fine-Tuning_ (Sa-sft). Sa-sft inserts a single _self-augmentation_ phase
before conventional supervised fine-tuning (SFT). The model first
fabricates a small _refresher_ dialogue corpus from its own knowledge,
then this corpus is _mixed_ with the downstream data. The resulting
dataset, requiring no additional post-processing, is then directly fed
into the model for fine-tuning.

#### Notation.

We denote by $`f_{\omega_{0}}`$ a pretrained language model with
parameters $`\omega_{0}`$, by
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}`$ the
domain-specific SFT dataset, and by $`\lambda\in(0,+\infty]`$ the
_mix-in ratio_.

#### Step 1: Self-augmentation.

Sample $`M=\lambda N`$ question–answer pairs with the frozen model:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}_{j}&\sim f_{\omega_{0}}\!\bigl(\,\cdot\mid p_{j}\bigr)\\
y^{\prime}_{j}&\sim f_{\omega_{0}}\!\bigl(\,\cdot\mid x^{\prime}_{j}\bigr)\end{aligned}\right.\quad j=1,\dots,M,
``` |  | (1) |

where each prompt $`p_{j}`$—either a Magpie [Xu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib29) special token or a Crescent [Sun et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib23) bait prompt—elicits a continuation treated as
$`x^{\prime}_{j}`$. Feeding $`x^{\prime}_{j}`$ back once produces the
answer $`y^{\prime}_{j}`$. We denote the resulting corpus
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}=\{(x^{\prime}_{j},\,y^{\prime}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{M}`$.

#### Generation hyper-parameters.

Unless stated otherwise, all self-generated samples use nucleus
(top-$`p`$) sampling with $`p{=}0.9`$, temperature $`T{=}0.7`$, and a
maximum length of 256 tokens; we found these default values balance
diversity and faithfulness across all base models tested.

#### Step 2: Mix-in.

Merge the two corpora

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}_{\text{mix}}\;=\;\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}\;\cup\;\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}},\lvert\mathcal{D}_{\text{mix}}\rvert\;=\;N(1+\lambda).
``` |  | (2) |

#### Fine-tuning objective.

Optimise parameters (full or LoRA) under the usual cross-entropy

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\min_{\omega}`$ | $`\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{(1+\lambda)N}\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}}\!\ell(f_{\omega}(x),y)}_{\text{task loss}}`$ |  | (3) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\;+`$ | $`\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{(1+\lambda)N}\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}}\!\ell(f_{\omega}(x),y)}_{\text{self-augmentation loss}}.`$ |  |  |

The objective corresponds to standard SFT on the concatenation of
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}`$ and $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}`$, i.e.,
minimizing the average token-level cross-entropy over the union of the
two datasets. The relative contribution of each component is implicitly
determined by its dataset size, with $`|\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}|=N`$
and $`|\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}|=\lambda N`$.

#### Implementation notes.

Sa-sft is model-agnostic and can be applied atop any pretrained
decoder-only architecture without modifying the base model or training
framework. The self-augmentation phase requires no gradient updates and
can be implemented as a lightweight sampling loop. Moreover, the ratio
$`\lambda`$ serves as a simple yet flexible knob to control the
influence of self-generated data, allowing practitioners to adapt Sa-sft
to a wide range of domains and data regimes with minimal tuning effort.

We evaluate five mainstream instruction-tuned LLMs that cover two

their original open-source releases and are kept frozen for the “Base”

#### Fine-tuning tasks.

We sample five heterogeneous tasks from the Super-NaturalInstructions
(SuperNI) benchmark [Wang et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib26) as our fine-tuning
suite. SuperNI is a large-scale collection of 1,616 diverse NLP tasks,
each accompanied by expert-written instructions, designed to evaluate a
model’s ability to generalize across task boundaries. The benchmark
spans 76 distinct task types, including classification, extraction,
sequence tagging, infilling, rewriting, and more, making it a rigorous
testbed for instruction-following capabilities. Among these, we select
five representative tasks that cover a broad functional spectrum:  1.
Question Answering (QA); 2. Question Generation (QG); 3. Sentiment
Analysis (SA); 4. Summarization (Sum.); 5. Translation (Trans.);

For training, we randomly sample 2,000 instruction response pairs per
task (10,000 in total), and reserve an additional 500 pairs per task
(2,500 total) for in-domain evaluation. These tasks were deliberately
chosen to reflect varied linguistic phenomena and task structures,
thereby simulating realistic multi-task fine-tuning scenarios.

#### Fine-tuning regimes.

For every model we train two adaptation flavours: full-parameter
supervised fine-tuning (full) and parameter-efficient Low-Rank
Adaptation (LoRA) [Hu et al. (2022)](#bib.bib9) with rank 8 and
$`\alpha`$ = 16. Within each flavour we consider six data regimes:

• Base:no additional training; • Task-only:fine-tune on the task corpus
alone; • Freeze:identical to the Task-only setting but with the bottom 6
layers, including the embedding layer, frozen during training, following
the layer-freezing settings proposed in prior work [Zheng et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib33), and applied only to *full* SFT. • Task+Alpaca:mix
the task corpus 1:1 with the Alpaca instruction-following dataset
(20,000 pairs in total); • Task+UC:mix the task corpus 1:1 with
UltraChat (20000 pairs in total); • Sa-sft:mix the task corpus 1:1 with
the $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}`$ from Sa-sft procedure (20000 in
total).

We use the LLaMA-Factory [Zheng et al. (2024)](#bib.bib34) framework for
all fine-tuning experiments. Training and evaluation are all performed
on NVIDIA A100-80 GB GPUs. All models are trained for 3 epochs with a
batch size of 32 under Deepspeed ZeRO-3 [Rasley et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib20) and a cosine schedule with 10 % warm-up; the
learning rate is $`2\times 10^{-4}`$ for LoRA and $`1\times 10^{-5}`$
for full SFT. All computations use bfloat16 precision.

#### Generation Configurations.

For the LLaMA3 family (8B and 3.2B) and Qwen2.5, we generate synthetic
data with the Magpie sampling scheme, adopting the same hyper-parameters
released in its official repository [Xu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib29).
Magpie is not fully compatible with the LLaMA2 series, so for LLaMA2-7B
and LLaMA2-13B we switch to the Crescent. We set the bait prompt as:
“*Give me some questions about everyday life*”, while all remaining
settings follow the values reported in the Crescent paper [Sun et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib23).

#### Benchmarks.

Our evaluation comprises two parts. First, to confirm in-domain
effectiveness, we test every model produced by each fine-tuning regime
on the in-domain test splits of the five SuperNI tasks and report their
*Averaged ROUGE-L* (AR) score following the evaluation settings in
[Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib10). Second, to quantify catastrophic
forgetting, we compare all the models on a suite of broad
benchmarks—GSM8K [Cobbe et al. (2021)](#bib.bib3), MMLU [Hendrycks
et al. (2021)](#bib.bib8), IFEval [Zhou et al. (2023)](#bib.bib36),
MedText [Melamud and Shivade (2019)](#bib.bib15), and the *Gaokao
Chinese* subset of AGIEval (AGIEval_G) [Zhong et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib35)—covering mathematical reasoning, multi-domain
knowledge, instruction following and other general-purpose knowledge and
abilities, where we report accuracy for all benchmarks except MedText,
for which we report ROUGE-L.

#### Results and Discussion

As shown in
[Table 1](#S2.T1 "In Synthetic Data. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models"),
our results fall into two complementary views: in-domain efficacy and
general-ability retention.

In-domain efficacy. Across the ten in-domain settings (five models
$`\times`$ full & LoRA), all five fine-tuning regimes—Task-only, Freeze,
Task + Alpaca, Task + UC, and Sa-sft outperform the untouched bases.
Every fine-tuning variant surpasses the Base models by a large margin.
The Freeze variant achieves comparable yet consistently lower scores
than Task-only, indicating that restricting lower-layer adaptation
hampers the model’s ability to efficiently acquire new task knowledge.

Meanwhile, introducing additional conversational data produces markedly
different effects depending on its origin. Task+Alpaca and Task+UC
achieve performance comparable to Task-only across most settings.
However, both remain slightly below the pure Task-only regime on
average, suggesting that while cross-domain data helps maintain learning
capacity, it introduces mild domain interference that limits full
specialization. Most importantly, Sa-sft continues to outperform all
other regimes in 8 out of 10 in-domain configurations, confirming that
self-generated data not only preserves domain focus but also accelerates
targeted knowledge acquisition—echoing findings from SCAR [Li et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib13).

General-ability retention. Turning to the general benchmarks, we
evaluate 50 different scenarios (5 models $`\times`$ 5 benchmarks
$`\times`$ 2 adaptation flavours). Task-only fine-tuning consistently
degrades performance relative to Base (Task-only $`<`$ Base),
reaffirming the ubiquity of catastrophic forgetting. Freeze provides an
unstable form of forgetting mitigation. While freezing the bottom six
layers can, in some cases, reduce representational drift and preserve
parts of general ability, its effect is highly sensitive to the base
model. For instance, on Qwen2.5-7B-I., the Freeze variant consistently
underperforms Task-only across all benchmarks, and even suffers abrupt
collapses on specific tasks such as GSM8K. These observations indicate
that layer freezing offers only limited and inconsistent protection
against forgetting. On the other hand, mixing task data with existing
instruction or dialogue corpora proves more effective overall. Both
Task+Alpaca and Task+UC alleviate forgetting to varying degrees, but
their success depends heavily on the alignment between auxiliary and
target domains. For example, on MedText, mixing Alpaca surpasses
UltraChat in all 10 cases, whereas on the mathematical GSM8K, UltraChat
outperforms Alpaca in 9 out of 10 cases. This illustrates a key
limitation of conventional data-mixing strategies: while capable of
mitigating forgetting, they demand *careful dataset selection and tuning
to remain effective across domains*.

In contrast, Sa-sft robustly mitigates forgetting in all 50 / 50 cases
and achieves the best overall performance in 40 / 50 scenarios,
maintaining or improving general-ability metrics across model scale
(3.2B–13B), architecture (LLaMA2, LLaMA3, Qwen2.5), and evaluation
domain. Its effectiveness remains consistent across different base
models and benchmark types, showing no pronounced sensitivity to model
size, backbone design, or task nature. This stability suggests that the
self-generated data from Sa-sft captures transferable knowledge that
regularizes fine-tuning in a model-agnostic manner, offering a universal
and reliable defence against catastrophic forgetting without requiring
dataset-specific tuning.

Together, these results demonstrate that letting a model self-augment
via a *task-aware*, *lightly curated* synthetic corpus is a more
effective and self-consistent defence against catastrophic forgetting
than external data mixing or layer freezing, while still *enhancing
in-domain performance*.

## 5 Analysis

### 5.1 Sensitivity to the Sa-sft Mix Ratio

To assess how sensitive Sa-sft is to the amount of self-generated data
and whether Sa-sft’s benefit over UltraChat emerges only at a narrow
span, we carry out a mix-ratio sensitivity study.

Starting from the task-specific training set $`D_{\text{task}}`$, we
create two augmented variants: (1) Task+UC: $`D_{\text{task}}`$ mixed
$`1:r`$ with UltraChat data; (2) Sa-sft: $`D_{\text{task}}`$ mixed
$`1:r`$ with self-generated data; where the ratio
$`r\in\{0.25\allowbreak,0.50\allowbreak,0.75\allowbreak,1.00\allowbreak,1.25\allowbreak,1.50\allowbreak,1.75\allowbreak\}.`$
For every setting we fine-tune LLaMA3-8B-I. with LoRA under settings in
[Section 4](#S4 "4 Main Experiments ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models"),
yielding 14 checkpoints (7 ratios × 2 sources). We then measure GSM8K
and in-domain performance on those models. The results are showed in
[Fig. 2](#S3.F2 "In 3 Methodology ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models").

#### Results.

Across both panels, we have the following key findings:

1.  1.
    Sa-sft consistently outperforms the Task+UC at every tested ratio,
    indicating that its advantage is *not confined to a narrow
    sweet-spot*.
2.  2.
    [Fig. 2(a)](#S3.F2.sf1 "In Figure 2 ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models")
    shows a general rise in GSM8K accuracy for both mixing strategies as
    the ratio $`r`$ increases: *adding more generic data can better
    restore general capabilities*.
3.  3.
    [Fig. 2(b)](#S3.F2.sf2 "In Figure 2 ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models")
    shows a sharp drop once $`r>1.25`$. Excessive generic data begins to
    *dilute task-specific signal*, harming downstream tasks.

Balancing these observations, a 1 : 1 mix ($`r=1.0`$) offers the best
trade-off—*maximizing general-domain retention without eroding in-domain
accuracy*. Crucially, Sa-sft ’s self-generation feature grants
practitioners fine-grained control over the data size, allowing them to
tailor the mix to any task *without hunting for external corpus*.

### 5.2 Step-Level Trajectory of Forgetting

Figure 3: GSM8K accuracy over training steps.

To pinpoint *when* catastrophic forgetting emerges and *how* different
fine-tuning regimes alter its course, we fine-tuned LLaMA3-8B-Instruct
for three epochs and saved a snapshot every 40 optimisation steps (0
$`\rightarrow`$ 240). At each snapshot we evaluated GSM8K accuracy. The
experiment compared five otherwise identical regimes that differ only in
the auxiliary data blended with the downstream task corpus:
(i)Task-only; (ii) Task+UC (1:1 mix); (iii) Sa-sft-our method (1:1 mix);
(iv) Task+Math-Instruct [Yue et al. (2024)](#bib.bib31) (1:1 mix of an
external mathematics corpus); (v) Task+GSM8K (approximately 1:0.7 mix of
GSM8K’s own train split);

[Fig. 3](#S5.F3 "In 5.2 Step-Level Trajectory of Forgetting ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models")
yields the following insights:  1. The Sa-sft curve remains *essentially
flat and consistently achieves the highest accuracy*, indicating that
self-generated data can provide stable and robust protection for math
reasoning throughout the entire fine-tuning process. 2. The Task+UC
curve *consistently under performs* compared to Sa-sft and exhibits
*greater fluctuation*, suggesting less stable resistance to forgetting.
3. Task-only exhibits the canonical monotonic decline, confirming
significant forgetting when fine-tuning on specific downstream tasks
without any augmentation. 4. Task+GSM8K follows a U-shaped trajectory:
performance drops sharply at first and then gradually recovers,
indicating that mixing data from *even the same distribution* as the
evaluation set does not prevent forgetting, but instead causes the model
to forget and then learn new knowledge (or adapt to new distribution).
This recovery process is slow, in our experiment, even by step 240—when
the model has already fit the fine-tuning task well—*it still has not
fully regained its math ability*. 5. Task+Math-Instruct improves
marginally over Task-Only, implying that simply injecting
high-quality—but *distribution-mismatched*—math corpus cannot restore
forgotten skills within the 240-step budget; much of the compute is
likely spent adapting to *new style*, rather than reinforcing
*transferable reasoning skills*.

#### Implications.

The contrast between Sa-sft ’s flat trajectory and the
recovery-after-collapse seen with Task+GSM8K highlights a crucial
distinction: *preventing forgetting during training versus repairing it
afterwards*. Generating a model-specific corpus before fine-tuning
offers a lightweight, computation-efficient safeguard that outperforms
both open-domain and in-domain external datasets.

### 5.3 Impact of Generator Model

One of our core claim: Sa-sft is most effective when the synthetic
corpus is produced by the very model that will later be fine-tuned. A
natural follow-up question is: *How sensitive is this benefit to the
choice of generator?* If we depart from the “same-model” principle—using
a generator that differs in architecture, training recipe, or parameter
size—does the resulting synthetic data still curb forgetting, or does
the mismatch degrade its value?

To answer this, we fix LLaMA3-8B-Instruct as the base model and consider
five alternative generators: (1) Qwen2.5-7B-I., (2) LLaMA2-7B-C., (3)
LLaMA2-13B-C., (4) LLaMA3.2-3B-I., (5) LLaMA3-70B-I..

|---------------|------|-------|------|-------|
| LLaMA3-8B-I.  | 45   | 17    | 38   | 1900  |
| Qwen2.5-7B-I. | 74   | 29    | 118  | 1779  |

dialogues (absolute counts)

For every generator we keep all other hyper-parameters identical to the
[Section 4](#S4 "4 Main Experiments ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models");
the synthetic corpus size is matched 1 : 1 to the fine-tuning tasks.
Each combined dataset is then fine-tuned on the base model using exactly
the same LoRA configuration as before. Finally, we evaluate: (i)

still falls short of the self-generated variant.

On the in-domain task, self-augmentation again performs competitively
(second only to the LLaMA2-13B-C. generator), while the latter suffers a
severe drop on the general benchmarks—a trade-off that self-generated
data avoids. It is to be noted that, on public leaderboards [Fourrier
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib7) , Qwen2.5-7B-I. and LLaMA3-70B-I. both outrank
the 8B model. Their synthetic data indeed sharpen instruction-following
ability (see the IFEval column), but they do not replicate the unique
anti-forgetting effect of “same-model” data.

Taken together, these results indicate that higher-quality but
stylistically mismatched data cannot match the effectiveness of truly
self-generated data.

### 5.4 What Really Shields Models from Forgetting?

The preceding experiments show that inserting a short Sa-sft phase
almost completely restores GSM8K accuracy, but *why* does it work? Two
competing explanations exist:

1.  A.
    The self-generated corpus happens to contain critical facts and
    skills (e.g. mathematical reasoning) that are later replayed during
    SFT, directly inoculating the model.
2.  B.
    The corpus merely matches the model’s own latent distribution; any
    low–perplexity text—regardless of topic—tempers the large,
    style–driven parameter shifts responsible for *format-induced
    forgetting*.

We distinguish A and B via a two-step study:

#### Step 1: How Much Math/Logic Is in the Data?

#### Protocol.

We select tow generators: LLaMA3-8B-I. and Qwen2.5-7B-I.—we uniformly
sample $`2\,000`$ dialogues. Each dialogue is classified by GPT-4o,
which assigns one of four mutually exclusive labels: (i)Math
(arithmetic, algebra, word problems); (ii) Logic (syllogisms, truth
tables, grid puzzles); (iii)Code (programming, debugging, algorithms);
(iv)Other (all remaining content).

#### Findings.

[Table 3](#S5.T3 "In 5.3 Impact of Generator Model ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models")
reveals that strictly *math-related* material is scarce: only
$`\approx 5\,\%`$ of the self-generated dialogues touch mathematics,
logic, or code for LLaMA3, ($`\approx 11\,\%`$ for Qwen2.5). Despite
this modest share,
[Section 4](#S4 "4 Main Experiments ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models")
showed a great recovery on GSM8K, hinting that factors beyond mere
replay of math problems may be at work.

#### Step 2: Removing *All* Math/Logic/Code

#### Construction of “logic-free” corpora.

Using the same GPT-4o filter, we *delete* every dialogue labelled Math,
Logic, or Code from the self-generated corpora of these two models. We
then down-sample the data to a fixed $`10\,000`$ pairs to keep the ratio
to 1:1, obtaining two new datasets: LLaMA3-NoLogic and Qwen2.5-NoLogic.

#### Fine-tuning.

We repeat LoRA fine-tuning under the identical settings of
[Section 4](#S4 "4 Main Experiments ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models")
and evaluate on GSM8K on LLaMA3-8B-I. and Qwen2.5-7B-I. models.
[Table 4](#S5.T4 "In Fine-tuning. ‣ 5.4 What Really Shields Models from Forgetting? ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models")
shows the outcome.

| Regime           | LLaMA3-8B-I. | Qwen2.5-7B-I. |
|------------------|--------------|---------------|
| Base             | 75.8         | 82.0          |
| Task-Only        | 65.6         | 77.7          |
| Sa-sft Full data | 74.2         | 82.1          |
| Sa-sft NoLogic   | 73.7         | 82.3          |

Table 4: GSM8K accuracy (%) after SFT with and without math/logic/code
content.

Key result: Even after purging every trace of math/logic/code, Sa-sft
data still prevents most of the GSM8K accuracy drop.

We therefore support explanation B: we argue that a substantial portion
of catastrophic forgetting in SFT arises from *style-induced gradient
drift* rather than the loss of underlying knowledge. By mixing
self-generated samples that are pretraining-aligned in surface form,
Sa-sft counteracts such spurious stylistic updates, thereby preserving
general capabilities while learning the target task. Theoretical
analysis is provided in
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Theoretical Analysis: Style-Mismatch–Induced Forgetting ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models").

## 6 Conclusion

Catastrophic forgetting need not be the price of specialization. With a
brief “self-talk” phase, our Self-Augmented SFT (Sa-sft) shields LLMs
from skill loss while boosting task accuracy—without external data,
extra losses, or manual curation. Across five public benchmarks, Sa-sft
not only recovers general ability and strengthens in-domain performance,
but also consistently outperforms mainstream baselines, offering the
most stable balance between retention and adaptation. Our theoretical
analysis further explains this robustness: self-generated,
in-distribution text implicitly regularizes training by counteracting
style-induced parameter drift. Because Sa-sft integrates seamlessly into
existing fine-tuning pipelines with minimal overhead, it provides an
immediate, low-effort safeguard for building stable and adaptive LLMs.

## 7 Limitations

While Sa-sft is simple and effective, it also has several limitations.

#### Dependence on the base model’s generative quality.

Sa-sft relies on the frozen base model to produce a self-authored corpus
that is sufficiently diverse and well-formed. For weaker or poorly
instruction-tuned models, self-dialogues may become repetitive,
low-information, or biased toward shallow conversational patterns, which
can reduce the regularization effect and may even introduce noise into
training. In such cases, Sa-sft may require more careful prompt design
or basic filtering to avoid degenerate samples.

#### Incomplete coverage of all forgetting mechanisms.

Our analysis highlights *style-induced parameter drift* as an important
contributor to catastrophic forgetting, and Sa-sft is particularly
suited to counteracting this failure mode via self-aligned text.
However, the *true causes* of catastrophic forgetting are still actively
debated and likely multifactorial: existing work attributes forgetting
to different sources, including gradient interference and representation
drift, task similarity and sequential training effects, and the
interaction between optimization dynamics and parameter importance
[Kemker et al. (2018)](#bib.bib12); [Ramasesh et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib19); [Luo et al. (2023)](#bib.bib14); [Zheng et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib33). As a result, Sa-sft should be viewed as a targeted
mitigation for the mechanism we identify and formalize. While our
empirical results suggest that self-aligned rehearsal can be effective
in many practical settings, we do not claim it resolves all possible
causes of forgetting, nor do we deeply investigate regimes where other
mechanisms dominate.

#### Extra generation cost and potential data risks.

Although Sa-sft avoids external datasets and additional loss terms, it
still introduces an upfront sampling step to create
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}`$, which incurs extra inference-time compute
and storage—especially when scaling to many tasks or continual updates.
Moreover, self-generated data can contain inaccuracies, unsafe content,
or spurious biases inherited from the base model, which may be amplified
when mixed into fine-tuning. Practical deployments may therefore benefit
from inexpensive safeguards (e.g., deduplication, toxicity screening, or
simple quality heuristics) to reduce such risks without undermining the
method’s lightweight nature.

## Appendix A Theoretical Analysis: Style-Mismatch–Induced Forgetting

### A.1 Intuitive Explanation

When fine-tuned on external SFT datasets, a pretrained language model
often encounters stylistic conventions—templated instruction formats,
repeated punctuation, or specific discourse patterns—that rarely appear
in its pretraining corpus. These stylistic artifacts trigger large
gradient updates as the optimizer attempts to mimic the new “surface
form.” Such updates, however, carry little semantic information and
primarily distort parameter directions that were tuned to the
pretraining style. Consequently, the model experiences catastrophic
forgetting: performance on general reasoning and pretraining-aligned
tasks declines, not because the underlying knowledge is overwritten, but
because the parameters have been rotated toward an unfamiliar stylistic
regime.

Our framework Sa-sft proposes that self-generated mixing alleviates this
issue. When we interleave a portion of self-generated samples—whose
linguistic style matches the model’s own pretraining distribution—the
optimizer’s updates become more style-balanced. Gradients arising from
familiar, in-distribution text statistically cancel the superficial
stylistic components introduced by the external SFT corpus. As a result,
the parameter trajectory remains closer to the pretraining manifold,
preserving previously acquired capabilities while still absorbing
task-specific information.

### A.2 Formal Setup

Let $`f_{\omega}`$ be a pretrained model with parameters $`\omega_{0}`$,
and let $`\ell(\cdot,\cdot)`$ denote the per-token cross-entropy loss.
For a distribution $`\mathcal{D}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\omega)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(f_{\omega}(x),y)].`$ |  | (4) |

Denote by $`\mathcal{D}_{0}`$ the pretraining distribution and by
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}`$ the external SFT dataset. After $`T`$
fine-tuning steps from initialization $`\omega_{0}`$, the amount of
forgetting is quantified as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{forget}}=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}(\omega_{T})-\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}(\omega_{0}).`$ |  | (5) |

The expected gradient at initialization is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}}[\nabla_{\omega}\ell(f_{\omega_{0}}(x),y)].`$ |  | (6) |

We interpret each example as generated from latent content $`c`$ and
style $`s`$, where $`s`$ captures superficial linguistic factors such as
formatting or register.

### A.3 Style–Semantic Decomposition

Empirically, gradients can be approximately decomposed into two
subspaces:

Assumption 1 (Approximate subspace separability): There exists a Style
subspace $`\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ and its orthogonal
complement $`\mathcal{S}^{\perp}`$ (the Semantic subspace) such that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{\text{task}}\approx g_{\text{sty}}+g_{\text{sem}},\quad g_{\text{sty}}\in\mathcal{S};g_{\text{sem}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\perp}.`$ |  | (7) |

Here $`g_{\text{sty}}`$ captures gradients that primarily change surface
form, while $`g_{\text{sem}}`$ modifies content-related representations.
When the stylistic distribution of $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}`$
deviates strongly from the pretraining distribution $`S_{0}`$, the Style
component $`g_{\text{sty}}`$ tends to dominate, producing parameter
drift unrelated to semantic improvement.

To quantify this mismatch, we define the style perplexity gap:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{ppl}}={}`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}}[-\log p_{\omega_{0}}(x)]`$ |  | (8) |
|  | $`\displaystyle-`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{0}}[-\log p_{\omega_{0}}(x)].`$ |  |  |

A larger $`\Delta_{\mathrm{ppl}}`$ indicates that the SFT text is less
typical under the base model’s pretraining style, correlating with
larger $`||g_{\text{sty}}||`$ and stronger forgetting.

### A.4 First-Order View of Forgetting

Assuming local $`L`$-smoothness of $`\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}`$ and
an SGD update rule
$`\omega_{t+1}=\omega_{t}-\eta g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})`$, a
first-order Taylor expansion yields:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{forget}}`$ | $`\displaystyle\approx-\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}(\omega_{0}),\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t-1})\right\rangle`$ |  | (9) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+O\!\big(L\sum_{t}\eta^{2}||g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t-1})||^{2}\big).`$ |  |  |

This expression shows that forgetting arises when the cumulative update
has a large projection onto directions that increase pretraining loss.
The complete derivation of this result is provided in
[Section B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Local Smoothness and Two-Sided First-Order Remainder ‣ Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models").
If the Style component dominates the update trajectory, the inner
product above becomes positive, leading to observable degradation on
pretraining-aligned benchmarks even in the absence of semantic conflict.

### A.5 Effect of Sa-sft

Let $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}`$ be the distribution of model-generated
samples. For a mixing ratio $`\varepsilon=\lambda/(1+\lambda)`$ induced
by dataset concatenation,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{mix}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}+\varepsilon\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}},`$ |  | (10) |
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{\text{mix}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)g_{\text{task}}+\varepsilon g_{\text{self}}.`$ |  |  |

Because self-generated text is style-aligned with the pretraining
distribution, we have $`g_{\text{self}}\approx g_{0}`$, and thus

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}}(g_{\text{mix}})\approx(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}}(g_{\text{task}}),`$ |  | (11) |

implying that self-mixing linearly attenuates the Style component of the
gradient. The complete derivation is provided in
[Section B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Effect of Mixing Self-Generated Data ‣ Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models").
Under the same local-smoothness assumption, the corresponding forgetting
can be upper-bounded (informally) as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{forget}}^{(\text{mix})}\lesssim(1-\varepsilon)\Delta_{\text{forget}}^{(\text{task})}+O(L\sum_{t}\eta^{2}||g_{\text{mix}}||^{2}),`$ |  | (12) |

suggesting that moderate ratios (e.g., $`\varepsilon\approx 0.5`$)
optimally suppress style-induced drift while maintaining sufficient task
signal. Notably, replacing $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}`$ with data
generated by other models is less effective: their stylistic
distribution $`S_{\text{other}}`$ deviates from $`S_{0}`$, resulting in
weaker cancellation of style gradients despite potential semantic
richness.

## Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details

### B.1 Preliminaries and Notation

Let $`\mathcal{R}(\omega)\equiv\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}(\omega)`$
denote the pretraining risk, and let $`\{\omega_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}`$ be the
parameter iterates under SGD on the SFT distribution
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}`$ with step size $`\eta>0`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\omega_{t+1}\;=\;\omega_{t}-\eta\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t}),`$ |  | (13) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})\;=\;\nabla_{\omega}\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}}\big[\ell(f_{\omega}(x),y)\big].`$ |  |  |

We write
$`\Delta\omega_{t}:=\omega_{t+1}-\omega_{t}=-\eta\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})`$
and

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{forget}}\;:=\;\mathcal{R}(\omega_{T})-\mathcal{R}(\omega_{0}).`$ |  |

### B.2 Local Smoothness and Two-Sided First-Order Remainder

We assume *local $`L`$-smoothness* of $`\mathcal{R}`$ around
$`\omega_{0}`$: for all $`\omega_{1},\omega_{2}`$ in a neighborhood of
$`\omega_{0}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\|\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{1})-\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{2})\|\;\leq\;L\,\|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\|.
``` |  | (14) |

Equivalently, the Taylor remainder is two-sided bounded:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle`$ | $`\displaystyle\big|\,\mathcal{R}(\omega_{2})-\mathcal{R}(\omega_{1})-\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{1}),\,\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}\rangle\,\big|`$ |  | (15) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\;\tfrac{L}{2}\,\|\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}\|^{2}.`$ |  |  |

Applying
([15](#A2.E15 "Equation 15 ‣ B.2 Local Smoothness and Two-Sided First-Order Remainder ‣ Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models"))
with $`\omega_{1}=\omega_{t}`$ and
$`\omega_{2}=\omega_{t+1}=\omega_{t}+\Delta\omega_{t}`$ yields the
per-step identity with a bounded remainder:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\omega_{t+1})-\mathcal{R}(\omega_{t})\;=\;\underbrace{\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{t}),\,\Delta\omega_{t}\rangle}_{\text{first-order term}}\;+\;\varepsilon_{t},`$ |  | (16) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle|\varepsilon_{t}|\;\leq\;\tfrac{L}{2}\,\|\Delta\omega_{t}\|^{2}.`$ |  |  |

Summing
([16](#A2.E16 "Equation 16 ‣ B.2 Local Smoothness and Two-Sided First-Order Remainder ‣ Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models"))
over $`t=0,\dots,T-1`$ gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle`$ | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{forget}}\;=\;\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{t}),\,\Delta\omega_{t}\rangle\;+\;\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\varepsilon_{t},`$ |  | (17) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\varepsilon_{t}\Big|\;\leq\;\tfrac{L}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\Delta\omega_{t}\|^{2}.`$ |  |  |

Using $`\Delta\omega_{t}=-\eta\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})`$,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle`$ | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{forget}}\;=\;-\eta\,\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{t}),\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})\big\rangle\;+\;\mathcal{E}_{1},`$ |  | (18) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{1}|\;\leq\;\tfrac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})\|^{2}.`$ |  |  |

### B.3 Linearization at $`\omega_{0}`$ and the First-Order Expansion

To expose the directional *alignment* driving forgetting, we linearize
$`\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{t})`$ at $`\omega_{0}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{t})\;=\;\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{0})\;+\;\delta_{t},`$ |  | (19) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\|\delta_{t}\|\;\leq\;L\,\|\omega_{t}-\omega_{0}\|.`$ |  |  |

Since
$`\omega_{t}-\omega_{0}=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\Delta\omega_{j}=-\eta\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{j})`$,
we obtain the bound

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\|\delta_{t}\|\;\leq\;L\,\eta\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\|g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{j})\|.
``` |  | (20) |

Plugging
$`\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{t})=\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{0})+\delta_{t}`$
into
([18](#A2.E18 "Equation 18 ‣ B.2 Local Smoothness and Two-Sided First-Order Remainder ‣ Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models"))
yields

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{forget}}\;=`$ | $`\displaystyle-\eta\,\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{0}),\,\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})\big\rangle`$ |  | (21) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\;\eta\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\langle\delta_{t},\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})\rangle\;+\;\mathcal{E}_{1}.`$ |  |  |

The drift term is bounded by Cauchy–Schwarz and
([20](#A2.E20 "Equation 20 ‣ B.3 Linearization at 𝜔_0 and the First-Order Expansion ‣ Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models")):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\Big|\eta\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\langle\delta_{t},\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})\rangle\Big|\;\leq\;\eta\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\|\delta_{t}\|\,\|g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})\|`$ |  | (22) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\;L\,\eta^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\|g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{j})\|\,\|g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})\|.`$ |  |  |

Under small steps and bounded gradients (the usual local analysis
regime), the double sum is
$`O\!\big(\sum_{t}\|g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t})\|^{2}\big)`$. Hence we
arrive at the signed first-order expansion with a *two-sided* remainder:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle`$ | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{forget}}\;=\;-\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{0}),\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t-1})\big\rangle\;+\;\mathcal{E},`$ |  | (23) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}|\;\leq\;C\,L\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta^{2}\,\|g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{t-1})\|^{2},`$ |  |  |

for some absolute constant $`C>0`$.
Equation ([23](#A2.E23 "Equation 23 ‣ B.3 Linearization at 𝜔_0 and the First-Order Expansion ‣ Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models"))
justifies the main-text use of “$`\approx`$” together with an
$`O(\cdot)`$ remainder: it is an equality up to a bounded, second-order
term whose sign is *a priori* unknown.

### B.4 Effect of Mixing Self-Generated Data

Let $`\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}`$ be a self-generated distribution (by
$`f_{\omega_{0}}`$), and define the mixture

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\text{mix}}\;=\;(1-\varepsilon)\,\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}\;+\;\varepsilon\,\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}},`$ |  | (24) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle g_{\text{mix}}(\omega)\;=\;(1-\varepsilon)\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega)+\varepsilon\,g_{\text{self}}(\omega).`$ |  |  |

#### Assumption (Style–Semantic subspace).

There exists a subspace $`\mathcal{S}`$ (Style) and its orthogonal
complement $`\mathcal{S}^{\perp}`$ (Semantic) such that
$`g_{\text{task}}=g_{\text{sty}}+g_{\text{sem}}`$ with
$`g_{\text{sty}}\in\mathcal{S}`$,
$`g_{\text{sem}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\perp}`$. Moreover, self-generated
samples are style-aligned with $`\mathcal{D}_{0}`$, so that
$`\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}}\,g_{\text{self}}(\omega_{0})\approx 0`$.
Then, at initialization,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}}\,g_{\text{mix}}(\omega_{0})\;=\;(1-\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}}\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{0})`$ |  | (25) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\;\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}}\,g_{\text{self}}(\omega_{0})\;\approx\;(1-\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}}\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{0}).`$ |  |  |

Replacing $`g_{\text{task}}`$ with $`g_{\text{mix}}`$ in
([23](#A2.E23 "Equation 23 ‣ B.3 Linearization at 𝜔_0 and the First-Order Expansion ‣ Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models"))
yields

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{forget}}^{(\text{mix})}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{R}(\omega_{0}),\,\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta\,g_{\text{mix}}(\omega_{t-1})\big\rangle`$ |  | (26) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\mathcal{E}^{(\text{mix})}.`$ |  |  |

Combining
([25](#A2.E25 "Equation 25 ‣ Assumption (Style–Semantic subspace). ‣ B.4 Effect of Mixing Self-Generated Data ‣ Appendix B Derivations and Additional Details ‣ Talking to Yourself: Defying Forgetting in Large Language Models"))
with the subspace assumption shows that the Style component of the
signed main term is reduced by a factor $`(1-\varepsilon)`$, while the
Semantic component is preserved. Under the same local-smoothness and
small-step conditions,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\big|\mathcal{E}^{(\text{mix})}\big|\;\leq\;C\,L\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\eta^{2}\,\|g_{\text{mix}}(\omega_{t-1})\|^{2},`$ |  | (27) |

and the Style-driven part of $`\Delta_{\text{forget}}^{(\text{mix})}`$
scales roughly as $`(1-\varepsilon)`$ times that of
$`\Delta_{\text{forget}}^{(\text{task})}`$, formalizing the attenuation
effect reported in the main text.

### B.5 Remarks on the Style Perplexity Gap

The quantity

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{ppl}}\;=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{task}}}\!\big[-\log p_{\omega_{0}}(x)\big]`$ |  | (28) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{0}}\!\big[-\log p_{\omega_{0}}(x)\big]`$ |  |  |

is a proxy for stylistic out-of-distribution distance measured by
$`f_{\omega_{0}}`$. Since
$`-\log p_{\omega_{0}}(x)=\sum_{t}-\log p_{\omega_{0}}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})`$,
it is the standard sequence NLL under the base model. Empirically,
larger $`\Delta_{\mathrm{ppl}}`$ correlates with larger
$`\|\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}}\,g_{\text{task}}(\omega_{0})\|`$,
consistent with the view that style mismatch enlarges the Style
component of the update.
````

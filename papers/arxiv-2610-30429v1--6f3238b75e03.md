# RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation

###### Abstract

Large language model (LLM) based multi-agent systems often rely on
predefined collaboration structures, limiting their adaptability to
evolving task requirements. Although recent approaches enable dynamic
orchestration, trajectory-level rewards they apply assign one outcome to
each decision, ofen struggling to identify the contributions of
individual structural changes. We propose RoleFlow, a self-evolving
multi-role agentic system that refines collaboration structures through
feedback-driven atomic edits and on-demand role synthesis. To make
structural adaptation learnable, we introduce Single-Edit Counterfactual
Policy Optimization (SECPO), which evaluates each edit against its
no-edit counterpart at the same chain state and assigns targeted credit
to edit-specific tokens. Across seven benchmarks spanning four domains,
RoleFlow achieves the optimal average performance, surpassing the
strongest competitor by 4.7 points. It leads on all three multi-hop QA
benchmarks by 3.2–7.5 F1 points and also shows supremacy on mathematical
reasoning, web interaction, and code generation. Further analyses
demonstrate cross-benchmark transferability, training-data efficiency,
and cross-executor generalization.

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2610.30429v1/fig1_teaser_single_col.png)

Figure 1: Motivation of RoleFlow. Hand-crafted structures lack
adaptability (top), while trajectory-level rewards obscure the
contributions of individual structural edits (middle). Our RoleFlow
enables effective structural evolution through same-state counterfactual
evaluation and edit-level credit assignment (bottom).

Large language model (LLM) based multi-agent systems have shown
capabilities in solving complex tasks through specialized agent
collaboration [Durante et al. (2025)](#bib.bib7); [Ghareeb et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib10). However, existing approaches typically rely on
predefined or pre-optimized collaboration structures that remain fixed
during execution. Such structures struggle to accommodate evolving task
requirements, where effective collaboration may demand adding, removing,
or reorganizing specialized roles. This raises a fundamental question:
_How can multi-role agentic systems autonomously evolve their
collaboration structures during task execution?_

Recent studies have explored reinforcement learning for dynamic agent
orchestration ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib16); [Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib37); [Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)). However, learning
structural adaptation introduces a critical challenge: _credit
assignment for individual structural decisions_. Conventional methods
like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24)) rely on trajectory-level rewards, assigning the same
advantage to decisions within a rollout regardless of their individual
contributions. As shown in
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"),
when collaboration structures undergo successive modifications, this
coarse-grained feedback cannot reliably identify which edits improve
performance and which undermine it.

Our key insight is that reliable structural evolution requires making
each structural modification individually assessable and learnable.
Rather than treating an evolving collaboration structure as a sequence
of decisions optimized only through its final outcome, we decompose
structural adaptation into atomic edits and evaluate each edit against
an unchanged counterpart under the same chain state. This isolates the
immediate contribution of a structural decision and provides a localized
learning signal, allowing the system to learn not only _how to modify_
collaboration structures but also _which modifications are worth
making_.

Motivated by this insight, we propose RoleFlow, a self-evolving
multi-role collaboration framework that continuously adapts agent
structures through execution feedback. A trainable Global Router (GR)
constructs task-specific role chains and refines them through atomic
structural edits, while synthesizing specialized roles on demand. To
optimize these decisions, we introduce Single-Edit Counterfactual Policy
Optimization (SECPO), which compares executions with and without each
candidate edit under the same chain state and incorporates the resulting
advantage into edit-specific token optimization. RoleFlow further
maintains a return-gated role library to retain useful synthesized
specialists during training. Experiments across seven benchmarks
spanning four domains demonstrate the effectiveness of RoleFlow,
achieving the highest average performance among evaluated multi-agent
systems.

Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  We introduce RoleFlow, a self-evolving multi-role agentic system that
  enables feedback-driven structural adaptation through atomic edits and
  on-demand specialist synthesis.
- •
  We propose SECPO, a structure-aware policy optimization method that
  estimates the counterfactual contribution of individual structural
  edits and assigns targeted credit to edit-specific tokens.
- •
  Experiments across seven benchmarks spanning four domains show that
  RoleFlow achieves the highest average performance. Further analyses
  validate structural adaptation, edit-level counterfactual learning,
  cross-benchmark transferability, training-data efficiency, and
  cross-executor generalization.

## 2 Related Work

#### Handcrafted Multi-Agent Structure and Content-Level Adaptation.

Existing multi-agent systems often rely on fixed collaboration
structures, such as the predefined pipeline in ChatDev ([Qian et al.,
2024](#bib.bib21)), sequential agents in Chain-of-Agents ([Zhang et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib39)), and graph-based retrieval in LinearRAG ([Zhuang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)). Other approaches adapt the information
processed within a fixed structure through retrieval or tool use ([Lewis
et al., 2020](#bib.bib15); [Schick et al., 2023](#bib.bib22); [Song
et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)). While effective at content-level adaptation,
these methods do not learn to modify agent roles or their interactions
in response to execution feedback.

#### Self-Evolving Agents and Workflow Search.

Existing self-evolving agents primarily optimize model behavior within
fixed roles, as in Multi-Agent Evolve ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)).
Alternatively, workflow optimization methods search for effective
collaboration structures through Monte Carlo tree search (AFlow) ([Zhang
et al., 2024a](#bib.bib36)), learned graph connections
(GPTSwarm) ([Zhuge et al., 2024](#bib.bib41)), offline workflow
generation (W4S) ([Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)), or task-specific
architecture generation (MaAS and MAS-GPT) ([Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35); [Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)). However, these
methods determine the collaboration structure before execution, limiting
their ability to address emerging task requirements through online
structural refinement.

#### Online Structural Adaptation and Credit Assignment.

Recent approaches incorporate execution feedback into agent
orchestration, including AgentFlow’s learned planner ([Li et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16)), AgentConductor’s dynamic topology ([Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib28)), and FlowSteer’s atomic workflow edits ([Zhang
et al., 2026a](#bib.bib37)). However, their trajectory-level rewards
provide coarse-grained credit across structural decisions ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24)). Counterfactual credit assignment has been explored
in COMA ([Foerster et al., 2018](#bib.bib8)), while COLLATOR ([Chen
et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)), SERO ([Ge and Guo, 2026](#bib.bib9)), and
EvoSteer ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib38)) evaluate individual
decisions using different references. Unlike these approaches, RoleFlow
evaluates each structural edit against its no-edit counterpart at the
same chain state. Its SECPO assigns the resulting counterfactual
advantage specifically to edit-related tokens, enabling fine-grained
credit assignment for evolving collaboration structures.

## 3 Approach

![Refer to caption](2610.30429v1/fig2_r9_v2.png)

Figure 2: Overview of RoleFlow. The Global Router assembles an initial
sequence of roles from the role library $`\mathcal{R}`$ and applies one
structural edit $`a_{t}\in\mathbb{E}`$ upon validation failure, for at
most $`T_{\max}`$ adaptation cycles. The role library grows through the
selective retention of synthesized roles during training.

As illustrated in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"),
RoleFlow is a self-evolving multi-role agentic system that adapts
collaboration structures during task execution. Its Global Router (GR)
selects and revises a chain of specialized roles using validation
feedback. To make these structural decisions learnable, we introduce
Single-Edit Counterfactual Policy Optimization (SECPO), which assigns
each candidate edit a same-state counterfactual advantage
(Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
For the subsequent content, we first define the structural adaptation
problem, then describe RoleFlow’s execution loop, SECPO, and the
mechanism for synthesizing and retaining roles.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

For a task $`x\in\mathcal{X}`$, RoleFlow uses a trainable Global Router
(GR) with parameters $`\theta`$ and a role library
$`\mathcal{R}=\{\rho^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{R}|}`$. Each role $`\rho`$
specifies a name, a system prompt, and capability tags. A RoleFlow chain
$`\mathrm{RFC}_{t}`$ is an ordered sequence of roles selected from the
available library. A frozen executor LLM runs these roles sequentially,
with each role receiving the task and the outputs of preceding roles. By
default, the chain begins with a Planner and ends with a Validator.

At cycle $`t`$, executing $`\mathrm{RFC}_{t}`$ produces an output
$`y_{t}`$, execution trace $`z_{t}`$, and cost $`c_{t}`$. A separate
task validator $`V`$ assesses the output and trace, returning a
pass/reject indicator $`h_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$, score $`s_{t}`$, and deficit
description $`d_{t}`$. Scratch memory $`\sigma_{t}`$ stores the
execution traces and validation history. Together, the chain, memory,
and available roles constitute the current chain state.

When validation fails, the GR selects a structural edit
$`a_{t}\in\mathbb{E}`$, where $`\mathbb{E}`$ contains
$`\textsc{Add}(\rho)`$ (insert $`\rho`$ before the final role),
$`\textsc{Prune}(\rho)`$ (remove $`\rho`$),
$`\textsc{Swap}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})`$ (replace $`\rho`$ by
$`\rho^{\prime}`$ in place), $`\textsc{Reroute}(p,q)`$ (exchange the
roles at positions $`p`$ and $`q`$), and the null edit $`\varnothing`$
(leave the chain unchanged,
$`\mathrm{RFC}_{t}\oplus\varnothing=\mathrm{RFC}_{t}`$). The execution
and adaptation cycle is

|     |                                      |                                                                                                                     |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle(y_{t},z_{t},c_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathrm{RFC}_{t}(\sigma_{t},x),`$                                                                   |     | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle(h_{t},s_{t},d_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=V(y_{t},z_{t}),`$                                                                                   |     |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle a_{t}`$              | $`\displaystyle\sim\pi_{\mathrm{GR},\theta}(\,\cdot\mid\mathrm{RFC}_{t},\sigma_{t},d_{t}),\;\;a_{t}\in\mathbb{E},`$ |     |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{RFC}_{t+1}`$  | $`\displaystyle=\mathrm{RFC}_{t}\oplus a_{t}.`$                                                                     |     |     |

The initial chain is executed at cycle $`0`$, followed by at most
$`T_{\max}`$ adaptation cycles, for a maximum of $`1+T_{\max}`$ chain
executions at inference. An edit is proposed only after rejection, and
each cycle applies at most one edit. This atomicity is important for
measuring the effect of an individual structural decision in SECPO
(§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).

#### Learning objective.

The executor remains frozen; only the GR is trained. We maximize
$`\mathbb{E}_{x}[R(\tau_{\theta}(x))]`$ over $`\theta`$, where
$`\tau_{\theta}(x)`$ is a rollout of
Eq. [1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation").
Its terminal cycle $`T`$ is the first passing cycle, or $`T_{\max}`$ if
none passes. The cycle reward is $`R(y_{t})=h_{t}+\lambda_{s}s_{t}`$,
with score weight $`\lambda_{s}`$, and the rollout reward is
$`R(\tau)=R(y_{T})`$. Because this terminal reward evaluates the entire
evolving chain, it does not reveal each edit’s contribution. We use
$`K`$ rollouts per task, indexed by $`i\in\{0,\ldots,K{-}1\}`$; $`t`$
indexes cycles, and $`\ell`$ indexes GR plan tokens.

### 3.2 RoleFlow: Feedback-Driven Structural Adaptation

RoleFlow evolves a chain within a task rather than fixing its structure
before execution. The GR first writes a plan that decomposes the task
and names the roles of $`\mathrm{RFC}_{0}`$. After each rejected output,
it uses the deficit $`d_{t}`$ and accumulated memory $`\sigma_{t}`$ to
propose one structural edit. The edited chain is then executed in the
next cycle, and the process stops upon validation success or after
$`T_{\max}`$ adaptation cycles
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
This one-edit-per-cycle design ensures that consecutive chains differ by
at most one edit; applying several edits at once would confound their
individual effects.

The available roles are not restricted to a fixed predefined set. If the
GR names a role absent from $`\mathcal{R}`$, a LibraryExpander
synthesizes its specification from the role name, task, and current
deficit. The new role is stored in rollout $`i`$’s temporary overlay
$`\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{(i)}`$, so that rollout can use roles from
$`\mathcal{R}\cup\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{(i)}`$. Moreover, if the GR proposes
a null edit despite a reported deficit, RoleFlow synthesizes a role
targeting that deficit and proposes its addition (_deficit-driven
synthesis_). We describe how synthesized roles are retained across
training steps in
§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 On-Demand Role Synthesis and Library Growth ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation").

At inference, each task starts with the domain’s seed library rather
than the library grown during training, and the required specialists are
synthesized for that task. Each chain is executed once per cycle; the
additional no-edit executions described next are used only during SECPO
training.

### 3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment

![Refer to caption](2610.30429v1/fig_s33_secpo.png)

Figure 3: The single-edit advantage of SECPO. A certain chain state is
executed with and without the candidate edit, and the reward difference
$`A^{(t)}_{i}`$
(Eq. [2](#S3.E2 "In Same-state counterfactual advantage. ‣ 3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"))
credits that edit and gates its commitment, enabling edit-level credit
assignment under evolving structural dynamics.

Structural adaptation presents a credit-assignment problem: the terminal
reward reflects the outcome of several decisions, even though an
individual edit may improve or worsen the chain. GRPO’s group-relative
advantage also mixes rollouts with different structures, while a learned
value critic would need to track changing chain states. Counterfactual
methods such as COMA ([Foerster et al., 2018](#bib.bib8)) require
evaluating alternative actions at each step. SECPO instead measures a
candidate edit against the unchanged chain at the _same_ state, while
retaining GRPO’s critic-free group of $`K`$ rollouts ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24)) and PPO’s clipped surrogate with KL
regularization ([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib23)).

#### Same-state counterfactual advantage.

For each task, as shown in
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"),
sample $`K`$ rollouts
$`\{\tau^{(i)}\}_{i=0}^{K-1}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{GR},\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)`$,
where $`\theta_{\mathrm{old}}`$ denotes the policy before an update.
Each rollout maintains its own role overlay $`\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{(i)}`$
until the training step ends. At cycle $`t`$, the candidate edit
$`\tilde{a}_{t}^{(i)}`$ is either the GR’s proposed edit or the Add
operation produced by deficit-driven synthesis
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 RoleFlow: Feedback-Driven Structural Adaptation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
Keeping $`\mathrm{RFC}_{t}^{(i)}`$ and $`\sigma_{t}^{(i)}`$ fixed, SECPO
executes the chain with the candidate edit,
$`(\mathrm{RFC}_{t}^{(i)}\oplus\tilde{a}_{t}^{(i)})(\sigma_{t}^{(i)},x)`$,
to obtain $`y_{i,t}^{+}`$, and without it,
$`\mathrm{RFC}_{t}^{(i)}(\sigma_{t}^{(i)},x)`$, to obtain
$`y_{i,t}^{0}`$. The resulting single-edit advantage is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       A^{(t)}_{i}\,=\,R(y^{+}_{i,t})-R(y^{0}_{i,t}).
       ```                                             |     | (2) |

This reward difference provides a structure-aware estimate of the
candidate edit’s immediate effect at the current chain state. SECPO also
uses it to decide whether the edit is committed during training: only
edits whose advantage exceeds a positive threshold $`\delta`$ are
retained,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
a^{(i)}_{t}=\begin{cases}\tilde{a}^{(i)}_{t},&A^{(t)}_{i}>\delta\\
\varnothing,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
``` |  | (3) |

Otherwise, the chain remains unchanged. Importantly, the counterfactual
pair contributes to the policy update even when the candidate edit is
not committed.

#### Edit-specific token advantage.

The group-relative GRPO advantage $`A_{\ell}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}`$ is the
terminal rollout return $`R(\tau^{(i)})`$, standardized across the
task’s $`K`$ rollouts and broadcast to all GR plan tokens. This signal
does not distinguish the tokens corresponding to different structural
edits. Let $`\mathcal{E}_{t}^{(i)}`$ denote the plan-token span spelling
candidate edit $`\tilde{a}_{t}^{(i)}`$. SECPO adds the single-edit
advantage only on that span
(Figure [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Policy optimization. ‣ 3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{A}_{\ell}\,=\,A^{\mathrm{GRPO}}_{\ell}+\lambda\,\mathbf{1}\!\left[\ell\in\mathcal{E}^{(i)}_{t}\right]A^{(t)}_{i},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\lambda`$ controls the edit-specific signal. The spans from
different adaptation cycles do not overlap, and tokens outside all edit
spans retain their GRPO advantages. We set $`\lambda=1`$; the GRPO
ablation in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
uses $`\lambda=0`$ and omits no-edit re-execution.

#### Policy optimization.

Using the token-level advantage $`\tilde{A}_{\ell}`$, we optimize the GR
with PPO’s clipped objective, averaged over the $`K`$ rollouts and their
GR plan tokens $`o_{\mathrm{GR}}^{(i)}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{multlined}\mathcal{L}^{\text{SECPO}}(\theta)=-\,\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\frac{1}{|o^{(i)}_{\mathrm{GR}}|}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|o^{(i)}_{\mathrm{GR}}|}\\
\min\!\big(\rho_{\ell}\,\tilde{A}_{\ell},\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_{\ell},1{-}\epsilon,1{+}\epsilon)\,\tilde{A}_{\ell}\big)\\
\,+\,\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),\end{multlined}
``` |  | (5) |

where
$`\rho_{\ell}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(o_{\ell}\mid o_{<\ell})/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(o_{\ell}\mid o_{<\ell})`$
is the per-token probability ratio, $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is the
reference policy, $`\epsilon`$ is the clipping parameter, and $`\beta`$
is the KL coefficient
(§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")),
and $`\pi_{\theta}`$ abbreviates $`\pi_{\mathrm{GR},\theta}`$.
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ A.2 Detailed Global Router Learning Procedure ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
in
Appendix [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Detailed Global Router Learning Procedure ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
summarizes the Global Router learning with SECPO.

![Refer to caption](2610.30429v1/fig_s34_token.png)

Figure 4: Token-level advantage of SECPO. Every plan token receives the
group-relative advantage $`A^{\mathrm{GRPO}}_{\ell}`$, and the
single-edit advantage $`A^{(t)}_{i}`$ is added with weight $`\lambda`$
on the edit span $`\mathcal{E}^{(i)}_{t}`$.

### 3.4 On-Demand Role Synthesis and Library Growth

RoleFlow uses temporary role overlays for task-specific specialists and
a shared library for roles retained across training steps. After each
training step, a *return gate* determines which synthesized role
specifications enter the shared library. A synthesized role
$`\hat{\rho}`$ from rollout $`i`$ is admitted only if its rollout return
exceeds that of reference rollout $`\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(i)}`$, defined
as the first rollout in its block of $`K`$ within the training batch
(Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ A.2 Detailed Global Router Learning Procedure ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
Thus, after training step $`s`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}_{s+1}=\mathcal{R}_{s}\cup\{\hat{\rho}\mid\exists\,i:\hat{\rho}\in\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{(i)},\,R(\tau^{(i)})>R(\tau^{(i)}_{\mathrm{ref}})\}.
``` |  | (6) |

The shared library is then available to subsequent training steps. This
gate applies to role specifications rather than gradients: even if a
synthesized role is not retained in the library, its execution rewards
still contribute to GR training. At inference, RoleFlow uses the
domain’s seed roles and synthesizes missing specialists on demand,
without loading the training-grown library.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Datasets.

We evaluate RoleFlow across four task domains: (1) Multi-hop QA with
HotpotQA ([Yang et al., 2018](#bib.bib32)), 2WikiMultiHopQA ([Ho et al.,
2020](#bib.bib13)) and MuSiQue ([Trivedi et al., 2022](#bib.bib27)); (2)
Mathematical reasoning with MATH-500 ([Lightman et al.,
2023](#bib.bib17)); (3) Code generation with MBPP ([Austin et al.,
2021](#bib.bib2)) and HumanEval ([Chen et al., 2021](#bib.bib3)); and
(4) Web interaction with WebShop ([Yao et al., 2022](#bib.bib33)) under
the AgentBench protocol ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)). We train a
domain-specific GR on the HotpotQA, MATH ([Hendrycks et al.,
2021](#bib.bib12)), MBPP, and WebShop training sets. We evaluate 2Wiki,
MuSiQue, and HumanEval without retraining, using the GR trained on
HotpotQA or MBPP, respectively. Dataset details appear in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation").

#### Baselines.

Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
compares four categories of baselines: (1) Hand-crafted pipelines:
Chain-of-Agents (CoA) ([Zhang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib39)) and
LinearRAG ([Zhuang et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)); (2) Automated workflow
search: GPTSwarm ([Zhuge et al., 2024](#bib.bib41)), AFlow ([Zhang
et al., 2024a](#bib.bib36)) and MaAS ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib35));
(3) Trained executors: MAE ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)) and
R1-Searcher ([Song et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)); and (4) Trained
orchestrators: AgentFlow ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)),
FlowSteer ([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib37)), W4S ([Nie et al.,
2025](#bib.bib19)) and MAS-GPT ([Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)). Every
multi-agent system and trained orchestrator executes its agents with
gpt-4o-mini ([OpenAI et al., 2024](#bib.bib20)); R1-Searcher, AgentFlow
and LinearRAG retrieve from Wikipedia. We also report direct prompting
of GPT-4o-mini, Claude-Haiku-4.5 ([Anthropic, 2025](#bib.bib1)),
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib31)),
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct ([Grattafiori et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)),
Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-32B ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)) and
DeepSeek-V3.2 ([DeepSeek-AI, 2025](#bib.bib6)) with each benchmark’s
official evaluation protocol
(Appendix [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Direct Prompting ‣ Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).

#### Metric.

We report F1 for QA, accuracy for MATH-500, Pass@1 for MBPP and
HumanEval, and the WebShop score. Results are means and standard
deviations over three training runs.

#### Implementation.

We train the GR with SECPO using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct in verl ([Sheng
et al., 2024](#bib.bib25)) on four NVIDIA H200 GPUs; roles are executed
by gpt-4o-mini. Each task uses $`K{=}5`$ rollouts, with 32 tasks per
training step, learning rate $`2\times 10^{-6}`$, KL coefficient 0.001,
clip ratio 0.2, $`T_{\max}{=}3`$, and commit threshold $`\delta=0.005`$.
Role prompts and baseline reproduction details are provided in
Appendices [C](#A3 "Appendix C Role Library and Prompt Templates ‣ B.4 Direct Prompting ‣ Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
and
[B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Reproduction of Compared Methods ‣ Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation").

### 4.2 Main Results

As shown in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"),
RoleFlow achieves the highest average score of 67.1 across seven
benchmarks, outperforming the strongest competing multi-agent system,
W4S, by 4.7 points and ranking first on five benchmarks. Notably,
RoleFlow consistently improves upon its underlying gpt-4o-mini executor
across all four task domains, demonstrating the effectiveness of learned
multi-role collaboration beyond individual model capabilities. The gains
are particularly substantial on multi-hop QA (10.0–19.0 F1), where
coordinating complementary reasoning roles is essential for handling
interdependent evidence and reasoning steps. Unlike predefined or
pre-optimized workflows, RoleFlow combines feedback-driven structural
refinement with edit-level counterfactual learning, enabling
collaboration structures to evolve in response to task requirements.
These results establish the overall effectiveness of self-evolving
collaboration, with subsequent ablations isolating the contributions of
structural adaptation and SECPO.

#### Online Refinement Matters.

Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
separates initial workflow selection from subsequent adaptation.
Replacing the GR with its most frequent fixed chain yields 45.7 F1 on
HotpotQA, essentially the direct executor’s 45.6, versus 55.6 for
RoleFlow. Even with the GR retained, disabling adaptation reduces
performance by 3.8–8.5 F1 across the three QA benchmarks. Thus, choosing
a task-specific chain is insufficient: revising it after execution
feedback accounts for a substantial part of the gain.

#### Role Synthesis Enables Adaptation.

Removing access to synthesized roles substantially reduces QA
performance
(Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"),
disable $`\mathcal{R}`$). The resulting chains contain only one or two
seed roles, with edits often collapsing to the null action. Structural
revision therefore needs not only a decision about *when* to edit, but
also the capacity to introduce a missing specialist.

#### SECPO Improves Structural Learning.

Replacing SECPO with GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib24)) lowers mean
F1 by 2.1, 2.5, and 2.6 points on HotpotQA, 2Wiki, and MuSiQue,
respectively
(Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
GRPO also varies more across runs; one router learns to edit only once
per question. This is consistent with trajectory-level credit failing to
distinguish useful revisions within a rollout. The advantage is not
universal: SECPO slightly trails GRPO on MBPP and ties it on HumanEval,
where validation triggers fewer corrective edits.

#### Atomic Edits Preserve Credit Granularity.

Training with up to two or four edits per cycle lowers QA performance
relative to single-edit SECPO
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ SECPO Improves Structural Learning. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
A shared counterfactual for multiple simultaneous edits measures their
joint effect, obscuring individual contributions. Since these variants
are evaluated with one edit per cycle, train–test mismatch also
contributes to the comparison; the evidence supports the single-edit
configuration without isolating that mechanism.

### 4.4 Structural Adaptation and Transfer

#### Structure and Specialization Are Complementary.

Preserving synthesized roles’ names and positions while replacing their
descriptions with generic ones retains roughly half to three-quarters of
their benefit
(Table [6](#A3.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix C Role Library and Prompt Templates ‣ B.4 Direct Prompting ‣ Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
Much of the gain therefore comes from inserting a useful step into the
chain, while tailored role instructions contribute further. Role
synthesis improves both collaboration structure and specialist behavior.

#### Adaptation Transfers Across Tasks.

The HotpotQA-trained GR transfers to 2Wiki and MuSiQue without
retraining
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
Its initial chains vary little, but final chains diverge almost three
times as much; on MuSiQue, about a quarter of questions finish with a
chain absent from the initial proposals. Almost all additional OOD
specialists are synthesized after rejection
(Table [6](#A3.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix C Role Library and Prompt Templates ‣ B.4 Direct Prompting ‣ Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
The transferable behavior is therefore not merely an initial template,
but the ability to revise collaboration in response to task feedback.

### 4.5 Efficiency and Generalization

#### Refinement Versus Resampling.

In
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Adaptation Transfers Across Tasks. ‣ 4.4 Structural Adaptation and Transfer ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")a,
RoleFlow achieves 55.6 F1 on HotpotQA at its measured API cost, while
chain-of-thought self-consistency ([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib29)) at
comparable cost remains near the executor baseline. Under this setting,
investing inference in revising collaboration yields a more favorable
quality–cost trade-off than sampling additional answers; it does not
imply universally lower compute.

#### Training-Data Efficiency.

As shown in
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Adaptation Transfers Across Tasks. ‣ 4.4 Structural Adaptation and Transfer ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")b,
RoleFlow achieves stronger performance with fewer labeled HotpotQA
questions than competing methods. Notably, RoleFlow^(†), trained with
half the questions, already matches the GRPO-trained router
(Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
This suggests that SECPO makes more effective use of training data by
extracting edit-level counterfactual signals from each task, rather than
relying solely on trajectory-level rewards. Such finer-grained
supervision improves data efficiency, albeit with additional
training-time executions
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ SECPO Improves Structural Learning. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).

#### Cross-Executor Generalization.

Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Cross-Executor Generalization. ‣ 4.5 Efficiency and Generalization ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
shows that the GR trained with gpt-4o-mini improves all evaluated
executors without retraining. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct gains 11.7 and 16.0
Pass@1 on MBPP and HumanEval, compared with 5.3 and 7.4 for gpt-4o-mini.
These results suggest that feedback-driven structural adaptation can
compensate for weaker executors, demonstrating transferable
collaboration strategies beyond executor-specific workflows.

## 5 Conclusion

We presented RoleFlow, a self-evolving multi-role agentic system that
adapts collaboration structures through feedback-driven edits and
on-demand role synthesis. We further introduced SECPO to enable
structure-aware learning through edit-level counterfactual credit
assignment. Experiments across seven benchmarks demonstrate superior
average performance and strong transferability. Overall, these findings
highlight the value of structure-aware credit assignment in enabling
effective and adaptive multi-agent collaboration.

## Limitations

RoleFlow requires a task validator that returns a pass or reject verdict
and a deficit for every cycle, so a new domain needs such a check before
the GR can be trained or run. Inference costs more than training-free
pipelines and SECPO training executes one additional chain per edit.

### A.2 Detailed Global Router Learning Procedure

provides the training procedure of the Global Router with SECPO that
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
describes. For every rollout it keeps a library overlay
$`\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{(i)}`$, synthesizes the roles the GR names but the
library lacks, executes the same chain state once with the candidate
edit and once without it, keeps the edit only when the reward difference
$`A^{(t)}_{i}`$ exceeds the threshold $`\delta`$
(Eq. [2](#S3.E2 "In Same-state counterfactual advantage. ‣ 3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")),
adds $`\lambda A^{(t)}_{i}`$ to the tokens that spell the edit on top of
the group-relative advantage
(Eq. [4](#S3.E4 "In Edit-specific token advantage. ‣ 3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")),
updates the GR with the SECPO loss
(Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "In Policy optimization. ‣ 3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"))
and admits synthesized roles through the return gate
(Eq. [6](#S3.E6 "In 3.4 On-Demand Role Synthesis and Library Growth ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).

          6 break

$`\tilde{a}_{t}^{(i)}`$

rollout’s return //

### A.3 Inference Procedure

At inference the GR keeps its trained parameters $`\theta^{*}`$, and the
role library holds the seed roles of the domain, written
$`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$. The roles grown during training are not loaded
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 RoleFlow: Feedback-Driven Structural Adaptation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
In
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ A.3 Inference Procedure ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"),
the GR writes a plan that decomposes the task, names the roles of the
initial RoleFlowChain $`\mathrm{RFC}_{0}`$ and, after every rejected
cycle, names one edit. We then execute the edited chain
$`\mathrm{RFC}_{t}\oplus a_{t}`$ in the next cycle
(Eq. [1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
A role the GR names but $`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$ lacks is synthesized by the
LibraryExpander, and a null edit under a deficit triggers deficit-driven
synthesis. The loop ends at the first accepted output or after
$`T_{\max}`$ adaptation cycles, so a chain is executed at most
$`1+T_{\max}`$ times, once per cycle. The counterfactual re-execution of
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 SECPO: Edit-Level Counterfactual Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
serves training.

Input: Task $`x`$; Global Router $`\mathrm{GR}_{\theta^{*}}`$; Seed
library $`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$.

Initialize $`\mathrm{RFC}_{0}`$ from the GR’s plan over
$`\mathcal{R}_{0}`$, synthesizing GR-named roles absent from it
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 RoleFlow: Feedback-Driven Structural Adaptation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")); $`\sigma_{0}\leftarrow\{\}`$; execute
it, giving $`(y_{0},z_{0},c_{0})`$.

for *$`t=0,\ldots,T_{\max}{-}1`$* do

   $`(h_{t},s_{t},d_{t})\leftarrow V(y_{t},z_{t})`$.

   if *$`h_{t}=1`$* then break

   $`a_{t}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{GR},\theta^{*}}(\cdot\mid\mathrm{RFC}_{t},\sigma_{t},d_{t})`$; synthesize
any GR-named role absent from the library; if $`a_{t}=\varnothing`$, set
$`a_{t}\leftarrow\textsc{Add}(\hat{\rho})`$ with $`\hat{\rho}`$
synthesized for $`d_{t}`$
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 RoleFlow: Feedback-Driven Structural Adaptation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"))

   $`\mathrm{RFC}_{t+1}\leftarrow\mathrm{RFC}_{t}\oplus a_{t}`$; $`(y_{t+1},z_{t+1},c_{t+1})\leftarrow\mathrm{RFC}_{t+1}(\sigma_{t+1},x)`$.

Output: Final answer $`y_{T}`$, with $`T`$ the terminal cycle
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).

Algorithm 2 RoleFlow Test-Time Inference

#### Sampling and selection.

One chain answers each QA question. On MATH-500 we sample twenty chains
per problem and take the majority answer. When no answer wins more than
half of the votes, one call of the executor LLM, which does not see the
reference answer, picks one of the candidate answers. On MBPP and
HumanEval we sample ten chains per problem and submit one program. We
pick the program that passes the most public example tests and, among
those, the most unit tests that the executor LLM writes from the task
statement
(Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix C Role Library and Prompt Templates ‣ B.4 Direct Prompting ‣ Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
On these two benchmarks, after a rejection, every role except the
OutputFormatter receives a repair note from the scratch memory
$`\sigma_{t}`$ with the rejected program, its deficit and the error
output of the public example test. On WebShop the GR trained on MBPP
runs one chain per round with the WebShop seed roles, for at most ten
rounds per task as in AgentBench.

#### Task validators.

In every domain the validator $`V`$ returns the verdict $`h_{t}`$, the
score $`s_{t}`$ and a deficit $`d_{t}`$ from a fixed set of categories
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
It is separate from the Validator role. On MATH-500 it checks that the
output contains a \boxed{} answer and asks the executor LLM, which does
not see the reference answer, whether the solution holds
(Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix C Role Library and Prompt Templates ‣ B.4 Direct Prompting ‣ Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).
On MBPP and HumanEval it runs the public example test, and the deficit
names one of six failures: no code, a syntax error, a missing entry
point, a runtime error, a wrong answer or a timeout. On WebShop it
rejects an action that is invalid on the current page or that leaves too
few rounds to finish a purchase. When the last reply of a round would
still leave no purchase reachable, it replaces the reply with the action
on the current page that keeps a purchase possible. On multi-hop QA it
scores the final answer and names the suspected failure, such as an
unverified bridge entity, an unchecked time constraint or scattered
evidence.

### A.4 Metrics

predicted and reference answers. On MATH-500 we extract the \boxed{}
answer, compare it with the reference after normalization and, when the
strings differ, ask gpt-4o-mini at temperature 0 whether the two answers
are equivalent (prompt below). On MBPP and HumanEval a problem counts as
solved when the single submitted program passes every test case of the
benchmark, and Pass@1 is the share of solved problems. On WebShop we
report the WebShop score of AgentBench: the purchase reward averaged
over the 500 tasks, times 100.

The QA test sets are 200 questions sampled from the development set of

problems, the 86 MBPP validation problems of the AFlow split and 100
WebShop development goals.

### B.3 Reproduction of Compared Methods

Every multi-agent system and trained orchestrator executes its agents
with gpt-4o-mini, and R1-Searcher, AgentFlow and LinearRAG retrieve from
Wikipedia
(§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")).

#### Hand-crafted pipelines.

CoA ([Zhang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib39)) runs our port of its worker and
manager chain on the task text. LinearRAG ([Zhuang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib40)) builds its graph over the Wikipedia passages
retrieved for each question, with the retrieval index shared with
R1-Searcher, and answers MBPP and HumanEval, which have no corpus,
without retrieval.

#### Automated workflow search.

GPTSwarm ([Zhuge et al., 2024](#bib.bib41)) runs with its released
graph. AFlow ([Zhang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib36)) uses the workflows the
authors released for HotpotQA and MBPP. For MATH-500 its search runs on
our 200 MATH training problems with gpt-4o-mini as optimizer and
executor, and for WebShop on the development goals. MaAS ([Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35)) trains its controller per domain: on our HotpotQA and
MATH training problems, on the 86 MBPP problems that its paper trains
on, and on the WebShop development goals.

#### Trained executors.

MAE ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)) uses the released 3B checkpoint.
R1-Searcher ([Song et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)) is retrained on HotpotQA
training questions with Wikipedia retrieval, and the same checkpoint
answers the remaining benchmarks.

#### Trained orchestrators.

AgentFlow ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) runs the released 7B planner
with its executor, verifier and generator modules served by gpt-4o-mini
and Wikipedia search as its tool. FlowSteer ([Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib37)) runs the released FlowSteer-8B with gpt-4o-mini as
executor. W4S ([Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)) runs its meta-agent,
retrained with the authors’ procedure on MBPP, and MAS-GPT ([Ye et al.,
2025](#bib.bib34)) runs the released MAS-GPT-32B generator. Both execute
the resulting systems with gpt-4o-mini.

#### Transfer to 2Wiki, MuSiQue and HumanEval.

No method searches, trains or tunes on these three benchmarks. Each
keeps what it obtained on HotpotQA or MBPP: RoleFlow keeps the GR and
uses the seed roles of the domain, AFlow keeps its workflow, MaAS its
controller and W4S the workflow its meta-agent wrote there. On MATH-500
and WebShop each method adapts to the task as described above.

## Appendix D Chain Evolution Case Studies

Five cases show how the chain evolves within a task and what each part
of RoleFlow does. Case 1 follows RoleFlow on a MATH-500 problem that
twelve of the eighteen compared methods answer incorrectly: the
validator rejects the first answer, and the chain edited with a role
synthesized for the reported deficit answers correctly. Cases 2 to 5
each isolate one component of RoleFlow. In Case 2 the validator accepts
the output of cycle 0 and no edit follows, in Case 3 a rejection opens
an adaptation cycle where the GR edits the chain, Case 4 compares the
chain before and after training, and Case 5 compares the GR trained with
and without SECPO. Cases 2 and 3 are from QA benchmarks, and Cases 4 and
5 share one repository-level coding task.

#### Case 1: a role synthesized for a deficit.

This case illustrates one adaptation cycle where the GR’s plan names the
null edit. On the MATH-500 problem of
Figure [7](#A4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix D Chain Evolution Case Studies ‣ Test writing for program selection. ‣ Task validator of MATH-500. ‣ Synthesized specialists. ‣ Role synthesis. ‣ Code seed roles. ‣ Role execution. ‣ C.2 Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix C Role Library and Prompt Templates ‣ B.4 Direct Prompting ‣ Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"),
twelve of the eighteen methods compared in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation")
give a wrong answer, among them GPT-4o-mini prompted directly, which
answers 5040 in each of its three runs. RoleFlow’s submitted answer, the
majority answer of twenty sampled chains, is 10080 and correct for all
three trained GRs. The figure shows a replayed chain: one plan that a
trained GR sampled for this problem is executed once more, separately
from the twenty chains, with every role output recorded. The chain of
cycle 0 computes $`7!=5040`$, and the validator $`V`$ rejects this
answer with the note that it does not account for equivalent rotations
properly. Since the plan names the null edit, deficit-driven synthesis
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 RoleFlow: Feedback-Driven Structural Adaptation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"))
creates a role for the reported deficit, RotationEquivalenceEvaluator,
and adds it before the OutputFormatter. The edited chain is executed
from its first role, and its roles receive neither the rejected answer
nor the note. The Planner’s strategy divides $`8!`$ by the four
rotations, the roles before the new one compute 10080, the new role
confirms this result, and $`V`$ accepts it.

#### Case 2: a RoleFlowChain against a direct answer.

This case shows how the roles of a RoleFlowChain split a two-hop

question of
Figure [8](#A4.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ Case 2: a RoleFlowChain against a direct answer. ‣ Appendix D Chain Evolution Case Studies ‣ Test writing for program selection. ‣ Task validator of MATH-500. ‣ Synthesized specialists. ‣ Role synthesis. ‣ Code seed roles. ‣ Role execution. ‣ C.2 Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix C Role Library and Prompt Templates ‣ B.4 Direct Prompting ‣ Appendix B Benchmarks and Compared Methods ‣ A.4 Metrics ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ RoleFlow: Self-Evolving Multi-Role Agentic Collaboration via Structural Adaptation"),

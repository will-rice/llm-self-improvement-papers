# LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades

Farnoosh Hashemi Affiliation: Cornell University    Shiyu Ji
Affiliation: Cornell University    Joice Chen Affiliation: Stanford
University    Michael W. Macy Affiliation: Cornell University

###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly operate in multi-agent systems
where they collaborate by responding to others’ choices, creating
endogenous feedback loops with vulnerability to social influence,
herding behavior and runaway cascades. Following the design of the
classic Music Lab experiment, we tested this vulnerability in large
populations of LLM agents that recommended unfamiliar songs, with or
without exposure to cumulative recommendation counts. Exposure led to
runaway cascades that generated extreme and unpredictable collective
recommendations increasingly divergent from a song’s intrinsic appeal.
Compared to humans, LLM populations were generally far more susceptible
to herding behavior, and unlike humans, LLM cascades were less able to
self-correct. This vulnerability has implications for multi-agent
collaboration in recommender systems, automated evaluation, complex
problem-solving, and unauthorized conspiracies.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly operate in multi-agent systems
in which each model’s decision becomes an input for other models,
creating endogenous feedback loops with self-reinforcing collective
behavior ([Rahwan et al., 2019](#bib.bib47); [Grossmann et al.,
2023](#bib.bib26); [Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib65); [Park et al.,
2023](#bib.bib42); [Brinkmann et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)). Yet little is
known about the vulnerability of these systems to runaway cascades that
fail to self-correct. Evaluating an isolated model may reveal whether it
produces accurate, useful, or biased judgments on its own but not how
the collective behavior of agent populations shapes the subsequent
individual choices that then aggregate as collective behavior.

In human populations, theories of herd behavior and information cascades
show how people may discount their own judgment after observing the
choices of others ([Banerjee, 1992](#bib.bib6); [Bikhchandani et al.,
1992](#bib.bib8)). An initial advantage can make an option more visible
or credible, attracting further choices that strengthen the original
signal. This cumulative advantage can concentrate success among a few
alternatives ([Merton, 1968](#bib.bib40)), leading to unpredictable
outcomes that are highly sensitive to early choices ([Salganik et al.,
2006](#bib.bib49)).

In the classic “Music Lab” study, Salganik, Dodds, and Watts placed
participants in parallel online markets containing the same 48
previously unfamiliar songs. In each of two experiments, eight “social
influence worlds” displayed accumulated download counts, whereas one
“independent world” omitted them. With exposure to social influence,
initial popularity became self-reinforcing, turning an arbitrary early
leader into a “cultural winner,” even with relatively weak intrinsic
appeal in the independent condition. Compared to the independent world,
success was both more unequal and more unpredictable in the social
influence worlds, especially when songs were listed in order of
popularity rather than arranged randomly on a grid ([Salganik et al.,
2006](#bib.bib49)).

Nevertheless, songs with high intrinsic appeal were able to eventually
recover ([Salganik and Watts, 2008](#bib.bib48)), and a reanalysis of
the Music Lab data found that popularity became increasingly aligned
with intrinsic appeal as choices accumulated, indicating that the
cascade could self-correct ([Van de Rijt, 2022](#bib.bib56)). Related
experiments found that social influence reduced the diversity underlying
the “wisdom of crowds” ([Lorenz et al., 2011](#bib.bib37)), that one
arbitrary positive rating generated herding ([Muchnik et al.,
2013](#bib.bib41)), that small arbitrary initial advantages produced
further success across multiple social domains ([van de Rijt et al.,
2014](#bib.bib57)), and that variability in early choices led partisan
populations to switch sides on policy issues from one trial to another
([Macy et al., 2019](#bib.bib38)). As with Music Lab, subsequent
analyses of some of these studies revealed a tendency for errant
cascades to self-correct.

The same feedback structure increasingly appears in human interaction
with AI agents and algorithmic systems. Individual LLMs are known to
favor responses that agree with a user’s stated position even when
agreement conflicts with accuracy, a behavior described as sycophancy
([Sharma et al., 2024](#bib.bib51)), and feedback between humans and AI
systems could amplify initially small AI biases beyond the amplification
observed among humans ([Glickman and Sharot, 2025](#bib.bib25)). In
recommender systems, past user behavior shapes what algorithms make
visible, and exposure reinforces subsequent behavior. These feedback
loops have been shown to alter the diversity of consumption ([Fleder and
Hosanagar, 2009](#bib.bib21)), homogenize choices, reduce utility
([Chaney et al., 2018](#bib.bib11)), and amplify existing popularity
biases ([Mansoury et al., 2020](#bib.bib39)).

A growing literature examines whether LLMs can approximate human
collective judgments, reproduce aggregate survey patterns, and replicate
findings from human behavioral experiments ([Argyle et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2); [Aher et al., 2023](#bib.bib1)). However, LLMs remain
imperfect substitutes for human data ([Santurkar et al.,
2023](#bib.bib50); [Bisbee et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)), and similarity in
aggregate responses does not establish that LLMs reproduce human social
dynamics. Interacting LLM agents have been found to exhibit consensus
and fragmentation dynamics ([Chuang et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)), to
conform to peer responses to a degree that varied with interaction
structure ([Han et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)), and to spontaneously develop
shared conventions, collective biases, and tipping dynamics in which
committed minorities shifted population-wide norms ([Flint Ashery
et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)).

Experiments on conformity and sycophancy have tested LLM susceptibility
to social influence using a majority answer, peer judgment, or user
opinion as an externally supplied input ([Sharma et al.,
2024](#bib.bib51); [Han et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)). However, social
influence does not necessarily lead to a cascade without a feedback loop
created by an endogenous social signal, and a cascade may fail to
self-correct, depending on the strength of social influence relative to
independent judgment.

The risk of herding behavior and runaway cascades in multi-agent AI
systems motivates seven research questions:

1.  1.  Does exposure to collective behavior generate self-reinforcing herd
        behavior?
2.  2.  Does herding generate errant cascades that diverge from independent
        judgments?
3.  3.  Are cascades sensitive to initial choices that make outcomes
        unpredictable?
4.  4.  Does susceptibility to cascade failure increase with the strength of
        social influence?
5.  5.  Do these cascades eventually self-correct?
6.  6.  Are LLMs more susceptible to cascades compared to a human benchmark?
7.  7.  Are reasoning models and models with higher intelligence less
        susceptible compared to other models?

In our study, the social signal is generated within the population:
recommendations by earlier agents become the popularity counts observed
by later agents. Using replicate influence worlds and an independent
baseline, we test whether LLMs are susceptible to cascades that fail to
self-correct, measure changes in this susceptibility as we vary the
strength of social influence, and compare LLM collective behavior with a
human benchmark. As multi-agent AI systems continue to rapidly
proliferate, these contributions will be increasingly relevant. A more
detailed discussion of related work is provided in
appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Related Work ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades").

## 2 Methods

We studied whether feedback from earlier LLM recommendations changes
subsequent choices and collective outcomes. Agents sequentially selected
and evaluated items from a shared catalog; in the influence conditions,
their recommendations updated counts observed by later agents. The
social signal was therefore endogenous, capturing a feedback structure
in which model-generated evaluations become inputs to subsequent
decisions. Following the parallel-world design of Music Lab ([Salganik
et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)), independent decisions provided a
model-specific baseline, while replicate influence worlds allowed us to
measure variation in collective outcomes under the same model, stimuli,
and procedures
(fig. [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.2 Parallel worlds and feedback conditions ‣ 2 Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).

### 2.1 Task and stimuli

All models evaluated the same 48 songs, newly generated using Suno v4.5
([Team Suno, 2025](#bib.bib55)). Each stimulus consisted of a title and
lyrics, without audio. Newly generated songs reduced the risk that
training exposure or established popularity would introduce an external
signal into the experiment. Lyrics provided a common text input across
all models and made large-scale evaluation practical, aligning the task
with language-based recommendation and automated evaluation.

Each persona-conditioned agent completed two stages, issued as separate
prompts
(figs. [S1](#A2.F1 "Figure S1 ‣ B.3 Prompts ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
and
[S2](#A2.F2 "Figure S2 ‣ B.3 Prompts ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
In the selection stage, it viewed all 48 titles and selected exactly
four distinct songs to assess. In the recommendation stage, it received
the full lyrics of those songs and made a yes/no recommendation decision
for each, allowing zero to four recommendations. In the influence
conditions, current recommendation counts were visible at both stages.
After the agent completed its decisions, each affirmative recommendation
increased that song’s count by one before the next agent acted.
Unselected songs could not receive a recommendation from that agent.

The fixed selection budget standardized evaluation opportunities across
agents; four approximated the average number of songs sampled by Music
Lab participants ([Salganik et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)). The budget also
captured a bottleneck relevant to LLM applications: downstream
evaluation can only act on candidates selected upstream. Recommender
systems separate candidate generation from ranking ([Covington et al.,
2016](#bib.bib15)); retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) conditions on a
restricted set of retrieved documents ([Lewis et al.,
2020](#bib.bib34)); and LLM rankers evaluate retrieved candidates ([Hou
et al., 2024](#bib.bib30)). Our task captures this shared selection
constraint. Popularity can therefore affect both which items enter the
consideration set and how those items are evaluated, allowing these
influences to accumulate through sequential feedback.

### 2.2 Parallel worlds and feedback conditions

For each model, we created 17 worlds: one independent world of 1,440
agents, eight weak-influence worlds of 720 agents, and eight
strong-influence worlds of 720 agents. This yielded 12,960 agents per
model and 168,480 agents across 13 models. All worlds began with zero
recommendation counts and evolved separately. In the independent world,
agents received no information about the choices of others. In the
influence worlds, agents saw the number of recommendations each song had
accumulated from previous agents in the same world, updated after every
agent. Agents acted sequentially, were unaware of other worlds, and did
not exchange messages. Earlier choices were conveyed only through
cumulative counts and, under strong influence, the resulting title
order.

In the independent condition, titles were randomly ordered for each
agent and recommendation counts were omitted. In the weak-influence
condition, counts were displayed, but title order was randomized
independently for each agent. In the strong-influence condition, counts
were displayed and titles were ordered by decreasing count, with ties
broken randomly. The strong-influence prompt also stated that titles
were ordered by recommendation counts. Comparing weak influence with
independent evaluation isolated the effect of displaying counts;
comparing strong with weak influence tested the additional effect of
linking popularity to list position. Because LLM outputs can depend on
where information appears in the input ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib36))
and LLM rankers exhibit position and popularity biases ([Hou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib30)), this contrast tested whether making popular items
more prominent in the input further strengthened feedback. The same
independent world served as the baseline for both influence conditions.
Replicate worlds shared the same model, stimuli, persona-generating
procedure, and inference settings, but each accumulated its own
recommendation history. Comparing their variability with the independent
baseline tested whether feedback amplified variation beyond that
observed under independent evaluation.

### 2.3 Model populations

We evaluated five reasoning models (GPT-5, GPT-5 nano, Gemini 3 Flash,
DeepSeek-R1, and Claude Sonnet 4.6) and eight non-reasoning models
(GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, Gemma 3 4B, Gemma 3 12B, Gemma 3 27B,
DeepSeek-V3.1, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Qwen3-Next 80B), classified
according to their developers’ designations of the inference mode. Each
population used a single model. We also assessed capability using the
Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.1.1 ([Artificial Analysis,
2026](#bib.bib3)), a composite benchmark spanning agentic tasks, coding,
general problem-solving, and scientific reasoning
(table [S3](#A3.T3 "Table S3 ‣ C.1 Model intelligence and susceptibility to social influence ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).

To introduce structured heterogeneity, each agent was assigned a persona
combining demographic attributes and primary and secondary preferred
music genres. The same persona-generating procedure was used across
models and conditions. Persona construction, complete prompts, and
inference settings are provided in
appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades").

### 2.4 Outcome measures

A song’s market share was the proportion of all recommendations it
received within its world:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       p_{w}(i)=r_{i}^{(w)}/R^{(w)},
       ```                            |     | (1) |

where $`r_{i}^{(w)}`$ is the number received by song $`i`$ in world
$`w`$ and $`R^{(w)}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}r_{i}^{(w)}`$ is the total over all
$`K=48`$ songs. Market shares in the independent world provided a
population-specific measure of each song’s relative intrinsic appeal.

Our primary outcome measures were Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence and
unpredictability. $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}`$ measures how far the distribution
of recommendations in each influence world departed from the independent
distribution:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(w)}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\widetilde{p}_{w}(i)\log\frac{\widetilde{p}_{w}(i)}{\widetilde{p}_{\mathrm{ind}}(i)},
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\widetilde{p}`$ denotes market shares after Jeffreys smoothing
(pseudocount 0.5), applied to both distributions
(appendix [B.6](#A2.SS6 "B.6 Outcome measures ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
Larger values indicate greater divergence from independent evaluation.
We report the arithmetic mean across the eight worlds in each influence
condition.

Unpredictability ($`U`$) measures the extent to which parallel worlds
with the same model, songs, and experimental procedures evolved toward
different collective outcomes ([Salganik et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
u_{i}=\binom{W}{2}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{W-1}\sum_{k=j+1}^{W}\left|p_{j}(i)-p_{k}(i)\right|,\qquad U=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}u_{i},
``` |  | (3) |

where $`W=8`$ and market shares were unsmoothed. For the independent
world, we randomly divided agents into two groups of 720, matching the
size of an influence world, and averaged their market-share differences
over 1,000 splits.

To test for self-correction, we followed the procedure applied to the
Music Lab data ([Van de Rijt, 2022](#bib.bib56)). For each influence
world, we split the chronological recommendation sequence into two
approximately equal halves and computed the Spearman correlation between
song recommendation counts within each half and independent-world
counts. Second-half counts excluded first-half recommendations. A
positive change, $`\Delta\rho^{(w)}=\rho_{2}^{(w)}-\rho_{1}^{(w)}>0`$,
indicates that later recommendations aligned more closely with intrinsic
appeal.

Together, these measures distinguish displacement from independent
judgment, variation across replicate worlds, and self-correction over
time. For the capability analysis, we used relative unpredictability,
$`U_{\mathrm{inf}}/U_{\mathrm{ind}}`$, to measure proportional
amplification above each model’s independent baseline; absolute $`U`$
remained the primary measure in the other analyses. Additional measures
(Gini inequality, Pearson correlation with independent shares, and
persistence of early recommendations), robustness checks, and
position-bias analyses are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

### 2.5 Human benchmark

We applied the same outcome definitions to the original Music Lab data
as a human benchmark ([Salganik et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)), with
downloads in place of recommendations. Weak influence corresponded to
Music Lab experiment 1, in which download counts were shown but songs
were arranged in a randomized grid, and strong influence corresponded to
experiment 2, in which songs were listed by download count. Because
stimuli, response tasks, and presentation differed, the human results
provide a qualitative benchmark rather than a controlled estimate of
human–LLM differences;
appendix [B.5](#A2.SS5 "B.5 Human benchmark and design differences ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
details this comparison.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 LLM susceptibility to herding

Fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 LLM susceptibility to herding ‣ 3 Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
shows how exposure to current recommendations generated herding behavior
in which agents’ recommendations diverged from independent judgment
($`p<0.001`$ for every model under both influence conditions). Mean KL
divergence was about nine times the human benchmark under weak influence
and four times under strong influence
(fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 LLM susceptibility to herding ‣ 3 Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")A).
Among LLMs, non-reasoning models diverged 2.4 and 3.2 times as far as
reasoning models under weak and strong influence, respectively. Under
strong influence, four of the five reasoning models diverged less than
every non-reasoning model; GPT-5 nano was the exception
(fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 LLM susceptibility to herding ‣ 3 Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades"),
B and C). GPT-5 approached human divergence, whereas Gemma 3 4B diverged
more than eight times as far. Except for Claude Sonnet 4.6, strong
influence increased mean divergence by 65%, with greater divergence in
12 of 13 models (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test across models,
$`p<0.01`$).

### 3.2 Social feedback can turn herding behavior into a stampede

Divergence from independent judgment can indicate herding behavior but
not necessarily a stampede. The latter also requires an endogenous
feedback loop that can amplify small initial differences, making
outcomes highly unpredictable.
Fig. [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2 Social feedback can turn herding behavior into a stampede ‣ 3 Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")A
shows that unpredictability in the independent condition was close to
zero across all models and slightly below the level observed among
humans. Compared to the independent condition, unpredictability was far
greater in the influence conditions for humans and LLMs, especially
among non-reasoning models in the strong-influence condition.

Given the similarly low independent baselines, the more instructive
comparison is within influence conditions. Non-reasoning models were
nearly twice as unpredictable as reasoning models at both influence
levels. Mean unpredictability among reasoning models was modestly above
the human benchmark, whereas non-reasoning models were more than twice
as unpredictable as humans under both influence conditions.

All 13 models exceeded human unpredictability under weak influence
(fig. [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2 Social feedback can turn herding behavior into a stampede ‣ 3 Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades"),
B and C). Under strong influence, three reasoning models—Gemini 3 Flash,
GPT-5, and Claude Sonnet 4.6—fell slightly below the human benchmark.
Strong influence increased unpredictability in 12 of 13 models, with
Gemini 3 Flash the lone exception (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test
across models, $`p<0.001`$).

Unpredictability reflects sensitivity to early choices, which can also
be measured directly. We also correlated market shares among the first
30 agents in each influence world with market shares among all later
agents in the same world. In LLM populations, early movers predicted
later popularity better than intrinsic appeal did in 12 of 13 models
under weak influence and in all 13 under strong influence, with the
largest gaps among non-reasoning models
(table [S2](#A2.T2 "Table S2 ‣ Correlation between early movers and later agents. ‣ B.6 Outcome measures ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
Among humans, early movers were the better predictor only under strong
influence
(appendix [C.4](#A3.SS4 "C.4 Early movers predicted later popularity better than intrinsic appeal ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).

### 3.4 Human cascades self-corrected; LLM cascades generally did not

Cascades can self-correct if independent preferences are sufficiently
strong relative to social influence ([Van de Rijt, 2022](#bib.bib56)).
Fig. [5](#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 3.4 Human cascades self-corrected; LLM cascades generally did not ‣ 3 Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
reports self-correction using the same diagnostic procedure that was
used with the original Music Lab data – the change in the Spearman
correlation between popularity and intrinsic appeal between the first
and second halves of the experiment. A larger second-half correlation
indicates movement toward the independent ranking; negative changes
indicate movement away.

Across the entire experiment, market shares remained positively
correlated with intrinsic appeal in every population, but this
correspondence weakened in 11 of 13 models under strong influence
(table [S2](#A2.T2 "Table S2 ‣ Correlation between early movers and later agents. ‣ B.6 Outcome measures ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
Splitting each experiment in half, human cascades self-corrected in 15
of 16 influence worlds, with the popularity-appeal correlation
increasing from $`\rho=0.633`$ to 0.773 under weak influence (all eight
worlds; one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, $`p<0.01`$) and from 0.575
to 0.660 under strong influence (seven of eight worlds; $`p<0.05`$).
Among LLMs, changes varied in direction across models and worlds,
averaging close to zero under strong influence. The human benchmark
therefore exhibited a corrective dynamic that was much less consistent
across LLM populations. In short, LLMs were more susceptible to “runaway
cascades,” persistent, history-dependent departures without reliable
self-correction over the observed experimental horizon. However, the
results do not establish that correction could never occur over longer
sequences.

### 3.5 Supplementary results

Social influence increased inequality (measured as the Gini coefficient)
in all 13 models under strong influence and in 12 under weak influence
(fig. [S3](#A3.F3 "Figure S3 ‣ C.2 Social influence increased inequality of market shares ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
Relative to their independent baselines, non-reasoning models showed
larger mean increases in inequality than humans, whereas reasoning
models showed smaller increases.

Popularity-ordered lists in the strong-influence condition could
confound responses to recommendation counts with responses to display
position. We tested for position bias using the fraction of first-stage
selections at each of the 48 display positions (not songs) in the
independent condition
(fig. [S4](#A3.F4 "Figure S4 ‣ C.5 Position bias could provide an alternative explanation for the effects of strong influence ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
Selections were approximately uniform across positions for most LLMs,
although Gemma 3 4B and GPT-4o mini showed a modest preference for
higher-ranked positions. Because agents were randomly distributed across
conditions, those in the influence conditions should not be expected to
have greater position bias than those in the independent condition. As
expected, position bias was much stronger among humans, given the lack
of the cognitive resources to process all 48 songs. Net of position
bias, susceptibility to self-reinforcing social influence among humans
may be substantially weaker than we reported, which means we may have
understated the difference in susceptibility with LLMs. See “Position
bias” in the SM for details and an extended discussion of the
implications.

Chain-of-thought reasoning traces in DeepSeek-R1 revealed explicit
references to song popularity in every log and in both influence
conditions, consistent with responsiveness to social influence.

Finally, we ran two robustness checks. Under strong influence, lowering
the temperature from 1.0 to 0.7 did not attenuate overall divergence or
unpredictability among non-reasoning models
(fig. [S7](#A3.F7 "Figure S7 ‣ Robustness to sampling temperature. ‣ C.7 Robustness analyses ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
Increasing the number of titles that could be chosen for assessment from
four to 12 reduced divergence and unpredictability but preserved the
main patterns across models
(figs. [S8](#A3.F8 "Figure S8 ‣ Robustness to the size of the consideration set. ‣ C.7 Robustness analyses ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")

## 4 Discussion

Large language models increasingly collaborate in multi-agent systems in
which model-generated judgments may become inputs for subsequent agents,
creating endogenous feedback loops. Evaluating collective AI behavior
therefore requires going beyond individual responses to a fixed signal
to test how model outputs shape subsequent inputs.

Across 13 experiments, comparisons with a human benchmark revealed
generally greater LLM susceptibility to herding and cascades and less
consistent self-correction. When popularity influences recommendation,
songs with an early advantage are more likely to be chosen for
assessment and to then be assessed more favorably.

Reasoning and higher-intelligence models were less susceptible to
herding behavior, but lower susceptibility did not ensure
self-correction. Going forward, as models rapidly advance,
susceptibility to influence on simple tasks like recommendation can be
expected to decline. However, our experiment precluded more powerful
influence mechanisms like communication, task incentives, and social
sanctioning. If task complexity increases alongside intelligence and
influence is reinforced with social pressure and incentives,
susceptibility to errant cascades may increase.

Our design preserved the feedback structure of Music Lab but adapted its
implementation to LLM agents: agents read the lyrics of newly generated
songs rather than listening to songs by unknown bands, recommended
rather than downloaded songs, assessed a fixed four songs rather than
choosing how many to sample, and saw a text list rather than a grid when
songs were not ordered by popularity
(appendix [B.5](#A2.SS5 "B.5 Human benchmark and design differences ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
These differences limit attribution of human–LLM contrasts to population
type. A matched human replication of this study is needed to rule out
these design differences as the explanation for observed behavioral
differences. Our experiment also studied homogeneous populations of a
single model, precluded explicit collaboration, and lacked incentives
for conformity or ground-truth accuracy. Generalization to heterogeneous
teams, more challenging tasks, multimodal systems, incentives for
compliance, and verbal influence remain to be tested. Nevertheless,
self-reinforcing departures from independent judgment identify a
potential risk for recommender systems, automated evaluation, and
collaborative problem-solving.

## Appendix A Related Work

### A.1 Conformity and sycophancy in individual LLMs

A large literature documents how individual LLMs shift their responses
toward opinions presented in their input. Studies of sycophancy found
that agreement with users increased with model size \[[Perez et al.,
2023](#bib.bib44)\], appeared across free-form tasks and was favored by
human preference judgments \[[Sharma et al., 2024](#bib.bib51)\], and
led models to abandon correct answers after user rebuttals \[[Fanous
et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)\]. A second line of work adapted the Asch
paradigm by displaying peer answers before the model responds. Weng et
al. introduced five interaction protocols, including multi-round
histories of correct guidance followed by incorrect guidance; all eleven
models exhibited conformity, while larger models were more independent
\[[Weng et al., 2025](#bib.bib59)\]. Zhu et al. found that conformity
increased with the number and confident tone of unanimous confederates
and was predicted by the model’s uncertainty \[[Zhu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib67)\]. Other studies linked conformity to group size and
task difficulty \[[Bellina et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)\], peers’ group
identity \[[Soffer et al., 2026](#bib.bib53)\], prior agreement with
peers \[[Song et al., 2026](#bib.bib54)\], and relative confidence
between the model and its peers \[[Cho et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)\].
Models were easier to mislead than to correct: wrong peer agreement
increased harmful revision more than correct agreement increased
beneficial revision \[[Qu et al., 2026](#bib.bib46)\]. Across 23 models,
mitigations generally traded resistance to incorrect peers against
receptivity to correct ones; reasoning improved both on derivable MMLU
tasks, but the trade-off persisted on GPQA and SimpleQA \[[Hussain and
Nielbo, 2026](#bib.bib31)\]. Popularity cues also influenced LLM judges
\[[Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib62)\] and product recommendations
\[[Filandrianos et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)\].

These studies primarily examine how individual models respond to
controlled social information, rather than how successive decisions
update a cumulative popularity signal. Our study asks how individual
susceptibility develops into population-level cascades when
recommendations generate the signal seen by subsequent agents. The
outcomes are divergence from independent judgment, unpredictability
across replicate worlds, and self-correction, rather than whether a
model adopts a supplied answer.

### A.2 Collective dynamics in interacting LLM populations

When agents respond to one another, social influence becomes endogenous.
Multi-agent debate improved factuality \[[Du et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)\], but also produced conformity \[[Zhang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib63), [Baltaji et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)\], deference to
larger or more capable groups \[[Choi et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)\], and
accuracy losses when agents abandoned correct answers \[[Wynn et al.,
2025](#bib.bib61)\]. In a seven-agent misinformation task, denser
networks accelerated convergence but raised the risk of confident
incorrect consensus \[[Han et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)\]. Larger
populations developed shared conventions, collective biases, and
minority-driven tipping \[[Flint Ashery et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)\],
reached consensus or fragmented depending on prompted bias \[[Chuang
et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)\], and became trapped in states opposed to
their intrinsic preferences \[[De Marzo et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)\].
Forum simulations found greater conformity in smaller models and greater
resistance in reasoning-optimized models \[[Lin et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35)\]. Observational analyses of Chirper.ai, a platform
populated by LLM agents, documented homophily and social influence among
approximately 32,000 agents \[[Hashemi and Macy, 2026](#bib.bib29),
[Hashemi and Macy, 2025](#bib.bib28)\].

Sequential decisions and accumulating social signals provide closer
comparisons. Jain and Krishnamurthy combined LLM outputs with Bayesian
decision mechanisms to study social learning and information cascades
\[[Jain and Krishnamurthy, 2025](#bib.bib32)\]. Zhong et al. had 100
agents rate a film in sequence while observing earlier agents’ mean
rating, producing anchoring and, with personas, majority dominance
\[[Zhong et al., 2025](#bib.bib66)\]. In a simulated microblogging
platform, responses to endogenous popularity counts varied with
information load and normative context \[[Peng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib43)\]. Wu et al. studied choices on a question-answering
platform with independent conditions, accumulating engagement counts,
and experimentally seeded popularity, finding reinforcement of existing
preferences and shifts toward socially favored alternatives \[[Wu
et al., 2025](#bib.bib60)\].

Our study extends this work by connecting individual susceptibility to
the distribution, unpredictability, and self-correction of collective
recommendations in one controlled design. Agents act once in sequence
and choose among fixed alternatives whose intrinsic appeal is measured
independently. We combine this design with a fixed selection budget,
separate selection and endorsement stages, and a manipulation of whether
popularity also determines list position. Separating selection from
endorsement makes attention a bottleneck: an item must first be selected
before it can receive a recommendation. Popularity can therefore
reinforce both exposure and evaluation. Comparing 13 models tests
whether greater individual capability is associated with collective
robustness. Replicated worlds reveal whether early choices redirect
collective outcomes, while temporal comparisons test whether
accumulating recommendations restore alignment with independent appeal.
A human benchmark places the magnitude of these effects in context.

### A.3 Hybrid human–AI societies and feedback loops

Human–AI interaction can amplify initially small judgment biases
\[[Glickman and Sharot, 2025](#bib.bib25)\]. In story-revision networks,
AI-only populations lost diversity whereas hybrid populations better
balanced creativity and diversity \[[Shiiku et al., 2026](#bib.bib52)\];
opinion-diffusion experiments reported preliminary evidence of lower
final polarization in hybrid networks \[[Gaubert et al.,
2026](#bib.bib24)\]. Recommender-system research likewise shows that
feedback can homogenize choices and amplify popularity bias \[[Fleder
and Hosanagar, 2009](#bib.bib21), [Chaney et al., 2018](#bib.bib11),
[Mansoury et al., 2020](#bib.bib39)\]. Our experiment isolates a loop
operating through cumulative recommendations, without changing model
weights or the alternatives available. It tests how the population’s own
choices reshape subsequent decisions within homogeneous LLM populations;
extending this design to mixed human–AI populations would test whether
population composition changes cascade strength and correction.

### B.2 Persona construction and agent heterogeneity

Persona conditioning introduced structured variation in the inputs
guiding agents’ preferences. With a fixed model, prompt, and stimuli,
stochastic decoding can produce different responses across repeated
calls, but these responses remain draws from the same underlying
conditional distribution. Assigning distinct demographic profiles and
musical preferences varied the conditioning information itself, allowing
us to study social feedback in a population with heterogeneous
preference prompts.

Persona conditioning is widely used to construct heterogeneous LLM
populations by conditioning model responses on individual
characteristics \[[Argyle et al., 2023](#bib.bib2), [Aher et al.,
2023](#bib.bib1), [Ashokkumar et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)\]. Prior work has
also found that persona prompting increased the diversity of model
responses in a systematic and repeatable manner \[[Fröhling et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23)\]. Each persona was assigned a primary and secondary
preferred music genre drawn from the following: Classical, Jazz, Blues,
Rock, Pop, Folk, Country, Gospel/Religious, Hip-Hop/Rap, R&B/Soul,
Electronic/Dance, Reggae, Latin, Metal, and World/Global. The persona
also included age, gender, and country of residence, sampled to
approximate the demographic composition of participants in the original
Music Lab experiment \[[Salganik et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)\]. However,
we do not claim that individual agents reproduced individual human
listeners. Persona-conditioned synthetic samples can still
underrepresent human variation \[[Santurkar et al., 2023](#bib.bib50),
[Bisbee et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)\]. The same persona-generating
procedure was used in the independent and influence conditions to avoid
systematic differences in population construction alongside exposure to
recommendation counts.

Non-reasoning models used a sampling temperature of 1.0. Reasoning

### B.5 Human benchmark and design differences

Human benchmark values were computed from the original Music Lab data
\[[Salganik et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)\] using the same outcome
definitions as for the LLM populations, with downloads in place of
recommendations. Weak influence corresponded to experiment 1 and strong
influence to experiment 2. Each human experiment included one
independent world and eight influence worlds, with its own independent
world serving as the baseline. The self-correction diagnostic was
applied to the same data following van de Rijt \[[Van de Rijt,
2022](#bib.bib56)\].

Our design preserved the causal structure of Music Lab: sequential
choices, parallel worlds that began from zero and evolved separately, an
independent world without social information, and two influence
conditions that differed in whether popularity also determined display
position. The implementation was adapted to LLM agents in six ways, each
discussed below.

#### Newly generated songs.

Music Lab used songs by unknown bands so that participants would not
know the songs or their popularity. For LLMs, reusing the original songs
risked prior exposure during model training, which could encode not only
familiarity with a song but also information about its popularity and
reception. Newly generated songs reduced the risk that such an external
popularity signal would contaminate the endogenous recommendation counts
as the source of influence.

#### Lyrics rather than audio.

We considered presenting audio to multimodal models but selected a
lyrics-only design for three reasons. First, audio would have required
audio-capable models, a relatively small class less likely to be used in
multi-agent systems, and the computational cost of audio evaluation for
over 160,000 agents was a practical constraint. Text allowed identical
materials to be presented to all 13 models and aligned the experiment
with language-based judgments in recommender systems, automated
evaluation, and multi-agent systems. Second, audio input does not ensure
that judgments depend on musical content: in a previous study, three of
five audio-language models showed no significant performance decline
when the original audio was replaced with noise or an unrelated track,
consistent with substantial reliance on textual cues \[[Weck et al.,
2024](#bib.bib58)\]. Third, even with newly generated audio, the stimuli
would still differ from the original songs. We therefore leave cascade
dynamics in multimodal populations to future research.

#### Recommendation rather than download.

Music Lab participants rated each song while listening and then decided
whether to download it. Downloading has no direct behavioral counterpart
for an agent, whereas recommendation is a widely used LLM task. The
substitution preserved the feedback mechanism: each affirmative choice
increased an observable count of prior choices and thereby changed the
information encountered by subsequent agents.

#### Fixed selection budget.

Music Lab participants chose how many songs to sample. We fixed the
budget at four, approximating the average number sampled by participants
(3.8 and 3.6 in experiments 1 and 2, respectively) \[[Salganik et al.,
2006](#bib.bib49)\], to standardize evaluation opportunities across
agents and models; the main text describes how this budget reflects
candidate-selection bottlenecks in LLM systems. Increasing the budget to
12 reduced divergence and unpredictability but preserved the main
patterns
(figs. [S8](#A3.F8 "Figure S8 ‣ Robustness to the size of the consideration set. ‣ C.7 Robustness analyses ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
and
[S9](#A3.F9 "Figure S9 ‣ Robustness to the size of the consideration set. ‣ C.7 Robustness analyses ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).

#### List rather than grid.

Music Lab displayed songs in a randomized grid in experiment 1 and in a
single-column list in experiment 2, ordered by downloads in the
influence worlds and randomly in the independent world \[[Salganik
et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)\]. A list is the natural text representation
for LLM input, so we used a randomized list in the independent and
weak-influence conditions and a popularity-ordered list under strong
influence. The weak-influence condition therefore preserved the defining
feature of the human weak-influence condition, popularity information
without popularity-based ordering, but not its interface. For the same
reason, human position bias under weak influence is not directly
comparable with that of LLM agents and is not reported
(fig. [S4](#A3.F4 "Figure S4 ‣ C.5 Position bias could provide an alternative explanation for the effects of strong influence ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).

#### Shared independent baseline.

Each Music Lab experiment had its own independent world. Because both
LLM influence conditions used the same model, stimuli,
persona-generating procedure, and inference settings, we compared all 16
influence worlds with a single independent world per model, so the weak-
and strong-influence conditions shared a common baseline.

These differences limit attribution of human–LLM contrasts to population
type, so we treat the human results as a qualitative benchmark.
Comparisons among LLMs used the same stimuli, task, and condition
definitions.

### B.6 Outcome measures

We measured market share using the distribution of song recommendation
counts in each world. Let $`K=48`$ denote the number of songs,
$`r_{i}^{(w)}`$ the number of recommendations received by song $`i`$ in
world $`w`$, and $`R^{(w)}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}r_{i}^{(w)}`$ the total number
of recommendations in that world. Following the original Music Lab
experiment \[[Salganik et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)\], the market share of
song $`i`$ was

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{w}(i)=\frac{r_{i}^{(w)}}{R^{(w)}},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_{w}(i)=1.
``` |  | (S1) |

Market shares accounted for differences in total recommendation counts
because agents could recommend between zero and four songs.
Independent-condition unpredictability and inequality were estimated
through resampling as described below.

#### KL divergence.

Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence compares each song’s market share under
influence with its independent share, measuring displacement from the
population-specific baseline. For influence world $`w`$, we calculated

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(w)}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\widetilde{p}_{w}(i)\log\frac{\widetilde{p}_{w}(i)}{\widetilde{p}_{\mathrm{ind}}(i)},
``` |  | (S2) |

where $`\widetilde{p}`$ denotes smoothed market shares. Divergence
equals zero when the two smoothed distributions coincide, and larger
values indicate greater departure from the independent baseline. We
reported the arithmetic mean over the eight worlds in each influence
condition.

To avoid infinite divergence when a song had positive influence-world
share but zero independent-world share, we applied Jeffreys smoothing to
both distributions by adding a pseudocount of $`\alpha=0.5`$ to every
song before normalization:

|     |                                                            |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \widetilde{p}_{w}(i)=\frac{r_{i}^{(w)}+0.5}{R^{(w)}+0.5K}.
       ```                                                         |     | (S3) |

The same procedure was applied to the independent distribution. Jeffreys
smoothing was used only for KL divergence; all remaining measures used
unsmoothed market shares or recommendation counts. Unlike Gini
inequality, which measures concentration within a world, KL divergence
also distinguishes which songs received larger shares relative to the
independent baseline.

#### Unpredictability.

Unpredictability measured the extent to which parallel worlds containing
the same songs and LLM model nevertheless evolved toward different
collective outcomes. Following Salganik, Dodds, and Watts \[[Salganik
et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)\], the unpredictability of song $`i`$ was the
average absolute difference in its market share across all pairs of
influence worlds:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
u_{i}=\binom{W}{2}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{W-1}\sum_{k=j+1}^{W}\left|p_{j}(i)-p_{k}(i)\right|,
``` |  | (S4) |

where $`W=8`$ is the number of worlds in each influence condition.
Overall unpredictability was the average across all $`K=48`$ songs:

|     |                                   |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       U=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}u_{i}.
       ```                                |     | (S5) |

Larger values indicate greater variation in collective outcomes across
worlds. Increased unpredictability under influence relative to
independent evaluation indicates greater dependence on the history
through which recommendations accumulated.

With eight influence worlds, each song’s unpredictability was based on
$`\binom{8}{2}=28`$ world pairs. For the independent condition, we
followed the original Music Lab procedure: we randomly split the
independent population into two equal subpopulations of 720 agents,
matching the size of an influence world, calculated the mean absolute
difference in market shares between them, and averaged the results over
1,000 random splits \[[Salganik et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)\].

#### Relative unpredictability.

The capability analysis divided each model’s influence-condition
unpredictability by its own independent baseline:

|     |                                                             |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       U_{\mathrm{rel}}=\frac{U_{\mathrm{inf}}}{U_{\mathrm{ind}}},
       ```                                                          |     | (S6) |

where $`U_{\mathrm{inf}}`$ is unpredictability in the specified
influence condition and $`U_{\mathrm{ind}}`$ is the independent estimate
obtained by repeated splitting for the same model. A ratio of one
indicates equal variability, while a ratio above one indicates
proportional amplification under influence.

#### Pearson correlation between worlds.

We measured the extent to which popularity reflected intrinsic appeal as
the Pearson correlation between market shares in the independent and
influence worlds. For each population and influence condition, we pooled
the 48 songs across the eight influence worlds, yielding
$`8\times 48=384`$ paired observations. Each influence-world market
share was paired with the corresponding song’s market share in the
independent world. Let $`x_{\ell}`$ denote the independent-world market
share and $`y_{\ell}`$ the corresponding influence-world market share
for pooled observation $`\ell`$. Pearson’s correlation was

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r=\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{KW}\left(x_{\ell}-\overline{x}\right)\left(y_{\ell}-\overline{y}\right)}{\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{KW}\left(x_{\ell}-\overline{x}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{KW}\left(y_{\ell}-\overline{y}\right)^{2}}}.
``` |  | (S7) |

Higher positive correlations indicate that songs with greater intrinsic
appeal acquired larger market shares under influence. The 384 pairs were
not 384 independent replicates: each song’s independent share was reused
in eight pairs, songs within a world shared the same feedback process,
and their shares summed to one. A conventional Pearson test treating
these pairs as independent would therefore give misleading significance
levels. Accordingly,
table [S2](#A2.T2 "Table S2 ‣ Correlation between early movers and later agents. ‣ B.6 Outcome measures ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
reports descriptive coefficients without significance tests.

#### Correlation between early movers and later agents.

#### Self-correction toward intrinsic appeal.

Cascades can self-correct if intrinsic appeal is sufficiently strong
relative to social influence \[[Van de Rijt, 2022](#bib.bib56)\].
Following van de Rijt \[[Van de Rijt, 2022](#bib.bib56)\], we divided
each influence world’s chronological sequence of recommendation events
into two approximately equal halves. Let $`q_{i}`$ denote the total
recommendations received by song $`i`$ in the corresponding independent
world, and $`r_{i,1}^{(w)}`$ and $`r_{i,2}^{(w)}`$ its counts within the
first and second halves of influence world $`w`$. Second-half counts
excluded first-half recommendations. We calculated the Spearman
correlation across the 48 songs:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{1}^{(w)}=\rho_{S}\left(\left(r_{i,1}^{(w)}\right)_{i=1}^{K},\left(q_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{K}\right),\qquad\rho_{2}^{(w)}=\rho_{S}\left(\left(r_{i,2}^{(w)}\right)_{i=1}^{K},\left(q_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{K}\right),
``` |  | (S8) |

where 1 and 2 index the first and second halves of the sequence of
submitted recommendations. Self-correction was indicated by the change
in correlation between halves:

|     |                                                 |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Delta\rho^{(w)}=\rho_{2}^{(w)}-\rho_{1}^{(w)}.
       ```                                              |     | (S9) |

Positive values indicate that popularity became more closely aligned
with intrinsic appeal as recommendations accumulated; values near zero
indicate little net change, and negative values indicate movement away
from the independent ranking. For each population, we report the mean
change across the eight influence worlds and the number of worlds in
which $`\Delta\rho^{(w)}>0`$. We tested the directional hypothesis of
positive change using one-sided sign and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
across the eight worlds.

#### Market-share inequality.

Following the original Music Lab experiment \[[Salganik et al.,
2006](#bib.bib49)\], we used the Gini coefficient to measure inequality
of market shares in each world:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
G_{w}=\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left|p_{w}(i)-p_{w}(j)\right|.
``` |  | (S10) |

The coefficient equals zero for equal shares and increases with
concentration, with a maximum of $`(K-1)/K`$. For the LLM independent
condition, we drew 1,000 bootstrap samples of 720 agents, calculated the
Gini coefficient for each sample, and reported their arithmetic mean. We
compared mean inequality across the eight influence worlds with this
independent baseline. (See Supplementary results below.)

### C.2 Social influence increased inequality of market shares

The original Music Lab study used the Gini coefficient to measure
inequality in the distribution of market shares in the independent and
influence worlds \[[Salganik et al., 2006](#bib.bib49)\]. Inequality
captures the concentration associated with cumulative advantage but not
the song-specific displacement of popularity from intrinsic appeal. We
therefore used KL divergence as the primary measure of displacement and
included inequality as a supplementary measure
(fig. [S3](#A3.F3 "Figure S3 ‣ C.2 Social influence increased inequality of market shares ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
Across the 13 LLM populations, the mean Gini coefficient was 0.446 in
the independent condition, compared with 0.706 and 0.794 under weak and
strong influence, respectively. The corresponding increases were 0.260
and 0.348 (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across models,
$`p<0.001`$ for both). The Gini coefficient exceeded the independent
baseline in all 13 models under strong influence and in 12 of 13 under
weak influence, with GPT-5 nano the only exception.

The magnitude of the increase differed substantially between reasoning
classes. Among reasoning models, the mean Gini coefficient rose from
0.528 in the independent condition to 0.657 and 0.722 under weak and
strong influence, respectively. Among non-reasoning models, mean Gini
rose from 0.395 to 0.737 and 0.838. The between-class difference in the
increase under strong influence was significant at the model level
(two-sided Mann–Whitney U test, $`p<0.05`$). The human benchmark showed
increases in inequality of 0.153 and 0.311 under weak and strong
influence, respectively.

Absolute levels of inequality require a different interpretation from KL
divergence because model populations already differed substantially from
humans and from each other before social information was introduced.
Independent-condition Gini was 0.446 across LLMs compared with 0.187 in
the human benchmark. Accordingly, the increase from each population’s
own independent baseline provides a more informative comparison.
Non-reasoning models showed larger mean increases in inequality than did
humans under both influence conditions, whereas reasoning models showed
smaller mean increases.

Unlike KL divergence and unpredictability, inequality did not show a
statistically significant increase across models when influence was
strengthened. Gini was higher under strong than weak influence in 9 of
13 models, but the difference did not reach conventional significance
(two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, $`p>0.05`$). This difference
between inequality and KL divergence reflects the different information
captured by the measures: two worlds can have similar inequality while
concentrating recommendations on entirely different songs.

### C.3 Social influence attenuated but did not eliminate the correspondence between intrinsic appeal and popularity

Table [S2](#A2.T2 "Table S2 ‣ Correlation between early movers and later agents. ‣ B.6 Outcome measures ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
reports the correlation between each song’s market share under social
influence and its market share under independent evaluation. The
relationship remained positive in every model and under both influence
conditions but weakened under strong influence. Across the 13 models,
the mean Pearson correlation declined from $`r=0.584`$ under weak
influence to $`r=0.472`$ under strong influence. Correlations were lower
under strong influence in 11 of 13 models, the exceptions being Claude
Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-4o. The difference was statistically significant
across all models (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, $`p<0.01`$).

This correspondence differed markedly across model classes. Reasoning
models retained relatively strong alignment with their independent
distributions, with mean correlations of $`r=0.738`$ and $`r=0.689`$
under weak and strong influence, respectively, compared with 0.487 and
0.336 among non-reasoning models. The between-class difference was
significant at the model level in both influence conditions (two-sided
Mann–Whitney U tests, $`n=5`$ and $`8`$, $`p<0.05`$). The human
correlation under weak influence ($`r=0.740`$) was close to that of the
reasoning models; under strong influence, the human correlation
($`r=0.557`$) fell between the two class means. In the strong-influence
condition, correspondence was particularly weak in three non-reasoning
models: Gemma 3 4B ($`r=0.118`$), GPT-4o mini ($`r=0.149`$), and
Qwen3-Next 80B ($`r=0.151`$).

In sum, exposure to cumulative recommendation counts weakened but did
not eliminate the correspondence between popularity and intrinsic
appeal: songs favored under independent evaluation generally remained
more popular under social influence.

### C.4 Early movers predicted later popularity better than intrinsic appeal

Table [S2](#A2.T2 "Table S2 ‣ Correlation between early movers and later agents. ‣ B.6 Outcome measures ‣ Appendix B Materials and Methods ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
also reports the correlation between market shares among the first 30
agents in each influence world and market shares among all later agents
in the same world. Comparing this correlation with the correlation
between influence and independent worlds indicates whether later
popularity was better predicted by early movers or by intrinsic appeal.

Early-mover correlations were high in every LLM population, ranging from
0.758 to 0.979 under weak influence and from 0.792 to 0.998 under strong
influence. They exceeded the correlation with intrinsic appeal in 12 of
13 models under weak influence, with GPT-5 the exception (0.823 vs.
0.885), and in all 13 models under strong influence (two-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests across models, $`p<0.01`$ and $`p<0.001`$,
respectively). Mean early-mover correlations were similar in the two
classes (0.878 and 0.882 among reasoning models and 0.866 and 0.932
among non-reasoning models under weak and strong influence,
respectively), but the gap was not. Under strong influence, the
early-mover correlation exceeded the correlation with intrinsic appeal
by 0.19 on average among reasoning models and by 0.60 among
non-reasoning models (two-sided Mann–Whitney U test, $`p<0.05`$); under
weak influence, the gaps were 0.14 and 0.38, and the class difference
was not significant. The gap was largest in Gemma 3 4B under strong
influence, where the first 30 agents predicted later market shares
almost perfectly ($`r=0.998`$) while intrinsic appeal predicted them
weakly ($`r=0.118`$). In such populations, later agents largely
reproduced whatever ranking the first agents happened to establish.

Humans showed this pattern only under strong influence. Under weak
influence, the first 30 participants predicted later market shares less
well ($`r=0.316`$) than intrinsic appeal did ($`r=0.740`$), whereas
under strong influence the early-mover correlation (0.723) exceeded the
correlation with intrinsic appeal (0.557). This is consistent with the
human self-correction reported in
fig. [5](#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 3.4 Human cascades self-corrected; LLM cascades generally did not ‣ 3 Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades"):
when popularity did not determine list position, later human choices
tracked intrinsic appeal more closely than the choices of early movers.
Among LLMs, early movers outweighed intrinsic appeal under both levels
of influence.

### C.5 Position bias could provide an alternative explanation for the effects of strong influence

Language models are known to be sensitive to position in multiple-choice
tasks \[[Zheng et al., 2024](#bib.bib64), [Pezeshkpour and Hruschka,
2024](#bib.bib45)\], to use information unevenly across the input
context \[[Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib36)\], and to exhibit item-position
bias when ranking candidates for recommendation \[[Hou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib30)\]. Position bias is also well documented in human
choices from ranked lists, including eye-tracking and click-log studies
of search results \[[Joachims et al., 2005](#bib.bib33), [Craswell
et al., 2008](#bib.bib16)\]. In the independent and weak-influence
conditions, title order was randomized for each agent, so a preference
for early positions fell on different songs for different agents and
could not systematically favor any song. Under strong influence,
however, popularity determined position. We therefore examined the
possibility that first-stage selections in the strong-influence
condition reflected a content-independent preference for early list
positions rather than a response to popularity.

Random ordering in the independent condition provided a direct
diagnostic for position bias.
Fig. [S4](#A3.F4 "Figure S4 ‣ C.5 Position bias could provide an alternative explanation for the effects of strong influence ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
shows that selections were approximately uniform across list positions
for most models, although Gemma 3 4B and GPT-4o mini showed a modest
preference for early positions. The preference for early positions was
far greater among humans than among most LLMs. (The figure does not
report the positional distribution for humans in the weak-influence
condition because Music Lab used a multi-column grid, not directly
comparable to the single-column list presented to LLM agents.) A
plausible explanation is human cognitive limitations that discourage
full exploration of the entire list of 48 titles.

Because position bias is an attribute of the agents, who were randomly
allocated to all three conditions, the position bias observed in the
independent condition, which was low for most LLMs and higher for
humans, may generalize to both influence conditions. The conditions
differed in whether songs were ordered randomly or by popularity, but
used the same single-column list. This generalization could be tested
directly by listing songs in increasing order of popularity: a generic
preference for early positions would then favor the least popular songs,
whereas a response to popularity would favor songs at the bottom of the
list.

If this inference is supported, part of the concentration observed under
strong influence reflected position bias acting on a popularity-ordered
list rather than a response to recommendation counts, and this component
was much larger for humans than for LLM agents. Our measures would then
overstate human susceptibility to popularity relative to that of LLMs.
The weak-influence comparison, in which position was unrelated to
popularity for both populations, is not subject to this bias. Pending an
experiment that controls for differences in position bias, the evidence
in
fig. [S4](#A3.F4 "Figure S4 ‣ C.5 Position bias could provide an alternative explanation for the effects of strong influence ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
suggests that the reported human–LLM differences in susceptibility to
cascade failure under strong influence are more likely to be understated
than overstated.

If the inference is not supported, for example because displaying
recommendation counts increased LLM agents’ preference for early
positions, position bias could account for part of the strong-influence
effect among LLMs. It could not account for the weak-influence effect,
however: title order was randomized for each agent, yet every model
diverged significantly from its independent baseline and was more
unpredictable than under independent evaluation
(figs. [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 LLM susceptibility to herding ‣ 3 Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")
and
[3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2 Social feedback can turn herding behavior into a stampede ‣ 3 Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
Nor would it negate the importance of popularity-driven endogenous
feedback. On the contrary, path-dependent sensitivity to initial
recommendations means that even a small position bias could tip a
multi-agent system to converge arbitrarily on whatever appeared at the
top of the list early on.

### C.7 Robustness analyses

Two features of the experimental design could affect the magnitude of
social influence: sampling temperature and the number of alternative
songs each agent was allowed to consider for possible recommendation. We
therefore repeated the multiple-world experiment under alternative
settings for each parameter. Both robustness checks used the independent
and strong-influence conditions.

#### Robustness to sampling temperature.

The main experiment used a sampling temperature of 1.0 for non-reasoning
models. Because temperature controls sampling variability, the observed
divergence and path dependence could depend on this inference setting.
We therefore repeated the experiment at temperature 0.7 for all eight
non-reasoning models
(fig. [S7](#A3.F7 "Figure S7 ‣ Robustness to sampling temperature. ‣ C.7 Robustness analyses ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Results ‣ LLM Multi-Agent Systems Are Vulnerable to Herding and Runaway Cascades")).
We restricted this manipulation to non-reasoning models because the
reasoning models used model-specific reasoning controls, making
temperature an inconsistent manipulation.

Lowering the temperature did not attenuate overall susceptibility to
social influence. Mean KL divergence across the eight models was 1.62 at
temperature 1.0 and 1.75 at temperature 0.7, increasing at the lower
temperature in seven of eight models. Model ordering by KL divergence
was identical across temperatures (Spearman $`\rho=1.00`$).
Influence-world unpredictability also remained high, increasing from
$`U=0.0292`$ to 0.0306. Independent-condition unpredictability was
nearly unchanged (0.00288 versus 0.00284).

Increasing the assessment set attenuated but did not eliminate
divergence. Mean KL divergence decreased from 1.19 with four selections
to 0.75 with 12, with a reduction in all 13 models. Model ordering was
strongly preserved across selection sizes (Spearman $`\rho=0.95`$), as
was the difference between model classes: with 12 selections, mean KL
divergence was 0.31 among reasoning models and 1.03 among non-reasoning
models.

Influence-world unpredictability also decreased, but only slightly, from
$`U=0.0239`$ to $`U=0.0201`$, with reductions in 11 of 13 models and
strongly preserved model ordering (Spearman $`\rho=0.96`$).
Independent-condition unpredictability also decreased, from 0.00278 to
0.00156. With 12 selections, the lowest value observed in an influence
condition ($`U=0.0048`$) was approximately 2.6 times the highest value
observed in the independent condition ($`U=0.0019`$). Relative to each
model’s corresponding independent baseline, mean
$`U_{\mathrm{inf}}/U_{\mathrm{ind}}`$ was 8.6 with four selections and
13.0 with 12 selections.

### C.8 Limitations and directions for future work

Several questions remain open. First, agents observed accumulated
recommendation counts without discussing choices or retaining a shared
conversational history. Future work could examine whether agents that
exchange arguments, remember earlier discussions, reconsider judgments,
or incentivize conformity amplify or attenuate the dynamics of
cumulative advantage.

Second, each population consisted of a single model, with heterogeneity
introduced through personas. Future work could examine further increases
in agent heterogeneity, by mixing together models of differing
capability, and by including both humans and AI agents in each world.

AI-assisted scientific peer review is another important application,
where agents recommend academic papers instead of songs. Future
experiments could test whether exposure to earlier recommendations
improves the quality of evaluations or produces self-reinforcing
cascades of acceptance and rejection.

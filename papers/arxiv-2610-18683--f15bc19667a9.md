###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as the primary paradigm for
optimizing Large Language Model (LLM) agents, yet efficient critic-free
algorithms (e.g., GRPO) suffer from a fundamental uniform credit
assignment dilemma that broadcasts trajectory-level advantages equally
to all actions. Our analysis reveals that this uniformity inherently
reinforces structurally unreasonable actions in preferred trajectories
and ignores each action’s downstream deviations in unpreferred ones. To
resolve this without introducing critic overhead, we propose
Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS), a plug-and-play framework that
leverages a dynamically evolving Agentic Reference Graph (ARG) to
achieve fine-grained credit assignment via Masking Unreasonable
Incentives (MUI) and Asymmetric Penalty Redistribution (APR). Extensive
experiments across Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
demonstrate that ProAS universally elevates mainstream group-based
baselines to new state-of-the-art performance, achieving peak success
rates of 91.9% and 94.3% on ALFWorld, alongside 66.7% and 70.8% on
WebShop, respectively. Furthermore, it accelerates training convergence
by up to $`2.44\times`$ while maintaining strong robustness against
imperfect or distilled ARGs. Codes:
[https://github.com/LINs-lab/ProAS](https://github.com/LINs-lab/ProAS).

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous
agents that make sequential decisions through interactions with external
environments. Their planning and reasoning capabilities have enabled
applications in software engineering (?), personal assistance (?), and
graphical user interface navigation (?).

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as the primary paradigm for
elevating the agentic capacity of LLMs (??). During RL rollouts, agents
generate trajectories composed of interleaved environment states and
actions, which introduces a fundamental credit assignment challenge:

![](arxiv-2610-18683--f15bc19667a9.figures/inline-1.svg)

Actor-critic algorithms (e.g., PPO (?)) address this by learning a
separate critic, which introduces substantial computational overhead and
optimization instability in long-horizon settings (??). Conversely,
critic-free algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO) (?) offer computational efficiency by broadcasting a
trajectory-level advantage uniformly to every action (?). However, this
inherently treats all intra-trajectory actions identically, completely
overlooking their critical differences in local validity, task
contribution, and downstream influence. As detailed in § 3.2, we term
this structural limitation the uniform credit assignment dilemma.

To systematically understand the uniform credit assignment dilemma, we
conduct a fine-grained action-intervention analysis (§ 4.2). Our
findings reveal a dual suboptimality. First, in preferred trajectories
with positive advantages, uniform credit inadvertently reinforces
structurally invalid actions (i.e., unreasonable actions) that pollute
the context history and impede the agent from reaching success. Second,
in unpreferred trajectories with negative advantages, uniform
penalization ignores the autoregressive nature of LLMs, where the later
deviations may drive from earlier actions. Consequently, they suppress
all actions equally but arbitrarily without considering their actual
downstream influence.

To resolve this dilemma without introducing critic overhead, we propose
Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS) in § 4.3. ProAS leverages an
Agentic Reference Graph (ARG, mentioned in § 4.1), which abstracts task
progression into dynamically evolving sub-goals to formally annotate
whether an action is _reasonable_ (structurally valid) or
_progress-advancing_ (driving task completion). Using these structural
annotations, ProAS asymmetrically reshapes trajectory-level advantages
in Eq. 7: (1) Masking Unreasonable Incentives (MUI) zeros out the local
advantages of _unreasonable_ actions in preferred trajectories to
prevent their inadvertent reinforcement. (2) Asymmetric Penalty
Redistribution (APR) utilizes Future-Drift (Eq. 6), the inclusive count
of downstream _non-advancing_ actions, to assign proportionally heavier
penalties to actions triggering severe downstream stagnation.

Extensive experiments in § 6 on ALFWorld (?) and WebShop (?) using
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (?) validate ProAS as a
versatile, plug-and-play enhancer. It universally elevates mainstream
group-based RL baselines (GRPO (?), DAPO (?), GiGPO (?)), boosting
success rates by up to 16.7 points (e.g., GRPO on 1.5B ALFWorld) and
accelerating training convergence by $`1.12\times`$ to $`2.44\times`$.
On top of that, this orthogonal enhancement propels these standard
algorithms to establish new state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance,
consistently eclipsing the strong segment-level baseline, MiGPO (?).
Notably, GiGPO+ProAS outperforms MiGPO by a massive 13.1 points on
WebShop with 1.5B model and secures the highest overall success rate of
94.3% on ALFWorld with 7B model. Furthermore, comprehensive ablations
(§ 6.2) show that ProAS’s core mechanisms are both synergistic and
superior to alternative advantage shaping variants, and confirm ProAS’s
robust efficacy against incomplete or distilled ARGs, underscoring the
necessity of ARG’s online evolution mechanism.

In summary, our primary contributions are:

- •
  Problem Identification: We expose the uniform credit assignment
  dilemma in critic-free agentic RL, demonstrating how uniform
  advantages inadvertently reinforce contextual noise in preferred
  trajectories and ignore the downstream influence of actions in
  unpreferred ones.
- •
  Algorithmic Innovation: We propose Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping
  (ProAS), a plug-and-play framework. Guided by a dynamically evolving
  Agentic Reference Graph (ARG), ProAS achieves fine-grained,
  critic-free credit assignment via Masking Unreasonable Incentives
  (MUI) and Asymmetric Penalty Redistribution (APR).
- •
  Empirical Superiority: ProAS acts as a universal enhancer across
  multiple model scales and environments. It significantly accelerates
  training convergence, propels standard group-based algorithms to
  surpass strong SOTA baselines (e.g., MiGPO), and exhibits high
  robustness against imperfect and distilled reference guidance.

## 2 Related Works

Multi-Turn Agentic Tasks. Large Language Model (LLM) agents are
increasingly deployed to solve complex real-world tasks (?). Given the
intricacies of these environments, agents must engage in multi-turn
interactions, continuously accumulating contextual knowledge and making
sequential decisions to drive task completion. To facilitate the
training and evaluation of such agents, the community has introduced
various simulated environments. Examples include ALFWorld (?) for
embodied household tasks, WebShop (?) for web navigation, AppWorld (?)
for application development, and $`\tau^{2}`$-Bench (?) for evaluating
dynamic conversational abilities in domains like aviation and retail.

Rather than constructing new environments, our work focuses on
developing more efficient and effective training paradigms tailored for
existing multi-turn agentic tasks.

RL for LLM Agents. Critic-free RL like GRPO (?) is widely adopted for
LLM agents (???), but suffers from sparse-reward advantage
vanishment (?) and coarse trajectory-level credit assignment (?). While
DAPO (?) and dense-reward methods (???) mitigate this vanishment, their
credit assignment remains trajectory-bound. To differentiate
intra-trajectory actions, GiGPO (?) and MiGPO (?) compute relative
advantages by matching anchor states or milestones across rollouts.

In this work, we systematically expose the flaws of uniform credit
assignment in both preferred and unpreferred trajectories, addressing
them through advantage shaping, which serves as a plug-and-play module
for existing critic-free RL algorithms.

## 3 Preliminary

### 3.1 Decision Process of LLM Agents

Given a task context $`\mathbf{x}\sim p(X)`$, an LLM agent aims to solve
the task within a maximum horizon of $`T`$ steps. At each step
$`t\in[1,T]`$, the agent receives an immediate environment state
$`s_{t}`$. Governed by a parameterized policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$, the
agent samples an action $`a_{t}`$ from the admissible action space
$`\mathcal{A}`$ (?), conditioned on the task context $`\mathbf{x}`$, the
interaction history $`h_{t-1}=\{(s_{i},a_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t-1}`$, and the
current state $`s_{t}`$. Formally, this decision-making process is
defined as:

```math
a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x},h_{t-1},s_{t}),\quad a_{t}\in\mathcal{A},\quad t\in[1,T] \tag{1}
```

In practice, some harnessing wrappers (?) often provide partial
observations or integrate summarized historical information (?) (denoted
as $`\hat{h}_{t-1}`$) into the context prior to the current state
$`s_{t}`$.

### 3.2 GRPO

GRPO (?) utilizes a group-level normalization strategy to estimate the
advantage for assessing the action value. In GRPO, given a task
$`\mathbf{x}`$, the old policy $`\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}`$ generates
a group of $`G`$ rollout trajectories $`\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$, each
evaluated by a verifiable outcome reward $`R(\tau_{i})`$. The
trajectory-level advantage $`A_{i}`$ for $`\tau_{i}`$ is computed by
normalizing the rewards within the group:

```math
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A_{i}}=\frac{R(\tau_{i})-\mu}{\sigma+\epsilon} \tag{2}
```

where $`\mu`$ and $`\sigma`$ denote the mean and standard deviation of
the group rewards $`\{R(\tau_{i})\}_{i=1}^{G}`$, and $`\epsilon`$ is a
small constant ensuring numerical stability. Excluding entropy and KL
regularization terms for simplicity, GRPO optimizes the expected
trajectory advantage as follows:

```math
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\,\mathbf{x},\;\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\tau_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_{i}|}\min\!\left(\rho_{i,t}(\theta){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{A}_{i}},\operatorname{clip}\big(\rho_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\big)\,{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{A}_{i}}\right)\right] \tag{3}
```

where $`|\tau_{i}|`$ is the trajectory length (in tokens), and
$`y_{i,t}`$ denotes the $`t`$-th generated token within $`\tau_{i}`$.
The importance ratio
$`\rho_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\mathbf{x},y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\mathbf{x},y_{i,<t})}`$
is used in a clipped surrogate with bounds
$`[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]`$ to limit improvements from favorable
ratio changes. Differentiating this surrogate gives (see Appx. A):

```math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\tau_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_{i}|}\underbrace{\textbf{1}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{i,t}\cdot{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{A}_{i}}\cdot\rho_{i,t}(\theta)\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\mathbf{x},y_{i,<t})}_{\text{gradient at step }(i,t)}\right] \tag{4}
```

where $`\textbf{1}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{i,t}`$ indicates whether the
surrogate’s gradient is active: it is zero when $`A_{i}>0`$ and
$`\rho_{i,t}>1+\varepsilon`$, or when $`A_{i}<0`$ and
$`\rho_{i,t}<1-\varepsilon`$, and one otherwise, with the boundary
convention specified in Appx. A.

Credit Assignment of GRPO. As shown in Eq. 4, the per-step gradient is
directly scaled by the credit multiplier
$`\textbf{1}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{i,t}\cdot{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{A}_{i}}\cdot\rho_{i,t}(\theta)`$.
Under the on-policy setting (where $`\rho_{i,t}(\theta)\equiv 1`$ and
thus $`\textbf{1}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{i,t}\equiv 1`$), this multiplier
strictly reduces to the trajectory-level advantage
$`{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{A}_{i}}`$. Consequently, all steps within the same
trajectory are indiscriminately updated by the identical scalar,
regardless of their individual difference. We term this systematic
limitation the dilemma of uniform credit assignment.

## 4 Methodology

Since RL fundamentally optimizes policies by incentivizing or
suppressing action likelihoods, tailored credit assignment requires
understanding both the intrinsic properties of each step (e.g.,
reasonableness and progress advancement) and how modulating them
dictates the final outcome. To quantify these step-level properties,
§ 4.1 constructs an Agentic Reference Graph (ARG). Guided by an
intervention study probing these modulation effects (§ 4.2), § 4.3
introduces Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS), a lightweight
framework designed to resolve the uniform credit dilemma via asymmetric
advantage shaping.

unreasonable actions. From an identical state, the counterpart injected

### 4.1 Agentic Reference Graph

As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), ARG abstracts the task-solving progression
into $`M`$ ordered sub-goals using pre-collected successful
trajectories. Below, we formalize its construction and online evolution,
followed by a two-tier annotation scheme to categorize step-level
actions.

Construction and Formalization. To ensure reliable action assessment, we
enforce strict graph purity by pruning invalid transitions and
self-loops (example in Appx. D.3). Furthermore, while sub-goals follow a
strict sequence, their internal atomic requirements are often
permutation-invariant (example in Appx. D.4). By modeling each
sub-goal’s completion as a state equivalence class, we seamlessly unify
these diverse execution paths. Formally, the ARG is defined as a
Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) $`\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})`$:

###### Definition 4.1 (Sub-goals as Vertices).

The vertex set $`\mathcal{V}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}\textbf{g}_{m}`$ comprises
$`M`$ sub-goals in sequential order. Each sub-goal
$`\textbf{g}_{m}=\{s_{m,1},s_{m,2},\dots\}`$ is an unordered set of
permutation-invariant, valid states, strictly excluding error-induced
states and redundant self-loops.

###### Definition 4.2 (Actions as Edges).

The edge set
$`\mathcal{E}\subseteq\bigcup_{m=1}^{M-1}(\textbf{g}_{m}\times\mathcal{A}_{m}\times\textbf{g}_{m+1})`$
captures deterministic transitions $`(s,a,s^{\prime})`$ between
consecutive sub-goals under admissible actions $`a\in\mathcal{A}_{m}`$.

Dynamic Evolution. To capture the diverse spectrum of valid solutions,
the ARG evolves dynamically. Upon verifying a novel successful
trajectory at the rollout stage of training iteration $`k`$, we extract
its new states ($`\Delta\textbf{g}_{m}`$) and actions
($`\Delta\mathcal{E}`$) to update the current graph
$`\mathcal{G}^{(k)}`$ via strict union:

```math
\textbf{g}_{m}^{(k+1)}=\textbf{g}_{m}^{(k)}\cup\Delta\textbf{g}_{m}\quad(1\leq m\leq M),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{E}^{(k)}\cup\Delta\mathcal{E} \tag{5}
```

These discrete updates strictly adhere to the purity principles,
providing an increasingly comprehensive reference for action attribution
over time (a raw-to-merged example is provided in Appx. D.3).

Action Attribution. We evaluate any rollout trajectory
$`\tau_{i}=\{(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})\}_{t=1}^{|\tau_{i}|}`$ generated at
iteration $`k`$ against $`\mathcal{G}^{(k)}`$ to formally annotate each
step $`t`$ using two binary criteria:

(1) Reasonableness ($`\text{Rea}_{t}`$): Evaluates if an action forms a
valid ARG transition:
$`\text{Rea}_{t}=\mathbb{I}[(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})\in\mathcal{E}^{(k)}]`$.
While it filters explicit errors, it ignores valid but redundant loops.

(2) Progress-Advancement ($`\text{PA}_{t}`$): Strictly quantifies task
progression. An action is progress-advancing ($`\text{PA}_{t}=1`$) if it
induces a valid ARG state $`s_{t+1}\in\mathcal{V}^{(k)}`$ while avoiding
the historical trajectory
$`\mathcal{S}_{\leq t}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{t}\}`$:
$`\text{PA}_{t}=\text{Rea}_{t}\cdot\mathbb{I}\big[s_{t+1}\in\mathcal{V}^{(k)}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\leq t}\big]`$

Thus, progress-advancing actions are a strict subset of reasonable ones
($`\text{PA}_{t}`$ = 1 $`\implies`$ $`\text{Rea}_{t}`$ = 1). Conversely,
non-advancing actions ($`\text{PA}_{t}=0`$) encapsulate both explicit
errors and structurally valid but stagnant steps.

### 4.2 Action-Intervention Analysis

By leveraging the _reasonableness_ and _progress-advancement_
annotations to assess fine-grained action quality, our pilot study
reveals how individual steps dictate outcomes, motivating our solution
to the uniform credit assignment dilemma.

Probing Unreasonable Actions. Unreasonable actions hinder task progress
by polluting the context history and disrupting subsequent
decisions (?). Notably, they constitute a substantial proportion of
successful GRPO trajectories (41.6% in ALFWorld, 39.6% in WebShop) of
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, where they are inadvertently reinforced by the
uniform credit applied to preferred rollouts. To probe their impact, we
select a clean ALFWorld trajectory (details in Appx. D.1) and sample 50
continuations, $`\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{50}`$, from a fixed state under two
context conditions. As illustrated in Fig. 1(b), the _clean control_
retains the original history, whereas the _noise-injected_ condition
prepends a four-step unreasonable segment. Despite sharing the identical
underlying environment state, this visible context noise degrades the
Expected Success Rate (ESR) from 82% to 58%.

![](arxiv-2610-18683--f15bc19667a9.figures/inline-2.svg)

Suppressing Actions with Varying Future-Drift. Conversely, non-advancing
(NA) actions are heavily concentrated in failed GRPO trajectories (93.1%
in ALFWorld, 76.1% in WebShop), where they are suppressed uniformly.
Given the autoregressive nature of LLMs, late-stage NA actions are often
inevitable consequences of an already polluted context rather than
making the mistake independently. Therefore, uniformly suppressing all
actions within a failed trajectory is inherently unfair. To quantify the
downstream deviation, we define Future-Drift ($`\mathcal{D}_{i,t}`$,
short as FD) as the inclusive count of downstream NA actions from step
$`t`$ in the trajectory $`\tau_{i}`$, with $`|\tau_{i}|`$ steps:

```math
\mathcal{D}_{i,t}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{|\tau_{i}|}\big(1-\text{PA}_{i,t^{\prime}}\big) \tag{6}
```

To assess whether actions with higher Future-Drift warrant heavier
penalization, we perform a targeted suppression intervention on a failed
ALFWorld trajectory generated by Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct as shown in
Fig. 1(c). By preserving the trajectory prefix up to specific steps,
forbidding the original action, and resampling 50 continuations, we
measure the recovered ESR. Suppressing the actions with FD values of 47,
23, 11, and 4 yields ESRs of 24%, 16%, 10%, and 0%, respectively (case
details in Appx. D.2). In this case, the higher the suppressed action’s
Future-Drift, the more likely the agent is to recover and complete the
task.

### 4.3 Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping

Building upon the empirical insights from § 4.2, we propose the
Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping (ProAS) framework, aiming to resolve
the uniform credit dilemma by asymmetrically reshaping the
trajectory-level advantage $`A_{i}`$ into a step-wise, progress-aware
signal $`\tilde{A}_{i,t}`$:

```math
\tilde{A}_{i,t}=\begin{cases}\text{Rea}_{t}\cdot A_{i},&\text{if }A_{i}>0,\\
\frac{\mathcal{D}_{i,t}}{\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{i}+\epsilon}\cdot A_{i},&\text{if }A_{i}<0\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T},\\
A_{i},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases} \tag{7}
```

where $`\mathcal{T}`$ denotes the steps sharing the uniform advantage,
$`\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{i}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{D}_{i,t^{\prime}}`$
is the average Future-Drift, and $`\epsilon`$ ensures numerical
stability. This formulation branches into two mechanisms: (1) Masking
Unreasonable Incentives (MUI): Aligning with Insight 1, it zeros out the
local advantages of invalid actions ($`\text{Rea}_{t}=0`$) in preferred
trajectories ($`A_{i}>0`$, usually successful) to prevent inadvertent
reinforcement. (2) Asymmetric Penalty Redistribution (APR): Reflecting
Insight 2, it leverages normalized Future-Drift to assign proportionally
heavier penalties to early pivotal actions in unpreferred trajectories
($`A_{i}<0`$, usually failed), rectifying the unfairness of uniform
suppression.

## 5 Experimental Setup

To empirically validate the proposed ProAS framework, we conduct
extensive experiments across complex, long-horizon agentic environments.
Our experiments are structured to answer the following core research
questions:

RQ1: Performance & Efficiency (§ 6). Can ProAS consistently elevate task
success rates and accelerate training convergence across different model
scales under different environments?

RQ2: Algorithm Compatibility (§ 6). Does ProAS seamlessly integrate with
mainstream group-based RL baselines (e.g., GRPO, DAPO, GiGPO) as a
robust plug-and-play module?

RQ3: Advantage Shaping Design (§ 6.2.1). Are MUI and APR each
individually necessary and mutually synergistic, and does ProAS’s
Future-Drift-based redistribution outperform a simpler masking
heuristic?

RQ4: Robustness to ARG (§ 6.2.2). Does ProAS remain effective when the
reference ARG is randomized, incomplete, static (non-evolving), or
distilled from a teacher model?

### 5.1 Models and Data

Models. Our experiments are conducted across two different model scales:
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (?), which are denoted as
1.5B and 7B for brevity.

Data. We train and validate on ALFWorld (?) and WebShop (?), two
classical long-horizon multi-turn agentic environments (details in
Appx. C). ALFWorld is a text-based embodied benchmark over six household
task categories (Pick, Look, Clean, Heat, Cool, Pick2, details in
Table. 5). Our 150-step training comprises 1777/1767/1784 unique
executable tasks of seed 0/1/2 for training and 128 for validation.
WebShop (Table 6) is an HTML-based online shopping environment, with
2015/2011/2036 tasks of seed 0/1/2 used for training and 128 for
validation.

#### 5.1.1 Evaluation Metrics

Performance Metrics. For ALFWorld, we report the average Success Rate
(SR) across the entire validation set, alongside the per-category
average SR. For WebShop, we report both the overall average SR and the
Task Score (TS). TS is a checklist metric on a scale of 0 to 100: it
averages the satisfaction of the requested attributes, options, and
price constraint, and then scales this score by how well the purchased
product matches the requested product type.

Efficiency Metrics. We quantify training efficiency via speedup
$`\text{Spd@}K=N_{\text{base}\to K}/N_{\text{ProAS}\to K}`$, which is
defined as the ratio of RL training steps required by the baseline
versus ProAS to first reach a target $`K`$ of SR. Reflecting task
difficulties, we set $`K=70\%`$ for ALFWorld and $`K=50\%`$ for WebShop.

#### 5.1.2 Implementation Details

We conduct experiments upon Verl-Agent (?), training all models for 150
RL steps. We validate the policy every 10 RL steps and report metrics
from the checkpoint achieving the highest SR. Unless otherwise
specified, all experiments are conducted across three random seeds (0,
1, and 2), reporting the mean and sample standard deviation (std). See
details in Appx. B.

ARG Initialization. Unless otherwise specified, we initialize the ARG in
ALFWorld using its official crowdsourced annotations. Given the strictly
sequential nature of tasks in ALFWorld, we formulate each individual
step as a distinct sub-goal. For WebShop, the ARG is initialized using
its officially annotated sub-goals alongside their corresponding states
and actions.

#### 5.1.3 Baselines

We consider baselines of different paradigms: (1) Proprietary models:
GPT-5.6-Sol (?), the state-of-the-art model from OpenAI. We apply the
reasoning effort as high, and evaluate it with ReAct prompting (?). (2)
Prompting: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, the starting
point to be trained. (3) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Tuning the agent
by imitating curated turn-level oracle trajectories from the same
training tasks as RL baselines (Details in Appx. B.3). (4) RL: the
classical actor-crtic algorithm PPO (?); mainstream group-based RL
algorithms GRPO (?), DAPO (?), GiGPO (?); the state-of-the-art (SOTA)
agentic RL method MiGPO (?) with segment-level credit assignment.

## 6 Results and Analysis

### 6.1 Main Results

Table 1 summarizes the main evaluation results on ALFWorld and WebShop.
We structure our analysis to directly address our research questions
regarding performance, efficiency (RQ1), and algorithm compatibility
(RQ2).

Baseline Comparisons. We first contrast ProAS against established
learning paradigms. While both SFT and ProAS leverage expert annotations
as supervisory signals, ProAS abstracts them into ARGs to structurally
guide RL, significantly outperforming SFT’s direct behavior imitation.
Specifically, GiGPO+ProAS achieves 91.9% and 94.3% SR on the 1.5B and 7B
ALFWorld benchmarks, respectively, surpassing SFT by 12.2 and 14.4
points. Furthermore, ProAS consistently eclipses PPO, a conventional
critic-based RL algorithm, across all settings. ProAS variants outpace
PPO by 5.5 to 18.7 points in ALFWorld SR, alongside steady improvements
of 4.1 to 12.2 points in WebShop TS and 3.4 to 11.5 points in WebShop
SR. Ultimately, these results confirm that explicitly integrating
ARG-derived progress signals into RL is vastly superior to both passive
trajectory imitation and conventional critic-based credit assignment.

### 6.2 Ablation Study

We ablate the core components of ProAS on the 1.5B model using GRPO as
the base algorithm. § 6.2.1 ablates our advantage shaping mechanisms,
demonstrating that disabling any single technique lowers the overall
success rate and disrupts their synergy. Subsequently, § 6.2.2 relaxes
the ARG construction constraints to investigate ProAS’s robustness
against reference quality and origin, while highlighting the critical
necessity of the online dynamic evolution mechanism.

#### 6.2.1 Advantage Shaping Ablation

To isolate the contributions of our specific advantage shaping
interventions, we evaluate five ProAS variants: PR (Progress-aware
Reward), ProAS₋, ProAS₊, ProAS$`{}_{\text{msk}\pm}`$ and
ProAS$`{}_{\text{suffix}}`$, as shown in Table 2(a).

First, we contrast advantage shaping against reward shaping. PR
incorporates ARG progress annotations directly as an auxiliary
step-level reward term rather than reshaping the advantage, as Eq. 14.
PR underperforms ProAS by 14.1 and 3.4 SR points on ALFWorld and
WebShop, respectively. This confirms that explicitly modulating
trajectory-level advantages is a superior mechanism for injecting
structural progress signals compared to the denser reward design in our
studies.

Next, we ablate individual core components, MUI and APR. ProAS₋ disables
MUI on preferred trajectories ($`A_{i}>0`$), incurring Success Rate (SR)
drops of 15.4 and 0.8 points on ALFWorld and WebShop, respectively;
ProAS₊ disables APR on unpreferred ones ($`A_{i}<0`$), causing a
3.7-point SR drop on ALFWorld and a 1.2-point Task Score (TS) drop on
WebShop. These results reveal complementary roles for MUI and APR: MUI
prevents reinforcement of unreasonable actions in positively advantaged
trajectories, whereas APR differentiates penalties for actions with
different Future-Drift in negatively advantaged trajectories. Although
their individual benefits vary by environment and metric, both
components contribute to ProAS’s overall effectiveness and are jointly
essential to its design.

Finally, we evaluate two simpler heuristics for negative trajectories
($`A_{i}<0`$). ProAS$`{}_{\text{msk}\pm}`$ preserves MUI but replaces
APR by naively masking reasonable actions ($`\text{Rea}_{t}=1`$) to
avoid their suppression, which underperforms ProAS by 14.1 and 0.8 SR
points on ALFWorld and WebShop. ProAS$`{}_{\text{suffix}}`$ replaces the
Future-Drift $`\mathcal{D}_{i,t}`$ in Eq.7 with a naive suffix length
($`\mathcal{D}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{suffix}}=|\tau_{i}|-t+1`$), trailing ProAS
by 5.5 and 1.6 SR points. Thus, Future-Drift goes beyond the naive
masking and simple positional heuristics. It quantifies each step’s
relative responsibility for the penalty, namely its negative credit, and
redistributes the penalty accordingly, which drives the superior
performance.

Notably, all three variants still outperform native GRPO on the
aggregate metrics, highlighting the fundamental necessity of leveraging
progress signals to resolve the uniform credit assignment dilemma.

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we expose the uniform credit assignment dilemma in
critic-free agentic RL, where uniform advantages reinforce contextual
noise in preferred trajectories ($`A_{i}>0`$) and ignore the downstream
deviation of actions in unpreferred ones ($`A_{i}<0`$). To address this
without critic overhead, we proposed Progress-Aware Advantage Shaping
(ProAS), which leverages a dynamically evolving Agentic Reference Graph
(ARG) to achieve fine-grained credit assignment via Masking Unreasonable
Incentives (MUI) and Asymmetric Penalty Redistribution (APR). Extensive
evaluations across multiple model scales and environments confirm ProAS
as a robust, universal enhancer that accelerates convergence and propels
mainstream baselines to new state-of-the-art performance. The ablation
study further confirms MUI-APR synergy, necessity of ARG evolution and
robustness under incomplete or distilled ARGs. Together, these results
establish a highly efficient, scalable pathway for elevating autonomous
language agents.

## Appendix A Derivation of the GRPO Policy Gradient

We derive Eq. 4 from the clipped surrogate in Eq. 3, omitting entropy
and KL regularization as in the main text. During each policy update,
the sampling policy $`\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}`$, sampled
trajectories, and group-normalized advantages $`A_{i}`$ are fixed; in
particular, $`\nabla_{\theta}A_{i}=0`$. Let
$`h_{i,t}=(\mathbf{x},y_{i,<t})`$ denote the conditioning context and
assume the old policy assigns positive probability to each sampled
token.

is

```math

```

```math
\displaystyle=\begin{cases}A_{i}\min(\rho,1+\varepsilon),&A_{i}>0,\\
A_{i}\max(\rho,1-\varepsilon),&A_{i}<0,\\
0,&A_{i}=0.\end{cases}
```

Thus, the surrogate is flat only when $`A_{i}>0`$ and
$`\rho>1+\varepsilon`$, or when $`A_{i}<0`$ and $`\rho<1-\varepsilon`$.
A ratio outside the clipping interval does not, by itself, imply zero
gradient: the gradient remains active when the ratio moves in the
unfavorable direction. Away from the clipping boundaries, define

```math
\textbf{1}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{i,t}=\begin{cases}0,&A_{i}>0\ \text{and}\ \rho_{i,t}>1+\varepsilon,\\
0,&A_{i}<0\ \text{and}\ \rho_{i,t}<1-\varepsilon,\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} \tag{9}
```

At a clipping kink, we adopt the derivative of the active linear branch
(indicator $`1`$), a valid generalized-gradient convention. For
$`A_{i}=0`$, the gradient is zero regardless of the indicator.

##### Importance-ratio derivative.

Since the denominator is fixed, the log-derivative identity gives

```math
\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\rho_{i,t}(\theta) \displaystyle=\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})} \tag{10}
```

```math
\displaystyle=\rho_{i,t}(\theta)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t}).
```

Applying the chain rule to Eq. 8 therefore yields

```math
\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{i,t}(\theta)=\textbf{1}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{i,t}A_{i}\rho_{i,t}(\theta)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t}). \tag{11}
```

##### Group-level gradient.

Under the usual conditions for exchanging differentiation and
expectation, the fixed sampling distribution introduces no additional
derivative term. Averaging the token-level derivatives gives

```math
\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta) \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\tau_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_{i}|}\textbf{1}^{\mathrm{clip}}_{i,t}A_{i}\rho_{i,t}(\theta) \tag{12}
```

```math
\displaystyle\cdot\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})\Bigg],
```

which recovers Eq. 4. At $`\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}`$,
$`\rho_{i,t}=1`$ and the clipping indicator is $`1`$ for
$`\varepsilon>0`$. Consequently, all tokens in a trajectory share the
same advantage multiplier $`A_{i}`$ (and trajectory normalization
$`1/|\tau_{i}|`$), although their score-function gradients can differ.
This is the uniform credit-assignment property discussed in Sec. 3.2.

## Appendix B Further Implementation Details

### B.1 Training Framework

For ALFWorld and WebShop, we adopt the Verl-Agent (?) framework. To
ensure robustness and reproducibility, we sample and freeze three
distinct training sets with environment seeds 0, 1, and 2 for
independent and repeated training. For fair evaluation, all resulting
model checkpoints are assessed on a unified, static validation set of
128 tasks. This validation set is strictly disjoint from the training
data, rigorously preventing any potential data leakage. Validation uses
a temperature of $`0.4`$ in both ALFWorld and WebShop.

RL and SFT share the same turn-level input format illustrated in
Tables 11 and 12: each prompt contains the task instruction, current
observation, admissible actions, and the visible interaction history
from the two most recent observation-action turns. The only difference
between SFT and RL data is the assistant response, which is curated
offline for SFT but generated online for RL.

All experiments are conducted on GPUs. For ALFWorld and WebShop, each
1.5B model run uses 2 GPUs, while each 7B model run uses 4 GPUs.

### B.2 RL Configuration

General. We follow the standard Verl-Agent (?) configuration and use the
same settings across all RL methods unless otherwise specified. Models
are optimized with AdamW using a learning rate of $`10^{-6}`$, weight
decay $`0.01`$, and gradient clipping at $`1.0`$. We use a PPO clipping
ratio of $`0.2`$, an entropy coefficient of $`0.001`$, a KL-loss
coefficient of $`0.01`$, and one policy-update epoch per rollout batch.
At each RL iteration, we sample 16 prompts and generate $`G=8`$
trajectories per prompt with temperature $`1.0`$ and top-$`p=1.0`$.

For ALFWorld, we set the maximum prompt and response lengths to 2,048
and 512 tokens, respectively, cap each episode at 50 steps, and use a
PPO mini-batch size of 256. For WebShop, the corresponding settings are
4,096 prompt tokens, 512 response tokens, 15 steps, and a PPO mini-batch
size of 64.

Reward. Across all runs, we use the same rule-based sparse outcome
reward $`R_{\text{o}}`$ in both environments: a successful trajectory
receives $`10`$, whereas an unsuccessful trajectory receives $`0`$. The
$`R_{o}`$ is broadcasted into each step in the trajectory. Each invalid
action additionally incurs a penalty of $`-0.1`$. Specifically, the
response of a step is marked invalid ($`\mathbb{I}[\text{invalid}]=1`$)
if it omits a complete \<think\>…\</think\> or \<action\>…\</action\>
block, or contains Chinese characters, otherwise, the response is valid
($`\mathbb{I}[\text{invalid}]=0`$). Consequently, the reward for step
$`t^{\prime}`$ in trajectory $`\tau_{i}`$ is given by:

```math
R_{i,t^{\prime}}=R_{\text{o}_{i}}-0.1\cdot\mathbb{I}[\text{invalid}_{i,t^{\prime}}] \tag{13}
```

For progress-aware reward (PR) mentioned in § 6.2.1, we add the
progress-aware reward term
$`R_{\text{p}_{i,t^{\prime}}}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{PA}_{i,t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_{i}|}\mathrm{PA}_{i,t}}`$
for the reward of each step:

```math
R_{i,t^{\prime}}=R_{o_{i}}-0.1\cdot\mathbb{I}[\mathrm{invalid}_{i,t^{\prime}}]+0.5\cdot R_{\text{p}_{i,t^{\prime}}} \tag{14}
```

This design incorporates the progress-advancing information obtained
from ARG into the reward signal.

PPO. For PPO (?), we estimate advantages using generalized advantage
estimation (GAE) with discount factor $`\gamma=1.0`$ and trace-decay
parameter $`\lambda=1.0`$. We train a separate critic with AdamW using a
learning rate of $`10^{-5}`$ and weight decay $`0.01`$, without a
critic-only warmup phase. Since PPO does not require multiple rollouts
per task for advantage estimation, we sample one rollout per task
($`G=1`$) and use a rollout batch of 128 tasks. This matches the 128
sampled trajectories per RL iteration used by the critic-free methods,
which sample 8 rollouts for each of 16 tasks. The policy learning rate,
regularization, and rollout sampling settings follow the shared
configuration above.

DAPO. For DAPO (?), we use asymmetric PPO clipping with lower and upper
ratios of $`0.2`$ and $`0.28`$, respectively, and a dual-clip
coefficient of $`10.0`$. We disable the actor KL loss and entropy
regularization, use token-mean loss aggregation, set the AdamW weight
decay to $`0.1`$ with 10 learning-rate warmup steps, and disable group
filtering.

GiGPO. For GiGPO (?), we set the temporal discount factor to
$`\gamma=0.95`$ and the step-level advantage weight to $`1.0`$. Step
advantages are normalized by both the group mean and standard deviation
in ALFWorld, and by the group mean only in WebShop.

GiGPO+ProAS. Since GiGPO adopts step-level group-normalized advantages,
different steps within the same trajectory need not share the same
advantage. This, however, does not affect the plug-in integration of
ProAS. We apply MUI to actions with excessively positive advantages, and
cluster negative-advantage actions within each trajectory according to
their individual advantage magnitudes, rescaling them by future-miss
normalization to a mean of one per cluster.

MiGPO. For MiGPO (?), we use mean-normalized segment-level advantages.
In ALFWorld, we set the milestone-matching threshold to $`0.95`$, the
reverse-decay factor to $`0.9`$, and the step-level advantage weight to
$`10.0`$. WebShop uses environment-provided dynamic milestone signals,
with a reverse-decay factor of $`0.95`$ and a step-level advantage
weight of $`1.0`$.

### B.3 SFT Details

#### B.3.1 Data Curation.

To construct a controlled imitation-learning baseline matching the RL
methods’ task exposure, we curate a supervised dataset for each
environment seed (0, 1, 2) from the frozen training tasks:

ALFWorld: We execute the official annotated expert trajectory to obtain
one demonstration per unique training game.

WebShop: Lacking official experts, we instantiate a deterministic,
goal-conditioned oracle from our ARG using task metadata (target
product, query, and options). The resulting trajectories strictly follow
the ARG’s sub-goal progression
($`\textbf{g}_{1}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow\textbf{g}_{6}`$, details in
Table 10), explicitly grounding the demonstrations in the same progress
structure used by ProAS.

Aligning with the data setting in RL experiments. we represent SFT data
as turn-level examples rather than complete trajectories. Each input
contains the task instruction, current observation, admissible actions,
and the two most recent interaction turns. Table 11 and Table 12
demonstrate the SFT data example of ALFWorld and WebShop.

Training Configuration. We perform full-parameter fine-tuning of
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with the SFT trainer based
on FSDP in Verl-Agent, minimizing the token-level negative
log-likelihood of the oracle responses. We train for 3 epochs with AdamW
($`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.95`$), a peak learning rate of
$`10^{-5}`$, weight decay of $`0.01`$, and gradient clipping at $`1.0`$.
The learning rate follows a cosine schedule with a $`3\%`$ warmup. We
use a maximum sequence length of 4,096 tokens with left truncation and
per-GPU micro-batch sizes of 4 and 2 for the 1.5B and 7B models,
respectively. We save the checkpoint at the end of each epoch and
evaluate every epoch checkpoint on the same frozen 128-task validation
set used for RL. The SFT result is reported from the checkpoint with the
highest validation success rate.

## Appendix D Case Study

### D.1 Unreasonable Action Probing Analysis in ALFWorld

We compare two visible-history conditions while fixing the underlying
environment state and rollout budget. Both conditions replay the same
three-action clean prefix and reach the same continuation point: the
agent is at desk 1, holding keychain 2, with 47 steps remaining. From
this state, we independently sample 50 continuations
$`\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{50}`$ under each condition. In the noise-injected
condition, four unreasonable actions are prepended only to the
policy-visible interaction history; they are not executed in the
continuation environment and therefore do not alter its starting state.

Table 7 first presents the task ARG and then traces the clean and
noise-injected conditions separately. Only the four injected
unreasonable actions are highlighted in pink. The sampled suffixes are
summarized by a continuation row, followed by the Expected Success Rate
(ESR) for each condition.

The two panels therefore differ only in the pink history segment; their
clean prefix, continuation state, and rollout budget are identical. The
observed ESR decrease from $`82\%`$ to $`58\%`$ thus diagnoses the
effect of unreasonable content in the policy-visible history rather than
a change in the underlying environment state.

### D.2 Varying Future-Drift Actions Suppression Analysis in ALFWorld

We examine the suppression of four non-advancing actions from the same
50-step failed ALFWorld rollout in Table  8 (c), which are actions of
steps $`3`$, $`28`$, $`40`$, and $`47`$. At each position, we preserve
the complete preceding prefix, reject only an exact match to the
original action, and independently sample 50 continuations
$`\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{50}`$.

The intervention results exhibit a monotonic relation in this case: the
ESR is $`24\%`$, $`16\%`$, $`10\%`$, and $`0\%`$ when suppressing
actions with FD $`47`$, $`23`$, $`11`$, and $`4`$, respectively. The
larger recovery at higher-FD positions supports Future-Drift as a
diagnostic proxy for an action’s downstream failure burden. Because the
four decisions occur at different prefixes and remaining horizons, this
case is intended as direct intervention evidence for the diagnostic
trend rather than a standalone estimate of a universal causal effect.

### D.3 The ARG Dynamic Evolution in ALFWorld

Table 9 illustrates the online ARG evolution mechanism using the task
Look at a pencil under the desk lamp. Because the environment’s success
criterion broadly accepts any pencil index (e.g., pencil 1 or pencil 2),
the task inherently possesses multiple valid solutions that a single
static ARG fails to cover. To capture this diversity, any rollout
verified as successful during training is first purified by pruning
invalid and inadmissible steps. The retained valid path is then
seamlessly merged into the current ARG, dynamically expanding its
coverage of viable solution trajectories.

### D.4 The Order-Agnostic of Actions in WebShop

Table 10 shows a WebShop case from Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct GRPO rollouts.
The task-level ARG is ordered across sub-goals, but its
option-configuration sub-goal $`\textbf{g}_{3}`$ contains an unordered
set of required options. A partial $`\textbf{g}_{3}`$ state satisfies
one required option, while $`\textbf{g}_{3}`$ is completed only after
all required options are selected. The price and attribute constraints,
$`\textbf{g}_{4}`$ and $`\textbf{g}_{5}`$, are checked at purchase time
before the trajectory reaches $`\textbf{g}_{6}`$.

The contrast highlights the intended granularity of action annotations
deriving from ARG. Trajectories A and B differ only within
$`\textbf{g}_{3}`$: they visit different intermediate option states but
reach the same completion-state equivalence class, so both option orders
are treated as progress-advancing. Trajectory C shows the complementary
cases: a wrong option and a premature purchase are unsupported by the
ARG, whereas a repeated click on an already selected option can be
reasonable but non-advancing. Thus, the ARG preserves ordering
constraints among high-level sub-goals while avoiding spurious penalties
for valid option permutations.

# Divergence controls entropy in distillation

Nicolas Zucchet    Scott W. Linderman Affiliation: Stanford University
Email: [{nzucchet,slinderman}@stanford.edu](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Distillation has become a core primitive of large language model
training, but its properties are not yet well understood. We take an
entropic perspective, studying how the entropy of the student depends on
the data and the divergence that define the distillation objective. We
prove that forward KL inflates the entropy of the student above that of
the teacher. Since cross-entropy training is a special case, this yields
an identity that we verify quantitatively in pretraining and supervised
finetuning. Other divergences come with no such guarantee: reverse KL
deflates entropy until the gap between student and teacher gets too
large, and interpolating between the two changes entropy smoothly early
in training but abruptly at convergence. The lower entropy of on-policy
distillation comes from token-level reverse KL, not from on-policy
sampling. The divergence therefore acts as an implicit entropy
regularizer, whose role is clearest in self-distillation: as
conditioning on privileged information deflates entropy, the divergence
hyperparameters that work best are those that compensate for it.

### 1 Introduction

Distillation consists of training a student model to mimic the behavior
of a teacher model and compress its knowledge ([Hinton et al.,
2015](#bib.bib24)). It is used in increasingly diverse contexts for
large language models, from pretraining lightweight, more efficient
models ([Muralidharan et al., 2024](#bib.bib43); [Gemma Team,
2025](#bib.bib21)), to solidifying reasoning abilities in posttraining
([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib39); [DeepSeek-AI, 2026](#bib.bib16)). In
practice, distillation amounts to minimizing a divergence between two
categorical probability distributions. While divergence minimization has
been extensively studied elsewhere ([Minka, 2005](#bib.bib42); [Bishop,
2006](#bib.bib6); [Amari, 2016](#bib.bib2)), its properties are not yet
well understood in the context of large language models.

In this work, we study the properties of these algorithms from an
entropic perspective, investigating how design choices affect the
entropy of the student. Entropy is fundamentally a measure of
uncertainty, whether in thermodynamics ([Boltzmann, 1877](#bib.bib8)) or
information theory ([Shannon, 1948](#bib.bib52)), and it is particularly
important for large language models as it relates to the diversity of
the output distribution ([Kirk et al., 2024](#bib.bib31); [Farquhar et
al., 2024](#bib.bib17)) and controls exploration in reinforcement
learning ([Cui et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Cheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11); [Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib59)). It is also easy to
compute and log during any training run. Here, we develop intuition for
how distillation shapes entropy.

Our contributions are as follows:

- –
  Decoupling sampling and divergence choice (Section
  [2](#S2 "2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")).
  We review distillation, decoupling the choice of the data generation
  process from that of the divergence, which enables us to study their
  effects on the student’s entropy independently.
- –
  From divergence to entropy (Section
  [3](#S3 "3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")).
  In a toy model of language modeling, we show that: forward KL inflates
  the student’s entropy above the teacher’s by the residual divergence;
  reverse KL deflates it until mimicking the teacher gets too difficult
  and then suddenly inflates it; and interpolating between the two
  varies entropy smoothly early in training but abruptly at convergence.
  Top-$`k`$ restrictions also shift entropy, depending on how the tail
  is handled.
- –
  At scale, the divergence implicitly controls entropy (Section
  [4](#S4 "4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")).
  After pretraining and supervised finetuning, the entropy of a language
  model matches the cross-entropy loss it was trained to minimize;
  larger models therefore have lower entropy. When distilling from a
  larger teacher, the divergence drives entropy more than on- vs.
  off-policy sampling; in self-distillation, it counters the entropy
  drop that conditioning on privileged information induces.

### 2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation

Figure 1: Decoupling divergence from sampling in distillation. A prompt
$`x`$ is completed by the sampling distribution $`p`$, and the student
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ is then compared to the teacher $`\pi^{*}`$ on each
token of the completion through a divergence $`\ell`$ between their
next-token distributions. Writing the objective at the token level keeps
the two choices independent, whereas a sequence-level divergence ties
them together. Details in Section
[2](#S2 "2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

Distillation algorithms, in particular on-policy ones, are often derived
by minimizing a divergence between the student’s and the teacher’s
distributions over sequences of tokens. As we will argue, this couples
the choice of token-level objective with the sampling distribution,
which makes their respective effects on the student hard to disentangle
and can yield suboptimal configurations. Following [Czarnecki et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib15) and [Agarwal et al. (2024)](#bib.bib1) inter alia,
we decouple the two, as summarized in Figure
[1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
and detailed in Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

###### Student, teacher and sampling distributions.

We consider data consisting of prompts $`x`$ and completions $`y`$
generated by a sampling distribution $`p`$. On these completions, the
student distribution $`\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)`$ parametrized by
$`\theta`$ is compared to that of the teacher $`\pi^{*}(y\mid x)`$. The
sampling distribution $`p`$ can either be the student itself, in which
case learning is on-policy, or the teacher, in which case we call it
off-policy distillation. The teacher can either be explicit or implicit,
as when training on internet text through next-token cross-entropy
minimization.

###### Distillation objective.

The objective of distillation is to minimize the expected divergence
between the student and the teacher over a dataset of prompts $`x`$ and
completions $`y`$,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}),\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\right)\right]\!,
``` |  | (1) |

with $`T`$ the length of the completion and $`\ell`$ a divergence
between the full next-token distributions of the two models, such as the
forward or the reverse KL. The objective is here defined at the token
level, which is what enables us to decouple the sampling distribution
$`p`$ from the divergence choice $`\ell`$. Otherwise, the sampling
distribution would be implicitly defined by the divergence choice—a
sequence-level reverse KL forces on-policy data and a sequence-level
forward KL forces off-policy data. In practice, gradients are typically
not backpropagated through the sampling process, even when it is the
student itself, with rare exceptions ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib22),
e.g.,). The same approximation is used when improving the reasoning
abilities of language models through reinforcement learning with
verifiable rewards ([Lambert et al., 2025](#bib.bib33); [Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23)). It amounts to ignoring the score function term,
which biases the gradient but considerably reduces its variance
([Williams, 1992](#bib.bib61); [Roeder et al., 2017](#bib.bib49);
[Naesseth et al., 2018](#bib.bib45); [Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)).
This observation motivates ignoring sequence modeling aspects of
distillation in our toy model of Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 A toy model of language modeling ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

###### Entropy.

Core to our analysis is the average entropy of the model $`\pi`$, which
we define as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{H}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}H\left(\pi(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\right)\right]\!,
``` |  | (2) |

with $`H`$ the Shannon entropy of a distribution over the vocabulary.
The distribution $`\pi`$ need not be the one that generates the contexts
$`(x,y_{<t})`$. When $`p=\pi=\pi_{\theta}`$, this is the student’s
normalized sequence-level entropy.

### 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy

Figure 2: A toy language model reveals phase transitions in how the
divergence affects entropy. Toy model (a). A linear softmax student maps
a fixed embedding $`e_{i}`$ per context $`i`$ to next-token
probabilities and is trained towards a teacher distribution per context
with forward KL, reverse KL, or the generalized Jensen–Shannon (JS)
divergence whose weight $`\lambda`$ interpolates between them (Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 A toy model of language modeling ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")).
Its shared weights $`W`$ prevent it from reproducing all teacher
distributions when there are too many contexts. Simulations (b–e). At
convergence, forward KL (blue) always inflates entropy relative to the
teacher and reverse KL (red) tends to deflate it, unless the teacher is
too hard to model because there are too many contexts (b) or because it
is too diffuse (c), in which case reverse KL inflates entropy too. Along
the generalized JS family (colors in between), entropy smoothly
decreases with the weight $`\lambda`$ early in training (e), whereas at
convergence it drops abruptly (d).

The formalism of the previous section enables us to study the effect of
the sampling distribution and the divergence choice independently. In
this section, we restrict ourselves to that of the divergence as it is
more tractable to analyze theoretically, and defer the empirical study
of the sampling distribution to the next section. We first introduce a
toy model that captures part of language modeling and use it as an
illustrative example to highlight the properties of different divergence
functions.

#### 3.1 A toy model of language modeling

The canonical example to illustrate the properties of forward and
reverse KL divergences is fitting a mixture of Gaussians with a single
Gaussian distribution ([Bishop, 2006](#bib.bib6)). While this has been
used to illustrate some of the properties of distillation or
reinforcement learning in the context of large language models ([Agarwal
et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib22); [Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10); [Nicolicioiu et al., 2026](#bib.bib47)), its
continuous and non-neural-network nature makes it an imperfect proxy for
what happens in practice.

Instead, we propose the following toy model, which takes as input a
random embedding $`e_{i}`$ associated with each context $`i`$ in the
data distribution and outputs a next-token distribution through

|     |                                                     |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i)=\mathrm{softmax}(We_{i}).
       ```                                                  |     | (3) |

Such a model can be understood as the prediction head of a language
model whose preceding sequence model provides random embeddings, as an
associative memory model ([Cabannes et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Nichani
et al., 2025](#bib.bib46); [Zucchet et al., 2025](#bib.bib69)), or as
the simplest neural network that can produce categorical distributions
over possible next tokens. This model has been shown to be able to fit a
number of contexts proportional to the number of parameters of the
linear layer ([Cabannes et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Nichani et al.,
2025](#bib.bib46)). Varying the number of contexts therefore allows us
to control the difficulty of the task, or equivalently the capacity of
an implicit teacher. Another knob is the entropy of the teacher
distribution, or equivalently the teacher perplexity
$`\exp(H(\pi^{*}))`$, that is the effective number of tokens over which
the teacher places probability mass; increasing it also makes the task
harder ([Zucchet et al., 2026](#bib.bib70)).

Contrary to language models, this toy model has no sequence modeling
component, which makes it easier to analyze theoretically. Little is
lost since gradients are not propagated through the sampling process: in
both cases, the distillation objective mostly aims at improving
next-token prediction and not at shaping the sequence sampling
distribution directly.

Throughout this section, we leverage this model when needed to
illustrate some of the theoretical properties of the different
divergences. In our simulations, as a default we set the embedding
dimension to $`128`$, the vocabulary size to $`64`$, the number of
contexts to $`2048`$, and sample the teacher perplexity log-uniformly
between $`1`$ and $`16`$. The model is trained with full-batch Adam
([Kingma & Ba, 2015](#bib.bib30)) and a learning rate of $`0.1`$ for
$`40`$k steps; Appendix
[B.7](#A2.SS7 "B.7 Setup of the toy model experiments ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
details the setup.

#### 3.2 Divergence choice only matters when student and teacher differ

Before characterizing what each divergence does to entropy, it is worth
delimiting when the choice matters at all. Indeed, a well-established
property of KL divergences is that the geometries of the forward and the
reverse KL are equivalent around optimality ([Amari, 2016](#bib.bib2)).
In our case, this means that if the student is capable of reproducing
the teacher and already starts close to it, the learning dynamics of
distillation with forward and reverse KL are the same. Both objectives
also share the same global optimum $`\pi_{\theta}=\pi^{*}`$. More
formally, when $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is close to $`\pi^{*}`$ on all contexts
$`i`$, we have

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i)\parallel\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i))\right]\approx\nabla_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i)\parallel\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i))\right]
``` |  | (4) |

up to first order. The student’s entropy is therefore also the same
along the training trajectory under either objective. We emphasize that
the statement is independent of the sampling distribution $`p`$, as long
as it stays constant during training. This argument applies more broadly
to any $`f`$-divergence, and in particular to the generalized
Jensen–Shannon family that we use later. A proof of the general
statement can be found in Appendix
[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Divergence choice does not matter around optimality ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

A consequence of that result is that any difference in behavior induced
by the choice of divergence occurs when the student and the teacher
distributions differ significantly, e.g., because the student cannot
represent the teacher or because the two start far from each other. The
rest of this section will implicitly assume that we are in such a
regime. Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")b
illustrates part of this property in our toy model: for a small enough
number of contexts, the student and the teacher have almost identical
entropy at convergence regardless of the divergence choice, as the
student is close to matching the teacher.

#### 3.3 Forward KL predictably inflates entropy

The forward KL divergence is known to be mode-covering ([Bishop,
2006](#bib.bib6); [Murphy, 2022](#bib.bib44)): as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i)\parallel\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i))=\sum_{j}\pi^{*}(j\mid i)\log\frac{\pi^{*}(j\mid i)}{\pi_{\theta}(j\mid i)}
``` |  | (5) |

the objective diverges whenever $`\pi^{*}(j\mid i)>0`$ and
$`\pi_{\theta}(j\mid i)\rightarrow 0`$, so the student tends to cover
all of the teacher’s support. From this intuition, we expect the student
to have higher entropy than the teacher; we now show that this does
indeed hold at optimality.

Take a neural network with a linear softmax head. This includes standard
language model heads as well as our toy model of Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 A toy model of language modeling ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").
At optimality, if we were to multiply the weight matrix by a scalar
factor, the loss would not change up to first order. The consequence of
that, which we prove in Appendix
[B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 The forward KL inflates entropy ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
following classical arguments from information geometry ([Amari,
2016](#bib.bib2)), is that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{H}(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}})=\bar{H}(\pi^{*})+\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i)\parallel\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}(\cdot\mid i))\right]
``` |  | (6) |

when $`\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}`$ is a local optimum of the forward KL
distillation objective. In particular, the student necessarily has
higher entropy than the teacher on average, which Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")b
and
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")c
confirm. This statement is only relative to the teacher: the forward KL
can still deflate entropy over the course of learning if the teacher’s
entropy is much lower, as Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")e
illustrates.

Given that the cross-entropy loss minimization objective can be seen as
off-policy distillation with the forward KL, in which continuations of
the data are samples from an implicit teacher, this result extends to
pretraining and supervised finetuning as well. Expanding the KL,
Equation
[6](#S3.E6 "In 3.3 Forward KL predictably inflates entropy ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
states that the average entropy of the student equals its average
cross-entropy with the teacher, that is its training loss, both measured
on the training data. This does not require the two distributions to be
close. It only requires a softmax head, as in large language models, and
a student at a stationary point of the average forward KL. A direct
consequence is that, among models trained to convergence, a higher loss
comes with a higher entropy: a student that cannot match its teacher
ends up more uncertain rather than overconfident. The experiments of
Section
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Cross-entropy predicts entropy in pretraining and supervised finetuning ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
will confirm that these statements hold quantitatively in practice.

#### 3.4 Reverse KL deflates entropy until the gap gets too large

Whereas the forward KL is mode-covering, the reverse KL is mode-seeking
([MacKay, 2003](#bib.bib40); [Bishop, 2006](#bib.bib6); [Margossian &
Saul, 2023](#bib.bib41)). This mode-seeking bias of the reverse KL
divergence suggests that a student trained with it would have lower
entropy than the teacher. In practice, this is indeed the case in a wide
range of scenarios, but breaks when the task becomes too hard, as
illustrated in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")b
and
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")c
when there are too many contexts to model or when the teacher is too
diffuse.

Theoretical analysis is not as easy as for the forward KL, so we focus
on providing intuition for why this happens.¹¹ 1 The stationarity
argument behind Equation
[6](#S3.E6 "In 3.3 Forward KL predictably inflates entropy ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
also applies to the reverse KL, but it then constrains the spread of the
log probabilities of the student rather than its entropy (Appendix
[B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Reverse KL: a flatness statement ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")).
Let us start by rewriting the reverse KL divergence as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i)\parallel\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i))=-\sum_{j}\pi_{\theta}(j\mid i)\log\pi^{*}(j\mid i)-H(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i)).
``` |  | (7) |

The first term is the mode-seeking part of the divergence: whenever
$`\pi^{*}(j\mid i)\rightarrow 0`$, the student must place low
probability mass on token $`j`$ to avoid a large loss. As the teacher
gets more diffuse, the first term flattens out, and the second one,
which pushes the student’s entropy up, dominates the objective. As a
result, the mode-seeking bias of the reverse KL disappears and the
student ends up inflating its entropy. We can formalize that intuition
by analyzing the behavior of the reverse KL divergence in the limit of a
flat teacher distribution. This analysis, provided in Appendix
[B.5](#A2.SS5 "B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
shows that the reverse KL divergence, as well as any divergence in the
generalized Jensen–Shannon family, becomes equivalent to the forward KL
divergence in that limit, and therefore inflates entropy. It does not
predict the exact point at which the transition occurs, but it does
quantify how the entropy gap between the teacher and the student
evolves: the gap widens as the number of contexts grows and narrows as
the teacher gets more diffuse, matching Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")b
and
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")c.

One may wonder whether the inflation part of the statement is relevant
in practice: in the toy experiments it only occurs when the number of
contexts is large or the teacher’s output diversity is high, cases in
which distillation is unlikely to bring significant performance
improvements. In our large language model experiments of Section
[4](#S4 "4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
we do not observe any average entropy inflation with the reverse KL.
That said, the analysis here still gives us some insight into the biases
of the reverse KL divergence. Even when inflation does not hold on
average, it should still occur on a subset of contexts, and the
intuition developed here hints at which ones are more likely to see the
entropy of their next-token distribution inflated.

#### 3.5 In between forward and reverse KL, entropy can sharply change

Forward and reverse KL are not the only divergences used in practice,
and it is common to interpolate between the two through the generalized
Jensen–Shannon divergence ([von Werra et al., 2020](#bib.bib57);
[Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib27);
[Zhao et al., 2026b](#bib.bib68); [Xing et al., 2026](#bib.bib63)). We
wonder whether the entropy of the student smoothly and monotonically
increases as we interpolate between the two divergences. The results,
illustrated in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")d
and
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")e,
show that it depends on the stage of training: early in training entropy
smoothly decreases as the interpolation weight $`\lambda`$ increases
(Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")e),
whereas at convergence it drops abruptly (Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")d).
That said, the phase transition boundary does evolve smoothly as a
function of $`\lambda`$, cf. Figure
[7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ B.2 Where the entropy gap changes sign ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
in the appendix, and divergences with larger $`\lambda`$ values need
harder tasks to inflate entropy. As a result, one of the consequences of
picking a divergence in the generalized Jensen–Shannon family is an
implicit regularization on the entropy of the student, which can be
either smooth or sharp depending on the training stage.

#### 3.6 Top-$`k`$ restrictions shift entropy depending on how the tail is handled

Figure 3: Restricting the divergence to the top-$`k`$ tokens shifts
entropy depending on how the support and the tail are handled. Same
setup as in Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
with $`512`$ contexts, this time varying $`k`$ out of a vocabulary of
$`64`$, when using the student (left) or the teacher (right) to select
the top-$`k`$ tokens and when using the renormalized or tail bucket
cropping strategy. Both choices substantially change how the restriction
affects entropy. Forward KL here; other divergences in Figure
[8](#A2.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ B.6 Additional results on top-𝑘 ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

An important design choice that we have not yet touched upon is how much
of the total probability distribution to take into account when
computing the divergence. So far, we have only used full vocabulary
divergences, but in practice it is common to restrict the divergence to
the top-$`k`$ tokens of the teacher or student distribution for compute
and bandwidth efficiency ([Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib19); [Hübotter et
al., 2026](#bib.bib27); [Xing et al., 2026](#bib.bib63)). Two choices
arise: which distribution selects the top-$`k`$ tokens (student or
teacher), and how the mass outside the selected set is handled. The
renormalized variant renormalizes both distributions over the top-$`k`$
tokens ([Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib19); [Xing et al., 2026](#bib.bib63)),
whereas the tail bucket keeps the tail mass as one extra bucket
([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib27); [Huang & Wei, 2026](#bib.bib26));
see Appendix
[A.2.2](#A1.SS2.SSS2 "A.2.2 Top-𝑘 restrictions: renormalization and tail bucket ‣ A.2 Practical evaluation of the token-level divergence ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

We find that top-$`k`$ restrictions can have a non-negligible effect on
the entropy of the student and that the effect depends strongly on the
two design choices above. When the student selects the top-$`k`$ tokens,
renormalization keeps entropy relatively independent of $`k`$, at least
early on, whereas the tail bucket yields increasingly large entropies as
$`k`$ decreases. When the teacher selects the top-$`k`$ tokens, the
early-training picture reverses. The results presented here hold for the
forward KL divergence, and we additionally sweep over the generalized
Jensen–Shannon divergence weight $`\lambda`$ in Figure
[8](#A2.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ B.6 Additional results on top-𝑘 ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
in the appendix, finding that these observations are mostly independent
of the choice of divergence, although we observe some interactions.

### 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios

We now turn to empirical validation of the intuition and of the
theoretical properties of divergences derived in the previous section,
as well as to studying the effect of the sampling distribution. We
consider three settings, which are both practically relevant and will
enable us to test different parts of the theory: pretraining,
distillation in posttraining for reasoning, and self-distillation.

#### 4.1 Cross-entropy predicts entropy in pretraining and supervised finetuning

Figure 4: Cross-entropy tracks entropy in pretraining and supervised
finetuning. Average entropy of the student $`\bar{H}(\pi_{\theta})`$
against its average cross-entropy loss on the data, both computed from
the student alone, for OLMo 2 at different sizes (marker size), before
(hollow) and after (filled) supervised finetuning (SFT), on the
pretraining (left, green) and finetuning (right, orange) mixtures; the
grey line is entropy equals cross-entropy. The identity holds on the
data each model was last trained on, as predicted, and also, without
guarantee, on the finetuning data after pretraining, whereas supervised
finetuning breaks it on the pretraining data.

Next-token cross-entropy minimization as used during pretraining and
supervised finetuning can be understood as sampled off-policy
distillation with forward KL, in which the teacher is an implicit
distribution over continuations of the data. In that case the divergence
is fixed: we only have access to samples from the teacher distribution,
so we cannot use reverse KL or any other divergence that would require
direct access to the teacher’s probabilities. As discussed in Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Forward KL predictably inflates entropy ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
Equation
[6](#S3.E6 "In 3.3 Forward KL predictably inflates entropy ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
predicts that the entropy of the student is equal to its cross-entropy
loss at convergence, both measured on the training data. Testing it
requires the cross-entropy form of the statement, which only needs
samples from the teacher, rather than its form in terms of the entropy
of the teacher and the KL divergence to it, which needs the
probabilities of the teacher. Yet, compute-optimal large language models
are typically stopped before convergence ([Kaplan et al.,
2020](#bib.bib29)), although the trend is to longer pretraining runs
([Hoffmann et al., 2022](#bib.bib25); [Sardana et al.,
2024](#bib.bib50)). We therefore wonder whether pretraining and
supervised finetuning runs are long enough for this relationship to
hold; we find it to hold surprisingly precisely in practice, as
illustrated in Figure
[4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.1 Cross-entropy predicts entropy in pretraining and supervised finetuning ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").
Despite the simplicity and robustness of that result, we are not aware
of any prior work that has reported it within the language model
literature.

To show this, we run experiments with the OLMo 2 suite of models ([Team
OLMo, 2025](#bib.bib56)), which provides multiple pretraining and
supervised finetuned checkpoints with varying model sizes, as well as
the pretraining and finetuning datasets that were used to train the
models. For each model size, we evaluate the different checkpoints on
both pretraining and finetuning data, and report the entropy as a
function of the cross-entropy loss. Overall, the identity holds, both
after pretraining and supervised finetuning. Cross-entropy loss
decreases as we scale models, so larger models have lower entropy.
Figure
[9](#A3.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ C.1 The identity across model families and pretraining stages ‣ Appendix C Additional experimental results ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
in the appendix confirms that the same holds for the Pythia suite of
models ([Biderman et al., 2023](#bib.bib5)). Entropy decreases
everywhere during finetuning even though we are using a forward KL
objective, which inflates entropy. We reiterate that the inflation is
relative to the teacher, and that lower entropy overall reflects the low
entropy of the finetuning data, which is for example a well-known
property of alignment data ([West & Potts, 2025](#bib.bib60);
[Lanchantin et al., 2025](#bib.bib34); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib66);
[Springer et al., 2026](#bib.bib54)).

Zooming out, this result informs us about how pretrained models fail to
capture the data distribution: they tend to leak probability mass
outside the support of the data distribution rather than overconfidently
predicting high probability tokens.

#### 4.2 On-policy distillation’s lower entropy is due to token-level reverse KL

Figure 5: The lower entropy of on-policy distillation is due to
token-level reverse KL, on-policy sampling nudges it up, and teacher
size barely matters. Average entropy of the Qwen3-0.6B-Base student on
its own MATH500 completions over distillation from Qwen3-Instruct
teachers of increasing size in non-thinking mode (left to right). Color
is the divergence, with the full color for on-policy and a lighter shade
for off-policy, and each curve is a single seed.

So far, we have focused on the divergence and kept the sampling
distribution fixed, which is the setting in which the results of Section
[3](#S3 "3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
hold. On-policy distillation is increasingly used in the posttraining of
large language models ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Gu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22); [Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib64); [Lu & Thinking
Machines Lab, 2025](#bib.bib37); [Xiao et al., 2026](#bib.bib62); [Yang
et al., 2026](#bib.bib65)), as training on its own completions removes
the exposure bias ([Bengio et al., 2015](#bib.bib4)) that models trained
off-policy face. It is usually paired with reverse KL, as both follow
from the same sequence-level derivation ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib22);
[Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025](#bib.bib37)), so that a loss of
diversity could a priori come from either choice. In this set of
experiments, we ablate the effects of the divergence (forward vs.
reverse KL) and the sampling distribution (on-policy vs. off-policy) on
entropy. We distill the Qwen3-Instruct models ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib64)) in non-thinking mode into a Qwen3-0.6B-Base student
on the math problems of DeepScaleR ([Luo et al., 2025](#bib.bib38)). We
measure entropy on MATH500 ([Lightman et al., 2024](#bib.bib36)) on
student completions in Figure
[5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.2 On-policy distillation’s lower entropy is due to token-level reverse KL ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
and on frozen teacher completions in Figure
[10](#A3.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ C.2 Additional on- vs. off-policy distillation results ‣ Appendix C Additional experimental results ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
in the appendix.

The divergence has the largest effect on entropy: forward KL raises the
entropy of the student and reverse KL lowers it, for every teacher and
both sampling distributions, consistently with the ordering of Section
[3](#S3 "3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").
On-policy distillation consistently yields higher entropy than
off-policy distillation, but this effect is two to three times smaller
than that of the divergence. [Zhao et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib67) similarly
report that the prefix source has a much smaller effect on entropy than
the direction of the KL, particularly on short completions. On-policy
sampling therefore modulates the effect of the divergence, and the lower
entropy of on-policy distillation is mainly due to reverse KL. Finally,
teacher size barely matters: the three teachers have almost the same
entropy on their own completions and are all obtained by distillation
from the same larger models ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib64)), and [Li
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib35) have shown that teachers trained with the
same pipeline induce nearly identical next-token distributions on the
contexts the student visits despite their difference in size.

#### 4.3 Self-distillation deflates entropy, divergence can compensate for it

In self-distillation, the teacher is the student itself, given
additional feedback on how to solve the task. This sidesteps the need
for a more capable model to distill from, and improves performance on a
variety of tasks. [Nicolicioiu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib47) have shown
that conditioning on task feedback decreases the output diversity of the
teacher, and thus that of the student. Here, we focus on how the choice
of divergence interacts with this effect. As the teacher is derived from
the student, any entropy the student loses is also lost by the teacher,
which can lead to entropy collapse.

We follow the experimental setup of [Hübotter et al. (2026)](#bib.bib27)
on the chemistry subset of SciKnowEval ([Feng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)), which consists of multiple-choice questions testing
scientific knowledge and reasoning in chemistry. We finetune Olmo 3 7B
Instruct ([Team Olmo, 2025](#bib.bib55)), starting from the default
hyperparameters of [Hübotter et al. (2026)](#bib.bib27), and varying
three knobs that are important in practice: the divergence within the
generalized Jensen–Shannon family, the frequency at which the teacher is
updated, and the number of tokens taken into account when computing the
divergence. As in the implementation of [Hübotter et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib27), these tokens are the top-$`k`$ tokens of the
student. The main results are reported in Figure
[6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.3 Self-distillation deflates entropy, divergence can compensate for it ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
and we run the same experiments on GSM8K ([Cobbe et al.,
2021](#bib.bib13)) in Appendix
[C.5](#A3.SS5 "C.5 Additional self-distillation experiments on GSM8K ‣ Appendix C Additional experimental results ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
with similar conclusions.

Overall, we find that the default hyperparameters are ones that
stabilize entropy, and that moving away from them can lead to entropy
collapse. When varying the weight of the generalized Jensen–Shannon
divergence (Figure
[6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.3 Self-distillation deflates entropy, divergence can compensate for it ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
left), entropy smoothly decreases with $`\lambda`$, consistently with
the early learning dynamics of the toy model (Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")e).
In the longer runs of Appendix
[C.5](#A3.SS5 "C.5 Additional self-distillation experiments on GSM8K ‣ Appendix C Additional experimental results ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
entropy sharply increases between $`\lambda=0.25`$ and $`\lambda=0`$,
although not as abruptly as in the toy model at convergence (Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")d).
This could be due to the almost on-policy nature of the data, which
makes different runs see different contexts. With reverse KL, entropy
collapses (Figure
[6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.3 Self-distillation deflates entropy, divergence can compensate for it ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
left): the entropy deflation of reverse KL compounds with the reduced
diversity of the teacher, which in turn lowers the entropy of the next
teacher, and so on. Entropy also collapses when the teacher is the live
student (Figure
[6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.3 Self-distillation deflates entropy, divergence can compensate for it ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
center), whereas an exponential moving average, even with a decay of
$`0.95`$, behaves like a frozen teacher and prevents the collapse.
Restricting the divergence to fewer tokens (Figure
[6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.3 Self-distillation deflates entropy, divergence can compensate for it ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
right) affects entropy as the toy model of Section
[3.6](#S3.SS6 "3.6 Top-𝑘 restrictions shift entropy depending on how the tail is handled ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
predicts early in training when the student selects the top-$`k`$ tokens
(Figure
[3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.6 Top-𝑘 restrictions shift entropy depending on how the tail is handled ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
left): with the tail bucket, entropy increases as $`k`$ decreases,
whereas renormalizing leaves it roughly independent of $`k`$.
Performance-wise, these different hyperparameters account for
differences of a few percentage points as long as entropy does not
collapse, cf. Figure
[11](#A3.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ C.4 Accuracy vs. entropy for self-distillation on the Chemistry dataset ‣ Appendix C Additional experimental results ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

Our results emphasize the importance of separating the choice of
divergence from the sampling distribution. Indeed, deriving
self-distillation from a sequence-level reverse KL to justify on-policy
sampling forces the choice of reverse KL, which collapses entropy and
damages performance. This is consistent with self-distillation papers
([Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib53); [Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib27); [Zhao et al., 2026b](#bib.bib68), e.g.,) not finding
reverse KL to work best in practice.²² 2 [Shenfeld et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib53) report using reverse KL in the paper, but the code
uses forward KL.

### 5 Conclusion

The divergence used in distillation is often inherited rather than
chosen: a sequence-level derivation ties reverse KL to on-policy data
and forward KL to off-policy data. Once we disentangled these choices,
we found that the divergence, more than the sampling distribution, acts
as an implicit regularizer on the student. Forward KL inflates the
entropy of the student above that of the teacher by exactly the residual
divergence on average, so the entropy of a converged pretraining run can
be read from its loss. Reverse KL deflates entropy, but only as long as
the student can mimic the teacher; whether it does is a property of the
divergence and the task together, not of the divergence alone. In
between, and depending on how the tail of the distribution is handled,
entropy can be tuned, smoothly early in training and abruptly at
convergence. These effects compound in self-distillation, where the
teacher is derived from the student: any entropy drop feeds back into
the next teacher, and the hyperparameters that work best are those whose
inflation counteracts the drop that conditioning on privileged
information induces.

Practically, our results suggest choosing the sampling distribution and
the divergence separately, and treating the latter as an entropy knob
rather than as a consequence of the former.

We obtained these results with a toy model of language modeling, simple
enough to admit precise mathematical statements yet rich enough to
reproduce key phenomena at scale. We hope that it will replace the
Gaussian mixtures inherited from variational inference as the go-to
example to reason about distillation in large language models, and that
it will prove useful beyond entropy.

### Appendix A The distillation framework

Section
[2](#S2 "2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
introduces the token-level distillation objective of Equation
[1](#S2.E1 "In Distillation objective. ‣ 2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
and states that writing the objective at the token level is what
decouples the data-generating process $`p`$ from the divergence
$`\ell`$. This appendix makes that claim precise. We first define the
sequence-level objective, the token-level one and the surrogate that
implementations actually differentiate, and compare them (Appendix
[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")).
We then discuss how the token-level objective is evaluated in practice
(Appendix
[A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Practical evaluation of the token-level divergence ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"))
and define the divergence families we sweep over (Appendix
[A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 The divergence families we consider ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")).

#### A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped

There are a few technical details in how distillation methods are
defined, mostly when the completions are sampled from the student, that
have important consequences. Here, we emphasize the difference between
token- and sequence-level objectives, and review how an implementation
detail, the stop-gradient, alters the objective that is actually
optimized.

##### A.1.1 The different objectives

###### Definition 1 (Sequence-level objective).

The sequence-level objective compares the student and teacher
distributions over entire sequences,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{seq}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\ell_{\mathrm{seq}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x),\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x)\right)\right],
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\ell_{\mathrm{seq}}`$ is a divergence between the two
probability distributions over the full sequence space. For the reverse
KL divergence,
$`\ell_{\mathrm{seq}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\parallel\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x))`$,
which hides an expectation over student-generated sequences. The forward
KL divergence instead hides an expectation over teacher-generated
sequences.

###### Definition 2 (Token-level objective).

The token-level objective is that of Equation
[1](#S2.E1 "In Distillation objective. ‣ 2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}),\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\right)\right],
``` |  | (9) |

with $`\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}`$ a divergence between the token-level
distributions in context $`(x,y_{<t})`$ and $`p`$ the data-generating
process from which the sequences are sampled.

###### Definition 3 (Surrogate token-level objective).

The surrogate token-level objective ignores the potential dependency of
the data-generating process on the parameters:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sur\text{-}tok}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\text{stop-gradient}[p](\cdot\mid x)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}),\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\right)\right].
``` |  | (10) |

Its gradient is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sur\text{-}tok}}=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p}\left[\sum_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}),\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}))\right],
``` |  | (11) |

and this is what most papers use in practice.

###### Remark 1.

When $`p=\pi^{*}`$, the data-generating process does not depend on
$`\theta`$ so the stop-gradient has no effect and
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sur\text{-}tok}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}}`$ have the same gradients. The surrogate
differs from the token-level objective only when sampling on-policy,
i.e. $`p=\pi_{\theta}`$, or more generally when the sampling
distribution depends on the student.

##### A.1.2 How the different objectives and their gradients compare

###### Fact 1 (The token- and sequence-level objectives can be equal).

Take $`p=\pi_{\theta}`$ with $`\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}`$ and
$`\ell_{\mathrm{seq}}`$ the reverse KL divergence, or $`p=\pi^{*}`$ with
$`\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}`$ and $`\ell_{\mathrm{seq}}`$ the forward KL
divergence. Then
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{seq}}`$, and in
particular
$`\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{seq}}`$.

###### Proof.

The proof relies on the chain rule of the KL divergence, which we state
for general autoregressive distributions before applying it to the
setting of interest. Let $`a(\cdot\mid x)`$ and $`b(\cdot\mid x)`$ be
two distributions. Their sequence-level log-ratio decomposes token by
token,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log\frac{a(y\mid x)}{b(y\mid x)}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\frac{a(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{b(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}.
``` |  | (12) |

Taking the expectation under $`y\sim a(\cdot\mid x)`$ and exchanging it
with the finite sum,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(a(\cdot\mid x)\parallel b(\cdot\mid x))=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim a(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\log\frac{a(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{b(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}\right].
``` |  | (13) |

The $`t`$-th summand depends on $`y`$ only through the prefix $`y_{<t}`$
and the current token $`y_{t}`$, so the future tokens $`y_{>t}`$
marginalize out (the tower property) and the expectation becomes

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y\sim a(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\log\frac{a(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{b(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}\right]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{<t}\sim a(\cdot\mid x)}\,\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim a(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})}\left[\log\frac{a(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{b(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}\right]`$ |  | (14) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{<t}\sim a(\cdot\mid x)}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(a(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\parallel b(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\right)\right],`$ |  | (15) |

the inner expectation being the KL divergence between the token-level
distributions in context $`(x,y_{<t})`$. Summing over $`t`$ and
collecting the per-step expectations back into a single expectation over
$`y\sim a(\cdot\mid x)`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(a(\cdot\mid x)\parallel b(\cdot\mid x))=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim a(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(a(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\parallel b(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\right)\right].
``` |  | (16) |

This has exactly the same form as the token-level objective of
Definition
[2](#Thmdefinition2 "Definition 2 (Token-level objective). ‣ A.1.1 The different objectives ‣ A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
with the data sampled from $`a`$ and $`\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}`$ the
per-token KL divergence between $`a`$ and $`b`$. The two subcases follow
by choosing $`(a,b)`$.

- –
  Taking $`a=\pi_{\theta}`$ and $`b=\pi^{*}`$ gives the reverse KL
  divergence with $`p=a=\pi_{\theta}`$.
- –
  Taking $`a=\pi^{*}`$ and $`b=\pi_{\theta}`$ gives the forward KL
  divergence with $`p=a=\pi^{*}`$.

In both, the outer expectation samples the prefixes from the first
argument of the divergence, which is precisely $`p`$. Equality of the
objectives implies equality of their gradients, as there are no
stop-gradients or additional implicit parameters held constant here. ∎

###### Remark 2.

Because the chain rule samples the prefixes from the first argument of
the divergence, deriving an objective at the sequence level fixes the
data-generating process at the token level: the reverse KL divergence
forces $`p=\pi_{\theta}`$ and the forward KL divergence forces
$`p=\pi^{*}`$. This is why we argued that sequence-level objectives
couple $`\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}`$ to $`p`$ in the main text. Conversely,
nothing couples the two once the objective is written at the token level
from the start, which is what lets us sweep the divergence at a fixed
data-generating process in Section
[3](#S3 "3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

##### A.1.3 What the stop-gradients used in practice ignore

In practice, the surrogate objective is typically used. It follows that
the resulting gradient is a biased estimate of that of the actual
objective.

###### Fact 2 (The token-level gradient and its surrogate).

Write
$`\ell_{t}(y,\theta)=\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}),\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}))`$
for the per-token loss along a sequence. The token-level gradient
decomposes as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}}=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sur\text{-}tok}}+g_{\mathrm{seq}},
``` |  | (17) |

with the visitation gradient in its causal form

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{\mathrm{seq}}=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(y_{s}\mid x,y_{<s})\sum_{t=s}^{T}\ell_{t}(y,\theta)\right],
``` |  | (18) |

each token weighted by its cost-to-go $`\sum_{t=s}^{T}\ell_{t}`$. When
$`p=\pi^{*}`$, $`g_{\mathrm{seq}}=0`$ and the three gradients coincide.
When $`p=\pi_{\theta}`$ it is generally nonzero, and only then does the
surrogate the algorithm optimizes depart from the true gradient of the
objective.

###### Proof.

Write $`L(y,\theta)=\sum_{t}\ell_{t}(y,\theta)`$ so that
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tok}}=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p}[L]`$,
and differentiate the sampled expectation with the score-function
identity,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p}[L]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p}\left[\nabla_{\theta}L\right]+\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p}\left[L\nabla_{\theta}\log p(y\mid x)\right],`$ |  | (19) |

using $`\nabla_{\theta}p=p\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p`$. The first term
differentiates only the losses, at fixed sampling distribution, and is
$`\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sur\text{-}tok}}`$ of Definition
[3](#Thmdefinition3 "Definition 3 (Surrogate token-level objective). ‣ A.1.1 The different objectives ‣ A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation");
the second is the visitation gradient. When $`p=\pi^{*}`$ does not
depend on $`\theta`$, this score term is absent and
$`g_{\mathrm{seq}}=0`$. It remains to bring the visitation gradient to
the causal form of the statement. The score factors over tokens,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\log p(y\mid x)=\sum_{s=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(y_{s}\mid x,y_{<s}),
``` |  | (20) |

and the losses $`\ell_{t}`$ with $`t<s`$ do not depend on the token
drawn at step $`s`$ given its prefix, so, conditioning on $`y_{<s}`$ and
using
$`\mathbb{E}_{y_{s}\sim p(\cdot\mid x,y_{<s})}[\nabla_{\theta}\log p(y_{s}\mid x,y_{<s})]=0`$,
their cross terms vanish in expectation. Only the cost-to-go
$`\sum_{t=s}^{T}\ell_{t}`$ survives against the step-$`s`$ score, giving
the causal form

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{\mathrm{seq}}=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p}\left[\sum_{s=1}^{T}\nabla_{\theta}\log p(y_{s}\mid x,y_{<s})\sum_{t=s}^{T}\ell_{t}(y,\theta)\right].
``` |  | (21) |

∎

###### Remark 3.

The per-token losses $`\ell_{t}`$ are per-step costs, and
$`g_{\mathrm{seq}}`$ is the credit assigned to each token for its effect
on the future costs it steers the sequence towards. Keeping it in full
is the undiscounted, full-horizon policy gradient, which corresponds to
a discount factor of $`\gamma=1`$; the surrogate gradient drops it
entirely, crediting each token only for its immediate cost, that is the
discount factor is taken to be $`\gamma=0`$ ([Lu & Thinking Machines
Lab, 2025](#bib.bib37)). The myopic token-level algorithm used in
practice is therefore the surrogate, not the sequence-level objective:
the two objectives are equal (Fact
[1](#Thmfact1 "Fact 1 (The token- and sequence-level objectives can be equal). ‣ A.1.2 How the different objectives and their gradients compare ‣ A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")),
and it is only the gradient that is truncated. This is why the surrogate
dynamics do not in general follow the gradient of any well-defined
objective ([Czarnecki et al., 2019](#bib.bib15)), the field
$`\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sur\text{-}tok}}`$ being
non-conservative once $`p=\pi_{\theta}`$ moves with $`\theta`$.

###### Remark 4.

Dropping $`g_{\mathrm{seq}}`$ is not only a modeling choice and can be a
practical necessity. As a score-function estimator over full rollouts,
the gradient $`g_{\mathrm{seq}}`$ is high-variance and expensive to
estimate, a difficulty at the heart of reinforcement learning with
policy gradients ([Williams, 1992](#bib.bib61); [Schulman et al.,
2016](#bib.bib51)). It is also the term that makes the student account
for how its own choices reshape the sequence distribution on which it is
later scored. This is consistent with the reward hacking observed when
solely optimizing the sequence-level reverse KL divergence ([Gu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22)).

###### Remark 5.

This is the observation that lets the rest of the paper ignore the
sequence-modeling aspects of distillation in our toy model of Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 A toy model of language modeling ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").
Once $`g_{\mathrm{seq}}`$ is dropped, every gradient the algorithm takes
is an average of per-context gradients of $`\ell_{\mathrm{tok}}`$, and
the contexts only enter through the distribution $`p`$ that generates
them. The toy model of Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 A toy model of language modeling ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
keeps exactly this structure, a set of contexts and a per-context
divergence, and discards the autoregressive coupling between them.

#### A.2 Practical evaluation of the token-level divergence

Evaluating the token-level divergence $`\ell`$ in Equation
[1](#S2.E1 "In Distillation objective. ‣ 2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
over the full vocabulary can be expensive. Two strategies reduce this
cost: estimating the divergence from a single sampled token, or
restricting the sum to a top-$`k`$ subset of the vocabulary.

##### A.2.1 Sampled-token evaluation

Instead of summing over the vocabulary, one can estimate $`\ell_{t}`$
from a single sampled token, reducing the cost to a single teacher
probability per context. This is the only option when the teacher is
implicit, as in next-token cross-entropy during pretraining, but is also
used with explicit teachers for efficiency. The gradient of this
estimate needs care when the sample comes from the student. We
illustrate with the reverse KL, where $`y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}`$; the
same applies to the student branch of the generalized Jensen–Shannon
divergence but not to the forward KL, which samples from the teacher.

Naively differentiating the sampled log-ratio at fixed $`y_{t}`$ gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi^{*}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}=\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t}),
``` |  | (22) |

since the teacher does not depend on $`\theta`$, and its expected value
vanishes as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}}[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})]=0,
``` |  | (23) |

whereas $`\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{t}`$ is generally nonzero, because this
estimate ignores that the distribution from which $`y_{t}`$ is drawn
itself depends on $`\theta`$. Accounting for it through the
score-function identity

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}}[f\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})+\nabla_{\theta}f]
``` |  | (24) |

with $`f`$ the log-ratio, the integrand term
$`\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}}[\nabla_{\theta}f]`$ is exactly the
vanishing naive estimate above, so only the score-function term remains,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi^{*}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})\right].
``` |  | (25) |

This is a second score function, taken at the output token level instead
of the sequence level as in the prefix-visitation gradient
$`g_{\mathrm{seq}}`$ of Fact
[2](#Thmfact2 "Fact 2 (The token-level gradient and its surrogate). ‣ A.1.3 What the stop-gradients used in practice ignore ‣ A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

###### Practical implementations.

The on-policy distillation recipe of [Lu & Thinking Machines Lab
(2025)](#bib.bib37) combines this approximation with the one of Remark
[3](#Thmremark3 "Remark 3. ‣ A.1.3 What the stop-gradients used in practice ignore ‣ A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").
It estimates $`\ell_{t}`$ from the sampled token, using
$`-\operatorname{stop-grad}[\log(\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})/\pi^{*}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t}))]`$
as a per-token advantage inside a standard policy-gradient pipeline, and
it truncates the cost-to-go to the immediate term ($`\gamma=0`$). The
first is the Monte Carlo approximation of the local loss gradient over
the action space discussed above. The second is the truncation of the
sequence-level gradient over time that Appendix
[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
analyzes. The two are independent of each other.

##### A.2.2 Top-$`k`$ restrictions: renormalization and tail bucket

When the teacher’s full probability vector is available, it is common to
restrict the divergence to a subset $`S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}`$ of the
vocabulary, typically the top-$`k`$ tokens of the teacher or the
student, for compute and bandwidth efficiency. Given such a subset,
there are two standard ways to handle the probability mass outside
$`S`$.

###### Renormalization.

Both distributions are renormalized over $`S`$ and the divergence is
computed between the restricted distributions:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\ell_{\text{renorm}}(\pi_{\theta},\pi^{*};S)=\ell\!\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}|_{S}}{M_{\theta}},\frac{\pi^{*}|_{S}}{M^{*}}\right),
``` |  | (26) |

where $`M_{\theta}=\sum_{j\in S}\pi_{\theta}(j)`$ and
$`M^{*}=\sum_{j\in S}\pi^{*}(j)`$ are the total masses on $`S`$, and
$`\pi|_{S}`$ denotes the restriction of $`\pi`$ to $`S`$. This discards
the tail entirely: the gradient with respect to logits outside $`S`$ is
zero, and the student receives no signal about how much total mass to
place on the top-$`k`$ set.

###### Tail bucket.

All mass outside $`S`$ is lumped into a single extra category and the
divergence is computed over $`k+1`$ tokens:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\ell_{\text{tail}}(\pi_{\theta},\pi^{*};S)=\ell\!\left(\tilde{\pi}_{\theta},\tilde{\pi}^{*}\right),\quad\text{with}\quad\tilde{\pi}(j)=\begin{cases}\pi(j)&j\in S,\\
1-\sum_{j^{\prime}\in S}\pi(j^{\prime})&j=\text{tail}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (27) |

This preserves the total-mass constraint: the student is trained to
match the teacher’s tail mass as a lump sum, which provides one
additional degree of freedom compared to renormalization.

As Section
[3.6](#S3.SS6 "3.6 Top-𝑘 restrictions shift entropy depending on how the tail is handled ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
and Appendix
[B.6](#A2.SS6 "B.6 Additional results on top-𝑘 ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
show, the choice between the two has a direct effect on the entropy of
the student.

#### A.3 The divergence families we consider

Forward and reverse KL are the two main focuses of our analysis, but the
transition between them is itself informative, so we also train on a
family that interpolates between the two. We use the generalized
Jensen–Shannon divergence, the objective family of [Agarwal et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib1) also used in other works ([Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib27), e.g.,), which compares both models to their mixture

|     |                                                    |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       m_{\lambda}=\lambda\pi^{*}+(1-\lambda)\pi_{\theta}
       ```                                                 |     | (28) |

through

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{JS}_{\lambda}(\pi^{*},\pi_{\theta})=\lambda D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}\parallel m_{\lambda})+(1-\lambda)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\parallel m_{\lambda}),
``` |  | (29) |

with $`\lambda\in[0,1]`$. The two KL divergences are recovered in the
limits, after dividing by the prefactor that vanishes there.

As $`\lambda\to 0`$ the mixture tends to $`\pi_{\theta}`$, so

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\parallel m_{\lambda})=O(\lambda^{2})
``` |  | (30) |

and

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\mathrm{JS}_{\lambda}}{\lambda}\to D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}\parallel\pi_{\theta}),
``` |  | (31) |

which is the forward KL.

As $`\lambda\to 1`$ the mixture tends to $`\pi^{*}`$ and symmetrically

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\mathrm{JS}_{\lambda}}{1-\lambda}\to D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\parallel\pi^{*}),
``` |  | (32) |

the reverse KL.

Both endpoints of Equation
[29](#A1.E29 "In A.3 The divergence families we consider ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
are identically zero, so only the interior of the family has a gradient;
we take the divergence at $`\lambda=0`$ and $`\lambda=1`$ to be its
normalized limits, that is the exact forward and reverse KL, which makes
the whole interval trainable, matching the implementations of [Agarwal
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib1) and [von Werra et al. (2020)](#bib.bib57).
This scale mismatch is not a problem to compare learning dynamics and
their observables (like entropy), as Adam ([Kingma & Ba,
2015](#bib.bib30)) is almost invariant to the overall scale of the loss
([Balles & Hennig, 2018](#bib.bib3)).

###### $`f`$-divergences.

Throughout the theory, we will sometimes use results that hold for a
broad class of divergences, $`f`$-divergences, which includes the
generalized Jensen–Shannon family and its two endpoints. We introduce
them here for completeness and make the connection to the generalized
Jensen–Shannon family explicit. Given a convex function $`f`$ with
$`f(1)=0`$, the $`f`$-divergence of a distribution $`a`$ from a
reference distribution $`b`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{f}(a\parallel b)=\sum_{j}b(j)f\left(\frac{a(j)}{b(j)}\right).
``` |  | (33) |

The reverse KL is recovered with $`f(t)=t\log t`$, the forward KL with
$`f(t)=-\log t`$, and the generalized Jensen–Shannon divergence with

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f_{\lambda}(t)=-\lambda\log\big(\lambda+(1-\lambda)t\big)+(1-\lambda)t\log\frac{t}{\lambda+(1-\lambda)t}.
``` |  | (34) |

What will matter to us is that all members of this class that are twice
differentiable at $`1`$ share the same local geometry around $`a=b`$,
namely the Fisher metric scaled by the curvature
$`f^{\prime\prime}(1)`$, which is the statement of Appendix
[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Divergence choice does not matter around optimality ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
and what makes the diffuse limit of Appendix
[B.5](#A2.SS5 "B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
divergence-independent. That curvature is $`f^{\prime\prime}(1)=1`$ for
both KL directions and
$`f_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}(1)=\lambda(1-\lambda)`$ for the generalized
Jensen–Shannon divergence, which vanishes at the two endpoints and is
brought back to $`1`$ by the $`1/\lambda`$ and $`1/(1-\lambda)`$
normalizations above.

### Appendix B Theory

#### B.1 Divergence choice does not matter around optimality

We now show the claim of Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Divergence choice only matters when student and teacher differ ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"):
at a fixed data-generating process, the forward and reverse KL
objectives have the same gradient to first order around
$`\pi_{\theta}=\pi^{*}`$. The statement is per context, so fix a context
$`i`$ and write $`\pi_{\theta}`$ and $`\pi^{*}`$ for the two next-token
distributions there. The forward KL gradient is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}\parallel\pi_{\theta})=-\sum_{j}\pi^{*}(j)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(j),
``` |  | (35) |

the teacher being independent of $`\theta`$. For the reverse KL,
differentiating the product and using
$`\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{\theta}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}`$
gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\parallel\pi^{*})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}(j)\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(j)}{\pi^{*}(j)}\right)+\sum_{j}\pi_{\theta}(j)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(j)`$ |  | (36) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\pi_{\theta}(j)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(j)\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(j)}{\pi^{*}(j)}\right),`$ |  | (37) |

the second term vanishing by the vocabulary-level score identity of
Equation
[23](#A1.E23 "In A.2.1 Sampled-token evaluation ‣ A.2 Practical evaluation of the token-level divergence ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").
Around optimality the log ratio linearizes as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(j)}{\pi^{*}(j)}\right)=-\log\left(\frac{\pi^{*}(j)}{\pi_{\theta}(j)}\right)\approx 1-\frac{\pi^{*}(j)}{\pi_{\theta}(j)},
``` |  | (38) |

and substituting it, the constant term vanishes by the score identity
again, leaving

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\parallel\pi^{*})\approx-\sum_{j}\pi^{*}(j)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(j)=\nabla_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}\parallel\pi_{\theta}).
``` |  | (39) |

Averaging the two sides over contexts sampled from any data-generating
process $`p`$ preserves the equality, as $`p`$ only weights the contexts
and weights them identically on both sides. This is why the statement is
independent of $`p`$: the surrogate gradients of Definition
[3](#Thmdefinition3 "Definition 3 (Surrogate token-level objective). ‣ A.1.1 The different objectives ‣ A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
for the forward and the reverse KL agree to first order whatever the
data-generating process is, as long as it is held fixed. Near
optimality, the choice between the two divergences is therefore
immaterial, and whatever difference remains between two distillation
runs has to come from the data they are trained on.

This equivalence is well known: it is a classical result from
information geometry that all suitably standardized $`f`$-divergences,
of which forward and reverse KL are special cases, agree up to second
order around $`\pi_{\theta}=\pi^{*}`$, with the shared local quadratic
form given by the Fisher information metric ([Amari, 2016](#bib.bib2);
[Polyanskiy & Wu, 2025](#bib.bib48)). The whole generalized
Jensen–Shannon family of Appendix
[A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 The divergence families we consider ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
is therefore also equivalent to its two endpoints in this regime.

Importantly, the expansion above requires the perturbation
$`\pi_{\theta}-\pi^{*}`$ to be small relative to $`\pi^{*}`$ on every
token, that is $`|\log(\pi_{\theta}(j)/\pi^{*}(j))|`$ uniformly small.
This is a much stronger requirement than a small average KL divergence,
since the KL weights log ratios by probability mass and is therefore
blind to large relative perturbations on low-probability tokens, which
is precisely where the gradients of the two objectives differ.

#### B.3 The forward KL inflates entropy

This appendix gives the formal backing for the claim in Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Forward KL predictably inflates entropy ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
that the forward KL inflates entropy. Assume the student family is
closed under temperature scaling of the logits, which holds for any
softmax model whose final linear layer can be rescaled, including
standard language model heads. As above we write $`i`$ for a context,
$`\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}(\cdot\mid i)`$ for the student at a
stationary point $`\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}`$ of the forward KL
objective and $`z(i)`$ for its logits there, and take the contexts to be
drawn from a data-generating process $`p`$ that is held fixed, as the
surrogate objective of Definition
[3](#Thmdefinition3 "Definition 3 (Surrogate token-level objective). ‣ A.1.1 The different objectives ‣ A.1 Token- and sequence-level objectives and where gradients are stopped ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
does.

Define
$`g(i)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}}[z(i)]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}[z(i)]`$,
the mean-logit mismatch in context $`i`$, which is also the derivative
of the loss in $`i`$ when sharpening the logits along the temperature
direction. To see this, perturb the logits along the temperature
direction, $`z(i,\varepsilon)=(1+\varepsilon)z(i)`$, so that the loss in
$`i`$ becomes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\ell^{\mathrm{for}}(i,\varepsilon)=-(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}[z(i)]+\log Z(i,\varepsilon)
``` |  | (40) |

up to a term that does not depend on the student, with
$`Z(i,\varepsilon)=\sum_{j}\exp(z(i,\varepsilon)_{j})`$ the partition
function of the perturbed student. Using
$`\nabla_{z}\log Z=\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}^{(\varepsilon)}`$,
the chain rule gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\ell^{\mathrm{for}}(i,\varepsilon)=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}[z(i)]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}^{(\varepsilon)}}[z(i)],
``` |  | (41) |

which at $`\varepsilon=0`$ reduces to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\ell^{\mathrm{for}}(i,0)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}}[z(i)]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}[z(i)]=g(i).
``` |  | (42) |

At any interior stationary point of the objective, the derivative of the
total loss along the temperature direction must vanish, which is exactly

|     |                         |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}_{i}[g(i)]=0,
       ```                      |     | (43) |

the expectation being over contexts drawn from $`p`$, each token
counting once as in Equation
[1](#S2.E1 "In Distillation objective. ‣ 2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

The difference in entropy between the student and the teacher can be
rewritten to make its dependence on $`g`$ explicit:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle H(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}})-H(\pi^{*})`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}}\left[\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}\left[\log\pi^{*}\right]`$ |  | (44) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}\left[\log\pi^{*}-\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}\left[\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}}\left[\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}\right]`$ |  | (45) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}\parallel\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}})+\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{*}}\left[z(i)-\log Z(i)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}}}\left[z(i)-\log Z(i)\right]`$ |  | (46) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}\parallel\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}})-g(i),`$ |  | (47) |

where the last equality comes from the fact that $`\log Z`$ is constant
in the token and therefore cancels. Leveraging the stationarity
condition on $`g`$ (Equation
[43](#A2.E43 "In B.3 The forward KL inflates entropy ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"))
yields

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[H(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[H(\pi^{*})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}\parallel\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}})\right].
``` |  | (48) |

Note that nothing in the argument constrains $`p`$: the identity is a
property of the forward KL alone, the data-generating process only
setting which contexts the two averages run over. When the teacher is
implicit and only accessible through its samples, as in pretraining, the
right-hand side is the cross-entropy loss, which gives the form of
Equation
[6](#S3.E6 "In 3.3 Forward KL predictably inflates entropy ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
that Section
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Cross-entropy predicts entropy in pretraining and supervised finetuning ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
tests.

This is an instance of a classical fact from information geometry: along
its temperature direction, the student is a one-parameter exponential
family with its own logits as sufficient statistic. Stationarity of the
forward KL divergence is moment matching of that statistic ([Wainwright
& Jordan, 2008](#bib.bib58)), and the entropy of a moment-matched
projection exceeds the entropy of its target by exactly their divergence
([Amari, 2016](#bib.bib2)).

###### From average to pointwise inflation.

So far, we have shown that entropy increases when averaging over
contexts. A natural follow-up question is whether the same holds in each
context individually, that is, whether the entropy gap is always
positive, or equivalently whether
$`g(i)<D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}\parallel\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}})`$
everywhere. The statement becomes pointwise as soon as the family
contains per-context temperature directions, meaning that for every
context the perturbation rescaling $`z(i)`$ while leaving the logits in
other contexts unchanged is tangent to the family. This is the case when
the model has a learned input-dependent temperature on its logits, and
approximately when the body is expressive enough to rescale its
last-layer representation in one context without affecting the others.
Stationarity along these directions forces $`g(i)=0`$ separately in
every context, and the identity becomes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}})=H(\pi^{*})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{*}\parallel\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{for}}})
``` |  | (49) |

in every context: the student entropy is strictly inflated wherever the
student fails to match the teacher, by exactly the misfit there. Note
that per-context temperature scaling is far weaker than realizability,
as a single scalar per context cannot match a full distribution, so the
pointwise statement is not vacuous. This average-versus-pointwise
structure echoes the calibration literature, where stationarity along a
single shared rescaling direction yields guarantees that hold on average
over inputs ([Clarté et al., 2023](#bib.bib12)), while near-optimality
against richer families of input-dependent post-processings yields
correspondingly stronger pointwise ones ([Błasiok et al.,
2023](#bib.bib7)).

#### B.4 Reverse KL: a flatness statement

A general statement on entropy similar to the one we obtained for the
forward KL does not hold for the reverse one. We apply the same recipe,
decomposing the misfit and then exploiting stationarity along the
temperature direction, but it yields only a flatness statement. Writing
$`\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}`$ for the student at a stationary
point $`\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}`$ of the reverse KL objective and
expanding the divergence as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}\parallel\pi^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}[\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}[\log\pi^{*}]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}[-\log\pi^{*}]-H(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}),
``` |  | (50) |

adding $`H(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}})`$ and subtracting
$`H(\pi^{*})`$ and rearranging gives, in every context,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}})=H(\pi^{*})+\Delta(i)-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}\parallel\pi^{*})\quad\text{with}\quad\Delta(i)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}[-\log\pi^{*}]-H(\pi^{*}).
``` |  | (51) |

The excess surprisal $`\Delta(i)`$ measures how much more surprising the
student’s samples are to the teacher than the teacher’s own samples are.

The student is therefore less confident than the teacher exactly when
$`\Delta(i)`$ exceeds the misfit
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}\parallel\pi^{*})`$.
The temperature-direction argument no longer pins this sign down; it
instead yields a flatness statement. Perturbing the logits along the
temperature direction, the derivative of the reverse KL loss in context
$`i`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\ell^{\mathrm{rev}}(i,0)=\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}})-\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}},\log\pi^{*}).
``` |  | (52) |

Requiring the total derivative to vanish gives the stationarity
condition on average over contexts,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}},\log\pi^{*})\right].
``` |  | (53) |

Bounding each covariance by Cauchy–Schwarz,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{Cov}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}},\log\pi^{*})\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}})\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi^{*})},
``` |  | (54) |

applying Cauchy–Schwarz a second time to the average over contexts,
dividing by
$`\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{i}[\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}})]}`$
and squaring gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\mathrm{Var}_{\pi_{\theta^{*}_{\mathrm{rev}}}}(\log\pi^{*})\right].
``` |  | (55) |

As a result, the same techniques that provided an entropy statement for
the forward KL only provide a flatness statement here.

#### B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit

Empirically (Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")),
we find that when the number of contexts is large (high load) or the
teacher is diffuse (high perplexity), the reverse KL stops being
mode-seeking and inflates entropy, as the forward KL does. Here, we
expand the distillation objective around a diffuse teacher and show that
to leading order, forward KL, reverse KL and the whole generalized
Jensen–Shannon family reduce to the same quadratic objective and
therefore share the same solution. This is why the top right corner of
Figure
[7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ B.2 Where the entropy gap changes sign ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
in which both the teacher perplexity and the number of contexts are
largest looks the same for every divergence.

###### The setup.

We work in the toy model of Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 A toy model of language modeling ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
in which the teacher $`\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i)`$ is drawn independently of
the embedding $`e_{i}`$, from a law that is invariant under permutations
of the vocabulary. Stack the embeddings into
$`E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times h}`$, whose $`i`$-th row is $`e_{i}^{\top}`$,
and write $`P_{E}`$ for the orthogonal projector onto its column space
in $`\mathbb{R}^{n}`$. For simplicity, we assume that $`n>h`$. Random
embeddings are in general position, so $`\mathrm{rank}(E)=h`$. The
student head produces the logit matrix
$`Z_{\theta}=EW^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}`$, so each of its
columns is constrained to the $`h`$-dimensional column space of $`E`$.
The ratio $`h/n`$ is therefore the fraction of the $`n`$ context
directions the student can exactly represent.

###### The diffuse limit.

We make the teacher diffuse, keeping $`h/n`$ arbitrary. We model it by
fixing the teacher logits $`z^{*}(i)`$ and scaling them down by a factor
$`\beta\to 0`$, so that the teacher distribution becomes uniform in the
limit. Write the teacher and the student in centered logit coordinates,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathrm{softmax}\big(\beta z^{*}(i)\big),`$ |  | (56) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathrm{softmax}\big(z_{\theta}(i)\big),`$ |  | (57) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{1}^{\top}z^{*}(i)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}^{\top}z_{\theta}(i)=0,`$ |  | (58) |

with the logits $`z^{*}(i)`$ fixed and drawn independently across
contexts.

All $`f`$-divergences that are twice differentiable at $`1`$ agree to
second order around coinciding distributions, and their common quadratic
form is the Fisher metric scaled by $`f^{\prime\prime}(1)`$ ([Amari,
2016](#bib.bib2)); this is the argument we used in Appendix
[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Divergence choice does not matter around optimality ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
for the two KL directions. In logit coordinates it reads

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{f}\big(\mathrm{softmax}(z+\delta)\parallel\mathrm{softmax}(z)\big)=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(1)}{2}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{softmax}(z)}(\delta)+O(\lVert\delta\rVert^{3}),
``` |  | (59) |

the variance being over tokens drawn from the reference distribution,
the second argument of $`D_{f}`$. The expansion holds as long as the
offset $`\delta`$ is small, which here means that all the logits
involved are $`O(\beta)`$: the teacher’s by construction, and the
student’s because we expand around the branch on which it stays that
close to uniform. We apply it with the teacher as the reference and
$`\delta=z_{\theta}(i)-\beta z^{*}(i)`$. The teacher is itself
$`O(\beta)`$ away from the uniform distribution, so
$`\mathrm{Var}_{\pi^{*}}`$ can in turn be replaced by
$`\mathrm{Var}_{u}`$ at the price of a further $`O(\beta^{3})`$ term,
and for centered logits
$`\mathrm{Var}_{u}(\delta)=\lVert\delta\rVert^{2}/d`$. Stacking the
logits into $`Z^{*},Z_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}`$, this gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[D_{f}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i)\parallel\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i)\big)\right]=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(1)}{2nd}\lVert Z_{\theta}-\beta Z^{*}\rVert_{F}^{2}+O(\beta^{3}).
``` |  | (60) |

Both KL directions are of this form, with $`\pi^{*}`$ as the reference
distribution and $`f^{\prime\prime}(1)=1`$, so they have the same
minimizer. The same argument applies to the generalized Jensen–Shannon
family of Appendix
[A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 The divergence families we consider ‣ Appendix A The distillation framework ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

Minimizing Equation
[60](#A2.E60 "In The diffuse limit. ‣ B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
over the achievable logits $`Z_{\theta}=EW^{\top}`$ is a least-squares
projection onto the column space of $`E`$, so the optimal student logits
are

|     |                                                   |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       Z_{\theta}^{\star}=\beta P_{E}Z^{*}+O(\beta^{2}).
       ```                                                |     | (61) |

The teacher’s logits have scale $`O(\beta)`$, the fitted ones only
$`O(\beta\sqrt{h/n})`$: the entries of $`Z^{*}`$ are independent and
$`P_{E}`$ is a projector of rank $`h`$ that does not depend on them, so

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{Z^{*}}\lVert P_{E}Z^{*}\rVert_{F}^{2}=\frac{h}{n}\mathbb{E}_{Z^{*}}\lVert Z^{*}\rVert_{F}^{2}.
``` |  | (62) |

As a result, increasing the number of contexts while keeping the model
size fixed, which is what we do in our experiments, makes the student
logits smaller relative to the teacher’s, and the student distribution
closer to uniform.

###### Entropy inflation.

Applying Equation
[59](#A2.E59 "In The diffuse limit. ‣ B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
again, this time with the uniform distribution as the reference and
combining it with $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\parallel u)=\log d-H(\pi)`$
provides an entropy expansion: a distribution
$`\mathrm{softmax}(\delta)`$ with small centered logits has entropy

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H(\mathrm{softmax}(\delta))=\log d-\lVert\delta\rVert^{2}/(2d)+O(\lVert\delta\rVert^{3}).
``` |  | (63) |

Applying this to the teacher and to the student at the optimum of
Equation
[61](#A2.E61 "In The diffuse limit. ‣ B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{H}(\pi^{*})=\log d-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2nd}\lVert Z^{*}\rVert_{F}^{2}+O(\beta^{3}),\qquad\bar{H}(\pi_{\theta})=\log d-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2nd}\lVert P_{E}Z^{*}\rVert_{F}^{2}+O(\beta^{3}),
``` |  | (64) |

so that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{H}(\pi_{\theta})-\bar{H}(\pi^{*})=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2nd}\lVert(I-P_{E})Z^{*}\rVert_{F}^{2}+O(\beta^{3}).
``` |  | (65) |

That is, for small enough $`\beta`$ values, the student inflates entropy
relative to the teacher.

If we further assume that the numbers of contexts and tokens are large
enough that we can replace the teacher-related quantities by their
expected values, the entropy gap only depends on the student through the
single ratio $`h/n`$. Indeed

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{Z^{*}}\lVert(I-P_{E})Z^{*}\rVert_{F}^{2}=\left(1-\frac{h}{n}\right)\mathbb{E}_{Z^{*}}\lVert Z^{*}\rVert_{F}^{2},
``` |  | (66) |

so that Equation
[65](#A2.E65 "In Entropy inflation. ‣ B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
becomes

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bar{H}(\pi_{\theta})-\bar{H}(\pi^{*})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2nd}\left(1-\frac{h}{n}\right)\mathbb{E}_{Z^{*}}\lVert Z^{*}\rVert_{F}^{2}+O(\beta^{3})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{h}{n}\right)\left(\log d-\bar{H}(\pi^{*})\right)+O(\beta^{3}),`$ |  | (67) |

the second line recognizing in the first the teacher’s entropy deficit
below uniform of Equation
[64](#A2.E64 "In Entropy inflation. ‣ B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").
It follows that, in the diffuse high-load limit, the gap increases as
the number of contexts $`n`$ increases, and decreases as the perplexity
of the teacher increases, consistently with Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")b
and
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")c.

Evaluating Equation
[60](#A2.E60 "In The diffuse limit. ‣ B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
at the optimum and comparing it to Equation
[65](#A2.E65 "In Entropy inflation. ‣ B.5 Reverse KL inflates entropy in the diffuse high-load limit ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
gives one last identity,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[D_{f}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i)\parallel\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i)\big)\right]=f^{\prime\prime}(1)\left(\bar{H}(\pi_{\theta})-\bar{H}(\pi^{*})\right)+O(\beta^{3}),
``` |  | (68) |

so for either KL direction the residual divergence equals the entropy
inflation. For the forward KL this is Equation
[6](#S3.E6 "In 3.3 Forward KL predictably inflates entropy ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
which holds for any number of contexts and any teacher; reverse KL obeys
it too in the diffuse limit.

#### B.7 Setup of the toy model experiments

This section details the toy model of Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 A toy model of language modeling ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
and the simulations of Figures
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
[3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.6 Top-𝑘 restrictions shift entropy depending on how the tail is handled ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
[7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ B.2 Where the entropy gap changes sign ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
and
[8](#A2.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ B.6 Additional results on top-𝑘 ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation").

###### Model.

The vocabulary has $`d=64`$ tokens and the embeddings have dimension
$`h=128`$. Each of the $`n`$ contexts is assigned an embedding $`e_{i}`$
drawn from a standard Gaussian distribution in $`\mathbb{R}^{h}`$ and
normalized to unit norm, which stays fixed during training. The student
is a linear layer followed by a softmax,
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid i)=\mathrm{softmax}(We_{i}+b)`$, with
$`W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times h}`$ initialized with LeCun normal
initialization and $`b`$ initialized at zero. The main text omits the
bias $`b`$ for simplicity.

###### Teachers.

For each context $`i`$, we draw a target perplexity $`P_{i}`$ and
Gaussian logits $`\zeta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})`$, and set
$`\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i)=\mathrm{softmax}(\zeta_{i}/\tau_{i})`$, with the
temperature $`\tau_{i}`$ chosen so that
$`H(\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid i))=\log P_{i}`$. By default, the perplexities are
drawn independently and log-uniformly in $`[1,16]`$, so that contexts
differ both in which tokens the teacher favors and in how diffuse it is.
The teacher perplexity sweep of Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")c
and the heatmaps of Figure
[7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ B.2 Where the entropy gap changes sign ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
instead give the same perplexity to all contexts. All contexts have the
same weight in the objective.

###### Training.

Every step uses all contexts, and the student is trained with Adam
([Kingma & Ba, 2015](#bib.bib30)) and a constant learning rate of
$`0.1`$ for $`40{,}000`$ steps. All results are averaged over $`5`$
seeds, each of which redraws the embeddings, the teachers and the
initialization.

###### Sweeps.

The default number of contexts is $`n=2048`$. Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")b
uses $`21`$ values of $`n`$ log-spaced between $`192`$ and $`67{,}584`$,
Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")c
uses $`21`$ perplexities log-spaced between $`1`$ and $`32`$, and Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")d
uses $`21`$ weights $`\lambda`$ log-spaced between $`0.001`$ and
$`0.99`$. The trajectories of Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")e
and the heatmaps of Figure
[7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ B.2 Where the entropy gap changes sign ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
use $`\lambda\in\{0,0.01,0.1,0.5,1\}`$, the heatmaps over $`10`$
perplexities log-spaced between $`1.5`$ and $`32`$ and $`8`$ numbers of
contexts log-spaced between $`128`$ and $`4096`$. The top-$`k`$
experiments of Figures
[3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.6 Top-𝑘 restrictions shift entropy depending on how the tail is handled ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
and
[8](#A2.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ B.6 Additional results on top-𝑘 ‣ Appendix B Theory ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
use $`n=512`$ contexts and $`k\in\{1,4,16,64\}`$, and Figure

accuracy each objective can reach.

#### C.3 Setup of the on- vs. off-policy distillation experiments

These experiments vary the sampling distribution and the divergence
independently, as in Section
[2](#S2 "2 Decoupling sampling and divergence choice in distillation ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
on a standard reasoning distillation task. Following [Zhao et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib67), we distill the Qwen3 instruct models with thinking
disabled, at $`1.7`$B, $`4`$B and $`8`$B parameters, into
Qwen3-0.6B-Base ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib64)), on the math problems
of DeepScaleR ([Luo et al., 2025](#bib.bib38)). The prefixes on which
the token-level divergence is evaluated are either completions sampled
from the teacher once and cached before training (off-policy), or
completions sampled from the current student at every step (on-policy).
The divergence is either the forward or the reverse KL, computed on the
full vocabulary at every position of the prefix. Crossing the two prefix
sources with the two divergences gives four objectives, and with three

###### The prompt frame.

A token-level divergence compares the two models on the same prefix, so
they have to share a prompt frame, yet they work best in different ones.
The base student degrades inside the chat template, from $`42.87\%`$ to
$`14.93\%`$ accuracy on MATH500 ([Lightman et al., 2024](#bib.bib36)),
whereas the instruct teachers remain accurate outside of it but almost
never stop, as there is no turn to end. We therefore prompt both models
without chat template, with the problem text followed by the instruction
“Let’s think step by step and output the final answer within \boxed{}.”,
cut each teacher completion right after its first boxed answer, and
append the end-of-text token of the student. This avoids a format warmup
stage, which would amount to off-policy forward KL distillation, that
is, to one of the four objectives being compared.

###### The teacher corpus and the training length.

Teacher completions are sampled at temperature $`1`$ without top-$`p`$
or top-$`k`$ truncation, unlike in [Zhao et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib67),
because the objective is an expectation under the distribution of the
teacher. Completions without a boxed answer within $`4096`$ tokens are
dropped, which keeps about two thirds of them and biases the corpus
towards problems that the teacher solves concisely. Training runs for
$`800`$ steps of $`32`$ prompts, just below one pass over the smallest
corpus, so that no teacher benefits from more repetitions than another.
We add Qwen3-1.7B to the two teachers of [Zhao et al.
(2026a)](#bib.bib67) because Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B are statistically
indistinguishable on AMC23 ($`66.2\%`$ against $`66.6\%`$ accuracy).

###### Evaluation.

We measure the entropy of the student on its own completions, whose
contexts drift during training, and on a fixed set of cached teacher
completions, on which only the weights of the student can change.
Evaluation completions are generated with a fixed seed, so that
neighboring evaluation points share contexts. We report the entropy
pooled across tokens, as [Zhao et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib67) do. As it
weights each completion by its length, on the student’s own completions
it also moves when which completions are long changes, and the
per-sequence average, which we also log, can disagree with it in sign.
The frozen MATH500 completions keep all $`200`$ problems, whereas the
held-out DeepScaleR problems go through the same filtering as the
training corpus and differ from one teacher to the other, so we compare
teachers on MATH500 only. Endpoint accuracy is measured at temperature
$`0.6`$ and top-$`p`$ $`0.95`$ with a budget of $`8192`$ tokens,
averaged over three samples per problem.

###### Pure bfloat16 training freezes the normalization gains.

Following [Zhao et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib67), we train in bfloat16
without float32 master weights. All normalization gains then stay
bit-identical throughout training, as the bfloat16 spacing at their
typical magnitude is four orders of magnitude above the optimizer step,
while the weight matrices do move. As the final normalization gain
controls the scale of the logits, these runs cannot test any statement
about that scale, and we make none.

#### C.5 Additional self-distillation experiments on GSM8K

The main text follows the experimental setup of [Hübotter et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib27) on the Chemistry dataset. We repeat it here on GSM8K
([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib13)) with Qwen3-0.6B ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib64)), a testbed cheap enough to sweep the learning rate
and to train ten times longer; Table
[2](#A3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ C.6 Hyperparameters of the self-distillation experiments ‣ Appendix C Additional experimental results ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
lists the hyperparameters of both setups. Figure
[12](#A3.F12 "Figure 12 ‣ C.5 Additional self-distillation experiments on GSM8K ‣ Appendix C Additional experimental results ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
is the counterpart of Figure
[6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.3 Self-distillation deflates entropy, divergence can compensate for it ‣ 4 Entropy in different distillation scenarios ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation"),
at a learning rate of $`10^{-5}`$, and shows the same qualitative
behavior: entropy decreases monotonically with the generalized
Jensen–Shannon weight $`\lambda`$, a teacher that follows the student
too closely collapses, and restricting the divergence to fewer tokens
raises entropy.

The learning rate sweep of Figure
[13](#A3.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ C.5 Additional self-distillation experiments on GSM8K ‣ Appendix C Additional experimental results ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
shows that the more a configuration deflates entropy, the smaller the
learning rate it tolerates. Reverse KL already loses to the base model
at $`10^{-5}`$, whereas the arms closer to forward KL keep improving up
to $`3\cdot 10^{-5}`$, and a slowly moving or frozen teacher tolerates
rates at which a live one has already collapsed.

We finally use the same testbed to train ten times longer, and ask
whether the sharp dependence of entropy on $`\lambda`$ that the toy
model predicts at convergence (Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")d)
appears once the runs are long enough. Figure
[14](#A3.F14 "Figure 14 ‣ C.5 Additional self-distillation experiments on GSM8K ‣ Appendix C Additional experimental results ‣ Appendix ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
shows that entropy does settle within $`500`$ steps. The trajectories
separate the two behaviors of Section
[3](#S3 "3 Properties of divergences and their effect on entropy ‣ Divergence controls entropy in distillation")
cleanly: the forward KL arm has entropy much larger than its initial
value, while every arm with $`\lambda>0`$ settles below it. The ordering
in $`\lambda`$ holds at every point of the trajectory, and it leaves the
gap between $`\lambda=0`$ and $`\lambda=0.25`$ as by far the largest of
the sweep, as a phase transition inside that interval would predict.
Five weights spaced $`0.25`$ apart are too coarse to confirm it.
However, note that each run is evaluated on on-policy data, that is
run-specific data, whereas the data was exactly the same in the toy
setup.
````

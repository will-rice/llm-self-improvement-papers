###### Abstract

Exploration bounds learning efficiency in reinforcement learning with
verifiable rewards (RLVR). Current methods explore mostly through
independent sampling, without fully using the information gathered along
the way, such as known wrong answers and environment feedback. We
propose LEAF, which injects a dynamic, problem-specific _memory_ into
the context before every rollout. The memory contains the most recent
response, its reward, and other environment feedback. It is updated
after every verifier call. We decouple policy updates from rollouts and
update the policy through self-distillation. On four benchmarks and two
open models, LEAF accelerates exploration by about $`4\times`$ compared
with a random sampling baseline. On unseen test sets, it improves
accuracy over the strongest baseline by 1.59 and 1.83 percentage points
on the two models, while using less compute than any RL baseline. These
results highlight the potential of LEAF in improving both exploration
and learning efficiency.

Code is available at:
[https://github.com/on1262/LEAF](https://github.com/on1262/LEAF).

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become the
main recipe for improving the reasoning and coding abilities of large
language models (LLMs) (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Yu et al.,
2025; Zheng et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025). It maximizes the expected
reward, which must be estimated at each rollout step. Since this
expectation cannot be computed exactly, most RLVR methods estimate it
with Monte Carlo methods (Metropolis and Ulam, 1949), which require
sampling several responses independently for each prompt.

In general RL, estimating advantages from rewards is challenging.
Group-based methods compute advantages using a group of independent
responses to the same prompt (Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025; Zheng
et al., 2025). A larger group yields more accurate estimates but reduces
rollout efficiency. Single-rollout methods such as PPO use a value
function (Schulman et al., 2017). However, this requires a critic
network. Learning an accurate critic is also challenging when training
LLMs (Shao et al., 2024; Kazemnejad et al., 2025).

Research on iterative refinement investigates how to improve later
responses using earlier attempts. Kumar et al. (2025) and Ekbote et al.
(2025) use multi-turn reinforcement learning to train revision behavior,
including intrinsic self-correction and refinement guided by execution
feedback. Some methods learn from feedback and transfer knowledge into
parameters. Song et al. (2026b) learns from feedback through
self-distillation or feedback modeling. Shi et al. (2026) uses
reinforcement learning combined with self-distillation.

Self-distillation methods (Hübotter et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; He
et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026;
Wu et al., 2026b) use the same model with privileged context, such as
feedback, memories, or skills, as the teacher for an unconditioned
student. Although self-distillation does not require duplicated
responses, model behavior changes only during the policy update phase,
without fully leveraging the model’s in-context learning ability.
Methods such as those proposed by Dilkes et al. (2026) and Liu et al.
(2026b) change exploration behavior using a fixed instruction or a
self-generated tip. However, they still use duplicated group sampling.
As Fig. 1 shows, our method is the only one of these designs that
combines rich feedback, inference adaptation and a single rollout per
step.

In our experiments, independent sampling tends to fail with the same
wrong answer. For example, with random sampling on MBPP+, 77.3%
(Qwen3-8B) and 78.0% (Olmo-3-7B-Instruct) of the failed samples
reproduce exactly the failing test output of an earlier sample for the
same problem, and on MATH-500 47% of the wrong answers repeat an earlier
wrong answer. When the previous answer is in the context, adding its
feedback significantly reduces the rate of repeating that wrong answer,
from 62.8% to 35.9% on average over MATH-500, LCB and MBPP+ and the two
models. Refer to Appendix A for details.

Motivated by the strong effect of in-context learning, this work asks
whether exploration can be improved by changing the _context_ of each
rollout. As illustrated by Fig. 2, our method keeps a memory for each
problem that contains the last response, its reward and its environment
feedback. During rollout, each problem only generates one sample per
step. We update the memory based on previous attempts on the same
problem. The memory is written into the rollout context as a short
dialogue, so each new attempt revises the previous one, as the
in-context refinement (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023). During
the policy update, the policy conditioned on the memory serves as the
teacher, and self-distillation (Agarwal et al., 2024; Snell et al.,
2022; Zhao et al., 2026) moves what the memory provides into the
parameters. We name it LEAF, short for Learning from Earlier Attempts
and Feedback, because each new attempt learns from the previous attempt
and the feedback on it in context, and the policy update then learns
from these attempts in its parameters.

We evaluate LEAF on four benchmarks, MATH-500 (mathematics),
LiveCodeBench and MBPP+ (code generation) and SciKnowEval (science),
with Qwen3-8B and Olmo-3-7B-Instruct. In exploration tasks, LEAF raises
the cumulative pass rate over Random Sampling by 4.88 pp on average and
reaches the final pass rate of Random Sampling with four times fewer
verifier calls. In generalization tasks on unseen test sets, LEAF has
the highest overall test accuracy, which is 1.59 pp (Qwen3-8B) and 1.83
pp (Olmo-3-7B-Instruct) above the strongest baseline. We perform
ablation studies on key components such as the feedback, the last
response and the restart schedule. Results show that removing any of
them lowers the final pass rate on both models.

## 2 Method

As illustrated in Fig. 2, in this section we introduce how LEAF explores
and how it updates the parameters. Refer to Appendix B for
implementation details and the exact prompts.

Figure 2: Overview of LEAF. Rollout: each problem keeps a memory of its
last response, reward and feedback, which forms the context of the next
attempt. The memory is updated after each trial. Policy update: after
all rollout steps are finished, the teacher conditioned on a successful
context teaches the student without context through self-distillation.

### 2.1 Preliminary

We first introduce the setting and notation. Given a set of problems
$`\mathcal{D}`$, each problem receives a budget of $`K`$ verifier calls,
and each call grades one answer. At step $`t`$, the environment returns
a rollout reward $`r_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$ and feedback $`f_{t}`$ for the
answer $`y_{t}`$. We measure exploration ability by the cumulative pass
rate

```math
\mathrm{pass}@k=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}}\max_{t\leq k}s_{t}(x), \tag{1}
```

where $`s_{t}(x)\in\{0,1\}`$ indicates whether $`y_{t}`$ is correct. The
environment feedback may expose $`s_{t}`$ only partially. In code
benchmarks the reward comes from public tests while $`s_{t}`$ also
requires hidden private tests, so $`\mathrm{pass}@k`$ is not a simple
aggregate of observed rewards.

### 2.2 Explore with memory

For problem $`x`$ at step $`t`$, the context $`c_{t}`$ is a short
dialogue: the problem $`x`$ as the first user turn, $`\hat{y}_{t-1}`$ as
the assistant turn, and a final user turn with the verdict $`r_{t-1}`$,
the feedback $`f_{t-1}`$ and an instruction to check the problem again
and give a corrected complete answer. We define the memory as
$`m_{t}=(\hat{y}_{t-1},r_{t-1},f_{t-1})`$, which is updated based on
previous attempts. In each step, the policy generates
$`y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{t})`$, and the verifier call on
$`y_{t}`$ produces $`m_{t+1}`$.

To prevent long previous answers from degrading the revision,
$`\hat{y}_{t-1}`$ in memory is a compressed response based on the raw
response $`y_{t-1}`$. We keep the last $`L=2{,}000`$ tokens of a longer
answer in $`\hat{y}_{t-1}`$ because the final result, code or conclusion
usually sits at the end.

When each attempt revises the previous one, the memory can also hold the
model on one line of solution. To keep previous attempts from misleading
subsequent ones, we therefore reset the memory every $`M=5`$ steps. At a
restart step, the policy drafts an answer
$`\tilde{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ and revises it once with a
blind instruction that asks it to check its own reasoning without any
feedback. Only the revision is sent to the verifier.

Exploration with memory can be used alone or together with a policy
update. In our experiments, updates during exploration changed the final
pass rate little (Section 3.3.3), so by default LEAF explores with fixed
parameters and updates the policy after all rollouts are done.

### 2.3 From memory to parameters

The memory changes the exploration behavior, but it cannot be applied to
a new problem. We distill the experience of rollouts into parameters by
self-distillation. Unlike methods that interleave sampling and training,
we first finish the rollout phase and then perform the policy update.
This keeps the implementation simple and avoids waiting for long outputs
inside every update step.

After a rollout of $`K`$ steps on the training problems, we collect the
set $`\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)`$ of contexts $`c_{t}`$ whose following
response $`y_{t}`$ is correct for each problem $`x`$. Each context holds
the memory that produced a correct answer, i.e. last response and
feedback. At each update step, we sample a batch $`\mathcal{B}`$ of
problems. For each $`x\in\mathcal{B}`$ we randomly draw a context from
$`\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)`$ and a student answer
$`y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ generated from the plain prompt. Then
we distill the token distribution from teacher to student.

At token position $`i`$ of $`y`$, the student distribution is
$`p_{i}=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<i})`$ and the teacher distribution
is $`q_{i}=\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c,y_{<i})]`$, where
$`\operatorname{sg}`$ stops the gradient. We compare them with a clipped
$`f`$-divergence over the full vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$,

```math
D_{f}^{\tau}(p_{i}\,\|\,q_{i})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\min\!\left(q_{i,v}\,f\!\left(\frac{p_{i,v}}{q_{i,v}}\right),\,\tau\right). \tag{2}
```

We use $`f(u)=u\log u`$ by default, which gives the reverse KL
divergence, i.e., $`\mathrm{KL}(p_{i}\,\|\,q_{i})`$. We also compare
other KL losses in Section 3.3.5. Following OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026), we
cap each summand of Eq. (2) at a threshold $`\tau`$ to avoid length
collapse. Entries above $`\tau`$ receive no gradient. We use
$`\tau=10^{-3}`$ on MATH and no cap ($`\tau=+\infty`$) on LiveCodeBench
and SciKnowEval. The final loss is described as follows:

```math
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{B}}|y^{x}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{|y^{x}|}w^{x}_{i}\,D_{f}^{\tau}\big(p^{x}_{i}\,\|\,q^{x}_{i}\big), \tag{3}
```

where $`y^{x}`$, $`p^{x}_{i}`$ and $`q^{x}_{i}`$ belong to problem
$`x`$, and $`w^{x}_{i}`$ is a truncated importance weight (Appendix B).
Algorithm 1 gives the complete procedure.

1: problems $`\mathcal{D}`$, policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$, $`K`$, $`M`$,
$`L`$, $`S`$

2: for each $`x\in\mathcal{D}`$ in parallel do

3:   $`\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)\leftarrow\emptyset`$

4:   for $`t=1,\dots,K`$ do

5:    if $`(t-1)\bmod M=0`$ then $`\triangleright`$ restart

6:      $`\tilde{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$

7:
     $`c_{t}\leftarrow\langle x,\,\operatorname{tail}_{L}(\tilde{y}),\,\text{blind instruction}\rangle`$

8:    else

9:      $`\hat{y}_{t-1}\leftarrow\operatorname{tail}_{L}(y_{t-1})`$

10:
     $`c_{t}\leftarrow\langle x,\,\hat{y}_{t-1},\,(r_{t-1},f_{t-1},\text{instruction})\rangle`$

   

11:    $`y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{t})`$;
$`(r_{t},f_{t})\leftarrow\mathrm{Env}(x,y_{t})`$

12:    if $`r_{t}=1`$ and $`y_{t}`$ not truncated then
$`\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)\leftarrow\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)\cup\{c_{t}\}`$      

13: for $`j=1,\dots,S`$ do $`\triangleright`$ policy update

14:   sample batch
$`\mathcal{B}\subseteq\{x:\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)\neq\emptyset\}`$

15:   $`c\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{C}^{+}(x))`$,
$`y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ for each $`x\in\mathcal{B}`$

16:   update $`\theta`$ with one gradient step on Eq. (3)

17: return $`\pi_{\theta}`$

assistant and user turns.

## 3 Experiments

Figure 3: Cumulative pass rate on four benchmarks against (a) verifier
calls per problem, (b) cumulative compute in PFLOPs (log scale) and (c)
cumulative GPU time. Both axes are averaged over the two models at each
step.

We compare our method with baselines on two tasks. In the exploration
task, we evaluate how quickly LEAF discovers correct solutions. To
measure this ability, we compute pass@k after each step. In the
in-distribution generalization task, we investigate whether the
knowledge in rollout trajectories can be transferred to model
parameters. The model first explores and updates policy on the training
set, then we evaluate its accuracy on a held-out test set. The results
of both tasks (Fig. 3, Table 1 and Table 2) are averaged over three runs
with different random seeds, as is the length analysis in Table 3. Other
ablation studies use a single seed.

### 3.1 Exploration tasks

#### 3.1.1 Settings

##### Datasets.

We collect four datasets for the exploration tasks: the 500 problems
from MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2024), 131
problems from release v6 of LiveCodeBench (LCB) (Jain et al., 2025b) in
the split used by SDPO (Hübotter et al., 2026), the 378 problems from
MBPP+ (Liu et al., 2023), and 400 open-ended problems from
SciKnowEval (Feng et al., 2024), with 100 problems each from biology,
chemistry, materials, and physics. The SciKnowEval problems are randomly
sampled, with 25% at level L5 and 75% at level L2. We filter out all
multiple-choice and binary problems to prevent the model from guessing
answers.

##### Verifiers and feedback.

For MATH-500, we use only correctness as feedback. For SciKnowEval, we
use a base LLM as a judge and provide only correctness as feedback. For
coding benchmarks such as LCB and MBPP+, the feedback includes runtime
errors, code, and test inputs and outputs. Only failed tests are
included in the feedback. We divide all test cases into public and
private sets. Rewards and feedback are based only on public tests. The
pass rate is computed based on solutions that pass all tests in both the
public and private sets. For a fair comparison, we assign $`K=24`$
verifier calls per problem and at most 5 GPU hours per benchmark. We
measure the wall time on one H200 GPU per run.

##### Decoding settings.

We use Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025a) with thinking disabled and
Olmo-3-7B-Instruct (Team Olmo, 2025). We sample from Qwen3-8B with a
temperature of 1.0 and top-$`p`$ of 1.0, and from Olmo-3-7B-Instruct
with a temperature of 0.6 and top-$`p`$ of 0.95. The maximum response
length is 8,192 tokens for all methods, except when evaluating Olmo-3 on
LiveCodeBench, where we use 16,384 tokens because we found that Olmo-3
produces longer reasoning sequences on this benchmark.

##### Baselines.

We compare our method with five baselines. Random Sampling draws one
independent sample per problem per step from the plain prompt. We
include four RL baselines: GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) is a standard RLVR
baseline. We use the higher clipping bound from DAPO (Yu et al., 2025).
SDPO (Hübotter et al., 2026) is the most relevant method, as it also
uses environment feedback and self-distillation. SCoRe (Kumar et al., 2025) trains on two-turn trajectories in which the second turn revises
the first. We include SCoRe as a self-improvement baseline. SEED (Wu et
al., 2026b) is a hybrid method that combines SFT and RL. For all
baselines with policy updates, we use LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022)
with rank 64 and a constant learning rate of $`10^{-6}`$.

##### Our method.

LEAF runs only the rollout part of Algorithm 1. We use a restart
interval of $`M=5`$ and a tail length of $`L=2{,}000`$ tokens. The
variant with parameter updates during exploration is studied in
Section 3.3.3. For a fair comparison, we count the rollout resource
usage as a part of our method. Refer to Appendix C for details.

### 3.2 In-distribution generalization tasks

#### 3.2.1 Settings

##### Datasets.

We train on three training sets that are disjoint from the test sets. In
the training sets, we use all test cases for reward and feedback. There
is no overlap between the training sets and the benchmarks in this task.
For LiveCodeBench, the training set contains 478 problems from releases
v2 to v5 of LiveCodeBench. For MATH, we randomly extract 500 Level 5
problems from the MATH training split (Hendrycks et al., 2021). For
SciKnowEval, we extract 400 open problems from
Biology/Chemistry/Material/Physics at level L5. We use the same test set
as the exploration task

##### Benchmarks.

The model trained on each training set is tested on the matching
benchmark, LCB v6, MATH-500 or SciKnowEval, which together hold the same
1,031 problems as in exploration. We sample each problem with four
responses and report the avg@4 as generalization performance.

##### SFT baseline.

To test whether the gain comes from the correct answers collected during
exploration rather than from self-distillation, we build an SFT baseline
from the same rollouts as LEAF. For each successful context and its
correct answer in our method, the base model rewrites the answer into a
self-contained solution without the earlier attempts and the references
to feedback. We keep only the correct rewrites and train the plain
prompt on them with the same LoRA, batch size and number of steps as
LEAF.

##### Other Experimental Settings.

Training rollouts and test samples use the decoding settings of the
exploration experiments. We compare four RL baselines, GRPO, SDPO, SCoRe
and SEED, and an SFT baseline trained on the same rollouts as LEAF. They
use the exploration settings except for a unified learning rate of
$`10^{-5}`$ and a budget of 20 verifier calls per training problem. For
SEED, we found its compute time is significantly longer than that of
other baselines. We lower the budget to 15 for a fair comparison. LEAF
first explores each training set for $`K=20`$ steps. It then runs
$`S=20`$ update steps with a batch size of 128. Refer to Appendix E for
more details.

#### 3.2.2 Results

##### LEAF achieves better performance on unseen test sets with lower cost.

In Table 2, we find that LEAF reaches an overall avg@4 of 64.69% on
Qwen3 and 63.01% on Olmo-3, exceeding the best baseline, GRPO on Qwen3
and SEED on Olmo-3, by 1.59 pp and 1.83 pp. LEAF is lower only on LCB
(25.45% versus 26.02% for GRPO) and SciKnowEval (76.77% versus 76.83%
for SEED) with Qwen3-8B. We found that our method uses significantly
less compute. LEAF uses 2.64 and 3.15 EFLOPs, about 1.9 times less than
SEED (4.88 and 5.94 EFLOPs). The GPU time of our method on Olmo-3 is
close to that of GRPO and SDPO, but our method achieves higher
performance.

##### LEAF improves accuracy without relying on better rollout data.

In Table 2 we find that SFT reaches an overall avg@4 of only 58.16% and
58.21%, which is 6.53 pp and 4.80 pp below LEAF, although its compute is
close to that of LEAF. SFT raises the accuracy on LCB and SciKnowEval
over Base on both models, but its MATH-500 accuracy falls 7.23 pp and
7.79 pp below Base. This may be because the rewritten solutions are
generated in a context that the student never sees, so imitating them
pulls the student away from its own distribution, while LEAF matches the
teacher on the student’s own answers. These results suggest that the
gain of LEAF comes from the distillation method and not from the correct
answers alone.

### 3.3 Ablation studies

#### 3.3.1 Effect of restart, last response, feedback and history length during exploration

We investigate the effect of the key components in our method. There are
four ablations on exploration tasks: the last response, the feedback,
the restart interval and the number of past rounds in the context. Each
ablation removes one component. Refer to Appendix G.2 for details.

Figure 4: Exploration ablations on key components. Final pass rate
against exploration GPU time, averaged over the two models. Our method
in main experiments is denoted as red star.

##### Last response and feedback cause most improvement.

In Fig. 4 we find that removing the compressed response of the last turn
significantly drops performance on both models. We design LEAF w/o last
response as a single user turn with the problem and the feedback without
the assistant turn. Its pass rate ends at 79.70% and 82.49% instead of
80.94% and 84.49% on exploration tasks. Removing the feedback (LEAF w/o
feedback) lowers the final pass rate more than removing the last
response on Qwen3-8B but less on Olmo-3-7B-Instruct, where it lowers the
mean accuracy by only 0.46 pp. This may be because the last response
lets the model edit a concrete attempt, while feedback alone only says
that an unseen attempt has failed.

##### Restarts cause a trade-off between pass@k and avg@k.

We find that LEAF w/o restarts has the highest mean accuracy but a lower
final pass rate compared with LEAF. Restarting every 2 steps has the
highest final pass rate, but it significantly increases GPU wall time
from 3.89 to 4.93 hours and drops the mean accuracy by 1.47 pp. One
possible explanation is that a memory never reset keeps refining one
line of solution, which helps on solved problems but explores less on
the others. We use restart every 5 steps to keep a balance between pass
rate and accuracy.

##### A longer history does not help consistently.

We find that keeping the last three assistant and user rounds in the
dialogue instead of one lowers the averaged final pass rate (82.71% to
82.67%) and raises the GPU time (3.89 to 4.29 hours). It may be because
older rounds repeat attempts that the last round already revised, so
they lengthen the context with little new information. We therefore keep
only the last round in our method.

## 4 Related Work

##### Exploration in RLVR.

Sampling a group of independent responses for each prompt is a
fundamental approach to estimating advantages in RLVR (Shao et al.,
2024; Yu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025). Prior work finds that the
efficiency of RLVR depends on which solutions are explored under a
limited verification budget (Zhang et al., 2026; Setlur et al., 2026; Wu
et al., 2026a). Larger groups allow for more extensive exploration that
can substantially improve the estimate. However, sampling in this way
suffers from a fundamental limitation: every response is generated from
the same problem prompt, so a failed attempt cannot inform the next
sample. Our work therefore aims to condition each rollout on earlier
attempts at the same problem, which brings new information in every
rollout trace.

##### Feedback guided refinement.

Prior work explores prompting LLMs to revise their own outputs using
information from earlier attempts. Shinn et al. (2023) stores verbal
reflection across interactions. Madaan et al. (2023) introduces
iterative self-refinement. Song et al. (2026a); Yu et al. (2026)
leverage in-context reinforcement learning and policy optimization. Some
methods consider training models to use feedback more effectively rather
than relying on prompting alone. They improve through textual critiques,
explicit reflection or retrospective credit assignment (Du et al., 2025;
Song et al., 2026b; Shi et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Ekbote et al.,
2025; Jin et al., 2026; Jain et al., 2025a; Yuan and Xie, 2025). Such
training may occur over multiple turns, such as Kumar et al. (2025),
which trains models with reinforcement learning on two turns of
self-correction. Mu et al. (2026) decouple multi-turn rollouts from a
weighted fine-tuning by importance. In contrast to these works, LEAF
generates one rollout per problem per step and updates the parameters by
self-distillation on single trajectories, without reward objectives on
groups of answers.

##### Self-distillation with privileged context.

The memory-conditioned policy in LEAF can be viewed as a teacher with
privileged context that is updated as the policy explores. Prior work
primarily considers distilling a richer context into a model that must
eventually answer without it (Snell et al., 2022; Agarwal et al., 2024),
where the context holds environment feedback (Hübotter et al., 2026),
privileged information on student-generated trajectories (Zhao et al.,
2026), self-revision (He et al., 2026), procedural memories (Liu et al.,
2026a) or skills (Wang et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026). Others combine
distillation with supervised fine-tuning, preference optimization or
reinforcement learning (Yang et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2026b).

## 5 Conclusion

Independent rollouts make exploration less efficient in LLM
reinforcement learning. We propose LEAF, which keeps a dynamic memory
for each rollout trace and updates the policy by self-distillation, to
improve learning efficiency. Our experiments found that LEAF reached the
final pass rate of Random Sampling with about four times fewer verifier
calls and ended above all RL baselines at lower compute without policy
update. The self-distillation further gave the best overall test
accuracy. We conducted several ablation studies on key designs. The
results highlight that LEAF can be a new framework with better
exploration efficiency.

## Limitations

On LiveCodeBench and MBPP+ the feedback includes execution results such
as errors and failed tests, while on MATH-500 and SciKnowEval it is only
a correctness verdict, so the effect of richer feedback is tested on
code tasks only. Although we conducted ablation studies on key
components, how memory affects exploration still needs further research.

## Ethical Considerations

We use public datasets and open-weight models without collecting new
personal data or involving human participants. We rely on the original
dataset curation without additional screening for personal information
or offensive content.

LEAF may inherit model biases and propagate errors from verifier
feedback, including judgments from the LLM judge. Benchmark gains do not
establish deployment safety: scientific answers require independent
verification, and generated code requires security review. Computational
costs are reported in Appendices C and E.

## Appendix A Repeated wrong answers under independent sampling

This section specifies how repeated wrong answers are measured and
reports the per-benchmark measurements behind the repeat statistics in
the Introduction. We analyze MATH-500, LCB and MBPP+ for Qwen3-8B and
Olmo-3-7B-Instruct. Unless stated otherwise, all runs in this section
are single runs with the data, budget and decoding settings of Table 1.

##### Error signatures.

We introduce error signatures to identify repetitive answers. On
MATH-500, the error signature of a wrong answer is its normalized final
answer. On LCB and MBPP+, the signature is the full public-test feedback
text, that is, the failing input, output or error message shown to the
model. Throughout this section, correctness on LCB and MBPP+ is judged
on the public tests only.

##### Repeats under independent sampling.

For each problem, we take the 24 Random Sampling responses in the order
in which they were drawn. A wrong response counts as a repeat if its
signature equals the signature of an earlier wrong response to the same
problem. Wrong responses without an extracted answer count as
non-repeats. The repeat rate is the number of repeats divided by the
number of wrong responses, both summed over problems. Table 7 reports
these rates.

##### Memory conditions and transitions.

To measure repeats when the previous attempt is in the context, we
compare three exploration conditions that differ only in the memory.
LEAF shows the previous response and its feedback. LEAF w/o feedback
shows the previous response followed by the blind correction
instruction, which contains no correctness signal or test output. LEAF
w/o last response shows the problem and the feedback in a single user
turn, without the previous response. These are the single runs of the
ablations in Figure 4, restricted to the first 24 steps. A transition is
a step $`t`$ that is not a restart step and whose previous step $`t-1`$
produced a wrong answer with an error signature. With $`M=5`$, the
restart steps are $`t=1,6,11,16,21`$. These steps are excluded because
the policy revises a fresh draft from the plain prompt instead of the
step-$`(t-1)`$ answer.

##### Repeat and fix rates.

Over transitions, the repeat rate is the fraction in which step $`t`$ is
wrong with the same signature as step $`t-1`$. The fix rate is the
fraction in which step $`t`$ is correct. For the paired comparison in
Table 8, we first average each problem’s repeat and fix rates over its
transitions. We then average over the problems that have at least one
transition under both LEAF and the comparison condition.

Table 8 reports the paired comparisons. Relative to LEAF w/o feedback,
LEAF has a lower repeat rate and a higher fix rate in all six pairs, and
its repeat rate averaged over the six pairs is lower by about 27 pp.
Relative to LEAF w/o last response, LEAF has a higher repeat rate in
five of the six pairs and a lower fix rate on MATH-500 for both models.

##### Base frequency under independent sampling.

To relate the repeat rates of LEAF to independent sampling, we use the
24 Random Sampling responses to the same problem. For each transition,
the base frequency is the fraction of those 24 responses that are wrong
with the same signature as the step-$`(t-1)`$ answer. Table 9 pools all
transitions of a condition, without the problem matching used in
Table 8. It reports the average repeat rate, the base frequency averaged
over transitions, and the ratio of the number of repeats to the sum of
base frequencies.

Table 9 compares these repeat rates with the base frequency under
independent sampling. The repeat rates differ from those in Table 8
because they weight each transition equally and are not restricted to
shared problems. The ratio for LEAF is lower than that for LEAF w/o
feedback in all six pairs. Olmo-3-7B-Instruct MBPP+ is the only cell
where the ratio for LEAF is below 1, so there LEAF repeats a wrong
answer less often than independent sampling.

## Appendix B Method details

This section specifies the memory context, the restart step, the prompts
and the policy update of LEAF in Algorithm 1. Unless stated otherwise,
the exploration runs (Table 1) and the rollouts on the training sets
that supply the teacher contexts for the policy update (Table 2) use the
same construction. Benchmarks, decoding parameters and budgets are given
in Appendix C and Appendix E.

### B.1 Memory context construction

##### Dialogue format.

At a carry step $`t`$, meaning a step that is not a restart, the context
$`c_{t}`$ is a chat dialogue with three parts. The first part is the
opening prompt of the problem. It is the user turn given below, preceded
by a system turn for Olmo-3-7B-Instruct on LCB. The second part is an
assistant turn that holds
$`\hat{y}_{t-1}=\operatorname{tail}_{L}(y_{t-1})`$. The third part is a
user turn made of the feedback section of step $`t-1`$ and an
improvement instruction, separated by a blank line. The assistant turn
holds only the previous response, so earlier attempts in the same
restart segment are not shown. For Qwen3-8B, we apply the chat template
with thinking mode disabled. Olmo-3-7B-Instruct uses its own chat
template, which ignores this flag.

##### Rendering of verdict and feedback.

The feedback section depends on the verifier of each benchmark. On LCB
and MBPP+, it starts with a sentence stating that the previous answer
was executed against the public tests. A verdict sentence follows, and
then the native feedback of the executor. When the executor returns no
details, a fixed fallback sentence is used in their place. On MATH-500
and SciKnowEval, the section has only a sentence stating that the answer
was checked against the reference, followed by the verdict. On MATH-500,
the extracted final answer is not quoted back. On SciKnowEval, neither
the judge’s reasoning nor the reference answer is shown.

On LCB, the native feedback comes from the SDPO code executor and covers
failing tests only. If any test raises a runtime error, only the first
such test is shown. Otherwise, the first timeout is shown, and failing
that, the first test with an incorrect format. If none of these occur,
the wrong answers are sorted by the combined length of the input and the
actual output, shortest first, and at most two are shown. A wrong answer
is rendered as the test index, the input, the actual output and the
expected output. A runtime error is rendered as the error message and
the last executed input. Each input line is capped at 250 characters and
each input at 8 lines. The expected and actual outputs are each capped
at 250 characters, and the whole feedback is capped at 2,000 characters.
Tracebacks keep at most 3 frames, all of them inside the submitted
solution. The program is taken from the longest fenced code block. On
the LCB training set used for the policy update, tests run in order and
stop at the first failure, so the feedback shows that one failure.

On MBPP+, each of the three public asserts is run and reported
separately. The feedback starts with “Public test results: $`p`$/$`n`$
asserts passed.” It then lists each failed assert together with its
exception, capped at 300 characters. When all asserts pass, the feedback
is “All public asserts passed.” Separate messages cover four cases: a
program that does not compile, an execution timeout after 30 seconds, an
exception raised before any test runs, and an answer from which no
program can be extracted. The whole feedback is capped at 2,000
characters. MATH-500 answers are graded with math-verify (Kydlíček, 2025) against the reference answer. SciKnowEval answers are graded by an
LLM judge, which is the run’s own base model without LoRA.

##### Tail truncation.

The transform $`\operatorname{tail}_{L}`$ uses $`L=2{,}000`$ tokens. If
a response has more than 2,000 tokens, the assistant turn holds the
decoded text of its last 2,000 token IDs, with special tokens skipped.
Otherwise the response is kept verbatim. The same transform is applied
to the restart draft $`\tilde{y}`$.

### B.2 Restart with blind revision

A restart occurs at step 1 and every $`M=5`$ steps after it. Within the
budget of $`K=24`$ calls, steps 1, 6, 11, 16 and 21 are restarts. At a
restart step, the policy first drafts
$`\tilde{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ from the opening prompt. The
context then becomes the opening prompt, an assistant turn holding
$`\operatorname{tail}_{L}(\tilde{y})`$, and a user turn holding the
blind correction instruction for the task type. The policy generates
$`y_{t}`$ under this context. Only $`y_{t}`$ is sent to the verifier and
counted toward the budget, and the draft is not graded. The blind
instruction contains no verdict and no test output. The next carry step
uses $`y_{t}`$ and its feedback as memory.

### B.4 Self-distillation implementation

##### Teacher contexts.

Before the policy update, LEAF runs $`K=20`$ steps of the rollout above
on each training set (LCB, MATH and SciKnowEval). For each problem,
$`\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)`$ holds the contexts whose final response was
correct and not truncated. A response that exactly repeats an earlier
correct response to the same problem is dropped. A context is the full
chat dialogue that produced the response. At a carry step, this dialogue
includes the earlier attempt and its feedback. At a restart step, it
includes the draft and the blind instruction. Only problems with a
non-empty $`\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)`$ enter the update. At each update step,
one context is drawn uniformly from $`\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)`$ for each
problem in the batch.

##### Batches and student samples.

The update runs $`S=20`$ steps with 128 problems per step and one
optimizer step per training step. Batches are cut from a fixed stream
that concatenates per-epoch permutations of the eligible problems, where
the permutation of epoch $`e`$ is seeded by the schedule seed plus
$`e`$. For each problem, one student response
$`y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ is sampled with vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) from the plain opening prompt.

##### Teacher distribution.

The teacher is the current policy with its current LoRA adapter. The
teacher reads the drawn context followed by the same student response.
Its logits are computed without gradients, which implements
$`\operatorname{sg}`$ in the definition of $`q_{i}`$. Student and
teacher logits are taken at every response position and divided by the
model’s sampling temperature. The divergence is computed over the full
vocabulary.

##### Importance weights.

The student response is sampled with vLLM, whose token probabilities
$`\mu`$ can differ slightly from those of the training engine. Before
the update step, the training engine recomputes the log probability of
every sampled token under the current parameters, and
$`w^{x}_{i}=\min\big(\pi_{\theta}(y^{x}_{i}\mid x,y^{x}_{<i})/\mu(y^{x}_{i}\mid x,y^{x}_{<i}),\,2\big)`$
in Equation 3, where $`\mu`$ is the vLLM probability of the sampled
token. The weights are constants in the loss.

##### Clipped $`f`$-divergence.

With the default $`f(u)=u\log u`$, each summand of Equation 2 is
$`p_{i,v}\log(p_{i,v}/q_{i,v})`$, so the token loss is

```math
D^{\tau}(p_{i}\,\|\,q_{i})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\min\!\left(p_{i,v}\log\frac{p_{i,v}}{q_{i,v}},\,\tau\right). \tag{4}
```

We use $`\tau=10^{-3}`$ on MATH. On LCB and SciKnowEval, no cap is
applied, and the loss is the unclipped reverse KL
$`\mathrm{KL}(p_{i}\,\|\,q_{i})`$. The forward-KL and JSD variants in
Section 3.3.5 replace only the summand and keep the same cap on MATH.

##### Parameterization and optimization.

We train a LoRA adapter with rank 64, alpha 64 and dropout 0.0 on all
linear layers, with the backbone frozen. We use AdamW (Loshchilov and
Hutter, 2019) with betas $`(0.9,0.999)`$, weight decay 0.01, a maximum
gradient norm of 1.0 and a constant learning rate of $`10^{-5}`$ with no
warmup. Training runs in bfloat16 with FSDP (Zhao et al., 2023),
gradient checkpointing, dynamic batch sizes and padding removal. Each
run uses one H200 GPU and trains a separate adapter for each training
set.

## Appendix C Exploration setup details

This section specifies the datasets, verifiers, cost measurement, and
baseline configurations underlying the exploration results in Table 1
and Figure 3. Unless stated otherwise, every method is run on both
Qwen3-8B with thinking disabled and Olmo-3-7B-Instruct, receives
$`K=24`$ verifier calls per problem, and is limited to 5 GPU hours per
benchmark on one H200 GPU. The prompts that build the memory dialogue of
LEAF are given in Appendix B.

### C.1 Datasets

The exploration pool contains 1,409 problems: 500 from MATH-500, 131
from LCB, 378 from MBPP+, and 400 from SciKnowEval. Each benchmark is
trained and evaluated separately. The problem order within each
benchmark file is shuffled with a fixed seed.

##### MATH-500.

We use all 500 problems without filtering. Answers are graded with
math-verify against the last \boxed{} expression in the response.

##### LCB.

We use the 131 problems from release v6 of LiveCodeBench with contest
dates from 2025-02-01 to 2025-04-30, which is the split used by SDPO.
The test cases of each problem are the private test cases released with
LiveCodeBench. The worked examples in the problem statement belong to
neither the public nor the private set defined below.

##### MBPP+.

We use all 378 tasks of EvalPlus (Liu et al., 2023) MbppPlus v0.2.0. The
prompt contains the task description followed by the three assert
statements that the code must satisfy.

##### SciKnowEval.

We start from the v2 test set and keep only the open-ended-qa and
relation_extraction types at levels L2 and L5, which excludes all
multiple-choice and true/false problems. We then drop entries whose
(instruction, question) pair occurs more than once. For each of the four
domains (Biology, Chemistry, Material, and Physics), we draw 75 problems
from L2 and 25 from L5 with a fixed seed, giving 100 problems per domain
and 400 in total.

### C.2 Public and private tests

On the two coding benchmarks, the verifier reward and the feedback shown
to the policy use only public tests. A response counts as correct for
the pass rate only if it passes both the public and the private tests
(Equation 1). For LCB, we split the test cases of each problem with a
fixed random seed: half of the cases form the public set, and the
remaining cases form the private set. Public tests run in the SDPO
executor. Private tests are graded offline with the official
LiveCodeBench runner. For MBPP+, the public set is the three asserts
shown in the prompt, and each assert is executed separately. The private
set is the MBPP+ plus_input cases.

### C.3 Verifiers and feedback

##### Coding feedback.

On LCB, the feedback lists failing public tests only. If a test raises a
runtime error, the feedback shows the first such test with its error
message and input. Otherwise, it shows the first timeout, then an
incorrect-format message, and otherwise at most two wrong answer cases,
chosen as the shortest by combined input and output length, each with
its input, actual output, and expected output. Truncated responses
receive a fixed message asking for a shorter response. Long inputs and
outputs are truncated, tracebacks keep at most three frames inside the
solution, and the whole feedback is capped at 2,000 characters. On
MBPP+, the feedback reports the number of passed asserts and, for each
failed assert, the assert and its exception. It also reports compilation
errors, timeouts, and responses from which no program can be extracted.

##### Verdict-only feedback.

On MATH-500 and SciKnowEval, the feedback states only whether the
previous answer was judged correct. On MATH-500, neither the reference
answer nor the extracted final answer is shown.

##### LLM judge.

SciKnowEval answers are graded by the base model of the run without
LoRA. The judge decodes greedily (temperature 0, top-$`p`$ 1) with at
most 512 new tokens. It sees only the text after the last \</think\>
tag. A response longer than 12,000 characters is shown as its first
3,000 and last 9,000 characters, with an omission marker in between. The
verdict is the last \boxed{yes} or \boxed{no} in the judge output, and
any other output counts as incorrect. The judge user message is:

> You are an expert grader for science tasks. Decide whether the
> response to the task below is correct, using the reference answer as
> the ground truth.
>
> \### Task
>
> {question}
>
> \### Reference answer
>
> {reference}
>
> \### Response
>
> {response}
>
> \### Grading instructions
>
> {criterion}
>
> Judge the final answer the response gives; its style and length do not
> matter. Be strict but fair. A response that gives no final answer,
> hedges between several answers, or is cut off before answering is
> incorrect.
>
> Explain your judgment in at most three sentences, then end with
> \boxed{yes} if the response is correct or \boxed{no} if it is not.

The {criterion} field is one of six texts selected by the SciKnowEval
subtask: relation set, composition, summary, key content, derivation,
and final result. The final-result criterion is:

> The response is correct if its final result is equivalent to the
> reference answer: the same formula, value, expression, structure or
> material, allowing equivalent notation, units and rounding. The
> reasoning does not need to match the reference. If the reference
> answers several parts, every part must match.

The verifier for each benchmark, including the judge, is the same for
every method.

### C.4 Metrics and compute measurement

##### Aligned steps.

Step $`k`$ of a problem refers to its first $`k`$ graded answers. Random
Sampling and LEAF produce one answer per problem per step. For the batch
methods (GRPO, SDPO, SCoRe, and SEED), we align on the $`k`$-th answer
of each problem rather than on the $`k`$-th training batch. The
cumulative pass rate at step $`k`$ follows Equation 1. The Pass column
of Table 1 is the mean of the four per-benchmark pass rates at the last
aligned step.

##### Budget cutoff.

Each run stops at $`\min(24,k_{5\mathrm{h}})`$ steps, where
$`k_{5\mathrm{h}}`$ is the last step whose cumulative GPU time does not
exceed 5 hours. A run cut by this cap is evaluated at the steps it
completed.

##### GPU hours.

All jobs run on a single H200 GPU (141 GB). For LEAF, we take the
measured rollout wall time, split it over steps in proportion to the
per-problem sampling time of each step, and add the start-up overhead.
For the methods based on verl (Sheng et al., 2025), the time of each
step is the initial overhead plus each answer’s share of the measured
update time. For Random Sampling, the measured time of each generation
chunk is allocated to steps by token count. The GPU hours in Table 1 are
the cumulative values at the last aligned step, summed over the four
benchmarks.

##### FLOPs.

We estimate compute as

```math
\mathrm{FLOPs}=2N\cdot T_{\mathrm{fwd}}+6N\cdot T_{\mathrm{train}}, \tag{5}
```

where $`N=8.19\times 10^{9}`$ for Qwen3-8B and $`7.30\times 10^{9}`$ for
Olmo-3-7B-Instruct. Here, $`T_{\mathrm{fwd}}`$ is the number of tokens
processed in forward passes, and $`T_{\mathrm{train}}`$ is the number of
tokens that receive gradients. For LEAF and Random Sampling,
$`T_{\mathrm{fwd}}`$ counts the prompt and completion tokens of every
generation call, including the restart drafts of LEAF and every judge
call, and $`T_{\mathrm{train}}=0`$. For the methods based on verl,
$`T_{\mathrm{fwd}}`$ counts the generated sequences, the old policy and
reference log-probability passes, and the judge calls, together with the
SDPO teacher forward pass, the second SCoRe turn, and the SEED analyzer
and teacher passes. $`T_{\mathrm{train}}`$ is the number of tokens in
the policy updates. For SEED, $`T_{\mathrm{fwd}}`$ also includes the
collection rollouts and analyses, and $`T_{\mathrm{train}}`$ includes
three epochs of SFT tokens. Table 1 reports the result in PFLOPs
($`10^{15}`$ FLOPs).

##### Random Sampling.

Random Sampling draws one response per problem per step from the plain
prompt with vLLM and does not update parameters. It uses the same
decoding settings, response limits, and verifiers as the other methods.

##### GRPO.

GRPO samples 8 responses per question and normalizes advantages by the
group standard deviation. It uses the vanilla clipped policy loss with a
lower clip ratio of 0.2 and the higher clip bound of 0.28 from DAPO,
without a KL term.

##### SDPO.

SDPO samples 8 responses per question and distills from a teacher whose
prompt adds a successful response from the same group and the
environment feedback on the failed attempt. A response is not used as
the demonstration for itself. The teacher prompt is:

> {prompt}{solution}{feedback}
>
> Correctly solve the original question.

Distillation uses the top-20 tokens with a tail bucket, $`\alpha=1.0`$,
an importance-sampling clip of 2.0, and an EMA teacher with update rate
0.01. The teacher prompt is limited to 10,240 tokens.

##### SCoRe.

SCoRe samples 4 two-turn trajectories per question, and the second turn
uses the blind correction instruction of the task type. It uses a KL
loss coefficient of 1.0 (low*var_kl), a clip ratio of 0.2,
$`\beta*{1}=0.01`$, and $`\beta\_{2}=0.25`$. The first 25% of updates,
rounded down, form stage 1 (11, 3, 9, and 9 updates on MATH-500, LCB,
MBPP+, and SciKnowEval), and stage 2 adds a correction bonus of 10.0.
Each turn is limited to 8,192 tokens, and the two turns together are
limited to 16,896 tokens.

##### SEED.

SEED reserves 16% of the updates, rounded to the nearest integer, for
collecting hindsight skills: 8, 2, 6, and 6 updates’ worth of rollouts
on MATH-500, LCB, MBPP+, and SciKnowEval. These collection rollouts
count toward the 24 verifier calls per problem. An analyzer writes
skills at temperature 0, with feedback limited to 2,048 tokens and
skills to 500 tokens. SFT on the collected data runs for 3 epochs with
batch size 8. RL then runs for the remaining 39, 11, 30, and 32 updates
with the SEED loss, a gate scale of 5.0, an on-policy distillation
coefficient of 0.01, a reference KL coefficient of 0.01 (low_var_kl),
and a clip ratio of 0.2. For Olmo-3-7B-Instruct, the token budget per RL
update is limited to its maximum position embedding length of 65,536.

##### LEAF.

LEAF runs the rollout part of Algorithm 1 without parameter updates,
using a restart interval of $`M=5`$ (restarts at steps 1, 6, 11, 16, and 21) and a tail length of $`L=2{,}000`$ tokens. Responses are generated
with vLLM using GPU memory utilization 0.9, at most 16,384 batched
tokens, and with prefix caching and chunked prefill enabled. The
decoding settings, response limits, verifiers, and judge are the same as
for Random Sampling.

## Appendix E Generalization setup details

This section specifies the training sets, test protocol, SFT baseline,
training budgets and cost accounting underlying the in-distribution
generalization results in Table 2. Unless stated otherwise, every method
trains a separate LoRA adapter on each of the three training sets and is
tested on the matching benchmark, and each run uses one H200 GPU.
Table 2 reports means over three run seeds. A run seed $`s`$ shifts the
problem order, the decoding seed, the context draw of LEAF, the analyzer
seed of SEED, the rewrite seed of the SFT baseline and the test sampling
seed by $`(s-1)\times 10^{8}`$. The dataset files and the problem
selection seed are fixed, so all three seeds use the same training and
test problems.

### E.1 Training sets

The three training sets are disjoint from the test sets. For LCB, we
take the problems in releases v2 to v5 of LiveCodeBench, that is,
release v5 without the problems of release v1. We then apply static
checks and remove duplicates as well as problems that collide with the
131 test problems, which leaves 478 problems. During training, all test
cases of a training problem are used for reward and feedback. On these
training sets, the code executor runs the tests in order and stops at
the first failure, so the feedback shows a single failing test. For
MATH, we sample 500 Level 5 problems from the MATH training split and
drop problems whose text matches a MATH-500 problem. For SciKnowEval, we
sample 100 open L5 problems from each of the biology, chemistry,
materials and physics domains, and none of these 400 problems is in the
400-problem test set.

Table 15 lists the training set sizes and the data collected by the
exploration rollout of LEAF. A problem enters the policy update only if
its rollout produced at least one correct, untruncated answer, i.e., if
$`\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)\neq\emptyset`$. The SFT baseline is built from the
same $`(c^{+},y^{+})`$ pairs.

The policy update uses most of the problems in every training set, from
about 63% on LCB for Qwen3-8B to about 94% on SciKnowEval for
Olmo-3-7B-Instruct. LCB has the smallest fraction for both models, and
Olmo-3-7B-Instruct uses a larger fraction than Qwen3-8B on every
training set. MATH gives the most pairs for both models.

### E.2 Test protocol

Each trained adapter is tested on its matching benchmark: LCB v6 (131
problems), MATH-500 (500 problems) or SciKnowEval (400 problems). We
sample four responses per problem with the decoding settings and
response length limit of training, using a separate seed for each
response. LCB responses are graded on all test cases, both public and
private. MATH-500 responses are graded with math-verify on the last
\boxed{}, and SciKnowEval responses with the base-model judge. Let
$`z_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}`$ indicate whether response $`j`$ to problem $`i`$
is correct. With $`P`$ test problems, we compute avg@4 and pass@4.
Overall is the arithmetic mean of avg@4 on LCB, MATH-500 and
SciKnowEval.

### E.3 SFT baseline

The SFT baseline tests whether training on the correct answers collected
by the rollout of LEAF gives the same gain as self-distillation. It uses
the same rollout as LEAF and runs three stages: rewriting, verification
and supervised training.

##### Rewriting.

For every pair of a successful context $`c^{+}`$ and its correct answer
$`y^{+}`$, the base model of the run rewrites $`y^{+}`$ into a
self-contained solution $`y^{*}`$. The request consists of the system
turn of the plain prompt, if there is one, and one user turn. When
$`c^{+}`$ contains an earlier attempt, the user turn is:

> {plain user prompt}
>
> Below is an earlier solving session for this problem: earlier
> attempts, the feedback on them, and a final solution that was verified
> to be correct.
>
> \<session\>
>
> {transcript}
>
> \</session\>
>
> \<correct_solution\>
>
> {y+}
>
> \</correct_solution\>
>
> Rewrite the correct solution into a complete, self-contained solution
> to the problem, written as if you were solving it for the first time.
> Remove the wrong attempts, the invalid derivations, the wrong
> intermediate results and every reference to earlier attempts,
> feedback, checking or corrections. Present the correct reasoning in a
> logical order, keeping its intermediate results. {ending} Output only
> the rewritten solution.

The transcript lists the turns of $`c^{+}`$ after the plain prompt,
labeled \[Problem prompt\], \[Earlier attempt\] and \[Feedback\]. When
$`c^{+}`$ has no earlier attempt, the sentence and the session block are
replaced by “Below is a solution to this problem that was verified to be
correct.” The ending keeps the final answer of $`y^{+}`$ fixed. For MATH
it is:

> The final answer must be exactly the same as in the correct solution:
> end with \boxed{{answer}}.

Here, {answer} is the last boxed answer of $`y^{+}`$. For LCB, the
ending requires the same complete program in a single python code block.
For SciKnowEval, it requires exactly the same final answer after “Final
answer:”. Rewrites are sampled at temperature 0.7 and top-$`p`$ 0.95,
with the response length limit of training.

Each rewrite is checked by the verifier at training time: all test cases

and 0.52 GPU hours.

### E.4 Training budgets

##### Shared training configuration.

All trained methods use LoRA adapters with the configuration of the
exploration experiments (Appendix C) and a constant learning rate of
$`10^{-5}`$. Training rollouts use the decoding settings of the
exploration experiments: temperature 1.0 and top-$`p`$ 1.0 for Qwen3-8B
with thinking disabled, and temperature 0.6 and top-$`p`$ 0.95 for
Olmo-3-7B-Instruct. The maximum response length is 8,192 tokens, except
for Olmo-3-7B-Instruct on LCB, where it is 16,384 tokens.

##### GRPO, SDPO and SCoRe.

Each of these baselines receives a budget of 20 verifier calls per
training problem. They sample 8 responses per problem (for SCoRe, 4
trajectories of 2 turns each) and use 32 problems per update, so the
budget gives 38, 40 and 32 updates on LCB, MATH and SciKnowEval,
respectively. The remaining settings are the same as in exploration.

##### SEED.

At a budget of 20 verifier calls per problem, the seed-1 SEED training
took 9.58 GPU hours on Qwen3-8B and 19.07 on Olmo-3-7B-Instruct,
compared with 5.32–6.00 and 14.03–17.65 for GRPO, SDPO and SCoRe. We
therefore lower its budget to 15 verifier calls per training problem.
This gives 29, 30 and 24 updates on LCB, MATH and SciKnowEval, of which
the first 5, 5 and 4 form the collection stage. SFT and RL follow as in
exploration.

##### LEAF.

LEAF first runs the rollout part of Algorithm 1 on each training set for
$`K=20`$ steps. It uses $`M=5`$, $`L=2{,}000`$ and the feedback of the
exploration experiments, so the MATH feedback contains only the verdict.
The policy update then runs $`S=20`$ steps with a batch of 128 problems
and one optimizer step per training step. For each problem in the batch,
the update samples one student response from the plain prompt and draws
a context uniformly from $`\mathcal{C}^{+}(x)`$, separately for each
step and problem. The divergence is the reverse KL in Equation 2, with
$`\tau=10^{-3}`$ on MATH and no cap on LCB and SciKnowEval, averaged
over tokens as in Equation 3.

### E.5 Cost accounting

GPU hours in Table 2 are the summed elapsed times of a run’s completed
or timed-out jobs on the three training sets. FLOPs use the estimate of
Appendix C: $`2N`$ per forward token plus $`6N`$ per trained token. For
LEAF, the cost includes the $`K=20`$ step exploration rollout and the
policy update. In the update, forward tokens cover student generation
and the teacher and behavior forward passes, and trained tokens are the
student tokens. For SFT, the cost includes the same rollout, the
rewriting and verification in Table 16, and supervised training. The
cost of SEED includes its collection stage. Averaged over the three run
seeds, the exploration rollout takes 1.58 GPU hours for Qwen3-8B and
9.93 for Olmo-3-7B-Instruct, and the policy update of LEAF takes 2.12
and 4.11 GPU hours.

##### History compression.

The tail length $`L`$ in LEAF controls how a previous response longer
than 2,000 tokens is shown in the memory. We compare the tail with five
other ways of showing such a response:

- •
  No compression shows the full response.
- •
  LLM summary shows the model’s own summary of at most 1,000 tokens,
  generated as a further turn of the same conversation.
- •
  Head and tail shows the first 1,000 and the last 1,000 tokens.
- •
  Summary block asks every solving turn to end with a summary block. It
  always shows that block, cut to at most 500 tokens, followed by the
  final code or final answer.
- •
  Lowest log-prob shows the 1,000 tokens with the lowest sampled
  log-probability, in their original order.

These runs use an earlier version of LEAF with different engine settings
and chunked execution, so their GPU hours are not directly comparable
with Table 22. No compression was run only on MATH-500 and LCB for

on LCB and SciKnowEval for Olmo-3-7B-Instruct. Table 24 reports the
results.

Overall pass rate and close in cost. On Olmo-3-7B-Instruct, the tail has
a higher Overall pass rate than head and tail. On Olmo-3-7B-Instruct
LCB, the tail has the highest pass rate of all treatments, and the
summary block has the lowest pass rate and the lowest mean accuracy.
Against no compression on Qwen3-8B, the tail has slightly higher pass
rates on MATH-500 and LCB.

##### Self-correction on the training set.

We also measure how the trained policies revise their own answers on the
training problems. The LEAF policy and the SFT baseline each run the
exploration procedure of LEAF for 10 steps on its training set, with
restarts at steps 1 and 6. For the base model, we use the first 10 steps
of the rollout that produced the policy-update data. We count only
adjacent steps $`(k,k+1)`$ where $`k+1`$ is not a restart step, which
gives 8 transitions per problem. We also count only the problems used in
the policy update. Three rates are conditioned on the previous step:

- •
  Wrong-to-right is the probability that step $`k+1`$ is correct, given
  that step $`k`$ is wrong.
- •
  Right-to-wrong is the probability that step $`k+1`$ is wrong, given
  that step $`k`$ is correct.
- •
  Repeat is the probability that step $`k+1`$ is wrong and has the same
  extracted final answer as step $`k`$, given that step $`k`$ is wrong.

Final answers are compared after removing whitespace. The extracted
answer is the boxed answer on MATH, the program on LCB and the text
after “Final answer:” on SciKnowEval. Table 28 reports these rates
together with the restart-step accuracy and the mean accuracy. Both
policies are the seed-1 checkpoints of Table 2.

On MATH and SciKnowEval, LEAF has lower repeat rates than the base model
on both models. On Olmo-3-7B-Instruct SciKnowEval, LEAF also raises
wrong-to-right from about 29% for the base model to about 51%, while SFT
stays at the base-model level. On Qwen3-8B LCB, LEAF instead has a lower
wrong-to-right rate than the base model but a higher restart-step
accuracy. SFT has a higher right-to-wrong rate than the base model on
Qwen3-8B LCB and SciKnowEval, and a lower mean accuracy than the base
model in four of the six cells.

### G.5 Divergence in the policy update

This section reports the divergence ablation in Table 6 separately for
each test set. As in Equation 2, $`p_{i}`$ is the student distribution
and $`q_{i}`$ is the teacher distribution. The three divergences are:

- •
  reverse KL, which is $`\mathrm{KL}(p_{i}\,\|\,q_{i})`$;
- •
  forward KL, which is $`\mathrm{KL}(q_{i}\,\|\,p_{i})`$;
- •
  JSD with $`\beta=0.5`$, which is
  $`0.5\,\mathrm{KL}(p_{i}\,\|\,m_{i})+0.5\,\mathrm{KL}(q_{i}\,\|\,m_{i})`$
  with $`m_{i}=(p_{i}+q_{i})/2`$.

On MATH, each vocabulary term of every divergence is capped at
$`\tau=10^{-3}`$. Only the divergence changes. The importance weights,
token averaging, learning rate, teacher contexts, seed and engine
settings are those of LEAF, and all three variants use the same
policy-update data. Table 30 reports test performance, and Table 31
reports truncation rates and training cost.

##### Internalization check.

We test how much of the with-context rollout accuracy the policy keeps
without the context. We fix the subset of training problems with at
least one correct answer, which are the problems used in the policy
update. On this subset, we compare three accuracies:

- •
  Base acc. is the avg@4 of the base model under the test generation and
  judging settings. On LCB, it uses all tests of the training problem.
- •
  Policy-update acc. is the accuracy of the last student response for
  each problem during the policy update. This response is sampled from
  the plain prompt and judged offline in the same way.
- •
  Rollout acc. is the correctness rate of the step-20 rollout of LEAF
  with context, which produced the policy-update data.

The relative gain is
$`(\text{Policy-update acc.}-\text{Base acc.})/(\text{Rollout acc.}-\text{Base acc.})`$.
A value above 100% means that the policy without context exceeds the
with-context rollout accuracy. Table 32 reports the results.

The policy without context exceeds the with-context rollout accuracy in
three cells: Qwen3-8B MATH, Qwen3-8B SciKnowEval and Olmo-3-7B-Instruct
SciKnowEval. In the other three cells, the relative gain is about half
on Qwen3-8B LCB and below a quarter on Olmo-3-7B-Instruct LCB and MATH.
The policy update accuracy is above the base model accuracy in all six
cells.

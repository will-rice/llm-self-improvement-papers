# Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents

Yoshiki Tanaka    Shuya Uchiyama    Daichi Hamaguchi    Michimasa Inaba
Affiliation: The University of Electro-Communications, Tokyo, Japan
Affiliation: {y-tanaka, s-uchiyama, d-hamaguchi, m-inaba}@uec.ac.jp

###### Abstract

Participating in a conversation involves not only deciding what to say
but also whether to speak at all. This judgment carries even greater
significance in environments like the Werewolf game, where participants’
interests conflict, because both utterances and silence are subject to
interpretation and assessment by others. Therefore, determining when to
speak, as well as what to say, is crucial for an LLM agent playing the
Werewolf game. In this study, we introduce a Werewolf AI agent developed
for the Anytime Utterance Track of the AIWolfDial shared task. Our
system builds on the existing Inner Thoughts framework, which determines
utterance timing during conversations by generating internal thoughts
and evaluating the value of each thought for expression at that moment.
Since existing methods were designed for general dialogue, we have
redesigned the system specifically for the competitive environment and
incomplete information setting of Werewolf games. We call our adapted
method Adversarial Inner Thoughts. Through experiments involving
analysis of self-play logs and human evaluations, we analyze and discuss
the effectiveness of our approach.

## 1 Introduction

Recent large language models (LLMs) [OpenAI (2026)](#bib.bib31);
[Anthropic (2026)](#bib.bib32) have demonstrated advanced conversational
capabilities and are increasingly used as assistants that respond to
user inputs. However, in human communication, participating in a
conversation involves not only deciding what to say but also choosing
whether to speak at all. This choice is governed by conversational
context and social norms, and carries even greater significance in
competitive dialogue environments.

The importance of choosing between speaking and remaining silent extends
beyond everyday conversation to games that involve communication. A
prominent example is the Werewolf game. The Werewolf game is an
incomplete information game in which players are divided into a villager
team and a werewolf team, each aiming to eliminate the opposing side
without their roles being revealed. In this game, utterances containing
contradictions may provide clues that allow others to infer one’s true
identity. Thus, utterances are constantly monitored and evaluated by
other players, serving as evidence that influences suspicion and trust.
At the same time, silence is also subject to interpretation. Refraining
from speaking may be perceived as non-cooperation in the discussion and
invite suspicion, while revealing too much information may make one a
target of the werewolf team. In other words, the Werewolf game
constitutes a dialogue environment in which both utterances and silence
carry strategic significance.

The AIWolfDial shared task [Kano et al. (2023)](#bib.bib2); [Kano et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib3); [Kano et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4) is an international
competition that promotes the development of Werewolf AI agents. Its
recently introduced Anytime Utterance Track allows each agent to freely
decide when to speak, with a limit of four utterances per day. Details
of the game rules and evaluation criteria are provided in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Task Details ‣ Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents").

Despite these efforts, LLM agent research targeting the Werewolf game
has primarily focused on enhancing quality of utterance content and
designing strategy [Tanaka et al. (2024)](#bib.bib5); [Sato et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib7); [Qi and Inaba (2024)](#bib.bib8); [Okada and Ito
(2025)](#bib.bib10). For example, approaches have been proposed that
employ prompt design to maintain consistency in utterances and actions,
or that introduce personas to ensure diversity in expression.
Consequently, the decision of when to speak and when to remain silent
has not been treated as a design variable. Meanwhile, research on
utterance timing in dialogue has advanced in recent years [Bhagtani et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib22); [Nama et al. (2026)](#bib.bib20); [Patel et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib26); [Yang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib21); [Kimura et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib23), but most of it assumes settings in which only
cooperation occurs, and does not consider environments where
participants’ interests conflict and silence functions as a strategy.

In this study, we formulate the Werewolf agent’s utterance timing as a
decision-making problem based on the evaluation of internal motivation.
Specifically, we adapt the framework proposed by [Liu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib1) to the Werewolf game. In this framework, an agent
generates internal thoughts behind the conversation and evaluates
whether each thought is worth expressing at that moment, producing an
utterance only when the evaluation exceeds a certain threshold. We
redesign the evaluation criteria to suit the adversarial and incomplete
information setting. Furthermore, since the Anytime Utterance Track
imposes a limit on the number of utterances per day, speaking without
discretion is costly, making this an environment where the effect of
such control is particularly pronounced.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

- •
  We propose Adversarial Inner Thoughts, an adaptation of the Inner
  Thoughts framework to multi-party dialogue in adversarial,
  incomplete-information games.
- •
  We implement our proposed method for werewolf AI and analyze the
  impact of utterance timing control on agent behavior through game logs
  and human evaluations.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Agents for the Werewolf Game

Since the Werewolf game requires reasoning under incomplete information,
cooperation, persuasion, and deception, it has served as a testbed for
intelligent agents [Toriumi et al. (2017)](#bib.bib15); [Shibata et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib16). Recently, the AIWolfDial shared task [Kano et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib2); [Kano et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3); [Kano et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib4) has driven the development and evaluation of agents
that play the game through natural language conversation.

Advances in LLMs have enabled more flexible and sophisticated designs
for Werewolf agents. Early work examined whether LLMs can play the game
through free-form conversation without additional training [Xu et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib12). Later studies augmented LLMs with components that
compensate for their limited strategic decision-making. An RL policy
selects among LLM-generated action candidates [Xu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib14), and an external module trained on human game
records handles domain-specific logical inference [Wu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib13). The AIWolfDial shared task has produced a range of
such designs. To enhance the consistency and expressive quality of
utterances, systems have summarized the dialogue history and conditioned
generation on persona information [Tanaka et al. (2024)](#bib.bib5), or
combined LLM-based generation with rule-based control [Sato et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib7). To support reasoning and strategic play, other
systems have explicitly represented the logical relations among
players [Watanabe and Kano (2024)](#bib.bib6), designed persuasion
strategies grounded in situation analysis [Qi and Inaba
(2024)](#bib.bib8), and incorporated BDI-based reasoning or manually
designed game strategies into LLM agents [Gondo et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib11); [Okada and Ito (2025)](#bib.bib10); [Harada and
Kano (2025)](#bib.bib9).

These studies share a common premise: a speaking opportunity is given,
and the agent decides what to say within it. We instead give the agent
the decision of whether to speak at all.

### 2.2 Utterance Timing in Multi-party Dialogue

Turn-taking is a fundamental mechanism by which participants coordinate
who speaks and when [Sacks et al. (1974)](#bib.bib17), and computational
work has long studied how to predict it in spoken dialogue [Ekstedt and
Skantze (2020)](#bib.bib27). However, even strong LLMs are reported to
know what to say but not when to say it [Umair et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib24). In text-based group chat, a message is complete
when posted, so there is no turn end to detect, and the agent must
decide at every moment whether to contribute at all.

This decision has been formulated explicitly as a choice between
speaking and remaining silent. Recent work trains and evaluates agents
that judge, at each point in an ongoing conversation, whether to
contribute [Bhagtani et al. (2026)](#bib.bib22); [Nama et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib20); [Patel et al. (2025)](#bib.bib26); [Yang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib21); [Kimura et al. (2026)](#bib.bib23), indicating that
appropriate silence has to be modeled or learned rather than expected to
emerge from general language ability. However, speakers also weigh
whether an intended utterance fits the current position [Jiang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib18), and the Inner Thoughts framework builds an agent
around this view [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1): it continuously
generates private thoughts alongside the conversation and evaluates the
intrinsic motivation to express each one, so that speaking and staying
silent follow from what the agent is thinking rather than from the state
of the conversation.

These settings are cooperative, and silence means that no useful
contribution is available. In Werewolf, silence is itself strategic: an
agent may withhold information that would expose its role, or delay an
accusation and let another player’s account settle. Two systems address
timing here. Werewolf Arena lets agents bid for the floor [Bailis et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib19), and Time to Talk separates a scheduler deciding
whether to speak from a generator producing the utterance [Eckhaus et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib25). In both, the decision is reduced to a single
quantity computed without reference to any candidate utterance. [Eckhaus
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib25) suggest generating a candidate first and then
judging whether to express it; we take this direction and ground the
decision in the agent’s own thoughts.

## 3 Methodology

In this study, we propose Adversarial Inner Thoughts, which redesigns
the Inner Thoughts framework by [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1) to adapt
it to the adversarial, incomplete-information setting of the Werewolf
game.
Figure [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.1 Agents for the Werewolf Game ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents")
shows an overview of the proposed method.

### 3.1 Original Framework

In the original Inner Thoughts framework, an AI agent continuously
generates internal thoughts in parallel to the ongoing conversation and
decides when to speak based on its intrinsic motivation to express these
thoughts. The framework operates through a five-step pipeline: (1)
Trigger, (2) Retrieval, (3) Thought Formation, (4) Evaluation, and (5)
Participation. This framework was originally tested under cooperative,
open-domain settings. We retain the pipeline structure and adapt each
step to the Werewolf game, except for the Trigger step, which we adopt
directly as its mechanism is equally applicable. In addition, the agent
maintains a game history and a conversation summary, which are
referenced as shared context throughout the pipeline.

### 3.2 Trigger

Since the original Trigger mechanism of detecting new utterances and
pauses is equally applicable to the Werewolf game, we adopt it directly.
Specifically, we define two types of triggers: on*new_message, which is
activated when an agent receives a new utterance; and on_pause, which
prompts the agent to think when no participant has spoken for a certain
period of time $`t*{\mathrm{pause}}`$.

### 3.3 Retrieval

Once a trigger is activated, the agent retrieves relevant information to
formulate its thoughts. Our framework maintains two types of memory for
dynamic retrieval: long-term memory and a thought reservoir. Long-term
memory stores entries describing the agent’s general strategies,
beliefs, and knowledge related to the game. Unlike the thought
reservoir, which accumulates automatically during play, long-term memory
entries are managed by predetermined logic: entries can be inserted or
removed at specific game phases depending on the agent’s role and the
game state. On the other hand, the thought reservoir accumulates inner
thoughts generated by the agent during the game, each stored with
metadata including a timestamp and the source utterance that prompted
the thought.

The agent retrieves the top-$`k`$ entries most salient to the latest
utterance. In the original framework, saliency is computed by directly
comparing the thought text with the query. In our method, we instead use
the source information of each entry: the memory text itself for
long-term memory entries, and the associated source utterance for
thoughts in the reservoir. This allows the agent to retrieve previous
thoughts triggered by similar conversational events, enabling it to
build on or refine its earlier reasoning when a comparable situation
arises.

where $`u_{\mathrm{interp}}`$ is an LLM-generated interpretation of

thoughts consistent with its earlier reasoning in similar situations,

### 3.4 Thought Formation

In the Thought Formation step, the agent generates multiple candidate
inner thoughts for expressing during their participation. An inner
thought is not the utterance itself, but a concise utterance policy that
describes a reaction to recent conversation, a topic to raise, or a
dialogue action to take.

Following [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1), who were inspired by dual
process theory ([Evans, 2008](#bib.bib28)) and observed in their
formative study that human participants’ thoughts during multi-party
conversation fall into reactive and deliberative patterns, inner
thoughts are categorized into two types: System 1 and System 2. System 1
generates immediate, reactive thoughts based primarily on the most
recent utterances. System 2 takes both the conversation history and the
game state as input to generate deliberative thoughts about the current
situation. This distinction is particularly relevant in the Werewolf
game, where an agent needs both immediate reactions to maintain natural
conversational flow and deliberative reasoning to plan strategic
behavior. The two systems generate thoughts independently via separate
prompts. System 1 produces a single thought per trigger, whereas System
2 generates three thoughts from a single prompt to encourage diversity
among candidates.

While the original framework targets general multi-party conversation,
our method generates thoughts as concrete utterance policies that lead
to decision-making and dialogue actions in the Werewolf game. System 2
in particular generates game-specific policies such as proposing a
direction for discussion, requesting confirmation of specific
information or claims, and performing role-dependent deception or
strategic persuasion. For example, an agent assigned the Possessed role
may generate a thought to falsely claim the Seer role in order to
protect the Werewolf.

### 3.5 Evaluation

In the Evaluation step, each generated thought in
Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Thought Formation ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents")
is evaluated for the strength of its motivation to be expressed, using
an LLM-as-a-Judge approach [Zheng et al. (2023)](#bib.bib29); [Liu et
al. (2023)](#bib.bib30). We follow the multi-criteria evaluation
framework of [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1) and restructure the criteria
to assess utterance appropriateness in the Werewolf game. Specifically,
drawing on the original framework’s evaluation criteria, we define three
general conversational criteria—Contextual Relevance, Naturalness, and
Temporal Appropriateness—and three game-oriented criteria—Information
Utility, Strategic Utility, and Risk
Control (Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.3 Retrieval ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents")).
On Day 0, where conversation is limited to greetings and preliminary
discussion, only the first three criteria (i.e., Contextual Relevance,
Naturalness, and Temporal Appropriateness) are used. From Day 1 onward,
all six criteria are applied.

Rather than scoring all six criteria independently, the LLM considers
the factors most relevant to the current situation, weighs the reasons
for and against expressing the thought, and outputs a single motivation
score from 1.0 to 5.0.

### 3.6 Participation

Based on the evaluation scores in
Section [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Evaluation ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents")
and the current turn situation, the agent decides whether to speak and
generates an utterance from the selected thought. The agent first
classifies the current turn situation into three types using an LLM:
open turn, where no specific participant is expected to speak; turn
allocated to self, where the agent is expected to respond; and turn
allocated to others, where another participant is expected to respond.

Let $`r^{*}=\max_{i}r_{i}`$ denote the highest evaluation score among
the System 2 thoughts, and let $`\theta_{\mathrm{speak}}`$ and
$`\theta_{\mathrm{interrupt}}`$
($`\theta_{\mathrm{speak}}<\theta_{\mathrm{interrupt}}`$) denote the
speaking and interrupt thresholds, respectively. The agent’s speaking
decision is as follows:

- •
  Turn allocated to self: The agent always speaks using the
  highest-rated thought.
- •
  Open turn: The agent speaks if $`r^{*}\geq\theta_{\mathrm{speak}}`$.
- •
  Turn allocated to others: The agent speaks only if
  $`r^{*}\geq\theta_{\mathrm{interrupt}}`$, as the agent should
  intervene only when the motivation to express a thought is strong
  enough to justify interrupting another player’s expected response.

If no System 2 thought meets the threshold, a System 1 thought may be
selected with a turn-type-dependent probability, enabling short reactive
responses and preventing excessive silence. If no thought is selected,
the agent remains silent.

### 3.7 Implementation Details for AIWolfDial 2026 Shared Task

We use gpt-5.6-luna for all pipeline steps. The pause trigger fires
after $`t_{\mathrm{pause}}=10`$ seconds of silence. For embedding-based
retrieval, we use text-embedding-3-small with $`k=3`$. The speaking and
interrupt thresholds are set to $`\theta_{\mathrm{speak}}=4.0`$ and
$`\theta_{\mathrm{interrupt}}=4.3`$, determined empirically.

In this shared task, each agent is assigned a character profile by the
game server, which includes a name, age, gender, and personality
description. Since the profile may not specify a speaking style, our
agent generates a brief speaking style description and example
utterances from the given personality at the start of each game, which
are used throughout the pipeline to maintain consistent
characterization. At the end of each day, the agent generates a summary
of the conversation, which is referenced in subsequent steps to
efficiently capture participants’ claims and stances.

In addition to the Inner Thoughts pipeline, we incorporate rule-based
behaviors for game-specific situations. Regardless of role, each agent
reserves its last utterance of each day for declaring a vote target. The
Seer reports divination results at the beginning of each day using a
single prompt, bypassing the pipeline. For the Possessed role, the
strategy guideline for a false Seer claim is inserted into long-term
memory at the start of Day 1 with a high weight ($`w=10^{6}`$), ensuring
it is almost always retrieved and thus strongly influencing thought
generation toward the intended deception. In the 5-player village, if
the Possessed survives to Day 2, the remaining players necessarily
consist of one Werewolf, one villager-team player, and the Possessed; in
this situation, the agent calls for cooperation with the Werewolf to
secure a majority vote, using a single prompt outside the pipeline. In
this shared task, we use long-term memory only for role-specific
strategy guidelines such as the false Seer claim described above,
leaving the accumulation of cross-game experience for future work. The
prompt templates used in each pipeline step are provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Prompt Templates ‣ Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents").

## 4 Experiments

To demonstrate that our Adversarial Inner Thoughts framework produces
appropriate and proactive utterance behavior in Werewolf game scenarios,
we conducted a human evaluation based on self-play logs in Japanese. We
recruited 10 participants via a crowdsourcing platform¹¹ 1
[https://crowdworks.jp](https://crowdworks.jp) to evaluate a total of 60
simulated game logs, with 30 games per condition.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Following [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1), instead of displaying static
game histories, we presented the logs as an animated chat interface that
replays the game in real time, allowing participants to perceive
utterance timing more accurately. The interface also displayed in-game
events such as voting, execution, and attack results.

For the self-play generation, we applied the following controls. To
ensure that the evaluation focused on game-relevant dialogue from Day 1
onward, each agent was restricted to a single utterance on Day 0. To
control for game length, only games completed within eight minutes were
included. In addition, since all agents share the same pause threshold
$`t_{\mathrm{pause}}`$, they would initiate speech simultaneously when
the threshold is reached. To avoid this, we added random noise sampled
from $`\mathcal{N}(0,1)`$ to each agent’s trigger timing.

### 4.2 Baseline System

To verify that the improvement in dialogue quality stems from our
thought generation and evaluation components rather than the
trigger-based turn detection alone, we define a baseline system. The
baseline is derived from our proposed system by retaining only the
trigger-based turn detection, which classifies the current turn
situation into one of three types: open turn, turn allocated to self,
and turn allocated to others. As in our system, the agent always speaks
in a turn allocated to self. Unlike our system, however, the baseline
always remains silent in a turn allocated to others. In an open turn,
the agent speaks with a probability of 0.3. Since utterance timing in
this design depends solely on the current turn situation, all agents may
simultaneously refrain from speaking, causing the conversation to stall
completely. To address this, if a turn allocated to others occurs twice
in a row, the subsequent turn is treated as an open turn to ensure
speaking opportunities. For a fair comparison, the proposed system
adopts the same fallback mechanism.

### 4.3 Evaluation Procedure

Prior to the evaluation, participants were provided with a manual
explaining the rules of the five-player Werewolf game as well as the
purpose and procedure of the study. At the start of the evaluation,
participants answered a single question about their prior experience
with the Werewolf game. They then completed a comprehension check
consisting of seven questions about the game rules and roles to confirm
their understanding of the game flow. Participants were required to
answer all questions correctly; those who answered any question
incorrectly were asked to retake the test until they passed.

Participants then watched six game logs in total. They were informed
that all dialogues were generated by AI agents. The first three games
were from one condition and the remaining three from the other, with the
order of conditions counterbalanced across participants. After watching
each game, participants evaluated the dialogue quality on six
dimensions. After watching all six games, participants completed a
comparative evaluation between the two conditions.

### 4.4 Measurements

After watching each game, participants evaluated the quality of the
dialogue rather than the agents’ in-game strategies or tactics. They
rated their agreement with six statements in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents")
on a 1–7 Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.
Participants were also asked to describe any specific parts where
utterance or silence timing felt unnatural in an open-ended response.
After evaluating all games, participants selected which condition felt
more natural and human-like from three options—the first half, tie, or
the second half—and provided an explanation with concrete examples.

### 4.5 Results

Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Beyond What to Say: Proactive Utterance Timing with Inner Thoughts for Werewolf Agents")
shows the distribution of ratings for each dimension. Our system
received higher mean scores than the baseline across all six dimensions
(Anthropomorphism: 4.2 vs. 3.9; Coherence: 4.5 vs. 4.2; Engagement: 5.0
vs. 4.3; Turn Appropriateness: 3.9 vs. 3.3; Initiative: 4.9 vs. 4.6;
Adaptability: 4.3 vs. 4.0). To account for the repeated-measures design,
we aggregated the ratings per participant and compared the two
conditions using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. None of the differences
reached statistical significance, with Engagement showing the smallest
p-value ($`p=.121`$). In the comparative evaluation, four participants
preferred the baseline, three preferred the proposed system, and three
rated them as comparable.

The open-ended responses revealed qualitative differences between the
two conditions. The most frequent criticism of the proposed system
concerned non-responses to directed questions. One participant noted:
“Many players did not respond to questions from others, even when it was
their chance to clear suspicion.” This is likely due to the higher
latency of our pipeline, which requires five LLM API calls per trigger
(retrieval, thought formation, evaluation, turn-taking, and response
generation), compared to two in the baseline. The resulting delay may
cause the agent to miss the appropriate timing for responding to a
directed question. A possible improvement would be to introduce a
streamlined response path for directly addressed turns, reducing the
number of processing steps when the agent is explicitly named or when an
immediate response is expected. On the other hand, the baseline system
was more frequently criticized for prolonged silences and inconsistent
utterance pacing. One participant described the silences as “unnaturally
long, even considering deliberation time.” This suggests that its
turn-taking mechanism, which relies solely on turn situation
classification, struggles to produce well-timed utterances.

Interestingly, several participants also noted that the proposed system
produced more substantive arguments supported by concrete reasoning. In
the comparative evaluation, one participant commented that “each
player’s claims were well supported by concrete reasoning, and the
discussion felt more heated,” while another noted that “the utterances
were aligned with others’ opinions and the overall flow of the
discussion, making it a substantive debate.” This may be because the
baseline only determines whether to speak based on who should take the
turn, whereas the proposed system additionally evaluates whether each
thought is worth expressing at that moment. Both conditions shared
common issues such as repetitive expressions and translation-like
phrasing, which are attributable to the underlying language model rather
than the utterance timing mechanism.

### 4.6 Case Study

utterance that redirects the discussion toward the Seer’s credibility.
Note that Shion’s utterance appears just 0.2 seconds earlier in the log
due to asynchronous generation; George’s response was generated
independently upon detecting the silence. Immediately after, Mio
directly addresses George, and the turn-taking classifier classifies
this as turn_allocated_to_self. The system then selects a thought that
challenges the basis of Mei’s werewolf result (rated 4.2), producing a
concrete rebuttal.

This example illustrates three key properties of our approach: the
system treats silence not as passive waiting but as a trigger to
initiate proactive speech; it evaluates the strategic value of each
thought rather than simply filling the silence; and it adapts its
response when directly addressed, shifting from a proactive stance to a
context-appropriate reply.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we adapted the Inner Thoughts framework to the
adversarial, incomplete-information setting of the Werewolf game. Human
evaluation showed that the proposed system numerically outperformed the
baseline across all dimensions, though a trade-off between utterance
quality and response timeliness was observed. Future work includes
reducing pipeline latency and exploring the use of long-term memory to
retain strategic experiences across games.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our evaluation is limited in
scale: we collected ratings for 60 games from 10 participants, with each
game rated by a single participant. This prevents us from measuring
inter-annotator agreement and likely contributes to the lack of
statistical significance despite consistent differences in mean scores.
A larger-scale evaluation with multiple ratings per game would allow us
to draw firmer conclusions and to conduct finer-grained analyses, such
as examining how evaluator familiarity with the Werewolf game affects
ratings.

Second, the evaluation was conducted exclusively in Japanese with the
five-player village configuration, and all games were self-play matches
in which every agent used the same system. In the AIWolfDial shared
task, agents compete against those developed by other teams, and whether
our approach functions equally well in such heterogeneous settings
requires further investigation.

Another limitation is that our proposed pipeline affects not only
utterance timing but also utterance content through the generation and
evaluation of multiple candidate thoughts. Therefore, the current
comparison does not isolate the effect of utterance timing itself.
Future work should explore appropriate evaluation designs for
disentangling the effects of utterance timing and content. For example,
we could control dialogue content while manipulating only the timing of
utterances.

Finally, the speaking and interrupt thresholds were determined
empirically rather than through systematic search. Since the shared task
assigns each agent a character profile, these thresholds could also be
adapted to reflect individual personalities, for example by making
talkative characters more prone to interrupting. We leave this direction
to future work.

## Appendix A Task Details

The Werewolf game is an incomplete information game in which each
player’s role is hidden from others, and players must infer others’
roles through dialogue. The AIWolfDial 2026 shared task is a competition
aimed at developing AI agents that play the Werewolf game using natural
language.

### A.1 Player Roles

Each player belongs to either the villager team or the werewolf team.
The villager team aims to eliminate all werewolves, while the werewolf
team aims to survive and outnumber the villagers. Since each player’s
role is hidden from others, players must infer others’ roles from
dialogue and in-game actions.

The Villager has no special ability. The Seer can divine one player each
night to learn their team. The Medium learns the team of the player who
was eliminated by a vote. The Bodyguard can protect one player each
night from werewolf attacks. The Werewolf selects a target to attack and
eliminate them at night. Since the Possessed has no special ability and
is human by species, they can act on behalf of the werewolf team. The
Medium and Bodyguard appear only in the 9-player village, where the two
werewolves can also coordinate through whisper.

### A.2 Game Procedure

The game begins at Day 0, which serves as an introductory phase for
greetings and preliminary discussion. The Seer performs their first
divination at the end of Day 0; however, no voting, guarding, or
attacking takes place. From Day 1 onward, each day proceeds as follows:
players discuss whom to eliminate, then vote, with the player receiving
the most votes being eliminated. After voting, role-specific night
actions (i.e., divination, whispering, guarding, and attacking) are
carried out. This cycle repeats until either all werewolves are
eliminated, resulting in a villager team victory, or the number of
surviving werewolves equals or exceeds that of surviving humans,
resulting in a werewolf team victory.

### A.3 Communication Tracks

This shared task offers two tracks: the Turn-Based Track and the Anytime
Utterance Track. In the Turn-Based Track, agents speak upon receiving an
utterance request from the game server. During each daytime turn, every
surviving agent is given one speaking opportunity in a randomly
determined order. Each agent therefore decides what to say when given
the opportunity by the server.

In the Anytime Utterance Track, other agents’ utterances are delivered
in real time, and each agent freely decides when to speak based on the
conversational situation, with a limit of four utterances per day within
a ten-minute conversation phase. Consequently, agents must autonomously
decide not only what to say, but also whether and when to speak.

### A.4 Evaluation

Agents are evaluated based on win rate computed from competition logs,
as well as subjective assessments by human judges and an LLM. The
subjective evaluation covers the following criteria: (A) naturalness of
expression, (B) naturalness of dialogue in context, (C) consistency of
utterance content, (D) coherence between game actions and dialogue
content, (E) richness and consistency of character portrayal based on
assigned profiles, and (F) teamwork within the same faction (9-player
village only). Strategically justified lies or contradictions consistent
with the agent’s role are permitted under criteria C and D.

# When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing

###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) has shown promise for training multimodal
agents to reason and use tools. However, it remains unclear when
fine-grained credit assignment improves learning and when it fails to
improve task performance. Video editing provides a useful testbed for
this question, combining multimodal perception, long-horizon planning,
and tool use with delayed outcome feedback. We construct four
movie-trailer editing task types—Content Selection, Narrative
Sequencing, Rough Cut, and Fine Cut—that reflect real-world editing
workflows and span different levels of task complexity and interaction
horizon. Decaying process rewards provides the largest benefit on Fine
Cut, with a 45.4% relative gain over outcome-only GRPO on tasks
averaging more than 60 turns under outcome-only GRPO. On the three
shorter-horizon task types averaging 30–40 turns under the same
baseline, outcome-only GRPO remains competitive, with decaying process
rewards yielding at most a 10.4% relative gain. Process rewards from an
LLM judge initially improve tool use and encourage visual inspection. As
training progresses, however, GRPO+PRM with a fixed process-reward
weight increasingly exhibits excessive reasoning and redundant tool
calls: process rewards continue to rise while task completion rates and
outcome scores decline. Decaying the process-reward weight to zero
mitigates this reward-hacking behavior while preserving the early gains.
Despite dense token-level supervision, on-policy self-distillation
(OPSD) yields little improvement or substantial degradation and is
highly sensitive to the choice of privileged information. Our analysis
reveals differences in teacher–student disagreement and gradient
magnitudes, and suggests possible dilution of informative signals over
long trajectories.

## 1 Introduction

Video agents are moving from video understanding towards long-horizon
multimodal tasks such as video production, where models must integrate
multimodal perception, planning, and tool use ([Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib42); [Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib55); [Sandoval-Castañeda
et al., 2025](#bib.bib31); [Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib63); [Zhang
et al., 2026b](#bib.bib60)). However, even the strongest agent systems
struggle on these tasks ([Cao et al., 2026](#bib.bib2); [Hu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). Reinforcement learning has shown promise for
improving their performance, enabling agents to refine their
decision-making and tool-use policies through feedback from task
execution ([Kulkarni and Fazli, 2026](#bib.bib15); [Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib59); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib56); [Zhang et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib61); [Wang et al., 2025d](#bib.bib48)). However, reward
formulation remains challenging because the quality of the resulting
video rests on subjective judgment ([Yan et al., 2026a](#bib.bib53);
[Wen et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)). In addition, feedback on the final
video becomes available only after a sequence of interdependent editing
decisions, making it difficult to determine how individual actions
contribute to the final outcome. Credit assignment is therefore central
to training these agents ([Arjona-Medina et al., 2019](#bib.bib1); [Feng
et al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Tan et al., 2026](#bib.bib38)). Yet it
remains unclear when finer-grained credit assignment helps or hurts
learning in long-horizon multimodal tasks.

Most work on video production builds a workflow around a model that it
never updates ([Sandoval-Castañeda et al., 2025](#bib.bib31); [Zhao
et al., 2026a](#bib.bib63); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib60)).
Crayotter trains an editing agent with segment-level credit, where
segments group consecutive editing events from the same stage ([Yan
et al., 2026a](#bib.bib53)). We ask how trajectory-, turn-, and
token-level credit compare across editing tasks under a shared training
setup. Elsewhere, several lines report that finer credit helps. VAGEN
gives a multi-turn visual agent dense turn-level supervision ([Wang
et al., 2025b](#bib.bib44)), TAPO repairs the credit that a
trajectory-level advantage misassigns to tool calls inside failed
rollouts ([Dong et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), and interface agents trained
on step-level progress signals beat actors trained on outcome rewards
alone ([Zhang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib58); [Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)). Other work reports negative results for process
rewards. Group-relative optimization with an outcome reward provably
carries an implicit process reward, which makes an explicit one look
redundant ([Sullivan and Koller, 2025](#bib.bib36)). A process reward is
dense but remains prone to bias and reward hacking ([Zheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib65); [Pan et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)). Outcome-only
training is argued to suffice on long horizons once exploration is wide
enough ([Pu et al., 2026](#bib.bib28)). How credit should be assigned in
long-horizon multimodal agents therefore remains unclear.

We study the question in movie-trailer editing. We generate 797 tasks
from 49 real movies and their released trailers. The work decomposes
into four stages that a human editor performs in order, which give the
task types Content Selection (CS), Narrative Sequencing (NS), Rough Cut
(RC), and Fine Cut (FC). The four types differ in editing requirements,
interaction length, and in the quality profile of the turns they elicit,
which lets us ask how the same credit signal behaves as the task
changes. We train Qwen3.5-9B ([Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib29)) for 50
updates on 716 of these tasks under four schemes that cover the three
granularities. At the trajectory level we use outcome-only GRPO. At the
turn level we compare GRPO+PRM (fixed $`\lambda`$) and GRPO+PRM
(decayed $`\lambda`$). Both use a process reward from Gemini 3.8 Flash,
averaged over three votes. At the token level we use on-policy
self-distillation (OPSD). Its teacher receives privileged information
that the student never sees: reference answers or turn-level
suggestions. Outcome scores come from Gemini 3.1 Pro, so the process
reward and the outcome reward never share a model. The schemes share the
base model, training tasks, and number of updates, while
objective-specific settings are detailed in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Training Configuration and Optimization ‣ Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing").
We evaluate every run on 81 validation tasks four times and report the
mean, and we record how the agent behaves as well as what it scores.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we develop a data-construction
pipeline that turns films and their released trailers into four types of
editing tasks reflecting real-world editing workflows. Training on these
tasks improves Qwen3.5-9B’s video editing performance on held-out films.
Second, we compare trajectory-, turn-, and token-level learning signals
under a shared setup. Decaying process rewards yields a 45.4% relative
gain over outcome-only GRPO on Fine Cut, while outcome-only GRPO remains
competitive on the three shorter-horizon task types. Third, we analyze
training dynamics and model behavior to identify limitations of the
tested finer-grained signals: fixed process rewards can undermine task
completion despite encouraging desirable behaviors, and OPSD is
sensitive to the privileged information provided to its teacher.

## 2 Related Work

#### Agents for video production.

Video-editing agents have evolved from executing language-guided actions
to managing long-horizon multi-stage production ([Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib42); [Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib55); [Sandoval-Castañeda
et al., 2025](#bib.bib31); [Wang et al., 2025c](#bib.bib46); [Ding
et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib63); [Li et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib18); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib60); [Liang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Yan et al., 2026b](#bib.bib54); [Zhou et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib66)). These systems focus on designing editing workflows
while keeping the underlying models fixed. Despite this progress,
advanced agents remain unreliable in professional video post-production
tasks ([Cao et al., 2026](#bib.bib2); [Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)).
Another line of work trains video-editing policies ([Tang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib40); [Hu et al., 2023](#bib.bib13); [Zhou et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib67); [Yu et al., 2026](#bib.bib57)). These methods use
policy gradients for shot composition or supervised and preference-based
post-training for tool-using planners. Crayotter assigns segment-level
credit based on rankings of finished videos produced for the same
task ([Yan et al., 2026a](#bib.bib53)). Our study compares trajectory-,
turn-, and token-level supervision across a shared suite of editing
tasks.

#### Credit assignment for long-horizon agents.

Attributing delayed outcomes to the decisions that caused them is a
long-standing challenge ([Arjona-Medina et al., 2019](#bib.bib1); [Ren
et al., 2021](#bib.bib30); [Harutyunyan et al., 2019](#bib.bib11);
[Mesnard et al., 2020](#bib.bib22); [Meulemans et al.,
2023](#bib.bib23)). Research on language agents builds explicit learning
signals at several granularities. Trajectory-level methods score a whole
rollout against its peers ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib33); [Wang
et al., 2025d](#bib.bib48)). Turn-level methods estimate value,
progress, or a relative advantage for each turn ([Zhou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib69); [Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib43); [Feng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6); [Xi et al., 2026](#bib.bib51); [Li et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib17); [Tan et al., 2026](#bib.bib38); [Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib21)). Finer methods assign an advantage to a reasoning
step or a segment within a response ([Kazemnejad et al.,
2024](#bib.bib14); [Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)). Multimodal work
follows the same three levels across visual, interface, embodied, and
video agents ([Wang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib44); [Pang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib26); [Dong et al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Tan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib39); [Zheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib65); [Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3); [Zhang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib58); [Zhou et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib68); [Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib52); [Kulkarni and Fazli,
2026](#bib.bib15); [Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib16); [Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib59); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib56); [Zhang et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib61)). Whether explicit finer-grained supervision improves
learning remains an empirical question. Under mild assumptions,
outcome-only GRPO can be written as a process-aware objective that
carries an implicit Monte Carlo process reward ([Sullivan and Koller,
2025](#bib.bib36)). Process reward models are also hard to build and to
evaluate. Synthesizing their labels by Monte Carlo estimation
generalizes worse than using a judge or a human annotator ([Zhang
et al., 2025b](#bib.bib62)), and benchmarks for fine-grained error
detection expose substantial weaknesses in current models ([Song et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35); [Sun et al., 2026](#bib.bib37)). Empirical studies
nevertheless report benefits from denser feedback ([Wang and
Ammanabrolu, 2025](#bib.bib45); [Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib50)),
alongside failure modes involving teacher guidance, reward-model
overoptimization, and proxy exploitation ([Li et al.,
2026d](#bib.bib19); [Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Skalse et al.,
2022](#bib.bib34); [Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib8); [Thaman,
2026](#bib.bib41); [Pu et al., 2026](#bib.bib28); [Gunjal et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9); [Shao et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)). Evidence remains
limited on when finer-grained supervision helps or hurts long-horizon
multimodal agents, and how these effects vary across tasks and training
stages.

## 3 Preliminaries

### 3.1 Long-Horizon Multimodal Agent

We consider a task $`x`$ drawn from a task distribution $`\mathcal{D}`$,
in which an agent interacts with an environment over many turns,
following standard formulations of long-horizon agentic RL ([Zhou
et al., 2024](#bib.bib69); [Wang et al., 2025d](#bib.bib48); [Feng
et al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Zhang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib61)). At turn
$`t`$ the agent observes a state $`s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}`$ and emits an
action $`a_{t}\in\mathcal{V}^{*}`$, and the environment returns the next
state,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_{t},x),\qquad s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot\mid s_{t},a_{t}).
``` |  | (3.1) |

The state $`s_{t}`$ carries images, tool outputs, and a mutable
workspace alongside the textual history. The episode yields a trajectory
$`\tau=(s_{0},a_{0},\ldots,a_{T-1},s_{T})`$ and a delivered artifact
$`y_{\tau}=\operatorname{Output}(s_{T})`$, which a grader maps to a
terminal reward $`R_{\mathrm{out}}(y_{\tau})\in[0,1]`$. No reward is
available before the agent commits.

### 3.2 Credit Assignment

We next present three credit assignment schemes at different
granularities.

#### Trajectory level.

At this granularity we study Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib33)) with an outcome-only reward. GRPO
estimates advantages from a group of rollouts without a learned critic.
For each task $`x`$, we sample $`G`$ trajectories
$`\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$ from $`\pi_{\mathrm{old}}`$ and score them
with $`o_{i}=R_{\mathrm{out}}(y_{\tau_{i}})`$. The group-normalized
advantage is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{o_{i}-\mu_{o}}{\sigma_{o}+\epsilon},
``` |  | (3.2) |

with $`\mu_{o}`$ and $`\sigma_{o}`$ the group mean and sample standard
deviation. We optimize the policy using rollout importance sampling with
token-mean reduction over valid assistant tokens
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).
Every token of $`\tau_{i}`$ receives the same $`A_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}`$,
so the signal does not vary within a trajectory. We write this setting
as outcome-only GRPO.

#### Turn level.

A process reward model (PRM) scores each action from its interaction
history and the tool feedback it produced ([Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib47)). We average the $`M`$ valid judgments of a turn into
$`r_{i,t}`$ and add a normalized process term to the shared outcome
term,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{i,t}=A_{i}^{\mathrm{out}}+\lambda A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{proc}},\qquad A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{proc}}=\frac{r_{i,t}-\mu_{t}}{\sigma_{t}+\epsilon},
``` |  | (3.3) |

where $`\mu_{t}`$ and $`\sigma_{t}`$ are the mean and sample standard
deviation of valid process scores at the same turn index $`t`$ across
rollouts of task $`x`$. We do not apply additional batch-level advantage
normalization. We call $`\lambda`$ the process-reward weight and compare
two settings. GRPO+PRM (fixed $`\lambda`$) keeps this weight fixed
throughout training. GRPO+PRM (decayed $`\lambda`$) reduces it to zero
partway through training. Later updates therefore use only the outcome
reward. Every token of action $`a_{i,t}`$ receives $`A_{i,t}`$, so the
signal varies across turns but not within one.

#### Token level.

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) matches a teacher distribution on the
student’s own prefixes ([Zhao et al., 2026b](#bib.bib64)). The teacher
$`\pi_{\mathrm{tea}}`$ is a frozen copy of the initial policy and
receives privileged information $`c_{t}`$ that the student never sees.
We compare two forms of privileged information: reference answers and
turn-level suggestions. For token $`k`$ of action $`a_{t}`$ we write
$`p_{t,k}(v)=\pi_{\theta}(v\mid s_{t},x,a_{t,<k})`$ for the student and
$`q_{t,k}(v)=\pi_{\mathrm{tea}}(v\mid s_{t},x,c_{t},a_{t,<k})`$ for the
teacher. All OPSD experiments use the forward KL divergence,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,k}\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{t,k}\|p_{t,k})\right],
``` |  | (3.4) |

taken over the generated assistant tokens. In practice we evaluate the
KL on the teacher’s top-$`K`$ support rather than the full vocabulary,
and
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
gives the details. OPSD trains on this loss with no reward term at all.
Every token receives its own target, so the signal varies within a turn
as well.

## 4 Task Suite

### 4.1 Tasks and Environment

We study movie-trailer editing as it is done in practice ([Murch,
2001](#bib.bib24); [Pearlman, 2012](#bib.bib27)). We decompose the
editing of one trailer into four stages that a human editor works
through in order, following the workflow-oriented view of
AgenticVBench ([Cao et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)). Each stage gives one task
type, namely Content Selection (CS), Narrative Sequencing (NS), Rough
Cut (RC), and Fine Cut (FC).

#### Scoring.

Each task is scored on the artifact the agent commits when the episode
ends. All tasks use the same three channels for this outcome score
$`R_{\mathrm{out}}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{out}}=w_{\mathrm{det}}R_{\mathrm{det}}+w_{\mathrm{ref}}R_{\mathrm{ref}}+w_{\mathrm{blind}}R_{\mathrm{blind}},
``` |  | (4.1) |

where $`R_{\mathrm{det}}`$ is a deterministic verifier that scores the
artifact against that reference answer, $`R_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ is a
multimodal LLM judge that scores the artifact with that reference, and
$`R_{\mathrm{blind}}`$ is the same judge asking whether the artifact
works on its own terms. Both judge channels score against a weighted
rubric, and one model call grades each rubric item with its own evidence
attached. The weights of
Equation [4.1](#S4.E1 "In Scoring. ‣ 4.1 Tasks and Environment ‣ 4 Task Suite ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
sum to one and vary with the type. In this paper, we use Gemini 3.1 Pro
as the multimodal LLM judge. The process rewards used during training
come from a different model.

Content Selection and Narrative Sequencing use only the deterministic
score, with weights $`(1,0,0)`$. Content Selection scores the unordered
selected shot set $`S`$ against the reference set $`S^{\star}`$ using
F1, $`2|S\cap S^{\star}|/(|S|+|S^{\star}|)`$. It evaluates selection
alone, with partial credit for overlap.¹¹ 1 Selecting every candidate
would yield a mean F1 of 0.478 on the 14 Content Selection validation
tasks. Narrative Sequencing scores agreement with the reference order.
Rough Cut uses $`(0.4,0.4,0.2)`$, combining deterministic cut-point
similarity with reference-conditioned and reference-free judge scores.
Fine Cut uses $`(0,0.4,0.6)`$ to assess sound design and transitions
through the two judge channels. These task-specific metrics measure
different editing objectives, so their absolute values are not a common
scale of task difficulty.
Table [16](#A3.T16 "Table 16 ‣ Deterministic metrics. ‣ C.6 Outcome Metrics and Rubrics ‣ Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Training settings.

We train Qwen3.5-9B ([Qwen Team, 2026](#bib.bib29)) for 50 updates on
the 716 training tasks
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Data Construction and Dataset ‣ 4 Task Suite ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing"))
under four different schemes for credit assignment: (i) outcome-only
GRPO, (ii) GRPO+PRM (fixed $`\lambda`$), (iii) GRPO+PRM
(decayed $`\lambda`$), and (iv) OPSD. For GRPO+PRM (fixed $`\lambda`$),
we use a process-reward weight of $`\lambda=0.5`$ throughout training.
GRPO+PRM (decayed $`\lambda`$) keeps the same weight until update 20. We
then decay it to zero over updates 20–30 and keep it at zero thereafter.
Section [6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2 GRPO+PRM: Reward Hacking and Mitigation ‣ 6 Credit Assignment: Benefits and Failure Modes ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
explains the choice of this interval, and
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
gives the decay schedule and full training configuration. For OPSD, we
compare two forms of privileged information: reference answers and
turn-level suggestions. Gemini 3.8 Flash generates the turn-level
suggestions from the student’s context at each turn. These suggestions
are provided to the frozen teacher as privileged information.

## 6 Credit Assignment: Benefits and Failure Modes

We examine trajectory-level credit on long horizons in
Section [6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 Task Structure and Trajectory-Level Credit ‣ 6 Credit Assignment: Benefits and Failure Modes ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing").
Section [6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2 GRPO+PRM: Reward Hacking and Mitigation ‣ 6 Credit Assignment: Benefits and Failure Modes ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
links process-reward hacking to declining task completion and studies
process-reward weight decay as a mitigation.
Section [6.3](#S6.SS3 "6.3 OPSD Is Sensitive to Privileged information ‣ 6 Credit Assignment: Benefits and Failure Modes ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
examines the sensitivity of OPSD to privileged information.

### 6.1 Task Structure and Trajectory-Level Credit

#### Gains vary across editing tasks.

As shown in
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Experiment Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing"),
outcome-only GRPO raises the overall score from 0.284 to 0.413. However,
Fine Cut improves by only 0.050, whereas the other three task types each
improve by at least 0.15. On Fine Cut, adding process rewards further
raises the score from 0.201 to 0.239 with a fixed weight and 0.293 with
a decaying weight. These improvements show that further gains on Fine
Cut are achievable beyond the outcome-only result. On the other three
task types, the tested finer-grained methods yield limited additional
gains over outcome-only GRPO: none improves Content Selection or Rough
Cut, and the largest gain on Narrative Sequencing is 0.052.

#### A possible explanation: long horizons mix good and bad turns.

To understand why outcome-only GRPO is less effective on Fine Cut, we
evaluate every turn in the untrained model’s validation rollouts from
the second evaluation repetition using Gemini 3.8 Flash. The judge
labels each turn as positive, neutral, or negative. We quantify rollout
mixing as $`2\min(n_{+},n_{-})/(n_{+}+n_{-})`$, where $`n_{+}`$ and
$`n_{-}`$ denote the numbers of positive and negative turns,
respectively.

As shown in
Table [4](#S6.T4 "Table 4 ‣ A possible explanation: long horizons mix good and bad turns. ‣ 6.1 Task Structure and Trajectory-Level Credit ‣ 6 Credit Assignment: Benefits and Failure Modes ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing"),
Fine Cut has a mixing score of 0.651, whereas the other three task types
range from 0.297 to 0.390. Trajectory-level credit assignment assigns
the same advantage to every turn in a rollout and cannot distinguish
between positive and negative turns. The higher mixing on Fine Cut is
consistent with greater ambiguity in assigning credit to individual
turns. This may partly explain the smaller gains from outcome-only GRPO
on Fine Cut.

### 6.2 GRPO+PRM: Reward Hacking and Mitigation

#### Reward hacking with a fixed process-reward weight.

All task types except Fine Cut score lower than outcome-only GRPO, as
shown in
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Experiment Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing").
Table [5](#S7.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 7 Model Behavior ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
further shows a sharp decline in the model’s ability to deliver a
result, with 44.1% of rollouts being truncated compared with only 0.3%
under outcome-only GRPO. Late in training, the model continues calling
tools to gather evidence but often stops before committing to a final
result.

We further analyze the training dynamics of GRPO+PRM
(fixed $`\lambda`$). As shown in
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")(c),
the process reward increases while the outcome reward declines after
update 30.
Appendix [C.7](#A3.SS7 "C.7 Additional Training Diagnostics ‣ Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
provides the full training curves. As shown in
Section [7](#S7 "7 Model Behavior ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing"),
process rewards encourage desirable behaviors such as visual inspection,
reasoning, and reflection. One possible interpretation is that
encouraging these behaviors helps improve task scores early in training.
Later, continued optimization with a fixed process-reward weight may
lead the model to overuse these behaviors, consuming its interaction
budget and reducing its ability to complete the task.

#### Process-reward weight decay mitigates reward hacking.

Observations in
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")(c)
motivate us to decay the process-reward weight $`\lambda`$ to zero
between updates 20 and 30. This schedule retains process guidance early
in training and shifts later updates to the outcome reward alone.
Results in
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Experiment Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
show that this approach is effective. On the first three task types, the
model achieves performance comparable to outcome-only GRPO, with an
average score of 0.568 versus 0.559. On Fine Cut, the method improves
performance over the untrained model by 0.141, whereas outcome-only GRPO
yields an improvement of only 0.050. As shown in
Section [7](#S7 "7 Model Behavior ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing"),
decaying the process-reward weight further encourages desirable
behaviors, such as longer reasoning and more frequent reflection,
compared with keeping the weight fixed.

### 6.3 OPSD Is Sensitive to Privileged information

With reference answers, OPSD scores 0.277, close to the untrained
model’s 0.284. With turn-level suggestions, its score drops to 0.044
(Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Experiment Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).

#### OPSD gradients depend on privileged information.

The two variants share the same distillation loss, frozen teacher,
student model, and training hyperparameters. Compared with reference
answers, turn-level suggestions yield an approximately 15-fold larger
mean forward KL divergence and increase the mean gradient norm from
$`0.084`$ to $`1.177`$
(Table [13](#A3.T13 "Table 13 ‣ Gradient magnitude by privileged information. ‣ C.5 OPSD Teacher Inputs and Diagnostics ‣ Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).

#### The OPSD signal can be diluted over a trajectory.

With reference answers, the most divergent $`5\%`$ of turns account for
$`47.4\%`$ of the total KL but only $`17.9\%`$ of the tokens
(Table [14](#A3.T14 "Table 14 ‣ How the divergence is spread over a trajectory. ‣ C.5 OPSD Teacher Inputs and Diagnostics ‣ Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).
This concentration suggests that averaging over valid assistant tokens
may dilute the contribution of the more divergent turns. However, KL
measures disagreement rather than the usefulness of teacher guidance,
and whether gradient magnitude or signal dilution causes the observed
outcomes remains to be tested.
Appendix [C.5](#A3.SS5 "C.5 OPSD Teacher Inputs and Diagnostics ‣ Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
provides further analysis of trajectory lengths, gradient norms, and KL
concentration.

#### Process rewards encourage desirable behaviors but do not ensure completion.

Compared with outcome-only GRPO, GRPO+PRM (fixed $`\lambda`$) encourages
more visual inspection, longer reasoning, and more frequent
self-correction markers
(Table [5](#S7.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 7 Model Behavior ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).
However, these changes accompany higher scores only on Fine Cut; scores
fall on the other three task types
(Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Experiment Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).
The truncation rate also rises from 0.3% to 44.1%. The value of these
behaviors therefore depends on the task and on whether the model can
still complete it.

#### GRPO gains accompany greater visual inspection.

Compared with the base model, outcome-only GRPO increases frames seen
from 32.2 to 84.2, without increasing tool calls or reasoning length
(Table [5](#S7.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 7 Model Behavior ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).
Greater visual input may help the three shorter-horizon tasks,
consistent with their larger gains, whereas Fine Cut may require more
complex tool use and reasoning to coordinate a longer sequence of
editing operations.

#### Less process guidance mitigates hacking and elicits better behavioral patterns.

Compared with GRPO+PRM (fixed $`\lambda`$), GRPO+PRM
(decayed $`\lambda`$) makes fewer tool calls while retaining substantial
visual inspection, producing longer reasoning, and showing more frequent
self-correction markers
(Table [5](#S7.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 7 Model Behavior ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).
These changes accompany a reduction in truncation from $`44.1\%`$ to
$`6.2\%`$ and an increase in overall task score from $`0.334`$ to
$`0.447`$
(Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Experiment Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).
Compared with outcome-only GRPO, GRPO+PRM (decayed $`\lambda`$) achieves
a substantial gain on Fine Cut, which may be associated with its longer
reasoning and more extensive visual inspection.

#### OPSD behavior depends on privileged information.

As shown in
Table [5](#S7.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 7 Model Behavior ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing"),
with reference answers, OPSD remains behaviorally close to the untrained
model, apart from a lower blind rate. With turn-level suggestions,
rollouts become much shorter, reasoning length and reflection frequency
drop sharply, and blind rollouts become more frequent. These behavioral
changes accompany the collapse in task performance
(Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Experiment Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).

## 8 Conclusion

Our experiments show when finer-grained credit helps and when it hurts.
Process guidance helps most on Fine Cut, while outcome-only GRPO remains
competitive on shorter-horizon tasks. Process guidance can hurt when
more visual inspection and reasoning accompany poorer task completion.
Decaying the process-reward weight improves completion while retaining
these behaviors. OPSD is sensitive to privileged information, showing
that dense supervision alone does not guarantee effective learning. Our
process-reward findings are based on GRPO+PRM with LLM-judged turn-level
rewards on movie-trailer editing tasks; whether they extend to untested
methods such as GiGPO ([Feng et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)) and Turn-PPO ([Li
et al., 2026b](#bib.bib17)) remains open. For OPSD, we identify failure
patterns without establishing effective remedies. How to improve teacher
guidance and training stability requires further study.

### A.1 Corpus and Summary Statistics

Let

|     |                                                          |     |       |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{edit}}=\{(F_{i},T_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},
       ```                                                       |     | (A.1) |

where $`F_{i}`$ is a source film and $`T_{i}`$ is its corresponding
trailer. The corpus contains $`N=49`$ pairs, comprising 101.80 hours of
source video and 2.09 hours of trailers. Instantiating four task types
for every retained chunk produces an initial pool of 2,352 tasks, before
task-level quality filtering and sampling. The final experimental
dataset contains 797 tasks
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Data Construction and Dataset ‣ 4 Task Suite ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).

### A.2 Media Processing and Chunking

Task construction converts each long film and short trailer into
localized editing problems. We normalize media geometry and create
analysis proxies, then detect shot boundaries in both videos. A
coarse-to-fine visual matching pipeline aligns trailer shots to
candidate source-film moments and rejects low-confidence matches.
Graphic cards, black frames, flashes, and other featureless intervals
are separated from usable footage. Speech timestamps, music activity,
and picture–sound relations are extracted only for constructing fine-cut
tasks and hidden evaluation evidence; the finished trailer mix and its
alignments are never exposed to the policy.

A semantic pass groups the trailer into broad story beats and then into
local chunks that do not cross beat boundaries. The construction
produces 622 raw chunks. We retain 588 chunks containing at least three
usable picture clips and two source-film anchors. The source films
contain 88,669 detected shots and the trailers contain 5,978 detected
shots.

### A.3 Construction by Task Type

The four tasks for a chunk are constructed independently. Narrative
Sequencing uses the reference selection, Rough Cut uses the reference
order, and Fine Cut starts from a reference rough cut rather than a
sampled upstream output. This prevents one task type’s errors from
dominating another task type’s training signal. The corpus therefore
evaluates stagewise editing decisions; composing all task types into a
fully end-to-end trailer editor is a separate setting.

Content Selection presents complete source shots rather than pre-trimmed
positives and mixes them with neighboring and duration-matched
distractors. In the final dataset, each task contains 21–36 candidates
and 3–14 positives. Narrative Sequencing independently renames and
shuffles the selected clips. Overlapping neighboring source windows are
separated where possible so that the task cannot be solved by matching
duplicate boundary frames. Rough Cut supplies the reference order but
requires new in- and out-points for every clip. Matching ambiguity is
retained as a timing tolerance, avoiding frame-exact penalties when
equivalent boundaries exist.

Fine Cut begins from a stable rough cut and exposes production-sound
handles, uncut dialogue candidates, an unaligned music window, and join
metadata. The agent selects and trims dialogue, places music, chooses
picture and sound transitions, and renders the result. Trailer-only
picture is muted so that released dialogue and music choices cannot be
recovered from its audio. The structured edit and rendered preview are
evaluated together.

### A.4 Task Inputs, Outputs, and Requirements

Each task package provides a local editing brief, the assets under
initial_state/, and a public manifest.json describing those assets. The
manifest identifies files and their local time coordinates; it is not an
answer key. Hidden selections, reference orders, source–trailer
alignments, and grading references are kept outside the policy-visible
package. The four interfaces expose different decisions, as detailed
below. Their scores use different criteria and should not be interpreted
as a common difficulty scale.

#### Content Selection (CS): choose an unordered subset.

The input is a written description of the local trailer stretch and a
pool of complete, silent source shots, with candidate IDs, file names,
and durations in the manifest. Positive shots are mixed with distractors
from the same film. Candidate numbering follows a shuffle and does not
indicate relevance. The agent must screen the pool and decide both which
clips to keep and how many to keep. It writes output/selection.json,
containing a selected array of candidate IDs. Each selected ID is listed
once; the order of IDs has no meaning. The task does not ask the agent
to rank the candidates, order the selected shots, choose temporal
boundaries, mix audio, or render a video.

The score is the set-selection F1 against the hidden selection:
precision is the fraction of selected valid candidates that belong to
the reference set, and recall is the fraction of reference candidates
selected. An empty selection receives zero. The reference selection size
is not supplied and is not a submission constraint; selecting too many
clips is penalized through precision and selecting too few through
recall. This is a continuous F1 score, not a binary task-success rate.
The task grader additionally reports missed and incorrect clips; its
diagnostic pass flag is separate from the reported score.

#### Narrative Sequencing (NS): order the supplied shots.

The input contains the reference-selected shots, independently shuffled
and renamed, together with a description of the desired narrative
stretch. Unlike the silent CS pool, the NS clips include production
sound. The agent can use action continuity, dialogue, eyelines,
movement, shot size, scene changes, and the brief to recover an
ordering. It writes output/order.json, whose order array lists every
supplied ID exactly once, from first to last. Dropping a shot,
duplicating an ID, or introducing a new ID is invalid. The agent does
not choose a subset, trim shots, or alter the sound mix.

The deterministic score combines three complementary properties of the
permutation: one minus normalized positional displacement, the
normalized longest increasing subsequence, and the fraction of directed
reference-adjacent pairs retained. These three factors are multiplied.
Thus, preserving some pairwise order alone does not ensure that the
sequence preserves the reference joins. The *Heretic* chunk 5 grader
used in our experiments accepts four explicitly enumerated orders and
takes the best match among them; this is a task-specific repair rather
than a general acceptance of arbitrary alternative orders.

#### Rough Cut (RC): choose an interval within every ordered clip.

The input supplies the selected clips in their reference order, their
available durations, a local editing brief, and a target total duration.
The agent writes output/cuts.json. Each object in its cuts array
contains clip, in_s, and out_s. Times are measured from the beginning of
the named source clip, rather than on a global film or output timeline.
All supplied clips must appear exactly once and in the supplied order.
Every kept interval must have a nonnegative start, an end after its
start, and boundaries within the available media. The sum of the kept
durations must stay near the supplied target. The agent chooses where an
action begins and ends and how much time each shot receives; it does not
reselect or reorder the material, add dialogue, or redesign the mix. The
grading pipeline renders the submitted cuts for evaluation.

The final RC score combines a deterministic timing component (weight
0.4), reference-informed judgment (0.4), and judgment without the
reference (0.2). The deterministic component is the fraction of scorable
shots for which both the in-point error and the kept-duration error are
within their reference tolerances. In-point tolerances are specific to
each shot, reflecting matching ambiguity; they are not a universal
frame-exact threshold. Shots with no source-film counterpart remain part
of the required sequence but are excluded from this timing-component
denominator. Submission validity and the full judging rubrics are
detailed in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing").

#### Fine Cut (FC): finish sound and transitions on a fixed picture edit.

The input is a reference rough cut with fixed shot order and picture
timing. The manifest supplies shot durations, the times and identities
of joins, available production-sound handles, an index of uncut dialogue
takes, and an unaligned music window. These are candidate materials, not
an assembled reference mix. Dialogue takes can contain surrounding
speech, and some takes do not belong in the requested stretch. A shot
made for the trailer can arrive without production sound; the prompt and
manifest identify such cases.

The agent must deliver both output/edit.json and output/preview.mp4. The
structured edit records the following choices:

- •
  Dialogue: which supplied takes to use, the source-local in_s/out_s
  interval in each take, the output-timeline placement at_s, and any
  gain adjustment. Unneeded takes may be omitted; a take may be reused
  when the edit calls for it.
- •
  Production sound: whether to retain or mute a shot’s own sound and,
  when a handle is used, its source interval and timeline placement.
  Silent shots cannot supply production sound that does not exist.
- •
  Music: the offset within the supplied cue that aligns with the start
  of the edit, and the resulting audible entry and balance. The agent
  must determine the alignment from the material.
- •
  Every join: a picture choice of cut, dip_to_black, dissolve, or other,
  and a sound choice of hard, carries_over, prelap, or under. These
  respectively distinguish a sound break, an L-cut, a J-cut, and a sound
  bridge. Every supplied join must be specified once; there is no quota
  requiring bridges or decorative transitions.
- •
  Cards, when required by the task: text and appearance for supplied
  card placeholders. These are task-dependent, not a requirement to add
  a card to every stretch.

The picture shots and their established timing remain fixed, subject to
the requested join treatments. The rendered preview must make the
declared choices audible and visible and stay within the duration
allowance used by the submission validator. Merely declaring a line in
JSON does not establish that it can be heard in the final mix. The FC
score combines judgment without the reference (weight 0.6) and
reference-informed judgment (0.4); there is no deterministic exact-match
component. The reference is one realized solution, and the rubrics
evaluate task-appropriate alternatives through their actual audiovisual
result.

### A.5 Audit, Repair and Sampling

We audit every constructed task with Gemini 3.8 Flash, giving it the
task description, the available tools, the video and audio assets, and
the hidden grading references. The audit checks whether the brief is
supported by the supplied media and whether the grading rules evaluate
decisions that can be inferred from the public input. For Content
Selection and Narrative Sequencing it checks whether visual and
narrative cues support the target selection or order. For Rough Cut and
Fine Cut it checks the feasibility of the timing, dialogue and
audiovisual requirements, and it allows several reasonable edits.

Tasks with contradictory briefs, missing or corrupted assets, answer
leakage, or unsupported reference constraints are repaired or dropped. A
repair makes targeted changes to prompts, references, graders or media,
followed by file-integrity, decoding and grader checks as applicable.
Unresolved tasks and incomplete audit responses leave the candidate
pool. Review coverage varies, and some candidates receive additional
independent-context reviews while others receive a single audit or
repair-author judgment.

Filtering and repair leave 925 candidates. We draw 797 of them by
fixed-seed sampling stratified by split, task type and film, which
preserves film coverage within each type and uses no policy reward
scores. The 128 unselected candidates remain valid and are set aside
only to fix the dataset size and the type proportions.
````

## Appendix B Editing Environment Details

### B.1 Episode State and Tools

Each task runs in a fresh filesystem workspace. The loader stages the
task prompt, a manifest, and only the media visible for the current task
type. The public manifest identifies the supplied assets and the timing
information appropriate to that task. Prepared frames and observation
tools support Content Selection, Narrative Sequencing, and Rough Cut.
Fine Cut additionally requires manipulation of the supplied picture,
dialogue, production-sound and music assets to build an audiovisual
timeline and render a preview. Hidden answers, matching records,
rubrics, and reference decisions remain in a separate evaluator-owned
directory.

The policy may maintain notes, compare candidates, write timeline
scripts, render intermediate results, inspect them, and revise its
output. A turn is one policy generation request; it may contain several
tool calls, so turn count and tool-call count differ. The episode
terminates when the policy produces a final response or exhausts a turn,
context, or output budget. The common turn limit is 100. Termination by
a final response does not establish that a valid deliverable exists or
that the task has been completed successfully.

### B.2 Tools

Beyond the harness’s own shell, which provides FFmpeg, ffprobe and
Python for local cutting, joining, mixing and probing, the policy calls
eight media tools over MCP.

| Tool           | What it returns                                                                                                           |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| view_image     | Prepared or policy-generated still images selected by path.                                                               |
| inspect_video  | Sampled video frames selected by timestamps or an interval; returns actual decoded times rather than continuous playback. |
| inspect_frames | Frame-index or time-based inspection near a cut, optionally cropped; returns a sheet or individual images.                |
| contact_sheet  | A numbered sheet of up to 16 requested source images or frames.                                                           |
| inspect_audio  | Duration, level, silence and an offline English transcript for a requested audio window.                                  |
| analyze_audio  | Loudness, true peak, channel-level measurements, silence, and onset or pulse candidates for an audio window.              |
| validate_media | Technical checks on a declared edit or rendered file, including timing, stream/decode information and schema constraints. |
| listen_music   | Pre-computed instrument, mood and energy descriptions for a selected music window.                                        |

Table 7: Media tools available to the policy. None of them exposes a
task label, a hidden reference decision or an editing-specific oracle.

The policy interface supports text and images. The audio tools return
analysis text rather than an audio waveform for the model to listen to.
A transcript may drop words, so the absence of a transcript does not
mean the absence of speech. The music tags come from a read-only store
built before training, not from an online model. Audio onsets and
periodicity estimates are candidates for editing decisions, not
guaranteed musical beats. The technical validator does not provide the
hidden outcome score or certify artistic quality. Thus observation and
validation tools can reduce uncertainty while still leaving the editing
decision to the policy.

### B.3 Harness

Episodes run in the official OpenCode harness at version 1.15.5, commit
d7a6e1da. We do not modify its binary or its upstream source. The media
tools above are served over MCP, and an image-registration plugin on the
tool.execute.after hook intercepts both native image attachments and MCP
image content, normalizing and registering each image before it reaches
the conversation history. The recorded configuration also disables
automatic compaction and pruning and retains the policy’s original
generation inputs for replay.

The plugin exists because the harness rearranges tool images into fresh
user messages and may compress or trim history. Registering images ahead
of that step keeps the request the inference backend receives identical
to the one a training replay reconstructs, so the policy is never
optimized against pixels it did not see. Images that arrive by URL, in
an unknown binary modality, or without registration are refused rather
than silently downscaled or dropped.

### B.4 Deliverable Validation

At termination, the environment collects only the named deliverables:
selection.json for Content Selection, order.json for Narrative
Sequencing, cuts.json for Rough Cut, and edit.json together with
preview.mp4 for Fine Cut. The submitted text response is not a
substitute for these artifacts. JSON validation checks types,
identifiers, finite time values and the schema required by the task.
Missing or malformed deliverables and violated submission constraints
receive zero outcome reward. Infrastructure failures and unavailable
grades are tracked separately rather than automatically counted as
policy failures.

#### Rough Cut checks.

Every supplied clip must occur exactly once and in the supplied order.
Each retained interval must have a nonnegative in-point, an out-point
after the in-point, and endpoints within the corresponding media. If the
manifest specifies a target duration $`T`$, the sum of all retained
intervals must be within $`\max(1,\min(3,0.1T))`$ seconds of $`T`$. This
common delivery rule supersedes older total-duration tolerance text in
the raw task prompt; it does not permit endpoints outside the source
media. The original task-specific reference tolerances still define the
separate deterministic quality metric
(Appendix [C.6](#A3.SS6 "C.6 Outcome Metrics and Rubrics ‣ Appendix C Training and Evaluation Details ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")).

#### Fine Cut checks.

The edit description must cover every supplied join exactly once.
Picture join types are cut, dip_to_black, dissolve or other; sound
relationships are hard, under, prelap or carries_over. Dialogue entries
must reference available takes with valid trim intervals and placement
times. Production sound must reference an existing source; explicit
timing requires a supplied handle and all timing fields. Required title
cards must be declared with nonempty text. A declared music offset must
lie inside the supplied cue. The preview must be probeable, contain a
video stream, and meet the same total-duration tolerance relative to the
locked picture sequence. These structural checks do not by themselves
prove that dialogue is intelligible, a declared transition appears in
the render, or the mix satisfies the brief; the outcome rubrics judge
the actual delivered audiovisual result.

### B.5 Isolation and Telemetry

Every trajectory executes in a disposable sandbox without network
access. Concurrent rollouts of the same task receive private workspaces,
so one sample cannot inspect or overwrite another sample’s output. The
environment logs turns, tool calls, images viewed, validation errors,
rendering status, and reward components. These records support the later
analysis of training cost, failure modes, and credit assignment without
enlarging the policy-visible state.

The same public task, observation tools and deliverable contract are
used for student training rollouts and evaluation. Reward computations
and OPSD teacher-only inputs are performed outside the policy sandbox.
They do not enlarge the student’s public state or expose validation
answers to it.

#### Policy optimization and advantage normalization.

The reward-based variants use token-level importance ratios between the
current policy and the rollout policy. Ratios outside $`(0.8,\,1.28)`$
receive zero weight; the retained ratios are treated as constants during
backpropagation. The policy loss uses token-mean reduction over valid
assistant tokens. For
Equation [3.3](#S3.E3 "In Turn level. ‣ 3.2 Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Preliminaries ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing"),
only valid process scores from turns with active loss tokens enter the
group statistics, with $`\epsilon=10^{-6}`$. The process advantage is
zero for turns with missing or invalid scores, groups with fewer than
two valid scores, or groups with sample standard deviation at most
$`\epsilon`$; the outcome term is retained.

#### Process-weight schedule.

The fixed variant uses $`\lambda=0.5`$ at every update. For the decayed
variant, the coefficient is $`0.5`$ through update 20, then
$`0.5[1-(u-20)/10]^{3}`$ for $`20<u<30`$, and zero from update 30
onward. Here $`u`$ is the consumer optimizer’s global update index,
rather than the index at which an asynchronous rollout began. For
example, updates 21 and 25 use coefficients 0.3645 and 0.0625. Setting
the coefficient to zero removes the process term from the policy
advantage; it does not change the outcome reward or the final evaluation
grader.

#### Token weighting and comparison scope.

The token-mean loss divides the summed contributions of valid assistant
tokens by the total number of such tokens in the optimization batch.
Thus each valid token has the same reduction weight; turns and
trajectories are not individually renormalized to have equal weight.
Prompt tokens, tool observations, padding, and positions with an
inactive loss mask do not contribute to this loss. The configured batch
targets eight trajectories for each of 16 tasks in the reward-based
variants and one trajectory for each of 16 tasks in each OPSD variant.
Matching 50 updates therefore does not match the number of sampled
trajectories or generated tokens across these objectives. The
comparisons in this paper are comparisons of the specified training
recipes.

### C.2 Process-Reward Judgments

#### Context and timing.

The process judge reviews consecutive windows of ten completed assistant
turns; the final window may be shorter. It receives the original public
task, public assistant actions and tool observations, registered images,
and typed facts derived from the available records. Separate
reasoning/thinking fields are excluded from this packet. Target turns
and a bounded selection of earlier turns retain detailed evidence;
omitted earlier material is explicitly marked as incomplete history. The
image packet prioritizes the current window and uses up to 16 original
images, reserving at most four for earlier context. These are
judge-input limits, not limits on the policy’s observations.

Each window receives three independent judgments from Gemini 3.8 Flash.
For a particular turn, valid scores are averaged; an abstention is
excluded from this average rather than treated as a neutral vote. If no
valid score remains, that turn receives no process contribution. The
judge is asked to cite only evidence available at or before the turn
being assessed. Since several target turns appear together in a
retrospective window, this citation rule does not establish that the
model’s judgment is causally independent of later turns in that window.

| Score   | Evidence required                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $`+1`$  | New task-relevant information or inference supported by observations; a verified correction; or a substantive edit that demonstrably advances a task requirement.     |
| $`0`$   | Routine file operations, an unchanged check, or an honest status update without verified new progress. A successful command or valid JSON alone does not earn credit. |
| $`-1`$  | An observed error, a demonstrated task violation, an invalid claim of verification, or a supported persistent repetition of already-resolved work.                    |
| Abstain | Material evidence is missing or inadequate for the proposed judgment. Unavailable media or an infrastructure failure alone does not establish an agent error.         |

Table 10: Process-judgment decision rules. These summarize the
implemented rubric; they specify intended supervision rather than
guarantee the correctness of every judgment.

#### Evidence validation.

Decisions identify the turn, event type, evidence scope, score, and
supporting record IDs. Validators check the allowed fields,
event-to-score mapping, record identities and time boundaries. Visual
claims require attached pixels; text metadata is not treated as direct
auditory evidence. In the archived process-judge adapter, authenticated
audio attachments are unavailable, so claims requiring direct listening
must abstain unless the available evidence supports a narrower metadata
or reasoning judgment. Routine readback of an unchanged JSON file is
neutral. The repetition rule is deliberately narrow: it requires the
same literal shell command and matching observed text over three
consecutive turns, together with evidence that the work is redundant.
Such validation checks provenance and protocol compliance; it does not
prove semantic correctness or eliminate reward hacking.

### C.4 Behavioral Measurements

The behavioral analysis uses the four recorded validation repetitions of
each Qwen3.5-9B variant. Each variant contributes 324 task–repetition
pairs. We count a turn from its policy-request identifier, so a request
that is interrupted before a completed generation still contributes to
interaction length. Tool calls count requested invocations, whereas
frames count image attachments returned in completed tool results; these
need not increase together. A contact sheet is one image attachment even
when it contains multiple sampled frames. Neither measure counts
distinct source-film frames. Blind rollouts are those with no returned
image attachment. Truncation indicates a recorded budget termination,
rather than an assessment of whether the final artifact is correct.

#### Reasoning length.

Reasoning length is measured in _characters_, not tokenizer tokens. For
each completed generation event, we parse the action text as JSON and
read its reasoning field. A missing or non-string field contributes zero
characters. We divide the total number of reasoning characters by the
number of completed generation events, pooling all tasks and repetitions
within a method. This is a turn-weighted average, not the average of
per-rollout ratios. It is distinct from the number of policy requests
used for interaction length, because an interrupted request may not emit
a completed generation event.

#### Reflection markers.

We apply the case-insensitive expression below to the same reasoning
field. A completed turn counts once if any alternative matches, even if
several markers occur. The reflection rate is the number of matching
turns divided by the number of completed generation events, again
pooling tasks and repetitions. Missing reasoning is a non-match. The
expression uses Python regular-expression syntax; \b is a word boundary
and \s is whitespace.

```ltx_verbatim
re.compile(
    r"\bwait[,.\s]"
    r"|\breconsider\b"
    r"|\blet me (re-?check|re-?examine|re-?visit|re-?think|"
    r"re-?do|re-?count|re-?calculate|re-?verify|"
    r"retry|try again)\b"
    r"|\b(was|were|is|are)\s+(wrong|incorrect|invalid)\b"
    r"|\bmy (mistake|error)\b"
    r"|\bshould have\b"
    r"|\b(did|does)\s?n[o’]?t work\b"
    r"|\btry again\b"
    r"|\bon second thought\b"
    r"|\bthat’?s not (right|correct)\b"
    r"|\b(previous|earlier|last) (attempt|approach|try|call)s?\b"
    r"|\bI (over|mis)[a-z]+", re.I)
```

This is a lexical indicator of explicit reconsideration. It does not
establish that the agent found an error, changed its next action, or
improved the edit. For example, an isolated “wait” or a mention of a
previous attempt can match without an effective correction. Longer
reasoning also provides more opportunities for a match. Consequently,
the behavior columns describe observed policy activity and should be
interpreted together with task outcome and completion, rather than as
independent measures of reasoning quality.

### C.5 OPSD Teacher Inputs and Diagnostics

#### Teacher and student.

The KL teacher is a frozen copy of the initial Qwen3.5-9B, initialized
from the same checkpoint as the student. It is not updated by an
exponential moving average or replaced by the trained student. The
student samples its own actions from the public task context.
Teacher-side privileged information changes the distribution evaluated
at those same student action tokens; it is not inserted into student
rollout or evaluation inputs, and it does not replace student actions
with supervised action labels.

#### Reference-answer conditioning.

The private answer card contains the selected clip IDs for Content
Selection; the reference order for Narrative Sequencing; and
clip-relative reference in/out intervals and target duration for Rough
Cut. For Fine Cut it contains reference join decisions, dialogue
identities and onset times, and a music source offset. The Fine Cut card
is explicitly a partial editing plan, not a ready-to-submit executable
edit: unprovided trim endpoints, gains, mixing envelopes and render
commands are not invented as reference targets. Rough Cut and Fine Cut
reference choices are marked non-unique. The card is inserted only into
the teacher’s existing system message while preserving the student’s
action-token and image suffix. Cards are bound to training task
identities and the corresponding stored answer and manifest; validation
tasks are excluded from this private catalog.

#### Turn-suggestion conditioning.

Gemini 3.8 Flash receives the original public context immediately before
turn $`t`$, including prior tool observations and admitted images, and
proposes one next action. It neither executes that action nor observes
the student’s current action or future outcome. An action may contain
multiple tool calls. The proposal is appended as a teacher-only note
after the original turn context, followed by the original student action
tokens. A separate teacher prefix is constructed for each turn; the note
from a previous or future turn is not carried into it. Gemini is the
suggestion generator, while the frozen Qwen3.5-9B remains the model that
supplies KL targets. Suggestions are checked for format, available tool
names, argument schemas and a maximum of 2,048 teacher-hint tokens.
These checks do not establish that a suggestion is semantically correct
or executable in the student’s state.

#### Teacher context and objective.

Both variants reserve up to 2,048 additional teacher-only tokens, giving
a teacher context limit of 133,120 tokens while the student’s limit
remains 131,072. The implemented forward-KL objective uses neither an
outcome-reward term nor a process-reward term, and does not apply
sampled-action importance weighting. Outcomes are still recorded for
evaluation and diagnostics.

#### Top-$`K`$+tail KL in OPSD.

We give the implemented form of
Equation [3.4](#S3.E4 "In Token level. ‣ 3.2 Credit Assignment ‣ 3 Preliminaries ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
here. Let $`p_{t,k}`$ and $`q_{t,k}`$ be its full-vocabulary student and
teacher distributions at token position $`k`$ of turn $`t`$. We use
uppercase $`K=64`$ for the support size, to distinguish it from the
token index $`k`$. At each position, the teacher selects

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
S_{t,k}=\operatorname{TopK}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}q_{t,k}(v),\qquad|S_{t,k}|=K.
``` |  | (C.1) |

Both models are evaluated on this same teacher-selected set; the student
does not select a separate top-$`K`$ set. The selection is made
independently at each prediction position on the student’s sampled
prefix.

All retained probabilities keep their respective full-vocabulary softmax
normalizers. We aggregate the remaining vocabulary into one tail class
$`\bot`$, with

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{t,k}^{\bot}=\sum_{v\notin S_{t,k}}q_{t,k}(v),\qquad p_{t,k}^{\bot}=\sum_{v\notin S_{t,k}}p_{t,k}(v).
``` |  | (C.2) |

Thus
$`\widetilde{q}_{t,k}=((q_{t,k}(v))_{v\in S_{t,k}},q_{t,k}^{\bot})`$ and
$`\widetilde{p}_{t,k}=((p_{t,k}(v))_{v\in S_{t,k}},p_{t,k}^{\bot})`$ are
normalized distributions over the same $`K+1=65`$ classes. We do not
renormalize the retained 64 entries to sum to one or discard the tail
mass. The per-token forward KL used in both OPSD variants is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\ell_{t,k}^{(K)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\widetilde{q}_{t,k}\|\widetilde{p}_{t,k})`$ |  | (C.3) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in S_{t,k}}q_{t,k}(v)\log\frac{q_{t,k}(v)}{p_{t,k}(v)}+q_{t,k}^{\bot}\log\frac{q_{t,k}^{\bot}}{p_{t,k}^{\bot}}.`$ |  |  |

The direction is teacher-to-student, $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)`$. The
student-sampled token need not belong to $`S_{t,k}`$: this is a
distribution-level target at every valid assistant prediction position,
not a loss evaluated only at the sampled token.

For an optimization batch $`\mathcal{B}`$, let $`m_{i,t,k}\in\{0,1\}`$
denote the valid assistant-token loss mask for trajectory $`i`$. The
implemented token-mean reduction is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{K}(\theta)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{t,k}m_{i,t,k}\,\ell_{i,t,k}^{(K)}}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{t,k}m_{i,t,k}}.
``` |  | (C.4) |

Prompt tokens, tool observations and padding are excluded. Turns and
trajectories are not separately normalized to have equal weight. Teacher
targets, selected vocabulary IDs and sampled prefixes are held fixed
within an update; gradients pass through the student probabilities,
including their full-vocabulary normalizer and aggregate tail mass.

#### Approximation and numerical implementation.

The top-$`K`$+tail construction preserves each selected probability and
the total probability outside the selected set. It does not preserve how
probability is distributed among individual tail tokens. Its KL is
therefore a coarsened objective, rather than the exact full-vocabulary
KL; it is no greater than the full KL and becomes equal when the
conditional teacher and student distributions within the tail agree.

This construction reduces each stored teacher target to 64 vocabulary
IDs and 65 log-probabilities per active position. It does not avoid the
full-vocabulary projection needed for normalization. For numerical
stability, the implementation computes each tail log-probability as the
log-sum-exp of the complementary logits minus the full-vocabulary
log-sum-exp, rather than subtracting a near-one retained probability
from one. Logits are projected in chunks of 128 active positions, and
the backward pass recomputes these projections instead of retaining a
trajectory-by-vocabulary tensor. Log-probability arithmetic uses FP32 in
the reported runs. We use the same $`K=64`$ in both variants and do not
claim that this value is optimal or that tail aggregation preserves all
full-vocabulary supervision.

#### Interpreting teacher–student disagreement.

Changing privileged information changes the teacher targets on
student-visited states. A larger KL identifies stronger distributional
disagreement, but does not by itself establish that the teacher proposes
a better action. Likewise, a larger parameter-gradient norm is an
observed optimization difference, not proof that a particular
probability ratio causes instability. The top-$`K`$+tail forward-KL
implementation differentiates through normalized student logits; for a
retained vocabulary entry, its logit derivative is the difference
between student and teacher probabilities. We therefore interpret the
loss, gradient and outcome measurements together, without treating the
derivative with respect to an unconstrained probability as a
parameter-gradient mechanism.

### C.6 Outcome Metrics and Rubrics

#### Outcome scoring.

The channel weights
$`(w_{\mathrm{det}},w_{\mathrm{ref}},w_{\mathrm{blind}})`$ of
Equation [4.1](#S4.E1 "In Scoring. ‣ 4.1 Tasks and Environment ‣ 4 Task Suite ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing")
are $`(1,0,0)`$ for Content Selection and Narrative Sequencing,
$`(0.4,0.4,0.2)`$ for Rough Cut, and $`(0,0.4,0.6)`$ for Fine Cut.

#### Deterministic metrics.

Content Selection uses set F1, $`2|S\cap S^{\star}|/(|S|+|S^{\star}|)`$,
on the submitted and reference clip IDs. Narrative Sequencing maps the
submitted permutation to reference ranks and multiplies three terms: one
minus normalized absolute displacement, the length of the longest
increasing subsequence divided by the number of clips, and the fraction
of consecutive submitted pairs that are consecutive in the
reference ([Cao et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)). Absolute displacement is
normalized by $`\lfloor n^{2}/2\rfloor`$ for $`n`$ clips. The Heretic c5
verifier used in our experiments takes the best score over its four
accepted orders. Kendall’s $`\tau`$ is logged as a diagnostic but is not
the outcome score.

For Rough Cut, a scorable shot is a hit only if both its in-point error
and its retained-duration error fall within the corresponding reference
tolerances. The deterministic score is the fraction of scorable shots
that are hits. In-point tolerances are shot-specific, derived from the
alignment’s self-similarity plateau; retained-duration tolerance comes
from the task key. Unmatched alternate takes remain in the delivered
sequence but are excluded from this reference-based denominator. Invalid
ranges are not exempted as unscorable shots. Fine Cut has no
deterministic similarity channel.

The two judge channels are rubric-based, and

#### Rubric interpretation.

The blind Rough Cut rubric assesses useful picture entry/exit points,
coverage of the requested visual beat, and continuity of the actual
joins. Its reference channel assesses entries, exits and pacing with the
released edit as a feasible comparison. Fine Cut evaluates delivered
dialogue, music, audiovisual continuity choices and transition
execution; its reference channel revisits dialogue, continuity and
music. In all subjective rubrics, band 4 denotes full task-appropriate
compliance, band 3 a minor defect, band 2 several or a meaningful
defect, band 1 substantial failure, and band 0 failure of an essential
requirement. The per-item criteria specify the relevant visible or
audible defect; this summary is not an additional scoring rule.
Appropriate silence, a hard cut, or an alternative edit is not penalized
merely for differing from the reference. Declarations, tool counts,
source transcripts and metadata alone do not prove a successful
delivered result.

#### Rough Cut duration band.

Let $`T`$ be the target duration and $`E`$ the absolute error in the
summed retained clip durations. The mechanical reference-channel item
assigns bands 4, 3, 2, and 1 when $`E`$ is at most 3%, 8%, 18%, and 35%
of $`T`$, respectively; each threshold has an absolute floor equal to
the task’s frame-based duration tolerance. Larger errors receive band 0.
This graded item is separate from the common submission-validity gate,
which allows total-duration error $`\max(1,\min(3,0.1T))`$ seconds. A
submission outside that gate receives zero outcome reward before
subjective quality can contribute.

#### Scoring exclusions.

The protocol accepts the first valid grade rather than the best
available one. In the initial evaluation pass, judge refusals are not
retried. Both rules avoid selecting for trajectories that happen to
score well on a later attempt. Refusals are rare but not absent: a small
number of Rough Cut trajectories trigger the judge’s content filter,
which excludes that trajectory and the group it belongs to. Where this
leaves a repetition incomplete we average over the remaining complete
repetitions and report the count. For the six Qwen3.5-9B variants
reported in
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Experiment Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Fine-Grained Credit Assignment Helps and Hurts: Multimodal Agentic Reinforcement Learning through Video Editing"),
recovered grades fill the initially excluded evaluations, giving
complete coverage of all 324 task–repetition pairs per variant.
Behavioral metrics for Qwen3.5-9B cover all four repetitions of the 81
validation tasks

#### Observed degradation in OPSD (turn-level suggestions).

The outcome and generation diagnostics make the degradation visible
beyond the training objective. In OPSD (turn-level suggestions), mean
training outcome reward decreases from 0.166 over updates 1–10 to 0.020
over updates 41–50, while mean generated assistant tokens per rollout
decrease from 13.9k to 2.9k. For OPSD (reference answers), the
corresponding outcome means are 0.255 and 0.245.
````

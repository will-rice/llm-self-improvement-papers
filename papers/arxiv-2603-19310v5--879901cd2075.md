###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning improves large language model
(LLM) reasoning using reward signals on sampled rollouts. When reward
labels are available for only a subset of rollouts, restricting policy
updates to that subset leaves the remaining rollouts unused for policy
optimization. Semi-supervised learning propagates labels from labeled to
unlabeled samples, but existing methods either diffuse labels with fixed
similarity weights or treat each sample independently, ignoring the
relations among queries, thinking processes, and answers. We propose
MemReward, a graph-based experience memory that propagates rewards to
unlabeled rollouts during online policy optimization. MemReward stores
queries and the rollouts (thinking processes and final answers) of an
initial LLM policy as nodes of a heterogeneous graph connected by
similarity and structural edges, and warms up a GNN on labeled rollouts
to propagate rewards over this graph. During online RL fine-tuning,
unlabeled rollouts are attached to the graph by query similarity, and
the policy is updated with ground-truth rewards on labeled rollouts and
GNN-predicted rewards on the rest. We evaluate MemReward on 13
benchmarks in mathematics, question answering, and code generation with
verifiable rewards, and further with LLM-judge, human-preference, and
continuous rewards. With ground-truth rewards on only 20% of rollouts,
MemReward achieves 96.6% of the performance of fully supervised training
(Oracle) on Qwen2.5-1.5B and 97.3% on Qwen2.5-3B, where it also
outperforms label propagation and FixMatch-style self-training. It
carries over to Llama backbones up to 8B and closely approaches Oracle
on out-of-domain tasks.

## 1 Introduction

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have been driven by
reinforcement-learning-based post-training, where rewards on sampled
rollouts guide policy optimization \[14, 46, 10, 19\]. We study a
limited-supervision setting in which reward labels are available for
only a subset of sampled rollouts. Training solely on this labeled
subset discards potentially useful unlabeled rollouts. Our goal is to
exploit these unlabeled rollouts while keeping the reward-label budget
fixed. A natural solution lies in semi-supervised learning, where models
trained on labeled data have proven effective in propagating labels to
unlabeled samples \[21, 18, 38\]. This suggests an analogous strategy
for RL fine-tuning: treating labeled rollouts and their rewards as early
experiences, linking unlabeled rollouts to these experiences, and
propagating rewards from labeled to unlabeled rollouts. Therefore, we
ask: Can we leverage early experience memories to construct a shared
reward model, enabling effective RL fine-tuning in label scarcity
scenarios?

Figure 1: MemReward approaches Oracle performance with only 20% labels.
Using the same 20% ground-truth labels, MemReward (purple) substantially
outperforms partial labels (R1-p, gray), closely approaching
fully-supervised Oracle performance (green) on both in-domain and
out-of-domain tasks across both model scales.

Addressing this question presents several challenges. First, the
relationships among experiences are complex and multi-faceted:
semantically similar queries may share reward patterns, multiple
rollouts for the same query compete in quality, and answer correctness
depends on both the reasoning path and the query context. Modeling such
intertwined dependencies calls for a relational structure such as a
graph, rather than treating each experience independently \[31, 17\].
Second, the relevant relations are themselves heterogeneous: spanning
query-query semantic similarity, query-to-reasoning association, and
reasoning-to-answer correspondence—and a homogeneous graph cannot
distinguish their differing semantics, motivating a heterogeneous
design \[42, 36\].

To address these challenges, we introduce MemReward, a graph-based
experience memory framework. We first use an initial LLM policy to
generate rollouts for each labeled query, where each rollout consists of
a thinking process and a final answer. Queries, thinking processes, and
answers form three types of nodes, connected by three types of edges:
query-query edges between semantically similar queries, query-thinking
edges from each query to its rollouts, and thinking-answer edges within
each rollout. A heterogeneous GNN is then trained jointly across
multiple domains (mathematics, QA, and code generation) over this graph
to predict rewards \[12, 20\]. During online RL fine-tuning, unlabeled
rollouts are attached to the graph via embedding similarity, and the GNN
predicts their rewards to augment the limited ground-truth signal during
policy optimization.

We evaluate MemReward on Qwen2.5-1.5B and 3B across 13 benchmarks
spanning 3 domains: mathematics, question answering, and code
generation. As shown in Figure 1, MemReward—using ground-truth rewards
on only 20% of rollouts and GNN-predicted rewards on the rest—achieves
96.6% of Oracle performance on 1.5B and 97.3% on 3B. On out-of-domain
tasks, MemReward closely approaches Oracle on both model scales, showing
that GNN-predicted rewards generalize effectively across domain
boundaries. Performance scales smoothly with the label budget, reaching
99.4% of Oracle at 70% labels. Beyond these settings, MemReward improves
over R1-p with Llama-3.2-3B and Llama-3.1-8B backbones, with LLM-judge
(TruthfulQA), human-preference (Chatbot Arena), and continuous token-F1
(HotpotQA) rewards, and when the GNN is trained on one domain and used
for RL in another. Across six random seeds, its gains over R1-p on both
Qwen model sizes are statistically significant. Our conclusions concern
label efficiency in the evaluated reward settings, while expert-graded
mathematical proofs remain a direction for future work (Appendix A).

## 2 Preliminaries

Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning-based LLM Fine-tuning. In recent
RL-based fine-tuning, an LLM policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ parameterized by
$`\theta`$ generates rollouts $`y`$ for a query $`q`$ \[14\]. Each
rollout (also referred to as an experience) consists of a thinking
process $`t`$ and a final answer $`a`$. The correctness of the final
answer $`a`$ determines the reward, and the policy is updated through
policy gradient methods to reinforce the entire thinking process $`t`$
that produced a correct answer.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO \[14\] is a widely
adopted RL algorithm for reasoning-based LLM fine-tuning. For each
query, it compares the results of multiple rollouts to obtain the
advantage values. It can be formulated as follows:

```math
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{j=1}^{|y_{i}|}\min\{\rho_{i,j}A_{i},\text{clip}(\rho_{i,j},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_{i}\}-\beta\text{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\Big] \tag{1}
```

where $`q\sim\mathcal{D}`$ is a query sampled from the data
distribution; $`y_{1...N}\sim\pi_{\theta}(q)`$ are $`N`$ rollouts
generated by the policy; $`|y_{i}|`$ is the token length of the rollout
$`y_{i}`$;
$`\rho_{i,j}=\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,j}|q,y_{i,<j})/\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,j}|q,y_{i,<j})`$
is the importance sampling ratio for the $`j`$-th token of rollout
$`i`$;
$`A_{i}=\frac{R(q,y_{i})-\text{mean}[R(q,y_{1...N})]}{\text{std}[R(q,y_{1...N})]}`$
is the standardized advantage; $`R(q,y_{i})`$ is the reward for the
rollout $`y_{i}`$; $`\epsilon`$ is the clipping range; $`\beta`$ is the
KL penalty weight; and $`\pi_{\text{ref}}`$ is the reference policy.

## 3 MemReward: Graph-Based Experience Memory for Reward Prediction

Figure 2: Overview of MemReward. Rollouts generated by the initial
policy are stored as experience memory and organized into a
heterogeneous graph for reward prediction. (Left) Warmup Phase: We
construct a heterogeneous graph from labeled rollouts, where query nodes
connect via embedding similarity, and each query links to its thinking
and answer nodes. A GNN is trained to predict rewards through relational
message passing. (Right) Online Phase: During GRPO training, labeled
rollouts receive ground-truth rewards while unlabeled rollouts connect
to the warmup graph via top-$`k`$ similarity edges and obtain
GNN-predicted rewards.

Overview. As illustrated in Figure 2, MemReward treats each
rollout—consisting of a query, a thinking process, and an answer—as a
labeled experience stored in a graph-based memory. It constructs a
cross-domain heterogeneous graph from these labeled experiences and
trains a GNN to predict rewards (Section 3.1). During online policy
optimization, the trained GNN predicts rewards for unlabeled rollouts,
which are combined with ground-truth rewards from labeled rollouts for
scalable reward acquisition (Algorithm 1).

### 3.1 Cross-Domain Heterogeneous Graph for Reward Prediction

Initialize node/edge features. In MemReward, we have three types of
nodes (query nodes, thinking nodes, and answer nodes) and three types of
edges (query-query, query-thinking, and thinking-answer). Their
embeddings at layer $`\ell`$ are denoted as $`h_{q}^{(\ell)}`$,
$`h_{t}^{(\ell)}`$, and $`h_{a}^{(\ell)}`$, with direction-specific
weight matrices $`W_{qq}`$, $`W_{tq}`$, $`W_{qt}`$, $`W_{at}`$, and
$`W_{ta}`$.

For node initialization, for each query $`q`$ with ground-truth labels
in the training set, we use the initial policy $`\pi_{0}`$ to generate
rollouts, each consisting of a thinking process $`t`$ and a final answer
$`a`$, and then encode the query, thinking, and answer to obtain
embeddings $`e_{q},e_{t},e_{a}`$.

For edge construction, query-query edges are established using the
top-$`k`$ cosine similarity between query embeddings. Query-thinking
edges directly connect each query node to its thinking nodes.
Thinking-answer edges pair each thinking node one-to-one with the
corresponding answer node. All edge weights are initialized to 1. Only
query-query edges depend on embedding similarity; thinking and answer
nodes enter the graph solely through structural edges within their own
rollout, so their length or template does not affect which experiences
are retrieved.

The graph represents relations among experiences rather than temporal
dependencies. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) variant could orient
query-query edges by generation time or label confidence, but query
similarity does not define a global acyclic ordering. Enforcing one
would introduce an ordering heuristic and could remove reciprocal
message-passing paths between related experiences. We therefore retain
the heterogeneous graph without imposing acyclicity and leave DAG-based
variants for future work.

Training the warmup GNN. We train a heterogeneous GNN on labeled
rollouts to enable cross-query knowledge transfer, as shown in Figure 2.

For aggregating different types of nodes and edges, we employ
heterogeneous aggregation with type-specific weights. The GNN derives
expressive node representations $`h`$ through iterative neighborhood
aggregation with learnable weights. The update of the embedding of the
node in the $`\ell`$-th layer is as follows:

```math
\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\ell)}=\text{ReLU}\Big(\text{Mean}\big(\sum_{\mathcal{N}_{q}}\alpha^{qq}W_{qq}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\ell-1)},\sum_{\mathcal{N}_{t}}\alpha^{tq}W_{tq}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell-1)}\big)\Big), \tag{2}
```

```math
\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}=\text{ReLU}\Big(\text{Mean}\big(\sum_{\mathcal{N}_{q}}\alpha^{qt}W_{qt}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\ell-1)},\sum_{\mathcal{N}_{a}}\alpha^{at}W_{at}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}_{a}^{(\ell-1)}\big)\Big), \tag{3}
```

```math
\mathbf{h}_{a}^{(\ell)}=\text{ReLU}\Big(\sum_{\mathcal{N}_{t}}\alpha^{ta}W_{ta}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell-1)}\Big), \tag{4}
```

where $`\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}`$ is the embedding of the node after
$`\ell`$ iterations. $`\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(\ell)}`$,
$`\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(\ell)}`$, $`\mathbf{h}_{a}^{(\ell)}`$ are initialized
as $`\mathbf{h}_{q}^{(0)}=e_{q}`$, $`\mathbf{h}_{t}^{(0)}=e_{t}`$,
$`\mathbf{h}_{a}^{(0)}=e_{a}`$. $`\alpha^{xy}`$ denotes the attention
weights for edge type $`x\to y`$, $`W_{xy}^{(\ell)}`$ denotes the
learnable edge type-specific weights, and $`\mathcal{N}_{x}`$ denotes
the neighbors of node type $`x`$.

Based on the final-layer embeddings for the query, thinking, and answer
nodes, we predict the reward score for each rollout using scaled dot
product scoring:

```math
r_{ij}=\frac{\phi_{q}\big(h_{q_{i}}^{(L)}\big)\cdot\phi_{r}\big([h_{t_{ij}}^{(L)}\|h_{a_{ij}}^{(L)}]\big)}{\sqrt{d}}+b, \tag{5}
```

where $`r_{ij}`$ is the predicted reward score for the $`j`$-th rollout
of query $`i`$; $`\phi_{q}`$ and $`\phi_{r}`$ are learnable linear
projections that map the query and rollout representations to a shared
$`d`$-dimensional space, respectively; $`h_{q_{i}}^{(L)}`$ is the
final-layer embedding of the $`i`$-th query; $`h_{t_{ij}}^{(L)}`$ and
$`h_{a_{ij}}^{(L)}`$ are the final-layer embeddings of the thinking
process and answer for the $`j`$-th rollout of query $`i`$; $`\|`$
denotes concatenation; and $`b`$ is a learnable bias.

The GNN is trained to minimize the binary cross-entropy loss:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j}\big[r_{ij}^{*}\log\sigma(r_{ij})+(1-r_{ij}^{*})\log(1-\sigma(r_{ij}))\big], \tag{6}
```

where $`\sigma(\cdot)`$ is the sigmoid function;
$`r_{ij}^{*}=r(a_{ij},a_{i}^{*})\in\{0,1\}`$ is the ground-truth reward,
equal to 1 if the answer is correct and 0 otherwise; $`r(\cdot,\cdot)`$
is the reward function that compares an answer with the ground-truth
label; $`a_{ij}`$ is the $`j`$-th generated answer for the $`i`$-th
query; $`a_{i}^{*}`$ is its ground-truth label; and $`N`$ is the total
number of rollouts for each query. For continuous rewards such as
token-level F1, we instead train the GNN with a regression loss to
predict the reward directly.

### 3.2 Adaptive Reward Acquisition for Online Policy Optimization

Using the trained GNN. During online policy optimization, we employ a
mixed reward acquisition strategy: labeled rollouts receive ground-truth
rewards, while unlabeled rollouts leverage GNN-predicted rewards.

Since unlabeled rollouts and their associated queries are not part of
the warmup graph, we connect them to the warmup graph (Section 3.1) at
inference time. For each query $`q`$ whose rollouts are unlabeled, we
first encode it and its rollouts using the same encoder to obtain
embeddings $`e_{q}`$, $`e_{t}`$, and $`e_{a}`$. We then establish
query-query edges between the new query embedding and its top-$`k`$ most
similar warmup query embeddings by cosine similarity. The initial
embeddings are then propagated through the trained GNN to obtain the
final layer representations $`h_{q}^{(L)}`$, $`h_{t}^{(L)}`$, and
$`h_{a}^{(L)}`$. Finally, we predict the reward of rollout $`j`$ for
query $`i`$ as shown in Equation (5).

Integrating GNN with GRPO. During online policy optimization, for each
query $`q_{i}`$, GRPO generates $`N`$ rollouts
$`\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\}`$ from the policy. The final reward for each
rollout is computed as follows:

```math
R_{ij}=\begin{cases}r_{ij}^{*}&\text{if }y_{ij}\in\mathcal{R}_{\text{labeled}},\\
\mathbb{I}[\sigma(r_{ij})>0.5]&\text{otherwise},\end{cases} \tag{7}
```

where $`R_{ij}`$ is the reward for the $`j`$-th rollout of query $`i`$,
serving as $`R(\cdot)`$ in Equation (1) for computing the standardized
advantage; $`r_{ij}^{*}`$ is the ground-truth reward and $`r_{ij}`$ is
the GNN-predicted reward score; $`\mathcal{R}_{\text{labeled}}`$ denotes
the set of labeled rollouts (i.e., rollouts whose queries have
ground-truth labels); and $`\mathbb{I}[\cdot]`$ is the indicator
function. For continuous rewards, the predicted score is used directly
in place of the indicator.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Settings

Datasets. We evaluate 13 datasets in total: 10 in-domain and 3
out-of-domain, spanning three task categories (math reasoning, question
answering, and code generation).

In-domain Benchmarks (10 datasets). We evaluate in three task
categories: Math: GSM8K \[8\], GSM-Symbolic \[29\], MATH \[16\]; Code:
MBPP+ \[1, 26\], HumanEval+ \[5, 26\]; QA: MMLU \[15\],
CommonsenseQA \[39\], OpenBookQA \[28\], ARC-Challenge \[7\],
GPQA \[34\]. Dataset statistics and detailed descriptions are given in
the Appendices D and E.

Out-of-domain Benchmarks (3 datasets). To assess generalization, we
evaluate three held-out datasets not seen during training:
NuminaMath \[23\] (competition math), SIQA \[35\] (social reasoning) and
PIQA \[3\] (physical common sense).

Baselines. All methods adopt GRPO \[14\] as the RL algorithm for policy
optimization, but differ in how rewards are acquired. We compare against
six baselines: (1) R1-p (Partial), which trains with only 20% labeled
rollouts and discards the remaining 80%; (2) R1-Oracle, which trains
with 100% ground-truth labels, serving as a fully supervised reference;
(3) kNN, which predicts rewards for unlabeled rollouts by majority
voting over the nearest labeled query embeddings; (4) LLM-RM, which
fine-tunes Qwen2.5-3B as a binary reward classifier via SFT on the 20%
labeled data and uses the frozen model to predict rewards for the
remaining 80%; (5) Label Propagation \[48\], which uses the same query
embeddings, similarity graph, labeled rollouts, and GRPO procedure as
MemReward but replaces learned heterogeneous message passing with fixed
label diffusion; and (6) FixMatch-style self-training \[38\], which
assigns pseudo-rewards to the unlabeled rollouts on which the policy is
most confident (Appendix K). In contrast, MemReward uses the same 20%
labeled rollouts as R1-p, but augments the remaining 80% with
GNN-predicted rewards.

Settings. We use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct as
backbone models, and additionally Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \[13\] (Section 4.4). For optimization of the
online policy, we adopt GRPO with hyperparameters in Table 5. The GNN
training configurations are shown in Table 6 (complete implementation
details are given in Appendix C). Case studies illustrating model output
are provided in Appendix E.5, and compute overhead is analyzed in
Appendix G.

Evaluation Metrics. We use task-specific metrics across task categories:
Exact Match for mathematical reasoning requiring precise numerical
answers; Accuracy for question answering involving multiple-choice or
classification; and Pass@1 for code generation, measuring functional
correctness.

### 4.2 MemReward Approaches Oracle Performance with Only 20% Labels

Here, labeled and unlabeled queries are random splits of the same
datasets, so this setting tests within-distribution reward propagation;
Sections 4.3 and 4.4 evaluate held-out datasets and cross-domain
transfer. Table 1 presents the performance of 10 benchmarks on two model
scales. In Qwen2.5-3B, MemReward achieves a mean score of 77.02%,
reaching 97.3% of Oracle performance while outperforming R1-p by 1.35
points. In Qwen2.5-1.5B, MemReward improves over R1-p by 5.38 points,
reaching 96.6% of Oracle. On both scales, MemReward reduces the gap
between partial labels and full supervision, showing that graph-based
reward propagation provides an effective training signal regardless of
model capacity (comparative case studies in Appendix E.6). Across six
random seeds, MemReward’s gains over R1-p are statistically significant
on both model sizes, in-domain and out-of-domain (two-tailed paired
t-test, $`p<0.05`$; Appendix J). R1-Oracle is a fully supervised
reference rather than a per-benchmark upper bound: MemReward exceeds it
on several benchmarks, notably GSM8K and GSM-Symbolic for 1.5B and MATH
for 3B, where GNN reward predictions are most accurate (Appendix Q).

MemReward outperforms all reward prediction baselines. On Qwen2.5-3B,
MemReward outperforms LLM-RM by +1.30 and kNN by +1.05. On Qwen2.5-1.5B,
the advantage widens: MemReward exceeds LLM-RM by +4.81 and kNN by
+3.61, consistent with graph-based reward propagation being more
valuable when the policy is weaker. At the reward prediction level, the
GNN achieves 83.8% test-set accuracy, compared with 77.5% for kNN and
73.5% for MLP (Appendix H). On 3B, Label Propagation reaches 75.97, only
0.30 above R1-p (75.67), and FixMatch-style self-training falls below
R1-p at 72.55, whereas MemReward reaches 77.02. The gain thus comes from
learned propagation over the graph rather than from adding
pseudo-rewards (Appendix K).

GNN reward prediction quality. On held-out validation data, the GNN
achieves 0.917 ROC-AUC across all domains, with the highest prediction
quality on mathematical reasoning (ROC-AUC 0.936–0.946; see Appendix E.4
for a per-dataset breakdown and Appendix E.7 for propagation traces).

Robustness to reward-prediction noise. Flipping each GNN-predicted
reward with probability $`p`$ (ground-truth rewards untouched;
Qwen2.5-1.5B) lowers the average score by 0.72 at $`p=30\%`$ and 1.01 at
$`p=50\%`$. At $`p=70\%`$ and $`90\%`$, where flipped predictions become
anti-correlated with the target by construction, the score drops by
10.00 and 21.00 points. Separately, the frozen GNN’s accuracy on
Qwen2.5-3B stays between 74.9% and 77.3% over 410 GRPO steps
(Appendix P).

Mathematical reasoning benefits the most from the predicted rewards of
the GNN; on 1.5B, GSM8K improves by 11.56 points and GSM-Symbolic by
14.89 over R1-p; on 3B, MATH improves by 6.44. This is consistent with
MemReward’s query-query similarity edges: mathematical queries with
similar problem structures share solution strategies, enabling effective
reward propagation. Quantitatively, the top-7 nearest neighbors share
the same reward label 80% of the time on average (66–80% for math,
80–86% for QA). The sentence encoder only proposes candidate neighbors;
the GNN can learn to down-weight reward-inconsistent ones
(Appendix E.7).

MemReward scales with label budget. Figures 5 and 5 summarize
per-benchmark improvements and label ratio scaling. On Qwen2.5-1.5B,
MemReward improves or matches R1-p on all 13 benchmarks, with the
largest gains in mathematical reasoning (GSM-Sym +14.9, GSM8K +11.6).
Performance scales smoothly with the ground-truth label ratio:
increasing from 20% to 70% labels narrows the gap to Oracle from 2.10 to
just 0.48 points (99.4% of Oracle), with diminishing returns at higher
ratios (Appendix B).

### 4.5 Each Architectural Component Contributes to Reward Prediction

We ablate three key architectural components of MemReward by comparing
against three variants: (1) MLP, which replaces the GNN with a
multi-layer perceptron that predicts rewards from query embeddings
alone, without graph structure; (2) Homogeneous Graph, which removes
edge type distinctions and treats all nodes uniformly; (3) w/o Thinking
Node, which removes the intermediate thinking nodes from the
heterogeneous graph.

Figure 3 presents results per-category on both model scales, where the
category-level accuracy is computed by averaging over all benchmarks
within each task category. On 3B, the full model achieves 80.1% in Math,
75.6% in QA, and 63.0% in Code, consistently outperforming all ablated
variants. On 1.5B, the full model achieves 72.4% in Math, 66.0% in QA,
and 51.3% in Code.

Graph structure captures inter-query reward patterns. The MLP baseline,
which predicts rewards from query embeddings alone without graph
structure, suffers the largest drop on QA: 72.0% on 3B (a gap of 3.6
points) and 63.2% on 1.5B (a gap of 2.8 points). QA queries span diverse
topics, including science, social reasoning, and physical commonsense,
where individual embeddings provide limited reward signals. This shows
that the edges of query-query similarity (Section 3.1) capture
exploitable structural dependencies between experiences, addressing the
challenge identified in Section 1.

Heterogeneous edge types benefit structured reasoning. The homogeneous
graph variant, which treats all edges uniformly, retains 74.1% on QA but
drops to 76.4% on Math, a gap of 3.7 points from the full model. Math
problems exhibit distinct structural relationships at different levels:
query-query edges capture problem similarity (e.g., two quadratic
equations), while query-thinking and thinking-answer edges capture
solution correctness. Collapsing these into a single edge type loses
this hierarchical signal, demonstrating that the heterogeneous edge
design (Section 3.1) preserves task-specific structural relationships
essential for accurate reward prediction.

Thinking nodes benefit multi-step reasoning tasks. Removing thinking
nodes degrades Code to 58.0% on 3B (a drop of 5.0 points) and Math to
77.6% (a drop of 2.5 points). In math and code, the thinking process
encodes intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., equation manipulations,
algorithmic logic) that determine whether the final answer is correct.
Without thinking nodes, the GNN can only compare queries and answers,
losing the fine-grained signal about _how_ the model reasons. On 1.5B,
the w/o Thinking variant similarly falls to 69.0% in Math (a gap of 3.4
points). The consistency of component contributions across both model
scales shows that the heterogeneous three-node design (Section 3.1)
captures reasoning structure that generalizes across model capacities.
We also evaluate reward threshold sensitivity in Appendix I.

## 5 Related Work

Reinforcement Learning for LLMs. Early work established the RLHF
paradigm \[30\], training reward models from human evaluations and
optimizing via PPO \[37\]. Subsequent efforts reduced pipeline
complexity through AI feedback \[22\] and direct preference
optimization \[32\]. Process-level supervision further improved reward
quality via step-by-step verification \[24\] and automated
labeling \[41\]. More recently, DeepSeek-R1 \[14\] used GRPO with
group-based advantage estimation for reasoning. These methods assume
full reward supervision, whereas MemReward addresses limited labels.
Recent work further addresses reward model reliability: ensemble-based
uncertainty penalization mitigates overoptimization \[9\], and
noise-corrected gradient estimation handles noisy verifiers \[27\].
MemReward is complementary, addressing the orthogonal challenge of label
scarcity rather than reward model calibration.

Graph Neural Networks for Language Tasks. GNNs model relational
structures through iterative message passing \[12\], with foundational
architectures including GCN \[20\], GAT \[40\], and GATv2 \[4\].
Heterogeneous extensions such as HAN \[42\] and R-GCN \[36\] handle
multiple node and edge types. Our work extends heterogeneous GNNs to
reward prediction, constructing graphs over queries, thinking processes,
and answers to propagate reward labels.

Semi-supervised Learning and Label Propagation. Semi-supervised learning
addresses label scarcity through graph-based label propagation \[48,
18\] and consistency-based methods such as pseudo-labeling \[21\] and
FixMatch \[38\]. In the LLM context, Constitutional AI \[2\] uses AI
feedback to reduce labeling. Self-training approaches iteratively expand
labeled sets using model predictions \[43\], but risk error accumulation
without structural constraints. More broadly, these approaches either
rely on fixed similarity metrics or operate on individual samples
without exploiting structural dependencies. MemReward bridges these
paradigms, predicting rewards through learnable message passing over
query-thinking-answer structures. We compare against both label
propagation and FixMatch-style self-training in Section 4.2.

## 6 Conclusion

We proposed MemReward, a graph-based experience memory framework that
addresses reward label scarcity in reinforcement learning for LLMs. By
organizing queries, thinking processes, and answers into a heterogeneous
graph, MemReward propagates rewards from a small set of labeled rollouts
to unlabeled ones during online policy optimization. Across 13
benchmarks spanning mathematics, question answering, and code generation
on two Qwen2.5 model scales, MemReward achieves near-Oracle performance
with only 20% ground-truth labels and closely approaches Oracle on
out-of-domain tasks; the gains carry over to Llama backbones up to 8B
and to LLM-judge, human-preference, and continuous rewards. Ablation
studies confirm that each architectural component contributes to
prediction quality. Overall, MemReward demonstrates that graph-based
experience memory is an effective and scalable approach for enabling
label-efficient RL fine-tuning of LLMs. We discuss limitations and
future directions in Appendix A.

## Appendix A Limitations, Future Work, and Broader Impact

Limitations. We evaluate verifiable rewards on 13 benchmarks with
Qwen2.5-1.5B and 3B, and on 10 benchmarks with Llama-3.2-3B and
Llama-3.1-8B. Additional experiments with Qwen2.5-3B use LLM-judge
(TruthfulQA), human-preference (Chatbot Arena), and continuous token-F1
(HotpotQA) rewards. Two design choices define the current scope. (1) The
GNN is frozen after warmup, which avoids a moving-target feedback loop
between the reward predictor and the policy, while the 20% ground-truth
rewards continuously anchor optimization. Its accuracy stays stable over
410 GRPO steps, and training remains robust when up to half of the
predicted rewards are randomly flipped (Appendix P). This study
characterizes robustness to random prediction errors; systematic
prediction bias and policy-dependent reward exploitation lie outside it.
(2) Query-query edges are built with a fixed general-purpose sentence
encoder (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) that is not trained for reward relevance. Its
neighborhoods nonetheless carry reward-relevant signal: the top-7
nearest queries share the reward label about 80% of the time, and a GNN
trained on one domain predicts rewards in another 12.95
(QA$`\rightarrow`$Math) and 7.35 (Math$`\rightarrow`$QA) points more
accurately than a graph-free MLP (Appendix N).

Future Work. Natural next steps include extending MemReward to further
forms of supervision, such as graded expert assessment of mathematical
proofs and multi-level human ratings, and to larger backbones;
characterizing systematic reward bias and policy-dependent reward
exploitation, together with safeguards against them; learning
reward-aware similarity jointly with the GNN; and exploring richer graph
topologies, such as directed or hierarchical experience graphs that
order experiences by generation time or label confidence. Scaling graph
construction to larger rollout pools is another promising avenue.

Broader Impact. By reducing the reliance on costly human labels through
graph-based reward propagation, MemReward has the potential to
democratize access to RL-based LLM training for researchers with limited
labeling budgets. We do not foresee specific negative societal
consequences beyond those generally associated with improving LLM
reasoning capabilities. As with all advances in LLM performance,
downstream applications should be deployed responsibly with appropriate
safeguards.

## Appendix C Implementation Details

We implement the heterogeneous GNN using PyTorch Geometric \[11\] with
HeteroConv as the heterogeneous message-passing wrapper and
GATv2Conv \[4\] as the per-relation convolution operator, combined with
torch_scatter for efficient sparse aggregation. The GNN architecture
consists of 2 layers with a hidden dimension of 512, 4 attention heads,
and a dropout rate of 0.1. For in-domain and out-of-domain experiments,
we encode queries, thinking processes, and answers using
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 \[33\] (384-dim). All embeddings are projected to the
hidden dimension via type-specific linear transformations. Query-query
edges are constructed using top-$`k`$ cosine similarity with $`k=7`$;
the kNN baseline uses the same $`k=7`$ for a fair comparison. The GNN is
trained with the Adam optimizer (learning rate $`1\times 10^{-3}`$)
using binary cross-entropy loss for 150 epochs with early stopping
(patience 20).

For GRPO training, we use the veRL framework with the following
hyperparameters: actor learning rate $`1\times 10^{-6}`$, batch size
128, micro-batch size 32, and maximum response length 1024. We enable KL
loss regularization ($`\beta=1\times 10^{-3}`$) and advantage clipping
($`\epsilon=0.2`$). Response generation uses a temperature of 1.0 for
diverse sampling. Each query generates $`N=8`$ responses for advantage
estimation. Training runs for 410 steps, with model selection based on
validation performance. The Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct experiments use the same settings. All experiments
are conducted on NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPUs with BF16 precision.

## Appendix E Dataset Descriptions

We provide detailed descriptions of all datasets used in our evaluation,
organized by task category.

### E.1 Mathematical Reasoning

GSM8K \[8\] is a dataset of 8.5K grade school math word problems
requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. Problems involve basic
operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division) and
require 2-8 reasoning steps to solve. We evaluate using an exact match
on the final numerical answer.

GSM-Symbolic \[29\] is a variant of GSM8K generated from symbolic
templates that vary names and numerical values while preserving the
underlying problem structure. This variant helps assess whether models
truly understand mathematical reasoning or merely memorize numerical
patterns.

MATH \[16\] contains 12.5K challenging competition mathematics problems
spanning algebra, geometry, number theory, counting and probability, and
precalculus. Problems are drawn from AMC, AIME, and other prestigious
competitions, requiring sophisticated multi-step reasoning and domain
knowledge.

NuminaMath \[23\] (out-of-domain) is a large-scale mathematical
reasoning dataset with problems collected from various mathematical
olympiads and competitions worldwide. It provides diverse problem types
and difficulty levels beyond standard benchmarks, serving as a
challenging test of mathematical generalization.

### E.2 Question Answering

MMLU \[15\] (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) covers 57
subjects across STEM, humanities, social sciences, and other domains.
Questions are multiple-choice and test both world knowledge and
reasoning ability, ranging from elementary to professional difficulty.

CommonsenseQA \[39\] contains 12.2K multiple-choice questions requiring
commonsense reasoning about everyday concepts and relationships.
Questions are generated from the ConceptNet knowledge graph and require
an understanding of implicit world knowledge not stated in the question.

OpenBookQA \[28\] presents 5.9K elementary science questions modeled
after OpenBook exams. Each question requires combining a core science
fact with additional commonsense knowledge, testing multi-hop reasoning
over scientific concepts.

ARC \[7\] (AI2 Reasoning Challenge) contains 7.8K natural science
questions from standardized tests. We use the Challenge set (ARC-C),
which contains questions that simple retrieval and word co-occurrence
methods fail to answer correctly, requiring genuine reasoning.

GPQA \[34\] (Graduate-level Google-Proof QA) is a highly challenging
benchmark of 448 multiple-choice questions in biology, physics, and
chemistry. Questions are designed to be “Google-proof,” answerable by
domain experts but not easily searchable online, testing deep domain
expertise.

SIQA \[35\] (Social Interaction QA, out-of-domain) tests reasoning about
people’s actions and their social implications. Questions require
understanding of emotional reactions, motivations, and social dynamics
in everyday situations, assessing social commonsense reasoning beyond
factual knowledge.

PIQA \[3\] (Physical Interaction QA, out-of-domain) evaluates physical
commonsense reasoning about everyday objects and their interactions.
Questions test intuitive physics knowledge, such as object affordances,
material properties, and physical causality that humans acquire through
embodied experience.

### E.3 Code Generation

HumanEval+ \[5, 26\] is an extended version of the original HumanEval
benchmark with additional test cases to reduce false positives. It
contains 164 programming problems with function signatures and
docstrings, requiring models to generate correct Python implementations
that pass all test cases.

MBPP+ \[1, 26\] extends the Mostly Basic Python Problems benchmark with
more rigorous test cases. The original MBPP dataset contains 974
crowd-sourced Python programming problems designed to be solvable by
entry-level programmers, testing basic programming skills and common
algorithmic patterns.

### E.7 GNN Reward Propagation Trace

To illustrate how the GNN propagates reward signals through the
experience graph, we trace the full inference path for two
representative validation queries (Table 14 and Table 15). For each
query, we show its top-7 nearest training neighbors (ranked by cosine
similarity of query embeddings), the reward labels of those neighbors,
and the GNN’s predicted scores versus ground-truth labels for each
response. These traces use the Qwen-2.5-3B model with 20% ground-truth
labels.

Case 1 (Table 14) demonstrates clear reward propagation: 4 of the 7
neighbors have mostly correct responses ($`\geq`$7/8), and the GNN
assigns uniformly high scores (0.78–0.98) to all 8 responses, matching
the ground truth perfectly (8/8). Because most neighbors share the
correct label, the GNN produces confident, accurate predictions.

Case 2 (Table 15) demonstrates fine-grained discrimination under mixed
neighbor signals: neighbor correctness rates range from 0/8 to 5/8, yet
the GNN correctly separates the 5 correct responses (scores 0.58–0.77)
from the 3 incorrect ones (scores 0.009–0.048), achieving 8/8 accuracy.
This shows that the GNN does not simply average neighbor labels but
leverages the thinking-process and answer embeddings to discriminate at
the individual response level, producing well-separated scores even when
neighbor-level signals are ambiguous.

Reward-inconsistent neighbors. Case 2 also shows how propagation handles
semantically similar neighbors with conflicting rewards. The query’s two
cross-domain GSM8K neighbors have 0/8 and 4/8 correct responses, and its
five same-domain neighbors have 5/8, 3/8, 3/8, 4/8, and 0/8. Neither
similarity nor domain membership alone determines whether a neighbor is
useful. The GNN instead combines the retrieved context with each
response’s thinking and answer nodes and separates the correct responses
from the incorrect ones. Reward-inconsistent neighbors remain a
potential source of error.

## Appendix G Computational Overhead

## Appendix K Semi-Supervised Baselines

Label Propagation \[48\] uses the same query embeddings, similarity
graph, labeled examples, and mixed-reward GRPO procedure as MemReward;
the only difference is that it uses fixed label diffusion instead of
learned heterogeneous message passing. Its per-benchmark results on
Qwen2.5-3B are included in Table 1. The FixMatch-style baseline \[38\]
uses the same 20% labeled data and estimates the confidence of each
unlabeled rollout by the policy’s average answer-segment probability.
Within each batch, only the 30% most confident unlabeled rollouts are
retained and assigned pseudo-labels; the corresponding confidence
threshold, $`\tau=0.058`$, is selected by maximizing F1 on the
validation set. Labeled rollouts continue to receive ground-truth
rewards, and the baseline uses no graph structure or cross-query
information.

Table 20 compares the overall scores. Confidence-filtered self-training
falls 3.12 points below R1-p, so the policy’s own confidence is not
calibrated enough to supply reliable rewards in this setting. Fixed

propagation adds 1.35.

## Appendix M Additional Reward Sources

The three experiments in Table 3 use Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct as the policy
and provide ground-truth rewards for only 20% of the training queries;
the remaining rollouts receive GNN-predicted rewards.

TruthfulQA (LLM-judge rewards). Responses to TruthfulQA \[25\] are
scored by DeepSeek-V4-Flash \[44\], used as a fixed judge, on whether
they are both truthful and informative. The data are split by query into
training, validation, and test sets with a 5:2:3 ratio. The same judge
and criterion provide the training rewards for the labeled queries and
evaluate the policy on the held-out test set. Chatbot Arena
(human-preference rewards). We sample 1,500 Chatbot Arena
conversations \[6, 47\] after excluding ties and formulate each as a
two-choice question: “Which response is better, A or B?” The data are
split by query into 750/300/450 training/validation/test examples. The
reward is whether the policy’s choice agrees with the human preference,
and we report preference accuracy on the 450 test queries.

HotpotQA (continuous rewards). We use HotpotQA \[45\] in the distractor
setting with a 5:2:3 split. The reward is the token-level F1 between the
predicted span and the gold answer, a continuous value in $`[0,1]`$. The
GNN is trained as a regression model to predict token-level F1, and its
checkpoint is selected by validation token-level F1.

## Appendix O Thinking Length and Similarity

Thinking texts are much longer than queries and answers, so one may ask
whether their embeddings are dominated by length or by a shared
reasoning template. In MemReward, only query embeddings determine graph
connectivity; thinking and answer nodes are attached through structural
edges within each rollout (Section 3.1). We also measured the effect
directly with the deployed all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings and the same
top-7 neighborhoods. The correlation between thinking length and top-7
thinking similarity is $`-0.287`$ across the population and is negative
in 7 of 9 benchmark-level measurements. Thinking similarity also does
not track query similarity: of two MATH queries, the one with higher
top-7 thinking similarity (0.6641 vs. 0.4702) has lower top-7 query
similarity (0.4577 vs. 0.6530). The w/o Thinking and homogeneous-graph
ablations (Section 4.5) show that modeling thinking as a separate node
type improves reward prediction.

## Appendix P Robustness to Reward-Prediction Noise

We randomly flip each GNN-predicted reward with probability

corrupted. We use Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and otherwise keep the GRPO
settings and 10-benchmark evaluation of the main experiments. The
$`p=0`$ condition already includes the GNN’s natural prediction errors.
As Table 24 shows, performance degrades by about one point up to

uncorrupted ground-truth rewards that anchor policy optimization with
reliable supervision, together with GRPO’s normalized, group-relative
advantages over eight rollouts per query. The latter reduce the

informative at the group level. This study covers random corruption;

## Appendix Q When MemReward Exceeds R1-Oracle

R1-Oracle uses ground-truth rewards for all rollouts, but it is trained
with a finite, stochastic, multi-domain GRPO objective, so full
supervision does not guarantee the best result on every benchmark. In
Table 1, MemReward exceeds R1-Oracle most notably on GSM8K and
GSM-Symbolic for 1.5B and on MATH for 3B. For the math benchmarks, the
explanation lies in domain-dependent reward quality: with Qwen2.5-1.5B
and 20% labels, the frozen GNN reaches a ROC-AUC of 0.918 on math, 0.871
on QA, and 0.754 on code. Because math rollouts receive the most
reliable predicted rewards, MemReward places relatively more effective
emphasis on math and less on the noisier QA and code gradients. To test
this explanation, we raise the ground-truth ratio for QA and code from
20% to 70% while keeping math at 20% (Table 26). QA and code improve,
while math drops by 2.01 points and no longer exceeds R1-Oracle, which
is consistent with a multi-task trade-off rather than predicted rewards
being better than ground truth.

      paper reproducible is important, regardless of whether the code

    - •
      If the contribution is a dataset and/or model, the authors should

    - •

      in various ways. For example, if the contribution is a novel

      the model. In general, releasing code and data is often one good
      way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided

      reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of the
      contribution. For example
      1.  (a)

          should make it clear how to reproduce that algorithm.
      2.  (b)
          If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the
          paper should describe the architecture clearly and fully.
      3.  (c)
          If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language
          model), then there should either be a way to access this model
          for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce the model
          (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to
          construct the dataset).
      4.  (d)
          We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases,
          in which case authors are welcome to describe the particular
          way they provide for reproducibility. In the case of
          closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is
          limited in some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should
          be possible for other researchers to have some path to
          reproducing or verifying the results.

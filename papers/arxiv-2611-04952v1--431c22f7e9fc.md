###### Abstract

Self-evolving agents write interaction experience into artifacts and
reuse them in later tasks. Most work supports such methods with three
proxy signals: artifacts accumulate, behavior changes once they are
loaded, and metrics rise. We call this signal-based evaluation. What
these signals are meant to indicate, however, has not been defined. To
fill this gap, we define capability formation from artifacts as five
sequentially dependent properties, two of which form a checkpoint for
capability formation, and introduce three-scale diagnosis, which judges
these properties at the state, game, and lineage levels. In freestyle
Gomoku, Reflexion, Voyager, and DGM evolve with the same base model and
all show the three signals, yet each exhibits a distinct
capability-formation break. Reflexion shows a legalization gain: its
pass rate rises only because more of its moves are legal. Voyager shows
a compositional override: components that are sound on their own combine
to miss all 20 available one-move wins. DGM shows loss-only drift: after
passing the checkpoint, later versions only lose passing states. These
counterexamples show that signal-based evaluation cannot determine
whether a capability has formed.

## 1 Introduction

Self-evolving agents generate artifacts through interaction with an
environment—textual reflections, executable skills, or code—and inject
them into a harness so that experience can be reused across tasks ([Xi
et al., 2023](#bib.bib1)). Work in this area typically argues that a
method is effective using three signals: artifacts accumulate, behavior
changes once they are loaded, and accuracy, pass rate, or evolutionary
score rises. We call this _signal-based evaluation_. All three signals
are proxies for capability. None directly tests whether a changed
behavior advances the task, whether an established effect is overturned
by another part of the system, or whether later evolution builds on what
has been gained. More fundamentally, what “effective” should refer to
has not been defined. We therefore ask: what verifiable task capability
do the artifacts a system generates and reuses actually leave behind?

![Refer to caption](2611.04952v1/gomoku_spatial_reasoning.png)

Figure 1: Different reasoning requirements from the same board state.

Prior work has argued that endpoint performance on a single episode
cannot characterize self-evolution, and has turned to long-horizon task
streams, capability trajectories across frozen versions, or separate
analyses of reflections, tool libraries, and transfer
(Section [2](#S2 "2 Related Work ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
These analyses still take episodes, versions, or tools as their objects.
Answering our question instead requires taking the transition from
artifact to capability itself as the object: observing how a single
action changes, whether that change survives in actual decision making,
and whether it carries over across versions. Such observations are hard
to make in open-ended tasks, where states cannot be replayed exactly,
the correctness of an action lacks an independent criterion, and
environmental noise and unstructured states confound attribution. We
therefore study the question in a controlled environment.

Such an environment must make states replayable, task effects
rule-judged, and games and versions fully recorded
(Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Environment: Freestyle Gomoku ‣ 4 Experimental Design ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
Inspired by ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib11)), who study world-model
representations with controlled board-game sequences, we use freestyle
Gomoku (Gomoku without forbidden-move restrictions) as a “microscope”
for self-evolving mechanisms. Gomoku meets these requirements. Simple
rules do not make the task simple. The agent reads the board only as a
sequence of coordinates and must reconstruct spatial relations along
rows, columns, and diagonals on its own
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment"));
without memory, about half of the model’s moves land on occupied points,
which leaves real room for evolution to improve.

In this environment, we define capability formation as five properties
that an artifact passes through in sequence, forming the
Artifact-to-Capability diagnostic framework
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Five Diagnostic Properties ‣ 3 Artifact-to-Capability Diagnostic Framework ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
An artifact must first form and enter decision making, and then change
actions. A change is neutral in itself: it must also produce a positive
effect without undermining effects already established, and only when
both hold has a capability formed. Later evolution should then extend
it. The five properties are judged at three scales—states, games, and
lineages—which we call _three-scale diagnosis_
(Section [4](#S4 "4 Experimental Design ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).

We let Reflexion ([Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib2)), Voyager ([Wang et
al., 2023](#bib.bib3)), and DGM ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib4))
evolve autonomously with the same base model, environment, and
opponents, then freeze and diagnose them. To show that signal-based
evaluation cannot establish capability formation, a single
counterexample suffices: a system that shows all three signals but has
not formed a capability. We present three, each breaking at a different
property—three _capability-formation breaks_. Reflexion shows a
_legalization gain_: its memories make the model play fewer illegal
moves but do not make it choose better among legal ones. Voyager shows a
_compositional override_: each component is sound on its own, but in
combination the calling script blocks the opponent and returns early at
conflict states, overriding an established win; across 20 games, it
completes five at none of its immediate-win states. DGM shows _loss-only
drift_: OB-3 passes the checkpoint, but later versions only lose passing
states, and the last barely changes behavior. That the three breaks fall
on different properties also shows that no property can stand in for
another.

Our contributions are:

1.  1.  Problem and definition. We pose a question that signal-based
        evaluation leaves open—what verifiable task capability do the
        artifacts a system generates and reuses actually leave behind?—and
        define capability formation as five sequentially dependent
        properties, with task-relation realization and boundary-safe reuse
        forming the checkpoint for capability formation.
2.  2.  Method. We introduce three-scale diagnosis, which judges these
        properties at the state, game, and lineage levels in controlled
        freestyle Gomoku.
3.  3.  Findings. We present three counterexamples in Reflexion, Voyager,
        and DGM: all three signals are present, yet the diagnosis detects a
        legalization gain, a compositional override, and loss-only drift,
        breaking at task-relation realization, boundary-safe reuse, and
        capability extension, respectively. Signal-based evaluation
        therefore cannot determine whether a capability has formed.

## 2 Related Work

Prior work has examined self-evolving agents and the roles of their
artifacts from several angles. Process-oriented evaluations track
performance changes along checkpoints or task streams. EvoPathBench
freezes successive artifact checkpoints and uses paired evaluations with
artifacts enabled and disabled on held-out episodes to test whether
performance depends on them ([Lin et al., 2026a](#bib.bib9)). Agent
Plasticity measures self-improvement at each checkpoint in environments
including board games; using LLM-generated observational annotations, it
distinguishes two bottlenecks—failure to reuse a relevant artifact and
failure even after reuse—and notes that controlled interventions such as
removal or replacement could further separate these mechanisms ([Singh
et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)). [Lin et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib21) trace the
limited gains of weaker models to artifacts that are either not loaded
or not followed after loading.

Other work documents negative effects of artifacts: when skills with
overlapping functions coexist, conflicts arise mainly when the system
selects which skill to read ([Yan et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)); as a skill
pool keeps growing, its pass rate first rises and then falls ([Shang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib23)); and reflective memory can lower success
relative to a memory-free retry in some settings ([Bai et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). These studies characterize change mainly through
aggregate performance over tasks, episodes, or checkpoints; we refer to
this as outcome analysis. Outcome analysis can reveal gains,
regressions, and retention, and combined with interventions it can also
test whether performance depends on artifacts.

Closer to the state level are studies that make paired comparisons on
the same input. Misevolution compares action safety with and without
memory for the same query ([Shao et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)); SkillApt
compares answer correctness and cost with and without a skill in the
same task state ([Guo et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)); Counterfactual Memory
Audit crosses two histories while holding a verified-identical input
fixed, distinguishing whether memory changes an action from whether it
leads to the correct task branch ([Zhang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib33));
and [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib18) classify transitions before and
after self-correction on the same problem, including
incorrect-to-correct and correct-to-incorrect changes.

Our object of diagnosis is capability formation itself: whether a frozen
system can turn behavioral change into task capability. We decompose
this transition into sequentially dependent properties
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Five Diagnostic Properties ‣ 3 Artifact-to-Capability Diagnostic Framework ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment"))
and pair each with independently assessable evidence: move differences
and rule-based judgments in state-level diagnosis, call traces in
game-level diagnosis, and newly passing and lost states in lineage-level
diagnosis. Within this framework, the two bottlenecks identified by
Agent Plasticity correspond to behavioral activation and task-relation
realization, whereas the breaks in Reflexion, Voyager, and DGM fall at
task-relation realization, boundary-safe reuse, and capability
extension. Additional related work appears in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Extended Related Work ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment").

## 3 Artifact-to-Capability Diagnostic Framework

### 3.1 Five Diagnostic Properties

![Refer to caption](2611.04952v1/self_evolution_loop.png)

Figure 2: The self-evolution loop: experience, evaluation, reflection,
artifact updates, and feedback.

In the self-evolution loop
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Five Diagnostic Properties ‣ 3 Artifact-to-Capability Diagnostic Framework ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")),
the system compresses part of its interaction trajectories into
persistent artifacts and invokes them in later decisions. Compression
makes experience reusable across tasks, but it also discards the state
conditions, failure causes, and alternative actions of the original
trajectory. We must therefore test whether the retained experience still
produces task-relevant effects in new decisions. We define five
properties along the stages an artifact passes through to become a
capability:

1.  1.  Artifact formation: a persistent structure has formed and is
        connected to subsequent decision making;
2.  2.  Behavioral activation: under the same state and interface
        conditions, actions with and without the artifact differ by more
        than sampling noise;
3.  3.  Task-relation realization: the artifact produces a positive
        effect—the resulting actions better satisfy the relevant task
        relation overall, rather than merely differing from the original
        ones;
4.  4.  Boundary-safe reuse: the artifact does not undermine an established
        effect—in actual decision making, an established positive relation
        is not overturned by another part of the system;
5.  5.  Capability extension: a later version adds new positive effects
        while preserving existing ones.

The dependency structure is
$`1\rightarrow 2\rightarrow(3\text{ and }4)\rightarrow 5`$; we call this
sequence the _capability chain_. Properties 3 and 4 form a single
_checkpoint_: the former asks whether the artifact produces a positive
effect, the latter whether it undermines an established one. When both
hold, we say that _capability formation_ has occurred. Property 4
concerns only established positive relations that are overturned in
actual decisions; gains and losses on fixed states already count toward
Property 3. Property 5 is meaningful only for versions that pass the
checkpoint. Diagnosis proceeds in this order: the first property that
fails is the _break_, and properties that depend on it are not judged.

The five properties apply at three scales. Properties 2 and 3 concern
single moves and are judged on fixed states; Property 4 concerns
established effects in actual decision making and is judged in complete
games; Property 5 concerns evolution and is judged along the version
lineage; and Property 1 is confirmed directly from call records. We call
this _three-scale diagnosis_, comprising state-level, game-level, and
lineage-level diagnosis
(Section [4](#S4 "4 Experimental Design ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
Game-level diagnosis does not judge wins and losses as such, but whether
an established effect is overturned.

A _task relation_ is defined by the task objective: it specifies which
actions advance the task in a given state, such as taking one’s own
immediate winning point, blocking the opponent’s, or forming an open
four. Environmental rules, such as not playing on an occupied point, are
not task relations: every legal action satisfies them, yet they advance
nothing. Property 3 therefore compares only legal actions; reducing rule
violations does not by itself count as a positive effect.

### 3.2 System-Level Unit of Analysis

Our object of diagnosis is the _frozen system_: all artifacts that
actually participate in decision making in a frozen version, taken as a
whole. Decisions are produced jointly by several _components_: skills
propose candidates, a calling script decides which skill to query first
and which answer to accept, and code branches override one another. A
component may be correct on its own yet have its effect overturned by
another once they are combined. With single components as the unit, a
failure could only be blamed on “a poorly written skill,” leaving open
whether the system as a whole has formed a capability. We therefore
judge Properties 1–4 on the frozen system; which component a break lies
in serves only as explanation and does not change the judgment. Formal
definitions appear in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Diagnostic Definitions ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment").

### 4.1 Environment: Freestyle Gomoku

Three properties of the environment make three-scale diagnosis possible
(Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2 System-Level Unit of Analysis ‣ 3 Artifact-to-Capability Diagnostic Framework ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
_Replayable states_: any board can be restored exactly, so state-level
diagnosis compares moves with and without an artifact on the same state,
and set A can be expanded by rotation and reflection. _Rule-judged task
effects_: a rule program enumerates the acceptable moves of each state
independently of the system under diagnosis and of any other model, so
“changed for the better” is separated from “merely changed” for
Properties 3 and 4. _Fully recorded games and versions_: every move,
call path, and version is archived, so game-level diagnosis can locate
conflict states and lineage-level diagnosis can count newly passing and
lost states.

The board is $`15\times 15`$. Players alternate placing stones on empty
intersections; playing on an occupied intersection or off the board is
illegal. Forming a horizontal, vertical, or diagonal line of five stones
(_five in a row_) wins, and game length is counted in moves.

A point at which one move completes five is an _immediate winning
point_. A state in which it is the agent’s turn and the agent has an
immediate winning point is an _immediate-win state_; if the opponent
also has one, it is a _conflict state_. Four in a row with both ends
open is an _open four_, and three in a row with both ends open is an
_open three_. In each state, the legal moves that satisfy the primary
task relation form the acceptable set $`G(s)`$; a move in $`G(s)`$
_passes_, and the fraction of states passed in a set is its _pass rate_.
All three opponents are programmatic (rule-based, threat-aware, and
search_2); the strongest, search_2, searches two plies ahead.
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Environment and Fixed States ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")
gives the full rules and criteria.

### 4.2 Three Scales

State-level diagnosis. We use 1,876 fixed states: set A contains 120
states obtained by rotating and reflecting manually constructed tactical
states, and set B contains 1,756 states drawn from actual games during
DGM evolution (construction in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Environment and Fixed States ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
The control removes the artifact under test: empty memory for Reflexion;
round1 for Voyager, which keeps only the first-round tactical skills and
leaves the calling script unchanged; and the agent’s initial strategy P0
for DGM. For Voyager and DGM, we thus test the effect of what evolution
added. Because Reflexion samples its moves, we use the difference
between two empty-memory runs as the noise baseline.

Game-level diagnosis. The frozen system plays 20 games against search_2,
each with a different random seed. We rebuild the board after every
move, identify all immediate-win states, and record whether the system
completes five; for games in which it does not, we keep the per-move
call trace for explanation.

Lineage-level diagnosis. This applies only to mechanisms with a version
lineage, here DGM. We judge Properties 1–4 on OB-3, the evolved version
with the most wins against search_2, and use lineage-level diagnosis to
test whether later versions retain and extend its effects. Along the
main lineage, we compare adjacent versions state by state on set B,
count newly passing and lost states, and compare wins. We also build a
differential pool of 7,234 states from archived evolution games and
compare the moves of the last two versions, OB-6 and OB-7, on every
state
(Appendix [D](#A4.SS0.SSS0.Px1 "OB-6/OB-7 differential pool. ‣ Appendix D DGM Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).

The three mechanisms cover three artifact forms; implementation details

Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Experimental Setup ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment").
Reflexion writes game feedback into textual memories, split by opponent

for example, detect_immediate_wins finds immediate winning points, and
create_open_three proposes attacking moves that form an open three. The
final move is chosen by auto_player, a calling script that the agent
writes itself; it queries skills in a fixed order and returns as soon as
one fires. DGM modifies strategy code directly, producing the lineage P0
$`\rightarrow`$ OB-1 $`\rightarrow`$ OB-2 $`\rightarrow`$ OB-3
$`\rightarrow`$ OB-5 $`\rightarrow`$ OB-6 $`\rightarrow`$ OB-7 (OB-4 is
a side branch).

### 5.1 Reflexion: Legalization Gain, Not Selection Gain

After memories are loaded, moves change on 59.8%–78.2% of B states,
above the noise baseline of 46.7% (Property 2 holds), and the overall
pass rate $`P(\text{pass})`$ rises on both sets
(Table [C.1](#A3.T1 "Table C.1 ‣ Appendix C Reflexion Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
Taken alone, this metric suggests that reflection helps the model play
better.

By definition, however,
$`P(\text{pass})=P(\text{legal})\times P(\text{pass}\mid\text{legal})`$,
and the rise in overall pass rate comes entirely from a higher legality
rate—a _legalization gain_
(Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5 Diagnostic Results ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
The legal pass rate, which measures move selection, improves in none of
the 12 combinations and, on the larger, lower-noise set B falls by
1.6–4.4 percentage points. State-level pairing agrees: most newly
passing states come from illegal moves becoming legal, and on states
where both conditions yield legal moves, the net change is negative
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Reflexion Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
Memory suppresses rule violations but produces no _selection gain_.
Task-relation realization (Property 3) fails, and the break is at
Property 3 (alternative explanations in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Alternative Explanations ‣ Appendix C Reflexion Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).

### 5.2 Voyager: Compositional Override

Ablating any component changes moves on 89 to 808 B states (Property 2
holds), and from round1 to full, 459 B states become passing and 73 are
lost (Property 3 holds; ablation data in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Voyager Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
Open fours in games also rise from 0/20 to 20/20.

In game-level diagnosis, however, Voyager reaches an immediate-win state
in all 20 games yet completes five at none of them (0/20), blocking the
opponent’s immediate winning point instead and losing every game
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.3 Three Mechanisms ‣ 4 Experimental Design ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
All 20 are conflict states. Call traces locate the break in how the
components combine. The “win if possible” logic in evaluate_threats is
correct on its own; the defense-first rule in auto_player is reasonable
when only the opponent has an immediate winning point; and
create_open_three drives attacks that form open fours. These attacks,
however, make conflict states frequent, and at such states auto_player
returns at its first branch, which blocks the opponent (defense-first
early return), so neither its own immediate-win check nor the “win if
possible” logic in evaluate_threats is ever reached
(Appendix [E.1](#A5.SS1 "E.1 Conflict States and Call Order ‣ Appendix E Voyager Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
Each component is sound on its own, yet in combination they overturn an
established effect—a _compositional override_. Boundary-safe reuse
(Property 4) fails, and the break is at Property 4 (alternative
explanations in
Appendix [E.2](#A5.SS2 "E.2 Alternative Explanations ‣ Appendix E Voyager Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).

### 5.3 DGM: Loss-Only Drift After Capability Formation

DGM shows all three signals
(Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.1 Environment: Freestyle Gomoku ‣ 4 Experimental Design ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
OB-3, the evolved version with the most wins, passes the checkpoint.
Relative to P0, 522 B states become passing and 139 are lost, a net gain
of 383 (Properties 2 and 3 hold). It completes five at all 19
immediate-win states in its 20 games (19/19; Property 4 holds) and wins
19 games, one fewer than P0. OB-3 has formed a capability.

Lineage-level diagnosis, however, shows no extension afterward
(Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.1 Reflexion: Legalization Gain, Not Selection Gain ‣ 5 Diagnostic Results ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
OB-5 and OB-6 add no newly passing states and only lose, while wins fall
to 0—_loss-only drift_. OB-7 differs from OB-6 on only one of the 7,234
states in the differential pool, so its modification barely reaches
actual decisions
(Appendix [D](#A4.SS0.SSS0.Px1 "OB-6/OB-7 differential pool. ‣ Appendix D DGM Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
Meanwhile, the evolutionary score still rises from OB-3 to OB-5
(Appendix [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Alternative Explanations ‣ Appendix D DGM Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
Capability extension (Property 5) fails, and the break is at Property 5
(alternative explanations in
Appendix [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Alternative Explanations ‣ Appendix D DGM Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).

## 6 Discussion

Although derived in Gomoku, the properties concern task relations rather
than the game; we conjecture, without having tested it, that the same
breaks arise wherever artifacts are reused through a fixed interface.
Consider a GUI agent with two skills. Skill A moves the cursor to a
target element and clicks; it can reach the target (a positive effect),
land on a non-interactive region (an ineffective change), or open an
unrelated page (a harmful change), and a version that only repairs
malformed coordinates would yield a legalization gain. Skill B, written
for pages behind a pop-up, first clicks to dismiss the pop-up and then
moves to its own target. Each skill reaches its target alone, but when B
runs after A, its first click hits whatever lies under the cursor on the
page A just reached and navigates away: a compositional override of A’s
established effect. Testing this requires replayable screens and a
rule-based checker of application state
(Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Environment: Freestyle Gomoku ‣ 4 Experimental Design ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).

## 7 Conclusion

Most work supports self-evolving methods with artifact accumulation,
behavioral change, and metric gains—signal-based evaluation—without
defining what counts as capability formation. We define the transition
from artifact to capability as five properties and judge them with
three-scale diagnosis. Under the same base model, environment, and
opponents, the diagnosis detects three capability-formation breaks: a
legalization gain in Reflexion, a compositional override in Voyager, and
loss-only drift in DGM. These counterexamples show that the three
signals are useful but insufficient to determine whether a capability
has formed. Evaluating the benefits of self-evolution requires directly
testing positive effects, the preservation of established effects, and
extension across versions, at the state, game, and lineage levels.

## Limitations

How the three break types are distributed across mechanisms and tasks
requires diagnosis at a larger scale. We cover three representative
self-evolving mechanisms, whose artifacts are textual memories,
executable skill libraries, and code-version lineages, but not every
form of self-evolution: multi-agent co-evolution or direct parameter
updates may break at other stages. Which properties can be judged also
depends on a mechanism’s structure; for example, capability extension
can be judged only for mechanisms with a version lineage. During
diagnosis, Reflexion chooses moves with the model directly, whereas
Voyager and DGM use evolved code. Whether different base models, longer
evolution, or repeated runs would move the breaks elsewhere remains to
be tested.

Finally, our empirical scope is limited to freestyle Gomoku. We chose it
because rules can independently judge whether a move is correct and
states can be replayed exactly; this also means that its task relations
are local and enumerable. Task relations in open-ended tasks are often
hard to determine from rules, and how to operationalize the framework
there remains open.

## Appendix A Diagnostic Definitions

Let $`s`$ be a fixed state, let $`\pi_{A}(s)`$ denote the move selected
by the system with the artifact under comparison loaded, and let
$`\pi_{\varnothing}(s)`$ denote the move under the control condition.
Let $`G(s)`$ be the set of legal moves in $`s`$ that satisfy the
relevant task relation; a move is counted as passing if and only if it
belongs to $`G(s)`$.

#### Behavioral activation.

We count the states for which $`\pi_{A}(s)\neq\pi_{\varnothing}(s)`$.
For mechanisms in which the model samples moves, we compare this count
with the same quantity computed between two independent control runs
(the noise baseline); a change counts as activation only if it exceeds
that baseline. Voyager and DGM select moves deterministically in code,
so any changed output counts as activation.

#### Task-relation realization.

At the call level, the pass rate decomposes as
$`P(\text{pass})=P(\text{legal})\times P(\text{pass}\mid\text{legal})`$;
we judge improvement only by whether $`P(\text{pass}\mid\text{legal})`$
increases. At the state level, newly passing states are classified by
source as “illegal $`\rightarrow`$ pass” or “legal non-pass
$`\rightarrow`$ pass.” The former indicates only that a move became
legal and does not count as a positive task effect.

#### Boundary-safe reuse.

We reconstruct each game move by move and identify every immediate-win
state. If there are $`n`$ such states and the system completes five in
$`k`$ of them, we report $`k/n`$; any $`k<n`$ indicates that an
already-established winning relation was overturned.

#### Capability extension.

Let $`C_{v}`$ be the set of states passed by version $`v`$ on set B.
Between adjacent versions, newly passing and lost states are
$`N=C_{v^{\prime}}\setminus C_{v}`$ and
$`L=C_{v}\setminus C_{v^{\prime}}`$, respectively; we also compare wins
against search_2. This criterion applies only to iterations after the
version that passes the checkpoint. Such an iteration fails to preserve
and extend capability when $`|N|=0`$ and $`|L|>0`$, or when the number
of wins declines. We classify an iteration as stagnant when $`|N|+|L|`$
is close to zero and the selected moves are almost unchanged.

## Appendix B Environment and Fixed States

An independent rule-based referee checks the legality and outcome of
every move. When the agent has an immediate winning point and it is its
turn, the uniquely correct move under the rules is to complete five,
even if the opponent also has an immediate winning point: completing
five ends the game immediately, whereas blocking only allows the game to
continue.

We construct $`G(s)`$ as follows. Set A is formed from 22 manually
constructed tactical states through rotations and reflections; we retain
120 states with unambiguous task relations. Set B consists of 1,756
deduplicated states sampled from games during DGM evolution and balanced
across task-relation categories. For both sets, a rule program
enumerates all legal moves satisfying the primary task relation in each
state; for example, the immediate-win relation includes every move that
completes five at once.

In game-level diagnosis, each system or version plays 20 complete games
against search_2, with a limit of 200 moves per game. Games exceeding
the limit are recorded as undecided.

### C.1 Alternative Explanations

#### Move changes are merely sampling noise.

Between the two empty-memory runs, the selected move differs on 46.7% of
states in set B. Passes change in both directions at similar rates (41
versus 37), suggesting little net change. With the six memories loaded,
moves change on 59.8%–78.2% of states, and every condition yields a
positive net change
(Table [C.2](#A3.T2 "Table C.2 ‣ Appendix C Reflexion Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")),
indicating that the memory-induced changes exceed the observed sampling
variation.

#### More legal moves dilute the legal pass rate.

Newly legal moves may occur on more difficult states, thereby lowering
the legal pass rate. We address this possibility by considering only
states for which both the empty-memory and loaded-memory conditions
produce legal moves. On set B, the net change within this legal subset
is $`-12`$ and $`-19`$ for the outcome and trace conditions,
respectively
(Table [C.3](#A3.T3 "Table C.3 ‣ Appendix C Reflexion Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).
Thus, although the number of states passing the diagnostic increases,
most of the increase comes from illegal actions becoming legal rather
than from improvements among actions that were already legal.

#### The memories may not match the diagnostic states.

The memories are organized into three opponent-specific groups, whereas
the diagnostic states do not encode opponent identity. Any memory may
therefore be irrelevant to some of the states. We consequently evaluate
all six memories separately. Across the 12 combinations of memory
condition and state set (A or B), the legal pass rate improves in none
of them
(Table [C.1](#A3.T1 "Table C.1 ‣ Appendix C Reflexion Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")),
so the conclusion does not depend on which memory is used. Because set A
contains relatively few states, its noise-induced fluctuation is
$`-4.2`$ percentage points; the main evidence therefore comes from set
B, where the corresponding fluctuation is only $`+0.1`$ percentage
points.

### D.1 Alternative Explanations

#### Using OB-3 as the reference is cherry-picking.

After OB-3, both OB-5 and OB-6 add zero newly passing states on set B.
The issue is therefore not simply that later versions failed to return
to the peak; they added no new passing states at all, regardless of the
reference version. Using OB-1, which achieves the highest pass rate, as
the reference leads to the same conclusion: the B pass rate only
decreases thereafter
(Table [D.1](#A4.T1 "Table D.1 ‣ Appendix D DGM Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).

#### Local misalignment between the score and the diagnostic results.

The composite score and the diagnostic results show broadly similar
trends across versions: from OB-1 to OB-3, both the score and the number
of wins against search_2 increase. However, a local mismatch appears
from OB-3 to OB-5: the score increases from 347.0 to 357.8, whereas the
number of wins decreases from 19 to 9. This occurs because the
game-length term in the evaluation function compensates for the decline
in the win term. Thus, the composite score broadly reflects changes in
version performance, but this local mismatch shows that a higher score
alone does not guarantee improved task capability. Whether later
versions preserve or extend existing capabilities must therefore be
assessed using both fixed-state diagnostics and complete-game outcomes.

In addition, the score remains unchanged from OB-6 to OB-7, while only
one move differs across the 7,234 replayed states. At the same time,
OB-6’s B pass rate (63.0%) still exceeds P0’s (48.7%). The simultaneous
occurrence of these independent state-level results and score stagnation
indicates that the later stagnation cannot be attributed solely to the
evaluation function; rather, the code changes barely entered the actual
decision path.

#### Set B is derived from DGM’s own games and may favor particular versions.

The direction of change on set A is consistent with that on set B: after
OB-3, the A pass rate decreases from 80.0% to 66.7%
(Table [D.1](#A4.T1 "Table D.1 ‣ Appendix D DGM Diagnostic Data ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")).

The order of checks in the frozen auto_player explains this outcome. Its
first branch checks the opponent’s immediate winning points and returns
as soon as one exists; the check for the agent’s own immediate win comes
second and is therefore never reached in a conflict state:

```ltx_verbatim
# 0. Check opponent immediate wins FIRST
opp_wins = detect_immediate_wins(board, opponent)
if opp_wins:
    row, col = opp_wins[0]
    write_move(...); return
# 1. Check my immediate win
my_wins = detect_immediate_wins(board, agent_color)
if my_wins:
    row, col = my_wins[0]
    write_move(...); return
```

Each branch is reasonable on its own: blocking is correct when only the
opponent can win, and completing five is correct when only the agent
can. The failure arises only when create_open_three drives the game into
states where both apply.

### E.2 Alternative Explanations

#### A single ineffective component cannot explain the failure.

Holding all other skills and the calling script fixed, removing
create_open_three still allows the system to form an open four in all 20
games and win all 20 games, whereas the full system wins 0/20 games.
This intervention changes the subsequent game trajectories, but that
trajectory change is part of the total effect of removing
create_open_three from the full system. The results show that, with
auto_player and the remaining skills retained, the system can still form
an open four and win. The call traces further show that prioritizing the
own-immediate-win branch in conflict states would also prevent the same
failure path. Thus, the problem is not that create_open_three or
auto_player fails to provide a positive contribution in isolation.
Rather, their combination uses a call order that does not correctly
handle conflict states.

## Appendix F Experimental Setup

All three mechanisms use DeepSeek-V4-Flash as the base model and evolve
through games against the same three programmatic opponents: rule-based,
threat-aware, and search_2. We do not intervene in the artifacts during
evolution, and we freeze them during diagnosis. The descriptions below
specify the implementations used in our experiments; they preserve the
core update paradigm of each mechanism.

Reflexion. Each opponent has a separate memory cell. After each game,
the system generates one textual lesson from the game feedback and
appends it to the corresponding cell. Each cell retains at most seven
memory entries. For diagnosis, we use the frozen state after the seventh
entry has been written. The outcome condition provides only the win/loss
result, whereas the trace condition also provides the complete move
sequence. At decision time, all entries in the current cell are included
in the move prompt, and the model directly outputs an action. Our
implementation does not retrieve, filter, or weight memories based on
the current state.

Voyager. The agent writes its experience as executable Python skill
files. The frozen configuration contains seven agent-written files:
seven skills and the calling script auto_player. Our diagnostic target
is the complete decision system comprising the skill files and
auto_player. Skills detect board features or propose candidate moves,
but do not directly output the final action. auto_player invokes the
skills in a fixed order and selects the move according to the branch
that fires. During evolution, the agent modifies existing skills or adds
new ones based on the previous round’s evaluation, then enters the next
round with the updated skill library.

DGM. At each round, the agent modifies the strategy source code to
produce a candidate version. Every version exposes a select_move(board,
player) interface, which the evaluator calls directly to obtain a move.
Each candidate plays $`5`$, $`10`$, and $`5`$ games against the
rule-based, threat-aware, and search_2 opponents, respectively. A
composite evaluation score determines whether the candidate is retained
and which version is used as the parent in the next round; candidates
that are not selected remain in the version archive. This process
produces a code lineage from the initial strategy P0 to later versions.

Lineage-level diagnosis uses OB-3 as the reference because it records
the largest number of wins against search_2 during evolution. The
evaluation score is computed programmatically and serves as a reference
signal for the agent’s retention decisions. For each candidate, the
score is computed from $`5`$, $`10`$, and $`5`$ games against
rule-based, threat-aware, and search_2, respectively:

|     |                        |                                                                                                                |     |     |
| --- | ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle S={}`$ | $`\displaystyle 50\,\mathrm{WR}_{\mathrm{RB}}+100\,\mathrm{WR}_{\mathrm{TA}}+150\,\mathrm{WR}_{\mathrm{SD2}}`$ |     | (1) |
|     |                        | $`\displaystyle+20\max_{o}B_{o}+\overline{L}_{\mathrm{SD2}}+2\sum_{o}F_{o},`$                                  |     | (2) |

where $`\mathrm{WR}`$ is the win rate against the corresponding
opponent, $`B_{o}`$ is the block rate against opponent $`o`$: among the
agent’s moves made when $`o`$ has an immediate winning point, the
fraction that occupy one of those points,
$`\overline{L}_{\mathrm{SD2}}`$ is the average game length (in moves)
against search*2, and $`F*{o}`$ is the number of open fours formed
against opponent $`o`$. The scores of the main-line versions are 150.4
for OB-1, 275.2 for OB-2, 347.0 for OB-3, 357.8 for OB-5, 312.6 for
OB-6, and 312.6 for OB-7. OB-5 scores higher than OB-3 despite winning
fewer games against search_2: the game-length term increases as games
are prolonged and offsets the decline in the win-rate term.

This appendix supplements the discussion with 20 related works;
Table [G.1](#A7.T1 "Table G.1 ‣ G.6 Design-Element Comparison ‣ Appendix G Extended Related Work ‣ Artifact-to-Capability: Diagnosing Breaks in Capability Formation of Self-Evolving Agents in a Controlled Environment")

### G.1 Process Evaluation and Version Comparison

- •
  BenchTrace ([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)) truncates a test snapshot
  before the target failure occurs, lets each framework and the ReAct
  baseline continue from the same prefix, and uses a rule-based detector
  to determine whether the failure is reproduced. It thereby measures
  failure-avoidance rate and also evaluates whether reflection can
  localize and diagnose failures. On Qwen3-32B and GPT-4.1, the
  end-to-end pass rate of reflection under this funnel-style, stage-wise
  evaluation is below 30%.
- •
  EvoAgentBench ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)) uses the accuracy gain
  over Vanilla for each “task $`\times`$ evolutionary state” cell and
  infers bottlenecks in experience encoding, routing, and adoption from
  the resulting gain patterns.
- •
  SEA-Eval ([Jiang et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)) measures token consumption
  and success rate at each position in task sequences of length 5, and
  distinguishes genuine evolution from pseudo-evolution according to
  whether the cost converges.
- •
  Huxley-Gödel Machine ([Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib26)) estimates the
  meta-productivity of nodes in a self-modifying tree using an aggregate
  of descendant performance (CMP). On SWE-Verified-60, its estimator has
  a weighted correlation of 0.778 with empirical CMP, compared with
  0.285 for DGM.
- •
  SAGE ([Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib27)) compares the incumbent skill
  with a candidate edit on the same validation set. It retains only
  items on which the two versions disagree and applies a one-sided exact
  conditional binomial test (equivalent to a one-sided exact McNemar
  test when $`\lambda=1`$) as a statistical admission criterion for the
  edit.

Our version-by-version comparison in the DGM experiment is similar to
SAGE in that both compare individual items in a fixed set. The
comparison target here is the rule-defined state-level pass rate.

### G.2 Memory and Reflection Artifacts

- •
  [Zhang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib32) compares different
  memory-integration strategies and finds that the utility of text
  memories continuously integrated by an LLM first increases and then
  decreases. On an ARC-AGI slice containing 19 problems that GPT-5.4
  solved completely without memory, accuracy still falls to 52.6% by the
  tenth round of streaming integration, even when the standard solution
  is provided at every step.
- •
  [Dixit et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14) analyzes publicly available
  Reflexion logs. In 16 ALFWorld environments with a reflection
  near-duplicate rate of at least 0.5, none of the 121 reflections
  mentions the correct object. Their no-memory control was run
  separately, with a different trial budget from the original logs.
- •
  ExpWeaver ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib34)) compares different timings
  for incorporating experience into decision making: not using it, using
  it only at initialization, using it at every step, and triggering it
  on demand. Its causal ablation either blanks out the retrieved content
  at the point where the agent triggers retrieval or randomly injects
  experience at the average trigger frequency. The outcome is measured
  by success over the entire trajectory. On Qwen3-32B, G-Memory, and
  ALFWorld, ExpWeaver achieves 83.33, compared with 75.37 when the
  retrieved content is blanked.
- •
  [Xiong et al. (2025)](#bib.bib29) finds that the more similar
  retrieved memories are to the current input, the more closely the
  agent’s output resembles the output recorded in memory. They also
  construct an “error-free” variant that keeps the retrieved examples
  unchanged but replaces the recorded execution with a correct
  execution, allowing them to compare the effect of memory-content
  quality.

These works measure memory effects at the task, trial, or trajectory
level. We instead determine the direction of memory-induced single-step
action changes on fixed states.

### G.3 Attributing the Effects of Skill and Code Artifacts

- •
  ASSAY ([Wang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib28)) randomly masks skills on
  software-development tasks and estimates each skill’s effect as the
  mean difference in binary task outcomes for each “skill $`\times`$
  task” pair. With GPT-5.1, 103 skills, and 15 development tasks, more
  than 90% of skills have a cross-task effect range greater than 0.40.
- •
  [Hu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib17) creates variants of a skill’s meaning,
  wording, name, and content in single-turn question answering and pairs
  them with a no-skill condition. It measures answer flips and the
  consistency of self-reported attribution. On mathematical tasks,
  attribution consistency does not exceed 0.43 across models and
  conditions.
- •
  [Dong et al. (2026)](#bib.bib15) compares full runs of the same task
  with and without a skill and manually annotates failure causes. Of 125
  functional failures, 68.8% are attributed to task implementation
  errors.
- •
  Counterfactual Trace Auditing ([Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib35)) uses
  dynamic time warping (DTW) to align stages in two independent
  trajectories, with and without a skill, and compares patterns of
  behavioral influence. The authors describe this comparison as
  descriptive rather than a causal estimand.
- •
  Skill Following ([Cho and Park, 2026](#bib.bib13)) computes the
  outcome difference between executions with and without a skill,
  restricted to tasks in which the agent actually retrieved the skill.
  On Math500, Llama-3.3-70B has an aggregate improvement of $`+14.2`$
  percentage points, whereas this metric is $`-39.4`$ percentage points.
- •
  Library Drift ([Zhang et al., 2026d](#bib.bib6)) enables or disables
  different skill-library governance mechanisms at the full-run level
  and tallies each skill’s successes and failures in injection trials.
- •
  [Pelleriti et al. (2026)](#bib.bib5) analyzes the types of code edits
  along an evolutionary lineage and replays saved generation prompts.
  About 30% of added lines exactly match lines removed earlier; after
  replaying 36 breakthrough events, the median score ratio is 0.76.

These works use tasks, single-turn answers, full runs, aligned stages,
or code edits as their paired or analytical units. Our unit of analysis
is the frozen system. We pair comparisons on fixed states within
multi-step games and trace how multiple components jointly affect the
system’s control flow.

### G.4 Failure Attribution

- •
  Who&When ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)) defines a decisive error
  step counterfactually: replacing the step with a correct action
  changes the outcome from failure to success. In practice, however,
  both human annotations and three LLM-based judgments are made by
  inspecting logs. On manually constructed systems, the Hybrid method
  achieves 12.28% step-level accuracy.
- •
  Agentic Attribution ([Qian et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)) fixes a target
  action, progressively reveals historical prefixes at the component
  level, and measures changes in the action’s likelihood. It also
  perturbs sentence-level context by deletion and retention to identify
  the components and sentences driving the action across eight
  trajectories.

We instead judge the task effect of each action with independent rules,
and our intervention target is the frozen artifact set.

### G.5 Games and Rule-Based Environments

- •
  GAMEBoT ([Lin et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)) decomposes each turn in eight
  games into two or three subproblems and checks intermediate reasoning
  against ground truth generated by rule-based algorithms. In its
  experiments, GPT-4o achieves the highest average intermediate score,
  0.52.
- •
  [Jung and Son (2026)](#bib.bib19) incrementally adds manually designed
  harness layers in noisy Battleship and measures win rate. Adding the
  second layer raises the win rate from 50.0% to 74.1%; this layer does
  not call an LLM.

We use rule-based verifiability in Gomoku to diagnose artifacts produced
through natural evolution.

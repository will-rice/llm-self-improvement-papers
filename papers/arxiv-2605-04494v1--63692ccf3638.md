###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has been popular for
aligning text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models with human preferences. As
a mainstream branch of RLHF, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) offers
a computationally efficient alternative that avoids explicit reward
modeling and has been widely adopted in diffusion alignment. However,
existing preference-based methods for diffusion alignment still rely on
reward-induced preference signals and typically assume that human
preferences can be adequately modeled by the Bradley–Terry (BT) model,
which may fail to capture the full complexity of human preferences. In
this paper, we formulate diffusion alignment from a game-theoretic
perspective. We propose Diffusion Nash Preference Optimization
(Diff.-NPO), an intuitive general preference framework for diffusion
alignment. Diff.-NPO encourages the current policy to play against
itself to achieve self-improvement and lead to a better alignment.
Empirically, we demonstrate the effectiveness of Diff.-NPO on the
text-to-image generation task via various metrics. Diff.-NPO
consistently outperforms existing preference-based diffusion alignment
methods.

## 1 Introduction

Diffusion models have become a dominant framework for text-to-image
generation due to their ability to produce high-fidelity images that are
well aligned with textual descriptions. Representative systems such as
Stable Diffusion \[[26](#bib.bib25), [31](#bib.bib24)\], GPT Image
2 \[[24](#bib.bib13)\], and DALL-E 2 \[[30](#bib.bib16)\] exemplify the
strong generative capability of this class of models. Although
large-scale pretraining on web data provides a powerful foundation, it
does not by itself guarantee outputs that adequately reflect human
preference. Drawing inspiration from alignment methods developed for
large language models, recent work has adapted preference-based
objectives such as DPO to diffusion models \[[29](#bib.bib15),
[17](#bib.bib3), [43](#bib.bib4)\] , enabling learning from preference
data without explicit reward modeling. By incorporating preference
signals directly into the optimization process, these methods have
demonstrated clear advantages over standard supervised
fine-tuning \[[35](#bib.bib49), [42](#bib.bib45)\].

Despite these advantages, most existing DPO-style diffusion alignment
methods are inherited from the RLHF paradigm, where preference learning
is often interpreted through an implicit reward induced by Bradley–Terry
\[[4](#bib.bib5)\] or Plackett–Luce (PL)\[[25](#bib.bib1)\] models. We
argue that the BT-based preference modeling fails to characterize the
complexity of human preferences. Human preferences can be intransitive
as in rock–paper–scissors: rock beats scissors, scissors beats paper,
and paper beats rock. Such cyclic preferences cannot be represented by a
single transitive ranking. In contrast, the BT model assumes
transitivity, implying that if A is preferred to B and B is preferred to
C, then A must be preferred to C. Therefore, this assumption may not
hold across diverse human groups and can contradict observed human
decision-making \[[22](#bib.bib8), [34](#bib.bib7)\]. These limitations
suggest that, rather than first converting human preference into a
reward-induced preference function by BT model, it may be preferable to
directly model and optimize the general preference relation itself.

Motivated by this observation, we propose Diff.-NPO, a diffusion
alignment method from the perspective of Nash Learning from Human
Feedback (NLHF)\[[23](#bib.bib14)\]. Instead of viewing alignment as the
maximization of a BT-induced likelihood function\[[35](#bib.bib49)\],
Diff.-NPO is derived from a minimax-style game in which one policy
attempts to generate samples preferred over those of another policy,
leading to the direct optimization of relative preference probabilities.
The quality of a response is characterized by its expected preference
win rate against samples drawn from the policy itself, as judged by the
preference function. Thus, responses are favored when they are more
likely to be preferred over the policy’s own typical outputs.
Concretely, Diff.-NPO can be interpreted as a game between the current
policy and the previous policy. The current policy is optimized to
increase the preference probability assigned to winning samples relative
to losing samples, while the previous policy serves as an evolving
opponent and the reference policy regularizes deviations of the current
policy from the pretrained distribution. A Nash equilibrium is reached
when both players adopt a policy $`\pi^{*}`$ such that no competing
policy can achieve a higher win rate over it.

Our objective brings three advantages. First, Diff.-NPO provides a
general preference perspective on preference alignment. Instead of
interpreting pairwise preferences through the combination of implicit
rewards and BT model, as in standard DPO-style derivations, Diff.-NPO
directly optimizes preference relations. Second, Diff.-NPO yields a
self-play alignment objective that connects DPO-style alignment and
self-play within a unified formulation through the regularization term.
In particular, different values of the regularization coefficient
interpolate between these two regimes. Third, Diff.-NPO makes the
modeling assumptions behind preference optimization explicit. Under our
formulation, the objective can be interpreted as fitting a
preference-induced target distribution constructed from three
components: the reference policy, the previous policy, and the
preference signal. The regularization term determines how much this
target distribution relies on the reference policy versus the previous
policy.

To evaluate the effectiveness of our method, we train Stable Diffusion
1.5 and SDXL on Pick-a-Pic \[[14](#bib.bib12)\], a large-scale dataset
of real-world human preferences over image pairs. Empirical results show
that Diff.-NPO consistently improves alignment quality over existing
preference-optimization baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of
on-policy Nash-style learning for diffusion model alignment. Our main
contributions are as follows:

- •
  We bridge the gap between NLHF and diffusion model alignment by
  formulating diffusion alignment as a general preference game that
  avoids the Bradley–Terry assumption, thereby establishing a foundation
  for future research on Nash-style learning for diffusion models.
- •
  We propose Diff.-NPO, a novel on-policy general preference
  optimization algorithm for diffusion models. Our method unifies
  standard DPO and self-play paradigms, offering a general perspective
  for existing on-policy preference optimization methods.
- •
  We empirically validate the effectiveness of Diff.-NPO on
  text-to-image tasks. Diff.-NPO achieves stronger alignment with human
  preferences than existing baselines.

## 2 Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the basic background on diffusion models
and Nash learning that will be used in our method, and defer a broader
discussion of related work to
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Related Work ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium").

### 2.1 Diffusion Models

Diffusion models learn a data distribution by reversing a gradual
noising process. The forward process starts from clean data
$`\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}`$ and produces a sequence
$`\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}`$ by adding Gaussian noise with a
schedule $`\{\beta_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}`$ such that $`0\leq\beta_{t}\leq 1`$.
Let $`\alpha_{t}:=1-\beta_{t}`$ and
$`\bar{\alpha}_{t}:=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}`$. A common forward
transition is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{t-1},(1-\alpha_{t})I\bigr).
``` |  | (1) |

The reverse (denoising) process is parameterized by a neural network
$`\epsilon_{\theta}`$ that predicts the noise component given a noisy
sample $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ at timestep $`t`$. Instead directly optimizing
over $`p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})`$, the diffusion model is trained to
maximize Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):

[TABLE]

which can be reduced to minimizing the denoising matching loss,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\arg\min_{\theta}\mathrm{KL}\bigl(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{0})\,\|\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t})\bigr)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\theta}\frac{(1-\alpha_{t})^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}\alpha_{t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})}\,\mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_{0},\epsilon}\Big[\,\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\|_{2}^{2}\Big].`$ |  |

where $`t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)`$, and
$`\sigma_{t}^{2}=\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\beta_{t}`$,
$`\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)`$ is the noise predicted by the
diffusion model at timestep $`t`$, and $`\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$
is the Gaussian noise added to the image.

### 2.2 Diffusion Model Alignment with Human Feedback

A common approach for aligning human preference for diffusion models is
through DPO. Given a prompt $`\mathbf{c}\sim d_{0}`$ and a clean image
$`\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})`$ generated by the
diffusion model, the standard RLHF objective can be written as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\min_{p_{\theta}}-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})}r(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}_{0})+\beta\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})||p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})),`$ |  | (3) |

where $`p_{\theta}`$ and $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ are probability
distributions of the diffusion model and reference model, and $`\beta`$
controls the KL-regularization. From ELBO trick in
Equation [2](#S2.E2 "In 2.1 Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium")
and re-parameterization, the reward is calculated as,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle r(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}_{0})=\beta\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{c})}\left[\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}\right]+\beta\log Z(\mathbf{c}),`$ |  | (4) |

where $`Z(\mathbf{c})`$ is the partition function. With BT model
assumption and approximation of the reverse process,  [Wallace et al.
\[35\]](#bib.bib49) get rid of summing over probability from the whole
chain and derive the objective below,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Diff-DPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}^{+},\mathbf{x}^{-})\sim\mathcal{D},t}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\beta\,T\,\omega(\lambda_{t})\,(\delta_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}^{+},t)-\delta_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}^{-},t))\big)\Big],
``` |  |

where
$`\delta_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}_{t},t):=-\Big(\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c},t)\|_{2}^{2}-\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c},t)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)`$
can be seen as implicit reward, and $`T`$ is the number of diffusion
steps and $`\omega(\lambda_{t})`$ reweights timestep contributions.

### 2.3 Nash Learning with Human Feedback

Many preference alignment methods rely on parametric preference models,
such as Bradley–Terry, that represent pairwise preferences as
differences in an underlying reward function\[[1](#bib.bib40),
[11](#bib.bib39), [39](#bib.bib50)\]. A key limitation is that such
models impose transitivity and therefore cannot faithfully represent
intransitive preferences that can arise in practice. Instead, we
directly work with the general preference probability
$`\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})`$,
and cast policy optimization as a two-player zero-sum game.

Given a prompt distribution $`d_{0}`$ and a reference policy
$`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$, for two policies $`p_{1}`$ (max-player) and
$`p_{2}`$ (min-player) we define

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle J(p_{1},p_{2})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{c}\sim d_{0}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{1}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c}),\,\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\sim p_{2}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})\big]`$ |  | (5) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\tau\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p_{1}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})\big)+\tau\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p_{2}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})\big)\Big].`$ |  |  |

where $`\tau>0`$ controls how strongly both players are regularized
toward $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ via KL regularization. Intuitively,
$`p_{1}`$ tries to increase its win-rate against $`p_{2}`$ while staying
close to the reference, and $`p_{2}`$ plays the adversary.

#### 2.3.1 Nash Policy and Duality Gap

We restrict attention to a policy class $`\mathcal{P}`$ (e.g., policies
with the same support as $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$). A Nash equilibrium is
defined by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{1}^{*},p_{2}^{*}\;:=\;\arg\max_{p_{1}\in\mathcal{P}}\;\arg\min_{p_{2}\in\mathcal{P}}\;J(p_{1},p_{2}).
``` |  | (6) |

In the symmetric setting, the two equilibrium policies coincide, and we
denote the unique Nash policy by $`p^{*}`$. For any candidate policy
$`p\in\mathcal{P}`$, we measure how close it is to equilibrium via the
duality gap

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{DualGap}(p)\;:=\;\max_{p_{1}\in\mathcal{P}}J(p_{1},p)\;-\min_{p_{2}\in\mathcal{P}}J(p,p_{2}).
``` |  | (7) |

By construction, $`\mathrm{DualGap}(p)\geq 0`$ and
$`\mathrm{DualGap}(p)=0`$ if and only if $`p`$ is a Nash policy. When
$`\mathrm{DualGap}(p)\leq\varepsilon`$, we call $`p`$ an
$`\varepsilon`$-approximate Nash policy.

## 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models

### 3.1 Overview

Our method draws inspiration from Iterative Nash Policy Optimization
(INPO)\[[51](#bib.bib20)\], but replaces its fixed-margin regression
loss with a pairwise logistic loss that encourages the model to assign a
higher trajectory likelihood to the preferred image than to the rejected
image. To make the Nash framework suitable for diffusion alignment, we
follow the online mirror descent principle used in prior Nash-style
alignment methods and derive a self-play objective tailored to diffusion
models. We denote the policy after $`s`$ training steps by $`p_{s}`$.

### 3.2 Online Mirror Descent for Nash Policy

Given the preference probability
$`\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})`$,
define the loss at step $`s`$ as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\ell_{s}(p)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{c}\sim d_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c}),\,\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\sim p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})\big]+\tau\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})\big)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{c}\sim d_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\sim p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c}),\,\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\sim p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})\big]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\tau\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\big).`$ |  | (8) |

Here we lift the image-level preference oracle to the reverse trajectory
space by assuming that preferences depend only on the final samples. The
loss function corresponds to the game objective of the max-player with
the min-player fixed to the current policy $`p_{s}`$. It consists of two
parts: the negative win rate of $`p`$ against $`p_{s}`$ and a KL penalty
term that keeps $`p`$ close to the reference policy
$`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$. A natural self-play strategy is to take the best
response

|     |                                                  |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       p_{s+1}=\arg\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\,\ell_{s}(p),
       ```                                               |     | (9) |

that is, the best response to $`p_{s}`$. However, this greedy update can
be unstable: the new policy $`p_{s+1}`$ may deviate significantly from
$`p_{s}`$, and such instability can lead to undesirable linear
regret \[[15](#bib.bib11)\]. To obtain a more stable update, we instead
adopt optimistic mirror descent with entropy regularization, also known
as Hedge \[[8](#bib.bib6)\], and define $`p_{s+1}`$ as the minimizer of

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\arg\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\Big\langle\nabla\ell_{s}(p_{s}),\,p\Big\rangle+\eta\,\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,p_{s}),
``` |  | (10) |

where $`\eta>0`$ and

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}\ell_{s}(p_{s})=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\sim p_{s}}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime})\big]+\tau\left(\log\frac{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}+1\right).
``` |  | (11) |

Compared with the greedy update, this objective introduces an additional
KL penalty that keeps $`p_{s+1}`$ close to $`p_{s}`$, thereby
encouraging a more stable learning dynamic.

### 3.3 Nash Preference Optimizaton

A closed-form solution of the update implied by the OMD objective can be
written in exponential-weights form; the full derivation is provided in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Mathematical Analysis ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium").

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{s+1}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\propto\exp\Big(\tfrac{1}{\eta}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\succ p_{s}\mid\mathbf{c})\Big)\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})^{\tfrac{\tau}{\eta}}\,p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})^{1-\tfrac{\tau}{\eta}},
``` |  | (12) |

where
$`\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\succ p_{s}\mid\mathbf{c})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\sim p_{s}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})}[\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})]`$.
Directly computing the normalization is intractable, so we optimize
pairwise log-ratios. For a response pair
$`(\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime})`$, define
$`h_{s}(p;\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime})`$ as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\tau}{\eta}\left(\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})}\right)+\frac{\eta-\tau}{\eta}\left(\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})}-\log\frac{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})}\right).
``` |  | (13) |

At optimum,
$`h_{s}(p_{s+1};\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime})`$
should equal the preference-margin difference:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h_{s}(p_{s+1};\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime})=\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\succ p_{s}\mid\mathbf{c})-\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\succ p_{s}\mid\mathbf{c})}{\eta}.
``` |  | (14) |

Equation ([14](#S3.E14 "In 3.3 Nash Preference Optimizaton ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium"))
shows that, at the OMD-induced optimum, $`h_{s}`$ should match the
relative preference advantage between two trajectories against the same
preference oracle. However, this target is difficult to compute exactly,
as it requires estimating the expected preference of each trajectory
against samples drawn from $`p_{s}`$. We therefore use observed
preferred–rejected pairs as supervision and optimize a pairwise logistic
loss based on $`h_{s}`$. This loss encourages
$`h_{s}(p;\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime})`$ to
have the correct preference direction, serving as a tractable surrogate
for matching the ideal preference-advantage difference in Equation (14).
Based on above observation, we define the loss function $`L_{s}(p)`$ as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle L_{s}(p):={}\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{c}\sim d_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\sim p_{s}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c})}\Big[-`$ | $`\displaystyle\log\sigma\Big(\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})}\Big)`$ |  | (15) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})}-\log\frac{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})}\Big)\Big)\Big],`$ |  |  |

where $`0\leq\tau/\eta\leq 1`$. This objective function further
clarifies how our method differs from Diffusion-DPO. Whereas
Diffusion-DPO is designed for the offline setting and mainly contrasts
the current policy with the reference policy $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$, our
method is inherently online and follows a self-play scheme in which each
update is evaluated against samples generated by the current policy
itself. Under this view, the next policy $`p_{s+1}`$ should stay close
to both $`p_{s}`$ and $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$, although these two
regularization effects serve different roles: proximity to $`p_{s}`$
promotes stable online optimization, while proximity to
$`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ acts as an anchor that mitigates reward hacking.

In the above derivation,
$`(\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{0:T})`$ denotes a generic pair
of trajectories sampled from the previous policy. In practice, after
sampling such a pair, the preference oracle is used to determine which
trajectory is preferred. We therefore relabel the preferred trajectory
as $`\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T}`$ and the rejected trajectory as
$`\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T}`$. For diffusion models, we identify the generic
policy $`p`$ with the trainable reverse process $`p_{\theta}`$, and
write the previous policy from last step as $`p_{\mathrm{prev}}`$.
Following the standard diffusion-DPO reduction from full-chain
likelihoods to per-step reverse transitions, we obtain the final
single-timestep objective, with details deferred to
Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Derivation of Final Objective (Equation ) ‣ Appendix C Mathematical Analysis ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium"):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{Diff.\mbox{-}NPO}}=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{c},t,\mathbf{x}_{0}^{+},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{-}\sim p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c}),\mathbf{x}_{t}^{+},\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{+},\mathbf{x}_{t}^{-},\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{-}}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{+}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}^{+},\mathbf{c})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t},\mathbf{c})}`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle-`$ | $`\displaystyle\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{+}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}^{+},\mathbf{c})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t},\mathbf{c})}-\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{+}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}^{+},\mathbf{c})}{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t},\mathbf{c})}\Big)\Big],`$ |  | (16) |

where $`t`$ is the time step sampled from $`\mathcal{U}(0,T)`$. Using
Gaussian reparameterization of the reverse process, the objective can be
further written as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{Diff.\mbox{-}NPO}}=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{c},t,\mathbf{x}_{0}^{+},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{-}\sim p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c}),\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\{+,-\}}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\{+,-\}}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\{+,-\}})}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(\beta T\omega(\lambda_{t})\big[\delta_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t},\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\tau/\eta\delta_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t},\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})-(\eta-\tau)/\eta\delta_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t},\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})\big]\Big)\Big],`$ |  |

where $`\delta_{\theta}`$ is defined as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\delta_{\theta}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t},\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})=-\left[\|\epsilon^{+}-\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{+},t,\mathbf{c})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\epsilon^{-}-\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{-},t,\mathbf{c})\|_{2}^{2}\right],`$ |  |

and $`\delta_{\mathrm{prev}}`$ and $`\delta_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ are defined
analogously by replacing $`\epsilon_{\theta}`$ with
$`\epsilon_{\mathrm{prev}}`$ and $`\epsilon_{\mathrm{ref}}`$,
respectively. The full algorithm for Diff.-NPO can be found in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Appendix A Diff.-NPO Pseudo-code ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium")
in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Diff.-NPO Pseudo-code ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium").

### 3.4 Theoretical Insights

In this section, we provide an interpretation of our objective through a
preference-induced conditional distribution. Inspired by the form of
guidance-based methods, we consider the following target distribution:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\propto p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})^{\gamma}p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})^{1-\gamma}p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{c})^{1/\beta},`$ |  | (17) |

where $`\theta^{*}`$ denotes the ideal target policy induced by the
conditional distribution
$`p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{c})`$, and $`\mathbf{y}`$ is
a latent variable representing the preference outcome. For simplicity we
set $`\gamma=\tau/\eta`$. The term
$`p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{c})`$ captures how likely a
sample is preferred under the oracle. Since directly accessing
$`p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{c})`$ is intractable, we
instead optimize relative preference likelihoods. Connecting this
assumption with our training objective, we obtain:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Diff.\mbox{-}NPO}}=`$ | $`\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{+},\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\Bigg[-\log\sigma\Bigg(\beta\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{+}|\mathbf{c})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{-}|\mathbf{c})}-\beta\Bigg[\gamma\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{+}|\mathbf{c})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{-}|\mathbf{c})}+(1-\gamma)\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{+}|\mathbf{c})}{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{-}|\mathbf{c})}\Bigg]\Bigg)\Bigg].`$ |  |

By absorbing normalization constants into the definition of
$`p(\mathbf{y}\mid\cdot)`$, we can express the objective as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Diff.\mbox{-}NPO}}=-\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{+},\mathbf{c})-\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{-},\mathbf{c})\big)\Big].`$ |  | (18) |

##### A Unified View of Preference Optimization.

Equation [18](#S3.E18 "In 3.4 Theoretical Insights ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium")
reveals that a wide class of preference optimization methods—including
DPO, self-play methods, and our approach—can be viewed as optimizing the
same fundamental pairwise logistic objective. The objective encourages
the model to assign a larger preference-induced likelihood to the
preferred sample than to the dispreferred sample. This view also
provides a useful interpretation of $`\beta`$. In standard RLHF,
$`\beta`$ is usually introduced as the coefficient of the KL
regularization term. Under our formulation, $`\beta`$ can be interpreted
as controlling the strength of the preference-induced conditional
distribution in
Equation [17](#S3.E17 "In 3.4 Theoretical Insights ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium").

The key distinction among these methods therefore lies not only in the
loss form, but in how the implicit target distribution is modeled. When
$`\gamma=1`$,
Equation [17](#S3.E17 "In 3.4 Theoretical Insights ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium")
reduces to

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\propto p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{c})^{1/\beta},
``` |  |

which corresponds to the Diffusion-DPO-style assumption: the target
distribution is obtained by preference-conditioning the reference
policy. While this provides a stable anchor, it may limit learning
because all improvement is measured relative to a fixed reference model.
When $`\gamma=0`$,
Equation [17](#S3.E17 "In 3.4 Theoretical Insights ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium")
instead becomes

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\propto p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0:T},\mathbf{c})^{1/\beta},
``` |  |

which corresponds to a pure self-play update. This forces each iteration
to move beyond the previous policy and can provide a stronger online
learning signal. However, without the reference policy as an anchor, the
model may drift away from the pretrained distribution and become
unstable. Our objective interpolates between these two extremes. The
reference policy provides a stable distributional anchor, while the
previous policy introduces an online self-improvement signal. As a
result, the model is not restricted to merely improving over the fixed
reference, nor is it forced into unconstrained self-play at every step.
This interpolation mitigates the two-sided failure mode of pure DPO and
pure self-play, yielding a more stable and expressive preference-induced
target distribution.

##### Why Square Loss in INPO is Unstable.

INPO-style objectives \[[51](#bib.bib20)\] justify the squared loss
under a specific population assumption: after sampling a pair
$`\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim p_{s}`$, the ordered pair
$`(\mathbf{x}^{+},\mathbf{x}^{-})`$ is drawn from the preference
distribution $`\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})`$, i.e.,
$`(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})`$ is selected with probability
$`\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbf{x}^{\prime})`$ and
$`(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x})`$ with probability
1-$`\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}\succ\mathbf{x}^{\prime})`$. However, in most
practical implementations, ordered preference pairs are constructed by
querying an oracle or a reward model on the two samples and assigning
the higher-scored sample as $`\mathbf{x}^{+}`$ and the lower-scored
sample as $`\mathbf{x}^{-}`$. Thus, the observed pair is
deterministically ordered by the oracle score, rather than sampled
according to the probabilistic preference distribution $`\lambda_{p}`$.
In our online diffusion setting, this discrepancy is more explicit: we
generate multiple candidate images, rank them using the preference
oracle, and select the best-ranked image as $`\mathbf{x}^{+}`$ and the
worst-ranked image as $`\mathbf{x}^{-}`$. Since these ordered pairs do
not follow the preference distribution assumption, directly applying the
squared surrogate becomes less natural for our data construction. We
empirically validate this behavior in
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Ablation Studies ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium").

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experiment Setup

##### Models, datasets, baselines, and evaluation metrics.

We fine-tune two text-to-image diffusion backbones, Stable Diffusion 1.5
(SD1.5) and SDXL. The prompts of training data are drawn from the
Pick-a-Pic v1 preference dataset \[[14](#bib.bib12)\]. We use prompts
from the Pick-a-Pic test split \[[14](#bib.bib12)\],
Parti-Prompts \[[46](#bib.bib51)\], and HPSV2 \[[38](#bib.bib9)\] for
testing. We compare against several representative baselines, including
online SFT trained on preferred images, online
Diffusion-DPO \[[35](#bib.bib49)\], SPIN \[[47](#bib.bib46)\] and
SEPPO \[[49](#bib.bib47)\], together with the original pretrained SD1.5
and SDXL models. We use the public available checkpoints for SPIN¹¹ 1
https://huggingface.co/UCLA-AGI/SPIN-Diffusion-iter3 and SEPPO²² 2
https://huggingface.co/DwanZhang/SePPO for evaluation. For text-to-image
alignment task, we adopt five widely used automatic metrics:
PickScore \[[14](#bib.bib12)\], HPSV2 \[[38](#bib.bib9)\],
CLIP \[[28](#bib.bib52)\], Aesthetic Score \[[32](#bib.bib54)\], and
ImageReward \[[40](#bib.bib53)\], and compare the metric scores and
winrates among different methods. More details are provided in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Additional Experimental Details ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium").

##### Online Generation and Preference Oracle.

During online generation, we use 10 inference steps for efficiency,
following prior work showing that a small number of denoising steps has
little impact on optimization performance \[[52](#bib.bib18),
[21](#bib.bib17)\]. For each prompt, we generate 8 candidate images and
use automatic preference oracles to construct preference pairs.
Specifically, we evaluate each candidate using five scoring models:
PickScore \[[14](#bib.bib12)\], HPSV2 \[[38](#bib.bib9)\],
CLIP \[[28](#bib.bib52)\], Aesthetic Score \[[32](#bib.bib54)\], and
ImageReward \[[40](#bib.bib53)\]. For each oracle, we rank the 8
candidates according to their scores, and then compute the average rank
across the five oracles as the final preference score. The candidate
with the best average rank is selected as the positive sample, while the
candidate with the worst average rank is selected as the negative
sample.

objective only regularizes toward the reference policy and reduces to
the DPO-style setting; when $`\tau/\eta=0`$, the objective only
regularizes toward the previous policy, corresponding to a pure
self-play update.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed Diffusion Nash Preference Optimization
(Diff.-NPO), a general preference optimization framework for diffusion
models derived from a nash learning perspective. By directly optimizing
relative preference probabilities through a self-play-style objective
with reference-policy regularization, Diff.-NPO provides a simple and
stable approach to online diffusion alignment. Experiments on Stable
Diffusion 1.5 and SDXL across Pick-a-Pic, Parti-Prompts, and HPSV2 show
that Diff.-NPO consistently improves alignment over strong
preference-optimization baselines. These results suggest that Nash-style
online learning is a promising alternative to reward-centric
formulations for diffusion-model alignment. One limitation of the
current study is that our average-rank preference construction relies on
five scoring models, and incorporating a broader set of scoring models
may provide a more comprehensive preference signal. In future work, we
plan to extend this framework to stronger online data-collection
strategies, and broader diffusion generation and editing tasks.

## Appendix A Diff.-NPO Pseudo-code

The Pseudo-code of Diff.-NPO is shown in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Appendix A Diff.-NPO Pseudo-code ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium").

1: Input: steps $`S`$, KL regularization $`\tau`$, OMD parameter
$`\eta`$, reference policy $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$, prompts
$`\mathbf{c}\sim d_{0}`$

2: Initialize $`p_{1}\leftarrow p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$

3: for $`s=1,2,\dots,S`$ do

4:   Sample a minibatch of prompts
$`\{\mathbf{c}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim d_{0}`$

5:   For each $`\mathbf{c}^{(i)}`$, draw a batch of size $`k`$, denoted
by
$`(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}^{(i)})\sim p_{s}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{c}^{(i)})`$

6:   Query the average ranking oracle to obtain scores
$`(r_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,r_{k}^{(i)})`$ for
$`(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}^{(i)})`$

7:   Rank the samples by their oracle scores and set

8:
   $`\mathbf{x}^{+,(i)}\leftarrow\arg\max_{\mathbf{x}_{j}^{(i)}}r_{j}^{(i)}`$
and
$`\mathbf{x}^{-,(i)}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}_{j}^{(i)}}r_{j}^{(i)}`$

9:   Form the preference pairs
$`\{(\mathbf{c}^{(i)},\mathbf{x}^{+,(i)},\mathbf{x}^{-,(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}`$

10:   Update the diffusion policy by minimizing the loss with objective
as
equation [16](#S3.Ex5 "In 3.3 Nash Preference Optimizaton ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium"):

11:
   $`\displaystyle p_{s+1}\leftarrow\arg\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\;L_{\mathrm{Diff.\mbox{-}NPO}}`$

12:   Implement soft update for the target policy:

13:
   $`\displaystyle p_{s+1}\leftarrow\lambda p_{s}+(1-\lambda)p_{s+1}`$
where $`\lambda`$ = $`\min(0.001*s,0.5)`$

14: end for

15: Output: $`p_{S+1}`$

## Appendix B Related Work

### B.1 Nash Learning with Human Feedback

In the Nash learning with human feedback paradigm, a well-aligned policy
is viewed as an optimal strategy that cannot be exploited by competing
policies, thereby achieving strategic optimality rather than mere
mediocrity. NLHF\[[23](#bib.bib14)\] first introduced this
game-theoretic perspective by formulating preference alignment as
regularized Nash equilibrium learning under general, potentially
non-transitive preferences. Subsequent work has explored this paradigm
through no-regret learning \[[51](#bib.bib20)\], optimistic mirror
descent \[[50](#bib.bib21)\], and extragradient
updates\[[53](#bib.bib22)\], thereby advancing both theoretical
guarantees and empirical stability relative to traditional RLHF. More
recently, Multiplayer Nash Preference Optimization
(MNPO)\[[37](#bib.bib23)\] extends this line of work from two-player
interactions to the multiplayer setting, enabling the modeling of richer
preference structures.

### B.2 Diffusion Model Alignment

Diffusion models \[[31](#bib.bib24), [26](#bib.bib25)\] have achieved
remarkable success in T2I generation. Recently, alignment methods
inspired by RLHF have been increasingly adopted for diffusion models,
both in direct fine-tuning \[[6](#bib.bib26), [2](#bib.bib27),
[5](#bib.bib28), [27](#bib.bib29), [7](#bib.bib30), [3](#bib.bib31)\]
and at inference time \[[33](#bib.bib32), [44](#bib.bib33),
[13](#bib.bib34), [48](#bib.bib35), [12](#bib.bib2)\], with the goal of
improving downstream reward-related objectives \[[21](#bib.bib17),
[35](#bib.bib49), [41](#bib.bib36)\]. Existing diffusion alignment
methods can be broadly grouped into three lines: (1) methods that
optimize explicit reward functions, e.g., GRPO-based
approaches \[[21](#bib.bib17), [41](#bib.bib36), [19](#bib.bib37),
[18](#bib.bib38)\]; (2) methods that optimize implicit rewards induced
from pairwise preferences, such as Diffusion-DPO \[[35](#bib.bib49),
[20](#bib.bib41), [36](#bib.bib42), [16](#bib.bib43), [9](#bib.bib44),
[42](#bib.bib45)\]; and (3) likelihood-free methods that optimize
forward scores and velocity fields directly \[[54](#bib.bib19),
[52](#bib.bib18)\]. Despite their differences, most of these methods
primarily focus on reward maximization, whereas only a few exploit
on-policy self-play. In particular,  [Yang et al. \[42\]](#bib.bib45)
extends DPO to diffusion models by formulating denoising as a multi-step
MDP and directly optimizing human preferences over sampled image pairs.
 [Yuan et al. \[47\]](#bib.bib46) introduces a self-play fine-tuning
scheme in which the current model learns to outperform samples generated
by earlier checkpoints.  [Zhang et al. \[49\]](#bib.bib47) reduces
reliance on human-labeled losing images by replacing them with reference
samples generated from previous checkpoints.  [He et al.
\[10\]](#bib.bib48) further constructs synthetic preference pairs by
self-ranking on-policy generated images and then applies DPO-style
optimization. In our work, we build on nash learning \[[51](#bib.bib20),
[37](#bib.bib23)\] and develop a more comprehensive game-theoretic
self-play framework for diffusion model alignment.

## Appendix C Mathematical Analysis

### C.1 Derivation of the OMD Update for Nash Policy

Given the preference probability
$`\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})`$,
define the loss at step $`s`$ as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\ell_{s}(p)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{c}\sim d_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c}),\,\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\sim p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})\big]+\tau\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})\big)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{c}\sim d_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c}),\,\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\sim p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})\big]+\tau\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\big)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{c}\sim d_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\sim p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c}),\,\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\sim p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\succ\mathbf{x}_{0:T}^{\prime}\mid\mathbf{c})\big]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\hskip 200.0003pt+\tau\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\big),`$ |  |

Since
$`\mathrm{KL}\big(p(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{c})\big)`$
is not directly tractable, we work on its upper bound
$`\mathrm{KL}\big(p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\,\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\big)`$,
which lower-bounds the objective. Starting from the entropy-regularized
online mirror descent update,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{s+1}=\arg\min_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\Big\langle\nabla\ell_{s}(p_{s}),p\Big\rangle+\eta\,\mathrm{KL}(p\|p_{s}),
``` |  |

The objective can be written (for a fixed prompt $`\mathbf{c}`$) as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\min_{p}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}}p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\,\nabla_{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}\ell_{s}(p_{s})+\eta\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}}p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})},`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}}p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})=1.`$ |  |

Introducing a Lagrange multiplier $`\lambda`$ for the normalization
constraint, the Lagrangian is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(p,\lambda)=\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}}p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\,\nabla_{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}\ell_{s}(p_{s})+\eta\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}}`$ | $`\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\lambda\Big(\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{0:T}}p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})-1\Big).`$ |  |

When the derivative of $`\mathcal{L}(p,\lambda)`$ with respect to
$`p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})`$ goes to zero, we get

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log\tfrac{p_{s+1}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}=-\tfrac{1}{\eta}\nabla_{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}\ell_{s}(p_{s})-1-\tfrac{\lambda}{\eta},
``` |  |

after exponentiating the left and right sides:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{s+1}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})`$ | $`\displaystyle\propto p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\exp\Big(-\tfrac{1}{\eta}\nabla_{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}\ell_{s}(p_{s})\Big),`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\propto p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\exp\Big(\tfrac{1}{\eta}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\succ p_{s}\mid\mathbf{c})\Big)\Big(\tfrac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}{p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})}\Big)^{\tfrac{\tau}{\eta}},`$ |  |

which can be collected as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{s+1}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})\propto\exp\Big(\tfrac{1}{\eta}\,\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\succ p_{s}\mid\mathbf{c})\Big)\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})^{\tfrac{\tau}{\eta}}\,p_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})^{1-\tfrac{\tau}{\eta}}.
``` |  |

### C.2 Derivation of Final Objective (Equation [16](#S3.Ex5 "In 3.3 Nash Preference Optimizaton ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium"))

To instantiate the full-chain policy objective in
Equation [15](#S3.E15 "In 3.3 Nash Preference Optimizaton ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium")
for diffusion models, we identify the generic policy notation with the
corresponding diffusion reverse process. Specifically, the current
policy $`\pi`$ is parameterized by the trainable diffusion model
$`p_{\theta}`$, the previous policy $`\pi_{s}`$ is denoted by
$`p_{\mathrm{prev}}`$, and the reference policy $`\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}`$
is denoted by $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$. Under the Markov structure of the
diffusion reverse process, the full-chain likelihood decomposes as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{c})=p(\mathbf{x}_{T})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c}),
``` |  |

and similarly for $`p_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ and $`p_{\mathrm{prev}}`$.
Therefore, the log-ratio over full trajectories in
Equation [15](#S3.E15 "In 3.3 Nash Preference Optimizaton ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium")
can be decomposed into a sum of per-step reverse-transition log-ratios.
Following the standard diffusion-DPO approximation, we sample a timestep
$`t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,T)`$, replace the intractable reverse-chain
sampling with the forward noising process
$`q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})`$, and optimize the resulting
single-timestep objective with the factor $`T`$ accounting for uniform
timestep sampling.

We start with
Equation [15](#S3.E15 "In 3.3 Nash Preference Optimizaton ‣ 3 NLHF for Diffusion Models ‣ Towards General Preference Alignment: Diffusion Models at Nash Equilibrium"),
the objective on reverse decomposition for current, reference, and
previous policies,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T},\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T}\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot)}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{\pi(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{\pi(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}-\log\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}\Big)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{\pi(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{\pi(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}-\log\frac{\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{\pi_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}\Big)\Big)\Big]`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0}),\mathbf{x}^{-}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0})}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}\Big)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}\Big)\Big)\Big],`$ |  |

where we use $`q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})`$ to approximate
$`p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0})`$ for efficient
training. The objective hence becomes,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle L=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0}),\mathbf{x}^{-}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0})}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}\Big)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0:T})}{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0:T})}\Big)\Big)\Big]`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0}),\mathbf{x}^{-}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0})}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}\Big)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}\Big)\Big)\Big]`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0}),\mathbf{x}^{-}_{1:T}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0})}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(T\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}\Big)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}\Big)\Big]\Big)\Big]`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t,t-1}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t,t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0}),\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t,t-1}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t,t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0})}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(T\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}\Big)+\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}\Big)\Big]\Big)\Big].`$ |  |

By applying Jensen’s inequality, we can lower bound the objective as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle L`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,\,\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0}),\\
\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0})\end{subarray}}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(T\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t},\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0}),\\
\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t},\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0})\end{subarray}}\Big[\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}\Big)+\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\Big(\log\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}-\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t})}{p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t})}\Big)\Big]\Big)\Big]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,\,\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}^{+}_{0}),\\
\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t}\sim q(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}^{-}_{0})\end{subarray}}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(-T\Big[\frac{\tau\beta}{\eta}\Big(\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{+}|\mathbf{x}_{0,t}^{+})\|\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t}))`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{-}|\mathbf{x}_{0,t}^{-})\|\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t}))\Big.`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\Big.-\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{+}|\mathbf{x}_{0,t}^{+})\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t}))+\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{-}|\mathbf{x}_{0,t}^{-})\|\,p_{\mathrm{ref}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t}))\Big)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{(\eta-\tau)\beta}{\eta}\Big(\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{+}|\mathbf{x}_{0,t}^{+})\|\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t}))-\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{-}|\mathbf{x}_{0,t}^{-})\|\,p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t}))\Big.`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\Big.-\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{+}|\mathbf{x}_{0,t}^{+})\|\,p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{+}_{t}))+\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}^{-}|\mathbf{x}_{0,t}^{-})\|\,p_{\mathrm{prev}}(\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}^{-}_{t}))\Big)\Big]\Big)\Big].`$ |  |

Since the distribution of reverse step $`t`$ of diffusion models
$`p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{0})`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{t-1}}{\alpha_{t}}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\epsilon(\mathbf{x}_{t})\right),\sigma_{t}^{2}I),`$ |  |

we can parameterize the loss as

|  |  |  |  |

## Appendix D Additional Experimental Details

### D.1 Datasets

##### Pick-a-Pic.

Pick-a-Pic is a large-scale public dataset of human preferences for
text-to-image generation \[[14](#bib.bib12)\] with over 500,000
examples. It is collected through a web interface in which users provide
prompts, compare multiple generated images, and indicate which image
they prefer, or whether the comparison results in a tie. Each example
therefore consists of a prompt, two generated images, and a preference
label. We use Pick-a-Pic v1 for training and its held-out test split for
evaluation.

##### PartiPrompts.

PartiPrompts is a curated benchmark designed to evaluate the
compositional and semantic capabilities of text-to-image
models \[[45](#bib.bib10)\]. It contains prompts covering a broad range
of visual concepts, including objects, attributes, styles, and complex
relational descriptions. Owing to this diversity, PartiPrompts has
become a standard benchmark for assessing prompt following,
compositional generation, and overall image quality. In our experiments,
we use PartiPrompts as a standardized evaluation suite for text-to-image
generation.

##### HPS v2.

HPS v2 is a human-preference benchmark built from over 25,000 prompts
and 98,000 images generated by Stable Diffusion, accompanied by 25,205
human preference annotations collected from the Stable Foundation
Discord community \[[38](#bib.bib9)\]. For each prompt, annotators
compare multiple candidate images and indicate the one they prefer.
These annotations are then used to train a CLIP-based preference model,
which defines the Human Preference Score (HPS). We use the HPS v2 test
set to evaluate how well our aligned diffusion models reflect human
preferences.

### D.2 Baselines

We compare Diff.-NPO against several representative baselines. The first
baseline is the original pretrained diffusion backbone, which provides
the starting point for alignment on both SD1.5 and SDXL. We then
consider online supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on preferred images,
including both offline and online variants. We also compare with
online-Diff-DPO in the main tables. In addition, we include two
representative self-play preference optimization methods,
SPIN \[[47](#bib.bib46)\] and SEPPO \[[49](#bib.bib47)\], with detailed
introductions in paragraphs below. Together, these baselines allow us to
compare our method with reference-anchored preference optimization, pure
self-play style updates, and simple supervised adaptation.

##### SPIN.

SPIN is a self-play fine-tuning method for text-to-image diffusion
models. Instead of relying on a fixed offline preference dataset, SPIN
iteratively improves the model by comparing samples generated by the
current model with samples from a previous or reference model. At each
iteration, the model is encouraged to assign higher likelihood to its
own improved generations than to weaker generations produced by an
earlier policy. This creates a self-play learning dynamic, where the
model continuously learns to outperform its past versions. In our
comparison, SPIN represents a pure self-play style baseline: it uses
online model-generated samples to construct preference pairs and update
the diffusion model, but does not explicitly combine both
reference-policy anchoring and previous-policy regularization in the
same way as Diff.-NPO.

##### SEPPO.

SEPPO is a semi-policy preference optimization method for diffusion
alignment. Its main idea is to bridge offline preference optimization
and online self-play by using samples generated from both the current
policy and an auxiliary policy, such as a reference or previous policy,
to construct preference pairs. Compared with standard Diffusion-DPO,
which is mainly anchored to a fixed reference model, SEPPO introduces
policy-dependent samples so that the training distribution can better
reflect the model’s current generation behavior. Compared with pure
self-play methods, however, SEPPO still maintains a semi-policy
structure rather than fully optimizing the current policy through an
explicit Nash-style online mirror descent objective.

### D.3 Implementation Details

We adopt AdamW for training SD1.5 and Adafactor for training SDXL. The
learning rate is set to $`1\times 10^{-8}`$ with linear warmup and is
scaled by the effective batch size. The global batch size is 2048: for
pairwise methods, the effective batch contains 2048 pairs. For SD1.5 and
SDXL, we set $`\beta=5000`$ for Online-Diffusion-DPO and Diff.-NPO. For
Diff.-NPO, we fix the ratio $`\tau/\eta=1/2`$ in all experiments unless
the ablation study. For SEPPO and SPIN, we directly evaluate the
official checkpoints released by their authors. All experiments are
conducted on eight NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs.

### E.2 Comparison with Offline SFT and DPO

We further compare Diff.-NPO with offline variants of SFT and DPO in

Offline SFT substantially outperforms online SFT, mainly because the
preferred images in the offline Pick-a-Pic dataset are generally of
higher quality than the images generated online with a small number of
inference steps. Nevertheless, offline SFT still underperforms

preferred images cannot fully exploit the relative preference
information contained in positive–negative pairs. We also observe that
offline DPO is weaker than online DPO in this setting, indicating that
online model-generated preference pairs better match the current policy
distribution. Overall, Diff.-NPO achieves the best win rates across all
five metrics, showing that the proposed online Nash-style objective
provides stronger preference alignment than both supervised adaptation
and standard DPO variants.
````

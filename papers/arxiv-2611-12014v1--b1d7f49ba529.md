#### **Abstract**

Large language models and the multi-agent systems built around them could accelerate materials discovery by connecting scientific knowledge with experimental decisions. Existing benchmarks establish capabilities in domain knowledge, multimodal interpretation and scientific tool use. As language models increasingly support materials research workflows, their ability to distinguish closely related material states and reason accurately with quantitative evidence remains insufficiently evaluated. These abilities are essential for comparing material responses, assessing mechanisms and maintaining reliable judgements as experimental evidence accumulates. We introduce MatScope to evaluate three connected capabilities. M1 tests unaided, condition-specific reasoning through 200 tasks spanning ten research functions, using aluminium and high-entropy alloys as representative material systems. M2 tests agent-assisted transfer of related knowledge to unresolved materials questions. MatEvoBench-20 tests initial forecasts across scientific dependency chains and revisions of still-hidden responses as observations accumulate. Across eleven model deployments, including GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1, given-input calculations and many physical explanations are handled well. Fine-grained response estimates, comparisons between similar states and quantitatively supported choices remain substantially weaker. Related literature improves selected estimates, while condition and baseline mismatches limit transfer. Progressive observations calibrate some downstream responses and destabilize earlier accurate predictions. MatScope establishes measurable targets for developing reliable scientific reasoning in future self-evolving materials laboratories and materials-aware world models.

**Keywords:** Materials informatics; Large language models; AI agents; Scientific reasoning; Benchmarking; Self-driving laboratories;

#### **Introduction**

Large language models (LLMs) and their multi-agent architectures are creating new routes to materials discovery. They connect scientific literature, computational models and experimental interfaces through flexible reasoning and tool coordination. Systems such as AtomAgents integrate language-based planning with physics simulations for alloy design, while Coscientist demonstrates language-model-driven experimental chemistry 1,2. These advances make autonomous and AIassisted laboratories a practical research direction. Their success also raises a central evaluation question: which scientific judgements can the language model reliably contribute to a workflow whose outcome depends jointly on models, retrieved knowledge, optimization and experimental control3,4 ?

Existing benchmarks provide essential foundations. MaScQA and MatSciBench evaluate materials knowledge, numerical problem solving and structured scientific reasoning 5,6. MaCBench extends assessment to multimodal extraction, experimental understanding and interpretation<sup>7</sup> . Strong performance on established knowledge tasks documents genuine progress: a common-protocol MaScQA evaluation reported 60.8% overall accuracy for GPT-4 and approximately 84% for GPT-4o and Claude-3.5-Sonnet 5. LightAlloy-Bench evaluates composition–process–property reasoning through mechanical-property selection and identifies numerical and mechanistic limitations<sup>8</sup> . These studies motivate a broader assessment of how language models interpret material states, evaluate quantitative evidence and maintain scientific judgements as research conditions and available information evolve 9-15 .

Agent benchmarks address complementary requirements. AstaBench and SciAgentGym evaluate research-tool access, execution and multi-step coordination 16,17. AILA and AFMBench assess real AFM operations, including planning, instrument control and analysis within an atomic-forcemicroscopy workflow <sup>18</sup>. This instrument-centred evaluation establishes important operational capabilities. Materials research also requires state-specific interpretation, comparison and inference across processing branches and structural scales. Evaluating these connected judgements links tool execution to the scientific understanding needed throughout an investigation 19-23 .

The distinction matters because material responses are path-dependent and multiscale, making quantitative understanding essential for reliable reasoning throughout an extended laboratory investigation. Atomic-scale chemistry and crystallography constrain nanometre-scale precipitates and interfaces. Micrometre-scale grains, segregation and defects influence specimen-scale strength, ductility and degradation. Several mechanisms act concurrently, and their relative contributions change with processing 24-26. Particle sizes and spacings, phase fractions and defect densities distinguish material states and constrain the contributions of these mechanisms. An incorrect phase fraction can misrepresent transformation progress, while an incorrect particle spacing can distort a strengthening calculation. Such errors affect state identification, mechanism assessment and comparisons between specimens. They can also enter subsequent reasoning as incorrect premises when later observations are interpreted. These requirements also apply to values supplied by simulations, numerical tools and experiments. Reliable materials judgement therefore requires the correct state, physical quantity, magnitude and comparison to be maintained throughout the investigation24-26 .

An evolving investigation requires three connected forms of competence. First, a model must apply its existing knowledge to the stated conditions and predict unknown responses. Its deployed weights remain fixed during the evaluation. Second, unresolved questions require the acquisition and adaptation of related knowledge. A useful analogy must preserve the relevant composition, processing regime and measurement definition. Third, observations from the current experiment must constrain predictions that remain untested. Effective adaptation combines accurate initial expectations, selective revision and sustained accuracy.

Here we introduce MatScope to evaluate the materials reasoning required of LLMs and agents in self-evolving laboratories, using alloy systems as representative cases within a generalizable evaluation framework. M1 tests unaided, node-level judgement through 200 alloy tasks spanning ten research functions. M2 tests whether agents can improve unresolved M1 node-level judgements by acquiring and applying knowledge from related studies. MatEvoBench-20 (ME20) evaluates these types of judgement across ninety nodes in twenty multistage research trajectories. Most nodes are connected through material evolution, shared states or cross-scale relationships, allowing evaluation of how successive observations affect reasoning across an investigation. In the 11 frontline model evaluations, the success rate of the M1 task ranged from 13.5% to 19.0%. The questions with universal material explanations and clear input calculations had high success rates (92.38–

99.05%), but performed poorly in making precise and granular inferences (22.74–31.21%). On twenty selected M2 failure cases, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 gain 1.60 and 4.22 percentage points, respectively. Across 48 shared ME20 endpoints, numerical rubric scores rise from 32.00% to 37.50% for Astra and from 21.42% to 37.67% for Fable between initial and last-hidden assessments. However, only four of seven initially top-band endpoints for Astra and one of three for Fable retain that accuracy throughout updating. Together, these evaluations distinguish limitations in initial materials understanding, transfer of external knowledge and maintenance of accurate judgements as evidence accumulates. This indicates that existing large language models still face difficulties in making continuous and in-depth correct inferences in the self-evolving material laboratory scenarios. MatScope provides measurable targets for scientific intelligence in future self-evolving materials laboratories and for materials-aware world models.

## **Results**

# **A capability framework for evolving materials laboratories**

MatScope evaluates whether LLMs and agents can maintain accurate materials understanding from local judgements to linked research trajectories (Fig. 1a–c). Laboratory reasoning includes interpreting characterization, comparing specimens, assessing mechanisms and revising plans. Each task therefore specifies a material, processing branch, specimen state and measurement object. Particle spacing, grain size and phase fraction remain distinct variables. Parallel heat treatments retain separate histories. The same requirements apply to values supplied by simulations, numerical tools or experiments. High scores require the requested quantities and relationships to be correct for the specified material conditions.

M1 and M2 examine local scientific judgements, while MatEvoBench-20 (ME20) examines their connection across a research pathway. M1 evaluates unaided interpretation, comparison and estimation for specified material states. M2 provides literature-search, reading and calculation tools for selected unresolved M1 requirements. The construction-source publications are excluded from retrieval. The agent must assess which relationships and response scales can inform the target problem. ME20 places comparable operations in independent research cases. Registered relationships connect processing branches, shared states and cross-scale measurements. Models first predict all requested hidden targets, then revise those still hidden after each observation. For example, grain size and matrix solute content can inform a strength estimate before tensile results are released.

Task construction draws on an inventory of 28,872 deduplicated literature works, with evaluation cases primarily covering aluminium alloys and high-entropy alloys. Bibliographic metadata, fulltext passages and structured composition–process–observation–property records describe complementary aspects of the corpus. Embeddings and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) show thematic distribution and the locations of task sources (Fig. 1d) <sup>27</sup>. Source selection requires identifiable samples, explicit conditions and supported quantitative or relational targets. Structured extractions locate candidate records. Original measurements and sourcesupported relationships establish the references. Coverage analyses retain material families, processing routes, characterization methods and properties separately. These summaries document sampling concentration and gaps within the collected corpus (Supplementary Notes 1–2 and Supplementary Table 1).

M1 contains 200 tasks and 1,124 targets across 96 source-connected groups. Its ten-function distribution is reported in Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1. M2 examines twenty shared-failure cases. ME20 comprises twenty investigations with ninety nodes. English questions specify the required conditions and response definitions. Reference outcomes and scoring rules remain separate from the model input. Target-specific rubrics assign graded credit for numerical proximity and precise scientific relationships. Numerical accuracy, ordering, mechanism scope and feasibility retain separate checks. Repeated responses measure numerical stability relative to the declared scoring resolution. Experimental variability, model variability and evaluator discrepancies are analysed separately. These measures distinguish partial understanding, accurate quantitative discrimination and completion of the required scientific goals.

#### **M1: Materials knowledge for quantitative experimental decisions**

M1 examines how far pretrained knowledge can support the operations required for a materials decision (Table 1, Fig. 2). Its ten functions preserve condition binding, quantitative comparison, cross-scale interpretation, processing histories and feasible route selection. Eleven deployments obtain mean rubric scores of 58.59–68.75% and complete task success of 13.5–19.0% across 200 tasks (Fig. 2a). Quantitative comparison is a shared weakness across all eleven deployments. Scores range from 40.47% to 53.46%, compared with 72.29–92.67% for limits of inference. The models are substantially better at identifying the scope of a conclusion than at determining the magnitude, direction and baseline of material differences (Table 1; Fig. 2b). Figures 2b,c compare the ten research functions and four numerical-observable groups across all eleven deployments. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the material-domain composition of each function.

The Fe45Co35Cr10V<sup>10</sup> case concerns an 80% cold-rolled alloy annealed at 750 °C for 300 s. Astra predicts 100% FCC mapped area and 50% recrystallization, Fable predicts 97% and 80%, and GPT-6 Sol predicts 95% and 25%, against respective references of 99.9% and 57.8%. All three models identify a nearly complete FCC state. Their recrystallization estimates deviate from the reference by −7.8, +22.2 and −32.8 percentage points for Astra, Fable and Sol, respectively. Phase reversion is therefore captured more accurately than the extent of recrystallization. The models only partially resolve the concurrent transformations within this annealed state. The strength–ductility decision in the die-cast Al–Si–Mg–Mn case and the route-dependent copper-addition comparison further distinguish quantitative inputs from the corresponding decision or transfer relationship. These cases separate accuracy in material quantities from accuracy in the associated relations or decisions (Fig. 2d). Answer-generation cost and score form a separate deployment comparison (Fig. 2e). The observed frontier is conditional on these configurations and excludes grading cost. Unit and ordering repairs use the same saved final and programmatic rule across deployments. The CS–CDS zinc-dispersion case's recorded semantic-relation concern remains a separate evaluator limitation (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Figs. 2–8, Supplementary Tables 2–3) 26,28-30 .

Models execute supplied quantitative relations far more reliably than they estimate responses for specific material states. Across 200 tasks, the 49 given-input calculation targets receive weighted normalized scores of 92.38–99.05%, compared with 22.74–31.21% for the 476 conditional numerical predictions. The calculation targets test formulas, units and arithmetic. The prediction targets test whether the model can supply quantities appropriate to the specified material state (Fig. 3a).

Many estimates are too inaccurate to distinguish nearby material states quantitatively. Across 75 common positive-valued targets, median factor errors range from 1.59 to 2.12 (Fig. 3b). A factor error above two places the estimate below half or above twice the reference. Such estimates fail to establish the requested material magnitude at the precision needed for close comparisons. The broader conditional-prediction analysis also records 26 errors of at least tenfold for Astra and 23 for Fable among their positive submissions (Supplementary Fig. 10). The weakness therefore extends from fine precision to gross errors in material magnitude.

Numerical estimates are also insufficiently repeatable at the resolution required for effective materials judgement. Across nineteen comparable triplets per deployment, median spans reach 4.57 and 5.00 grade-five error thresholds for Astra and Opus 5.5, respectively (Fig. 3c). Only seven Astra triplets and four Opus triplets remain within one such threshold. Derived quantities show larger normalized spans than direct responses, with medians of 20.00 versus 1.67 for Astra and 10.47 versus 4.33 for Opus (Supplementary Fig. 14). Repeated answers thus provide different numerical premises under unchanged experimental conditions. This variability weakens the reproducibility of subsequent state comparisons and scientific reasoning.

In the TiC-modified Al7068 case, the source gives a mean diameter of 180 nm for constituent TiC particles within agglomerates and a mean inter-agglomerate spacing of 6 μm. Astra predicts 60 nm and 0.5 μm. Fable predicts 200 nm and 2 μm. Their answers distinguish relevant particlestrengthening and grain-pinning roles, yet underestimate spacing twelve-fold and three-fold. Mechanism recognition exceeds the models' ability to quantify the microstructure to which the mechanism applies. The answers identify relevant strengthening and pinning roles, yet misrepresent the particle spacing needed for quantitative analysis (Fig. 3d). The Zn-dispersion case exposes an error in the processing comparison itself. After 12 h at 450 °C, the reference standard deviation is 40.3% lower in CDS than in CS. Submissions above the equality line reverse this ordering and identify the wrong route as having lower intragranular Zn dispersion (Fig. 3e). State ratios for MgZn<sup>2</sup> diameter and spacing further distinguish preservation of a heat-treatment response from recovery of its absolute scale (Fig. 3f). Together, these cases show that plausible material explanations can coexist with incorrect magnitudes and reversed comparisons. Those errors leave the specific material state and processing response poorly resolved 31,32 .

Matching an average magnitude can conceal a completely wrong material comparison. DeepSeek Pro predicts a −40-K temperature offset for all four compositions, against references of +61, +55, +36 and +5 K. Its mean absolute offset of 40 K is close to the 39.25-K reference average. However, every sign is reversed and the composition dependence is lost. The mean absolute error is 79.25 K. The answer therefore fails both the direction and the composition sensitivity of the required correction. Across 136 common tasks containing prediction and mechanism targets, only 1–4 tasks per deployment satisfy both requirements. Every prediction must reach grade 5, and every mechanism target must reach at least grade 4. The central weakness is combining a relevant mechanism explanation with accurate quantities for the same material state.

The overall scores of GPT-6 Sol and Astra are similar, but there are significant differences in their fine-grained material capabilities. GPT-6 Sol and Astra obtained 67.1955% and 67.6616%, respectively. The paired difference (Sol minus Astra) was -0.4660 percentage points (source-group bootstrap 95% interval, -2.24 to 1.29). Sol scored higher on 69 tasks, Astra on 70, and 61 received equal scores. Their mean absolute task-level difference was 8.87 points (Fig. 3g,h). Sol's mean advantage was 8.98 points in joint-source tasks and 2.42 points in model-calculation tasks, whereas Astra's advantage was 10.43 points in process–state relations. The joint-source stratum contains only eight tasks and six source groups. These post hoc descriptive contrasts identify different bottlenecks within the specified task set. The differences describe the functions and materials in this task set. Better performance requires state-specific quantitative responses and coherent relations, with specific quantitative detail.

#### **M2: Agent-assisted knowledge transfer for unresolved materials problems**

M2 evaluates how agents mobilize prior research when their unaided materials judgements remain incomplete (Fig. 4). Its scientific demand is to identify compatible evidence and translate it into a constraint on the current problem. Related studies may supply a mechanism, scaling relation or response range. Successful transfer requires preserving the material state and experimental comparison to which that information applies. Astra and Fable each answer twenty cases using a bounded literature-and-calculation harness. The paired analysis retains 64 shared-failure subquestions and 66 targets, including 59 numerical targets (Supplementary Note 6, Supplementary Table 7) 33,34 .

Agent-assisted knowledge transfer produces limited and uneven improvements across the selected unresolved materials problems. Astra changes from 17.82% to 19.42% (Fig. 4a), a gain of 1.60 percentage points with a source-group 95% interval of −3.60 to 6.75. Fable changes from 15.19% to 19.41%, gaining 4.22 points with an interval of 1.01 to 7.73. Complete success is zero for both models on the selected requirements. Raw numerical errors decrease on 14 targets for Astra and 26 for Fable, and increase on 17 and 23. Five and fourteen improvements remain within the same scoring band. Continuous errors reveal useful movement towards the required response, while the bands retain its unresolved precision requirements (Table 2, Supplementary Table 8).

Removing the largest-gain source group leaves Fable with a 3.04-point gain and Astra with 0.11, revealing different sensitivity to case composition. # **MatEvoBench-20: Cross-scale predictive adaptation along experimental pathways**

ME20 tests whether models can maintain accurate materials judgements as evidence accumulates across linked states and measurements (Fig. 1c). The initial submission forecasts the full sequence of required responses. Later submissions revisit the same hidden targets as observations arrive. Scientific dependencies connect atomic structure, precipitates, grains, defects and macroscopic properties across processing or loading histories. Each prediction is committed before its verifying observation is released. This design assesses the prospective value of evidence and the persistence of accurate judgement. Twenty nodes represent later physical states, and seventy represent unrevealed measurements of an existing state. The ninety nodes cover grains and features, phases and particles, local chemistry, defects and damage, interfaces and layers, and material responses (Fig. 5a). The T6 case holds the material state fixed while grain, solute, precipitate and tensile records are revealed in sequence (Fig. 5b). It tests whether measured structure constrains the model's judgement of properties that remain hidden (Supplementary Note 7, Supplementary Table 10, Supplementary Fig. 21)<sup>36</sup> .

Initial forecasts misrepresent both the magnitude and the pattern of material evolution (Fig. 5c,d).

Both models underestimate dislocation accumulation during tensile loading. In the thermal case, they predict increasing surface-feature size with temperature, whereas the reference shows an overall decrease. The checkpoint matrices reveal when later observations correct these errors and where they persist (Fig. 5e,h). Fable gains more from the observations, but both models retain low final numerical precision. Across twenty fixed-policy cases and 48 common numerical endpoints, case-equal scores rise from 32.00% to 37.50% for Astra and from 21.42% to 37.67% for Fable. Fable's larger gain closes its initial disadvantage without producing a higher final score. Absolute errors decrease on 16 Astra endpoints and 21 Fable endpoints, but increase on nine and fourteen. New observations therefore produce selective quantitative repair alongside harmful revisions (Supplementary Note 8, Supplementary Table 11, Supplementary Figs. 22–27).

Earlier measurements can substantially improve quantitative predictions of downstream material states. In continuously loaded laser-powder-bed-fused AlSi10Mg, Astra revises the hidden 5% strain dislocation density from 1.2 × 10<sup>15</sup> to 1.02 × 10<sup>16</sup> m-2 after preceding density observations. Against the 9.5 × 10<sup>15</sup> m-2 reference, relative error falls from 87.4% to 7.4% (Fig. 5f). The model successfully uses the preceding defect population to calibrate a later deformation state.

For TiB2/AlSi10Mg undergoing solution treatment and ageing, Astra's tensile-strength prediction moves from 445 through 435 and 430 MPa before returning to 445 MPa. The reference is 431.3 MPa. Its error first falls from 13.7 to 1.3 MPa, then returns to 13.7 MPa as further structural evidence is incorporated. Additional evidence can also destabilize an initially accurate prediction when competing mechanisms must be combined. Astra temporarily reaches the experimental strength but loses this accuracy after further updates. This reveals a weakness in maintaining the quantitative balance among network degradation, solute redistribution and precipitation strengthening. The NbMoTaW-film case shows partial calibration followed by renewed error. For the hidden 625 °C surface-feature size, Astra changes from 360 to 100 and then 150 nm; Fable changes from 290 to 115 and then 140 nm. The reference is 51 nm (Fig. 5g,h). Both final estimates remain more than 2.7 times the reference, and both are worse than their earlier estimates. Additional observations correct part of the initial overestimate but fail to establish a stable quantitative description of the thermal response 37,38 .

Accurate route selection requires quantitative consistency between predicted properties and design constraints. Fable selects the reference T6 route while predicting yield and tensile strengths below the stated thresholds, and acknowledges the shortfalls. The model identifies the reference route but cannot justify its feasibility using its own quantitative predictions. This inconsistency limits the reliability of its materials-design judgement. The largest gap in ME20 lies between understanding released experimental records and inferring material responses that remain unknown. Both Astra and Fable recover all released records, reaching 100% across the twenty cases (Fig. 5i). However, their scores for hidden forecasts remain at 49.2% and 46.0%, respectively. Even at the last hidden endpoints, the scores reach only 51.0% for Astra and 58.0% for Fable. These results reveal a substantial gap between assimilating observed data and using it to infer unresolved material states. Progressive evidence provides partial improvements, but the models still struggle to maintain quantitatively reliable judgements across extended research pathways. This limitation is critical for laboratory reasoning systems, where each new measurement must inform the interpretation of subsequent states, mechanisms and properties (Supplementary Table 12, Supplementary Fig. 28). Prediction gains are uneven across material properties, even when models receive the same sequence of multiscale observations (Supplementary Fig. 29). Across the same 48 terminal numerical targets per model, Astra gains three and loses two top-band predictions. Fable gains four and loses one (Supplementary Fig. 30). Both models achieve a small net gain in top-band predictions, but some initially accurate responses become incorrect after updating. Endpoint gains alone therefore overestimate the reliability of sustained materials reasoning.

Together, M1, M2 and ME20 reveal three connected limitations in laboratory-facing materials reasoning: inaccurate condition-specific estimates, incomplete transfer of related knowledge and unstable predictions during evidence updates. M1 and M2 examine scientific judgements at individual research nodes, while ME20 tests how these judgements interact across processing histories, structural scales and sequential observations. Their combined results show that reliable scientific intelligence requires quantitative accuracy to be established, transferred and maintained throughout an evolving investigation.

#### **Discussion**

MatScope reveals a fundamental gap between general scientific competence and the state-specific quantitative reasoning required for self-evolving materials laboratories. Across leading LLMs, strong performance in established calculations and physical explanations does not translate into reliable discrimination of material states, effective transfer of related knowledge, or stable prediction updates as experimental evidence accumulates. These findings identify a central requirement for scientific intelligence: models must establish accurate material-specific judgements, transfer relevant knowledge across conditions, and maintain quantitative consistency throughout evolving investigations. By connecting local reasoning, knowledge acquisition and sequential evidence updates, MatScope provides a unified evaluation framework for materials research functions beyond the alloy systems examined here. It establishes actionable targets for developing and evaluating scientific agents, materials-aware world models and self-evolving laboratory systems.

#### **Methods**

## **Corpus, task construction and information control**

The analyses use the English Core200 questions, twenty paired M2 cases and twenty ME20 pathways. The corpus contains 28,872 deduplicated works and 716,080 indexed text passages. Structured extractions index candidate measurements and scientific relationships. Source text and structured measurements provide the reference values and relationships. Each subquestion is assigned a scientific purpose, a source-supported reference and a target-specific scoring rule. The question specifies the relevant composition, processing history, specimen state and measurement definition. Calculation targets include the required operands. Empirical prediction targets ask for a response under the stated conditions. Comparisons specify their reference direction, and decision targets specify their constraints. Samples, processing branches, states, locations, physical quantities and statistical definitions remain associated. Original observations, calculations and author interpretations are distinguished. Metadata, embeddings and UMAP characterize coverage. Historical task refinement informed the final development set, so its composition is reported explicitly. Current model-facing experiments use text and numerical records of characterization 39,40 .

sources. Each task begins in an independent context with fixed model weights. The evaluation retains model, provider, generation settings, requests, submitted answers and termination states. Each M1 task starts in an independent conversation. The model receives the English question and its answer requirements. Tools and retrieved documents are disabled. Only the submitted final answer is scored. Returned reasoning and operational records are retained for diagnostic analysis. M2 evaluates twenty selected problems from M1 nineteen source-connected groups. The paired comparison uses the same 64 subquestions and 66 targets in M1 and M2. Each episode starts without the earlier M1 answer, score or grader feedback. M2 adds the registered literature-search, reading and calculation tools within isolated episodes. The agent selects a search, passage-reading, calculation or submission action. The client executes the action and returns its observation to the same conversation. Each episode permits three searches, eight actions excluding submission, ten assistant turns and 6,000 observation tokens. The generated-token ceiling is 65,536, with 4,096 tokens reserved for the final answer. The episode ends at final submission or the 30-minute limit. Search combines BM25 and dense retrieval. Each initially supplies fifty candidates. Eligible candidates are combined by reciprocal-rank fusion and reranked with a Qwen model. Each search returns up to five permitted passages, capped at 500 tokens each. Additional reads return up to 800 tokens. After construction-source exclusion, retrieval searches and ranks candidates from 25,325 works and 712,778 chunks. Before text reaches the agent, a separate permission filter limits returned passages to 1,972 works and 63,639 chunks. Search coverage and readable body text therefore have different sizes. The agent chooses queries, reads current or adjacent passages, calculates and submits through tool calls (Supplementary Note 6, Supplementary Table 7). Twelve cases retain full-question inputs and eight use selected-question projections. Comparisons retain the same selected targets while recording this input-scope difference <sup>41</sup> .

#### **Target-specific rubrics and experimental variability**

Each target specifies its scientific object, reference, accepted equivalences, required relationships, weight and scoring policy. Submitted final answers supply the scored claims. Scoring starts from the saved final answer. Reference-blind extraction identifies the submitted value, unit, object and state. Python checks numerical errors, unit conversions, identities and ordering. Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite evaluates the required explanatory relationships and their object and state assignments. The effective target grades are then combined using the task's registered weights and dependency rules. The range is 0–5. The highest grade requires all target-specific scientific requirements to be satisfied. Correct terminology alone does not satisfy a missing quantity, comparison or required relationship. Independent numerical and explanatory targets retain their separate grades. General statements earn credit only where they satisfy a stated scientific requirement. Contradictions and missing necessary relationships constrain the corresponding target. Joint M1 diagnostics require every prediction target to reach grade 5 and every mechanism target to reach at least grade 4. Verified extraction and scoring errors are corrected using the saved final answers. Remaining judgement concerns and their score sensitivity are reported separately (Supplementary Note 9, Supplementary Table 2) 42-44 <sup>45</sup> . Numerical targets use absolute, relative or logarithmic-ratio errors according to the physical quantity. Signed differences retain their reference direction. Fraction errors preserve the distinction between percentage points and relative percentages. Empirical predictions receive graded credit under declared resolution bands. Deterministic calculations use calculation and rounding precision. Source-reported uncertainty and measurement definitions are retained where available. Prediction grades measure proximity to the empirical response at a declared resolution. Each reference retains its material state, measurement definition and reported statistic. Source-reported dispersion is recorded separately from the scoring bands. Raw errors, response orderings and constraint margins provide complementary measures of the scientific consequences of an error. Raw errors, orderings and constraint margins supplement rubric scores so that a near-threshold miss can be distinguished from an incorrect scale or decision.

Within a task, applicable target grades are combined using specified normalized weights and declared dependency rules. The task score has a 20% ceiling when none of its scored claims meets the rubric's criterion for a material-specific discriminating response. Mean Rubric Score averages task scores. Task Success follows the rule specified for each task, including designated target thresholds and, where specified, terminal research-goal conditions. Target-type analyses retain their own denominators. Missing final answers enter the end-to-end denominator as zero and receive a separate operational label. Completed-answer analyses are reported separately. Identified extraction, unit, ordering and semantic discrepancies are documented in the evaluation records, with their effect on scores reported separately. The question set removes two targets with an unspecified local sampling object or ledger operand, consecutively renumbers the retained subquestions, and replays saved answers under the shared unit and ordering repairs. Original requests, answers and scores are retained.

# **Sequential forecasting and prediction stability**

ME20 records the process, material-state, measurement and information-release graphs separately. A scientific node specifies a material state, measurement object and requested response. A checkpoint specifies when the model submits predictions and receives an observation. Seventy nodes concern hidden measurements of an existing state, and twenty concern later physical states. Material relationships determine which hidden targets may be informed by each observation. The initial response predicts all requested hidden targets before any observation is disclosed. Temporal evolution, shared-state measurements and cross-scale links connect earlier nodes to relevant later quantities. Parallel measurements can constrain a farther endpoint or a joint choice while leaving the next-numbered node unchanged (Supplementary Note 7, Supplementary Table 10). At each checkpoint, the model predicts the targets that remain hidden. An explicit KEEP response refers to the same target at a named earlier checkpoint. Omitted targets remain missing. Values submitted after disclosure are excluded from hidden-response scores. Reproduction of released records is scored separately. Subsequent observations are then disclosed. Forecast scores exclude submissions made after target disclosure. Released-fact recovery is assessed separately. Each target is averaged over its required hidden checkpoints. Nodes combine numerical and relation components at the specified 70:30 weighting, followed by node- and case-level averaging. Initial and last-hidden endpoint comparisons retain the same targets. Design analysis distinguishes option identity, feasibility under the model's own numbers, and accuracy of the numerical support.

The uniform scoring snapshot contains 52 saved trajectories and 340 checkpoint finals, with all 52 whole-trajectory evaluations closed. Each deployment has 20 fixed-policy and six active-policy trajectories. The same specified judge configuration was retained for targeted completion of saved answers, without regenerating any scientific response. Whole-episode fixed-policy comparisons now use all twenty common cases. The 48 matched hidden numerical endpoints retain their separate twenty-case denominator. No historical mixed-configuration grade is borrowed.

Numerical repeatability was also examined on twenty preselected M1 tasks, using one target per task and three independent responses from Astra and Opus 5.5. Nineteen comparable triples were available per model. We divide the range of same-unit predictions by the target's declared top-band error scale. This measures response variability relative to evaluation resolution. It is distinct from experimental scatter and from accuracy against the reference. Statistical intervals resample connected source groups, with 2,000 replicates for the current M1, M2 and ME20 summaries. These intervals describe task-source variation. Repeat-generation results describe model variability. Each analysis reports its data source, sample size and scoring criteria (Supplementary Note 5, Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Figs. 12–14) <sup>46</sup> .

# Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models

Mojtaba Nafez Affiliation: EPFL & Idiap Affiliation: Lausanne,
Switzerland Email: [mojtaba.nafez@epfl.ch](mailto:)    James Henderson
Affiliation: Idiap Research Institute Affiliation: Martigny, Switzerland
Email: [james.henderson@idiap.ch](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Uniform-state diffusion models (USDMs) can revise any token at any
denoising step, which lets them correct their own mistakes, a key
advantage over masked diffusion. Self-correction, however, requires both
revising incorrect tokens and retaining correct ones, and we show that
current USDMs lack the latter. Even under greedy-tail decoding,
state-of-the-art USDMs (DUO, UDLM, and uniform-noise SEDD) keep revising
173–270 of 512 positions at every step, and these large, uncoordinated
edits collapse sample diversity. A random-token corruption experiment
traces this deficit to the models themselves: they reconstruct clean and
corrupted tokens with nearly identical accuracy, even though clean
tokens are easier targets. A decomposition of the validation NELBO shows
that training barely rewards retention: incorrect predictions are
heavily penalized at corrupted positions but almost free at clean ones.
We propose Correct-Token Retention Regularization (CTR-Reg), a simple
but effective auxiliary loss that trains the model to retain tokens left
unperturbed by the forward process and requires no change to the
sampler. CTR-Reg improves clean-token accuracy by 26.5 percentage points
on average across six benchmarks, while leaving corrupted-token accuracy
virtually unchanged, and its per-step revisions converge to only 3–11
positions. With just five greedy-tail steps, generative perplexity more
than halves under CTR-Reg for all three models while diversity is
preserved, and these gains hold across sampling budgets. Our results
identify correct-token retention as a key missing ingredient for
self-correcting diffusion language models, and demonstrate an effective
fix.

## 1 Introduction

Diffusion models have achieved remarkable success in image, video, and
audio generation ([Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015](#bib.bib18); [Ho et al.,
2020](#bib.bib10); [Polyak et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Huang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib3); [Rombach et al., 2021](#bib.bib1); [Blattmann et al.,
2022](#bib.bib2)), driven largely by iterative refinement: progressively
correcting errors over many denoising steps yields realistic and diverse
samples ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib32); [Dhariwal and Nichol,
2021](#bib.bib5); [Song et al., 2023](#bib.bib6); [Ma et al.,
2025](#bib.bib7)). Recent work has extended diffusion to discrete data,
with promising results in language modeling ([Sahoo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13); [Shi et al., 2024](#bib.bib12); [Lou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9); [Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib8)): unlike
left-to-right autoregressive models, diffusion language models decode
many tokens in parallel using bidirectional context, enabling
substantially faster generation while remaining competitive on
downstream tasks ([Labs et al., 2025](#bib.bib15); [Song et al.,
2025](#bib.bib14)).

Discrete diffusion models interpolate between clean data $`x`$ and a
fixed prior $`\pi`$ via the forward process
$`q_{t}(z_{t}\mid x;\alpha_{t})=\mathrm{Cat}(z_{t};\alpha_{t}x+(1-\alpha_{t})\pi)`$,
with a monotonically decreasing noise schedule $`\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]`$
([Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib8); [Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)).
The choice of $`\pi`$ yields two families. Masked Diffusion Models
(MDMs), or absorbing-state diffusion ([Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib8);
[Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)), set $`\pi`$ to a \[MASK\] token: a
masked position is revealed once during denoising and never revisited,
permanently locking in any early mistake. Uniform-state Diffusion Models
(USDMs) ([Schiff et al., 2024](#bib.bib16); [Sahoo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13)) instead set $`\pi`$ to the uniform distribution over
the vocabulary; since a corrupted token is then indistinguishable from a
clean one, the model must reconsider every position at every step,
enabling self-correction ([Deschenaux et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)).

Self-correction, however, requires two distinct capabilities: revising
incorrect tokens and retaining correct ones (_correct-token retention_);
we find that current USDMs lack the latter. The deficit surfaces under
the _Greedy-Tail Sampler_ of DUO ([Sahoo et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)),
which appends one per-position argmax step after ancestral sampling; we
also consider $`k`$ such steps, each a single forward pass followed by a
per-position argmax, with no stochastic sampling. Under this sampler,
three state-of-the-art USDMs, DUO, UDLM ([Schiff et al.,
2024](#bib.bib16)), and SEDD with uniform noise ([Lou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9)) (hereafter SEDD), exhibit a substantial collapse in
sample diversity, measured by entropy
(Figure [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).

To identify its source,
Figure [3](#S1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
tracks how many positions change prediction at each greedy-tail step:
173–270 of the 512 positions are revised at every step, with no sign of
convergence. Such churn indicates a lack of correct-token retention:
because positions are predicted independently rather than jointly, these
large-scale simultaneous edits drive the collapse, since even locally
plausible edits, applied by the hundreds at once, can compound into
globally inconsistent sequences ([Gu et al., 2018](#bib.bib19)).
Samplers such as ancestral sampling mitigate this by limiting the
average amount of revision per step, but do not determine _which_
positions are revised; absent a reliable model preference for correct
tokens, this selection is governed by sampling stochasticity.

To disentangle revision from retention, we design a random-token
corruption experiment
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")):
we split a corpus into 512-token chunks, replace a random $`p\%`$ of
tokens in each with tokens drawn uniformly from the vocabulary,
reconstruct each sequence with a single forward pass, and report
accuracy separately on the corrupted $`p\%`$ and the clean $`(100-p)\%`$
of tokens, averaged over $`p\in\{10,20,30,40\}`$. For current
state-of-the-art USDMs the two accuracies are nearly identical, even
though clean tokens are the easier target at these corruption rates: the
models modify tokens indiscriminately, failing to recognize and preserve
already-correct ones.

We trace this deficit to the training objective: standard NELBO training
provides little incentive for retention. Decomposing the validation
NELBO of current USDMs by position type
([Figure 5](#S3.F5 "In The NELBO barely rewards retention. ‣ 3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
we find that at low noise levels an incorrect prediction costs 13–100
nats more than a correct one at a corrupted position, but only 0.09–1.32
nats more at a clean one. Overall, mispredicted clean positions make up
23–31% of all positions yet only 3.5–5.2% of the NELBO, versus 90–92%
for mispredicted corrupted ones. We therefore propose Correct-Token
Retention Regularization (CTR-Reg), a simple auxiliary loss term that
supplies this missing signal by explicitly training the model to retain
tokens left unperturbed by the noising process, and hence already
correct.

Our contributions are: (i) we identify a _correct-token retention_
deficit in USDMs: they revise correct and incorrect tokens
indiscriminately, reconstructing both with nearly identical accuracy,
and thus collapse in sample diversity under greedy-tail sampling; (ii)
we trace this deficit to the NELBO, which barely penalizes incorrect
predictions at clean positions, and propose CTR-Reg, which supplies this
missing signal without modifying the sampler; (iii) CTR-Reg improves
clean-token accuracy by 21.5–29.3 points per model and corpus
([Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models");

and (iv) across DUO, UDLM, and SEDD, this yields substantially
higher-quality generations with diversity above the training-data

with 5 greedy-tail steps, perplexity drops from 105.86 to 49.57 (DUO),
93.28 to 44.31 (UDLM), and 194.59 to 87.19 (SEDD), with gains holding

and under MAUVE ([Pillutla et al., 2021](#bib.bib25))

## 2 Background and Related Work

##### Notation.

Let $`V:=\{v\in\{0,1\}^{K}:\sum_{i=1}^{K}v_{i}=1\}`$ denote the set of
one-hot encodings of discrete random variables over $`K`$ categories
(the vocabulary size), and let $`\Delta^{K}`$ denote the corresponding
$`K`$-simplex. For $`v\in\Delta^{K}`$, $`\mathrm{Cat}(\cdot\,;v)`$
denotes the categorical distribution with $`\mathbb{P}(u_{i}=1)=v_{i}`$
for $`u\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\cdot\,;v)`$, $`u\in V`$. For vectors $`a,b`$,
let $`\langle a,b\rangle`$ and $`a\odot b`$ denote their dot and
Hadamard products, and let $`\mathbf{1}`$ denote the all-ones vector. We
write $`x\in V`$ for a single token and $`x^{(1:L)}\in V^{L}`$ for a
sequence of $`L`$ tokens, with $`x^{(\ell)}`$ its $`\ell`$-th entry; the
same convention applies to the noisy latents $`z_{t}`$. Let
$`\pi\in\Delta^{K}`$ denote a designated prior distribution.

### 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models

Diffusion models ([Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015](#bib.bib18); [Ho et al.,
2020](#bib.bib10)) learn a parameterized reverse process $`p_{\theta}`$
that inverts a fixed forward corruption process $`q`$, which gradually
maps clean data $`x`$ to increasingly noisy latent variables $`z_{t}`$
over time $`t\in[0,1]`$. Discrete diffusion models extend this framework
to categorical data such as text ([Hoogeboom et al., 2021](#bib.bib26);
[Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib8); [Lou et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Sahoo
et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)). Following the interpolating formulation of
[Sahoo et al. (2024)](#bib.bib11), the forward marginal at time $`t`$ is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
q_{t}(z_{t}\mid x)=\mathrm{Cat}\big(z_{t};\,\alpha_{t}x+(1-\alpha_{t})\pi\big),
``` |  | (1) |

where the noise schedule $`\alpha_{t}\in[0,1]`$ decreases monotonically
in $`t`$, with $`\alpha_{0}\approx 1`$ and $`\alpha_{1}\approx 0`$, and
noise is applied independently to each position of a length-$`L`$
sequence. With $`T`$ denoising steps, we follow [Schiff et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib16) and set $`t(i)=(i+1)/T`$ and $`s(i)=i/T`$ for
$`i=0,\ldots,T-1`$, and write $`q_{s|t}(z_{s}\mid z_{t},x)`$ for the
forward posterior between consecutive times $`s<t`$. A denoising network
$`x_{\theta}(z_{t}^{(1:L)},t)\in(\Delta^{K})^{L}`$ predicts the clean
sequence from the noisy one, and the reverse transition substitutes this
prediction into the forward posterior ([Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib11);
[Schiff et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{\theta}\big(z_{s}^{(1:L)}\mid z_{t}^{(1:L)}\big)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{s|t}\Big(z_{s}^{(\ell)}\,\Big|\,z_{t}^{(\ell)},\,x=x_{\theta}^{(\ell)}\big(z_{t}^{(1:L)},t\big)\Big).
``` |  | (2) |

The reverse process therefore factorizes across positions given
$`z_{t}^{(1:L)}`$: each position is updated independently of the others
at every step. Following [Austin et al. (2021)](#bib.bib8) and [Schiff
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16), the network is trained by minimizing the
negative evidence lower bound (NELBO), writing
$`q^{(i)}_{s|t}:=q_{s|t}(z_{s(i)}\mid z_{t(i)},x)`$ and
$`p^{(i)}_{\theta}:=p_{\theta}(z_{s(i)}\mid z_{t(i)})`$, and
$`q_{t(T)},p_{t(T)}`$ for $`q(z_{t(T)}\mid x),p(z_{t(T)})`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vb}}=\mathbb{E}_{q}\Big[\underbrace{-\log p_{\theta}(x\mid z_{t(0)})}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recons}}}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{T}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q^{(i)}_{s|t}\,\|\,p^{(i)}_{\theta}\big)}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diffusion}}}+\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_{t(T)}\,\|\,p_{t(T)}\big)}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}}\Big]
``` |  | (3) |

whose continuous-time limit $`T\to\infty`$ gives a tighter bound ([Sahoo
et al., 2024](#bib.bib11); [Schiff et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)).

### 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models

Uniform-state diffusion models (USDMs) set the prior to the uniform
distribution over the vocabulary, $`\pi=\mathbf{1}/K`$, so a corrupted
token is replaced by a token drawn uniformly at random ([Hoogeboom et
al., 2021](#bib.bib26); [Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib8)). In the
discrete-time D3PM framework of [Austin et al. (2021)](#bib.bib8), the
$`n`$-th forward step applies
$`Q_{n}=(1-\beta_{n})I+\beta_{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}/K`$ with
$`\beta_{n}\in[0,1]`$ to every token; products of such matrices keep the
same form, so the marginal after $`n`$ steps is
equation [1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
with $`\pi=\mathbf{1}/K`$ and
$`\alpha=\prod_{n^{\prime}\leq n}(1-\beta_{n^{\prime}})`$, and the
forward posterior has the closed form ([Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib8);
[Schiff et al., 2024](#bib.bib16))

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{s|t}(z_{s}\mid z_{t},x)=\mathrm{Cat}\!\left(z_{s};\,\frac{K\alpha_{t}\,z_{t}\odot x+(\alpha_{t|s}-\alpha_{t})\,z_{t}+(\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t})\,x+(1-\alpha_{t|s})(1-\alpha_{s})\,\mathbf{1}/K}{K\alpha_{t}\langle z_{t},x\rangle+1-\alpha_{t}}\right),
``` |  | (4) |

with $`\alpha_{t|s}=\alpha_{t}/\alpha_{s}`$. Unlike absorbing-state
diffusion, where a decoded token is kept deterministically ([Sahoo et
al., 2024](#bib.bib11)),
equation [4](#S2.E4 "Equation 4 ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
assigns non-zero probability both to keeping $`z_{t}`$ and to moving to
any other token, so every position can be revised at every step; this is
the source of the self-correction ability of USDMs ([Sahoo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13); [Deschenaux et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)).

##### Continuous-time objective (NELBO).

Current state-of-the-art USDMs adopt different parameterizations, but
all optimize a bound on the negative log-likelihood (NELBO), and up to
parameterization these bounds coincide with a single continuous-time
objective, which we state here for $`\pi=\mathbf{1}/K`$. UDLM ([Schiff
et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)) reaches it from the variational perspective,
by bounding a discrete-time chain and taking that bound to the
continuous-time limit, as MDLM ([Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib11)) does
for the absorbing state. DUO ([Sahoo et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)) trains
with the same bound but rewrites its per-token term to avoid
materializing an intermediate $`K`$-dimensional quantity, and computes
certain expectations analytically via Rao-Blackwellization, lowering
memory, training time and gradient variance. SEDD ([Lou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9)) instead formulates the process directly as a
continuous-time Markov chain ([Campbell et al., 2022](#bib.bib27)) and
predicts ratios of marginals (the concrete score ([Meng et al.,
2022](#bib.bib28))) rather than the clean data, training with the
diffusion-weighted denoising score entropy; [Schiff et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib16) show that under a mean parameterization of the
concrete score this objective is equivalent to the same bound, and
report that CTMC-based models underperform MDLM and UDLM.

Concretely, the bound is the $`T\to\infty`$ limit of
equation [3](#S2.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"):
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}`$ and $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recons}}`$
vanish, and the diffusion term converges to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}\,\mathbb{E}_{z_{t}\sim q_{t}(\cdot\mid x)}\textstyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}f\big(z_{t}^{(\ell)},x_{\theta}^{(\ell)}(z_{t}^{(1:L)},t),\alpha_{t};x^{(\ell)}\big),
``` |  | (5) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f\big(z_{t},x_{\theta}(z_{t},t),\alpha_{t};x\big)=\frac{\alpha_{t}^{\prime}}{K\alpha_{t}}\Bigg[\frac{K}{\bar{x}_{i}}-\frac{K}{(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}}-\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\bar{x}_{j}}{\bar{x}_{i}}\log\frac{(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}\,\bar{x}_{j}}{(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{j}\,\bar{x}_{i}}\Bigg],
``` |  | (6) |

where $`i`$ indexes the non-zero entry of $`z_{t}`$,
$`\bar{x}=K\alpha_{t}x+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{1}`$,
$`\bar{x}_{\theta}=K\alpha_{t}x_{\theta}(z_{t},t)+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{1}`$,
and $`\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=d\alpha_{t}/dt`$. The explicit per-token
objectives of DUO and SEDD, and their equivalence to this bound, are
given in
[Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C Per-Model USDM Objectives ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").
equation [5](#S2.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
is the objective to which CTR-Reg is added in
[Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Correct-Token Retention Regularization (CTR-Reg) ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").

## 3 Methodology

This section develops both the diagnosis and the remedy. We first show
that current USDMs revise positions without regard to whether the token
already there is correct, that this deficit surfaces as a collapse in
sample diversity under greedy-tail decoding, and that the NELBO training
objective barely penalizes it
([Section 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
We then propose Correct-Token Retention Regularization (CTR-Reg), a
single auxiliary term that targets correct-token retention directly
([Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Correct-Token Retention Regularization (CTR-Reg) ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).

### 3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention

##### Multi-step greedy-tail sampling.

We study three state-of-the-art USDMs, DUO ([Sahoo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13)), UDLM ([Schiff et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)), and
SEDD ([Lou et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)), under a multi-step extension of
the *Greedy-Tail Sampler* of [Sahoo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib13). Given a
total step budget $`T=n+g`$, the sampler takes $`n`$ ancestral steps
(equation [2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
with $`(s,t)=(s(i),t(i))`$) followed by $`g`$ greedy-tail steps, each a
single forward pass at the terminal diffusion time $`\delta`$ followed
by a per-position $`\arg\max`$, with no stochastic sampling:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
z^{(\ell)}\;\leftarrow\;\mathrm{onehot}\Big(\arg\max_{j}\big[x_{\theta}^{(\ell)}\big(z^{(1:L)},\delta\big)\big]_{j}\Big)\qquad\forall\,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,L\}.
``` |  | (7) |

Setting $`g=0`$ recovers pure ancestral sampling and $`g=1`$ the sampler
of [Sahoo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib13), whose final greedy step lowers
sample entropy much as nucleus sampling does ([Holtzman et al.,
2020](#bib.bib29)); fixing $`T`$ while sweeping $`g`$ keeps comparisons
compute-matched. Under this sampler, all three USDMs suffer a
substantial collapse in sample diversity, measured by entropy
([Figure 4](#S2.F4 "In Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
even the official DUO checkpoint trained on 26$`\times`$ more data,
whose entropy falls from 5.54 to 2.14 over 20 greedy-tail steps and
stays below the real-text level at its sequence length of 1024 (5.40)
for every $`g\geq 1`$
([Appendix F](#A6 "Appendix F Training on a Larger Corpus Does Not Fix the Problem ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).

##### Where the collapse comes from.

To identify the mechanism, we generate 512 sequences of length 512 per
USDM with $`n=500`$ ancestral and $`g=20`$ greedy-tail steps, and track
the number of positions whose decoded token changes at each greedy-tail
step, averaged over samples
([Figure 3](#S1.F3 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
For all three NELBO-only models, this number starts near half the
sequence and shows no sign of convergence: even at the twentieth step,
173–270 positions still change. Because all positions are updated
independently and simultaneously
(equation [2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
each step rewrites a large fraction of the sequence, and even locally
plausible edits, made by the hundreds at once, compound into globally
inconsistent sequences ([Gu et al., 2018](#bib.bib19)), producing the
low-diversity outputs in
[Figures 4](#S2.F4 "In Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
and [7](#A1.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").
This persistent churn stems from the models’ inability to recognize and
retain tokens that are already correct: without such a signal, a greedy
step has no basis for leaving a position unchanged.

##### Ancestral sampling masks the correct-token retention deficit.

The retention seen under ancestral sampling comes from the noise
schedule, not from the model. Under
equation [2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"),
a position that disagrees with the model’s prediction adopts it with
probability only $`\tfrac{\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}`$
([Appendix D](#A4 "Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
which is $`O(1/T)`$ for any smooth schedule
([Section D.2](#A4.SS2 "D.2 The schedule bounds the number of changes per step ‣ Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")):
decoded tokens persist largely because the sampler rarely acts on the
model’s prediction, not because the model would keep them. This masking
is weakest near the terminal region, where the greedy tail operates, yet
even there at most roughly one in five disagreeing positions adopts the
prediction (under the log-linear schedule of DUO and UDLM, for which
$`\alpha_{t}=1-t`$;
[Table 2](#A4.T2 "In D.2 The schedule bounds the number of changes per step ‣ Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
Absent genuine correct-token retention, which positions get revised is
thus decided by sampling stochasticity rather than by the model;
removing this bound, as the greedy tail does, exposes the deficit
directly.

##### The NELBO barely rewards retention.

To quantify how much the training objective penalizes this deficit, we
split validation positions into clean ($`z_{t}=x`$) and corrupted
($`z_{t}\neq x`$), and by whether the model’s prediction
$`\hat{x}_{\theta}=\mathrm{onehot}(\arg\max_{j}[x_{\theta}]_{j})`$
equals the clean token, and average the per-token NELBO of NELBO-only
models within each group and noise level
([Figure 5](#S3.F5 "In The NELBO barely rewards retention. ‣ 3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
At corrupted positions, an incorrect prediction is heavily penalized:
for $`1-\alpha_{t}<0.4`$ it costs 13–100 nats more than a correct one.
At clean positions, the same error costs only 0.09–1.32 nats more;
overall, mispredicted clean positions make up 23–31% of all positions
yet only 3.5–5.2% of the NELBO, versus 90–92% for mispredicted corrupted
ones
([Section E.2](#A5.SS2 "E.2 Decomposing the NELBO by Position Type and Prediction ‣ Appendix E Likelihood Analysis ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
This holds for all three models on all six corpora
([Figures 5](#S3.F5 "In The NELBO barely rewards retention. ‣ 3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
and [8](#A5.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ E.2 Decomposing the NELBO by Position Type and Prediction ‣ Appendix E Likelihood Analysis ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
The NELBO thus provides almost no signal for retaining correct tokens,
consistent with their near-identical clean- and corrupted-token
accuracies
([Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).

### 3.2 Correct-Token Retention Regularization (CTR-Reg)

Since the NELBO provides almost no signal at clean positions
([Figure 5](#S3.F5 "In The NELBO barely rewards retention. ‣ 3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
we add one explicitly. CTR-Reg applies only at positions left
unperturbed by the forward process ($`z_{t}=x`$), where the displayed
token is already correct, and rewards the model for predicting that
token with high probability:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f_{\mathrm{CTR}}\big(z_{t},x_{\theta}(z_{t},t);x\big)\;=\;\mathbb{I}\big[z_{t}=x\big]\;\Big(-\log\big\langle x_{\theta}(z_{t},t),\,x\big\rangle\Big),
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\langle x_{\theta},x\rangle=(x_{\theta})_{m}`$ is the
probability the denoiser assigns to the clean token and $`m`$ indexes
its non-zero entry; the indicator restricts the term to correct tokens
and directly reinforces their retention. The full objective adds this
term to the model’s NELBO $`\mathcal{L}^{\infty}`$
(equation [5](#S2.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"),
whose per-token form differs across USDMs) through a single scalar
$`\lambda\geq 0`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}\;=\;\mathcal{L}^{\infty}\;+\;\lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}\,\mathbb{E}_{z_{t}\sim q_{t}(\cdot\mid x)}\textstyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}f_{\mathrm{CTR}}\big(z_{t}^{(\ell)},x_{\theta}^{(\ell)}(z_{t}^{(1:L)},t);x^{(\ell)}\big).
``` |  | (9) |

##### CTR-Reg preserves the likelihood bound.

Since $`\langle x_{\theta},x\rangle\in(0,1]`$, we have
$`-\log\langle x_{\theta},x\rangle\geq 0`$; with $`\lambda\geq 0`$ and
the indicator non-negative, the CTR-Reg term is non-negative pointwise.
Hence

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}\;=\;\mathcal{L}^{\infty}+\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\big[f_{\mathrm{CTR}}\big]\;\geq\;\mathcal{L}^{\infty}\;\geq\;-\log p_{\theta}(x).
``` |  | (10) |

Adding a non-negative quantity to an upper bound leaves an upper bound:
training with CTR-Reg still minimizes a valid bound on the negative
log-likelihood, merely a looser one. Empirically, the likelihood cost is
small: validation perplexity changes by $`+1.5\%`$, $`+1.2\%`$, and
$`-2.4\%`$ on average for DUO, UDLM, and SEDD
([Section E.1](#A5.SS1 "E.1 Validation Perplexity ‣ Appendix E Likelihood Analysis ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
even though clean-token accuracy rises by 26.5 points
([Section 4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 CTR-Reg Improves Retention Without Hurting Revision ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
further indicating that the likelihood is largely insensitive to
retention.

##### Cost and hyperparameter.

CTR-Reg acts only at training time and reuses the same forward pass as
the NELBO, so it adds no computational overhead and leaves the sampler
unchanged. We use $`\lambda=0.02`$ for every USDM, and ablate it in
[Section 4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Regularization Strength Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").

### 3.3 Applying CTR-Reg to Score-Parameterized Models

UDLM and DUO predict the clean data directly, so
equation [8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Correct-Token Retention Regularization (CTR-Reg) ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
applies verbatim. SEDD, in contrast, outputs the log concrete score
$`\log s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)`$, whose $`j`$-th entry estimates
$`\log\big(q_{t}(j)/q_{t}(i)\big)`$ relative to the displayed token
$`i`$. These ratios determine the reverse transition rates from $`i`$ to
$`j`$ ([Lou et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)); because the uniform forward rate
is identical across all $`j\neq i`$, $`s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)_{j}`$
directly measures how strongly the model favors moving from $`i`$ to
$`j`$. The displayed token has a fixed self-score
$`s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)_{i}=1`$, so the preference for retaining $`i`$ is
encoded jointly by the off-diagonal scores and increases as they
decrease. Consequently,
equation [8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Correct-Token Retention Regularization (CTR-Reg) ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
does not apply directly to SEDD. We instead formulate the penalty over
the off-diagonal scores, penalizing scores above SEDD’s training target
with a smooth hinge:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
f_{\mathrm{CTR}}^{\mathrm{score}}\;=\;\mathbb{I}\big[z_{t}=x\big]\,\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{softplus}\!\left(\log\frac{s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)_{j}}{\rho_{t}}\right),\qquad\rho_{t}=\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{K\alpha_{t}+1-\alpha_{t}}.
``` |  | (11) |

Here, $`\rho_{t}=q_{t}(j\mid x)/q_{t}(m\mid x)`$ is the target value of
every off-diagonal score at an unperturbed position ($`i=m`$)
([Section G.2](#A7.SS2 "G.2 The training target 𝜌_𝑡 ‣ Appendix G CTR-Reg for Score-Parameterized Models: Details ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
We use a softplus hinge to obtain a non-negative penalty that vanishes
smoothly below the target; adding
$`\lambda\,\mathbb{E}\big[f_{\mathrm{CTR}}^{\mathrm{score}}\big]`$ to
SEDD’s objective therefore preserves the bound argument of
[Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Correct-Token Retention Regularization (CTR-Reg) ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").
Dividing by $`\rho_{t}`$ places the hinge at the training target:
without this normalization, $`\mathrm{softplus}(\log s)`$ becomes
negligible as soon as $`s<1`$, which can be substantially above
$`\rho_{t}`$. With it, the penalty pushes scores above $`\rho_{t}`$
downward and fades below it, where SEDD’s own objective already holds
them
([Section G.3](#A7.SS3 "G.3 Placing the hinge at the target ‣ Appendix G CTR-Reg for Score-Parameterized Models: Details ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate the effectiveness of CTR-Reg on three USDMs, namely
DUO ([Sahoo et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)), UDLM ([Schiff et al.,
2024](#bib.bib16)), and SEDD with uniform noise ([Lou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9)), each trained with the standard NELBO objective
(*NELBO-only*) and with NELBO + CTR-Reg. We address three questions:
(i) does CTR-Reg improve correct-token retention without impairing the
revision of incorrect tokens
([Section 4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 CTR-Reg Improves Retention Without Hurting Revision ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"));
(ii) does better retention translate into better unconditional
generation
([Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Better Retention Yields Better Generations ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"));
and (iii) how does CTR-Reg prevent the diversity collapse under
greedy-tail decoding
([Section 4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 How CTR-Reg Resolves the Diversity Collapse ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"))?

##### Implementation Details.

We train the three models with the two objectives above. All models use
the same 169M-parameter transformer and are trained from scratch on
OpenWebText ([Gokaslan and Cohen, 2019](#bib.bib23)) ($`L=512`$, 77K
steps, batch size 512, $`{\approx}`$20B tokens), with the optimizer and
remaining settings following each model’s official implementation:
log-linear noise for DUO and UDLM, geometric noise for SEDD, and DUO’s
Gaussian curriculum ([Sahoo et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)) over the first
half of training. CTR-Reg uses a constant $`\lambda=0.02`$ for all
models; NELBO-only baselines use identical settings with $`\lambda=0`$.
Unless stated otherwise, we sample with 512 steps and report Gen PPL
under GPT-2 Large ([Radford et al., 2019](#bib.bib31)) and mean unigram
entropy over 1,024 samples, and MAUVE ([Pillutla et al.,
2021](#bib.bib25)) between 5,000 generated and 5,000 OpenWebText
sequences.

### 4.1 CTR-Reg Improves Retention Without Hurting Revision

##### Protocol.

We evaluate retention using a controlled random-token corruption
experiment. We split each evaluation corpus into sequences of $`L=512`$
tokens and randomly replace $`p\%`$ of the positions with tokens drawn
uniformly from the vocabulary. Each model then reconstructs the
corrupted sequence in a single forward pass, taking the argmax of
$`x_{\theta}^{(\ell)}`$ at each position, with the time input set to the
corruption level corresponding to $`p`$. We report accuracy, i.e., the
fraction of positions whose prediction matches the original token,
separately on the corrupted positions (*Corrupted-Token Accuracy*,
measuring revision) and the remaining $`(100-p)\%`$ of positions
(*Clean-Token Accuracy*, measuring retention), averaged over
$`p\in\{10,20,30,40\}\%`$. For a model that recognizes and preserves
correct tokens, clean positions are the easier target: the correct
prediction is simply the observed token.

##### Results.

[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
reports both accuracies on the OpenWebText validation split (last 100K
documents) and arXiv ([Cohan et al., 2018](#bib.bib22));
[Table 9](#A8.T9 "In Appendix H Random-Token Corruption on Additional Corpora ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
([Appendix H](#A8 "Appendix H Random-Token Corruption on Additional Corpora ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"))
extends the evaluation to six corpora spanning web, news, scientific,
biomedical, and encyclopedic text by adding LM1B ([Chelba et al.,
2014](#bib.bib24)), AG News ([Zhang et al., 2016](#bib.bib21)),
PubMed ([Cohan et al., 2018](#bib.bib22)), and WikiText-103 ([Merity et
al., 2017](#bib.bib20)). NELBO-only models achieve nearly identical
accuracy on clean and corrupted positions despite clean positions being
the easier target, indicating that they revise tokens indiscriminately.
This is not an artifact of undertraining: the official DUO checkpoint,
trained on 26$`\times`$ more data, raises both accuracies by 5–6 points
but leaves the clean-minus-corrupted gap at 1.1 points (vs. 2.0 at 20B),
and CTR-Reg at 20B exceeds the checkpoint’s clean-token accuracy by 24.7
points (77.4 vs. 52.7;
[Appendix F](#A6 "Appendix F Training on a Larger Corpus Does Not Fix the Problem ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
CTR-Reg improves Clean-Token Accuracy for every model and corpus, by
26.5 points on average, while changing Corrupted-Token Accuracy by only
0.7 points per cell on average
([Appendix H](#A8 "Appendix H Random-Token Corruption on Additional Corpora ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
CTR-Reg thus substantially improves retention without compromising
revision.

### 4.2 Better Retention Yields Better Generations

We next test whether the single-step retention gains from CTR-Reg
translate into better full generations. We sample with the multi-step
greedy-tail sampler
([Section 3.1](#S3.SS1.SSS0.Px1 "Multi-step greedy-tail sampling. ‣ 3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
which appends a few deterministic denoising steps (a forward pass
followed by a per-position argmax, with no stochastic sampling) after
standard ancestral sampling. Because no sampling randomness is involved,
whether a position is kept or rewritten depends only on the model’s
prediction. A model that cannot recognize already-correct tokens
therefore has no basis for leaving them untouched, so greedy-tail
decoding directly exposes the retention deficit identified in
[Section 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").
We evaluate the quality and diversity of the resulting unconditionally
generated samples to assess the effectiveness of CTR-Reg.

##### Generative Perplexity vs. Entropy.

[Figure 4](#S2.F4 "In Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
plots Gen PPL against entropy as the number of greedy-tail steps grows
from $`g=0`$ to $`g=20`$ at $`T=512`$ (exact values in
[Section I.1](#A9.SS1 "I.1 Exact Values for the Greedy-Tail Step Sweep ‣ Appendix I Additional Experimental Results ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
For NELBO-only models, greedy-tail steps lower Gen PPL but push entropy
below that of real text, so the lower Gen PPL reflects a collapse toward
repetitive output rather than better text, consistent with the retention
deficit measured in
[Section 4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 CTR-Reg Improves Retention Without Hurting Revision ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").
CTR-Reg instead moves all three models toward lower Gen PPL at higher
entropy, reaching substantially lower Gen PPL while keeping entropy at
or above that of real OpenWebText. A few steps suffice: from $`g=0`$ to
$`g=5`$, CTR-Reg lowers Gen PPL from 105.86 to 49.57 for DUO, from 93.28
to 44.31 for UDLM, and from 194.59 to 87.19 for SEDD. The benefit
already appears under DUO’s default sampler ($`g=1`$), with no change to
sampling: CTR-Reg reduces DUO’s Gen PPL from 110.88 (NELBO-only) to
64.67 while raising entropy from 4.96 to 5.09. These gains hold across
step budgets from 16 to 1024, including the ultra-low-step regime
([Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Step-Budget Analysis ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).

##### MAUVE.

To confirm the Gen PPL and entropy trends, we additionally evaluate
MAUVE ([Pillutla et al., 2021](#bib.bib25)). Whereas Gen PPL and entropy
measure quality and diversity separately, MAUVE captures both jointly by
comparing the distributions of generated and human-written text.
[Figure 3](#S1.F3 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
reports MAUVE as a function of $`g`$ (exact values in
[Section I.3](#A9.SS3 "I.3 Exact Values for the MAUVE Greedy-Tail Sweep ‣ Appendix I Additional Experimental Results ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
At $`g=0`$, NELBO-only and CTR-Reg models score comparably, so CTR-Reg
does not degrade standard ancestral sampling. As $`g`$ grows, NELBO-only
models collapse: within 6–9 steps, MAUVE falls from $`\sim`$0.70–0.75 to
as low as 0.13–0.17 for DUO and UDLM, and from 0.47 to 0.15 for SEDD.
CTR-Reg models instead improve with additional steps, reaching 0.81
(DUO), 0.83 (UDLM), and 0.58 (SEDD).

### 4.3 How CTR-Reg Resolves the Diversity Collapse

We repeat the token-change analysis of
[Section 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
with the same setup
([Figure 3](#S1.F3 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
There, NELBO-only models still revise 173–270 of the 512 positions at
the twentieth greedy-tail step, and these independent, simultaneous
edits compound into the entropy and MAUVE collapse of
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Better Retention Yields Better Generations ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").
CTR-Reg removes this churn: for all three models, the number of changed
positions at the first greedy-tail step is already about one third of
the NELBO-only count and keeps decreasing to a stable plateau of 3–11
positions, so greedy decoding converges to a near-fixed point instead of
repeatedly rewriting the sequence. The corruption experiment
([Section 4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 CTR-Reg Improves Retention Without Hurting Revision ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
where ground truth is available, confirms that this selectivity is well
placed: CTR-Reg models retain clean tokens while still correcting
corrupted ones as often as NELBO-only models. Bounded, targeted revision
thus preserves sequence structure, whereas indiscriminate revision
destroys it, which accounts for the Gen PPL, entropy, and MAUVE gaps in
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Better Retention Yields Better Generations ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").

### 4.4 Regularization Strength Analysis

We vary $`\lambda\in\{0,0.02,0.1\}`$ on DUO and UDLM ($`\lambda=0`$:
NELBO-only; main experiments use $`\lambda=0.02`$). Raising $`\lambda`$
from 0.02 to 0.1 further improves Clean-Token Accuracy (DUO:
77.4%$`\to`$87.2%; UDLM: 72.8%$`\to`$83.6%), while Corrupted-Token
Accuracy stays largely unchanged
([Table 1](#S4.T1 "In 4.3 How CTR-Reg Resolves the Diversity Collapse ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models");
per-rate results in
[Section I.6](#A9.SS6 "I.6 Per-Corruption-Rate Breakdown for the CTR-Reg Coefficient Ablation ‣ Appendix I Additional Experimental Results ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
To verify that CTR-Reg does not simply learn to copy its input, we also
report the copy rate: the percentage of corrupted positions where the
model keeps the random replacement token instead of recovering the
original one. It is near zero for NELBO-only models (0.003–0.007%) and
grows with $`\lambda`$ (DUO: 0.05%/0.22%; UDLM: 0.03%/0.11% at
$`\lambda=0.02/0.1`$), yet stays below 0.25% throughout: CTR-Reg retains
tokens selectively rather than copying them. Stronger retention,
however, does not yield better generation
([Figure 6](#S4.F6 "In Table 1 ‣ 4.3 How CTR-Reg Resolves the Diversity Collapse ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")):
$`\lambda=0.1`$ matches smaller $`\lambda`$ under ancestral sampling
($`g=0`$) and avoids the diversity collapse under greedy-tail decoding,
but gains less from additional greedy-tail steps than $`\lambda=0.02`$.
Consistent with its higher copy rate, excessive retention can thus
hinder the revision of earlier sampling mistakes, whereas
$`\lambda=0.02`$ better balances retaining correct tokens and revising
uncertain ones, yielding stronger generation for both models.

## 5 Conclusion

Self-correction in USDMs requires both revising incorrect tokens and
retaining correct ones; current USDMs lack retention, and their NELBO
barely rewards it. CTR-Reg, a simple auxiliary loss on unperturbed
tokens that leaves the sampler unchanged, raises clean-token accuracy by
26.5 points on average without hurting revision and more than halves
generative perplexity within five greedy-tail steps while preserving
diversity, across DUO, UDLM, and SEDD. Correct-token retention is thus a
key ingredient for self-correcting diffusion language models.

## Appendix C Per-Model USDM Objectives

This appendix gives the explicit training objectives of DUO and SEDD,
both of which are equivalent to the continuous-time bound of
equation [5](#S2.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
that UDLM optimizes directly. We reuse the notation of
[Section 2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"):
$`i`$ indexes the non-zero entry of $`z_{t}`$,
$`\bar{x}=K\alpha_{t}x+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{1}`$,
$`\bar{x}_{\theta}=K\alpha_{t}x_{\theta}(z_{t},t)+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{1}`$,
and $`\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=d\alpha_{t}/dt`$.

##### DUO.

[Sahoo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib13) rewrite
equation [6](#S2.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
so that $`\bar{x}`$ never has to be materialized, and compute certain
expectations analytically via Rao-Blackwellization; the authors describe
the result as an efficient, low-variance formulation with minimal memory
overhead. With $`m`$ the index of the non-zero entry of $`x`$ and
$`\rho_{t}=(1-\alpha_{t})/(K\alpha_{t}+1-\alpha_{t})`$, the per-token
loss becomes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}f_{\mathrm{DUO}}={}&\frac{\alpha_{t}^{\prime}}{K\alpha_{t}}\Bigg[\frac{K}{\bar{x}_{i}}-\frac{K}{(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}}-\big(\rho_{t}\,\mathbb{I}[z_{t}=x]+\mathbb{I}[z_{t}\neq x]\big)\sum_{j=1}^{K}\log\frac{(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}}{(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{j}}\\
&-\frac{K\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}}\,\mathbb{I}[z_{t}\neq x]\log\frac{(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}}{(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{m}}-\Big((K-1)\rho_{t}\,\mathbb{I}[z_{t}=x]-\frac{\mathbb{I}[z_{t}\neq x]}{\rho_{t}}\Big)\log\rho_{t}\Bigg].\end{split}
``` |  | (12) |

DUO further accelerates training with a curriculum that relaxes the
discrete latents through an underlying Gaussian diffusion ([Sahoo et
al., 2025](#bib.bib13)), which [Deschenaux et al. (2026)](#bib.bib17)
later make more memory-efficient.

##### SEDD and CTMC-based formulations.

Rather than bounding a discrete-time chain and taking that bound to the
continuous-time limit, [Campbell et al. (2022)](#bib.bib27) and
SEDD ([Lou et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)) formulate discrete diffusion
directly with continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs); both still optimize
a bound on the likelihood. The forward process is defined by a rate
matrix $`R_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}`$ through
$`q(z_{t}=z^{\prime}\mid z_{s}=z)=\delta_{z^{\prime},z}+R_{t}(z,z^{\prime})/T+o(1/T)`$
with $`s=t-1/T`$. For the uniform interpolating process,
$`R_{t}=-\frac{\alpha_{t}^{\prime}}{K\alpha_{t}}(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-KI)`$ ([Schiff
et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)), which coincides with SEDD-Uniform’s rate
$`\sigma(t)(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}/K-I)`$ for
$`\alpha_{t}=e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}`$ and
$`\bar{\sigma}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(u)\,du`$. Reversing this chain
requires ratios of marginals: for $`z^{\prime}\neq z`$, the reverse rate
is
$`\hat{R}_{t}(z,z^{\prime})=\frac{q_{t}(z^{\prime})}{q_{t}(z)}R_{t}(z^{\prime},z)`$.
These ratios, the concrete score ([Meng et al., 2022](#bib.bib28)),
replace the score $`\nabla\log q_{t}`$ used by continuous score-based
models ([Song et al., 2021](#bib.bib30)), since discrete data admit no
gradient. Because tokens are corrupted independently, only sequences
that differ from $`z_{t}`$ in a single position have non-zero rate.
Whereas UDLM, DUO and [Campbell et al. (2022)](#bib.bib27) predict the
clean data, SEDD’s network outputs these ratios directly, one per
position and candidate token:
$`s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times K}`$ with
$`s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)_{z^{\prime}}\approx q_{t}(z^{\prime})/q_{t}(z_{t})`$.
It is trained with the diffusion-weighted denoising score entropy, which
forms a NELBO ([Lou et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SEDD}}=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}\,\mathbb{E}_{z_{t}\sim q_{t}(\cdot\mid x)}\sum_{z^{\prime}\neq z_{t}}R_{t}(z^{\prime},z_{t})\Big[s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)_{z^{\prime}}-r_{t}(z^{\prime})\log s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)_{z^{\prime}}+g\big(r_{t}(z^{\prime})\big)\Big],
``` |  | (13) |

where $`r_{t}(z^{\prime})=q_{t}(z^{\prime}\mid x)/q_{t}(z_{t}\mid x)`$
and $`g(a)=a(\log a-1)`$. Under the mean parameterization
$`s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)_{z^{\prime}}=(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{j}/(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}`$,
with $`j`$ indexing the non-zero entry of $`z^{\prime}`$, [Schiff et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib16) show that
equation [13](#A3.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ SEDD and CTMC-based formulations. ‣ Appendix C Per-Model USDM Objectives ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
is equivalent to
equation [5](#S2.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").

## Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes

We show that the ancestral sampler’s noise schedule, rather than the
denoiser’s predictions, is what prevents the sampler from adopting the
model’s predictions en masse. The schedule assigns most of its
probability mass to retaining the currently decoded token, which bounds
the number of positions that can be revised toward the model’s
prediction at any single step.

### D.1 Single-position transition probabilities

Consider a position $`\ell`$ with $`z_{t}^{\ell}\neq x_{\theta}^{\ell}`$
(both one-hot), so that we are asking how readily the sampler
incorporates a change the model has already decided on. The cross term
in the reverse posterior
(equation [2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"))
then vanishes, since $`z_{t}^{\ell}\odot x_{\theta}^{\ell}=\mathbf{0}`$
and $`\langle z_{t}^{\ell},x_{\theta}^{\ell}\rangle=0`$, leaving

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{s|t}(z_{s}^{\ell}\mid z_{t}^{\ell},x_{\theta}^{\ell})=\mathrm{Cat}\left(\cdot\,;\,\frac{(\alpha_{t|s}-\alpha_{t})z_{t}^{\ell}+(\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t})x_{\theta}^{\ell}+(1-\alpha_{t|s})(1-\alpha_{s})\mathbf{1}/K}{1-\alpha_{t}}\right).
``` |  |

Collecting the weight assigned to each of the $`K`$ categories and
dropping $`O(1/K)`$ terms (negligible for $`K\approx 50\text{k}`$)
yields three outcomes:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P(\text{stay})\approx\frac{\alpha_{t|s}-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}},
``` |  | (14) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P(\text{jump-to-prediction})\approx\frac{\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t}}{1-\alpha_{t}},
``` |  | (15) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P(\text{jump-elsewhere})\approx\frac{(1-\alpha_{t|s})(1-\alpha_{s})}{1-\alpha_{t}}.
``` |  | (16) |

Summing the exact, un-truncated weights and applying
$`\alpha_{t|s}\alpha_{s}=\alpha_{t}`$ confirms that these normalize to
one. Note that
equation [14](#A4.E14 "Equation 14 ‣ D.1 Single-position transition probabilities ‣ Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")–equation [16](#A4.E16 "Equation 16 ‣ D.1 Single-position transition probabilities ‣ Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
assume no functional form for $`\alpha_{t}`$ and therefore hold for any
interpolating noise schedule.

### D.2 The schedule bounds the number of changes per step

Under the log-linear noise schedule used in our experiments,
$`\bar{\sigma}(t)=-\log\big(1-(1-\epsilon)t\big)`$, so
$`\alpha_{t}=e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}=1-(1-\epsilon)t`$ is linear in $`t`$;
with $`\epsilon=10^{-3}`$ we write $`\alpha_{t}\approx 1-t`$. With step
size $`h=1/T`$, we have $`\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t}=(1-\epsilon)h\approx h`$
at every step, so
equation [15](#A4.E15 "Equation 15 ‣ D.1 Single-position transition probabilities ‣ Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
simplifies to

|     |                                                            |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       P(\text{jump-to-prediction})\approx\frac{h}{1-\alpha_{t}}.
       ```                                                         |     | (17) |

This is $`O(1/T)`$ for any $`\alpha_{t}`$ bounded away from one, and
approaches $`O(1)`$ only as the remaining noise budget $`1-\alpha_{t}`$
shrinks toward $`h`$. For a general smooth schedule,
$`\alpha_{s}-\alpha_{t}\approx|\alpha^{\prime}_{t}|h`$, so
equation [17](#A4.E17 "Equation 17 ‣ D.2 The schedule bounds the number of changes per step ‣ Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
becomes $`|\alpha^{\prime}_{t}|h/(1-\alpha_{t})`$: the $`O(1/T)`$
per-step suppression holds for any such schedule, which enters only
through $`|\alpha^{\prime}_{t}|`$. We specialize to the linear case for
concreteness, matching our experimental setup.

Consequently, if $`m`$ positions disagree with the denoiser’s prediction
at step $`t`$, the expected number that adopt that prediction on the
step is

|     |                                                             |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[\#\text{changed}]\approx\frac{mh}{1-\alpha_{t}},
       ```                                                          |     | (18) |

which remains small whenever $`h\ll 1-\alpha_{t}`$. Through most of the
trajectory the sampler therefore revises only a small fraction of the
disagreeing positions per step, never all $`m`$ simultaneously; the
bound loosens only near the terminal region.

| $`\alpha_{t}`$ | stay  | jump-to-prediction | jump-elsewhere |
|----------------|-------|--------------------|----------------|
| 0.01           | 0.835 | 0.002              | 0.163          |

### D.3 Why the greedy tail reveals what ancestral sampling hides

The greedy tail sets $`z_{s}^{\ell}=x_{\theta}^{\ell}`$
deterministically, so $`P_{g}(\text{jump-to-prediction})=1`$ for every
$`\alpha_{t}`$ and $`h`$. This removes the suppression term in
equation [17](#A4.E17 "Equation 17 ‣ D.2 The schedule bounds the number of changes per step ‣ Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
and lifts the bound in
equation [18](#A4.E18 "Equation 18 ‣ D.2 The schedule bounds the number of changes per step ‣ Appendix D Ancestral Sampling Suppresses Indiscriminate Token Changes ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
entirely: all $`m`$ disagreeing positions update at once, rather than
the $`O(mh)`$ fraction permitted by ancestral sampling. A denoiser whose
argmax is unstable across steps therefore exhibits little visible
instability under ancestral sampling, while the same instability is
fully exposed under the greedy tail. The near-complete, non-converging
rewrites in
[Figure 3](#S1.F3 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
indicate that this instability was present throughout ancestral sampling
and was simply not surfaced.

### E.2 Decomposing the NELBO by Position Type and Prediction

To measure how much the likelihood rewards retention, we split every
validation position into four groups: *clean* ($`z_{t}=x`$) or
*corrupted* ($`z_{t}\neq x`$), and whether the model’s argmax prediction
$`\hat{x}_{\theta}`$ equals the clean token ($`\hat{x}_{\theta}=x`$) or
not ($`\hat{x}_{\theta}\neq x`$). For SEDD, $`\hat{x}_{\theta}`$ is
taken directly over the scores, which yields the same ranking as
$`x_{\theta}`$
([Section G.1](#A7.SS1 "G.1 Reading predictions from scores ‣ Appendix G CTR-Reg for Score-Parameterized Models: Details ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).
Positions where a uniform draw happens to coincide with the original
token count as clean, consistent with the NELBO. We average the
per-token NELBO within each group and within ten bins of the noise level
$`1-\alpha_{t}`$, the expected fraction of corrupted tokens. For DUO and
UDLM, whose log-linear schedule gives $`1-\alpha_{t}\approx t`$, these
bins are bins of $`t`$. SEDD uses a geometric schedule, so its bins are
bins of $`1-\alpha_{t}=1-e^{-\sigma(t)}`$, and its per-token magnitudes
are not directly comparable to those of the other two models.

## Appendix F Training on a Larger Corpus Does Not Fix the Problem

Owing to computational cost, our main experiments train DUO, UDLM, and
SEDD from scratch on 20B OpenWebText tokens (169M parameters, sequence
length 512). A natural question is whether the correct-token retention
deficit we identify is simply an artifact of undertraining, and would
disappear if the model were trained on substantially more data. To test
this, we repeat both the random-token corruption analysis and the
greedy-tail sampling analysis on the official, publicly released DUO
checkpoint ([Sahoo et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)), which is trained on
approximately 524B tokens ($`\sim`$26$`\times`$ more than our 20B-token
models) with a native sequence length of 1024 (rather than 512). For the
corruption experiment, sequences are chunked to this checkpoint’s native
length of 1024 tokens rather than 512, so that the model is evaluated
in-distribution; because of this sequence-length mismatch, we do not
directly compare PPL or entropy magnitudes between the 524B-token
checkpoint and our 512-length models (see below).

##### Random-token corruption at scale.

Table [7](#A6.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Random-token corruption at scale. ‣ Appendix F Training on a Larger Corpus Does Not Fix the Problem ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
reports corrupted-token, clean-token, and overall reconstruction
accuracy for the 524B-token DUO checkpoint alongside our 20B-token
NELBO-only and NELBO + CTR-Reg models, following the same protocol as
Table [9](#A8.T9 "Table 9 ‣ Appendix H Random-Token Corruption on Additional Corpora ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").
Scaling to 26$`\times`$ more training tokens raises corrupted-token
accuracy substantially (avg. 45.2% $`\rightarrow`$ 51.6%), as expected:
more data simply makes the model a better denoiser. However, clean-token
accuracy improves by only a comparable amount (avg.
47.2% $`\rightarrow`$ 52.7%), so the model still cannot tell
already-correct tokens apart from corrupted ones: the average
clean-minus-corrupted gap is 2.0 points at 20B tokens and only 1.1
points at 524B tokens, i.e., scaling by 26$`\times`$ leaves this gap
unchanged within noise. In contrast, CTR-Reg opens a 31.1-point average
gap using only the 20B-token budget, and its clean-token accuracy
exceeds the 524B-token checkpoint’s by nearly 25 percentage points on
average (77.4% vs. 52.7%) despite seeing 26$`\times`$ fewer training
tokens. This indicates that correct-token retention is not a data-scale
bottleneck: training on $`26\times`$ more data does not close the gap,
whereas CTR-Reg closes it within the 20B-token budget.

## Appendix G CTR-Reg for Score-Parameterized Models: Details

This appendix supports
[Section 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Applying CTR-Reg to Score-Parameterized Models ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models").
Throughout, $`i`$ indexes the displayed token, $`m`$ the clean token,
and we write $`s_{v}`$ for $`s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)_{v}`$.

### G.1 Reading predictions from scores

The network emits $`\log s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)`$, so the scores $`s_{v}`$
are positive. Under the mean parameterization
$`s_{v}=(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{v}/(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}`$ they determine
$`\bar{x}_{\theta}`$ only up to its common factor
$`(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}`$, which is positive since
$`\bar{x}_{\theta}\geq 1-\alpha_{t}>0`$ entrywise. Inverting
$`\bar{x}_{\theta}=K\alpha_{t}x_{\theta}+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{1}`$ then
gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
(x_{\theta})_{v}=\frac{(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}\,s_{v}-(1-\alpha_{t})}{K\alpha_{t}},
``` |  | (19) |

which is strictly increasing in $`s_{v}`$ because
$`(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}>0`$ is shared by all $`v`$. Ranking tokens by
score therefore ranks them by $`x_{\theta}`$, whatever the value of the
common factor, and argmax predictions, in greedy-tail decoding
(equation [7](#S3.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ Multi-step greedy-tail sampling. ‣ 3.1 Current USDMs Lack Correct-Token Retention ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"))
and in the random-token corruption experiment
([Section 4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 CTR-Reg Improves Retention Without Hurting Revision ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")),
are taken directly over the scores. With $`s_{i}=1`$, the rule reads
directly: $`s_{j}<1\iff(x_{\theta})_{j}<(x_{\theta})_{i}`$, so the
displayed token is kept exactly when the model ranks it above every
alternative.

### G.2 The training target $`\rho_{t}`$

##### SEDD’s objective is a per-coordinate regression onto marginal ratios.

In
equation [13](#A3.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ SEDD and CTMC-based formulations. ‣ Appendix C Per-Model USDM Objectives ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models"),
each off-diagonal score is trained through a term
$`s_{j}-r_{t}(j)\log s_{j}`$, minimized at $`s_{j}=r_{t}(j)`$, where
$`r_{t}(j)=q_{t}(j\mid x)/q_{t}(i\mid x)`$; the remaining factor
$`g(r_{t}(j))`$ does not involve $`\theta`$, and for uniform noise the
weight $`R_{t}(z_{t},z^{\prime})`$ is identical across all $`j\neq i`$.
The sum runs over $`j\neq i`$ only, so the displayed token’s own score
is never trained: it equals $`q_{t}(i\mid x)/q_{t}(i\mid x)=1`$ by
definition, which the network enforces by fixing the corresponding
log-score to zero. Writing the forward marginal
equation [1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
with $`\pi=\mathbf{1}/K`$ as
$`q_{t}(v\mid x)=\alpha_{t}[v=m]+(1-\alpha_{t})/K`$, and recalling
$`\bar{x}=K\,q_{t}(\cdot\mid x)`$, the targets are
$`r_{t}(j)=\bar{x}_{j}/\bar{x}_{i}`$, that is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{t}(j)=\begin{cases}\rho_{t}=\dfrac{1-\alpha_{t}}{K\alpha_{t}+1-\alpha_{t}},&\text{clean position }(i=m),\ \forall j\neq i,\\[8.0pt]
1/\rho_{t},&\text{corrupted position }(i\neq m),\ j=m,\\[4.0pt]
1,&\text{corrupted position},\ j\notin\{i,m\}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (20) |

Whenever $`K\alpha_{t}\gg 1-\alpha_{t}`$, that is for all but the
noisiest $`t`$, $`\rho_{t}\ll 1`$: at a clean position the objective
pulls every off-diagonal score well below one, so that the displayed
token is retained, while at a corrupted one it pulls $`s_{m}`$ far above
one, so that the position is revised to $`m`$, leaving the remaining
wrong tokens at one. Substituting the parameterization
$`s_{j}=(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{j}/(\bar{x}_{\theta})_{i}`$ into
equation [13](#A3.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ SEDD and CTMC-based formulations. ‣ Appendix C Per-Model USDM Objectives ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
reproduces
equation [5](#S2.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ Continuous-time objective (NELBO). ‣ 2.2 Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
term by term, which is the sense in which the two objectives are
equivalent ([Schiff et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)).

equation [20](#A7.E20 "Equation 20 ‣ SEDD’s objective is a per-coordinate regression onto marginal ratios. ‣ G.2 The training target 𝜌_𝑡 ‣ Appendix G CTR-Reg for Score-Parameterized Models: Details ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
is what
equation [11](#S3.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ 3.3 Applying CTR-Reg to Score-Parameterized Models ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
builds on: at an unperturbed position every off-diagonal score has the
same target $`\rho_{t}`$, and the hinge is placed there
([Section G.3](#A7.SS3 "G.3 Placing the hinge at the target ‣ Appendix G CTR-Reg for Score-Parameterized Models: Details ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")).

### G.3 Placing the hinge at the target

The derivative of $`\mathrm{softplus}\big(\log(s_{j}/c)\big)`$ with
respect to $`\log s_{j}`$ is $`s_{j}/(s_{j}+c)`$: close to one for
$`s_{j}\gg c`$, one half at $`s_{j}=c`$, and vanishing for
$`s_{j}\ll c`$. The threshold $`c`$ therefore sets how far the penalty
pushes. With $`c=1`$ the push fades as soon as a score falls below one,
which by
equation [20](#A7.E20 "Equation 20 ‣ SEDD’s objective is a per-coordinate regression onto marginal ratios. ‣ G.2 The training target 𝜌_𝑡 ‣ Appendix G CTR-Reg for Score-Parameterized Models: Details ‣ Know When to Hold ’em: Correct-Token Retention in Uniform-State Diffusion Language Models")
is the target for tokens at a corrupted position, far above
$`\rho_{t}`$: at $`K=50257`$ and $`\alpha_{t}=0.9`$, where
$`\rho_{t}\approx 2.2\times 10^{-6}`$, the gradient at $`s_{j}=10^{-3}`$
is about $`10^{-3}`$ with $`c=1`$ but close to one with $`c=\rho_{t}`$.
With $`c=\rho_{t}`$ the push persists until the score reaches the
clean-position target and fades below it, since $`\rho_{t}`$ is already
the value SEDD’s own objective trains toward. Note that $`\log\rho_{t}`$
is constant in $`\theta`$; it matters only because it lies inside the
nonlinearity.
````

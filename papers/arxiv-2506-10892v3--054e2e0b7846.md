###### Abstract

Uniform-state discrete diffusion models hold the promise of fast text
generation due to their inherent ability to self-correct. However, they
are typically outperformed by autoregressive models and masked diffusion
models. In this work, we narrow this performance gap by leveraging a key
insight: Uniform-state diffusion processes naturally emerge from an
underlying Gaussian diffusion. Our method, Duo, transfers powerful
techniques from Gaussian diffusion to improve both training and
sampling. First, we introduce a curriculum learning strategy guided by
the Gaussian process, doubling training speed by reducing variance.
Models trained with curriculum learning surpass autoregressive models in
zero-shot perplexity on 3 of 7 benchmarks. Second, we present Discrete
Consistency Distillation, which adapts consistency distillation from the
continuous to the discrete setting. This algorithm unlocks few-step
generation in diffusion language models, accelerating sampling by two
orders of magnitude. We provide the code, model checkpoints, and video
tutorials on the project page:

[https://s-sahoo.com/duo](http://s-sahoo.github.io/duo)

Machine Learning, ICML

An eternal theme in mathematics is that discreteness emerges from
underlying continuity. From quantum mechanics, where the quantized
energy states of electrons arise as solutions to continuous wave
equations, to the binary logic of digital circuits, fundamentally driven
by smooth analog currents, discreteness has repeatedly and naturally
emerged from an underlying continuum. Our work continues this tradition
by demonstrating that a discrete diffusion process is, in fact, an
emergent phenomenon of an underlying continuous Gaussian diffusion
process. This perspective enables the design of faster training and
sampling algorithms for discrete diffusion models.

Diffusion models ([Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015](#bib.bib51)) are
powerful generative models inspired by physics. Gaussian diffusion
models excel at synthesizing realistic and high-quality
continuous-valued data such as images ([Ho et al., 2020](#bib.bib22);
[Rombach et al., 2022](#bib.bib43)), audio ([Kong et al.,
2021](#bib.bib29); [Liu et al., 2023b](#bib.bib33)), and videos ([Ho et
al., 2022](#bib.bib23); [Wu et al., 2023](#bib.bib62); [Esser et al.,
2023](#bib.bib15); [Blattmann et al., 2023](#bib.bib5)). Gaussian
diffusion is well studied—the success of these models is rooted in
techniques such as efficient parameterizations of the denoising model,
which improve upon the standard mean-parameterization ([Ho et al.,
2020](#bib.bib22); [Salimans & Ho, 2022](#bib.bib48); [Zheng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib66)), faster training techniques ([Kingma et al.,
2021](#bib.bib27)), efficient samplers ([Karras et al.,
2022](#bib.bib26)), and distillation schemes that enable single-step
generation ([Song et al., 2023](#bib.bib55); [Song & Dhariwal,
2023](#bib.bib53); [Yin et al., 2024](#bib.bib63)).

While Gaussian diffusion is well-studied, it underperforms discrete
diffusion models on tasks involving discrete data– such as text ([Sahoo
et al., 2024b](#bib.bib46); [Arriola et al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Schiff
et al., 2025](#bib.bib49); [Sahoo et al., 2025](#bib.bib47)), graphs
([Liu et al., 2023a](#bib.bib32)), and molecules ([Lee et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30)). However, from the perspective of Gaussian diffusion,
the design space for discrete diffusion models remains primitive: mean
parameterization for the denoising model ([Sahoo et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib45); [Schiff et al., 2025](#bib.bib49)) and slow
ancestral sampling ([Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib3)) are still the
dominant approaches. Recent work on distilling Masked Discrete Diffusion
Models (MDMs) improves sampling speed ([Deschenaux & Gulcehre,
2024](#bib.bib12)), but performance degrades severely in the few-step
regime. Unlike Gaussian diffusion models with Probability Flow ODEs
([Song et al., 2020](#bib.bib54)), MDMs lack an “implicit” property: a
deterministic mapping from noise to data. This property is vital for
few-step distillation methods ([Song et al., 2023](#bib.bib55); [Frans
et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)), but MDMs forgo it due to their deterministic
prior, requiring stochasticity in sampling to model the data
distribution.

Our objective is (1) to design a framework for discrete diffusion that
enables the transfer of advanced training and inference techniques from
Gaussian diffusion to discrete diffusion models. And (2) create language
models that support few-step generation. To this end, we focus on
Uniform-state Diffusion Models (USDMs) ([Austin et al.,
2021](#bib.bib3)). In this paper, we discover a remarkable property of
USDMs–these emerge from Gaussian diffusion processes as illustrated in
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ The Diffusion Duality"). We
call this phenomenon– the Diffusion Duality which expands the design
space of USDMs, making it possible to incorporate techniques developed
for Gaussian diffusion. Notably, USDMs models allow token updates during
reverse sampling unlike MDMs, naturally correcting earlier mistakes
without requiring costly predictor-corrector ([Zhao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib65); [Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib61)) steps—saving
function evaluations (NFEs). However, these models have historically
underperformed compared to MDMs ([Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib3); [Lou
et al., 2023](#bib.bib34)), raising the key question: Can USDMs be made
competitive with MDMs? And more importantly, can the implicit property
of the underlying Gaussian diffusion be leveraged for fast, few-step
generation?

We answer both questions with Duo, a rich framework of theoretical
connections between USDMs and Gaussian diffusion. Duo enriches the
design space of USDMs by incorporating Gaussian diffusion, which allows
us to develop efficient training strategies that accelerate the training
of USDMs, significantly reducing the performance gap between MDMs and AR
models on standard language generation benchmarks. Notably, we surpass
AR models on 3 out of 7 zero-shot datasets (Table
[2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Zero-Shot Likelihood Evaluation ‣ 5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
Furthermore, this duality allows us to adapt consistency distillation
([Song et al., 2023](#bib.bib55)) from Gaussian to discrete diffusion,
reducing NFEs from 1024 to 8 with minimal effect on sample quality (Sec.
[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Improved Sampling ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
Importantly, in the low-NFE regime, Duo outperforms MDMs. Our main
contributions are threefold: (1) We establish a theoretical connection
between continuous and discrete diffusion, demonstrating that discrete
diffusion arises from an underlying continuous Gaussian diffusion. This
insight enables the transfer of techniques from the continuous domain to
the discrete setting, opening up new possibilities. (2) Our framework
doubles the training speed of USDMs by introducing a low-variance
curriculum, and (3) accelerates sampling by two orders of magnitude by
adapting efficient distillation methods from continuous diffusion
models.

## 2 Background

##### Notation

We represent scalar discrete random variables that can take $`K`$ values
as ‘one-hot’ column vectors and define
$`\mathcal{V}=\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{K}:\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=1\}`$
as the set of all such vectors. Define
$`\text{Cat}(\cdot;\text{$\bm{\mathit{\pi}}$})`$ as the categorical
distribution over $`K`$ classes with probabilities given by
$`\text{$\bm{\mathit{\pi}}$}\in\Delta`$, where $`\Delta`$ denotes the
$`K`$-simplex. Additionally, let $`{\bm{1}}=\{1\}^{K}`$ and
$`\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle`$ and
$`\mathbf{a}\odot\mathbf{b}`$ respectively denote the dot and Hadamard
products between two vectors $`\mathbf{a}`$ and $`\mathbf{b}`$. We use
$`{\mathbf{x}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}`$
and
$`{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbf{x}}^{\ell}}]^{L}_{\ell=1}}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}`$
to denote sequences of length $`L`$.

### 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models

Consider the clean data $`{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{V}`$ drawn from the
data distribution $`q_{\text{data}}`$. In the discrete diffusion
framework ([Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015](#bib.bib51); [Austin et al.,
2021](#bib.bib3)) the complex data distribution $`q_{\text{data}}`$ is
mapped to a simple distribution through a sequence of Markov states.
[Sahoo et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib45) propose a simplified variant—an
interpolating noise framework—where the forward process
$`(q_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}`$ smoothly transitions from $`q_{\text{data}}`$ to
a prior distribution $`\text{Cat}(.;\text{$\bm{\mathit{\pi}}$}{})`$, by
introducing latent variables $`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\in\mathcal{V}`$ whose
marginals conditioned on $`{\mathbf{x}}`$ at time $`t`$ are given by:

|     |                                                           |                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |     |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q\_{t}}(. | {\mathbf{x}};{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha*{t}})=\text{Cat}(.;{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha*{t}}{\mathbf{x}}+(1-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha\_{t}})\text{$\bm{\mathit{\pi}}$}{}),`$ |     | (1) |

where the diffusion parameter
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}\in[0,1]`$ is a strictly
decreasing function in $`t`$, with
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t=0}}\approx 1`$ and
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t=1}}\approx 0`$. A discrete
diffusion process is characterized by the time evolution of marginals
follows a linear ordinary differential equation ([Anderson,
2012](#bib.bib1)):

|     |                                                                                                                                      |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\frac{{\text{d}}}{{\text{d}}t}{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t}}=Q_{t}{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t}},`$ |     | (2) |

where $`Q_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}`$ is the state transition matrix.

There are two main variants of interpolating noise frameworks: MDMs
([Sahoo et al., 2024b](#bib.bib46)), which use a masked token prior
$`\text{$\bm{\mathit{\pi}}$}={\mathbf{m}}`$ with
$`{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{V}`$ as a special mask token, and USDMs
([Schiff et al., 2025](#bib.bib49)), which uses a uniform prior over
$`\mathcal{V}`$ ($`\text{$\bm{\mathit{\pi}}$}=\mathbf{1}/K`$). These
frameworks differ in their forward corruption dynamics. In MDMs, the
clean data $`{\mathbf{x}}`$ either stays unchanged or transitions to the
mask token $`{\mathbf{m}}`$, after which it remains masked for the rest
of the process. In contrast, USDMs allow each token to either stay the
same or transition uniformly to any other token in $`\mathcal{V}`$, with
the transition probability determined by the diffusion timestep (see
Fig.
[5](#A1.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Rao Blackwellized NELBO (Ours) ‣ A.6 Rao-Blackwellized Negative Evidence Lower Bound ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
for examples). These forward dynamics impact the reverse generation
process: USDMs permit continual token updates, while MDMs fix tokens
once unmasked. To mitigate this limitation, predictor-corrector methods
have been proposed for MDMs ([Campbell et al., 2022](#bib.bib6); [Gat et
al., 2024](#bib.bib17); [Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib61)), but at the
cost of added computation. In contrast, USDMs naturally exhibit a
self-correcting property absent in AR and MDM approaches. As a result,
our work focuses primarily on the USDM framework.

[Lou et al. (2023)](#bib.bib34); [Schiff et al. (2025)](#bib.bib49) show
that for USDMs, the state transition matrix $`Q_{t}`$ is given by:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
Q_{t}=\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha^{\prime}_{t}}}{K{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}}[{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}}^{\top}-K\mathbf{I}_{K}],
``` |  | (3) |

where $`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha^{\prime}_{t}}`$ is the
time derivative of $`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}`$ and
the true reverse posterior for a timestep $`s<t`$ is given as:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{s|t}}}(.\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t},`$ | $`\displaystyle{\mathbf{x}})=\text{Cat}\Bigg(.;\frac{K{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\odot{\mathbf{x}}+({\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t|s}}-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}){\mathbf{z}}_{t}}{K{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}\langle{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+1-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{({\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{s}}-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}){\mathbf{x}}+(1-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t|s}})(1-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{s}}){\bm{1}}/K}{K{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}\langle{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle+1-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}}\Bigg)`$ |  | (4) |

where
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t|s}}={\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}/{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{s}}`$.
Since $`{\mathbf{x}}`$ is unavailable during inference, we approximate
it with a neural network
$`{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}:\mathcal{V}\times[0,1]\to\Delta^{K}`$ with
parameters $`\theta`$. The resulting approximate reverse posterior is
defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{s|t}}(.|{\mathbf{z}}_{t})={\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{s|t}}(.\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)).`$ |  | (5) |

The goal is to learn an approximate reverse process $`p_{\theta}`$ which
minimizes the Negative Evidence Lower Bound (NELBO):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\text{NELBO}\left({\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q},p_{\theta};{\mathbf{x}}\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}};{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}})}\;\textit{f}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t),{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}};{\mathbf{x}}),`$ |  | (6) |

where f is defined in
([49](#A1.E49 "Equation 49 ‣ A.6 Rao-Blackwellized Negative Evidence Lower Bound ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
Sampling from this model begins with the prior
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{t=1}\sim{\bm{1}}/K`$, and proceeds via ancestral
denoising, i.e., by drawing
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{s}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{s|t}}(.|{\mathbf{z}}_{t})`$
at each step.

### 2.2 Gaussian Diffusion Models

Gaussian diffusion maps a data distribution $`q_{\text{data}}`$ to a
simple prior distribution usually a Normal distribution
$`\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{K})`$, through a sequence of noisy latents
$`{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t}}(.|{\mathbf{x}})`$,
whose marginal distribution is given by:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t}}(.|{\mathbf{x}};{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})=\mathcal{N}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}{\mathbf{x}},(1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2})\mathbf{I}_{K}),`$ |  | (7) |

where the diffusion parameter
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}\in[0,1]`$ is a
monotonically decreasing function in $`t`$. For
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t=0}}=1`$ and
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t=1}}=0`$, the NELBO
for such a process is given as ([Kingma et al., 2021](#bib.bib27)):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\text{NELBO}\left({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}},p_{\theta};{\mathbf{x}}\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t}}({\mathbf{w}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}};{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})}\nu^{\prime}(t)\|{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{w}}_{t},t)\|_{2}^{2}`$ |  | (8) |

where $`\nu^{\prime}(t)`$ is the time derivative of the signal-to-noise
ratio
$`\nu(t)={\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}/(1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2})`$
for the Gaussian diffusion process.

### 2.3 Consistency Distillation

Consistency models ([Song et al., 2023](#bib.bib55); [Song & Dhariwal,
2023](#bib.bib53)) are a class of generative models that define a
bijective mapping between the samples from the noise distribution
$`\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{K})`$ and the data distribution
$`q_{\text{data}}`$. They build on deterministic samplers for Gaussian
diffusion ([Song et al., 2020](#bib.bib54); [Song et al.,
2021](#bib.bib52)), specifically using the Probability-Flow ODE
(PF-ODE). Given a pre-trained Gaussian diffusion model
$`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}`$, which requires hundreds or thousands of
sampling steps, Consistency Distillation is a popular technique to
distil them down to fewer steps generation that enables much faster
generation. The distillation begins with a teacher model
$`{\mathbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}^{-}}`$, often the Exponentially Moving
Average (EMA) of the student model $`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}`$ obtained
during the course of training. A noisy sample $`{\mathbf{w}}_{t}`$ is
drawn from the forward process
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t}}(.|{\mathbf{x}})`$
([7](#S2.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 2.2 Gaussian Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
and a less noisy sample $`{\mathbf{w}}_{s}`$ is obtained by numerically
solving one PF-ODE step using $`{\mathbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}^{-}}`$. The
student model is then trained to match the teacher’s estimate of the
clean sample minimizing the following loss:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\mathbf{\theta}^{-})=\lambda(t)d\left(\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{w}}_{t},t),{\mathbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}^{-}}({\mathbf{w}}_{s},s)\right),
``` |  | (9) |

where $`d:\mathbb{R}^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}`$ denotes
the error between the teacher model’s reconstruction
$`{\mathbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}^{-}}({\mathbf{w}}_{s},t)`$ and the student
model’s reconstruction $`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{w}}_{t},t)`$ of
the original sample and $`\lambda:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}`$ is a
weighting function that scales the loss based on the diffusion time-step
$`t`$.

## 3 The Diffusion Duality

Unlike discrete diffusion, Gaussian diffusion is replete with
well-established empirical techniques, which have driven significant
advances in both training ([Ho et al., 2020](#bib.bib22); [Salimans &
Ho, 2022](#bib.bib48); [Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib66)) and sampling
([Karras et al., 2022](#bib.bib26); [Song et al., 2023](#bib.bib55);
[Song & Dhariwal, 2023](#bib.bib53); [Yin et al., 2024](#bib.bib63)).
Our goal in this section is to establish a theoretical bridge between
discrete-state diffusion and continuous-state diffusion, which will
enable us to leverage tools from the latter to improve the former.

We propose a simple method to map a Gaussian latent to the discrete
space: the $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ operator. But does this
transformation of latents also transform a Gaussian diffusion process
into a discrete one? A necessary and sufficient condition for this is
that the marginal distribution of the discretized vector satisfies the
characteristic ODE of a discrete diffusion process
([2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
We first derive a closed-form expression for this marginal and show that
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ maps the marginals of a Gaussian
diffusion to those of a Uniform-state discrete diffusion, including a
transformation of the diffusion parameters
([10](#S3.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ Discrete Marginals ‣ 3.1 Gaussian Diffusion under the arg⁢max Pushforward ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
Finally, we verify that this marginal evolves according to
([12](#S3.E12 "Equation 12 ‣ Time Evolution of Marginals ‣ 3.1 Gaussian Diffusion under the arg⁢max Pushforward ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
establishing that $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ transforms a
Gaussian diffusion process into a Uniform-state discrete diffusion
process.

### 3.1 Gaussian Diffusion under the $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ Pushforward

We begin by defining a Gaussian diffusion process on
$`{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{V}`$ as per
([7](#S2.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 2.2 Gaussian Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
with
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t=0}}\approx q_{\text{data}}`$
and
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t=1}}=\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{K})`$.
Let
$`{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t}}(.|{\mathbf{x}};{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})`$
be an intermediate latent at time $`t`$. Next, define the operation
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathcal{V}`$ to map
a continuous vector $`{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}`$ to the one-hot
vector corresponding to the index of its largest entry in
$`{\mathbf{w}}`$, i.e.,
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}})={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathcal{V}}\;{\mathbf{z}}^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}`$.

##### Discrete Marginals

Let
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})`$
and $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}P_{t}}(.|{\mathbf{x}})`$ denote its
conditional pmf marginalized over
$`{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t}}(.|{\mathbf{x}};{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})`$.
In Suppl.
[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Discrete Marginals ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we show:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}P_{t}}\left(.|{\mathbf{x}};{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})\right)=\text{Cat}\left(.;{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}){\mathbf{x}}+(1-{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}))\frac{{\bm{1}}}{K}\right),`$ |  | (10) |

where the function
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}:[0,1]\to[0,1]`$ is the Diffusion
Transformation operator, defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\boxed{{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})=\frac{K}{K-1}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi\left(z-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}}{\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}}\right)\Phi^{K-1}(z){\text{d}}z-\frac{1}{K}\right],}`$ |  | (11) |

Next, we examine how the discrete marginal

### 3.2 Discrete–Gaussian Samplers and Likelihoods

Given an approximate reverse process for Gaussian diffusion,
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}{\bar{p}^{\theta}}_{t}}`$, there
exists a reverse process in the discrete domain,
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p^{\theta}_{t}}}`$, such that

|  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p^{\theta}_{t}}}=[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}{\bar{p}^{\theta}}_{t}}\quad\forall\,t\in[0,1].`$ |  | (14) |

$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}{\bar{p}^{\theta}}_{t}}`$ share the

is given by

|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{s|t}}(.|{\mathbf{z}}_{t})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}\int{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{s|t}}({\mathbf{w}}_{s}|{\mathbf{w}}_{t})\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}{\bar{p}^{\theta}}_{t}}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})}{{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p^{\theta}_{t}}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})}\mathds{1}_{{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})={\mathbf{z}}_{t}}{\text{d}}{\mathbf{w}}_{t}`$ |  | (15) |

##### Likelihood

Note that the Uniform-state and the underlying Gaussian diffusion are
separate Markov processes with no transitions between them, and they
induce distinct marginal distributions over the data, leading to
different log-likelihoods.

###### Theorem 3.2.

Let $`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$ and
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$
denote the approximations to the training data distribution induced by
the discrete and Gaussian samplers
([14](#S3.E14 "Equation 14 ‣ 3.2 Discrete–Gaussian Samplers and Likelihoods ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
respectively. Then the marginal likelihood of
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$ under
the true data distribution $`q_{\text{data}}`$ is at least as high as
that of
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\boxed{\;\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_{\text{data}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}_{\text{Discrete Likelihood}}\geq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_{\text{data}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}_{\text{Gaussian Likelihood}}\;\;}`$ |  | (16) |

We provide a proof in Suppl.
[A.5](#A1.SS5 "A.5 Discrete Likelihood vs Gaussian Likelihood ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality").

is that, for any Gaussian diffusion process, there exists an equivalent
discrete diffusion process that induces a higher marginal log-likelihood
on the true data distribution. Since USDMs provide an improved
likelihood estimate, it is advantageous to design the denoising model to
operate on discrete latents. Consequently, we adopt
([6](#S2.Ex2 "In 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))

### 3.3 Duo: Sampling and Improved Training Objective

In this subsection, we describe the samplers for Duo and introduce an
improved low-variance training objective.

##### Sampler

To sample from Duo, we use ancestral sampling for USDMs (Sec.
[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
Furthermore, to improve text quality, we propose Greedy-Tail Sampler,
which reduces sample entropy similarly to nucleus sampling in AR models
([Holtzman et al., 2020](#bib.bib24)). Specifically, during the final
denoising step, instead of sampling the clean sequence via
$`\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}\sim p^{\theta}_{0|\delta}(.)`$, we perform greedy
decoding:
$`\tilde{{\mathbf{x}}}={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}(p^{\theta}_{0|\delta}(.))`$
where $`\delta`$ denotes the time discretization.

##### Rao-Blackwellized NELBO

The term f in
([6](#S2.Ex2 "In 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
requires explicitly materializing the one-hot vector
$`\bar{{\mathbf{x}}}`$, which increases memory usage and slows down
training. We instead derive an equivalent Rao–Blackwellized objective,
given in
([17](#S3.Ex5 "In Rao-Blackwellized NELBO ‣ 3.3 Duo: Sampling and Improved Training Objective ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
that avoids materializing one-hot vectors and reduces the variance of
the training objective, leading to faster optimization and lower
perplexity (Sec.
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\textit{f}_{\text{Duo}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t),{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}};{\mathbf{x}})=-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}^{\prime}}{K{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}}\Bigg[\frac{K}{\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}-\frac{K}{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta})_{i}}`$ |  |  |

diffusion into the design space of USDMs. This allows us to design a
low-variance training algorithm that leads to faster training (Sec.
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
and a distillation scheme that unlocks few-step generation in diffusion
language models (Sec.
[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).

## 4 Applications

We now present two applications where discrete diffusion models benefit
from leveraging the underlying Gaussian diffusion. In Sec.
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we introduce a curriculum learning strategy that reduces training
variance and leads to faster training. Then, in Sec.
[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we propose a distillation algorithm that cuts the number of sampling
steps by two orders of magnitude with minimal impact on sample quality.

### 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning

Curriculum learning ([Bengio et al., 2009](#bib.bib4)) gradually exposes
models to increasingly complex data, starting with simpler,
easier-to-denoise noise patterns and progressing to more challenging
ones. Here, we design a curriculum for USDMs by exploiting the
underlying Gaussian diffusion.

Similar to relaxation methods in discrete gradient estimation ([Jang et
al., 2017](#bib.bib25); [Maddison et al., 2017](#bib.bib35)), our
curriculum is centered around annealing the temperature parameter of a
smooth approximation of $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$. We
reformulate the NELBO for discrete diffusion in terms of
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ over Gaussian latents (Sec.
[4.1.1](#S4.SS1.SSS1 "4.1.1 Discrete NELBO with Gaussian Latents ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
The denoising model is then trained to operate on the
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ of these Gaussian variables. We
then relax the $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ using a tempered
softmax (Sec.
[4.1.2](#S4.SS1.SSS2 "4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
which yields a lower-variance but biased estimator of the ELBO.
Initially, the model operates on fully relaxed, continuous Gaussian
latents. As training progresses, the temperature is gradually decreased,
transitioning the model’s inputs from soft (continuous) to hard
(discrete). By the end of the curriculum, the model effectively operates
on discrete latents, closing the gap between training and inference-time
behavior.

#### 4.1.1 Discrete NELBO with Gaussian Latents

Consider the discrete diffusion NELBO
([18](#S3.Ex10 "In Sequence-Level Discrete Diffusion ‣ 3.3 Duo: Sampling and Improved Training Objective ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),

$`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t}}(.|{\mathbf{x}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L};{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}})`$.
Our goal is to re-express this objective in terms of Gaussian latents
$`{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t}}(.|{\mathbf{x}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L};{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})`$
such that marginalizing over
$`{\mathbf{w}}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}}`$ yields the
same numerical value for the NELBO. In Suppl.
[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Discrete NELBO with Gaussian Latents ‣ Appendix B Additional Proofs ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we show:

#### 4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss

To reduce training variance, we replace
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}^{\ell}_{t})`$ in the
denoising model input
([19](#S4.Ex11 "In 4.1.1 Discrete NELBO with Gaussian Latents ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
with a tempered softmax. We argue that this substitution eases recovery
of the clean sequence from its noisy counterpart, and that the
difficulty of this recovery is regulated by the temperature parameter.

As shown in prior work ([Jang et al., 2017](#bib.bib25); [Maddison et
al., 2017](#bib.bib35)), $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ is a
limiting case of $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathrm{softmax}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}^{\ell}_{t})=\lim_{\tau\to 0^{+}}{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathrm{softmax}}({{\mathbf{w}}^{\ell}_{t}}{/\tau}).`$ |  | (20) |

We relax this operation by setting the temperature parameter $`\tau>0`$.
While computing the NELBO in
([19](#S4.Ex11 "In 4.1.1 Discrete NELBO with Gaussian Latents ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
note that the discrete diffusion parameter
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})`$
spans the interval $`[0,1]`$, as does its Gaussian counterpart
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}`$. The diffusion
transformation operator $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}`$
([11](#S3.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ Discrete Marginals ‣ 3.1 Gaussian Diffusion under the arg⁢max Pushforward ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
has a crucial property: as the vocabulary size $`K`$ increases, a small
sub-interval $`[a,1]_{0\leq a<1}`$ within the domain of
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}`$ is sufficient to map onto the
full range $`[0,1]`$. For instance, in Suppl.
[C.7](#A3.SS7 "C.7 Curriculum Learning ‣ Appendix C Experimental details ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we observe that for $`K=30`$K, when
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}\in[0.85,1]`$,
the corresponding
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})`$
nearly spans the entire interval $`[0,1]`$. This observation is
counter-intuitive: since the Gaussian latents mostly resemble
$`{\mathbf{x}}`$, one might expect the discrete NELBO to approach zero
when evaluated with
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}`$ restricted to
such a narrow range. However, in practice, the NELBO remains largely
unchanged. Why? The key reason lies in the discretization step. Even
small amounts of Gaussian noise in $`{\mathbf{w}}^{t}_{\ell}`$ can cause
the output of the $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ operation to
change drastically, as it is highly sensitive to perturbations. As a
result, much of the extra signal is lost due to discretization. To
mitigate this, we allow the denoising model $`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}`$ to
access the continuous latent $`{\mathbf{w}}^{\ell}_{t}`$ through a
tempered softmax in
([20](#S4.E20 "Equation 20 ‣ 4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
This relaxation helps preserve more of the signal, making the
reconstruction task easier. In this way, the temperature parameter
$`\tau`$ effectively controls the difficulty of the learning problem.

Hence, unlike prior discrete diffusion methods, we design the denoising
model
$`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}:\Delta^{L}\cup\mathcal{V}^{L}\times[0,1]\to\Delta^{L}`$
to handle both continuous latents and discrete latents; see Suppl.
[C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Denoising Model ‣ Appendix C Experimental details ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
for details. During training, we sample
$`t\sim{\mathcal{U}}[\beta,\gamma]_{0\leq\beta<\gamma\leq 1}`$ from a
sub-interval so that
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}\in[a,b]_{0\leq a<b\leq 1}`$.
Following [Arriola et al. (2025)](#bib.bib2), we set $`b<1`$ as
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}=1`$ doesn’t
provide much training signal. Thus, we propose the following training
loss:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}},t\sim{\mathcal{U}}[\beta,\gamma],{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t}}}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\;\textit{f}_{\text{Duo}}\;\Big({\mathbf{z}}^{\ell}_{t}:={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}^{\ell}_{t}),`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{x}_{\theta}^{\ell}({\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathrm{softmax}}({{\mathbf{w}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}\ell^{\prime}}}_{t}/}{\tau})}]^{L}_{\ell^{\prime}=1}},t),\;{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}:={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}});{\mathbf{x}}^{\ell}\Big).`$ |  | (21) |

This loss doesn’t correspond to a valid NELBO because the denoising
model operates on a continuous-time random variable (r.v.), while the
loss is defined for a discrete diffusion process. It only becomes a
valid NELBO in the limiting case $`\lim_{\tau\to 0^{+}}`$ with
$`\beta=0`$ and $`\gamma=1`$. During evaluation, we evaluate the model
as a discrete diffusion model using
([18](#S3.Ex10 "In Sequence-Level Discrete Diffusion ‣ 3.3 Duo: Sampling and Improved Training Objective ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
As shown in Figure
[2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
and Table
[4](#A4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ D.1 Curriculum Learning Ablation ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
this approach results in lower training variance compared to previous
MDMs and USDMs, ultimately improving model likelihood (Sec.
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).

### 4.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation

In this section, we present a new method to exploit the duality property
of USDMs. This duality enables USDMs to adopt Consistency Distillation—a
technique developed for Gaussian diffusion models to distil them into
few-step generation models. However, standard discrete diffusion models
cannot use this approach due to the absence of such PF-ODEs. To address
this, we introduce Discrete Consistency Distillation (DCD), which
sidesteps this limitation by utilizing the PF-ODE of the underlying
Gaussian diffusion model to construct deterministic trajectories.

##### Deterministic Discrete Trajectories (DDT)

Consistency Distillation relies on a PF-ODE parameterized by the
denoising model. In our setting, the trained discrete denoiser
$`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}`$ cannot be used to parameterize the ODE in the
Gaussian space, as it operates only on discrete samples—the temperature
$`\tau`$ in
([21](#S4.Ex14 "In 4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
is reduced to zero by the end of the training. To circumvent this, we
construct a deterministic trajectory in Gaussian space by reversing the
PF-ODE using an *optimal denoiser*, and then project this trajectory to
the discrete domain. Let $`\mathcal{P}_{\text{ODE}}`$ denote such a
trajectory. Given a clean data point
$`{\mathbf{x}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}\sim q_{\text{data}}`$
and Gaussian noise
$`\bm{\epsilon}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}=\{\bm{\epsilon}^{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{K})\forall\ell\in[L]\}`$,
$`\mathcal{P}_{\text{ODE}}({\mathbf{x}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L},\bm{\epsilon}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L})=\left\{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\ell}+\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}\bm{\epsilon}^{\ell}}]^{L}_{\ell=1}}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}`$
; see Suppl.
[B.2.1](#A2.SS2.SSS1 "B.2.1 Optimal Gaussian PF-ODEs ‣ B.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ Appendix B Additional Proofs ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
for a detailed discussion. Next, we project this trajectory to the
discrete space via the $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ operator:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\text{DDT}}({\mathbf{x}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L},\bm{\epsilon}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left\{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\ell}+\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}\bm{\epsilon}^{\ell})}]^{L}_{\ell=1}}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}.`$ |  | (22) |

$`\mathcal{P}_{\text{DDT}}`$ serves as a proxy for the absence of a
proper PF-ODE defined in the discrete space; see Fig.
[5](#A1.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Rao Blackwellized NELBO (Ours) ‣ A.6 Rao-Blackwellized Negative Evidence Lower Bound ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
for an illustrative example.

##### Distillation

Given a teacher model $`{\mathbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}^{-}}`$, our goal is to
distill it into a student model $`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}`$ that generates
samples of similar quality but in fewer steps. To perform distillation,
we sample an adjacent pair of latents
$`({\mathbf{z}}_{s}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}},{\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}})\sim\{({\mathbf{z}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}}_{j-\delta},{\mathbf{z}}_{j}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}})|{\mathbf{z}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}_{\{.\}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{DDT}}({\mathbf{x}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L},\bm{\epsilon}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}),j\in[\delta,1]\}`$
for a given step size $`\delta\in[0,1]`$. Here,
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}}`$ is noisier
than $`{\mathbf{z}}_{s}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}}`$ and
serves as the input to the student model. Let
$`{\mathbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}^{-}}({\mathbf{z}}_{s}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}},s)`$
and
$`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}},t)`$
denote the output distributions over clean samples produced by the
teacher and the student models, respectively. Following [Deschenaux &
Gulcehre (2024)](#bib.bib12), we train the student by minimizing the KL
divergence between these distributions:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{DCD}}(\bm{\theta};\bm{\theta}^{-})=\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\text{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\theta}^{\ell}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}},t),{\mathbf{x}}_{\bm{\theta}^{-}}^{\ell}({\mathbf{z}}_{s}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}},s)\right).`$ |  | (23) |

The distillation process proceeds in $`N`$ rounds, each consisting of
$`M`$ training steps. At the end of each round, the teacher weights are
updated with the current student weights. The full procedure is outlined
in Algo.

## 5 Experiments

We evaluate Duo on standard language modeling benchmarks, training on
LM1B ([Chelba et al., 2014](#bib.bib7)) and OpenWebText (OWT) ([Gokaslan
et al., 2019](#bib.bib18)) with sequence packing ([Raffel et al.,
2020](#bib.bib42)). We train our models for 1M steps with a batch size
of 512 on both datasets. For LM1B, we use a context length of 128 with
the bert-base-uncased tokenizer ([Devlin et al., 2018](#bib.bib13)) with
sequence packing ([Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib3); [Arriola et al.,
2025](#bib.bib2)) and without it ([Sahoo et al., 2024a](#bib.bib45);
[Lou et al., 2023](#bib.bib34); [He et al., 2022](#bib.bib21)). For OWT,
we use a context length of 1024 with the GPT-2 tokenizer ([Radford et
al., 2019](#bib.bib41)). Following [Sahoo et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib45),
we reserve the last 100K documents for validation. Consistent with prior
work, our model is a 170M-parameter modified diffusion transformer (DiT)
([Peebles & Xie, 2023](#bib.bib40)) with rotary positional encoding ([Su
et al., 2023](#bib.bib57)) and adaptive layer norm for conditioning on
diffusion time. Training is conducted on 8×H100s with bfloat16
precision. We train Duo using
([21](#S4.Ex14 "In 4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
which requires computing the integral in
([11](#S3.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ Discrete Marginals ‣ 3.1 Gaussian Diffusion under the arg⁢max Pushforward ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
To reduce computation overhead, we pre-compute and cache 100K
$`({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}},{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}))`$.
The Gaussian diffusion parameter
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}`$ is
parameterized using a linear schedule i.e.,
$`({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}=1-t)_{t\in[0,1]}`$.

### 5.1 Improved Training

Our experiments show that (1) the proposed curriculum learning strategy

accelerates training by $`\mathbf{2}\times`$ and achieves a new
state-of-the-art among USDMs (Table
[1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Zero-Shot Likelihood Evaluation ‣ 5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
and (2) Duo performs competitively with Absorbing State diffusion across
major language modeling benchmarks, even surpassing AR models on 3/7
zero-shot PPL benchmarks (Table
[2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Zero-Shot Likelihood Evaluation ‣ 5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).

##### Experimental Setup

The primary baselines for Duo are the leading USDMs (SEDD Uniform ([Lou
et al., 2023](#bib.bib34)) and UDLM ([Schiff et al., 2025](#bib.bib49)))
and Gaussian diffusion method, PLAID ([Gulrajani & Hashimoto,
2024](#bib.bib19)). Additionally, we compare Duo with an AR model and
MDMs such as MDLM ([Sahoo et al., 2024a](#bib.bib45)) (SOTA), SEDD
Absorb ([Lou et al., 2023](#bib.bib34)), and D3PM Absorb ([Austin et
al., 2021](#bib.bib3)). While training Duo, we set $`\tau`$ as a
function of the training iteration $`n`$ for Duo: $`\tau=0.001`$ for the
first 500K steps ($`n<500\text{K}`$) with $`\beta=0.03`$ and
$`\gamma=0.15`$ in
([21](#S4.Ex14 "In 4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
and $`\tau=0`$ for the remaining steps up to 1M ($`n\geq 500\text{K}`$).
To compute PPL for Duo, we use
([18](#S3.Ex10 "In Sequence-Level Discrete Diffusion ‣ 3.3 Duo: Sampling and Improved Training Objective ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
with $`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}=1-t`$.

##### Bias-variance Tradeoff

First, we study the effect of $`\tau`$ on the training dynamics in Fig.
[8](#A4.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ D.1 Curriculum Learning Ablation ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
by training Duo on the LM1B dataset over 150K steps with a fixed
$`\tau\in\{0,0.001,0.01,0.1\}`$. Here, $`\tau=0`$ (blue) corresponds to
([18](#S3.Ex10 "In Sequence-Level Discrete Diffusion ‣ 3.3 Duo: Sampling and Improved Training Objective ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
i.e., no curriculum. Recall that a larger $`\tau`$ introduces more bias
but reduces training variance. Ideally, $`\tau`$ should strike a
balance—minimizing both the bias (measured by deviation from the blue
curve) and the variance in the loss curve. For $`\tau=0.1`$ (red), the
loss drops sharply, indicating excessive bias, making it suboptimal. As
$`\tau`$ decreases to 0.01 (orange) and 0.001 (purple), the loss curves
become more stable. Among them, $`\tau=0.001`$ is the most desirable, as
it closely follows the blue curve (low bias) while exhibiting
significantly lower variance.

##### Faster Training

Notably, after just 10K steps of fine-tuning as a discrete diffusion
model, i.e., at 510K steps, Duo achieves a PPL of $`35.2`$—almost 1.5
points better than UDLM trained for 1M steps—indicating that curriculum
learning accelerates convergence by at least $`{2}\times`$. In Fig.
[2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we compare the summed gradient variance of the top 100 weights with the
highest variance for Duo with (blue) and without (grey) curriculum. For
these weights, we notice that curriculum learning reduces the gradient
variance by an order of magnitude, which also manifests as lower loss
variance in Fig.
[7](#A3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ C.7 Curriculum Learning ‣ Appendix C Experimental details ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
and Table
[4](#A4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ D.1 Curriculum Learning Ablation ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality").

### 5.2 Improved Sampling

Our sampling experiments show that for undistilled models, (1) Duo
generates higher-quality samples than all previous diffusion models
(Fig.
[9](#A4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ D.2 Undistilled Models: Quantitative Sample Quality Analysis ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"));
(2) combining DCD with the Greedy-Tail sampler reduces the number of
sampling steps by two orders of magnitude (Fig.
[4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Experimental Setup ‣ 5.2 Improved Sampling ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"));
and (3) the distilled Duo model outperforms a distilled MDLM model,
especially in the low NFE regime.

##### Experimental Setup

We distill Duo on OWT using DCD, following the same setup as our main
baseline: MDLM distilled with SDTT ([Deschenaux & Gulcehre,
2024](#bib.bib12)). We run $`N=5`$ distillation rounds, starting with
discretization step $`\delta=1/512`$ in Algo.
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Distillation ‣ 4.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
and doubling it every $`M=10`$K steps. To assess sample quality, we
report GPT-2 Large generative perplexity (Gen PPL) and average sequence
entropy for diversity. As noted by [Zheng et al. (2024)](#bib.bib67),
masked diffusion models can suffer from low diversity and misleading Gen
PPL under low-precision sampling. To address this, we use float64
precision in all sampling experiments.

Figure 4: Sample quality of the base Duo model vs. Duo distilled for 5
rounds with DCD. With ancestral sampler, the distilled model matches
base quality in 16 steps (vs. 1024), and with Greedy-Tail needs only 8
steps but with slightly reduced sample diversity.

##### Sample Quality

In Fig.
[9](#A4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ D.2 Undistilled Models: Quantitative Sample Quality Analysis ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
Duo consistently outperforms all MDMs and USDMs in Gen PPL across
sampling steps $`T\in\{8,\dots,1024\}`$, with particularly strong
performance at low NFEs. In Fig.
[3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Zero-Shot Likelihood Evaluation ‣ 5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we compare Duo distilled with DCD to MDLM distilled with SDTT after 5
rounds (entropy values in parentheses). Under ancestral sampling, Duo
performs significantly better than MDLM for $`T\leq 32`$. This is
because MDMs generate many tokens independently and cannot revise them,
leading to incoherence at low NFEs. In contrast, USDMs are
self-correcting: they can fix earlier errors in later denoising steps.
At higher NFEs, MDLM outperforms the distilled Duo. While MDLM (with
SDTT) matches the Gen PPL of the AR model, its lower entropy (5.4 vs.
5.6) indicates reduced diversity.

##### Greedy-Tail vs Ancestral Sampler

In Fig.
[3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Zero-Shot Likelihood Evaluation ‣ 5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
the Greedy-Tail sampler improves Gen PPL by reducing sample entropy. In
Fig.
[4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Experimental Setup ‣ 5.2 Improved Sampling ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we observe that the ancestral sampler enables a 64× speedup (reducing
NFEs from 1024 to 16) while maintaining Gen PPL. Greedy-Tail offers an
even greater 128× speedup, with a slight drop in entropy. Interestingly,
as shown in Fig.
[11](#A4.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Sample Diversity vs Distillation round ‣ D.3 Discrete Consistency Distillation: Quantitative Sample Quality Analysis ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
and Fig.
[12](#A4.F12 "Figure 12 ‣ Sample Diversity vs Distillation round ‣ D.3 Discrete Consistency Distillation: Quantitative Sample Quality Analysis ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
each distillation round improves both Gen PPL and diversity when using
the Greedy-Tail sampler—unlike ancestral sampling—suggesting that
Greedy-Tail is particularly effective for distilled models.

##### Ablation

In Algo.
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Distillation ‣ 4.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we use the denoising model weights as the teacher, deviating from the
common practice of using EMA weights in consistency models. To test this
choice, we modify Algo.
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Distillation ‣ 4.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
to use EMA weights (Algo.
[2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ B.2.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation Ablation ‣ B.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ Appendix B Additional Proofs ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
instead. As shown in Fig.
[10](#A4.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ Sample Diversity vs Distillation round ‣ D.3 Discrete Consistency Distillation: Quantitative Sample Quality Analysis ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
using the denoising model as a teacher leads to a better distilled
model.

## 6 Related Work

##### Diffusion Models for Discrete Data

Prior work on diffusion models for discrete data either operates
directly in discrete space ([Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib3); [Lou et
al., 2023](#bib.bib34); [Sahoo et al., 2024a](#bib.bib45); [Schiff et
al., 2025](#bib.bib49); [Arriola et al., 2025](#bib.bib2)) or in
continuous space by injecting Gaussian noise into continuous token
embeddings ([Li et al., 2022](#bib.bib31); [Han et al.,
2022](#bib.bib20); [Dieleman et al., 2022](#bib.bib14); [Gulrajani &
Hashimoto, 2024](#bib.bib19)). In contrast, our framework Duo features a
combination of a discrete diffusion process (USDMs) with a Gaussian
diffusion process defined directly over one-hot token representations
rather than their embeddings. Training USDMs on Gaussian latents both
accelerates optimization and improves model likelihood, yielding better
performance than methods restricted to either domain alone (Table
[1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Zero-Shot Likelihood Evaluation ‣ 5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).

##### Distillation for Faster Sampling

Distillation techniques in Gaussian diffusion ([Salimans & Ho,
2022](#bib.bib48); [Song et al., 2023](#bib.bib55)) rely on
deterministic PF-ODE trajectories, which are unavailable for discrete
diffusion. SDTT ([Deschenaux & Gulcehre, 2024](#bib.bib12)) addresses
this by distilling along stochastic trajectories. Our method, Discrete
Consistency Distillation (Sec.
[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
instead constructs deterministic PF-ODE trajectories in Gaussian space
and maps them to the discrete domain via
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$, yielding distilled models that
surpass prior methods in few-step generation.

##### Argmax Differentiation

The softmax annealing trick was introduced by [Jang et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib25); [Maddison et al. (2017)](#bib.bib35) to enable
backpropagation through the $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$
operation among other methods ([Vlastelica et al., 2019](#bib.bib59);
[Sahoo et al., 2023](#bib.bib44)). Here, we repurpose it in a different
context: we first show that the discrete NELBO can be written in terms
of a $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ over Gaussian latents
([19](#S4.Ex11 "In 4.1.1 Discrete NELBO with Gaussian Latents ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).
During training, we relax this
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$—which maps a Gaussian latent to a
clean or noisy token—using a tempered softmax, yielding a superposition
of clean and noisy tokens. This reduces training variance (Sec.
[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
and improves model likelihood (Table
[3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Ablation ‣ 5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we establish a theoretical connection between
continuous-space Gaussian diffusion and Uniform-state discrete
diffusion. This connection enables 2× faster training (Sec.
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
and up to two-orders-of-magnitude faster sampling (Sec.
[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Improved Sampling ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
in USDMs. While USDMs lag behind MDMs in perplexity, they outperform
them in the few-step generation regime.

### 7.1 Limitations

The curriculum learning scheme introduced in this paper has two
hyperparameters: $`\tau`$, which controls the degree of
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ relaxation, and the curriculum
duration. While the same settings work well on LM1B and OWT, other
domains may require manual tuning. When such tuning is infeasible, we
recommend using Duo without curriculum learning, as it still outperforms
prior work (Tabs.
[1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Zero-Shot Likelihood Evaluation ‣ 5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
and
[3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Ablation ‣ 5.1 Improved Training ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
due to the Rao–Blackwellized training objective
([17](#S3.Ex5 "In Rao-Blackwellized NELBO ‣ 3.3 Duo: Sampling and Improved Training Objective ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")).

### 7.2 Future work

Theorem
[3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.2 Discrete–Gaussian Samplers and Likelihoods ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
relates the reverse discrete diffusion process to the underlying
Gaussian diffusion. We highlight two concrete directions that build on
this connection.

##### (1) Improved Guidance with Discrete Samplers

Discrete samplers
([5](#S2.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
materialize a discrete sample at every denoising step, which is
problematic for classifier-based guidance in settings like drug
discovery or molecule generation ([Nisonoff et al., 2024](#bib.bib38);
[Schiff et al., 2025](#bib.bib49)), where one seeks to maximize a
differentiable reward from a classifier. Gaussian samplers are ideal for
this task as they allow classifier gradients to accumulate throughout
generation, slowly moving toward the target distribution, but they
underperform discrete samplers Sec.
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Discrete–Gaussian Samplers and Likelihoods ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality").
A promising direction is to sample in the discrete space while using the
underlying Gaussian diffusion only for guidance via
([15](#S3.Ex4 "In Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.2 Discrete–Gaussian Samplers and Likelihoods ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
letting the Gaussian latent continuously accumulate classifier gradients
to steer the discrete sampler.

##### (2) Gaussian Parameterizations for Discrete Denoising Models

Another direction is to exploit
([A.4](#A1.Ex51 "A.4 Marginal preserving samplers ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
which expresses the discrete denoising model in terms of a
Gaussian-space denoiser. This makes it possible to directly incorporate
$`\epsilon`$-parameterization ([Ho et al., 2020](#bib.bib22)) and the
velocity parameterization ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib66)) into the
discrete denoising model. Such parameterizations may yield more
expressive and better-conditioned discrete denoisers, with the potential
to reduce training and sampling cost.

We hope this foundation spurs work that exploits the Gaussian connection
to further improve Uniform-state diffusion, a link absent in Masked
diffusion.

## Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine
Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work,
specifically those related to the generation of synthetic text. Our work
can also be applied to the design of biological sequences, which carries
both potential benefits and risks.

## Appendix A The Diffusion Duality

Let $`{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{V}`$ s.t. $`{\mathbf{x}}_{k}=1`$ i.e.,
$`{\mathbf{x}}`$ contains 1 at the $`k^{\text{th}}`$ index. Consider a
r.v.
$`\bar{\mathbf{w}}={\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}{\mathbf{x}}+{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\sigma}_{t}}\bm{\epsilon}`$
where $`\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{K})`$ and
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\sigma}_{t}}=\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}`$.

### A.1 Discrete Marginals

Our goal in this section is to derive the pmf of the r.v.
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}(\bar{\mathbf{w}})`$. The proof has
three parts. In part 1, we derive pdf of the the random variables
$`\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}`$ and $`\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i\neq k}`$. Next in
part 2, we derive the pdf of the random variable
$`Z_{\neq k}=\max(\{\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i}:i\neq k\})`$. Finally in part
3, we derive the distribution of
$`\max(Z_{\neq k},\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k})`$ which is the key to
constructing the pmf of the r.v.
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}(\bar{\mathbf{w}})`$.

##### Part 1

It can be easily seen that every entry in $`\bar{\mathbf{w}}`$ is a
Gaussian r.v. with

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}},{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{t}})`$ |  | (24) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i\neq k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{t}}).`$ |  | (25) |

##### Part 2

Since, $`\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i\neq k}`$ follows a Gaussian distribution
with 0 mean and
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\sigma}_{t}}`$ standard
deviation, the probability of $`\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i\neq k}<l`$ where
$`l\in\mathbb{R}`$ is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle P(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i\neq k}<l)=\Phi\left(\frac{l}{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\sigma}_{t}}}\right)`$ |  | (26) |

where
$`\Phi(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\exp(-t^{2}/2){\text{d}}z/\sqrt{2\pi}`$ is
the cumulative distribution function of the Gaussian distribution. This
allows us to compute the pdf of the r.v.
$`Z_{\neq k}=\max(\{\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i}:i\neq k\})`$ in the following
manner:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle P(Z_{\neq k}<l)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Pi_{i\neq k}P(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i}<l)=\Phi^{K-1}\left(\frac{l}{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\sigma}_{t}}}\right),`$ |  | (27) |

where $`P(Z_{\neq k}<l)`$ is the probability that $`Z_{\neq k}<l`$ for
$`l\in\mathbb{R}`$.

##### Part 3

Let $`P({\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}(\bar{\mathbf{w}})_{k}=1)`$
denote the probability that the index $`k`$ is the index of the maximum
entry in $`\bar{\mathbf{w}}`$. This is equal to the probability of every
other entry $`\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i\neq k}<\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}`$. Let
$`\phi(z)=\exp(-z^{2})/\sqrt{2\pi}`$ denote the standard Normal
distribution. Hence,

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle P({\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}(\bar{\mathbf{w}})_{k}=1)`$ | $`\displaystyle=P(Z_{\neq k}<\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k})`$ |  |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P(Z_{\neq k}<l)P(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{k}=l){\text{d}}l`$ |  |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P(Z_{\neq k}<l)\left[\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\sigma}_{t}}}\phi\left(\frac{l-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\sigma}_{t}}}\right)\right]{\text{d}}l`$ | From ([24](#A1.E24 "Equation 24 ‣ Part 1 ‣ A.1 Discrete Marginals ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")) |  |  |

### A.3 Properties of Discretized Gaussian Trajectories

Let $`{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{V}`$ undergo Gaussian diffusion with
$`\{{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}`$ denoting the Gaussian trajectory.
The central question is: does the corresponding discretized trajectory
$`\{{\mathbf{z}}_{t}:={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})\}_{t\in[0,1]}`$
correspond to a valid discrete diffusion trajectory?

To address this, we must examine how the
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ operation links the Gaussian
transition kernel—which models the transition
$`{\mathbf{w}}_{s}\to{\mathbf{w}}_{t}`$—to the discrete diffusion
transition kernel, which models the transition
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{s}\to{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$ in the discrete space, for
$`0\leq s<t\leq 1`$. The Gaussian transition kernel is given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}}_{t}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t|s}}(.|{\mathbf{w}}_{s})=\mathcal{N}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t|s}}{\mathbf{w}}_{s},(1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t|s}}^{2})\mathbf{I}_{K}),`$ |  | (37) |

where
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t|s}}={\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}/{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{s}}`$.
The discrete diffusion transition kernel is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t|s}}(.|{\mathbf{z}}_{s})=\text{Cat}\left(.;{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t|s}}{\mathbf{z}}_{s}+(1-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t|s}})\frac{{\bm{1}}}{K}\right),`$ |  | (38) |

where
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t|s}}={\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}/{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{s}}`$.

A necessary and sufficient condition for the discretized trajectory
$`\{{\mathbf{z}}_{t}:={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})\}_{t\in[0,1]}`$
to follow a discrete diffusion process is that the pushforward
distribution
$`[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t|s}}(.\mid{\mathbf{w}}_{s})`$
must equal
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t|s}}(.\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{s})`$. We
will now show that this does not hold.

##### Step 1: Analyzing the discrete kernel

Let $`k`$ denote the index such that $`({\mathbf{z}}_{s})_{k}=1`$.
First, consider
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t|s}}(.\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{s})`$.
From
([38](#A1.E38 "Equation 38 ‣ A.3 Properties of Discretized Gaussian Trajectories ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
it is clear that for any $`i,j\neq k`$, the probabilities satisfy

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t|s}}(\bar{{\mathbf{z}}}_{i}=1\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{s})={\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t|s}}(\bar{{\mathbf{z}}}_{j}=1\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{s});\;\;\forall i,j\neq k,
``` |  |

where $`\bar{{\mathbf{z}}}\in\mathcal{V}`$ is a discrete random
variable.

##### Step 2: Analyzing the pushforward of the Gaussian kernel

Now define
$`P:=[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t|s}}(.\mid{\mathbf{w}}_{s})`$.
Let $`\bar{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}`$ be a continuous random
variable. Using the same argument as in Suppl.
[A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Discrete Marginals ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
we can show:

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle P({\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}(\bar{\mathbf{w}})_{i}=1)`$ | $`\displaystyle=P(\max(\{\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}:j\neq i\})<\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i})`$ |  |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{j\neq i}P(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{j}<l)P(\bar{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=l){\text{d}}l`$ |  |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{j\neq i}\Phi\left(\frac{l-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t|s}}({\mathbf{w}}_{s})_{j}}{\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t|s}}^{2}}}\right)\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t|s}}^{2}}}\phi\left(\frac{l-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t|s}}({\mathbf{w}}_{s})_{i}}{\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t|s}}^{2}}}\right)\right]{\text{d}}l`$ | From ([37](#A1.E37 "Equation 37 ‣ A.3 Properties of Discretized Gaussian Trajectories ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")) |  | (39) |

where $`\phi(z)=\exp(-z^{2}/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}`$ is the standard normal
distribution and $`\Phi(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\phi(t){\text{d}}t`$ is its
cumulative distribution function.

##### Step 3: Comparing the two distributions

Clearly, from
([39](#A1.Ex40 "In Step 2: Analyzing the pushforward of the Gaussian kernel ‣ A.3 Properties of Discretized Gaussian Trajectories ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
we observe that
$`P({\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}(\bar{\mathbf{w}})_{i}=1)=P({\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}(\bar{\mathbf{w}})_{j}=1)\;\forall i,j\neq k`$
if and only if
$`({\mathbf{w}}_{s})_{i}=({\mathbf{w}}_{s})_{j}\;\forall i,j\neq k`$.
This condition rarely holds (in fact, the probability of exact equality
is essentially zero). Thus, for a given
$`{\mathbf{w}}_{s}\sim{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{s}}(.|{\mathbf{x}};{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t|s}}(.\mid{\mathbf{z}}_{s}:={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}_{s}))\neq[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{q}_{t|s}}(.\mid{\mathbf{w}}_{s}).`$ |  | (40) |

##### Conclusion

Therefore, the discretized trajectory
$`\{{\mathbf{z}}_{t}:={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})\}_{t\in[0,1]}`$
does not necessarily follow a discrete diffusion process.

### A.4 Marginal preserving samplers

Our goal in this section is to express both the transition kernel of the
discrete reverse process and the associated denoising model in terms of
the Gaussian reverse process. As described in Theorem
[3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.2 Discrete–Gaussian Samplers and Likelihoods ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
the reverse transition kernel
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{s|t}}`$ in the discrete
space that ensures
$`\left({\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p^{\theta}_{t}}}=[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}{\bar{p}^{\theta}}_{t}}\right)_{t\in[0,1]}`$
is given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{s|t}}(.|{\mathbf{z}}_{t})=[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}\int{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{s|t}}({\mathbf{w}}_{s}|{\mathbf{w}}_{t})\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}{\bar{p}^{\theta}}_{t}}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})}{{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p^{\theta}_{t}}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})}\mathds{1}_{{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}}_{t})={\mathbf{z}}_{t}}{\text{d}}{\mathbf{w}}_{t}.`$ |  | (41) |

Proof. We prove the claim by mathematical induction on $`t`$.

Base case. For $`t=1`$ we have the discrete prior
$`p^{\theta}_{t=1}(\cdot)=\text{Cat}(\cdot;{\bm{1}}/K)`$ and the
Gaussian prior $`\bar{p}^{\theta}_{t=1}(\cdot)=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{K})`$.
By symmetry, for $`{\mathbf{w}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{K})`$ each index is
equally likely to be the value of
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\mathbf{w}})`$, so

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{t=1}}=[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{t=1}}.`$ |  | (42) |

Inductive step. Assume that for some $`t`$ the relation

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p^{\theta}_{t}}}=[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}{\bar{p}^{\theta}}_{t}}
``` |  |

holds. Let $`s=t-\delta`$ with $`0<\delta<t`$. We show that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{s}}=[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{s}},
``` |  |

and that
([15](#S3.Ex4 "In Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.2 Discrete–Gaussian Samplers and Likelihoods ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
yields a reverse process with this property.

We start from the marginal at time $`s`$ and expand it via the joint
with $`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p}^{\theta}_{s}}({\mathbf{z}}_{s})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{z}}_{t}}{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p}^{\theta}_{s}}({\mathbf{z}}_{s},{\mathbf{z}}_{t})`$ |  |  |

Thus, the discrete marginal at time $`s`$ is the pushforward of the

### A.5 Discrete Likelihood vs Gaussian Likelihood

As per Theorem
[3.2](#S3.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 3.2. ‣ Likelihood ‣ 3.2 Discrete–Gaussian Samplers and Likelihoods ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality"),
the marginal likelihood of the Uniform-state diffusion process
($`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$) under
the true data distribution $`q_{\text{data}}`$ is at least as high as
that of the underlying Gaussian diffusion process
($`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_{\text{data}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}_{\text{Discrete Likelihood}}\geq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_{\text{data}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}_{\text{Gaussian Likelihood}}}`$ |  |

Proof. Before proceeding, we recall a standard result from [Csiszár
(1964)](#bib.bib11); [Cover & Thomas (2012)](#bib.bib10): for a broad
class of statistical divergences $`D`$—such as the Kullback–Leibler (KL)
divergence, Total Variation Distance (TVD), or Rényi divergence—and for
any Markov kernel $`k:X\to Y`$ mapping distributions on a measurable
space $`X`$ to distributions on another measurable space $`Y`$, the
following inequality holds:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle D\left([k]_{\filledstar}q,[k]_{\filledstar}p\right)\leq D(q,p),`$ |  | (46) |

where $`q`$ and $`p`$ denote probability distributions on $`X`$, and
$`[k]_{\filledstar}`$ denotes the pushforward operation induced by the
kernel $`k`$.

Let $`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$ and
$`{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$
denote the approximations to the training data distribution induced by
Uniform-state diffusion and its underlying Gaussian diffusion process by
the marginal preserving samplers defined in
([15](#S3.Ex4 "In Theorem 3.1. ‣ 3.2 Discrete–Gaussian Samplers and Likelihoods ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
respectively. The likelihoods of the Uniform-state diffusion process
($`\log{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$)
and its underlying Gaussian diffusion process
($`\log{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}`$)
are related as follows:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\text{D}_{\text{KL}}\left([{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}q_{\text{data}},[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})\right)\leq\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(q_{\text{data}},{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}})\;\;\;\;\text{(\footnotesize From (\ref{eqn:dpi_inequality}))}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\because q_{\text{data}}`$ defines a distribution over categorical random variables, we get: |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\implies\text{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(q_{\text{data}},[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}\right)\leq\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(q_{\text{data}},{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\implies\sum_{\mathbf{x}}q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\log\frac{q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})}{[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}\leq\sum_{\mathbf{x}}q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\log\frac{q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})}{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\implies\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\cancel{q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\log q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})}-q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\log{[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\leq\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\cancel{q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\log q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})}-q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\log{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\implies\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left[-q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\log{[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}\right]\leq\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left[-q_{\text{data}}({\mathbf{x}})\log{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})\right]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\implies\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_{\text{data}}}\log\underbrace{[{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}]_{\filledstar}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}_{\equiv{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_{\text{data}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\implies{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_{\text{data}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}p^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}_{\text{Discrete Likelihood}}\geq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_{\text{data}}}\log{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\bar{p}^{\theta}_{\text{data}}}({\mathbf{x}})}_{\text{Gaussian Likelihood}}}`$ |  | (47) |

The key insight from
([47](#A1.Ex53 "In A.5 Discrete Likelihood vs Gaussian Likelihood ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
is that, for a given Gaussian diffusion process, there exists an
equivalent discrete diffusion process that induces a higher marginal
likelihood on the training data. Since USDMs provide an improved
likelihood estimate, it is advantageous to design the denoising model to
operate on discrete latents. Consequently, we adopt
([6](#S2.Ex2 "In 2.1 Discrete Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
as our training and evaluation objective.

### A.6 Rao-Blackwellized Negative Evidence Lower Bound

[Schiff et al. (2025)](#bib.bib49) show that the NELBO for USDMs is
given as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{NELBO}\left({\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q},p_{\theta};{\mathbf{x}}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}q_{t}}({\mathbf{z}}_{t}|{\mathbf{x}};{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}})}\;\textit{f}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t),{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}};{\mathbf{x}}),`$ |  | (48) |

where f is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\textit{f}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t),{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}};{\mathbf{x}})=-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}^{\prime}}{K{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}}\left[\frac{K}{\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}-\frac{K}{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta})_{i}}-\sum_{j}\frac{\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}{\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\log\frac{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta})_{i}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}{(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta})_{j}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i}}\right].`$ |  | (49) |

where the subscript $`i`$ denotes the $`i^{\text{th}}`$ index of a
vector,
$`\bar{\mathbf{x}}=K{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}{\mathbf{x}}+(1-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}){\bm{1}}`$,
$`\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{\theta}=K{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)+(1-{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}){\bm{1}}`$,
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}^{\prime}`$ denotes the
time-derivative of the $`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}`$,
and we define $`i=\arg\max_{j\in[K]}({\mathbf{z}}_{t})_{j}`$ to be the
non-zero entry of $`{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$.

##### Rao Blackwellized NELBO (Ours)

To reduce GPU memory usage and training time, we rewrite the original
ELBO objective in
([49](#A1.E49 "Equation 49 ‣ A.6 Rao-Blackwellized Negative Evidence Lower Bound ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
by eliminating the need to explicitly materialize the one-hot vector
$`\bar{\mathbf{x}}`$. This leads to a more efficient formulation that
significantly improves practicality. The resulting objective, shown in
([17](#S3.Ex5 "In Rao-Blackwellized NELBO ‣ 3.3 Duo: Sampling and Improved Training Objective ‣ 3 The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
not only removes $`\bar{\mathbf{x}}`$ but also applies
Rao-Blackwellization to analytically compute certain expectations,
thereby reducing variance. We now derive the improved loss:

### A.7 Reverse Process Visualizations

[Figure 5](#A1.F5 "In Rao Blackwellized NELBO (Ours) ‣ A.6 Rao-Blackwellized Negative Evidence Lower Bound ‣ Appendix A The Diffusion Duality ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
illustrates the differences among four diffusion processes:
autoregressive models (which can be viewed as a form of left-to-right
diffusion), masked diffusion, uniform diffusion, and Duo with Discrete
Consistency Distillation (DCD). Each method demonstrates a distinct
pattern of token evolution during generation.

##### Autoregressive Models

Autoregressive models generate tokens one at a time, sequentially from
left to right. At each forward pass of the neural network, a single
token is produced, which limits the throughput. Finally, tokens are not
revised once they have been generated.

##### Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs)

In masked diffusion, all tokens in the original sequence are masked,
when at the highest noise level, and are progressively unmasked
throughout the diffusion process until the data sequence is fully
denoised. Hence each token can take on only one of two possible values.

##### Uniform-state Diffusion Models (USDMs)

Uniform-state diffusion models allow tokens to be updated at every
diffusion step, using a uniform prior over the vocabulary. In contrast
to autoregressive or masked models, each token can be updated multiple
times throughout the diffusion process.

##### $`\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\textbf{DDT}}`$

This reprsents a deterministic trajectory between the clean data
$`{\mathbf{x}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}`$ and a sample
from the uniform prior:
$`{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbf{z}}_{t=1}^{\ell}}]^{L}_{\ell=1}}={\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\ell}+\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}\bm{\epsilon}^{\ell})}]^{L}_{\ell=1}}`$
for
$`\bm{\epsilon}^{\ell}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{K})\;\forall\ell\in[L]`$.
As shown in
([22](#S4.Ex15 "In Deterministic Discrete Trajectories (DDT) ‣ 4.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
the value of each token at the $`\ell^{\text{th}}`$ position at an
intermediate timestep $`t`$ is given by
$`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\ell}+\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}\bm{\epsilon}^{\ell})`$.
This expression represents the $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}`$ of a
linear interpolation between $`{\mathbf{x}}^{\ell}`$—the one-hot vector
of the clean data—and the Gaussian noise vector
$`\bm{\epsilon}^{\ell}`$, both of which remain fixed throughout the
entire generation process. Consequently, the intermediate token can take
on only one of two values: $`{\mathbf{x}}^{\ell}`$ (when $`t`$ is close
to 0) or $`{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\argmax}(\bm{\epsilon}^{\ell})`$
(when $`t`$ is close to 1). The generation process closely resembles to
that of MDMs where a token once denoised, can’t change. This is called
the “carry over” operation in MDMs ([Sahoo et al., 2024a](#bib.bib45)).
Please note that the $`\mathcal{P}_{\text{DDT}}`$ is only used during
distillation to generate the teacher and student targets
Alg.([1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Distillation ‣ 4.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
and that Duo isn’t trained to generate samples with “carry over”: i.e.
once a token changes, it never changes again.

#### B.2.1 Optimal Gaussian PF-ODEs

For a Gaussian diffusion process (see Sec.
[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Gaussian Diffusion Models ‣ 2 Background ‣ The Diffusion Duality")),
the probability flow ODE (PF-ODE) can be reversed using the DDIM sampler
([Song et al., 2021](#bib.bib52)), whose update step is given by:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{s}={\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{s}}\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)+\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{s}}^{2}}\epsilon_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)`$ |  | (57) |

where $`s<t`$,
$`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\times[0,1]\to\Delta`$ is the
denoising model, and
$`\epsilon_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)=({\mathbf{z}}_{t}-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t))/\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}`$.

Assuming an optimal denoiser such
that$`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}({\mathbf{z}}_{t},t)={\mathbf{x}}\forall t\in[0,1]`$,
and given
$`{\mathbf{z}}_{t=1}=\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{K})`$
and $`{\mathbf{x}}\sim q_{\text{data}}`$,
([57](#A2.E57 "Equation 57 ‣ B.2.1 Optimal Gaussian PF-ODEs ‣ B.2 Discrete Consistency Distillation ‣ Appendix B Additional Proofs ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
simplifies to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}_{s}={\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}{\mathbf{x}}+\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}\bm{\epsilon}`$ |  | (58) |

This holds $`\forall s\in[0,1]`$. Thus, the optimal PF-ODE trajectory
$`\mathcal{P}_{\text{ODE}}({\mathbf{x}},\bm{\epsilon})`$ is given as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\text{ODE}}({\mathbf{x}},\bm{\epsilon})=\left\{{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}{\mathbf{x}}+\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}\bm{\epsilon}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}.`$ |  | (59) |

We can easily extend this to sequences:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\text{ODE}}({\mathbf{x}}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L},\bm{\epsilon}^{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L})=\left\{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}{\mathbf{x}}^{\ell}+\sqrt{1-{\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}}^{2}}\bm{\epsilon}^{\ell}}]^{L}_{\ell=1}}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}`$ |  | (60) |

## Appendix C Experimental details

### C.1 Plaid Baseline

For PLAID on LM1B, we retrained it without self-conditioning ([Chen et
al., 2023](#bib.bib8)) to match our denoising model’s parameter count.
While self-conditioning improves PPL and can be applied to both discrete
and Gaussian diffusion models, we omit it for consistency with baselines
such as MDLM, SEDD, UDLM, and D3PM. Since higher training precision
benefits discrete diffusion models ([Shi et al., 2025](#bib.bib50)), we
use bfloat16 for the forward pass through the denoising model while
keeping float64 for other computations to stabilize PLAID training. Due
to their inefficient open-source codebase¹¹ 1
[https://github.com/igul222/plaid](https://github.com/igul222/plaid), we
report PLAID results for LM1B at 100K steps, as further training was
infeasible. For OWT, we report results from [Lou et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib34), where PLAID was trained at higher precision for
1.3M steps, favoring the baseline.

### C.2 Denoising Model

Unlike prior discrete diffusion approaches, we design the denoising
model $`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}:\mathcal{V}^{L}\times[0,1]\to\Delta^{L}`$ to
operate on both continuous latents
$`\mathbf{y}_{\text{c}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}}\in\Delta^{L}`$
and discrete latents
$`\mathbf{y}_{\text{d}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.4688,0.4688,0.4688}1:L}}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}`$.
We implement $`\mathbf{x}_{\theta}`$ as a Transformer ([Vaswani et al.,
2017](#bib.bib58)), where token embeddings in the first layer are
computed via matrix multiplication:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle(\mathbf{y}_{\text{c}}^{\ell})_{\ell\in[L]}^{\top}\texttt{vocab\_embeddings}`$ |  |

with $`\texttt{vocab\_embeddings}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times m}`$ denoting
the vocabulary embedding matrix and $`m`$ the embedding dimension. For
discrete inputs
$`(\mathbf{y}_{\text{d}}^{\ell})_{\ell\in[L]}\in\mathcal{V}`$, we
perform standard embedding lookups. In contrast, continuous inputs
$`(\mathbf{y}_{\text{c}}^{\ell})_{\ell\in[L]}\in\Delta^{K}`$ act as
“soft lookups”, producing a convex combination of the vocabulary
embeddings.

### C.3 Low Discrepancy Sampler

To reduce variance during training we use a low-discrepancy sampler,
similar to that proposed [Kingma et al. (2021)](#bib.bib27).
Specifically, when processing a minibatch of $`N`$ samples, instead of
independently sampling $`N`$ from a uniform distribution, we partition
the unit interval and sample the time step for each sequence
$`i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}`$ from a different portion of the interval
$`t_{i}\sim{\mathcal{U}}\left[\frac{i-1}{N},\frac{i}{N}\right]`$. This
ensures that our sampled timesteps are more evenly spaced across the
interval $`[0,1]`$, reducing the variance of the ELBO.

### C.4 Likelihood Evaluation

We use a single monte-carlo estimate for $`t`$ to evaluate the
likelihood. We use a low discrepancy sampler ([Kingma et al.,
2021](#bib.bib27)) to reduce the variance of the estimate. We evaluate
likelihood using the true ELBO, not the curriculum learning objective.

### C.5 Language Modeling

We detokenize the One Billion Words dataset following [Lou et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib34); [Sahoo et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib45), whose code can
be found
[here](https://github.com/louaaron/Score-Entropy-Discrete-Diffusion/blob/main/data.py)²²
2
https://github.com/louaaron/Score-Entropy-Discrete-Diffusion/blob/main/data.py.
We tokenize the One Billion Words dataset with the bert-base-uncased
tokenizer, following [He et al. (2022)](#bib.bib21). We concatenate and
wrap sequences to a length of 128 ([Raffel et al., 2020](#bib.bib42)).

We tokenize OpenWebText with the GPT2 tokenizer. We concatenate and wrap
them to a length of 1,024. When wrapping, we add the eos token
in-between concatenated sequences. Since OpenWebText does not have a
validation split, we leave the last 100k docs as validation.

We parameterize our autoregressive baselines, UDLM, SEDD, and MDLM with
the modified diffusion transformer architecture ([Peebles & Xie,
2023](#bib.bib40)) from [Lou et al. (2023)](#bib.bib34); [Sahoo et al.
(2024a)](#bib.bib45). We use 12 layers, a hidden dimension of 768, 12
attention heads, and a timestep embedding of 128 for the uniform
diffusion models (SEDD Uniform, UDLM, Duo). Word embeddings are not tied
between the input and output. We train the SEDD and MDLM baselines using
the original code provided by their authors.

We use the AdamW optimizer with a batch size of 512, constant learning
rate warmup from 0 to a learning rate of 3e-4 for 2,500 steps. We use a
constant learning rate for 1M, 5M, or 10M steps on One Billion Words,
and 1M steps for OpenWebText. We use a dropout rate of 0.1.

### C.6 Zeroshot Likelihood

We evaluate zeroshot likelihoods by taking the models trained on
OpenWebText and evaluating likelihoods on the validation splits of 7
datasets: Penn Tree Bank (PTB; [Marcus et al. (1993)](#bib.bib36)),
Wikitext ([Merity et al., 2016](#bib.bib37)), One Billion Word Language
Model Benchmark (LM1B; [Chelba et al. (2014)](#bib.bib7)), Lambada
([Paperno et al., 2016](#bib.bib39)), AG News ([Zhang et al.,
2015](#bib.bib64)), and Scientific Papers (Pubmed and Arxiv subsets;
[Cohan et al. (2018)](#bib.bib9)). We detokenize the datasets following
[Lou et al. (2023)](#bib.bib34). For the AG News and Scientific Papers
(Pubmed and Arxiv), we apply both the Wikitext and One Billion Words
detokenizers. Since the zeroshot datasets have different conventions for
sequence segmentation, we wrap sequences to 1024 and do not add eos
tokens in between sequences.

### C.7 Curriculum Learning

We visualize the diffusion parameter

not face this issue. Furthermore, Fig.
[6](#A3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ C.7 Curriculum Learning ‣ Appendix C Experimental details ‣ The Diffusion Duality")
shows that for $`t\in[\beta,\gamma]`$, the Gaussian latent retains a
higher signal level than its discrete counterpart, making it easier for
the denoising model to recover the clean signal, as discussed in Sec.
[4.1.2](#S4.SS1.SSS2 "4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality").

To mitigate these issues, we restrict the training window to
$`t\in[\beta,\gamma]`$ in
([21](#S4.Ex14 "In 4.1.2 Low-Variance Training Loss ‣ 4.1 Faster Training using Curriculum Learning ‣ 4 Applications ‣ The Diffusion Duality"))
when training on Gaussian latents, thereby avoiding the region where
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}^{\prime}\approx 0`$. For
the discrete diffusion process, we set the time range such that
$`{\color[rgb]{0.043,0.3242,0.5898}\alpha_{t}}={\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0}\mathcal{T}}({\color[rgb]{0.9023,0.5703,0.2188}\tilde{\alpha}_{t}})\in[0.05,0.95]`$.
While this range depends on the vocabulary size $`K`$, we found it to be
similar for both the gpt-2 and bert-base-uncased tokenizers,
corresponding to $`[\beta,\gamma]=[0.03,0.15]`$. Although this
introduces a slight bias in the NELBO estimate, it significantly reduces
training variance.

To compare distilled Duo with SDTT, we distill an MDLM on LM1B for 5
rounds of 10k training steps with a batch size of 128 and a learning
````

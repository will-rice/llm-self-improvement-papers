# Sharpen Before You Adapt: Data-Free Entry-State Sharpening for Test-Time Reinforcement Learning

Zhanming Zhang Affiliation: Independent Researcher Affiliation: New

###### Abstract

Test-time reinforcement learning (TTRL) adapts language models on
unlabeled test problems using supervision derived from their own
samples. This makes the checkpoint’s _entry state_ consequential: a
diffuse policy provides noisier self-supervision and may spend much of a
limited adaptation budget merely concentrating probability mass before
reliably expressing capability it already possesses. We propose
entry-state sharpening: use data-free training _before_ TTRL to prepare
a general-purpose checkpoint in a state that subsequent label-free
adaptation can exploit more efficiently. The idea is not tied to one
training recipe; different data-free objectives can move the same base
model to different entry states. Across five data-free checkpoints
derived from Qwen3-4B and evaluated under an identical 15-step TTRL
protocol, entry policy entropy strongly rank-orders endpoint conversion
efficiency, a reliability-to-reachability measure (Spearman
$`\rho=-0.90`$; $`\rho=-0.99`$ after controlling for entry
reachability). The contrast across objectives is striking: R-Zero
remains diffuse at $`3.39`$ nats and finishes below the untuned base in
6/6 matched comparisons across MATH, GPQA, and AMC, whereas SPIRAL
reaches $`0.07`$ nats and achieves the highest post-TTRL accuracy on
MATH and GPQA despite its self-play stage using no math training data.
An in-domain label-free self-distillation intervention further shows
that the entry state can be deliberately sharpened. These results
motivate treating checkpoint preparation as a _state-control problem_:
use data-free training to improve TTRL readiness, with entry entropy as
a label-free control signal and reachable capability as the constraint.

## 1 Introduction

Test-time reinforcement learning (TTRL) \[[40](#bib.bib40)\] adapts a
language model directly on unlabeled test problems, using agreement
among the model’s own samples as supervision \[[28](#bib.bib28)\]. This
makes TTRL attractive when labels or verifiers are unavailable: a model
can continue improving from a few hundred target problems and a small
number of optimizer steps.

But TTRL has an implicit prerequisite that is rarely treated as a design
variable: the _state of the checkpoint when adaptation begins_. Its
learning signal is generated from the checkpoint’s own distribution. If
that distribution is diffuse, two problems arise simultaneously: the
model’s self-consensus is less reliable, and part of the limited
adaptation budget must first be spent concentrating probability mass
before reachable capability can be expressed consistently. A checkpoint
can therefore be strong at frozen inference yet poorly positioned for
further self-improvement.

We propose entry-state sharpening: use data-free training _before_ TTRL
to prepare a general-purpose checkpoint in a state that subsequent
label-free adaptation can exploit more efficiently. The proposal is
deliberately broader than a particular training recipe. Self-play,
self-evolution, and self-distillation can all reshape the policy handed
to TTRL; the key question is what _entry state_ they produce. We
therefore treat checkpoint preparation as a state-control problem rather
than as a choice of upstream recipe.

This view follows from evidence that reinforcement learning with
verifiable or self-generated rewards often improves small-sample
performance by re-weighting behaviors already accessible to the base
model \[[35](#bib.bib35), [31](#bib.bib31), [8](#bib.bib8)\]. In this
regime, TTRL is largely a _conversion_ process: turning reachable
capability into reliable output. To separate these quantities we define
conversion efficiency $`\eta=\mathrm{mean@16}/\mathrm{best@16}`$, where
mean@16 measures sampled accuracy and best@16 estimates reachability
(formalized in
§[3](#S3 "3 Method: Entry-State Sharpening ‣ Sharpen Before You Adapt: Data-Free Entry-StateSharpening for Test-Time Reinforcement Learning")).
A useful entry state should raise this conversion efficiency without
reducing what the model can still reach.

We study five data-free checkpoints derived from the same Qwen3-4B
base \[[20](#bib.bib20)\], each evaluated under an identical 15-step
TTRL protocol. Across these checkpoints, entry policy entropy strongly
rank-orders endpoint conversion efficiency: Spearman $`\rho=-0.90`$,
strengthening to $`\rho=-0.99`$ after controlling for entry
reachability. The ordering is also stable across seeds ($`\rho=1.00`$,
$`p=0.017`$), whereas raw-accuracy ordering is not. Entry entropy
therefore provides a label-free readout of a checkpoint property closely
associated with its ability to convert capability under TTRL.

The contrast between training objectives makes the entry-state problem
concrete. R-Zero \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] enters highly diffuse at $`3.39`$
nats and finishes below the untuned base in all 6/6 matched comparisons
across MATH, GPQA, and AMC. SPIRAL \[[15](#bib.bib15)\], despite never
training on math, enters at $`0.07`$ nats and yields the strongest
post-TTRL checkpoint. Thus data-free training is not inherently
preparatory: different objectives can move the same base family toward
very different states for subsequent adaptation.

We further show that this state can be manipulated directly. Our
in-domain label-free self-distillation operator moves the Base
checkpoint from $`1.186`$ to $`0.183`$ nats and increases endpoint
conversion efficiency from $`0.941`$ to $`0.945`$. At this high
preparation strength, however, final reachability falls from $`0.820`$
to $`0.811`$. Entry-state sharpening is therefore not entropy
minimization; its goal is to improve conversion while preserving
reachable capability.

Our contributions are:

- •
  Entry-state sharpening. We formulate checkpoint preparation for TTRL
  as a data-free state-control problem: reshape the model before
  adaptation so that its existing capability can be converted more
  efficiently.
- •
  A measurable entry-state signal. Across five data-free checkpoints,
  entry policy entropy strongly rank-orders endpoint conversion
  efficiency, including after controlling for entry reachability.
- •
  Evidence that preparation objectives matter through the states they
  produce. R-Zero and SPIRAL move the same base family to opposite ends
  of the entropy axis and exhibit correspondingly different TTRL
  behavior.
- •
  A direct in-domain intervention. Label-free self-distillation
  substantially sharpens the entry state and improves conversion, while
  its high-strength endpoint identifies reachability as the constraint
  on further sharpening.

## 2 Related Work

Test-time adaptation and label-free self-improvement. Test-time training
adapts models on unlabeled test inputs using self-supervised
objectives \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. TTRL \[[40](#bib.bib40)\] brings this
idea to language-model reasoning by optimizing a majority-vote reward
with GRPO \[[23](#bib.bib23)\]; related methods instead use model
confidence, entropy, or self-certainty as the adaptation
signal \[[19](#bib.bib19), [1](#bib.bib1), [5](#bib.bib5),
[36](#bib.bib36), [39](#bib.bib39), [11](#bib.bib11)\]. Recent work also
regulates uncertainty _within_ the adaptation loop to avoid unreliable
pseudo-labels or premature collapse \[[30](#bib.bib30),
[14](#bib.bib14), [37](#bib.bib37), [32](#bib.bib32),
[12](#bib.bib12)\]. We study a complementary question: rather than
modifying TTRL itself, what state should the checkpoint be in _before_
adaptation begins? This distinction is motivated by evidence that RL
primarily re-weights behaviors already accessible to the base
policy \[[35](#bib.bib35), [31](#bib.bib31), [8](#bib.bib8)\].

Data-free self-evolution and preparation for downstream RL. A growing
family of methods improves language models without external labeled
data, including uncertainty-driven co-evolution in
R-Zero \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] and its multi-solver disagreement
extension \[[21](#bib.bib21)\], verifier-grounded self-play in Absolute
Zero \[[38](#bib.bib38)\], self-instruction in SeRL \[[3](#bib.bib3)\],
and game self-play in SPIRAL \[[15](#bib.bib15)\]. Label-free
self-training methods similarly fine-tune models on their own selected
generations \[[10](#bib.bib10), [34](#bib.bib34), [6](#bib.bib6)\].
These works primarily evaluate the resulting checkpoint as an endpoint;
we instead study the checkpoint as an _initialization for further
test-time learning_. This connects to a broader line on preparing models
for reinforcement learning: domain-adaptive pretraining and mid-training
can substantially affect downstream RL performance and
scalability \[[7](#bib.bib7), [25](#bib.bib25), [29](#bib.bib29),
[26](#bib.bib26), [4](#bib.bib4), [22](#bib.bib22)\]. SPIRAL is
particularly close: inserting self-play before RLVR improves over RLVR
alone \[[15](#bib.bib15)\]. Our setting differs in that the downstream
learner is itself label-free and operates on the test distribution, and
we characterize preparation through a measurable property of the
resulting checkpoint rather than through the upstream recipe alone.

Concentration, entropy, and coverage. Self-training and distillation are
known to concentrate output distributions and, when pushed far enough,
reduce multi-sample diversity or coverage \[[13](#bib.bib13),
[18](#bib.bib18), [17](#bib.bib17)\]. This motivates treating sharpening
as a controlled preparation step rather than unconditional entropy
minimization. At the same time, long-horizon RL literature often treats
entropy collapse as a failure mode and explicitly preserves
exploration \[[2](#bib.bib2), [33](#bib.bib33), [27](#bib.bib27),
[16](#bib.bib16)\]. Our regime is different: TTRL uses a short
adaptation horizon and self-generated supervision, where entry
concentration can improve the reliability and efficiency of subsequent
updates. We therefore distinguish _entry-state concentration_ from
global entropy collapse, and study how much concentration is useful
before reachable capability begins to erode.

## 3 Method: Entry-State Sharpening

Let $`\pi_{0}`$ be a general-purpose checkpoint and $`\mathcal{A}_{T}`$
a fixed $`T`$-step test-time adaptation procedure on unlabeled target
problems $`\mathcal{X}`$. Standard TTRL adapts directly from
$`\pi_{0}`$:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \pi_{T}=\mathcal{A}_{T}(\pi_{0};\mathcal{X}).
       ```                                            |     |

We instead insert a data-free preparation stage,

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\pi_{\mathrm{entry}}=\mathcal{P}_{m}(\pi_{0};\tau_{m}),\qquad\pi_{T}=\mathcal{A}_{T}(\pi_{\mathrm{entry}};\mathcal{X}),
``` |  |

where $`m`$ denotes a preparation objective and $`\tau_{m}`$ its
method-specific training strength. We call this framework entry-state
sharpening.

The preparation operator is not tied to one algorithm. Self-play,
self-evolution, and self-distillation can all reshape the checkpoint
before TTRL; what matters is the *state* they produce. Their training
strengths are not directly comparable—e.g., self-play iterations and
self-distillation steps measure different quantities—so we compare them
through a common checkpoint-level readout.

We separate reachable capability from how reliably it is expressed using

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\eta=\frac{\mathrm{mean@16}}{\mathrm{best@16}},\qquad\mathrm{mean@16}=\eta\cdot\mathrm{best@16},
``` |  |

where mean@16 is sampled accuracy and best@16 estimates reachability.
Entry-state sharpening therefore aims to improve subsequent conversion
efficiency $`\eta`$ while preserving reachability.

Our common state variable is entry policy entropy. Let $`x`$ be a
problem from the first TTRL batch, $`z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{L})`$ a sampled
reasoning trace, $`z_{<t}`$ its prefix at token $`t`$, and
$`\mathcal{V}`$ the vocabulary. We measure

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H_{\mathrm{entry}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x,z}\left[\frac{1}{|z|}\sum_{t=1}^{|z|}-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi(v\mid x,z_{<t})\log\pi(v\mid x,z_{<t})\right]
``` |  |

before the first TTRL update. It requires no ground-truth labels and is
comparable across preparation methods. We compute $`H_{\mathrm{entry}}`$
on the checkpoint’s own rollouts from the first TTRL training batch,
using TTRL’s prompt template, tokenization, and rollout temperature.

We study three data-free routes to different entry states. We first
consider two representative paradigms of data-free self-evolution.
Game-based self-play generates experience through interaction and learns
from environment-verified outcomes; we use SPIRAL \[[15](#bib.bib15)\]
as a representative instance. Curriculum-based self-evolution instead
generates or selects problems according to the model’s current behavior
and trains on self-generated supervision; we use
R-Zero \[[9](#bib.bib9)\], whose uncertainty-driven curriculum targets
problems on which the Solver disagrees with itself. These two paradigms
provide a useful contrast because both require no labeled target-domain
data, yet their objectives exert very different pressures on the
resulting policy. Finally, we construct an in-domain self-distillation
checkpoint as a direct sharpening intervention. Before TTRL, the base
model generates reasoning traces on unlabeled target problems; traces
consistent with its self-consistency majority are retained as
pseudo-demonstrations and used for supervised cross-entropy
fine-tuning \[[10](#bib.bib10), [34](#bib.bib34), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. We
use self-distillation here as one concrete operator for deliberately
moving the entry state.

## 4 Experimental Setup

All checkpoints derive from Qwen3-4B \[[20](#bib.bib20)\]. We evaluate
them under one frozen TTRL protocol: 15 optimizer steps; validation at
steps $`0,5,10,15`$; 32 reward votes; 16 rollout/update samples and 16
validation samples per question; and matched seeds. No ground-truth
labels enter adaptation. The questions used for training are identical
across the checkpoints.

Our primary roster contains five checkpoints, all produced without
labeled target answers: Base; R-Zero \[[9](#bib.bib9)\];
SPIRAL@400 \[[15](#bib.bib15)\], the public multi-game SPIRAL self-play
release (a completed run at SPIRAL’s nominal 400-step budget);
SPIRAL@32, an early step-32 endpoint of a Kuhn-Poker self-play run; and
our in-domain self-distilled checkpoint.

For each validation question, we report mean@16, best@16, and conversion
efficiency $`\eta`$. best@16 is a 1,000-resample bootstrap estimate of
reachability rather than the literal fraction of questions with one
observed correct response. Entry best@16 is used as the capability
control in partial-correlation analyses.

Training configuration. TTRL optimizes the majority-vote reward with
GRPO \[[23](#bib.bib23)\] using verl. Each of the 15 updates uses 32
unlabeled problems. The actor learning rate is $`5\times 10^{-7}`$ with
cosine decay. Rollouts are generated with vLLM at temperature $`1.0`$,
while validation uses 16 samples per problem at temperature $`0.6`$ and
top-$`p=0.95`$. Each run uses eight NVIDIA A100 40 GB GPUs.

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Frozen accuracy does not imply TTRL readiness

The motivation for entry-state sharpening is that a checkpoint’s static
capability and its ability to continue learning can diverge.

it, while SPIRAL@400 finishes highest despite never training on math.

The three focal checkpoints enter MATH with similar reachability but

checkpoint is a good substrate for TTRL.

### 5.3 Data-free training effectively moves the entry state

The preceding result would be less useful if entry state were merely a
fixed property of pretraining. Instead, different data-free objectives
move the same base family to very different states.

R-Zero and SPIRAL@400 provide the clearest contrast. R-Zero’s curriculum
repeatedly selects problems on which the Solver disagrees with itself,
whereas SPIRAL trains on discrete game outcomes verified by the
environment. Consistent with these objectives, R-Zero enters at
$`3.391`$ nats while SPIRAL@400 enters at $`0.073`$. Both are data-free,
yet they produce opposite TTRL substrates. The relevant property is
therefore not data-free training itself, but the entry state induced by
the training objective.

Our in-domain self-distillation experiment provides a direct sharpening
intervention. Starting from Base, label-free self-distillation moves
entry entropy from $`1.186`$ to $`0.183`$ nats and increases endpoint
conversion efficiency from $`0.941`$ to $`0.945`$. This shows that the
entry state can be deliberately shifted in the direction predicted by
the cross-checkpoint relationship.

At this high preparation strength, however, final reachability is lower
than Base ($`0.811`$ vs. $`0.820`$), leaving final mean@16 marginally
lower. This marks the constraint on sharpening. Entry entropy should
therefore be treated as a calibration signal rather than an objective to
minimize: preparation is useful insofar as it improves conversion
without sacrificing reachable capability.

The two self-play checkpoints add complementary evidence that greater
self-play concentrates the entry state further. SPIRAL@32, an early
(step-32) endpoint of a Kuhn-Poker self-play run, enters at $`0.344`$
nats with $`\eta=0.937`$, whereas the completed multi-game SPIRAL@400
enters at $`0.073`$ nats with $`\eta=0.957`$. We see this as directional
evidence that stronger self-play concentrates the policy and improves
conversion.

## 6 Limitations

Our main empirical result is observational across checkpoints with
different training histories: entry entropy strongly predicts how
efficiently short-horizon TTRL converts reachable capability, and
data-free training can substantially move that entry state. A controlled
within-method preparation-strength sweep is still required to establish
the causal effect of calibrated entry-state sharpening on final TTRL
performance.

Our experiments focus on the Qwen3-4B family and a fixed 15-step TTRL
regime. MATH provides the strongest matched-seed evidence, while GPQA
and AMC support the same directional pattern across additional domains.
Whether the relationship generalizes across model families, adaptation
horizons, and broader task distributions remains open.

Finally, we use mean token-level policy entropy as a practical proxy for
entry-state concentration. This quantity is label-free and easy to
measure, but it does not fully capture diversity at the answer or
solution level; future work should test whether semantic or answer-level
uncertainty provides a more direct measure of the reachable solution
set.

## 7 Conclusion

We introduced entry-state sharpening: preparing a general-purpose
checkpoint with data-free training before test-time reinforcement
learning. The central premise is that TTRL largely converts reachable
capability into reliable output, so part of its limited adaptation
budget can be moved upstream by placing the model in a more concentrated
entry state.

Across five data-free checkpoints derived from the same Qwen3-4B base,
entry policy entropy strongly rank-orders endpoint conversion
efficiency, including after controlling for entry reachability, and the
same ordering replicates on GPQA and AMC. Importantly, these differences
in conversion are reflected in end-task performance: appropriately
prepared checkpoints can also finish above the untuned Base after TTRL,
with SPIRAL achieving the highest final accuracy on MATH and GPQA and
the self-distilled checkpoint marginally highest on AMC. The contrast
between R-Zero and SPIRAL further shows that data-free training alone is
not sufficient: different objectives can move the same base family
toward sharply different entry states and correspondingly different TTRL
outcomes. Finally, our in-domain self-distillation experiment shows that
this state can be deliberately shifted, while also revealing the
constraint on sharpening: improved conversion is useful only while
reachable capability is preserved.

These results suggest a different way to prepare models for test-time
learning. Rather than treating the checkpoint entering TTRL as fixed, we
can treat its entry state as something to design. Entry entropy provides
a simple label-free signal for that preparation; the remaining question
is how strongly to sharpen a given checkpoint before reachability begins
to deteriorate.

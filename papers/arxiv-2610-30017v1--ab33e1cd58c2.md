###### Abstract.

This study introduces GuideEvolve, a self-evolving agent framework
incorporating expert guidance and three autonomous regulatory
mechanisms—collectively termed the 3Ts: Think Fast and Slow, Throwback,
and Termination by choice. The framework addresses critical limitations
in extant self-evolving agent paradigms through dual innovations: (1) a
task-specific expert-evaluation-driven evolutionary pipeline, and (2)
the integration of 3Ts mechanisms to facilitate cognitive optimization,
performance recovery via temporal rollback, and adaptive termination
protocols.

Empirical validation across the MGSM and MMLU benchmarks demonstrates
that GuideEvolve attains performance metrics comparable to current
state-of-the-art models (89.8% accuracy versus Gödel Agent’s 90.6% on
MGSM; 89.1% versus 87.9% on MMLU), while exhibiting notably reduced
variance (25–59%) and enhanced robustness. Furthermore, the framework
achieves a 100% evolution success rate—superior to the baseline’s
82%—and diminishes required optimization iterations by approximately 50%
(from 30 iterations in baseline to 15.1 and 13.4 across tasks), and
provides improved stability for production deployment.

## 1. Introduction

Self-evolving agents represent a significant advancement in autonomous
AI systems, offering the potential for continuous self-improvement
through iterative learning and adaptation. While the Gödel Agent
framework ([Yin et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)) demonstrated promising
results using OpenAI models, its practical implementation faces
substantial challenges. This paper addresses these challenges through
the lens of Amazon Bedrock and Claude models integration, proposing
crucial innovations that enhance the robustness and reliability.

The original implementation using OpenAI models, while groundbreaking,
exhibited significant stability issues with 4% of times experiencing
unexpected termination during evolution process and 14% optimization
failure, severely limiting its practical utility in production
environments. When replicating the Gödel Agent in Bedrock using Claude
models, we identified that the original framework’s evaluation feedback
mechanism, which passes task questions and solver responses without
optimization, frequently leads to immediate token exhaustion during the
evolution process. These challenges are further compounded by Bedrock’s
response formatting requirements, necessitating fundamental
architectural adjustments for reliable operation.

To address these challenges, we introduce GuideEvolve agent, featuring
two key innovations. First, we introduce a novel guided self-evolution
framework, where a specialized LLM-based task expert provides analytical
feedback to facilitate the agent’s autonomous improvement. The expert
module generates task-specific evaluations and recommendations, which
are then processed by the self-evolving agent to formulate optimization
strategies for the solver. This hierarchical architecture maintains
distinct contextual boundaries between evaluation logic and evolution
control, enabling efficient token utilization while ensuring focused
improvement pathways for task solutions. Notably, rather than
implementing direct multi-agent interactions, this framework employs a
unidirectional guidance mechanism where the expert’s analytical insights
are systematically integrated into the agent’s self-evolving process,
leading to iterative refinements in the solver’s operational logic.
Second, we develop the 3Ts self-governing mechanisms for the
self-evolving agent - Think Fast and Slow, Throwback, and Termination by
choice - enabling agents to optimize their cognitive processing,
retrieving previous best-performed logic for either recovery from
deterioration or facilitate the brainstorm for further improvement on
the solutions, and intelligently determine evolution endpoints.

Our framework is designed with practical applications in mind,
particularly targeting enterprise use cases such as service improvement
recommendations in AWS through Service360 product, and retail business
strategy recommendations. By addressing the critical issues of
convergence and stability, GuideEvolve agent represents a significant
step toward making self-evolving agents viable for practical uses.
Through comprehensive resource management and robust error handling, our
framework transforms the challenges of Bedrock integration into
opportunities for enhanced reliability and performance.

## 2. Background

Self-evolving agents represent an emerging paradigm in artificial
intelligence that combines recursive self-improvement with large
language models (LLMs). These systems aim to autonomously enhance their
problem-solving capabilities through iterative refinement of their
solution strategies. The theoretical foundations of self-evolving agents
draw from several key areas in AI research. Constitutional AI frameworks
([Bai et al., 2022](#bib.bib1)) provide principles for maintaining
reliable and controlled behavior in autonomous systems. Recent advances
in AI planning and reasoning, such as Plan-and-Solve ([Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2)) and ReAct ([Yao et al., 2023](#bib.bib3)), offer
insights into structuring decision-making processes. Additionally,
developments in memory management and state tracking, exemplified by
GraphRAG (([Han et al., 2024](#bib.bib4))) and IRCoT ([Trivedi et al.,
2022](#bib.bib5)), provide crucial mechanisms for maintaining coherent
evolution paths.

In the realm of self-evolving agent architectures, Gödel Agent ([Yin et
al., 2025](#bib.bib14)) stands out as a pioneering framework for
autonomous self-modification. Its self-referential architecture enables
recursive code-level evolution, marking a significant advance in the
field of architecturally mutable agent systems. Several related research
efforts provide context for Gödel Agent’s approach. The Darwin Gödel
Machine ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)) offers a similar paradigm for
open-ended evolution, using version control to enable non-linear
evolutionary paths. AgentSquare ([Shang et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) takes
a more constrained approach, optimizing modular components within a
predefined design space. AlphaEvolve ([Novikov et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17)) applies evolutionary principles to specific
algorithmic domains, while ADAS ([Hu et al., 2024a](#bib.bib18)) and
AFlow ([Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) focus on higher-level agent
system design through formal optimization methods.

Among these, Gödel Agent and the Darwin Gödel Machine represent the most
comprehensive implementations of recursive self-improvement, which allow
for fundamental structural modifications and demonstrate the field’s
progress towards increasingly adaptable systems. However, current
approaches often lack efficient evaluation mechanisms, leading to
potential degradation in solution quality over iterations. The Gödel
Agent framework, for example, reported stability issues with 4% of times
experiencing unexpected termination and 14% optimization failure,
highlighting the need for more reliable evolution control mechanisms.
These challenges are particularly acute in enterprise environments,
where system stability and consistent performance are paramount.

## 3. GuideEvolve Framework

The GuideEvolve framework introduces a self-evolution paradigm that
strategically separates task expertise from evolution control, as shown
in Figure
[1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3. GuideEvolve Framework ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms").
At its core, a self-evolving agent orchestrates the evolution process by
coordinating between two specialized LLM-based modules: a solver module
focused on task execution and an expert module dedicated to performance
evaluation. This architectural design offers two significant advantages.
First, it enables unbiased evaluation through an independent expert
system that focuses solely on task-specific assessment without knowledge
of the solver’s implementation details, while allowing the self-evolving
agent to leverage its comprehensive visibility of the environment,
historical performance, and solver logic to make informed evolution
decisions. Second, this separation addresses the critical challenge of
context management in evolution control: by delegating task-specific
evaluation to a specialized expert module, the self-evolving agent can
more efficiently process and integrate guidance while maintaining
essential contextual information about the environment, goals, and
evolution strategies. This design fundamentally addresses limitations in
existing self-evolving frameworks, where the lack of specialized,
task-specific guidance and context overflow often constrain evolution
effectiveness.

Expert Evaluation Module. The expert module functions as a specialized
analytical consultant, maintaining focused task-specific context derived
from dataset descriptions and required capabilities. This module’s
isolation from the solver’s implementation details and evolution state
ensures purely objective assessment based on performance outcomes. The
expert system processes strategically sampled success and failure cases,
analyzing task questions and LLM responses to generate comprehensive
performance insights. This stateless architecture serves dual purposes:
it maintains evaluation objectivity through isolation from evolution
mechanics while significantly reducing the context burden that would
otherwise impact the self-evolving agent’s efficiency. By processing raw
task-solver interactions independently, the expert system can generate
focused, high-quality feedback without the overhead of evolution-related
contextual information.

Guidance Integration and Evolution Mechanism. The framework implements a
structured guidance pipeline that optimizes information flow between its
core components. The expert evaluation module generates three critical
forms of guidance: quantitative performance metrics, detailed
strength/weakness analysis, and actionable insights derived from case
studies. The self-evolving agent, as the central control unit, processes
this concentrated feedback while maintaining its broader contextual
awareness of the environment, historical evolution patterns, and solver
behavior. This hierarchical architecture enables efficient information
processing: while the evaluation module focuses exclusively on
task-specific assessment and recommendations, the self-evolving agent
synthesizes these insights with its comprehensive contextual knowledge
to formulate targeted evolution strategies. The agent then translates
this synthesized understanding into concrete modifications of the
solver’s operational logic. This modular design not only ensures more
focused and effective guidance but also allows the self-evolving agent
to manage its context budget more efficiently, dedicating computational
resources to critical evolution decisions rather than raw task
evaluation processing. The clear separation of evaluation and evolution
control enables the agent to maintain a comprehensive view of the
evolution trajectory while receiving specialized task-specific guidance.

## 4. Understanding Guided Evolution Through an Optimization Lens

Our intuition for why expert guidance improves both efficiency and
robustness is based on an analogy to optimization theory. While the
self-evolution process is not literally gradient descent, viewing it
through this lens helps explain our empirical observations and suggests
which architectural choices matter most.

We model the evolution as optimizing solver logic $`x`$ to maximize
expected performance $`f(x)`$ on the task distribution:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (1) |     |

             ``` math
             x^{*}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x}\mathbb{E}[f(x)]
             ```                                                 |     |

Key Insight. The baseline Gödel Agent approach ([Yin et al.,
2025](#bib.bib14)) resembles zeroth-order optimization, where the agent
must infer improvement directions from noisy function evaluations alone,
without explicit gradient information. In contrast, GuideEvolve
resembles first-order optimization, where the expert module acts as an
oracle providing gradient-like directional guidance. This analogy
predicts the observed efficiency gains and enhanced stability of our
approach.

### 4.1. Baseline Evolution as Zeroth-Order Search

Following the Gödel Agent framework ([Yin et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)),
the self-evolving agent must simultaneously evaluate performance and
determine update directions. At each iteration $`t`$, the agent’s
observation of performance is corrupted by context pollution:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (2) |     |

````math
\tilde{f}(x_{t})=f(x_{t})+\epsilon_{\text{eval}}+\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}
``` |  |

where $`\epsilon_{\text{eval}}`$ represents stochastic sampling noise
from evaluating on limited training examples, and
$`\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}`$ represents systematic observation bias.
Concretely, $`\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}`$ corresponds to the challenge of
extracting actionable insights when the agent must process raw
task-solver interactions (e.g., question-answer pairs with their full
context) mixed with evolution history within a shared context. The agent
then computes updates:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (3) |  |
``` math
x_{t+1}=x_{t}+\alpha\cdot[\text{direction}(\tilde{f}(x_{t}),\text{history}_{t})+\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}]
``` |  |

where $`\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}`$ represents processing
bias from context overflow when maintaining long evolution histories
containing raw evaluation data alongside decision-making logic. Just as
noisy gradient estimates harm optimization convergence, the compounding
effect of polluted observations ($`\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}`$) and bloated
processing context ($`\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}`$) leads to
inconsistent evolution trajectories across trials and potential
convergence to suboptimal solutions.

### 4.2. Guided Evolution as First-Order Approximation

Our framework decomposes the optimization into a two-level structure.
The expert module acts as an oracle that processes raw performance data
in isolation:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (4) |  | $`\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\text{clean}}(x_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=f(x_{t})+\epsilon_{\text{eval}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle h(x_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\text{expert}(\tilde{f}_{\text{clean}}(x_{t}),x_{t})`$ |  |

The expert processes only task-specific information without access to
evolution mechanics, producing concentrated guidance $`h(x_{t})`$
analogous to curated gradient information. In practice, $`h(x_{t})`$ is
a structured report containing strengths/weaknesses analysis and
actionable recommendations (see
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E An Example of Task Expert’s Performance Report ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms")
for an example). This resembles replacing noisy finite-difference
gradients with analytical gradients in optimization. The self-evolving
agent then performs guided updates:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (5) |  |
``` math
x_{t+1}=x_{t}+\alpha\cdot[\text{direction}(h(x_{t}),\text{history}_{t})+\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{low})}]
``` |  |

Critically, GuideEvolve eliminates context bias at two levels: (1)
observation level—the agent receives clean guidance $`h(x_{t})`$ rather
than polluted observations $`\tilde{f}(x_{t})`$ (eliminating
$`\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}`$), and (2) processing level—the agent’s history
no longer contains raw evaluation data, substantially reducing context
load such that
$`\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{low})}\ll\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}`$.

### 4.3. Predicted Benefits from the Analogy

This optimization perspective generates three testable predictions:

Improved Accuracy through Cleaner Signals. The expert’s context
isolation eliminates systematic observation bias, providing cleaner
directional signals. For knowledge-intensive tasks (MMLU), this enables
discovery of better local optima through more accurate gradient
approximations, resulting in superior convergence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (6) |  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[f(x^{*}_{\text{GuideEvolve}})]\geq\mathbb{E}[f(x^{*}_{\text{baseline}})]
``` |  |

For tasks with fundamental capability constraints (e.g., MGSM requiring
symbolic computation), both approaches converge to similar accuracy
plateaus determined by underlying model capabilities.

Enhanced Stability through Variance Reduction. The dual-level
elimination of context bias should fundamentally improve trajectory
consistency across independent trials. We can conceptualize the variance
in final performance as composed of multiple sources:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (7) |  | $`\displaystyle\text{Var}[f(x^{*})]\approx`$ | $`\displaystyle\underbrace{\sigma^{2}_{\text{eval}}}_{\text{from }\epsilon_{\text{eval}}}+\underbrace{\sigma^{2}_{\text{ctx}}}_{\text{from }\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}}`$ |  |
|  |  |  | $`\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sigma^{2}_{\text{proc}}}_{\text{from }\delta_{\text{proc}}}+\underbrace{\sigma^{2}_{\text{path}}}_{\text{trajectory randomness}}`$ |  |

Under this conceptual model, GuideEvolve should reduce three of the four
terms: (a) $`\sigma^{2}_{\text{ctx}}`$ eliminated through expert’s clean
observation (removing $`\epsilon_{\text{ctx}}`$), (b)
$`\sigma^{2}_{\text{proc}}`$ substantially reduced via lean evolution
history without raw evaluation data (reducing
$`\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{high})}`$ to
$`\delta_{\text{proc}}^{(\text{low})}`$), and (c)
$`\sigma^{2}_{\text{path}}`$ reduced via consistent, unbiased guidance
signals. Note that $`\sigma^{2}_{\text{eval}}`$ should remain unchanged
as both approaches use identical sampling strategies.

Faster Convergence through Informed Search. The first-order-like
guidance $`h(x_{t})`$ should enable more efficient exploration compared
to zeroth-order search with polluted observations and bloated context:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (8) |  |
``` math
\text{Iter.}_{\text{GuideEvolve}}\ll\text{Iter.}_{\text{baseline}}
``` |  |

### 4.4. Empirical Support and Scope of the Analogy

Our experimental results
(Section [7](#S7 "7. Experiments Results ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms"))
are consistent with these predictions. The accuracy patterns across
tasks match the expectation that cleaner signals benefit
knowledge-intensive tasks (MMLU) more than capability-constrained tasks
(MGSM). The observed 25-59% variance reduction
(Table [2](#S7.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 7.1. Performance Analysis ‣ 7. Experiments Results ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms"))
is consistent with the predicted elimination of
$`\sigma^{2}_{\text{ctx}}`$ and reduction of
$`\sigma^{2}_{\text{proc}}`$ and $`\sigma^{2}_{\text{path}}`$, while
$`\sigma^{2}_{\text{eval}}`$ remains present as both approaches use
identical sampling strategies. The 50% reduction in required iterations
(Table [3](#S7.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 7.1. Performance Analysis ‣ 7. Experiments Results ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms"))
supports the informed search prediction. This optimization lens—while
not a formal proof—provides valuable intuition for why specific
architectural choices (expert isolation, lean context management, and
clean information flow) translate into the observed stability and
efficiency improvements.

## 5. Self-Governing Evolution Mechanisms (3Ts)

Our framework introduces three critical self-governing mechanisms -
Think fast and slow, Throwback, and Termination by Choice (3Ts) - that
enable autonomous and efficient evolution control.

Think, Fast and Slow. Our framework implements a dual-mode cognitive
processing paradigm through prompt engineering. "Fast thinking" enables
efficient execution of multiple low-risk actions in sequence, while
"slow thinking" enforces careful step-by-step planning for
resource-intensive or error-prone operations. This balance optimizes
resource utilization while maintaining solution quality through dynamic
planning strategies. Notably, performance evaluation through the expert
module is only initiated after confirmed successful modifications to the
solver’s logic, representing a key "slow thinking" checkpoint in the
evolution process. The details can be found in goal prompt in Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Goal Prompt ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms").

Throwback. The throwback mechanism represents a crucial innovation in
evolution control, extending beyond simple temporal rollback
functionality. The system maintains two distinct tracking mechanisms:
comprehensive performance monitoring across the entire evolution
history, and state preservation of the solver logic at its previous
best-performing state. While the performance tracking provides a
complete evolutionary trajectory, the selective state preservation
minimizes storage overhead while maintaining the capability for both
defensive rollback and proactive learning of the self-evolving agent.
This design choice reflects our focus on efficient memory utilization
while ensuring the agent can either revert to proven optimal states or
analyze previous success factors for continued improvement.

We observe three distinct patterns in the agent’s autonomous throwback
decisions: 1) Strategic state examination, where the agent revisits
previous high-performing states to identify untapped optimization
opportunities; 2) Defensive rollback in response to significant
performance deterioration, restoring the solver to a known optimal
state; 3) Context-driven recall during progressive message reduction,
where the agent strategically retrieves and analyzes historical solver
logic to maintain evolution coherence. An example of throwback is
illustrated in Figure
[2](#acmlabel2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5. Self-Governing Evolution Mechanisms (3Ts) ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms").

This mechanism differs fundamentally from traditional tree-based
approaches by enabling flexible state examination without enforcing
strict hierarchical relationships between states. The agent can
autonomously decide when and how to utilize historical information,
whether for performance recovery or optimization insight.

Termination By Choice. The framework implements a flexible termination
mechanism that balances autonomous decision-making with performance
guarantees, as illustrated in Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Illustration of Termination by Choice ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms").
Our observations reveal emerging behavioral patterns in self-evolving
agents: some "lazy" agents that attempt early termination after minimal
trials, and some "diligent" agents that persist well beyond suggested
termination conditions. This demonstrates the full autonomy of
termination by agent’s choice. To prevent premature termination,
especially from "lazy" agents, the system implements validation checks
that provide feedback such as "you need to have at least five trials,
keep going" to enforce further exploration of the solution space.

## 6. Resource Management System

The integration in Bedrock using Claude models necessitates careful
management of token limitations and response completeness. Our framework
implements a comprehensive resource management system that transforms
these constraints into opportunities for optimization.

Token Budget Optimization. Our system employs a dynamic token allocation
strategy that prioritizes essential operations while maximizing
efficiency. The framework reserves token budget primarily for tool calls
— the critical mechanism for action execution — while maintaining system
messages as immutable components. A dynamic sliding window manages
action history, automatically adjusting output token limits based on
input context size. This preventative approach optimizes token
utilization while ensuring tool call generation under varying context
loads.

Response Validation and Context Management. The framework implements a
two-tier approach to handle potential token overflow scenarios. First,
it leverages Bedrock’s structured response format to implement robust
completion detection through JSON validation and tool call verification.
When token limitations lead to response truncation, the system activates
a progressive context reduction mechanism that systematically preserves
essential information while reducing context size. This context
reduction process operates sequentially: 1) Removal of older action
history messages while maintaining recent; 2) Progressive increase in
reduction scope if truncation in response persists; 3) Maintenance of
minimal viable context with at least one recent action message.

This integrated approach, coupled with the framework’s throwback
capability, ensures operational continuity while minimizing information
loss. The system effectively balances immediate operational needs with
long-term evolution stability, transforming platform constraints into
mechanisms for enhanced reliability.

## 7. Experiments Results

We evaluate GuideEvolve on two benchmark datasets: Mathematics Grade
School Multi-source (MGSM) and Massive Multitask Language Understanding
(MMLU). MGSM tests multi-step mathematical reasoning and cross-lingual
problem-solving capabilities, while MMLU evaluates comprehensive
knowledge and problem-solving abilities across 57 diverse academic and
professional domains through multiple-choice questions.

Experimental Setup. We conduct our experiments using Anthropic’s Claude
3.5 Sonnet v2 as the default initial model for GuideEvolve’s components
(self-evolving agent, solver, and expert module). The self-evolving
agent can autonomously decide to switch models among Anthropic Claude
3.5 Haiku, Anthropic Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2 (implementation identifier:
`SONNET_V36`), and Anthropic Claude 3.7 Sonnet (`SONNET_V37`) during the
evolution process. Note that the model selected plays a critical role in
performance, as also reported in the original Gödel agent paper (([Yin
et al., 2025](#bib.bib14))).

Following Gödel’s experimental protocol for fair comparison, we evaluate
on 800 test samples and perform 6 independent trials for each task,
where each trial represents a complete self-improvement process with 20
training samples for validation during evolution. GuideEvolve
autonomously determines when to terminate based on either reaching a
performance plateau or a maximum of 30 iterations, though in practice,
it never requires the full 30 iterations. All quantitative performance
results reported in Tables

Improved Stability with Competitive Accuracy. As shown in Table
[2](#S7.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 7.1. Performance Analysis ‣ 7. Experiments Results ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms"),
we report the test accuracy along with the 95% bootstrap confidence
intervals on test sets, where bold indicates the best performing method.
GuideEvolve demonstrates improved stability across both tasks while
maintaining competitive accuracy. Across 6 independent trials with 800
test samples per task (identical to Gödel’s experimental setup),
GuideEvolve achieves 89.8% accuracy ($`\pm`$1.5%) on MGSM, delivering
performance comparable to Gödel-free (90.6% $`\pm`$2.0%) but with 25%
lower variance. For MMLU, GuideEvolve reaches 89.1% accuracy
($`\pm`$0.9%), surpassing Gödel-free’s 87.9% ($`\pm`$2.2%) by 1.2
percentage points while achieving 59% lower variance.

The performance differences across tasks reveal important insights into
domain-specific characteristics of self-evolution. For mathematical
reasoning tasks (MGSM), while the self-evolving agent successfully
explores solver prompts and workflow modifications, the inherent
limitations of LLMs in mathematical computation without external tools
constrain the achievable performance gains, resulting in comparable
accuracy to the baseline. In contrast, for knowledge-intensive verbal
reasoning tasks (MMLU), the self-evolution process demonstrates greater
efficacy in optimizing solver logic through prompt engineering and
reasoning strategies, leading to measurable accuracy improvements over
the baseline. These results align with the intuition that self-evolution
mechanisms are more effective in domains where linguistic and reasoning
optimizations can substantially impact performance, whereas domains
requiring symbolic computation or external tool integration present
fundamental capability boundaries.

The observed accuracy and variance improvements are primarily
attributable to our architectural innovations rather than underlying
model differences. Our ablation study
(Table [4](#S7.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 7.3. Ablation Study ‣ 7. Experiments Results ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms"))
confirms that removing the expert module and throwback mechanism—while
maintaining the same Claude models—results in 17% accuracy decrease and
substantial performance degradation, isolating the framework’s
contribution. These improvements stem from two key mechanisms: First,
our task expert module provides objective performance evaluation and
actionable proposals, efficiently guiding the self-evolving agent
towards better performance, in contrast to Gödel’s approach of relying
on the agent to self-evaluate raw task-solver interactions, which often
leads to context overflow and potentially biased judgments. Second,
GuideEvolve’s throwback mechanism enables the agent to revisit and
analyze its previous successful strategies, facilitating comparative
learning from its own successes and failures. This self-introspection
capability becomes particularly valuable when the agent encounters
performance plateaus, allowing it to draw insights from its evolution
history to push performance boundaries further.

Enhanced Robustness. In Table
[3](#S7.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 7.1. Performance Analysis ‣ 7. Experiments Results ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms"),
GuideEvolve demonstrates robustness with a 100% success rate in
evolution convergence and finding an improved solution over initial
state for both tasks. This perfect convergence rate significantly
outperforms Gödel’s 82% success rate on MGSM (with unreported
convergence on MMLU), where Gödel experiences stability issues in 4% of
trials (unexpected termination during evolution) and optimization
failures in 14% of trials. Our method’s success rate across both tasks
underscores the stability and reliability of our guided evolution
approach. This robustness is achieved through two features: the
throwback functionality enables the agent to recover from suboptimal
evolution paths by rolling back to previous successful states which
prevents optimization failures, while termination by choice allows the
agent to intelligently halt the evolution process when reaching a
performance plateau, avoiding deterioration from forced continuation
beyond the agent’s current capability limits.

Higher Evolution Efficiency. GuideEvolve demonstrates remarkable
efficiency in its self-improvement process, as shown in
Table [3](#S7.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 7.1. Performance Analysis ‣ 7. Experiments Results ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms").
It requires only 15.1 iterations for MGSM and 13.4 iterations for MMLU
on average, compared to Gödel’s 30 iterations—a reduction of
approximately 50% and 55% in evolving effort respectively. This
efficiency is particularly noteworthy given that GuideEvolve achieves
competitive or superior accuracy in fewer iterations. This efficiency
boost is attributed to: 1) expert guidance facilitates faster
convergence towards the best solver; 2) GuideEvolve’s intelligent
self-regulation capability to autonomously determine termination based
on performance plateaus avoids unnecessary iterations.

### 7.4. Limitations and Threats to Validity

Model Confound. Our primary comparison evaluates Claude-based
GuideEvolve against GPT-based Gödel Agent. While our ablation study
(Table [4](#S7.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 7.3. Ablation Study ‣ 7. Experiments Results ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms"))
isolates the contribution of our architectural innovations by comparing
variants using identical Claude models, we acknowledge that full
cross-model validation (e.g., GuideEvolve with GPT models or Gödel with
Claude models) would strengthen claims of framework-independent
improvements. The observed stability gains are consistent across our
Claude-based ablation experiments, suggesting architectural rather than
model-driven benefits.

Task Scope. Our evaluation focuses on two benchmark tasks (MGSM and
MMLU) with clear ground truth and quantitative metrics. Generalization
to open-ended business applications with multifaceted success criteria
remains future work, as discussed in
Section [8](#S8 "8. Future Work ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms").

Computational Cost. GuideEvolve introduces additional LLM calls through
the expert module. While this increases per-iteration cost compared to
Gödel, the 50% reduction in required iterations
(Table [3](#S7.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 7.1. Performance Analysis ‣ 7. Experiments Results ‣ GuideEvolve: Self-Evolving Agent Frameworkwith Expert Guidance and 3Ts Mechanisms"))
partially offsets this overhead. Detailed cost-benefit analysis for
production deployment is warranted.

## 8. Future Work

Our framework opens several promising research directions for future
development. While currently focusing on solver evolution, future
research could explore simultaneous evolution of both solver and expert
components through co-adaptive improvement, implementing meta-evaluation
capabilities where the agent assesses both guidance effectiveness and
solver performance. Moreover, integration with the Strands Agents
framework presents opportunities for enhanced production readiness and
scalability. Additionally, extending GuideEvolve framework to business
applications presents unique challenges due to lack of ground truth and
multifaceted success criteria, necessitating research into robust
evaluation metrics for complex business scenarios and methods for
decomposing business problems while maintaining coherent evolution
strategies.

We present representative examples of optimal solvers discovered by
GuideEvolve for both the MGSM and MMLU tasks. As noted in in the

3.5 Sonnet v2 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet respectively. Notably, GuideEvolve
was not provided with explicit model descriptions or capabilities;
rather, it autonomously discovered and leveraged the relative strengths
of these models through its evolutionary process. This demonstrates the
system’s ability to independently discern and utilize the varying
capabilities of different LLM models.

initial solver’s single response approach, implementing a more robust
strategy that generates multiple responses at three different

voting. Furthermore, the prompt has been enhanced with task-specific
guidance. A particularly noteworthy adaptation emerged in handling
Bengali mathematics questions, where the solver fallback to become a

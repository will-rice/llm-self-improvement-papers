# On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models

Leon Sick^(1,2,$`\ast`$)    Lukas Hoyer¹    Yannick Strümpler¹   
Christian Reisswig¹ Affiliation: Timo Ropinski²    Federico Tombari¹
Affiliation: ¹Google    ²Ulm University

###### Abstract

On-policy self-distillation enables post-training foundation models
without relying on stronger external teachers. However, existing
approaches rely on ground-truth question-answer pairs, human-curated
problem sets, or other supervised signals. In this work, we reformulate
on-policy self-distillation as a self-training framework, driven by
self-generated question-answer pairs and privileged reasoning
demonstrations. We push supervision to the limit: guided solely by
coarse, high-level topic descriptions, the model synthesizes its own
curriculum of queries and privileged reasoning traces without access to
any human-curated prompts or answers. We find that self-distillation on
fully synthetic privileged information enables a model to improve across
multiple domains while retaining its capabilities on domains it is not
self-trained on. We demonstrate the generality of this framework across
text and multimodal domains, achieving consistent self-improvement on
standard language benchmarks and Visual Question Answering. Our findings
demonstrate that foundation models possess rich latent capabilities that
can be amplified without external task supervision or curated problem
distributions.

## 1 Introduction

Post-training is a primary driver of capability gains in large language
models ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib23)), and its paradigms differ
along two axes: whether training trajectories come from the model
itself, and how dense the learning signal is. Supervised finetuning
(SFT) imitates curated demonstrations, providing dense token-level
targets on teacher-forced trajectories the model never visits at
inference, which induces exposure bias ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib9);
[Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)) and erodes existing capabilities
([Shenfeld et al., 2026b](#bib.bib29)). Reinforcement learning with
verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains on the model’s own rollouts ([Guo
et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib27)), avoiding
this mismatch but compressing supervision into a single scalar per
trajectory. On-policy distillation (OPD) combines the two: the student
samples its own trajectories, and a teacher supplies dense next-token
targets at the states it visits ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Gu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)). On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) removes
the need for a stronger teacher by constructing teacher and student from
the same parameters under asymmetric conditioning ([Zhao et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib40); [Shenfeld et al., 2026a](#bib.bib28)). Across this
progression, external supervision is relocated rather than removed: SFT
requires reference solutions, RLVR gold answers, OPD a stronger teacher,
and OPSD a privileged context, typically the ground-truth solution.

Curating this required supervision at scale is costly: process-level
annotation of a single mathematics corpus required about 800,000 human
step labels ([Lightman et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)), and open instruction
datasets rest on crowdsourcing efforts spanning over 13,000 volunteers
([Köpf et al., 2023](#bib.bib17)). This cost has motivated a shift
toward synthetic supervision, in which the model generates its own
instructions ([Wang et al., 2023b](#bib.bib32)), rationales ([Zelikman
et al., 2022](#bib.bib36)), or pseudo-solutions ([Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib20)). However, these methods still start from
human-written problems, so human curation still decides which problems
the model trains on and, in turn, what it can learn.

In language models, self-training is an established approach to
self-improvement ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib36); [Huang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib14); [Singh et al., 2024](#bib.bib30); [Zuo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib42)). The model solves problems, filters its solutions by
agreement with a reference answer or its own majority vote, and is
finetuned on those it retains. These methods, however, still train on a
curated problem set, which must be collected anew for every task ([Singh
et al., 2024](#bib.bib30)). Yet a language model can pose problems, not
only solve the ones it is given ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib39); [Chen
et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)). Therefore, we investigate whether a language
model can self-train without labels or a curated problem set, requiring
only topic descriptors from which it generates its own training
problems.

In this work, we propose On-Policy Self-Training (OPST), an approach to
self-train a language model with dense supervision derived from
self-generated privileged information
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")).
We remove both ground-truth answers and the curated problem set:
conditioned only on a coarse topic descriptor, the model synthesizes
both a query and the privileged information (PI) used to train on it.
Since the generated question-answer pairs are no longer human-curated,
the quality of this data can be noisy. Hence, we filter the generated
data along two axes: Pseudo-verification ensures that a problem is
well-formed, requiring the model to reproduce its answer in a fresh
context and under a paraphrase. Further, we apply a consensus gate to
ensure that the problem is informative, discarding problems whose answer
the student already consistently reproduces across on-policy rollouts.
Because the gate is evaluated against the current student, the
difficulty of the retained curriculum implicitly evolves as the student
model improves. On the quality-filtered samples, we train the model with
on-policy self-distillation
(Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")),
where the teacher provides high-quality token-level supervision to the
student, limiting catastrophic forgetting of existing capabilities
acquired from supervised data ([Shenfeld et al., 2026b](#bib.bib29);
[Shenfeld et al., 2026a](#bib.bib28)). These effects come from only the
teacher being conditioned on the privileged information, while the
student receives the question alone.

This asymmetry is crucial in a purely synthetic setting. SFT on the
generated answer, or RL rewarding agreement with it, treats the
generated answer as the optimization target, which bounds the
supervision by the quality of the generation. We show that providing the
synthetic answer as context to the teacher instead enables it to produce
a reasoning rollout from its own parametric knowledge that serves as an
enriched learning signal to the student. The student’s supervision is
therefore extended by what the teacher can infer from the privileged,
generated context, exceeding what the generated answer alone provides.
Iterating this loop improves the model over successive rounds, while an
exponential moving average (EMA) teacher stabilizes against self-induced
distribution drift.

In our experiments, we show that OPST improves language and multimodal
reasoning without human-curated questions or answers, outperforming
alternative training strategies. On Gemma 4 E2B ([Gemma Team et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7)), self-training increases MMLU ([Hendrycks et al.,
2021a](#bib.bib11)) accuracy by $`+10.2`$ points, with consistent gains
on GSM8K ([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib5)), GPQA ([Rein et al.,
2024](#bib.bib25)), and HellaSwag ([Zellers et al., 2019](#bib.bib37)).
We also find that self-training in-domain transfers to
out-of-distribution domains, with performance either remaining stable or
also improving. Applied to vision-language models with unlabeled images
alone, Visual OPST improves VQAv2 accuracy by $`+6.9`$ points.

## 2 Related Work

On-policy self-distillation. OPSD ([Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib40))
constructs both policies from a single model, conditioning the teacher
on a verified reference solution. Since on-policy methods train on the
model’s own samples, they forget less than SFT ([Shenfeld et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib29)), and self-distillation has been shown to enable
continual learning across multiple domains ([Shenfeld et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib28)). Subsequent work varies the privileged context
between expert demonstrations ([Shenfeld et al., 2026a](#bib.bib28)),
privileged observations ([Penaloza et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)), and
environmental feedback ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). Others
address information leakage and instability ([Yang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib35)) or extend the setting to agentic tasks ([Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib22)). Closest to our work, [Li et al. (2026)](#bib.bib20)
replace the gold solution with a majority-vote consensus over the
model’s own rollouts, eliminating answer supervision. All of these,
however, operate on a given distribution of problems. OPST removes that
assumption: the model synthesizes its own queries from a coarse topic
descriptor, so neither the problems nor their solutions are externally
supplied.

LLM Self-Training. Early self-training methods filter solutions by
agreement with a reference answer ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib36);
[Singh et al., 2024](#bib.bib30)) or by self-consistency across samples
([Huang et al., 2023](#bib.bib14)), and label-free reinforcement
learning has since replaced the reference answer with majority votes
([Zuo et al., 2025](#bib.bib42)) or the model’s own confidence ([Zhao
et al., 2026b](#bib.bib41)). These methods remove answers but not
problems: each still trains on a curated problem set, which must be
collected anew for every task ([Singh et al., 2024](#bib.bib30)).
Self-play methods let a proposer generate tasks, seeded by as little as
a topic description and checked by a code executor or by majority voting
over the solver’s answers ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib39); [Huang
et al., 2026](#bib.bib13); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)).
Self-generated problems, however, carry information that self-play
reduces to a scalar reward: the answer and rationale the model produces
while posing them. Used as privileged context for a self-distillation
teacher, it instead yields dense, token-level supervision. On curated
problems, [Li et al. (2026)](#bib.bib20) show that self-generated
answers are considerably more effective as teacher context than as a
reward, and [Zhang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib38) combine self-play with
self-distillation for search agents. While their method requires no
external training data, question construction is grounded in documents
retrieved from an external corpus, whereas OPST generates problems from
the model’s parametric knowledge alone. Whether a model can improve from
problems it poses itself, with dense token-level supervision and without
retrieval or other external grounding, has to our knowledge not yet been
studied for general reasoning.

Figure 2: Model training. As in OPSD ([Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib40)),
the student sees only the question and matches the next-token
distribution of a teacher that also sees privileged information. In
OPST, this information is self-generated, and the teacher is an EMA of
the student.

## 3 Method

In this section, we present On-Policy Self-Training. We first define the
data-free post-training setting
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Problem Formulation: Data-Free Post-Training ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models"))
and review on-policy self-distillation
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Preliminaries: On-Policy Self-Distillation ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")).
Following, we describe how the model generates problems and privileged
information from topic descriptors
(Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Topic-Seeded Curriculum Generation ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")),
how it filters them intrinsically
(Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Pseudo-Verification & Consensus Gate ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")),
and how it trains on the result
(Section [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Model Training ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")).
Repeating these steps yields multiple rounds of self-training
(Section [3.6](#S3.SS6 "3.6 Iterative Self-Training ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")).
Finally, we extend OPST to vision-language models
(Section [3.7](#S3.SS7 "3.7 Visual OPST ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")).

### 3.1 Problem Formulation: Data-Free Post-Training

Post-training typically relies on a curated dataset of human-written
queries and ground-truth answers $`\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{*})\}`$,
or at least on a curated prompt distribution
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{prompts}}=\{x_{i}\}`$. In _data-free
post-training_, neither is available. Training is seeded only by a set
of coarse topic descriptors $`\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{K}\}`$,
where each $`\tau_{k}`$ names a broad subject area, such as _“Abstract
Algebra”_ or _“Commonsense Causal Reasoning”_. From $`\mathcal{T}`$, the
model generates a synthetic training set
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{syn}}=\{(x_{i},z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}`$, where
$`x_{i}`$ is a generated query and $`z_{i}`$ its privileged information,
consisting of a rationale and a candidate answer
(Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Topic-Seeded Curriculum Generation ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")).

The setting has two objectives. First, the model must improve its
reasoning in the seeded domains using only self-generated problems and
privileged information. Second, because the training signal comes from
its own generations without human oversight, the model must retain its
general reasoning and previously acquired parametric knowledge on
domains it is not self-trained on, a prerequisite for repeating the
process over multiple rounds.

### 3.2 Preliminaries: On-Policy Self-Distillation

On-policy self-distillation ([Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib40)) derives
the student $`\pi_{\theta}`$ and the teacher $`\pi_{\phi}`$ from the
same model and replaces the capability gap of standard distillation
([Kim & Rush, 2016](#bib.bib16)) with an information gap. Given a
problem $`x`$, the student samples a response
$`y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$, with next-token
distribution

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       p_{t}=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})
       ```                                     |     | (1) |

at step $`t`$. The teacher evaluates the same prefix $`y_{<t}`$, but is
additionally conditioned on privileged information $`z`$ that the
student does not see:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       q_{t}=\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x,z,y_{<t}).
       ```                                      |     | (2) |

The student is trained to match the teacher along its own trajectory,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{OPSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,z)\sim\mathcal{D},\;y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{t}M_{t}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}M_{t}\,D(p_{t}\,\|\,q_{t})\right],
``` |  | (3) |

where $`D`$ is a divergence between distributions, $`M_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$
masks padding tokens, and no gradient flows through the teacher. In
standard OPSD, $`z`$ is a reference solution ([Zhao et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib40)).

Existing OPSD methods, however, assume a curated problem set and
typically use a reference solution as $`z`$. The following sections
describe how OPST generates both with the model itself.

### 3.3 Topic-Seeded Curriculum Generation

Instead of drawing problems from a curated distribution, OPST generates
them from the topic descriptors $`\mathcal{T}`$. Given a topic
$`\tau\in\mathcal{T}`$, a generator $`\pi_{\text{gen}}`$ produces a
query $`x`$ together with privileged information $`z`$,

|     |                                                         |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       (x,z)\sim\pi_{\text{gen}}(\cdot\mid\tau),\quad z=(r,a),
       ```                                                      |     | (4) |

where $`r`$ is a rationale and $`a`$ a candidate answer. Queries can be
multiple-choice or open-ended, and we sample at temperature
$`T_{\text{gen}}`$ to obtain diverse problems. While the student
receives the query $`x`$ alone, the teacher also receives $`z`$ as
additional privileged information. We embed the topic name into a
targeted data generation prompt, asking the model to pose problems on
the given topic along with generated privileged information. In
practice, the model generates partial or full answers to the posed
problem. Where available, topics are data-domain category labels. The
applied prompt templates are provided in
Appendix [A.7](#A1.SS7 "A.7 OPST Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models").

We instantiate $`\pi_{\text{gen}}`$ either as the EMA teacher
$`\pi_{\phi}`$
(Section [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Model Training ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")),
so that data quality can improve as training progresses, or as a frozen
copy of the initial model $`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$. The student does not
generate data, but its rollouts determine which samples are retained
(Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Pseudo-Verification & Consensus Gate ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")).

### 3.4 Pseudo-Verification & Consensus Gate

Removing human supervision removes not only human data curation but also
quality assurance. Generated samples can contain ill-posed questions or
incorrect candidate answers, and training on them propagates these
errors. At the same time, not every well-posed sample is worth training
on: if the student already solves a problem reliably, the teacher
conditioned on $`z`$ produces nearly the same next-token distribution as
the student, and the sample contributes little beyond its generation and
rollout cost.

We therefore filter any generated sample for two criteria:
*Pseudo-verification* first estimates whether a sample is well-formed,
and the *consensus gate* then approximates whether it is informative for
the current student.

#### Pseudo-Verification.

We evaluate if a given generated sample possesses two properties of a
well-posed problem: its answer should be reproducible without the
generator’s own intent as context, and it should not depend on the
particular wording of the question.

To pseudo-verify the generated answer, we require the generator to
recover it when solving the question in a fresh context, without access
to $`z`$ and at a low temperature, which yields $`\hat{a}(x)=a`$. This
separates authoring from solving: when query and answer are generated in
one pass, a flawed or ambiguous question can still receive a confident
answer. To pseudo-verify the generated problem statement, we prompt the
generator to paraphrase $`x`$ into
$`x^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\text{gen}}(\cdot\mid x)`$ with an instruction to
preserve its meaning, solve $`x^{\prime}`$ under the same conditions,
and require the answer to be unchanged, $`\hat{a}(x^{\prime})=a`$. A
well-posed question has the same answer regardless of its wording, so
this rejects questions whose answer depends on incidental phrasing,
which typically indicates that the question admits more than one
reading. A sample is retained only if:
$`v(x,z)=\mathbb{I}\big[\hat{a}(x)=a\big]\cdot\mathbb{I}\big[\hat{a}(x^{\prime})=a\big]`$

#### Consensus Gate.

We assume that the samples that carry the most signal are those the
student cannot yet answer reliably: a question that is trivial for the
student yields the same answer on every rollout, and the privileged
teacher has little to add. We therefore measure answer agreement across
sampled reasoning paths as in self-consistency ([Wang et al.,
2023a](#bib.bib31)). For each verified sample, the student samples $`G`$
rollouts $`\{y_{g}\}_{g=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$, from
which we extract answers $`\{\hat{a}_{g}\}_{g=1}^{G}`$. Samples with
full agreement with the candidate answer,
$`c(x,z)=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\mathbb{I}[\hat{a}_{g}=a]=1`$, provide
little new signal for the model and are discarded.

The two filters act in opposite directions by design.
Pseudo-verification keeps samples that are stable enough for the
generated answer to serve as privileged information, while the consensus
gate discards samples the student already solves. The retained samples
therefore lie at the student’s capability frontier, and since the gate
depends on the current student, the difficulty of the curriculum evolves
with the model.

### 3.5 Model Training

Given a pseudo-verified batch
$`\mathcal{B}=\{(x_{i},z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{B}`$, we update the student with
on-policy self-distillation
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Preliminaries: On-Policy Self-Distillation ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")),
where $`p_{i,t}`$ and $`q_{i,t}`$ denote the student and teacher
distributions
(Eqs. [1](#S3.E1 "In 3.2 Preliminaries: On-Policy Self-Distillation ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")
and [2](#S3.E2 "In 3.2 Preliminaries: On-Policy Self-Distillation ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models"))
at step $`t`$ of the student’s rollout on sample $`i`$.

#### Generalized Divergence.

Following the generalized formulation of prior work ([Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1); [Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib40)), we instantiate the
divergence $`D`$ in
Eq. ([3](#S3.E3 "In 3.2 Preliminaries: On-Policy Self-Distillation ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models"))
as a skewed Jensen–Shannon divergence. With the interpolated mixture
distribution $`\bar{q}_{i,t}=1-\alpha)\,q_{i,t}+\alpha\,p_{i,t}`$ for
$`\alpha\in(0,1)`$, the per-token divergence is defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\alpha}(p_{i,t}\,\|\,q_{i,t})=\alpha\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{i,t}\,\|\,\bar{q}_{i,t})+(1-\alpha)\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{i,t}\,\|\,\bar{q}_{i,t}).
``` |  | (5) |

In the limits,
$`D_{\alpha}/\alpha\to D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{i,t}\,\|\,p_{i,t})`$ as
$`\alpha\to 0`$, recovering the mode-covering forward KL divergence, and
$`D_{\alpha}/(1-\alpha)\to D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{i,t}\,\|\,q_{i,t})`$ as
$`\alpha\to 1`$, recovering the mode-seeking reverse KL divergence.
Unlike unbounded KL objectives, $`D_{\alpha}`$ is upper-bounded by the
binary entropy $`H(\alpha)`$, ensuring that tokens to which one
distribution assigns near-zero probability, such as localized artifacts
introduced by an imperfect synthetic hint, are prevented from dominating
the gradient update. We set $`\alpha=0.8`$, which favors the
mode-seeking regime. The final objective is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{OPST}}(\theta;\mathcal{B})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\,\frac{1}{\sum_{t}M_{i,t}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}M_{i,t}\,D_{\alpha}(p_{i,t}\,\|\,q_{i,t}).
``` |  | (6) |

#### EMA Teacher.

The teacher is initialized from the student, $`\phi_{0}=\theta_{0}`$,
and updated after every gradient step as
$`\phi_{k}=(1-\beta)\,\phi_{k-1}+\beta\,\theta_{k}`$. Since the teacher
produces the dense supervision, it must remain stable. With $`\beta=1`$,
a single noisy batch immediately changes the targets for subsequent
batches, and errors can reinforce themselves. The EMA instead averages
out such fluctuations over roughly $`1/\beta`$ steps. Averaging the
student’s weights also yields a more accurate model than the student
itself
(Table [6](#A1.T6 "Table 6 ‣ A.2 Further Ablations ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")d),
consistent with momentum teachers in vision ([Caron et al.,
2021](#bib.bib2)). For this reason, the teacher is the model we
evaluate.

### 3.6 Iterative Self-Training

A single round of OPST is limited by the quality of the data and
supervision that the initial model provides. We therefore repeat
generation, verification, and training over $`N`$ rounds. The student
and teacher weights carry over from one round to the next. For each
additional round, $`\pi_{\text{gen}}`$ generates a new training set,
from which we remove problems that duplicate those of earlier rounds.

The iterative nature of our loop lets student and teacher co-evolve.
Training on new data further improves the student, and through the EMA
update, also the teacher. A stronger teacher, in turn, benefits the next
round in three ways. It provides more accurate token-level targets on
the student’s rollouts. As the generator, it produces better problems
and privileged information. And as the verifier
(Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Pseudo-Verification & Consensus Gate ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")),
it judges its own data more reliably, so fewer incorrect samples reach
training. Because the teacher is an average over many training steps,
only improvements that persist across batches enter this cycle.

### 3.7 Visual OPST

OPST is modality-agnostic and can be extended to the multimodal setting
by adding an unlabeled modality. We therefore explore this for
vision-language tasks by adding images to the process. Visual OPST uses
a collection of unlabeled images $`\mathcal{I}`$ and visual topic
descriptors $`\mathcal{T}_{\text{vis}}`$, such as *“Spatial Relations”*,
*“Object Counting”*, or *“Attribute Recognition”*. Unlike the text
setting, it requires unlabeled images as input, from which we generate
the same data as detailed in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Topic-Seeded Curriculum Generation ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models").

#### Visual Curriculum Generation.

Given an image $`I\in\mathcal{I}`$ and a topic
$`\tau\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{vis}}`$, the generator produces a question
about the image together with privileged information,
$`(x,z)\sim\pi_{\text{gen}}(\cdot\mid I,\tau)`$. We consider two forms
of privileged information. In the first, $`z`$ is a detailed scene
caption that the generator writes for $`I`$, describing its objects,
attributes, and spatial arrangement. In the second, we prompt the model
to detect the objects in the scene, from which we bootstrap bounding-box
grounded spatial relations. The privileged caption informs the teacher
rollout with explicit visual details that the student must extract from
the image itself, while the detected spatial relations help ground the
teacher with actual object positions. Both forms can be combined by
conditioning the teacher on the caption and the spatial relations.

#### Visual Verification.

Visual verification tests whether the candidate answer is supported by
the image rather than by a hallucinated premise. The generator solves
$`x`$ on $`K`$ augmented views $`\mathcal{A}_{k}(I)`$, obtained with
color jitter, rescaling, and random cropping, and we measure agreement
with the candidate answer:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v_{\text{aug}}(I,x,z)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{I}\big[\hat{a}(\mathcal{A}_{k}(I),x)=a\big].
``` |  | (7) |

Horizontal flips are excluded because they change the answer to
questions about left and right.

#### Model Training.

Training follows
Eq. ([6](#S3.E6 "In Generalized Divergence. ‣ 3.5 Model Training ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models"))
with the image added to both contexts: the student samples
$`y_{i}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid I_{i},x_{i})`$, and the teacher
provides $`q_{i,t}=\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid I_{i},x_{i},z_{i},y_{i,<t})`$.

## 4 Experiments

Our experimental evaluation focuses on three aspects: in-domain
self-improvement without human-curated data, knowledge retention on
out-of-domain (OOD) benchmarks when mixing data generation domains, and
transfer to multimodal inputs with Visual OPST.

## 6 Conclusion

We introduced On-Policy Self-Training (OPST), which post-trains a model
on self-generated problems and privileged information, seeded only by
coarse topic descriptors. We showed that conditioning the teacher on the
generated answer, rather than training on it, lets the teacher fill in
omitted reasoning steps and limits the influence of potentially noisy
data. We found that OPST outperforms SFT and GRPO on the same generated
data and methods that rely on curated questions, and extends to multiple
domains and vision-language models, demonstrating that models can
self-improve from self-generated data alone.

#### Limitations.

OPST can only amplify existing knowledge, and since pseudo-verification
measures self-consistency rather than correctness, consistent errors
still enter training. Our topic descriptors are hand-chosen, and our
experiments are limited to small models. Promising directions include
label-free stopping criteria and tool- or retrieval-grounded
verification.

### AI use statement

In this work, we used generative AI tools for implementing training
infrastructure and experiment scripts and for feedback on the
experimental design. We have not used generative AI tools for generating

### A.1 Self-Distillation Objectives on Generated Data.

Unlike a ground-truth reference solution, generated privileged
information $`z=(r,a)`$ can be ambiguous: the rationale $`r`$ may miss
important aspects, and the candidate answer $`a`$ may be partially or
fully incorrect. Therefore, training a model on $`z`$ directly inherits
these defects. SFT on the generated pairs maximizes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log\pi_{\theta}(z\mid x)=\sum_{j=1}^{|z|}\log\pi_{\theta}(z_{j}\mid x,z_{<j}),
``` |  | (8) |

which supervises the model only on the tokens of $`z`$. Critically, it
treats every token as correct, including any potential omissions and
errors. OPSD instead uses $`z`$ as context: at every state $`y_{<t}`$
the student visits, the teacher supplies a full next-token distribution,
so supervision covers the entire student rollout rather than the $`|z|`$
tokens of the generation. Crucially, the teacher produces this rollout
from its own knowledge under the exposure to the generated privileged
information. This leads to a more nuanced, higher-quality supervision,
since the teacher reweights the model’s own next-token distribution by
how consistent each continuation is with the privileged information.
This has three consequences for generated data. First, $`z`$ need not
spell out a step for the teacher to supervise it: tokens that move the
rollout toward $`a`$ are upweighted, so the teacher completes reasoning
that $`r`$ omits. Second, the target is expressed in the model’s own
distribution rather than in the surface form of $`z`$, so the student
does not imitate the phrasing or formatting of the generation. Third,
the prior limits the influence of a faulty $`z`$: an error shifts the
target only to the extent that the model finds it plausible, and where
the prior favors a different step, the teacher retains that step instead
of copying the error. The student therefore learns from what the model
can infer given $`z`$ as a hint, not from $`z`$ itself. Consequently,
conditioning the teacher on $`z`$ yields dense, prior-grounded
token-level supervision that filters generation noise, making on-policy
self-distillation well suited for learning from purely synthetic data.

The EMA teacher is meant to provide stable targets despite noisy updates

Without an EMA ($`\beta=1`$), the teacher follows the student’s current
weights, and OPST barely improves over the base model ($`38.1`$ vs.
$`37.5`$). Too much smoothing also slows progress: with $`\beta=0.01`$,
the teacher incorporates the student’s improvements too slowly and
reaches $`45.2`$, whereas $`\beta=0.05`$ performs best ($`47.7`$).

#### Dataset Size per Round.

The number of samples per round trades off the amount of data against
how often the improving teacher regenerates it. We vary it from $`57`$
to $`570`$ samples
(Table [6](#A1.T6 "Table 6 ‣ A.2 Further Ablations ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")b).
Accuracy increases up to $`285`$ samples and then saturates, decreasing
slightly to $`46.9`$ at $`570`$ samples. Larger rounds therefore add
generation cost without improving results.

#### Number of Rounds.

Iterating lets student and teacher improve each other, but errors in the
generated data can also accumulate over rounds
(Section [3.6](#S3.SS6 "3.6 Iterative Self-Training ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")).
We evaluate the model after different numbers of rounds
(Table [6](#A1.T6 "Table 6 ‣ A.2 Further Ablations ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")c).
Significant improvements emerge after the first three rounds ($`+5.6`$),
and accuracy is highest after seven rounds ($`47.7`$). Further rounds
bring no additional improvement, but performance remains stable at
around $`46`$, well above the base model.

#### Evaluation Target.

Because the EMA teacher averages over the student’s recent updates, it
may also be the better model to deploy. We compare the student and the
EMA teacher at the end of training
(Table [6](#A1.T6 "Table 6 ‣ A.2 Further Ablations ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")d).
The EMA teacher is $`1.8`$ points more accurate, consistent with the
student’s weights being affected by noise from recent batches that
averaging removes.

### A.5 OPST Algorithm

Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ A.5 OPST Algorithm ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")
summarizes the complete OPST training procedure. Both the student
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ and the privileged teacher $`\pi_{\phi}`$ are
initialized from the same weights $`\theta_{0}`$, so training requires
no external teacher model. Each of the $`N`$ rounds consists of two
stages: (1) a fresh curriculum of problems $`x`$ with privileged
information $`z`$ is synthesized from the topic set $`\mathcal{T}`$ and
filtered via pseudo-verification and the consensus gate
(Sections [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Topic-Seeded Curriculum Generation ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")
and [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Pseudo-Verification & Consensus Gate ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models")),
and (2) the student is trained on its own on-policy rollouts
$`y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ by minimizing
$`\mathcal{L}_{\text{OPST}}`$
(Eq. [6](#S3.E6 "In Generalized Divergence. ‣ 3.5 Model Training ‣ 3 Method ‣ On-Policy Self-Training: Data-Free Self-Improvement in Large Language Models"))
against the teacher conditioned on $`(x,z)`$. After every update, the
teacher tracks the student through an exponential moving average with
rate $`\beta`$. The teacher therefore improves alongside the student
while remaining a stable distillation target. After the final round, the
EMA teacher $`\pi_{\phi}`$ is returned as the final model.

### A.6 Evaluation Protocol

We follow the task formulations of the EleutherAI
lm-evaluation-harness ([Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib6)) and adapt them to
the Gemma 4 chat template. Without thinking, we score all
multiple-choice benchmarks, i.e., MMLU, GPQA Diamond, HellaSwag,
ARC-Challenge, and Winogrande, by log-likelihood over the closed set of
answer options. No answer is therefore parsed from generated text. We
use the harness defaults, i.e., 5-shot prompting for MMLU, zero-shot
prompting for the remaining multiple-choice benchmarks, and
length-normalized accuracy where the harness specifies it. GSM8K is
evaluated with 8-shot chain-of-thought prompting ([Wei et al.,
2022](#bib.bib33)).

With thinking, multiple-choice answers are parsed from the final Answer:
X line. GSM8K is graded by exact match of the final numerical answer,
and MATH-500 by symbolic equivalence of the final \boxed{} expression
using SymPy. For all models and benchmarks, both before and after
self-training, the rate of parsing failures and invalid answers is 0%.
Performance differences in the thinking regime therefore do not stem
from changes in answer formatting. All remaining settings follow the
harness defaults.

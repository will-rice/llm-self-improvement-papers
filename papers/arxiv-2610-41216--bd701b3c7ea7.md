# TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnesses to Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents

Kehong Gong    Xiaoxu Zheng    Guanli Hou    Seunghun Lee    Mengqi Guo
   Runjia Tan Affiliation: Huawei Technologies Co., Ltd  Huawei Noah
Research Institute  Independent Researcher^(\*)Corresponding author   
Zhengyu Wen    Qi Wang    Xiaoyu He    Mingyuan Zhang    Michael Bi Mi

###### Abstract

Robot agents built on vision–language models depend on both the planner
and its harness: the tools, feedback, memory, and recovery logic around
it. Can improvements to a large teacher’s harness be carried into a
compact agent with lower inference compute cost? We present TinyAstral,
a two-stage approach. We first freeze an open-weight teacher and improve
its harness through recursive self-improvement (RSI): frontier LLM
agents read the teacher’s trajectories and propose harness edits, and
each edit is accepted only if it passes a paired statistical test. We
then freeze the final harness, collect teacher trajectories with
reasoning and failure recovery, and distill a small student that
replaces the teacher in the same harness. On RoboTwin, harness evolution
raises the teacher’s success from 33.4% to 63.6%; the final 4B student
reaches 62.8%. Across four harness versions, distilled 4B students score
close to their corresponding teachers, and later versions yield stronger
students, showing that harness evolution gains carry into compact
models. The harness gains also transfer to other frozen planners without
retraining. On identical episodes, the final 4B student outperforms open
4B agents, the open 27B harness-RSI model SenseNova-RoboRSI, and our own
teacher model placed in three competitors’ harnesses. It stays close to
its teacher on all three training benchmarks, and on three benchmarks
never used for RSI or training it scores close to a 552B planner running
the same harness. On the measured deployment episodes, it uses about one
fifth of its teacher’s accelerator time.

## 1 Introduction

Vision–language models (VLMs) can operate robot arms by reading images
and calling perception and motion tools \[[25](#bib.bib19),
[18](#bib.bib20), [11](#bib.bib21), [12](#bib.bib24)\]. The model
chooses the object, action sequence, and stopping point; the surrounding
_harness_ grounds these choices, executes them, and reports what
happened. Tool interfaces, feedback, task memory, and failure recovery
therefore contribute to the agent’s performance alongside the model
itself \[[57](#bib.bib1), [40](#bib.bib7), [46](#bib.bib8),
[10](#bib.bib38)\].

Two lines of work offer complementary ways to build these agents.
Harness evolution improves the system around a frozen
planner \[[40](#bib.bib7), [46](#bib.bib8), [10](#bib.bib38)\]; agent
distillation trains compact models from a stronger agent’s
trajectories \[[27](#bib.bib5), [24](#bib.bib36), [56](#bib.bib37)\]. We
connect these approaches by asking: _can we first improve a large
teacher’s harness, then transfer the resulting decision-making
capability to a small model?_ This would allow the improved system to
run with substantially less inference compute.

We present TinyAstral, a family of 0.8B–4B manipulation agents built in
two stages
([Fig. 2](#S3.F2 "In 3 Method ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
First, we freeze the weights of an open-weight teacher,
Qwen3.8-Flash-Next \[[34](#bib.bib41)\], and improve its harness through
a master-guided recursive self-improvement (RSI) loop. Humans specify
the rules and budget in advance. Frontier LLM agents then inspect
failures and propose harness edits, which the system automatically
accepts or rejects according to pre-registered paired statistical tests.
Second, we freeze the final harness and collect teacher trajectories
containing reasoning, tool calls, and recovery from injected failures.
Together with auxiliary offline real-robot supervision, these
trajectories train a compact student to interpret feedback, choose
actions, recover from failure, and decide when to finish. At deployment,
the student replaces the teacher in the same frozen harness.

We evaluate both stages and the connection between them. Harness
evolution raises the frozen teacher’s RoboTwin success from 33.4% to
63.6%, and distillation produces a 4B student at 62.8%. To test whether
this connection holds beyond the final endpoint, we distill students at
four harness versions. The four students score 36.2%, 49.8%, 60.0%, and
62.8%, each close to its corresponding teacher. The same evolved harness
also improves DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and the untrained 4B initialization,
showing that its gains are reusable across planners. On identical
episodes the student outperforms every open competitor we ran, including
our own teacher model inside three competitors’ harnesses (93.6% vs.
78.9% on Guava; 78.3% vs. 57.3% and 53.0% on LIBERO-PRO), and on unseen
benchmarks the small 4B student scores close to a 552B planner in the
same harness.

Our contributions are:

- •
  Harness evolution followed by distillation. A two-stage approach first
  improves a frozen teacher’s manipulation harness, then trains a
  compact planner from that teacher’s reasoning and recovery
  trajectories for deployment in the same harness.
- •
  Evidence that evolution gains transfer. The harness improves other
  frozen planners, and distilled students at four harness versions track
  the performance of their corresponding teachers, showing that system
  improvements carry into compact models.
- •
  Compact agents with lower inference compute cost. A single
  TinyAstral-4B model covers six benchmarks: on identical episodes it
  outperforms every open competitor we ran, including our own teacher
  model inside three competitors’ harnesses, scores close to its teacher
  on the three training benchmarks and close to a 552B planner on unseen
  ones, and uses about one fifth of the teacher’s accelerator time per
  episode in our deployment measurement.

## 2 Related Work

#### Large models operating robots through a harness.

Language and vision-language models can operate robots by writing code
or issuing tool calls over perception and motion
primitives \[[25](#bib.bib19), [18](#bib.bib20), [11](#bib.bib21),
[12](#bib.bib24)\], with spatial-reasoning models supplying
grounding \[[32](#bib.bib22), [50](#bib.bib23)\]. Recent work shows that
the harness around the model, namely its tool interface, feedback,
memory, and recovery logic, is a first-order factor. Harness
VLA \[[57](#bib.bib1)\] composes a frozen VLA with memory-guided agents,
RoboDawn \[[14](#bib.bib3)\] and MotorMind \[[22](#bib.bib4)\] expose
closed-loop interfaces to a general VLM, and
PyRUA-Lean \[[37](#bib.bib9)\] cuts token cost with interactive code
execution. A growing line also _evolves_ the harness from execution
traces: PhysEvo \[[40](#bib.bib7)\] runs recursive self-improvement
around a single frozen model, HarnessPAI \[[46](#bib.bib8)\] evolves an
executable program, ASPIRE \[[28](#bib.bib2)\] accumulates a skill
library, and the two RoboRSI systems \[[10](#bib.bib38),
[47](#bib.bib35)\] refine skills and agent components with validation
before release. Concurrent with our work,
SenseNova-RoboRSI \[[10](#bib.bib38)\] also trains a 27B VLM on
validated interaction experience, and World Action
Agent \[[56](#bib.bib37)\] distills the tool-call traces of its
skill-evolved harness into a 9B VLM. We share the view that the harness
is what improves while model weights stay fixed. TinyAstral differs in
three ways: the harness of a locally hosted open-weight teacher evolves
continually under paired statistical selection, its gains are measured
across planners at successive snapshots, and the evolved teacher is
distilled into 0.8B, 2B, and 4B students that stay close to it.

#### Small manipulation agents and VLAs.

End-to-end VLAs such as OpenVLA \[[21](#bib.bib18)\],
$`\pi_{0}`$ \[[3](#bib.bib16)\], and $`\pi_{0.5}`$ \[[31](#bib.bib17)\]
map observations directly to actions and are trained on large robot
datasets, which makes them compact at inference but dependent on
in-distribution demonstrations; benchmarks such as
LIBERO-PRO \[[58](#bib.bib12)\] and RoboTwin 2.0 \[[8](#bib.bib10)\]
probe how far they carry under perturbation. A second route trains a
small model to be the _agent_ instead. Guava \[[27](#bib.bib5)\] designs
a manipulation harness and distills reasoning and recovery trajectories
from a frontier VLM into a 4B open model, PyRUA-Lean \[[37](#bib.bib9)\]
trains a 4B coder with GRPO on 45 LIBERO-PRO instances, and Robo-Harness
K1 \[[24](#bib.bib36)\] and World Action Agent \[[56](#bib.bib37)\]
fine-tune small VLM students on harness traces. TinyAstral first evolves
the teacher harness and then freezes it for data collection and
distillation. Distillation at several frozen snapshots tests whether the
preceding harness improvements carry into student performance.

#### Self-improving agents and agent distillation.

Language agents can improve through stored experience and
reflection \[[44](#bib.bib25), [36](#bib.bib26)\], and recent systems
let a model rewrite its own scaffold or code: STOP \[[51](#bib.bib27)\],
ADAS \[[17](#bib.bib29)\], Gödel Agent \[[49](#bib.bib28)\], and the
Darwin Gödel Machine \[[54](#bib.bib30)\], following the classical
notion of recursive self-improvement \[[13](#bib.bib39)\];
GEPA \[[2](#bib.bib50)\] reflects on execution traces to evolve prompts.
ADAS also reports transfer of discovered agents across models and
domains. TinyAstral evolves the tools, execution feedback, task memory,
and recovery logic of a manipulation harness under paired statistical
selection, and carries the evolved system into a tiny student. Knowledge
distillation \[[15](#bib.bib31), [35](#bib.bib32)\] and its agent
variants, FireAct \[[6](#bib.bib33)\] and
AgentTuning \[[53](#bib.bib34)\], transfer trajectories from a strong
language agent to a smaller one on text and tool-use tasks; Distilling
Step-by-Step \[[16](#bib.bib49)\] uses teacher rationales as extra
supervision, and BetterTogether \[[38](#bib.bib51)\] alternates prompt
optimization with fine-tuning of the same model. We supervise rationales
together with tool calls inside a closed-loop visual interaction
history, and distill from a teacher whose harness has already been
evolved, into a student of a different size.

### 3.1 Planner, harness, and scoring

#### Agent and harness.

A planner model $`\pi`$ controls a robot through a harness $`H`$. At
turn $`t`$ the harness gives $`\pi`$ an observation $`o_{t}`$, and
$`\pi`$ produces a reasoning trace followed by exactly one tool call
$`a_{t}`$. The harness executes the call and returns a structured result
$`r_{t}`$. The harness determines _how_ an action is carried out:
perception (SAM 3 segmentation \[[5](#bib.bib15)\] lifted to depth point
clouds), grasp and placement geometry, collision-free motion planning,
and closed-loop corrections. The planner determines _what_ to do: which
object, which part of it, which reference and spatial relation, which
arm, in what order, and when the task is finished. The model never
outputs joint angles or coordinates unless a tool explicitly requests
them.

Observations combine camera images, the task instruction, recent tool
results, and gripper state. Object-level tools provide grasping,
placement, verification, and recovery primitives; the full interface is
specified in
[Appendix G](#A7 "Appendix G Harness interface details ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents").

#### Autonomous termination and scoring.

The agent ends an episode itself by calling done with the predicates it
believes hold (_e.g_. on(a,b), upright(a), lifted(a,cm)). The harness
checks these predicates with the cameras and gripper sensors; if a check
fails, it rejects the call and returns the measurement so the agent can
keep working. After two rejections, the next done ends the episode
without a check. An episode also ends if the agent calls give_up or
exhausts its budget (30 tool calls on RoboTwin; per-benchmark limits in
[Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Competitor protocols ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
The environment’s success flag never enters the model’s input. Our
primary metric, strict success $`S`$, counts an episode only if the
agent ended it with done and the benchmark’s success predicate holds in
the final state. We also report _ever_ success, which counts an episode
if the task was achieved after any tool call.

### 3.2 Stage 1: Evolving the teacher harness

The self-improving system consists of the frozen teacher $`\pi_{T}`$,
the harness $`H`$, and the master $`M`$. Only $`H`$ changes. The weights
of $`\pi_{T}`$ stay fixed throughout, and $`M`$ does not act in the
environment. Humans specify the rules (including the pre-registered
acceptance criteria) and budget before the loop begins. The master is a
set of frontier LLM agents that read trajectories, propose edits,
implement them, and audit one another’s code. The system applies the
pre-registered paired statistical tests to determine acceptance. Each
round runs the current teacher, analyzes its failures, proposes edits,
and tests a candidate against the current harness.

#### 1. Read trajectories.

The current accepted harness $`H_{k}`$ is run on development seeds. The
master inspects the logged images, tool calls, tool results, and teacher
reasoning, and assigns each failure to one of ten failure classes, such
as off-target placement, false completion (done accepted but the final
state fails), missing primitives, task-memory errors, and instance
misidentification.

#### 2. Propose edits.

For a target cluster of failing tasks, the master writes candidate
components: new tools or primitives, more honest tool returns,
perception fixes, failure handling, or local corrections to the task
memory. Edits must be general. Code may not branch on the task name. In
the final integration phase, the system prompt and existing tool
descriptions must remain byte-identical to the parent. Memory may change
only for the targeted tasks. In addition, every new component must fire
through an explicit trigger that writes a component-specific marker to
the log. A separate code audit verifies that, whenever the trigger does
not fire, the candidate behaves step-for-step like the parent.

#### 3. Test and accept edits.

Candidates are compared with the current harness on the same task/seed
pairs. Training, selection, and evaluation seeds occupy disjoint ranges.
The pre-registered gate requires positive evidence of improvement on
targeted or triggered pairs and checks for regressions elsewhere;
unsuccessful candidates are rejected or stopped early. The final
integration phase accepted three of seven candidates. Evaluation seeds
are excluded from acceptance tests. The acceptance rules and capability
ledger are in
[Appendix E](#A5 "Appendix E RSI details ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents").

#### Task memory.

RoboTwin instructions often leave the success condition implicit (_e.g_.
“lift” requires holding the object at least 10 cm up). For RoboTwin
only, the harness therefore reads one short end-state note per task; the
notes contain no actions or trajectories, are refined during RSI on
development seeds, and are frozen and read-only at evaluation. The other
five benchmarks, including all zero-shot ones, use no task memory.

#### From evolution to a frozen teacher system.

We number accepted main-line snapshots chronologically from the starting
harness $`H_{0}`$; the lineage proceeds through $`H_{16}`$ to the final
$`H^{*}`$
([Tab. 8](#A5.T8 "In Appendix E RSI details ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
These accepted versions are distinct from the 296 candidate capabilities
and the seven integration cycles in the final phase. We freeze $`H^{*}`$
before collecting the final teacher trajectories for distillation.

### 3.3 Stage 2: Collecting supervision from the frozen system

The teacher $`\pi_{T}`$ collects trajectories through the frozen
$`H^{*}`$. These trajectories supervise a student $`\pi_{S}`$ to reason
about observations and feedback and choose the next tool call. The
teacher weights and harness remain fixed during data collection and
student training. The distillation recipe combines reasoning targets,
recovery branches, strict-success filtering, and training samples
exported from the exact harness interactions.

#### Keep the teacher’s reasoning.

Each training target supervises the teacher’s stated rationale together
with its next tool call, _i.e_. \<think\>$`\ldots`$\</think\> and then
the call. Emptying the reasoning text while keeping all samples
identical lowers success by 19.3 pp on LIBERO-PRO, 11.2 pp on SimplerEnv
Google VM, and 10.0 pp on VLABench
([Sec. 4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Ablations and recovery behavior ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).

#### Recovery branches.

Successful episodes rarely show how to recover from failure, so we add
_recovery branches_. Starting from a successful teacher episode, we
replay its first $`k`$ calls in a fresh simulator with the same seed,
using the recorded results and without querying the model. At the branch
point we inject a perturbation: on RoboTwin, the grasped object slips,
or the target object is moved by 6–10 cm before the next grasp; on Guava
and LIBERO, a grasp misses, a push falls short, or a placed object is
displaced. The teacher then continues online. A branch is kept only if
the perturbation took effect, the replay reproduced every recorded
result code, and the teacher still reached strict success. Its samples
begin at the first decision after the perturbation. On RoboTwin,
branches are subsampled to 20% of the RoboTwin samples (3,471 branch and
13,857 main-episode samples).

#### Strict successes only.

Main episodes run at temperature $`0`$, as in evaluation. A training
seed on which the teacher fails is retried up to twice at temperature
$`0.7`$ with an unchanged prompt. We keep only episodes that the teacher
ended with done and that the benchmark judges successful in the final
state. Episodes that succeeded at some point but were later undone are
discarded. Success labels are used offline for this selection and never
appear in the training messages.

#### Offline real-robot supervision.

To expose the student to real images, we add offline data from public
real-robot datasets (AgiBot World \[[1](#bib.bib53)\];
Fractal \[[4](#bib.bib54)\], BridgeData V2 \[[43](#bib.bib55)\],
DROID \[[19](#bib.bib56)\], and Berkeley UR5 \[[7](#bib.bib57)\] from
Open X-Embodiment \[[29](#bib.bib58)\]), cast as three auxiliary tasks
in the harness format: state and failure judgment (27,918 samples),
sub-goal planning (4,187), and next tool call (9,771). For next-call
samples, we convert each recorded action sequence into a tool-call
trajectory: gripper events and segment boundaries place the calls, and
dataset annotations name the objects and spatial relations. Each step is
rendered with the final harness’s observation and history format, so the
student sees real scenes through the same interface it uses in
simulation. The teacher then supplies the reasoning. It first predicts
the call without seeing the reference; if the two match, its reasoning
is kept. Otherwise, as in the rationalization step of
STaR \[[52](#bib.bib52)\], it is given the reference call and asked to
explain it, and the explanation is kept only if it passes consistency
and quality checks. In both cases the dataset-derived call is the action
target (details in
[Appendix F](#A6.SS0.SSS0.Px1 "Real-robot next-call pool. ‣ Appendix F Training details ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).

### 3.4 Student training and deployment

Teacher data comes from training seeds of all 50 RoboTwin tasks, the 7
seen Guava tasks, and LIBERO-90 \[[26](#bib.bib11)\]; the final set has
83,753 decision samples, half of them simulated (composition and
hyper-parameters in
[Appendix F](#A6 "Appendix F Training details ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
We fine-tune all language-model parameters of
Qwen3.5-4B \[[33](#bib.bib40)\] for 2 epochs; at deployment the student
replaces the teacher inside the frozen harness, served in bf16 on one
accelerator.

## 4 Experiments

We first show how harness evolution improves frozen planners and how
distillation transfers these gains to compact students. We then compare
the final student with its teacher and other agents, and test its
generalization to unseen benchmarks. Finally, we analyze training
ingredients, recovery behavior, and inference compute cost.

### 4.1 Setup

#### Benchmarks.

We report six benchmarks. Three belong to benchmark families that supply
training data: RoboTwin 2.0 \[[8](#bib.bib10)\] with all 50 tasks
$`\times`$ 10 evaluation seeds (500 episodes), Guava \[[27](#bib.bib5)\]
with 15 tasks $`\times`$ 30 official seeds (450 episodes; 7 tasks seen
in training, 8 unseen), and LIBERO-PRO \[[58](#bib.bib12)\] with 300
episodes (three LIBERO \[[26](#bib.bib11)\] suites, original and four
perturbation types, 2 seeds each). Three are never touched by RSI or
training and test zero-shot transfer: SimplerEnv \[[23](#bib.bib13)\]
(Visual Matching, 960 episodes on the Google Robot and WidowX),
VLABench \[[55](#bib.bib6)\] (the first 500 episodes of the official
list over five tracks), and EB-Manipulation \[[48](#bib.bib14)\] (228
episodes). RoboTwin is also the RSI development benchmark. Acceptance
decisions use only selection seeds; evaluation seeds
($`\geq 100{,}000`$) are disjoint from selection and training seeds
([Sec. 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Stage 1: Evolving the teacher harness ‣ 3 Method ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
Guava evaluation seeds are disjoint from our Guava training seeds, and
LIBERO-PRO training data comes only from LIBERO-90.

#### Evaluation protocol.

We report strict success $`S`$
([Sec. 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Planner, harness, and scoring ‣ 3 Method ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents"))
on the three training benchmarks and each benchmark’s official success
measure on the zero-shot benchmarks
([Tab. 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Performance and generalization ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
VLABench stops at the official success event and EB-Manipulation latches
success; neither defines a final-state predicate after done. All rates
use fixed denominators. Episodes with undetermined outcomes count as
failures; upper bounds counting them as successes and _ever_ success are
reported in
[Tab. 5](#A1.T5 "In Appendix A All runs ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents").
Comparisons between systems we run are paired on identical (task, seed)
episodes; published results are listed separately. We report success
rates and paired differences in percentage points. Within our harness,
all evaluated planners decode at temperature 0 and share perception and
motion-planning services. Competitor implementations and
benchmark-specific configurations are detailed in
[Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Competitor protocols ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents").

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash \[[9](#bib.bib42)\], which was not the planner used
during harness evolution, improves by 30.6 pp without model-specific
harness changes. The untrained 4B planner improves from 14.6% to 41.2%.
All three improve across the four measured versions.

#### Distilling evolution gains.

We train a separate 4B student for each harness version. Student success
increases from 36.2% at $`H_{1}`$ to 62.8% at $`H^{*}`$, with each
student scoring close to its corresponding teacher
([Tab. 1](#S4.T1 "In 4.2 Harness evolution and transfer to compact students ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
Thus, improvements to the teacher’s harness carry into the distilled
students. Within the final harness $`H^{*}`$, the distilled student also
outperforms the untrained 4B model by 21.6 percentage points (62.8% vs.
41.2%), showing the additional benefit of distillation.

#### Student versus teacher.

TinyAstral-4B stays close to its teacher on all three training
benchmarks: 62.8% vs. 63.6% on RoboTwin, 93.6% vs. 94.7% on Guava, and
78.3% vs. 80.0% on LIBERO-PRO
([Tab. 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Performance and generalization ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
In the same harness, it also improves over the untrained 4B model by
18.4 pp on Guava and 42.0 pp on LIBERO-PRO; counting all undetermined
outcomes as successes raises the untrained model’s LIBERO-PRO score to
at most 57.3%.

#### Comparison with other agents.

On the same evaluation episodes, TinyAstral-4B outperforms the tested
open agents, including Guava-4B, PyRUA-Lean-4B, and
SenseNova-RoboRSI-27B
([Tab. 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Performance and generalization ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
It also exceeds systems using our teacher model, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, in
other harnesses: 93.6% vs. 78.9% for the Guava harness, and 78.3% vs.
57.3% and 53.0% for MotorMind and PyRUA-Lean on LIBERO-PRO.
PyRUA-Lean-4B is trained and self-evaluated with a per-step environment
success signal; without this signal, which our protocol withholds, it
often declares success prematurely. These results highlight the
contribution of the evolved harness and distilled policy to the final
system’s performance. Competitor-specific execution and scoring
protocols are detailed in
[Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Competitor protocols ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents").

#### Generalization to unseen benchmarks.

TinyAstral-4B also generalizes to three benchmarks never used for RSI or
training: SimplerEnv, VLABench, and EB-Manipulation. Its success rates
are within 5.0 pp of its teacher and within 2.4 pp of the 552B
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, all using the same harness
([Tab. 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Performance and generalization ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
Compared with the untrained 4B model, distillation improves performance
across all four evaluation settings, with gains of 10.0 pp on SimplerEnv
Google VM and 13.6 pp on VLABench. Despite receiving no training on
these benchmarks, the student also exceeds published results from
$`\pi_{0}`$-FAST \[[30](#bib.bib43)\] on both SimplerEnv robots and
$`\pi_{0}`$ \[[3](#bib.bib16)\] on VLABench, although these baselines
were fine-tuned on in-domain data (protocol details in
[Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C Published comparisons ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).

#### Smaller students.

TinyAstral-0.8B reaches 61.8% on RoboTwin and 93.8% on Guava, close to
the 4B student, but falls to 54.7% on LIBERO-PRO compared with 78.3%. On
the zero-shot benchmarks, both the 0.8B and 2B students remain within
3.0 pp of the 4B model across all four settings. The effect of model
size is therefore most pronounced on LIBERO-PRO among the evaluated
benchmarks
([Tab. 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Performance and generalization ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).

## 5 Conclusion

TinyAstral first improves a frozen teacher through harness evolution,
then distills its reasoning and recovery behavior into a compact student
deployed in the final harness. Across four harness versions, students
trained from later versions perform better and stay close to their
corresponding teachers, showing that harness improvements carry into
compact agents. The harness gains also transfer to other frozen
planners. The final 4B student outperforms every open competitor we ran
on identical episodes, stays close to its teacher on three training
benchmarks, scores close to a 552B planner on unseen benchmarks, and
uses about one fifth of the teacher’s accelerator time in our deployment
measurement. These results support harness evolution followed by
distillation as a route to capable robot agents with lower inference
compute cost. Future work will extend the approach to more RSI
environments.

## Appendix B Competitor protocols

#### Our budgets.

Every planner run in our harness uses the same per-episode tool-call
limit on a given benchmark
([Tab. 6](#A2.T6 "In Our budgets. ‣ Appendix B Competitor protocols ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
Guava uses its official per-task round limits; the other limits were set
by us before evaluation.

|                 |            |                                              |
| --------------- | ---------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Benchmark       | Tool calls | Basis                                        |
| RoboTwin        | 30         | set by us                                    |
| Guava           | 25 / 30    | official per-task limits \[[27](#bib.bib5)\] |
| LIBERO-PRO      | 40         | set by us                                    |
| SimplerEnv      | 25         | set by us, as Guava                          |
| VLABench        | 40         | set by us, as LIBERO-PRO                     |
| EB-Manipulation | 25         | set by us, as Guava                          |

Table 6: Per-episode tool-call limits of our harness.

#### Competitors.

All competitors run from their released code and weights on our
accelerators; no closed-source API is used. Guava-4B \[[27](#bib.bib5)\]
runs in the official Guava harness (weights at revision 19a8c77,
official prompt and parser, temperature 0) on our 450 Guava episodes,
with the same per-task round limits; its result is re-scored with our
unified metric (380/450). PyRUA-Lean-4B \[[37](#bib.bib9)\] runs in its
official harness on all 500 RoboTwin episodes, whose task/seed pairs
match ours one to one, and is scored with the same strict metric
(64/500; it calls finish in 478 episodes; 20 episodes with undetermined
outcomes count as failures).

#### Same teacher in alternative systems.

Guava harness with Qwen3.8-Flash-Next uses the official prompt and
parser with medium reasoning effort and default sampling, whereas our
teacher uses its default xhigh reasoning and temperature 0. Both allow
25/30 calls per task. MotorMind with this planner uses its official
stop-on-success protocol and uncapped calls (median 77, compared with 8
for our student on LIBERO-PRO). PyRUA-Lean with this planner uses
benchmark-trained VLA executors and allows 30/40 calls on
RoboTwin/LIBERO-PRO; it runs on all 500 RoboTwin episodes (91/500) and
our 300 LIBERO-PRO episodes, and its reported success requires
finish(success) and final-state success.

#### Additional competitor metrics.

For PyRUA-Lean-4B on LIBERO-PRO, we report its final-state success,
53/300 (17.7%), marked separately in
[Tab. 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Performance and generalization ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents");
38 of these episodes also end with finish(success).
SenseNova-RoboRSI-27B runs with its official stop-on-success rule, 40
policy calls, and an empty task-memory package, and reaches 211/300.

#### Direction screen.

Candidates first pass a cheap _direction screen_ on small seed blocks. A
candidate is discarded if the task-averaged 95% upper bound of its gain
on the target cluster falls below $`+2`$ pp. Surviving components are
merged into one candidate $`H^{\prime}`$. $`H^{\prime}`$ and its parent
$`H_{k}`$ then run concurrently, interleaved on the same $`\sim`$1,150
pre-registered (task, seed) pairs, so that the two arms share load and
infrastructure conditions.

#### Statistical verdict.

Let $`J`$ denote the pairs in the target cluster together with the
non-target pairs in which a candidate marker fired, and let $`U`$ denote
the remaining pairs. $`H^{\prime}`$ is accepted if (i) the one-sided
97.5% lower bound of the paired gain on $`J`$, computed by a
task-clustered bootstrap, is positive, and (ii) the net number of wins
on the target cluster is positive. $`H^{\prime}`$ is vetoed if the
untriggered set $`U`$ regresses by at least 2 pp with a two-sided 95%
upper bound below zero. At the halfway point, the test stops for
futility if the net gain on $`J`$ is non-positive or the conditional
power falls below 10%. The number of pairs is fixed in advance.

#### Capability ledger.

Every candidate is recorded in a ledger with the failure that triggered
it, its proposer, its acceptance evidence, and its fate. Over the whole
run, the master proposed 296 candidate capabilities. Of these, 121 were
adopted, 16 were adopted and later superseded, 142 were rejected, and 17
remained undecided. The final harness contains 101 capabilities, 87
verbatim and 14 in a successor form; almost all adopted capabilities
missing from it were accepted on side branches that were never merged
into the main line.

## Appendix F Training details

The 41,877 simulated samples comprise 13,857 RoboTwin, 9,054 Guava, and
11,159 LIBERO main-episode samples, plus 3,471, 2,048, and 2,288
recovery-branch samples, respectively. Each sample is one decision, with
the loss applied only to the final assistant turn. The vision encoder
and projector stay frozen. Training uses learning rate $`10^{-5}`$ with
cosine decay and 5% warm-up, a global batch of 32, and a maximum length
of 12,288 tokens. The 2B and 0.8B students are trained on the same data
with the same recipe.

#### Real-robot next-call pool.

Explanations given the reference call are rejected if they refer to the
supplied answer, claim unmeasured success, omit the target object or
required reference, or violate the length and format constraints.
Next-call relabeling retained 19,598 of 23,070 candidate steps: 4,509
whose reasoning comes from a matching first prediction and 15,089
explained given the reference call. The 9,771 next-call training samples
are drawn from this pool.

## Appendix G Harness interface details

#### Observations.

On RoboTwin, $`o_{t}`$ contains RGB images from the head camera and both
wrist cameras, the task instruction together with the names of scene
objects, a proprioceptive line with the measured gripper state of each
arm, the result $`r_{t-1}`$ of the previous call, and a counter of calls
used. Images are kept for the two most recent turns only. Earlier turns
retain their text, and earlier assistant messages retain only the tool
call, without reasoning. On RoboTwin the first turn also includes the
task note
([Sec. 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Stage 1: Evolving the teacher harness ‣ 3 Method ‣ TinyAstral: Transferring Self-Evolving Harnessesto Tiny Generalist Manipulation Agents")).
Guava and LIBERO-PRO use the same planner loop and tool semantics
through benchmark adapters, keeping the three most recent single-view
images.

#### Tools.

The RoboTwin harness exposes object-level tools: locate, grasp (with an
optional part such as handle, rim, or end), place and carry (relative to
a reference object, with optional facing or turning), handover, lift,
articulate, press, push, pour, move, release, home, bimanual (two calls
executed in parallel), verify, done, and give_up. Tools report what they
measured rather than what they intended. For example, place re-measures
the object after release and returns OFF_TARGET with the offset if the
object missed its target. A check whose value cannot be measured
reliably is returned as unknown (ok=null) rather than as a pass.

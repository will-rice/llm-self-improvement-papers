###### Abstract

Bridging the gap between human intent and machine logic remains a
critical challenge in data access for non-technical users, yet Large
Language Models (LLMs) remain unreliable: they hallucinate schema
elements, generate syntactically invalid queries, or fail to capture
user intent. These limitations are especially critical in domains such
as insurance, where KPIs, regulatory constraints, and legacy relational
systems demand high precision. We introduce a multi-agent,
persona-driven NL2SQL framework that combines (1) domain relevance
detection, (2) schema-grounded knowledge construction, (3) KPI-aware SQL
synthesis, (4) syntax-level self-repair using sqlite execution feedback,
and (5) semantic consistency checking via SQL-to-NL reverse engineering.
Our system significantly reduces query hallucination and improves
semantic fidelity compared to direct LLM prompting. Evaluated on 60
queries (50 from a HuggingFace text-to-SQL dataset and 10 custom domain
queries), the system achieves 90% correctness using Mistral across seven
coordinated LLM calls showing that this approach can substantially
increase the reliability of NL2SQL pipelines in high-stakes
environments.

## 1 Introduction

The insurance industry operates on complex, data-intensive processes
that rely heavily on structured information about policies, claims,
customers, agents, and submissions. These datasets carry critical
business functions, including pricing, underwriting, fraud detection,
portfolio analysis, and KPI-driven performance monitoring. Although
insurers store decades of transactional data in relational databases,
this information is often locked behind SQL interfaces accessible only
to technically trained analysts. As a result, underwriters, financial
planners, and operations teams depend on technical intermediaries to
obtain even basic metrics, delaying decisions in a domain where timing
and accuracy are crucial.

Despite increasing adoption of AI tools, querying these legacy systems
remains challenging \[[13](#bib.bib5)\]. Their schemas are
domain-specific, include numerous interlinked tables, and require
precise understanding of insurance KPIs such as loss ratios or claim
frequencies. Modern LLMs like ChatGPT can translate natural language
into SQL, but they frequently hallucinate table or column names, produce
syntactically invalid queries, or generate outputs misaligned with
business intent. Such unreliability makes them unsuitable for
high-stakes industries like insurance, where incorrect queries can lead
to flawed reports, mispriced risk, or regulatory compliance issues.

To address these challenges, we develop a multi-agent, persona-driven
NL2SQL system tailored for insurance databases. Our approach integrates
(1) domain-relevance validation to ensure user queries align with
insurance concepts (2) schema-grounded knowledge generation
\[[6](#bib.bib11)\], including KPI decomposition (3) iterative SQL
correction using sqlite-based syntax checks and (4) semantic validation
via SQL-to-NL reverse engineering paired with LLM-as-a-judge comparison.
By combining domain expertise, guided decomposition, and
error-correcting feedback loops, our system produces SQL that is both
syntactically correct and semantically faithful to user intent. Through
this structured methodology, we demonstrate that reliable
natural-language querying of legacy insurance databases is achievable,
overcoming the limitations of direct LLM prompting and enabling
decision-makers to access critical insights without technical barriers.

## 2 Related Work

Research in text-to-SQL has evolved through specialized architectures,
prompting strategies, and evaluation frameworks aimed at improving the
reliability of NL2SQL systems. Early work focused on neural
architectures that explicitly model schema-linking and relational
structures. For example, RAT-SQL \[[10](#bib.bib6)\] introduced a
relation-aware encoder that aligns natural-language queries with
database schemas through semantic linking, achieving strong performance
on cross-domain benchmarks. This class of models demonstrates the
importance of grounding language understanding in structural
representations of the database.

As large language models (LLMs) became more prominent, prompting
strategies emerged as a practical alternative to heavy model training.
Techniques such as Divide-and-Prompt and Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
prompting \[[8](#bib.bib7)\] have shown that breaking NL queries into
smaller reasoning steps can improve SQL generation accuracy. These
methods emphasize guiding the model toward intermediate reasoning,
reducing some forms of hallucination and strengthening schema awareness.

It is necessary to use evaluation frameworks such as LLM-as-a-Judge or
SQL2NL \[[7](#bib.bib8)\] comparison to provide more reliable
assessments of semantic equivalence than simple string matching. These
methods compare the intent of the NL query with the intent expressed by
the generated SQL, offering a closer proxy for actual correctness.

However, even with advanced prompting, generating accurate SQL remains
difficult. The base paper influencing our work is SQL-of-Thought
\[[1](#bib.bib9)\] which proposed a multi-agent, error-correcting
pipeline where LLMs iteratively refine SQL queries based on execution
feedback but this approach only relied on syntactic correctness of the
generated query.

Unlike prior NL2SQL systems that rely solely on schema-linking
architectures, prompting techniques, or single-pass SQL generation, our
approach introduces a persona-driven, KPI-aware, multi-stage correction
pipeline specifically tailored for the insurance domain. We extend
beyond traditional parsing by incorporating domain relevance filtering,
explicit knowledge generation (including tables, columns, and KPI
formulas), and a guided refinement loop that uses both syntactic
validation through SQLite execution and semantic validation via
SQL-to-NL reverse engineering.

### 3.1 Schema Overview

The database models a standard insurance lifecycle, comprising five core
relational entities that track the customer journey from lead
acquisition to policy management and claims processing.

- •
  customers: Serves as the central dimension table containing detailed
  demographic and financial profiles, including attributes such as
  annual_income, credit_score, risk_tolerance, and marital_status. This
  table enables fine-grained segmentation for risk analysis.
- •
  policies: Records the contractual details of issued insurance
  products. Key attributes include premium_amount, coverage_amount,
  policy_risk_level, and temporal fields like policy_start_year and
  policy_end_year, allowing for longitudinal portfolio analysis.
- •
  claims: A transactional table tracking liabilities. It logs
  claim_amount, approved_amount, fraud_flag, and processing_time_days,
  which are critical for calculating loss ratios and fraud detection
  metrics.
- •
  agents: Stores metadata on the salesforce, including
  agent_license_number, agency_name, and sales channel. This table is
  essential for performance attribution and channel efficacy reporting.
- •
  submissions: Represents the early-stage funnel of insurance quotes and
  leads. It tracks the status of applications (e.g., statusid for
  “Bound” vs. “Quote Only”) and links prospective policies to specific
  agents.

## 4 Methodology

We define the Text-to-SQL task as the generation of an executable SQL
query $`s`$ given a natural language query $`q`$ and a database schema
$`S`$. Unlike standard one-shot generation approaches, we model this
process as a multi-stage iterative system, as shown in
[2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ NL2SQL with Iterative Self-Refinement")
comprising an initial generation phase followed by a self-refinement
loop driven by dual feedback mechanisms.

![Refer to caption](2610.22968v1/flow.png)

Figure 2: Flow of Execution

### 4.1 Query Generation

The process begins with a Generation Step, denoted as
$`f_{\text{gen}}`$, which synthesizes an initial SQL query candidate
$`s_{0}`$. This function utilizes an intermediate knowledge
representation $`K(q,S)`$, comprising relevant tables, columns, and KPI
formulas derived from the schema $`S`$ rather than mapping directly from
$`q`$.

Formally, the initial state is defined as:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       s_{0}=f_{\text{gen}}(q,K(q,S),S)
       ```                               |     | (1) |

where:

- •
  $`q`$ represents the Natural Language (NL) query.
- •
  $`S`$ represents the database schema and metadata.
- •
  $`K(q,S)`$ denotes the generated guidance (schema linking, KPI
  decomposition, and reasoning steps).

Before generating the query, we first check the relevance of each user
query to the insurance domain using Chain-of-Thought prompting
\[[11](#bib.bib3)\]. For example, when asked about a loss ratio, the
system produces the following explanation:

Query: “What is the loss ratio in 2023 and 2024?”

Relevance Explanation: “The query asks for the loss ratio, which
directly refers to a key performance indicator (KPI) in the insurance
domain.”

In contrast, when presented with a query that is unrelated to the
domain, the system identifies the mismatch:

Query: “What is the cost of this swimming pool?”

Relevance Explanation: “The query asks for the cost of a swimming pool,
which is not related to the insurance domain.”

If the query not relevant, the generation is stopped here. Otherwise,
the next step is to generate knowledge \[[3](#bib.bib10)\] relevant to
the query by embedding KPI and schema information in the prompt. An
example of the generated knowledge is given below:

### 4.2 Query Execution and Self-Refinement

To ensure both syntactic correctness and semantic alignment, the system
enters a Refinement Step \[[4](#bib.bib4)\], denoted as
$`f_{\text{ref}}`$. This process iteratively updates the SQL query
$`s_{t}`$ at step $`t`$ by incorporating feedback from two distinct
error reflection functions: $`E_{\text{syn}}`$ (syntax error reflection)
and $`E_{\text{log}}`$ (logical error reflection).

The state transition for the query refinement is defined recursively as:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
s_{t+1}=f_{\text{ref}}(q,s_{t},E_{\text{syn}}(s_{t}),E_{\text{log}}(s_{t}))\quad\text{for }t=0,1,\dots,N
``` |  | (2) |

where:

- •
  $`s_{t}`$ is the generated SQL query at iteration $`t`$.
- •
  $`E_{\text{syn}}(s_{t})`$ represents the syntax error feedback
  obtained from executing $`s_{t}`$ on the target database.
- •
  $`E_{\text{log}}(s_{t})`$ represents the logical error reflection
  derived from reverse-engineering $`s_{t}`$ back into natural language
  and comparing it against $`q`$.
- •
  $`N`$ is the maximum number of allowed refinement iterations.

This formulation ensures that $`s_{t+1}`$ is strictly conditioned on the
specific failure modes of $`s_{t}`$, allowing the model to converge
toward a solution that is both executable and semantically faithful to
the user’s intent.

The generated SQL query is executed on the sqlite3 database, if the
query is executed, it shows that the query is syntactically correct and
it is then moved to logical error analysis phase. If there are syntactic
errors, the self-refinement loop begins by passing these errors and the
SQL query as another LLM call for suggestions to correct the query.
These suggestions are then used to generate the corrected query. An
example of syntax error refinement is given below:

In the logical error analysis phase, the system evaluates the semantic
correctness of the generated SQL query using a reverse engineering
strategy. Specifically, the SQL query is translated back into a
structured natural language description that captures its intent,
constraints, and aggregation logic. This generated explanation is then
systematically compared against the user’s original natural language
query using an LLM-as-a-Judge \[[12](#bib.bib1)\] mechanism, where a
large language model scores semantic alignment between the two
representations.

If the reverse-engineered description is judged to be semantically
consistent with the user’s intent, the SQL query is executed against the
target database and the results are returned. If a semantic mismatch is
detected, the system automatically triggers a correction routine that
produces a revised SQL query. This corrected query is fed back into a
self-refinement loop, enabling iterative improvement through repeated
generation, validation, and repair. This feedback-driven process helps
prevent silent logical failures, reduces hallucinated joins or filters,
and ensures that the final query reflects both the syntactic structure
and the semantic intent of the user’s request.

## 5 Results and Discussion

Our experimental evaluation on a curated set of 60 domain-specific
queries comprising 50 instances from gretelai synthetic
dataset\[[2](#bib.bib13)\] and 10 custom KPI based complex queries,
achieving an aggregate accuracy of approximately 90%. Performance
stratification by task type reveals that the system attains near-perfect
reliability (100%) in “Data Retrieval” tasks, which primarily involve
direct selection and filtering. In contrast, the more abstract
“Analytics and Reporting” scenarios, which require multi-step
aggregation and KPI derivation, maintained a robust accuracy of
approximately 85%. Furthermore, the model exhibits resilience across
varying levels of SQL complexity as shown in
[4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5 Results and Discussion ‣ NL2SQL with Iterative Self-Refinement"),
handling basic queries with high precision while maintaining substantial
accuracy in generating advanced structures involving subqueries and
window functions.

This system used many personas in its verification process such as

Regarding operational constraints, the framework utilizes the
mistral-large-2411 model \[[5](#bib.bib12)\] to power its reasoning
agents. The pipeline consisting of Chain-of-Thought relevance reasoning,
knowledge generation, syntax validation, and logical refinement,
necessitates an average of 7 LLM calls per query execution. This results
in an average end-to-end latency of approximately 20 seconds. While this
latency is higher than direct text-to-SQL inference, it produces
accurate and reliable queries without hallucinating relations, attribute
names, etc.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we presented a persona-driven, KPI-aware framework for
Natural Language to SQL (NL2SQL) generation, specifically designed to
address the accessibility gap in the insurance domain. By formalizing
the generation process as a recursive function with dual-feedback loops,
we successfully mitigated common LLM failure modes, including schema
hallucination and syntactic invalidity. Our approach, which couples
"Generate Knowledge Prompting" (GKP) with a rigorous "Reverse
Engineering" validation step through self-refinement, allows
non-technical users to reliably query legacy databases without knowledge
of the underlying SQL structure. Experimental results demonstrate that
this iterative refinement strategy achieves approximately 90% accuracy,
proving that agentic workflows can effectively bridge the divide between
human intent and machine logic in high-stakes enterprise environments.

While our current implementation establishes a robust baseline, we
envision several enhancements to improve the framework’s utility. Future
iterations of this work will transition from static prompting to dynamic
prompt modulation, automatically adjusting Chain-of-Thought depth and
employing few-shot dynamic example selection based on query complexity.
Concurrently, we intend to assess model agnosticism by benchmarking
across many LLMs ranging from lightweight open-source models to
proprietary frontiers to optimize the trade-off between latency,
inference cost and reasoning accuracy. Finally, we plan to extend the
pipeline’s capabilities beyond mere retrieval by integrating a
post-processing agent that summarizes SQL results into actionable
natural language insights, thereby transforming the system into a
comprehensive decision-support analyst

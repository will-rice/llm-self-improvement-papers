###### Abstract.

Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly support decision-making in
high-stakes domains, but they often hallucinate and express confidence
that is misaligned with factual correctness. Response-level confidence
is a coarse signal: a single generation can mix correct and incorrect
statements, so a single number is not actionable for users that must
accept, reject, or verify individual pieces of information. We study
_claim-level confidence calibration_ as a decision-relevant uncertainty
signal: each response is decomposed into atomic, verifiable claims, and
each claim is assigned a calibrated confidence using inference-time
signals from consistency across samples and self-verification. Our
framework operates in closed-box settings (no logits, no fine-tuning)
and applies post-hoc calibration directly at the claim level, enabling
selective intervention such as evidence retrieval or human review for
low-confidence claims. Across TriviaQA and TruthfulQA we evaluate seven
baselines on six recent models (Llama-3.1, Mistral, Qwen2.5,
DeepSeek-R1, GPT-4, GPT-4o), and show that claim-level decomposition
combined with post-hoc calibration reduces expected calibration error on
factual questions while exposing failure modes on adversarial
false-premise questions where decision-makers most need reliable
uncertainty estimates.

## 1. Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are being deployed as components in
decision-support pipelines, where a downstream user must decide whether
to accept, reject, or further verify the model’s output. In this
setting, miscalibrated confidence is not merely a statistical nuisance:
a confidently stated but incorrect claim can directly drive a wrong
decision ([6](#bib.bib29); [14](#bib.bib1); [48](#bib.bib33)). Three
broad directions have emerged to mitigate this risk: (1) *uncertainty
quantification and calibration*, which align reported confidence with
empirical accuracy ([12](#bib.bib23); [32](#bib.bib28));
(2) *inference-time consistency checks*, which sample multiple
generations and treat agreement as a reliability signal
([40](#bib.bib20)); and (3) *self-verification and cross-examination*,
which probe a candidate response for contradictions and unsupported
content ([30](#bib.bib32); [5](#bib.bib3)).

We focus on (2) and (3) because they are model-agnostic, inference-time
controls applicable to closed-box deployments where access to logits or
fine-tuning is impossible. However, prior work applies these signals at
the _response_ level. This is too coarse for decision-making: a single
response can contain four claims, three of which are correct, and a
response-level score conflates the reliable parts with the unreliable
ones. A decision-maker who only knows that the response has $`0.7`$
confidence cannot tell which sentence to trust. Decomposing responses
into atomic claims makes hallucinations measurable at the granularity at
which decisions are actually made ([2](#bib.bib4); [46](#bib.bib5)).

Relation to prior claim-level work. Most closely related is Yuan et
al. ([46](#bib.bib5)), which proposes fact-level calibration and
_correction_ for long-form generation. Our objective is different: we
calibrate per-claim confidence to align with empirical correctness (ECE,
smECE, Brier) without modifying responses, apply post-hoc TS/PS at the
claim level, and conduct a systematic multi-model, multi-baseline
evaluation including adversarial false-premise questions. We also differ
from HalluMeasure ([2](#bib.bib4)) (no calibration step, ternary NLI
labels rather than continuous confidence) and from LMvsLM
([5](#bib.bib3)) (response level, no claim aggregation). A detailed
comparison and an extended related work appear in

## 2. Claim-Level Confidence at Inference Time

Problem. Given a prompt $`x`$ and an LLM response $`r`$, we seek (i) a
decomposition into atomic, verifiable claims
$`\mathcal{E}(r)=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}`$ and (ii) a confidence score
$`\mathrm{conf}(c_{i})\in[0,1]`$ for each claim such that confidence
aligns with the empirical probability that $`c_{i}`$ is supported by
evidence. Aggregated appropriately, claim-level confidence supports
concrete decision actions: accept the response, reject it, retrieve
evidence for low-confidence claims, or escalate to a human. Background
on uncertainty types, calibration fundamentals (TS, PS, smECE, etc.),
and full definitions are deferred to
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Preliminaries and Calibration Background ‣ Claim-Level Confidence Calibration for Reliable Decision Making with Large Language Models").

Pipeline
(Fig. [1](#acmlabel1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2. Claim-Level Confidence at Inference Time ‣ Claim-Level Confidence Calibration for Reliable Decision Making with Large Language Models")).
Our inference-time pipeline has five stages: _(1) In-context
reasoning_—greedy decoding for the primary response and optional
$`N`$-sample stochastic decoding; _(2) Claim extraction_—an LLM-based
extractor (GPT-4o-mini) emits self-contained, atomic claims of
$`\leq 15`$ words with pronouns resolved; _(3) Claim verification_—each
claim is scored by an auxiliary verifier (LLM, RAG, or web search); _(4)
Per-claim confidence_—we convert verifier outputs into a numeric score
in $`[0,1]`$; _(5) Calibration and aggregation_—we apply post-hoc
temperature or Platt scaling _at the claim level_, and aggregate to a
response-level score via mean (or min) when needed.

Per-claim confidence signal. For closed-box models (GPT-4, GPT-4o,
DeepSeek-R1) we obtain a verbalized confidence on a 14-level
decisiveness scale ([35](#bib.bib17)) mapped to $`[0,1]`$. For open-box
models (Llama-3.1, Mistral, Qwen2.5) we use the average token
log-probability of the claim,
$`\mathrm{ver}(c_{i})=\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{|c_{i}|}\sum_{t}\log p(c_{i,t})\bigr)`$.
The general framework supports a weighted combination with a
cross-sample consistency signal,

````math
Claim-level post-hoc calibration. We apply temperature scaling (TS) and
Platt scaling (PS) to the per-claim scores rather than to response-level
likelihoods. This is what makes the signal usable for selective
intervention: a calibrated $`0.85`$ on a single claim concretely means
*accept this claim with $`\sim`$15% expected error*, whereas a
response-level $`0.85`$ averages over heterogeneous sentences.
Calibration parameters are fit on a held-out subset of the training
split.

Why claim-level is closed-box-friendly. Unlike methods requiring
fine-tuning or token-level probabilities (e.g., ([9](#bib.bib50))), the
verbalized variant only requires text outputs. This makes it directly
applicable to API-only models (GPT-4, GPT-4o) which are common
decision-support deployments.

Baselines. We compare against (i) length-normalized sequence likelihood
with and without CoT ([23](#bib.bib40)); (ii) verbalized confidence
([35](#bib.bib17)); (iii) post-hoc TS/PS on sequence likelihoods
([12](#bib.bib23); [34](#bib.bib24)); (iv) HalluMeasure
([2](#bib.bib4)), a claim-level NLI-based detector; and (v) LMvsLM
([5](#bib.bib3)), an iterative cross-examination protocol.
Implementation details are in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Implementation Details ‣ Claim-Level Confidence Calibration for Reliable Decision Making with Large Language Models").

What works for decision making
(Tab. [1](#acmlabel2 "Table 1 ‣ 3. Experiments ‣ Claim-Level Confidence Calibration for Reliable Decision Making with Large Language Models")).
On TriviaQA, parametric calibration (PS/TS) and our claim-level method
yield substantially lower ECE than uncalibrated few-shot baselines for
every model. For Llama and Mistral, PS reduces ECE to $`\leq 0.013`$.
For Qwen and Mistral on TruthfulQA, TS cuts ECE by $`3`$–$`4{\times}`$
over the few-shot baseline. On Mistral/TriviaQA, our method attains an
ECE of $`0.123`$ at $`\alpha{=}0.3`$, outperforming LMvsLM ($`0.270`$)
and approaching HalluMeasure ($`0.107`$); notably, this is the only
setting where the best mixture weights self-verification more heavily
than cross-sample consistency ($`\alpha{<}0.5`$), indicating that the
relative value of the two signals is model-dependent. These reductions
translate directly into a more usable accept/reject signal for
downstream decision-makers: a calibrated $`0.9`$ now reliably means
$`\sim`$10% expected error.

Selective prediction vs. calibration (AURC). For Llama-3.1, AURC reveals
a complementary picture to ECE. Because PS and TS only rescale
probabilities, they preserve the few-shot ranking and thus share its
AURC ($`0.523`$ on TriviaQA; $`0.001`$ on TruthfulQA)—calibration does
not improve selective prediction by itself. Conversely, HalluMeasure
attains the lowest TriviaQA AURC ($`0.400`$) despite its high ECE
($`0.242`$), and our method sits mid-pack ($`0.466`$). On TruthfulQA,
every method achieves a very low AURC ($`0.001`$–$`0.007`$), indicating
that the ranking task is easy once the model’s systematic errors
concentrate the low-confidence mass. The takeaway for decision-makers is
that *calibration* and *selective-prediction ranking* are distinct
objectives: a method can be well-calibrated yet rank claims poorly, or
vice versa.

Where claim-level decomposition pays off. HalluMeasure achieves the
lowest ECE on DeepSeek-R1 / TriviaQA ($`0.001`$) and our method
outperforms the temperature-scaling baseline on GPT-4o / TriviaQA by
 15x. The benefit of claim-level scoring is largest in
*mixed-correctness* regimes (long, multi-claim factual responses) where
a response-level number averages over heterogeneous content. This is
exactly the regime where a decision-maker needs to know *which* sentence
to trust, not just an overall score.

Where it fails: adversarial false premises. On TruthfulQA, claim-level
methods consistently underperform on models that confidently state
false-premise-induced claims. For GPT-4o, our method’s ECE rises to
$`0.412`$ versus $`0.118`$ for TS—a model-level *consensus failure*:
GPT-4o produces fluent, high-probability claims that are systematically
wrong, and our pipeline faithfully reflects the model’s own (mistaken)
confidence rather than correcting it. LMvsLM mitigates this on GPT-4o
($`0.112`$) and DeepSeek-R1 ($`0.066`$) because cross-examination
actively probes for contradictions. The implication for decision-makers
is that closed-box, confidence-only signals are not sufficient under
adversarial inputs: an external probe or evidence source is required.

Reasoning models. DeepSeek-R1 emits long CoT traces that, when
claim-extracted in full, produce many intermediate reasoning claims with
weakly calibrated per-step confidence. Mean aggregation dilutes the
final-answer signal; restricting extraction to the final answer segment
is a practical fix that we leave as a deployment recommendation (see
Appendix [C.6](#A3.SS6 "C.6. Reasoning Model Diagnosis ‣ Appendix C Implementation Details ‣ Claim-Level Confidence Calibration for Reliable Decision Making with Large Language Models")).
Across both GPT-4o and DeepSeek-R1, verbalized confidence is better
calibrated than on the open-source models, suggesting reasoning-focused
training improves the model’s ability to express uncertainty in natural
language.

premises, misconceptions), an active verification step (e.g.,

confidence alone is unreliable. (iii) Reasoning models produce better

## 4. Conclusion

We studied claim-level confidence calibration as a decision-relevant
uncertainty signal for LLM deployments. Decomposing responses into
atomic claims and applying post-hoc calibration at the claim level
yields actionable per-claim confidence in closed-box settings, and
reduces expected calibration error on factual questions. The main
failure mode is adversarial false-premise inputs, where models
confidently produce incorrect claims and confidence-only signals are
insufficient—a concrete decision-making implication is that production
pipelines should combine claim-level confidence with an active
verification step (cross-examination or retrieval). Open directions
include adaptive sampling, hierarchical confidence over interdependent
claims, and domain-specific calibration for high-stakes decision-support
applications.

## Appendix A Preliminaries and Calibration Background

### A.1. Key Definitions

Claim. A *claim* is an atomic, verifiable statement extracted from an
LLM response. Given a prompt $`x`$ and response $`r`$, a claim
extraction function $`\mathcal{E}:r\rightarrow\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}`$
produces $`n`$ atomic claims. Each claim $`c_{i}`$ should be (i)
*atomic* (a single factual assertion); (ii) *verifiable* (assessable as
supported, contradicted, or unknown given evidence); and (iii)
*self-contained* (interpretable without the full response context).

Claim verification. Given $`c_{i}`$ and optional evidence $`e`$, a
verifier $`\mathcal{V}:(c_{i},e)\rightarrow\{\text{verbalized
confidence}\}`$ produces a verification label or numeric score. The
verifier may be an auxiliary LLM, a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
system, or an external knowledge source.

Inference-time signals. Statistics derivable during generation that
correlate with correctness without requiring access to parameters or
training. We use two types: (i) *consistency signals*, derived by
sampling $`N`$ responses and measuring agreement; and
(ii) *self-verification signals*, obtained by prompting the model or an
auxiliary verifier.

Claim-level confidence. For each claim we estimate
$`\mathrm{conf}(c_{i})\in[0,1]`$ that should be calibrated, i.e.
$`\mathrm{conf}(c_{i})`$ should align with the empirical probability
that $`c_{i}`$ is supported by evidence.

### A.2. Uncertainty: Epistemic vs. Aleatoric

Uncertainty is commonly split into epistemic (model) uncertainty and
aleatoric (data) uncertainty ([39](#bib.bib30); [21](#bib.bib36);
[4](#bib.bib37)). Epistemic uncertainty arises from limited knowledge
and is reducible with more data or a better model. Aleatoric uncertainty
arises from input noise and is irreducible ([36](#bib.bib38)). Many
estimation approaches use entropy-based statistics on softmax outputs
([38](#bib.bib35); [41](#bib.bib21)), with recent work on probabilistic
contrastive learning to better represent data uncertainty
([22](#bib.bib34)).

### A.3. Calibration Methods for LLMs

Existing calibration methods for LLMs fall into three groups by access
level.

Open-box calibration fine-tunes the model or a small adapter using
internal logits/representations. Calibrated fine-tuning penalizes
overconfident incorrect answers ([19](#bib.bib13)); LitCab
([29](#bib.bib8)) adds a single linear layer on top of last-layer logits
with a max-margin objective; Yang et al. ([44](#bib.bib9)) integrate
Laplace approximation with LoRA; SLiC-HF ([50](#bib.bib12)) uses
preference data to calibrate token ranking. Other work fine-tunes models
to output “unsure” ([49](#bib.bib11); [43](#bib.bib10)). Open-box
methods are costly at LLM scale and often fail to generalize across
model families ([35](#bib.bib17)).

Post-hoc calibration rescales final-layer outputs. Temperature scaling
(TS) ([12](#bib.bib23)) introduces a temperature $`T`$ tuned on a
held-out set,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (2) |  |
``` math
p(y\mid x)=\frac{\exp(\mathrm{logit}\,f(x)/T)}{\sum_{j}\exp(\mathrm{logit}\,f_j(x)/T)}.
``` |  |

Platt scaling (PS) ([34](#bib.bib24)) learns scale $`a`$ and bias $`b`$,

|     |     |                                                       |     |
|-----|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|
| (3) |     |
             ``` math
             p(y_{i}\mid x)=\sigma(a\,\mathrm{logit}\,f_{i}(x)+b).
             ```                                                    |     |

Both can fail under distribution shift ([32](#bib.bib28)). Histogram
binning ([47](#bib.bib26)) is a non-parametric alternative; scale
binning combines parametric and non-parametric pieces
([25](#bib.bib25)).

Closed-box calibration requires no internal access. Linguistic
calibration mitigates misaligned verbal confidence ([31](#bib.bib19);
[42](#bib.bib18); [35](#bib.bib17)). Prompt-augmentation strategies
(paraphrase, permutation, in-context examples) can be ensembled
([18](#bib.bib16)). APRICOT ([37](#bib.bib27)) trains a small auxiliary
calibrator on LLM outputs.

### A.4. Calibration Metrics

ECE. Expected Calibration Error,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (4) |  |
``` math
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{|B_{i}|}{N}\,\bigl|\mathrm{acc}(B_{i})-\mathrm{conf}(B_{i})\bigr|,
``` |  |

with $`k`$ confidence bins $`B_{i}=[(i-1)/k,i/k]`$, bin-wise accuracy
$`\mathrm{acc}(B_{i})=\frac{1}{|B_{i}|}\sum_{j\in B_{i}}\mathds{1}(y_{j}=y_{\mathrm{pred},j})`$
and bin-wise mean confidence $`\mathrm{conf}(B_{i})`$. We use $`k=10`$.

SmoothECE (smECE). Replaces binning with Gaussian kernel smoothing,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (5) |  |
``` math
\mathrm{smECE}_{K,\sigma}=\int\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\bigl|K(t,\mathrm{conf}(y_{i}))(\mathrm{conf}(y_{i})-\mathrm{acc}(y_{i}))\bigr|\,dt,
``` |  |

with $`K(t_{1},t_{2})=\exp(-(t_1-t_2)^2/2\sigma)/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}`$,
providing a binless approximation to the Wasserstein distance to perfect
calibration.

Brier Score (BS).
$`\mathrm{BS}=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i}(\mathrm{acc}(y_{i},y_{\mathrm{pred}})-\mathrm{conf}(y_{\mathrm{pred}},x_{i}))^{2}`$,
also known as the mean squared error ([7](#bib.bib6)). BS measures how
much data variance the model captures ([11](#bib.bib7)).

AUROC. Area under the receiver-operating curve for misprediction
detection on confidence scores ([37](#bib.bib27)),
$`\mathrm{AUROC}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{TPR}(\mathrm{FPR}^{-1}(t))\,dt.`$

BLEU. Bilingual evaluation understudy ([33](#bib.bib49)) as a
generation-quality reference. BLEU is sensitive to length and is
included only as a reference signal; the calibration analysis relies on
ECE/smECE/BS/AUROC.

UCE, RCE, cECE, ACE, AURC. Additional definitions (uncertainty
calibration error, rank calibration error, class-wise ECE, adaptive
calibration error, area under risk-coverage curve) follow the
formulations in ([24](#bib.bib22); [11](#bib.bib7); [37](#bib.bib27));
we omit the formulas for brevity but use them consistently with prior
work.

## Appendix B Extended Related Work

Uncertainty estimation for LLMs spans open-box, post-hoc, and closed-box
methods (cited above). Closely related inference-time approaches sample
multiple generations: self-consistency ([40](#bib.bib20)), universal
self-consistency ([3](#bib.bib15)), semantic uncertainty
([23](#bib.bib40)), and self-evaluation for long-form calibration
([15](#bib.bib14)). Self-verification methods include SelfCheckGPT
([30](#bib.bib32)); cross-examination methods include LMvsLM
([5](#bib.bib3)) and multi-agent debate ([26](#bib.bib2)). Token-level
uncertainty quantification for fact checking has been studied by Fadeeva
et al. ([9](#bib.bib50)); this requires per-token log-probabilities and
is therefore inapplicable to closed-box deployments that are our primary
target.

Claim-level evaluation. HalluMeasure ([2](#bib.bib4)) decomposes
responses into atomic claims and classifies each via NLI labels;
entailment-based verification uses NLI or LLMs as verifiers
([28](#bib.bib31)).

Closest relation: Yuan et al. ([46](#bib.bib5)). Their work proposes
fact-level confidence calibration and *correction* for long-form
generation. Distinctions: (i) Calibration vs. correction. Yuan et al.
*rewrite* incorrect facts; we estimate whether expressed per-claim
confidence aligns with empirical correctness probability (ECE, smECE)
without modifying responses. (ii) Post-hoc calibration. Yuan et al. do
not apply TS/PS at the claim level; we treat per-claim confidence as a
quantity to be calibrated. (iii) Evaluation metrics. They use
precision/recall/correction accuracy; we use
ECE/smECE/BS/AUROC—confidence-accuracy alignment, not rewriting quality.
(iv) Breadth. We evaluate 6 model families, 2 datasets (factual vs.
false-premise), and 7 baselines. (v) Failure-mode analysis. We diagnose
adversarial false premises and extended CoT traces, perspectives absent
in Yuan et al.

## Appendix C Implementation Details

### C.1. Datasets

TriviaQA ([20](#bib.bib42)) contains $`>`$650K question–answer–evidence
triples, plus 95K author-written QA pairs. Following ([37](#bib.bib27))
we sample 12K examples for training and 1.5K for testing. We include 10
in-context exemplars ([23](#bib.bib40); [10](#bib.bib39)).

TruthfulQA ([27](#bib.bib41)) contains 817 questions across 38
categories designed to elicit common misconceptions. We use a zero-shot
setting where each question has true and false target answers with
supporting sources. The dataset is split evenly between training and
testing; for the few-shot baselines we add 10 in-context exemplars.

| Method         | API calls/query                  | Relative cost      |
|----------------|----------------------------------|--------------------|
| Few-shot / CoT | 1                                | $`1\times`$        |

| Verbalized CoT | 1                                | $`1\times`$        |

| LMvsLM         | 5 rounds $`\times`$ 2            | $`\sim\!10\times`$ |

Our method requires one generation, one claim-extraction call, and one
verification per extracted claim ($`\approx`$4 on average). This is
comparable to HalluMeasure and substantially cheaper than LMvsLM. For
deployment, the overhead is acceptable when fine-grained per-claim
confidence justifies the cost (high-stakes decision making,
human-in-the-loop review).

### C.6. Reasoning Model Diagnosis

DeepSeek-R1 emits long chain-of-thought traces that, when
claim-extracted in full, produce many intermediate reasoning steps with
weakly calibrated per-step confidence. Mean aggregation dilutes the
final-answer signal. Restricting extraction to the final answer segment
is a practical deployment fix; we observe consistent improvement when
the extractor is gated to the final answer span. This is a configuration
recommendation, not a fundamental limitation of the framework.

## Appendix E Discussion: When the Method Helps Decision Making

Best-case scenarios. (i) *Mixed correctness*: when responses contain
both correct and incorrect claims, claim-level scores support targeted
intervention. (ii) *Closed-box deployment*: verbalized confidence at the
claim level avoids the logit-access constraint of TS/PS-on-likelihoods
and Fadeeva et al. ([9](#bib.bib50)). (iii) *Moderate complexity*:
multi-claim responses where decomposition adds value beyond a single
score.

Failure modes. (i) *Consensus failure*: all sampled responses agree on
an incorrect claim (TruthfulQA on GPT-4o). (ii) *Verifier weakness*:
errors in claim extraction or verification propagate.
(iii) *Unverifiable claims*: future predictions or creative content
where no external check exists.

Practical recommendations. Cost-benefit: apply claim-level decomposition
selectively when fine-grained confidence matters; for simple queries
response-level may suffice. Verifier selection: verifier quality
dominates; consider hybrid LLM+retrieval verifiers. Selective
application: use fast response-level screening to flag suspicious
responses, then claim-level analysis only for those. Threshold
calibration: safety-critical domains require conservative thresholds;
exploratory uses can tolerate lower confidence.

Future directions. Adaptive sampling (early-stop $`N`$ when samples
agree); hierarchical confidence over interdependent claims;
domain-specific calibration (medical/legal/scientific);
uncertainty-aware training that incorporates claim-level signals into
objectives.
````

# TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains

Samarth Varshney    Ritu Yadav

###### Abstract

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets large language models (LLMs)
answer knowledge-intensive questions from retrieved evidence, but
adapting a general-purpose RAG system to a specialized domain such as
medicine is difficult when only an unlabeled corpus is available.
Self-training approaches such as SimRAG address this by letting the LLM
write its own question–answer pairs from domain passages and fine-tuning
on the pairs that survive a round-trip consistency filter. We observe
that these approaches treat every retained example identically: they
never ask whether the student already knows the answer, already extracts
it correctly from the retrieved context, or fails—and if so, whether the
failure lies with the reader or with the retriever. To tackle this, we
propose TriageRAG, a failure-aware self-training approach that probes
the student on each self-generated question with and without retrieved
context, triages it into one of four categories (known, solved,
context-failure, retrieval-failure), and routes each category to the
training signal that addresses it, including a parametric route that
teaches the reader to answer when retrieval misses. Probing is cheap and
repeated every round, so the training mixture follows the student, and
the triage histogram doubles as a label-free diagnostic of where the
domain gap lies. Experiments on 11 datasets spanning three domains with
a Qwen3-4B backbone, and on CS-Bench with Qwen3-8B, demonstrate that
TriageRAG outperforms a budget-matched SimRAG by 1.2–6.4 points and is
the only method that recovers the closed-book level in every case where
retrieval hurts the base model. A single ablation identifies the
mechanism: round-trip filtering removes every training context that
lacks the evidence, and a reader that never sees such contexts learns to
over-trust noisy retrieval.

## 1 Introduction

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [Lewis et al. (2020)](#bib.bib21);
[Gao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6); [Asai et al. (2024)](#bib.bib1) grounds
the answers of large language models (LLMs) in retrieved evidence, which
adapts them to long-tail knowledge without retraining [Ovadia et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib29) and reduces hallucination [Shuster et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib35). Adapting a general-purpose RAG system to a
specialized domain such as medicine is nevertheless hard: reader and
retriever both face a distribution shift, and labeled domain QA data is
scarce or private, so fine-tuning has to rely on synthetic data. SimRAG
[Xu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib42) is a strong recent recipe. It fine-tunes
an instruction-tuned LLM on general retrieval-oriented data, prompts the
same model to write short-span, multiple-choice and claim-verification
questions from unlabeled domain passages, keeps the pairs whose source
passage is retrieved in the top-$`k`$ for the generated question
(round-trip consistency), and fine-tunes on the survivors with retrieved
contexts.

We start from an observation about what such a pipeline does _not_ look
at: every retained pseudo-labeled pair receives the same treatment.
Nothing in the loop asks whether the student already knows the answer
without context, whether it already extracts it correctly from the
retrieved passages, or whether it fails—and if so, whether the failure
belongs to the reader (the evidence is in the context but the answer is
wrong) or to the retriever (the evidence never reached the context).
These problems have different remedies: contextual fine-tuning teaches a
reader to use evidence that is present, but it cannot help when the
evidence is absent. Worse, the round-trip filter removes exactly the
pairs whose evidence is absent, so the fine-tuned reader never sees a
context that lacks the answer and, in a domain where the retriever
misses often, learns to trust whatever it is given.

We propose TriageRAG¹¹ 1 Failure-aware, _triage_-based self-training for
RAG., which lets the student’s own behaviour decide how each
self-generated example is used
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).
After synthesizing question–answer pairs as in SimRAG, TriageRAG
_probes_ the student on every pair with and without retrieved context,
_triages_ it into known, solved, context-failure or retrieval-failure,
and _routes_ each category to a different treatment: contextual
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for context failures; a _parametric_ route
that trains the reader to produce the verified answer despite an
unhelpful retrieved context—the situation it faces whenever retrieval
misses—for retrieval failures; and small consolidation shares for the
rest. Probing costs a fraction of fine-tuning, so the diagnosis is
repeated every round and the mixture follows the student. The triage
histogram is itself a label-free diagnostic of where the domain gap
lies.

We evaluate on 11 datasets from medicine, natural science and computer
science (CS) with a Qwen3-4B backbone, and on CS with Qwen3-8B as well,
against SimRAG, a RAFT-style mixture and a random-selection control that
share the same Stage-I model, synthetic pool, filter, recipe and budget.
Our contributions are:

- •
  A student-aware view of self-training for domain RAG in which each
  self-generated question is classified by _failure type_ with cheap
  closed-book and open-book probes, yielding a label-free diagnostic of
  the domain gap.
- •
  TriageRAG, which routes the four categories to different training
  signals and iterates, with SFT only and no reward model, judge or
  labeled data.
- •
  Experiments on three domains and two backbone sizes in which TriageRAG
  outperforms a budget-matched SimRAG by 1.2–6.4 points, with three
  training seeds and paired bootstrap intervals on every headline
  comparison; an ablation that identifies the parametric route as the
  ingredient behind the gap; and a controlled negative result on tuning
  the retriever with retrieval failures.

## 2 Related Work

#### Retrieval-augmented generation.

RAG is a standard tool for knowledge-intensive tasks [Lewis et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib21); [Gao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6); recent work refines
when to retrieve [Jiang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib12); [Asai et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib1), makes readers robust to irrelevant passages [Yoran
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib43); [Shi et al. (2023)](#bib.bib34); [Cuconasu
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib5) and instruction-tunes LLMs for RAG [Lin et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib23); [Liu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24). For specialized
domains, RAFT [Zhang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib45) fine-tunes a reader on
GPT-4-written answers with the oracle document and distractors,
including distractor-only examples, and [Schimanski et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib33) distill context-aware QA data from GPT-4. SimRAG [Xu
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib42) removes the proprietary teacher by giving the
student question-answering and question-generation skills and keeping
round-trip-consistent pairs [Bartolo et al. (2021)](#bib.bib2).
TriageRAG keeps SimRAG’s self-generation and adds the missing piece: the
student’s behaviour decides how each generated example is used.

#### Self-training and self-improvement.

Self-training fits a student on teacher-produced labels and has been
applied to LLM instruction tuning [Wang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib38); [Li
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib22), alignment [Yuan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib44);
[Gulcehre et al. (2023)](#bib.bib8) and, in SimRAG, to RAG; its main
weakness is label noise. Recent self-play methods co-evolve a challenger
and a solver and use the solver’s pass rate to keep tasks near its
frontier [Huang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib11); [Kuba et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib17); [Lu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib26); [Kwan et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib18); they optimize task _difficulty_ for reinforcement
learning of reasoning agents, whereas TriageRAG classifies the _type_ of
failure of a corpus-grounded RAG system to choose among training
signals, with plain SFT.

#### Knowledge-aware fine-tuning.

[Gekhman et al. (2024)](#bib.bib7) and [Kang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib16)
show that fine-tuning on facts the model does not know encourages
hallucination; KaFT [Zhong et al. (2025)](#bib.bib46) weights domain SFT
examples by the conflict between parametric and contextual knowledge;
[Longpre et al. (2021)](#bib.bib25) and [Mallen et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib27) study when to trust memory over context. These works
use labeled data. TriageRAG applies knowledge probing to
_self-generated_ data and adds the dimension that matters for
RAG—whether the evidence was retrieved—so that reader and retriever
failures are treated differently.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Problem Setup

In a RAG problem, a retriever $`\mathcal{R}`$ returns the top-$`k`$
passages $`D=\{d_{1},\dots,d_{k}\}`$ from a corpus $`\mathcal{C}`$ for a
query $`q`$, and the LLM $`\mathcal{M}_{\theta}`$ generates an answer
$`a`$ from $`(q,D)`$. We aim to improve the QA capability of
$`\mathcal{M}_{\theta}`$ in a specialized domain where only the
unlabeled corpus $`\mathcal{C}`$ is available. Following SimRAG, we
first fine-tune the model on retrieval-oriented general-domain data
(Stage-I) and then adapt it to the domain with self-generated
pseudo-labeled tuples (Stage-II). TriageRAG changes Stage-II: instead of
treating all retained tuples identically, it diagnoses the student on
each tuple and routes the tuple to the training signal that fits the
diagnosis. The retriever is kept frozen in the main configuration;
Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 A Retriever Route ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
discusses a retriever route.

### 3.2 Stage-I: Retrieval-oriented Fine-tuning

We fine-tune an instruction-tuned backbone on a scaled-down version of
SimRAG’s Stage-I blend (16,000 examples;
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Stage-I Data ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")):
general context-aware QA in short-span, yes/no and multiple-choice
formats, and the two skills used in synthesis, _answer generation_
(candidate answer spans from a passage) and _answer-conditioned question
generation_.

### 3.3 Stage-II: Failure-Aware Self-Training

Each round $`t`$ takes the current student $`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$
($`\mathcal{M}_{1}`$ is the Stage-I model) through four steps.

#### (1) Synthesis.

For a sampled passage $`d\in\mathcal{C}`$ the student proposes candidate
answer spans $`a\sim\mathcal{M}_{t}(\cdot\mid d)`$ and then an
answer-conditioned question $`q\sim\mathcal{M}_{t}(\cdot\mid a,d)`$. As
in SimRAG we generate three formats: short-span questions;
multiple-choice questions, which reuse a span question and add three
distractor spans drawn from other passages, matched on whether the
answer is numeric; and claims that are supported or refuted by $`d`$. We
keep a tuple $`(q,a,d)`$ only if the student recovers $`a`$ when given
the gold passage $`d`$ (_self-answerability_). This label-noise filter
is applied identically to every method we compare. We do _not_ gate on
round-trip retrieval consistency: whether the source passage is
retrieved is recorded as a signal and used by the triage. The synthesis
is run once per domain and the pool is shared by all methods and rounds.

#### (2) Probing.

For every retained tuple we draw $`n`$ samples from the student with no
context and $`n`$ samples with the top-$`k`$ passages
$`D=\mathcal{R}(q)`$ retrieved with the question alone, as at test time,
and compute the closed-book and open-book pass rates
$`p_{\mathrm{cb}}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\mathbb{1}[\hat{a}_{i}^{\mathrm{cb}}\equiv a]`$
and
$`p_{\mathrm{ob}}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\mathbb{1}[\hat{a}_{i}^{\mathrm{ob}}\equiv a]`$,
where $`\equiv`$ is exact match for letters and yes/no answers and a
normalized token-overlap match for spans. The evidence hit is
$`h=\mathbb{1}[d\in D\ \lor\ a\subseteq\mathrm{text}(D)]`$.

#### (3) Triage.

With a threshold $`\tau`$ ($`0.75`$ throughout) each tuple falls into
exactly one category (the tests are applied in this order):

- •
  known: $`p_{\mathrm{cb}}\geq\tau`$; the student already knows the
  answer without context.
- •
  solved: $`p_{\mathrm{cb}}<\tau`$, $`h=1`$,
  $`p_{\mathrm{ob}}\geq\tau`$; the reader uses the retrieved evidence
  correctly.
- •
  context-failure: $`p_{\mathrm{cb}}<\tau`$, $`h=1`$,
  $`p_{\mathrm{ob}}<\tau`$; the evidence is in the context but the
  reader fails or is unstable.
- •
  retrieval-failure: $`p_{\mathrm{cb}}<\tau`$, $`h=0`$; the evidence was
  not retrieved, so no contextual training can make the question
  answerable from $`D`$.

The category histogram is a diagnostic of the domain gap
(Section [5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Study on the Triage ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).

#### (4) Routing and training.

Given a budget of $`B`$ Stage-II examples per round, we fill
per-category quotas of 60%/20%/15%/5% for
context-failure/retrieval-failure/solved/known; an unfilled quota is
topped up from the remaining context failures, then solved items,
without duplicates. In our pools the context-failure category is smaller
than its quota in every domain, so every context failure is used and the
shortfall is filled with solved items (realized shares in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Baseline and Synthesis Details ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).
Each category receives a different treatment:

- •
  Contextual SFT for context-failure: the target is $`a`$ given
  $`(q,D)`$; when the category exceeds its quota, tuples are sampled
  without replacement with probability $`\propto 1-p_{\mathrm{ob}}`$, so
  the items the student gets most wrong are preferred.
- •
  Parametric SFT for retrieval-failure: the target is $`a`$ given
  $`(q,D)`$ where $`D`$ does _not_ contain the evidence. The answer is
  verified against the source passage, so the label is trustworthy, but
  the reader must produce it from parametric knowledge and learns that a
  retrieved context can be unhelpful—the condition it meets at test time
  whenever retrieval misses.
- •
  Consolidation for solved and known: contextual SFT at small shares,
  plus a fixed replay of general-domain context-QA examples, to preserve
  answer formats and general skills.

The mixture is used for one epoch of LoRA fine-tuning [Hu et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib10); the merged model becomes $`\mathcal{M}_{t+1}`$ and
re-probes the pool in the next round.

### 3.4 A Retriever Route

retrieval-failure tuples are also natural retriever training data:
$`(q,d)`$ is a positive pair and the top-ranked retrieved passage is a
mined hard negative. In a variant of TriageRAG we fine-tune the dense
retriever on these pairs with a contrastive loss after each round and
re-index.
Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Ablation Studies ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
shows that this degrades both retrieval and accuracy, so our main
configuration keeps the retriever frozen.

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Tasks and Datasets

We evaluate on 11 datasets from three domains (details in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Test Data Details ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).
For the medical domain we use the five datasets of the MIRAGE benchmark
[Xiong et al. (2024)](#bib.bib41)—PubMedQA [Jin et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib14), BioASQ [Tsatsaronis et al. (2015)](#bib.bib37),
MedQA [Jin et al. (2021)](#bib.bib13), MedMCQA [Pal et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib30) and the medical subsets of MMLU [Hendrycks et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib9)—and add the text subset of MedXpertQA [Zuo et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib47), a recent expert-level benchmark with up to ten
options. For the scientific domain we use ARC-Challenge [Clark et al.
(2018)](#bib.bib4), SciQ [Welbl et al. (2017)](#bib.bib39) as four-way
multiple choice, and the 14 scientific subsets of MMLU used by SimRAG.
For computer science we use the English multiple-choice (MC) and
assertion (AS) questions of CS-Bench [Song et al. (2025)](#bib.bib36),
which can be scored automatically. All questions are multiple-choice,
yes/no or yes/no/maybe, and accuracy is the metric throughout.

The unlabeled corpora, which also serve as the retrieval index at test
time, are MedRAG textbook snippets plus a PubMed slice for medicine
(950,746 passages), SciQ supports and OpenBookQA facts for science
(10,863) and the Wikipedia computer-science subset used by SimRAG for CS
(6,237); see
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Test Data Details ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains").

### 4.2 Baselines

Because our question is how self-generated data should be _used_, all
Stage-II methods share the Stage-I model, the synthesized pool, the
self-answerability filter, the budget $`B`$, the general replay and the
training recipe, and differ only in which examples are selected and how
their contexts are constructed. (1) _No domain adaptation_: the backbone
closed-book, the backbone with RAG, and the Stage-I model with RAG
(equivalent to “SimRAG w/o Stage-II”). (2) _Self-training baselines at
equal budget_: SimRAG^(‡), our re-implementation, which keeps tuples
whose source passage is among the top-$`k`$ (round-trip consistency) and
samples them uniformly with retrieved contexts; _Random selection_,
which samples $`B`$ tuples uniformly from the full pool with retrieved
contexts and isolates the effect of selection from that of volume; and
RAFT-style^(‡) [Zhang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib45), which places the gold
passage among $`k{-}1`$ retrieved distractors for 80% of the examples
and uses distractors only for the remaining 20%. (3) _Ablations_ of
TriageRAG
(Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Ablation Studies ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).
We do not compare with GPT-4-distilled readers or with domain LLMs
trained on task data overlapping our test sets; all rows use the same
retriever and prompt.

### 4.3 Implementation Details

Backbones are Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 and Qwen3-8B in non-thinking mode
[Qwen Team (2025)](#bib.bib31). All fine-tuning uses LoRA ($`r{=}64`$,
$`\alpha{=}128`$, all linear layers), one epoch, learning rate
$`10^{-4}`$ with cosine decay, global batch 64 and maximum length 3,072.
The retriever is bge-base-en-v1.5 [Xiao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib40) with
a flat FAISS index [Johnson et al. (2021)](#bib.bib15) and $`k{=}5`$ for
probing, training and evaluation. Synthesis uses 12,000 medical, 8,000
science and all 5,089 CS passages of at least 40 words at temperature
0.7, yielding pools of 40,540, 32,686 and 20,669 self-answerable tuples.
Probing uses $`n{=}4`$ samples per condition at temperature 0.7 and
$`\tau{=}0.75`$. The per-round budget is $`B{=}12{,}000`$ for medicine
and $`6{,}000`$ for science and CS (30%, 18% and 29% of the respective
pools, so that selection matters), plus 2,000 general replay examples.
TriageRAG runs $`T{=}3`$ rounds; SimRAG runs one round as in the
original paper and, as a control, three. Evaluation is zero-shot with
greedy decoding; parse failures count as wrong
(Appendix [E](#A5.SS0.SSS0.Px2 "Answer parsing. ‣ Appendix E Additional Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).
One medical round of TriageRAG (4B) takes about 15 minutes of probing
with vLLM [Kwon et al. (2023)](#bib.bib20), 80 minutes of LoRA training
(two A100s) and 10 minutes of evaluation. Prompts are in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Prompt Details ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains").

### 5.1 Main Results

Tables [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains"),
[3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
and
[3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
present the results for the medical, scientific and computer-science
domains; we make four observations.

\(1\) _TriageRAG consistently outperforms budget-matched self-training
baselines._ Against SimRAG at the same budget it gains 1.2 points on
average in medicine, 1.9 in science and 6.4 on CS-Bench with the 4B
backbone, and 4.4 on CS-Bench with the 8B backbone; it is the best
fine-tuned method on average in every domain and the best or second best
on 8 of the 11 datasets.

\(2\) _Retrieval is not uniformly helpful, and self-training on
retrieved contexts can lock in its harm._ In medicine, five retrieved
passages add 9.3 points to the backbone, almost entirely on the
literature-grounded PubMedQA and BioASQ. In science and CS the backbone
_loses_ 2.1 and 4.4 points when given five passages
(87.4$`\rightarrow`$85.3, 68.0$`\rightarrow`$63.6), and neither Stage-I
nor SimRAG repairs the loss; TriageRAG is the only method that returns
to or exceeds the closed-book level in a single round in all three cases
(87.6 vs. 87.4; 68.2 vs. 68.0; 70.1 vs. 69.8 for Qwen3-8B), random
selection does so in science only (87.5). In medicine its gain over
Stage-I (+0.9) is concentrated on the exam-style datasets (MedQA +2.0,
MedMCQA +2.2, MMLU-Med +3.3), while the literature yes/no datasets, on
which retrieval already supplies the answer, move by at most 1.3 points.
MedXpertQA stays near the ten-way chance level for every configuration
and does not separate methods at this scale.

\(3\) _Selection matters less than what the selected contexts look
like._ The random-selection control differs from SimRAG only in dropping
the round-trip filter, yet it is 5.7, 1.8 and 0.5 points above SimRAG on
CS, science and medicine (paired bootstrap over questions: $`+5.7`$
\[3.4, 8.1\] on CS, $`+1.8`$ \[1.2, 2.4\] on science) and within 0.1–0.7
of TriageRAG with the 4B backbone. The triage’s own contribution over
random selection is therefore small at 4B—$`+0.6`$ \[-0.9, 2.1\] on CS,
$`+0.1`$ \[-0.4, 0.6\] on science, $`+0.7`$ \[0.1, 1.3\] in medicine—and
becomes clear at 8B, where TriageRAG leads random selection by $`+2.3`$
\[0.2, 4.4\] on CS-Bench and on every training seed
(Section [5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 Robustness: Training Seeds and Confidence Intervals ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).
Two effects are at work—whether the mixture contains evidence-absent
contexts (random selection, RAFT-style and TriageRAG do; SimRAG does
not) and which questions are chosen—which
Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Ablation Studies ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
isolates.

\(4\) _Iterating helps TriageRAG more than SimRAG, but a fixed pool
saturates quickly._ TriageRAG improves in round two on science and CS
(87.6$`\rightarrow`$88.0, 68.2$`\rightarrow`$68.8) and plateaus in
medicine (64.8, 64.7, 64.5), whereas SimRAG, whose selection does not
depend on the student, drifts down in medicine when iterated
(63.7$`\rightarrow`$63.0) and gains only 1.0 point on CS
(61.8$`\rightarrow`$62.8), staying 5.5 points below TriageRAG. With the
8B backbone the second CS round regresses on the assertion subset.

### 5.2 Ablation Studies

#### Effect of the parametric route.

The single ablation that separates TriageRAG from SimRAG is the
treatment of the retrieval-failure examples. In
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.1 Main Results ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
we keep the triage, the budget and the frozen retriever, and only
replace the parametric route by the “obvious” alternative of inserting
the gold passage into the context of these examples, so that the reader
is again trained exclusively on contexts that contain the evidence. On
CS-Bench this variant drops from 68.2 to 61.9, exactly the level of
SimRAG (61.8) and of the Stage-I model (61.9); in the scientific domain
it drops from 87.6 to 84.3, below SimRAG (85.7); and in the medical
domain from 64.8 to 63.7, again the SimRAG level (63.7).
Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.3 Study on the Triage ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
(bottom) makes the pattern visible across all three domains: mixtures in
which no training context lacks the evidence (SimRAG, the ablation) sit
at or below the Stage-I model; mixtures that contain such contexts
(random selection, RAFT-style with 20% distractor-only examples,
TriageRAG with its 20% parametric route) sit within a point of or above
the closed-book model. A reader fine-tuned only on contexts that contain
the answer learns to trust the context; in a domain where the retriever
misses often, that reader is worse than no retrieval at all. Round-trip
filtering, introduced in SimRAG to improve label quality, has the side
effect of removing precisely the examples that teach a reader when _not_
to trust its context.

#### Effect of the triage.

Replacing the triage by uniform selection from the same pool (“w/o
triage”) costs 0.7 points in medicine, 0.6 on CS-Bench and 0.1 in
science with the 4B backbone, and 2.3 on CS-Bench with the 8B backbone;
adding the round-trip filter on top (“= SimRAG”) costs a further 0.5,
5.7 and 1.8. In medicine both selections gain alike on the exam-style
datasets relative to Stage-I (three-seed means within a point of each
other on MedQA, MedMCQA and MMLU-Med); the difference between them is
PubMedQA, which random selection lowers by 3.5 points on average (77.6,
81.0, 76.8 vs. 82.0 for Stage-I) and TriageRAG preserves (81.6, 82.6,
82.2;
Figure [3](#A4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Case studies. ‣ Appendix D Additional Analyses ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).

#### Effect of retriever tuning (a negative result).

The obvious remedy for retrieval-failures is to fix the retriever with
the failed pairs
(Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 A Retriever Route ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).
Fine-tuning bge-base on them and re-indexing each round _reduced_
recall@5 of source passages on the medical pool from 0.48 to 0.34 and
accuracy from 64.8 to 59.1
(Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.1 Main Results ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains"));
with the round-1 reader of that run held fixed, swapping in the tuned
index alone lowers PubMedQA/BioASQ/MedQA from 81.4/90.9/62.1 to
61.8/83.7/60.6
(Table [7](#A4.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Case studies. ‣ Appendix D Additional Analyses ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")).
On CS the tuned retriever raised pool recall (0.87$`\rightarrow`$0.92)
yet lowered CS-Bench accuracy (65.9 vs. 68.2); in science it was
neutral. The failed pairs are a biased sample—many questions are
legitimately not specific to their source passage—so the contrastive
update teaches the encoder noise and moves it away from the benchmark
queries. We therefore keep the retriever frozen; curated negatives and
answer-aware positives are left as the way to make a retriever route
work.

#### What the triage reveals.

Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.3 Study on the Triage ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
and
Table [6](#A4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Case studies. ‣ Appendix D Additional Analyses ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
(appendix) show the category shares: about 41% of the self-generated
questions in medicine and CS, and 57% in science, are known: the Stage-I
student answers them closed-book at least three times out of four, so
they carry little information, yet SimRAG spends a proportional part of
its budget on them. In CS and science the retriever finds the evidence
for 84–89% of the questions and the student solves most of them;
context-failures are 4–6% of the pool and retrieval-failures 7%. In
medicine the source passage is retrieved for only 48% of the questions
and 29% of the pool are retrieval-failures, so a round-trip gate
discards half of the pool and, more importantly, _all_ the examples in
which the context lacks the answer. Multiple-choice and claim items are
overwhelmingly known; 80–94% of the failures come from short-span
questions, so SimRAG’s format diversity mostly supplies format practice
rather than new knowledge.

#### Why round-trip filtering hurts.

Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.3 Study on the Triage ‣ 5 Experimental Results ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
(bottom) plots each mixture’s accuracy against the share of its examples
whose training context lacks the evidence: zero for SimRAG by
construction and at most 2% for the ablation without the parametric
route; the miss rate of the sampled tuples for random selection (46% in
medicine, 16% in science, 10% in CS); 20% for RAFT-style and 21–22% for
TriageRAG. In the two domains where naive retrieval hurts, this share
separates the mixtures into two groups—at most 2% (Stage-I level) and
10% or more (within a point of the closed-book level or above)—and the
ablation falls onto the SimRAG point. The same mechanism fits the
per-dataset pattern
(Figure [3](#A4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Case studies. ‣ Appendix D Additional Analyses ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")):
exam questions are the case in which the retrieved passages are
topically related but lack the answer, so the reader must combine them
with what it knows.

## 6 Conclusion

We introduce TriageRAG, a failure-aware self-training framework for
adapting retrieval-augmented LLMs to specialized domains from an
unlabeled corpus: it probes the student closed-book and open-book on
every self-generated question, triages it by failure type and routes it
to the training signal that fits. Across 11 datasets in three domains,
and at two backbone sizes on CS-Bench, it consistently outperforms a
budget-matched SimRAG, and a single ablation explains why: round-trip
filtering removes every training context that lacks the evidence, and a
reader that never sees such contexts over-trusts noisy retrieval. The
triage doubles as a label-free diagnostic of where a domain gap lies;
making retrieval failures useful to the retriever itself remains open.

## 7 Limitations

Our rounds re-probe a pool synthesized once, so the informative examples
are consumed early and accuracy plateaus; regenerating the pool between
rounds is future work. Our 4B/8B backbones, 16k-example Stage-I blend
and PubMed slice make absolute numbers incomparable with SimRAG’s 8B/27B
models on full PubMed, textbooks and Wikipedia, and MedXpertQA is at
chance for every configuration. Headline cells have three seeds and
paired intervals; the other cells are single runs, and CS-Bench
differences below one point should be read as ties. The parametric route
teaches the reader to answer, not to abstain, when the context is
unhelpful, and our negative retriever-tuning result covers one recipe
(Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Extended Limitations ‣ TriageRAG: Failure-Aware Self-Training for Adapting Retrieval-Augmented Language Models to Specialized Domains")
expands each point).

## Appendix A Stage-I Data

The Stage-I training set is a uniform sample of 16,000 examples from a
20,930-example general blend that mirrors the three components of
SimRAG’s Stage-I data: (1) context-aware QA—SQuAD [Rajpurkar et al.
(2016)](#bib.bib32) short-span questions (3,000), BoolQ [Clark et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib3) yes/no questions (3,000) and OpenBookQA [Mihaylov
et al. (2018)](#bib.bib28) multiple-choice questions with the supporting
fact as context (3,000); (2) answer-span generation—SQuAD passages with
all their annotated answer spans as the target (2,930); and (3) question
generation—SQuAD answer-conditioned question generation (3,000) and
Natural Questions [Kwiatkowski et al. (2019)](#bib.bib19) question
generation from a passage without a given answer (6,000). We omit
SimRAG’s general instruction-following data because our backbones are
already instruction-tuned. One epoch of LoRA training (250 steps at
global batch 64) took 21 minutes on four A100s for the 4B backbone and
67 minutes on two A100s for the 8B backbone. The 2,000 general replay
examples used in every Stage-II round are context-QA examples drawn from
the same blend.

## Appendix C Baseline and Synthesis Details

#### Synthesis.

Passages with fewer than 40 words are skipped. Candidate answer spans
must appear verbatim in the passage and have at most eight words.
Generated questions must end with a question mark and must not contain
the answer string. Multiple-choice distractors are candidate spans from
other passages that do not occur in the source passage, matched on the
presence of digits and within three words of the answer’s length; option
order is shuffled. Claims alternate between supported and refuted per
passage. Self-answerability uses greedy decoding with the source passage
as the only context. The pools contain 40,540 (medical; from 12,000
passages), 32,686 (science; 8,000 passages) and 20,669 (CS; 5,089
passages: 11,908 span, 4,997 multiple-choice, 3,764 claim)
self-answerable tuples.

#### SimRAG^(‡).

Tuples whose source passage is among the top-5 retrieved passages for
the generated question are kept (48%, 78% and 87% of the medical,
science and CS pools); $`B`$ of them are sampled uniformly and trained
with the retrieved passages as context, plus the 2,000 general replay
examples.

#### Random selection.

$`B`$ tuples are sampled uniformly from the full pool and trained with
the retrieved passages as context.

#### RAFT-style^(‡).

$`B`$ tuples are sampled uniformly; for 80% the gold passage replaces
one of the retrieved passages at a random position, for 20% only
retrieved passages other than the gold one are used. Targets are the
short answers rather than GPT-4 chain-of-thought answers, so that all
methods share the same label space.

#### Realized TriageRAG mixture.

With the 60/20/15/5% quotas, the round-1 mixtures contain 2,324
context-failure (all of them), 2,400 retrieval-failure, 6,676 solved and
600 known tuples in medicine ($`B{=}12{,}000`$; 19/20/56/5%); 1,960
(all), 1,200, 2,540 and 300 in science (33/20/42/5%); and 914 (all),
1,200, 3,586 and 300 on CS ($`B{=}6{,}000`$; 15/20/60/5%), plus the
2,000 general replay examples in every case. The measured share of
training contexts that lack the evidence is 22.0%, 20.7% and 20.6%
(medicine, science, CS) for TriageRAG, 46.2%, 15.7% and 10.3% for random
selection, 19.8% and 19.4% for RAFT-style (medicine, CS), and 2.0%, 0.7%
and 0.6% for the ablation without the parametric route.

#### Retriever tuning.

bge-base-en-v1.5 is fine-tuned for one epoch on the round’s
retrieval-failure pairs (question, source passage) with a
multiple-negatives ranking loss over in-batch negatives plus one mined
hard negative per pair, the top-ranked retrieved passage other than the
source (batch 64, learning rate $`2\times 10^{-5}`$, maximum length
256); the corpus is then re-embedded and re-indexed.

#### Answer parsing.

Letters are parsed from the first line of the output (patterns A, (A),
A., “answer is A”); yes/no/maybe and true/false are matched as whole
words. Unparsable outputs are counted as wrong. Across all evaluations
of fine-tuned models the number of unparsable outputs was at most 3 per
dataset; the untuned backbone with retrieval produced 86 unparsable
outputs on MedMCQA and 32 on CS-Bench MC (it sometimes answers with a
sentence instead of a letter), which slightly disadvantages the “+ RAG”
rows.

## Appendix F Extended Limitations

#### Fixed pool and saturation.

Our rounds re-probe a pool that is synthesized once; the informative
examples are largely consumed in the first round and accuracy plateaus.
Regenerating the pool from new passages between rounds, as the shifting
triage suggests, is left to future work.

#### Scale and corpora.

We use 4B and 8B backbones, a Stage-I blend of 16k examples and a PubMed
slice rather than all of PubMed, so absolute numbers are not comparable
with SimRAG’s 8B/27B models, which use full PubMed, textbooks and
Wikipedia; all our comparisons are internal and budget-matched.
MedXpertQA is at chance level for every configuration we trained and
cannot separate methods at this scale.

#### Seeds and intervals.

The headline round-1 cells were replicated with three training seeds and
every comparison in the main text carries a paired bootstrap interval;
the remaining cells (rounds 2–3, RAFT-style and the retriever-tuned
variants) are single runs, and CS-Bench differences of one point or less
between the strong variants should be read as ties.

#### Abstention and calibration.

The parametric route teaches the reader to produce a verified answer
when the context is unhelpful; it does not teach abstention, and we did
not measure calibration or behaviour on unanswerable questions.

#### Retriever route.

Our negative result covers one reasonable recipe (contrastive
fine-tuning of bge-base on failed pairs with retrieved hard negatives).
Curated negatives, smaller learning rates or mixing with the original
training data may reverse it.

## Appendix G Prompt Details

#### Inference and training.

System message: “You are a helpful, knowledgeable assistant. Use the
provided context when it is relevant.” User message:

> Context:  
> \[1\] passage 1 …\[5\] passage 5  
> {dataset-specific instruction, e.g. ‘‘Answer the following question by
> selecting one of the provided options with A, B, C, or D. Please
> answer with the capitalized alphabet only, without adding any extra
> phrase or period.’’}  
> Question: {question}  
> A. … B. … C. … D. …

Closed-book probing uses the same message without the context block.

#### Answer generation.

> Context:  
> {passage}  
> Based on the context, generate several candidate spans within the
> passage that are likely to be answers to a question. The answers can
> be entities, verbs or even numbers. Make sure that the answers are
> different and diverse. Separate different candidate answers with a
> semicolon (‘;’). Output only the candidate answers.

#### Question generation.

> Context:  
> {passage}  
> Based on the context, please generate a question that is relevant to
> the information provided. The question should stand alone and not
> refer back to the context explicitly. The question should be clear and
> understandable without needing the context. The answer to the question
> should be: {answer}  
> Output only the question.

#### Claim generation.

> Context:  
> {passage}  
> Based on the context, please write one specific factual claim that is
> {supported \| refuted} by the context. The claim should stand alone
> and be understandable without the context. Output only the claim.

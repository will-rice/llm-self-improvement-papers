## 1 Introduction

Length generalisation asks whether a model trained on short problems can
solve longer instances of the same task \[[1](#bib.bib29),
[7](#bib.bib14)\]. Sequential state tracking provides a controlled
setting for studying this question: each operation updates a state used
by later operations \[[19](#bib.bib27)\]. A rule-table chain, for
example, uses each retrieved value to select the next lookup, while
object tracking updates the locations of objects after each move
\[[28](#bib.bib4), [29](#bib.bib31)\]. In these tasks, the rules remain
unchanged as the sequence grows; the model must apply them correctly
over more steps. Throughout this paper, length refers to the number of
task operations.

Looped language models offer a way to make computational depth grow with
this length. They repeatedly apply a shared transformer block to hidden
states, allowing additional computation without generating a text
description of each step \[[5](#bib.bib23), [9](#bib.bib16)\], and can
be added to pretrained language models \[[20](#bib.bib13)\]. Iterations
have thus been proposed as a scaling axis alongside parameters,
improving test-time reasoning \[[9](#bib.bib16)\] and following
predictable training trends \[[22](#bib.bib12)\]. On longer problems,
however, neither axis helps by itself: on $`S_{5}`$ trained with at most
16 operations, an untied model with four times the core depth and a
recurrent model run with more iterations both extrapolate less than
$`2\times`$, and fixed-depth Qwen2.5 models from 1.5B to 7B stay near
chance at $`16\times`$
(§[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Depth extrapolation ‣ 5 Main Results"),
§[5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Larger and different backbones ‣ 5 Main Results")).
Prior work demonstrates length generalisation by tying the loop count to
task length \[[7](#bib.bib14)\], and shows that intermediate-state
supervision can teach a pretrained model to perform successive rule
lookups \[[28](#bib.bib4)\]. We build on these results to study how to
sustain accurate execution over much longer operation sequences.

A central training choice is what to ask each intermediate loop to
predict. A final-answer target does not specify the state after only
some operations. On graph reachability, [Chen \[4\]](#bib.bib5) finds
that asking for the final answer at every loop encourages early
predictions and weakens extrapolation. Intermediate-state targets define
progress more directly, but their effectiveness also depends on the
recurrence itself. In our state-tracking experiments, such targets
without norm anchoring lose accuracy beyond short extrapolation
distances
(§[6.1](#S6.SS1 "6.1 Both conditions are necessary ‣ 6 Ablation Studies")).
We therefore study progress-aligned supervision together with control of
the scale of states passed between loops.

We introduce Progress-Aligned Recurrence (PAR) for pretrained language
models. At a supervised iteration $`k`$, the prediction is trained to
match the state after the first $`k`$ operations
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction")a). This _depth–length
correspondence_ assigns successive task stages to successive loop
outputs: on a rule-table chain, for instance, iteration $`k`$ predicts
the value reached after $`k`$ lookups. To control state scale, PAR
rescales each token’s recurrent state to its first-iteration
root-mean-square norm. The targets specify the intended progress, while
norm anchoring fixes state magnitude as the shared block is reused.

The correspondence also guides when to read the answer and how to obtain
intermediate targets. A correct answer can deteriorate if the model
continues looping after completion
(§[5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Learning when to stop ‣ 5 Main Results")). We train
the existing output layer to predict whether execution is complete, and
use a short confirmation window to select the answer without a supplied
stopping depth. When intermediate labels are unavailable, we use
prefixes that are themselves valid shorter problems. Our _prefix
self-distillation_ uses predictions on such prefixes as targets for the
corresponding iterations on the full input, learning from final-answer
labels alone.

Our contributions are:

- •
  We formulate length generalization in looped models as establishing a
  depth–length correspondence, identifying state drift and unaligned
  loop targets as twin failure modes and proposing Progress-Aligned
  Recurrence (PAR) to couple progress supervision with RMS norm
  anchoring.
- •
  We extend PAR with a parameter-free learned halting rule to stop
  inference autonomously and prefix self-distillation to induce the
  correspondence without intermediate step labels.
- •
  We demonstrate $`16\times`$ extrapolation on swap-$`S_{5}`$
  ($`95.5\%`$ full-state accuracy vs. $`55.7\%`$ for RLT and $`0.8\%`$
  for explicit CoT), show consistent benefits across backbones up to 7B,
  and isolate the necessity of both components through controlled
  ablations.

## 2 Related Work

Looped and recurrent-depth transformers. Weight-sharing across depth
originated in Universal Transformers \[[5](#bib.bib23)\], and recurrence
can extend computation to harder instances \[[27](#bib.bib18),
[3](#bib.bib17)\]. In language models, recurrent depth has been trained
from scratch \[[9](#bib.bib16)\] or retrofitted into pretrained models
\[[20](#bib.bib13)\]. Looped architectures can simulate chain of thought
\[[25](#bib.bib26)\], exhibit stable scaling behavior
\[[22](#bib.bib12)\], share representations across depths
\[[15](#bib.bib7)\], and relate to implicit models \[[26](#bib.bib15)\],
though depth is frequently underutilized \[[33](#bib.bib9)\]. We address
this by defining what each iteration computes.

Length extrapolation and halting. Prior work links loop count to input
length \[[7](#bib.bib14)\] or explicit reasoning steps
\[[8](#bib.bib6)\]. From-scratch looped models examine stochastic
stopping \[[16](#bib.bib10)\], mechanistic limits \[[18](#bib.bib11)\],
stability conditions \[[17](#bib.bib8)\], and fast–slow recurrence
\[[30](#bib.bib3)\]; among them, RLT \[[41](#bib.bib1)\] achieves strong
swap-$`S_{5}`$ performance via token-level feedback. In retrofitted
models, per-loop supervision yields modest extrapolation
\[[28](#bib.bib4)\], whereas step-wise final-answer supervision impairs
compositional generalization \[[4](#bib.bib5)\]. For terminating
recurrence, adaptive computation time \[[10](#bib.bib24)\] and PonderNet
\[[2](#bib.bib25)\] train halting probabilities with dedicated modules
and ponder penalties. Under our correspondence, the existing output head
detects completion directly, requiring neither extra parameters nor
ponder losses.

Latent reasoning and self-supervision. Coconut \[[12](#bib.bib21)\] and
stepwise internalization \[[6](#bib.bib22)\] internalize reasoning along
the sequence dimension using chain-of-thought data. In contrast, we
compute along depth, using intermediate labels or prefix
self-distillation inspired by mean-teacher consistency
\[[31](#bib.bib30)\], in the spirit of work that derives training
signals from a model’s own outputs, interactions, or environments
\[[38](#bib.bib35), [35](#bib.bib40), [34](#bib.bib39),
[13](#bib.bib38), [42](#bib.bib42), [32](#bib.bib41)\]. While randomized
positional encodings improve sequence generalization
\[[24](#bib.bib20)\], our positional stretch stabilizes the recurrent
step map when scaling to longer contexts without step labels.

The coda jointly predicts task completion (Yes/No); inference terminates
when a multi-step confirmation window is met.

Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence") outlines
the PAR framework. We realize the depth–length correspondence by
combining retrofitted recurrence with RMS norm anchoring to maintain a
stationary step map
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 A retrofitted looped language model ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence"),
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Keeping the step map stationary ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")),
supervising each loop with progress-aligned targets
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Defining an iteration: progress-aligned targets ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")),
reading completion autonomously via learned halting
(§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Reading completion: learned halting ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")),
and extending the supervision to label-free settings through prefix
self-distillation
(§[3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Correspondence without step labels: prefix self-distillation ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")).

### 3.1 A retrofitted looped language model

Following [McLeish et al. \[20\]](#bib.bib13), we split the 24 layers of
Qwen2.5-0.5B \[[14](#bib.bib32)\] into a prelude $`P`$ (layers 1–8), a
weight-tied core $`C`$ (layers 9–16) and a coda $`Q`$ (layers 17–24), as
shown in Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")a.
With static input features $`e=P(x)`$ and $`s_{0}=e`$, the adapter $`W`$
projects the concatenated representations into the core, iterating

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       s_{k}=C\!\left(W[e;\,s_{k-1}]\right),\qquad k=1,\dots,R,
       ```                                                       |     | (1) |

and reads an answer $`y_{k}=Q(s_{k})`$ from any iteration. The adapter
$`W\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times 2h}`$ is initialised to $`[0\,|\,I]`$, so with
$`R=1`$ the retrofitted model initially computes the same function as
its parent. All weights are trained, gradients flow through the last 8
iterations, and answers are read with a classification head over the
candidate answer tokens at the query position.

### 3.2 Defining an iteration: progress-aligned targets

Consider a problem whose answer is computed by $`d`$ sequential steps
(for state tracking, $`d`$ moves applied to a state). Let $`z_{j}`$ be
the answer after the first $`j`$ steps, so $`z_{d}`$ is the final
answer. As depicted in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")b, the
correspondence asks iteration $`k`$ to hold $`z_{k}`$ until the problem
is finished. With step size $`c`$ (we use $`c=1`$), the training loss is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PAR}}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\operatorname{CE}\!\left(Q(s_{k}),\;z_{\min(ck,\,d)}\right),
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\mathcal{K}`$ contains the iterations inside the gradient
window. Each iteration must advance the computation by exactly $`c`$
steps, so the iterations needed for a problem grow as $`d/c`$ by
construction. Supervising $`z_{d}`$ at every iteration breaks the
correspondence: early iterations are asked for an answer they cannot yet
compute, and the loss rewards shortcuts that do not scale with $`d`$.

Two depth schedules satisfy
Eq. ([2](#S3.E2 "In 3.2 Defining an iteration: progress-aligned targets ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")).
Under *forced depth*, each batch contains a single depth $`d`$, the
model runs $`R=\lceil d/c\rceil`$ iterations, and there is no loss after
completion. Under *hold*, $`R`$ is drawn from a heavy-tailed
distribution ($`1+\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)`$ with log-normal
$`\lambda`$, mean 12, cap 48; [9](#bib.bib16)) and iterations past
$`d/c`$ keep $`z_{d}`$. Forced depth is our default (PAR);
§[5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Learning when to stop ‣ 5 Main Results") removes its
need to know $`d`$ at test time.

### 3.3 Keeping the step map stationary

Extrapolation applies the step map hundreds of times, far beyond the
training depths, so anything that drifts with $`k`$ or with absolute
position eventually changes what the map computes. We remove two such
drifts. *State-norm anchoring* rescales every token’s state to the RMS
norm of its first-iteration state after each iteration
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")a),
$`s_{k}\leftarrow s_{k}\cdot\operatorname{rms}(s_{1})/\operatorname{rms}(s_{k})`$,
with a stop-gradient on the reference; $`R=1`$ is unchanged. *Positional
stretch* addresses long prompts, in which the query lies far from early
moves: during a short fine-tuning stage we multiply position indices by
$`s\sim U[1,s_{\max}]`$, ramping $`s_{\max}`$ from 1 to 8
\[[24](#bib.bib20), cf.\], and test with ordinary positions.
Label-supervised PAR reaches $`16\times`$ without the stretch; the
label-free variant did not reach $`16\times`$ until fine-tuned with it
(§[6.3](#S6.SS3 "6.3 Teacher and positional stretch in prefix self-distillation ‣ 6 Ablation Studies")).

### 3.4 Reading completion: learned halting

Under the correspondence, whether the computation has finished is a
property of the iteration index and the problem, so it can be
supervised. As illustrated in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")d, the coda
read-out that produces the answer is also trained to put probability on
the token Yes when $`ck\geq d`$ and on No otherwise, using
class-balanced cross-entropy with weight 0.5 on every supervised
iteration plus one earlier iteration as an extra negative. No parameters
are added, and training never runs an iteration beyond $`d`$. At test
time we select the first answer iteration $`R`$ with
$`P(\texttt{Yes})>\tau`$ such that at least $`k`$ of the iterations
$`R,\dots,R+m-1`$ also exceed $`\tau`$. After checking this confirmation
window, we return the prediction made at $`R`$. We use $`\tau=0.5`$,
$`k=6`$, $`m=8`$;
§[6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2 Stopping rule and alternatives to halting ‣ 6 Ablation Studies")
explains how this rule was chosen.

### 3.5 Correspondence without step labels: prefix self-distillation

Intermediate answers $`z_{j}`$ come for free from a synthetic generator
but not from most real data. The correspondence supplies them anyway:
the first $`j`$ steps of a problem form a shorter problem whose answer
is $`z_{j}`$, and shorter problems are the ones a model learns first. As
shown in Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")c,
prefix self-distillation therefore uses only final answers. Iterations
$`k\geq\lceil d/c\rceil`$ receive cross-entropy on $`z_{d}`$. For each
example, up to $`n_{k}`$ earlier iterations $`k<\lceil d/c\rceil`$ are
trained to match, by KL divergence, the answer distribution of a teacher
that reads the same prompt truncated after $`ck`$ steps, decoded at its
own completion iteration. The teacher is an exponential moving average
(EMA) of the student \[[31](#bib.bib30)\] or the live student.

### 3.6 Training

We use AdamW with learning rate $`2{\times}10^{-5}`$ ($`10^{-4}`$ for
the adapter), 200 warm-up steps and one epoch over $`{\approx}400`$k
examples spread uniformly over training depths (compute details in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Additional Results")). No intermediate
tokens are generated at training or test time.

## 4 Experimental Setup

Tasks. *Group word problems.* A prompt lists $`d`$ moves on five boxes,
each holding a distinct item, and queries a box. In *swap-$`S_{5}`$*
each move is the identity or one of the 10 transpositions; in $`A_{5}`$
each move is a 3-cycle. Chance accuracy per query is $`0.2`$.
*Swap-$`S_{5}`$ protocol* \[[11](#bib.bib19), [41](#bib.bib1)\]: we
train on at most 32 moves and test on $`1{,}024`$ sequences per length.
A sequence counts as correct only if all five boxes are answered
correctly (*full-state accuracy*). *Benchmarks from prior literature*:
the rule-table chain of [Shapiro \[28\]](#bib.bib4) (unary depth, train
$`\leq 8`$, test to 64) and the graph reachability task of [Chen
\[4\]](#bib.bib5) (train $`\leq 5`$ hops, test to 24).

Metrics. Accuracy is reported in % unless stated otherwise. On $`S_{5}`$
and $`A_{5}`$ we also report the extrapolation factor
$`G=d_{50}/d_{\text{train}}`$, where $`d_{50}`$ is the first test depth
at which accuracy with $`R=d`$ iterations falls below the midpoint
between chance and 100%. With test depths up to 256 and
$`d_{\text{train}}=16`$, $`G=16`$ is the largest measurable value.

Baselines. With the same retrofitted Qwen2.5-0.5B backbone, data and
step budget as PAR, we compare explicit CoT fine-tuning that writes the
full state trace, a fixed-depth model ($`R=1`$), an untied model with
the core repeated four times (48 layers), and random-$`R`$ recurrence
with state-norm \[[9](#bib.bib16)\]. On the swap-$`S_{5}`$ protocol we
also compare with Transformer and RLT, using the values reported by
[Zhang et al. \[41\]](#bib.bib1) for their best split, $`4+4`$ (mean
over 3 seeds). Variants of PAR that remove one of its components,
including the supervision schemes of prior work, are studied separately
in §[6](#S6 "6 Ablation Studies").

Table 4: Learned halting: full-state accuracy (%) without access to the

Forced-depth models answer correctly at $`R=L`$ but degrade rapidly
thereafter, dropping to near-zero accuracy within a few extra iterations
at $`16\times`$. Inference therefore requires identifying when execution
finishes. With the “6 of 8” rule
(§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Reading completion: learned halting ‣ 3 Depth–Length Correspondence")),
the jointly trained read-out halts without knowing problem length $`L`$
(Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.3 Learning when to stop ‣ 5 Main Results")):
accuracy closely matches the oracle iteration count across all seeds and
reaches $`92.9\%`$ at $`16\times`$ (vs. $`55.7\%`$ for RLT).

This stopping mechanism succeeds because jointly trained models enter a
persistent finished state
(Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.3 Learning when to stop ‣ 5 Main Results")a),
with $`P(\texttt{Yes})`$ jumping from near zero before $`L`$ to near 1
afterwards. Because the answer representation itself remains valid for
only a few steps past completion
(Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.3 Learning when to stop ‣ 5 Main Results")b),
the rule reliably reads the prediction from the onset of the finished
signal.

queries). Solid purple: joint training (6 seeds, 1,024 sequences);
dashed red: read-out added by fine-tuning (2 seeds, 1,024 sequences).
The shaded region marks iterations before completion ($`R<L`$) and the
dashed vertical line marks $`R=L`$. (a) The completion read-out stays at

dashed line: chance).

*Final-answer targets do not define an iteration.* Supervising $`z_{d}`$

\[4\]](#bib.bib5), while supervising it only at iteration $`d`$ reaches
$`G=1.48`$. Adding norm anchoring to iteration-$`d`$ supervision extends
extrapolation for a single seed ($`G=8`$), but the remaining seeds
degrade earlier; across seeds, accuracy at depth 256 averages 26.8%, far
below the near-perfect accuracy maintained by PAR.

*The correspondence needs a stationary step map.* Prefix targets without
norm anchoring stall at $`{\sim}1.8\times`$ under either schedule,
consistent with findings in unanchored recurrence \[[28](#bib.bib4)\],
as state representations drift when iterated repeatedly.

*Together, the two conditions extrapolate at least $`16\times`$.* PAR
reaches $`G=16`$ across all seeds with near-ceiling accuracy at
$`d=256`$. PAR-hold also extrapolates beyond $`12\times`$, though with
lower consistency across seeds.

Positional stretch alone does not create the correspondence. Applied as
a fine-tune to the fixed-depth and random-$`R`$ models, positional
stretch leaves both at chance by depth 48 (23% and 18%;
Table [10](#A0.T10 "Table 10")). Applied to a PAR-hold model with step
size $`c=2`$, which already has the correspondence, the same fine-tune
keeps 99% accuracy at depth 512, $`32\times`$ the training depth, in one
run with a fixed $`R=288`$. Stationarity makes an existing step map
reusable; it does not create one.

A one-pass initialisation blocks the correspondence. On $`A_{5}`$, PAR
trained from the pretrained parent reaches $`G=16`$ on both seeds
(Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.1 Depth extrapolation ‣ 5 Main Results")).
Warm-starting all layers from a fixed-depth model that already solves
$`d\leq 16`$ in a single pass gives $`G\approx 1.4`$: the model keeps
its one-pass solution and never learns the step map. Warm-starting only
the prelude succeeds on one of two seeds.

Stopping rule. Evaluating $`P(\texttt{Yes})`$ at a single iteration is
brittle
(Table [8](#S6.T8 "Table 8 ‣ 6.2 Stopping rule and alternatives to halting ‣ 6 Ablation Studies")),
as transient probability spikes cause premature stopping at long
horizons. Conversely, requiring all 8 consecutive iterations to confirm
completion is overly strict when probabilities fluctuate, delaying
stopping until predictions drift. The “6 of 8” rule balances robustness
and responsiveness, matching oracle stopping within 1.6 points across
all evaluated sequences.

Joint training versus a fine-tuned read-out. Adding the read-out by
fine-tuning an existing PAR checkpoint yields different dynamics
(Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.3 Learning when to stop ‣ 5 Main Results")a):
$`P(\texttt{Yes})`$ spikes sharply at completion and decays within two
iterations. As a result, multi-step confirmation windows fail to
trigger, whereas single-iteration stopping recovers oracle accuracy
across both seeds
(Table [11](#A1.T11 "Table 11 ‣ Appendix A Additional Results")).
However, fine-tuning causes minor accuracy degradation on one seed at
$`16\times`$ compared to joint training.

Training on extra iterations is not a substitute. An alternative to an
explicit halting signal is making completion an absorbing state by
training models on extra iterations that must retain $`z_{d}`$. While
this preserves in-distribution accuracy, it severely harms
extrapolation: accuracy collapses below 1% at $`16\times`$ across
evaluated seeds. Asking the recurrent core to advance intermediate
states while simultaneously acting as a fixed point introduces
conflicting objectives; an independent completion read-out avoids this
competition.

## 7 Conclusion

A looped language model extrapolates in depth when each of its
iterations has a defined job. Aligning iteration $`k`$ with the first
$`k`$ steps of the problem, and keeping the step map stationary, turns
extra iterations into extra reasoning. A retrofitted 0.5B model trained
this way surpasses the best published latent-recurrence model on
swap-$`S_{5}`$ at $`8\times`$ and $`16\times`$ the training length. The
same correspondence lets the model learn when to stop and lets it learn
its intermediate states from final answers alone. Defining what an
iteration means is thus a training target in its own right, alongside
choosing how many iterations to run.

## Limitations

We evaluate on algorithmic and templated state-tracking tasks, where
intermediate states are well defined; applying the correspondence to
open-ended natural-language reasoning \[[39](#bib.bib37),
[37](#bib.bib36), [36](#bib.bib34)\] is left to future work. Tasks
without a sequential decomposition, such as graph reachability, fall
outside its scope. Our scaling study covers Qwen2.5 backbones up to 7B
and Qwen3 backbones up to 4B, with three seeds each, in the single-query
$`S_{5}`$ setting only; swap-$`S_{5}`$, learned halting and prefix
self-distillation are evaluated only on Qwen2.5-0.5B. On the larger
backbones, as with prefix self-distillation, results vary across seeds
far more than for PAR on Qwen2.5-0.5B (standard deviations of 33–43
points at $`16\times`$ on Qwen2.5-1.5B to 7B), accuracy does not rise
monotonically with Qwen2.5 size, and no Qwen3 backbone approaches
Qwen2.5-0.5B at $`16\times`$. These results therefore do not establish a
monotonic trend with model size; characterizing the mechanisms of seed
variance on larger backbones and stabilizing recurrent scaling remain
open questions for future work.

## Ethics Statement

This work studies training methods on synthetic reasoning tasks with
publicly available models \[[14](#bib.bib32), [40](#bib.bib2)\]. It
involves no human subjects and no personal data. Latent reasoning writes
no intermediate steps, which makes it less transparent than chain of
thought. Under the correspondence, however, every iteration can be
decoded into the intermediate answer it holds, which offers a way to
inspect the latent computation.

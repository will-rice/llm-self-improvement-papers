# Learning from Self-Generated Responses: Reliability–Uncertainty Self-Distillation for Reasoning

Jinhao Pan    Bowen Wei    Yuqing Zhou    Ziwei Zhu Affiliation: George
Mason University Affiliation: {jpan23, bwei2, yzhou31, zzhu20}@gmu.edu

###### Abstract

Reference solutions and question-specific rubrics are not always
available to guide the teacher in self-distillation. A large language
model’s own solution attempts offer an alternative, but their quality is
difficult to assess in open-ended tasks without automatic correctness
checks, and even correct attempts can involve uncertain token
predictions. We propose Reliability–Uncertainty Self-Distillation (RUSD)
to learn from these attempts. The same model serves as teacher and
student: the teacher receives the question and completed attempt, and
the student matches the teacher’s token-level predictions using a
question-only prompt. RUSD weights each attempt’s distillation loss by
additively combining judged reliability, estimated from a self-judge’s
score and confidence, with student predictive uncertainty. Across eleven
reasoning benchmarks, RUSD outperforms all five baselines in average
accuracy, reaching 70.47% on Qwen3-4B and 76.69% on Qwen3-8B (8.90 and
2.10 percentage points above the base models). Data and code are
available at
[https://github.com/JP-25/RUSD](https://github.com/JP-25/RUSD).

## 1 Introduction

Self-distillation trains large language models (LLMs) by using the model
itself as a teacher (Zhao et al., 2026a; Gu et al., 2026). In recent
approaches, the teacher receives the question together with additional
_teacher context_, such as a reference solution; the student uses a
question-only prompt and learns by matching the teacher’s next-token
predictions along student-generated responses.

#### Challenge 1: Reference solutions and rubrics are not always available to guide the teacher.

SDFT and OPSD supply demonstration responses (example answers) or
reference solutions as teacher context (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Zhao et
al., 2026a). RCSD and RGSD use question-specific rubrics, which specify
what a good response should contain (Gu et al., 2026; Rezaei et al.,
2026). When these solutions or rubrics are unavailable, preparing them
can require expensive human annotation or additional model generation.
One alternative is to learn from the model’s own generated responses
without supplied reference solutions (Pei et al., 2026; Li et al.,
2026). These self-generated _solution attempts_, however, may contain
errors.

#### Challenge 2: Assessing attempt quality is difficult in open-ended reasoning.

On open-ended reasoning tasks, reliable automatic verifiers for these
self-generated attempts are often unavailable (Ye et al., 2026a). In
reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), mathematical
answers can be checked against known results, and code can be run
against tests (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). Without comparable checks or
reference answers, it is difficult to determine which attempts are
correct and how much to learn from them. An LLM _judge_, a model
prompted to assess response quality, can score these attempts, but its
ratings can be wrong (Zheng et al., 2023). And using a larger external
judge can increase the computation or API fees needed to score training
attempts (Verga et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2026).

Our intuition is to learn from correct attempts generated with uncertain
token predictions, so the model can produce the reasoning more
confidently. We propose _Reliability–Uncertainty Self-Distillation_
(RUSD, Figure 1) based on this intuition. For each question, the current
model generates one solution attempt. As teacher, the same model
receives the question and completed attempt in its prompt to provide
token-level guidance. A judge assesses the attempt’s quality: high
scores supported by confident judgments indicate greater _judged
reliability_. As student, the model learns to match the teacher’s
guidance with only the question as its prompt. We weight each attempt’s
distillation loss using judged reliability and the student’s uncertainty
in predicting its tokens, giving priority to attempts judged reliable
and to attempts with uncertain token predictions.

Our contributions are threefold: 1. We introduce RUSD, which uses one
model-generated attempt as teacher context without supplied solutions or
question-specific rubrics. 2. We design response weights that combine
judged reliability and student uncertainty, giving greater learning
priority to attempts judged reliable and to attempts with uncertain
token predictions. 3. Across eleven reasoning benchmarks, RUSD
outperforms all five baselines in macroaverage accuracy at both scales,
improving Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B by 8.90 and 2.10 over the base models.
External ratings, training analyses, and ablations examine its weighting
signals and learning behavior.

## 2 Related Work

#### Self-distillation with and without supplied references.

Self-distillation trains a model to match its own predictions. In recent
on-policy LLM methods, the teacher receives additional context and the
student matches the teacher’s token distributions along
student-generated responses (Zhao et al., 2026a). SDFT and OPSD supply
demonstrations or reference solutions; RCSD and RGSD supply
question-specific rubrics (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026a; Gu
et al., 2026; Rezaei et al., 2026). Without supplied solutions, NSD
constructs negative teacher conditions from model responses, whereas
Canon and u-OPSD select a self-generated solution by majority-answer
consensus across multiple responses (Pei et al., 2026; Gkountouras et
al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). RUSD uses one completed attempt as teacher
context, without negative conditions or answer consensus.

#### Assessing self-generated attempts.

SDPO supplies environment feedback or successful responses to the
teacher, while SD-Zero trains a reviser with binary correctness feedback
(Hübotter et al., 2026; He et al., 2026). Such feedback requires
response assessment; consensus requires rules for matching answers. RUSD
estimates attempt reliability from judge score and confidence without
correctness labels. Reliability controls the response weight; the
teacher supplies next-token predictions.

#### Weighting by confidence and uncertainty.

LLKD’s weighted variant combines teacher confidence and student
uncertainty for classification (Li et al., 2025). EGRSD weights token
updates using teacher entropy, PW-OPSD uses token position, and COSE
uses evaluator confidence for policy updates and replay (Ke et al.,
2026; Liu et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026). RUSD combines judged
reliability with student predictive uncertainty to weight each attempt’s
distillation loss. Appendix A gives fuller comparisons and the scope of
our baselines.

## 3 Method

#### Overview.

Figure 1 illustrates the RUSD workflow, and Algorithm 1 in Appendix B
shows the training procedure. RUSD trains on questions
$`\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}`$ without supplied reference solutions
or question-specific rubrics. The current model $`\pi_{\theta}`$ serves
as teacher and student under different prompts. The initial model
$`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$ is kept frozen throughout training and serves as
the judge. The current model generates one attempt, which the teacher
uses as additional context to provide token-level guidance
(![](arxiv-2610-19176v1--a8f15cdcd8bf.figures/inline-6.svg)).
The judge’s score and confidence estimate the attempt’s reliability
(![](arxiv-2610-19176v1--a8f15cdcd8bf.figures/inline-7.svg)).
We also measure the student’s uncertainty in predicting the attempt’s
tokens
(![](arxiv-2610-19176v1--a8f15cdcd8bf.figures/inline-8.svg)).
These signals determine the attempt’s training weight
(![](arxiv-2610-19176v1--a8f15cdcd8bf.figures/inline-9.svg)).
The student learns to match the teacher’s token distributions, with each
attempt’s contribution scaled by its weight
(![](arxiv-2610-19176v1--a8f15cdcd8bf.figures/inline-10.svg)).
Table 4 in Appendix B gives the three prompts.

### 3.1 Using the attempt as teacher context

For each sampled question $`\mathbf{x}_{i}`$, the current model
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ generates one solution attempt $`\mathbf{y}_{i}`$:

```math
\mathbf{y}_{i}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})), \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{S}}`$ is the question-only prompt. The
teacher prompt
$`\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i})`$ contains the
question and completed attempt. The prompt describes the attempt as
potentially useful or misleading and asks the model to solve the problem
independently using any useful information.

Following on-policy self-distillation (Zhao et al., 2026a), we evaluate
teacher and student next-token distributions at every position within
this same sampled response. At position $`t`$, both receive the
preceding response tokens
$`\mathbf{y}_{i,<t}=(y_{i,1},\ldots,y_{i,t-1})`$. Only the teacher
prompt also contains a complete copy of $`\mathbf{y}_{i}`$, giving it
access to the response’s later reasoning when predicting earlier tokens:

```math
\displaystyle\pi^{\mathrm{S}}_{i,t}(v) \displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(v\mid\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{S}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\mathbf{y}_{i,<t}),
```

```math
\displaystyle\pi^{\mathrm{T}}_{i,t}(v) \displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(v\mid\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}),\mathbf{y}_{i,<t}), \tag{2}
```

where $`v\in\mathcal{V}`$ is a vocabulary token, and
$`\pi^{\mathrm{S}}_{i,t}`$ and $`\pi^{\mathrm{T}}_{i,t}`$ are the
student and teacher next-token distributions, respectively.

### 3.2 Estimating reliability from self-judgment

The judge $`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$ assesses each completed attempt to
provide a quality signal for response weighting; the teacher provides
token distributions for the student to match. For efficiency, we reuse
the initial model as judge rather than rely on a larger external model.
Given the question and attempt, the judge evaluates logical validity,
internal consistency, factual plausibility, relevance, and completeness
using one five-level rubric shared across questions (Table 4,
Appendix B).

For question–attempt pair $`i`$, let $`\ell^{\mathrm{J}}_{i,k}`$ denote
the judge’s logit for rating $`k\in\{1,\ldots,5\}`$; the superscript
$`\mathrm{J}`$ labels judge quantities. The judge is prompted to return
one rating digit. We read its five rating-token logits and normalize
them into a distribution, with $`j`$ indexing the possible ratings in
the denominator:

```math
\pi^{\mathrm{J}}_{i}(k)=\frac{\exp(\ell^{\mathrm{J}}_{i,k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{5}\exp(\ell^{\mathrm{J}}_{i,j})}. \tag{3}
```

#### Judge score.

We take the judge’s most probable rating as the quality score: 1 means
fundamentally incorrect, and 5 means fully correct and well justified.
We rescale the score to $`[0,1]`$ for use in the response weight:

```math
s_{i}=\arg\max_{k\in\{1,\ldots,5\}}\pi^{\mathrm{J}}_{i}(k),\qquad\bar{s}_{i}=\frac{s_{i}-1}{4}. \tag{4}
```

#### Judge confidence.

Judge confidence distinguishes attempts with the same score: the judge
may strongly favor that rating for one attempt but be split between
ratings for another. We quantify this distinction using one minus
normalized rating entropy:

```math
c_{i}^{\mathrm{J}}=1-\frac{H(\pi^{\mathrm{J}}_{i})}{\log 5}, \tag{5}
```

where $`H(p)=-\sum_{j}p(j)\log p(j)`$ is Shannon entropy and $`\log 5`$
is the maximum entropy over five ratings. Confidence is zero for equally
likely ratings and approaches one as probability concentrates on one
rating. It measures decisiveness, not a calibrated probability of
correctness.

#### Judged reliability.

We combine the normalized judge score and confidence into judged
reliability:

```math
r_{i}^{\mathrm{J}}=\bar{s}_{i}c_{i}^{\mathrm{J}}. \tag{6}
```

The product is high only when the judge assigns a high score with high
confidence; uncertain high scores and confident low scores both receive
lower reliability. Appendix B gives an example, and Section 4.3 relates
judge score and confidence to externally assessed response quality.

### 3.3 Measuring student uncertainty

We use student uncertainty to prioritize attempts whose tokens the
student predicts less confidently, including attempts judged reliable.
We measure this uncertainty by averaging normalized next-token entropy
over the generated response $`\mathbf{y}_{i}`$ of length
$`n_{i}=|\mathbf{y}_{i}|`$:

```math
u_{i}^{\mathrm{S}}=\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{i}}\frac{H(\pi^{\mathrm{S}}_{i,t})}{\log|\mathcal{V}|}. \tag{7}
```

We use the full-vocabulary student distribution at the distillation
temperature (Appendix B); prompt and padding positions are excluded.
Normalization bounds $`u_{i}^{\mathrm{S}}`$ in $`[0,1]`$, and averaging
avoids an automatic increase with response length. A larger value
indicates more diffuse token predictions along that response, rather
than a direct estimate of whether its reasoning is correct.

### 3.4 Combining reliability and uncertainty

We combine the two signals to weight each attempt’s distillation loss:

```math
w_{i}=\alpha r_{i}^{\mathrm{J}}+(1-\alpha)u_{i}^{\mathrm{S}},\qquad\alpha\in[0,1]. \tag{8}
```

The reliability term prioritizes attempts judged to be high quality with
high confidence, while the uncertainty term prioritizes attempts for
which the student’s token predictions are uncertain.

The additive rule lets either priority contribute independently:
uncertainty can contribute when judged reliability is low, and
reliability can contribute when the student is already certain. The
endpoints $`\alpha=1`$ and $`\alpha=0`$ use reliability and uncertainty
alone, respectively. We use $`\alpha=0.5`$, which gives both signals
equal coefficients; their realized contributions depend on their
magnitudes. Section 4.5 evaluates both components and their balance.

### 3.5 Training objective

We train the student to match the teacher’s token distributions, scaling
each response’s average forward-KL loss by its weight $`w_{i}`$:

```math
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}}\!\left[\frac{w_{i}}{n_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{i}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^{\mathrm{T}}_{i,t}\,\|\,\pi^{\mathrm{S}}_{i,t})\right], \tag{9}
```

where the expectation is over questions from $`\mathcal{D}`$ and
attempts drawn from Equation 1. Following OPSD (Zhao et al., 2026a), we
optimize a coordinate-clipped surrogate of this loss, with gradients
passing only through student predictions. Appendix B gives the clipping
and model-update details.

and confidence reflect response quality? RQ3: During training, does the

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Models and Training Data.

We evaluate Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), with component
ablations and hyperparameter studies on 4B. All methods share 30,000
NaturalReasoning questions (Yuan et al., 2025), sampled with seed 42
after filtering for non-empty reference answers so that reference-based
baselines can use the same questions. RUSD learns from questions and its
own online attempts; the dataset’s reference answers and generated
responses are excluded from its teacher and judge prompts.

#### Baselines.

We compare five representative supervision strategies on the same
training questions. _Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)_ (Ouyang et al., 2022)
learns from the dataset’s Llama3.3-70B-Instruct responses. _GRPO_ (Shao
et al., 2024) uses scalar rewards from an external LLM judge. _RCSD_ (Gu
et al., 2026) supplies question-specific rubrics as teacher context.
_OPSD_ (Zhao et al., 2026a) uses the dataset’s Llama3.3-70B-Instruct
responses as teacher context and matches teacher and student predictions
along student-generated responses. _NSD_ (Pei et al., 2026) generates
negative conditions from model responses and suppresses behavior
encouraged by the negatively conditioned teacher. Thus SFT, OPSD, RCSD,
and GRPO rely on supervision beyond the training questions (marked with
^(†) in Table 1), whereas NSD and RUSD use only the questions and the
model’s own outputs.

#### Benchmarks.

We evaluate eleven benchmarks: GPQA Diamond (Rein et al., 2024),
SciBench (Wang et al., 2024), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a), LiveBench
Reasoning (White et al., 2025), and OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018)
for scientific and general reasoning; GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), MATH
(Hendrycks et al., 2021b), AMC (AI-MO, 2024), Minerva Math (Lewkowycz et
al., 2022), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024) for mathematics; and
ZebraLogic (Lin et al., 2025) for logic-grid reasoning.

#### Evaluation.

We report Avg@4: correctness averaged over four generated responses per
question and then across questions, expressed as a percentage. _Average_
in Table 1 is the unweighted mean of the eleven benchmark scores. All
accuracy gains are absolute differences on this 0–100 scale, calculated
before rounding. Appendix B gives the decoding and scoring settings.

#### Implementation.

All methods use NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPUs and shared training settings. At
both model sizes, results are reported after 150 training steps, except
GRPO, which uses 500 steps as in RCSD’s GRPO setting. For RUSD, we train
LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022) on four GPUs with $`\alpha=0.5`$ and
student and teacher prompts in thinking mode. Appendix B gives the
hyperparameters (Table 5), model updates, GRPO judge, and
evaluation-step details.

In Table 1, RUSD reaches 70.47% macroaverage accuracy at 4B and 76.69%

base model. At 8B, the four externally supervised baselines change the
base average by $`-`$0.08 to +0.88, compared with RUSD’s +2.10. Thus,
RUSD improves both tested models without supplied solutions or
question-specific rubrics. Every evaluated 4B training step from 50 to

#### Higher judge scores and confidence are associated with higher GPT scores.

We ask gpt-5.6sol (OpenAI, 2026) to rate 200 randomly sampled saved
training responses, one per question, using the same 1–5 quality rubric
as the training judge. These ratings, termed _GPT scores_, serve only as
an external measure of response quality and are not used to train RUSD.
Table 2 reports linear regressions that estimate GPT scores from judge
score, judge confidence, or both. In the joint regression, higher judge
scores are associated with higher GPT scores at the same confidence, and
higher judge confidence is associated with higher GPT scores at the same
judge score.

We report the multiple correlation coefficient $`R`$, defined as the
Pearson correlation between fitted and observed GPT scores. Using judge
score and confidence together gives $`R=0.77`$ and explains 59% of the
in-sample variation in GPT scores, compared with 21% for judge score
alone and 39% for judge confidence alone. Adding confidence to score
increases explained variation by $`\Delta R^{2}=0.38`$. Thus, judge
confidence helps distinguish responses with the same judge score but
different GPT scores.

These results support combining judge score and confidence to assess
response quality. Section 4.5 tests the product of normalized judge
score and judge confidence as a training weight. GPT scores are external
quality assessments, not verified correctness labels. Appendix B gives
the regression definitions and coefficients.

### 4.4 Training Dynamics (RQ3)

In this experiment, we analyze the Qwen3-4B run with $`\alpha=0.5`$.
Figures 2 and 3 show changes in judge assessments and student
uncertainty during training; Table 8 in Appendix E provides counts and
detailed comparisons.

At reported steps 100, 200, and 300, scores 4 and 5 account for at least
99.2% of completed responses, with score 5 becoming more frequent
(Figure 2a). Score-only weighting therefore assigns almost all responses
either 0.75 or 1.0. Confidence distinguishes attempts with the same
score; RQ2 links higher confidence to higher GPT scores at the same
judge score.

Both the number and percentage of responses with judge score 5 and high
confidence increase. From step 50 to 300, this group grows from 319 to
638 responses, or 26.5% to 42.8% of completed score-4/5 responses
(Figure 3a; Table 8). Within score 5, the high-confidence proportion
($`c_{i}^{\mathrm{J}}\geq 0.7`$) rises from 49.0% to 68.5% (Figure 2b).
The score-4/high-confidence group also grows as a share of score-4/5
responses; its within-score proportion rises from 35.3% to 61.6%, with
fluctuations.

Mean student uncertainty falls 3.86-fold from step 50 to 300. Across
completed score-4/5 responses, mean $`u_{i}^{\mathrm{S}}`$ falls from
0.2649 to 0.0687, and the middle half of uncertainties also shifts
downward (Figure 2c). Student predictions thus become more concentrated.

Within the score-5/high-confidence group, both the number and percentage
with low student uncertainty increase overall. Here, low uncertainty
means below the mean of all completed score-4/5 responses in the same
summary. The count rises from 156 of 319 responses (48.9%) at step 50 to
463 of 638 (72.6%) at step 300 (Figure 3b; Table 8). The percentage
peaks at 74.9% at step 200. This threshold measures relative uncertainty
because the mean changes across summaries.

From step 100 onward, low student uncertainty is more frequent in
high-confidence than low-confidence groups at both scores. At score 5,
the percentages are similar at step 50 but reach 72.6% versus 39.8% at
step 300 (Table 8). Among high-confidence responses, the below-mean
percentage is also higher for score 5 than score 4 from step 100 onward
(Figure 3b).

Together, rising judge confidence and falling student uncertainty are
consistent with consolidation, but these summaries do not track
individual attempts being learned. The two changes affect the response
weight in opposite directions: higher judge confidence at a fixed score
raises the reliability contribution, while lower student uncertainty
reduces the uncertainty contribution.

Further, accuracy reaches 70.47% at step 150, then falls despite
declining student uncertainty and increasing high-confidence score-5
judgments (Figure 5, Appendix C.2). Greater predictive certainty and
judge confidence therefore do not establish continued improvement in
reasoning. A selected heat-calculation case illustrates retained
correctness with increasing certainty: both responses are correct and
receive score 5, while judge confidence rises from 0.572 to 0.771 and
student uncertainty falls from 0.1728 to 0.0388. Table 7 gives the
complete final responses; Appendix D gives the calculations and
selection details.

### 4.5 Ablations and Hyperparameters (RQ4)

In this analysis, we study response weighting, loss type, and the
weighting coefficient $`\alpha`$ on Qwen3-4B using the eleven-benchmark
macroaverage. To assess what judge confidence and student uncertainty
add beyond the judge score, our weighting ablations compare against
score-only weighting rather than uniform weighting, which assigns the
same loss weight to every attempt. Teacher context and forward KL remain
fixed across the weighting variants. The loss ablations retain the full
weighting rule and change the divergence. Table 3 summarizes the
ablations; Table 6 in Appendix C.1 gives the per-benchmark comparison
with score-only weighting.

use the forward-KL configuration; loss variants use the full weighting
rule at $`\alpha=0.5`$. Jensen–Shannon divergence uses mixture

_Ablation studies._ Adding judge confidence improves average accuracy by
1.68. Score-only weighting, $`w_{i}=\bar{s}_{i}`$, reaches 68.44%;
multiplying the normalized score by judge confidence, which gives
reliability-only weighting
$`w_{i}=r_{i}^{\mathrm{J}}=\bar{s}_{i}c_{i}^{\mathrm{J}}`$, raises
accuracy to 70.12%. Score-only weighting assigns the same weight to all
attempts with the same rating; confidence differentiates attempts within
each score level. The gain is consistent with the quality audit, in
which confidence explains additional variation in external ratings.

Combining reliability and uncertainty gives the highest average accuracy
among all the weighting variants. Reliability-only weighting prioritizes
assessed quality, while uncertainty-only weighting prioritizes diffuse
student predictions. The full rule combines both priorities and reaches
70.47%, exceeding reliability-only weighting by 0.35 and
uncertainty-only weighting by 0.54. It is also higher than score-only
weighting on all eleven benchmarks, with a macroaverage gain of 2.03.
Adding confidence gives the larger observed gain (1.68), while adding
student uncertainty to reliability gives a smaller further gain in this
single-run comparison. Both single-signal variants exceed the strongest
baseline average, while their combination gives a further improvement.

Forward KL gives the highest average among the tested loss types.
Response weights determine each attempt’s contribution, while the loss
type determines how teacher and student token distributions are matched.
With the full weighting rule and $`\alpha=0.5`$, the forward-KL
configuration reaches 70.47%, compared with 69.85% for reverse KL and
69.12% for Jensen–Shannon divergence with mixture coefficient 0.5.

_Hyperparameter study._ $`\alpha=0.5`$ gives the highest observed
average accuracy. In Equation 8, $`\alpha=0`$ gives uncertainty-only
weighting and $`\alpha=1`$ gives reliability-only weighting, matching
the corresponding ablations in Table 3. Larger $`\alpha`$ assigns a
higher coefficient to judged reliability, emphasizing quality supported

settings reach at least 70.13%. The highest observed average favors
using both signals in this configuration.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we address two challenges in self-distillation: obtaining
teacher guidance without supplied reference solutions, and assessing
self-generated attempts without reliable correctness feedback. RUSD uses
the attempts as teacher context and weights distillation by judged
reliability and student uncertainty. Across eleven benchmarks, it
improves Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B macroaverage accuracy by 8.90 and 2.10
over the base models, respectively. At 4B, adding judge confidence
improves average accuracy, and combining reliability with uncertainty
gives the highest observed average among the weighting variants.
External ratings support judge confidence as a quality signal, while
training summaries show more high-confidence judgments alongside lower
student uncertainty.

## Limitations

#### Model coverage.

Our experiments use Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B. Further evaluation across
model families and sizes is needed to establish how well RUSD
generalizes beyond these backbones.

#### Judge design.

For efficiency, the judge assesses completed reasoning responses with
thinking mode disabled, directly reading rating-token logits without
generating an evaluation rationale. Whether enabling judge thinking or
updating the judge improves assessment quality remains untested.
Enabling thinking would add decoding cost and latency, depending on the
reasoning budget; judge-update costs depend on whether updates reuse
student parameters or require separate optimization. We also do not
compare larger judges or judges from other model families within RUSD,
so their effects on accuracy and cost remain untested.

#### Self-judgment and learning dynamics.

The judge can confidently endorse incorrect responses and share biases
with the generating model. Its confidence measures concentration over
ratings, rather than a calibrated probability of correctness. The
additive rule also assigns uncertainty-based weight when judged
reliability is low. The 200-response audit measures in-sample agreement
with automated quality ratings, rather than verified correctness.
Training summaries follow changing response populations and describe
associations rather than causal learning mechanisms. The later decline
in accuracy despite falling entropy shows that greater predictive
certainty does not guarantee better reasoning.

#### Component evidence.

Component studies use Qwen3-4B. Judged reliability and student
uncertainty both lie in $`[0,1]`$; their interaction and relative
influence on learning across training stages warrant deeper analysis.

#### Cost and scope.

Each attempt requires a teacher forward pass conditioned on the complete
attempt and a separate judge forward pass. The teacher does not generate
a second response. We do not report total compute across all experiments
or compare methods at matched compute budgets, and we do not analyze
response length or truncation rates. Experiments use one
NaturalReasoning training selection, English questions, and thinking
mode for response generation. All benchmarks have automatically
checkable answers, so performance on open-ended tasks remains untested.

## Ethical Considerations

This study uses publicly available reasoning resources and
model-generated responses. NaturalReasoning is released under CC BY-NC
4.0, and the Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B backbones under Apache 2.0.¹¹ 1 See
the official [NaturalReasoning dataset
card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/facebook/natural_reasoning) and
[Qwen3-4B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-4B)/[Qwen3-8B model
cards](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-8B). The dataset’s
Llama3.3-70B-Instruct responses, which only the SFT and OPSD baselines
use, carry additional obligations under the [Llama 3.3 Community
License](https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct/blob/main/LICENSE)
when used to train distributed models. Reuse and redistribution should
respect these licenses and the terms of individual evaluation resources,
including the training dataset’s noncommercial restriction.

Self-distillation can reinforce factual errors and social biases
inherited from the model or its training data. The teacher and judge
share a pretrained backbone, so errors may persist even when judge
confidence increases and student uncertainty decreases. The proposed
weighting signals are fallible assessments, so more confident responses
should not be treated as verified answers.

The external quality check uses automated gpt-5.6sol ratings of 200
responses. It provides evidence about assessed response quality, rather
than human verification or a safety evaluation. Our experiments do not
measure fairness, privacy, or robustness to harmful requests.
Deployment, especially in high-stakes settings, would require separate
assessment and safeguards appropriate to the intended use.

## Appendix A Related Work

#### Distillation and self-training.

Knowledge distillation transfers teacher predictions to a student
(Hinton et al., 2015; Kim and Rush, 2016); on-policy distillation
applies this guidance along student-generated responses (Agarwal et al.,
2024). Context distillation transfers behavior elicited by additional
teacher context (Askell et al., 2021; Snell et al., 2022; Ye et al.,
2026b), connecting to learning with privileged information (Vapnik and
Vashist, 2009; Lopez-Paz et al., 2016). Self-training selects generated
responses using consistency, correctness checks, or rewards (Huang et
al., 2023; Zelikman et al., 2022; Gulcehre et al., 2023; Dong et al.,
2023; Singh et al., 2024). Self-Refine instead revises responses at
inference time, whereas self-rewarding models construct preferences for
training (Madaan et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024).

#### Sources of teacher guidance.

SDFT uses demonstrations for continual learning, and OPSD uses reference
solutions for reasoning (Shenfeld et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026a).
RCSD first trains a rubric generator through a teacher given reference
answers, then supplies its rubrics to the reasoning teacher (Gu et al.,
2026). RGSD provides rubrics to a frozen teacher and trains a
question-only student (Rezaei et al., 2026). Experiential Learning
obtains textual coaching feedback for non-verifiable tasks (Ye et al.,
2026a). Recent analyses find that OPSD’s gains need not depend on the
paired reference solution, because solutions to other problems can
provide comparable teacher context (Ichihara et al., 2026; Shrestha and
Tessier, 2026). These sources of teacher guidance differ from RUSD’s use
of the completed attempt itself as additional teacher context.

#### Attempts, feedback, and reference-free supervision.

SDPO constructs teacher context from textual feedback, or successful
responses when only scalar rewards are available (Hübotter et al.,
2026). H²SD combines verified successful attempts with reference hints
for failures, while PAST uses complete attempts, reference solutions,
and correctness-based routing (Cai et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026).
SD-Zero trains a reviser using binary correctness feedback in
mathematics and coding, then distills its predictions into the generator
(He et al., 2026). Activation-Conditioned Self-Distillation uses
verified outcomes to construct teacher-steering activation contrasts (Lu
et al., 2026). OASIS conditions the teacher on another unverified
attempt and uses final-answer labels (Hossain et al., 2026), and
conditioning the teacher on a sampled correct response can reduce output
diversity (Nicolicioiu et al., 2026). Without supplied solutions, Canon
and u-OPSD condition the teacher on a self-generated solution that
reaches the majority answer over multiple samples; CoDA additionally
penalizes minority trajectories (Gkountouras et al., 2026; Li et al.,
2026; Guo et al., 2026). NSD suppresses tokens favored by negative
conditions generated from its own responses (Pei et al., 2026). Unlike
these methods, RUSD uses the completed attempt itself as additional
teacher context, without correctness feedback, a reference solution, or
answer consensus, and weights that attempt by judged reliability and
student uncertainty.

#### Confidence and uncertainty.

Confidence and uncertainty guide pseudo-label selection and weighting
(Chen et al., 2023; Mukherjee and Awadallah, 2020). LLKD’s weighted
variant adds normalized teacher confidence and student uncertainty to
scale selected classification losses (Li et al., 2025). For reasoning,
EGRSD attenuates token updates using teacher entropy, while COSE weights
PPO updates and prioritizes replay using evaluator confidence (Ke et
al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026). PW-OPSD weights reference-conditioned
distillation by token position, motivated by diagnostics of
teacher-token reliability (Liu et al., 2026). Other approaches optimize
predictive confidence through entropy minimization or confidence rewards
(Agarwal et al., 2025; Prabhudesai et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026b).
RUSD distinguishes confidence in judging an attempt’s quality from
uncertainty in predicting its tokens; evaluator bias and calibration
remain relevant to interpreting both signals (Zheng et al., 2023;
Panickssery et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2017).

#### Scope of comparison.

Our experiments compare representative supervision strategies on fixed
NaturalReasoning questions. RGSD shares OPSD’s teacher-conditioning
principle, using rubrics in place of reference solutions (Rezaei et al.,
2026). We include OPSD with NaturalReasoning’s Llama-generated responses
as teacher context and rubric-conditioned RCSD as representative
baselines, without separately evaluating RGSD. Verification-based
methods require correctness feedback, while answer consensus requires
rules for matching free-form answers. LLKD selects and weights
pseudo-labels in a fixed classification label space, and COSE jointly
generates tasks and updates its agents. Entropy-minimization and
self-certainty methods also require only questions (Agarwal et al.,
2025; Zhao et al., 2026b); because student entropy decreases during RUSD
training, they are a natural control that we leave to future comparison.
Other demonstration-, rubric-, feedback-, and uncertainty-based methods
remain applicable or adaptable alternatives outside the present
comparison.

#### Teacher and judge.

Generation, teacher prediction, and student prediction use the same
current LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022). Gradients through student
predictions update these shared adapters, so the teacher also uses the
updated parameters on the next step. We refer to this as a dynamic
teacher; a fixed teacher retains the initial parameters. The judge uses
the initial backbone with adapters disabled and reads the five
score-token logits after its prompt, without generating text. For each
update, teacher predictions and response weights are computed without
gradient tracking; generation and judging are also outside the
differentiated loss.

#### Judged reliability example.

A judge that splits its probability between ratings 4 and 5 as 0.44/0.56
assigns score 5 ($`\bar{s}_{i}=1`$) but has confidence of only about
0.57, so $`r_{i}^{\mathrm{J}}\approx 0.57`$; a decisive rating of 5
gives reliability close to 1.

#### Prediction settings.

The generation notation in Equation 1 denotes one attempt from
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ using the temperature and top-$`p`$/top-$`k`$ settings
in Table 5. Student and teacher predictions in Equation 2 instead use a
full-vocabulary softmax with logits divided by the distillation
temperature. Student entropy uses this same full-vocabulary
distribution, without the generation filters. The judge’s scoring logits
in Equation 3 are its five rating-token logits divided by the judge
temperature, which is 1.0 in the main configuration.

#### Loss implementation.

We use the OPSD implementation (Zhao et al., 2026a), which caps each
teacher-weighted log-probability-ratio contribution at $`\kappa=0.05`$
before summing over vocabulary coordinates. This is a coordinate-clipped
surrogate of the forward-KL objective in Equation 9, rather than exact
KL; the cap can change the update direction (Hossain et al., 2026; Huang
et al., 2026). Distillation and student entropy use the full output
vocabulary without generation sampling filters.

#### Masks and reduction.

The implementation excludes prompt and padding tokens from entropy and
loss calculations. Equations 7 and 9 sum over the unpadded response
positions of retained responses. Responses that reach the 8,192-token
generation limit are excluded from distillation and receive zero weight.
A response with no valid tokens contributes no loss and has
$`u^{\mathrm{S}}_{i}=0`$. The main configuration uses one question per
device microbatch, so the valid-token-normalized loss is the
per-response average in Equation 9. The implementation sums weighted
token losses and divides by the microbatch’s valid-token count before
gradient accumulation and distributed averaging. With larger
microbatches, this is token-count weighting rather than an equal average
of response means. Algorithm 1 presents the per-question computation;
optimizer updates follow the accumulation schedule described above. No
gradient passes through generation, judging, teacher predictions, or
weights.

#### Data.

We use the public 1.1M-question release of NaturalReasoning (Yuan et
al., 2025). Each row pairs a question with a reference answer derived
from its source document, when one exists, and a response generated by
Llama3.3-70B-Instruct. All methods we train share one selection: we keep
rows whose reference-answer field is non-empty, shuffle with seed 42,
and take the first 30,000 questions. In RUSD, the reference field is
used only for this selection, and neither dataset answer field is used
in training prompts. The student prompt contains the question and task
instructions; teacher and judge prompts also include the model’s own
completed attempt.

#### GRPO reward.

For GRPO, scalar response-quality rewards are supplied by a frozen
external Qwen3.8-27B judge (Qwen Team, 2026).

#### Training steps for reported results.

At both Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B, the main SFT, RCSD, OPSD, NSD, and RUSD
results are reported after 150 training steps, and GRPO results after
500 training steps. Training steps denote optimizer updates. For RUSD,
the reporting step was fixed at 150 independently of the checkpoint
evaluations. The additional evaluations in Appendix C.2 characterize
performance over training.

#### Evaluation decoding.

We use temperature 1.0, top-$`p=0.95`$, top-$`k=-1`$ (disabled),
min-$`p=0`$, and presence penalty 0. We enable thinking mode and
generate four responses per question. These evaluation settings differ
from the training generation settings in Table 5.

#### Benchmark scoring.

Benchmark-specific automatic scoring compares parsed multiple-choice
answers with annotated options and extracted mathematical answers with
references using numerical or symbolic comparison. ZebraLogic requires
every grid assignment to be correct. These scores use neither the
training judge nor an external LLM judge.

#### Training-dynamics summaries and intervals.

Figures 2(b–c) and 3 use the same reported summaries of completed
score-4/5 responses. Figure 2(a) uses the corresponding saved summaries
at steps 100, 200, and 300, including all completed responses with
scores 1–5. For Figure 2(b), the denominator is all responses with the
corresponding judge score, and the numerator counts high-confidence
judgments. For Figure 3(b), $`n`$ is the judge-group response count and
$`k`$ counts responses with student entropy below the pooled mean of the
corresponding summary. Both proportion plots use 95% Wilson confidence
intervals with $`z=1.96`$. For example, the score-5/high-confidence
group at step 100 has $`k=231`$ and $`n=358`$, giving 64.5% and an
interval of 59.4–69.3%; at step 300, $`463/638`$ gives 72.6% with an
interval of 69.0–75.9%. These intervals describe response proportions,
rather than variation across independent training runs. The band in
Figure 2(c) instead spans the 25th–75th percentiles of response entropy;
it is not a confidence interval.

#### External quality audit.

We randomly sample 200 saved question–response pairs and ask gpt-5.6sol
(OpenAI, 2026) to act as an external judge using the same five-level
quality rubric in Table 4. Each of the 200 questions contributes one
response and one observation. The three regressions include an intercept
and predict the external GPT rating from the training judge’s score,
confidence, or both. We report descriptive in-sample fit and coefficient
estimates. The multiple correlation coefficient $`R=\sqrt{R^{2}}`$ is
the correlation between fitted and observed GPT ratings. Its square,
$`R^{2}`$, is the fraction of external-rating variation explained within
the fitted sample. $`R`$ is nonnegative; the signs of the regression
coefficients give the direction of the fitted conditional associations.
The joint coefficients are $`b_{\mathrm{score}}=0.84`$ per rating level
and $`b_{\mathrm{confidence}}=3.78`$. Thus, a 0.1 increase in confidence
is associated with a 0.378 increase in the external rating at the same
judge score; an increase of 1 in the judge score is associated with a
0.84 increase in the rating at the same confidence. These estimates
describe response-quality ratings, rather than verified correctness or
calibrated probabilities.

#### Checking both solutions.

The solid must first reach its melting point and then undergo the phase
change. Heating requires
$`mc\Delta T=2.5\times 390\times(90-40)=48{,}750`$ J. Melting requires
$`mL_{f}=2.5\times 4{,}000=10{,}000`$ J. The total is therefore
$`58{,}750`$ J, matching the supplied reference answer. Both saved final
responses explicitly apply these formulas and obtain these values. A
temperature difference of $`50\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}`$ equals $`50`$ K, so
their temperature conversion is also correct. This check concerns the
saved final explanations shown in the case record.

#### What changes when correctness is retained?

The judge score remains 5, while the rating distribution becomes more
concentrated: the probabilities assigned to scores 4 and 5 change from
approximately 0.438/0.562 to 0.119/0.879. Judge confidence consequently
rises from 0.572 to 0.771. Meanwhile, mean normalized student entropy
falls from 0.1728 to 0.0388, approximately a 4.5-fold reduction. The
later response preserves the same correct reasoning with less predictive
uncertainty; it also states the energy unit next to its final boxed
value. The fixed score thus conceals two changes with different effects
on the additive weight: greater judged reliability raises its
reliability contribution, while lower student uncertainty reduces its
uncertainty contribution. This is the response-level pattern motivating
learning from a correct attempt whose generation and assessment can
still become more certain.

#### Selection and interpretation.

The case was selected post hoc through a non-blinded review of repeated
training observations. Reference answers were used for this selection
and the subsequent correctness check, not for training supervision. The
displayed case was chosen after checking its full saved final
explanations for correctness at both observations and requiring an
unchanged judge score.

The case illustrates retained correctness with increasing certainty; it
neither identifies an initial random guess nor isolates the cause of the
change.

# Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents

Tianqiang Yan Affiliation: Faculty of Information Technology, Monash
University, Melbourne, Australia^(∗)Corresponding author:
lizhen.qu@monash.edu    Lizhen Qu Affiliation: Faculty of Information
Technology, Monash University, Melbourne, Australia^(∗)Corresponding
author: lizhen.qu@monash.edu

###### Abstract

Understanding language-agent behaviour requires learning how actions are
organised across a trajectory and how this organisation relates to
context and outcomes. We propose a general framework that connects
data-driven behavioural discovery with analysis and experimental
investigation. Categories developed from recorded activity define what
is measured. Analyses preserve the behaviours’ positions in the process
and express their relationships as interpretable hypotheses. Statistical
assessment and tests in held-out contexts examine the strength and scope
of the evidence. Experiments, including prompt steering, then test how
behaviour and outcomes change under deliberately varied conditions. A
study of iterative self-reflection illustrates how discovery, dependency
learning and prompt comparisons can inform one another. We develop this
approach for trajectories of varying length, event order and agent
roles, and identify advances in coding, analysis and experimentation
needed to support its wider use.

Language agents plan, call tools, revise intermediate results and
coordinate with other
agents^([87](#bib.bib85), [55](#bib.bib58), [72](#bib.bib59), [92](#bib.bib86)).
These activities leave trajectories: recorded sequences of messages,
actions and observations. An agent may repeat an invalid tool call after
corrective feedback; a revision may retain the error identified in the
preceding critique. Understanding such behaviour requires learning how
actions are organised across a trajectory and how that organisation
relates to outcomes. This aim belongs to the broader study of machine
behaviour^([67](#bib.bib1), [16](#bib.bib4), [14](#bib.bib5), [35](#bib.bib6))
and its implications for human and social
systems^([10](#bib.bib2), [78](#bib.bib3), [13](#bib.bib87), [42](#bib.bib79)).

Task evaluation provides a starting point for this inquiry. Benchmarks
assess
performance^([52](#bib.bib39), [36](#bib.bib40), [86](#bib.bib41), [46](#bib.bib43)),
and AgentBoard tracks progress towards task
completion^([54](#bib.bib91)). Broader evaluation frameworks relate task
demands to model capabilities^([91](#bib.bib9)). Behavioural studies
test specified actions such as self-correction, verification and
backtracking^([32](#bib.bib60), [40](#bib.bib61), [23](#bib.bib36));
MAST develops failure categories from interaction traces and
investigates targeted changes^([12](#bib.bib44)). An account of this
process connects these measurements, interpreting each action in
relation to preceding events and subsequent responses.

Such an account begins with identifying what to observe. Developing
behavioural categories from recorded activity allows recurring patterns
and unexpected responses to shape the analysis. Inductive coding offers
one approach to developing these categories, or
_codes_^([25](#bib.bib73), [70](#bib.bib72)). Explicit definitions
locate instances in a trajectory; their positions distinguish checking
before an action from checking after an error. The resulting
measurements retain the behavioural meanings needed to interpret
relationships across the process.

We propose a general framework that connects behavioural discovery,
analysis of trajectories and experimental investigation
(Fig. [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")).
It draws on the behavioural sciences’ emphasis on observable activity
and
context^([77](#bib.bib11), [2](#bib.bib16), [6](#bib.bib12), [34](#bib.bib13), [28](#bib.bib14), [18](#bib.bib15)).
Behavioural definitions connect the stages: the activity identified in a
record is also the object of analysis and experimental measurement.
Statistical assessment quantifies evidence for learned relationships;
testing in held-out contexts examines how far they extend. These
relationships guide experiments that vary instructions or interaction
conditions and measure the resulting behaviours and outcomes. Analytical
methods follow the behavioural question. Together, the stages turn
recorded processes into interpretable accounts of behaviour that can be
tested and refined.

Our work on ReBeCA^([84](#bib.bib90)) provides a concrete illustration
in iterative self-reflection. Behavioural descriptions proposed from
trajectories and consolidated by human review became variables indexed
by round. Causal discovery modelled their statistical
dependencies^([74](#bib.bib63), [17](#bib.bib64), [3](#bib.bib71)),
identifying small behavioural sets that predicted outcomes better than
models using all recorded variables. Their explicit meanings allowed the
findings to inform single and combined instructions. Results varied
across models and prompt combinations, illustrating the need to examine
responses to instruction changes directly^([60](#bib.bib62)). ReBeCA
thus connects behavioural discovery to interpretable hypotheses and
experimental comparisons. We develop this logic for trajectories whose
length, event order and agent roles vary.

## Learning behavioural variables from trajectories

A behavioural account begins with categories that reflect the activity
being studied. An _episode_ is one run of an agent on a problem; its
trajectory records that run’s messages, actions and observations. In
ReBeCA, a language model proposed candidate behavioural descriptions,
which human review consolidated^([84](#bib.bib90)). More systematic
category development can draw on inductive
coding^([25](#bib.bib73), [70](#bib.bib72)) and language-model-assisted
approaches^([93](#bib.bib56), [90](#bib.bib52)). The aim is to let
patterns in the records shape what enters the analysis.

The range of those records matters. Sampling diverse trajectories,
including unusual responses, broadens the activity from which categories
can emerge. Withholding outcomes during category development separates
the identification of behaviour from its subsequent association with
success. Comparing candidate categories against supporting and
contrasting passages helps refine definitions, consolidate overlaps and
uncover omissions. Separate development records provide a further test
of coverage. Once the rules are fixed, independent trajectories allow
assessment of per-category errors and human disagreement. These
comparisons make the influence of sampling and interpretation open to
inspection.

Category development leads to a measurement problem: the same
behavioural description can refer to different activity depending on its
evidence and context. Ethograms similarly define observable units of
behaviour^([77](#bib.bib11), [2](#bib.bib16), [83](#bib.bib20), [19](#bib.bib18), [64](#bib.bib19)).
For agents, preserving that meaning requires connecting each coded
occurrence to the record, its occasion, its position and the actors
involved
(Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Learning behavioural variables from trajectories ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")).

Figure 2: Measuring behaviour in context. a, An illustrative coding

evidence. c, A binary absence code merges an error without recovery with
an episode without an error. Opportunity-aware coding uses $`\bot`$ for

d, The same triggering event can occur at different ordinal steps.
Event-aligned windows compare the corresponding occasions rather than a
fixed step number. All examples are schematic.

The evidence establishes what a code measures. “I will verify the
result”, a verification call, a subsequent revision and a correct final
answer are distinct observations. Studies of verbal reasoning show why
its correspondence with the model’s computations warrants
assessment^([79](#bib.bib30), [47](#bib.bib31), [15](#bib.bib32), [5](#bib.bib35), [49](#bib.bib80)).
A codebook can distinguish statements, executed actions and recorded
state changes, and use counts, duration or latency where these answer
the behavioural question
(Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Learning behavioural variables from trajectories ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")b).

An occurrence also has meaning relative to the opportunity for it.
Recovery after a tool error requires an error; responding to an
objection requires an objection. Distinguishing a response, absence
despite an opportunity and no opportunity preserves this context. Shared
opportunities can themselves induce dependencies. Representing the
opportunity alongside the response allows both to be studied;
restricting analysis to episodes where it arose instead concerns
behaviour given that opportunity
(Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Learning behavioural variables from trajectories ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")c).

The same need for context extends to temporal position. Fixed reflection
rounds provide common positions, whereas the fifth message of an
open-ended episode can serve different purposes across runs. Windows
anchored to a tool error, objection or hand-over offer a basis for
comparison. Their interpretation depends on how absent and repeated
events, overlapping windows and asynchronous actions are represented.
Anchoring at the first occurrence gives one window per episode and
confines the analysis to that occasion
(Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Learning behavioural variables from trajectories ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")d).

Within such a window, the trigger and the actor’s role further specify
the behaviour. A timeout and an invalid argument can call for different
responses. An objection from a reviewer to a worker differs from one
raised by a worker to a planner. Recording these distinctions, tool
returns and external state lets a category retain the context in which
an action occurred
(Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Learning behavioural variables from trajectories ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")a).
Together, these choices turn descriptions into measurements of activity
within a process.

The generation conditions give those measurements their empirical scope.
With model parameters fixed and memory and external state reset,
repeated episodes can be generated without learning from previous runs.
Where the relevant state can be restored, alternative continuations can
begin from the same history^([9](#bib.bib37), [47](#bib.bib31)). Such
comparisons depend on access to the agent’s state and environment.
Expectations of training or evaluation can also change
behaviour^([27](#bib.bib46), [58](#bib.bib47)). Recording these
conditions makes it possible to relate learned patterns to the
circumstances that produced them.

## Learning behavioural structure across a trajectory

These measurements allow analysis to move from identifying behaviours to
learning how they relate. A tool error may precede a retry, a change of
tool or a revised plan; checking and revision may recur together.
Indexing observations by round or event preserves where these relations
arise. The behavioural question determines the analysis. Joint
modelling, for example, examines which relationships remain informative
given other recorded variables.

This conditional view can change the interpretation of an outcome
association. Information carried by an early behaviour may already be
represented by a later revision. Conditioning on the revision can then
remove the earlier variable’s additional predictive contribution.
ReBeCA’s _pivotal behaviour identification_ uses this logic to seek
variables retaining outcome information given the other recorded
variables^([62](#bib.bib81), [63](#bib.bib82)). In its translation
analysis, 11 of 15 code-derived variables were individually associated
with the COMET outcome at an unadjusted $`P<0.05`$, while the selected
set contained one such variable^([84](#bib.bib90)). The contrast
illustrates how marginal association and conditional dependence answer
different behavioural questions.

ReBeCA implements this analysis through causal discovery. Its Greedy
Equivalence Search (GES) scores candidate graphs by data fit and model
complexity^([17](#bib.bib64)). BIC, for example, requires an improvement
in fit sufficient to offset additional parameters. ReBeCA compares AIC,
BIC and two Bayesian scores, while excluding backward-round edges and
outgoing edges from the final outcome^([84](#bib.bib90)). Temporal
restrictions encode background knowledge about admissible
order^([3](#bib.bib71)). Joint conditioning, complexity control and
process constraints offer possible safeguards against redundant or
spurious associations. Their interpretation rests on the recorded
variables and modelling assumptions; independent assessment examines the
resulting findings.

What can be learned also depends on how the process is represented.
Process mining and causal discovery applied to agent–tool invocation
records model workflow dependencies^([22](#bib.bib93)). CAMO combines
computable factors from agent simulations with conditional structure
learning and controlled simulator studies^([88](#bib.bib84)). Invocation
types, run-level factors and behaviours indexed by round or event
describe different aspects of activity. Choosing among them follows the
question and the behavioural meanings the analysis needs to preserve.

The outcome definition makes a further difference. Variables informative
about final correctness may differ from those informative about recovery
time or constraint violations; adding a revision code may change which
earlier behaviours retain outcome information. Examining sensitivity to
these choices shows how much a finding depends on its representation. A
relationship with explicit behavioural meaning can then become a
hypothesis for further study, including experiments that examine
responses to instructions informed by that relationship.

## Interpretability from measurement to experiment

Interpreting a learned relationship means relating it to identifiable
activity. A conclusion specifies which behaviours are related, when,
under what conditions and with what supporting evidence. If early
checking and later revision jointly predict success, researchers should
be able to locate both in the records and formulate a hypothesis about
their relationship. Their meanings connect the statistical finding to
activity that can be examined experimentally.

The same connection can make behavioural and internal analyses
informative to one another. Internal analyses relate representations and
computations to model
outputs^([31](#bib.bib88), [8](#bib.bib89), [61](#bib.bib22), [76](#bib.bib23), [50](#bib.bib24), [71](#bib.bib27));
high-level models of internal computation also offer an approach to
interpretability^([24](#bib.bib25)). Relating these computations to
defined behaviours would connect model mechanisms to the processes
observed across a task. Neuroscientists have similarly advocated
detailed behavioural analysis to inform mechanistic
interpretation^([45](#bib.bib17), [37](#bib.bib21)).

Following a hypothesis through analysis and experiment requires
distinguishing what each comparison establishes
(Box [3](#S3 "Interpretability from measurement to experiment ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")).
In ReBeCA, marginal and conditional associations describe different
aspects of the recorded relationships, while prediction assesses their
information on new episodes. Instruction comparisons examine what
happens when prompts change. Measuring behaviour under those conditions
connects the experimental result to the hypothesis that motivated it.
Supplementary Note 1 defines the corresponding quantities.

## Testing behavioural hypotheses

A learned relationship concerns the conditions in which its trajectories
were collected. Significance tests, effect estimates and uncertainty
intervals quantify evidence within that setting. For predictive
analyses, comparison with simpler models shows what the selected
behaviours add. ReBeCA uses cross-validated log-likelihood to compare
selected sets with an outcome-only model and a model using all recorded
variables (Supplementary Note 2)^([73](#bib.bib67)). Separating
selection from final assessment, through independent data or nested
resampling, protects this comparison from the optimism introduced by
choosing a candidate on its evaluation data.

Evidence within a setting raises the question of how far the
relationship extends. Resampling examines whether selected variables
recur across samples^([59](#bib.bib65)); varying coding choices and
scoring rules tests sensitivity to the analysis. Assessment in contexts
excluded from discovery, fitting and model selection examines
generalisation across tasks, environments or generation
conditions^([66](#bib.bib66)). Random data splits address variation
within the sampled setting. These assessments give a behavioural
hypothesis an empirical scope that can guide its next test.

Experiments extend this inquiry by deliberately varying the conditions
under which behaviour occurs. Explicit behavioural definitions make
prompt steering one practical approach: a hypothesis informs an
instruction, and the resulting trajectories show how the agent responds.
Factorial designs compare single and combined instructions on new test
problems, using the same problems and models in each
condition^([21](#bib.bib68)). Where state restoration is possible,
continuations from the same history hold the preceding interaction
fixed^([9](#bib.bib37), [47](#bib.bib31)). The comparisons follow the
logic of interventional research^([20](#bib.bib69)); their estimates
concern the tested prompt conditions and account for uncertainty,
repeated observations and multiple comparisons.

Behavioural measurement is what connects these estimates back to the
hypothesis. An instruction can change other behaviours, response length
or cost alongside its intended target. Recoding selected and unselected
behaviours identifies these accompanying changes. For an inconclusive
performance contrast, the records help establish whether the target was
elicited. For an improvement, they help identify changes for further
tests. This evidence informs the next prompts, controls and hypotheses,
allowing observation and experiment to refine one another.

## Lessons from the ReBeCA prototype

ReBeCA^([84](#bib.bib90)) brings these stages together in a fixed-round
implementation of Self-Refine^([55](#bib.bib58)), in which models
critique and revise their own answers. Four Qwen3 models with 0.6–8
billion parameters^([85](#bib.bib78)) completed 417 mathematics episodes
and 400 translation episodes, each with five
rounds^([30](#bib.bib77), [4](#bib.bib53)). At each step, the most
central response (medoid) in the largest semantic cluster among 20
sampled responses was retained. Outcomes were final-answer correctness
for mathematics and change in translation quality under two model-based
metrics^([68](#bib.bib54), [39](#bib.bib55)).

The trajectories expose a distinction between revision and improvement.
In the 399 fully graded mathematics episodes, text changed in 77.7–100%
of episodes per round, while correctness flipped in only 1.3–1.8%;
initial and final accuracy were 75.2% and 76.4%. Learning what the
revisions involved required behavioural measurements. Four behaviour
codes proposed with outcomes withheld and consolidated by human review,
together with two uncertainty indicators per round, yielded 30 variables
per episode. The automated coder agreed with the majority label of three
human coders on 89 of 100 instances. Fixed rounds supplied common
positions and opportunities for critique
(Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Lessons from the ReBeCA prototype ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")).

These measurements yielded small sets of outcome-relevant behaviours.
The mathematics set contained clarity and organization at round two
($`\mathrm{CO}_{2}`$) and specificity at round four
($`\mathrm{Sp}_{4}`$); the translation set contained clarity and
organization at round three ($`\mathrm{CO}_{3}`$). Both sets achieved
better cross-validated predictive fit than the outcome-only and
full-variable models
(Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Lessons from the ReBeCA prototype ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")c).
Conditional associations of $`\mathrm{CO}_{2}`$ and $`\mathrm{Sp}_{4}`$
with success averaged 29.8 and 16.0 percentage points across 20 disjoint
subsets
(Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Lessons from the ReBeCA prototype ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")e).
Comparisons across four groups of subsets yielded no detectable
differences; cross-environment transfer remains untested. Selection and
comparison reused the same cross-validation, and associations were
estimated from the selection data. The findings therefore identify
candidates for further tests.

The selected dimensions then informed instructions targeting
$`\mathrm{CO}_{2}`$, $`\mathrm{Sp}_{4}`$ or both on 50 AIME problems,
using the same four models. Pooled correct-answer counts were 59 of 200
at baseline, 64 and 62 for the single instructions, and 49 for their
combination. Baseline comparisons were non-significant after Holm
correction across all 24 model-specific pairwise tests (Supplementary
Note 3). The model-specific counts reveal differing responses
(Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Lessons from the ReBeCA prototype ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")d):
the specificity instruction solved 33 of 50 problems in the 4B model,
against 21 at baseline, but 18 in the 8B model, against 24. In the 4B
model, specificity alone outperformed the combined instruction (33
versus 19 correct). It solved 15 problems that the combined instruction
missed; the reverse occurred on one problem (exact McNemar test,
Holm-adjusted $`P=0.012`$). The pooled interaction estimate was $`-9`$
percentage points, with a problem-clustered 95% bootstrap interval of
$`-19`$ to $`1`$. These contrasts motivate further tests of the
combination; the pooled interval remains compatible with zero
interaction
(Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Lessons from the ReBeCA prototype ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")f).

Interpreting these responses returns the analysis to measurement. The
instructions restricted reflection to selected dimensions rather than
simply adding the named behaviours. The coder saw reference outputs,
agreement was pooled across codes, and each code combined the dimension
evaluated in feedback with the dimension changed in revision. Medoid
selection favoured common responses over rare ones. Recoding the
steering trajectories, distinguishing feedback from revision and
comparing alternative instructions for the same behaviours would clarify
the findings. Transfer to other model families and less constrained
tasks remains open. The prototype thus connects discovery with
experiments while exposing the measurement and evaluation questions that
a broader framework must address.

## Extending the framework to open-ended behaviour

The broader task is to preserve this connection as the organisation of
activity changes. Planning, tool use and collaboration involve sequences
of varying length and order
(Table [1](#S6.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Extending the framework to open-ended behaviour ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")).
Repeated events, absent opportunities and actions involving several
agents change what constitutes a comparable observation. Tool returns
and external state help establish what happened^([86](#bib.bib41)); an
initiator, recipient, executor and verifier may each contribute to a
collaborative action^([92](#bib.bib86), [12](#bib.bib44)). Preserving
these distinctions makes relationships across agents and events
interpretable.

| Behaviour                                          | Observations to distinguish                                                  | Opportunities                                 | Relations to assess                                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Planning^([87](#bib.bib85))                        | Stated plan; precondition check; revised plan; executed step                 | New information; failed step                  | Replanning after new information; correspondence between stated plans and subsequent actions |
| Tool use^([86](#bib.bib41))                        | Tool choice; arguments; returned output; error response; state verification  | Timeout; invalid argument; refused permission | Feedback preceding retries, tool changes and replanning; associations with recovery and cost |
| Self-reflection^([84](#bib.bib90))                 | Identified problem; proposed change; implemented change; renewed check       | Critique; new evidence                        | Correspondence between critiques and revisions; variation across reflection rounds           |
| Collaboration^([92](#bib.bib86), [12](#bib.bib44)) | Work assignment; evidence request; objection; hand-over; shared-state update | Role-specific messages; transferred work      | Interaction patterns associated with coordination, omission and duplicated work              |

For example, recovery after errors can be studied through the frequency
of replanning after a first error and after repeated errors. Joint
analysis can examine the relation given other recorded variables, and
prompt comparisons can test instructions intended to change the
response. Tracking replanning in both studies connects the observational
finding to the experimental comparison. Recurrent and asynchronous
events may call for sequence or event-based models that preserve the
relevant behavioural meanings.

Where explicit symbolic actions and states are available, the process
can also inform the analysis through learned action rules. A recommended
extension of ReBeCA would combine Safe Action Model learning with
causal-discovery-based structural dependency learning. Safe Action Model
learning derives action preconditions and effects from observed
transitions^([38](#bib.bib92)). Within its planning model, next-state
predictions are guaranteed correct for actions satisfying the learned
preconditions. Mapping these rules to behavioural variables could
constrain candidate dependencies before graph search. Hard exclusions
require a justified impossibility rule; uncertain support can guide
preferences among structures. Comparing rule-guided and unguided models
on independent trajectories would assess the value of this extension.

## Building cumulative knowledge of agent behaviour

Extending the framework across settings also creates an opportunity to
connect findings. A relationship observed in one task becomes more
informative when its behavioural meaning can be assessed in others. Such
comparison depends on shared understanding of what was measured, how the
trajectories arose and which evidence supported the conclusion
(Box [7](#S7 "Building cumulative knowledge of agent behaviour ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")).

Behavioural definitions provide the first connection across
studies^([77](#bib.bib11), [2](#bib.bib16), [70](#bib.bib72)).
Vocabularies induced from large conversation collections offer starting
points^([75](#bib.bib50), [33](#bib.bib51)), while cross-model coding
studies can examine where definitions transfer. Per-code prevalence,
human disagreement and error types reveal where measurements need
revision; automated coders also warrant checks for judge
biases^([89](#bib.bib57)). Complete trajectory records let researchers
revisit these judgements. Releasing them alongside
scores^([11](#bib.bib8), [41](#bib.bib42)), with tool returns, state
changes, generation conditions and sampling rules, makes alternative
analyses possible, subject to privacy and access constraints. Rare and
exceptional events broaden the activity available for discovery.

This basis for comparison could connect behavioural questions now
studied for different purposes. Codes that predict improvement under
reinforcement learning^([23](#bib.bib36)) may also inform hypotheses
about how behaviour changes during learning. Documented coordination
failures suggest targets for experiments^([12](#bib.bib44)). Safety
studies generate evaluations^([65](#bib.bib49)) and elicit deception,
sandbagging and insider-threat
behaviour^([58](#bib.bib47), [53](#bib.bib45), [57](#bib.bib48));
behavioural measurement can relate such findings to the recorded
process. Verbal reasoning also offers a monitoring signal whose
reliability requires assessment^([7](#bib.bib33), [44](#bib.bib34)).
Internal interventions provide another way to examine
hypotheses^([69](#bib.bib26), [80](#bib.bib38), [24](#bib.bib25)), with
appropriate controls informing their
interpretation^([1](#bib.bib29), [56](#bib.bib28)).

Experiments can in turn improve the behavioural account from which they
began. Single and combined prompts, graded instructions, repeated
continuations and internal manipulations probe different aspects of a
hypothesis^([69](#bib.bib26), [80](#bib.bib38)). Measuring targeted
behaviours and accompanying changes connects their results. For
graphical models, experimental observations can inform further structure
learning when the manipulations and their targets are
understood^([29](#bib.bib70)). Comparing prompt formulations and
evaluation contexts examines how far findings
extend^([27](#bib.bib46), [58](#bib.bib47)). Stating which observations
support, revise or reject a hypothesis, and which populations the
estimates describe, makes successful and unsuccessful tests informative
to subsequent studies^([11](#bib.bib8), [82](#bib.bib10)).

Extending this cycle brings new research questions. A category developed
in one model or language may need revision in another; comparing its
instances can reveal which behavioural distinctions persist. Recurring
and asynchronous actions raise a related problem of representing
interactions while preserving their meanings. Experimental findings can
inform these choices, and adaptive designs could select tests that
distinguish competing hypotheses. Automated discovery and
experimentation^([43](#bib.bib74), [81](#bib.bib75), [26](#bib.bib76), [51](#bib.bib83), [48](#bib.bib7), [88](#bib.bib84))
could help sustain this exchange between behavioural observation and
model refinement.

## Outlook

The framework connects discovery, analysis and experiment through
explicit behavioural meanings, allowing findings to be tested and
refined across contexts. ReBeCA illustrates one implementation in
self-reflection. Its broader promise is an empirical account of how
agents respond to information, coordinate their actions and reach
outcomes.

## Supplementary Information

### Supplementary Note 1 $`\mid`$ Behavioural variables and comparison quantities

The definitions below illustrate the statistical comparisons used in the
Perspective. An agent with fixed parameters $`\theta`$ generates an
episode $`\tau`$ on a problem $`x`$ under conditions $`c`$, including
its instructions, tools, decoding and stopping rule. A codebook gives
each behavioural category a definition and a rule linking it to the
trajectory record. In a fixed-round study, indexing the codes and
numerical indicators by round yields a finite set of variables $`V`$.
Event-anchored studies would require a corresponding rule for recurring
or absent events. A code may indicate presence, frequency or duration.
Where opportunity matters, it can distinguish absence despite an
opportunity from no opportunity.

Let $`Y(x,\tau)`$ be an outcome defined independently of the codebook,
and $`P_{c}`$ the joint distribution of $`(V,Y)`$ under the specified
conditions. An instruction change $`\eta`$ produces conditions
$`c^{\eta}`$, and $`Q`$ is a prespecified distribution of test problems.

Observational and steering quantities. The association $`A(v)`$ compares
outcomes when a binary behaviour $`v`$ is present or absent. Conditional
dependence $`D(v)`$ measures the information that $`v`$ adds given the
other variables. The performance contrast $`\Delta(\eta)`$ compares
expected outcomes under the original and modified instructions. The
interaction $`\iota`$ measures departure from additivity when two
instruction changes are combined:

|     |                                            |                                                                                                                                                                           |     |      |
| --- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle A(v)`$                     | $`\displaystyle=P_{c}(Y{=}1\mid v{=}1)-P_{c}(Y{=}1\mid v{=}0),`$                                                                                                          |     | (S1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle D(v)`$                     | $`\displaystyle=I\big(Y;\,v\mid V\setminus\{v\}\big),`$                                                                                                                   |     |      |
|     | $`\displaystyle\Delta(\eta)`$              | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,c^{\eta})}Y(x,\tau)-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim P_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,c)}Y(x,\tau)\Big],`$ |     |      |
|     | $`\displaystyle\iota(\eta,\eta^{\prime})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Delta(\eta\eta^{\prime})-\Delta(\eta)-\Delta(\eta^{\prime}).`$                                                                                           |     |      |

Here $`I`$ is conditional mutual information and $`\eta\eta^{\prime}`$
denotes the combined instruction. The definition of $`A`$ assumes binary
$`v`$ and $`Y`$; $`D`$ also applies to multivalued variables. Unlike
$`A`$ and $`D`$, which describe observed episodes, $`\Delta`$ and
$`\iota`$ compare experimental conditions. They summarize the
implemented prompt conditions, which may differ in several aspects of
generated behaviour.

These definitions distinguish observational and steering comparisons.
Under strict positivity, variables with $`D>0`$ form the unique Markov
boundary, the minimal set carrying all recorded predictive information
about $`Y`$^([62](#bib.bib81), [63](#bib.bib82)). Finite-sample search
estimates this population target. The contrasts $`\Delta`$ and $`\iota`$
compare instructions on a specified test population. Estimating those
contrasts requires observations under the corresponding prompt
conditions.

### Supplementary Note 2 $`\mid`$ Predictive assessment

For a candidate variable set $`M\subseteq V`$, cross-validated
log-likelihood measures predictive fit on data excluded from model
fitting:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathrm{CVLL}(M)=\frac{1}{F}\sum_{f=1}^{F}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{f}^{\mathrm{test}}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{f}^{\mathrm{test}}}\log\hat{p}_{f}\big(y_{i}\mid M_{i};\,\mathcal{D}_{f}^{\mathrm{train}}\big),
``` |  | (S2) |

where $`F`$ is the number of folds, $`\hat{p}_{f}`$ is fitted on the
data outside test fold $`\mathcal{D}_{f}^{\mathrm{test}}`$, and
$`M_{i}`$ gives the values of the selected variables in episode $`i`$.
The empty set predicts outcomes without behavioural variables; $`V`$
uses all recorded variables. Higher values indicate better predictive
fit under the specified estimator and fold construction. Equal averaging
across folds weights contexts equally when folds represent contexts; it
differs from pooling episode-level log-likelihoods when fold sizes vary.
The selected set’s score requires independent or nested assessment if
the same criterion was used to choose it.

### Supplementary Note 3 $`\mid`$ Verification of supporting summaries

The supplied source archive contains 418 mathematics records: 417 have
the initial answer and five revisions, and one has only five outputs. Of
the 417 complete-output episodes, 399 have non-missing grades at every
position;
Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Lessons from the ReBeCA prototype ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")b
uses this subset throughout. The two translation-metric exports contain
the same 400 trajectories, assessed under two metrics. The plot omits a
legacy boxed-expression comparison, which conflates answer changes with
extraction failures and syntactic differences. The verification output
retains this diagnostic for transparency.

The instruction-test CSV contains 50 distinct problems and binary
outcomes for four models under four conditions. Bootstrap resampling
selects problems with replacement, retaining all model and condition
outcomes within each problem; the same four models are retained in every
resample. The 95% percentile intervals use 20,000 resamples, NumPy’s
default random generator with seed 0 and linear percentile
interpolation. Exact two-sided McNemar tests use the discordant pairs,
with a single Holm correction across the 24 tests formed by six
condition pairs in each of four models. Cochran’s $`Q`$ uses its
asymptotic chi-squared reference with three degrees of freedom, with
Holm correction over four models. Two implementations of the paired
problem-level bootstrap produced the same contrasts and intervals.
Code-level annotations and fitted fold models are absent from this
archive; the CVLL and coefficient summaries in
Fig. [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Lessons from the ReBeCA prototype ‣ Behaviour analysis for large language model-driven agents")c,e
remain attributed to the ReBeCA preprint as reported estimates.
Source-file hashes and numerical verification summaries accompany the
manuscript. Verification scripts are available to editors and reviewers
upon request.
````

# AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning

Simon Tang, Wenjie Shi, Yongjing Yin, Keheng Wang, Jialin Liu,Zheng
Zhang, Linvo Lin, Xiangyuan Xue, Kan Ren, Jingang Wang, Xunliang Cai

###### Abstract

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) is one of the most promising paths
toward an AI singularity for superintelligence. Achieving this vision
requires AI systems to undertake the full training pipeline for
iterative improvement. Reinforcement learning (RL) plays an essential
role in this cycle to improve model capabilities and generalization, yet
automating RL remains difficult due to unstable training dynamics and
the need for sustained supervision. We introduce AutoRL, a multi-agent
system for automating RL training, as a cornerstone for future RSI.
Different from conducting research by end-to-end complete experiments,
AutoRL monitors the evolving training state and integrates process-level
supervision and research into an ongoing RL training, following the
practical workflow of an RL researcher. Specifically, AutoRL consists of
three RL-specific agentic roles. Perceptors track training dynamics,
rollouts, evaluation, and efficiency, triggering deliberation when they
detect anomalies, degradation, or stagnation. Three complementary
Proposers diagnose problems and refine candidate interventions through
shared evidence and checks. The Orchestrator reviews their
recommendation and applies approved changes through online hot updates
or checkpoint-based resumption. Experiments on Qwen3 and
LongCat-Flash-Lite demonstrate the superiority of AutoRL over static RL
recipes and a general agent. On Qwen3-4B, AutoRL outperforms both
baselines across nine benchmarks. In the coding-agent experiment,
automated supervision supports 14.7 days of training with 11 runtime
failure fixes, with pass@1 increasing 12.84% on SWE-related tasks. The
research records show that existing agents can handle unattended RL
supervision and dynamic adjustment, such as hyperparameter tuning,
implementation repair, and reward refinement. The difficulty for future
RSI lies in developing fundamental methods that are valuable for
resource-consuming validation.

## 1 Introduction

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) aims to drive AI systems to iteratively
optimize and evolve themselves ([Chen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib50); [Zhang
et al., 2025a](#bib.bib51)). As these systems become stronger at
developing their successors, RSI could promote an improvement flywheel
for intelligence explosion ([Chan et al., 2026](#bib.bib52)) and an AI
singularity ([Jafari et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)), pointing out a
promising route to superintelligence ([Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)).
Sustaining such self-directed improvement remains difficult, as
identifying useful changes and reliably assessing their effects still
pose substantial challenges ([Chen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib50)). A
practical step pursued by recent work is to place a more powerful
existing model in charge of optimizing another model, delegating
decisions about training and subsequent experiments to AI agents ([Shi
et al., 2026](#bib.bib16); [Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib19); [Zhou et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15)). Within this model-driven optimization process,
reinforcement learning (RL) is an essential technique for improving
model capabilities and a particularly demanding stage to automate.
Learning from reward feedback supports improvements that generalize
beyond the training examples ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib26); [Shao
et al., 2024](#bib.bib25)), making RL central to frontier post-training
recipes ([Kimi Team and others, 2026](#bib.bib27); [DeepSeek-AI and
others, 2026](#bib.bib28)). Yet maintaining a productive RL run requires
responding to a training process with continually dynamic states. As the
policy is optimized, the distribution of generated experience evolves,
which further alters the learning signal used for later updates.
Distributed execution adds numerical and operational failures that can
interrupt this process ([Fu et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)). Even a run that
executes successfully may stagnate under a flawed reward or an
unsuitable recipe. Thus, automating RL requires following the evolving
training state and deciding when and how to revise the current
procedure.

Existing autonomous-training systems largely organize research across
successive experiments, using the results of one attempt to inform the
next recipe ([Fang et al., 2026](#bib.bib1); [Chen et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib2); [He et al., 2026](#bib.bib3); [Zhou et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15)). Differently, human RL researchers also investigate
the experiment while it is running. A change in reward may lead them to
inspect generated responses and repair the grader, while a persistent
plateau can prompt reconsideration of the recipe. They apply supported
adjustments directly or resume from a checkpoint when a modification
requires restarting, then examine the subsequent behavior. Motivated by
human RL research workflow, we focus on integrating this process-level
supervision and research into an ongoing RL experiment. Within a
predefined task and resource budget, the agent system uses the evolving
training state to guide investigation and execution. There are three
practical challenges for process-level autonomous RL.

- •
  RL training requires timely intervention. Failures, numerical
  instability, and performance degradation can emerge while training is
  still running. Waiting until an experiment finishes delays diagnosis
  and can waste substantial computation. Early stopping and live
  modification provide useful intervention mechanisms ([Fang et al.,
  2026](#bib.bib1); [Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)), but sustained
  supervision also requires deciding when to intervene, how to respond,
  and whether the response worked as training continues.
- •
  Monitoring and improvement research operate at different rhythms.
  Monitoring requires frequent checks and prompt responses, whereas
  analyzing a plateau and developing a new training strategy require
  sustained investigation. Handling both in one agent context makes them
  compete for attention, with execution output potentially crowding out
  analysis ([He et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)). A long-running system needs
  to keep watching training while investigating how to improve it.
- •
  Validating RL hypotheses is expensive. A plausible proposal may rest
  on an incorrect diagnosis or contain an implementation error, yet
  testing every proposal in a separate RL run consumes substantial GPU
  resources. Independent review can improve proposal scrutiny ([Du et
  al., 2026](#bib.bib19)), but useful validation also needs to
  distinguish questions that code inspection and existing evidence can
  resolve from those that require further training. Effective validation
  requires making this distinction before committing resources, then
  assessing the intervention through subsequent feedback.

To address these challenges, we propose AutoRL, which organizes the
responsibilities of an expert RL researcher into specialized agent roles
as shown in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 AutoRL ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning").
Four Perceptors maintain complementary views of the running experiment.
They use generated trajectories and evaluation results to interpret
optimization behavior, while tracking execution health. Signals of an
anomaly or stalled progress initiate research by three Proposers. Each
first develops an independent diagnosis before reviewing the others’
proposals. The Tuner explores adjustments to the current recipe, while
the Innovator investigates alternative mechanisms. The Reviewer examines
implementation correctness and challenges unsupported assumptions.
During deliberation, they challenge one another’s causal explanations
using training records and code evidence, performing CPU-only checks to
resolve concrete uncertainties. Proposers then revise, merge, or
withdraw candidates in response to these findings before selecting a
recommendation. An Orchestrator then decides whether and how to apply
the recommendation. It reviews the proposed modification against the
current state and applies approved modifications through a HOT online
update when supported. Otherwise, it coordinates a COLD resumption from
a compatible checkpoint, which also provides a path for repairing and
recovering a failed run. Confirming runtime activation establishes which
recipe governs subsequent observations. RL-specific tools support these
operations, while persistent memory and shared records retain the
evidence behind earlier decisions. Perceptors continue observing the
resulting training behavior and trigger further investigation when
needed for the next loop. This division keeps monitoring active during
research and carries research conclusions into training as it
progresses.

We evaluate AutoRL on mathematical reasoning and agentic coding tasks
with Qwen3 ([Yang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib31)) and
LongCat-Flash-Lite ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib42)). On Qwen3-4B, AutoRL
yields stronger trained models than static RL recipes and supervision by
the native general agent. The advantage holds across all nine
benchmarks, extending beyond mathematics to general knowledge and
coding. Training records show how repairing a flawed recipe and
investigating a plateau lead to renewed gains. During the 14.7-day
coding-agent run, checkpoint-based recovery allows training to continue
after 11 runtime failures. On SWE-related tasks, pass@1 rises from
52.29% to 65.13%, demonstrating AutoRL can obtain continued improvement
on unattended long-term RL experiments across interruptions. Compared
with the general agent, AutoRL achieves these gains while investigating
more broadly and reverting fewer interventions. Preserving more training
progress when applying or withdrawing a modification also allows
subsequent research to build on the model’s accumulated learning.
Meanwhile, in the GPT-driven Qwen3-4B run, 131 initial candidates yield
20 deployed plans, all refining the existing recipe. Three candidates
attempt to redesign the learning rule, yet none reaches deployment. It
suggests the scope and limitations of current agents in AutoRL: they can
monitor the training process and its evolving state, conducting local
refinements and dynamically adjusting the recipe, but struggle to
produce fundamental methodological breakthroughs in our experiments. We
believe that the proposed AutoRL is not the only framework or organizing
form for autonomous RL, but we hope it can become a pioneering agentic
behavior research for developing future RSI.

Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  We present a pioneering study of process-level autonomous RL as a
  crucial component of future RSI, analyzing the challenges of
  sustaining supervision and research within an ongoing RL experiment.
- •
  We introduce AutoRL, a multi-agent framework in which Perceptors
  monitor training and signal the need for research. Proposers discuss
  diagnoses and refine interventions using shared evidence. The
  Orchestrator reviews and applies their recommendations, while
  Perceptors observe the effects to inform subsequent research and
  adjustment.
- •
  Experiments show that AutoRL sustains unattended RL monitoring and
  research, delivering continued gains over static recipes and a general
  agent. Behavioral analysis reveals that current agents can monitor
  training and dynamically adjust recipes, but are limited in delivering
  fundamental methodological breakthroughs in our experiments.

## 2 AutoRL

Figure 2: Framework of AutoRL. Four Perceptors monitor training and
diagnose problems. Three specialized Proposers discuss candidates,

### 2.1 Task and Monitoring Loop

Given an initial model, training data, objectives, a resource budget,
and authorized tools specified by the researcher, AutoRL automates
training supervision and investigates improvements within this task. It
aims to reduce the need for continuous human monitoring and intervention
while improving the model under a fixed evaluation protocol. As shown in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 AutoRL ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning"),
it couples RL training with agent-based supervision. The training
process repeatedly performs rollout generation, reward computation, and
policy updates, with periodic evaluation and checkpointing. Separate
supervisory roles handle monitoring, research, and execution control
alongside the training process. After the Orchestrator launches the main
training run, four Perceptors monitor optimization, rollouts,
evaluation, and efficiency. Signals of anomalies, degradation, or
stagnation trigger three Proposers to independently diagnose the issue,
challenge candidate interventions, and refine their proposals. The
Orchestrator evaluates their recommendation, applies approved changes
through an online hot update or checkpoint-based cold resumption, and
confirms runtime activation. Intervention records inform subsequent
perception and deliberation, which continue alongside training. Stopping
or resumption occurs when needed for an approved change or failure
recovery.

Research decisions adapt the active training run using current evidence
and prior interventions. We denote the $`k`$-th decision as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       a_{k}=\mathcal{F}(E_{k},H_{k}),
       ```                              |     | (1) |

where $`E_{k}`$ contains the training observations and evidence used in
the decision, $`H_{k}`$ denotes retrievable training and intervention
history, and $`a_{k}`$ is the action taken after the Orchestrator’s
review. The procedure $`\mathcal{F}`$ is implemented by the specialized
roles, evidence-based deliberation, and execution controls described
below. The index $`k`$ counts research decisions, while $`(r,s)`$
identifies a training position by its run $`r`$ and discrete step $`s`$.

### 2.2 Specialized Roles and Shared Memory

To alleviate context pressure and concentrate attention on relevant
evidence, AutoRL separates the training lifecycle into management,
continuous perception, and improvement research. Each role operates in
its own context and coordinates through shared evidence and run records:

- •
  Orchestrator: coordination and execution control. The Orchestrator
  manages launch, stopping, resumption, and rollback, schedules
  observations and deliberation, and evaluates recommendations against
  the current training state. It coordinates approved interventions,
  verifies their actual activation, and records the resulting
  intervention and recovery history.
- •
  Perceptors: continuous perception and diagnosis. Four Perceptors
  examine complementary training signals. The Train Perceptor monitors
  gradients, losses, and numerical stability; the Rollout Perceptor
  examines training rewards, sample quality, and generated behavior; the
  Eval Perceptor tracks performance and failure modes under the fixed
  evaluation protocol; and the Efficiency Perceptor monitors progress,
  throughput, and runtime failures. RL-specific tools and procedures
  support each role’s analysis, linking issue signals to the relevant
  run, step, and evidence window.
- •
  Proposers: improvement research and candidate review. Three Proposers
  independently examine the same evidence. The Tuner develops local
  adjustments to existing mechanisms; the Innovator explores alternative
  mechanisms within the task; and the Reviewer traces implementation
  defects and counterexamples. Cross-examination, code inspection, and
  applicable CPU-only checks refine their candidates into a
  recommendation with a concrete modification, expected effects, and
  supporting evidence.

Specialized perception turns training observations into actionable
research questions. Each Perceptor follows a predefined workflow for
collecting fresh evidence, using tools to compute rolling statistics and
trends. These procedures guide diagnosis, while the LLM interprets the
evidence and chooses focused follow-up queries. For example, it checks a
gradient spike against losses and clipping, or examines falling entropy
in the context of training progress to distinguish collapse from
ordinary convergence. Explicit rules cover clear triggers, including
verified NaN/Inf values and a configured streak of evaluations without a
new best score. Before issuing a signal, a Perceptor links the observed
behavior to its run, step, measurement window, and source artifacts. A
synchronous observation barrier gathers the relevant reports and freezes
their signals and initial evidence into a common discussion batch. An
ordinary observation cycle without a signal leaves training to continue.

When a decision depends on a missing fact, the Orchestrator can request
a targeted observation from the appropriate role. Persistent memory
connects these decisions across the training lifecycle. Local histories
retain role-specific observations and experience, while shared records
connect runs, evidence, candidates, checks, and interventions.
Retrieving these records allows agents to reuse prior analysis and check
its relevance to the current state. This division of responsibilities
aligns each role’s context, evidence access, and execution authority.

### 2.3 Evidence-based Deliberation

AutoRL develops intervention proposals by combining independent
diagnosis with evidence-based deliberation. The three Proposers start
from the same initial evidence and examine it from their complementary
perspectives. Each candidate specifies the target problem, a mechanism
hypothesis, a concrete modification, the expected observable effects,
and unresolved questions. Independent initial judgments bring distinct
directions into the discussion, where cross-examination tests their
premises. Deliberation proceeds through proposal generation, critique,
revision, ranking, and voting, for at most five rounds.

During proposal development and revision, Proposers inspect code,
metrics, and historical evidence. They can perform applicable CPU-only
tests or replay stored training trajectories in isolated working
directories. For example, a reward-rule change can be checked against
past misjudgments and previously correct cases, while a filtering change
can be checked for sample-group integrity and replenishment behavior.
Results are shared between rounds and support candidate revision,
withdrawal, or merging. The resulting evidence provides the basis for
ranking the surviving candidates. Each Proposer ranks candidates by
their expected benefit under the fixed evaluation protocol and the
strength of the supporting evidence, considering whether intervention is
needed. Risk and reversibility also matter, together with the cost of
interrupting training and any overlap with other candidates.

The protocol aggregates these individual rankings using Borda scoring.
With $`m`$ surviving candidates, each ranking awards $`m`$ points to the
first choice, decreasing by one to a single point for the last. Scores
are summed across the three Proposers, and at most two candidates
advance, with ties resolved by candidate identifier. Each Proposer then
casts one vote with a rationale, producing a 3:0 or 2:1 recommendation.
A sole finalist still requires three confirmation votes. Deliberation
can end earlier when all three Proposers explicitly endorse the same
eligible candidate in a completed round. In either case, failed checks
or unresolved blockers prevent publication. The recommendation retains
dissenting views and unresolved questions.

The progression from independent proposals to a final recommendation is

|     |                                      |                                                                           |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k}^{0}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\bigcup_{j=1}^{3}\mathcal{G}_{j}(E_{k}^{0},H_{k}),`$      |     | (2) |
|     | $`\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{k}`$     | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{Debate}(\mathcal{C}_{k}^{0},E_{k},H_{k}),`$ |     |     |

where $`E_{k}^{0}`$ is the frozen initial evidence, $`\mathcal{G}_{j}`$
is the proposal procedure of Proposer $`j`$, and $`\mathcal{C}_{k}^{0}`$
collects their initial candidates. Evidence gathering and CPU-only tests
augment $`E_{k}`$ and inform candidate revision and selection. The
Orchestrator reviews $`\widehat{a}_{k}`$ and its evidence to determine
$`a_{k}`$, which may apply a modification or leave the recipe unchanged.
Inexpensive checks address logic and implementation issues that can be
tested in advance, while subsequent training feedback assesses the
remaining questions. This integrates candidate development and
validation within deliberation and directs GPU training resources toward
approved interventions in the main run.

### 2.4 Process-Level Intervention

The Orchestrator reviews the evidence supporting a recommendation and
approves, defers, rejects, or requests further investigation. Before
approving a change, it checks that the expected effect can be assessed
against a pre-intervention reference and identifies the evidence needed
for the next review. Interventions can change rewards, losses, advantage
computation, or data selection, and can repair implementation defects.
The task configuration defines the permitted scope, while the evaluation
protocol remains fixed. Researchers retain control over changes to the
task objectives, access to additional data sources, and the budget for
subsequent stages. Actions on the main training process take three
forms:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
a_{k}\in\bigl\{\mathrm{NONE},\;\mathrm{HOT}(\delta_{k}),\;\mathrm{COLD}(\delta_{k},b_{k})\bigr\}.
``` |  | (3) |

Here, $`\delta_{k}`$ is a control update, code patch, or data
modification, and $`b_{k}`$ is a compatible, complete checkpoint. NONE
leaves the training configuration unchanged. HOT applies controls
supported by the running backend at an allowed training-step boundary. A
Proposer selects this channel only when the complete modification fits
the documented online-control interface and is permitted by the task
configuration. The Orchestrator verifies that the running backend
actually consumes the proposed controls. COLD handles code,
configuration, or data changes that require stopping the run. For
example, replacing a supported policy-loss object can be HOT, whereas
changing the data loader or editing training code requires COLD. The
readiness check enforces this distinction by rejecting HOT patches that
modify files outside the online-control file.

For a COLD intervention, the Orchestrator coordinates checkpointing and
shutdown, authorizes the patch and regression checks, and resumes from a
compatible checkpoint. When the run has already failed, recovery uses an
existing complete checkpoint. These paths allow adjustments and repairs
to preserve accumulated training progress.

To express how the two intervention paths preserve training progress,
let $`x_{k}^{-}=(\theta_{k}^{-},z_{k}^{-},c_{k}^{-})`$ denote the state
immediately before execution, including model parameters, training
runtime state, and effective configuration. The intervention changes
this state according to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{k}^{+}=\begin{cases}x_{k}^{-},&a_{k}=\mathrm{NONE},\\
(\theta_{k}^{-},z_{k}^{-},c_{k}^{-}\oplus\delta_{k}),&a_{k}=\mathrm{HOT}(\delta_{k}),\\
(\theta_{b_{k}},z_{b_{k}},c_{k}^{-}\oplus\delta_{k}),&a_{k}=\mathrm{COLD}(\delta_{k},b_{k}).\end{cases}
``` |  | (4) |

Here, $`z`$ groups the optimizer, trainer, rollout, and data-consumption
states. The operator $`\oplus`$ applies the modification and reconciles
which effective controls carry forward. HOT retains the current
parameters and runtime state, while COLD restores them from $`b_{k}`$
and continues with the modified configuration.

Training then continues from the resulting state. The Orchestrator
records the actual change, its effective position $`(r,s)`$, intended
effects, and recovery information. Checkpoint lineage distinguishes the
trajectory inherited by the current run from other attempts retained as
research experience. Runtime receipts confirm activation, after which
Perceptors use the intervention records to interpret fresh observations.
At subsequent reviews, the Orchestrator compares this evidence with the
pre-intervention reference, accounting for the effective step and the
amount of relevant training completed. For a numerical repair, it checks
for finite updates and resumed progress. For a reward adjustment, it
examines the behavior of affected training examples, while model
performance is assessed through the unchanged evaluation protocol.

Verified fixes and beneficial changes are retained. When relevant
samples or evaluations are still pending, the Orchestrator specifies the
next observation and continues monitoring. Persistent failure or
regression prompts rollback or renewed deliberation to revise the
intervention; a new anomaly or plateau can likewise trigger further
research through the Perceptors. Follow-up uses the existing observation
cycles, with the intervention and its observed effects entering the
shared history for subsequent decisions.

## 3 Experiments

We conduct RL experiments on mathematical reasoning and agentic coding
to evaluate whether AutoRL can automate training supervision and recipe
improvement. We first compare the trained models across nine benchmarks
to assess performance gains at different model scales. We then study
what AutoRL can carry out and how AutoRL achieves these gains during
training, focusing on repairing a flawed recipe and overcoming a
performance plateau. Finally, we examine a two-week coding-agent run to
understand how AutoRL handles recurring failures while preserving
learning progress. Together, the experiments show that AutoRL can carry
out most tasks in ongoing RL supervision and research, such as recipe
repair, plateau investigation, and failure recovery during training.

### 3.1 Settings

Models and datasets. We study four settings. Qwen3-1.7B and
Qwen3-4B ([Yang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib31)) are trained on
DAPO-Math-17K ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)), using 17,390 processed
mathematical problems. LongCat-Flash-Lite ([Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib42)) is trained on private collections of 9,340
mathematical problems for reasoning and 700+ repository-level software
engineering tasks for agentic coding. Coding-agent evaluation uses
SWE-related tasks. We evaluate the Qwen models on AIME
2024/2025 ([Mathematical Association of America, 2024](#bib.bib33)),
HMMT 2025 ([MathArena, 2025](#bib.bib34)), Beyond AIME ([ByteDance Seed,
2025](#bib.bib35)), and IMO-AnswerBench ([Luong et al.,
2025](#bib.bib36)) for mathematics; MMLU-Pro ([Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib37)) and GPQA-Diamond ([Rein et al., 2023](#bib.bib38))
for general knowledge and reasoning; and MBPP ([Austin et al.,
2021](#bib.bib39)) and LiveCodeBench v6 Hard ([Jain et al.,
2024](#bib.bib40)) for coding. AutoRL’s supervisory agents use
gpt-6.1-sol ([OpenAI, 2026b](#bib.bib43)) in both Qwen experiments and
glm-5.2 ([Z.ai, 2026](#bib.bib44)) in both LongCat-Flash-Lite
experiments. The general agent baseline uses native Codex ([OpenAI,
2026a](#bib.bib45)) with all built-in tools and features enabled, backed
by the same gpt-6.1-sol model as the Qwen AutoRL runs.
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Performance with Different LLMs ‣ 4 Analysis ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")
additionally evaluates GLM-5.2 as the supervisory backbone for Qwen3-4B.

Training recipes. Both Qwen models start from a GRPO recipe ([Shao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib25)) with a learning rate of $`10^{-6}`$, sampling
eight responses per prompt and allowing up to 8,192 response tokens.
LongCat reasoning uses the same learning rate and group size with a
131,072-token response limit. Coding-agent training uses fully
asynchronous updates, 16 trajectories per task, and a learning rate of
$`5\times 10^{-6}`$, allowing up to 400 interaction turns within a
262,144-token context. AutoRL adapts these initial recipes during
training while keeping evaluation protocols fixed. Evaluation uses
temperature 0.6 for Qwen, with thinking mode disabled, and 1.0 for
LongCat. We sample each question or task four times to compute the final
results. We report mean accuracy for reasoning and knowledge tasks and
pass@1 for coding. For Qwen comparisons, a training step denotes one
rollout-and-training iteration, while cumulative counts include steps
repeated after checkpoint rollback. Additional details are provided in
Appendices [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Task Configurations and Reproducibility ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")
and [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Runtime Control and Checkpoint Continuity ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning").

degradation in later stages. AutoRL can revise the recipe during
training, while the comparison run keeps it unchanged. Both use the same
evaluation protocol.

### 3.4 Performance Plateau Breakthrough

Beyond correcting recipe defects, AutoRL aims to discover further
improvements when a working training run reaches a plateau. To evaluate
this ability, we compare AutoRL and static-recipe GRPO using Qwen3-4B,
starting from the same model with the same initial training and
evaluation settings.
Figure [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.4 Performance Plateau Breakthrough ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")
shows the transition from early gains to a prolonged plateau and
subsequent improvement. We examine both the performance after the
plateau and the sequence of interventions leading up to it.

indicate initial improvement, the plateau, and renewed improvement.
Light lines and markers show raw evaluations, and dark curves show a
smooth average. Dashed lines mark applied interventions. Hollow diamonds
mark COLD resumptions from checkpoints 100 and 135, with the

Improvement beyond the plateau. AutoRL reaches 41.58% accuracy at step
180, exceeding its plateau maximum of 29.74% by 11.84%. This peak is
also 10.26% above the best GRPO (Static Recipe) result of 31.32% within
the plotted interval. The advantage persists at the common endpoint of
step 190, where AutoRL achieves 37.89% compared with 30.26% for GRPO
(Static Recipe). Thus, the gain is visible both at the peak and at the
end of the comparison, indicating that AutoRL can sustain further
improvement after an extended period of stagnation.

Developing interventions during stagnation. AutoRL leaves the training
configuration unchanged for the first 100 steps while continuing to
monitor and discuss potential improvements. During this period, it
defers an entropy-adjustment proposal to gather more evidence. The first
applied change fixes answer-option binding in the training grader at
step 101, following a COLD resumption from checkpoint 100. AutoRL then
sets the entropy coefficient to 0.001 at step 108 and adds an auxiliary
penalty at step 111 for incorrect responses that satisfy a strict
looping criterion. Subsequent updates raise the entropy coefficient to
0.002 and 0.003 at steps 121 and 129. The sequence moves from diagnosing
the learning signal to adjusting exploration, with each decision
informed by the current run.

Connecting interventions to subsequent gains. Accuracy remains at 29.21%
at step 130 and rises to 32.89% at step 135, exceeding the plateau
maximum for the first time. The entropy increase at step 129 is the last
applied change before this rise, suggesting that stronger exploration
may contribute alongside the earlier reward corrections. AutoRL
continues refining the recipe afterward, repairing two training
references at step 136 after resuming from checkpoint 135, increasing
the entropy coefficient to 0.004 at step 153, and using a proof
certificate to refine answer-option binding at step 171. It suggests
that AutoRL’s ability to overcome performance plateaus and achieve
further gains may stem from the cumulative benefits of ongoing
interventions from multiple perspectives as training progresses.

This plateau study illustrates how AutoRL achieves additional gains
through online adjustments and research over a standard normal RL
recipe. AutoRL initially keeps the current recipe running while
monitoring training, gathering evidence to guide targeted improvements
during the subsequent plateau. After prolonged stagnation, model
performance resumes improving and reaches a higher level on evaluation.
It suggests that a plateau can serve as a starting point for further
research, with training signals, repetitive behaviors, and exploration
intensity informing testable, actionable improvements. AutoRL’s strength
lies in sustaining this research process and implementing candidate
modifications within ongoing training. Even after overcoming the
plateau, AutoRL continues to examine reference answers and exploration
settings, using feedback from one improvement to guide subsequent
research. This ability to adapt its actions to the evolving training
state provides an important foundation for sustained improvement over
long horizons.

### 3.5 Anomaly Handling in Long Training

Automating RL supervision requires handling runtime failures and
preserving the learning accumulated before an interruption. We evaluate
this capability in a LongCat-Flash-Lite coding-agent run spanning 352.5
hours, approximately 14.7 days. The run adopts fully asynchronous
training across $`28\times 8`$ GPUs with an MoE model. Each coding
trajectory involves tool use and code execution, allowing up to 400
interaction turns in the initial configuration. These long interactions,
together with distributed execution and precision-sensitive MoE
computations, create a demanding setting for failure diagnosis and
recovery.

We study the failures that occur during this run, tracking both
agent-directed repairs and restarts.
Figure [5](#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 3.5 Anomaly Handling in Long Training ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")
aligns pass@1 evaluations on SWE-related tasks with failure events and
the checkpoints used to resume training. This experiment tracks how
recovery decisions change as new issues emerge and whether the model
continues to improve across interruptions.

Figure 5: Recovering from failures during LongCat-Flash-Lite
coding-agent training. The curve illustrates evaluations on SWE-related
tasks, with a star marking the highest pass@1. Red dashed lines indicate
failures. Paired crosses and dots connect the 11 failures to their
recovery checkpoints, while the labels give the failure step and
restored checkpoint. Two representative repairs are highlighted. The
upper axis gives elapsed wall-clock time.

Continued learning across failures. The run encounters 11 distinct
failures, comprising seven NaN failures, one dtype mismatch, and three
worker or communication failures. Training resumes from a valid
checkpoint after each failure, with agent-directed repairs and restarts
together allowing the run to reach step 79. Across 15 evaluations
through step 75, pass@1 rises from 52.29% to a peak of 65.13% at step 55
and finishes at 63.20%. The final score remains 10.91% above the
starting model, following a peak gain of 12.84%. Checkpoint recovery and
targeted repair preserve learning progress across repeated
interruptions.

Adapting the recipe for recovery. Repeated NaN failures and stalled jobs
prompt AutoRL to revise the numerical settings before resuming from
saved checkpoints. Specifically, it tightens log-ratio clipping from
$`[-13,5]`$ to $`[-5,3]`$ and sets the probability-ratio spike limit to
5. It also lowers both the learning rate and minimum learning rate from
$`5\times 10^{-6}`$ to $`2\times 10^{-6}`$, while enabling FP32
accumulation in the MoE components. By adjusting update magnitude and
numerical precision, AutoRL changes the conditions that led to failure
before continuing the run. Restoring model and optimizer states allows
it to test the revised configuration while building on prior learning.

Revising a repair when new problems emerge. An attempted repair also
introduces a new failure. Enabling FP32 residuals causes a dtype
mismatch, which AutoRL addresses by disabling that option while
retaining the reduced learning rate, MoE precision setting, and existing
update constraints. Training then resumes from checkpoint 22. Several
attempts reuse this checkpoint, explaining the repeated step labels in
the figure. A later NaN failure near step 43 is likewise followed by
resumption. This example demonstrates the importance of reassessing
earlier actions, since a change intended to improve stability can
introduce a different incompatibility. This recovery depends on
identifying which part of a repair failed. Reverting the incompatible
residual setting preserves the other numerical adjustments, allowing the
next attempt to build on the earlier diagnosis.

These observations show that AutoRL can sustain stable supervision and
continuous research during ongoing RL training, achieving sustained
performance gains even in substantially more complex coding-agent
environments involving multi-node communication, frequent numerical
errors, and long-horizon interactions. These findings highlight AutoRL’s
potential to reduce the supervisory burden of human RL researchers and
enable long-horizon unattended RL training.

Most research aims to improve the existing training process. Grading and
data refinement account for 110 candidates (84.0%), making the
correctness of the learning signal the dominant research focus. The
adopted plans comprise ten modifications to grading or data, nine
parameter adjustments, and one reward extension. These include
correcting answer-choice mismatches and erroneous references, increasing
entropy regularization, and penalizing incorrect repetitive responses.
AutoRL therefore carries out useful research beyond hyperparameter
search while retaining the underlying GRPO learning rule.

Learning-rule ideas rarely reach execution. Three candidates (2.3%)
introduce a different gradient-selection or weighting rule, and none is
adopted. Reviewing their discussion reveals two obstacles. The
covariance-based proposal exhibits sensitivity to sequence packing and
becomes inactive with constant advantages during local checks. The other
two proposals reweight positive-advantage or high-entropy tokens, but
the discussion favors less disruptive entropy adjustments over
restarting training to introduce a new loss component. These cases
locate the gap between generating an algorithmic idea and developing it
into a justified training experiment. The agents explore modifications
to the learning rule, yet the realized improvements come from tuning,
repair, and local reward refinement.

This distribution reveals the capabilities and limitations of current
agents in autonomous RL. Agents can handle recurring monitoring and
refinement within a defined task, reducing the need for researchers to
continuously track entropy, rewards, and runtime health. They can even
analyze individual rollout data points online to repair graders, revise
reference answers, and counter reward hacking. However, in our
experiments, we did not observe agents within our framework generating
conceptual leaps or proposing fundamental innovations that break with
existing architectures. We regard this limitation as a major unresolved
challenge on the path toward RSI. Nevertheless, these findings support a
practical division of labor: AutoRL handles unattended supervision and
incremental research, while human researchers devote more effort to
conceptual breakthroughs and transformative innovations.

## 5 Related Work

### 5.1 Autonomous Post-Training

Automated post-training systems study training strategies, data recipes,
and training tools ([Fang et al., 2026](#bib.bib1); [He et al.,
2026](#bib.bib3); [Chen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib2)), extending to
sustained multi-agent research with shared checkpoints and repeated
experiments from fixed starting points ([Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib15);
[Shi et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)). These approaches differ in their
intervention scope, experiment organization, and reuse of experience.
Particularly relevant are systems that incorporate feedback during
training. LLMZero composes RL stages through checkpoint tree search and
stops candidates using intermediate feedback. AgentJet separates mutable
agent logic from persistent training servers, allowing clients to be
replaced during training while preserving optimizer state; its A3R
leader–worker workflow supports multi-day RL research ([Fang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib1); [Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)). EvoTrainer jointly
evolves policies and training tools through diagnosis, branch
backtesting, and reusable skills, while retaining human confirmation for
training launches and version promotion in its reported setup ([Chen et
al., 2026a](#bib.bib2)).

AutoRL organizes supervision and local research around the ongoing RL
process. Whereas Athanor coordinates general research agents through
shared histories and checkpoints ([Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)),
AutoRL assigns monitoring, proposal development, and execution control
to distinct roles. Evidence and intervention records connect their
decisions to the active training state, while online updates and
checkpoint-based resumption let them act on that state.

### 5.2 Autonomous Research

Autonomous research systems organize literature, hypotheses,
implementation, and experimental feedback into iterative
workflows ([Schmidgall et al., 2025](#bib.bib5); [Yang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib9); [Tang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib10)). Complementary
benchmarks assess experimental design, execution, and iterative
improvement across machine learning tasks ([Nathani et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17); [Lupidi et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). The AI
Scientist-v2 combines tree search with stage management for feasibility
checks, tuning, and ablation studies ([Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)).
Closer to model improvement, RSI-Master records experimental actions and
evidence in a persistent experiment graph and organizes exploration
through a research DAG of Workers and Reviewers, with Reviewers
independently checking evidence ([Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)). These
systems illustrate how research organization and review can jointly
support sustained experimentation.

Experiment search also requires controlling validation cost.
ImproveAnyTask checks execution using small samples of real data and
short training runs before full post-training, while retaining validated
strategies and scripts. AutoLLMResearch learns to select expensive
configurations from low-fidelity experiments ([Yao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13); [Guo et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)). AutoRL integrates
evidence review and CPU-only checks into proposal deliberation.
Proposers inspect claims and test candidate logic before the
Orchestrator applies an approved change to the main training process and
verifies its activation. Perceptors continue observing training and
trigger renewed deliberation when problems arise. This places
inexpensive validation inside proposal development and uses subsequent
training feedback to guide further intervention.

### 5.3 Multi-Agent Collaboration

Multi-agent frameworks organize tool use and collaboration through
roles, conversation protocols, and standard operating procedures ([Wu et
al., 2023](#bib.bib6); [Hong et al., 2023](#bib.bib7); [Tang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib29); [Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)). Memory systems
support experience reuse through structured notes, linked retrieval, and
incremental consolidation ([Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [Zhang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib21)), while long-horizon evaluations examine state
updates and evidence aggregation under interference ([Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). For collective research, Agora stores findings,
hypotheses, and verification records as an append-only DAG in Git,
allowing agents in separate sessions to retrieve and build on shared
work ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)). AutoRL associates local
histories and shared records with concrete runs, steps, interventions,
and parent checkpoints, enabling agents to verify the current state and
distinguish reusable experience from training history that no longer
applies.

Collaboration quality also depends on candidate generation, the use of
counterevidence, and final selection. Recent debate studies distinguish
complementary proposal coverage from evidence-based selection, and show
that unstructured discussion among homogeneous small models can increase
cost without improving accuracy ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib23);
[Bertalanič and Fortuna, 2026](#bib.bib24)). AutoRL organizes proposal
generation, cross-examination, revision, ranking, and voting around a
frozen initial observation context. Evidence gathered and CPU checks
performed during deliberation are recorded and shared to support
candidate revision.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented AutoRL, a multi-agent framework for automating RL
supervision and research. Specialized Perceptors, complementary
Proposers, and an Orchestrator connect monitoring, evidence-based
deliberation, and process-level intervention, taking on work that
otherwise requires repeated researcher attention. Experiments with Qwen
and LongCat-Flash-Lite demonstrate recipe repair, renewed gains after
plateaus, and recovery from runtime failures. On Qwen3-4B, AutoRL
outperforms static recipes and a native general agent across nine
benchmarks. Both GPT-6.1 and GLM-5.2 support improvements, and a
coding-agent run continues for 14.7 days through 11 failures. Current
agents are effective at tuning, repair, and local reward refinement
within an existing recipe. Developing fundamentally different learning
methods remains the harder research problem.

#### Optimization and rollout settings.

Qwen uses group-normalized advantages and a token-mean policy objective,
while the LongCat recipes use the Dr. GRPO advantage estimator \[[Liu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib41)\]. All runs use AdamW \[[Loshchilov and Hutter,
2019](#bib.bib46)\] and gradient clipping at 1.0. The AdamW parameters
are $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.999`$, and weight decay 0.1 for
Qwen, and $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.95`$, and weight decay 0.01
for LongCat. Reasoning runs start with a constant learning rate.
Coding-agent training starts with both the learning rate and its minimum
set to $`5\times 10^{-6}`$ and allows rollout staleness of up to three
policy versions. Entropy regularization is initially zero for Qwen, and
KL regularization is initially zero for all three recipes.

#### Evaluation subsets and sampling.

The reasoning monitoring set contains 190 problems, with 30 each from
AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and HMMT 2025, and 100 from Beyond AIME. Each
problem is sampled twice, yielding 380 responses per evaluation. Curves
report the fraction of correct responses over this combined set. Qwen
uses temperature 0.6, top-$`p`$ 1.0, and a 31,744-token response limit.
LongCat uses temperature 1.0, top-$`p`$ 0.95, top-$`k`$ 10, and a
131,072-token response limit. Coding-agent evaluation samples one
trajectory per SWE-related task at temperature 1.0 and top-$`p`$ 1.0.

Additional Qwen evaluations sample four responses per problem using
independent random seeds, temperature 0.6, top-$`p`$ 1.0, and
prompt/response limits of 4,096/32,768 tokens. Thinking mode is disabled
in Qwen training and evaluation. MMLU-Pro uses a fixed, subject-balanced
sample of 1,000 test questions (seed 42) and five in-domain
demonstrations. GPQA-Diamond uses all 198 questions with zero-shot
prompts. MBPP uses tasks 11-510 and up to three demonstrations.
LiveCodeBench uses the cumulative release-v6 collection of 1,055
problems, with
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.2 Main Results ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")
reporting the 350-problem Hard subset. IMO-AnswerBench uses all 400
problems in answerbench-v2. Both LiveCodeBench and IMO-AnswerBench use
zero-shot prompts. Scoring uses answer equivalence for mathematics,
option matching for multiple-choice questions, and executable tests for
coding. Reported accuracy and pass@1 average correctness across the
sampled responses.

#### Execution layout and evaluation cadence.

Training uses Megatron-LM-based optimization \[[Shoeybi et al.,
2019](#bib.bib47)\] and vLLM generation \[[Kwon et al.,
2023](#bib.bib48)\] on NVIDIA H800 GPUs. Each Qwen run time-shares eight
GPUs among the actor, training generator, and evaluation generator,
using tensor parallelism of 2 and data parallelism of 4. LongCat
reasoning initially allocates 64 GPUs: 32 for the colocated optimization
roles, 16 for training generation, and 16 for evaluation generation. The
fully asynchronous coding-agent recipe initially allocates 224 GPUs
across 28 nodes, with 64 for optimization, 128 for training generation,
and 32 for evaluation generation. GPU totals count colocated roles once.
The Qwen static-recipe runs evaluate every 10 steps, while the Qwen3-4B
AutoRL trajectory evaluates every 5 steps. LongCat reasoning evaluates
and checkpoints every 2 steps; coding-agent training evaluates every 5
steps and checkpoints every step. Save-and-stop operations additionally
produce checkpoints at intervention boundaries.

### A.2 Agent Prompts, Tools, and Memory

#### Agent configuration.

The Qwen campaigns use gpt-6.1-sol with high reasoning effort for the
Orchestrator, four Perceptors, and three Proposers. The LongCat
campaigns use glm-5.2. The additional Qwen3-4B backbone study also uses
GLM-5.2 within the AutoRL role structure. The general agent baseline
runs native Codex with gpt-6.1-sol and all built-in tools and features
enabled. Each AutoRL role uses a separate conversation. The Orchestrator
coordinates seven subagents, comprising four Perceptors and three
Proposers.

first establishes the active run and its effective configuration, then
schedules observations and reviews recommendations. A Perceptor

#### Observation and evidence reuse.

Observation tools summarize metric windows using counts, means, standard
deviations, slopes, and standardized deviations. Perceptors interpret
these statistics alongside training behavior and attach the run, step,
observation window, and source artifacts to each report. A shared
barrier collects the reports before discussion begins. If no role
reports an issue, training continues without a debate. Missing evidence
can be requested through a targeted observation. Cached outputs retain
source paths and hashes, allowing Proposers to work from the same
observations without rescanning the logs.

#### Memory and session continuity.

Each role retains recent observations and reasoning in its conversation
history. Shared Markdown records summarize experiment status, data
characteristics, and research findings, while a tool-accessible database
stores sourced domain insights. Summaries link to the underlying
observation, deliberation, control, and checkpoint records. After
context compaction or session resumption, agents use these records to
recover the active run and unfinished work. The launcher stores the
Orchestrator’s session identity and resumes interrupted sessions until
the task is marked complete. Session recovery restores the supervisory
workflow; checkpoint recovery restores training.

#### Evaluation visibility.

Online supervision uses aggregate evaluation results and sanitized
diagnostic reports through the evaluation observer. Proposers inspect
training examples and replay stored TRAIN trajectories when developing
reward or data changes. The evaluation inputs, scoring code, and
sampling protocol remain fixed during intervention. The additional Qwen
post-training benchmarks are run on the selected checkpoints through a
separate evaluation pipeline.

### A.3 Deliberation and Low-Cost Validation

#### From independent proposals to a recommendation.

A discussion has at most five rounds. The Proposers first diagnose the
same frozen evidence independently and submit candidates. They then
critique the candidates, propose combinations, and revise or withdraw
their submissions. Combining proposals requires agreement from their
authors. In round 4, Borda scores \[[Zwicker, 2016](#bib.bib49)\] select
at most two finalists. Round 5 produces a majority or unanimous
recommendation and records dissent. Discussion can end after an earlier
round if all three Proposers explicitly select the same eligible
candidate. Leaving the recipe unchanged is also a valid recommendation.

A candidate describes the observed problem, mechanism, concrete
modification, expected effects, implementation conditions, and rollback
procedure. The Orchestrator then approves, rejects, defers, or requests
further evidence. A request for evidence opens a targeted observation
cycle, allowing the next decision to use newly collected facts while
preserving the original discussion record.

#### Checks within deliberation.

Proposers test candidates in CPU workspaces containing copies of the
required source files and authorized training inputs. Each check records
the candidate revision, inputs, command, tested behavior, and outputs.
Successful and failed executions receive PASS and FAIL results. Timeouts
and unavailable dependencies are marked INCONCLUSIVE; an inapplicable
check includes a reason. Code revisions receive separate check records,
and newly gathered evidence is shared at the next round boundary. These
checks examine implementation behavior before deployment, while
subsequent training supplies performance feedback.

#### A reward-hook revision.

In discussion 43 of the GLM-driven Qwen3-4B run, a proposed reward hook
removed the truncation penalty but accidentally bypassed the existing
repetition penalty when a helper lookup failed. The Reviewer traced the
fallback path, and the Tuner changed the hook to call the baseline
grader before undoing only the truncation penalty. The corrected hook
took effect at local training step 154.

We replayed the archived hooks after the run using four synthetic
fixtures and a mock baseline grader
(Table [6](#A1.T6 "Table 6 ‣ A reward-hook revision. ‣ A.3 Deliberation and Low-Cost Validation ‣ Appendix A Appendix ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")).
Starting from score 1.0, the mock grader subtracts 0.7 for repetition.
The revised hook preserves this penalty in both repetition cases,
returning 0.3 as intended; the original fallback incorrectly returns
1.0. Cases without repetition return 1.0 under both hooks.

|-----------------|--------------|--------------|----------|
| Fixture         | Initial hook | Revised hook | Expected |

| Repetition only | 1.0          | 0.3          | 0.3      |
| Truncation only | 1.0          | 1.0          | 1.0      |

#### Complementary proposals and independent review.

Discussion 31 combines two compatible adjustments. The Tuner proposes an
entropy coefficient of 0.001, and the Innovator proposes increasing the
upper clipping allowance from 0.20 to 0.28. After the Reviewer
identifies that both settings belong to the same loss object, the
authors merge the candidates in round 3. Both changes take effect online
at local step 141. In contrast, the Orchestrator rejects discussion 1’s
unanimous entropy recommendation because evaluation and early training
signals are still improving. For discussions 31 and 43, missing

### A.4 Runtime Control and Checkpoint Continuity

#### Training progress and resumption.

Figures [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.3 Flawed Recipe Fixing ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")–[5](#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 3.5 Anomaly Handling in Long Training ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")
follow the retained checkpoint lineage. A resumed job inherits its
checkpoint’s step counter. Training after a rollback can therefore
revisit earlier step indices. Upper axes show elapsed wall-clock time,
including evaluation, waiting, and recovery.
Figures [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.1 Comparison with General Agent ‣ 4 Analysis ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")
and [7](#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4.2 Performance with Different LLMs ‣ 4 Analysis ‣ AutoRL: A Multi-Agent System towards Autonomous Reinforcement Learning")
instead align runs by cumulative completed steps, counting those
revisits.

#### HOT controls.

A HOT control file specifies the full set of active overrides. Supported
controls include KL and advantage-estimation settings, the policy-loss
object, training-grader adjustments, and save-and-stop. Setting a field
to None restores its launch-time value, so a new control file must
retain any earlier overrides that should remain active. Similarly,
replacing a loss object requires carrying forward its unchanged
clipping, entropy, and KL settings. The task configuration determines
which controls may be used. Qwen AutoRL keeps sampling, sequence
budgets, batch sizes, and learning rate fixed while investigating loss,
reward, and data changes. The coding-agent task also permits
learning-rate and precision changes.

The synchronous trainer loads controls at a training-step boundary,
applies trainer settings, and activates training-grader changes on the
rollout path. It records the effective step and source revision,
retaining the previous state if activation fails. The fully asynchronous
backend applies trainer-local controls before dispatching a policy
update. We timestamp an applied intervention using this activation
record.

#### COLD modifications and state restoration.

Changes requiring reinitialization use COLD resumption. Before stopping,
the Orchestrator decides which active HOT controls to incorporate into
the next launch, reapply afterward, or remove. It requests
save-and-stop, waits for shutdown, and grants the code lock for
implementation. The next run restores the model, optimizer, trainer, and
rollout state from a compatible checkpoint, including the saved step
counter and available random and data-consumption states. If the
intervention replaces training inputs, the new run checks the effective
sampler and data loader. Recovery from an already failed job uses an
existing complete checkpoint.

#### Lineage-aware observation.

Each resumed run records its parent and checkpoint. Its active history
follows the parent only through that checkpoint and then continues with
the new run. For example, resuming checkpoint 100 retains observations
through step 100; later observations from the abandoned continuation
remain available as research history. The plateau study resumes
checkpoints 100 and 135, with changes taking effect at steps 101 and
136. Linking controls and evaluations through these parent records lets
Perceptors interpret later behavior in the context of the state actually
inherited.

### A.5 Resource Accounting and Measurement Definitions

#### Data adaptation during continued training.

The coding-agent task pool changes from an initial pool of 700+ tasks to
656 in epoch 2 and 453 in epoch 3. Epoch 4 restores 58 previously
removed tasks and removes 55 others, producing 456 tasks. Selection
therefore revisits the full pool rather than only pruning the preceding
subset. The final executed selection contains 271 tasks. These changes
adjust the composition of the supplied data without introducing a new
training objective.

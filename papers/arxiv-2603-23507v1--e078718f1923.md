###### Abstract

While Masked Diffusion Language Models (MDLMs) relying on token masking
and unmasking have shown promise in language modeling, their
computational efficiency and generation flexibility remain constrained
by the masking paradigm. In this paper, we propose Deletion-Insertion
Diffusion language models (DID) that rigorously formulate token deletion
and insertion as discrete diffusion processes, replacing the masking and
unmasking processes in current MDLMs. DID improves training and
inference efficiency by eliminating two major sources of computational
overhead in MDLMs: the computations on non-informative 1) \<MASK\>
tokens inherent to the paradigm, and 2) \<PAD\> tokens introduced in
variable-length settings. Furthermore, DID offers greater flexibility
by: 1) natively supporting variable-length sequences without requiring
fixed-length padding, and 2) an intrinsic self-correction mechanism
during generation due to insertion that dynamically adjusts token
positions. To train DID, we design a score-based approach that assigns
scores to token insertion operations and derive appropriate training
objectives. The objectives involve subsequence counting problems, which
we efficiently solve via a parallelized dynamic programming algorithm.
Our experiments across fixed and variable-length settings demonstrate
the advantage of DID over baselines of MDLMs and existing
insertion-based LMs, in terms of modeling performance, sampling quality,
and training/inference speed, without any hyperparameter tuning.

## 1 Introduction

Diffusion language models (DLMs) ([Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib2);
[Campbell et al., 2022](#bib.bib4); [Lou et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)) have
rapidly emerged as a powerful paradigm for language modeling, offering a
compelling alternative to the dominant autoregressive (AR) approach.
They offer distinct advantages, including bidirectional context modeling
and the potential for parallel decoding. Within this domain, a body of
work on Masked Diffusion Language Models (MDLMs) ([Nie et al.,
2024](#bib.bib28); [Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib29); [Ou et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31); [Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib36); [Shi et al.,
2024](#bib.bib40)) is the most widely studied. These models operate
through a forward process that progressively corrupts each token into an
absorbing state \<MASK\> and a backward process that reconstructs the
original sequence by iteratively unmasking tokens from a fully masked
sequence, with a fixed sequence length during the diffusion process.

Despite their success, MDLMs are fundamentally limited by their fixed
sequence length. The first issue lies in their restricted generation
flexibility, which leads to challenges in modeling variable lengths and
performing self-correction: once a token is unmasked, its content and
position become fixed, thereby risking error accumulation in a similar
sense to autoregressive models. The second issue is their substantial
computational inefficiency, as the model must repeatedly process
full-length sequences. Under the typical log-linear noise schedule,
about half of the FLOPs are allocated to the non-informative \<MASK\>
tokens during both training and inference. Further, if MDLMs are applied
to variable-length sequences, their fixed-length nature demands padding
to the same length ([Nie et al., 2024](#bib.bib28); [Nie et al.,
2025](#bib.bib29); [Wu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib46); [Gong et al.,
2024](#bib.bib16))
(Fig. [1(a)](#S1.F1.sf1 "Figure 1(a) ‣ Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
allocating extra FLOPs to the non-informative \<PAD\> tokens. This means
generating a shorter sequence is not faster.

To address these issues, we propose Deletion-Insertion Diffusion
language models (DID), a novel discrete diffusion paradigm that
fundamentally differs from MDLMs. DID replaces the masking-unmasking
processes in MDLMs with deletion-insertion processes
(Fig. [1(b)](#S1.F1.sf2 "Figure 1(b) ‣ Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")).
Specifically, tokens are progressively deleted in the forward process
until the sequence is empty; in the backward process, generation starts
from an empty sequence and iteratively inserts tokens until a complete
sequence is reconstructed. DID eliminates the \<MASK\> and \<PAD\>
tokens used in MDLMs, saving FLOPs and improving computational
efficiency. Regarding generation flexibility, DID natively supports
variable-length data, and as an insertion-based language model, features
an intrinsic self-correction mechanism that dynamically adjusts token
positions during generation.

We implement DID by addressing several non-trivial design and training
challenges. First, we rigorously formulate the deletion and insertion
processes within the discrete diffusion framework, and develop a
score-based approach built upon the Denoising Score Entropy (DSE) ([Lou
et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)) objective to train DID. Concretely, we define
an insertion score that models the probability of inserting any token at
any position of a sequence at a given time interval, and derive a
corresponding Denoising Insertion Score Entropy (DISE) training
objective. The DISE objective is based on a ratio of subsequence counts
in the clean data after and before an insertion, which serves as the
training target for the insertion score. To efficiently compute the
ratio, we develop a parallelized dynamic programming algorithm that
exploits data parallelism. Moreover, we demonstrate that under the
fixed-length setting of MDLMs, the DISE objective can be further
simplified to a form resembling cross-entropy, further improving
parameterization and learning of the insertion score.

Comprehensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of DID in
enhancing efficiency and flexibility. In fixed-length language modeling
benchmarks, DID achieves superior performance compared to strong MDLM
baselines (e.g., RADD ([Ou et al., 2025](#bib.bib31))) when aligned by
computational budget (FLOPs)

In summary, our main contributions are as follows:

- •
  We propose DID, a novel diffusion LM based on deletion-insertion
  processes that eliminates the use of \<MASK\> and \<PAD\> tokens,
  improving the computational efficiency and generation flexibility of
  DLMs.
- •
  We develop DISE, a score-based training objective, and an efficient
  parallel dynamic programming implementation that enables effective
  learning of DID’s insertion process for language generation.
- •
  Our experiments demonstrate the superior efficiency and flexibility of
  DID over baselines of MDLMs and other insertion-based LMs on
  language/length modeling, generation quality, and training/inference
  speed.

## 2 Preliminaries and Related Works

### 2.1 Continuous-Time Discrete Diffusion

A continuous-time discrete diffusion model consists of a forward noising
process and a backward denoising process, both continuous-time Markov
chains. In the forward process, the training samples are progressively
corrupted into pure noise. The model aims to learn its backward process
that inverts this corruption, and generate new samples by sampling
through the backward process from noise.

Continuous-time Markov chain. We consider a discrete state space
$`\mathcal{X}`$. A continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) is a process
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ on $`\mathcal{X}`$ with $`{t\in[0,1]}`$, starting from
an initial data distribution $`p_{0}({\bm{x}}_{0})`$. A CTMC is
characterized by a time-dependent transition rate matrix
$`Q_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|\times|\mathcal{X}|}`$. For distinct
states $`{\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}\in\mathcal{X}`$,
$`Q_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\geq 0`$ defines the instantaneous
transition rate from $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ to $`{\bm{y}}`$. This means, at an
infinitesimal time interval $`[t,t+\Delta t]`$, the transition
probability is given by:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
p_{t+\Delta t|t}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})=\delta({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})+Q_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\Delta t,
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\delta`$ is the Kronecker delta. In other words, the evolution
of the marginal distribution $`p_{t}\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{X}|}`$ follows
the Kolmogorov forward equation $`\frac{dp_{t}}{dt}=p_{t}Q_{t}`$. Note
that the diagonal entries of $`Q_{t}`$ should satisfy
$`Q_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t})=-\sum_{{\bm{x}}_{t}\neq{\bm{y}}}Q_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$
to ensure the weights of $`p_{t}`$ add up to $`1`$.

Determining the forward process. In the forward process, a common
parameterization ([Campbell et al., 2022](#bib.bib4)) of the transition
rate matrix is $`Q_{t}=\sigma(t)Q`$, where $`\sigma(t)`$ is a scalar
noise schedule and $`Q`$ is a constant rate matrix determined by the
model design. Taking this to the Kolmogorov forward equation, one can
analytically solve the marginal distributions by $`p_{t}=p_{s}P_{t|s}`$,
where $`P_{t|s}=\exp((\bar{\sigma}(t)-\bar{\sigma}(s))Q)`$ is the
transition probability matrix from time $`s`$ to time $`t`$, and
$`\bar{\sigma}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\tau)d\tau`$.

Learning the backward process. It is known that the time reversal of
this process is also a CTMC, with its infinitesimal transition
probability similar to
Eq.[1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 2.1 Continuous-Time Discrete Diffusion ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Works ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{t-\Delta t|t}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})=\delta({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})+\tilde{Q}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\Delta t.
``` |  | (2) |

The reverse transition rate matrix $`\tilde{Q}_{t}`$ is associated to
its forward counterpart $`Q_{t}`$ by the identity
$`\tilde{Q}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})s({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}`$
for $`{\bm{x}}_{t}\neq{\bm{y}}`$, and
$`\tilde{Q}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t})=-\sum_{{\bm{x}}_{t}\neq{\bm{y}}}\tilde{Q}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ ([Lou
et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)). Here,
$`s({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}={p_{t}({\bm{y}})}/{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}`$
is the concrete score, which is generally intractable and commonly
approximated by a parameterized network
$`s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}`$ trained with the Denoising
Score Entropy (DSE) objective ([Lou et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)), a
negative evidence lower bound (ELBO) for discrete diffusion models
(details in
Appendix [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Derivation of the Denoising Score Entropy Loss (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}\neq{\bm{x}}_{t}}Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\Bigg[s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}-\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}+K\left(\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)\Bigg]},`$ |  | (3) |

where the expectation is taken over $`t\sim\text{Unif}([0,1])`$ and
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})`$, and
$`K(a)=a(\log a-1)`$.

### 2.2 Insertion-Based Language Models

Classical insertion-based models, such as the Insertion
Transformer ([Stern et al., 2019](#bib.bib42)), Levenshtein
Transformer ([Gu et al., 2019](#bib.bib17)), and InsNet ([Lu et al.,
2022](#bib.bib23)), pioneered sequence generation via iterative
insertion or edit operations. These models demonstrate the potential of
flexible decoding orders and parallel decoding. However, their training
objectives are defined at the level of local edit policies, rather than
arising from a probabilistic modeling of the global data distribution.

Recently, insertion operations have been integrated into discrete
diffusion and flow matching. Flexible Masked Diffusion Models
(FlexMDMs) ([Kim et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)) augment MDMs with a learned
insertion expectation to insert additional \<MASK\> tokens during
generation; this enables variable-length generation but still relies on
the masking-unmasking paradigm. Edit Flows ([Havasi et al.,
2025](#bib.bib18)) instead define a CTMC directly over variable-length
sequences via insertion, deletion, and substitution edits. To make
sequence-level flow matching tractable, Edit Flows augment the state
space with auxiliary edit-path variables and estimate the training
objective by Monte Carlo sampling of these paths. While this preserves
theoretical correctness, it adds implementation complexity and an
additional source of stochasticity beyond the randomness of the CTMC
forward process. Insertion Language Models (ILMs) ([Patel et al.,
2025](#bib.bib33)) draw inspiration from diffusion approaches and
formulate a CTMC forward process that deletes tokens to learn a backward
insertion process. However, ILMs are not diffusion models and cannot
learn the true backward insertion process since their training objective
is more heuristic rather than likelihood-bounded. They also suffer from
several practical limitations; for instance, they can only insert one
token per step and require another network to determine when to stop the
generation.

DID bridges the gap between flexible insertion-based generation and
rigorous discrete diffusion. Unlike classical insertion models and ILMs,
DID is well-grounded in a continuous-time diffusion framework: deletion
and insertion form the forward and backward CTMCs over the full
variable-length sequence space, and the DISE objective is inherited from
the DSE objective to enable likelihood-bounded training. Unlike Edit
Flows, however, DID does not introduce auxiliary edit-path variables.
Thanks to the independence structure of the deletion process, the
forward transition probabilities admit a closed-form expression in terms
of subsequence counts, and the subsequence count ratios that appear in
DISE can be computed exactly via parallel dynamic programming.
Therefore, DID avoids the additional variance and engineering overhead
associated with sampling edit paths, while maintaining a principled,
likelihood-bounded training objective and completely eliminating the use
of computationally wasteful \<MASK\> and \<PAD\> tokens.

### 3.1 Forward Process: Deletion

The forward process of DID is a CTMC on the state space
$`\cup_{d=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{V}^{d}`$ that gradually shortens the
sequence length by deleting tokens, thus equipping the model with
variable-length ability. Similar to MDLMs, we define this forward
process through independent token-level deletions with rate
$`\sigma(t)`$. Specifically, at the token level, a token
$`v\in\mathcal{V}`$ can be deleted (denoted by transition to an empty
state $`\varnothing`$) with an infinitesimal rate $`\sigma(t)`$, or
remain unchanged otherwise. Thus, the transition probability within
infinitesimal time $`\Delta t`$ is:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{t+\Delta t|t}(v^{\prime}|v)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma(t)\Delta t,&v^{\prime}=\varnothing,\\
1-\sigma(t)\Delta t,&v^{\prime}=v.\end{cases}`$ |  | (4) |

Based on this independent token-level process, the sequence-level
transition probability between timesteps $`s`$ and $`t`$ with
$`0<s<t<1`$ can be derived as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{t|s}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{s})=(1-e^{-(\bar{\sigma}(t)-\bar{\sigma}(s))})^{|{\bm{x}}_{s}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}e^{-(\bar{\sigma}(t)-\bar{\sigma}(s))|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{s}).`$ |  | (5) |

Here, $`|{\bm{x}}|`$ denotes the length of the sequence $`{\bm{x}}`$,
$`\bar{\sigma}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\tau)d\tau`$ is the integrated
noise rate, and $`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{s})`$ is the number of
occurrences of $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ as distinct subsequences in
$`{\bm{x}}_{s}`$. ¹¹ 1 For example,
$`N(\texttt{<BOS>}\text{ b a g},\texttt{<BOS>}\text{ b a b g b a g})=5`$.
The distinct subsequences are (highlighted in bold): \<BOS\> b a b g b a
g, \<BOS\> b a b g b a g, \<BOS\> b a b g b a g, \<BOS\> b a b g b a g,
\<BOS\> b a b g b a g. This number accounts for the multiplicity of all
the possible independent deletion paths from $`{\bm{x}}_{s}`$ to
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$; see
Appendix [D.2](#A4.SS2 "D.2 Derivation of the Sequence-Level Transition Probability (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
for the proof of
Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.1 Forward Process: Deletion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").

The infinitesimal transition of the forward process is captured by the
sequence-level transition rate matrix $`Q_{t}`$. Due to token-wise
independence, at most one deletion can occur within an infinitesimal
time interval with non-negligible $`(\Omega(\Delta t))`$ probability.
Thus, the rate $`Q_{t}(\bm{y},\bm{x}_{t})`$ is non-zero only when
$`\bm{y}=\bm{x}_{t}`$ or $`{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}`$, i.e.
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ is the result of deleting exactly one token from
$`{\bm{y}}`$, and the rate for $`{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ is
(details in
Appendix [D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Derivation of the Transition Rate (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\underset{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}{\text{lim}}\frac{p_{t+\Delta t|t}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{y}})}{\Delta t}=\sigma(t)N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}).`$ |  | (6) |

Note that, in our implementation, we prepend an undeletable special
token \<BOS\> at the beginning of each sequence, so the above
derivations should exclude \<BOS\>. Therefore, the fully noised sequence
is a single \<BOS\>, which also serves as the initial input token at the
first generation step to represent an empty sequence.

### 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion

As in
Sec. [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Continuous-Time Discrete Diffusion ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Works ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"),
we aim to learn the time reversal of the forward process, a CTMC with
rate matrix
$`\tilde{Q}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})s({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}`$,
where $`s`$ is the concrete score. Since $`Q_{t}`$ involves single-token
deletions, the backward process $`\tilde{Q}_{t}`$ only considers
single-token insertions (i.e., $`{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}`$).

Directly applying the concrete score learning approach used in MDLMs is
impractical here. The reason is that given $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$, the number
of possible resulting states $`{\bm{y}}`$ is variable, because different
insertions can lead to the same result.²² 2 For example, inserting ‘a’
after the 1st or 2nd index of ‘\<BOS\> b a g’ both yield ‘\<BOS\> b a a
g’. Consequently, targeting the concrete score requires calculating the
number of possible $`{\bm{y}}`$ values and enabling the model to produce
a variable-shaped output representing the concrete score for each
$`{\bm{y}}`$. These requirements collectively introduce significant
complexity and implementation challenges.

As an alternative, we target the insertion score $`\bar{s}`$. We learn a
score for every insertion, regardless of whether the resulting
$`{\bm{y}}`$ are identical. We define an insertion action $`(i,v)`$ as
inserting token $`v`$ after³³ 3 We use a prepended, non-deletable
\<BOS\> token (index 0) for insertions at the start. the $`i`$-th
position of $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$, resulting in
$`\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v)=({\bm{x}}_{\leq i},v,{\bm{x}}_{>i})`$.
The definition of insertion score is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})]},\quad\forall(i,v)\in[0,|{\bm{x}}_{t}|)_{\mathbb{Z}}\times\mathcal{V},`$ |  | (7) |

whose shape is $`|{\bm{x}}_{t}|\times|\mathcal{V}|`$, tractable for
transformer-based models.

Since the CTMC dynamics are based on the concrete score, to sample with
the insertion score, we show that the concrete score is a scaled average
of insertion scores, and the reverse transition rate is a summation of
the rate of insertion actions (details in
Appendix [D.4](#A4.SS4 "D.4 Derivation of the Relationship between Concrete Score and Insertion Score (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle s({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{\bm{y}}=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})],`$ |  | (8) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\underbrace{\left(\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})]\right)}_{\text{Rate of action }(i,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}))},`$ |  | (9) |

where $`I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ is the set of viable insertion
positions and $`v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ is the inserted token from
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ to $`{\bm{y}}`$.⁴⁴ 4 For example, if
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}=\texttt{<BOS>}\text{ b a g}`$ and
$`{\bm{y}}=\texttt{<BOS>}\text{ b a a g}`$, then
$`\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},1,\text{a})=\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},2,\text{a})={\bm{y}}`$,
$`v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=\text{a}`$,
$`I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=\{1,2\}`$, and
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=|I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})|=2`$.

Based on
Eq. [9](#S3.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"),
we can transform the concrete score-based sampling in
Eq. [2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.1 Continuous-Time Discrete Diffusion ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Works ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
into an equivalent insertion score-based sampling without computing
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$, $`I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$, or
$`v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ (details in
Appendix [D.5](#A4.SS5 "D.5 Derivation of the Sampling Probability (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle p_{t-\Delta t|t}^{\theta}((i,v)|{\bm{x}}_{t})=\begin{cases}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\Delta t,&v\neq\varnothing,\\
1-\sum_{w\neq\varnothing}p_{t-\Delta t|t}^{\theta}((i,w)|{\bm{x}}_{t}),&v=\varnothing,\end{cases}`$ |  | (10) |

where $`\varnothing`$ indicates no insertion, and
Tau-leaping ([Gillespie, 2001](#bib.bib14)), a very popular approximate
simulation method, could be adopted to sample all insertions
simultaneously for parallel decoding.

### 3.3 Training Objective: Denoising Insertion Score Entropy

We aim to train the insertion score $`\bar{s}_{\theta}`$ using the DSE
objective
(Eq. [3](#S2.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 2.1 Continuous-Time Discrete Diffusion ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Works ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
with the derived transition rate $`Q({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})`$
(Eq. [6](#S3.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 3.1 Forward Process: Deletion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
conditional distribution $`p_{t|s}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{s})`$
(Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.1 Forward Process: Deletion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
and parameterized concrete score $`s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{\bm{y}}`$
(Eq. [8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
for DID. Here, directly substituting $`s_{\theta}`$
(Eq. [8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
into DSE is challenging. Since $`s_{\theta}`$ involves a summation of
insertion scores $`\bar{s}_{\theta}`$, it results in an intractable
log-sum structure within the DSE objective
(Appendix [D.6](#A4.SS6 "D.6 Derivation of the DISE Objective (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
we apply Jensen’s inequality to derive a tractable variational upper
bound, the Denoising Insertion Score Entropy (DISE).

###### Proposition 1 (Denoising Insertion Score Entropy (DISE)).

The DSE objective for the deletion-insertion process is upper bounded by
the DISE objective,
$`\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{\text{DISE}}({\bm{x}}_{0})`$,
which is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{\text{DISE}}({\bm{x}}_{0})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\Bigg\{{\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{i,v}\bigg[\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]-\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]+C\bigg]\Bigg\}},`$ |  | (11) |

where
$`C=K\big(\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\big)`$
is a $`\theta`$-free constant, $`t\sim\text{Unif}([0,1])`$, and
$`\bm{x}_{t}\sim p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})`$.

The proof
(Appendix [D.6](#A4.SS6 "D.6 Derivation of the DISE Objective (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
utilizes Jensen’s inequality and a summation identity
(Lemma [1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1 (Summation Change of Variables). ‣ D.6 Derivation of the DISE Objective (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
to transform the objective from state-based ($`{\bm{y}}`$) to
action-based ($`(i,v)`$).

### 3.4 Efficient Parallel Dynamic Programming for Subsequence Counting Problems

A fundamental challenge for the DISE objective
(Eq. [11](#S3.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ Proposition 1 (Denoising Insertion Score Entropy (DISE)). ‣ 3.3 Training Objective: Denoising Insertion Score Entropy ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
is to efficiently solve the ratios of
$`N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})`$ and
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})`$ for all possible insertions of
$`(i,v)`$ on $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$. Suppose the lengths of $`{\bm{x}}_{0}`$
and $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ are $`m`$ and $`n`$, solving a single subsequence
counting problem of $`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})`$ has a well-known
time complexity of $`O(mn)`$ through dynamic programming, performing it
naively for $`n\times V`$ times would be prohibitive. Here, we show that
$`N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})`$ for all $`(i,v)`$ pairs
could be efficiently solved based on the intermediate results of solving
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})`$ just twice (via a prefix DP in
Eq. [13](#S3.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ 3.4 Efficient Parallel Dynamic Programming for Subsequence Counting Problems ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
and a suffix DP in
Eq. [14](#S3.E14 "Equation 14 ‣ 3.4 Efficient Parallel Dynamic Programming for Subsequence Counting Problems ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
reducing the time complexity to compute all ratios from $`O(mn^{2}V)`$
to $`O(mn)`$, and making the training of DID practical.

Counting $`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})`$. Here we briefly introduce the
classic prefix DP and suffix DP solutions to this problem. Using
Python’s slicing syntax, the base cases for empty sequences are
initialized as $`1`$ (other entries are $`0`$):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[:0]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[:j]})=N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[n:]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j:]})=1,\quad\forall{j}\in\{{0,...,m}\}.`$ |  | (12) |

The prefix DP iteratively computes
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[:i]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[:j]})`$ from the solved subproblems
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[:i]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[:j-1]})`$ and
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[:i-1]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[:j-1]})`$ to get the final result
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[:n]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[:m]})`$, and the state transition
is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[:i]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[:j]})=N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[:i]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[:j-1]})+\delta({\bm{x}}_{t}{[i-1]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j-1]})\cdot N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[:i-1]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[:j-1]}).`$ |  | (13) |

The suffix DP iteratively computes
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[i:]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j:]})`$ from the solved subproblems
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[i:]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j+1:]})`$ and
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[i+1:]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j+1:]})`$ to get the final result
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t}[0:],{\bm{x}}_{0}[0:])`$, and the state transition is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[i:]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j:]})=N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[i:]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j+1:]})+\delta({\bm{x}}_{t}{[i]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j]})\cdot N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[i+1:]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j+1:]}).`$ |  | (14) |

The time complexities are $`O(mn)`$ for both the prefix and suffix DPs.
Notably, they could be batched and parallelized along the
$`i`$-dimension, thus supporting vectorization and parallel execution,
and it only needs to sequentially loop over the $`j`$-dimension for
$`m`$ times.

Counting $`N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})`$. It could be
efficiently solved based on the results of prefix and suffix DP:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})=\underbrace{\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{m}\Big[\delta({\bm{x}}_{0}{[j]},v)}_{\text{index addition}}\cdot\underbrace{N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[:i]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[:j-1]})}_{\text{prefix DP result}}\cdot\underbrace{N({\bm{x}}_{t}{[i:]},{\bm{x}}_{0}{[j:]})}_{\text{suffix DP result}}\Big],
``` |  | (15) |

due to the form of
Eq. [15](#S3.E15 "Equation 15 ‣ 3.4 Efficient Parallel Dynamic Programming for Subsequence Counting Problems ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"),
results for all $`(i,v)`$ pairs can be solved in parallel with an
elementwise multiplication of the prefix and suffix DP result matrices,
followed by an index addition that could be efficiently implemented with
a sparse tensor coalescence. Algorithm details and a PyTorch
implementation are in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Algorithm Details ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").

### 3.5 Simplified Model for Fixed-Length Setting

To facilitate a fair comparison with MDLMs on the widely-adopted
fixed-length language modeling benchmarks
(Tab. [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 DID for Fixed-Length Language Modeling ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
and clearly isolate the superior FLOPs efficiency of DID, we develop a
set of optimizations to enhance DID in the fixed-length setting. We show
that when $`|{\bm{x}}_{0}|`$ is a constant, 1) the insertion score
becomes time-independent as the time-dependent terms of
$`(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}`$ in
Eq. [7](#S3.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
could be canceled out, which leads to 2) a sequence-level normalization
property (details in
Appendix [D.7](#A4.SS7 "D.7 Derivation of the Sequence-Level Normalization Property (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i,v}\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]=|{\bm{x}}_{0}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|.`$ |  | (16) |

This benefits the parameterization and training of DID from two aspects.
First, the time-independence means that the network does not require
time $`t`$ as input in the fixed-length setting, thus the
parameterization reduces to $`\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})`$, saves
the parameters for time embedding, and enables a cache mechanism similar
to ([Ou et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)) if the sequence is not changed
between steps. Second, the output of the insertion score network could
be explicitly normalized with a summation of
$`|{\bm{x}}_{0}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|`$ as in
Eq. [16](#S3.E16 "Equation 16 ‣ 3.5 Simplified Model for Fixed-Length Setting ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").
Therefore, the summation term of outputs from the insertion score
network in the DISE objective
(Eq. [11](#S3.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ Proposition 1 (Denoising Insertion Score Entropy (DISE)). ‣ 3.3 Training Objective: Denoising Insertion Score Entropy ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
turns into a constant, giving rise to a simplified Denoising Insertion
Cross Entropy (DICE) objective (details in
Appendix [D.8](#A4.SS8 "D.8 Derivation of the DICE Objective for Fixed-Length Data (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DICE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\Bigg\{\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{\sum_{i,v}\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\bigg[-\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]+C\bigg]\Bigg\}},`$ |  | (17) |

where
$`C=\log\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}`$
is a $`\theta`$-free constant. DICE can be interpreted as a weighted
cross-entropy loss between the predicted insertion scores and the ground
truth of subsequence count ratios, hence the name.

(Sec. [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Efficient Parallel Dynamic Programming for Subsequence Counting Problems ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
which is a constant overhead independent of the model size, thus the
speedup could be scaled up with model size. Besides, a less developed
system-level support for variable-length data also constrains the
speedups.

Generative performance. We report the generation quality and speed of
direct sampling for variable-length models in
Tab. [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.2 DID for Variable-Length Language Modeling ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").
Compared with both baselines, DID maintains a significantly lower
generative PPL (evaluated by GPT2 Large) with relatively high diversity
and is more stable across different numbers of steps. This is important
because strong sensitivity and non-convergence to the manually
predefined number of generation steps are undesirable.

Regarding inference speed, DID achieves a speedup of up to
3.79$`\times`$ compared to RADD, due to the savings of \<MASK\> and
\<PAD\> tokens for both model calling and distribution sampling. On the
other hand, ILM achieves the fastest speed despite its lowest quality.
We credit this to its over-simplified generation mechanism, which
samples a token at exactly one designated position per step. In
contrast, DID and RADD sample from all possible token positions to
enable parallel decoding. ILM benefits considerably from this
simplification, since categorical sampling is a major bottleneck in
small models ([Zheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib50)). Moreover, ILM is
limited to generating text shorter than the total steps (see
Tab. [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.2 DID for Variable-Length Language Modeling ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
which also contributes to its faster sampling.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce DID to improve the computational efficiency
and generation flexibility of diffusion language models by eliminating
the use of \<MASK\> and \<PAD\> tokens in our paradigm. Theoretically,
we formulate the diffusion processes for deletion and insertion, define
an insertion score, derive and implement the corresponding training
objectives and sampling algorithm for DID. We evaluated DID on modeling
performance, generation quality, and training/inference speed,
demonstrating the superiority of DID over the baselines of MDLM and
existing insertion-based LMs in both fixed-length and variable-length
settings. A discussion of the limitations and future works of this paper
is provided in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Limitations and Future Works ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").

## Appendix A Limitations and Future Works

This work presents the core framework of DID and, unlike the more
established MDLMs, has not yet integrated many optimizations, such as
advanced inference algorithms ([Wu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib45); [Wang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib44)), or hybrid models combining autoregressive
approaches ([Arriola et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Sahoo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37)). Since these optimizations are not inherently tied to
a specific diffusion process, adapting them to DID represents a
promising future direction. Second, although we have demonstrated the
effectiveness, efficiency, and flexibility of DID, our models were
trained at a relatively small scale due to resource constraints. As a
result, their performance on larger and more complex tasks remains
unexplored, and we leave scaling up DID to future work.

Following ICLR guidelines, we wish to clarify our use of Large Language

The research ideas, methodology, experimental design, and analysis

## Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations

### D.1 Derivation of the Denoising Score Entropy Loss (Eq.[3](#S2.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 2.1 Continuous-Time Discrete Diffusion ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Works ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))

The training objective for discrete diffusion models is derived by
minimizing a variational upper bound on the negative log-likelihood
(NLL) of the data, $`-\log p_{0}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})`$.

Let $`\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}`$ denote the path measure (the
probability distribution over entire trajectories) of the true posterior
reverse process conditioned on the data $`{\bm{x}}_{0}`$. Let
$`\mathbb{P}^{\theta}`$ denote the path measure of the learned reverse
process parameterized by $`\theta`$. By the data processing inequality,
we have:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle-\log p_{0}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\delta_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\|p_{0}^{\theta}\right)\leq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta}).`$ |  | (18) |

We define the training objective as this variational upper bound:
$`\mathcal{L}(\theta)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\theta})`$,
assuming both processes share the same prior distribution at $`t=1`$.

Both processes are Continuous-Time Markov Chains (CTMCs). Let
$`\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ denote the true
conditional reverse transition rate (for $`\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}`$)
and $`\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ denote the
parameterized reverse transition rate (for $`\mathbb{P}^{\theta}`$).

The KL divergence between path measures can be decomposed using the
chain rule. If we consider a discrete-time approximation with
infinitesimal step $`\Delta t`$, the total KL divergence is the sum of
the expected KL divergences at each step. We analyze the KL divergence
between the infinitesimal transition probabilities
$`p^{0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})=\delta({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})+\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\Delta t+o(\Delta t)`$
and
$`p^{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})=\delta({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})+\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\Delta t+o(\Delta t)`$.
The instantaneous KL divergence is ([Opper & Sanguinetti,
2007](#bib.bib30)):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\lx@nobreakspace\lx@nobreakspace D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{0}(\cdot|{\bm{x}}_{t})\|p^{\theta}(\cdot|{\bm{x}}_{t}))=\Delta t\sum_{{\bm{y}}\neq{\bm{x}}_{t}}\left(\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\log\frac{\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}{\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}+\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})-\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\right)+o(\Delta t).`$ |  | (19) |

Summing these contributions and taking the continuous limit
($`\Delta t\to 0`$) yields the integral form for the path KL divergence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\int_{0}^{1}\underset{{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim p_{t|0}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{{\bm{y}}\neq{\bm{x}}_{t}}\left(\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\log\frac{\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}{\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}+\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})-\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\right)\right]dt.`$ |  | (20) |

We now substitute the specific definitions of these transition rates.
The true conditional reverse rate $`\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}`$ is related
to the forward rate $`Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})`$ by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{0}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}.`$ |  | (21) |

The parameterized reverse rate $`\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}`$ is defined
using the score network $`s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}.`$ |  | (22) |

We substitute these rates
(Eq.[21](#A4.E21 "Equation 21 ‣ D.1 Derivation of the Denoising Score Entropy Loss (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
and
Eq.[22](#A4.E22 "Equation 22 ‣ D.1 Derivation of the Denoising Score Entropy Loss (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
into the expression inside the summation in
Eq.[20](#A4.E20 "Equation 20 ‣ D.1 Derivation of the Denoising Score Entropy Loss (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").
The expression becomes:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\left(Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)\log\frac{Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}}{Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}}\lx@nobreakspace+Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}-Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}.`$ |  | (23) |

We simplify by canceling $`Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})`$ inside the
logarithm and factoring it out from the entire expression:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle=Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\left[\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log\frac{\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}}{s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}}+s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}-\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right].`$ |  | (24) |

We expand the logarithm ($`\log(A/B)=\log A-\log B`$) and rearrange the
terms:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\Bigg[\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\left(\log\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}-\log s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}\right)+s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}-\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\Bigg]`$ |  | (25) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\Bigg[s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}-\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}\lx@nobreakspace\lx@nobreakspace+\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\left(\log\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}-1\right)\Bigg].`$ |  | (26) |

Letting $`K(a)=a(\log a-1)`$. Recognizing that the time integral
$`\int_{0}^{1}dt`$ combined with the expectation over
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}\sim p_{t|0}`$ is equivalent to the expectation over
uniform time $`t\sim U(0,1)`$ and the corresponding conditional
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$, the objective $`\mathcal{L}(\theta)`$ is exactly the
DSE loss
(Eq.[3](#S2.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 2.1 Continuous-Time Discrete Diffusion ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Works ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\neq{\bm{x}}_{t}}Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\Bigg[s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}-\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}+K\left(\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)\Bigg].`$ |  | (27) |

### D.2 Derivation of the Sequence-Level Transition Probability (Eq.[5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.1 Forward Process: Deletion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))

We derive the sequence-level transition probability
$`p_{t|s}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{s})`$ based on the definition of the
DID forward process as an independent token-level deletion process. We
begin by analyzing the dynamics of a single token.

The token-level process
(Eq.[4](#S3.E4 "Equation 4 ‣ 3.1 Forward Process: Deletion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
is a Continuous-Time Markov Chain (CTMC) on the state space
$`\{v,\varnothing\}`$, where $`v\in\mathcal{V}`$ denotes the presence of
a token and $`\varnothing`$ denotes the deleted state. The transition
rate matrix $`Q_{t}^{\text{tok}}`$ at time $`t`$, indexed by
$`(v,\varnothing)`$, is defined by the deletion rate $`\sigma(t)`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle Q_{t}^{\text{tok}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sigma(t)&\sigma(t)\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.`$ |  | (28) |

Let $`P_{v}(\tau)`$ be the probability that the token is in state $`v`$
at time $`\tau\in[s,t]`$, given it started in state $`v`$ at time $`s`$
($`P_{v}(s)=1`$). The evolution of this probability follows the
Kolmogorov forward equation:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{dP_{v}(\tau)}{d\tau}`$ | $`\displaystyle=P_{v}(\tau)Q_{\tau}^{\text{tok}}(v,v)+P_{\varnothing}(\tau)Q_{\tau}^{\text{tok}}(\varnothing,v)=-\sigma(\tau)P_{v}(\tau).`$ |  | (29) |

Solving this first-order ordinary differential equation by integrating
from $`s`$ to $`t`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\frac{dP_{v}(\tau)}{P_{v}(\tau)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}-\sigma(\tau)d\tau\implies\ln(P_{v}(t))-\ln(P_{v}(s))=-(\bar{\sigma}(t)-\bar{\sigma}(s)).`$ |  | (30) |

Thus, the probability that a single token survives during the interval
$`[s,t]`$ is $`P_{v}(t)=e^{-(\bar{\sigma}(t)-\bar{\sigma}(s))}`$.
Conversely, the probability of deletion is
$`1-e^{-(\bar{\sigma}(t)-\bar{\sigma}(s))}`$.

We now consider the sequence-level transition from $`{\bm{x}}_{s}`$ to
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$. Let
$`\Delta\bar{\sigma}=\bar{\sigma}(t)-\bar{\sigma}(s)`$. A transition
occurs if the tokens forming $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ survive and the remaining
$`|{\bm{x}}_{s}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|`$ tokens are deleted. Since token
deletions are independent, the probability of a specific path (a
specific occurrence of $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ in $`{\bm{x}}_{s}`$) is
$`(e^{-\Delta\bar{\sigma}})^{|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}\times(1-e^{-\Delta\bar{\sigma}})^{|{\bm{x}}_{s}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}`$.

The total transition probability $`p_{t|s}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{s})`$
is the sum over all distinct paths, counted by the subsequence count
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{s})`$. Therefore, we obtain:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\lx@nobreakspace\lx@nobreakspace p_{t|s}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{s})=N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{s})(1-e^{-(\bar{\sigma}(t)-\bar{\sigma}(s))})^{|{\bm{x}}_{s}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}e^{-(\bar{\sigma}(t)-\bar{\sigma}(s))|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}.`$ |  | (31) |

### D.3 Derivation of the Transition Rate (Eq.[6](#S3.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 3.1 Forward Process: Deletion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))

We derive the transition rate $`Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})`$ from a
sequence $`{\bm{y}}`$ to a sequence $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$, where
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ is obtained from $`{\bm{y}}`$ by deleting a single
token (denoted as $`{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}`$). This implies
$`|{\bm{y}}|=|{\bm{x}}_{t}|+1`$.

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle\triangleq\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{p_{t+\Delta t|t}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{y}})}{\Delta t}`$ |  | (32) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{(1-e^{-(\bar{\sigma}(t+\Delta t)-\bar{\sigma}(t))})^{|{\bm{y}}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}e^{-(\bar{\sigma}(t+\Delta t)-\bar{\sigma}(t))|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}{\Delta t}`$ |  | (33) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{(1-e^{-\sigma(t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t)})^{1}\cdot e^{-(\sigma(t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t))|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}\cdot N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}{\Delta t}`$ |  | (34) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{(\sigma(t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t))\cdot(1-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|\sigma(t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t))\cdot N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}{\Delta t}`$ |  | (35) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\sigma(t)\Delta t+o(\Delta t)}{\Delta t}\cdot N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ |  | (36) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sigma(t)N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}).`$ |  | (37) |

### D.4 Derivation of the Relationship between Concrete Score and Insertion Score (Eq. [8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))

We aim to prove the identity stated in
Eq. [8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").
This identity concerns the backward process where transitions occur such
that $`{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ . Note that this condition
implies $`|{\bm{y}}|=|{\bm{x}}_{t}|+1`$.

First, we derive the explicit form of the concrete score
$`s({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{\bm{y}}=p_{t}({\bm{y}})/p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})`$ by
expanding the marginal distributions using the forward transition
probability
(Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "Equation 5 ‣ 3.1 Forward Process: Deletion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{\bm{y}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{p_{t}({\bm{y}})}{p_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})]}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|-|{\bm{y}}|}e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)|{\bm{y}}|}N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)|{\bm{y}}|}(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{-|{\bm{y}}|}}{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\overset{|{\bm{y}}|=|{\bm{x}}_{t}|+1}{=}\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]}.`$ |  | (38) |

Next, we examine the right-hand side (RHS) of
Eq. [8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
and substitute the definition of the insertion score $`\bar{s}`$
(Eq. [7](#S3.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | RHS | $`\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})]`$ |  | (39) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})),{\bm{x}}_{0})]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}\right).`$ |  | (40) |

By definition of the index set $`I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$, for any
$`i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$, we have
$`\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}))={\bm{y}}`$.
Therefore:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | RHS | $`\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}\right).`$ |  | (41) |

The term inside the summation is independent of the index $`i`$. We
factor it out and utilize the property that
$`\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}1=|I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})|=N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | RHS | $`\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}\right)N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ |  | (42) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]}.`$ |  | (43) |

This matches the derived concrete score in
Eq. [38](#A4.E38 "Equation 38 ‣ D.4 Derivation of the Relationship between Concrete Score and Insertion Score (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"),
completing the proof.

### D.5 Derivation of the Sampling Probability (Eq.[10](#S3.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))

We aim to derive the probability of executing a specific insertion
operation—inserting token $`v`$ after position $`i`$—within an
infinitesimal time interval $`[t-\Delta t,t]`$ during the backward
process.

The backward process is a CTMC characterized by the parameterized
reverse transition rate matrix $`\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}`$. The rate of
transition from state $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ to a different state $`{\bm{y}}`$
is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\lx@nobreakspace\lx@nobreakspace\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}.`$ |  | (44) |

In the DID framework, backward transitions occur only when
$`{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}`$. We substitute the forward transition
rate
(Eq.[6](#S3.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 3.1 Forward Process: Deletion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
$`Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})=\sigma(t)N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$. We
also substitute the parameterized concrete score $`s_{\theta}`$, which
is derived by parameterizing the relationship between the concrete score
and the insertion score
(Eq.[8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{\bm{y}}=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{j\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[j,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})].`$ |  | (45) |

Substituting these expressions into the definition of
$`\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left(\sigma(t)N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\right)\cdot\left(\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{j\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[j,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})]\right)`$ |  | (46) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sigma(t)\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{j\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[j,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})].`$ |  | (47) |

This result demonstrates that the total transition rate from
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ to $`{\bm{y}}`$ is decomposed into a summation of
individual components. Each term in the summation,
$`\sigma(t)\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[j,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})]`$,
corresponds to the instantaneous rate of the specific insertion
operation $`(j,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}))`$ that transforms
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ into $`{\bm{y}}`$.

By the definition of a CTMC, the probability of a specific operation
$`(i,v)`$ occurring within the infinitesimal interval $`\Delta t`$ is
given by its corresponding rate multiplied by $`\Delta t`$. For
$`v\neq\varnothing`$, we identify this probability directly from the
decomposition above:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{t-\Delta t|t}^{\theta}((i,v)|{\bm{x}}_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left(\sigma(t)\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\right)\Delta t+o(\Delta t)`$ |  | (48) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\Delta t+o(\Delta t).`$ |  | (49) |

This confirms the first case of
Eq.[10](#S3.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").
The probability of no insertion occurring at position $`i`$ (i.e.,
$`v=\varnothing`$) is determined by the normalization constraint,
ensuring the sum of probabilities for all possible events at that
position equals 1.

To confirm the equivalence between this action-based sampling and the
required state-based transition, we verify that the action probabilities
correctly recover the state transition probability
$`p_{t-\Delta t|t}^{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})`$. A transition to
$`{\bm{y}}`$ occurs if any of the actions indexed by
$`I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ occurs. In the infinitesimal limit
$`\Delta t\to 0`$, these actions are mutually exclusive events:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{t-\Delta t|t}^{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}p_{t-\Delta t|t}^{\theta}((j,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}))|{\bm{x}}_{t})+o(\Delta t)`$ |  | (50) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left(\sigma(t)\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{j\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[j,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})]\right)\Delta t+o(\Delta t).`$ |  | (51) |

By
Eq. [47](#A4.E47 "Equation 47 ‣ D.5 Derivation of the Sampling Probability (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"),
the term in the parenthesis is exactly
$`\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$. Thus,
$`p_{t-\Delta t|t}^{\theta}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})=\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\Delta t+o(\Delta t)`$,
confirming that the action-based sampling correctly implements the
dynamics of the backward CTMC.

### D.6 Derivation of the DISE Objective (Eq.[11](#S3.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ Proposition 1 (Denoising Insertion Score Entropy (DISE)). ‣ 3.3 Training Objective: Denoising Insertion Score Entropy ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))

We derive the Denoising Insertion Score Entropy (DISE) objective
(Eq.[11](#S3.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ Proposition 1 (Denoising Insertion Score Entropy (DISE)). ‣ 3.3 Training Objective: Denoising Insertion Score Entropy ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
starting from the general DSE objective
(Eq.[3](#S2.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 2.1 Continuous-Time Discrete Diffusion ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Works ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
demonstrating that DISE is a variational upper bound on DSE,
$`\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{\text{DISE}}({\bm{x}}_{0})\geq\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})`$.

We begin with the DSE objective:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}{\sum_{{\bm{y}}\neq{\bm{x}}_{t}}Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})\Bigg[s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}-\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}+K\left(\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)\Bigg]},`$ |  | (52) |

where $`K(a)=a(\log a-1)`$. In the deletion–insertion process, the
transition rate $`Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})`$ is non-zero only when
$`{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}`$.

We now substitute the specific definitions for the DID process. The
transition rate is
$`Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})=\sigma(t)N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$. The
conditional probability ratio is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0})}=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}.`$ |  | (53) |

The parameterized concrete score (from parameterized
Eq.[8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})].`$ |  | (54) |

We examine the expression inside the bracket of the DSE objective by
substituting these definitions. Let $`v=v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ for
brevity in the following block:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}-\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log s_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)_{{\bm{y}}}+K\!\left(\frac{p_{t|0}({\bm{y}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0})}{p_{t|0}({\bm{x}}_{t}\mid{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)`$ |  | (55) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\left(\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\left(\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)\log\!\left(\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\left(\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\right)\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+K\!\left(\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right).`$ |  |  |

We expand the $`K(\cdot)`$ term using $`K(a)=a(\log a-1)`$. By expanding
the logarithms ($`\log(AB)=\log A+\log B`$), we observe that the terms
involving the time factor
$`\log(\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}})`$ cancel out
exactly.

The expression inside the bracket simplifies significantly by factoring
out the time prefactor:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\left(\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\right)`$ |  | (56) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log\left(\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\left(\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\right)\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\left(\log\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}-1\right)\Bigg].`$ |  |  |

Substituting this simplified bracket back into the main objective
equation and multiplying by
$`Q_{t}({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{t})=\sigma(t)N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$. The
DSE objective can be decomposed into three terms (T1, T2, T3):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\text{T1}+\text{T2}+\text{T3}.`$ |  | (57) |

We define these terms based on the components derived above:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | T1 | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\left[\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\right].`$ |  | (58) |
|  | T2 | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\left[-\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log\left(\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\right)\right].`$ |  | (59) |
|  | T3 | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})K\left(\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right).`$ |  | (60) |

We now apply Jensen’s inequality to T2. The term inside the logarithm is
an average of insertion scores. Because the logarithm function is
concave, $`\log(\frac{1}{N}\sum a_{i})\geq\frac{1}{N}\sum\log a_{i}`$.
Since the logarithm is negated, this leads to an upper bound on T2:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle-\log\left(\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\right)\leq-\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v].`$ |  | (61) |

We define T2$`{}_{\text{Bound}}`$ as the upper bound for T2:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{T2}\leq\text{T2}_{\text{Bound}}=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}}N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\left[-\frac{1}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]\right].`$ |  | (62) |

We define the DISE objective as the upper bound obtained by replacing T2
with T2$`{}_{\text{Bound}}`$, ensuring
$`\mathcal{L}^{\text{DISE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})\geq\mathcal{L}^{\text{DSE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DISE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\text{T1}+\text{T2}_{\text{Bound}}+\text{T3}.`$ |  | (63) |

To simplify the nested summations and transform the objective from
state-level ($`{\bm{y}}`$) to operation-level ($`(i,v)`$), we rely on
the following identity.

###### Lemma 1 (Summation Change of Variables).

Let $`v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ denote the unique inserted token and
$`I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ the set of valid insertion positions that
yield $`{\bm{y}}`$ from $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$. For any function $`G`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}}\ \sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}G\!\big(i,\,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}),\,{\bm{y}}\big)\;=\;\sum_{i,v}\ G\!\big(i,\,v,\,\mathrm{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v)\big).`$ |  | (64) |

###### Proof.

Define the index sets

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{({\bm{y}},i):\ {\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t},\ i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})\},\qquad\mathcal{B}=\{(i,v):\ i\in\{0,\dots,|{\bm{x}}_{t}|\},v\in\mathcal{V}\}.`$ |  | (65) |

The map $`g:\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{A}`$ defined by
$`g(i,v)=(\mathrm{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),\,i)`$ is a bijection, with its
inverse $`f:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}`$ defined by
$`f({\bm{y}},i)=(i,\,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}}))`$. Changing variables
over this bijection gives the claimed identity. ∎

We apply
Lemma [1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1 (Summation Change of Variables). ‣ D.6 Derivation of the DISE Objective (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
to simplify T1, T2$`{}_{\text{Bound}}`$, and T3.

For T1, the $`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ terms cancel before the
summation.

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | T1 | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})]`$ |  | (66) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{i,v}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v].\quad\text{(Using Lemma~\ref{lemma:summation_identity})}`$ |  | (67) |

For T2$`{}_{\text{Bound}}`$, cancellation of
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})`$ yields:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{T2}_{\text{Bound}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}-\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})].`$ |  | (68) |

Using
Lemma [1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1 (Summation Change of Variables). ‣ D.6 Derivation of the DISE Objective (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"),
noting that $`{\bm{y}}=\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v)`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{T2}_{\text{Bound}}=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{\sum_{i,v}-\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]}.`$ |  | (69) |

For T3, we first utilize the fact that
$`N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})=\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}1`$.

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | T3 | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{{\bm{y}}\succ_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t}}\sum_{i\in I({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{y}})}K\left(\frac{N({\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right).`$ |  | (70) |

Applying
Lemma [1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1 (Summation Change of Variables). ‣ D.6 Derivation of the DISE Objective (Eq.) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
to T3:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{T3}=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{\sum_{i,v}K\left(\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)}.`$ |  | (71) |

We combine T1, T2$`{}_{\text{Bound}}`$, and T3 to obtain the final DISE
objective. Let $`C`$ denote the constant term T3’s summand.

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{\text{DISE}}({\bm{x}}_{0})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\text{T1}+\text{T2}_{\text{Bound}}+\text{T3}`$ |  | (72) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\Bigg\{{\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{i,v}\bigg[\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]-\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)[i,v]+C\bigg]\Bigg\}}.`$ |  | (73) |

### D.7 Derivation of the Sequence-Level Normalization Property (Eq. [16](#S3.E16 "Equation 16 ‣ 3.5 Simplified Model for Fixed-Length Setting ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))

In the fixed-length setting (Sec.
[3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Simplified Model for Fixed-Length Setting ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
we assume $`|{\bm{x}}_{0}|=K`$ (a constant) for all data samples. Under
this assumption, the insertion score becomes time-independent. We aim to
prove the normalization property
(Eq. [16](#S3.E16 "Equation 16 ‣ 3.5 Simplified Model for Fixed-Length Setting ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
restated here for the fixed-length context:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i,v}\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]=K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|.`$ |  | (74) |

The proof relies on a fundamental combinatorial identity regarding
subsequence counts.

###### Lemma 2 (Subsequence Count Identity).

For any sequences $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ and $`{\bm{x}}_{0}`$ such that
$`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ is a subsequence of $`{\bm{x}}_{0}`$, the following
identity holds:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{i,v}N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})=N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})(|{\bm{x}}_{0}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|).
``` |  | (75) |

###### Proof of Lemma [2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma 2 (Subsequence Count Identity). ‣ D.7 Derivation of the Sequence-Level Normalization Property (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").

We prove the statement
$`\sum_{i,v}N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})=N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})(|{\bm{x}}_{0}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|)`$
via a bijective proof, by constructing two sets of equal cardinality.
Let $`S({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})`$ denote the set of index tuples
corresponding to all occurrences of a subsequence $`{\bm{x}}`$ in
$`{\bm{z}}`$, such that $`N({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})=|S({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})|`$.

First, consider the set A, defined as the set of pairs $`(I,j)`$, where
$`I`$ is the index tuple of an occurrence of $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ in
$`{\bm{x}}_{0}`$, and $`j`$ is an index in $`{\bm{x}}_{0}`$ that is not
part of that occurrence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A=\{(I,j):I\in S({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0}),j\in\{1,\ldots,|{\bm{x}}_{0}|\}\setminus I\}.
``` |  | (76) |

The cardinality of A is
$`|A|=N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})(|{\bm{x}}_{0}|-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|)`$.

Second, consider the set B, defined as the set of pairs $`((i,v),J)`$,
where $`(i,v)`$ is an insertion operation on $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$, and $`J`$
is the index tuple of an occurrence of the resulting sequence,
$`\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v)`$, in $`{\bm{x}}_{0}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
B=\{((i,v),J):J\in S(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})\}.
``` |  | (77) |

The cardinality of B is
$`|B|=\sum_{i,v}N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})`$.

We now establish a bijection between A and B. For any element
$`(I,j)\in A`$, we define a mapping to an element in B as follows: let
the inserted token be $`v={\bm{x}}_{0}[j]`$, and let the insertion
position relative to $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ be $`i=|\{k\in I:k<j\}|`$. The new
index tuple is $`J=I\cup\{j\}`$, sorted. The subsequence
$`{\bm{x}}_{0}[J]`$ is precisely $`\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v)`$ by
construction. This defines a unique mapping $`f:A\to B`$.

Conversely, for any element $`((i,v),J)\in B`$, we can define an inverse
mapping. The index of the inserted token in $`{\bm{x}}_{0}`$ is the
$`(i+1)`$-th element of the sorted tuple $`J`$; let this be $`j`$.
Removing this index yields the tuple $`I=J\setminus\{j\}`$, which
corresponds to an occurrence of $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$. This defines a unique
mapping $`g:B\to A`$.

Since a one-to-one correspondence exists between the sets, their
cardinalities must be equal. Therefore, $`|A|=|B|`$, which proves the
lemma. ∎

Under the fixed-length assumption ($`|{\bm{x}}_{0}|=K`$), the definition
of the insertion score
(Eq. [7](#S3.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 3.2 Backward Process: Insertion ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
simplifies because the time-dependent terms
$`(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{|{\bm{x}}_{0}|}`$ are constant
$`(1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)})^{K}`$ and cancel out, leading to the
time-independent score:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}.`$ |  | (78) |

We sum this score over all possible insertion operations $`(i,v)`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i,v}\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i,v}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}`$ |  | (79) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[\sum_{i,v}N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})\right].`$ |  | (80) |

We apply the combinatorial identity
(Lemma [2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma 2 (Subsequence Count Identity). ‣ D.7 Derivation of the Sequence-Level Normalization Property (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
to the summation inside the expectation, substituting
$`|{\bm{x}}_{0}|=K`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i,v}N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})=N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})(K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|).`$ |  | (81) |

Substituting this back:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i,v}\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})(K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|)\right].`$ |  | (82) |

Since $`(K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|)`$ is constant with respect to the expectation
over $`{\bm{x}}_{0}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i,v}\bar{s}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]`$ | $`\displaystyle=(K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|)\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\left[N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}}[N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})]}=K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|.`$ |  | (83) |

This confirms the normalization property stated in
Eq. [16](#S3.E16 "Equation 16 ‣ 3.5 Simplified Model for Fixed-Length Setting ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").

### D.8 Derivation of the DICE Objective for Fixed-Length Data (Eq. [17](#S3.E17 "Equation 17 ‣ 3.5 Simplified Model for Fixed-Length Setting ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))

In the fixed-length setting
(Section [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Simplified Model for Fixed-Length Setting ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
we assume $`|{\bm{x}}_{0}|=K`$ (constant). This assumption leads to
time-independent insertion scores
$`\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]`$ (as shown in
Appendix [D.7](#A4.SS7 "D.7 Derivation of the Sequence-Level Normalization Property (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"))
and allows for the exact simplification of the DISE objective into the
Denoising Insertion Cross-Entropy (DICE) objective.

We start from the DISE objective
(Eq.[11](#S3.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ Proposition 1 (Denoising Insertion Score Entropy (DISE)). ‣ 3.3 Training Objective: Denoising Insertion Score Entropy ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{\text{DISE}}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{i,v}\bigg[`$ | $`\displaystyle\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]-\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+K\left(\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)\bigg].`$ |  | (84) |

We rearrange the expression inside the square brackets using the
definition $`K(a)=a(\log a-1)`$.

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\bar{s}_{\theta}[i,v]-\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\log\bar{s}_{\theta}[i,v]+\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\left(\log\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}-1\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left(\bar{s}_{\theta}[i,v]-\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad+\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\left(\log\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}-\log\bar{s}_{\theta}[i,v]\right).`$ |  | (85) |

We substitute this rearrangement back into the DISE objective:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\theta}^{\text{DISE}}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{i,v}\Bigg[`$ | $`\displaystyle\left(\bar{s}_{\theta}[i,v]-\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\left(\log\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}-\log\bar{s}_{\theta}[i,v]\right)\Bigg].`$ |  | (86) |

We now utilize the crucial normalization properties derived from the
fixed-length assumption ($`|{\bm{x}}_{0}|=K`$). From
Lemma [2](#Thmlemma2 "Lemma 2 (Subsequence Count Identity). ‣ D.7 Derivation of the Sequence-Level Normalization Property (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
(Appendix [D.7](#A4.SS7 "D.7 Derivation of the Sequence-Level Normalization Property (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")),
the true subsequence count ratios satisfy:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i,v}\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}=K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|.`$ |  | (87) |

As discussed in
Section [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Simplified Model for Fixed-Length Setting ‣ 3 DID: Deletion-Insertion Diffusion Language Models ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"),
we design the network architecture such that the parameterized scores
$`\bar{s}_{\theta}`$ exactly satisfy the same normalization constraint:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i,v}\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]=K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|.`$ |  | (88) |

Because both the true ratios and the parameterized scores sum to the
same value, the summation of the first group of terms in
Eq. [85](#A4.E85 "Equation 85 ‣ D.8 Derivation of the DICE Objective for Fixed-Length Data (Eq. ) ‣ Appendix D Detailed Proofs and Derivations ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")
vanishes:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{i,v}\left(\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]-\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\right)=(K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|)-(K-|{\bm{x}}_{t}|)=0.`$ |  | (89) |

Therefore, the DISE objective simplifies exactly. We define this
simplified form as the DICE objective, which is exactly equal to the
DISE loss under the fixed-length setting
($`\mathcal{L}^{\text{DICE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\mathcal{L}^{\text{DISE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})`$):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DICE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{i,v}\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\left(\log\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}-\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]\right).`$ |  | (90) |

Rearranging the terms inside the summation yields the final DICE
objective:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{DICE}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{0})=\underset{t,{\bm{x}}_{t}}{\mathbb{E}}\Bigg\{{\sum_{i,v}\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\bar{\sigma}(t)}}\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}\bigg[-\log\bar{s}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t})[i,v]+C\bigg]\Bigg\}},`$ |  | (91) |

where
$`C=\log\frac{N(\text{Ins}({\bm{x}}_{t},i,v),{\bm{x}}_{0})}{N({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{0})}`$
is a $`\theta`$-free constant.

## Appendix F Experimental Details

### F.1 Fixed-Length Training Details

Model architecture. Following RADD, our models use an encoder-only
transformer architecture with a dropout rate 0.02, rotary position
embedding ([Su et al., 2023](#bib.bib43)), and untied word embeddings
between input and output. Other model architecture hyperparameters are
summarized in
Tab. [7](#A6.T7 "Table 7 ‣ F.1 Fixed-Length Training Details ‣ Appendix F Experimental Details ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").
The FFN dimension is 4$`\times`$ hidden dimension.

|        |        |                  |                 |                          |
|--------|--------|------------------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| Size   | Layers | Hidden Dimension | Attention Heads | Non-Embedding Parameters |
| Small  | 12     | 768              | 12              | 85M                      |
| Medium | 24     | 1024             | 16              | 302M                     |
| Large  | 36     | 1280             | 20              | 708M                     |

Table 7: Model architecture hyperparameters for difference model sizes
of RADD and DID.

We use FlashAttention ([Dao et al., 2022](#bib.bib9)) to support
attention computation for packed variable-length sequences, while RADD
uses PyTorch’s standard scaled dot product attention for its
fixed-length batched inputs, which is also based on FlashAttention.

Dataset and pre-processing. For a fair comparison, we train DID on
OpenWebText dataset ([Gokaslan & Cohen, 2019](#bib.bib15)) with the same
steps (400K steps) or compute budget (800K steps) as RADD ([Ou et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31)), under the log-linear noise schedule
$`\bar{\sigma}(t)=-\log(1-t)`$, we train DID with a batch size 512, and
a context length 1024. We tokenize OpenWebText dataset with the
GPT2 ([Radford et al., 2019](#bib.bib35)) tokenizer, the vocabulary size
is 50257. We follow the data pre-processing adopted in RADD,
concatenating all sequences in the training dataset, then splitting it
into chunks of fixed length 1024. We add a \<BOS\> special token to the
first position of each chunk to model the insertion behavior before the
first normal token by modeling the insertion after the \<BOS\> special
token. We use the same token as \<EOS\> for \<BOS\>. We evaluate the
zero-shot language modeling perplexity on WikiText, Lambada, Scientific
Papers (Arxiv and Pubmed, abstract parts), AG News, LM1B, and PTB
datasets ([Merity et al., 2017](#bib.bib25); [Paperno et al.,
2016](#bib.bib32); [Cohan et al., 2018](#bib.bib8); [Zhang et al.,
2015](#bib.bib48); [Chelba et al., 2014](#bib.bib5); [Marcus et al.,
1993](#bib.bib24)) in
Tab. [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 DID for Fixed-Length Language Modeling ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes").

Optimization. For a fair comparison, we did not do a hyperparameter
search, our optimization configuration strictly follows RADD, we use
AdamW ([Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019](#bib.bib21)) optimizer with
$`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.999`$, $`\epsilon=`$ 1e-8, a weight
decay rate of 0.03, a constant learning rate 3e-4 that linearly warmed
up from 0 over the first 2500 steps, a gradient norm clipping value 1,
an exponential moving average (EMA) with a decay rate 0.9999, and
float16 is enabled for mixed-precision training. To avoid OOM errors
during model training, we set the gradient accumulation steps to 1, 2, 2
for DID small, medium, and large; and 2, 4, 4 for RADD small, medium,
and large.

### F.2 Variable-Length Training Details

Model architecture. Following ILM ([Patel et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)), we
train a small model with 85M non-embedding parameters. Different from
the fixed-length model, the variable-length model is not
time-independent, i.e. we need to input the time information into the
network. Therefore, we employ an adaptive layernorm for each transformer
block as in the practice of DiT ([Peebles & Xie, 2023](#bib.bib34)) (as
well as the time-dependent masked diffusion models like SEDD ([Lou
et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)) and MDLM ([Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib36))),
the condition embedding dimension is 128, which brings about extra
parameters. To keep the total parameter amount of a transformer block
unchanged, we reduce the FFN dimension to 3.5$`\times`$ hidden
dimension. Following ILM, we use a dropout rate of 0.1, rotary
positional embedding, and untied word embeddings between input and
output. FlashAttention is also employed for the efficient computation of
variable-length data.

Dataset and pre-processing. Following ILM, we train variable-length
models on the Stories dataset. We tokenize it with the Bert-Base-Uncased
tokenizer, whose vocabulary size is 30522. Each sentence in the datasets
is an individual training datapoint; hence, the training sequences are
of variable lengths. The Stories dataset is truncated with a maximum
context length of 1024. The batch size is 512. Data is also noised with
a log-linear noise schedule in the diffusion forward process.

Optimization. Following the experiment settings of ILM, we use an AdamW
optimizer with $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$, $`\beta_{2}=0.999`$, $`\epsilon=`$
1e-8, a weight decay of 0.01 on all parameters (including biases and
normalization layers), a constant learning rate 1e-4 that linearly
warmed up from 0 over the first 1000 steps, a gradient norm clipping
value 1, an exponential moving average (EMA) with a decay rate 0.9999,
and bfloat16 is enabled for mixed-precision training. Stories dataset is
trained for 60k steps. To avoid OOM errors during model training, we set
the gradient accumulation steps to 1, 1, 1 for DID small, medium, and
large; and 2, 4, 4 for RADD small, medium, and large.

### F.3 Sampling Details

Timestep discretization. We use a standard uniform timestep
discretizaition grid, i.e. $`t(i)=i/N`$, where $`N`$ is the total number
of denoising steps in generation.

The number of samples. We evaluate the averaged generative perplexity
(evaluated by GPT2 Large), unigram entropy, generation length, and
inference time over 1024 samples with a batch size of 32.

Data precision. According to the precision issue of Gumbel-argmax
sampling with float32 discussed in ([Ou et al., 2025](#bib.bib31);
[Zheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib50)), we use float64 precision for all
generation tasks to ensure accurate categorical samplings.

### F.4 Evaluation Metrics

Zero-shot language modeling perplexity is used to evaluate how well a
model is trained, which uses the model to be evaluated to calculate the
exponential of the average negative log-likelihood per token on datasets
unseen by the model (i.e. zero-shot):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{PPL}=\exp\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}(x)}\Big[-\frac{\log p_{\theta}(x)}{L(x)}\Big]\Big),`$ |  | (92) |

where the likelihood can be exact (e.g. in auto-regressive models), or
bounded (e.g. in diffusion models), and $`L(x)`$ is the length of $`x`$.

Generative perplexity is a widely used metric to evaluate the quality of
model-generated text, whose definition is also as in
Eq.[92](#A6.E92 "Equation 92 ‣ F.4 Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix F Experimental Details ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes"),
the differences are $`p_{\text{data}}`$ is generated by the model to be
evaluated, and $`\theta`$ is another off-the-shelf model to calculate
the likelihood.

Unigram entropy is a metric to evaluate the diversity of model-generated
text, which is based on the token occurrence frequency in a sentence.
For a sentence $`x`$ with length $`L`$, the entropy is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle H=-\sum_{i=1}^{L}\frac{N(x_{i},x)}{L(x)}\log_{2}\frac{N(x_{i},x)}{L(x)},`$ |  | (93) |

where $`N(x_{i},x)`$ is the occurrence time of token $`x_{i}`$ in
sentence $`x`$, and $`L(x)`$ is the length of $`x`$.

## Appendix H More Discussions

Here we discuss the theoretical insights for the advantages of DID over
MDM, we additionally summarize four structural advantages that come from
using insertion instead of unmasking during generation.

Context-aware decoding order. In standard RADD sampling (e.g.,
$`\tau`$-leaping), the decision of which positions to update is governed
solely by the noise schedule (random unmasking). The probability that
any masked position $`i`$ is unmasked in a step is governed by a scalar
function $`\psi(t,s)`$, which depends only on the noise schedule (e.g.,
$`\frac{t-s}{t}`$ for log-linear):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\left[x_{s}^{(i)}\neq[\text{M}]\mid\bm{x}_{t}\right]=\psi(t,s).
``` |  |

The probability is uniform across all masked positions and independent
of the context $`\bm{x}_{t}^{\mathrm{UM}}`$. The learned model only
determines what token to fill in if a position is unmasked; it does not
influence where updates occur. When steps are few, this forces the model
to predict many tokens at random absolute positions simultaneously,
often leading to incoherence.

In contrast, DID learns where to insert based on the existing content
(via insertion score). The probability of an insertion after position
$`i`$ is directly determined by the learned insertion score
$`\bar{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t)`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{P}\left[\text{Insertion at position }i\mid\bm{x}_{t}\right]\approx\Delta t\cdot\frac{\sigma(t)e^{-\overline{\sigma}(t)}}{1-e^{-\overline{\sigma}(t)}}\sum_{v\neq\varnothing}\bar{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x}_{t},t)[i,v].
``` |  |

Crucially, this mechanism is content-dependent. The model naturally
chooses to generate tokens in positions where it is most probable first,
improving the quality of each sampling step.

Self-correction mechanism. RADD relies on absolute positions. Once a
token is unmasked, its content and position are fixed, so any early
suboptimal predictions (common in fast sampling) lead to error
accumulation. DID predicts relative order. Even if the model generates
imperfect tokens early on, it can still refine the sentence structure in
later steps by inserting new tokens between existing ones (see our
demonstration in
Appendix [I.3](#A9.SS3 "I.3 Demonstration of the Intermediate Generation Process ‣ Appendix I Generation Examples ‣ Beyond Masks: Efficient, Flexible Diffusion Language Models via Deletion-Insertion Processes")).
This dynamic refinement capability makes DID more robust to the errors
inherent in few-step generation.

Cautious early generation. RADD works on the whole sequence length from
the very first step, so it can unmask exceedingly many tokens at the
earlier stage using inaccurate model predictions. In DID, we allow at
most $`n+1`$ insertions if the current context length is $`n`$. (For
example, the very first sampling step will insert at most 1 token.) This
means we perform fewer insertions at the earlier stage, acting more
cautiously than RADD.

Better handling of adjacent token dependence. Both DID and MDM employ
the $`\tau`$-leaping method for fast sampling, which assumes multiple
token transitions occur independently at each step. This independence
assumption is a major source of sampling error. Note that DID will not
insert multiple adjacent tokens at once, so the transitions of adjacent
tokens are never independent. (Adjacent tokens are always inserted
sequentially.) In contrast, $`\tau`$-leaping in MDM poses a stronger
assumption that any pair of tokens are mutually independent. It is fair
to hypothesize that adjacent tokens bear more dependence than separated
tokens — thus, we believe DID handles token interdependence better.
````

# Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry

Khouloud Saadi ^(†)^(†)thanks: Corresponding author    Di Wang
Affiliation: Department of Computer Science Affiliation: King Abdullah
University of Science and Technology Affiliation: KAUST, Thuwal, Saudi
Arabia Email: [{khouloud.saadi,di.wang}@kaust.edu.sa](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Chemical AI workflows routinely represent the same molecule through
multiple co-referring identifiers, including trivial names, IUPAC names,
and SMILES, implicitly assuming that these representations provide
interchangeable access to an LLM’s knowledge. To test this assumption,
we first introduce a controlled benchmark and show that it does not
hold. Across foundation models spanning 7B to 72B parameters, even
chemistry-specialized models, co-referring molecular identifiers
consistently expose different subsets of the model’s knowledge. We then
conduct a series of controlled behavioral, representational, and causal
analyses to characterize this failure. We show that even when a model
can identify a molecule from its SMILES and retrieve a fact through the
corresponding name, the same fact can remain inaccessible directly from
SMILES. We term this phenomenon _cross-identifier binding failure_.
Further analysis of internal representations, conflicting identifiers,
and parameter editing shows that neither improved chemical
representation competence nor modification of individual facts is
sufficient to establish reliable access across identifiers. Together,
these results indicate that the core failure lies in the binding between
an identifier and knowledge already present in the model. Motivated by
this diagnosis, we introduce Binding Self-Distillation, a simple
mitigation that uses lexical identity during training to transfer the
model’s existing knowledge access to the structural route. This enables
factual retrieval directly from SMILES at inference time, without
requiring a name lookup or training the underlying model from scratch.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as general-purpose
interfaces to knowledge and reasoning ([Brown et al., 2020](#bib.bib5);
[Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib30); [Kojima et al., 2022](#bib.bib23)).
Beyond natural-language generation ([Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib36)),
they support applications in programming ([Chen et al.,
2021](#bib.bib6)), medicine ([McDuff et al., 2025](#bib.bib27); [Johri
et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)), and scientific discovery ([Singhal et al.,
2023](#bib.bib33); [Zhang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib43)). In chemistry,
LLMs are used for chemical question answering ([Zhang et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib40); [Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib44)), molecular property
prediction ([Jablonka et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)), and molecule
design ([Bhattacharya et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)). As these models become
components of chemical workflows, an important question is whether the
knowledge they contain can be accessed reliably under the different
representations used to describe the same molecule.

A single molecule can be referred to through multiple co-referring
identifiers ([Kim et al., 2023](#bib.bib22)). For example, as shown in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry")(A),
aspirin may appear as the trivial name aspirin, the systematic IUPAC
name 2-acetyloxybenzoic acid ([Favre & Powell, 2013](#bib.bib8)), or the
SMILES string CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O ([Gómez-Bombarelli et al.,
2018](#bib.bib9)). These representations are routinely mixed across
chemical workflows: biomedical resources commonly use lexical
names ([Hastings et al., 2016](#bib.bib14)), whereas reaction datasets
and molecular generative pipelines frequently use structural
representations such as SMILES ([Gómez-Bombarelli et al.,
2018](#bib.bib9); [Schwaller et al., 2019](#bib.bib32)). Consequently,
the same LLM may be queried about the same molecule through different
identifiers. This practice relies on an implicit assumption:
_co-referring identifiers should be interchangeable routes to the
model’s knowledge_. More generally, equivalence at the level of the
underlying scientific entity is implicitly expected to translate into
equivalence as an interface to the model’s memory.

In this work, we first show that this assumption does not hold. We
construct ChemIDBench, a controlled benchmark for evaluating knowledge
access across co-referring molecular identifiers, covering approved
drugs with matched trivial names, IUPAC names, and SMILES, and evaluate
seven chemistry-specialized and general-purpose LLMs. Holding the
underlying molecule and queried property fixed while changing only its
identifier reveals a systematic double dissociation: as shown in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry")(B,C),
lexical names provide substantially stronger access to entity-associated
factual knowledge, whereas structure-descriptive identifiers provide
stronger access to properties computable from molecular form. Identifier
choice therefore does more than change the difficulty of a query; it
changes _which knowledge is reachable_. More surprisingly, this
asymmetry persists even when the model recognizes the molecule. We
isolate cases in which a model can generate the correct trivial name
from SMILES and retrieve a fact through that name, yet still fails to
retrieve the same fact directly from SMILES. We call this phenomenon
_cross-identifier binding failure_. It separates three notions that are
often implicitly conflated: recognizing the entity, possessing factual
knowledge about it, and binding a particular identifier to that
knowledge.

We next ask what prevents knowledge from transferring across identifiers
and whether the missing access can be recovered. Supplying the lexical
name restores much of the inaccessible factual knowledge, showing that
the knowledge is present but not reached through the structural route.
More importantly, activation patching provides causal evidence for this
account: transplanting lexical-route states into a structural query
restores inaccessible factual retrieval under SMILES alone. Layer-wise
analysis further shows that chemistry-specific training can improve
alignment between structural forms without establishing comparable
name-structure binding. This weak binding is also a reliability problem:
when conflicting identifiers are presented together, different access
routes can control different answers, producing inconsistencies that
models often fail to detect. Supplying the lexical identity is an
effective inference-time remedy, but it requires an identifier lookup on
every query. Standard knowledge editing does not reliably propagate
factual access across co-referring identifiers, suggesting that
modifying the factual association alone is insufficient. Motivated by
this diagnosis, we introduce Binding Self-Distillation, which targets
the missing access relation by transferring the model’s own
name-conditioned access behavior to the SMILES route. It substantially
improves factual retrieval directly from SMILES across seven models,
without requiring the lexical name at inference time or training a model
from scratch. A detailed discussion of related work is provided in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Related Work ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").
In summary, our contributions are:

- •
  We introduce ChemIDBench, a controlled benchmark for evaluating
  knowledge access across co-referring molecular identifiers, covering
  matched trivial names, IUPAC names, and SMILES and evaluating seven
  LLMs spanning 7B–72B parameters.
- •
  We characterize how molecular identifier choice affects access to
  different types of knowledge, contrasting entity-associated factual
  queries with structure-computable queries and testing whether
  successful molecular recognition is sufficient for factual retrieval.
- •
  Through lexical-bridge controls, conflicting-identifier experiments,
  representation analysis, and activation patching, we show that entity
  recognition, factual knowledge, and identifier-conditioned access are
  separable, providing behavioral and causal evidence for
  _cross-identifier binding failure_.
- •
  We show that standard knowledge editing does not reliably propagate
  factual access across co-referring identifiers, revealing that
  modifying the factual association alone is insufficient. Motivated by
  this diagnosis, we introduce Binding Self-Distillation, which directly
  targets the missing access relation and substantially improves
  retrieval from SMILES without requiring the lexical name at inference
  time.

## 2 Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access

### 2.1 Benchmark Design

Our benchmark ChemIDBench isolates the effect of molecular identifier
choice while holding the underlying molecule and queried property fixed.
Each molecule is represented by three co-referring molecular
identifiers: a trivial name, an IUPAC name, and a canonical SMILES
string. We select these three because they are widely used across
chemical and biomedical workflows  ([Hastings et al., 2016](#bib.bib14);
[Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2018](#bib.bib9); [Schwaller et al.,
2019](#bib.bib32)) while providing complementary interfaces to the same
molecular entity: trivial names serve as familiar lexical references,
IUPAC names provide systematic chemical nomenclature ([Favre & Powell,
2013](#bib.bib8)), and SMILES provides a compact structure-based
representation ([Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2018](#bib.bib9)). We begin
with 3,025 molecules with approved drug–indication records in
ChEMBL ([Mendez et al., 2019](#bib.bib29); [Hunter et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16)), using the ChEMBL preferred name as the trivial-name
identifier. We then apply a strict entity-cleaning pipeline, removing
salts, multi-fragment molecules, duplicate names and structures, invalid
structures, and extreme molecule sizes, leaving 1,553 unambiguous
molecular entities. We further retain only molecules with scorable
therapeutic indications, yielding 897 candidates, and resolve their
IUPAC names through PubChem ([Kim et al., 2023](#bib.bib22)). This
produces 896 molecules with matched trivial-name, IUPAC-name, and SMILES
representations. Full filtering criteria, indication normalization, and
identifier-resolution checks are provided in
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Benchmark Design ‣ Appendix B Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access: Additional Details ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

For each task, we query the same molecule independently through its
trivial name, IUPAC name, and SMILES. The benchmark spans two
complementary types of queries: (1) _Entity-associated queries_ test
access to information tied to the molecular entity, including molecule
identification and therapeutic indication; (2) _Structure-computable
queries_ test properties that can be derived from molecular structure,
including molecular formula, benzene-ring presence, nitrogen-atom count,
ring count, and heavy-atom count, with ground truth computed using
RDKit ([Landrum, 2026](#bib.bib24)). Models use greedy decoding;
entity-associated outputs are scored against normalized accepted
answers, while structure-computable properties are evaluated against
RDKit-derived ground truth. Exact task definitions, prompts,
ground-truth construction, decoding settings, and scoring procedures are
provided in
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Evaluation Protocol ‣ Appendix B Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access: Additional Details ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

We evaluate seven LLMs spanning 7B–72B parameters, including both
chemistry-specialized and general-purpose models. To isolate the effect
of chemistry-specific training on cross-identifier behavior, we compare
ChemDFM-v2.0-14B and ChemDFM-v1.5-8B ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib44))
with Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct ([Yang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib39)) and
Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct ([Grattafiori et al., 2024](#bib.bib10)),
respectively. We further include Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ([Yang et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib39)), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct ([Grattafiori et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10)), and Qwen2-72B-Instruct ([Yang et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib38)).

### 2.2 Equivalent Identifiers Expose Different Knowledge

If co-referring identifiers were interchangeable interfaces to model
knowledge, changing only how a molecule is represented should have a
negligible effect on the model’s answer.
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2.2 Equivalent Identifiers Expose Different Knowledge ‣ 2 Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry")
shows the opposite: changing the identifier can nearly erase access to
entity-associated knowledge while improving access to
structure-computable properties. For ChemDFM-v2.0-14B,
therapeutic-indication accuracy collapses from $`38.8\%`$ under the
trivial name to $`2.1\%`$ under SMILES. Yet on the same molecules, the
direction reverses for benzene-ring detection: accuracy rises from
$`76.7\%`$ to $`94.6\%`$. This reversal holds across all seven models,
spanning 7B to 72B parameters and including both chemistry-specialized
and general-purpose LLMs. Across these models, trivial names are
strongest for therapeutic indication, whereas SMILES outperforms the
trivial name on benzene detection by $`13.3`$–$`28.3`$ percentage
points. Nitrogen counting shows the same broad reversal, though the
better structural representation, IUPAC or SMILES, varies across models.
Thus, structure-descriptive identifiers are not simply harder inputs:
they can be better interfaces when the requested information is
recoverable from molecular form.

Because each molecule is evaluated under every identifier, these
differences can be tested molecule by molecule rather than only through
aggregate accuracy. Paired McNemar tests ([McNemar, 1947](#bib.bib28))
confirm both directions: trivial names outperform SMILES for
therapeutic-indication retrieval, whereas SMILES outperforms trivial
names for benzene detection, with both effects significant across all
seven models ($`p<10^{-7}`$). Exact paired counts, $`p`$-values, and
results for all seven benchmark tasks are reported in
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Cross-Identifier Additional Results and Statistical Tests ‣ Appendix B Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access: Additional Details ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").
The key result is therefore not that one identifier is generally easier
than another. Instead, the identifier determines _which knowledge is
accessible_: lexical names favor entity-associated knowledge, while
structure-descriptive identifiers favor information recoverable from
molecular form. This raises a key question: is the SMILES failure simply
a failure of molecular recognition?

### 2.3 Recognition Does Not Imply Retrieval

To test whether failed molecular recognition explains the retrieval gap,
we evaluate two complementary recognition criteria. First, we use a
two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) test, a standard forced-choice
paradigm in which the model must select the correct answer from two
alternatives. Given a SMILES string, the model chooses between the
correct trivial name and one incorrect candidate; we retain only
molecules answered correctly across three independent decoy conditions.
Second, in the stricter free-form test, the model must generate the
correct trivial name directly from SMILES without candidate answers.
Full recognition protocols and subset-construction details are provided
in
Appendix [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Recognition and Cross-Identifier Binding ‣ Appendix B Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access: Additional Details ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

same result: among the 62 molecules whose trivial names are correctly
generated from SMILES, indication accuracy is 40.3% through the name but
only 3.2% through SMILES, compared with 3.6% outside the recognized
subset. ChemDFM-v1.5-8B shows the same broader separation: under 2AFC,
the recognized molecules reach 22.0% through their trivial names but
only 3.7% through SMILES. The broader 2AFC pattern also holds across the
other models with recognized subsets. Recognition therefore does not
explain the retrieval gap: a model can map a structural identifier to
the correct lexical identity while access to entity-associated knowledge
remains strongly identifier-dependent. We refer to this phenomenon as
cross-identifier binding failure.

## 3 Knowledge Is Present but Unreachable

Recognition alone does not restore factual retrieval
(Section [2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Recognition Does Not Imply Retrieval ‣ 2 Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry")).
We therefore ask whether the missing knowledge is absent from the model
or instead inaccessible through the structural identifier.

### 3.1 Lexical Identity Restores Factual Access

We supply the missing identifier correspondence by augmenting each
SMILES with its correct trivial name, without providing any information
about the queried indication. As shown in
Table [3](#S3.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 3.1 Lexical Identity Restores Factual Access ‣ 3 Knowledge Is Present but Unreachable ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry"),
this simple intervention nearly closes the gap to name-only retrieval
across all models, recovering 91.8–99.4% of the lost accuracy. For
ChemDFM-v2.0-14B, indication accuracy rises from 2.1% with SMILES alone
to 36.3% with SMILES+Name, compared with 38.6% from the name alone.
Qwen2.5-14B similarly rises from 1.3% to 37.6%, versus 37.8% from the
name. Thus, supplying lexical identity is sufficient to make much of the
otherwise inaccessible factual knowledge reachable. Exact prompts, the
recovery definition, and reverse-direction controls are provided in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Identifier-Bridge Protocol ‣ Appendix C Identifier-Bridge and Conflict Experiments ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

### 3.2 Conflicting Identifiers Expose Separate Access Routes

To test whether lexical and structural identifiers access a single
coherent entity representation, we pair the SMILES of molecule $`A`$
with the incorrect trivial name of molecule $`B`$. The resulting answers
split by query type: identity and therapeutic indication largely follow
the supplied name $`B`$, whereas benzene-ring remains close to those
obtained from $`A`$’s SMILES alone. As shown in
Table [4](#S3.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 3.2 Conflicting Identifiers Expose Separate Access Routes ‣ 3 Knowledge Is Present but Unreachable ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry"),
for ChemDFM-v2.0-14B, the conflicting input produces $`B`$’s identity in
85.5% of cases and retains 32.3% indication accuracy for $`B`$, while
benzene accuracy for $`A`$ remains 95.3%, compared with 94.5% from its
SMILES alone. Full results are provided in

The conflict is often undetected. We next ask whether models recognize
that the two supplied identifiers are incompatible. When explicitly
asked whether a SMILES and trivial name refer to the same molecule,
models reject obvious mismatches much more reliably than structurally
similar ones. Across the tested models, only 17.5–43.1% of similar
incorrect names are rejected; even in a two-choice identification test,
accuracy is only 56.4–73.9%. Thus, conflicting identifiers can drive
different answers without the model reliably detecting the
inconsistency. Full prompts, decoy construction, and discrimination

## 4 Diagnosing and Restoring Cross-Identifier Access

### 4.1 Causal Analysis via Activation Patching

The behavioral results suggest that factual knowledge is accessible
through a molecule’s trivial name but not through its SMILES. We test
whether this difference can be causally traced to the internal state
induced by the identifier. We use *activation patching* ([Zhang & Nanda,
2024](#bib.bib41)), a causal intervention that replaces an internal
activation in a target computation with the corresponding activation
from a source computation, to test whether the lexical state is
sufficient to restore factual retrieval. For matched prompts querying
the same fact, we treat the trivial-name run as the source and the
SMILES run as the target. At each tested layer, we replace the
residual-stream state at the final prompt token of the SMILES run with
the corresponding source state. We measure recovery as the fraction of
the name–SMILES teacher-forced log-probability gap closed by the
intervention; a different-molecule activation with a different gold
answer serves as the control. Full prompts, scoring, and implementation
details are provided in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Activation-Patching Details ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

stronger restoration; peak depth is descriptive, while the control is
expected not to yield positive recovery.

As shown in
Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.1 Causal Analysis via Activation Patching ‣ 4 Diagnosing and Restoring Cross-Identifier Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry"),
the correct lexical state substantially restores factual retrieval in
every model, with peak recovery ranging from 76.4% to 138.4%. In
contrast, every different-molecule control is negative. The effect peaks
at relative depths 0.50–0.83, rather than at a single
architecture-specific layer. These interventions provide causal evidence
that the lexical route produces a retrieval-supporting state that the
SMILES route fails to construct on its own. We next ask whether this
access asymmetry is reflected in the geometry of the two identifier
representations.

### 4.2 Probing Cross-Identifier Binding in Model Representations

We examine whether co-referring molecular identifiers become aligned at
the level of individual entities. If two identifier representations
encode the same molecular identity in a shared geometry, the
representation of a molecule under one identifier should retrieve its
co-referring representation under another identifier from a large
candidate set. We therefore measure cross-identifier top-1 retrieval.
For each of the 896 molecules, we extract representations from
identifier-specific prompts and use the representation under one
identifier to retrieve the corresponding SMILES representation from all
896 candidates. We evaluate retrieval across hidden-state depths and
report the maximum top-1 accuracy. Throughout this section, Name–SMILES
denotes trivial name $`\rightarrow`$ SMILES retrieval, whereas
IUPAC–SMILES denotes IUPAC name $`\rightarrow`$ SMILES retrieval. Full
representation extraction, pooling, centering, normalization, and
layer-wise evaluation details are provided in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Cross-Identifier Representation Analysis ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

retrieval changes only from $`0.7\%`$ to $`0.8\%`$. Thus, the
chemistry-specialized models exhibit substantially stronger alignment
between the two structure-descriptive identifiers than their
general-purpose reference models without producing comparable binding
between molecular structure and lexical identity. This representational
asymmetry mirrors the behavioral access gap. Stronger chemical
representations do not by themselves make entity-associated factual
knowledge reliably accessible through SMILES: the models can
substantially align IUPAC names with molecular structure while leaving
the lexical route through which much of their factual knowledge is
accessed only weakly connected to that same structure.

### 4.3 Lexical Routing Restores Factual Access from SMILES

The lexical bridge above suggests a simple inference mitigation: resolve
a SMILES string to its lexical identity before asking for the fact. We
test this over 891 molecules and 2,955 available molecule–relation pairs
spanning therapeutic indication, molecular target, mechanism of action,
and ATC class. For each query, we compare direct SMILES access, SMILES
augmented with the molecule’s ChEMBL preferred name obtained by database
lookup, and the preferred name alone. The lookup supplies only the
identifier correspondence, not the queried fact. Full construction,
prompts, scoring, and controls are given in

### 4.4 Standard Editors Do Not Reliably Transfer Across Identifiers

Can the lookup required above be avoided by modifying the model weights?
We test this diagnostically with ROME ([Gupta et al.,

indication is edited through the molecule’s trivial name and then
queried through its co-referring SMILES. Because an edit may also
increase production of the target for unrelated molecules, we subtract
the corresponding locality leakage,
$`T_{\mathrm{specific}}=T_{\mathrm{SMILES}}-T_{\mathrm{leak}}`$. Full

sometimes propagate across identifiers, but this behavior is highly

association does not reliably repair the structural route used to access
it.

## 5 Binding Self-Distillation Transfers Lexical Access to SMILES

The editing results suggest that changing a factual association is not
enough: the missing object is the _access route_ from SMILES to
knowledge that is already reachable through the molecule’s lexical
identity. We therefore ask whether this access behavior itself can be
written into the model. Our goal is not to teach a new fact, but to make
the SMILES route reproduce knowledge access that the same model already
exhibits when the correct name is available. We introduce Binding
Self-Distillation (BSD). For a molecule with SMILES $`s`$, trivial name
$`n`$, and a generic continuation stem $`z`$, we construct matched
teacher and student contexts,

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
x_{T}=[\mathrm{SMILES}(s);\text{ ``known as }n\text{''};z],\qquad x_{S}=[\mathrm{SMILES}(s);z].
``` |  |

Teacher and student use the same pretrained model. The teacher is frozen
and receives the lexical identity, while the student receives only
SMILES and learns a lightweight LoRA adapter by minimizing
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{T})\;\|\;p_{\theta,\phi}(\cdot\mid x_{S})\bigr).`$
Thus, the training signal is the model’s own name-conditioned predictive
behavior: no factual labels, generated target answers, or external
teacher are used. We train one adapter per molecule using generic
continuation stems, with the downstream factual evaluation prompts
excluded from training. At inference time, the name is removed and only
SMILES is provided. Full training configuration, prompts, and controls
are in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Binding Self-Distillation: Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

Retention controls for these two highlighted settings also show that the
intervention need not destroy behavior that already works: benzene
verdicts are preserved for 94.1% of ChemDFM-v2.0-14B examples and 92.9%
of Qwen2-72B-Instruct examples, with additional locality analyses
reported in
Appendix [H.7](#A8.SS7 "H.7 Retention and Locality ‣ Appendix H Binding Self-Distillation: Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").
Together, these results provide a constructive complement to the editing
diagnostic in
Section [4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Standard Editors Do Not Reliably Transfer Across Identifiers ‣ 4 Diagnosing and Restoring Cross-Identifier Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").
Standard editors modify factual associations but propagate them
inconsistently across identifiers; Binding Self-Distillation instead
targets the missing access relation. The result shows that knowledge
already exposed by a lexical identifier can be made substantially more
accessible from SMILES without requiring that lexical identifier at
inference time.

## 6 Conclusion

In chemistry pipelines, molecular identity is often treated as a solved
interface problem: once two identifiers are known to denote the same
molecule, an LLM is implicitly expected to access the same underlying
knowledge. We introduce ChemIDBench, a controlled benchmark for
evaluating knowledge access across molecular representations, and find
that across LLMs spanning 7B to 72B, changing only the identifier can
change which knowledge is reachable. Surprisingly, a model can identify
a molecule from SMILES and retrieve a fact through its trivial name, yet
fail to retrieve the same fact directly from SMILES. When identifiers
conflict, different routes can also drive different answers within the
same prompt, often without the model detecting the inconsistency.
Recognition controls, conflict experiments, representation analysis, and
activation patching together show that entity recognition, knowledge
possession, and identifier-conditioned access are separable
capabilities. We call this cross-identifier binding failure. Standard
knowledge editors do not reliably restore access across identifiers; we
therefore introduce Binding Self-Distillation, which directly targets
the missing access relation and substantially improves factual retrieval
from SMILES without requiring the lexical name at inference time. Our
current formulation performs this repair per entity, showing that
cross-identifier access is repairable while leaving generalization to
unseen entities as a future direction. Beyond chemistry, the broader
identifier-dependent-access phenomenon also appears in our
protein-domain replication with opaque UniProt accessions ([Ahmad
et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)) in
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Generality Beyond Chemistry: Protein-Domain Replication ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").
Overall, our results show that for LLMs to be used in science, knowledge
is only useful if it remains accessible across the different
representations of the same entity.

## Appendix A Related Work

#### LLMs and molecular representations in Chemistry.

LLMs have increasingly been adapted to chemistry through domain-specific
pretraining and instruction tuning. ChemLLM ([Zhang et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib40)) and ChemDFM ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib44)), for
example, train language models on chemical knowledge and tasks that
include textual molecular representations such as
SMILES ([Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2018](#bib.bib9); [Schwaller et al.,
2019](#bib.bib32)). In practice, the same molecule may be presented to
these models through a trivial  ([Gómez-Bombarelli et al.,
2018](#bib.bib9); [Schwaller et al., 2019](#bib.bib32)), a systematic
IUPAC name ([Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2018](#bib.bib9); [Schwaller
et al., 2019](#bib.bib32)), or a SMILES string ([Gómez-Bombarelli
et al., 2018](#bib.bib9); [Schwaller et al., 2019](#bib.bib32)). Our
work asks whether these co-referring representations are interchangeable
not simply as inputs for downstream task performance, but as access
routes to knowledge already stored in the model.

#### Representation dependence and consistency in chemistry.

Recent work has shown that molecular representation can substantially
affect LLM behavior. [Baker et al. (2025)](#bib.bib3) compare multiple
molecular representations for property prediction and find strong
representation preferences across pretrained LLMs. [Yan et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib37) study cross-representation consistency between
chemically equivalent SMILES and IUPAC inputs, showing that equivalent
representations can produce inconsistent predictions and that
consistency regularization only partially reduces this gap. Even within
SMILES, alternative valid encodings of the same molecule can change
learned representations and downstream performance ([Kikuchi et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)). These studies establish that chemically equivalent
representations are not behaviorally interchangeable. We ask a different
question: whether co-referring identifiers provide access to the *same
knowledge already present in the model*. We distinguish failed
recognition from failed retrieval, separate entity-associated facts from
structure-computable properties, and examine what happens when two
individually valid but mutually inconsistent identifiers are presented
together.

#### Mechanistic analysis of molecular and factual representations.

Recent mechanistic work has examined what chemical language models
encode in their internal representations ([Cohen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib7); [Varadi et al., 2025](#bib.bib35); [Kenneth et al.,
2026](#bib.bib20); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib26); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib42)). Sparse-autoencoder analyses have identified latent
features associated with molecular syntax, structural motifs,
physicochemical properties, and pharmacological concepts  ([Cohen
et al., 2025](#bib.bib7); [Kenneth et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)), while
layerwise probing and causal interventions have been used to distinguish
information that is merely decodable from information that is
functionally used by molecular models  ([Hao et al., 2026](#bib.bib13);
[Varadi et al., 2025](#bib.bib35)). These studies primarily ask what
molecular structure and properties are represented inside models trained
on molecular strings. Our question is different: why can knowledge about
the same entity be accessible through one co-referring identifier but
not another? We therefore study not only whether information is
represented, but whether identifier-specific internal states provide
effective access to the same factual knowledge.

#### Knowledge editing.

Knowledge editing methods aim to modify selected factual associations
while preserving unrelated model behavior. ROME and fine-tuning  ([Gupta
et al., 2024b](#bib.bib12); [Gupta et al., 2024a](#bib.bib11)) provide
representative parameter-based approaches, while subsequent work has
broadened evaluation beyond edit success and paraphrase generalization.
Recent evaluations  ([Hsueh et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Jiang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)) further test whether an edit propagates to logically
related facts. In chemistry, MolEdit ([Lei et al., 2026](#bib.bib25))
considers knowledge editing in multimodal molecule–language models and
evaluates reliability, locality, and generality for molecule–text
generation tasks. We introduce a different generalization requirement:
*cross-identifier transfer*. If two identifiers denote the same entity,
an edit made through one identifier should remain accessible when that
entity is queried through another. We find that standard editors do not
reliably satisfy this requirement for trivial-name–to–SMILES transfer,
motivating cross-identifier binding as a distinct intervention target
from conventional edit efficacy, paraphrase generalization, or
cross-modal editing.

## Appendix B Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access: Additional Details

### B.1 Benchmark Design

#### Initial ChEMBL retrieval.

We select ChEMBL ([Mendez et al., 2019](#bib.bib29)) drug-indication
records corresponding to approved indications (phase 4) and group them
by molecule. For each molecule, we retain its ChEMBL preferred name,
canonical SMILES, and all qualifying MeSH indication headings. This
produces 3,025 unique molecules before subsequent entity and structure
filtering.

#### Entity and structure filtering.

Because our benchmark compares different identifiers for the *same*
molecular entity, we first restrict the collection to molecules with
well-defined lexical and structural representations. We retain only
candidates with both a ChEMBL preferred name and canonical SMILES, and
restrict preferred names to at most two whitespace-separated words to
exclude long, formulation-like labels. Preferred names are stripped and
lowercased before filtering. To focus on single molecular entities
rather than salts, counterions, or mixtures, we remove salt and
counterion forms by exact whitespace-token matching against a fixed
manually specified lexicon,¹¹ 1 The lexicon contains sulfate, sulphate,
hydrochloride, chloride, sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, acetate,
citrate, tartrate, maleate, mesylate, besylate, fumarate, succinate,
phosphate, nitrate, bromide, iodide, carbonate, gluconate, lactate,
oxalate, salicylate, stearate, tosylate, dihydrate, monohydrate,
hydrate, hydrobromide, bitartrate, pamoate, and valerate. and exclude
multi-fragment structures whose ChEMBL canonical SMILES contains “.”.

We further remove duplicate preferred names and structures so that an
entity is not represented multiple times in the benchmark. Duplicate
preferred names are identified by exact equality after lowercasing,
retaining the first molecule in sorted ChEMBL-ID order. Remaining SMILES
are parsed with RDKit ([Landrum, 2026](#bib.bib24)), ensuring that
structure-derived ground truth can be computed reliably, and we restrict
the parsed molecular graphs to 6–70 atoms to bound the benchmark to a
consistent molecular-size range. For structure deduplication, each
parsed molecule is canonicalized as an isomeric SMILES and duplicate
structures are removed by exact equality of the resulting strings. These
filters leave 1,553 candidate molecules.

#### Therapeutic-indication target construction.

For each retained molecule, we collect all MeSH headings associated with
phase-4 drug indications in ChEMBL. To make answer matching robust to
superficial formatting differences, we normalize each heading by
lowercasing, replacing non-alphanumeric characters with spaces, and
collapsing repeated whitespace. For headings containing commas, we
additionally include a comma-inverted variant to accommodate alternative
word orderings of the same MeSH heading. Duplicate variants are removed,
and strings of length at most three characters are discarded to avoid
extremely short, non-discriminative matches.

We then compute document frequency over the accepted aliases at the
molecule level, counting each alias at most once per molecule, and
remove aliases occurring in more than 2% of the 1,553 candidates. This
excludes overly generic indication terms that provide little
molecule-specific signal. Because a molecule may have multiple
qualifying MeSH headings, we define a single deterministic reference
indication as the lexicographically first unique original heading; this
is only a reproducible tie-breaking rule and does not imply clinical
priority. A molecule is retained only if at least one accepted
indication alias remains and its reference indication is still scorable
under the resulting alias set, ensuring that every retained molecule has
a valid target for the indication task. This filtering leaves 897
molecules.

#### IUPAC-name resolution.

For each of the 897 remaining molecules, we retrieve an IUPAC name from
PubChem ([Kim et al., 2023](#bib.bib22)) using the PubChem PUG REST
interface. We first query PubChem by the ChEMBL preferred name and take
the first returned IUPAC name. If this lookup does not yield an IUPAC
name, we query PubChem for compound identifiers associated with the
preferred name, select the first returned compound, and retrieve its
IUPAC name. Only if both name-based procedures fail do we query PubChem
using the molecule’s canonical SMILES. PubChem mappings are additionally
validated against the corresponding ChEMBL structures using the InChIKey
connectivity block. An IUPAC name is resolved for 896 of the 897
candidates; the single unresolved molecule is discarded, yielding a
final benchmark of 896 molecules.

#### Prompt construction.

Each molecule is evaluated independently under three identifier
conditions: trivial name, IUPAC name, and canonical SMILES. For a given
molecule and task, the queried property and target answer are held
fixed; only the identifier used to refer to the molecule changes.

The benchmark contains two entity-associated tasks and five
structure-computable tasks. Identify asks the model to recover the
molecule’s trivial name, while Indication asks for a therapeutic
indication associated with the molecule. The remaining tasks ask for
molecular formula, benzene-ring presence, nitrogen-atom count, ring
count, and heavy-atom count. Exact task instructions are provided in

#### Ground-truth construction.

For Identify, the ChEMBL preferred name is used as the gold answer. For
Indication, predictions are evaluated against the strict set of accepted
MeSH-derived aliases constructed as described in
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Benchmark Design ‣ Appendix B Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access: Additional Details ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").
Ground truth for the five structure-computable tasks is derived from the
canonical SMILES using RDKit ([Landrum, 2026](#bib.bib24)). Molecular
formula is calculated from the parsed molecular structure. Benzene-ring
presence is determined by testing for an aromatic six-membered carbon
ring. Nitrogen count is the number of nitrogen atoms in the molecule,
ring count is the number of rings returned by RDKit’s ring-information
representation, and heavy-atom count is the number of non-hydrogen
atoms.

#### Decoding.

All models are evaluated using greedy decoding with a maximum of 40
generated tokens per query. Sampling is disabled, and input sequences
are truncated to at most 1,536 tokens. The same decoding procedure is
used across identifier forms and benchmark tasks.

#### Entity-associated scoring.

For Identify, generated responses and ChEMBL preferred names are
normalized before matching. Normalization lowercases text, removes
possessive suffixes and punctuation, collapses repeated whitespace, and
applies a light plural normalization that removes a terminal “s” from
tokens longer than four characters unless they end in “ss”. A response
is counted as correct when the normalized preferred name occurs as a
complete word-boundary-matched phrase in the normalized response. For
Indication, empty responses and refusals are rejected, as are responses
containing more than 12 normalized tokens. Remaining responses are
normalized using the same text-processing procedure and counted as
correct when any alias in the strict accepted-answer set occurs as a
complete word-boundary-matched phrase in the normalized response.

#### Structure-computable scoring.

For Formula, generated responses are parsed into element-count
compositions after normalizing formatting variations including
subscripts, letter case, and punctuation. The scorer tests up to the
first four formula-like candidates in the response and, if none matches,
parses the complete response. A prediction is counted as correct when
the resulting element counts exactly match the RDKit-derived gold
formula.

For the integer-valued tasks, Nitrogens, Ring Count, and Heavy Atoms,
the first integer appearing in the generated response is extracted and
compared with the corresponding RDKit-derived gold value. A response
containing no integer is counted as incorrect.

For Benzene, responses are interpreted using a negation-first yes/no
rule: explicit negative cues are evaluated before positive cues,
ensuring that statements such as “does not contain” are classified as
negative. Empty, refusal, or otherwise unparseable responses are counted
as incorrect.

two identifier conditions $`A`$ and $`B`$, let $`n_{10}`$ denote the
number of molecules answered correctly under $`A`$ but incorrectly under
$`B`$, and $`n_{01}`$ the number answered incorrectly under $`A`$ but
correctly under $`B`$. Under the null hypothesis of no directional
difference, the two types of discordant pair are expected to occur
equally often. We use the continuity-corrected statistic:

|     |                                                         |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \chi^{2}=\frac{(|n_{10}-n_{01}|-1)^{2}}{n_{10}+n_{01}},
       ```                                                      |     |

which is compared with a $`\chi^{2}`$ distribution with one degree of
freedom.

### B.4 Recognition and Cross-Identifier Binding

This section provides the implementation details for the recognition
controls used in
Section [2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Recognition Does Not Imply Retrieval ‣ 2 Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").
We evaluate whether a model can recover the trivial name associated with
a SMILES string using two complementary criteria: two-alternative forced
choice (2AFC) and free-form generation.

#### 2AFC recognition.

For each molecule, the model is shown its canonical SMILES together with
the correct trivial name and one incorrect candidate:

> A compound is given as SMILES {SMILES}.
> Which name identifies it?
> A) {option_a}
> B) {option_b}
> Reply with a single letter only.

The order of the correct and incorrect candidates is randomized.
Responses are scored by extracting a standalone A or B.

We construct three decoy conditions. In the *near-analogue* condition,
the incorrect candidate is sampled from molecules with
Morgan-fingerprint similarity of at least 0.4. We use radius-2,
2,048-bit Morgan fingerprints ([Rogers & Hahn, 2010](#bib.bib31)) and
compute molecular similarity using the Tanimoto coefficient ([Bajusz
et al., 2015](#bib.bib2)). If no molecule satisfies the similarity
threshold, the decoy is sampled from the five nearest molecules. In the
*random* condition, the decoy is sampled uniformly from all other
eligible molecules. In the *gross-mismatch* condition, the decoy is
sampled from molecules whose heavy-atom count differs from the target by
more than a factor of two, falling back to the full eligible pool when
necessary. Heavy-atom counts follow the RDKit-based definition in
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Evaluation Protocol ‣ Appendix B Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access: Additional Details ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").
Sampling uses a fixed random seed of 0. Decoys are sampled independently
across conditions and therefore need not be distinct. A molecule is
considered recognized under 2AFC only if the model answers correctly
under all three decoy conditions.

#### Free-form recognition.

The stricter free-form control uses the standard SMILES-to-name
identification task defined in
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Evaluation Protocol ‣ Appendix B Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access: Additional Details ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry"):
given only the canonical SMILES, the model must generate the molecule’s
trivial name without candidate answers. Predictions and gold names are
normalized by lowercasing, removing possessive suffixes and
non-alphanumeric punctuation, normalizing whitespace, and lightly
singularizing words longer than four characters that end in s but not
ss. Recognition succeeds when the normalized gold name occurs as a
word-boundary-delimited substring of the normalized response.

#### Recognized-subset evaluation.

For either recognition criterion, let $`R`$ denote the set of recognized
molecules that also appear in the therapeutic-indication evaluation.
Table [2](#S2.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 2.3 Recognition Does Not Imply Retrieval ‣ 2 Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry")
reports three quantities. Name is therapeutic-indication accuracy when
molecules in $`R`$ are queried through their trivial names; SMILES is
accuracy on the same molecules when queried through canonical SMILES;
and Rest is canonical-SMILES accuracy on molecules outside $`R`$. Thus,
the Name and SMILES columns always compare exactly the same recognized
molecules. All therapeutic-indication results reported in
Table [2](#S2.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 2.3 Recognition Does Not Imply Retrieval ‣ 2 Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry")
use the strict indication-scoring criterion defined in
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Evaluation Protocol ‣ Appendix B Benchmarking Cross-Identifier Knowledge Access: Additional Details ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

### C.1 Identifier-Bridge Protocol

> Consider the molecule with SMILES {SMILES}, which is known as
> "{NAME}".
> What medical condition is this substance primarily used to treat?
> Answer in English. Reply with the condition only. Do not explain.

#### Name only.

> Consider the molecule commonly known as "{NAME}".
> What medical condition is this substance primarily used to treat?
> Answer in English. Reply with the condition only. Do not explain.

### C.3 Direct Detection of Identifier Conflicts

We separately test whether models can explicitly recognize that a
supplied SMILES and trivial name are inconsistent. Each canonical SMILES
is evaluated with its correct name and with three types of incorrect
names.

#### Mismatch construction.

For the near-analogue condition, we represent each molecule using a
radius-2, 2,048-bit Morgan fingerprint ([Rogers & Hahn,
2010](#bib.bib31)), a circular molecular fingerprint that encodes local
structural subpatterns, and measure pairwise similarity using the
Tanimoto coefficient ([Bajusz et al., 2015](#bib.bib2)). The incorrect
name is sampled uniformly from molecules with Tanimoto similarity at
least 0.4 to the target. If no such candidate exists, the decoy is
sampled from the five nearest molecules, with all other eligible
molecules used as a final fallback.

For the random condition, the decoy is sampled uniformly from all other
molecules with valid fingerprints. For the gross-mismatch condition, the
decoy is sampled from molecules whose heavy-atom count differs from the
target by more than a factor of two, falling back to the full eligible
pool when necessary. Sampling uses a fixed random seed of 0; decoys are
sampled independently across conditions and therefore need not be
distinct.

#### Direct consistency check.

The exact prompt is:

> A compound is given as SMILES {SMILES} and is labelled
> "{LABEL}".
> Does this label correctly identify the structure shown?
> Reply with exactly one word: yes or no.

The response parser evaluates negative cues before positive cues.
Negative cues include no, not, incorrect, mismatch, wrong, different,
and false; positive cues include yes, correct, match, and true.

#### Near-analogue 2AFC.

We additionally ask the model to choose between the true trivial name
and the selected near-analogue:

> A compound is given as SMILES {SMILES}.
> Which name identifies it?
> A) {OPTION A}
> B) {OPTION B}
> Reply with a single letter only.

The order of the true and incorrect candidates is randomized.

#### Discrimination metric.

Mismatch-rejection rate alone can be misleading because a model could
obtain a high rejection rate simply by rejecting most name–SMILES pairs.
We therefore also report the signal-detection sensitivity index
$`d^{\prime}`$ ([Stanislaw & Todorov, 1999](#bib.bib34)), which accounts
for false alarms on correctly matched pairs. Let $`H`$ denote the
probability of rejecting a mismatched pair and $`F`$ the probability of
incorrectly rejecting a correctly matched pair. We compute

|     |                       |     |
|-----|-----------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       d^{\prime}=Z(H)-Z(F),
       ```                    |     |

where $`Z`$ is the inverse standard-normal cumulative distribution
function. Larger $`d^{\prime}`$ indicates better discrimination between
matched and mismatched identifier pairs. Before applying $`Z`$, both
probabilities are clipped to $`[0.001,0.999]`$. No sample-size-dependent
correction is applied.

| ChemDFM-v1.5-8B      | 65.5     | 43.1   | 0.22           | 62.0   | 0.70           | 71.9 |

## Appendix D Activation-Patching Details

This section provides the implementation details for the causal
intervention in
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Causal Analysis via Activation Patching ‣ 4 Diagnosing and Restoring Cross-Identifier Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

### D.1 Source and Target Construction

For each molecule, we construct matched prompts querying the same
therapeutic indication through either the trivial name (*source*) or
canonical SMILES (*target*):

> The molecule commonly known as "{NAME}" is primarily used to treat

> The molecule with SMILES {SMILES} is primarily used to treat

The gold continuation is the reference indication preceded by a single
space. For chat models, the source or target string is placed in the
user turn with the system message You are a helpful assistant. and the
model’s chat template is applied with a generation prompt. The gold
continuation is then scored autoregressively under teacher forcing.

### D.2 Item Selection

We first compute the unpatched mean gold-token log-probability under the
trivial-name and SMILES prompts. We retain only molecules for which
lexical access is measurably stronger,

|     |                                                                    |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathrm{lp}_{\mathrm{name}}-\mathrm{lp}_{\mathrm{SMILES}}\geq 0.20
       ```                                                                 |     |

nats per gold token. This ensures that recovery is evaluated on examples
that exhibit a pre-existing name–SMILES access gap. For
Qwen2-72B-Instruct, 491 of the 896 benchmark molecules satisfy this
criterion, spanning 176 distinct indication strings.

### D.3 Activation Intervention

At a tested transformer block $`\ell`$, we capture the block-output
residual state at the final prompt token in the trivial-name source run.
We then run the matched SMILES prompt and replace the complete
block-output vector at its final prompt position with the captured
source vector:

|     |                                                                    |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       h^{(\ell)}_{\mathrm{SMILES}}\leftarrow h^{(\ell)}_{\mathrm{name}}.
       ```                                                                 |     |

All other token positions remain unchanged, and computation proceeds
normally through the remaining transformer blocks. Each layer
intervention is evaluated independently.

Layers are searched using a coarse-to-fine procedure: an initial
regularly spaced sweep identifies a coarse peak, after which the
surrounding layers are evaluated more densely. The reported peak is the
tested layer with the largest mean recovery. Relative depths in the main
text normalize the selected layer index by model depth.

### D.4 Teacher-Forced Scoring and Recovery

For a gold continuation $`g_{1},\ldots,g_{T}`$, we compute mean token
log-probability

|     |                                                                  |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathrm{lp}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(g_{t}\mid x,g_{<t}),
       ```                                                               |     |

where $`x`$ is the rendered prompt. Gold tokens are scored
autoregressively under teacher forcing. Logits are converted to float32
before applying log_softmax.

Recovery is computed separately for every molecule $`i`$ and tested
layer $`\ell`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{i,\ell}=\frac{\mathrm{lp}^{\mathrm{patched}}_{i,\ell}-\mathrm{lp}^{\mathrm{SMILES}}_{i}}{\max(\mathrm{lp}^{\mathrm{name}}_{i}-\mathrm{lp}^{\mathrm{SMILES}}_{i},10^{-6})}.
``` |  |

The reported layer-level recovery is the arithmetic mean of
$`R_{i,\ell}`$ across selected molecules, multiplied by $`100`$. Thus,
$`100\%`$ indicates that patching closes the original name–SMILES gap on
average, while values above $`100\%`$ indicate that the patched
computation exceeds the corresponding name-conditioned score. Standard
errors are computed across the per-molecule recovery values.

### D.5 Different-Entity Control

To test whether recovery arises from injecting arbitrary lexical
activity, each focal molecule is assigned a control molecule with a
different reference indication. The control is sampled uniformly from
the selected items using a fixed random seed of $`0`$. The assignment is
made once and reused at every tested layer.

At each layer, we therefore repeat the intervention using the control
molecule’s trivial-name activation while retaining the focal molecule’s
SMILES target and gold indication. Control recovery is computed using
the same denominator as the matched intervention:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R^{\mathrm{ctrl}}_{i,\ell}=\frac{\mathrm{lp}^{\mathrm{ctrl}}_{i,\ell}-\mathrm{lp}^{\mathrm{SMILES}}_{i}}{\max(\mathrm{lp}^{\mathrm{name}}_{i}-\mathrm{lp}^{\mathrm{SMILES}}_{i},10^{-6})}.
``` |  |

The consistently negative controls in
Table [5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.1 Causal Analysis via Activation Patching ‣ 4 Diagnosing and Restoring Cross-Identifier Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry")
show that recovery is not produced merely by inserting an arbitrary
lexical-state vector.

#### Qwen2-72B-Instruct layer sweep.

For the 80-layer Qwen2-72B-Instruct model, the initial sweep evaluates
every fourth layer. After a coarse peak at layer 68, the surrounding
region is searched densely, yielding 32 tested layers in total. The
final peak occurs at layer 66, with mean recovery $`138.4\%\pm 8.0`$
percentage points (SEM); the corresponding different-entity control is
$`-30.9\%`$.

## Appendix E Cross-Identifier Representation Analysis

This section provides the implementation details and complete results
for the representation analysis in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Probing Cross-Identifier Binding in Model Representations ‣ 4 Diagnosing and Restoring Cross-Identifier Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").

### E.1 Representation Extraction

For each of the 896 benchmark molecules, we construct three
identifier-specific prompts:

> the molecule commonly known as "{NAME}"
>
> the molecule named {IUPAC_NAME}
>
> the molecule with SMILES {SMILES}

Each complete prompt is tokenized using the checkpoint’s tokenizer with
its default special-token behavior. Batches are right-padded and
truncated to at most 512 tokens. Hidden states are extracted for every
state returned by the model.

For molecule $`i`$, identifier form $`a`$, and hidden-state index
$`\ell`$, let $`\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_{i,a,t}`$ denote the hidden state at
token position $`t`$, and let $`m_{i,a,t}\in\{0,1\}`$ be the
corresponding attention-mask value. We represent the prompt by the
arithmetic mean over all non-padding tokens:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{i,a}=\frac{\sum_{t}m_{i,a,t}\mathbf{H}^{(\ell)}_{i,a,t}}{\sum_{t}m_{i,a,t}}.
``` |  |

Thus, the representation is a mean over the complete non-padding prompt
rather than over the identifier tokens alone. It includes the fixed
prompt prefix, the molecular identifier, punctuation such as quotation
marks when present, and any special tokens added by the tokenizer.
Padding tokens are excluded.

We use the hidden-state sequence returned directly by the model.
Hidden-state index $`0`$ corresponds to the embedding output, followed
by the states returned after successive transformer computations. We
therefore report the exact hidden-state index used by the implementation
rather than converting it to a separately defined block number.

The extraction pipeline also supports pooling at the final non-padding
prompt token, but all results reported in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Probing Cross-Identifier Binding in Model Representations ‣ 4 Diagnosing and Restoring Cross-Identifier Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry"),
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Probing Cross-Identifier Binding in Model Representations ‣ 4 Diagnosing and Restoring Cross-Identifier Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry"),
and this appendix use the mean-pooled representations defined above. We
do not select the pooling strategy separately for different identifier
pairs.

### E.2 Cross-Identifier Retrieval

We evaluate whether the representation of a molecule under one
identifier can retrieve the representation of the same molecule under
another identifier. The candidate set contains $`N=896`$ molecule rows.

Before retrieval, representations are centered separately for each
identifier form and hidden-state index. For identifier form $`a`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bm{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{i,a},
``` |  |

and each centered representation is L2-normalized:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}^{(\ell)}_{i,a}=\frac{\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{i,a}-\bm{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{a}}{\left\|\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{i,a}-\bm{\mu}^{(\ell)}_{a}\right\|_{2}+10^{-8}}.
``` |  |

For a query molecule $`i`$ represented under identifier $`a`$, retrieval
under identifier $`b`$ is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{j}=\arg\max_{j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{h}}^{(\ell)}_{i,a}\right)^{\!\top}\tilde{\mathbf{h}}^{(\ell)}_{j,b}.
``` |  |

Because the vectors are L2-normalized, this dot product is cosine
similarity after mean-centering. No PCA, whitening, or other
representation transformation is applied.

Top-1 retrieval is correct when $`\hat{j}=i`$. With 896 candidate rows,
uniform chance performance is

|     |                              |     |
|-----|------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{1}{896}\approx 0.11\%.
       ```                           |     |

Throughout the paper, Name–SMILES denotes the directional retrieval task

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \text{trivial name}\rightarrow\text{SMILES},
       ```                                           |     |

and IUPAC–SMILES denotes

|     |                                            |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \text{IUPAC name}\rightarrow\text{SMILES}.
       ```                                         |     |

The reported metric is not an average over the two retrieval directions.

The 896-row candidate set contains distinct molecule identifiers,
trivial names, and canonical SMILES. Two IUPAC strings each occur for
two distinct molecule rows, yielding 894 distinct IUPAC strings overall.
Evaluation remains row-index based: the representation corresponding to
the same molecule row is treated as the gold target.

### F.2 Lexical-Routing Conditions

For each available molecule–relation pair, we evaluate four conditions:

1.  1.
    SMILES: query the relation directly from the molecule’s canonical
    SMILES.
2.  2.
    Self-resolution: first ask the model to generate the molecule’s
    common name from SMILES, then insert the generated name into the
    factual query.
3.  3.
    Lookup: supply the ChEMBL preferred name associated with the
    molecule together with its SMILES before asking the factual
    question.
4.  4.
    Name: query the relation directly from the ChEMBL preferred name.

The lookup condition implements the routing path

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{SMILES}\;\xrightarrow{\text{name lookup}}\;\mathrm{Lexical\ identity}\;\xrightarrow{\text{LM}}\;\mathrm{Fact}.
``` |  |

The lookup supplies only the correspondence between the SMILES and the
lexical identifier; it contains no information about the queried
relation. In the implementation, the name is the ChEMBL preferred name
already associated with the molecule in the benchmark record. No live
resolver API is called during inference.

All factual queries use

> Answer the question about the compound below.
> Compound: {REFERENCE}
> Question: {QUESTION}
> Answer in English. Reply with the answer only. Do not explain.

The relation-specific questions are

> what medical condition is it primarily used to treat?
> what is its primary molecular target?
> what is its mechanism of action?
> what therapeutic class does it belong to?

with the following reference strings:

> SMILES: SMILES {SMILES}
> Self-resolution: SMILES {SMILES} (identified as {GENERATED_NAME})
> Lookup: SMILES {SMILES} (resolved by name lookup to {NAME})
> Name: {NAME}

For self-resolution, the model first receives

> A database lookup returned this compound as SMILES: {SMILES}
> What is its common generic name?
> Answer in English. Reply with the name only.

Prompts are wrapped in the model’s chat template when available, using
the system message You are a helpful assistant. Generation is greedy
(do_sample=False) with at most 32 new tokens. Across 2,955 available
molecule–relation pairs, the four factual-query conditions produce
11,820 factual queries.

### F.3 Answer Normalization and Scoring

Predictions and accepted answers are normalized by lowercasing, removing
possessive suffixes, replacing non-alphanumeric punctuation with spaces,
collapsing whitespace, and lightly singularizing words longer than four
characters that end in s but not ss. Empty responses, refusals, and
responses longer than 14 normalized words are counted as incorrect.

For all four relations, a response is correct when an accepted
normalized label occurs as a word-boundary-delimited phrase in the
normalized prediction. For molecular target and mechanism of action, we
additionally accept a prediction when it covers at least 60% of the
content words of an accepted label after removing stopwords and tokens
of length at most two. Therapeutic indication and ATC class use only the
phrase-matching criterion.

where $`A_{\mathrm{SMILES}}`$, $`A_{\mathrm{lookup}}`$, and

Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct.

### G.1 Edit Construction and Evaluation

For each model, we retain molecules whose therapeutic indication is
already retrievable through the trivial name, yielding 389, 390, 351,
and 389 molecules for ChemDFM-v2.0-14B, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct,
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, respectively.

For each molecule $`x`$, we replace its original indication with a
counterfactual indication $`y^{\ast}`$ sampled from another eligible
molecule. Candidates sharing normalized content words with any accepted
true indication of $`x`$ are excluded before uniform sampling with seed
$`0`$. The original raw indication string is used as the editing target.
The edit request is

> {common name} is primarily used to treat

with $`y^{\ast}`$ as the desired continuation. Each edit is applied and
evaluated independently, after which the modified weights are restored.

We evaluate the edited target under the following prompts:

|----|----|

#### Editing implementation details.

For each model and editing method, we first evaluated candidate editing
layers on a 30-example pilot sweep and used the pilot results to select
the layer for the full experiment. Because the pilot exhibited different
efficacy–locality trade-offs across models and methods, we did not
impose a single scalar selection metric across all settings. The
resulting ROME/FT layers were 24/24 for ChemDFM-v2.0-14B, 30/36 for
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, 17/14 for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and 20/16 for
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, respectively. ROME edits the MLP down-projection
at the selected layer and optimizes the target vector for at most 25
steps using Adam with learning rate $`0.5`$, weight decay $`10^{-3}`$.
FT optimizes the same down-projection for 25 steps using Adam with
learning rate $`5\times 10^{-4}`$, batch size 1, zero weight decay, and
element-wise updates constrained to $`5\times 10^{-4}`$ from the
original weights. Both methods use FP32 models, and evaluation uses
greedy decoding with at most 24 new tokens.

estimate this background effect using locality probes spanning molecular
similarities based on radius-2, 2,048-bit Morgan fingerprints and
Tanimoto similarity.

We define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{leak}}=\operatorname{mean}\bigl(\texttt{post\_new}\mid\texttt{axis=locality}\bigr),

|     |                                                              |     |

|     |
       ``` math

       ```                                                           |     |

Confidence intervals are obtained from 10,000 percentile-bootstrap
resamples with seed 20260921, resampling at the edited-molecule level
and carrying the associated locality probes with each sampled molecule.

### H.2 Teacher–Student Construction

For a molecule with SMILES $`s`$, trivial name $`n`$, and generic
continuation stem $`u`$, the student receives only the structural
identifier,

|     |                                               |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       x_{S}=\texttt{The molecule with SMILES }s\;u,
       ```                                            |     |

whereas the teacher receives the same context augmented with the lexical
identity,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
x_{T}=\texttt{The molecule with SMILES }s\texttt{, which is known as ``}n\texttt{'', }u.
``` |  |

Teacher and student are two forward passes through the same pretrained
model. The teacher remains frozen, while a LoRA adapter $`\phi`$ on the
student is optimized using

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bind}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{T})\,\|\,p_{\theta,\phi}(\cdot\mid x_{S})\right).
``` |  |

The KL divergence is computed over the full vocabulary at the final
prompt position. No teacher continuation is generated and no factual
answer is provided as a training target. Binding Self-Distillation
therefore transfers the model’s own predictive behavior induced by
lexical identity rather than training on external factual labels.

At every optimization step, six generic continuation stems are sampled
uniformly with replacement from a pool of 24. These include stems such
as is a compound that, is known for, is a drug that, acts on, and is
associated with. The exact downstream evaluation prompts are excluded
from the training pool. Some stems nevertheless cue broad biomedical
relation families, so we interpret the resulting gains as cross-prompt
transfer across multiple relations rather than strictly
relation-held-out generalization.

#### Anchor regularization.

Each binding intervention additionally uses eight unrelated anchor
molecules sampled without replacement from the remaining eligible
entities using random.Random(0). At each optimization step, an anchor is
paired with a newly sampled generic stem. The frozen model provides the
original next-token distribution for that prompt, and an additional KL
term with weight $`1.0`$ regularizes the adapted model toward the
original behavior.

## Appendix I Generality Beyond Chemistry: Protein-Domain Replication

Our main experiments concern molecules, raising the possibility that
identifier-dependent knowledge access is specific to SMILES or
structural notation. We therefore repeat the key behavioral and causal
tests in a qualitatively different domain: proteins. A protein can be
addressed through a familiar lexical identifier such as its gene symbol
or through an exact UniProt accession ([Ahmad et al., 2025](#bib.bib1))
such as P04637. Unlike SMILES, an accession is an opaque database key
and encodes no biological structure. A similar asymmetry therefore
cannot be attributed to difficulty interpreting structural notation.

### I.1 Dataset Construction and Evaluation

We query the UniProt REST API ([Ahmad et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)) using
(reviewed:true) AND (organism_id:9606), requesting accession, primary
gene, recommended protein name, subcellular-location annotation, and
disease annotation. The data-generation script does not preserve an
authoritative UniProt release identifier or download timestamp.

For each returned entry, we retain the primary accession, the primary
gene symbol from the first gene record, and the recommended full protein
name; entries missing any of these fields are discarded. We additionally
extract the first recognized subcellular location and the first disease
identifier. An entry is retained if either annotation is available, so a
recognized location is not required for inclusion. No explicit
accession, gene-symbol, or protein-name deduplication is subsequently
applied.

This produces 16,388 entries with 16,388 unique accessions and 16,336
distinct gene symbols. Of these, 15,925 have a recognized subcellular
location. ChemDFM-v2.0-14B is evaluated on all 16,388 entries. For the
other four models, entries are sorted by accession and the first 1,000
are used; this is deterministic truncation rather than random sampling.
The experiment scripts do not preserve a reason for this 1,000-entry
limit.

Subcellular location is mapped to the ordered label set

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\{\text{nucleus},\text{cytoplasm},\text{cell membrane},\text{mitochondrion},\text{endoplasmic reticulum},\text{golgi apparatus},\text{secreted},\text{lysosome},\text{peroxisome},\text{cytoskeleton}\}.
``` |  |

After normalization, the mapper returns the first label in this order
whose text occurs in a UniProt location annotation; the first recognized
annotation is retained. There is no additional alias table or
multi-label evaluation. The cytoskeleton class does not occur in the
resulting data, leaving nine observed labels. In the full set, the
largest class is cytoplasm ($`4{,}349/15{,}925=27.31\%`$); among the
first 1,000 entries, 979 have locations and the largest class is cell
membrane ($`281/979=28.70\%`$).

#### Prompts and scoring.

All behavioral prompts have the form

> Consider {phrase}.
> {question}
> Answer in English. {format} Do not explain.

The identifier-dependent phrase is one of

> the human protein encoded by the gene {symbol}
> the human protein {protein_name}
> the human protein with UniProt accession {accession}
> the human protein with UniProt accession {accession}, encoded by the
> gene {symbol}
> the human protein with UniProt accession {accession}, encoded by the
> gene {decoy_symbol}.

For identification, the question is What is the official gene symbol for
this protein? with Reply with the gene symbol only. Thus, “Symbol ID,”
“Protein-name ID,” and “Accession ID” all measure recovery of the
official gene symbol when conditioning on the corresponding identifier.

For factual retrieval, the question is What is its primary subcellular
location?, and the model is asked to reply with one of the ten location
labels above. Bridge and conflicting-label conditions use the gene
symbol as the lexical identifier.

Generation is greedy with at most 32 new tokens and prompt truncation at
1,024 tokens. Models supporting chat templates additionally receive the
system message You are a helpful assistant.

The scorer rejects empty or refusal-like outputs and normalized
responses longer than 12 words. Identification succeeds when the gold
gene symbol appears as a case-insensitive whole phrase; no gene-symbol
aliases are used. For location, generated text and the stored gold label
are lowercased, possessives and punctuation are removed, whitespace is
normalized, and light depluralization is applied. A whole-phrase
occurrence of the gold label is then counted as correct. If several
locations are mentioned, the response therefore succeeds whenever the
stored gold label appears.

### I.3 Activation Patching in the Protein Domain

We next repeat the causal intervention from
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Causal Analysis via Activation Patching ‣ 4 Diagnosing and Restoring Cross-Identifier Access ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry").
Unlike the behavioral experiment, patching does not select examples
according to generated-answer correctness. It begins with
accession-sorted entries having a mapped location and uses the
raw-completion prompts

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{src}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\texttt{The human protein encoded by the gene \lx@text@lbrace symbol\lx@text@rbrace
 is primarily located in the},`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{tgt}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\texttt{The human protein with UniProt accession \lx@text@lbrace accession\lx@text@rbrace
 is primarily located in the}.`$ |  |

The gold completion is the canonical location label with a leading
space.

For each item, we compute the teacher-forced mean log-probability per
gold token under the lexical source and accession target. We retain
examples for which the lexical route exceeds the accession route by at
least $`0.20`$ nats per token. Examples are sorted by this gap, capped
at 80 per gold label, and then capped at 1,000 total examples. Because
only nine location labels occur and eligibility varies by model, the
resulting sets contain 607–689 examples.

At layer $`L`$, we capture the residual-stream output of decoder block
layers\[L\] at the *last prompt token* of the lexical source and
overwrite the corresponding last-token state in the accession
computation. Thus, the intervention patches one residual-stream position
after decoder block $`L`$; it does not patch the identifier token span
itself. Layer indexing is zero-based.

Let $`s_{i}`$ and $`t_{i}`$ denote source and untouched-target mean
gold-token log-probabilities for item $`i`$, and $`p_{i,L}`$ the
corresponding score after patching. Normalized recovery is

|     |                                                          |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{i,L}=\frac{p_{i,L}-t_{i}}{\max(s_{i}-t_{i},10^{-6})}.
       ```                                                       |     |

Reported recovery is $`100`$ times the arithmetic mean of $`R_{i,L}`$
over items, without clipping. Recovery can therefore exceed $`100\%`$
when the patched target assigns greater probability to the gold
continuation than the lexical source itself.

For the control, seed 0 selects another item from the same patching set
with a different gold location. Its source state is patched identically,
giving

|     |                                                          |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       C_{i,L}=\frac{c_{i,L}-t_{i}}{\max(s_{i}-t_{i},10^{-6})}.
       ```                                                       |     |

The same control assignment is reused across layers. Negative control
values therefore indicate that an incompatible entity state reduces the
gold probability below that of the untouched accession route.

Patching the matched lexical state yields $`88.0`$–$`109.9\%`$
normalized recovery across all five models, whereas different-location
controls strongly decrease the gold score. The intervention therefore
provides causal evidence that information produced along the lexical
route is sufficient to improve prediction from the otherwise weak
accession route.

#### Ranking analysis.

As a complementary threshold-free measure, we score the complete gold
strings for every location represented in the patching set using mean
teacher-forced log-probability per token. Although the benchmark defines
ten possible labels, cytoskeleton is absent from the data, so the
executed ranking analysis contains nine candidates and uniform chance is
$`11.1\%`$. The correct location initially ranks first only
$`11.6`$–$`12.7\%`$ of the time, but rises to $`37.8`$–$`62.6\%`$ after
patching.

The implementation computes this ranking at the *coarse-sweep peak*,
rather than at the final dense-sweep maximum. We therefore report its
actual evaluation layer separately in
Table [29](#A9.T29 "Table 29 ‣ I.3 Activation Patching in the Protein Domain ‣ Appendix I Generality Beyond Chemistry: Protein-Domain Replication ‣ Same molecule, Different Knowledge: Cross-Identifier Binding Failures in LLMs For chemistry")
and do not treat the ranking values as measurements at the final peak
layer.

#### Layer selection and localization.

The patching sweep first evaluates layers $`0,4,8,\ldots`$ below each
model’s depth. It selects the coarse layer with highest mean normalized
recovery, then evaluates all previously untested layers within $`\pm 8`$
layers of this coarse peak. The final reported peak is the layer with
maximum mean recovery over the complete saved sweep; rank and control
metrics do not influence selection.

The effective layers are substantially later than in chemistry.
Chemistry peaks at relative depths $`0.50`$–$`0.75`$, whereas protein
patching peaks at $`0.78`$–$`0.98`$. At these depths, especially
$`0.97`$–$`0.98`$ for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and ChemDFM-v2.0-14B, the
intervention occurs close to the output of the network. We therefore
interpret the protein experiment as evidence of *causal sufficiency*,
not as a replication of the chemistry-specific localization pattern.
````

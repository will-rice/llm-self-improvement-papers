# When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?

Enoch Yin Affiliation: Department of Statistics, The George Washington
University    Bin Liu Affiliation: Department of Statistics and Data
Science, School of Management, Fudan University    Zhengling Qi
Affiliation: Department of Decision Sciences, School of Business, The
George Washington University^(\*)Corresponding authors

###### Abstract

Self-evolving large language model (LLM) systems repeatedly propose,
evaluate, and incorporate updates to prompts, skills, or other
persistent artifacts. Despite their growing effectiveness, these systems
typically operate under a predetermined iteration or compute budget,
without a principled criterion to determine when further evolution is no
longer worthwhile. This can lead to two undesirable consequences:
unnecessary computation after performance has saturated and the risk of
returning late updates that overfit or exploit the evaluation signal.
These issues motivate us to study two fundamental questions: _when
should a self-evolving system stop, and what should it output once it
stops?_ We address the first by formulating an online sequential testing
problem and constructing an anytime-valid restart detector using the
per-item paired evaluation outcomes already produced by self-evolving
LLM systems. We address the second by formulating a change-point
estimation problem and using the estimated transition to select an
earlier artifact for output. The resulting procedure is plug-and-play
and requires no modification of the underlying self-evolving algorithms.
Across two self-evolving frameworks, three LLM model families, and five
benchmarks, our method substantially reduces computation costs while
maintaining comparable unseen-test performance. For example, on SearchQA
with SkillOpt and DeepSeek V4 Flash, our method stops at round 4 rather
than the full budget of 40, reducing token usage by 91.6% while
achieving 82.43% unseen-test accuracy versus 82.00% under the
full-budget run.

## 1 Introduction

LLM agents are increasingly designed to improve themselves through
repeated interaction, feedback, and adaptation rather than remain fixed
after their initial construction. This emerging paradigm of
_self-evolving agents_ has appeared in a wide range of settings,
including iterative self-correction, self-training and self-play, prompt
and skill evolution, memory and experience refinement, and multi-agent
co-evolution ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib11); [Shinn et al.,
2023](#bib.bib27); [Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib33); [Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib44); [Qi et al., 2025](#bib.bib18); [Xia et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Yang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib37); [Agrawal et al., 2026](#bib.bib1); [Yuksekgonul
et al., 2025](#bib.bib40); [Ni et al., 2026](#bib.bib13); [Yang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib38); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib42); [Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9); [Zhang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib43); [Shi et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25)). Despite substantial differences in what is evolved
and how updates are generated, these systems share a similar iterative
structure: the system generates or applies an update, evaluates its
effect using feedback, reward, or validation signals, and carries the
resulting state into subsequent rounds.

In practice, self-evolving loops are typically run for a fixed number of
iterations or until a pre-specified compute budget is exhausted,
regardless of whether further evolution remains meaningful. This can
have two undesirable consequences. First, once meaningful improvement
has saturated, the system may continue proposing, evaluating, and
committing updates that provide little or no additional benefit,
incurring substantial unnecessary computation. Second, repeatedly
adapting to the same validation signal can encourage evaluation
overfitting or reward hacking ([Amodei et al., 2016](#bib.bib2); [Skalse
et al., 2022](#bib.bib30)), so later updates may appear favorable under
the evaluation signal without representing genuine improvement.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")
illustrates how these two consequences can compound in practice: the
evolution continues well after meaningful gains have saturated,
consuming substantial additional computation, while continued adaptation
can ultimately lead to a degraded final artifact on unseen test data.

This limitation raises two fundamental questions that every
self-evolving system must answer:

The first question asks when there is sufficient evidence that further
evolution is no longer worthwhile. The second is distinct: even after
deciding to stop, the most recent artifact may not be the best one to
return and one needs to decide what to output. Current self-evolving
systems generally leave both decisions unaddressed in a fixed iteration
budget.

![Refer to caption](2610.04756v1/figure/searchqa_val_vs_test.png)

Figure 1: A self-evolving run on SearchQA with DeepSeek V4 Flash.
Performance improves during the first few rounds and then plateaus, yet
the fixed-budget loop continues to round 40. Our procedure stops at
round 4 and selects round 1 as the output, achieving comparable
unseen-test performance while avoiding most of the subsequent
computation.

We answer these two questions with a unified online procedure using the
per-item paired outcomes already produced by the evaluation gate in most
existing systems. For _when to stop_, we formulate sequential hypothesis
tests and construct an anytime-valid detector that signals when evidence
for continued meaningful improvement has weakened, building on testing
by betting ([Shafer, 2021](#bib.bib22)), betting
martingales ([Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024](#bib.bib34)), and
e-detectors ([Shin et al., 2024](#bib.bib26)). For _what to output_, we
formulate a change-point estimation problem ([Page, 1954](#bib.bib14);
[Lorden, 1971](#bib.bib10)): after stopping, we estimate when meaningful
improvement ended and return an earlier artifact. The procedure requires
no modification to the underlying self-evolving system and uses only
outcomes it already computes for incumbents and candidates. In
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?"),
for example, it stops at round 4, estimates the change point at round 2,
and returns an earlier artifact from round 1 with comparable unseen-test
performance to the full 40-round run while using $`91.6\%`$ fewer tokens
([Table 1](#S4.T1 "In 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")).

In detail, we make three main contributions. First, we identify _when to
stop_ and _what to output_ as the two fundamental decisions a
self-evolving loop must make, and show that both can be addressed online
using the per-item paired outcomes already produced by the system,
without modifying the underlying self-evolving algorithm
([Section 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")).
Second, we develop a unified statistical procedure that combines an
anytime-valid sequential stopping rule with change-point-based output
selection: the stopping rule determines when further improvement is no
longer worthwhile, while the estimated change point determines which
earlier artifact should be returned
([Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Proposed Method ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")).
Third, we evaluate the procedure across two self-evolving frameworks,
SkillOpt ([Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib37)) and GEPA ([Agrawal et al.,
2026](#bib.bib1)), three model families, and five benchmarks, showing
substantial reductions in computation while maintaining comparable
unseen-test performance
([Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")).
In particular, in a benchmark where continued evolution eventually
produces a reward-hacking update, our procedure stops before this late
degraded artifact is reached and returns an earlier artifact instead.

## 2 Related work

Self-evolving LLM systems. A rapidly growing body of work lets an LLM
improve itself by repeatedly editing a persistent artifact and
evaluating it on held-out data: skill documents ([Yang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib37); [Ni et al., 2026](#bib.bib13); [Yang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib38); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib42); [Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib41); [Zhang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib43); [Shi et al.,
2026](#bib.bib25); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)), prompts ([Agrawal
et al., 2026](#bib.bib1); [Yuksekgonul et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)), or
programs. These systems differ in what they evolve, but share an
analyze–propose–gate loop and report strong gains from it. Their
reliability has two known failure points. The loop can be gamed: because
it optimizes against its own evaluation signal, degenerate solutions
that exploit a leaky benchmark score well without real capability. This
is reward hacking, identified as a core safety problem ([Amodei et al.,
2016](#bib.bib2)), formalized by [Skalse et al. (2022)](#bib.bib30),
catalogued across environments ([Krakovna et al., 2020](#bib.bib7)), and
shown to worsen with optimization pressure and agent capability ([Pan
et al., 2022](#bib.bib15); [Gao et al., 2023](#bib.bib6)); it is now
documented in self-evolving agents ([Shao et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)).
And the per-round commit can be spurious, which [Shawn
(2026)](#bib.bib24) addresses by recasting the accept-or-reject gate as
a sequential test. This line of work sharpens the _individual steps_ of
the loop, its proposer and its gate, but takes the loop’s overall length
and its final output as given, leaving open when the loop should stop
and which of its artifacts to return.

Sequential change-point detection and optimal stopping. Detecting online
when a stream’s distribution has changed is classical, from Wald’s SPRT
([Wald, 1945](#bib.bib32)) and Page’s CUSUM ([Page, 1954](#bib.bib14))
to the Shiryaev–Roberts procedure ([Shiryaev, 1963](#bib.bib28);
[Roberts, 1966](#bib.bib21)) and its optimality theory ([Lorden,
1971](#bib.bib10); [Pollak, 1985](#bib.bib16); [Lai, 1998](#bib.bib8);
[Tartakovsky et al., 2014](#bib.bib31)). These classical procedures
typically rely on specified parametric models for the pre- and
post-change distributions, whereas our setting is defined through a
conditional mean criterion with an unknown change time. We therefore
build on anytime-valid inference ([Shafer, 2021](#bib.bib22); [Ramdas
et al., 2023](#bib.bib19)), specifically the betting martingales of
[Waudby-Smith and Ramdas (2024)](#bib.bib34) and the e-detectors of
[Shin et al. (2024)](#bib.bib26), which provide sequential validity
under optional stopping. In
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Additional Related Work ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?"),
we review more related work on stopping rules for training and
inference.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

We consider a self-evolving system with initial artifact
$`\theta_{0}\in\Theta`$, where $`\Theta`$ may contain prompts, skill
documents, or programs. At each round $`t\geq 1`$, the system uses its
current incumbent $`\theta_{t-1}`$ and accumulated information to
propose a candidate $`\theta^{\prime}_{t}`$. The evolution mechanism may
use training data, search, or reflection to generate a candidate
$`\theta^{\prime}_{t}`$. The incumbent and candidate are evaluated on a
fixed validation set consisting of $`n`$ tasks:
$`D_{\mathrm{val}}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}`$, with binary correctness
$`\ell(\theta,x_{i})\in\{0,1\}`$ and validation accuracy
$`L(\theta)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\theta,x_{i})`$. A greedy gate sets
$`\theta_{t}=\theta^{\prime}_{t}`$ if
$`L(\theta^{\prime}_{t})>L(\theta_{t-1})`$ and retains
$`\theta_{t}=\theta_{t-1}`$ otherwise. Without a stopping rule,
evolution continues until a prescribed round budget $`N`$ or compute
budget is exhausted. One naive approach is to monitor $`L(\theta_{t})`$
and detect the change point. However, tracking only $`L(\theta_{t})`$ is
insufficient because rejected proposals leave it unchanged, making a
flat trajectory uninformative about proposal quality. For example, an
apparent plateau may later be followed by further improvement, as in
[Figure 3](#S4.F3 "In 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").
We therefore accumulate evidence from candidate–incumbent paired
outcomes for sequential detection.

![Refer to caption](2610.04756v1/figure/pipeline.png)

Figure 2: Our stopping and output-selection Plug-and-Play module
monitors paired validation outcomes from a self-evolving loop.

To monitor whether proposed updates continue to provide worthwhile
gains, let $`b_{t,i}=\ell(\theta_{t-1},x_{i})`$ and
$`c_{t,i}=\ell(\theta^{\prime}_{t},x_{i})`$ denote the incumbent and
candidate outcomes on validation task $`x_{i}`$. Define the paired
differences and their round-wise average as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
X_{t,i}=c_{t,i}-b_{t,i}\in\{-1,0,+1\},\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{t,i}=L(\theta^{\prime}_{t})-L(\theta_{t-1}).
``` |  | (1) |

Each difference $`X_{t,i}`$ records whether the candidate worsens,
preserves, or improves an outcome, regardless of whether it is accepted.
Let $`\varepsilon>0`$ denote the minimum expected proposal gain
considered worthwhile. The gate accepts any positive observed gain,
while stopping asks whether the conditional expected proposal gain
remains at least $`\varepsilon`$. Thus, a candidate may be accepted even
when its expected gain is too small to justify another round. Writing
$`\mathcal{F}_{t-1}`$ for the information available after the
round-$`t`$ candidate is proposed but before its validation outcomes are
observed, we define the pre-change condition as

|     |                                                        |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\geq\varepsilon.
       ```                                                     |     | (2) |

The round average measures the proposed validation gain, while the
paired outcomes retain the item-level information used by our proposed
sequential tests in
[Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Proposed Method ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").

We seek to determine both when to stop and which retained artifact to
return. Let $`\nu`$ be the first round from which
$`\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]<\varepsilon`$, and assume this
low-improvement regime persists once entered. Our first objective is to
detect this transition online by accumulating evidence against the
pre-change condition and stopping at an alarm time
$`T_{\mathrm{alarm}}`$, defined by the sequential rule in
[Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Proposed Method ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").
Because evidence takes time to accumulate, the alarm can follow the
transition by several rounds. The pre-transition output is
$`\theta_{\nu-1}`$, the final retained artifact before expected proposal
improvement becomes non-worthwhile. Our second objective is therefore to
estimate $`\nu`$ and return $`\theta^{\ast}=\theta_{\nu-1}`$, the
incumbent immediately preceding the estimated transition. This separates
the time spent gathering evidence from the round whose artifact is
selected for output.
[Figure 2](#S3.F2 "In 3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")
shows how our stopping and output-selection module interfaces with this
loop.

### 3.2 Proposed Method

We now develop a sequential procedure based on the paired improvement
signals $`\{X_{t,i}\}_{i=1}^{n}`$ defined in
[Section 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").
We use these signals to address the two problems formulated above.
First, we construct sequential tests of the pre-change condition from
different starting rounds and combine their evidence to determine when
evolution should stop. Second, we use the same restart statistics to
estimate when the non-worthwhile-improvement regime began and determine
which earlier incumbent should be returned.

#### 3.2.1 When to Stop: Sequential Testing with Restart Processes

A weak proposal can occur even while further evolution remains
worthwhile. We therefore accumulate evidence against continued
worthwhile improvement using successive paired outcomes defined before.
Following [Shin et al. (2024)](#bib.bib26), for each starting round
$`s`$, we test $`H_{0}^{(s)}`$: the pre-change condition in
[Equation 2](#S3.E2 "In 3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")
holds for every subsequent round $`u\geq s`$.

We represent this evidence by a restart wealth process with betting
fractions $`\lambda_{s,u}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
W_{t}^{(s)}=\prod_{u=s}^{t}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1+\lambda_{s,u}\left(\varepsilon-X_{u,i}\right)\right],\qquad\text{with}\qquad W_{s-1}^{(s)}=1.
``` |  | (3) |

For positive betting fractions, an outcome $`X_{u,i}<\varepsilon`$
contributes a factor greater than one, while an outcome above
$`\varepsilon`$ contributes a factor below one. Multiplication combines
these contributions across items and rounds, so a large wealth
represents accumulated evidence against $`H_{0}^{(s)}`$.

We use two betting schemes for $`\lambda_{s,u}`$. Under adaptive
aGRAPA ([Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024](#bib.bib34)), $`\lambda_{s,t}`$
is chosen predictably from past data. See
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Betting fraction and detectability floor ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")
for more details. Under the fixed construction, we set
$`\lambda_{s,t}\equiv\lambda`$ for a constant $`\lambda\in\Lambda`$, and
form a fixed mixture by averaging the resulting wealth processes over
$`\lambda\in\Lambda`$. We use $`\Lambda=\{0.1,0.2,0.4\}`$. In the main
paper, we report experimental results under adaptive aGRAPA and we
evaluate both schemes throughout our experiments in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Alarm-time comparison ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").

A process started during useful early rounds may lose wealth and then
need to recover those losses before detecting a later stall. We
therefore start a fresh process at every round, allowing evidence after
a transition to accumulate without losses from earlier gains. Following
the CUSUM-style e-detector construction of [Shin et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib26), we retain the strongest current evidence across
starting rounds and raise an alarm when it crosses $`1/\delta`$, for a
user-specified $`\delta\in(0,1)`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle M_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq s\leq t}W_{t}^{(s)},\qquad\text{and}`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{alarm}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\geq 1:M_{t}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}\right\}.`$ |  | (5) |

The threshold determines how much evidence is required to stop: smaller
$`\delta`$ requires larger wealth and makes the rule more conservative.
Under the pre-change condition and the assumptions in
[Section 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?"),
$`M_{t}`$ is a valid e-detector, so this threshold guarantees an average
run length to false alarm of at least $`1/\delta`$.

1: Worthwhile-gain threshold $`\varepsilon`$, false-alarm control
parameter $`\delta`$.

2: Initialize restart wealth processes.

3: for $`t=1,2,\dots,N`$ do

4:   Observe $`(b_{t,i},c_{t,i})_{i=1}^{n}`$ and set
$`X_{t,i}=c_{t,i}-b_{t,i}`$.

5:   Initialize a new restart at $`s=t`$ and update all wealths with
aGRAPA or the fixed mixture.

6:   $`M_{t}\leftarrow\max_{1\leq s\leq t}W_{t}^{(s)}`$.

7:   if $`M_{t}\geq 1/\delta`$ then

8:    $`T_{\mathrm{alarm}}\leftarrow t`$.

9:
   $`\hat{\nu}\leftarrow\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\leq s\leq t}W_{t}^{(s)}`$.

10:    stop and return $`\theta_{\hat{\nu}-1}`$.

11:   end if

12: end for

13: If no alarm is raised, the stopping module remains inactive and the
underlying system returns its final artifact.

#### 3.2.2 What to Output: Change-Point Estimation

As discussed above, restarting the sequential evidence process at each
round helps the procedure respond more quickly once improvement becomes
non-worthwhile. The resulting collection of restart processes also
provides a natural way to locate when this transition occurred. A
process started too early includes pre-transition observations that tend
to work against wealth growth, whereas a process started too late misses
part of the evidence accumulated after the transition. This suggests
that a process started near the transition should attain relatively
large wealth at the alarm time. We therefore estimate the change point
as the starting round of the restart process with the largest observed
wealth at $`T_{\mathrm{alarm}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\nu}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\leq s\leq T_{\mathrm{alarm}}}W_{T_{\mathrm{alarm}}}^{(s)}.
``` |  | (6) |

As shown in
[Section 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?"),
this intuition can be formalized in the common fixed-$`\lambda`$
setting. We then use the estimated change point $`\hat{\nu}`$ to
determine the returned artifact according to $`\theta^{\ast}`$. Thus,
the same restart construction is used both to stop the evolution and to
select the output artifact: $`T_{\mathrm{alarm}}`$ determines when to
stop, while $`\hat{\nu}`$ determines which artifact to return.

Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.2.1 When to Stop: Sequential Testing with Restart Processes ‣ 3.2 Proposed Method ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")
summarizes the complete stopping and output-selection procedure.

### 3.3 Theoretical Analysis

We establish stopping validity and change-point guarantees below. All
proofs are in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Proofs ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").

Stopping validity. Recall that $`\mathcal{F}_{t-1}`$ includes the
proposed candidate but not its validation outcomes. Assume that
$`X_{t,1},\ldots,X_{t,n}`$ are conditionally independent given
$`\mathcal{F}_{t-1}`$, and each betting fraction
$`\lambda_{s,t}\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$ is predictable and fixed across
items within round $`t`$.

###### Proposition 1 (Restart and stopping validity).

Under these assumptions, for each start $`s`$, $`H_{0}^{(s)}`$ implies
that $`W_{t}^{(s)}`$ is a nonnegative supermartingale initialized at one
and $`\Pr(\sup_{t\geq s}W_{t}^{(s)}\geq 1/\delta)\leq\delta`$ for
$`\delta\in(0,1)`$. If the pre-change condition holds at every round,
then $`M_{t}`$ is a valid e-detector:
$`\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\tau]`$ for every stopping time
$`\tau`$. Consequently,
$`\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathrm{alarm}}]\geq\frac{1}{\delta}.`$

Proposition
[1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Restart and stopping validity). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")
covers adaptive betting including aGRAPA, while fixed mixtures inherit
the same validity by convexity, with average time to false alarm at
least $`1/\delta`$. Intuitively, the detector stops only when enough
evidence accumulates that further proposal gains are no longer
worthwhile. A smaller $`\delta`$ raises the alarm threshold, increasing
the lower bound on $`\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathrm{alarm}}]`$ under the
pre-change regime and lowering the probability of making the false
alarm.

Change-point identification and localization. For this analysis, assume
one common fixed betting fraction $`\lambda\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$ across
all rounds and restarts. Define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
V_{t,i}=\log\!\left(1+\lambda(\varepsilon-X_{t,i})\right),\qquad Y_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{t,i},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu_{t}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}].
``` |  | (7) |

###### Proposition 2 (Change-point identification and localization).

Assume that (a) $`\nu`$ marks the sign change in fixed-$`\lambda`$
expected log growth: $`\mu_{t}<0`$ for $`t<\nu`$ and $`\mu_{t}>0`$ for
$`t\geq\nu`$; Then, for any fixed $`T\geq\nu`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nu=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\leq s\leq T}\mathbb{E}[\log W_{T}^{(s)}].
``` |  |

For localization, further assume that (b) $`\mu_{t}\leq-\kappa`$ before
$`\nu`$ and $`\mu_{t}\geq\kappa`$ from $`\nu`$ onward for some
$`\kappa>0`$; (c) Conditioning on $`\mathcal{F}_{t-1}`$, the $`V_{t,i}`$
are independent and each centered increment
$`V_{t,i}-\mathbb{E}[V_{t,i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]`$ is
$`\sigma^{2}`$-sub-Gaussian ($`\sigma>0`$). Then, for any
$`\alpha\in(0,1)`$, with probability at least $`1-\alpha`$, the bound
holds uniformly over all finite $`T\geq\nu`$, including
$`T_{\mathrm{alarm}}`$ on $`\{\nu\leq T_{\mathrm{alarm}}<\infty\}`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
|\hat{\nu}_{T}-\nu|<\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{n\kappa^{2}}\log\!\left(\frac{2}{\alpha\left(1-e^{-n\kappa^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}\right)}\right).
``` |  |

Proposition
[2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Change-point identification and localization). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")
justifies selecting the largest observed restart wealth for localizing
the change point. It further shows that for fixed $`\kappa`$ and
$`\sigma`$, our change point estimation error diminishes at the rate of
$`1/n`$.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Setup

We evaluate our method with SkillOpt and GEPA across DeepSeek V4 Flash,
Qwen3-32B, and GPT-5.6 Luna, using SearchQA, GSM8K, OfficeQA, LiveMath,
and SpreadsheetBench. Main experiments fix SkillOpt and DeepSeek V4
Flash while varying the benchmark; ablations vary the model, evolving
system, validation-set size, and detector construction.

Unless otherwise noted, we use $`\varepsilon=0.01`$ and false-alarm
control parameter $`\delta=0.05`$; $`t=0`$ denotes the initial artifact.
For $`\lambda_{s,u}`$ in the construction of the wealth process, we run
both aGRAPA and the fixed-mixture detector with
$`\Lambda=\{0.1,0.2,0.4\}`$. Since the two betting schemes produce the
same estimated change point $`\hat{\nu}`$ across matched settings, with
differences appearing only in the alarm round, we report aGRAPA in the
main results for conciseness and provide the comparison in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Alarm-time comparison ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").
We report the alarm round $`T_{\mathrm{alarm}}`$, estimated change point
$`\hat{\nu}`$, computation consumed through the alarm relative to the
full run, and returned artifact $`\theta_{\hat{\nu}-1}`$. When an unseen
test set is available, we compare the returned artifact with the one
selected by the full run on the same examples, reporting their
accuracies, paired accuracy difference, and its 95% confidence interval.

### 4.2 Main Results

Our method stops substantially before the fixed budget. The detector
saves 48.4%–91.6% of tokens across the three main benchmarks
([Table 1](#S4.T1 "In 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")).
The evolution trajectories and detailed stopping behavior are provided
in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Main Evolution Trajectories ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").

![Refer to caption](2610.04756v1/figure/spreadsheet_deepseek.png)

Figure 3: SpreadsheetBench trajectory under SkillOpt with DeepSeek V4
Flash.

Early stopping retains comparable unseen-test performance while
improving token efficiency. After the detector stops, our method uses
the estimated change point $`\hat{\nu}`$ to return the incumbent
immediately preceding the estimated transition,
$`\theta_{\hat{\nu}-1}`$. We compare this returned artifact with the
artifact selected by the full run on the same unseen-test examples. The
paired accuracy difference is defined as
$`\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}=\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{returned}}-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{full}}`$,
and we report its 95% confidence interval. The returned artifact
slightly outperforms the full-run selection on SearchQA ($`+0.43`$ pp),
while the differences on GSM8K and OfficeQA are $`-0.80`$ pp and
$`-1.16`$ pp, respectively. In all three cases, the confidence interval
includes zero, and the observed differences are small relative to their
uncertainty, indicating no clear loss in unseen-test performance from
stopping early. At the same time, the reduction in computation
substantially improves the cost–performance tradeoff. Measured as
millions of tokens per percentage point of unseen-test accuracy gained
over the baseline, the returned artifacts are $`12.9\times`$,
$`9.3\times`$, and $`1.8\times`$ more token-efficient than running the
full evolution budget on SearchQA, GSM8K, and OfficeQA, respectively.
The gains are largest on SearchQA and GSM8K, where most of the
unseen-test improvement occurs early. Overall, the stopping rule
substantially reduces evolution cost while maintaining comparable
unseen-test performance in our experiments.

![Refer to caption](2610.04756v1/figure/livemath_reward_hack.png)

Figure 4: LiveMath trajectory under SkillOpt with DeepSeek V4 Flash.

The detector stays silent when improvement continues. A useful stopping
rule should remain inactive while meaningful improvement continues.
SpreadsheetBench serves as a negative control: its validation accuracy
rises from $`0.375`$ to $`0.70`$ over $`16`$ rounds, with further gains
as late as round $`11`$
([Figure 3](#S4.F3 "In 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")).
Under the reported aGRAPA configuration ($`\varepsilon=0.01`$,
$`\delta=0.05`$), no alarm is raised within the observed horizon. This
complements the main results by showing that the detector remains silent
on a trajectory that continues to improve.

Early stopping avoids a degraded late artifact. LiveMath further
illustrates the value of combining principled stopping with
change-point-based output selection. As shown in
[Figure 4](#S4.F4 "In 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?"),
SkillOpt makes an early genuine improvement and then enters a long
low-improvement regime. If the system were allowed to run for the full
budget, it would eventually reach round 16, where it accepts a
degenerate skill that instructs the model to answer “A” without
reasoning, exploiting the benchmark’s skewed answer distribution and
producing a large accuracy increase. Our detector instead raises an
alarm at $`T_{\mathrm{alarm}}=8`$, stopping the evolution well before
this late artifact is ever reached. It also estimates the change point
at $`\hat{\nu}=2`$, and the output-selection rule returns the artifact
from round 1. Thus, the stopping rule prevents the system from
continuing into the later degraded regime, while the change-point
estimate determines which earlier artifact should be returned. The
detector does not explicitly identify reward hacking; it stops because
sustained meaningful improvement has already ceased.

We next test whether the stopping rule remains reliable beyond the main

then examine sensitivity to the validation-set size and detector
parameters.

Across self-evolving systems. We first test whether the stopping rule
transfers across different self-evolving algorithms. Holding the
underlying model and benchmarks fixed, we replace SkillOpt with GEPA,

## 5 Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced a stopping and output-selection procedure for
self-evolving systems that uses sequential betting to determine when
further evolution is no longer worthwhile and change-point estimation to
select an earlier artifact for output. The procedure operates on paired
evaluation outcomes already produced during evolution and requires no
modification to the underlying system. Across multiple benchmarks,
models, and self-evolving systems, it stops well before the fixed budget
is exhausted while substantially reducing computation and retaining
comparable unseen-test performance.

Future work could extend the localization guarantee to adaptive betting
schemes such as aGRAPA, study data-driven ways to calibrate the stopping
rule, and generalize the framework to richer evaluation signals beyond
binary correctness. It would also be useful to examine how the method
behaves under different validation-set sizes, noisier feedback, and more
complex self-evolving systems in which proposals are generated and
evaluated through longer interaction loops. More broadly, our results
suggest that self-evolving systems need not rely on predetermined fixed
budgets: sequential evidence can guide when to stop, while change-point
estimation can help determine which earlier artifact to return.

## Ethics Statement

This work studies when to stop self-evolving AI systems in order to
avoid unnecessary computation while preserving useful improvements. Our
experiments use existing benchmarks and language models and do not
involve human subjects or the collection of new personal data. The
proposed stopping procedure may help reduce redundant model calls and
associated computational cost. As with other evaluation-based stopping
methods, its behavior depends on the representativeness of the
validation data, and care should be taken when applying it in
high-stakes settings.

## Appendix A Proofs

We prove the two propositions in
[Section 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").
Throughout, recall that

|     |                                         |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       Z_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{t,i},
       ```                                      |     |

and that the pre-change condition is

|     |                                                        |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\geq\varepsilon.
       ```                                                     |     | (8) |

We assume that $`X_{t,1},\ldots,X_{t,n}`$ are conditionally independent
given $`\mathcal{F}_{t-1}`$ and that each betting fraction
$`\lambda_{s,t}\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$ is predictable.

### A.1 Validity of Each Restart Wealth Process

###### Proof.

Fix a restart time $`s`$. For $`t\geq s`$, write the multiplicative
increment of the restart wealth as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
L_{t}^{(s)}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1+\lambda_{s,t}\left(\varepsilon-X_{t,i}\right)\right],
``` |  |

so that

|     |                                                              |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       W_{t}^{(s)}=W_{t-1}^{(s)}L_{t}^{(s)},\qquad W_{s-1}^{(s)}=1.
       ```                                                           |     |

We first verify non-negativity. Since $`X_{t,i}\in\{-1,0,1\}`$,
$`\varepsilon\in(0,1)`$, and $`\lambda_{s,t}\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
1+\lambda_{s,t}(\varepsilon-X_{t,i})\geq 1+\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon-1)=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{2}>0.
``` |  |

Hence $`W_{t}^{(s)}`$ is nonnegative.

Next, define

|     |                                                     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mu_{t,i}=\mathbb{E}[X_{t,i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}].
       ```                                                  |     |

Because $`\lambda_{s,t}`$ is $`\mathcal{F}_{t-1}`$-measurable and the
item-level signals are conditionally independent given
$`\mathcal{F}_{t-1}`$,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{t}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left[1+\lambda_{s,t}\left(\varepsilon-\mu_{t,i}\right)\right].`$ |  | (9) |

All factors in the product are positive, so by the arithmetic–geometric
mean inequality,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{t}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(1+\lambda_{s,t}(\varepsilon-\mu_{t,i})\right)\right]^{n}`$ |  | (10) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left[1+\lambda_{s,t}\left(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{t,i}\right)\right]^{n}.`$ |  | (11) |

Since

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{t,i}=\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\geq\varepsilon
``` |  |

under the pre-change condition, we obtain

|     |                                                     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[L_{t}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\leq 1.
       ```                                                  |     |

Therefore,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{t}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]`$ | $`\displaystyle=W_{t-1}^{(s)}\mathbb{E}[L_{t}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]`$ |  | (12) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq W_{t-1}^{(s)}.`$ |  | (13) |

Thus $`\{W_{t}^{(s)}\}_{t\geq s-1}`$ is a nonnegative supermartingale
initialized at one.

Ville’s inequality then gives, for any $`h>0`$,

|     |                                                                 |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Pr\left(\sup_{t\geq s}W_{t}^{(s)}\geq h\right)\leq\frac{1}{h}.
       ```                                                              |     |

∎

### A.2 E-Detector Validity and False-Alarm Run-Length Guarantee

We next show that combining the restart processes through the maximum
restart wealth produces a valid e-detector. This construction
corresponds to the CUSUM-style e-detector of [Shin et al.
\[2024\]](#bib.bib26).

###### Proof.

Extend each restart process by setting $`W_{t}^{(s)}=1`$ for $`t<s`$. By
[Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Restart and stopping validity). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?"),
each $`\{W_{t}^{(s)}\}_{t\geq 0}`$ is a nonnegative restart e-process
under the pre-change condition.

Recall that

|     |                                        |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       M_{t}=\max_{1\leq s\leq t}W_{t}^{(s)}.
       ```                                     |     |

For any stopping time $`\tau`$,

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       M_{\tau}\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\tau}W_{\tau}^{(s)}.
       ```                                           |     |

Taking expectations and writing the random upper limit using indicators
gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}]`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{s\leq\tau\}W_{\tau}^{(s)}\right].`$ |  | (14) |

Because $`\{s\leq\tau\}\in\mathcal{F}_{s-1}`$ and $`W^{(s)}`$ is a valid
restart e-process, optional stopping gives

|     |                                                                   |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}\left[W_{\tau}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s-1}\right]\leq 1.
       ```                                                                |     |

Therefore,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{s\leq\tau\}W_{\tau}^{(s)}\right]`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\Pr(s\leq\tau).`$ |  | (15) |

Summing over $`s`$,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}]`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\Pr(s\leq\tau)`$ |  | (16) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\tau].`$ |  | (17) |

Hence $`M_{t}`$ satisfies the defining e-detector condition

|     |                                          |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[M_{\tau}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\tau]
       ```                                       |     |

for every stopping time $`\tau`$, and is therefore a valid e-detector in
the sense of [Shin et al. \[2024\]](#bib.bib26).

Now recall the alarm rule

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{alarm}}=\inf\left\{t\geq 1:M_{t}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}\right\}.
``` |  |

If $`T_{\mathrm{alarm}}`$ is not almost surely finite, then
$`\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathrm{alarm}}]=\infty`$ and the desired result holds
immediately. Otherwise,

|     |                                             |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       M_{T_{\mathrm{alarm}}}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}.
       ```                                          |     |

Applying the e-detector inequality at $`\tau=T_{\mathrm{alarm}}`$ gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{1}{\delta}\leq\mathbb{E}[M_{T_{\mathrm{alarm}}}]\leq\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathrm{alarm}}],
``` |  |

and therefore

|     |                                                     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[T_{\mathrm{alarm}}]\geq\frac{1}{\delta}.
       ```                                                  |     |

Thus the stopping rule has average run length to false alarm of at least
$`1/\delta`$ whenever the pre-change condition continues to hold. ∎

### A.3 Population Characterization of the Change Point

###### Proof of the population conclusion in Proposition [2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Change-point identification and localization). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").

For this result, consider the fixed betting fraction $`\lambda`$ used in
[Proposition 2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Change-point identification and localization). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").
Taking logarithms of the restart wealth gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log W_{T}^{(s)}=\sum_{u=s}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(1+\lambda(\varepsilon-X_{u,i})\right).
``` |  |

Let $`g_{u}=n\mathbb{E}[Y_{u}]`$, with $`Y_{u}`$ defined in
[Equation 7](#S3.E7 "In 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").
By the tower property, $`g_{u}<0`$ before $`\nu`$ and $`g_{u}>0`$ from
$`\nu`$ onward. Thus,

|     |                                                   |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}[\log W_{T}^{(s)}]=\sum_{u=s}^{T}g_{u}.
       ```                                                |     | (18) |

Suppose first that $`s<\nu`$. Then

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log W_{T}^{(s)}]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{u=s}^{\nu-1}g_{u}+\sum_{u=\nu}^{T}g_{u}`$ |  | (19) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle<\sum_{u=\nu}^{T}g_{u}=\mathbb{E}[\log W_{T}^{(\nu)}],`$ |  | (20) |

because $`g_{u}<0`$ for all $`u<\nu`$.

Now suppose that $`s>\nu`$. Then

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log W_{T}^{(s)}]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{u=s}^{T}g_{u}`$ |  | (21) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{u=\nu}^{T}g_{u}-\sum_{u=\nu}^{s-1}g_{u}`$ |  | (22) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle<\sum_{u=\nu}^{T}g_{u}=\mathbb{E}[\log W_{T}^{(\nu)}],`$ |  | (23) |

because $`g_{u}>0`$ for all $`u\geq\nu`$.

Thus every $`s\neq\nu`$ has strictly smaller expected log restart wealth
than $`s=\nu`$, and hence

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nu=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\leq s\leq T}\mathbb{E}[\log W_{T}^{(s)}].
``` |  |

This is a population-level characterization for the common
fixed-$`\lambda`$ setting considered here. For the corresponding
realized restart wealth, the logarithm is strictly increasing, so

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{s}W_{T}^{(s)}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{s}\log W_{T}^{(s)}.
``` |  |

Thus realized wealth and realized log wealth induce the same restart
selection in this setting. ∎

### A.4 Change-Point Localization Error Bound

###### Proof of the localization bound in Proposition [2](#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2 (Change-point identification and localization). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").

Recall that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Y_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{t,i},\qquad V_{t,i}=\log\left(1+\lambda(\varepsilon-X_{t,i})\right),
``` |  |

and therefore

|     |                                        |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \log W_{T}^{(s)}=n\sum_{u=s}^{T}Y_{u}.
       ```                                     |     |

By assumption, conditional on $`\mathcal{F}_{t-1}`$, the centered
variables $`V_{t,i}-\mathbb{E}[V_{t,i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]`$ are
independent and $`\sigma^{2}`$-sub-Gaussian. Hence,

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       Y_{t}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
       ```                                           |     |

is conditionally $`\sigma^{2}/n`$-sub-Gaussian.

First consider a starting round $`s<\nu`$. Since the processes
initialized at $`s`$ and $`\nu`$ share all factors from round $`\nu`$
through $`T`$,

|     |                                                               |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \log W_{T}^{(s)}-\log W_{T}^{(\nu)}=n\sum_{u=s}^{\nu-1}Y_{u}.
       ```                                                            |     |

Let $`k=\nu-s`$. Then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
W_{T}^{(s)}\geq W_{T}^{(\nu)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{u=s}^{\nu-1}Y_{u}\geq 0.
``` |  |

Since $`\mathbb{E}[Y_{u}\mid\mathcal{F}_{u-1}]\leq-\kappa`$ for
$`u<\nu`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{u=s}^{\nu-1}\mathbb{E}[Y_{u}\mid\mathcal{F}_{u-1}]\leq-k\kappa.
``` |  |

Thus, defining

|     |                                                     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       D_{u}=Y_{u}-\mathbb{E}[Y_{u}\mid\mathcal{F}_{u-1}],
       ```                                                  |     |

the event $`\sum_{u=s}^{\nu-1}Y_{u}\geq 0`$ implies

|     |                                      |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \sum_{u=s}^{\nu-1}D_{u}\geq k\kappa.
       ```                                   |     |

Because each $`D_{u}`$ is conditionally $`\sigma^{2}/n`$-sub-Gaussian,
the sum over $`k`$ rounds is conditionally
$`k\sigma^{2}/n`$-sub-Gaussian. Therefore,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\left(W_{T}^{(s)}\geq W_{T}^{(\nu)}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{nk\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).
``` |  |

Now consider $`s>\nu`$. In this case,

|     |                                                                |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \log W_{T}^{(s)}-\log W_{T}^{(\nu)}=-n\sum_{u=\nu}^{s-1}Y_{u}.
       ```                                                             |     |

Let $`k=s-\nu`$. Then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
W_{T}^{(s)}\geq W_{T}^{(\nu)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{u=\nu}^{s-1}Y_{u}\leq 0.
``` |  |

Since $`\mathbb{E}[Y_{u}\mid\mathcal{F}_{u-1}]\geq\kappa`$ for
$`u\geq\nu`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{u=\nu}^{s-1}\mathbb{E}[Y_{u}\mid\mathcal{F}_{u-1}]\geq k\kappa.
``` |  |

Hence the event $`\sum_{u=\nu}^{s-1}Y_{u}\leq 0`$ implies

|     |                                      |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \sum_{u=\nu}^{s-1}D_{u}\leq-k\kappa.
       ```                                   |     |

Applying the lower-tail sub-Gaussian bound gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\left(W_{T}^{(s)}\geq W_{T}^{(\nu)}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{nk\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).
``` |  |

If $`|\hat{\nu}_{T}-\nu|\geq m`$, then there exists some starting round
$`s`$ with $`|s-\nu|\geq m`$ such that
$`W_{T}^{(s)}\geq W_{T}^{(\nu)}`$. Applying a union bound over starting
points on both sides of $`\nu`$ gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\left(|\hat{\nu}_{T}-\nu|\geq m\right)\leq 2\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{nk\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).
``` |  |

Evaluating the geometric series yields

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\left(|\hat{\nu}_{T}-\nu|\geq m\right)\leq\frac{2\exp\left(-\frac{nm\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{n\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}.
``` |  |

To obtain the equivalent high-probability form, let $`\alpha\in(0,1)`$
and choose $`m`$ such that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{2\exp\left(-\frac{nm\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{n\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}\leq\alpha.
``` |  |

Solving for $`m`$ gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
m\geq\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{n\kappa^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2}{\alpha\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{n\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right)}\right).
``` |  |

Let $`r_{\alpha}`$ denote the right-hand side and choose
$`m=\lceil r_{\alpha}\rceil`$. Since $`|\hat{\nu}_{T}-\nu|`$ is
integer-valued, the events $`\{|\hat{\nu}_{T}-\nu|\geq r_{\alpha}\}`$
and $`\{|\hat{\nu}_{T}-\nu|\geq m\}`$ coincide. Therefore, with
probability at least $`1-\alpha`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
|\hat{\nu}_{T}-\nu|<\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{n\kappa^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2}{\alpha\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{n\kappa^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right)}\right),
``` |  |

which proves the stated result. ∎

## Appendix B Betting fraction and detectability floor

By
[Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Restart and stopping validity). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?"),
any predictable $`\lambda_{s,t}\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$ yields a valid
restart wealth process. We instantiate the betting rule in two ways:
adaptive aGRAPA and a fixed mixture.

##### Fixed mixture.

Fix a finite grid
$`\Lambda=\{\lambda^{(1)},\dots,\lambda^{(m)}\}\subset[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$
and nonnegative weights $`w_{j}`$ summing to $`1`$; we use uniform
weights over $`\Lambda=\{0.1,0.2,0.4\}`$. For each start $`s`$, run one
wealth process per grid value and average them:

|     |                                                                  |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \overline{W}_{t}^{(s)}=\sum_{j}w_{j}\,W_{t}^{(s,\lambda^{(j)})}.
       ```                                                               |     |

For the fixed-mixture betting scheme, this averaged wealth serves as the
restart wealth $`W_{t}^{(s)}`$ used by the stopping and output-selection
procedure.

###### Proposition 3 (The mixture is valid).

If each $`W_{t}^{(s,\lambda^{(j)})}`$ is a nonnegative supermartingale
under $`H_{0}`$, then so is $`\overline{W}_{t}^{(s)}`$, and the
false-alarm bound of
[Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Restart and stopping validity). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")
applies with the same threshold $`1/\delta`$.

###### Proof.

Non-negativity is immediate. By linearity of conditional expectation,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\overline{W}_{t}^{(s)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\bigr]=\sum_{j}w_{j}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[W_{t}^{(s,\lambda^{(j)})}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\bigr]\leq\sum_{j}w_{j}\,W_{t-1}^{(s,\lambda^{(j)})}=\overline{W}_{t-1}^{(s)},
``` |  |

and $`\overline{W}_{s-1}^{(s)}=\sum_{j}w_{j}=1`$. A convex combination
of nonnegative supermartingales is a nonnegative supermartingale. ∎

The mixture requires no online estimation and remains a valid
supermartingale regardless of sample size.

##### Growth-optimal plug-in (aGRAPA).

A Kelly gambler maximizes $`\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\lambda G_{t,i})]`$; a
second-order expansion yields the approximate maximizer
$`\lambda^{\star}=\mu/(\sigma^{2}+\mu^{2})`$ with
$`\mu=\mathbb{E}[G_{t,i}]`$ and
$`\sigma^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(G_{t,i})`$. Replacing the moments by
predictable plug-ins over $`[s,t-1]`$ and truncating to
$`[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$ gives, for a gambler started at $`s`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\lambda_{s,t}=\min\!\left(\frac{\max(\hat{\mu}_{s,t-1},\,0)}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{s,t-1}+\hat{\mu}_{s,t-1}^{2}},\ \tfrac{1}{2}\right),\quad\hat{\mu}_{s,t-1}=\varepsilon-\bar{Z}_{s,t-1},\quad\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{s,t-1}=\bar{q}_{s,t-1}-\bar{Z}_{s,t-1}^{2},
``` |  |

where $`\bar{Z}_{s,t-1}=\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{u=s}^{t-1}Z_{u}`$ and
$`\bar{q}_{s,t-1}=\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{u=s}^{t-1}\frac{n_{01}(u)+n_{10}(u)}{n}`$
is the mean discordant rate; on the empty window set
$`\lambda_{s,s}=\lambda_{0}`$ for a fixed
$`\lambda_{0}\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$. We set the ratio to zero when
$`\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{s,t-1}+\hat{\mu}_{s,t-1}^{2}=0`$. The moment
identities follow from $`G=\varepsilon-X`$ with $`X\in\{-1,0,+1\}`$:
$`\mathbb{E}[G]=\varepsilon-\mathbb{E}[X]`$ gives
$`\hat{\mu}=\varepsilon-\bar{Z}`$, while $`X^{2}=\mathbf{1}\{X\neq 0\}`$
gives
$`\operatorname{Var}(G)=\operatorname{Var}(X)=\bar{q}-\bar{Z}^{2}`$,
with $`\varepsilon`$ cancelling.

###### Proposition 4 (aGRAPA is valid).

The fraction $`\lambda_{s,t}`$ above is predictable and lies in
$`[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$, hence the aGRAPA wealth process is a nonnegative
supermartingale under $`H_{0}`$ by
[Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Restart and stopping validity). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").

###### Proof.

The window $`[s,t-1]`$ excludes round $`t`$, so $`\hat{\mu}_{s,t-1}`$
and $`\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{s,t-1}`$ are $`\mathcal{F}_{t-1}`$-measurable
and $`\lambda_{s,t}`$ is predictable. The inner $`\max(\cdot,0)`$ and
outer $`\min(\cdot,\tfrac{1}{2})`$ force
$`\lambda_{s,t}\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]`$, with $`\lambda_{s,t}=0`$ when the
denominator vanishes. Validity follows from
[Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Restart and stopping validity). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?").
∎

##### Detectability floor.

###### Proposition 5 (Approximate plateau length required to fire).

On a pure plateau with $`Z_{u}=0`$, so every item pays
$`G_{u,i}=\varepsilon`$, a single gambler with constant fraction
$`\lambda`$ multiplies its wealth by $`(1+\lambda\varepsilon)^{n}`$ per
round. Starting from wealth of order $`1`$ at the onset of the plateau,
the approximate number of consecutive plateau rounds required to reach
$`1/\delta`$ is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{need}}\approx\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n\log(1+\lambda\varepsilon)}.
``` |  |

For the mixture over a grid $`\Lambda`$, this becomes

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{need}}\approx\frac{\log(1/\delta)+\log|\Lambda|}{n\log(1+\lambda_{\max}\varepsilon)},\qquad\lambda_{\max}=\max\Lambda.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

On a plateau round every item pays $`\varepsilon`$, so the per-round
wealth factor of a constant-$`\lambda`$ gambler is
$`(1+\lambda\varepsilon)^{n}`$ and after $`T`$ rounds the log-wealth
grows by $`T\,n\log(1+\lambda\varepsilon)`$. Setting this equal to
$`\log(1/\delta)`$ and solving for $`T`$ gives the first display; for
the mixture, the fastest-growing grid value dominates up to a factor
$`|\Lambda|`$, adding $`\log|\Lambda|`$. ∎

###### Corollary 1 (Resolution scales with the validation set).

For small $`\lambda\varepsilon`$,
$`\log(1+\lambda\varepsilon)\approx\lambda\varepsilon`$, so
$`T_{\mathrm{need}}\approx(\log(1/\delta)+\log|\Lambda|)/(n\lambda_{\max}\varepsilon)`$.
For a fixed target plateau length the smallest resolvable
$`\varepsilon`$ scales like $`1/n`$: doubling $`D_{\mathrm{val}}`$
halves the worthwhile gain the detector can resolve.

When $`n`$ or $`\varepsilon`$ is small relative to the available plateau
length, the detector correctly stays silent. This is not a failure:
validity
([Proposition 1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Restart and stopping validity). ‣ 3.3 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 3 Method ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?"))
holds regardless of $`n`$, and only power degrades.

## Appendix C Paired Unseen-Test Comparison

We compare the artifact returned by our stopping procedure and the
artifact selected by the full run on the same unseen-test examples.
Because each example produces a paired binary correctness outcome, the
observations can be summarized by the $`2\times 2`$ table

|     |                                                                   |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \begin{array}[]{c|cc}&\text{Full incorrect}&\text{Full correct}\\
       \hline\cr\text{Returned incorrect}&n_{00}&n_{01}\\
       \text{Returned correct}&n_{10}&n_{11}\end{array}.
       ```                                                                |     |

Here, $`n_{01}`$ counts examples answered incorrectly by the returned
artifact but correctly by the full-run artifact, while $`n_{10}`$ counts
examples on which the reverse occurs.

##### Paired accuracy difference.

For $`N=n_{00}+n_{01}+n_{10}+n_{11}`$ paired examples, the difference in
test accuracy is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{acc}}=\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}_{\mathrm{returned}}-\widehat{\mathrm{Acc}}_{\mathrm{full}}=\frac{n_{10}-n_{01}}{N}.
``` |  |

Thus, positive values favor the returned artifact and negative values
favor the full-run artifact. Let

|     |                                 |     |
|-----|---------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \hat{q}=\frac{n_{01}+n_{10}}{N}
       ```                              |     |

denote the fraction of discordant pairs. Since the per-example paired
difference takes values in $`\{-1,0,+1\}`$, an estimated standard error
is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{q}-\widehat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{acc}}^{\,2}}{N}}.
``` |  |

We report the corresponding normal-approximation 95% confidence interval

|     |                                                                 |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \widehat{\Delta}_{\mathrm{acc}}\pm 1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}.
       ```                                                              |     |

The paired accuracy difference quantifies both the magnitude and
uncertainty of the performance change. We report it together with its

## Appendix D GEPA alignment and token estimation

##### Seed alignment.

SkillOpt begins from a fixed skill-document template with an empty skill
body, whereas GEPA begins from an initial prompt. To make the two
systems’ starting points comparable, we align the GEPA seed to
SkillOpt’s template: the seed prompt carries the same task framing, with
an empty-skill placeholder in place of SkillOpt’s initial skill body.
This ensures that round $`t{=}0`$ denotes a comparable “no learned
content” state in both systems, so that alarm times and $`\hat{\nu}`$
are measured from the same origin.

##### Per-item outcomes.

GEPA’s gate selects along a Pareto front over minibatch subscores rather
than scoring on a single full-validation pass. We read the per-item
outcomes $`b_{t,i},c_{t,i}`$ that the detector consumes directly from
GEPA’s saved validation-subscore matrix, which records each candidate’s
per-item correctness on the shared validation split.

##### Token estimation.

SkillOpt logs token usage per agent call, so its token counts are exact.
GEPA’s public logs record the number of metric calls (LM inferences over
the validation set) made during discovery, but not a per-call token
breakdown. We therefore estimate GEPA token usage as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{tokens}\;\approx\;(\text{number of metric calls})\times(\text{average tokens per call}),
``` |  |

with the average tokens per call derived from the matched
SkillOpt–DeepSeek rollout on the same benchmark: $`2249`$ tokens/call on
SearchQA and $`1354`$ tokens/call on GSM8K. Because a metric call is an
LM inference over the same validation set in both systems, the per-call
cost is comparable across systems, so this estimate is accurate up to
differences in prompt length between the two seeds. The GEPA token
figures in
[Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.3 Ablation and Sensitivity ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ When Is Enough Enough in Self-Evolving LLM Systems?")
should be read as estimates on this basis; the alarm and $`\hat{\nu}`$
values, which depend only on the per-item outcomes, are exact.

## Appendix G Additional Related Work

##### Stopping rules for training and inference.

Early stopping has long been used in model training to reduce
computation and mitigate overfitting, typically by terminating
optimization when validation performance ceases to improve according to
a patience rule or threshold \[[Prechelt, 2012](#bib.bib17), [Caruana
et al., 2001](#bib.bib3)\]. Subsequent work has studied more principled,
data-dependent stopping criteria for iterative learning
procedures \[[Yao et al., 2007](#bib.bib39), [Raskutti et al.,
2014](#bib.bib20)\]. Related stopping mechanisms have also been explored
in modern LLM systems, primarily at inference time. Semantic patience
terminates iterative multi-agent refinement when successive outputs
stabilize \[[Shrivastava, 2026](#bib.bib29)\]; statistical stopping
rules have been proposed to limit overthinking within a reasoning
trajectory \[[Xie et al., 2026](#bib.bib36)\]; hidden-state diagnostics
have been used to detect premature commitment during inference \[[Mehta,
2026](#bib.bib12)\]; and always-valid e-processes have been applied to
determine when a generate–verify procedure has accumulated sufficient
evidence to release an output \[[Cho and Sun, 2026](#bib.bib5)\]. These
approaches study when to terminate training or inference under their
respective signals and objectives. In contrast, our setting concerns a
self-evolving propose–evaluate–commit loop over persistent artifacts,
where stopping must be determined from repeated paired validation
outcomes and is coupled with the additional problem of selecting which
previously retained artifact to return.
````

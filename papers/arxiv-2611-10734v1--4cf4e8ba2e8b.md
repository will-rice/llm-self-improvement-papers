# Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation

Yujuan Pang    Wenhao Xu    Yinglei Teng Affiliation: Beijing University
of Posts and Telecommunications Affiliation: Beijing, China

###### Abstract

Multimodal large language models have made progress in general visual
tasks. However, limitations remain in fine-grained visual tasks
requiring evidence from minute image regions. Existing methods primarily
train models using evidence-related regions while overlooking the
influence of extensive background regions, resulting in a limited
ability to resist interference from irrelevant regions. We propose
selective positive-negative on-policy self-distillation (SPN-OPSD),
which considers both evidence and background regions. Supervision from
both views improves the model’s ability to resist interference from
irrelevant regions. Furthermore, selective token-level distillation
provides more informative supervision, improving the model’s ability to
attend to evidence regions in Fine-Grained Visual Tasks. On Qwen3.5-2B,
SPN-OPSD improves accuracy on V\* Bench, ZoomBench, and
MME-RealWorld-Lite from 76.44%, 45.92%, and 57.43% to 84.82%, 54.56%,
and 59.56%, respectively. Similar gains are also observed on
Qwen3.5-0.8B.

###### Index Terms: 

fine-grained visual tasks, multimodal large language models, on-policy
self-distillation

## I Introduction

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have demonstrated strong
capabilities in general visual understanding and
reasoning \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2), [3](#bib.bib3)\]. However,
they still face challenges in fine-grained visual tasks, where answers
often depend on small but crucial details that occupy only a small
portion of an image \[[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5)\]. Extensive
backgrounds and similar objects further complicate the identification of
relevant evidence.

The recent “Thinking with Images” paradigm acquires local evidence by
iteratively zooming into and cropping regions of interest during
inference \[[6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib8)\]. Other
methods develop the ability to perceive local visual details through
local-view supervision during training \[[9](#bib.bib9),
[10](#bib.bib10)\], or enhance fine-grained visual perception through
visual evidence localization \[[11](#bib.bib11), [12](#bib.bib12)\] and
attention alignment \[[13](#bib.bib13)\]. These methods indeed improve
model capabilities by teaching models “what to attend to.” However,
inspired by the observation that background regions can also introduce
interference \[[6](#bib.bib6), [14](#bib.bib14)\], we argue that it is
also necessary to teach models “what not to attend to.”

To enhance fine-grained visual perception, DeFacto \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]
constructs counterfactual examples by masking evidence regions and
trains models to output “Unknown” when crucial evidence is absent.
VA-OPD \[[15](#bib.bib15)\] defines Visual Advantage as the difference
in teacher token log-probabilities with and without fine-grained visual
details, and uses it to adjust distillation weights. Despite their
progress on fine-grained visual tasks, these methods have a limitation:
they use counterfactual signals to learn abstention behavior or adjust
positive distillation weights, but do not explicitly guide the student
away from predictive distributions formed in the absence of evidence.
They therefore lack a direct constraint on erroneous predictions
supported by nonessential visual information.

![Refer to caption](2611.10734v1/figures/buchongtu1.png)

Fig. 1: Visual Attention Visualization

To address this limitation, we propose selective positive-negative
on-policy self-distillation (SPN-OPSD), a positive–negative teacher
framework for on-policy self-distillation that uses both evidence and
background regions as privileged information. Specifically, the positive
teacher takes a cropped view of the evidence region as input and
provides a target distribution grounded in local evidence. The negative
teacher takes a view in which crucial evidence is suppressed while
background information is retained, capturing predictive tendencies in
the absence of crucial evidence. The student takes the original image as
input and receives selective negative supervision while continuously
learning from the positive teacher’s distribution. This mechanism
enables the student to learn to use crucial visual evidence while
selectively moving away from predictive distributions unsupported by
that evidence. As shown in
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ I Introduction ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
the student trained with SPN-OPSD maintains its focus on the evidence
region and devotes less attention to irrelevant regions than its
counterpart trained with positive-only distillation, demonstrating a
greater ability to resist interference from irrelevant image regions.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

- •
  We introduce SPN-OPSD for fine-grained visual tasks. Complementary
  evidence-enhanced and evidence-suppressed views incorporate both
  relevant evidence and background interference into the distillation
  process.
- •
  We develop a token-level selection mechanism based on the
  distributions of the student and both teachers. Teacher disagreement
  identifies valuable supervision positions, while the student’s
  relative difference to each teacher identifies predictions susceptible
  to background interference. The student approaches the positive
  teacher at all valid positions and moves away from the negative
  teacher at selected positions, promoting evidence use while reducing
  irrelevant interference.
- •
  We validate the effectiveness of our method on three benchmarks and at
  two model sizes. Our method outperforms both existing
  positive-evidence methods and the evaluated open-source models.

## II Our Proposed Method

![Refer to caption](2611.10734v1/figures/yanshiwengao.png)

### II-A Visual Views for the Student and Teachers

Let $`\mathcal{D}=\{(I_{i},q_{i},R_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}`$, where $`R`$ is
the question-relevant evidence region. We enhance and suppress the same
region to obtain the positive and negative teacher views:

|     |                         |                                                    |     |     |
| --- | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle I^{+}`$ | $`\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Crop}(I,R)`$        |     | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle I^{-}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{GaussianBlur}(I,R)`$ |     |     |

The crop operation extracts the evidence region, and the resulting crop
is enlarged by a factor of two to emphasize local details needed to
answer the question. Gaussian blur is applied only within the evidence
region, weakening decisive details while preserving the surrounding
background.

We instantiate the student and both teacher policies from the same MLLM
$`p_{\theta}`$ under different input conditions:

|     |                                                            |                                                             |     |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p^{s}(\cdot\mid I,q)`$                     | $`\displaystyle=p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid I,q)`$                 |     | (2) |
|     | $`\displaystyle p^{t^{+}}(\cdot\mid I^{+},q)`$             | $`\displaystyle=p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid I^{+},q)`$             |     |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle p^{t^{-}}(\cdot\mid I^{-},q\oplus u^{-})`$ | $`\displaystyle=p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid I^{-},q\oplus u^{-})`$ |     |     |

where $`\oplus`$ denotes text concatenation. Because questions originate
from cropped regions, we mark $`R`$ with a red box in the full image and
append “Answer the question by focusing only on the object inside the
red bounding box” to $`q`$ to avoid referential ambiguity. The negative
teacher additionally receives $`u^{-}`$: “Ignore visual information
inside the red box and answer the question using only visual information
outside the red box.” The auxiliary instruction further restricts
reliance on the evidence region. Enhancing and suppressing the same
evidence creates complementary conditions that encourage the student to
use local evidence while reducing its reliance on background
information. The proposed framework is illustrated in
Fig. [2](#S2.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ II Our Proposed Method ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation").

### II-B Positive and Negative Distillation

The student first generates a response

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{|y|})\sim p^{s}(\cdot\mid I,q)
       ```                                                |     | (3) |

Both teachers re-evaluate it along the same prefix
$`y_{<n}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1})`$, giving token-level distributions:

|     |                             |                                                                   |     |     |
| --- | --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle y_{n}^{s}`$ | $`\displaystyle=p^{s}(\cdot\mid I,q,y_{<n})`$                     |     | (4) |
|     | $`\displaystyle y_{n}^{+}`$ | $`\displaystyle=p^{t^{+}}(\cdot\mid I^{+},q,y_{<n})`$             |     |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle y_{n}^{-}`$ | $`\displaystyle=p^{t^{-}}(\cdot\mid I^{-},q\oplus u^{-},y_{<n})`$ |     |     |

Using the student’s own generated prefixes aligns the state
distributions encountered during training and
inference \[[16](#bib.bib16)\], avoiding the prefix mismatch underlying
error accumulation in off-policy distillation \[[17](#bib.bib17)\].

#### II-B1 Positive distillation

The evidence crop gives the positive teacher privileged local
information, so we minimize the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) between
the student and positive teacher at every position:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       d_{s,t^{+}}^{(n)}=D\left(y_{n}^{s}\,\|\,y_{n}^{+}\right)
       ```                                                       |     | (5) |

The corresponding positive distillation loss is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{positive}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(I,q,R)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
y\sim p^{s}(\cdot\mid I,q)\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{n=1}^{|y|}d_{s,t^{+}}^{(n)}\right]
``` |  | (6) |

Positive distillation covers all valid prediction positions,
independently of token selection for negative supervision, and
continuously preserves a clear direction for evidence learning. Compared
with the sparse sequence-level rewards used in reinforcement learning
with verifiable rewards (RLVR), token-level distribution matching
provides denser supervision.

#### II-B2 Negative distillation

Negative supervision is applied selectively to informative tokens
through a two-stage process based on teacher disagreement and the
student’s current prediction preference.

Stage 1: Selecting candidate tokens by teacher disagreement. The
teachers can produce the same prediction despite observing different
views. At such positions, pulling the student toward the positive
teacher while pushing it away from the negative teacher can introduce
conflicting supervision. We therefore identify candidate tokens with
clear teacher disagreement.

The teacher disagreement at position $`n`$ is

|     |                                                       |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Delta^{(n)}=D\!\left(p_{n}^{+}\,\|\,p_{n}^{-}\right)
       ```                                                    |     | (7) |

Let $`v_{n}^{\pm}=\arg\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{n}^{\pm}(v)`$ denote the
teachers’ top-1 predictions over vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$. The
first-stage selection mask is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g^{(n)}=\mathbb{I}[v_{n}^{+}\neq v_{n}^{-}]\,\mathbb{I}[\Delta^{(n)}>\delta]
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\mathbb{I}[\cdot]`$ is an indicator function and $`\delta`$ is
the disagreement threshold. The two conditions jointly assess prediction
preference and disagreement strength. Different top-1 predictions ensure
distinct preferred tokens, while the $`\Delta^{(n)}`$ threshold controls
the magnitude of distributional disagreement.

How Does the Degree of Disagreement Between Positive and Negative
Teachers Affect Negative Supervision? We examine Qwen3.5-0.8B responses
on the 5.8K training examples, with both teachers evaluating the
student-generated prefixes under their respective views. To characterize
the student’s tendency at each position, we define the interference
score (IS) as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{IS}^{(n)}=D(p_{n}^{s}\,\|\,p_{n}^{+})-D(p_{n}^{s}\,\|\,p_{n}^{-})
``` |  | (9) |

A positive IS indicates that the student is closer to the negative
teacher.
Fig. [3](#S2.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ II-B2 Negative distillation ‣ II-B Positive and Negative Distillation ‣ II Our Proposed Method ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")
shows that incorrect answers contain a higher fraction of positive-IS
tokens than correct answers, and this gap generally widens with the
disagreement threshold. This observation motivates concentrating
negative supervision on positions with stronger teacher disagreement.
However, increasing the threshold also reduces candidate coverage. In
the disagreement-only comparison, $`\delta=0.5`$ balances corrective
value and coverage and achieves the best average accuracy
(Table [I](#S2.T1 "TABLE I ‣ II-B2 Negative distillation ‣ II-B Positive and Negative Distillation ‣ II Our Proposed Method ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")(a)).

candidate positions, negative supervision should correct predictions
closer to the negative teacher and strengthen predictions closer to the

the student–negative-teacher difference and relative difference as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle d_{s,t^{-}}^{(n)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=D\!\left(p_{n}^{s}\,\|\,p_{n}^{-}\right)`$ |  | (11) |
|  | $`\displaystyle d_{s,T}^{(n)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=d_{s,t^{+}}^{(n)}-d_{s,t^{-}}^{(n)}`$ |  |  |

Eq. [9](#S2.E9 "In II-B2 Negative distillation ‣ II-B Positive and Negative Distillation ‣ II Our Proposed Method ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation").

How Much Positive Advantage Do Student Tokens Need? An intuitive choice
is to constrain only tokens favoring the negative teacher, whereas our
experiments support extending supervision to some tokens already
favoring the positive teacher but lacking sufficient positive advantage.
As shown in
Table [I](#S2.T1 "TABLE I ‣ II-B2 Negative distillation ‣ II-B Positive and Negative Distillation ‣ II Our Proposed Method ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")(b),
moderately increasing the required positive advantage helps improve
performance, with $`m=0.5`$ achieving the highest accuracy. An
appropriate margin extends negative supervision beyond tokens favoring
the negative teacher to include tokens that favor the positive teacher
but lack sufficient positive advantage. An excessively large margin,
however, may include tokens already exhibiting sufficient positive
advantage, imposing unnecessary negative constraints and consequently
impairing prediction performance.

## III Experiments

### III-A Experimental Setup

#### III-A1 Benchmarks

Evaluation covers three benchmarks for Fine-Grained Visual Tasks, with
POPE additionally used to assess object hallucination. V\*
Bench \[[6](#bib.bib6)\] measures the accuracy of locating and
recognizing small visual targets in complex scenes.
ZoomBench \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] contains questions whose answers depend on
visual details at different zoom levels.
MME-RealWorld-Lite \[[18](#bib.bib18)\] covers real-world scenarios with
high-resolution images. POPE \[[25](#bib.bib25)\] evaluates the extent
of object hallucination in multimodal large language models.

#### III-A2 Model training

SPN-OPSD is trained on the 5.8K Vision-OPD \[[10](#bib.bib10)\] examples
with fewer than 4,096 input tokens, using Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-2B.

#### III-A3 Baselines

We conduct two groups of comparisons. The first compares SPN-OPSD with
existing multimodal models: (a) closed-source MLLMs, including
GPT-5.2 \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] and GPT-5.4 \[[20](#bib.bib20)\]; (b)
open-source MLLMs, including Qwen3.5 \[[21](#bib.bib21)\],
MiniCPM-V-4.5 \[[22](#bib.bib22)\], and MiMo-V2.5 \[[23](#bib.bib23)\];
and (c) thinking-with-images agents, including DeepEyes
V2 \[[24](#bib.bib24)\] and Thyme \[[8](#bib.bib8)\].

The second group compares training strategies for fine-grained visual
tasks under the same training data and backbone.
DeFacto \[[11](#bib.bib11)\] uses evidence localization,
CompoDistill \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] uses attention alignment, and
VA-OPD \[[15](#bib.bib15)\] adjusts distillation weights with
counterfactual signals.

### III-B Main Results

As shown in
Table [II](#S3.T2 "TABLE II ‣ III-A3 Baselines ‣ III-A Experimental Setup ‣ III Experiments ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
SPN-OPSD consistently outperforms the corresponding Qwen3.5-0.8B and
Qwen3.5-2B base models across all three benchmarks, indicating
successful internalization of the ability to reason from local evidence.
At 2B it also exceeds the larger open-source models, GPT-5.4, and the
thinking-with-images agents while using standard full-image decoding
without inference-time tools. At both model sizes, SPN-OPSD achieves the
highest average accuracy among the compared training strategies for
fine-grained visual tasks. The results support the effectiveness of
combining positive distillation with selective negative supervision.

## IV Experimental Analysis

The present section answers the following question: Does Selective
Negative Teacher Supervision Provide Additional Benefits?

### IV-A Both Components Are Necessary

To determine whether the negative teacher provides gains beyond positive
distillation and whether the gains depend on supervision positions, we
compare six configurations at both model sizes.
Table [III](#S4.T3 "TABLE III ‣ IV-A Both Components Are Necessary ‣ IV Experimental Analysis ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")
shows full SPN-OPSD achieves the best overall performance. Applying
negative constraints to all tokens degrades performance on some
benchmarks. The negative teacher therefore provides additional
information beyond positive supervision, but selective use is necessary
for consistent gains. Removing either selection stage reduces
performance relative to full SPN-OPSD, indicating partial recovery of
the improvement on some benchmarks with single-stage configurations,
whereas combining both stages yields the full benefit.

#### IV-A1 Gains from Teacher Disagreement Filtering Stem from Effective Selection of Supervision Positions

Because changing $`\delta`$ alters both the number and locations of
selected tokens, we add a count-matched random-selection control.
Teacher-disagreement selection outperforms random selection on all three
benchmarks
(Table [IV](#S4.T4 "TABLE IV ‣ IV-A3 Contributions of negative constraints ‣ IV-A Both Components Are Necessary ‣ IV Experimental Analysis ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")(a)),
confirming that disagreement identifies more informative positions

#### IV-A2 Gains from token selection based on student relative difference

Varying $`m`$ changes both supervision count and positions, so we again
use a count-matched random control; relative-difference selection beats
random selection on all three benchmarks
(Table [IV](#S4.T4 "TABLE IV ‣ IV-A3 Contributions of negative constraints ‣ IV-A Both Components Are Necessary ‣ IV Experimental Analysis ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")(b)).

#### IV-A3 Contributions of negative constraints

The active negative hinge
$`\max(0,m+d_{s,t^{+}}^{(n)}-d_{s,t^{-}}^{(n)})`$ encourages both
separation from the negative teacher and alignment with the positive
teacher. The observed gains could therefore partly arise from stronger
positive supervision at the selected positions. To test the additional
value of the repulsive constraint, we introduce a positive-upweighting
control. The control retains teacher-disagreement selection and
margin-based activation but replaces the active negative term with the
student–positive-teacher divergence. Specifically,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{auxiliary}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(I,q,R)\sim\mathcal{D}\\
y\sim p^{s}(\cdot\mid I,q)\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{\sum\limits_{n=1}^{|y|}h^{(n)}d_{s,t^{+}}^{(n)}}{\sum\limits_{n=1}^{|y|}g^{(n)}+\varepsilon}\right]
``` |  | (16) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h^{(n)}=g^{(n)}\mathbb{I}\!\left[m+d_{s,t^{+}}^{(n)}-d_{s,t^{-}}^{(n)}>0\right]
``` |  | (17) |

The control optimizes
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{positive}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{negative}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}`$,
with the other settings unchanged. Full SPN-OPSD outperforms positive
upweighting on all three benchmarks in
Table [IV](#S4.T4 "TABLE IV ‣ IV-A3 Contributions of negative constraints ‣ IV-A Both Components Are Necessary ‣ IV Experimental Analysis ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")(b).
Encouraging the student to move away from the negative teacher therefore
provides additional gains beyond increasing positive supervision at the
same selected positions.

### IV-B SPN-OPSD Improves the Use of Local Visual Evidence

Models often err on Fine-Grained Visual Tasks because they struggle to
locate relevant evidence \[[10](#bib.bib10)\]. We assess whether
SPN-OPSD alleviates the difficulty in the following three ways.

In
Fig. [4](#S4.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ IV-C Performance with very small evidence regions ‣ IV Experimental Analysis ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")(a),
SPN-OPSD yields lower student–positive-teacher JSD (top) and higher
answer accuracy (bottom) than positive-only distillation over most of
training. Under selective negative supervision, closer alignment thus
accompanies higher accuracy.
Fig. [4](#S4.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ IV-C Performance with very small evidence regions ‣ IV Experimental Analysis ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")(d)
illustrates how the gain is reflected in the student’s predictions.
Asked about the outline color of “Pirata,” the SPN-OPSD student assigns
greater probability to the correct answer, “red,” than its positive-only
counterpart. It assigns almost none to “blue,” the base model’s answer
after the evidence region is blurred. SPN-OPSD improves accuracy by
increasing confidence in correct answers and assigning less probability
to incorrect alternatives.

In
Fig. [4](#S4.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ IV-C Performance with very small evidence regions ‣ IV Experimental Analysis ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")(b),
the accuracy gap between full-image and cropped-image inputs generally
narrows during training. The final gap is smaller for SPN-OPSD than for
positive-only distillation, the base model, or GPT-5.4, suggesting more
effective use of local evidence in the full image.

Fig. [4](#S4.F4 "Fig. 4 ‣ IV-C Performance with very small evidence regions ‣ IV Experimental Analysis ‣ Tiny Evidence Region Localization with Selective Contrastive On-Policy Self-Distillation")(c)
compares predictions before and after blurring only the evidence region.
For each model, we consider questions answered incorrectly in both
views. SPN-OPSD repeats the same wrong answer less often than
positive-only distillation, suggesting less reliance on unchanged
background cues and a greater ability to resist interference from
irrelevant regions.

## V Conclusion

This paper proposes SPN-OPSD, which uses both evidence and background
regions to provide supervision and improve the model’s resistance to
interference from irrelevant regions. By combining continuous positive
distillation with selective negative distillation, SPN-OPSD learns
evidence-supported distributions while identifying token-level positions
with high value for negative supervision, enabling targeted
distillation. We hope SPN-OPSD can advance the recognition and reasoning
capabilities of multimodal large language models in fine-grained visual
tasks.

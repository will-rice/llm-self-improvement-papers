###### Abstract

Language provides a natural interface for humans to specify and reason
about goals, but training language-conditioned robotic policies and
reasoning typically requires expensive manual annotations. We propose
Action Self-Distillation (ASD), a method for learning
language-conditioned reasoning for robotic navigation without
human-annotated language or reasoning labels. ASD uses an open-source
VLM, which contains broad world knowledge and reasoning capabilities, as
_both_ a teacher network and a student policy. During training, the
self-teacher receives privileged trajectory feedback and infers the
underlying navigation goal together with goal-oriented reasoning. The
student is then trained to condition on the inferred navigation goal,
predict future ego-frame trajectory, and provide a description of the
scene semantics, plan, and reasoning, from onboard observations alone.
Across 13 challenging indoor and outdoor real-world navigation tasks,
our method improves success rate over the strongest baseline by $`30\%`$
in indoor settings, $`30\%`$ on multi-stage tasks, and achieves $`95\%`$
success rate on outdoor tasks, validating that privileged trajectory
feedback can enable self-distillation for scalable generalist navigation
policies without human annotations.

## 1 Introduction

Vision-language-action (VLA) models have emerged as a successful
approach to robot learning, enabling policies to condition on
natural-language commands while grounding them in visual
observations \[1, 2\]. For navigation, this paradigm is especially
appealing because language provides a deployable interface for
specifying goals in human-centered environments, without requiring
privileged map coordinates or goal images \[3, 4, 5, 6\]. However,
training VLMs to end-to-end navigation typically requires datasets with
goal descriptions, scene semantics or reasoning traces, followed by
supervised fine-tuning \[7, 4\]. These annotations are expensive to
obtain at scale, and many widely used robot navigation datasets contain
only sensor observations and trajectories, with no language
supervision \[8, 9, 4, 10, 11, 12\].

![](arxiv-2610-17593v1--74bbaf2ce94e.figures/figure-1.webp)

Figure 1: Action self-distillation. The self-teacher uses privileged
ground-truth trajectory feedback to extract scene semantics, infer a

to the self-teacher, while pink arrows denote the student pathway. The
student observes only deployable inputs and learns to align its
reasoning and trajectory tokens with the self-teacher, removing the need
for external goal or reasoning annotations.

Prior approaches address this gap either by relying on human annotators
for high-quality language labels \[7, 4, 13, 14\], or by orchestrating
multiple external models to generate goals, semantic descriptions, or
reasoning supervision. Departing from these approaches, we investigate
whether a single pretrained VLM can supervise itself from
trajectory-only navigation data. This motivates our research question:
How can we learn language goal-conditioned end-to-end navigation
policies from trajectory-only robot datasets, without relying on dataset
language annotations or multi-model annotation pipelines?

In human interaction with frontier VLMs, feedback plays a central role
in refining model behavior when initial responses do not match the
user’s intent. A key observation is that when a model is conditioned on
corrective or informative feedback about its own outputs, it can recover
coherent goal-aligned predictions even when it could not do so from the
input alone \[15, 16, 17\]. Inspired by this feedback-driven reasoning
capability, we treat trajectory feedback as privileged information
during training. Given such feedback, a VLM can infer the underlying
goal, extract relevant scene semantics, and produce planning-oriented
reasoning during training. To this end, we propose Training Generalist
Navigation Policies via Action Self-Distillation (ASD), a framework in
which a single model extracts language goals from trajectory-conditioned
scene reasoning as self-teacher, and then learns to generate
goal-conditioned trajectories together with corresponding reasoning
traces.

ASD instantiates this idea through a self-distillation procedure with
two passes. In the teacher pass, the VLM receives the current
observation together with ground-truth future trajectory, projected onto
an egocentric observation image. This ground truth trajectory as
privileged feedback information guides the model to produce structured
perception-prediction-planning reasoning and to extract a natural
language goal. In the student pass, the model is trained to condition on
the extracted goal and predict both future actions and structured
reasoning from onboard observations alone. The self-teacher is
implemented as an EMA-updated copy of the student \[18\], allowing
language-goal and reasoning supervision to be generated within the
training loop. Critically, ASD operates directly on raw image-trajectory
pairs, requiring no human-provided language goals or reasoning
annotations. To our knowledge, this is the first application of
feedback-conditioned self-distillation on robot control, converting
existing unannotated navigation datasets into goal-conditioned reasoning
supervision without external labeling.

We deploy ASD on a FrodoBot rover in zero-shot manner across a range of
out-of-distribution natural language goals in both indoor and outdoor
environments. The policy is trained on a mixture of RECON \[8\] and
FrodoBot-2K \[9\], without any language goal or reasoning annotations.
Across 9 indoor and 4 outdoor language-conditioned real-world
experiments, we compare ASD against competitive VLA navigation
baselines, including OmniVLA \[7\] and NaVILA \[19\]. ASD demonstrates
language goal steerability across directional, color-based, and
object-centric commands, and further generalizes to both multi-stage and
indoor navigation tasks that are _unseen_ during training. Across all 13
real-world tasks, ASD achieves the highest overall success rate,
outperforming the strongest baseline, OmniVLA, by $`30\%`$ in the indoor
setting and reaching a $`95\%`$ success rate in the outdoor setting. On
multi-stage tasks, ASD further improves over the leading baseline,
NaVILA, by $`30\%`$.

## 2 Related Work

Learning-based Ground Navigation. Early learning-based navigation
networks like BADGR \[20\] showed that ground robots can learn
navigation directly from real-world experience, moving away from
hand-designed cost functions. ViNG \[21\] and RECON \[8\] further
demonstrated visual-goal navigation and open-world exploration by
combining local navigation with topological information or latent goals.
More recent foundation-style navigation policies, including GNM \[10\],
ViNT \[11\], and NoMaD \[12\], scale this idea by training
goal-conditioned visual navigation policies on heterogeneous robot data,
with NoMaD introducing diffusion policies and goal masking to unify
goal-directed navigation and exploration. While these methods
demonstrate strong large-scale learned navigation capabilities, they
remain limited to visual goal conditioning and do not support natural
language instructions, which are easier to specify and more practical
for real-world deployment.

Natural Language Goals and Reasoning in Navigation. Language has long
been studied as a natural interface for robot instruction following and
vision-and-language navigation (VLN), where agents map free-form
commands to navigation actions from visually grounded observations \[22,
23, 24, 25\]. Recent work has explored language as a goal modality for
robot navigation, recognizing that natural language is the most
accessible interface for human control. LM-Nav \[3\] composes an LLM,
CLIP-style image-language grounding, and a visual navigation model to
follow language instructions without language-annotated robot data.
NavGPT \[5\] and NavGPT-2 \[6\] study explicit LLM reasoning for
vision-and-language navigation, while NaVILA \[19\] and VAMOS \[14\]
extend this toward real robot navigation with embodiment-specific
planning modules. More recently, OmniVLA \[7\] and LeLaN \[4\] achieve
language-conditioned navigation on real robots by relying on external
labeling pipelines to supply language annotations at training time.
While these works demonstrate that language conditioning can benefit
navigation, none address the annotation bottleneck from within the
training loop. Our approach instead elicits language goals and
contextual descriptions entirely through self-distillation, requiring no
external annotation of any kind.

Reasoning Distillation and Self-Distillation. Our framework builds on
EMA-based self-supervised learning \[18\], which showed that models can
supervise themselves under richer context without external labels.
STaR \[15\] extended this idea to language by bootstrapping reasoning
from self-generated rationales, while related methods \[16, 26, 27\]
demonstrated that distilling a privileged-context teacher into a
deployment-time student is effective for capability acquisition. The
closest control-domain analogue is SelfD \[28\], which pseudo-labels
driving videos with action supervision and retrains a student policy,
but predicts actions rather than language goals or contextual
descriptions. Recent embodied reasoning works \[29, 30, 31\] similarly
use privileged VLM reasoning to improve deployable policies, but rely on
separate external teacher models. In contrast, we use the same
open-source VLM as both teacher and student, grounding it through
annotated trajectory feedback to elicit language goals and contextual
descriptions within the training loop. To our knowledge, this is the
first application of self-distillation as a grounding mechanism for
robot control.

## 3 Training Generalist Navigation Policies via Action Self-Distillation

We propose a novel method for training robotic navigation policies via
Action Self-Distillation. Policies trained with ASD can navigate to
goals specified by natural language, while also reasoning about their
actions. We briefly introduce notation and formulation, and then
describe ASD.

![](arxiv-2610-17593v1--74bbaf2ce94e.figures/figure-2.webp)

Figure 2: Action Self-Distillation. A single pre-trained VLM is used as
both the self-teacher $`\pi_{\phi}`$ and student $`\pi_{\theta}`$.
During training, the self-teacher receives privileged information by
projecting the ground-truth future trajectory $`y`$ onto the front-view
observation as $`o^{*}_{0}`$, enabling it to infer a language goal
$`\hat{g}`$ and scene-grounded reasoning trace $`R`$. The student
observes only deployable inputs $`o_{0}`$ and learns to match the
teacher’s reasoning tokens with a KL loss, while predicting future
trajectory tokens $`\tilde{\tau}_{0:H}`$ with a cross-entropy loss
against the ground-truth trajectory $`y`$. The teacher weights are
updated as an EMA of the student.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

We consider a ground robot that navigates using ego-centric single-view
observations. At time $`t`$, the robot receives a front-camera image
$`o_{t}`$, and its state as a two-dimensional birds-eye view location
$`s_{t}=[x,y]\in\mathbb{R}^{2}`$. Given a history of $`T`$ past states
$`\tau_{-T:0}=\{s_{-T},\ldots,s_{0}\}`$, the goal of our policy is to
predict a future trajectory $`\tau_{0:H}=\{s_{0},\ldots,s_{H}\}`$ over a
planning horizon $`H`$. We build on a pretrained vision-language model
(VLM) $`\pi`$ that autoregressively produces text tokens; trajectories
are serialized in a JSON-style text format and tokenized by the language
model, enabling trajectory prediction via next-token prediction. We seek
a policy that additionally emits a _structured reasoning trace_ $`R`$
and can be conditioned on a natural-language goal $`g`$:

```math
\hat{\tau}_{0:H},\;R\;\sim\;\pi\!\left(\,\cdot\mid o_{-T:0},\,\tau_{-T:0},\,g\right). \tag{1}
```

Crucially, neither reasoning annotations nor goal labels exist in any of
the navigation datasets we train on \[8, 9, 4, 10\]; all language
supervision is generated by the method itself.

### 3.2 Training: Language Following and Reasoning via Action Self-Distillation

We use the ground-truth trajectory as a privileged feedback information
to elicit structured semantics and goal information. This privileged
feedback conditioned forward pass constitutes the _self-teacher_, which
generates both a structured reasoning trace and a semantic navigation
goal. The _student_ is trained using only robot observations,
conditioned on the language goal extracted by the self-teacher, and
supervised to generate both the reasoning trace and the future
trajectory. At inference time, the privileged trajectory input is
removed and the extracted training-time goal is replaced by a free-form
language goal provided by a human operator, enabling
language-conditioned navigation without requiring human-annotated
reasoning or goal labels during training. Both teacher and student are
instances of the same pretrained VLM; the self-teacher weights $`\phi`$
are maintained as an exponential moving average (EMA) of the student
weights $`\theta`$: $`\phi\leftarrow\alpha\,\phi+(1-\alpha)\,\theta`$ .

Self-Teacher Pass. As listed in Line 1 of Algorithm 1, the self-teacher
$`\pi_{\phi}`$ receives the current camera view $`o_{0}`$ augmented with
a polyline overlay of the ground-truth trajectory $`y=\tau_{0:H}`$
(annotated observation $`o^{*}_{0}`$), and produces language goal
$`\hat{g}`$, reasoning $`R`$, and trajectory $`\hat{\tau}_{0:H}`$ with
prompt $`P`$ as

```math
\hat{g},\;R,\;\hat{\tau}_{0:H}\;\sim\;\mathrm{sg}\!\left(\pi_{\phi}\!\left(\,\cdot\mid P,\,o^{*}_{0},\,\tau_{-T:0}\right)\right). \tag{2}
```

Student Pass. The student pass is summarized in Line 1 of Algorithm 1.
Specifically, the student $`\pi_{\theta}`$ receives the image
observation $`o_{0}`$, state history $`\tau_{-T:0}`$, and the
self-teacher’s extracted goal $`\hat{g}`$, and produces a reasoning
trace $`\tilde{R}`$ and trajectory with the mirrored prompt
$`\tilde{P}`$ as

```math
\tilde{R},\;\tilde{\tau}_{0:H}\;\sim\;\pi_{\theta}\!\left(\,\cdot\mid\tilde{P},\,o_{0},\,\tau_{-T:0},\,\hat{g}\right). \tag{3}
```

Training Objective. The output token sequence is partitioned at the
onset of the trajectory block. Tokens in the reasoning span (Perception,
Agents, Prediction, Planning) are supervised by a KL divergence loss
against the self-teacher’s logits, that is

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\,R\;\|\;\tilde{R}\,\right). \tag{4}
```

Tokens in the trajectory span are supervised by cross-entropy against
the ground-truth trajectory

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{traj}}=\mathrm{CE}\!\left(\tilde{\tau}_{0:H},\;y\right). \tag{5}
```

The combined objective is
$`\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{traj}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}`$,
summarized in Line 1-1 of Algorithm 1 .

Structured Reasoning Format. The self-teacher prompt enforces a fixed
output structure: Task (navigation goal; goal-conditioned mode only),
Perception (visible terrain and structures), Agents (moving entities
present in the scene, or “none”), Prediction (expected agent motion),
and Planning (a discrete action from a fixed vocabulary, justified by
named scene features), followed by the executable trajectory. The
structured format constrains the teacher to ground its reasoning in
observable scene content; the teacher prompt explicitly instructs the
model to describe only what is visible in the raw image, so that any
claim can be verified against the unannotated observation. Full prompt
templates for each dataset are provided in the Section B of
supplementary material.

### 3.3 Deployment: Navigating to Language Goals

At deployment, the self-teacher is absent and no ground-truth trajectory
is available. The student policy $`\pi_{\theta}^{\star}`$ plans a
trajectory and reasoning trace directly from the current observation and
history as

```math
\tilde{R},\;\tilde{\tau}_{0:H}\;\sim\;\pi_{\theta}^{\star}\!\left(\,\cdot\mid\tilde{P},\,o_{0},\,\tau_{-T:0}\right). \tag{6}
```

In the goal-conditioned setting, a human operator supplies a
natural-language goal $`g^{\circledast}`$ in place of the self-teacher’s
extracted $`\hat{g}`$ via

```math
\tilde{R},\;\tilde{\tau}_{0:H}\;\sim\;\pi_{\theta}^{\star}\!\left(\,\cdot\mid\tilde{P},\,o_{0},\,\tau_{-T:0},\,g^{\circledast}\right). \tag{7}
```

The goal $`g^{\circledast}`$ may take several forms, including a path
description (e.g. ‘‘navigate along the sidewalk’’), or an object
reference (e.g. ‘‘navigate towards the red building’’). Because the
student is trained on goals $`\hat{g}`$ produced by the self-teacher
from diverse scenes across the full training distribution, this
constitutes zero-shot language-conditioned navigation. The model
generalizes to operator-supplied instructions at test time without ever
seeing human-specified goals during training.

In this section, we conduct experiments to assess ASD by addressing the
following two questions: Q1: How well does ASD generalize across
real-world environments compared with state-of-the-art navigation VLAs?
Q2: What capabilities are induced by ASD?

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

ASD uses Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct \[32\] as the base VLM, hereafter
referred to as Qwen2.5-VL, and is trained through the self-distillation
pipeline introduced in Section 3, on a mixture of the RECON \[8\] and
FrodoBot2k \[9\] datasets. The training details are summarized in
Section B of the Supplementary Material. We evaluate it in real-world
settings across both indoor and outdoor environments. We focus on
language-conditioned navigation tasks, where user prompts ask the robot
to navigate to a specific goal. All experiments are deployed on a
FrodoBot EarthRover Mini equipped with a front-facing camera. At each
planning step, ASD predicts receding-horizon trajectory, and converted
by the robot’s built-in API into linear and angular velocity commands
for execution.

### 4.2 Indoor Language-Conditioned Navigation

We conduct indoor real-world navigation experiments in a laboratory
environment with typical office objects, including chairs, trash bins,
boxes, and monitors. We evaluate 9 tasks covering different levels of
difficulty, from simple directional and object-identification goals,
such as ‘‘Navigate to the blue chair’’, to multi-stage goals, such as
‘‘Navigate through the door, turn right, then follow the hallway’’. For
each task, we use fixed language prompts and test each method under four
different initializations, where the initial position and orientation of
the robot and objects are varied. We compare ASD against
state-of-the-art language-conditioned VLAs, OmniVLA \[7\] and
NaVILA \[19\]. See Section C of the Supplementary for detailed tasks
information and evaluation.

Indoor language-conditioned navigation results are reported in Table 1.
Our ASD achieves consistently high success rates across all tasks and
obtains the best average success rate as $`84\%`$. Among three hard
tasks, including ‘‘Navigate to the right chair’’, ‘‘Navigate to the
monitor’’ and ‘‘Navigate through the door, turn right, then follow the
hallway’’, ASD significantly outperforms leading baseline by $`60\%`$,
$`50\%`$ and $`10\%`$ respectively, demonstrating strong generalization
capability in complex tasks.

The task performance of ASD remains relatively consistent compared with
competitive baselines. Across all evaluated tasks, ASD achieves success
rates above $`50\%`$. In contrast, OmniVLA and NaVILA obtain
substantially lower success rates on several tasks and even exhibit
near-total failure in certain scenarios, such as OmniVLA, which achieves
$`0\%`$ success in Hallway. These results suggest that the baselines
have limited robustness to specific navigation scenarios, which we
further analyze in Section 4.4.

### 4.3 Outdoor Language-Conditioned Navigation

We conduct outdoor real-world navigation experiments in a campus
environment. We evaluate 4 tasks, including navigate along the sidewalk,
and navigate to the statue, tree and car. We follow the same
experimental setup and compare to the same baselines as in the indoor
setting. See Supplementary Section C for tasks and evaluation details.
Results are reported in Table 2, where we observe that ASD achieves
consistently high success rates across all tasks and obtains the best
average success rate of $`95\%`$. In contrast, the baselines either show
lower success rates on specific tasks, such as OmniVLA achieving
60$`\%`$ on Statue, or frequently fail on several tasks, such as NaVILA
achieving 0$`\%`$ on Tree and 10$`\%`$ on Statue. We further discuss the
result in Section 4.4.

### 4.4 Behavior Assessment

In this section, we provide a detailed assessment and discussion of
ASD’s learned capabilities, qualitative behavior and relative
performance gains compared to baselines.

Zero-Shot Navigation to Out-of-Distribution Objects and Goals. Indoor
navigation as described in Sec. 4.2 is evaluated in Table 1,
highlighting ASD’s ability to generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD)
objects, goals, and environments. In particular, our indoor experiments
involve laboratory objects and indoor navigation goals that are absent
from the training data, since both RECON and FrodoBot-2K are primarily
outdoor navigation datasets. Despite this large domain shift, ASD
maintains 84$`\%`$ average success, indicating strong generalization to
novel object categories, language goals, and indoor environments. We
observe a similar trend in the outdoor experiments in Table 2: "navigate
to the statue" requires grounding an OOD object category, yet ASD
achieves an $`80\%`$ success, outperforming OmniVLA by $`20\%`$ and
NaVILA by $`70\%`$.

Steerability. ASD is steerable with different language goals within the
same environment. Fig. 3 shows that ASD can be directed by different
Directions, interpreting directional commands, and different Objects,
requiring to ground distinct object categories. In the Car example, ASD
distinguishes object attributes, such as color. These examples validate
the generalization and language-following capabilities. This is notable
because our training data follows a one-on-one scene and goal
supervision: each observation is paired with a single language goal and
a single demonstrated trajectory, without counterfactual trajectory
augmentation. In contrast, methods such as OmniVLA rely on broader
multimodal training and over 9,500 hours of data collected from 10
platforms. Despite using substantially less data, ASD generalizes to
produce distinct trajectories conditioned on different language goals in
the same scene, demonstrating more data-efficient steerable behavior.

## 5 Conclusion

We present Training Generalist Navigation Policies via Action
Self-Distillation, a method for learning instruction-following
navigation policies from trajectory-only robot data. Using privileged
trajectory feedback during training, ASD generates language goals and
structured reasoning without human annotations, enabling the student
policy to follow complex natural-language instructions and act in the
real world. Across $`13`$ real-world zero-shot tasks on a mobile robot,
ASD demonstrates strong generalization to OOD directions, colors,
objects, indoor laboratory environments, and multi-stage tasks. In
indoor settings, ASD outperforms the strongest baseline, OmniVLA, by
$`30\%`$; in outdoor settings, it achieves $`100\%`$ success on
in-distribution tasks and $`80\%`$ success on the OOD commands,
outperforming OmniVLA by $`20\%`$ and NaVILA by $`70\%`$. For
multi-stage tasks, ASD further improves over the leading baseline,
NaVILA, by $`30\%`$, despite being trained on significantly less data.
Akin to training reasoning-enabled foundation models of language, we
hope that ASD serves as a generic recipe for training generalist robot
policies with reasoning without requiring expensive human annotations.

Limitations. While ASD removes the need for language annotations, it
still relies on demonstration trajectories as privileged feedback;
future work could replace these with interactive human feedback for more
precise task-level guidance. The quality of self-generated goals and
reasoning also depends on the pretrained VLM and may degrade in
ambiguous scenes, with small targets, or under fine-grained spatial
instructions.

## Appendix A Comparing ASD vs. Supervised Finetuning

In this section, we provide further insight into the experimental
findings reported in the main paper. We observe that ASD, despite being
fine-tuned on substantially less data ($`\sim 100`$ hours demonstration
data (ASD) v.s. $`\sim 9500`$ hours demonstration data (OmniVLA)) than
OmniVLA and NaVILA, exhibits better generalization on indoor
out-of-distribution evaluations where ASD has not been explicitly
fine-tuned.

In the following, we provide insight into why supervising the reasoning
span of our policy with the self-distillation loss
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}`$ in Eq. (4) leads to better
generalization and retention of the pretrained VLM’s capabilities than
standard offline imitation.

### A.1 Privileged Soft-label Distillation Objective

During training, the self-teacher $`\pi_{\phi}`$ generates a reasoning
trace using privileged trajectory feedback

```math
R\;\sim\;\mathrm{sg}\!\left[\pi_{\phi}\!\left(\cdot\mid P,o^{*}_{0},\tau_{-T:0}\right)\right], \tag{8}
```

where $`o^{*}_{0}=f(o_{0},y)`$ is the annotated observation obtained by
projecting the ground-truth future trajectory $`y=\tau_{0:H}`$ onto the
front-camera observation $`o_{0}`$. The stop-gradient prevents the
reasoning loss from updating the self-teacher directly. The student
receives only deployable inputs and the self-teacher’s extracted goal
$`\hat{g}`$ as

```math
\tilde{R}\;\sim\;\pi_{\theta}\!\left(\cdot\mid\tilde{P},o_{0},\tau_{-T:0},\hat{g}\right). \tag{9}
```

The reasoning span is supervised by a forward-KL distillation objective

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\,R\;\|\;\tilde{R}\,\right). \tag{10}
```

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\mathrm{sg}\!\left[\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid P,o^{*}_{0},\tau_{-T:0})\right]\,\middle\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{P},o_{0},\tau_{-T:0},\hat{g})\right), \tag{11}
```

where the KL divergence is applied only over the reasoning-token span.
Equivalently, for the reasoning tokens $`R=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{N})`$, this
can be written autoregressively as

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{R_{<i}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid P,o^{*}_{0},\tau_{-T:0})}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\mathrm{sg}\!\left[\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid P,o^{*}_{0},\tau_{-T:0},R_{<i})\right]\,\middle\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{P},o_{0},\tau_{-T:0},\hat{g},R_{<i})\right)\right]. \tag{12}
```

This form makes explicit that ASD transfers the teacher’s full token
distribution at each reasoning step, rather than only a single hard
token.

Expanding Eq. (11) over teacher-generated reasoning traces gives

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}=\mathbb{E}_{R\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid P,o^{*}_{0},\tau_{-T:0})}\left[\log\frac{\pi_{\phi}(R\mid P,o^{*}_{0},\tau_{-T:0})}{\pi_{\theta}(R\mid\tilde{P},o_{0},\tau_{-T:0},\hat{g})}\right]. \tag{13}
```

Since the teacher distribution is passed into a stop-gradient, the first
term is constant with respect to $`\theta`$. Thus, optimizing Eq. (13)
is equivalent to minimizing the teacher-weighted cross entropy

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}\equiv-\mathbb{E}_{R\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid P,o^{*}_{0},\tau_{-T:0})}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(R\mid\tilde{P},o_{0},\tau_{-T:0},\hat{g})\right]+\mathrm{const}. \tag{14}
```

The resulting gradient is

```math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reason}}=-\mathbb{E}_{R\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid P,o^{*}_{0},\tau_{-T:0})}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(R\mid\tilde{P},o_{0},\tau_{-T:0},\hat{g})\right]. \tag{15}
```

Therefore, the student is trained to place probability mass on reasoning
traces that the self-teacher considers likely.

### A.2 Comparison to Standard SFT

Standard SFT can also be viewed as matching a behavior-policy
distribution. Let $`\pi_{b}(R\mid\tilde{P},o_{0},\tau_{-T:0},\hat{g})`$
denote the offline annotation process that produces reasoning traces.
Then SFT minimizes

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}=-\mathbb{E}_{R^{\star}\sim\pi_{b}}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(R^{\star}\mid\tilde{P},o_{0},\tau_{-T:0},\hat{g})\right]=H(\pi_{b})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{b}\,\middle\|\,\pi_{\theta}\right). \tag{16}
```

However, in typical offline datasets, $`\pi_{b}`$ is observed only
through one hard sampled annotation $`R^{\star}`$ per input. Thus, the
empirical target distribution collapses to

```math
\hat{\pi}_{b}(R\mid\cdot)=\mathbf{1}[R=R^{\star}], \tag{17}
```

and SFT reduces to hard-label imitation.

In contrast, ASD constructs a privileged self-teacher distribution

```math
\pi_{\phi}(R\mid P,o_{0}^{*},\tau_{-T:0}), \tag{18}
```

where $`o_{0}^{*}=f(o_{0},y)`$ exposes the demonstrated future
trajectory to the teacher. Therefore, the target is not merely an
offline annotation sampled from a static behavior policy, but a
feedback-conditioned reasoning distribution that explains why the
demonstrated action is appropriate for the current observation.

This enables ASD to achieve better generalization, because a given scene
may admit multiple valid descriptions and reasonings. For example, in
the car task, visualized in Figure 7(l), we use the prompt "Navigate to
the red car." In this case, there can be multiple valid reasoning
traces. The Perception field could describe the scene as "The red car is
on the left of the gray car." or equivalently as "The gray car is on the
right of the red car." Both descriptions are correct.

However, hard-label imitation in standard SFT, as in Equation 17, forces
the model to match one particular description, even when several
alternatives are valid. This may limit the model’s ability to represent
diverse but correct reasoning patterns. In contrast, our method learns
from a distributional target, as in Equation 15, which can capture
multiple valid perception modes and therefore provides more flexible
supervision.

## Appendix B Additional Training Details

### B.1 Datasets

We use a mixture of data from the RECON \[8\] and FrodoBots-2K \[9\]
datasets for training and validation of ASD. RECON is an outdoor visual
navigation dataset collected with a Clearpath Jackal ground robot
exploring an unstructured university-campus environment under diverse
weather and lighting conditions. Each trajectory pairs a fisheye
front-camera image stream with the robot’s commanded actions, and covers
scenes ranging from paved sidewalks and grassy fields to parking lots
and pedestrian-shared spaces. FrodoBots-2K is a large-scale sidewalk
navigation dataset collected with the Earth Rover Zero platform across
more than 10 cities, providing thousands of hours of teleoperated
driving in pedestrian-shared urban environments under varied lighting,
weather, and crowd conditions. Due to the size of the dataset and
infeasibility of full training in the project timeline, we choose to
evaluate on a subset of the dataset consisting of 25% of the scenes,
randomly selected from the available online splits. Compared to RECON’s
single-campus Jackal collection, FrodoBots-2K tests generalization on a
substantially larger and more visually diverse corpus. The
train/validation split is set to 90%:10%.

### B.2 Model and Training Setup

We use Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct \[32\] as the backbone VLM for both the
student and the self-teacher. The student is initialized from the
released pretrained checkpoint, and the self-teacher is initialized with
the same weights. During training, the self-teacher is updated as an
exponential moving average of the student. We train ASD on a server with
8 A6000 Pro, 256G of RAM, AMD Threadripper 7980X CPU with 64 cores. The
key hyperparameters used in our ASD are reported in Table 5.

### C.3 Evaluation Fairness, Execution and Stopping Criteria

Evaluation Fairness and Role of Reasoning. Across 10 trials per task,
evaluators did _not_ provide corrective interventions during execution
(e.g. repositioning or issuing recovery commands); so, mean steps to
corrective intervention is not applicable. During planning, we evaluate
the safety and reasonability of the predicted trajectory, & give all
methods three times to replan per episode (same protocol for all
models). Episode fails if the robot collided with an obstacle or if the
goal is no longer in its FOV.

Execution Behavior and Stopping. Similar to OmniVLA in
evaluation/stopping criteria, task completion is judged by
method-blinded human evaluator, who stops the episode once the
instruction is satisfied. ASD has no learned stopping criterion. Pauses
in multi-stage demonstrations are not emergent; they reflect observation
and inference latency as ASD replans after executing 8 of 16 predicted
actions. OmniVLA predicts only 8 actions, all of which are executed for
fairness.

### C.4 Real World Experiments Baselines Implementation

#### C.4.1 OmniVLA

We evaluate OmniVLA \[7\], a vision-based navigation policy that takes a
language prompt as the goal input. We use the pretrained
omnivla-original checkpoint. During the inference, OmniVLA is queried
with the current front-view image and the goal text, and outputs a
sequence of 8 future actions. Before execution, the predicted action
sequence is checked by human intuitive judgment to determine whether it
is safe and reasonable. If the plan is considered unreasonable, OmniVLA
is required to replan. We allow at most 3 such heuristic interventions
per run; once this limit is exceeded, the run is marked as a failure.
For trajectory continuity and smoothness, all eight predicted actions
are executed before the next front-view image is acquired. Each run is
terminated when the human evaluator judges the task to be successful, or
when a collision occurs.

#### C.4.2 NaVILA

We also evaluate NaVILA \[19\], a two-level navigation framework that
combines a vision-language-action model with low-level locomotion
skills. We use the pretrained NaVILA’s VLA checkpoint to generate
high-level language commands and execute them using the built-in
controller of the FrodoBot. At each planning step, the model predicts a
future action command, such as “forward 75 cm” or “turn right 45^(∘)”.
The robot then executes these commands before receiving a new plan. For
the multi-stage tasks, Doorway and Hallway, we use the 64-frame
checkpoint, which conditions on the previous 64 front-camera frames. For
the remaining tasks, we use the 8-frame checkpoint. As in the OmniVLA
evaluation, we allow at most three heuristic interventions per run; once
this limit is exceeded, the run is marked as a failure. Each run
terminates when the human evaluator judges the task to be successful, or
when a collision occurs.

#### C.4.3 Vanilla Qwen2.5-VL

We perform inference by replacing the ASD checkpoint with the vanilla
Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct model, while keeping all other inference settings
unchanged.

#### C.4.4 Claude Opus 4.7

Claude Opus 4.7 is run in auto mode with low reasoning effort. At each
inference step, Claude receives the current front-view image, reasons
about the navigation goal, and generates executable motion commands,
including the duration for turning and moving forward. The robot then
executes the generated command before the next inference step. We choose
low reasoning effort because Claude’s reasoning latency is very high,
and using low effort reduces the inference time. We use two helper
functions, save_fig.py and send_command.py, to interact with the robot.
The function save_fig.py saves the current front-view image from the
robot to disk, while send_command.py sends the generated control command
to the robot. We then prompt Claude to navigate the robot using the
following instruction:

Task: {task-specific prompt listed in Table 6}.

Please read the current front-view image saved by save_fig.py. Based on
this image and the task goal, generate a control command and send it to
the robot using send_command.py. After the command is executed, call
save_fig.py again to obtain the updated front-view image. Repeat this
observe-plan-act loop until the task is completed. Stop the process when
you judge that the robot has reached the target.

## Appendix D Additional Real World Qualitative and Open-Loop Quantitative Results

### D.1 Open-Loop Evaluation

We conduct the open-loop experiments using the non-goal-conditioned ASD
model, where no language goal is provided to the student. The purpose of
this comparison is to evaluate ASD in the open-loop trajectory
prediction setting and is the best among competitive supervised
finetuning baselines, including Gaussian Policy, Gaussian Mixture model,
Diffusion Policy, Qwen2.5-VL + SFT, Alpamayo-1+SFT and OpenVLA+SFT.

### D.2 Open-Loop Experiment Setup

In addition to the real-world language-conditioned navigation tasks
reported in Section 4, we conduct an open-loop evaluation on the
validation splits of RECON and FrodoBots-2K. Following the inference
formulation in Equation (7), the model receives the current front-view
image and the past ego-frame trajectory as input, and predicts a future
trajectory without closed-loop execution. We train our ASD with 8 past
waypoints as history and predict of future waypoints, corresponding to a
6.4-second horizon at 2.5 Hz. The predicted trajectory is compared
directly with the ground-truth future trajectory. All baselines are
trained and evaluated on the same train/validation split.

### D.3 Open-Loop Baselines

We compare against two groups of baselines. The first group consists of
non-VLA policies trained directly on the same data, including Gaussian
Policy, Gaussian Mixture Model \[35\], and Diffusion Policy \[36\]. The
second group consists of VLA backbones fine-tuned with standard SFT,
including Qwen2.5-VL \[32\], Alpamayo \[37\], and OpenVLA \[2\]. The
training details of each baseline are:

##### Gaussian Policy.

We adopted the robomimic’s built-in BC_Gaussian algorithm \[38\].
Robomimic is a framework for robot learning from demonstration, which
offers a broad set of offline learning algorithms to learn from
datasets. The current front image is encoded by a ResNet18 with spatial
softmax pooling, then linearly projected into a flat feature vector. The
8 previous ego-frame waypoints are flattened into a 16 dimensional
vector. We concatenate the visual vector and position vector into a
fusion vector, then pass it through a shared two-layer MLP, after which
two parallel MLPs predict the mean and standard deviation of a single
32-dimensional diagonal Gaussian that jointly parameterized the 16
future waypoints. The training objective is the negative log-likelihood
of the ground-truth action under this distribution. All the training
hyperparameters are kept at robomimic defaults. At evaluation we
deterministically output the predicted Gaussian mean.

##### Gaussian Mixture Model.

We adopted robomimic’s built-in BC_GMM algorithm. The observation
encoder, history fusion, and shared MLP trunk are identical to the
Gaussian policy; the only architectural difference is the policy head,
which parametrizes a 5-mode mixture of 32-dimensional diagonal Gaussians
instead of a single one. Training uses the negative log-likelihood of
the ground-truth action under the mixture density. At evaluation we draw
one sample from the predicted mixture.

##### Diffusion Policy.

We implement the Diffusion Policy baseline on top of the official
codebase \[36\]. Diffusion Policy was originally proposed for robot
manipulation, where a DDPM \[39\] iteratively denoises Gaussian noise
into an end-effector trajectory conditioned on visual observations. The
observation encoder and history fusion are identical to the BC_Gaussian
and BC_GMM baselines (ResNet18 + spatial-softmax pooling, concatenated
with the 16-d history vector). As for the policy, we use a conditional
U-Net that convolves along the temporal dimension to predict the noise
given the fused observation. The network is trained to predict the added
noise. At evaluation we sample one noise and denoise it to get the
trajectory.

##### Qwen+SFT.

This is implemented as ASD’s student policy training.

##### Alpamayo-1+SFT.

We evaluate Alpamayo-R1 \[37\], a vision-language trajectory model that
pairs a frozen Qwen3-VL-8B visual encoder with a small action-expert
denoiser that generates the future trajectory via diffusion-style
sampling. We fine-tune the expert denoiser (Stage 2 only, VLM frozen) on
RECON and FrodoBots-2K for 5 epochs at batch size 8 and learning rate
1e-4. At inference, the model draws a single trajectory sample per query
(num-traj=1) for fair single-prediction comparison against the other
baselines.

##### OpenVLA+SFT.

We evaluate OpenVLA \[2\], a 7B-parameter Prismatic
vision-language-action model that emits a single discretized (v_lin,
v_ang) action token pair per image, which we then integrate through a
unicycle model to obtain the predicted trajectory. We add a LoRA adapter
of rank 32 and fine-tune on RECON and FrodoBots-2K for 5 epochs at batch
size 32 and learning rate 5e-4, deriving action targets from each
sample’s first ground-truth future step.

### D.4 Open-Loop Quantitative Evaluation.

Table 7 summarizes 2-second open-loop trajectory prediction results on
RECON and FrodoBots-2K, where we report the Absolute Trajectory Error
(ATE), Average Displacement Error (ADE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and
Mean Squared Error (MSE). ASD achieves the lowest error on 7 out of 8
comparisons. ASD has a significant improvement over its direct baseline
Qwen+SFT. Since both models share the same Qwen2.5-VL backbone, training
data, and optimization, the gap is attributable to the reasoning-token
supervision rather than to capacity or training recipe. ASD also
uniformly surpasses non-VLA behavior cloning methods, indicating that
the gain is not merely closing a VLA-to-specialist gap. The remaining
VLA baselines, Alpamayo-1 and OpenVLA, fall well behind under SFT alone,
suggesting that the choice of pretrained backbone is itself a strong
factor and that navigation behavior does not transfer automatically from
any driving VLA. Based on these results, we conclude that the
feedback-conditioned reasoning distillation improves short-horizon
trajectory prediction over standard SFT, and the gain persists at a
larger and more diverse data scale.

OmniVLA, and NaVILA on the Statue task. OmniVLA fails to generate a
suitable trajectory toward the statue (Figure 11c), and NaVILA moves

##### Model Collapse and Self-Teacher Reasoning Drift.

(1) Self-teacher stays close to the non-fine-tuned Qwen checkpoint. With
$`\alpha=0.999`$ and one EMA update every 10 training steps,
approximately $`93\%`$ of the teacher’s EMA weight remains assigned to
the pretrained checkpoint, and $`7\%`$ stems from subsequent student
updates. (2) Even the most-changed tokens remain stable. In a more
aggressive stress test ($`\alpha=0.99`$, updated every step),
$`\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Qwen}\|\text{our ckpt})`$ increases smoothly from
$`0.34`$ to $`0.68`$ nats/token across 500 scenes, without abrupt
changes. The most sensitive tail also changes only modestly: p95
surprisal increases from $`4.8`$ to $`6.7`$, while the maximum just
increases from $`16.8`$ to $`18.0`$ nats, indicating proper amount task
adaption rather than collapse. (3) Reasoning remains neither repetitive
nor random. Collapse may move reasoning-token entropy toward two
extremes: zero for repetitive sentences, or the uniform limit
($`\approx 12.4`$ nats) for random texts. Our entropy shifts mildly
($`1.36\to 1.67`$ nats); it neither suffers from the repetitive
reasoning nor random text.

### E.1 Multi-Stage Task

We first show multi-stage demos: Navigate through the door, turn right
and then follow the hallway (Hallway); Navigate right to the sidewalk
and navigate along the sidewalk. Those demos are impressive as ASD can
generalize decently well to multi-stage task prompts with the training
only on the single-stage tasks.

ASD demonstrates strong generalization, achieving a $`60\%`$ success
rate on multi-stage Hallway navigation tasks despite training only on
single-stage tasks, outperforming the $`50\%`$ rate of the NaVILA
baseline. While NaVILA is adept at multi-stage goals, it shows limited
task generalization, with success rates of only $`27\%`$ indoors and
$`33\%`$ outdoors on single-task prompts, as detailed in Tables 1 and 2.

We further evaluate ASD on a more complex, outdoor multi-stage task to
demonstrate its advanced contextual reasoning capabilities. In this
scenario, the agent is presented with a choice between two distinct

cannot rely on simple forward movement; it must first spatially reason

ahead and the one situated to its right. It then must successfully
execute the sequence of navigating to and following the correct path.
This demo highlights the model’s ability to handle fine-grained spatial
ambiguity in unconstrained outdoor environments.

### E.2 Extreme OOD Task

We further demonstrate ASD’s generalization capability by evaluating it

the robustness of this evaluation, we vary the initial starting
positions, forcing the agent to execute specific left or right steering

### E.3 Indoor Tasks

We present video demonstrations comparing ASD against leading baselines
on medium- and hard-difficulty tasks, highlighting instances of
successful navigation by our method alongside baseline failures.

In the hard mode’s directional capability task, "Navigate to the right
chair," the agent must select the correct target from among three
identical chairs placed in front of it. On this task, ASD achieves an
$`80\%`$ success rate, significantly outperforming OmniVLA at $`20\%`$
and NaVILA at $`10\%`$. The video qualitative results show that the
strongest baseline, OmniVLA, consistently selects the incorrect
direction—steering toward the left chair instead of navigating to the
rightmost option. This failure mode shows our method’s better
directional semantic understanding during goal-directed navigation.

In the medium-difficulty object selection task, the agent is presented
with two objects in front of it: a box and a chair. Given the target
prompt, "Navigate to the chair," ASD achieves a $`90\%`$ success rate,
substantially outperforming the leading baseline, OmniVLA, which
achieves only $`40\%`$. The video qualitative results show that OmniVLA
consistently deviates to the black net on the right. In contrast, ASD
demonstrates an efficient, direct, and clean trajectory toward the
target object.

In the hard-difficulty object selection task, the agent is presented
with three distinct objects: a trash can, a monitor, and a chair. When
evaluated on the target prompt, "Navigate to the monitor," ASD achieves
a $`70\%`$ success rate, significantly outperforming OmniVLA’s $`20\%`$
success rate. Consistent with the trends observed in the
medium-difficulty task, the qualitative video results show that OmniVLA
often deviates toward the black net on the right. In contrast, ASD
exhibits a much more direct trajectory toward the correct target.

In the long-horizon single-stage evaluation, the prompt "Navigate
through the door" explicitly tests the model’s long-horizon planning and
execution capabilities. The qualitative video results demonstrate that
ASD outperforms the leading baseline, OmniVLA, by a margin of $`10\%`$.

OmniVLA crashes into the door frame, whereas ASD successfully navigates
through the narrow opening without any collisions.

In the multi-stage task, "Navigate through the door, turn right, then
follow the hallway," the leading baseline, NaVILA, performs comparably
to our method, with ASD outperforming it by a margin of $`10\%`$.
Notably, while the video demonstrations show that both models achieve

during turns. Furthermore, our model achieves this performance running

### E.4 Outdoor Tasks

We present video comparisons of ASD against leading baselines on the

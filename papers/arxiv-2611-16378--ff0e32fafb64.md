# Evidence-Governed Adoption of Self-Generated Updates in Autonomous AI Agents

Subodh Lonkar Affiliation: Independent Researcher

###### Abstract

Autonomous agents increasingly modify prompts, memories, tool
configurations, workflows, and control policies in response to
interaction histories. The ability to generate a plausible modification,
however, does not establish that it should become persistent system
state. Candidate selection is adaptive, evaluators may be imperfect or
manipulable, and repeated local improvements can conceal degradation
relative to an earlier operating baseline. This paper develops
Evidence-Governed Evolution (EGE), a statistical and operational
framework for governing persistent adoption of self-generated agent
modifications. EGE separates candidate generation from confirmatory
adoption, evaluates frozen candidates on fresh paired audit samples,
requires practically meaningful improvement on a prespecified primary
objective, protects declared capabilities through reference-relative
non-inferiority tests, and applies mandatory governance constraints
before bounded canary deployment. The statistical contribution is an
evaluator-calibrated simultaneous lower-bound construction for a
countable sequence of adaptively generated candidates. It combines
candidate-wise Hoeffding bounds with independent, candidate-specific
calibration samples that compare the operational evaluator with a
prespecified reference evaluation procedure. A second result formalises
reference replacement through explicit bridge tests and derives a
cumulative degradation bound along a versioned reference lineage. We
also give a conservative audit-planning condition that links effect-size
margin, evaluation sample size, calibration sample size, and a lower
bound on acceptance probability. A reproducible Monte Carlo stress test
illustrates the importance of calibration under candidate-dependent
evaluator optimism; it is explicitly a stylised statistical experiment,
not an evaluation of a deployed agent. The paper does not claim
empirical validation on a real autonomous-agent system. All guarantees
are conditional on bounded paired scores, fresh sampling, a valid
reference-evaluation construct, and the declared evaluation
distributions; they do not establish universal agent safety or
robustness to arbitrary distribution shift.

## 1 Introduction

Autonomous agents are systems whose behaviour depends not only on
foundation-model weights but also on prompts, memory, tools, workflows,
planning procedures, routing policies, and execution constraints. An
agent can therefore change its behaviour without modifying its
underlying model. Candidate changes may be generated from execution
traces, user feedback, self-reflection, search, or optimisation.

A candidate that performs well on recently encountered tasks is not
necessarily a reliable improvement. It may overfit to development data,
reduce performance on previously supported tasks, increase latency or
resource use, or violate operational constraints. The risk is amplified
when an agent repeatedly proposes and deploys changes with limited human
intervention.

Prior work studies reflection, iterative refinement, and skill
acquisition in language-model agents (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al.,
2023; Wang et al., 2023). Automated prompt optimisation and evolutionary
search provide additional candidate-generation mechanisms (Guo et al.,
2024). These methods motivate the ability to generate changes; they do
not by themselves settle whether a proposed change should become
persistent system state. Broader work on AI safety identifies
specification failures, distributional failures, and unsafe exploration
as recurring challenges (Amodei et al., 2016; García and Fernández,
2015).

The adoption problem has several properties that make it different from
ordinary offline model selection. First, candidate proposals are often
adaptive: the next proposal may depend on previous successes, failures,
evaluator feedback, or deployment traces. Second, the candidate can
modify the mechanism that will later produce evidence, for example by
changing a prompt, retrieval policy, tool-selection rule, or
evaluator-facing output format. Third, the system may optimise a narrow
objective while being expected to preserve a much broader capability
profile. Fourth, deployment is not a purely statistical event. A
candidate can pass a benchmark and still fail under production load,
interact unexpectedly with tools, or create external side effects that
cannot be undone by restoring an earlier configuration.

These properties motivate a layered adoption design. The proposal
mechanism is permitted to explore the design space, but it does not
control the confirmatory audit process. The audit process measures a
frozen candidate against prespecified comparators and margins. A
governance layer enforces requirements that must not be traded against
average task performance. Finally, an operational deployment layer
limits exposure while collecting evidence about behaviour in the target
environment. The layers are related but not interchangeable: a
confidence bound cannot replace a permission boundary, and a canary
cannot repair an invalid statistical comparison.

The scope of EGE is deliberately broader than parameter-level continual
learning. A candidate may change a prompt, memory schema, retrieval
strategy, tool interface, workflow, routing rule, or orchestration
policy. The same principles can be applied to model-weight changes, but
those changes may require additional controls for training-data
provenance, model integrity, supply-chain security, and compatibility.
EGE does not specify a universal metric set; metric selection remains an
application-specific modelling and governance decision.

This paper addresses the adoption decision through Evidence-Governed
Evolution (EGE). EGE separates candidate generation from evidence-based
adoption and combines: (i) fresh audit data collected after a candidate
is frozen; (ii) meaningful improvement on at least one prespecified
primary objective; (iii) non-inferiority on every prespecified protected
capability relative to a stable reference; (iv) mandatory governance
checks; and (v) bounded canary deployment before persistent commitment.

The paper makes four bounded contributions. First, it formalises
persistent adoption as a distinct decision problem for adaptively
generated agent configurations, with separate development, calibration,
confirmatory-audit, and deployment stages. Second, it gives a
simultaneous lower-confidence-bound construction that accounts for
candidate-dependent evaluator discrepancy using independent calibration
data rather than assuming a known calibration allowance. Third, it
formalises reference replacement through bridge comparisons and derives
a cumulative capability-degradation bound along the reference lineage;
this result makes explicit the statistical price of changing the
protected baseline. Fourth, it provides an audit-planning inequality and
a reproducible Monte Carlo stress test that exposes a failure mode of
evaluator-naive acceptance. The Monte Carlo experiment is not presented
as evidence about a real agent system. A real-agent evaluation remains
necessary to establish operational usefulness and is identified as an
outstanding empirical requirement.

The distinction from prior work is deliberately narrow. EGE does not
introduce a new concentration inequality, a new general-purpose
sequential-testing principle, or a complete agent safety architecture.
Its contribution is a specific composition and formalisation for
persistent agent-update adoption: candidate-wise confirmatory testing,
candidate-specific evaluator calibration, protected-capability reference
lineage, and an operational commitment protocol. The novelty claim is
therefore about this problem formulation and the resulting guarantees,
not about the underlying Hoeffding or union-bound tools.

The results are conditional statistical guarantees, not a proof of
universal agent safety. In particular, rollback restores configuration
but cannot reverse irreversible external actions.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Reflection, Refinement, and Persistent Skills

Reflexion uses verbal feedback and episodic memory to improve subsequent
agent attempts (Shinn et al., 2023). Self-Refine iteratively critiques
and revises model outputs (Madaan et al., 2023). Voyager accumulates
reusable skills through interaction with an environment (Wang et al.,
2023). EGE is complementary: these methods can generate candidate
changes, whereas EGE governs whether a candidate is retained
persistently.

EvoPrompt connects language models with evolutionary algorithms for
discrete prompt optimisation (Guo et al., 2024). Such optimisation makes
the distinction between development evidence and final audit evidence
especially important: the data used to select a candidate should not
also be treated as an untouched confirmation sample.

Recent surveys organise self-evolving agents by the components and
stages of evolution, examine self-improvement in modern agentic systems,
and study endogenous observations generated within agent interaction
loops (Gao et al., 2026a; Ren et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). These
works motivate reliability concerns around self-generated learning
signals; EGE focuses on the statistical and operational gate at adoption
time.

### 2.2 Adaptive Data Analysis and Sequential Inference

Hoeffding’s inequality gives finite-sample concentration bounds for
independent bounded observations (Hoeffding, 1963). Repeated adaptive
reuse of a holdout can invalidate ordinary fixed-sample inference;
reusable-holdout methods address this problem through controlled
information release (Dwork et al., 2015). Confidence sequences provide
time-uniform inference when data are monitored repeatedly (Howard et
al., 2021). Classical group-sequential designs and alpha-spending
methods likewise address repeated interim testing (Lan and DeMets, 1983;
DeMets and Lan, 1994). Conformal risk control offers a complementary
approach to controlling expected monotone losses under its own
exchangeability and procedure-specific assumptions (Angelopoulos et al.,
2024).

EGE’s simple construction is a union-bound allocation across candidate
indices and metrics. It is valid when each candidate is frozen before a
fresh audit sample is collected and the stated conditional sampling
assumptions hold. It does not permit unrestricted reuse or repeated
optional inspection of a fixed sample.

### 2.3 Safe Adaptation and Risk Constraints

Safe policy improvement studies evidence-supported policy changes
relative to a baseline (Laroche et al., 2019). Broader safe
reinforcement-learning research considers performance and safety
constraints during learning and deployment (García and Fernández, 2015;
Chow et al., 2018). EGE extends the governance perspective to
heterogeneous agent configuration changes and multiple protected
capabilities. Its non-inferiority tests do not imply that every possible
failure mode is controlled.

### 2.4 Agent Self-Evolution Evaluation and the Adoption Gap

Recent work has begun to study self-evolving agents as a distinct
research area. Gao et al. organise the field around what, when, and how
an agent evolves, while Ren et al. survey self-improvement mechanisms in
modern agentic systems (Gao et al., 2026a; Ren et al., 2026). Zhang et
al. analyse endogenous observations as learning signals generated by an
agent’s own generation, evaluation, and interaction processes (Zhang et
al., 2026). Benchmarking work such as EvoAgentBench evaluates ability
transfer during agent self-evolution (Gao et al., 2026b). Lin et al.
distinguish the ability to produce useful harness updates from the
ability of a task-solving agent to benefit from those updates,
underscoring that update generation and update utility are separate
capabilities (Lin et al., 2026). These studies strengthen the motivation
for evaluating agent evolution directly. EGE addresses a different
stage: after a candidate update has been generated, what evidence should
be required before it is allowed to alter persistent system state?

## 3 Problem Formulation

### 3.1 Agent Configuration and Candidate Generation

Let $`I_{j}`$ denote the incumbent configuration immediately before
candidate index $`j`$ is assessed. Its components are represented by

```math
I_{j}=(M_{j},P_{j},\mathcal{M}_{j},T_{j},C_{j},G_{j}), \tag{1}
```

where $`M_{j}`$ is the model, $`P_{j}`$ the prompt and policy
configuration, $`\mathcal{M}_{j}`$ persistent memory, $`T_{j}`$ tools,
$`C_{j}`$ orchestration logic, and $`G_{j}`$ governance constraints. A
subset of these components may be immutable.

Given experience $`\mathcal{E}_{j}`$, a candidate generator produces

```math
\widetilde{A}_{j}=\mathcal{U}(I_{j},\mathcal{E}_{j}). \tag{2}
```

The generator may be an optimiser, an LLM-based editor, a search
procedure, or a human-supervised process. EGE does not prescribe the
generator.

### 3.2 Metrics and Paired Estimands

Let $`\mathcal{J}`$ be a nonempty finite set of primary objectives and
$`\mathcal{K}`$ a finite set of protected capabilities. Their roles,
score definitions, and evaluation distributions must be declared before
audit outcomes are observed. Scores are normalised to $`[0,1]`$, so
paired score differences lie in $`[-1,1]`$. The reference-evaluation
procedure defines the target estimand used in the statistical
guarantees; it need not be interpreted as error-free ground truth.

For metric $`r`$, let $`q^{R}_{r}(A,Z)`$ denote the reference-procedure
score of configuration $`A`$ on instance $`Z`$. Define

```math
D^{R}_{j,r}(Z)=q^{R}_{r}(\widetilde{A}_{j},Z)-q^{R}_{r}(B_{j,r},Z), \tag{3}
```

where

```math
B_{j,r}=\begin{cases}I_{j},&r\in\mathcal{J},\\
A_{\mathrm{ref}},&r\in\mathcal{K}.\end{cases}
```

Thus primary objectives are compared with the incumbent, while protected
capabilities are compared with a fixed, versioned reference. If a metric
belongs to both sets with different comparators or thresholds, the two
roles are represented as distinct tests.

The target difference is

```math
\Delta^{R}_{j,r}=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{r}}[D^{R}_{j,r}(Z)\mid\mathcal{H}_{j}], \tag{4}
```

where $`\mathcal{H}_{j}`$ is the information available immediately
before candidate $`j`$ is frozen and its fresh samples are drawn. The
distribution $`\mathcal{D}_{r}`$ is the prespecified target distribution
for metric $`r`$. For a single configuration, define
$`Q^{R}_{r}(A)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{r}}[q^{R}_{r}(A,Z)]`$;
paired differences are therefore differences of these
reference-procedure expectations when the same distribution is used.

### 3.3 Capability Representation

For exposition, an application may organise its evaluation metrics into
a capability vector such as

```math
\mathbf{Q}(A;\mathcal{D})=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{\mathrm{task}}&Q_{\mathrm{gen}}&Q_{\mathrm{ret}}&Q_{\mathrm{rel}}&Q_{\mathrm{eff}}&Q_{\mathrm{cal}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}, \tag{5}
```

where the entries represent task performance, generalisation, retention,
reliability, efficiency, and calibration. This vector is illustrative
rather than prescriptive. Each component must be translated into an
operational metric with a defined scoring rule, task population,
aggregation procedure, and direction of preference. For example, latency
and cost are naturally minimisation metrics; they can be transformed
into a higher-is-better score or handled by a separately specified upper
confidence bound. The operational importance of these dimensions is also
reflected in LLM-serving systems that optimise KV-cache memory use and
the throughput–latency trade-off, including PagedAttention and
Sarathi-Serve (Kwon et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2024). The present
theory assumes higher scores are better and scores are bounded in
$`[0,1]`$.

The distinction between primary and protected metrics is a policy choice
made before candidate evaluation. A metric can be primary in one update
campaign and protected in another. If a metric is assigned both roles
for the same candidate but with different comparators, it should be
represented as two tests rather than silently combining the roles. This
avoids ambiguity in the number of tests and in the family-wise error
allocation.

## 4 Evidence-Governed Adoption Rule

For each primary objective $`r\in\mathcal{J}`$, let $`\delta_{r}>0`$ be
the minimum practically meaningful improvement. For each protected
capability $`r\in\mathcal{K}`$, let $`\epsilon_{r}\geq 0`$ be the
maximum tolerated degradation. These margins must be fixed before audit
evaluation.

The population-level acceptance requirements are

```math
\displaystyle\exists r\in\mathcal{J}:\quad\Delta^{R}_{j,r}\geq\delta_{r}, \tag{6}
```

```math
\displaystyle\forall r\in\mathcal{K}:\quad\Delta^{R}_{j,r}\geq-\epsilon_{r}, \tag{7}
```

```math
\displaystyle\forall m\in\mathcal{G}:\quad G_{m}(\widetilde{A}_{j})=\mathrm{true}, \tag{8}
```

where $`\mathcal{G}`$ is the set of mandatory governance checks.
Examples include tool permissions, data-access restrictions, resource
ceilings, and human-approval requirements. A high task score cannot
compensate for failure of a mandatory constraint. Governance checks
should be classified by enforcement mechanism. Deterministic checks can
include allow-listed tool permissions, schema validation, network egress
restrictions, resource ceilings, required approvals, and immutable audit
logging. Human-review checks can cover changes with high impact or
ambiguous consequences. Learned classifiers may assist triage, but
should not be the sole enforcement mechanism for requirements whose
violation is unacceptable. A check should specify its input, pass/fail
semantics, version, owner, and failure action.

###### Definition 1 (Evidence-governed candidate).

A candidate is evidence-governed if it is frozen before its audit sample
is drawn, evaluated using fresh audit observations not used to construct
or tune that candidate, and assessed using prespecified metrics,
margins, and governance checks.

### 4.1 Evaluator Calibration

Let $`q^{E}_{r}(A,Z)`$ denote the operational evaluator score and
$`q^{R}_{r}(A,Z)`$ the score produced by a prespecified
reference-evaluation procedure. The reference procedure may be a
deterministic verifier, an adjudicated human protocol, or another
independently governed measurement procedure. The target estimand in
this subsection is the mean paired difference under the reference
procedure, not an unobservable notion of “true capability” unless the
reference procedure is itself a valid ground-truth measurement.

For candidate $`j`$, comparator $`B_{j,r}`$, and instance $`Z`$, define

```math
\displaystyle D^{E}_{j,r}(Z) \displaystyle=q^{E}_{r}(\widetilde{A}_{j},Z)-q^{E}_{r}(B_{j,r},Z), \tag{9}
```

```math
\displaystyle D^{R}_{j,r}(Z) \displaystyle=q^{R}_{r}(\widetilde{A}_{j},Z)-q^{R}_{r}(B_{j,r},Z), \tag{10}
```

```math
\displaystyle W_{j,r}(Z) \displaystyle=D^{E}_{j,r}(Z)-D^{R}_{j,r}(Z). \tag{11}
```

Scores are in $`[0,1]`$, so $`D^{E},D^{R}\in[-1,1]`$ and $`W\in[-2,2]`$.
Write $`\mu^{E}_{j,r}=\mathbb{E}[D^{E}_{j,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j}]`$,
$`\Delta^{R}_{j,r}=\mathbb{E}[D^{R}_{j,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j}]`$, and
$`b_{j,r}=\mathbb{E}[W_{j,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j}]`$. Then
$`\mu^{E}_{j,r}-\Delta^{R}_{j,r}=b_{j,r}`$.

Unlike a fixed, unexplained allowance, EGE estimates the discrepancy for
each frozen candidate using an independent calibration sample drawn from
the same declared metric distribution. This is intentionally demanding:
a candidate-specific calibration set is needed because an update may
change output style or behaviour in ways that affect evaluator
agreement. A global calibration result can replace this design only if a
separate uniform-generalisation argument covers the complete candidate
class and adaptive selection process.

The calibration procedure does not eliminate construct-validity
concerns. If the reference evaluator is systematically wrong, the
resulting guarantee is only relative to that reference procedure. If
human adjudication is used, the paper’s target is the expected
adjudicated score under the stated protocol; inter-rater disagreement
and adjudication reliability must be reported. The reference procedure,
rubric, blinding, version, and sampling frame must be fixed before
confirmatory results are examined.

### 4.2 Evidence Partitioning and Audit Integrity

A practical implementation should distinguish at least three information
partitions. The development partition is available to the candidate
generator and may be used for prompt iteration, debugging, reflection,
and search. The validation partition may be used to compare alternative
candidate designs during development, but repeated adaptive use means it
should not be treated as a final confirmatory sample. The audit
partition is reserved for the frozen candidate and is revealed only
after the candidate, comparator, metrics, margins, and analysis
procedure have been committed.

The audit partition should be controlled as an operational resource.
Access logs should record which candidate was evaluated, which audit
instances were used, the evaluator version, the scoring rubric, and
whether any audit result was exposed to the candidate generator. If an
audit result is used to modify the candidate, that sample is no longer
fresh for the modified candidate. The modified candidate must receive a
new confirmatory sample or be evaluated with a method that explicitly
accounts for adaptive reuse. This requirement is stronger than merely
labelling a dataset as a test set: the information flow from audit
results back into candidate construction must also be controlled.

Where tasks are clustered by user, session, domain, or environment,
sampling individual rows independently may be inappropriate. The unit of
sampling should reflect the dependence structure. For example, if
several test cases arise from one user session, treating them as
independent can understate uncertainty. A cluster-level design, a
concentration inequality for dependent observations, or an appropriately
conservative aggregation is then required. The Hoeffding result below is
not automatically valid for arbitrary correlated task traces.

## 5 Sequential Statistical Adoption

### 5.1 Sampling and Calibration Assumptions

For each candidate $`j`$ and metric $`r`$, collect $`n_{j,r}`$ fresh
confirmatory observations $`d^{E}_{i,j,r}`$ and $`m_{j,r}`$ independent
calibration observations $`w_{i,j,r}`$. Define their means by

```math
\displaystyle\bar{d}^{E}_{j,r} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{j,r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j,r}}d^{E}_{i,j,r}, \tag{12}
```

```math
\displaystyle\bar{w}_{j,r} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{j,r}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{j,r}}w_{i,j,r}. \tag{13}
```

###### Assumption 1 (Conditional fresh sampling).

Conditional on the pre-audit history $`\mathcal{H}_{j}`$, candidate and
comparator are frozen before either sample is drawn. Confirmatory
observations are independent, lie in $`[-1,1]`$, and have common
conditional mean $`\mu^{E}_{j,r}`$. Calibration observations are
independent, lie in $`[-2,2]`$, and have common conditional mean
$`b_{j,r}`$. The two samples are mutually independent conditional on
$`\mathcal{H}_{j}`$, and neither is used to construct or tune the frozen
candidate. Samples are drawn from the declared distribution for metric
$`r`$.

###### Assumption 2 (Finite prespecified metric family).

The primary and protected test sets are fixed and finite. Let
$`R=|\mathcal{J}|+|\mathcal{K}|`$, counting a metric twice if it appears
in both roles with different comparators or thresholds. Metric
definitions, score ranges, distributions, margins, and
reference-evaluation procedures are fixed before confirmatory outcomes
are observed.

###### Assumption 3 (Reference-procedure validity).

For each test, the reference evaluator defines the target estimand
$`\Delta^{R}_{j,r}`$. Any claim that this estimand represents an
external capability relies on the validity of the reference procedure
and its sampling frame. The theorem below does not establish that
construct-validity claim.

### 5.2 Evaluation and Calibration Bounds

For an evaluation error allocation $`e_{j,r}\in(0,1)`$, Hoeffding’s
inequality gives

```math
L^{E}_{j,r}=\bar{d}^{E}_{j,r}-\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(1/e_{j,r})}{n_{j,r}}}, \tag{14}
```

with $`\Pr(L^{E}_{j,r}>\mu^{E}_{j,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j})\leq e_{j,r}`$.

For a calibration error allocation $`c_{j,r}\in(0,1)`$, the one-sided
Hoeffding inequality for observations in an interval of width four gives

```math
U^{b}_{j,r}=\bar{w}_{j,r}+\sqrt{\frac{8\ln(1/c_{j,r})}{m_{j,r}}}, \tag{15}
```

with $`\Pr(b_{j,r}>U^{b}_{j,r}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j})\leq c_{j,r}`$.

Since $`\Delta^{R}_{j,r}=\mu^{E}_{j,r}-b_{j,r}`$, a lower confidence
bound for the reference-procedure estimand is

```math
L^{R}_{j,r}=L^{E}_{j,r}-U^{b}_{j,r}. \tag{16}
```

On the joint event $`L^{E}_{j,r}\leq\mu^{E}_{j,r}`$ and
$`b_{j,r}\leq U^{b}_{j,r}`$, we have

```math
L^{R}_{j,r}\leq\mu^{E}_{j,r}-U^{b}_{j,r}\leq\mu^{E}_{j,r}-b_{j,r}=\Delta^{R}_{j,r}.
```

The calibration bound is one-sided because only optimistic evaluator
discrepancy can make an evaluator-based lower bound overstate the
reference-procedure effect. Negative discrepancy is not subtracted as if
it were harmful bias; the resulting lower bound remains valid for the
signed target estimand.

### 5.3 Error Allocation Across Candidates and Tests

Let $`\alpha\in(0,1)`$ be the global error budget. For global
audit-event index $`j=1,2,\ldots`$ and test $`r=1,\ldots,R`$, allocate

```math
e_{j,r}=c_{j,r}=\frac{3\alpha}{\pi^{2}j^{2}R}. \tag{17}
```

Because $`\sum_{j\geq 1}j^{-2}=\pi^{2}/6`$, the evaluation allocations
sum to $`\alpha/2`$ and the calibration allocations also sum to
$`\alpha/2`$. Therefore,

```math
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(e_{j,r}+c_{j,r})=\alpha. \tag{18}
```

###### Theorem 1 (Simultaneous evaluator-calibrated coverage).

Under Assumptions 1–3, with the allocations in (17),

```math
\Pr\left\{L^{R}_{j,r}\leq\Delta^{R}_{j,r}\ \text{for every }j\geq 1,\ r\in\{1,\ldots,R\}\right\}\geq 1-\alpha. \tag{19}
```

No independence across candidate indices or metric tests is required for
the final union-bound step.

###### Proof.

For each fixed $`(j,r)`$, Assumption 1 and Hoeffding’s inequality imply
that the evaluation-bound failure probability is at most $`e_{j,r}`$ and
the calibration-bound failure probability is at most $`c_{j,r}`$. By the
deterministic implication preceding the theorem, failure of
$`L^{R}_{j,r}\leq\Delta^{R}_{j,r}`$ is contained in the union of those
two events. Hence

```math
\Pr\{L^{R}_{j,r}>\Delta^{R}_{j,r}\}\leq e_{j,r}+c_{j,r}.
```

A union bound over the countable candidate sequence and finite metric
family yields

```math
\Pr\{\exists j,r:L^{R}_{j,r}>\Delta^{R}_{j,r}\}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(e_{j,r}+c_{j,r})=\alpha.
```

This proves the result. Adaptivity of candidate generation is permitted
because the candidate is fixed conditional on $`\mathcal{H}_{j}`$ before
its independent fresh evaluation and calibration samples are drawn. The
index is a global audit-event index, allocated by the audit controller
to candidate tests and reference-bridge tests alike; it cannot be reset
after rejection, rollback, or a change in proposal strategy. ∎

###### Remark 1 (What the theorem does not establish).

The theorem controls simultaneous statistical undercoverage relative to
the reference-evaluation estimand. It does not prove that the reference
evaluator measures the intended real-world capability, that the audit
distribution matches deployment, or that an agent cannot cause harm
through unmeasured actions. Those are separate validity and governance
questions.

### 5.4 Audit Planning and a Conservative Power Condition

Define the evaluation and calibration radii

```math
h^{E}_{j,r}=\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(1/e_{j,r})}{n_{j,r}}},\qquad h^{b}_{j,r}=\sqrt{\frac{8\ln(1/c_{j,r})}{m_{j,r}}}.
```

For a test with threshold $`\tau_{r}`$ and true reference-procedure
effect $`\Delta^{R}_{j,r}=\tau_{r}+g`$ for $`g>0`$, the event
$`\bar{d}^{E}\geq\mu^{E}-h^{E}`$ and $`\bar{w}-b\leq h^{b}`$ implies

```math
L^{R}_{j,r}\geq\Delta^{R}_{j,r}-h^{E}_{j,r}-2h^{b}_{j,r}.
```

Consequently, on the joint event
$`\bar{d}^{E}_{j,r}\geq\mu^{E}_{j,r}-h^{E}_{j,r}`$ and
$`\bar{w}_{j,r}-b_{j,r}\leq h^{b}_{j,r}`$, the constructed lower bound
obeys

```math
L^{R}_{j,r}\geq\Delta^{R}_{j,r}-2h^{E}_{j,r}-2h^{b}_{j,r}.
```

The factor of two on the evaluation radius is necessary because the
lower confidence bound itself subtracts one radius from the observed
mean, while the event used to lower-bound that observed mean contributes
another radius. Therefore, if $`2h^{E}_{j,r}+2h^{b}_{j,r}\leq g`$, the
test passes with probability at least $`1-e_{j,r}-c_{j,r}`$. This is a
sufficient, conservative power condition, not a necessary condition.

For any split parameter $`\lambda\in(0,1)`$, it is sufficient to choose

```math
\displaystyle n_{j,r} \displaystyle\geq\frac{8\ln(1/e_{j,r})}{\lambda^{2}g^{2}}, \tag{20}
```

```math
\displaystyle m_{j,r} \displaystyle\geq\frac{32\ln(1/c_{j,r})}{(1-\lambda)^{2}g^{2}}. \tag{21}
```

The parameter $`\lambda`$ allocates the available margin between
confirmatory evaluation and calibration uncertainty. The evaluation
bound above is four times the fixed-radius requirement in (24) because
the power argument must reserve margin both for the LCB construction and
for downward sampling fluctuation. These bounds expose the cost of
candidate-wise calibration: the required calibration sample can be
substantial when the desired margin is small or the candidate index is
large. They also show why a claim of practical efficiency requires
empirical cost reporting rather than only a validity theorem.

### 5.5 Statistical Acceptance Predicate

The candidate passes the statistical gate if

```math
\displaystyle\operatorname{Accept}_{j}^{\mathrm{stat}}={} \displaystyle\left[\exists r\in\mathcal{J}:L^{R}_{j,r}\geq\delta_{r}\right]
```

```math
\displaystyle\land\left[\forall r\in\mathcal{K}:L^{R}_{j,r}\geq-\epsilon_{r}\right]. \tag{22}
```

The lower-bound rule is intentionally conservative. A candidate does not
pass merely because its observed mean is positive; the lower confidence
bound must exceed the practical improvement margin. This distinguishes
statistical evidence from effect-size relevance. A very small positive
effect can be statistically distinguishable from zero yet still be
operationally unimportant. Conversely, a candidate with a large observed
effect but a small audit sample may remain inconclusive.

The adjusted lower bound can be read as the operational-evaluator sample
mean minus the evaluation radius and the one-sided calibration upper
bound. A primary test passes when
$`\bar{d}^{E}_{j,r}-h^{E}_{j,r}-U^{b}_{j,r}\geq\delta_{r}`$. A protected
test passes when
$`\bar{d}^{E}_{j,r}-h^{E}_{j,r}-U^{b}_{j,r}\geq-\epsilon_{r}`$. This
decomposition is useful for audit planning because it shows whether
sampling uncertainty or evaluator discrepancy dominates an inconclusive
decision.

### 5.6 Interpretation of Inconclusive Outcomes

The framework has three operationally distinct outcomes. A candidate can
be accepted when every required statistical and governance condition
passes. It can be rejected when a hard constraint fails or the evidence
establishes that a required condition is not met. It can also remain
inconclusive when the lower bound does not pass a threshold but the data
do not establish a material regression. In a conservative deployment
policy, both rejection and inconclusive outcomes retain the incumbent;
however, the distinction should be preserved in logs because it matters
for subsequent candidate development and audit-cost analysis. Additional
sampling is permissible only if the sampling plan was prespecified or if
the monitoring procedure is sequentially valid.

The complete offline acceptance predicate is

```math
\operatorname{Accept}_{j}=\operatorname{Accept}_{j}^{\mathrm{stat}}\land\bigwedge_{m\in\mathcal{G}}\{G_{m}(\widetilde{A}_{j})=\mathrm{true}\}. \tag{23}
```

###### Corollary 1 (Family-wise control of false statistical acceptance).

Under the theorem’s assumptions and the evaluator-calibration procedure,
the probability that any candidate in the audit campaign is
statistically accepted while failing at least one population requirement
in (6)–(7) is at most $`\alpha`$.

###### Proof.

On the simultaneous-coverage event, every adjusted lower bound is no
greater than its target population difference. If all primary objectives
fail their respective meaningful-improvement thresholds, no primary
lower bound can pass. If any protected capability violates its
non-inferiority margin, its lower bound cannot pass. Thus a
population-invalid candidate cannot pass the statistical gate on the
simultaneous-coverage event. The complement has probability at most
$`\alpha`$. ∎

## 6 Reference-Anchored Capability Stability and Reference Lineage

A fixed reference prevents the protected baseline from moving after
every accepted update. If the reference is eventually replaced, however,
a new baseline can reintroduce cumulative drift unless the replacement
is explicitly compared with its predecessor. EGE therefore treats
reference replacement as a separately audited governance event.

###### Proposition 1 (Fixed-reference non-inferiority).

On the simultaneous-coverage event in Theorem 1, every candidate that
passes all protected-capability tests in a fixed-reference epoch
satisfies

```math
\Delta^{R}_{j,r}(\widetilde{A}_{j},A_{\mathrm{ref}})\geq-\epsilon_{r},\qquad r\in\mathcal{K}.
```

###### Proof.

For every protected test, simultaneous coverage gives
$`L^{R}_{j,r}\leq\Delta^{R}_{j,r}`$. Acceptance requires
$`L^{R}_{j,r}\geq-\epsilon_{r}`$. Combining the inequalities proves the
claim. ∎

### 6.1 Reference Replacement and Bridge Tests

Let $`A^{(0)},A^{(1)},\ldots,A^{(K)}`$ denote a sequence of approved
references. At transition $`k`$, a bridge test compares the proposed new
reference $`A^{(k+1)}`$ with the previous reference $`A^{(k)}`$ on the
same protected metric and target distribution. Let its target difference
be

```math
B_{k,r}=Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(k+1)})-Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(k)}).
```

The bridge test must use fresh evaluation and calibration samples and
receive its own error allocation within the global budget. In
implementation, every candidate test and every bridge test receives a
unique, non-reusable index from one global audit controller; the
summable allocation in (17) is applied to this global event index.
Reference transitions therefore cannot reuse error budget already spent
on candidate tests. Let $`L^{\mathrm{bridge}}_{k,r}`$ be its
evaluator-calibrated lower confidence bound. The replacement is
permitted only if $`L^{\mathrm{bridge}}_{k,r}\geq-\rho_{k,r}`$, where
$`\rho_{k,r}\geq 0`$ is a prespecified bridge tolerance. A reference
change is therefore not an administrative reset: it consumes statistical
evidence and is recorded as a distinct lineage transition.

###### Theorem 2 (Cumulative capability bound along a reference lineage).

Assume all candidate tests and all bridge tests are included in one
simultaneous-coverage family with total error budget $`\alpha`$. Assume
every committed configuration $`A`$ in epoch $`K`$ passes its
protected-capability test relative to $`A^{(K)}`$ with tolerance
$`\epsilon_{K,r}`$, and every accepted reference transition satisfies
$`B_{k,r}\geq-\rho_{k,r}`$. Then, on the simultaneous-coverage event,

```math
Q^{R}_{r}(A)-Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(0)})\geq-\left(\epsilon_{K,r}+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\rho_{k,r}\right).
```

Thus the maximum allowed degradation relative to the original reference
is explicit and accumulates only through declared epoch tolerances.

###### Proof.

By the candidate’s protected-capability acceptance condition and
simultaneous coverage,
$`Q^{R}_{r}(A)-Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(K)})\geq-\epsilon_{K,r}`$. For each bridge
transition, simultaneous coverage and its acceptance condition give
$`Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(k+1)})-Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(k)})\geq-\rho_{k,r}`$. Add these
inequalities and telescope the intermediate reference terms:

```math
Q^{R}_{r}(A)-Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(0)})=Q^{R}_{r}(A)-Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(K)})+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left[Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(k+1)})-Q^{R}_{r}(A^{(k)})\right].
```

The claimed lower bound follows. ∎

The result requires the same metric definition and target distribution
across all bridge comparisons. If the target distribution changes, the
terms are not directly additive without an additional transport or
standardisation argument. The bound is also only as meaningful as the
reference-evaluation procedure. It formalises reference lineage; it does
not prove that the initial reference is optimal or that the selected
protected metrics exhaust all relevant capabilities.

## 7 Evidence-Governed Deployment

Offline statistical acceptance does not imply safe behaviour under
production load or on unrepresented tasks. EGE therefore separates
statistical acceptance from persistent commitment.

### 7.1 Two-Phase Commitment

The operational state machine has four states: (1) _Candidate_, where a
proposed modification is frozen; (2) _Audit-passed_, where statistical
and governance requirements pass; (3) _Canary_, where the candidate runs
under bounded traffic, permissions, workload, or execution scope; and
(4) _Committed_, where it becomes the persistent configuration after
prespecified canary criteria pass.

A candidate failing offline evaluation is rejected. A candidate failing
canary criteria is contained and the incumbent configuration is
restored. Configuration rollback does not reverse irreversible external
side effects; systems with consequential tool access need transaction
controls and compensating-action procedures.

The canary scope can be limited along several dimensions: the fraction
of eligible requests routed to the candidate, the set of users or
tenants exposed, the classes of tools enabled, the maximum number of
tool calls, the permitted data domains, the execution time, and the
maximum resource budget. The appropriate controls depend on the agent’s
action space. For a read-only summarisation agent, a traffic-split
canary may be sufficient. For an agent that can modify records or
trigger external workflows, a shadow mode or approval-gated mode may be
more appropriate than live execution.

The canary must have an explicit stop condition and an explicit
authority to stop. A useful operational record includes the candidate
hash, incumbent hash, canary start and end times, exposure volume,
metric values, alert events, rollback reason, and any compensating
actions. The candidate should not be permitted to change itself during
the canary; otherwise, the object being monitored is no longer the
object that passed the offline audit. If adaptation during canary
operation is required, each resulting version should be treated as a new
candidate or covered by a separately justified online-learning protocol.

### 7.2 Operational Monitoring

Canary criteria should be application-specific and fixed before
deployment. They may include task success, tool-error rate, latency,
resource consumption, policy violations, escalation rate, and
user-impact indicators. Repeated monitoring requires sequentially valid
procedures or deterministic operational limits; a fixed-sample
confidence interval must not be repeatedly inspected as if it were
time-uniform.

### 7.3 Risk-Tiered Deployment

A practical deployment controller can assign candidate changes to risk
tiers before evaluation. A low-impact change might alter wording or
reorder a non-critical retrieval preference while preserving permissions
and tool interfaces. A medium-impact change might modify memory
retrieval, task routing, or multi-step workflow logic. A high-impact
change might modify access to sensitive data, external-action
permissions, financial or operational workflows, or safety-relevant
control logic. These examples are illustrative; the organisation must
define its own classification.

The risk tier should determine the required canary scope, duration,
approval level, and rollback authority. It should not change the
statistical meaning of the audit results after they have been observed.
Higher-risk changes may require stricter margins, more protected
metrics, deterministic checks, or mandatory human approval. Such
requirements should be encoded before the candidate is evaluated. This
preserves the separation between evidence and policy while allowing
governance controls to scale with consequence.

### 7.4 Rollback and Recovery

Rollback should be implemented as a tested recovery procedure rather
than an informal instruction to restore a prior prompt. A complete
recovery package may include the prior configuration, memory-schema
compatibility information, tool and API versions, feature flags, routing
state, and any migration steps required to restore service. If a
candidate changes persistent memory, rollback may require restoring a
snapshot or applying a reversible migration. If it writes to external
systems, recovery may require compensating transactions and an incident
process. Some side effects cannot be reversed; the system should
identify those actions before enabling the candidate and apply stricter
controls accordingly.

## 8 Evaluation

This section reports one executed, fully specified Monte Carlo stress
test and defines the additional real-agent evaluation required to assess
operational usefulness. The Monte Carlo study is synthetic by
construction: it tests a statistical failure mode under known
data-generating assumptions. It is not evidence that EGE improves the
behaviour of an implemented autonomous agent.

### 8.1 Monte Carlo sensitivity study: evaluator bias and audit size

#### Purpose and design.

We ran a targeted Monte Carlo sensitivity study to examine two distinct
properties of the proposed gate: (i) whether candidate-specific
calibration prevents harmful acceptance when the operational evaluator
is optimistic, and (ii) how much acceptance power is lost when
calibration and audit samples are small. This is a synthetic statistical
experiment, not an autonomous-agent benchmark. Each condition comprises
3,000 independent campaigns with 10 candidates per campaign. Each
candidate is beneficial or harmful with probability one half. The
reference-procedure primary effect is $`+0.25`$ for beneficial
candidates and $`-0.10`$ for harmful candidates; the protected effect is
zero. The primary threshold is $`\delta=0.05`$, the protected
non-inferiority margin is $`\epsilon=0.20`$, and the global error budget
is $`\alpha=0.05`$. The operational evaluator’s primary-score optimism
is varied over $`b\in\{0,0.10,0.25\}`$; protected-metric bias is zero.
Confirmatory paired differences are sampled from $`\{-1,+1\}`$ with the
specified means, while calibration discrepancies are sampled from
$`\{-2,+2\}`$ with the specified mean. The confirmatory and calibration
samples are independent. We vary both sample sizes together over
$`n=m\in\{5{,}000,20{,}000,80{,}000\}`$ per candidate and metric. The
candidate-wise evaluation and calibration allocations are each
$`3\alpha/(\pi^{2}j^{2}R)`$, so the two families jointly consume the
global error budget. The random seed is 20261112.

### 8.3 Required Real-Agent Evaluation Protocol

A real-agent study remains necessary before submission to a venue
expecting evidence of practical utility. The study should use a
reproducible agent whose editable harness is limited to declared
components such as prompts, retrieval settings, tool-routing rules, or
workflow configuration. Candidate generation must be separated from
audit and reference-evaluation services. Each candidate should be frozen
and content-hashed before independent audit and calibration samples are
drawn.

#### Tasks and metrics.

Use a task suite with deterministic verifiers wherever possible;
benchmark design and red-teaming studies can inform breadth and
adversarial coverage (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Perez et al., 2022).
Include at least one primary objective and multiple protected
capabilities chosen before the experiment. Candidate and comparator
should be evaluated on the same task instances when pairing is valid.
For stochastic agents, record model version, decoding settings, tool
versions, seeds, timeouts, and retry rules. If tasks are clustered by
user/session or share state, use cluster-level sampling or a
concentration result appropriate to the dependence structure. Do not
apply the independent-observation Hoeffding bound to correlated traces
without justification.

#### Candidate generation and baselines.

Use the same candidate proposal stream for all adoption strategies where
feasible. Compare (i) greedy observed-score acceptance, (ii) fixed
observed-mean thresholds, (iii) confidence-bound acceptance without
evaluator calibration, (iv) calibrated EGE without reference protection,
(v) calibrated EGE with incumbent-only protection, and (vi) full EGE.
Report candidate counts, accepted candidates, harmful acceptances, false
rejections, inconclusive decisions, audit observations, calibration
observations, elapsed time, and canary exposure.

#### Ablations and stress conditions.

Vary candidate index, audit size, calibration size, evaluator optimism,
evaluator drift, protected-capability regression, and task-distribution
shift. Include an evaluator-manipulation condition in which candidate
changes alter output style or structure while leaving task success
unchanged. Report evaluator/reference disagreement by candidate class
and style. Use blinded human adjudication or deterministic verifiers as
the reference procedure; if human ratings are used, report the rubric,
rater training, agreement, adjudication, and sampling frame. The
reference procedure must not be described as ground truth unless that
claim is justified.

#### Statistical reporting.

Report campaign-level false-acceptance rates with exact or otherwise
justified uncertainty intervals, acceptance power under prespecified
effect sizes, per-capability regression rates, and audit cost. Report
simulation seeds and number of repetitions. For agent experiments,
report uncertainty across independent update episodes and avoid treating
multiple tasks from one shared history as independent episodes. Separate
offline false acceptance from canary failure: the former is controlled
only under the theorem’s assumptions, whereas the latter depends on
deployment representativeness and monitoring design.

#### Canary study.

A candidate that passes offline evaluation should enter a bounded,
immutable canary. Predeclare exposure limits, permitted tools,
monitoring metrics, stop rules, and rollback authority. If canary
metrics are inspected repeatedly, use a sequentially valid procedure or
deterministic stop thresholds; do not repeatedly inspect a fixed-sample
interval as if it were time-uniform. Record irreversible side effects
and compensating actions. The canary stage must not be described as
statistically guaranteed by the offline theorem.

### 8.4 Reproducibility and Availability

The supplementary simulation script specifies the random seed,
data-generating process, allocation, sample sizes, thresholds, and
outcome calculations. A real-agent reproduction package should
additionally include candidate hashes, incumbent and reference versions,
task provenance, evaluator prompts and versions, calibration records,
raw aggregate scores, exclusion rules, and canary logs. Privacy- or
security-sensitive task content may be withheld, but the paper should
explain the resulting reproducibility limitation. No real-agent code or
dataset is claimed to accompany this version.

## 9 Discussion

### 9.1 Generation Is Not Adoption

Candidate generators optimise the production of plausible modifications.
Adoption mechanisms decide whether evidence justifies changing
persistent state. If the same agent proposes, evaluates, and endorses
changes using overlapping evidence, a self-confirming loop can arise.
EGE makes evidence separation explicit without requiring human approval
for every low-impact update. Human review remains appropriate for
high-impact changes or uncertain evaluators. This separation also
clarifies accountability: the candidate generator is responsible for
proposing a change, the evaluation service is responsible for producing
a measurement under a fixed protocol, and the governance authority is
responsible for defining which risks and trade-offs are acceptable.
These responsibilities may be implemented by separate services or teams,
but they should remain distinguishable in logs and incident reviews.

### 9.2 Evaluator Validity

Fresh audit data do not guarantee evaluator correctness. The
candidate-specific calibration construction accounts for sampling
uncertainty in the observed discrepancy, but it does not repair an
invalid reference construct or a calibration sample drawn from the wrong
population. Candidate-specific calibration is expensive and may itself
be vulnerable if the reference procedure is not blinded or independent.
A model-based judge may favour a candidate’s style or exploit a scoring
rubric. Deterministic verifiers and blinded human adjudication are
preferable where feasible. For subjective tasks, inter-rater agreement
and construct validity matter as much as nominal sample size. A
numerically precise interval around a systematically invalid construct
is not evidence that the intended capability has been measured.

### 9.3 Distribution Shift and Unmeasured Failure

The statistical result concerns the declared distribution
$`\mathcal{D}_{r}`$. It does not automatically transfer to shifted
deployment tasks. Monitoring, re-audit, and suspension of automatic
adoption may be needed when representativeness deteriorates. No finite
metric set can guarantee preservation of capabilities that are not
measured. Distribution monitoring should therefore be treated as a
trigger for reassessment, not as proof that the original guarantees
remain valid. A shift detector can itself produce false alarms or miss
subtle changes, so its thresholds and response policy should be
documented. When a material shift is detected, the organisation may
suspend automatic adoption, collect a new representative audit set,
revise the target distributions, or require additional human review. Any
change to the reference distribution or protected metric definitions
should be versioned.

### 9.4 Conservatism and Cost

The allocation in (17) is intentionally conservative: later candidate
indices receive smaller per-test error levels. This can require larger
samples. Alternatives include a finite prespecified candidate horizon,
confidence sequences, or more efficient concentration bounds. Any
replacement must preserve the relevant family-wise guarantee under its
own assumptions. There is also a distinction between the error budget
and the desired power of the procedure. The alpha-spending schedule
controls false statistical acceptance, but it does not guarantee that
beneficial candidates will be accepted. Later candidates receive smaller
error allocations, which can increase the required sample size and the
probability of an inconclusive outcome at a fixed sample size. A
deployment study should therefore report both error control and power or
acceptance probability under specified effect sizes. The system owner
must choose an acceptable balance between audit cost, decision delay,
and missed improvements.

## 10 Limitations and Future Work

The guarantee assumes bounded paired observations and conditionally
independent fresh confirmatory and calibration samples. Real agent tasks
may be dependent, nonstationary, or affected by shared infrastructure.
The reference-evaluation procedure must be sufficiently valid for the
intended construct; the theorem only controls discrepancy relative to
that procedure. Candidate-specific calibration is sample-intensive, and
the Monte Carlo study is a stylised stress test rather than a real-agent
evaluation. Extensions to clustered sampling, martingale differences,
nonstationary evaluation, and efficient uniform calibration over
candidate classes are important. A reference can become obsolete; the
bridge-test result controls only declared metrics on a common
distribution. Configuration rollback cannot undo external actions. The
immediate empirical priority is to implement the protocol in a
representative agent system and measure both audit cost and operational
outcomes.

A further limitation concerns metric multiplicity within a capability. A
single aggregate score can conceal subgroup regressions, while a large
collection of subgroup metrics increases the number of simultaneous
tests and therefore the required error allocation. Hierarchical testing
or structured metric families may improve efficiency, but they require a
prespecified hierarchy and a valid multiplicity procedure. Another
limitation is that bounded normalised scores can discard information
about severity. For example, a rare catastrophic event may be poorly
represented by an average score in $`[0,1]`$. Such outcomes should be
represented by separate hard constraints, tail-risk measures, or
event-rate requirements rather than being diluted in an average
capability score.

The framework also assumes that the comparator can be evaluated on the
same instance as the candidate. This paired design is attractive because
it removes some task-to-task variation, but it may not be feasible when
candidate changes alter task eligibility, tool availability, or
execution pathways. In such cases, the experiment may require matched
cohorts, randomised assignment, or a different estimand. The resulting
inference must reflect the actual design; the paired Hoeffding formula
should not be applied mechanically.

Finally, EGE is a governance framework, not a complete agent security
architecture. It does not by itself prevent prompt injection, data
exfiltration, supply-chain compromise, malicious tool responses, or
failures in the underlying model. Permission isolation, secure
execution, logging, identity management, and incident response remain
necessary. The contribution is narrower: EGE specifies a statistically
explicit gate for persistent adoption and a deployment protocol that
complements those controls.

## 11 Conclusion

This paper developed Evidence-Governed Evolution for the persistent
adoption of self-generated modifications to autonomous-agent
configurations. The framework separates candidate generation from
confirmatory adoption, requires fresh paired evaluation, calibrates the
operational evaluator against an independently governed reference
procedure, protects declared capabilities relative to a versioned
reference, and places accepted candidates behind bounded canary
deployment. Under explicit conditional sampling assumptions, the
evaluator-calibrated lower bounds provide simultaneous coverage over a
countable sequence of adaptively generated candidates. A
reference-lineage result makes the cost of replacing a protected
baseline explicit, while an audit-planning inequality relates the
effect-size margin to confirmatory and calibration sample requirements.

The Monte Carlo stress test demonstrates one narrow point:
candidate-dependent evaluator optimism can make an evaluator-naive gate
accept harmful updates, while candidate-specific calibration can prevent
those acceptances in a stylised setting. It does not establish that EGE
improves real-agent performance, is operationally efficient across
realistic workloads, or remains valid under arbitrary dependence and
distribution shift. Those questions require a controlled agent-task
study and careful validation of the reference evaluator. EGE should
therefore be viewed as a formal adoption protocol with testable
assumptions, not as a proof of universal agent safety. The central
principle remains that generating a plausible update is not evidence
that it should become persistent system state.

## Appendix A Hoeffding Bound Derivation

For independent $`X_{i}\in[-1,1]`$ with common mean $`\mu`$, Hoeffding’s
inequality gives

```math
\mathbb{P}(\bar{X}-\mu\geq u)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2nu^{2}}{(1-(-1))^{2}}\right)=\exp(-nu^{2}/2).
```

Setting this upper bound equal to $`a`$ yields $`u=\sqrt{2\ln(1/a)/n}`$,
and hence

```math
\mathbb{P}\left\{\mu\geq\bar{X}-\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(1/a)}{n}}\right\}\geq 1-a.
```

This is the evaluation radius used in (14); variance-sensitive
alternatives such as empirical Bernstein bounds can be tighter when
their assumptions are appropriate (Maurer and Pontil, 2009; Boucheron et
al., 2013).

## Appendix B Calibration-Bound Derivation

For independent calibration observations $`W_{i}\in[-2,2]`$ with mean
$`b`$, the one-sided Hoeffding inequality gives

```math
\Pr(b-\bar{W}\geq u)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2mu^{2}}{(2-(-2))^{2}}\right)=\exp(-mu^{2}/8).
```

Setting the right-hand side to $`c`$ yields $`u=\sqrt{8\ln(1/c)/m}`$.
Therefore $`\Pr(b\leq\bar{W}+u)\geq 1-c`$, which establishes the
one-sided calibration upper bound in (15).

## Appendix C Evaluation and Calibration Sample-Size Bounds

To make the evaluation radius no greater than $`h_{E}>0`$, it is
sufficient that

```math
n_{j,r}\geq\frac{2\ln(1/e_{j,r})}{h_{E}^{2}}. \tag{24}
```

To make the calibration radius no greater than $`h_{b}>0`$, it is
sufficient that

```math
m_{j,r}\geq\frac{8\ln(1/c_{j,r})}{h_{b}^{2}}.
```

For a target-effect gap $`g`$, the sufficient condition
$`2h_{E}+2h_{b}\leq g`$ yields the acceptance-probability bound
described in Section 5.4. These are conservative worst-case bounds;
variance-adaptive alternatives require their own validity arguments.

## Appendix E Worked Illustrative Decision

Consider one primary test and two protected tests for candidate $`j=1`$,
with $`R=3`$ and global error budget $`\alpha=0.05`$. Under (17), each
evaluation and calibration allocation is $`3\alpha/(\pi^{2}R)`$. For
each metric, the controller collects independent confirmatory and
calibration samples, computes $`L^{E}_{j,r}`$ from (14), computes
$`U^{b}_{j,r}`$ from (15), and sets
$`L^{R}_{j,r}=L^{E}_{j,r}-U^{b}_{j,r}`$. The candidate passes only if
the primary lower bound exceeds its prespecified meaningful-improvement
margin and both protected lower bounds exceed their respective
non-inferiority thresholds. This example is procedural: the margins must
be selected from application requirements, not tuned after observing
results.

A positive observed operational-evaluator mean is insufficient. If the
evaluator discrepancy is large or poorly calibrated, the candidate may
remain inconclusive even when the operational score is high. Likewise,
failure of one protected test blocks acceptance even if the primary
objective improves substantially. Governance checks are evaluated
separately and cannot be offset by a numerical score.

## Abstract

Global supply chains rely on the interactions of thousands of
heterogeneous agents, posing significant challenges for optimization.
While traditional optimization often assumes homogeneity to ensure
tractability, Reinforcement Learning struggles with sample inefficiency
when scaling to high-dimensional state spaces. We propose a
Differentiable Agent-Based Model ($`\partial`$ABM) for multi-echelon
inventory management. By implementing stochastic dynamics via
differentiable tensor operations, we enable direct gradient propagation
through the simulation. We validate the framework by recovering
theoretical Newsvendor optima without supervision. Crucially, we
demonstrate “Mass Personalization” by optimizing 10,000 distinct agent
policies simultaneously in seconds, achieving a 53% cost reduction
compared to scalar baselines. Furthermore, we utilize gradient
sensitivity analysis to generate causal heatmaps, accurately recovering
inherent lead-time delays from the computation graph. This approach
bridges the gap between simulation and optimization, enabling the
efficient training of self-correcting Digital Twins.

Keywords: differentiable simulation, agent-based modeling, supply chain
optimization, multi-echelon supply chain, explainable AI.

## 1 Introduction

Global supply chains are complex adaptive systems \[[18](#bib.bib1)\],
shaped by the interactions of thousands of heterogeneous actors spanning
retailers, wholesalers, and manufacturers. Small perturbations in
downstream demand can propagate nonlinearly upstream, amplifying into
the well-known Bullwhip Effect \[[10](#bib.bib2)\]. The resulting
volatility forces firms to trade off excess inventory against costly
stockouts, leading to systemic inefficiencies rather than isolated local
failures \[[11](#bib.bib19)\].

The central challenge in supply chain management lies not in optimizing
individual agents, but in managing their interactions. Classical
optimization methods provide analytically optimal policies under
restrictive assumptions such as stationary demand and simple network
structures \[[16](#bib.bib18)\]. These assumptions are routinely
violated in practice, where agents react strategically to distorted
demand signals and endogenous delays \[[9](#bib.bib3)\]. While
discrete-event simulations can faithfully reproduce these dynamics, they
function as black boxes: inputs generate outputs without exposing the
causal pathways required for efficient optimization. As a result, tuning
system parameters typically relies on trial-and-error methods such as
Reinforcement Learning (RL) or evolutionary search \[[19](#bib.bib4)\],
which are computationally expensive and scale poorly.

We propose a Differentiable Agent-Based Modeling ($`\partial`$ABM)
framework that reformulates multi-echelon supply chains as
differentiable computation graphs. By replacing discrete control logic
with smooth, differentiable relaxations, the entire simulation becomes
amenable to gradient-based optimization. Agents are represented as
nodes, goods as tensors, and inventory dynamics as differentiable flow
equations. This structure preserves the gradient of the loss function
across time, enabling direct optimization of agent policies via
backpropagation \[[2](#bib.bib5)\].

Rather than inferring optimal policies through stochastic exploration,
$`\partial`$ABM computes exact gradients of system-wide cost with
respect to each agent’s decision parameters. Stockouts or overages at
any time step propagate gradient signals upstream through the simulation
history, allowing the network to self-correct. This approach transforms
supply chain optimization from a black-box search problem into a
transparent, scalable learning process. This paper makes three
contributions: (i) a fully vectorized, GPU-accelerated multi-echelon
inventory simulator; (ii) an empirical demonstration of simultaneous
optimization of 10,000 heterogeneous agent policies, achieving
substantial cost reductions over scalar baselines; and (iii) a
gradient-based interpretability methodology that recovers lead-time
delays and causal propagation paths directly from the computation graph.
Together, these contributions bridge simulation and optimization,
enabling self-tuning Digital Twins for large-scale supply chains.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews
the existing literature, contrasting the structural rigidity of
traditional optimization with the sample inefficiency of deep RL.
Section 3 details the methodological framework, formally defining the
vectorized state space, differentiable relaxations, and the
gradient-based optimization loop. Section 4 presents the empirical
evaluation, demonstrating the framework’s validity against theoretical
baselines, its scalability and its capacity for causal discovery.
Section 5 discusses the implications of the results, analyzing the
trade-offs between interpretability and computational complexity.
Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper and outlines directions for
future research.

## 2 Related Work

The optimization of multi-echelon supply chains has been a central focus
of industrial engineering for over six decades. The literature is
divided into distinct methodological traditions.

### 2.1 Traditional Optimization: The Analytical Foundation

Traditional optimization forms the analytical backbone of inventory
theory, seeking provably optimal control policies under explicit
structural assumptions. Seminal work by Clark and Scarf
\[[3](#bib.bib6)\] established decomposition principles for serial
multi-echelon systems, enabling stage-wise optimization via base-stock
$`(s,S)`$ policies. Subsequent refinements by Simchi-Levi and Zhao
\[[13](#bib.bib7)\] and Axsäter \[[1](#bib.bib8)\] formalized stochastic
service models assuming known demand distributions, allowing closed-form
cost evaluation.

However, analytical tractability deteriorates rapidly as supply chains
depart from serial structures. General network topologies introduce
non-convex dependencies and feedback loops that defeat dynamic
programming. To address this, the Guaranteed Service Model (GSM)
\[[7](#bib.bib9)\] replaces stochastic dynamics with deterministic
service guarantees, yielding solvable MINLP formulations at the cost of
reduced fidelity. Such simplifications suppress tail risks,
state-dependent lead times, and crisis dynamics critical to real-world
performance.

A fundamental limitation of classical OR models is the assumption of
agent homogeneity. To control complexity, agents at the same echelon are
typically assigned identical parameters, producing symmetric policies
optimized for the average case. Real supply chains are inherently
heterogeneous: demand volatility, risk tolerance, and operating
conditions vary widely across locations. Aggregating these differences
induces systematic inefficiencies, as a single policy cannot
simultaneously serve high- and low-variance environments. Scaling
analytical optimization to thousands of distinct agents remains
computationally infeasible, motivating a shift toward computational
learning-based approaches.

### 2.2 Reinforcement Learning in Supply Chains

RL reframes inventory control as a Markov Decision Process (MDP),
enabling agents to learn policies directly from interaction data. Early
value-based methods, such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN), struggled with the
continuous nature of ordering decisions, requiring discretization of the
action space and leading to inefficiencies \[[19](#bib.bib4)\]. This
limitation motivated a shift toward policy-gradient methods,
particularly Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \[[12](#bib.bib12)\],
which directly handle continuous actions and demonstrate strong
empirical performance in non-stationary or disrupted environments where
static OR policies fail \[[15](#bib.bib16)\].

Despite these advances, RL remains fundamentally sample-inefficient
\[[4](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib15)\]. The simulator is treated as a
black box, providing scalar rewards without exposing derivatives.
Learning relies on stochastic exploration to approximate gradients,
often requiring millions of episodes. Delayed rewards caused by lead
times further exacerbate the credit assignment problem, obscuring causal
links between actions and outcomes.

Scalability poses an even greater challenge \[[5](#bib.bib14)\].
Optimizing thousands of heterogeneous agents induces an exponentially
large joint action space, rendering standard multi-agent RL intractable.
Parameter sharing is commonly employed to restore tractability, but at
the cost of reintroducing the homogeneity assumption. Consequently, RL
faces a structural trade-off between scalability and personalization
that limits its applicability to large-scale supply chains.

### 2.3 Differentiable Physics and Simulation

Differentiable simulation has emerged as a third paradigm that combines
the structural clarity of analytical models with the learnability of
gradient-based methods. Originating in robotics and graphics,
differentiable physics engines such as Brax \[[6](#bib.bib10)\] and
DiffTaichi \[[8](#bib.bib11)\] reformulate physical interactions as
differentiable tensor operations, enabling direct backpropagation
through time.

We argue that supply chains obey analogous physical principles.
Inventory dynamics follow conservation laws, material flows propagate
through directed networks, and operational constraints act as energy
barriers in the loss landscape. By relaxing discrete logic into smooth
penalty functions, supply chain simulations become fully differentiable
systems rather than black boxes.

This perspective transforms economic simulation into a white-box
optimization problem. Cost gradients can be traced from downstream
failures back through upstream decisions, allowing heterogeneous
policies to be optimized simultaneously. Differentiable Agent-Based
Modeling thus resolves the scalability–heterogeneity impasse that
constrains both classical optimization and reinforcement learning.

## 3 Methodology: The Differentiable Chain

We propose a framework for Differentiable Agent-Based Modeling
($`\partial`$ABM). Unlike traditional discrete-event simulations, which
rely on queue-based logic, we formulate the supply chain as a
discrete-time dynamical system implemented entirely via tensor
operations. This architecture allows the simulation to serve as a
computational graph, enabling the direct backpropagation of gradients
from objective functions to policy parameters.

### 3.1 System Architecture

We consider a system of $`N`$ independent supply chains operating in
parallel. This setup mimics a large-scale economy where thousands of
distinct stock-keeping units (SKUs) or geographic regions operate
simultaneously under varying environmental conditions.

Each individual supply chain follows a serial Multi-Echelon topology
with three distinct tiers (agents):

1.  1.  The Factory ($`F`$): The upstream source. It assumes infinite raw
        material availability but operates under production constraints. It
        fulfills orders from the Wholesaler.
2.  2.  The Wholesaler ($`W`$): The mid-stream distributor. It holds
        inventory to buffer against volatility between the Factory and
        Retailer. It fulfills orders from the Retailer.
3.  3.  The Retailer ($`R`$): The downstream node. It faces stochastic
        consumer demand directly. Its primary objective is to satisfy this
        demand while minimizing holding costs.

The flow of goods is unidirectional downstream ($`F\to W\to R`$), while
the flow of information (orders) is unidirectional upstream
($`R\to W\to F`$).

#### 3.1.1 Vectorized State Space

To leverage GPU acceleration and automatic differentiation, we abandon
the Object-Oriented Programming (OOP) paradigm typical of simulation
software (e.g., AnyLogic). Instead, we adopt a Data-Oriented Design. The
state of the entire economy at timestep $`t`$ is represented not by
objects, but by a set of global tensors.

Let $`N`$ be the number of environments (e.g., $`N=10,000`$). The global
state $`\mathcal{S}_{t}`$ is defined by the following tensor components:

- •
  Inventory Tensor ($`\mathbf{I}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}`$): A
  matrix representing the on-hand stock for all agents. The element
  $`I_{t}^{(i,k)}`$ denotes the inventory level of the $`i`$-th supply
  chain at echelon $`k\in\{F,W,R\}`$.
- •
  Demand Tensor ($`\mathbf{D}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}`$): A vector
  representing the realized consumer demand for each retailer at time
  $`t`$. While demand is stochastic, the realization at any specific
  step is a concrete tensor value used to calculate gradients.
- •
  Order Tensor ($`\mathbf{O}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}`$): A matrix
  recording the replenishment decisions made by each agent.
  $`O_{t}^{(i,R)}`$ is the order placed by the $`i`$-th Retailer to the
  $`i`$-th Wholesaler.
- •
  Pipeline Tensor ($`\mathbf{P}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}`$):
  Represents goods currently in transit between echelons. In a
  differentiable framework, lead times are modeled as delay buffers. For
  a lead time of $`L`$, the pipeline tensor stores the history of
  shipments to be released at $`t+L`$.

#### 3.1.2 Heterogeneity via Parameter Tensors

A core contribution of this work is the handling of heterogeneity. In
traditional models, policy parameters (such as safety stock targets) are
scalars shared across all agents. In our architecture, these parameters
are elevated to tensors.

We define the Policy Parameter Tensor
$`\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}`$. Each entry $`\theta_{i,k}`$
represents the unique target inventory level for the $`k`$-th agent in
the $`i`$-th supply chain. Because $`\Theta`$ is a tensor in the
computation graph, we can compute the partial derivative of the global
loss with respect to every individual parameter simultaneously:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\nabla_{\Theta}\mathcal{L}=\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_{1,F}},\dots,\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_{N,R}}\right]
``` |  | (1) |

This formulation transforms the "Mass Personalization" problem from a
combinatorial search into a standard high-dimensional optimization task.

### 3.2 Differentiable Relaxations

A fundamental barrier to optimizing simulations via gradient descent is
the presence of discrete logic. Traditional supply chain models rely
heavily on discontinuous operations, such as "if-then-else" statements
and boolean flags. These operations break the computation graph. A
boolean check has a derivative of zero almost everywhere and is
undefined at the threshold. Consequently, a gradient signal cannot
propagate through a discrete decision to update the parameters that
caused it.

To overcome this, we employ Differentiable Relaxations. We replace
discontinuous logical control structures with continuous, differentiable
approximations. This ensures that the mapping from policy parameters to
the objective function remains differentiable almost everywhere.

#### 3.2.1 Smooth Approximations of Control Logic

The most common discrete structure in inventory management is the
reorder decision. A standard Base Stock policy dictates:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{Order}_{t}=\begin{cases}\theta-I_{t}&\text{if }I_{t}<\theta\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
``` |  | (2) |

In this form, the gradient with respect to $`\theta`$ is lost if the
condition is not met, and the discontinuity at $`I_{t}=\theta`$ can
cause optimization instability. We relax this using the Rectified Linear
Unit (ReLU), a standard activation function in deep learning:

|     |                                                   |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbf{O}_{t}=\text{ReLU}(\Theta-\mathbf{I}_{t})
       ```                                                |     | (3) |

This formulation preserves the physical constraint that orders cannot be
negative. Crucially, it provides a gradient of 1 when ordering is
active, allowing the optimizer to adjust the target $`\Theta`$ based on
downstream outcomes.

Similarly, physical constraints such as shipping limits are
traditionally modeled with conditional logic (e.g., "ship the order
amount unless inventory is insufficient"). We replace this with the
differentiable minimum operator:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{Shipment}_{A\to B}=\min(\text{Inventory}_{A},\text{Order}_{B})
``` |  | (4) |

While $`\min(a,b)`$ is non-smooth at $`a=b`$, it provides informative
subgradients. If inventory is the limiting factor, the gradient flows to
the upstream stock. If the order size is the limiting factor, the
gradient flows to the downstream policy. This allows the system to
assign blame correctly during stockouts.

#### 3.2.2 The Dynamics Module: Conservation of Mass

Once decisions are made via relaxed policies, the state of the system
evolves according to the laws of physics. In a supply chain, this is the
conservation of mass. Unlike neural network layers which involve matrix
multiplications and non-linearities, the transition dynamics of a supply
chain are linear tensor operations.

We implement the Dynamics Module as a set of additive update equations.
Because addition and subtraction are linear operators, they are
perfectly “transparent” to gradients. This allows the error signal to
flow backward through time (Backpropagation Through Time) without
vanishing or exploding.

The state update for the Wholesaler echelon ($`W`$) at time $`t+1`$ is
defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{I}_{t+1}^{W}=\mathbf{I}_{t}^{W}+\underbrace{\mathbf{S}_{t}^{F\to W}}_{\text{Inflow from Factory}}-\underbrace{\mathbf{S}_{t}^{W\to R}}_{\text{Outflow to Retailer}}
``` |  | (5) |

Where $`\mathbf{S}_{t}`$ represents the shipments calculated using the
differentiable minimum function described above.

Similar conservation equations govern the Factory (production minus
shipments) and the Retailer (shipments minus consumption). By expressing
the entire physics engine as a sequence of tensor additions, we ensure
that the causal link between a production decision at $`t=0`$ and a
stockout cost at $`t=T`$ is mathematically preserved in the computation
graph.

### 3.3 The Optimization Loop

The final component of the framework is the optimization loop. In
traditional simulations, optimization is an external process that wraps
around the model. In a differentiable framework, optimization is
intrinsic to the model’s execution. We treat the simulation trajectory
as the forward pass of a recurrent neural network and the policy update
as the backward pass.

#### 3.3.1 The Differentiable Loss Function

The objective of supply chain management is to minimize total cost. This
cost is a trade-off between two competing forces: the cost of carrying
inventory and the cost of failing to meet demand. We formulate this as a
differentiable loss function $`\mathcal{L}`$ accumulated over the
simulation horizon.

Let $`\mathbf{I}_{t}^{(i,R)}`$ be the inventory level of the $`i`$-th
Retailer at time $`t`$. We define the loss for a single step as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\ell_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\alpha\cdot\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{I}_{t}^{(i,R)})+\beta\cdot\text{ReLU}(-\mathbf{I}_{t}^{(i,R)})\right)
``` |  | (6) |

Here, $`\alpha`$ represents the unit holding cost, and $`\beta`$
represents the unit shortage (or backlog) penalty.

- •
  The term $`\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{I}_{t})`$ isolates positive inventory,
  penalizing overstocking.
- •
  The term $`\text{ReLU}(-\mathbf{I}_{t})`$ isolates negative inventory
  (backlog), penalizing stockouts.

Typically, $`\beta\gg\alpha`$ (e.g., $`10:1`$), reflecting the high cost
of lost revenue compared to storage. By using the ReLU function, we
ensure that the loss surface is continuous and convex with respect to
the inventory state, providing non-zero gradients even when the system
is far from the optimal operating point.

#### 3.3.2 Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT)

To optimize the policy parameters $`\Theta`$, we employ Backpropagation
Through Time (BPTT). This is the standard training algorithm for
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs).

The simulation runs for a fixed horizon of $`T`$ time steps (e.g.,
$`T=50`$ weeks). During the forward pass, the system records the
computation graph, tracking how every tensor operation (ordering,
shipping, consumption) contributes to the state at the next step.

Once the total cumulative loss $`\mathcal{L}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}`$ is
computed, we execute the backward pass. The gradient of the loss with
respect to the policy parameters is calculated via the chain rule:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\Theta}\mathcal{L}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial\ell_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{I}_{t}}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{I}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{O}_{t-L}}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{O}_{t-L}}{\partial\Theta}
``` |  | (7) |

This equation captures the causal dependencies of the system.

- •
  $`\frac{\partial\ell_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{I}_{t}}`$ measures the
  immediate pain of the current inventory state.
- •
  $`\frac{\partial\mathbf{I}_{t}}{\partial\mathbf{O}_{t-L}}`$ captures
  the physical delay. It indicates how an order placed $`L`$ steps ago
  affected the current inventory.
- •
  $`\frac{\partial\mathbf{O}_{t-L}}{\partial\Theta}`$ captures the
  policy sensitivity. It indicates how a change in the target parameter
  would have altered that order.

By summing these gradients over the entire history, the algorithm
identifies parameter updates that minimize cost over the long term,
rather than just greedily at the current step. We update the parameters
using the Adam optimizer, which adapts the learning rate for each of the
10,000 agents individually based on the curvature of their specific loss
landscape.

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate the performance of the proposed differentiable framework
through three distinct experiments. These tests are designed to assess
validity, scalability, and interpretability. We begin by benchmarking
the model against established theoretical baselines to ensure the
gradient-based updates converge to mathematically optimal solutions.

### 4.1 Experiment I: Validation and Disentanglement

The first experiment tests the fundamental hypothesis of differentiable
simulation: can the system automatically identify and optimize for
heterogeneous environmental conditions without explicit supervision?

Setup. We construct a simulation with $`N=1,000`$ independent retailers.
We divide these agents into two hidden subpopulations:

- •
  Group A (Low Demand): 500 agents with mean demand $`\mu_{A}=5`$.
- •
  Group B (High Demand): 500 agents with mean demand $`\mu_{B}=15`$.

Crucially, the optimization algorithm is not given these labels. We
initialize the policy parameter (the inventory target $`\theta`$) to an
identical value of $`12.0`$ for all 1,000 agents. This represents a
naive “one-size-fits-all” policy.

We set the unit holding cost to $`\$1`$ and the unit shortage penalty to
$`\$10`$. Under standard inventory theory (the Newsvendor model), the
optimal target inventory is defined by the critical fractile
$`CR=\frac{p}{p+h}=\frac{10}{11}\approx 0.91`$. The theoretical optimum
is the 91st percentile of the demand distribution.

Results. We ran the optimization loop for 25 epochs. Figure 1
illustrates the trajectory of the learned parameters.

Figure 1: Automatic Policy Disentanglement. Starting from an identical
initialization ($`\theta=12`$), the gradient descent algorithm
automatically drives the policies of the two groups in opposite
directions. Group A (Blue) converges downward to the low-demand optimum,
while Group B (Red) converges upward to the high-demand optimum. The
model successfully disentangles the population based solely on the cost
gradients flowing from the environment.

The results demonstrate a rapid and automatic disentanglement of the
agent policies.

- •
  Group A: For the low-demand agents, the initial target of 12.0
  resulted in excessive holding costs. The computed gradient with
  respect to inventory was positive, pushing the parameter $`\theta`$
  downward. The policy settled near $`7.8`$, closely matching the
  theoretical Newsvendor optimum of $`\approx 7.7`$.
- •
  Group B: For the high-demand agents, the initial target of 12.0 caused
  frequent stockouts. The gradient signal was dominated by the shortage
  penalty, pushing $`\theta`$ upward. The policy converged near
  $`20.1`$.

It is notable that Group B settled slightly higher than the single-stage
theoretical optimum ($`\approx 17.7`$). This deviation is expected in a
multi-echelon setting. Unlike the static Newsvendor equation, the
simulation captures the dynamic risk of upstream delays. The gradient
descent “learned” to hold a slightly larger safety buffer to account for
the time-dependent variance of replenishment, a nuance that static
formulas often miss.

This experiment confirms that the differentiable simulation respects the
underlying economic physics. It successfully recovers distinct, optimal
policies for heterogeneous actors starting from a blank slate.

### 4.2 Experiment II: The Scale and Quality Trade-off

While Experiment I validated the correctness of the gradients,
Experiment II addresses the core motivation of this work: solving the
heterogeneity gap at scale. In traditional operations management,
optimizing thousands of unique parameters is considered computationally
intractable. Consequently, firms rely on scalar policies—applying a
single average set of parameters to large clusters of agents.

This experiment tests the capability of differentiable simulation to
optimize a distinct policy for every single agent in a massive
population, and quantifies the economic value of doing so.

Setup. We initialize a population of $`N=10,000`$ independent retailers.
To simulate extreme environmental diversity, we assign demand profiles
using a linear gradient. Agent 0 faces a mean demand of $`\mu=5.0`$,
while Agent 9,999 faces a mean demand of $`\mu=25.0`$, with all
intermediate agents interpolated linearly between these extremes.

We compare two optimization regimes:

- •
  Scalar Baseline (The “Corporate Mandate”): We constrain the optimizer
  to learn a single scalar parameter $`\theta_{global}`$ applied to all
  10,000 agents. This represents the standard centralized approach.
- •
  Heterogeneous Optimization (Mass Personalization): We allow the
  optimizer to learn a tensor $`\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{10000}`$, giving
  every agent a unique, independent policy parameter.

Results. The results reveal a stark trade-off between policy granularity
and system performance. Figure 2 illustrates the convergence behavior
and the final policy distribution.

Figure 2: Mass Personalization at Scale. (Left) The convergence curves
show the Scalar policy (Gray) plateauing at a high cost, while the
Heterogeneous policy (Blue) continues to improve, achieving a 53%
reduction in total loss. (Right) The policy distribution explains the
performance gap. The Scalar policy settles for a "least worst" average
($`\approx 24.2`$), largely overstocking low-demand agents to prevent
costly stockouts at the high end. In contrast, the Heterogeneous policy
learns a perfect linear mapping, tailoring inventory targets to the
specific demand mean of each individual agent.

Economic Efficiency: The Scalar baseline converged to a final loss of
$`102.9`$. Because the penalty for stockouts ($`\$10`$) significantly
outweighs the cost of holding ($`\$1`$), the global policy was forced to
cater to the highest-demand agents to avoid catastrophic shortages. The
resulting target ($`\theta\approx 24.2`$) effectively served the
high-demand tail but forced the thousands of low-demand agents to hold
massive amounts of unnecessary safety stock.

In contrast, the Heterogeneous optimization converged to a loss of
$`47.9`$, representing a 53.3% cost reduction. As shown in the scatter
plot (Figure 2, Right), the differentiable simulation automatically
learned a bespoke policy for each of the 10,000 agents, forming a
diagonal line that perfectly tracks the environmental demand gradient.

Computational Efficiency: Crucially, this massive increase in parameter
count incurred negligible computational overhead. The wall-clock time
per training step was $`0.10`$ seconds for the Scalar model and $`0.08`$
seconds for the Heterogeneous model.

This counter-intuitive result—that optimizing 10,000 parameters is as
fast as optimizing one—is due to the vectorized nature of the
simulation. In a GPU-accelerated environment, element-wise tensor
operations (e.g., updating 10,000 weights via Adam) are effectively
parallelized. This demonstrates that the “curse of dimensionality” that
plagues black-box optimization does not apply to differentiable
agent-based models, unlocking the potential for mass personalization in
global supply chains.

### 4.3 Experiment III: Causal Interpretability

A critical advantage of differentiable simulation over model-free RL is
explainability. An RL agent provides a policy, but it cannot explain why
a specific action was taken. In contrast, a differentiable engine allows
us to inspect the causal dependencies of the system directly via
gradient sensitivity analysis.

In this experiment, we test whether the computation graph correctly
captures the temporal topology of the supply network. We ask: Does the
model understand the “Arrow of Time” and the physical delays inherent in
shipping goods?

Setup. We simulate a single supply chain for $`T=30`$ time steps. The
system is initialized in a fragile state with minimal inventory
($`I_{0}=5`$), ensuring that flow constraints are binding.

- •
  The Shock: We introduce a catastrophic demand spike at $`t=15`$,
  increasing demand from $`10`$ units to $`50`$ units.
- •
  The Probe: Instead of optimizing a policy, we treat the Factory’s
  production schedule $`\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{30}`$ as a leaf
  variable.
- •
  The Metric: We calculate the total “Unmet Demand” (Lost Sales) over
  the entire episode and compute its gradient with respect to the
  production schedule: $`\nabla_{\mathbf{P}}\mathcal{L}`$.

The magnitude of this gradient,
$`|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial P_{t}}|`$, represents the Impact
Metric. It quantifies how much an additional unit of production at time
$`t`$ would have reduced the shortage caused by the shock at $`t=15`$.

Results. The temporal sensitivity plot (Figure 3) effectively maps the
causal physics of the supply chain.

Figure 3: The Resilience Horizon. We induce a demand shock at $`t=15`$
(Red Dashed Line). The curve shows the gradient magnitude, indicating
the impact of factory production at time $`t`$ on minimizing the
eventual shortage. The gradient remains high for all steps preceding the
shock, confirming the model respects conservation of mass (inventory
carryover). The decay after $`t=15`$ reveals the “Resilience
Horizon”—the duration required for the system to recover from the
deficit.

The analysis reveals three distinct temporal regimes that validate the
model’s structural fidelity:

1.  1.
    The Memory Effect ($`t<13`$): The gradient is consistently high
    ($`\approx 1.0`$) for early time steps. This confirms that the
    simulation correctly models Inventory Conservation. A unit produced
    at $`t=0`$ is stored, shipped, and carried forward to eventually
    mitigate the shortage at $`t=15`$. The model understands that early
    preparation is as valuable as just-in-time production.
2.  2.
    The Topology Recovery ($`t\approx 13`$): The gradient magnitude
    begins to roll off exactly two steps before the crash. This
    accurately recovers the Network Topology. In our 3-tier system, it
    takes one step to ship from Factory to Wholesaler, and one step from
    Wholesaler to Retailer. The computation graph implicitly learned
    that production after $`t=13`$ arrives too late to fully satisfy the
    initial spike at $`t=15`$.
3.  3.
    The Resilience Horizon ($`t=15\to 28`$): Crucially, the gradient
    remains positive after the shock occurs. While a standard prediction
    model might assume the event is “over,” the differentiable
    simulation captures the lingering aftershocks. The massive spike
    drained the system’s inventory, creating a deficit that persisted
    for 13 steps. The non-zero gradient in this region indicates that
    extra production at $`t=20`$ still helps reduce the accumulated
    backlog. This visualizes the system’s recovery time, or resilience
    horizon.

By $`t=29`$, the gradient hits zero. The system has stabilized, and the
backlog is cleared. Production beyond this point yields no reduction in
lost sales. This experiment proves that the differentiable framework
does not merely curve-fit; it encodes the fundamental laws of lead time,
storage, and flow.

## 5 Discussion

The results presented in the previous section demonstrate that
Differentiable ABM ($`\partial`$ABM) can solve supply chain optimization
problems that are intractable for traditional methods. However, this
capability introduces new trade-offs regarding computational resources
and modeling fidelity. Here, we analyze the structural implications of
moving from black-box simulation to differentiable physics.

Interpretability vs. Black Box Control. A pervasive criticism of DRL in
industrial applications is the "Black Box" nature of the learned
policies. An RL agent trained via PPO may achieve high rewards, but it
provides no mechanism to explain why a specific action was taken. The
learning signal is a scalar reward value, which aggregates all
downstream complexities into a single number. This loss of information
makes it difficult for operators to trust the system, as they cannot
distinguish between a policy that learned a robust causal law and one
that exploited a simulation artifact.

In contrast, the differentiable framework generates a dense signal: the
gradient vector. As demonstrated in Experiment III, the gradient
$`\nabla_{\text{Action}}\mathcal{L}`$ serves as a Sensitivity Map. It
quantifies the causal weight of every decision. By tracing the gradient
magnitude backwards through time, we recovered the inherent lead-time
structure of the network without having to code it explicitly into the
analysis.

This shifts the role of the simulation from a training ground to a
diagnostic tool. In a differentiable supply chain, a "stockout event" is
not just a penalty; it is a differentiable node that "blames" specific
upstream parameters. This allows for root-cause analysis that is
mathematically derived from the system’s equations of motion, providing
a level of transparency that model-free methods cannot replicate.

Computational Efficiency: The Memory-Sample Trade-off. While
$`\partial`$ABM offers superior sample efficiency, it incurs a
significant cost in memory complexity.

- •
  Sample Efficiency: Because the gradient provides a precise direction
  of steepest descent, the optimizer converges in very few iterations
  (e.g., 30 epochs in Experiment I). Derivative-free methods like
  Evolution Strategies or PPO must estimate this direction by sampling
  thousands of noisy perturbations. Consequently, $`\partial`$ABM is
  orders of magnitude more sample-efficient.
- •
  Memory Complexity: The constraint on $`\partial`$ABM is GPU memory
  (VRAM). To compute the gradient via Backpropagation Through Time
  (BPTT), the system must store the entire computational graph of the
  simulation episode. The memory requirement scales linearly with time
  $`O(T\times N)`$.

For a simulation with $`N=10,000`$ agents and $`T=50`$ steps, this graph
fits comfortably on a modern GPU. However, for extremely long horizons
(e.g., $`T=10,000`$ steps), the memory requirements would exceed
available hardware capacity. In contrast, RL agents only need to store a
small batch of transitions, making them more memory-efficient for
infinite-horizon problems. Future work could address this limitation in
$`\partial`$ABM using gradient checkpointing or implicit differentiation
techniques, which trade computation time for reduced memory usage.

Limitations: The Discretization Gap. The validity of the differentiable
simulation relies on the quality of the "relaxations" described in
Section 3.2. We replaced discrete logic (if-then-else) with smooth
approximations (ReLU, SoftMin). While effective for optimization, this
introduces a "Sim-to-Real" gap. Also, real-world supply chains are
governed by strictly discrete constraints. For example, shipping
logistics often involve integer constraints: a truck has a fixed
capacity, and one cannot ship 1.5 trucks. A smoothed model might
recommend shipping 1.5 units to minimize a loss function, whereas the
physical reality forces a choice between 1 or 2. Additionally, when the
optimal solution lies exactly on a sharp discontinuity (e.g., a
step-function cost), the smoothed gradient may provide misleading
guidance or vanish. While this paper demonstrates robustness in
inventory flow (which is continuous at scale), the framework may require
hybrid approaches (combining gradients with discrete solvers) to handle
combinatorial constraints like vehicle routing or facility location.

## 6 Conclusion

This paper introduced a Differentiable ABM framework for multi-echelon
supply chains that unifies simulation fidelity with gradient-based
optimization. By reformulating discrete inventory dynamics as a
differentiable computation graph, we enabled direct policy optimization
via backpropagation rather than trial-and-error search. Empirical
results demonstrate that the framework recovers known theoretical optima
without supervision and scales to 10,000 heterogeneous agents, achieving
a 53% cost reduction over scalar policies while revealing causal
topology, lead-time delays, and resilience horizons through gradient
sensitivity analysis. While this study focused on optimizing control
parameters within fixed topologies, future work can extend the framework
toward gradient-assisted calibration of Digital Twins using real
transaction data and toward graph neural policies that enable
decentralized, topology-aware coordination \[[17](#bib.bib17)\].
Treating supply chains as differentiable systems provides a principled
path toward autonomous, self-correcting logistics networks.
````

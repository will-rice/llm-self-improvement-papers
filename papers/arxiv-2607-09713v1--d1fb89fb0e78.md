###### Abstract

A key step toward autonomous industrial operation is the ability to
create and reconfigure control policies from natural-language
requirement specifications, with minimal or no manual redesign. In this
setting, policy generation by AI agents can be a credible path when
paired with a plant-aware validator (e.g., a digital twin) that can
check generated candidate actions before execution. However, practical
deployment is constrained by inference latency and compute footprint:
large cloud-based models are often too slow, opaque, or data-sensitive
for edge closed-loop use. This work investigates whether a compact Small
Language Model (SLM) can be retrained for control reasoning and embedded
in a validator-guided correction loop. We use a Qwen2.5-1.5B model
aligned via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), combined with (i)
an action agent, (ii) a symbolic/digital-twin-style validation layer,
and (iii) a reprompting agent that iteratively steers outputs toward
valid actions. In randomized thermal-control simulations (30 experiments
with 500 steps each), the framework achieves 91.5% average
action-alignment accuracy (86.3%–100% across cases) at 3.84 s mean
inference latency. Under symbolic re-mapping, it maintains a 95%
in-range rate, indicating robust physical regulation despite reduced
token-level agreement. These results support SLM+validator architectures
as a practical path toward reconfigurable autonomous control at the
edge.

Keywords: Autonomous systems, Small language models, Edge AI, Generative
AI

## I Introduction

A central requirement for autonomous industrial operation is the ability
to _create and reconfigure control policies_ from high-level,
natural-language requirement specifications with minimal manual
redesign. This is particularly important in environments where
objectives and constraints evolve over time (e.g., energy priorities,
safety envelopes, and operating policies). In this setting, policy
generation by AI agents can be a credible path when paired with a
plant-aware validator (e.g., a digital twin) that can check generated
candidate actions before execution \[[1](#bib.bib19)\].

Conventional industrial control architectures are highly effective for
deterministic numerical regulation, but they are less flexible when
symbolic operating intent must be incorporated quickly. Translating new
expert directives into deployed logic typically requires manual
re-engineering of rules, supervisory layers, or interlocks, which slows
reconfiguration and increases engineering overhead. This creates a
persistent gap between high-level operational intent and low-level
control execution.

Language models provide a promising interface for closing this gap
because they can parse and operationalize natural-language instructions
and support iterative self-correction during inference \[[2](#bib.bib9),
[3](#bib.bib14)\]. However, deploying cloud-hosted Large Language Models
(LLMs) in closed-loop industrial workflows remains challenging: API
dependence introduces variable end-to-end latency, while external data
transfer raises governance and sovereignty concerns for sensitive
operational telemetry \[[4](#bib.bib17), [5](#bib.bib16)\]. These issues
are especially limiting for edge and offline autonomy.

This work investigates whether a compact Small Language Model (SLM) can
be trained to perform control-oriented reasoning and embedded into a
validator-guided correction loop. We develop a local architecture built
around Qwen2.5-1.5B \[[6](#bib.bib18)\], where policy behavior is
aligned using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
\[[7](#bib.bib7)\]. A key advantage of this RL-based alignment is that
the model autonomously generates its own chains-of-thought (CoT). While
a brief warm-up distillation is employed, it serves strictly for format
alignment rather than task-specific memorization. Furthermore, the
framework operates in a zero-shot capacity regarding corrective
interactions; although GRPO is utilized solely to enhance the agent’s
numerical and logical reasoning, the Action Agent is never exposed to
the diverse reprompting hints during training. Nevertheless, it
demonstrates an emergent ability to dynamically update its decisions
based on such corrective feedback. The controller is implemented as
three cooperating components: (i) an Action Agent that proposes control
moves, (ii) a symbolic/digital-twin-style validator that checks action
validity, and (iii) a Reprompt Agent that provides corrective guidance
when validation fails. This design targets a practical balance between
reasoning capability, latency, and edge deployability.

Contributions: The main contributions are threefold: (1) Rule-aligned
SLM policy learning, providing a pipeline that aligns a 1.5B SLM with
thermal control logic using GRPO; (2) a zero-shot validator-guided
inference loop that enables compact SLMs to exhibit emergent correction
capabilities without explicit corrective training; and (3) an empirical
evaluation demonstrating strong physical regulation (95% IRR) under
interface perturbations in randomized simulations.

## II Related Work

### II-A LLM-driven Autonomous Control and its Advantages

Traditional automation systems primarily rely on rigid, hard-coded logic
to manage industrial processes. While these systems are highly reliable
for numerical regulation, they struggle to integrate complex, symbolic
expert intents. Recent research has shifted toward LLM-based Autonomous
Agents, where models act as "reasoning engines" to bridge this gap. This
paradigm shift has demonstrated significant potential in laboratory
discovery \[[8](#bib.bib4)\], digital workflows \[[9](#bib.bib1)\], and
robotic reward engineering \[[10](#bib.bib8)\].

### II-B Enhancing Numerical Reasoning in Language Models

Thermal management requires precise numerical comparison and
threshold-based logic, areas where standard LLMs often struggle with
"stochastic drift" or calculation errors \[[11](#bib.bib10)\]. Current
strategies to mitigate this include:

Reinforcement Learning (RL): Techniques like PPO and particularly Group
Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) \[[7](#bib.bib7), [12](#bib.bib12)\]
have emerged to align model outputs with verifiable rewards, reinforcing
the logical consistency behind numerical decisions.

Self-Correction and Recursive Reasoning: To stabilize control outputs,
recent research employs external validation loops and reprompting agents
\[[13](#bib.bib20), [14](#bib.bib2)\]. However, purely external designs
often lack the intrinsic logical depth required for real-time stability.
Frameworks like the Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) \[[15](#bib.bib11)\]
address this by enabling models to iteratively refine their own
reasoning chains (Chain-of-Thought) based on successful outcomes. This
allows the model to internalize complex logic during training—an
evolution we extend by using GRPO to foster the "Aha-moment"
\[[7](#bib.bib7)\] within small-scale architectures, ensuring they
reason correctly without the overhead of heavy external supervision.

Tool Utilization: Although agents can call external calculators
\[[16](#bib.bib5), [17](#bib.bib3)\], the resulting communication
latency and instability make this approach not suitable for all
problems.

### II-C Small Language Models (SLMs) for Edge Deployment

While Large Language Models (LLMs) possess superior reasoning, their
prohibitive inference latency and deployment costs hinder real-time
industrial integration. Small Language Models (SLMs), when properly
optimized, are being explored to achieve functional performance in
specialized tasks while offering the potential for local execution in
thermal control \[[18](#bib.bib6)\]. Recent studies emphasize using SLMs
as "control-specific" agents that internalize expert experience to
replace massive general-purpose architectures \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. This
paper extends this direction by investigating the feasibility of
addressing the reasoning gap in 1.5B-parameter SLMs through GRPO-based
heuristic internalization.

## III Methodology

Our architecture employs a multi-agent collaboration to ensure system’s
self-correction.

### III-A Collaborative Multi-Agent Architecture

The core of the closed-loop system consists of three specialized agents
that partition the tasks of decision-making, verification, and logical
correction.

Action Agent (Reasoning Core): Powered by Qwen2.5-1.5B and aligned via
GRPO, this agent processes the state $`s_{t}`$ and context to generate a
control action $`u_{t}`$ alongside a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning
path.

Validation Agent (Symbolic Guardrail): Enforcing a strict logical
filter, this component flags the output $`u_{t}`$ as UNSAFE if it
diverges from the ground-truth expert rule $`u_{gt}`$.

Reprompt Agent (Logical Interpreter): Also utilizing Qwen2.5-1.5B, this
agent diagnoses discrepancies between $`u_{t}`$ and $`u_{gt}`$.
Fine-tuned on the Action Agent’s error distributions, it constructs a
semantic Corrective Hint $`h_{t}`$ regarding boundary or rule oversights
to guide zero-shot self-correction.

### III-C Control Logic and System Design

The symbolic rules embedded within the framework are engineered to
emulate an expert thermal engineer’s heuristics, focusing on three
operational pillars:

#### III-C1 Control Logic Design

The symbolic rules in
Table [I](#S3.T1 "TABLE I ‣ III-C1 Control Logic Design ‣ III-C Control Logic and System Design ‣ III Methodology ‣ Closed-Loop Control with Rule-Aligned Small Language Models and Multi-Agent Self-Correction")
are engineered to emulate the decision-making process of a thermal
system. Unlike reactive systems, our framework incorporates temporal
state persistence: the agent’s current decision is conditioned not only
on the thermal state $`S`$ but also on the memory of the previous action
($`A_{prev}`$). This "Latching" mechanism (P3) ensures mission
continuity and prevents oscillatory switching during thermal
transitions, maintaining stability until the setpoint is reached.

| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Priority                             | Condition (Current State $`S`$)                 | Action  |
| 1. Safety Override (Critical, P1)    |                                                 |         |
| Safety H                             | $`T_{cur}\geq T_{safe,high}`$                   | $`-2Q`$ |
| Safety L                             | $`T_{cur}\leq T_{safe,low}`$                    | $`+2Q`$ |
| 2. Boundary Trigger (Tracking, P2)   |                                                 |         |
| Upper B                              | $`T_{cur}>T_{target,high}`$                     | $`-Q`$  |
| Lower B                              | $`T_{cur}<T_{target,low}`$                      | $`+Q`$  |
| 3. Mission Latching (Hysteresis, P3) |                                                 |         |
| Maint. H                             | $`T_{cur}\in[T_{low},T_{high}]\cap A_{prev}>0`$ | $`+Q`$  |
| Maint. C                             | $`T_{cur}\in[T_{low},T_{high}]\cap A_{prev}<0`$ | $`-Q`$  |
| Default                              | All other applicable cases                      | $`0`$   |

#### III-C2 Closed-Loop Reasoning and Validation

The control loop is organised as a _verify-then-execute_ pipeline
(Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ III-B The Control Algorithm ‣ III Methodology ‣ Closed-Loop Control with Rule-Aligned Small Language Models and Multi-Agent Self-Correction")).
At each step, a candidate action is first proposed by the reasoning
agent and then screened by a validation layer before it is applied to
the plant.

#### III-C3 System Dynamics and Environment

The thermal environment is modeled via a first-order energy balance
equation:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       C\frac{dT_{t}}{dt}=UA(T_{\text{out}}-T_{t})+a_{t}+\epsilon
       ```                                                         |     | (1) |

where $`C`$ is the room thermal capacity, $`UA`$ is the overall
heat-transfer coefficient, and $`T_{\text{out}}`$ is the ambient outdoor
temperature. The control action is selected from the discrete set
$`a_{t}\in\{-2Q,-Q,0,Q,2Q\}`$, where $`Q=5.0`$ is the base power unit.

### III-D ALGORITHM REFINEMENT: GRPO AND DISTILLATION

To optimize the SLM’s reasoning capabilities, we employ a strategy of
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) followed by Knowledge
Distillation.

#### III-D1 GRPO and Reward Function

Unlike PPO which requires a value function $`V(s)`$, we adopt GRPO to
stabilize training via group-based relative advantages
\[[7](#bib.bib7)\]. For a given state $`s_{t}`$, we sample $`G`$
reasoning trajectories $`\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{G}\}`$. The reward
function $`R`$ is formulated to align the model with symbolic logic. For
each trajectory $`i`$, a reward $`r_{i}`$ is assigned based on the logic
detailed in Table
[II](#S3.T2 "TABLE II ‣ III-D1 GRPO and Reward Function ‣ III-D ALGORITHM REFINEMENT: GRPO AND DISTILLATION ‣ III Methodology ‣ Closed-Loop Control with Rule-Aligned Small Language Models and Multi-Agent Self-Correction").

|                                       |              |                    |           |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------ | ------------------ | --------- |
| Condition                             | Ground truth | Output             | Score     |
| Task persistence (latching)           |              |                    |           |
| $`a_{t-1}\in\{+Q,+2Q\}^{\mathrm{a}}`$ | $`+Q`$       | $`0`$ (early exit) | $`-0.5`$  |
| $`a_{t-1}\in\{-Q,-2Q\}^{\mathrm{a}}`$ | $`-Q`$       | $`0`$ (early exit) | $`-0.5`$  |
| Critical errors                       |              |                    |           |
| Polarity inversion                    | e.g., $`+Q`$ | e.g., $`-Q`$       | $`-0.75`$ |
| Invalid format                        | Any          | Unknown            | $`-1.0`$  |
| Correct execution                     | $`a^{*}`$    | $`\hat{a}=a^{*}`$  | $`1.0`$   |

TABLE II: Reward logic and penalty mapping for GRPO training

The optimization follows the GRPO algorithm \[[19](#bib.bib15)\]. For
each state $`s_{t}`$, the SLM generates a group of $`G=8`$ independent
reasoning trajectories.

#### III-D2 Action Agent Initialization via Distillation

The overall training and alignment workflow is illustrated in Fig.
[2](#S3.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ III-D3 Iterative Error-Driven Distillation ‣ III-D ALGORITHM REFINEMENT: GRPO AND DISTILLATION ‣ III Methodology ‣ Closed-Loop Control with Rule-Aligned Small Language Models and Multi-Agent Self-Correction").
To prevent divergence during the subsequent reinforcement learning
phase, the Action Agent undergoes an initial brief distillation stage
using DeepSeek-R1-671B as the teacher. Following the distilling
step-by-step paradigm \[[20](#bib.bib13)\], the agent learns to
internalize the structured reasoning paths (CoT) alongside the control
labels using a subset of 5,000 trajectories. This stage provides a
necessary “warm-start” policy, ensuring that the agent maintains a
structured format before being optimized for numerical precision via
GRPO.

#### III-D3 Iterative Error-Driven Distillation

Inspired by the STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner) framework
\[[15](#bib.bib11)\], we develop an iterative distillation pipeline for
the Reprompt Agent, forming the feedback loop shown in the right panel
of Fig.
[2](#S3.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ III-D3 Iterative Error-Driven Distillation ‣ III-D ALGORITHM REFINEMENT: GRPO AND DISTILLATION ‣ III Methodology ‣ Closed-Loop Control with Rule-Aligned Small Language Models and Multi-Agent Self-Correction").
The process is executed in three stages:

1. Error Profiling: After the Action Agent completes its GRPO
   optimization, we deploy it in challenging thermal environments to
   collect corner-case failure trajectories. 2) Teacher Correction: These
   error cases are fed into the DeepSeek-R1-671B teacher model to generate
   5,000 corrective samples. 3) Aha-Moment Distillation: Drawing
   inspiration from the “Aha moment,” the Reprompt Agent is fine-tuned to
   initiate feedback with a “Wait…” prefix, forcing a secondary
   verification.

## IV Experimental Results and Analysis

We report three evaluation protocols: (i) randomized closed-loop
robustness over 30 experiments with 500 steps each (15,000 total steps),
(ii) cross-model comparison on a separate 1,500-sample benchmark, and
(iii) symbolic re-mapping stress tests for interface robustness. Metrics
are reported within each protocol and are not mixed across protocols.

### IV-A Experimental Setup and Baselines

The thermal system is simulated with physical parameters $`C=20.0`$ and
$`UA=0.5`$, using a base power unit $`Q=5.0`$.

We employ three distinct baselines: (1) General-Purpose Large Language
Models (LLMs), including GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 as high-capacity neural
baselines; (2) an Ablation Baseline (Base-SLM), utilizing the original
Qwen2.5-1.5B without GRPO; and (3) a Heuristic Rule-based Controller
(HRBC), representing the upper bound of purely symbolic consistency.

### IV-B Performance on Randomized Scenarios

To evaluate the control robustness and generalization capability of the
proposed framework, we conducted simulations encompassing 30 randomized
test cases, totaling 15,000 operational steps.

A key objective of these tests is to assess the agent’s robustness. The
randomized parameters include temperature values and disturbance
profiles that were explicitly excluded from the training set, thereby
testing the model’s ability to generalize its learned control logic to
unseen scenarios. The robust performance is verified across 30
randomized trials (15,000 steps total) with unseen initial states
$`T_{0}\in[0,45]\,^{\circ}\text{C}`$, setpoints
$`T_{\text{set}}\in[0,40]\,^{\circ}\text{C}`$, and dynamic ambient
disturbances $`T_{\text{out}}\in[-20,40]\,^{\circ}\text{C}`$.

We evaluate performance using two metrics: (1) Action Alignment Accuracy
(Acc), which measures the logical consistency between the predicted
action $`u_{t}`$ and expert ground truth $`u_{gt}`$, defined as
$`\textit{Acc}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(u_{t}=u_{gt})`$
(where $`\mathbb{I}(\cdot)`$ is the indicator function); and (2)
Inference Latency (Lat), evaluating real-time feasibility by averaging
the computational time per step:
$`\textit{Lat}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}(t^{\text{end}}_{t}-t^{\text{start}}_{t})`$,
where $`t^{\text{start}}_{t}`$ and $`t^{\text{end}}_{t}`$ denote the
timestamps at the beginning and completion of inference for step $`t`$,
respectively.

### IV-C Symbolic Generalization and Robustness

To evaluate the system’s operational consistency, we conducted a
Symbolic Re-mapping stress test where the heating power label $`Q`$ was
re-indexed to an arbitrary label $`F`$. This experiment investigates
whether the model’s reasoning is grounded in the underlying task logic
or merely sensitive to specific symbolic strings.

In this re-mapped scenario, the SLM maintained 100% format consistency,
strictly adopting the new symbolic prefix ($`F`$) in all outputs. While
the exact action alignment accuracy decreased to 74.1%, the system
achieved an In-Range Rate (IRR) of 95%(defined here as the percentage of
operational steps where the temperature remains within the prescribed
target range). This disparity between symbolic accuracy (74.1%) and
physical success (95%) is highly significant; it indicates that even
when the agent fails to select the "expert-identical" token, its
decisions remain functionally effective for maintaining thermal
stability.

## V Conclusion

This paper investigated whether a compact, locally deployed Small
Language Model (SLM) can support closed-loop thermal control within a
validator-guided correction architecture. We aligned a Qwen2.5-1.5B
model using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and combined it
with a validation layer and reprompting mechanism to iteratively steer
decisions toward valid actions.

In simulation, the proposed framework achieved 91.5% average
action-alignment accuracy (86.3%–100% across 30 experiments of 500 steps
each), with a mean per-step inference latency of 3.84 s in the reported
benchmark. In a symbolic re-mapping stress test, the controller
maintained a 95% in-range rate (IRR), indicating that physical
regulation remained strong even when token-level agreement decreased.
Overall, these results support the feasibility of SLM+validator loops
for edge-oriented, reconfigurable control workflows, while avoiding
reliance on cloud-hosted inference.

### V-A Limitations and Future Work

While promising, several limitations guide our future directions. 1)
physical validation via hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) testing is required
to assess performance under sensor noise and actuator non-idealities. 2)
we aim to enhance validator fidelity by replacing the symbolic rule
layer with a predictive digital twin. 3) comprehensive latency
characterization must profile jitter, variance, and tail latency on
specialized embedded hardware rather than just mean latency. 4) we will
extend the framework beyond single-zone thermal benchmarks to
multi-variable settings to evaluate the scalability of the multi-agent
control architecture.

###### Abstract

Critiques are important for enhancing the performance of Large Language
Models (LLMs), enabling both self-improvement and constructive feedback
for others by identifying flaws and suggesting improvements. However,
evaluating the critique capabilities of LLMs presents a significant
challenge due to the open-ended nature of the task. In this work, we
introduce a new benchmark designed to assess the critique capabilities
of LLMs. Unlike existing benchmarks, which typically function in an
open-loop fashion, our approach employs a closed-loop methodology that
evaluates the quality of corrections generated from critiques. Moreover,
the benchmark incorporates features such as self-critique,
cross-critique, and iterative critique, which are crucial for
distinguishing the abilities of advanced reasoning models from more
classical ones. We implement this benchmark using eight challenging
reasoning tasks. We have several interesting findings. First, despite
demonstrating comparable performance in direct chain-of-thought
generation, classical LLMs significantly lag behind the advanced
reasoning-based model o1-mini across all critique scenarios. Second, in
self-critique and iterative critique settings, classical LLMs may even
underperform relative to their baseline capabilities. We hope that this
benchmark will serve as a valuable resource to guide future
advancements. The code and data are available at
[https://github.com/tangzhy/RealCritic](https://github.com/tangzhy/RealCritic).

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Benchmark results for self-critique and cross-critique
abilities of representative LLMs. Our findings reveal a clear seperation

model demonstrating improvement in self-critique tasks, while also
achieving the most significant gains in cross-critique performance.

Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) ([Hurst et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10); [Dubey et al., 2024](#bib.bib5); [Mistral-AI,
2024](#bib.bib20); [Qwen-Team, 2024](#bib.bib23)) have demonstrated
remarkable capabilities across lots of tasks, ranging from natural
language understanding to problem-solving. Notably, reasoning-based LLMs
have surpassed human expertise in specialized domains such as
competition-level mathematical reasoning and code generation ([OpenAI,
2024](#bib.bib22)). However, as LLMs continue to evolve, further
improving their performance has become increasingly challenging. One of
the key areas of focus in this regard is the development of critique
abilities ([McAleese et al., 2024](#bib.bib18))—the capability of LLMs
to provide detailed analysis, constructive feedback, and refinement
suggestions for solutions generated by other models or even themselves,
thereby driving continuous improvement in their outputs.

An important step toward advancing critique abilities is the
establishment of high-quality benchmarks for evaluation. Without such
benchmarks, it is difficult to objectively measure progress in this
area. However, evaluating the quality of critiques is difficult due to
the open-ended nature, which lacks a single ground truth answer for
definitive judgment. Furthermore, critiques often serve as intermediate
variables within a continuous improvement pipeline, where their ultimate
value lies in their ability to refine and enhance subsequent outputs
rather than being judged in isolation.

Significant efforts have been made to benchmark the critique
capabilities of LLMs. For instance, CriticBench ([Luo et al.,
2023](#bib.bib17); [Zheng et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)) requires LLMs to
generate critiques accompanied by binary verdicts (C+V) for input
solutions, where the verdicts predict whether the solutions are correct
or incorrect. The quality of the critiques is then evaluated by
comparing these verdicts to ground truth labels, providing a measure of
accuracy. CriticEval ([Lan et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)) conducts a
comprehensive evaluation of LLMs’ critique abilities, introducing two
key dimensions relevant to our study: feedback and correction. The
feedback dimension assesses the quality of the analysis within the
critique by assigning a prediction score, while the correction dimension
evaluates the quality of revisions based on the critique’s analysis.
Beyond dedicated benchmarks, other works such as Shepherd ([Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib29)) and Auto-J ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib14)) have
proposed using GPT-4 to perform comparative evaluations between
different critiques.

Despite these advancements, the key question remains: how can we
effectively measure the quality of critiques? This challenge is
supported by our preliminary study on CriticBench. Using human
evaluators, we find that the benchmark suffers from a high misjudgment
rate (about 30% of low-quality critiques are erroneously classified as
high quality; see
[Table 2](#S2.T2 "In 2 Toward Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques")
in
[Section 2](#S2 "2 Toward Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques")).
Additionally, we observe that existing benchmarks often lack support for
essential critique tasks, such as self-critique and iterative critique,
which are believed to be crucial for reasoning-based LLMs ([Wu et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib32)).

In this paper, we propose RealCritic, a new benchmark designed to
evaluate critique abilities based on their effectiveness in driving
refinement; see
[Table 1](#S1.T1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques")
for an overview. Recognizing that a critique is high-quality if it
contributes meaningfully to the self-improvement of LLMs, we propose to
measure critique quality directly based on the correction it enables.
Our approach is closed-loop, drawing inspiration from control theory,
where the output (correction) is fed back into the system to assess the
effectiveness of the input (critique). In contrast, previous methods for
measuring critique quality are often open-loop, evaluating critiques in
isolation without considering their downstream impact on solution
refinement or treating correction abilities separately from critique
abilities. Furthermore, our benchmark introduces advanced evaluation
scenarios to assess LLMs’ critique capabilities. Specifically, we
distinguish between self-critique and cross-critique abilities and
incorporate iterative critique, providing a comprehensive framework for
evaluating critique effectiveness.

We operationalize RealCritic through 8 challenging tasks: 5 from
open-ended mathematical reasoning and 3 from general-domain
multiple-choice questions. These tasks are sufficiently challenging and,
importantly, have clear ground truth answers for evaluating solution
correctness, even though critiques themselves lack ground truth. We
evaluate several state-of-the-art models, including
LLaMA-3.1-70B-Instruct ([Dubey et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)),
Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct ([Qwen-Team, 2024](#bib.bib23)),
Mistral-Large-Instruct-2407 ([Mistral-AI, 2024](#bib.bib20)), and
o1-mini ([OpenAI, 2024](#bib.bib22)). One of our key findings is
illustrated in
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques"),
with additional results and analyses detailed in the main text.

To summarize, our contributions are three fold:

- •
  We introduce a simple yet effective approach to measure critique
  quality directly based on its impact on solution refinement.
- •
  We develop a benchmark that supports essential critique functions,
  including self-critique, cross-critique, and iterative critique,
  providing a comprehensive evaluation framework tailored to the needs
  of reasoning-based LLMs.
- •
  We conduct a comprehensive analysis of existing LLMs’ critique
  abilities using the proposed framework. This analysis highlights the
  strengths and limitations of current models.

## 2 Toward Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation

In this section, we discuss strategies for constructing an effective
benchmark for evaluating critiques. Evaluating the critique capabilities
of LLMs is inherently challenging due to the multifaceted and open-ended
nature of the task. A high-quality critique must fulfill several
criteria: it should identify flaws or weaknesses in the solution,
provide constructive and actionable feedback, and maintain clarity and
logical consistency. These requirements complicate the evaluation
process, as each sub-part allows for a wide range of valid responses.
While human evaluators may perform well in assessing critique quality,
human-based evaluation methods are neither automatic nor scalable.

To address these challenges, it is crucial to leverage task-specific
properties. Reasoning tasks, for instance, are particularly well-suited
for this purpose, as they share a key characteristic: the final
generated answer is typically a single, definitive response. This
property can be utilized to facilitate the evaluation of solutions.
CriticBench ([Luo et al., 2023](#bib.bib17); [Lin et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15)) leverages this property to evaluate critiques through
the following two-step process:

- •
  Critic Generation: This benchmark requires LLMs to generate critiques
  that provide a detailed analysis of a solution. In addition to the
  analysis, the model must also produce a verdict predicting whether the
  input solution is correct or incorrect.
- •
  Quality Evaluation: The benchmark assesses the quality of the critique
  based on the correctness of the verdict. If the verdict is correct,
  the critique is deemed high quality. Conversely, if the verdict is
  incorrect, the critique is judged as low quality.

CriticEval ([Lan et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)) uses a similar approach in
its evaluation framework, particularly in the dimension they define as
“feedback”. We would like to point out that the fundamental premise of
this approach is to evaluate critique quality based on the accuracy of
predicting the solution’s correctness. We argue that this evaluation
method may not always be effective, as an LLM might correctly predict
the solution’s accuracy without providing a constructive analysis or
critique. This misalignment stems from the fact that the evaluation
approach does not fully capture the intuition that a critique is
considered high-quality if it leads to an improved solution, rather than
predicting the accuracy of the input solution. A specific example
illustrating this limitation is shown in
[Figure 2](#S2.F2 "In 2 Toward Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").
Furthermore, when using human evaluators to provide ground truth
assessments of critique quality, we find that CriticBench suffers from a
high misjudgment rate, further highlighting its limitations as shown in
[Table 2](#S2.T2 "In 2 Toward Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques")
(see details and more concrete examples in
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Human Evaluation Protocol for Critique Quality ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques")).

We believe the aforementioned fragility issue arises from the open-loop
nature of the evaluation method employed. Specifically, the quality of
the generated critique is not directly linked to its impact on
subsequent refinement, leading to potential inconsistencies in
evaluation. While this issue is briefly mentioned in CriticEval ([Lan et
al., 2024](#bib.bib12)), the work does not empirically validate the
severity of the issue, as we have done in our study.

In addition to the limitations in evaluation metrics discussed earlier,
we also find that existing benchmarks lack comprehensive modes for
evaluating critique abilities. First, most benchmarks focus on
critiquing input solutions that have been previously collected,
operating in a cross-critique mode. However, there is no systematic
exploration of another important mode: self-critique, where input
solutions are model-specific and dynamically generated. Additionally,
existing benchmarks do not include an evaluation mode for multi-round
iterative critique, which is essential for examining the long-horizon
critique abilities of LLMs and could be particularly crucial for
evaluating emergent models such as o1 [OpenAI (2024)](#bib.bib22). We
plan to address these limitations in the next section by introducing a
new benchmark.

## 3 Our Evaluation Framework

![Refer to caption](2501.14492v1/openloop_vs_closedloop.png)

Figure 3: Comparison between the evaluation method used in CriticBench
and our framework, RealCritic. Our framework operates in a closed-loop
manner by assessing the quality of a critic through the quality of the
new solution generated based on its feedback.

In this section, we present our evaluation framework for benchmarking
the critique abilities of LLMs in mathematical reasoning domains. Our
framework is built around three key components: the closed-loop
evaluation method described in
[Section 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 From Open-Loop Evaluation to Closed-Loop Evaluation ‣ 3 Our Evaluation Framework ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques"),
the spectrum of critique abilities outlined in
[Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 What to Critique and How to Critique? ‣ 3 Our Evaluation Framework ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques"),
and the targeted datasets we curate for evaluation, as detailed in
[Section 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Dataset Collection ‣ 3 Our Evaluation Framework ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").

### 3.1 From Open-Loop Evaluation to Closed-Loop Evaluation

As discussed in
[Section 2](#S2 "2 Toward Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques"),
we observe that the majority of existing benchmarks rely on open-loop
evaluation. This method fails to account for critiques and their ongoing
refinement processes, thereby limiting the effectiveness of these
benchmarks. To address this limitation, we propose a closed-loop
methodology designed to evaluate the model’s critique ability through a
critique-and-correction framework. In this framework, the LLM’s
critiques are not only generated but also tested by applying them to
refine the initial solution.

Specifically, we assess the quality of critiques by examining the
accuracy of the solution generated after the critique is applied and
refined. The key insight driving this approach is that a high-quality
critique should naturally lead to a more accurate solution. In other
words, the critique is not an isolated task but an integral part of a
dynamic correction process that should improve the solution
incrementally. This allows us to measure the effectiveness of the
critique by evaluating the quality of the final solution. Formally, we
define

|     |                                                                            |                                                                                             |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{performance}`$                                | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}[\mathbb{I}(y_{\operatorname{correction}})],`$        |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle(y_{\operatorname{critic}},y_{\operatorname{correction}})`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{Critic-LLM}}(x,s,I_{\operatorname{critic}}),`$ |     |

where $`x`$ denotes a question drawn from the distribution $`\rho`$. The
term $`\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{Critic-LLM}}`$ represents the
distribution over outputs of the (critic) LLM, with $`s`$ as an initial
solution (response) and $`I_{\operatorname{critic}}`$ as the instruction
prompting both the critique $`y_{\operatorname{critic}}`$ and correction
$`y_{\operatorname{correction}}`$. The notion $`\mathbb{I}`$ is the
indicator function. A specific example is shown in
[Figure 2](#S2.F2 "In 2 Toward Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").

We would like to clarify several points. First, while previous works
([Luo et al., 2023](#bib.bib17); [Lin et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Lan et
al., 2024](#bib.bib12)) also explored measuring the accuracy of the
refined solution, this is done in a separate stage and is not used to
measure the quality of the critique itself. Second, our approach
leverages the quality of the refined solution to measure the quality of
the generated critique. However, there are potential shortcuts where
models may directly generate a new solution instead of critiquing the
original one. To address this, we have carefully tuned the prompt and
designed a post-check process to mitigate such behaviors in our
evaluations (see Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Post-Check Mechanism for Critique Quality Assurance ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques")).

### 3.2 What to Critique and How to Critique?

After defining the evaluation criteria, the next step is to design test
cases that assess both the model’s critique ability and the underlying
critique process. We highlight two key axes often overlooked in existing
benchmarks. The first axis distinguishes between self-critique, where
the model evaluates its own generated solution, and cross-critique,
where it critiques solutions provided by other models. While
cross-critique has been widely studied, self-critique remains
underexplored, despite its importance for o1-style models that refine
outputs through internal feedback loops. The second axis examines
whether the critique process is single-round or iterative. Complex tasks
often require multi-round critique, where the model refines its feedback
across iterations to address errors in initial assessments. This
iterative process provides a more comprehensive test of the model’s
long-horizon reasoning capabilities. We discuss these points in detail
below.

Self-Critique Paradigm: The self-critique paradigm focuses on assessing
a model’s ability to identify and improve errors in its own generated
solutions. Formally, the quality of critique is measured by:

|     |                                                                            |                                                                                               |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{performance}_{\operatorname{self}}`$          | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}[\mathbb{I}(y_{\operatorname{correction}})],`$          |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle(y_{\operatorname{critic}},y_{\operatorname{correction}})`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{Critic-LLM}}(x,s,I_{\operatorname{critic}}),`$   |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle s`$                                                        | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{Critic-LLM}}(x,I_{\operatorname{generation}}).`$ |     |

A key feature of this paradigm is that the input solutions for critique
are not fixed or pre-collected but are instead model-specific. This
paradigm evaluates the model’s introspective capabilities, specifically
its ability to recognize its own errors, analyze their root causes, and
implement effective corrections. However, since the critic and solution
generator are the same model, they inherently share the same knowledge
base and potential biases, which may limit the diversity and objectivity
of the critiques.

Cross-Critique Paradigm: The cross-critique paradigm evaluates a model’s
ability to analyze and improve answers generated by other models. This
approach is widely used in benchmarks, partly because it is
straightforward to implement, as input solutions for critique can be
pre-collected. This paradigm is crucial for understanding how
effectively a model can serve as an external evaluator. Formally, the
quality of critique is measured by:

|     |                                                                            |                                                                                             |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{performance}_{\operatorname{cross}}`$         | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}[\mathbb{I}(y_{\operatorname{correction}})],`$        |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle(y_{\operatorname{critic}},y_{\operatorname{correction}})`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{Critic-LLM}}(x,s,I_{\operatorname{critic}}),`$ |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle s`$                                                        | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{LLM}}(x,I_{\operatorname{generation}}),`$      |     |

where $`{\mathbb{P}}_{\operatorname{LLM}}`$ may involve any language
model distribution, which is distinct from the critic’s distribution and
could even be a combination of multiple models. This diversity means
that the critic must adeptly handle a wide range of solution styles and
error types, ensuring versatile evaluation capabilities.

Iterative Critique-Correction Paradigm: Drawing on the success of
o1-style models ([OpenAI, 2024](#bib.bib22)), it becomes evident that
multi-round self-reflection and iterative refinement play a decisive
role in mathematical reasoning. By enabling several cycles of
critique-and-correction, we can thoroughly evaluate the model’s
continuous improvement across successive iterations, investigate the
convergence properties underpinning its error-correction process, and
analyze how self-supervision mechanisms influence performance—ultimately
shedding light on the model’s capacity for robust and adaptive problem
solving.

Formally, we define the iterative process as follows:

|     |                                                                                    |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{performance}_{\operatorname{iterative}}^{t}`$         | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}[\mathbb{I}(y_{\operatorname{correction}}^{t})],`$                                                                                                                                               |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle(y_{\operatorname{critic}}^{t},y_{\operatorname{correction}}^{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{Critic-LLM}}(x,s,I_{\operatorname{critic}},y_{\operatorname{critic}}^{1},y_{\operatorname{correction}}^{1},\ldots,y_{\operatorname{critic}}^{t-1},y_{\operatorname{correction}}^{t-1}),`$ |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle s`$                                                                | $`\displaystyle\sim\mathbb{P}_{\operatorname{LLM}}(x,I_{\operatorname{generation}}),`$                                                                                                                                                 |     |

where $`t`$ denotes the iteration index compared with previous notions.
This formulation highlights the model’s capability to refine its output
through repeated self-critique, shedding light on its long-horizon
reasoning and error-correction dynamics.

### 3.3 Dataset Collection

![Refer to caption](2501.14492v1/create_benchmark.png)

Figure 4: Data collection process for constructing solutions to assess
the critique abilities of LLMs.

In this section, we present the strategies we propose for constructing
high-quality datasets to benchmark critique abilities. An overview of
our data collection process is provided in
[Figure 4](#S3.F4 "In 3.3 Dataset Collection ‣ 3 Our Evaluation Framework ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").
This process involves two key steps: first, collecting questions from a
variety of tasks, and second, generating solutions to these questions
using multiple models. Both the questions and solutions will be used to
evaluate the critique abilities of LLMs.

Task Sources. Our approach began with a comprehensive review of existing
evaluation datasets used in prior studies that benchmark
well-established models, such as the o1 model ([OpenAI,
2024](#bib.bib22)). We applied a set of selection criteria to filter
relevant datasets for our study. Specifically, the datasets had to
satisfy the following conditions: they should encompass a variety of
difficulty levels, and they must provide explicit final answers to
facilitate the verification of solution correctness. After applying
these filters, we identified 20 candidate datasets, which encompassed
both open-ended math reasoning tasks and general-domain multiple-choice
reasoning tasks. For further details on the attributes of these
datasets, please refer to Appendix
[C](#A3 "Appendix C Details On The Attributes Of Candidate Datasets ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").
Given the large number of datasets, evaluating all 20 candidates was not
feasible within the scope of this study. To ensure a cost-effective and
manageable evaluation process, we selected 8 representative datasets
that cover a range of difficulty levels and task types.

For open-ended math reasoning tasks, we chose five datasets: GSM8K
([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib3)), Math ([Hendrycks et al.,
2021](#bib.bib8)), College Math ([Tang et al., 2024](#bib.bib27)),
Minerva Math ([Lewkowycz et al., 2022](#bib.bib13)), and Olympiad Bench
([He et al., 2024](#bib.bib6)). These datasets span a broad spectrum of
mathematical difficulty, from standard high school problems to advanced
Olympiad-level challenges, providing a diverse set of tasks that test a
model’s reasoning abilities. For general-domain reasoning tasks with
multiple-choice questions, we included three datasets: GPQA-diamond
([Rein et al., 2023](#bib.bib24)), ARC-Challenge ([Clark et al.,
2018a](#bib.bib1)), and MMLU-STEM ([Hendrycks et al., 2020](#bib.bib7)).
These datasets assess performance across various domains, with an
emphasis on selecting the correct answer from multiple choices. To
reduce evaluation costs, we sampled up to 300 items from each dataset,
including all items from smaller sets and randomly selecting 300 from
larger ones to maintain a diverse yet manageable data pool.

Solution Sources. To generate solutions for the collected questions, we
established a model pool primarily composed of open-source models to
minimize costs. The pool includes the Qwen2.5 series ([Qwen-Team,
2024](#bib.bib23)) (1.5B, 7B, and 72B), the LLaMA-3.1 series (8B and
72B) ([Dubey et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)), and the Deepseek
math-specialized model ([DeepSeek-AI, 2024](#bib.bib4)) (7B). Given the
varying downstream capabilities of these models, we carefully designed a
strategy to ensure both the quality and diversity of the generated
solutions.

Specifically, we sample each model once on all data points to generate
their solutions. The data is then cleaned using the following steps.
First, we remove overly long solutions where models fail to stop
generating, resulting in repeated outputs until the maximum token limit
is reached. Second, we eliminate questions that all models answered
correctly, as these are considered too simplistic to yield meaningful
insights. Both incorrect and correct solutions are valuable for testing
the critique capabilities of LLMs, as they provide distinct challenges
for evaluation. To ensure a comprehensive assessment, we apply different
strategies for collecting correct and incorrect solutions, which are
detailed below.

For incorrect solutions, we prioritize stronger models over weaker ones.
Specifically, if multiple models provide incorrect answers to the same
question, we select the solution from the strongest model. As
illustrated in
[Table 8](#A5.T8 "In Appendix E Priority-based Filtering Strategy in Solution Selection ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques"),
when both strong and weak models make errors on the same question, the
strong model’s solution typically includes more detailed reasoning. This
offers the critic model richer information to analyze and identify
errors. In contrast, weaker models often produce vague or incomplete
incorrect solutions, making it more difficult for the critic model to
pinpoint the exact failure points.

For correct solutions, we prioritize weaker models over stronger ones.
As shown in
[Table 9](#A5.T9 "In Appendix E Priority-based Filtering Strategy in Solution Selection ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques"),
strong models generally produce correct solutions with minimal errors in
the reasoning process, making them easier for the critic model to
evaluate. However, when a weaker model generates a correct solution, it
often includes ambiguous or even incorrect intermediate steps, creating
a more challenging scenario for the critic model to assess.

Benchmark Format and Usage Protocol. Finally we detail the key
components and usage protocols of our benchmark:

| ------- | --- | ----- | ---- | ------- | ------- | ---- | ---- | -------- |

  Dataset Composition: As shown in Table

  our benchmark comprises eight distinct datasets spanning various
  mathematical reasoning domains. For each dataset, we maintain a
  balanced distribution between correct and incorrect solutions
  (approximately 1:1 ratio) for cross-critique. We also prepare a
  separate set of deliberately incorrect solutions for each dataset,
  with results presented in Appendix
  [D](#A4 "Appendix D Pure Critique Performance Analysis ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").
- •
  Self-Critique Protocol: The model first generates an initial solution
  using Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting ([Wei et al.,
  2022](#bib.bib30)), then reviews and corrects its own solution using
  our critique system prompt.
- •
  Cross-Critique Protocol: Given both a question and a pre-collected
  solution, the model analyzes the solution using the same critique
  system prompt, identifying potential errors and suggesting specific
  improvements.
- •
  Iterative Critique Protocol: For both self-critique and
  cross-critique, our benchmark supports multi-turn sessions where the
  model iteratively refines its analysis until reaching a predefined
  maximum number of iterations.
- •
  Concrete Prompts: Our benchmark utilizes three types of prompts
  (detailed in Appendix
  [F](#A6 "Appendix F Concrete Prompts ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques")):
  a direct CoT prompt for initial solution generation, a core critique
  prompt shared by both protocols for systematic analysis and
  correction, and a follow-up prompt for iterative rounds that enforces
  fresh analysis. These prompts collectively support our closed-loop
  evaluation methodology, with concrete examples provided in Appendix
  [G](#A7 "Appendix G Concrete Examples ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").

#### 4.2.1 Self-Critique Performance Analysis

Our analysis of self-critique capabilities reveals interesting patterns
across different models. Most models show varying degrees of performance
changes in self-critique compared to their direct CoT solutions, with
changes ranging from -5.1% to +3.3% on average. This suggests that
recognizing and correcting one’s own mistakes remains a challenging task
even for state-of-the-art language models. However, we notice that
o1-mini stands out as a remarkable exception, demonstrating strong
self-improvement capabilities with a +3.3% average improvement over
direct solutions. This exceptional performance is particularly evident
across multiple tasks: College Math (+24.0%), ARC (+19.4%), MATH
(+10.1%), and notably even on the challenging OlympiadBench (+6.4%). The
consistent improvement across diverse mathematical tasks suggests that
o1-mini’s architecture or training approach might be particularly
well-suited for self-reflection tasks.

Furthermore, we observe a concerning pattern across all models in
self-critique performance on certain specialized tasks. Models including
o1-mini consistently struggle with MMLU-STEM and GPQA tasks, showing
significant performance degradation ranging from -10% to -35.6%. This
pattern is particularly pronounced in Qwen2.5-72B-Math-Instruct’s
performance on MMLU-STEM (-35.6%) and o1-mini’s performance on MMLU-STEM
(-28.0%). This suggests that self-critique abilities may be more
challenging to maintain in complex, specialized domains that require
deep subject matter expertise.

#### 4.2.2 Cross-Critique Performance Analysis

Cross-critique evaluation reveals a more nuanced picture of models’
critique capabilities. The baseline performance is approximately 50%, as
half of the input solutions are correct and half are incorrect. When
compared to this baseline, most models show substantial improvements (up
to 40%) on mathematical reasoning tasks. This suggests that models are
particularly adept at identifying and correcting errors in basic
mathematical reasoning. Similar to the case of self-critique, we observe
that o1-mini emerges as the strongest performer in cross-critique tasks.
For specialized tasks, models—except for o1-mini—again show degraded
performance on average.

#### 4.2.3 Comparative Analysis of Self-Critique and Cross-Critique

In this section, we present a more detailed analysis, focusing on two
key metrics: I→C, which measures the transformation of incorrect input
solutions into correct ones after critique and correction, and C→I,
which captures the reversal of originally correct solutions to incorrect
ones after critique and correction. These metrics will help us gain
deeper insights into the effectiveness of the critique process. The
results are reported in

![Refer to caption](2501.14492v1/critique_analysis.png)

Figure 5: Performance of C→I and I→C in self-critique and cross-critique
scenarios. Here, “C” denotes correct, “I” denotes incorrect, and the
arrow indicates the accuracy change from the input solution to the

A striking observation is the consistent asymmetry between improvement
capability (I→C) and solution maintenance (C→I) across both critique
paradigms. Specifically, for self-critique, most models show limited
ability to improve incorrect solutions (I→C improvements typically below
5%), while exhibiting significant degradation when critiquing initially
correct solutions (C→I degradations often exceeding -10%). But, o1-mini
stands as a notable exception, demonstrating remarkable improvement
capabilities (I→C improvements of 25.85% on ARC and 24.37% on College
Math) while still struggling with solution maintenance. For
cross-critique, we observe substantially higher improvement capabilities
across all models (I→C improvements of 30-45% on fundamental tasks like
ARC and GSM8K). However, the degradation issue persists, particularly in
specialized domains like GPQA and MMLU-STEM (C→I degradations of -15% to
-30%). This comparative analysis reveals that while cross-critique
generally enables higher potential for improvement, it also carries
higher risks of solution degradation. The effectiveness of both critique
paradigms appears to be strongly influenced by task complexity and
domain specificity, with fundamental mathematical reasoning tasks being
more amenable to reliable critique than specialized domain tasks.

We observe distinct trends in how critique effectiveness—measured by

## 5 Related Work

Prior to our work, several efforts have been made to advance the
benchmarking of LLMs’ critique abilities ([Luo et al.,
2023](#bib.bib17); [Lin et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Lan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib12)). We have summarized their key features in
[Table 1](#S1.T1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").
In contrast to these benchmarks, our approach focuses specifically on
reasoning tasks and introduces a tightly coupled evaluation framework.
This framework validates critique quality through direct correction
outcomes and supports diverse critique modes, including self-critique
and iterative critique.

Understanding LLMs’ ability to self-critique and improve their reasoning
has emerged as a critical research direction. [Huang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib9) demonstrated that LLMs often struggle with
self-correction without external feedback, while [Stechly et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib25) found that external verification from independent
reasoners can significantly improve reasoning accuracy. [Wu et al.
(2024b)](#bib.bib33)’s ProCo framework showed promise in enabling
self-correction through minimal prompting and iterative verification.
[Tyen et al. (2023)](#bib.bib28) revealed an interesting dynamic: while
LLMs may struggle to detect reasoning errors independently, they can
effectively correct them when error locations are explicitly identified.
Our work builds upon these findings by introducing a novel evaluation
framework that examines both self-critique and cross-model critique
capabilities.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we present a new benchmark designed to evaluate the
critique capabilities of LLMs in reasoning tasks. We investigate three
distinct scenarios: self-critique, cross-critique, and iterative
critique. Our findings reveal that in nearly all cases, the o1-mini
model demonstrates the most impressive performance. While other models
may exhibit comparable performance to o1-mini in direct CoT generation,
they significantly lag behind in critique abilities. We provide a
detailed analysis of these results, offering insights into the
underlying factors contributing to these differences. We hope that our
findings will inspire and guide future advancements in the development
of LLMs.

## Appendix A Human Evaluation Protocol for Critique Quality

We conducted a systematic human evaluation study to establish ground
truth labels for critique quality. This section details our evaluation
protocol.

### A.1 Data Preparation

We utilized the evaluation framework from CriticBench \[[Lin et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15)\], which provides a comprehensive dataset containing
mathematical problems, ground truth solutions, and student solutions.
Following their setup, we used 100 problems from their MATH \[[Hendrycks
et al., 2021](#bib.bib8)\] test set, which already contains mathematical
problems, ground truth solutions, and student attempted solutions. We
leveraged CriticBench’s evaluation code and prompt templates, replacing
their default model with Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct\[[Qwen-Team,
2024](#bib.bib23)\] to generate critiques in their standard JSON format:

```ltx_verbatim
{
    "analysis": "Detailed analysis of the solution...",
    "verdict": "correct" or "wrong"
}
```

### A.2 Evaluation Framework

Our evaluation framework relies on two key dimensions:

1.  1.  The correctness of the student’s solution (compared to ground truth)
2.  2.  The verdict provided in the critique (”correct” or ”wrong”)

Based on these dimensions, we developed a comprehensive argument mapping
framework to evaluate critique quality, as shown in
Table [5](#A1.T5 "Table 5 ‣ A.2 Evaluation Framework ‣ Appendix A Human Evaluation Protocol for Critique Quality ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").

### A.3 Evaluation Process

For each critique evaluation, STEM graduate students were asked to
perform a systematic analysis following a standardized procedure:

1.  1.  First, evaluators thoroughly reviewed the mathematical problem and
        its ground truth solution to establish a clear understanding of the
        correct approach and key concepts involved.
2.  2.  They then examined the student’s attempted solution in detail,
        comparing it with the ground truth solution to identify any
        discrepancies or errors.
3.  3.  Next, they analyzed the generated critique, paying particular
        attention to both its verdict (”correct” or ”wrong”) and the
        specific reasoning provided in the analysis.
4.  4.  Based on the solution’s correctness and the critique’s verdict,
        evaluators were presented with the corresponding argument from
        Table [5](#A1.T5 "Table 5 ‣ A.2 Evaluation Framework ‣ Appendix A Human Evaluation Protocol for Critique Quality ‣ RealCritic: Towards Effectiveness-Driven Evaluation of Language Model Critiques").
5.  5.  Finally, evaluators provided: A binary judgment: ”True” (indicating
        the argument applies and the critique is low-quality) or ”False”
        (indicating the argument doesn’t apply and the critique is
        high-quality)

To ensure the validity of our closed-loop evaluation framework, we
implement a rigorous post-check mechanism to verify that the
critique-correction process strictly follows our intended paradigm. This
process is crucial for preventing potential shortcuts where models might
generate new solutions directly without properly critiquing the original

### B.1 Verification Process

We employ a carefully designed prompt to evaluate whether each critique
adheres to our step-by-step critique-and-correct paradigm in Table

### B.2 Implementation and Validation

We utilize Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct as our verification model to evaluate
all generated critiques. To validate the reliability of this automated
verification process, we conducted a thorough human evaluation study. We
randomly sampled 100 critique instances from our dataset for manual
review by expert annotators. The results showed a 98% compliance rate
with our critique format requirements, demonstrating the effectiveness
of our post-check mechanism in ensuring the quality and validity of the
critique-correction process. This verification step is crucial for
maintaining the integrity of our evaluation framework, ensuring that the
measured critique effectiveness truly reflects the model’s ability to
identify and correct errors in a systematic manner. Given this high
compliance rate, we did not implement post-check as a mandatory step in
our evaluation pipeline, as the results indicate that models generally
adhere to the intended critique-correction paradigm.

exclusion. For the GPQA series, we select the most representative
subset, GPQA-diamond. From the MMLU series, we choose MMLU-STEM, which
is most closely related to reasoning, for inclusion in RealCritic.

###### Abstract

Vision-language models (VLMs) have achieved strong performance in
multimodal reasoning, yet they remain prone to generating plausible but
incorrect answers. Self-verification offers a practical way to improve
answer reliability without relying on external judges, but existing
methods typically depend on a single verification criterion or fixed
prompt, resulting in incomplete and unstable reliability estimates. We
first systematically analyze how verifier capability and prompt design
affect verification performance. Our findings show that stronger
verifiers provide more reliable judgments, while verification
performance is highly sensitive to prompt choice, with no single prompt
consistently dominating across tasks. Guided by these findings, we
propose MOTIVE, a Multi-View Self-VerificatiOn wiTh RelIability-Guided
SelectiVE Rethinking framework for reliable multimodal reasoning. MOTIVE
evaluates each candidate answer from complementary verification
perspectives and learns a correctness-aligned reliability score through
correctness-grounded multi-view verification learning. During inference,
this score governs an accept-or-rethink decision, allowing reliable
answers to be returned directly while uncertain ones trigger
history-guided rethinking. Extensive experiments across diverse
multimodal benchmarks and VLM backbones demonstrate that MOTIVE
consistently outperforms strong self-verification and self-correction
baselines. Further results show that reliable verification improves
accept-or-rethink decisions and reduces unnecessary reasoning turns,
enabling more reliable and efficient self-verification without an
external judge.

## 1  Introduction

Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated
remarkable capabilities in multimodal understanding, enabling strong
performance across diverse visual question answering and reasoning
tasks ([Dai et al., 2023](#bib.bib41); [Dong and Crisan,
2025](#bib.bib42); [Bordes et al., 2024](#bib.bib43)). However, despite
their impressive capabilities, VLMs remain prone to generating plausible
but incorrect answers due to visual ambiguity and reasoning
errors ([Huang et al., 2025](#bib.bib44); [Hou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib45); [Chen et al., 2024b](#bib.bib46)). Therefore,
enabling VLMs to assess the reliability of their own predictions is
crucial for building more trustworthy multimodal systems ([Ghosh et al.,
2024](#bib.bib50)).

To improve the reliability of VLMs, recent studies have explored
self-verification mechanisms that enable models to examine and assess
their own generated answers ([Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib47); [Chen et
al., 2024a](#bib.bib48); [Cheng et al., 2024](#bib.bib49)). Compared
with external verification approaches that require additional models or
computational resources, self-verification provides a more efficient way
to evaluate predictions within the same multimodal model ([Campbell et
al., 2024](#bib.bib51); [Anis et al., 2025](#bib.bib52)). However,
existing self-verification methods typically rely on a single
verification criterion or a fixed checking procedure ([Madaan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib7); [Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib8)), which may fail to
capture diverse types of errors in multimodal reasoning. As a result,
how to obtain more reliable and comprehensive verification signals
remains an open challenge.

To better understand the factors affecting self-verification
reliability, we conduct preliminary analyses on verification capability
and prompt design in
Section [2](#S2 "2 Preliminary Analysis ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").
Our studies reveal two important observations. ❶ Verification
effectiveness depends on verifier capability. Stronger verifiers provide
more reliable assessments by better understanding visual evidence,
question semantics, and answer consistency, indicating that verification
quality is closely related to the reasoning ability of the verifier. ❷
Verification performance is sensitive to verification prompt design.
Different verification prompts exhibit complementary strengths, while no
single prompt consistently performs best across different multimodal
tasks. These findings suggest that reliable self-verification benefits
from diverse verification perspectives and the effective aggregation of
complementary signals.

Guided by these findings, we introduce MOTIVE (Multi-View
Self-VerificatiOn wiTh RelIability-Guided SelectiVE Rethinking), a
self-verification framework for reliable multimodal reasoning. MOTIVE
evaluates each candidate answer through complementary verification views
and aggregates their outputs into a unified reliability score.
Correctness-grounded verification learning supervises both view-specific
judgments and the aggregated score using automatically derived
candidate-correctness labels. During inference, the learned score
governs an accept-or-rethink decision: reliable answers are returned
directly, whereas uncertain ones trigger history-guided rethinking. This
design improves verification decisions within the same backbone VLM
without requiring an external judge.

Our efforts are unfolded with the following four thrusts:

- $`\star`$
  (Preliminary Analysis) We systematically investigate verification
  capability and prompt design, showing that stronger verifiers improve
  answer reliability, while self-verification remains highly
  prompt-sensitive and no single prompt consistently dominates across
  tasks.
- $`\star`$
  (Algorithm) Guided by these findings, we propose MOTIVE, a multi-view
  self-verification framework that uses correctness-grounded
  verification supervision to support reliability-guided
  accept-or-rethink decisions without an external judge.
- $`\star`$
  (Experiments) Extensive experiments on five benchmarks and three VLM
  backbones demonstrate that MOTIVE consistently outperforms strong
  self-verification and self-correction baselines.
- $`\star`$
  (Extra Findings) We further find that reliable verification enables
  earlier stopping with fewer unnecessary rethinking turns, while the
  best individual verification view varies across datasets and
  backbones, confirming their task-dependent complementarity.

## 2  Preliminary Analysis

Before presenting our method, we analyze how verification capability and
prompt design affect the answer reliability of VLMs in visual question
answering.

### 2.1  Verification Capability

We first examine whether explicit verification improves the answer
reliability of VLMs. Given a multimodal query, the baseline directly
uses Qwen2.5-VL-3B or Qwen2.5-VL-7B ([Bai et al., 2025a](#bib.bib53)) to
generate the final answer. In the verification setting, the backbone
first produces an initial answer, which is evaluated together with the
image and question by an external judge constrained to output Yes or No.
A Yes verdict retains the answer, whereas No triggers rethinking and
regeneration. We use InternVL3-2B, 8B, 14B, and 38B ([Zhu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib57)) as judges and evaluate on VQA ([Antol et al.,
2015](#bib.bib54)), ScienceQA ([Lu et al., 2022](#bib.bib55)), and
TableVQA ([Kim et al., 2024](#bib.bib56)). The main observations are
summarized below.

Figure 1: Effect of Verification Judge Capability. Accuracy on three
benchmarks using Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B backbones and InternVL3 judges of
different scales. Dashed lines denote performance without verification.

① Verification Enhances Final Answer Reliability. As shown in
Figure [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.1 Verification Capability ‣ 2 Preliminary Analysis ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models"),
introducing verification consistently improves final-answer performance
over direct answering across different backbones and benchmarks. This
indicates that the initial responses of VLMs are not always sufficiently
reliable. By determining whether an answer should be accepted or
regenerated, verification provides an opportunity to correct unreliable
responses without modifying or fine-tuning the backbone model.

② Stronger Judges Improve Verification Effectiveness. We further observe
that larger InternVL3 judges generally achieve better final performance
across different Qwen backbones and multimodal benchmarks. This
indicates that verification effectiveness depends on the judge’s
multimodal reasoning capability, particularly its ability to assess the
consistency among visual evidence, question semantics, and the generated
answer.

External verification improves VLM reliability but requires an auxiliary
judge and introduces additional computational cost, motivating
self-verification within the backbone VLM. This provides a more
practical way to assess answer reliability without requiring an
additional model during use.

### 2.2  Verification Prompt Design

We next examine how prompt design affects self-verification performance.
Since external judges introduce additional computational cost, we use
the same backbone VLM for both answer generation and verification. Based
on representative self-correction and self-verification protocols  ([He
et al., 2025](#bib.bib12); [Weng et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Dhuliawala
et al., 2024](#bib.bib58)), we compare two prompt families: five
predefined Direct Verification Prompts (DVP-1–DVP-5), provided in
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Direct Verification Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix B Prompt Details ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models"),
which assess the initial answer from complementary perspectives, and
Generated Verification Questions (GVQ-1–GVQ-3), which generate and
answer one, two, or three auxiliary verification questions,
respectively, before producing a final Yes/No verdict. A Yes verdict
retains the initial answer, whereas No triggers rethinking and
regeneration. We conduct the analysis using Qwen2.5-VL-3B on VQA,
ScienceQA, and TableVQA, varying only the verification prompt. The main
observations are summarized below.

| DVP-4  | 52.11 | 81.85     | 57.00    |

① Self-Verification Is Highly Prompt-Sensitive. As shown in
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2.2 Verification Prompt Design ‣ 2 Preliminary Analysis ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models"),
changing only the verification prompt leads to clear performance
variations under the same backbone, datasets, and verification protocol.
This indicates that self-verification cannot be reliably elicited by an
arbitrary checking instruction. Its effectiveness depends on whether the
prompt provides a clear criterion for assessing the initial answer, as
different prompts may lead the same VLM to produce inconsistent
verification decisions across multimodal tasks.

② No Single Direct Prompt Consistently Dominates. Among DVP-1 to DVP-5,
the best-performing prompt varies across benchmarks, showing that no
single direct prompt provides a universally reliable verification view.
Although all DVP prompts follow the same direct Yes/No verification
format, their effects are task-dependent, suggesting that each prompt
captures only part of the evidence needed for reliable answer checking.
Relying on one fixed prompt may therefore overfit the verification
behavior to a specific benchmark or question type. This motivates the
use of complementary direct prompts to obtain more reliable and
informative verification signals across tasks.

③ Generated Verification Questions Introduce Instability. GVQ-style
prompts are generally less stable than direct verification prompts, and
their performance further decreases as more auxiliary questions are
generated. Although GVQ makes the verification process more explicit,
these intermediate questions are generated by the same VLM that produced
the initial answer. As a result, they may inherit the model’s original
uncertainty, shift attention away from the original query, or introduce
additional reasoning errors before the final verdict. This suggests that
increasing the number of self-generated verification steps does not
necessarily strengthen verification, but can amplify intermediate
uncertainty and error propagation.

These findings indicate that the central challenge of self-verification
is not adding more reasoning steps, but establishing a reliable
criterion for judging whether the initial answer should be trusted. A
single direct prompt provides incomplete and prompt-sensitive signals,
while generated verification questions introduce uncertainty. This
motivates a multi-view self-verification framework that aggregates
complementary signals and learns to distinguish reliable answers from
unreliable ones.

## 3  Method

We propose MOTIVE, a calibrated verification framework for reliable
multimodal reasoning, as shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Method ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").
Given an image–question pair $`(I,q)`$, a multimodal policy first
generates a candidate answer and then evaluates its reliability through
multiple verification views. Instead of treating self-verification as a
post-hoc prompting heuristic, MOTIVE learns a correctness-aligned
reliability signal that determines whether the model should accept the
current answer or allocate additional computation to rethink. The
framework jointly optimizes answer generation, correctness-grounded
multi-view verification, and reliability-guided selective rethinking.

![Refer to caption](2610.07018v1/overall_v2.png)

Figure 2: Overview of MOTIVE. The backbone VLM evaluates each candidate
answer using five Direct Verification Prompts (DVP-1–DVP-5) and
aggregates their scores to guide an accept-or-rethink decision. Accepted
answers are returned, while rethinking updates the interaction history
and generates a revised candidate.

#### Problem Setup.

Let $`(I,q,y^{*})`$ denote an image, a question, and its ground-truth
answer. Given $`(I,q)`$, a multimodal policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ generates
a candidate answer through an iterative answer–verify–rethink process.
At reasoning turn $`t`$, the policy conditions on the interaction
history $`h_{t}`$ and samples

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid I,q,h_{t}).
       ```                                          |     | (1) |

The model must then decide whether to return $`a_{t}`$ or allocate
additional computation to revise it. We formulate this as a binary
decision $`d_{t}\in\{\mathrm{accept},\mathrm{rethink}\}.`$ If
$`d_{t}=\mathrm{accept}`$, reasoning terminates and $`a_{t}`$ is
returned. Otherwise, verification feedback is incorporated into the
history and the policy generates a revised candidate at the next turn.

#### Multi-View Verification.

Self-verification can be sensitive to the verification prompt, such that
different criteria may yield different judgments for the same candidate
answer. Motivated by this observation, we evaluate each candidate from
multiple complementary verification views. Let $`\mathcal{E}`$ denote a
set of $`K`$ verification views. For each $`\eta_{k}\in\mathcal{E}`$, we
construct a view-specific verification input and estimate the
probability that $`a_{t}`$ is correct:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
x_{t}^{(k)}=\mathcal{V}_{\eta_{k}}(I,q,h_{t},a_{t}),\qquad p_{t}^{(k)}=\pi_{\theta}(\mathrm{YES}\mid x_{t}^{(k)}),\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.
``` |  | (2) |

Here, $`\mathcal{V}_{\eta_{k}}`$ specifies the verification instruction
associated with the $`k`$-th view, and $`p_{t}^{(k)}`$ denotes its
view-specific reliability estimate. We aggregate these estimates into a
unified reliability score

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{t}=\mathrm{Agg}\!\left(p_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,p_{t}^{(K)}\right),
``` |  | (3) |

which is subsequently used to govern selective rethinking. In our
implementation, we use simple averaging for $`\mathrm{Agg}`$ and
consider $`K=5`$ predefined verification views motivated by the
prompt-sensitivity analysis in
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Verification Prompt Design ‣ 2 Preliminary Analysis ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").
The details of multi-view are provided in
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Direct Verification Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix B Prompt Details ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").

###### Proposition 3.1 (Variance reduction from complementary views)

Suppose each verification view provides an unbiased reliability estimate
$`p_{t}^{(k)}=\eta_{t}+\epsilon_{t}^{(k)}`$, with
$`\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_{t}^{(k)})\leq\sigma^{2}`$ and pairwise
$`\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_{t}^{(j)},\epsilon_{t}^{(k)})\leq\rho\sigma^{2}`$
for $`j\neq k`$. For mean aggregation,
$`s_{t}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{t}^{(k)}`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}\!\left[(s_{t}-\eta_{t})^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2}\left(\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{K}\right).
``` |  |

#### Correctness-Grounded Multi-View Verification Learning.

Outcome-level rewards supervise the final answer but do not explicitly
teach the model whether an intermediate candidate should be trusted. We
therefore introduce an auxiliary verification objective that aligns both
view-specific and aggregated reliability estimates with candidate
correctness. For candidate $`a_{i,t}`$ at turn $`t`$ of rollout
$`o_{i}`$, we define its correctness label as
$`z_{i,t}=\mathrm{M}(a_{i,t},y^{*})\in\{0,1\}`$, where $`\mathrm{M}`$ is
the task-specific correctness function. Using binary cross-entropy
$`\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(z,p)=-z\log p-(1-z)\log(1-p)`$, we first supervise
each verification view independently:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{view}}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(z_{i,t},p_{i,t}^{(k)}).
``` |  | (4) |

Here, $`G`$ denotes the number of rollouts and $`T_{i}`$ the number of
candidate-answer turns in rollout $`o_{i}`$. This view-level supervision
discourages individual views from becoming uninformative and encourages
each to provide a correctness-sensitive verification signal. We further
align the aggregated reliability score with candidate correctness:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{agg}}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(z_{i,t},s_{i,t}).
``` |  | (5) |

Because $`s_{i,t}`$ is subsequently used as a continuous decision
signal, its probability magnitude is also important: two predictions
with the same binary decision can imply substantially different
accept-or-rethink behavior. We therefore complement the log-loss
objective with a Brier-style squared-error regularizer,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cal}}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}(s_{i,t}-z_{i,t})^{2},
``` |  | (6) |

which directly penalizes deviations between the reliability score and
the observed correctness outcome. Rather than replacing the
probabilistic supervision provided by binary cross-entropy, this term
provides a complementary constraint on the numerical scale of
$`s_{i,t}`$, making it better suited for threshold-based selective
inference. The resulting verification objective is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ver}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{view}}+\beta_{\mathrm{agg}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{agg}}+\beta_{\mathrm{cal}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cal}},
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\beta_{\mathrm{agg}}`$ and $`\beta_{\mathrm{cal}}`$ control the
contributions of aggregate-level correctness alignment and
reliability-score regularization, respectively.

#### Reliability-Guided Selective Rethinking.

The calibrated reliability score provides a stopping criterion for
inference-time reasoning. The initial prompt is shown in
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Initial Answer Generation Prompt ‣ Appendix B Prompt Details ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").
At turn $`t`$, after generating a candidate answer $`a_{t}`$, MOTIVE
evaluates it under $`K`$ verification views and computes the aggregated
reliability score $`s_{t}`$. The model accepts the current answer when
its reliability score reaches the threshold $`\tau`$; otherwise, another
rethinking step is performed unless the maximum reasoning budget has
been reached:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{t}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{accept},&s_{t}\geq\tau\ \text{or}\ t=T,\\
\mathrm{rethink},&s_{t}<\tau\ \text{and}\ t<T.\end{cases}
``` |  | (8) |

If no candidate reaches the acceptance threshold before the maximum
reasoning budget is exhausted, the final candidate answer $`a_{T}`$ is
returned.

When rethinking is triggered, the candidate answer, the verification
outputs, and a rethinking instruction are appended to the history:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h_{t+1}=\operatorname{Update}\!\left(h_{t},a_{t},v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(K)},u_{\mathrm{rethink}}\right),
``` |  | (9) |

where $`v_{t}^{(k)}`$ denotes the binary verification output from the
$`k`$-th view, and $`u_{\mathrm{rethink}}`$ instructs the model to
reconsider the image, question, and previous answer. The updated history
$`h_{t+1}`$ is then used to generate a revised answer $`a_{t+1}`$.

To discourage unnecessary rethinking, we include a computation-aware
reward:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{r}_{i}=M\!\left(a_{\mathrm{sel}}^{(i)},y^{*}\right)-c\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\mathbb{I}\!\left[d_{i,t}=\mathrm{rethink}\right],
``` |  | (10) |

where $`c`$ is the computational penalty associated with an additional
rethinking turn. This encourages the model to improve final-answer
correctness while avoiding excessive computation.

#### Overall Objective.

The final training objective combines outcome-level answer optimization
with correctness-grounded multi-view verification:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MOTIVE}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{ver}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ver}},
``` |  | (11) |

where $`\lambda_{\mathrm{ver}}`$ balances answer-generation learning and
verifier supervision. In practice, $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}`$ is
applied only to answer-generation tokens, while
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ver}}`$ is applied to verification outputs. This
separates final-answer optimization from reliability estimation, while
still allowing the verifier to guide rethinking behavior during
inference.

## 4  Experiments

#### Datasets.

We conduct experiments on five visual question answering benchmarks:
ReFocus ([Fu et al., 2025](#bib.bib60)), VQA ([Antol et al.,
2015](#bib.bib54)), SLaKE ([Liu et al., 2021](#bib.bib59)),
ScienceQA ([Lu et al., 2022](#bib.bib55)), and TableVQA ([Kim et al.,
2024](#bib.bib56)). The selected benchmarks cover general image
understanding, structured-image reasoning, medical VQA, scientific
multimodal reasoning, and table-based visual question answering.
Specifically, VQA evaluates general visual question answering over
natural images; ReFocus focuses on structured visual inputs such as
charts and tables; SLaKE targets medical VQA with radiology images;
ScienceQA requires image-grounded scientific reasoning; and TableVQA
tests fine-grained understanding of table images. We follow the official
splits and evaluation protocols for all datasets. More dataset details
are provided in
Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Additional Dataset Details ‣ Appendix A Experiment Settings ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").

#### Models and Baselines.

We evaluate MOTIVE on three representative publicly available VLM
backbones: Qwen2.5-VL-3B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B ([Bai et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib53)), and InternVL3-2B ([Zhu et al., 2025](#bib.bib57)).
These models cover different parameter scales and model families,
allowing us to evaluate whether MOTIVE consistently improves
verification reliability across diverse vision-language backbones rather
than being specific to a particular model architecture. We compare
MOTIVE with several groups of baselines. The first baseline is the
vanilla VLM, which directly generates the final answer without
self-verification. The second group includes prompt-level self-reasoning
and self-verification methods, including Self-Consistency ([Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib61)), Self-Verification ([Weng et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)),
and Chain-of-Verification ([Dhuliawala et al., 2024](#bib.bib58)). The
third group includes recent multimodal self-improvement and
self-correction methods, including ADPO ([Qiu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib62)), R³V ([Cheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib63)), and
LLaVA-SCo ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib64)). For methods originally
developed for language-only reasoning, we adapt their verification or
correction prompts to the vision-language setting by conditioning on the
same image-question input. All baselines are evaluated under the same
datasets, answer-matching protocol, and decoding budget unless otherwise
specified.

#### Implementation Details.

For each backbone, all methods are evaluated using the same dataset
splits and answer-matching protocol. We train MOTIVE with the AdamW
optimizer using an initial learning rate of $`1\times 10^{-6}`$ and BF16
precision. During GRPO training, we use a rollout group size of $`G=8`$
and a sampling temperature of $`1.0`$. We employ $`K=5`$ verification
views and aggregate their reliability scores by arithmetic averaging.
The acceptance threshold is set to $`\tau=0.5`$, the maximum number of
candidate-answer turns is $`T=3`$, and the verification objective is
weighted by $`\lambda_{\mathrm{ver}}=0.1`$. We use official evaluation
scripts when available; otherwise, multiple-choice and open-form answers
are evaluated by exact option matching and normalized answer matching,
respectively. Additional hyperparameters are provided in
Appendix [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Additional Implementation Details ‣ Appendix A Experiment Settings ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").

## 5  Related Work

Self-Verification in Multimodal Reasoning. Recent advances in multimodal
reasoning have increasingly explored self-verification and
self-correction mechanisms to improve the reliability of vision-language
models ([Ding et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)). Rather than relying solely on
a single-pass prediction, these methods encourage models to re-examine
their intermediate reasoning or final answers through reflection ([Yan
et al., 2024](#bib.bib3); [Cao et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib6); [Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib5); [Wang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib14)), critique ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib7); [Shinn
et al., 2023](#bib.bib8); [Wu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib15)),
verifier-guided assessment ([Weng et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Ding and
Zhang, 2025](#bib.bib13); [Wu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib16)), or iterative
correct ([Gou et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Manakul et al.,
2023](#bib.bib11); [Cheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib63); [He et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib64)). Such designs have
shown promise in reducing inconsistent predictions and improving
performance on complex visual reasoning tasks ([Qian et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18); [Lee et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)), where errors often
arise from weak visual grounding or unstable multi-step inference ([Yin
et al., 2024](#bib.bib20); [Si et al., 2021](#bib.bib21)). However, most
existing approaches treat verification primarily as an inference-time
strategy for refining final outputs ([Luo et al., 2025](#bib.bib22); [Li
et al., 2026a](#bib.bib23)), instead of explicitly modeling the
supervision carried by the verification process itself. As a result, the
fine-grained signals embedded in multi-turn self-verification,
especially those associated with intermediate candidate answers, remain
underexploited during training.

Verifiable Supervision and Training Signals for VLMs. Beyond
inference-time correction, another line of research seeks to improve
reasoning models by turning verifiable outcomes into explicit training
signals ([Cobbe et al., 2021](#bib.bib24); [Lightman et al.,
2023](#bib.bib25); [Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib26); [Wu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib2); [Wu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib1)). In tasks where answer
correctness can be automatically checked, prior work has explored
reward-based learning ([Bai et al., 2025b](#bib.bib40); [Meng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30)), process supervision ([Zhang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib37); [Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib38); [Han et al.,
2026](#bib.bib39)), and intermediate guidance to enhance model
reliability without relying entirely on manual annotations ([Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib27); [Zhang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib47); [Zhan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib28); [Shen et al., 2025](#bib.bib29)). These methods
demonstrate that verifiable feedback can serve as an effective and
scalable supervision source for vision-language models, especially in
settings that require structured reasoning and precise decision
making ([Chen and Song, 2025](#bib.bib31); [Marsili and Gkioxari,
2025](#bib.bib32); [Zhou et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)). Nevertheless,
existing approaches typically emphasize final-answer correctness ([Wang
et al., 2025b](#bib.bib34); [Ong et al., 2025](#bib.bib35); [Zhou et
al., 2024](#bib.bib36)), while paying less attention to the supervision
value of intermediate verification trajectories. In particular, they
seldom model the consistency between round-wise candidate answers and
corresponding verification judgments, leaving multi-turn
self-verification signals insufficiently exploited during training.

## 6  Conclusion

In this work, we investigated self-verification in vision-language
models and showed that its effectiveness depends on verifier capability
and prompt design, with no single verification prompt consistently
dominating across tasks. Guided by these findings, we proposed MOTIVE, a
multi-view self-verification framework that aggregates complementary
verification signals and learns correctness-aligned reliability through
view-level and aggregate-level verification supervision. The resulting
reliability score supports accept-or-rethink decisions, enabling
uncertain answers to be selectively revised within the same backbone VLM
without requiring an external judge. Experiments across five benchmarks
and three VLM backbones demonstrate that MOTIVE improves final-answer
performance, verification decision quality, and early stopping.

## Appendix A Experiment Settings

### A.1  Additional Dataset Details

#### ReFocus.

ReFocus ([Fu et al., 2025](#bib.bib60)) evaluates structured-image
understanding over visually complex tables and charts. The benchmark
contains 2,219 question–answer pairs collected from six subsets covering
Wikipedia tables, synthetically rendered tables, table verification,
scientific charts, and horizontal and vertical bar charts. Its questions
require fine-grained information extraction, comparison, counting,
aggregation, and multi-step reasoning over structured visual content.

#### VQA.

VQA ([Antol et al., 2015](#bib.bib54)) is a large-scale open-ended
visual question answering benchmark based primarily on natural images
from Microsoft COCO. Its real-image portion contains approximately
204,000 images and 614,000 human-written questions covering object
recognition, attributes, counting, spatial relationships, activities,
and commonsense reasoning. Multiple human answers are collected for each
question to account for linguistic variation and answer ambiguity.

#### SLaKE.

SLaKE ([Liu et al., 2021](#bib.bib59)) is a bilingual medical VQA
dataset containing 642 radiological images and 14,028 English–Chinese
question–answer pairs. It includes CT, MRI, and X-ray images covering
the head, neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvic cavity. The questions examine
imaging modality, anatomical structures, organ positions, abnormalities,
and medical knowledge. We use the English-language questions in our
experiments.

#### ScienceQA.

ScienceQA ([Lu et al., 2022](#bib.bib55)) contains 21,208
multiple-choice questions collected from elementary- and high-school
science curricula, of which 10,332 are associated with image contexts.
The questions span natural science, social science, and language science
and may include scientific diagrams, natural images, textual contexts,
candidate answers, lectures, and explanations. We use the image-based
subset to evaluate multimodal scientific reasoning.

#### TableVQA.

TableVQA-Bench ([Kim et al., 2024](#bib.bib56)) contains 1,500
question–answer pairs constructed from table images across multiple
domains, including Wikipedia and financial documents. The tables exhibit
diverse layouts, styles, fonts, borders, and cell structures. The
benchmark evaluates visual cell recognition, row–column alignment,
comparison, aggregation, numerical calculation, and reasoning over
structured table content.

### A.2  Additional Implementation Details

All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA B200 GPUs. ¹¹ 1 All experiments,
data and model access, and processing activities were conducted by the
University of Leicester. The internal weights of the verification
objective are set to $`\beta_{\mathrm{agg}}=1.0`$ and
$`\beta_{\mathrm{cal}}=0.1`$, while the computation-aware reward
coefficient is set to $`c=0.05`$. The verification loss weight is set to
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{ver}}=0.1`$. During inference, MOTIVE uses $`K=5`$
verification views, an acceptance threshold of $`\tau=0.5`$, and a
maximum of $`T=3`$ reasoning turns. For each verification view, the
view-specific reliability estimate is given by the probability assigned
to the Yes output, and the final reliability score is obtained by
averaging the estimates across all verification views. The
computation-aware rollout reward is used to compute group-relative
advantages in GRPO, while the auxiliary verification objective jointly
supervises view-specific judgments and aggregated reliability scores.

## Appendix B Prompt Details

### B.1  Direct Verification Prompt Templates

In
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Verification Prompt Design ‣ 2 Preliminary Analysis ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models"),
we analyze different verification prompt designs and observe that no
single direct verification prompt consistently dominates across all
benchmarks. Based on this analysis, we adopt the five predefined Direct
Verification Prompts (DVPs) as complementary verification views in
MOTIVE, as described in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").
These prompts evaluate a candidate answer from different perspectives,
covering overall correctness, visual evidence support, backward evidence
checking, global contextual consistency, and error discovery.
Specifically, DVP-1 performs general correctness verification without
additional constraints; DVP-2 focuses on whether the answer is directly
grounded in observable image evidence; DVP-3 verifies whether the
evidence required by the answer can be identified by reasoning back from
the predicted conclusion; DVP-4 examines the consistency between the
answer, the question context, and the overall visual scene; and DVP-5
adopts an error-seeking perspective to detect potential contradictions
or mistakes in the candidate answer. Together, these complementary views
provide diverse criteria for evaluating answer reliability. The complete
DVP templates are summarized in
Figure [7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ B.1 Direct Verification Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix B Prompt Details ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").

Figure 7: Overview of the five Direct Verification Prompts (DVPs) used
in MOTIVE. Each prompt evaluates the same candidate answer from a
different verification perspective and produces a binary YES/NO
judgment.

### B.2  Initial Answer Generation Prompt

The initial candidate answer is generated using a fixed prompt template,
as shown in
Fig. [8](#A2.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ B.2 Initial Answer Generation Prompt ‣ Appendix B Prompt Details ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").
This prompt is shared across all experiments to maintain a consistent
answer-generation protocol and ensure that the performance improvements
mainly come from the subsequent verification process rather than prompt
variations at the initial stage. The template defines the image-question
input format, the general answering requirements, and the expected
output structure, allowing the VLM to produce a standardized candidate
answer before entering the verification procedure. In particular, the
generated response serves as the initial hypothesis that is evaluated by
the verification modules and may be further refined through the
rethinking process when the reliability estimation indicates
insufficient confidence. By fixing the initial generation prompt, all
comparisons remain under the same inference setting, enabling a fair
evaluation of the proposed verification framework.

### B.3  Rethinking Prompt

![Refer to caption](2610.07018v1/fig/rethinking_prompt.png)

Figure 9: Rethinking prompt. The prompt template used to trigger answer
regeneration when the previous response is considered unreliable by the
verification process.

When the current answer is not accepted by the verification process, the
model enters a rethinking stage using the prompt template shown in
Fig. [9](#A2.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ B.3 Rethinking Prompt ‣ Appendix B Prompt Details ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models").
Different from the initial answer generation prompt, the rethinking
prompt explicitly incorporates the previous response together with the
original image, question, and initial prompt head, allowing the model to
reconsider its earlier prediction under the same task context. The
instruction “A verifier disagrees with your response. Think again.”
encourages the model to revise potentially unreliable answers rather
than simply repeating the previous output. This prompt provides a simple
and consistent mechanism for triggering answer refinement, enabling the
proposed framework to selectively improve uncertain predictions while
avoiding unnecessary regeneration for already reliable responses.

## Appendix C Additional Experiments

### C.1  Sensitivity Analysis of Acceptance Threshold

We further investigate the effect of the acceptance threshold $`\tau`$
used in the accept-or-rethink decision. While a lower threshold allows
the model to terminate verification more easily, it may accept
unreliable answers, whereas a higher threshold encourages additional
verification but may introduce unnecessary rethinking. As shown in
Figure [10](#A3.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ C.1 Sensitivity Analysis of Acceptance Threshold ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiments ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models"),
the performance remains relatively stable across different threshold
values, indicating that MOTIVE is not sensitive to a specific manually
selected threshold. The default setting of $`\tau=0.5`$ achieves the
best performance across all five benchmarks, providing an effective
balance between reliable answer acceptance and selective rethinking.
These results demonstrate that the learned reliability score provides a
reliable decision signal for controlling the verification process.

![Refer to caption](2610.07018v1/fig/acceptance_threshold.png)

Figure 10: Effect of the acceptance threshold $`\tau`$. Accuracy (%) on
five benchmarks under different values of $`\tau`$ using Qwen2.5-VL-3B.

### C.2  Computation-Aware Reward Analysis

We further analyze the effect of the computation-aware reward
coefficient $`c`$ by varying its value while keeping all other training
configurations unchanged. The coefficient $`c`$ penalizes unnecessary
rethinking turns, encouraging the model to terminate verification once a
reliable answer is obtained. As shown in
Fig. [11](#A3.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ C.2 Computation-Aware Reward Analysis ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiments ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models"),
increasing $`c`$ consistently reduces the number of rethinking turns
across different benchmarks, while the final accuracy remains largely
stable. Although larger penalties achieve stronger computation
reduction, an excessive value ($`c=0.10`$) may cause premature stopping
and slightly degrade accuracy. The selected coefficient $`c=0.05`$
provides a favorable trade-off between maintaining answer quality and
avoiding redundant rethinking.

![Refer to caption](2610.07018v1/fig/computation_aware_reward.png)

Figure 11: Effect of computation-aware reward coefficient $`c`$.
Accuracy and rethinking turns are reported on five benchmarks using
Qwen2.5-VL-3B.

### C.3  Maximum Reasoning Turns Analysis

We further analyze the effect of the maximum reasoning budget $`T`$
while keeping all other settings unchanged. As shown in
Fig. [12](#A3.F12 "Figure 12 ‣ C.3 Maximum Reasoning Turns Analysis ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiments ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models"),
increasing the maximum number of reasoning turns from $`T=2`$ to $`T=3`$
consistently improves the performance across VQA, ScienceQA, and
TableVQA, showing that one additional rethinking opportunity is
beneficial for correcting uncertain answers. In contrast, further
increasing the budget to $`T=4`$ or $`T=5`$ only yields marginal gains,
indicating diminishing returns from additional reasoning turns.
Therefore, we adopt $`T=3`$ as the default setting, since it captures
most of the performance benefit while avoiding unnecessary extra
computation.

Figure 12: Effect of the maximum reasoning turns $`T`$. Accuracy (%)
under different maximum reasoning budgets on three benchmarks using
Qwen2.5-VL-3B.

### C.4  Sensitivity Analysis of Verification Views

We further investigate how the number of verification views affects the
performance of MOTIVE. Starting from the predefined verification view
bank, we randomly sample $`K`$ views for verification while keeping all
other training and inference configurations unchanged. As shown in
Table [8](#A3.T8 "Table 8 ‣ C.4 Sensitivity Analysis of Verification Views ‣ Appendix C Additional Experiments ‣ When to Rethink: Learning Multi-PerspectiveSelf-Verification for Vision-Language Models"),
using a single verification view leads to inferior performance due to
limited and potentially biased verification signals. Increasing the
number of views from $`K=1`$ to $`K=3`$ consistently improves accuracy
across all benchmarks, demonstrating the benefit of aggregating
complementary verification perspectives. Further increasing the number
of views to $`K=5`$ achieves the best overall performance, while the
gain over $`K=3`$ becomes smaller, indicating diminishing returns from
additional verification views. Therefore, we adopt five verification
views in MOTIVE to provide sufficient verification diversity.

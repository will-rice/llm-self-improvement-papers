###### Abstract

Advances in large language models (LLMs) are rapidly transforming formal
theorem proving. Yet further progress remains constrained by the lack of
a transparent and effective data recipe for systematically improving
theorem-proving models. In this work, we argue that effective agentic
provers require strong mathematical skill priors to ground theorem
proving and agentic trajectories to enable long-horizon, interactive
proof construction. To this end, we synthesize an agent-native
33.7B-token mid-training corpus covering decomposition, search,
self-verification, and revision, together with verifier-grounded agentic
trajectories. We further introduce the daVinci-Prover harness, which
enables models to construct proofs and interact with a Lean verifier in
an isolated workspace, and use it to collect approximately 36K
high-quality agent trajectories for supervised fine-tuning. Our
mid-training recipe yields an average improvement of 13.7 points over
the matched Qwen3-8B SFT-only baseline across MiniF2F, FATE-M, and
ProofNet. When applied to the Qwen3.5 series, our approach also yields
strong performance across theorem-proving benchmarks and achieves
near-saturating MiniF2F performance with memory compaction. The results
highlight agent-native mid-training as an effective foundation for
developing stronger interactive theorem-proving agents.

## 1 Introduction

Formal theorem proving turns mathematical definitions, statements, and
proofs into computational objects that can be checked, reused, and
extended. Proof assistants such as Lean ([De Moura et al.,
2015](#bib.bib1); [Moura and Ullrich, 2021](#bib.bib2)) provide a
particularly attractive interface for language models: they combine the
reasoning trajectories required by mathematics with machine-checkable
feedback. Recent large language models (LLMs) have made rapid progress
in formal proof generation, demonstrating increasingly strong
performance across problems ranging from high-school olympiads to
research-level mathematics ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Ren et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4); [Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)).

Existing LLM-based theorem provers broadly follow three paradigms. (i)
Whole-proof generation produces a complete proof in a single pass,
offering a simple and efficient inference procedure ([Lin et al.,
2025](#bib.bib5)). (ii) Agentic proving places the model in an
executable environment, where it can inspect the workspace, query
libraries, run Lean, interpret verifier feedback, and iteratively revise
its proof ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15)). (iii) Orchestrated systems coordinate multiple
reasoning or proving components within structured workflows ([Chung et
al., 2026](#bib.bib23)). These paradigms are complementary rather than
mutually exclusive. Across all three, success depends heavily on the
underlying model’s ability to decompose the problem effectively, explore
alternative proof steps, diagnose failures, and integrate feedback over
long reasoning horizons.

Despite this progress, existing formal-reasoning corpora are still
dominated by _whole-proof_ trajectories, where models primarily observe
completed theorem–proof pairs, sometimes augmented with verifier
feedback or revised proofs ([Lin et al., 2025](#bib.bib5); [Ma et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7)). In contrast, many behaviors essential to effective
proving are exposed more explicitly in post-training or inference-time
procedures, including decomposing goals into useful sub-theorems ([Chen
et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)), exploring alternative proof steps ([Ren et
al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Xin et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)), verifying
intermediate reasoning ([Shao et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)), and localizing
and repairing failures. Rather than leaving these capabilities to be
acquired primarily during post-training, we argue that they can be
injected into mid-training data as reusable _mathematical skill priors_,
an idea supported by recent findings on self-improving
reasoners ([Gandhi et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)). Beyond these, agentic
provers also require a distinct form of training data that captures
executable interactions with Lean, including observing proof states,
interpreting verifier feedback, and revising failed attempts. These
agentic trajectories during mid-training can reduce the distribution gap
between static model training and agentic inference, where the model
operates within an executable proving environment ([Zeng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib29); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)).

In this work, we introduce davinci-Prover, a family of formal
theorem-proving models trained on our curated mid-training corpus and
high-quality supervised fine-tuning (SFT) data collected through a
lightweight prover harness. As shown in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving"),
our mid-training corpus covers informal mathematical reasoning, proof
decomposition and assembly, verifier-grounded subgoal search, and error
diagnosis and revision. We further incorporate agent-native trajectories
that expose models to authentic tool calls, execution results,
intermediate proof states, and Lean feedback. Together, these data teach
the model not only to generate valid proofs, but also to develop the
reasoning and interaction patterns required for effective proving in an
executable environment. We instantiate this recipe with a 33.7B-token
mid-training corpus. For post-training, we build a lightweight prover
harness that provides a persistent workspace, typed tools, Lean
verification, and trusted completion checks. Using a strong teacher
model within this harness, we collect and filter approximately 36K
domain-diverse agent trajectories for SFT. We evaluate the resulting
models on various Lean 4 benchmarks. Our main contributions are as
follows:

- •
  We construct a 33.7B-token corpus combining mathematical skill priors
  with environment-native agent trajectories to prepare models for
  interactive theorem proving.
- •
  We develop the daVinci-Prover harness, a lightweight prover harness,
  and curate approximately 36K high-quality, domain-diverse agent
  trajectories for supervised fine-tuning.
- •
  Our mid-training improves Qwen3-8B pass@1 by an average of 13.7 points
  over the matched Base + SFT baseline across MiniF2F, FATE-M, and
  ProofNet, while consistently improving both Qwen3.5-9B and
  Qwen3.5-35B-A3B over their corresponding Base + SFT baselines.

## 2 Background

Publicly available Lean provers for academic use remain relatively
weak ([Lin et al., 2025](#bib.bib5); [Ren et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)),
while stronger systems are either unreleased ([Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)) or prohibitively expensive to run ([Varambally et al.,
2026](#bib.bib21); [Requena et al., 2026](#bib.bib22)). Existing
approaches can be broadly categorized into three directions as
follows ([Chung et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)):

(1) Whole-proof generation and refinement. These methods generate a
complete Lean proof directly from the theorem statement. Representative
approaches include DeepSeek-Prover ([Ren et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)),
Goedel Prover ([Lin et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)), and Kimina-Prover ([Wang
et al., 2025a](#bib.bib24)). More recent systems augment this paradigm
with iterative repair. Goedel-Prover-V2 ([Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib6))
introduces verifier-guided self-correction, where a complete proof is
revised using feedback from the Lean compiler. OProver ([Ma et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7)) further combines whole-proof refinement with retrieval
and compiler feedback, and trains the prover directly on such
multi-round repair trajectories.

![Refer to
caption](2611.16433v1/midtrain_data_example_cards_editable.png)

Figure 2: Overview of our midtrain data generation pipeline.

(2) Agentic provers. Agentic provers are driven by a single LLM agent
that interacts with external tools and adapts its proof strategy based
on their outputs. AxProverBase ([Requena et al., 2026](#bib.bib22))
augments off-the-shelf frontier LLMs with iterative proof refinement,
memory, and search capabilities. Numina-Lean Agent ([Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib27)) similarly builds on Claude Code, exposing Lean
verification, theorem retrieval, and auxiliary reasoning tools through
MCP, thereby enabling a general-purpose coding agent to carry out formal
proof development through iterative tool use.

(3) Orchestrated prover systems. In contrast to single-agent approaches,
orchestrated systems coordinate multiple reasoning or proving components
within a predefined workflow, often separating stages such as
decomposition, proof sketching, verification, and refinement.
Representative systems include Seed-Prover 1.5 ([Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3)), Hilbert ([Varambally et al., 2026](#bib.bib21)), and
LongCat-Flash-Prover ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)), which combine
multiple stages or specialized components to guide formal proof
construction. Goedel-Architect ([Chung et al., 2026](#bib.bib23))
constructs and iteratively refines a dependency-graph blueprint of
formal lemmas, optionally seeded by a natural-language proof, and
dispatches the resulting subgoals to tool-equipped Lean provers in
parallel.

However, sophisticated orchestrated systems rely on capable agentic
provers as their core building blocks; in this work, we focus on
advancing the capability of the agentic prover itself. More related work
can be found in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Related Work ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving").

## 3 Mid-training Data Construction

This section presents our mid-training data construction framework for
developing prover-oriented foundation models. The corpus comprises two
complementary components. The first is designed to instill core
theorem-proving skill priors, including (1) proof decomposition and
assembly, (2) proof search, (3) error diagnosis and revision, and (4)
self-verification. The second consists of environment-native agent
trajectories that capture authentic interactions with tools, execution
outcomes, and verifier feedback.

### 3.1 Theorem-proving Skill Priors

Construction of Decomposition and Assembly Trajectories. We construct
decomposition and assembly trajectories ([Ren et al., 2025](#bib.bib4);
[Zhao et al., 2023](#bib.bib9); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)) to
develop lemma-based proof planning and composition. As shown in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Background ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving")(a),
a teacher LLM receives a Lean theorem and its verified reference proof,
and generates a natural-language proof plan, auxiliary lemmas with
dependency information, and a complete assembled Lean proof. The teacher
is instructed to specify each lemma’s required variables and hypotheses
and preserve the original theorem statement. These trajectories are used
to identify useful intermediate results, formulate self-contained
lemmas, and combine them into a coherent proof.

Construction of Verified Subgoal Search Data. We replay verified Lean
proofs to extract intermediate states and executable snapshots, as shown
in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Background ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving")(b).
For each state, a proposal model generates diverse tactics, which we
combine with the reference tactic, anonymize, shuffle, and execute
independently from the same snapshot. We retain successful closures,
accepted open states, and Lean errors, while excluding timeouts and
infrastructure failures. Each trajectory pair links candidate tactics
and their search intents with the corresponding execution outcomes,
teaching the model to compare alternatives, interpret formal feedback,
and distinguish valid intermediate transitions from completed proofs.

Construction of Revision Trajectories. We construct revision
trajectories that explicitly connect error diagnosis to proof repair.
Given failed proof attempts, diagnostic feedback, and reference
corrections, a teacher LLM synthesizes the diagnosis.
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Background ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving")(c)
shows a structured diagnosis with four fields: location identifies the
affected lemma or proof step; symptom describes the observed failure;
cause explains the underlying issue; and fix proposes a targeted
correction. Each diagnosis is followed by an explicit revision plan and
a revised lemma or proof. These trajectories train the model to localize
errors, explain their causes, and formulate targeted corrections.

Construction of Informal Reasoning and Verification Trajectories. We
incorporate informal trajectories from Nemotron-Math-Proofs-v2 using
DeepSeekMath-V2-style prompts ([Shao et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)). The
data covers proof generation with self-evaluation, proof verification,
and meta-verification. Proof verification identifies gaps and assesses
proof quality, while meta-verification reviews either the generator’s
self-evaluation or an independent verifier’s critique, checking whether
the reported defects and assigned scores are justified. These
complementary trajectories aim to develop rigorous natural-language
reasoning, calibrated self-assessment, and evidence-based critique of
both proofs and their evaluations.

### 3.2 daVinci-Prover harness

![Refer to
caption](2611.16433v1/figures/davinci_prover_harness_swe_agent_style.png)

Figure 3: daVinci-Prover agent harness

The harness formulates theorem proving as an iterative edit–check–revise
loop with Lean4. The input theorem is copied verbatim into
scratch/solution.lean, the agent’s main proof file. At each turn, the
agent selects from seven tools (see
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Tool Schema ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving"))
to edit candidates, inspect files, retrieve Lean declarations, and run
focused checks. The writable scratch/ directory stores proof candidates
and auxiliary files, while sketch/ holds optional lemma decomposition
drafts. The read-only target/ directory provides the Lean/Mathlib
project used for verification. Every Lean-file edit automatically
triggers verification. Draft checks expose local goals and diagnostics,
while placeholder-free candidates undergo strict verification. Tool
results are added to the conversation to guide subsequent revisions. The
episode terminates successfully only when a candidate passes strict Lean
verification and checks confirming that its declaration and elaborated
type match the original theorem.

### 3.3 The Final Dataset: Davinci-prover 33.7B Collection

Data Source. We use a curated corpus drawn from NuminaMath-LEAN ([Wang
et al., 2025a](#bib.bib24)), AoPS ([Art of Problem Solving,
2026](#bib.bib33)), Lean Workbook ([Ying et al., 2024](#bib.bib30)),
Goedel LM/SFT_dataset_v2 ([Lin et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)), and
OProofs ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)). To mitigate data
contamination ([Zhu et al., 2024](#bib.bib20)), we remove duplicate
problems and exclude examples that overlap with our evaluation
benchmarks. These filtering steps remove 0.03427% of the 33.7B-token
mid-training corpus, with token counts computed using the Qwen3.5
tokenizer; see
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Decontamination ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving").

Data Cleaning and Reconstruction. Following
Section [3](#S3 "3 Mid-training Data Construction ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving"),
we use DeepSeek-V4-Flash and DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 ([Xu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10)) to construct an approximately 29B-token mid-training
corpus, whose composition is shown in
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.3 The Final Dataset: Davinci-prover 33.7B Collection ‣ 3 Mid-training Data Construction ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving").
We remove repetitive system prompts, tool schemas, and source-specific
boilerplate while retaining the problems, reasoning traces, tool
interactions, and outcomes. Each example is then reconstructed into a
unified training document and semantically chunked to at most 32K tokens
at natural boundaries.

Data Replay. We define a parent example as one complete logical
trajectory before 32K semantic chunking. We assign each source a replay
budget according to the desired data mixture and select high-quality
parent examples using source-specific scores. To preserve diversity, we
cover distinct problems before selecting additional trajectories from
the same problem. Once a parent example is selected, all of its semantic
chunks are replayed together and processed using the same reconstruction
and packing pipeline as the original corpus. This adds 5.11B replay
tokens, resulting in a final corpus of 33.7B tokens.

## 4 Supervised Fine-Tuning

![Refer to
caption](2611.16433v1/figures/sft_v12_domain_distribution.png)

Figure 4: SFT Data Overview

Data Source. Our SFT training corpus consists of trajectories generated
through harness rollouts. We sample problems from the
Goedel-LM/RL_dataset_V2 dataset ([Lin et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)), which
contains approximately 99K unverified problems, and use
Goedel-LM/SFT_dataset_V2 for domain supplementation. We limit our
interaction turns to 30 turns with a maximum of 200 tool calls. The
trajectories are collected using DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731.

SFT Data Overview. We ultimately collect approximately 36K high-quality
SFT trajectories from successful theorem-proving runs. The resulting
dataset spans a broad range of mathematical domains, including algebra,
number theory, geometry, analysis, combinatorics, topology, probability
and statistics, linear algebra, abstract algebra, and logic and set
theory, as shown in
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 Supervised Fine-Tuning ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving").
Following the filtering strategy of [Raoof et al. (2026)](#bib.bib16),
we remove low-quality trajectories exhibiting undesirable behaviors,
such as excessive repetition or unnecessary tool calls, as well as
trajectories containing fewer than 5 turns. We further retain only
trajectories that fit within a 144K-token context window.

### 5.2 Agentic Prover Performance

The overall performance results are presented in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving").
We provide the following analysis:

Agentic prover outperforms existing whole-proof baselines.
DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, evaluated with the daVinci-Prover harness,
achieves the highest reported scores across all five benchmarks. Its
interactive pass@8 results exceed the reported pass@32 results of
whole-proof generation and refinement methods. The results highlight the
potential of agentic proving to enable strong theorem proving with whole
proof.

High-quality SFT trajectories effectively elicit theorem-proving
capabilities. We further use the proof trajectories generated by the
daVinci-Prover harness as SFT data to train Qwen3.5 models. Despite
their substantially smaller model sizes, the resulting models achieve
strong performance across all benchmarks, indicating that trajectories
collected by the harness provide effective supervision for transferring
its theorem-proving behavior to smaller models.

Mid-training consistently improves performance over the base models.
Across both model scales, the Midtrain + SFT variants consistently
outperform their corresponding Base + SFT counterparts. The gains are
particularly pronounced on more challenging benchmarks such as
MathOlympiad and Putnam. For Qwen3.5-9B, mid-training improves
MathOlympiad from 32.3 to 35.3 and Putnam from 22.3 to 24.9, while
maintaining consistent improvements on the other benchmarks. A similar
trend is observed for Qwen3.5-35B. These results suggest that
mid-training provides a stronger initialization for subsequent
theorem-proving SFT, with particularly clear benefits on more difficult
mathematical reasoning tasks.

## 6 IN-DEPTH ANALYSES

### 6.1 Training Process

![Refer to

22.5B, 30B, and 33.7B tokens. We apply the same two-epoch SFT procedure

Analysis. Agentic theorem-proving performance exhibits an approximately
log-linear trend with mid- training data scale. Much of the improvement
is achieved within the first 15B tokens, yielding gains of 3.96 and
11.11 percentage points over the base model on miniF2F and FATE-M,

targeted mid-training can effectively develop agentic theorem proving
capabilities.

### 6.4 Ablation on daVinci-Prover

Setup. We explore the contribution of each data component on the
Qwen3.5-9B models.

Analysis. As shown in
Table [5](#S6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 6.4 Ablation on daVinci-Prover ‣ 6 IN-DEPTH ANALYSES ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving"),
the ablation highlights the importance of _agent-native_ supervision
beyond generic theorem-proving skill acquisition. Despite using only
8.4B tokens, $`\mathcal{D}_{agent}`$ produces larger gains than the
25.3B-token $`\mathcal{D}_{skill}`$ on MiniF2F and Fate-M. A likely
explanation is distribution alignment: $`\mathcal{D}_{agent}`$ exposes
the model to the same interaction primitives encountered at inference
time, thereby bridging the gap between static next-token training and
interactive proving. In contrast, $`\mathcal{D}_{skill}`$ primarily
strengthens reusable reasoning priors such as decomposition, search,
diagnosis, and self-verification. Importantly, these two forms of
supervision are complementary rather than substitutable. Combining them
consistently outperforms either component alone, indicating that agentic
proving benefits from both knowing how to reason about a proof and
knowing how to realize that reasoning through environment interaction.

### 6.5 Comparison with Existing Corpora

Setup. We perform mid-training on Qwen3-8B-Base using 33B tokens,
followed by agentic SFT for 5 epochs. We compare the resulting model
against Qwen3-8B-Base without mid-training and Oprover-8B-Base ([Ma et
al., 2026](#bib.bib7)), which is trained with 65B mid-training tokens.
For all base models, we use YaRN ([Peng et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)) to
extend the context length from 32K to 256K.

Analysis. As shown in
Table [5](#S6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 6.4 Ablation on daVinci-Prover ‣ 6 IN-DEPTH ANALYSES ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving"),
our mid-training substantially improves performance over Qwen3-8B-Base
across all three benchmarks, confirming that the gains cannot be
attributed solely to the subsequent agentic SFT. Moreover, our model
consistently outperforms Oprover-8B-Base, with particularly large
margins on Fate-M and ProofNet, despite using only 33B mid-training
tokens compared with Oprover’s 65B. This suggests that the effectiveness
of mid-training depends not only on data scale, but critically on its
composition: our combination of theorem-proving skill priors and
agent-native trajectories provides more targeted supervision for
downstream interactive proving and transfers effectively across diverse
formal reasoning problems.

Setup. To support long-horizon difficult proofs, we use a simple,
model-free trajectory compression method. Once the context length
reaches 200K tokens, we trigger compression by replacing older
interactions with deterministically extracted tool evidence, while
preserving the original theorem, current Lean proof, and recent
interactions. We allow up to 8 compactions, and other settings remain
unchanged.

Analysis. Memory compaction consistently improves performance, with the
largest gains on the more challenging FATE-H benchmark. The improvement
is especially pronounced for the 35B model, suggesting that effective
context management is important for long-horizon proving and can better
expose the capacity of larger models.

## 7 Conculusion

We introduced daVinci-Prover, combining agent-native mid-training with
verifier-grounded supervised fine-tuning for interactive theorem proving
based on a single harness. Experiments demonstrate consistent
improvements over SFT-only baselines, while ablations support the
complementary roles of mathematical skill priors and environment-native
trajectories. These findings highlight agent-native mid-training as an
effective foundation for developing stronger theorem-proving agents.

## Limitation

During the mid-training phase, we do not replay any generalization data.
However, we believe that incorporating generalization replay is both
necessary and important for maintaining and improving the model’s
generalization capabilities.

## Appendix A Related Work

#### Mid-training.

Recent work views mid-training as a useful bridge between pre-training
and post-training. Instead of moving directly from pre-training to SFT
or RL, mid-training uses cleaner and more task-relevant data to better
prepare the model, often with learning-rate annealing ([Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib32); [Tu et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)). For example,
OctoThinker ([Wang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib19)) shows that
reasoning-focused mid-training can improve both RL efficiency and final
performance.

#### Agentic Proving

Agentic provers make the interaction loop part of the solution. Prover
Agent coordinates informal reasoning, formal proving, auxiliary lemmas,
and Lean feedback ([Baba et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)). OProver retrieves
verified proofs, repairs failures, and feeds successful interactions
back into training ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)); LongCat-Flash-Prover
trains auto-formalization, sketching, proving, and tool use through
agentic reinforcement learning ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). Thus,
interaction traces can serve as training data rather than only
inference-time scaffolding.

#### Agent-Native Mid-Training

Software-engineering research offers a complementary training
perspective: Kimi-Dev treats structured workflow training as
transferable skill priors, while DaVinci-Dev combines contextually and
environmentally native trajectories during mid-training ([Yang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11); [Zeng et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)). We also focus on
the stage at which agents acquire capabilities. We organize
decomposition, search, revision, and environment interaction as
complementary mid-training skills, then study whether these priors
transfer from whole-proof generation to downstream interactive proving.

Effect of midtraining on proof decomposition. Midtrained models produce
more explicit intermediate steps in their first checked target proof,
particularly on harder problems. In the high-difficulty group, defined
as problems unsolved by the DeepSeek reference across eight attempts,
the average number of local have/suffices steps increases from 5.13 to
9.57 at 9B and from 5.73 to 9.63 at 35B. The corresponding step
densities also increase, from 6.22 to 9.33 and from 6.28 to 8.15 per
1,000 lexical source tokens, respectively. Thus, these differences are
not explained solely by longer code. By contrast, Base and Midtrain
models show similar step counts in the low difficulty group. This
pattern suggests that midtraining is associated with more explicit
decomposition when constructing initial proofs for challenging problems.

Comparison with DeepSeek. DeepSeek produces more local steps in absolute
terms, especially in the medium-difficulty group, but its step density
is lower than that of the Midtrained Qwen models. In the high-difficulty
group, its average step count is 10.17, close to the approximately ten
steps produced by both Midtrained Qwen models. Therefore, DeepSeek’s
higher overall success rate cannot be attributed simply to producing
more intermediate steps.

## Appendix C Decontamination

To ensure a fair evaluation, we perform benchmark-level decontamination
against all evaluation sets listed in
Table [7](#A3.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Appendix C Decontamination ‣ daVinci-Prover: Agent-Native Mid-Training for Formal Theorem Proving").
For Lean data, we extract the theorem signature by removing nested
comments and proof bodies, normalize Unicode characters, tokenize the
remaining statement, and anonymize the declaration name. We then perform
exact matching against all available benchmark variants. To avoid false
positives caused by short or generic statements, a token-level formal
match is automatically confirmed only if the anonymized signature
contains at least 20 tokens and 80 normalized characters. Exact
provenance links and byte-identical raw statements are also treated as
confirmed matches.

For informal data, we apply Unicode and math-aware normalization and
require an exact match of the complete problem statement with at least
12 content tokens. Matches based only on theorem names or approximate
textual similarity are not automatically classified as contamination;
instead, they are reserved for manual review. For each confirmed match,
we remove the entire logical parent example, including all of its
semantic chunks and any replayed copies derived from it.

Before filtering, we identify 29 overlapping problems among the 2,137
evaluation problems (1.36%). When conservatively counting every token in
each matched parent trajectory, these examples account for at most
11.55M tokens, corresponding to 0.03427% of the 33.7B-token mid-training
corpus.

## Appendix F Data Synthesis Costs

Teacher deployment. We deployed DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 on 48 NVIDIA H200
GPUs for approximately 20 days, with the teacher service shared across
mid-training and SFT data synthesis. This corresponds to approximately
$`48\times 20\times 24=23{,}040`$ allocated H200 GPU-hours. This
deployment-level budget includes unsuccessful attempts, discarded
generations, and idle periods, in addition to generations retained in
the final datasets.

CPU resources for Lean verification. The CPU service supporting Lean
verification used approximately eight worker instances, each provisioned
with 20 CPU cores and 80 GB of memory, for roughly the same 20-day
period. The estimated aggregate allocation was therefore 160 CPU cores
and 640 GB of memory, corresponding to approximately
$`8\times 20\times 20\times 24=76{,}800`$ allocated CPU core-hours.

Public-cloud rental equivalent. For a public-cloud comparison, Runpod
lists H200 Secure Cloud instance and H200 SXM cluster rates of US\$5.29
and US\$6.35 per GPU-hour, respectively, as of November 9, 2026.¹¹ 1
[https://www.runpod.io/pricing](https://www.runpod.io/pricing), accessed
November 9, 2026. Applying these rates to the teacher deployment yields

and excludes the additional CPU service for Lean verification and
separate storage charges.

## Appendix G Hyperparameters

### G.1 Mid-Training Details

We perform full-parameter mid-training using the autoregressive
next-token prediction objective, while retaining the one-layer
multi-token prediction (MTP) objective with a loss weight of 0.1. For
the MoE model, we retain the pretrained routing configuration and apply
a global-batch auxiliary load-balancing loss with a coefficient of
$`10^{-3}`$. We use AdamW with a weight decay of 0.1. The learning rate
is linearly warmed up for the first 3% of training steps and then
cosine-decayed to zero. For offline-packed 32K-token sequences, we
enforce sample-level isolation using per-sample cumulative sequence
boundaries. These boundaries induce block-diagonal causal attention,
reset positional indices and Gated DeltaNet states, and mask next-token

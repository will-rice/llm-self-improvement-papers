###### Abstract.

Existing LLM-based approaches for software Requirements Engineering (RE)
typically rely on basic prompting strategies or rudimentary agent
collaboration, under-utilizing the full potential of multi-agent
systems. Meanwhile, available datasets focus on isolated subtasks, such
as requirements extraction, classification, and completeness detection,
leaving the absence of an end-to-end RE benchmark that spans from
requirements elicitation to generation. We present Agent4RE -- a
self-refining multi-agent RE system that orchestrates specialized agents
and incorporates two iterative improvement loops. To support evaluation,
we construct RE-E2E¹¹ 1 We provide RE-E2E benchmark dataset, Agent4RE
source code, and supplementary materials at
[https://github.com/markustyj/Agent4RE_ASE-2026](https://github.com/markustyj/Agent4RE_ASE-2026).
– a real-world dataset built from human-written requirement
specifications, enabling end-to-end assessment of RE workflows. Building
on this foundation, we further propose two enhanced Agent4RE versions
that incorporate either autonomous self-refinement or structured human
feedback, and analyze their strengths and limitations across different
scenarios. Evaluation on 8 Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrates
that all three Agent4RE variants consistently outperform a
domain-context-augmented prompting baseline by average 8% in text-based
metrics. The two enhanced variants achieve the highest LLM-as-a-judge
and human ratings, surpassing two RE baselines by approximately 0.8
points on a four-point scale. This consistent performance establishes
Agent4RE as a practical end-to-end RE solution for industrial
environments.

## 1. Introduction

Requirements Engineering (RE) serves as a preliminary stage in software
development, establishing a shared understanding of stakeholders’ needs,
goals, and constraints while providing the foundation for subsequent
software design, implementation, and verification ([Laplante and Kassab,
2022](#bib.bib1)). Traditionally, this process has required substantial
effort from multiple roles – developers, business analysts, domain
experts, and project managers – who must iteratively perform
elicitation, analysis, specification, and validation together to produce
complete, consistent, and clear requirement documentation ([Nuseibeh and
Easterbrook, 2000](#bib.bib2)). Given this labor-intensive nature and
the fact that defects originating from this phase are significantly more
expensive to fix in later stages, improvements in RE efficiency and
quality can yield substantial benefits across the entire software
development lifecycle.

Recently, advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have opened new
avenues for requirements engineering ([Ferrari and Spoletini,
2025](#bib.bib8)). With superior capabilities in domain knowledge
inference, natural language understanding, and ambiguity detection, LLMs
are well suited to accelerate and assist diverse RE sub-tasks, such as
extracting requirements from unstructured inputs, identifying
inconsistencies in RE specifications, and generating documentation based
on stakeholder instructions ([Zadenoori et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)).
However, existing studies predominately focus on such isolated RE
sub-tasks, achieving success only on individual activities, yet offering
limited support for the complex, interconnected requirements engineering
processes in real-world scenarios. While recent efforts have explored
LLM-based interactive systems and agents to aggregate these isolated
capabilities ([Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib48)), no systematic end-to-end
solution has been proposed and evaluated for the full RE lifecycle.
Similarly, this fragmentation extends to data resources: accessible
datasets are limited to separate RE tasks and no comprehensive
open-source benchmark has been released to assess the entire
requirements engineering process.

To address this gap, we propose Agent4RE – a self-refining multi-agent

iterative elicitation, generation, and traceable refinement of

Combined with RE-E2E – an end-to-end RE dataset, our framework mitigates
the bottleneck of current LLM-based RE research, bridging isolated
task-specific approaches with complete RE workflows. The main
contribution of this work can be summarized as follows:

- (i)
  We propose Agent4RE – an autonomous multi-agent system for
  requirements engineering, which features two iterative improvement
  loops for self-refinement.
- (ii)
  We construct RE-E2E – an RE benchmark built from full-scale
  human-written requirement specifications. To the best of our
  knowledge, this represents the first end-to-end dataset designed for
  complete requirements engineering processes, spanning from initial
  project descriptions to finalized requirement specifications.
- (iii)
  We analyze the performance of Agent4RE by integrating eight frontier
  LLMs covering different performance tiers, studying its effectiveness
  and robustness across diverse model capabilities and computational
  budgets. Furthermore, we showcase the self-refinement features of all
  language models by analyzing their iterative improvement loops.
- (iv)
  We validate our approach by comparing the generated requirement
  specifications with the ground-truth in RE-E2E. Evaluation results
  reveal that all Agent4RE variants consistently outperform a
  context-augmented prompting baseline by average 8% in text-based
  metrics. Furthermore, the two enhanced variants achieve the highest
  LLM-as-a-judge and human ratings, surpassing two baselines by
  approximately 0.8 points on a four-point scale.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1. Large Language Models for RE-related Tasks

Extensive language model-based approaches have been proposed for diverse
stages throughout the requirements engineering cycle. Building on the
early transformer architecture ([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib11)),
researchers fine-tuned BERT to detect anaphoric ambiguity in
requirements and evaluated it against traditional supervised entity
recognition techniques ([Ezzini et al., 2022](#bib.bib9)). A similar
approach was introduced to identify intra-domain ambiguities in the
requirements of interdisciplinary projects ([Moharil and Sharma,
2023](#bib.bib10)). Given BERT’s encoder-based architecture, parallel
studies trained specialized BERT variants for requirements
classification, achieving improved accuracy over machine learning
techniques like decision trees and support vector machines ([Hey et al.,
2020](#bib.bib13); [Luo et al., 2023](#bib.bib12)). As language models
have scaled up, strategic few-shot prompting has emerged as a more
streamlined solution to requirements classification, delivering on-par
performance with fine-tuned models while eliminating the computational
overhead of training ([El-Hajjami et al., 2023](#bib.bib16); [Tang et
al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Tang et al., 2025](#bib.bib14)).

The superior reasoning capabilities of these large-scale models enable
effective requirements analysis while their generative nature aligns
with the needs for requirements elicitation and specification
generation. Given this potential, a preliminary evaluation ([Arora et
al., 2024](#bib.bib17)) explored the role of LLMs across different RE
stages and suggested tailored prompt patterns for elicitation, analysis,
specification, and validation tasks. Building on this foundation, LLMREI
([Korn et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)) employs LLMs as automated interviewers
within a chatbot to simulate the requirements elicitation process with
human stakeholders, designed to minimize human intervention and mitigate
common interview-related errors. SpecGen ([Ma et al., 2025](#bib.bib19))
leveraged a two-phase prompting approach to generate precise behavioral
comments for program-level specifications, while ReqGPT ([Schiller et
al., 2025](#bib.bib20)), a 7B-parameter domain-specific LLM fine-tuned
on over one hundred curated requirement lists, was developed to improve
specification quality. Despite these breakthroughs in specific RE
aspects, existing methods still fall short in delivering complete
requirement specifications for production environments, indicating the
need for more robust and comprehensive approaches.

### 2.2. LLM-based Agentic Systems for Automated RE Process

The collaborative workflow of multi-agent systems offers a promising
paradigm to tackle more complex end-to-end RE challenges ([Tang and
Runkler, 2026](#bib.bib21)). As an early exploration ([Sami et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22)), researchers implemented an LLM-based agentic system
with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to automate user story elicitation, requirements
generation, and prioritization for agile software projects. Extending
this prior work, a subsequent study proposed a role-based multi-agent
system that emulates stakeholder roles to collaboratively generate and
prioritize requirements from project descriptions ([Sami et al.,
2026](#bib.bib23)). Another modular architecture ReqInOne breaks down
requirements generation into three sub-tasks: summary, extraction, and
classification ([Zhu et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)), using tailored prompt
templates to improve the quality and consistency of LLM outputs. In
comparison, Elicitron has focused on more specific RE activities ([Ataei
et al., 2024](#bib.bib24)). The system simulates elicitation interviews
to explore product experiences and identify stakeholders’ needs, thus
enabling the discovery of unforeseen use cases through structured LLM
interactions. Moving toward end-to-end automation, iReDev ([Jin et al.,
2025](#bib.bib25)), previously also called MARE ([Jin et al.,
2024](#bib.bib26)), represents a knowledge-driven multi-agent framework
designed to address the full requirements engineering process. It
incorporates human expert knowledge into six agents via chain-of-thought
prompting and structured reasoning, enabling them to manage an
event-driven artifact pool collaboratively. Nevertheless, due to the
absence of resources for end-to-end RE assessment, the evaluation
focused primarily on intermediate steps such as requirements modeling
and entity extraction. The final specifications were evaluated only
through subjective metrics such as completeness, correctness, and
consistency.

## 3. Agent4RE

Our multi-agent system offers three different versions, each with
distinct advantages: 1) a sequential workflow without feedback for
streamlined RE processes; 2) a workflow with self-refinement that
enables the system to iteratively improve the outputs; 3) a workflow
with human feedback that incorporates domain experts to guide the
refinement process.

The iterative requirements elicitation process represents the core step
of this workflow and is self-controlled by an interviewer agent
$`A_{I}`$ and a stakeholder agent $`A_{S}`$. Given a message from the
orchestrator agent, the interviewer agent initiates the conversation by
posing a list of project-related questions $`q`$. Meanwhile, the
stakeholder agent familiarizes itself with the project domain by reading
the orchestrator-generated project summary and combing its own knowledge
base to answer interviewers’ questions.

With the detailed report, the requirements generation agent $`A_{G}`$
produces the initial specification document: $`D_{0}=A_{G}(R,T_{re})`$.
To ensure structural compliance, the required specification templates
$`T_{re}`$ ([Standard, 1998](#bib.bib29); [Standard, 2018](#bib.bib30))
are integrated into LLMs’ system message as a reference, steering the
agent to strictly follow the structure. Due to the document length, the
generation time typically ranges from a few seconds to over one minute,
depending on the model size and inference configuration. Once finished,
the generated specification is returned to the orchestrator for
validation, which verifies the document before handing over to human
users. In case of any deficiencies, it leverages its supervisory control
over other agents to perform corrective action. Further investigation of
this self-controlled iterative elicitation loop is provided in Section

### 3.2. Agentic Workflow with Self-refinement

As illustrated in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ Agent4RE: A Self-Refining Multi-agent Framework for End-to-End Software Requirements Engineering and Benchmarking"),
an additional refinement loop (in orange) is introduced to further
enhance the quality of the generated specifications. A refactoring agent
$`A_{R}`$ serves as the central component in this process, analyzing the
verification results from the initial loop (in blue) and applying
pre-defined requirements quality criteria such as unambiguity,
completeness, and consistency to formulate improvement suggestions.
These generated refactoring suggestions are then forwarded to the
generation agent, which refines the original specification accordingly.
This agent-based self-refinement loop can also be executed multiple
times until the specification meets the demanded quality standards,
which can be formally represented as: $`A_{R}\big(D_{k},F_{a}\big)`$,
where $`D_{k}`$ represents the generated specification document in the
$`k`$-th round and $`F_{a}`$ the set of feedback from agents.

### 3.3. Agentic Workflow with Human Feedback

While automated agentic workflows streamline the RE process, including
human-in-the-loop at critical decision points can further enhance the
quality and reliability of the generated specifications ([Natarajan et
al., 2025](#bib.bib28)). Therefore, in the third version of Agent4RE, we
incorporate human feedback $`F_{h}`$ after the initial sequential loop
and prior to the refactoring loop. In this case, human developers
provide domain-specific insights and expertise alongside the
verification comments from the orchestrator agent to guide the
refactoring agent. In our experiments, all human participants are
experienced software engineers and they have access to the ground-truth
specifications from the annotated dataset. However, to simulate
realistic scenarios, human feedback is limited to concise bullet points
or high-level hints regarding specification structure rather than
detailed corrections.

The progression of the document from state $`D_{k}`$ to $`D_{k+1}`$
across the three distinct versions of Agent4RE can be formulated as:

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (3) |     |

````math
D_{k+1}=A_{G}\Big(D_{k},\;A_{R}\big(D_{k},F_{a},F_{h}\big)\Big).

Existing data resources for RE predominantly focus on individual stages,
suffering from several limitations: incomplete coverage, poor community
visibility, and limited support for end-to-end evaluation ([Motger et
al., 2025](#bib.bib3)). For instance, PROMISE ([Li et al.,
2014](#bib.bib4)), a requirements classification benchmark extensively
used in prior work, contains only isolated requirement items. Similarly,
PURE ([Ferrari et al., 2017](#bib.bib5)) provides requirement
specification documents for various software systems, but lacks a
unified structural template for standardized processing and systematic
evaluation. And a more recent RE dataset ([Bragilovski et al.,
2024](#bib.bib6)) provides a collection of user stories pairing with the
corresponding domain models, yet it still excludes complete requirement
specifications. As LLM capabilities and agentic workflows advance
continuously, the ambition within the research community and industry
has grown beyond addressing isolated RE tasks toward autonomous
management of the entire RE lifecycle. Addressing data scarcity has
become an urgent priority to enable the exploration of end-to-end
agentic solutions.

To address this research gap, we curate RE-E2E, a dataset used to
benchmark the performance of different frameworks for end-to-end
software requirement engineering tasks. We selectively collect 50
complete requirement specifications in PDF format from real-world
software repositories ([Repositories, 2025](#bib.bib32)). Detailed
statistics of the files are presented in Table
[4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.1. RE-E2E Dataset ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Agent4RE: A Self-Refining Multi-agent Framework for End-to-End Software Requirements Engineering and Benchmarking").
All files originate from experienced software engineers and cover
various software domains, such as software control, e-commerce, and
security verification systems, and translation software.

Based on the original specification template used by developers, we
divide the collected PDF files into two sets: one adhering to IEEE 830
([Standard, 1998](#bib.bib29)), which defines the structure for
traditional software requirements specifications, and another following
IEEE 29148 ([Standard, 2018](#bib.bib30)), an updated standard that
incorporates systems and software engineering requirements with more
modern practices. Specifically, we use regular expressions ([Friedl,
2006](#bib.bib33)) to clean noise such as unexpected newline characters
and consecutive spaces, obtaining normalized plain text. However, in
some edge cases, the original section names of human-written
specifications do not strictly follow the IEEE format. To create a
dataset with unified structure, we match these sections with the
official IEEE section names based on their semantic embeddings. To
ensure dataset reproducibility, we use the open-source
Qwen3-embedding-8B to match RE-sections. If the cosine similarity
between two sections falls below 0.7, human annotators must intervene.
They review each section independently and reach consensus on whether to
remove or retain it as empty. The cross-annotator agreement is 96.7%,
meaning that all annotators make the same binary decision in most cases.
For the remaining 3.3%, annotators follow majority vote. In Table
[4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.1. RE-E2E Dataset ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Agent4RE: A Self-Refining Multi-agent Framework for End-to-End Software Requirements Engineering and Benchmarking"),
we record the section empty rates.

Based on the finalized specifications, human annotators manually write
the initial project descriptions, which are then syntactically checked
by LLMs to ensure grammatical correctness and formatting compliance.
Notably, these annotators also serve as human evaluators in experiments.
Their prior experience with the annotation process ensures their
familiarity with the content. Further details about their information
are provided in Section
[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3. Metrics ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Agent4RE: A Self-Refining Multi-agent Framework for End-to-End Software Requirements Engineering and Benchmarking").
The resulting project descriptions serve as inputs for Agent4RE. The
processed descriptions and software requirement specifications are
provided in the supplementary materials.

### 4.2. Selected Language Models

Our experiments involve eight LLMs spanning different performance tiers
to investigate the full potential of our agentic framework. We deploy
four frontier models via APIs, including GPT-5 ([OpenAI,
2025a](#bib.bib36)), GPT-4.1 ([OpenAI, 2025b](#bib.bib35)), GPT-4o
([Hurst et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)), and Gemini-3-flash ([Comanici et
al., 2025](#bib.bib37)), which represent the forefront of LLM
capabilities. To complement these proprietary models, we incorporate
four open-source alternatives that ensure reproducibility and broader
applicability: LLaMA3.1-8B from Meta AI ([Dubey et al.,
2024](#bib.bib38)), Qwen3-8B from Alibaba ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib39)), Ministral3-8B from Mistral ([MistralAI,
2025](#bib.bib40)), and Gemma4-4B from Google ([Deepmind,
2026](#bib.bib41)).

### 4.3. Metrics

To compare the generated requirement specifications with the
ground-truth in RE-E2E, we employ an extensive set of evaluation
approaches, including text-based metrics, semantic-based metrics,
LLM-as-a-judge, and human evaluators.

- 1)
  Regarding text-based metrics, we apply METEOR ([Banerjee and Lavie,
  2005](#bib.bib42)), BLEU-1 ([Papineni et al., 2002](#bib.bib44)), and
  ROUGE-L ([Lin, 2004](#bib.bib43)). METEOR evaluates text similarity
  through a harmonic mean of precision and recall, incorporating
  stemming, synonymy, and word order penalties. BLEU-1 measures the
  proportion of individual words (unigrams) in the generated text that
  match the reference text, while ROUGE-L evaluates the longest common
  subsequence between the candidate and reference texts.
- (2)
  For semantic-based metrics, we apply BERTScore ([Zhang et al.,
  2019](#bib.bib45)) and SentenceBert ([Reimers and Gurevych,
  2019](#bib.bib46)). BERTScore computes token-level semantic similarity
  by comparing BERT-based word embeddings between the candidate and
  reference texts, while SentenceBert measures sentence-level similarity
  by encoding texts into dense vector representations and calculating
  their cosine similarity.
- (3)
  LLM-as-a-judge leverages prompt-based assessment to grade the
  generated requirement specifications. Figure
  [4](#acmlabel4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.3. Metrics ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Agent4RE: A Self-Refining Multi-agent Framework for End-to-End Software Requirements Engineering and Benchmarking")
  presents the prompt template used for this approach, with grades
  assigned on a scale from 1 to 4. To assess RE quality in practice,
  developers typically require a language checker for ambiguity, an
  app-lifecycle tool for completeness, and a formal/model checker for
  logical consistency, leading to increased toolchain complexity. Since
  these quality criteria are fundamentally grounded in written text and
  requirements, LLMs are natural requirements evaluators given their
  expertise in language processing. However, this suitability does not
  apply to all model scales. While large-scale models demonstrate
  promising capabilities in judging text quality, compact models often
  struggle ([Gu et al.,](#bib.bib47) ) – even the best one misjudges.
  Therefore, we deploy this evaluation strategy exclusively to four
  top-tier models (GPT-5, GPT-4.1, GPT-4o, and Gemini-3-flash).
- (4)
  We further include human evaluation for comparison. To align with
  LLM-as-a-judge, three software developers from industry assess
  summaries independently on a scale from 1 to 4, with final scores
  averaged for fairness. We select human evaluators based on their
  software development experience and RE domain expertise. Finally, we
  choose three senior software engineers, each with two to three years
  of development experience in industry. In addition to their own
  judgment criteria, we also provide them with the prompt and grading
  schema used in LLM-as-a-judge to ensure consistent evaluation.

### 4.4. RE Specification Generation

Since iterative human feedback has no theoretical upper limit on
performance, we perform one optimization loop for all Agent4RE variants
to ensure fair comparison. Table
[5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 4.4. RE Specification Generation ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Agent4RE: A Self-Refining Multi-agent Framework for End-to-End Software Requirements Engineering and Benchmarking")
demonstrates the evaluation results for end-to-end specification
generation in the next page. Agent4RE significantly outperforms the two
baselines in most considered metrics, with each variant exhibiting
superior performance in different evaluation dimensions. For traditional
text- and semantic-based metrics, incorporating high-level feedback from
human developers yields the best results, achieving 0.251 in METEOR,
0.273 in Rouge-L, 0.063 in BLEU-1, 0.838 in BERT F1 score, and 0.392 in
Sentence-BERT similarity. As for LLM-as-a-judge, pure self-refinement
with GPT-5 slightly outperforms the human-in-the-loop variant, with both
GPT-5 and GPT-4.1 models securing perfect 4.0 ratings when evaluated by
GPT-4.1. However, upon human evaluation, self-refinement with human
feedback still performs best, scoring the highest 3.29 out of 4. In
comparison, the two baselines fall short, reaching 2.22 and 2.87
respectively in human evaluation.

If we zoom in model performance for detailed analysis, three GPT models
lead the performance in most metrics, especially when deployed within
the sequential workflow without feedback or self-refinement with human
feedback. Among them, the latest GPT-5 model demonstrates exceptional
performance in LLM-as-a-judge and human evaluation, achieving
near-perfect scores from other LLM evaluators when integrated into the
Agent4RE framework. However, an unexpected finding emerges with pure
self-refinement: iterative improvement loops lead to degraded
performance on semantic-based metrics, as evidenced by Sentence BERT.
This phenomenon may stem from LLMs’ deliberate attempts to modify
content, and such purposeful revisions can result in substantial
semantic drift. We provide a case study in the supplementary materials,
including an example of software project description, human feedback,
and the generated requirement specifications. Due to the length of the
generated content, a separate JSON file recording the output of each
agent throughout the complete agentic workflow is included in the
uploaded repository.

Following the discussion of model performance on RE, we compare the
baselines and all Agent4RE variants by averaging the performance across
all LLMs. For conciseness, we present the results using four
representative metrics from each category: human evaluation,
GPT-5-as-a-judge, BERTScore, and METEOR.

As illustrated in Figure

all three Agent4RE variants outperform the two baselines.
Self-refinement with human feedback achieves the best average score
across all four metrics. Compared to the prompting baseline, this
variant achieves approximately 4% improvements in METEOR and an average
of 0.8 points in human and LLM evaluation. However, we notice a dip in
performance in the lower bound of pure self-refinement, particularly in
METEOR and BERTScore, which indicates that less capable language models
struggle to support iterative self-improvement, and a minimum threshold
of model capacity is required to make self-refinement strategies
effective. Furthermore, an upward trend is observed from left to right,
reflecting the progressive advancements in three key dimensions: from
basic prompting strategies to more complicated agentic approaches, from
linear workflows to iterative self-refined loops, and from model-only
generation to human-guided collaborative systems.

## 5. Conclusions and Future Work

The proposed Agent4RE framework significantly outperforms existing
baselines on the newly released RE-E2E dataset, leveraging five
collaborative self-refining agents. While the pure self-refinement
variant strengthens the basic sequential workflow, it requires
sufficient model capacity to be effective. After multiple iterative
improvement loops, less capable models may struggle and produce content
with substantial semantic drift. In comparison, strategically
incorporating human expertise at critical decision point proves to be
beneficial for the agentic system, guiding them toward desired quality
and generating consistent RE specifications. Furthermore, we analyze the
impact of self-refinement loops for each LLM. The results reveal that
model stability, i.e. lower variance across iterations, serves as a
stronger performance indicator than the total number of executed
iterations.

This work opens several promising avenues for future research. Building
on Agent4RE, we encourage exploring alternative self-refining
architectures, incorporating specialized agents to optimize the
performance. Moreover, as LLMs continue to evolve, benchmarking new
models within this agentic framework will provide valuable insights into
how model capability enhancement translates to improved specification
generation performance. Beyond RE-E2E, the research community also
requires more end-to-end benchmarks that involve structured RE
specification annotations. While our dataset uses well-formed project
descriptions to establish a controlled baseline, real-world briefs could
be ambiguous. To better reflect such settings, future work could
introduce a stress-test subset with ambiguous briefs to assess their
impact on evaluation quality.
````

# The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation

Jiaxin Zhang ^(†)^(†)thanks: Correspondence to {jiaxin.zhang,
wu.jason}@salesforce.com    Xiangyu Peng    Qinglin Chen    Qinyuan Ye

###### Abstract

On-policy distillation (OPD) is an increasingly important paradigm for
post-training language models. However, we identify a pervasive Scaling
Law of Miscalibration: while OPD effectively improves task accuracy, it
systematically traps models in severe overconfidence. We trace this
failure to an information mismatch: teacher supervision is formed under
privileged context available during training, whereas the deployed model
must report confidence using only deployment-time information. We
formalize this perspective theoretically, showing that
teacher-conditioned success is generally not a valid target for
deployment-time confidence and that helpful privileged context induces
entropy collapse and a systematic optimism bias. To address this, we
propose a calibration-aware OPD framework, CaOPD, that estimates
empirical confidence from model rollouts, replaces self-reported
confidence with this student-grounded target, and distills the revised
response through the same self-distillation pipeline. Experiments across
various models and domains show that CaOPD achieves Pareto-optimal
calibration while maintaining competitive capability, generalizing
robustly under out-of-distribution and continual learning. Our findings
highlight that capability distillation does not imply calibrated
confidence, and that confidence should be treated as an essential
objective in post-training.

## 1 Introduction

As LLMs achieve breakthroughs in complex reasoning and problem-solving,
their role is shifting from text generators to autonomous agents and
scientific co-pilots. For AI to be a trustworthy collaborator in
rigorous domains, it must possess reliable uncertainty signaling, the
ability to accurately self-assess and express doubt. Unfortunately,
modern LLMs consistently fail this criterion. As shown in
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
we observe a pervasive Scaling Law of Miscalibration: even the most
advanced proprietary models (e.g., GPT ([Achiam et al.,
2023](#bib.bib37)), Claude ([Anthropic, 2024](#bib.bib39)),
Gemini ([Team et al., 2023](#bib.bib38))) and scaled-up open-weight
models (e.g., DeepSeek ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)), Kimi ([Team et
al., 2026](#bib.bib36))) exhibit severe overconfidence. Scaling up
capability does not resolve this blind optimism.

This widespread pathology is deeply rooted in the modern post-training
paradigm. To achieve advanced capabilities, developers increasingly rely
on On-Policy Distillation (OPD) ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Lu
and Lab, 2025](#bib.bib16); [Ye et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)) and
self-distillation frameworks ([Sang et al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Kim et
al., 2026](#bib.bib4); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib34)). Methods such as
SDPO ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)) and SDFT ([Shenfeld et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)) generate high-quality training trajectories by
conditioning a teacher policy on privileged contexts, such as
environment verifier feedback, expert demonstrations, or ground-truth
solutions. While OPD is phenomenally successful at transferring
capability, it completely destabilizes confidence calibration. Indeed,
as recently argued by [Kalai et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30), modern language
models are effectively trapped in a "test-taking mode," incentivized to
output plausible falsehoods rather than admitting uncertainty. We trace
the algorithmic root of this epidemic directly to the OPD paradigm
itself.

We demonstrate that this overconfidence is not a random artifact but a
mathematical consequence of OPD, rooted in a fundamental information
asymmetry between training and deployment ([Pechyony and Vapnik,
2010](#bib.bib3)). The teacher operates “open-book,” conditioning on
privileged context to produce low-entropy reasoning paths; the deployed
model operates “closed-book” with only the user prompt. Minimizing the
divergence against this privileged distribution forces the student to
artificially sharpen its logits (Entropy Collapse; [Müller et al.,
2019](#bib.bib42)) and to inherit the declarative style of
success-filtered trajectories (Optimism Bias; [Anthony et al.,
2017](#bib.bib43)). In short: capability transfers by imitation, but
confidence cannot safely transfer across asymmetric information states.
Existing efforts to tame overconfidence typically rely on Reinforcement
Learning (RL) with explicit reward shaping, such as penalizing Brier
scores via RLCR ([Damani et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)). While these methods
can suppress absolute confidence, they suffer from a severe capability
tax ([Askell et al., 2021](#bib.bib12)), the models become overly
conservative and sacrifice fundamental reasoning accuracy to avoid RL
penalties.

To break this capability-calibration trade-off, we propose
Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD). Rather than fighting
the optimizer with complex reward shaping, CaOPD addresses the root
cause via target decoupling. We separate what the model should answer
from how certain it should be. We sample multiple rollouts from the
student policy, which under SDPO are already generated at zero extra
cost, to compute a student-grounded, unbiased empirical success rate. We
then perform a non-invasive target replacement: we preserve the
teacher’s high-quality trajectory for capability cloning, but overwrite
the implicit confidence token with the student’s actual confidence. By
distilling this revised joint target, CaOPD effectively amortizes the
heavy computational expense of test-time confidence estimation directly
into the parameters of a single-pass inference model. Our main
contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  Identifying the Scaling Law of Miscalibration: We empirically
  demonstrate that standard capability scaling and OPD inherently induce
  systematic overconfidence, a pervasive pathology that traps frontier
  LLMs in blind optimism.
- •
  Theoretical Formalization: We mathematically formalize the information
  asymmetry in standard OPD, proving that distilling from a privileged
  teacher context inherently induces entropy collapse and optimism bias.
- •
  The CaOPD Framework: We propose CaOPD, a compute-efficient
  post-training framework that decouples behavior imitation from
  confidence supervision. CaOPD amortizes expensive test-time empirical
  confidence into a single-pass model.
- •
  Pareto-Optimal Performance and Robustness: Across OPD regimes (SDFT,
  SDPO) and domains (Science Q&A, Tool Use), CaOPD achieves
  Pareto-optimal calibration without the capability tax of RL-based
  methods, generalizing robustly under OOD shifts and preventing
  "calibration forgetting" during continual learning.

## 2 Problem Formulation and Preliminaries

### 2.1 On-Policy Distillation and Self-Distillation

Let $`\mathcal{X}`$ denote the space of input queries. To study
confidence calibration explicitly, we augment the standard generation
format with a _verbalized confidence instruction_ ([Kadavath et al.,
2022b](#bib.bib44); [Zhou et al., 2023](#bib.bib23); [Tian et al.,
2023](#bib.bib49)), prompting the model to append an explicit confidence
statement after its reasoning. Each complete generation
$`y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T})`$ is partitioned into two contiguous segments:
$`y=(a,c)`$, where $`a=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T_{a}})`$ is the reasoning
trajectory (including the final answer), and
$`c=(y_{T_{a}+1},\dots,y_{T})`$ is the confidence segment, the token
subsequence encoding a verbalized confidence statement such as
“Confidence: 0.85”. We write $`\mathrm{val}(c)\in[0,1]`$ for the scalar
confidence score extracted by parsing the confidence segment $`c`$.

Modern OPD methods employ a _self-distillation_ architecture in which a
single model plays both roles: Student policy
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$: generates $`y`$ conditioned _only_ on the
input query $`x`$, simulating deployment conditions; Teacher policy
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,z)`$: the _same_ model, but additionally
conditioned on a _privileged context_ $`z\sim\mathcal{Z}(x)`$. Depending
on the OPD paradigm, $`z`$ may instantiate as an expert demonstration,
environment feedback, or a ground-truth solution. The shared structural
property is that the teacher is conditioned on strictly richer evidence
than the deployed model will ever observe.

### 2.2 The Ideal Calibration Objective

Let $`R:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\{0,1\}`$ be an objective
verification function, where $`R(x,a)=1`$ indicates that the reasoning
trajectory $`a`$ yields a correct answer. For the deployed model, the
true probability of success on input $`x`$ is marginalized over its own
generation distribution:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mu(x)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[R(x,a)\big]\;=\;\Pr_{\pi_{\theta}}(R=1\mid x).
``` |  | (1) |

A *calibrated* model should produce a confidence segment whose parsed
value faithfully reflects this probability:
$`\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{val}(c_{\theta}(x))]=\mu(x)`$. Therefore,
$`\mu(x)`$ serves as the ground-truth target for confidence alignment.

### 2.3 The OPD Objective and Its Theoretical Blind Spot

Following SDFT and SDPO, on-policy self-distillation minimizes the
*reverse* KL divergence between the student and the privileged teacher.
The student first generates a trajectory
$`y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$; both the student
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)`$ and the teacher
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)`$ then compute token-level
distributions along this trajectory. The per-token reverse KL objective
is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x,\,z\sim\mathcal{Z}(x)}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)\;\big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)\big)\right].
``` |  | (2) |

Because the generation $`y=(a,c)`$ includes confidence tokens, this loss
operates over *all* token positions, both the reasoning segment and the
trailing confidence segment. This is the theoretical blind spot: the
teacher, armed with the privileged context $`z`$ (e.g., the correct
answer), produces a distribution at confidence positions that is
concentrated near extreme values such as $`c\approx 1.0`$. Standard OPD
therefore entangles two objectives: it transfers the teacher’s reasoning
capability *and simultaneously* forces the student to imitate the
teacher’s unjustified certainty.

## 3 Why Does Capability Distillation Exacerbate Overconfidence?

### 3.1 Intuition: Information Asymmetry and Distributional Warping

The root cause of OPD-induced overconfidence is the information
asymmetry between the teacher’s privileged evidence $`z`$ and the
student’s accessible input $`x`$. When the student minimizes the
per-token reverse KL against $`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)`$
(Equation ([2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.3 The OPD Objective and Its Theoretical Blind Spot ‣ 2 Problem Formulation and Preliminaries ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")))
without access to $`z`$, it is forced to artificially sharpen its logits
to approximate the teacher’s deterministic-like behavior. At deployment,
this translates into extreme verbalized confidence — the model learns
the teacher’s certainty without possessing the teacher’s evidence. We
now formalize the three mechanisms through which this asymmetry induces
miscalibration.

### 3.2 Theoretical Analysis: The Mechanics of Miscalibration

Proposition 1 (Information Gap and Non-Identifiability). Suppose the
privileged teacher context $`Z`$ is informative about the correctness
$`R`$ beyond the input $`X`$, such that the conditional mutual
information $`I(R;Z\mid X)>0`$. Then, the teacher-conditioned success
probability $`\mu_{T}(X,Z)`$ is not measurable with respect to $`X`$
alone. Specifically, there exists no deterministic function $`g(X)`$
such that $`g(X)=\mu_{T}(X,Z)`$ almost surely. Furthermore, under
squared error, the optimal $`X`$-measurable predictor of
$`\mu_{T}(X,Z)`$ is exactly the student’s deployment success probability
$`\mu(X)`$, leaving a strictly positive irreducible projection error:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\min_{g}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-g(X)\big)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\text{Var}\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big)\right]>0.
``` |  | (3) |

Proposition 2 (Entropy Collapse via Privileged Conditioning). Let
$`H(\cdot)`$ denote the Shannon entropy. Assume the privileged context
$`Z`$ provides actionable information about the correct reasoning
trajectory $`A`$ that is not present in $`X`$, such that the conditional
mutual information $`I(A;Z\mid X)>0`$. Then, the expected entropy of the
teacher’s trajectory distribution is strictly lower than the conditional
entropy of the trajectories given only the accessible input $`X`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{X,Z}\big[H(\pi_{\theta}(A\mid X,Z))\big]<\mathbb{E}_{X}\big[H(A\mid X)\big].
``` |  | (4) |

Furthermore, by minimizing the per-token reverse KL against
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},X,Z)`$, the model
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},X)`$ is penalized for expressing the
natural uncertainty $`H(A\mid X)`$, forcing its internal logits to
become artificially sharpened.

Proposition 3 (Selection Bias Induces Optimism). In OPD, the privileged
context $`Z`$ is not sampled randomly, but is typically conditioned on
successful or high-quality outcomes (e.g., demonstrations, correct
solutions). Assume this training distribution of contexts, denoted
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}`$, is strictly helpful, meaning it
increases the expected correctness of the teacher over the student’s
marginal capability:
$`\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X]\geq\mu(X)`$,
with strict inequality on a set of positive measure. Then, the implicit
target distilled into the student is an upward-biased surrogate for the
true deployment-time success probability:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{X,Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\big]>0.
``` |  | (5) |

Full proofs and extended discussions for all three propositions are
provided in Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Full Theoretical Statements and Proofs ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation").

## 4 Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD)

Theoretical analysis establishes a grim reality: capability distillation
and confidence calibration are fundamentally misaligned under asymmetric
information. OPD forces the student to chase an unidentifiable,
artificially sharpened, and systematically optimistic target. To
overcome this mathematical limitation, the supervision target for
confidence must be explicitly decoupled from the teacher’s capability
trajectory. It must be anchored to an unbiased estimator of $`\mu(x)`$
that relies solely on the student’s deployment-time information state.
This mandate directly derives our proposed framework: CaOPD.

### 4.1 Constructing the Student-Grounded Target

The first step of CaOPD is to obtain an unbiased estimate of the model’s
true deployment-time success probability $`\mu(x)`$, without relying on
the privileged context $`z`$. Since $`\mu(x)`$ is defined as the
expectation over the model’s own generation distribution
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$
(Equation ([1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 2.2 The Ideal Calibration Objective ‣ 2 Problem Formulation and Preliminaries ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"))),
we approximate it via Monte Carlo estimation. For a given input $`x`$,
we sample $`K`$ independent trajectories
$`(a_{k},c_{k})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ for $`k=1,\dots,K`$,
evaluate each with the task verifier $`R(x,a_{k})\in\{0,1\}`$, and
compute the empirical success rate:

|     |                                                   |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \hat{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R(x,a_{k}).
       ```                                                |     | (6) |

A key practical advantage is that OPD methods already generate multiple
rollouts per prompt. Under SDPO, CaOPD reuses the rollouts already
generated by the base training loop to compute $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$,
requiring only a lightweight verifier evaluation on these existing
trajectories. The distillation trajectory itself is sampled separately
and incurs no extra cost beyond what SDPO already requires. By
definition, $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ is a student-grounded, execution-based
target. While we utilize objective task verifiers in our primary
evaluations, this target is highly modular; for open-ended domains
lacking ground-truth verifiers, $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ can be approximated
using *Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency*
(Appendix [B.6](#A2.SS6 "B.6 Extending CaOPD Without Objective Verifiers: Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency ‣ Appendix B Algorithm and Implementation Details ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")).

### 4.2 Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective

With the empirical target $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ computed, we integrate it
into the OPD pipeline via a *Target Replacement* operation that modifies
both the *completion* and the *teacher context* before computing the
loss, while leaving the reverse-KL machinery entirely unchanged.
Concretely, given a student-generated trajectory $`y=(a,c)`$:

1.  (i)
    Revise the completion. Replace the confidence segment $`c`$ with
    $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$, yielding the revised trajectory
    $`\tilde{y}=(a,\;\hat{\mu}(x))`$.
2.  (ii)
    Revise the teacher context. In the privileged context $`z`$,
    overwrite the confidence score (originally near $`1.0`$) with
    $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$, producing a revised context $`\tilde{z}`$.

Both the student and teacher then score the revised completion
$`\tilde{y}`$ under their respective conditioning ($`x`$ and
$`(x,\tilde{z})`$). Recall that the token positions of $`\tilde{y}`$
partition into reasoning $`\mathcal{I}_{a}=\{1,\dots,T_{a}\}`$ and
confidence $`\mathcal{I}_{c}=\{T_{a}{+}1,\dots,T\}`$. The CaOPD
objective is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CaOPD}}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x,\,\tilde{z}}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{y}}\bigg[\;&\underbrace{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{a}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x)\;\big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x,\tilde{z})\big)}_{\text{Capability Cloning (preserved)}}\\[4.0pt]
+\;\;&\underbrace{\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x)\;\big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x,\tilde{z})\big)}_{\text{Confidence Calibration (student-grounded)}}\;\bigg].\end{split}
``` |  | (7) |

The target replacement is a clean decoupling of capability from
calibration:

- •
  Capability Cloning is preserved. At reasoning positions
  $`t\in\mathcal{I}_{a}`$, the prefix $`\tilde{y}_{<t}`$ contains only
  reasoning tokens (the confidence replacement has not yet appeared),
  and the privileged reasoning content in $`\tilde{z}`$ is left
  unmodified. The per-token KL at these positions is therefore
  *effectively identical* to standard OPD
  (Equation ([2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.3 The OPD Objective and Its Theoretical Blind Spot ‣ 2 Problem Formulation and Preliminaries ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"))).
- •
  Confidence Imitation $`\to`$ Calibration. At confidence positions
  $`t\in\mathcal{I}_{c}`$, the tokens being scored encode
  $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ rather than the student’s original $`c`$, and the
  teacher’s context reflects $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ rather than $`1.0`$. The
  student is thus trained to produce the *student-grounded empirical
  success rate*, directly resolving the entropy collapse (Proposition 2)
  and optimism bias (Proposition 3).

Unlike RL-based calibration methods that inject scoring-rule penalties
into the reward function, often causing optimization instability and a
severe capability tax ([Damani et al., 2025](#bib.bib17); [Askell et
al., 2021](#bib.bib12)), CaOPD operates entirely within the standard
distillation pipeline via target replacement, requiring no reward
modification or additional optimization stages. The complete procedure

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experiment Setup

Datasets and Models. We conduct experiments across two distinct domains:
Science Question Answering (Science Q&A ([Feng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib25))) and Tool Use (ToolAlpaca ([Tang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib26))). We select two representative open-weight LLMs as
our primary testbeds: Qwen3-8B ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib27)) and
Olmo-3-7B-Instruct ([Olmo et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)). We expand our
evaluation to the broader Qwen3 family to conduct scaling analysis from
0.6B to 32B (see Appendix
[C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Datasets and Models ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Setup ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")).

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate models across two dimensions: capability
and calibration. For capability, we report final-answer Accuracy. For
calibration, we report Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier Score
(BS) ([Brier, 1950](#bib.bib20); [Guo et al., 2017a](#bib.bib22); [Desai
and Durrett, 2020](#bib.bib21)). To explicitly quantify the direction of
miscalibration and the systematic optimism bias induced by
post-training, we introduce the Overconfidence Gap (OCG), defined as the
mean confidence minus the empirical accuracy. A large positive OCG
indicates severe overconfidence. Finally, to measure confidence
discrimination, we introduce Strict Pairwise Ranking (SPR). SPR measures
the probability that a correct response receives a strictly higher
confidence score than an incorrect one ($`\mathbb{P}(c_{+}>c_{-})`$).
Unlike AUROC, SPR assigns zero credit to ties, heavily penalizing the
confidence saturation (e.g., uniformly predicting $`c=1.0`$) prevalent
in OPD. More details are provided in
Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Extended Evaluation Metrics ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Setup ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation").

Baselines. We evaluate CaOPD against two distinct categories of
post-training methods. To illustrate the severe overconfidence
degradation inherent to capability-focused post-training, we benchmark
against state-of-the-art OPD paradigms (SDFT ([Shenfeld et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)), SDPO ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib15))) as well
as a standard RL baseline (GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib29))). To
demonstrate CaOPD’s superiority over specialized calibration techniques,
we compare with calibration-aware RL methods, specifically RLCR ([Damani
et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)) and CAR ([Xuan et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)),
which explicitly penalize miscalibration via reward shaping. Detailed
descriptions are provided in
Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Detailed Baseline Descriptions ‣ Appendix C Additional Experimental Setup ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation").

### 5.2 Does On-Policy Distillation Inherently Exacerbate Overconfidence?

OPD exacerbates baseline overconfidence. As shown in
Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
base instruction-tuned models already exhibit severe optimism bias
(e.g., a massive +58.7% OCG for Qwen3-8B on Science Q&A). Strikingly,
applying standard OPD methods (SDFT and SDPO) systematically worsens
this pathology. Across almost all settings, OPD drives mean confidence
toward extreme saturation (e.g., 0.996 on Tool Use), expanding the OCG
(highlighted in red). This strongly validates our theoretical analysis:
optimizing student behavior under a privileged teacher context induces
an optimism bias, forcing the model to blindly inherit absolute
certainty without possessing the underlying evidence.

CaOPD structurally realigns confidence. By decoupling capability
imitation from confidence supervision, CaOPD successfully mitigates this
dangerous drift. Replacing the teacher’s privileged confidence with the
student’s empirical success drastically reduces mean confidence to
realistic levels. Notably, for Qwen3-8B on Tool Use, CaOPD collapses the
massive OCG from +32.0% (SDFT) down to an exceptionally aligned -0.7%.
This demonstrates that CaOPD structurally corrects the confidence
distribution, proving that true calibration can be explicitly learned
during distillation.

### 5.3 Aligning Confidence Without Sacrificing Capability

The next critical question: Can we explicitly calibrate the model’s
confidence without damaging the reasoning capabilities acquired through
privileged distillation?

Overcoming OPD Saturation with Strong Discrimination.
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.1 Experiment Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")
and [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Does On-Policy Distillation Inherently Exacerbate Overconfidence? ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")
compare CaOPD against SDFT, SDPO, GRPO and other baselines across
different domains. CaOPD drastically reduces ECE and BS while
maintaining or strictly improving task accuracy. Crucially, true
calibration requires confidence to accurately rank correctness, which is
measured by SPR. For instance, on Qwen3-8B Tool Use, standard SDFT
yields a highly low SPR of 0.085, indicating a near-complete loss of
discriminative power. CaOPD restores this metric to 0.555, proving it
actively learns to assign strictly higher confidence to correct answers
over mistakes.

Avoiding the Capability Tax of RL-based Calibration. RL with explicit
reward shaping is a common alternative for taming overconfidence.
However, as shown in
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.2 Does On-Policy Distillation Inherently Exacerbate Overconfidence? ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
pushing calibration penalties into the RL reward fundamentally creates a
capability-calibration trade-off, severely taxing the model’s task
performance. On Qwen3-8B, Science Q&A, RLCR and CAR drop accuracy to
65.8% and 61.6%, respectively, lagging far behind GRPO (74.5%) and SDPO
(80.6%). In contrast, CaOPD operates via direct target replacement
rather than fighting the RL optimizer. It matches or exceeds the high
capability of SDPO (e.g., boosting Tool Use accuracy from 66.2% to
70.9%) while achieving superior calibration metrics.

explains why CaOPD bypasses this capability penalty. The left panel
shows that CaOPD’s accuracy trajectory perfectly shadows SDPO throughout
training; the middle panel shows the calibration loss converging rapidly

distillation and confidence calibration are algorithmically orthogonal.
The model learns what to answer from the teacher’s context, while
learning how certain it should be from the empirical target.

### 5.5 The Scaling Law of Miscalibration: Decoupling Capability from Calibration

A natural question is whether OPD-induced overconfidence is merely an
artifact of smaller models. Does scaling up parameters automatically
cure miscalibration?

Scaling rigidly entrenches blind optimism. As demonstrated in our
macro-level analysis of leading LLMs
(Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
left), we observe a pervasive Scaling Law of Miscalibration: whether
utilizing massive proprietary APIs or scaling up open-weight models,
increasing capability (moving right along the X-axis) does not resolve
the optimism bias. Models remain trapped in the overconfidence zone. To
dissect this dynamically,
Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.4 Generalization and Continual Learning ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")
tracks the Qwen3 family from 0.6B to 32B parameters. Under standard
SDFT, mean confidence forms a flat horizontal line saturated near 1.0.
Whether the model has 0.6B or 32B parameters, OPD forces it into
absolute certainty, rigidly cementing the information asymmetry.

CaOPD achieves Pareto-optimal decoupling. CaOPD shatters this scaling
curse. As models scale, CaOPD systematically pushes the Pareto frontier
toward the top-right corner
(Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.4 Generalization and Continual Learning ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
left and middle), achieving strictly better Reliability and
Discrimination (SPR). Crucially, CaOPD’s mean confidence dynamically
scales with its true accuracy, proving that capability distillation and
calibration alignment can be safely decoupled. Ultimately, this
decoupling enables a compact 8B model to achieve calibration quality
rivaling frontier LLMs

## 6 Related Work

##### On-Policy Distillation for Post-Training.

One recent paradigm shift in the field of LLM post-training is trending
toward "On-Policy Distillation" (OPD). ([Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib7); [Lu and Lab, 2025](#bib.bib16); [Ye et al.,
2026](#bib.bib6)) and self-distillation ([Sang et al., 2026](#bib.bib5);
[Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib4); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib34); [Askell
et al., 2021](#bib.bib12); [Snell et al., 2022](#bib.bib11); [Choi et
al., 2022](#bib.bib10)). Methods such as Self-Distillation Policy
Optimization (SDPO) ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)),
Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning (SDFT) ([Shenfeld et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)), and Self-Distilled Reasoner (On-Policy
Self-Distillation (OPSD)) ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib34)) have
demonstrated that models can bootstrap their own reasoning capabilities
by acting as their own teachers. A shared characteristic of these
state-of-the-art frameworks is the use of privileged context such as
environment verifier feedback, expert demonstrations, ground-truth
solutions, or user interactions ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib1);
[Buening et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)), to generate high-quality teacher
trajectories. However, these works are overwhelmingly
capability-centric. They focus on transferring the teacher’s reasoning
accuracy while mathematically ignoring the information asymmetry between
the privileged teacher and the deployed student. Our work is the first
to formalize how this asymmetry inherently induces entropy collapse and
systematic overconfidence, and we propose a framework to fix this blind
spot.

##### Confidence Calibration and Reward Shaping.

The epidemic of overconfidence in modern LLMs has been widely
documented, prompting significant interest in calibration methods ([Guo
et al., 2017b](#bib.bib47); [Kadavath et al., 2022a](#bib.bib50); [Lin
et al., 2022a](#bib.bib51); [Lin et al., 2023](#bib.bib52); [Tian et
al., 2023](#bib.bib49); [Groot and Valdenegro-Toro, 2024](#bib.bib53)).
The prevailing approach involves Reinforcement Learning (RL) augmented
with explicit reward shaping. Works such as RLCR ([Damani et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17)), Rewarding Doubt ([Bani-Harouni et al.,
2025](#bib.bib18)), CAR ([Xuan et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)), and Taming
Overconfidence ([Leng et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) integrate proper
scoring rules (e.g., Brier score penalties) directly into the PPO
([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib41)) or RL objective to penalize
overconfident mistakes. While effective at suppressing absolute
confidence, these RL-based interventions often suffer from optimization
instability and a severe capability tax ([Askell et al.,
2021](#bib.bib12)), the models become overly conservative, and their
fundamental reasoning accuracy degrades to avoid penalties. In contrast,
CaOPD avoids fighting the RL optimizer. By employing a non-invasive
target replacement strategy on the confidence token, CaOPD achieves
Pareto-optimal alignment, decoupling capability distillation from
confidence calibration without sacrificing capability.

##### Test-Time Amortization and Hallucination.

As recently highlighted by [Kalai et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30), modern
language models are heavily incentivized to behave as aggressive
"test-takers," opting to bluff or hallucinate rather than accurately
signaling uncertainty ([Kuhn et al., 2023a](#bib.bib54); [Geng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib48); [Kirchhof et al., 2025](#bib.bib55)). To detect and
mitigate such hallucinations, substantial research has leveraged
test-time multi-sampling. Frameworks like SelfCheckGPT ([Manakul et al.,
2023](#bib.bib31)) and SAC³ ([Zhang et al., 2023](#bib.bib32)) estimate
uncertainty by measuring the consistency across multiple independent
generations. While highly reliable, these sampling-based approaches
increase inference latency and compute costs by a factor of $`O(K)`$,
making them prohibitive for real-time or agentic deployment. CaOPD
acknowledges that multi-sampling yields the reliable uncertainty signal,
but fundamentally shifts when this computation occurs. By calculating
the empirical success rate during the data generation phase and
distilling it into the model, CaOPD amortizes the expensive test-time
search cost into a single-pass inference model, resolving the
hallucination-bluffing pathology with zero deployment (inference)
overhead.

## 7 Conclusion

We identify a pervasive pathology in modern LLM post-training: the
Scaling Law of Miscalibration. We demonstrate, both theoretically and
empirically, that standard OPD traps models in an “illusion of
certainty.” To break this capability-calibration trade-off, we propose
Calibration-Aware OPD (CaOPD), which decouples capability distillation
from confidence supervision via a simple target replacement mechanism.
Extensive evaluations confirm that CaOPD collapses the Overconfidence
Gap across various scales, domains, and distributional shifts, without
incurring the capability tax typical of RL-based reward shaping. By
enabling models to produce calibrated confidence in a single forward
pass, CaOPD provides a practical foundation for deploying trustworthy,
uncertainty-aware agents.

## 8 Limitations and Future Work

Limitations. Like all on-policy distillation methods, CaOPD inherits the
requirement that the base model possesses sufficient in-context learning
capacity to benefit from the privileged teacher context $`z`$. Our
scaling analysis
(Section [5.5](#S5.SS5 "5.5 The Scaling Law of Miscalibration: Decoupling Capability from Calibration ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"))
shows that even at 0.6B parameters, CaOPD delivers meaningful
calibration improvements; however, capability gains are naturally
bounded by the base model’s reasoning capacity. Additionally, verbalized
confidence requires the model to produce a parsable confidence
segment $`c`$ matching the specified format. While CaOPD’s target
replacement provides a strong formatting signal during training
(Appendix [D.4](#A4.SS4 "D.4 Training Dynamics and Format Adherence Across Model Scales ‣ Appendix D Extended Results and Analysis ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")),
occasional formatting failures can occur at test time, a limitation
shared with all verbalized uncertainty methods and one we also observed
in proprietary API evaluations. Finally, computing $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$
requires $`K`$ rollouts per prompt during training, increasing the
training compute budget. We find $`K=8`$ sufficient in practice
(Appendix [D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Detailed Analysis: Effect of Rollout Sample Size and Confidence Reshaping ‣ Appendix D Extended Results and Analysis ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")),
and this cost is fully amortized at deployment where inference remains a
single $`O(1)`$ forward pass.

Future Work. Our work highlights several exciting directions for future
research:

- •
  Step-level calibration for long-horizon reasoning. CaOPD currently
  provides utterance-level confidence. In agentic or multi-step CoT
  settings, extending the target replacement principle to dense,
  per-step uncertainty (e.g., integrating with Process Reward Models)
  could enable finer-grained error detection and early termination of
  flawed reasoning chains.
- •
  Efficient rollout strategies. The current approach uses a fixed
  rollout budget $`K`$ for all prompts. Adaptive allocation, e.g., early
  stopping when initial rollouts return unanimous verifier scores, could
  reduce training cost without degrading target quality. Extending CaOPD
  to preference optimization frameworks (DPO, RLHF) is also an open
  direction.
- •
  Confidence-based cascade routing. As discussed in
  Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Broader Impact: Calibrated Small Models as Cascade Routers ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
  CaOPD-calibrated small models could serve as lightweight triage
  routers in cascade inference systems, deferring uncertain queries to
  larger models. Empirically validating cost-accuracy tradeoffs under
  this architecture is a natural next step.

## Appendix A Full Theoretical Statements and Proofs

### A.1 Proof of Proposition 1

##### Statement:

Let $`\mu(X):=\mathbb{P}(R=1\mid X)`$ and
$`\mu_{T}(X,Z):=\mathbb{P}(R=1\mid X,Z)`$. If $`I(R;Z\mid X)>0`$, then
$`\mu_{T}(X,Z)`$ is not $`X`$-measurable. The unique square-integrable
minimizer $`g^{*}(X)`$ of $`\mathbb{E}[(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-g(X))^{2}]`$ is
$`\mu(X)`$, with a minimum achievable error of
$`\mathbb{E}[\text{Var}(\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X)]`$.

Proof: We prove the proposition in three steps.

Step 1: $`\mu_{T}(X,Z)`$ is not $`X`$-measurable. Recall that
$`\mu_{T}(X,Z)=\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]`$. Suppose, for the sake of
contradiction, that $`\mu_{T}(X,Z)`$ is $`X`$-measurable. Then there
exists some measurable function $`g(X)`$ such that
$`\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]=g(X)`$ almost surely. Taking the conditional
expectation with respect to $`X`$ on both sides yields:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]\mid X\big]=\mathbb{E}[g(X)\mid X]\implies\mathbb{E}[R\mid X]=g(X)\text{ a.s.}
``` |  | (8) |

This implies that $`\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]=\mathbb{E}[R\mid X]`$ almost
surely. Consequently, the conditional distribution of $`R`$ given
$`(X,Z)`$ depends only on $`X`$, which means $`R`$ and $`Z`$ are
conditionally independent given $`X`$. This is equivalent to
$`I(R;Z\mid X)=0`$, which directly contradicts our assumption that
$`I(R;Z\mid X)>0`$. Therefore, $`\mu_{T}(X,Z)`$ cannot be
$`X`$-measurable.

Step 2: The optimal $`X`$-measurable predictor is the conditional
expectation. We seek to minimize $`\mathbb{E}[(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-g(X))^{2}]`$
over all $`X`$-measurable functions $`g`$. By the standard property of
minimum Mean Squared Error (MSE) estimation, the unique minimizer is the
conditional expectation of the target given the observable variables:

|     |                                                            |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       g^{*}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\mid X}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big].
       ```                                                         |     | (9) |

Step 3: The optimal projection equals deployment-time success. We
substitute the definition of $`\mu_{T}(X,Z)`$ into the optimal predictor
$`g^{*}(X)`$ and apply the Law of Total Expectation (the tower
property):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g^{*}(X)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\mid X}\big[\mathbb{E}[R\mid X,Z]\mid X\big]=\mathbb{E}[R\mid X]=\mu(X).
``` |  | (10) |

Finally, substituting $`g^{*}(X)=\mu(X)`$ into the MSE objective yields
the irreducible error:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\big)^{2}\right]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\mathbb{E}_{Z\mid X}\left[\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mathbb{E}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X]\big)^{2}\mid X\right]\right]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\text{Var}\big(\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big)\right].`$ |  |

Since $`\mu_{T}(X,Z)`$ is not $`X`$-measurable (from Step 1), the
variance $`\text{Var}(\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X)`$ is strictly positive on a
set of non-zero measure, making the irreducible projection error
strictly greater than zero. This completes the proof. $`\blacksquare`$

##### What this means for OPD:

Proposition 1 formally explains the Information Gap introduced in
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Intuition: Information Asymmetry and Distributional Warping ‣ 3 Why Does Capability Distillation Exacerbate Overconfidence? ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation").
Because the teacher uses extra evidence $`z`$ (e.g., a correct
demonstration) to form its internal confidence, the student simply
cannot "guess" this exact confidence value at test time. The best the
student could theoretically do is predict its own marginal success rate
$`\mu(x)`$. However, standard OPD minimizes the KL divergence point-wise
on each trajectory, forcing the student to memorize the inaccessible
$`\mu_{T}(x,z)`$ rather than learning the optimal projection $`\mu(x)`$.
This structural mismatch guarantees that confidence distillation will
fail to produce calibrated predictions.

### A.2 Proof of Proposition 2

##### Statement:

Let $`A`$ represent the generated reasoning trajectory, $`X`$ the user
input, and $`Z`$ the privileged context. If the context is informative
about the trajectory such that $`I(A;Z\mid X)>0`$, then the expected
entropy of the model’s distribution under privileged conditioning is
strictly lower than the conditional entropy given only $`X`$:
$`\mathbb{E}_{X,Z}[H(\pi_{\theta}(A\mid X,Z))]<H(A\mid X)`$.

Proof: The proof relies on the fundamental properties of Shannon entropy
and conditional mutual information.

Step 1: Expanding the Conditional Entropy. By definition, the
conditional entropy $`H(A\mid X)`$ measures the average uncertainty
remaining about the reasoning trajectory $`A`$ when only the input $`X`$
is observed:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H(A\mid X)=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P(x)\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}P(a\mid x)\log P(a\mid x).
``` |  | (11) |

Similarly, the expected entropy of the model under privileged
conditioning (i.e., conditioned on both $`X`$ and $`Z`$) is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
H(A\mid X,Z)=-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X},z\in\mathcal{Z}}P(x,z)\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_{\theta}(a\mid x,z)\log\pi_{\theta}(a\mid x,z)=\mathbb{E}_{X,Z}\big[H(\pi_{\theta}(A\mid X,Z))\big].
``` |  | (12) |

Step 2: Applying the Chain Rule of Information. Using the chain rule for
Shannon entropy, the relationship between these two quantities can be
expressed through the conditional mutual information $`I(A;Z\mid X)`$,
which quantifies the reduction in uncertainty about $`A`$ provided by
knowing $`Z`$, given that $`X`$ is already known:

|     |                                       |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       H(A\mid X)=H(A\mid X,Z)+I(A;Z\mid X).
       ```                                    |     | (13) |

Step 3: Utilizing the Non-Negativity of Mutual Information. By the
assumption of the proposition, the privileged context $`Z`$ is
informative, meaning $`I(A;Z\mid X)>0`$. Rearranging the equation from
Step 2, we obtain:

|     |                                       |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       H(A\mid X,Z)=H(A\mid X)-I(A;Z\mid X).
       ```                                    |     | (14) |

Since $`I(A;Z\mid X)>0`$, it strictly follows that:

|     |                          |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       H(A\mid X,Z)<H(A\mid X).
       ```                       |     | (15) |

Substituting the expectation notation back into the inequality yields:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{X,Z}\big[H(\pi_{\theta}(A\mid X,Z))\big]<\mathbb{E}_{X}\big[H(A\mid X)\big].
``` |  | (16) |

Step 4: Implication on the Reverse KL Objective. In standard OPD, the
model minimizes the per-token reverse KL divergence
(Equation ([2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.3 The OPD Objective and Its Theoretical Blind Spot ‣ 2 Problem Formulation and Preliminaries ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"))).
For a given prefix $`y_{<t}`$ generated by the model in student mode,
the per-token loss decomposes as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)\;\big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)\big)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\pi_{\theta}(v\mid y_{<t},x)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(v\mid y_{<t},x)}{\pi_{\theta}(v\mid y_{<t},x,z)},
``` |  | (17) |

where $`\mathcal{V}`$ is the token vocabulary. The reverse KL is
mode-seeking: it heavily penalizes the model (in student mode) for
placing probability mass on tokens where the privileged-conditioned
distribution $`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x,z)`$ assigns low
probability. Because the privileged distribution is sharply concentrated
(as proven in Steps 1–3), the student-mode distribution
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t},x)`$ is driven to collapse its mass onto
the same narrow set of high-probability tokens. This forces the model’s
internal logits to become artificially sharpened in order to match the
teacher’s certainty, even though the model in student mode lacks access
to the mutual information $`I(A;Z\mid X)`$ that justifies this
concentration. This completes the proof. $`\blacksquare`$

##### What this means for OPD:

Proposition 2 formally explains the Entropy Collapse mechanism
introduced in
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Intuition: Information Asymmetry and Distributional Warping ‣ 3 Why Does Capability Distillation Exacerbate Overconfidence? ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation").
Because the model under privileged conditioning possesses the “cheat
sheet” $`z`$ (e.g., correct demonstrations or verifier feedback), its
next-token distribution is highly concentrated (low entropy). The
reverse KL objective forces the model in student mode to match this
sharp distribution. Being mode-seeking, the reverse KL drives the model
to collapse its probability mass onto the teacher’s high-confidence
modes, drastically scaling up its internal logit magnitudes even though
it lacks the evidence $`z`$ to justify such certainty. When the model is
subsequently asked to verbalize its confidence, it translates this
artificially low-entropy internal state into an unjustified,
near-absolute confidence score (e.g., $`\mathrm{val}(c)=0.99`$).

### A.3 Proof of Proposition 3

##### Statement:

Let $`\mu(X):=\mathbb{P}(R=1\mid X)`$ and
$`\mu_{T}(X,Z):=\mathbb{P}(R=1\mid X,Z)`$. Let
$`\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}`$ be the distribution of the privileged
context $`Z`$ used during OPD training. Assume that conditioning on
$`Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}`$ strictly improves expected
correctness on a set of inputs $`\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{X}`$ with
$`\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathcal{S})>0`$, such that
$`\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X=x]>\mu(x)`$
for all $`x\in\mathcal{S}`$, and
$`\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X=x]\geq\mu(x)`$
otherwise. Then,
$`\mathbb{E}_{X,Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)]>\mathbb{E}_{X}[\mu(X)]`$.

Proof: We evaluate the expectation of the difference between the
teacher-conditioned success target and the true deployment-time success
probability.

Step 1: Applying the Law of Total Expectation. We take the expectation
of the difference over the joint training distribution of inputs $`X`$
and privileged contexts $`Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}`$. By the
linearity of expectation and the Law of Total Expectation (the tower
property), we can condition on $`X`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\big]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\mid X\big]\Big]`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big]-\mu(X)\Big].`$ |  | (18) |

Note that $`\mu(X)`$ acts as a constant with respect to the inner
expectation over $`Z`$.

Step 2: Incorporating the Helpful Context Assumption. By the premise of
the proposition, the training context $`Z`$ is selected to be helpful
(e.g., successful rollouts or gold demonstrations). Therefore, for every
input $`X`$, the expected success probability of the teacher under this
filtered context distribution is greater than or equal to the student’s
marginal success probability without the context:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X\big]-\mu(X)\geq 0\quad\text{almost surely}.
``` |  | (19) |

Step 3: Establishing the Strict Inequality. Furthermore, we assumed this
inequality is strict on a subset of inputs $`\mathcal{S}`$ with non-zero
probability measure ($`\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathcal{S})>0`$). Let the
difference be denoted by
$`\Delta(X)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}[\mu_{T}(X,Z)\mid X]-\mu(X)`$.
We can split the outer expectation over $`\mathcal{X}`$ into
$`\mathcal{S}`$ and its complement $`\mathcal{S}^{c}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X}[\Delta(X)]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Delta(x)d\mathbb{P}(x)+\int_{\mathcal{S}^{c}}\Delta(x)d\mathbb{P}(x).`$ |  | (20) |

Since $`\Delta(x)>0`$ on $`\mathcal{S}`$ and
$`\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S})>0`$, the first integral is strictly positive.
Since $`\Delta(x)\geq 0`$ on $`\mathcal{S}^{c}`$, the second integral is
non-negative. Therefore:

|     |                              |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbb{E}_{X}[\Delta(X)]>0.
       ```                           |     | (21) |

Substituting back the definition of $`\Delta(X)`$, we conclude:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{X,Z\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{helpful}}}\big[\mu_{T}(X,Z)-\mu(X)\big]>0.
``` |  | (22) |

This proves that mimicking the teacher under success-conditioned
privileged context mathematically injects a strictly positive optimism
bias into the student’s expected confidence. $`\blacksquare`$

##### What this means for OPD:

Proposition 3 is the mathematical formalization of the Optimism Bias
introduced in
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Intuition: Information Asymmetry and Distributional Warping ‣ 3 Why Does Capability Distillation Exacerbate Overconfidence? ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation").
Methods like SDFT and SDPO do not train on random garbage; they
specifically construct $`z`$ to ensure that teacher has high-quality
contexts. Consequently, the student is exclusively trained to imitate
the highly certain, declarative linguistic style of a successful
derivation. The model perfectly learns the "voice of a winner." At test
time, when the student generates an incorrect answer due to its limited
capability, it lacks the mechanism to express doubt. Instead, it blindly
applies this inherited optimistic tone, resulting in the massive

## Appendix B Algorithm and Implementation Details

### B.1 The CaOPD Framework Pipeline

To provide an intuitive understanding of the Calibration-Aware On-Policy
Distillation (CaOPD) framework,
Figure [5](#A2.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ B.1 The CaOPD Framework Pipeline ‣ Appendix B Algorithm and Implementation Details ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")
illustrates the complete end-to-end pipeline used during the
post-training data generation and optimization phases. The framework
explicitly decouples capability learning from confidence alignment
through a five-step procedure:

![Refer to caption](2604.16830v1/caopd_method.png)

Figure 5: Overview of the CaOPD Framework. The pipeline consists of five
stages: (1) querying the student for a base response with verbalized
confidence, (2) approximating the true empirical confidence via student
rollouts and an objective verifier, (3) revising the completion by
replacing the original confidence tokens with the empirical success
rate, (4) constructing the privileged teacher context with the same
revised confidence, and (5) computing the per-token reverse KL between
the student and teacher distributions on the revised completion.

1. Generate Verbalized Confidence: Given an input prompt $`x`$, the
model (in student mode, i.e., conditioned only on $`x`$) generates a
complete trajectory $`y=(a,c)`$, where $`a`$ is the reasoning trajectory
and $`c`$ is the confidence segment. In standard OPD,
$`\mathrm{val}(c)`$ would typically be near $`1.0`$, reflecting the
teacher’s optimism inherited during training.

2. Approximate True Confidence: Instead of trusting the single-pass
verbalized confidence, we estimate the student’s actual competence via
empirical execution. We sample $`K`$ independent rollouts
$`(a_{k},c_{k})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ and evaluate each with an
objective task verifier to compute the empirical success rate
$`\hat{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R(x,a_{k})`$. This serves as a
statistically rigorous, student-grounded approximation of the true
deployment-time confidence.

3. Revise the Completion (Target Replacement): This is the core
decoupling step of CaOPD. We take the student’s reasoning trajectory
$`a`$ from Step 1, but explicitly discard its original confidence
segment $`c`$. We construct the revised completion
$`\tilde{y}=(a,\;\hat{\mu}(x))`$ by overwriting the confidence tokens
with the string representation of the empirical success rate. This
guarantees that the supervision target for confidence reflects the
student’s actual competence rather than the teacher’s privileged
certainty.

4. Construct Revised Teacher Context: To ensure the model learns
advanced reasoning capabilities, we construct a privileged context $`z`$
by prepending the user question with environmental feedback,
ground-truth demonstrations, or correct solutions, creating an
“open-book” setting for the teacher. Crucially, the confidence score
within $`z`$ — originally near $`1.0`$ — is also overwritten with
$`\hat{\mu}(x)`$, producing a revised context $`\tilde{z}`$. This
ensures that both the completion and the teacher’s conditioning reflect
the same student-grounded confidence target.

5. Compute Reverse KL Loss: Both the student
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x)`$ and the teacher
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x,\tilde{z})`$ perform a forward
pass on the revised completion $`\tilde{y}`$. The per-token reverse KL
divergence
(Equation ([7](#S4.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 4.2 Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective ‣ 4 Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD) ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")))
is computed between these two distributions. As discussed in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective ‣ 4 Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD) ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
this naturally decomposes into a Capability Cloning component at
reasoning positions and a Confidence Calibration component at confidence
positions.

The detailed pseudocode for this entire process is formally summarized
in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.2 Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective ‣ 4 Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD) ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation").

##### Key Properties of CaOPD

The CaOPD framework fundamentally restructures how post-trained LLMs
learn to express uncertainty, exhibiting three desirable properties:

- •
  True Decoupling of Behavior and Self-Assessment. Unlike RL methods
  (e.g., RLCR) that inject calibration penalties into a global reward
  function — often degrading task accuracy — CaOPD operates via direct
  target replacement within the standard distillation pipeline. The loss
  (Equation ([7](#S4.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 4.2 Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective ‣ 4 Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD) ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")))
  preserves the teacher’s capability signal at reasoning positions while
  redirecting the confidence signal to the student-grounded target.
- •
  Meta-Skill Generalization. Because CaOPD supervises confidence using
  the student’s actual execution outcomes rather than arbitrary
  heuristic penalties, the model learns a generalizable meta-skill:
  mapping its internal uncertainty to an accurate verbalized scale. As
  demonstrated in
  Section [5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 Generalization and Continual Learning ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
  this allows calibrated confidence to generalize out-of-distribution
  and persist through continual training.
- •
  Test-Time Compute Amortization. Obtaining reliable confidence via
  self-consistency or multi-sampling (i.e., computing $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$
  online) is computationally prohibitive at deployment. CaOPD computes
  $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ during the training phase via iterative on-policy
  rollouts. By distilling this signal into the model’s parametric
  weights, CaOPD amortizes the cost of test-time search into a
  single-pass inference model.

confidence instruction into the existing task-specific formatting
templates. The core instruction appended to the formatting rules is:

Confidence: your confidence level (0.0-1.0) regarding your answer, must
be in numerical format, no other words or explanation.

### B.3 Constructing the Privileged Teacher Context ($`z`$) and Target Replacement

In
Section [3](#S3 "3 Why Does Capability Distillation Exacerbate Overconfidence? ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
we theorized that On-Policy Distillation (OPD) suffers from information
asymmetry because the teacher, the same model conditioned on a
privileged context $`z`$, produces artificially certain distributions.
To concretize this theoretical framework, we detail exactly how this
privileged context is constructed in practice for both the SDFT and SDPO
baselines, and crucially, where CaOPD intervenes to replace the
confidence target.

Target Replacement during CaOPD: By structuring the output with an
explicit Confidence: \[value\] suffix, it becomes trivial to extract the
scalar confidence score using rule-based parsing. During the CaOPD
training phase
(Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.2 Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective ‣ 4 Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD) ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")),
we perform two replacements: (1) in the *student’s completion*, we parse
the confidence segment, drop the original numeric value, and overwrite
it with the string representation of $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$; (2) in the
*teacher’s privileged context* $`z`$, we likewise overwrite any
confidence value (typically $`1.0`$) with $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$, producing
the revised context $`\tilde{z}`$. The per-token reverse KL loss is then
computed on this revised completion under both the student conditioning
$`(x)`$ and the teacher conditioning $`(x,\tilde{z})`$.

While the student is prompted strictly with the user query
(Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Verbalized Confidence Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix B Algorithm and Implementation Details ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")),
the teacher receives a heavily augmented prompt, the same query
prepended with the privileged context $`\tilde{z}`$. Below, we provide
the exact, full text of the teacher prompts from our Tool Use domain
experiments. We use colored annotations to highlight the exact token
where CaOPD overwrites the default overconfidence with the student’s
empirical target $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$.

#### B.3.1 SDFT: Offline Demonstration Injection.

using the ground-truth golden responses provided in the dataset. The

demonstration, effectively transforming the generation into an

#### B.3.2 SDPO: Online Retrospection from Successful Rollouts.

In SDPO, the privileged context $`z`$ is constructed dynamically. The
system samples multiple trajectories, uses the verifier to identify a
successful rollout from the current batch, and then prepends this

observing this verified correct path, inevitably leading to a highly
saturated confidence generation.

### B.4 Handling Target Non-Stationarity: Online Estimation of $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$

A critical consideration in the CaOPD framework is the potential
non-stationarity of the empirical confidence target. As the model
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ undergoes gradient updates and improves its reasoning
capabilities, an empirical target $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ computed from a stale
snapshot would rapidly become misaligned with the student’s true
competence.

To strictly prevent target staleness, our implementation of CaOPD does
not rely on a static, pre-computed offline dataset. Instead,
$`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ is dynamically refreshed within an iterative, on-policy
training loop. Specifically, the training dynamics proceed as follows:

1.  1.
    EMA Teacher Tracking: In self-distillation, the teacher is the same
    model with different conditioning
    (Section [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 On-Policy Distillation and Self-Distillation ‣ 2 Problem Formulation and Preliminaries ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")).
    To stabilize the teacher’s distribution during training, we maintain
    an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) ([Tarvainen and Valpola,
    2017](#bib.bib40)) copy of the model weights
    $`\theta_{\mathrm{ema}}`$, updated at every gradient step:
    $`\theta_{\mathrm{ema}}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\,\theta_{\mathrm{ema}}+\alpha\,\theta`$.
    The teacher distribution is then
    $`\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{ema}}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{y}_{<t},x,\tilde{z})`$,
    providing a smoothed target for the reverse KL.
2.  2.
    Dynamic Rollout Generation: Periodically during training, the latest
    model weights $`\theta`$ are synchronized to a high-throughput
    inference engine (vLLM ([Kwon et al., 2023](#bib.bib9))). The engine
    generates a fresh batch of $`K`$ *student* rollouts
    $`(a_{k},c_{k})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ for each prompt
    on-the-fly.
3.  3.
    Online Target Computation: These newly generated student rollouts
    are immediately evaluated by the objective task verifier
    $`R(x,a_{k})`$. The empirical success rate
    $`\hat{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R(x,a_{k})`$ is computed
    strictly from this current batch of rollouts.
4.  4.
    Target Replacement: The freshly computed $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ is used to
    construct the revised completion $`\tilde{y}`$ and revised teacher
    context $`\tilde{z}`$ for the subsequent gradient optimization
    steps.

This online, on-policy generation mechanism ensures that the supervision
signal for confidence dynamically adapts to the model’s learning
trajectory. As the model becomes more capable, the fresh rollouts yield
a higher success rate, and the target $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ naturally scales
upward. Consequently, CaOPD completely avoids the optimization
instability associated with stale targets in standard off-policy
algorithms.

Note on Terminology: When we refer to the computational cost of rollouts
being “amortized offline” in the main text, we specifically mean that
this cost is absorbed prior to deployment (i.e., during the training
phase), rather than requiring $`O(K)`$ test-time compute from the
end-user.

### B.5 Extended Discussion: Justification of the Empirical Target

A potential concern regarding the CaOPD framework is whether the
sampling-based empirical frequency, $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$, serves as a
rigorous representation of the model’s true internal confidence, rather
than merely a heuristic proxy. We argue that $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ is, in
fact, the most statistically rigorous and unbiased target for confidence
alignment, supported by both the mathematical definition of calibration
and extensive literature on epistemic uncertainty.

Statistical Unbiasedness. By the strict definition of calibration, a
model’s predicted confidence should perfectly equal its expected
accuracy on a given prompt. For the deployed model
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$, its true expected success probability is
exactly the marginal expectation over its own generation distribution:
$`\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta}}[R(x,a)]`$. Therefore, the Monte Carlo
estimator $`\hat{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}R(x,a_{k})`$ is a
statistically unbiased estimate of the model’s objective capability on
query $`x`$. Training the model to produce
$`\mathrm{val}(c)=\hat{\mu}(x)`$ by replacing the confidence tokens in
the distillation target
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective ‣ 4 Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD) ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")),
ensures that the verbalized confidence honestly reflects the model’s own
generation distribution.

Connection to Behavioral Consistency and Hallucination Detection. Our
formulation strongly aligns with established findings in the
hallucination and uncertainty estimation literature. Extensive prior
work has demonstrated that sampling-based behavioral consistency is the
“gold standard” for measuring an LLM’s epistemic uncertainty. For
instance, Self-Consistency ([Wang et al., 2022](#bib.bib33)) leverages
multiple rollouts to find the most reliable reasoning paths. More
directly, hallucination detection frameworks such as
SelfCheckGPT ([Manakul et al., 2023](#bib.bib31)) and SAC3
(Semantic-aware Cross-check) ([Zhang et al., 2023](#bib.bib32)) operate
on the principle that when an LLM hallucinates (i.e., operates outside
its knowledge boundary), its sampled responses exhibit high entropy and
self-contradiction. Conversely, confident and factual knowledge yields
highly consistent samples.

CaOPD explicitly adopts this gold-standard consistency signal as the
training target. However, while methods like SelfCheckGPT are applied at
test time, incurring an expensive $`O(K)`$ inference cost, CaOPD
computes this signal during the training-phase data generation
(Appendix [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 Handling Target Non-Stationarity: Online Estimation of 𝜇̂⁢(𝑥) ‣ Appendix B Algorithm and Implementation Details ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")).
By substituting it as the confidence target in the revised completion
$`\tilde{y}`$, CaOPD amortizes the expensive test-time search cost into
the model’s parametric weights, achieving self-consistency-level
calibration in a single $`O(1)`$ forward pass.

### B.6 Extending CaOPD Without Objective Verifiers: Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency

A potential critique of CaOPD is its reliance on an objective task
verifier $`R(x,a)`$ to compute the empirical target $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$. To
address this, we first clarify the dependency structure of On-Policy
Distillation (OPD), and then demonstrate how CaOPD can seamlessly
operate in open-ended domains using Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency.

The “Free Lunch” of Verifier Dependency in OPD. It is crucial to
recognize that the requirement for an objective verifier or ground-truth
label is not uniquely introduced by CaOPD; it is a fundamental
prerequisite of the underlying OPD paradigms themselves. SDPO explicitly
requires environment verifiers (e.g., compilers or reward models) to
generate the privileged feedback $`z`$. SDFT requires ground-truth
demonstrations to construct the privileged teacher context $`z`$.
Because the base OPD algorithms already assume access to verification
signals to construct the privileged context, CaOPD simply reuses this
existing infrastructure to score the student rollouts, introducing zero
additional assumptions or dependencies.

Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency. In scenarios where an objective
verifier is strictly unavailable (e.g., open-ended alignment tasks),
CaOPD can substitute $`R(x,a)`$ with an unsupervised agreement metric.
We propose Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency. Instead of computing the
majority vote among the student’s $`K`$ rollouts — which is vulnerable
to “consistently wrong” snowballing hallucinations — we generate a
reference trajectory from the model under privileged conditioning,
$`a_{\mathrm{ref}}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,z)`$, and compute the
agreement rate between the student rollouts and this reference:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{SC}}(x)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{I}\Big(\mathrm{Equiv}(a_{k},a_{\mathrm{ref}})\Big)
``` |  | (23) |

where $`\mathrm{Equiv}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ is a semantic equivalence function
(e.g., LLM-as-a-Judge).

This formulation solves the consensus hallucination problem: if the
student consistently converges on a false premise (e.g., 8 identical
wrong answers), but the model under privileged conditioning provides the
correct answer, the equivalence score remains exactly
$`\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{SC}}(x)=0/8`$. The confidence target is properly
set to $`0.0`$, accurately reflecting the student’s inability to reach
the correct state without privileged information.

Empirical Validation of TA-SC. To empirically validate the effectiveness
of Teacher-Anchored Self-Consistency (TA-SC), we conducted an ablation
study using Qwen3-8B under the SDFT backbone. We replaced the exact
objective verifier with the unsupervised TA-SC target. Specifically, we
utilized a rule-based exact-match for Science Q&A, and a lightweight LLM
judge to evaluate semantic equivalence for Tool Use.

### B.7 Logit-Based vs. Verbalized Calibration: The Decoupling of Internal Entropy

A fundamental question regarding the CaOPD framework is its choice of
targeting explicitly verbalized confidence rather than internal
token-level probability distributions (logits), and whether altering
this verbalized output inadvertently corrupts the model’s internal
reasoning confidence.

Why Verbalized Confidence? As summarized in
Table [5](#A2.T5 "Table 5 ‣ B.7 Logit-Based vs. Verbalized Calibration: The Decoupling of Internal Entropy ‣ Appendix B Algorithm and Implementation Details ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
estimating LLM uncertainty typically falls into three paradigms:
logit-based ([Kadavath et al., 2022b](#bib.bib44)), sampling-based
([Kuhn et al., 2023b](#bib.bib45); [Wang et al., 2022](#bib.bib33)), and
verbalized confidence ([Lin et al., 2022b](#bib.bib46)). While
logit-based methods (e.g., perplexity) are mathematically
straightforward, they suffer from critical deployment limitations: they
are entirely inaccessible for black-box proprietary APIs and require
complex length-normalization for long-horizon reasoning trajectories.
Sampling-based methods are robust but computationally prohibitive
($`O(K)`$ latency) at test time.

Verbalized confidence represents the ideal deployment paradigm — it
requires only a single forward pass and operates perfectly via black-box
API interactions. However, as documented in our work, its fatal flaw is
that it is severely miscalibrated and overconfident by default after
standard post-training. CaOPD specifically targets this paradigm because
solving verbalized overconfidence unlocks the most scalable and
universally applicable form of uncertainty signaling.

Does Verbalized Humility Bleed into Internal Logit Entropy? A natural
question is whether training the model to output low verbalized
confidence targets ($`\hat{\mu}(x)\approx 0.0`$) inadvertently causes
the internal token-level distributions at reasoning positions
($`t\in\mathcal{I}_{a}`$) to become flattened. In other words, does the
induced “humility” at confidence positions destructively bleed into the
model’s core reasoning capability?

Our empirical training dynamics provide a clear answer: No. The
autoregressive structure of the generation
$`\tilde{y}=(a,\hat{\mu}(x))`$ provides a natural causal decoupling. The
per-token reverse KL
(Equation ([7](#S4.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 4.2 Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective ‣ 4 Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD) ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")))
at reasoning positions $`t\in\mathcal{I}_{a}`$ is computed on prefixes
$`\tilde{y}_{<t}`$ that contain only reasoning tokens — the confidence
replacement has not yet appeared in the autoregressive context.
Consequently, the teacher’s distribution at these positions remains
anchored to the privileged reasoning signal from $`\tilde{z}`$,
unaffected by the confidence replacement. The gradient adjustments
pulling the confidence tokens toward $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ are isolated to
the final few sequence positions ($`t\in\mathcal{I}_{c}`$).

This decoupling is empirically confirmed by the left panel of
Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.2 Does On-Policy Distillation Inherently Exacerbate Overconfidence? ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"):
CaOPD’s accuracy trajectory perfectly shadows SDPO throughout training.
If the low-confidence targets at $`\mathcal{I}_{c}`$ were corrupting the
logit entropy at $`\mathcal{I}_{a}`$, we would observe a tangible
capability degradation. Instead, the Transformer architecture
successfully maintains sharp internal distributions for reasoning while
selectively routing epistemic uncertainty into the allocated confidence
tokens.

### C.2 Extended Evaluation Metrics

We introduce several metrics to comprehensively evaluate the capability
and calibration of post-trained language models. Here, we provide their
formal definitions and theoretical justifications.

Overconfidence Gap (OCG). While standard proper scoring rules like
Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier Score (BS) measure absolute
conditional miscalibration, they obscure the direction of the error
(i.e., whether the model is overconfident or underconfident). To
explicitly quantify the systematic optimism bias induced by
post-training, we track OCG, defined as the difference between the
expected verbalized confidence and the empirical accuracy over the test
set:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{OCG}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{val}(c_{\theta}(X))\big]-\mathbb{E}[R]
``` |  | (24) |

A large positive OCG strictly indicates severe overconfidence, the
primary pathology induced by standard OPD, whereas a value near zero
reflects well-calibrated marginal expectations.

Strict Pairwise Ranking (SPR) vs. AUROC. We introduce Strict Pairwise
Ranking (SPR) to measure the discrimination ability of the model’s
confidence. This metric is specifically designed to expose confidence
saturation, a severe pathology observed in on-policy distillation where
models uniformly report maximum confidence (e.g., $`c=1.0`$) regardless
of actual correctness.

Let $`c_{+}`$ denote the confidence score assigned to a correct
response, and $`c_{-}`$ denote the confidence score assigned to an
incorrect response. The standard Area Under the Receiver Operating
Characteristic curve (AUROC) can be formulated probabilistically as:

|     |                                                               |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       AUROC=\mathbb{P}(c_{+}>c_{-})+0.5\cdot\mathbb{P}(c_{+}=c_{-})
       ```                                                            |     | (25) |

AUROC awards partial credit (0.5) when the confidence scores are tied
($`c_{+}=c_{-}`$). Consequently, a completely saturated model that
outputs $`c=1.0`$ for every single response will yield an AUROC of 0.5.
While statistically representing a "random guessing" baseline, this
value often visually obscures the complete collapse of the model’s
discriminative power.

To strictly penalize this saturation, SPR removes the credit awarded for
tied predictions:

|     |                             |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       SPR=\mathbb{P}(c_{+}>c_{-})
       ```                          |     | (26) |

Under SPR, a tie yields 0 points. Therefore, if a model uniformly
predicts $`c=1.0`$, its SPR drops exactly to 0. This metric directly
answers the most critical question for deployment: Does the model
actually assign a strictly higher confidence to its correct answers
compared to its mistakes? By penalizing uninformative ties, SPR provides
a much more sensitive and realistic measure of confidence discrimination
for post-trained language models.

### C.3 Detailed Baseline Descriptions

To comprehensively evaluate our proposed CaOPD, we compare it against
two distinct categories of post-training methods: capability-focused
on-policy training and calibration-aware reinforcement learning (RL).

1. Capability-Focused Paradigms. These methods primarily optimize for
task success (capability) and serve to empirically illustrate the severe
overconfidence degradation typical in standard post-training pipelines.

- •
  SDFT ([Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)) and SDPO ([Hübotter et al.,
  2026](#bib.bib15)): These represent the current state-of-the-art in
  on-policy self-distillation. The model generates its own trajectories
  and is trained via reverse KL against itself conditioned on privileged
  context (e.g., demonstrations or environment feedback).
- •
  GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib29)): GRPO serves as a standard
  reinforcement learning baseline. It demonstrates the calibration
  effects of standard reward maximization without any specialized
  confidence penalties.

2. Calibration-Aware RL Methods. To demonstrate that CaOPD is superior
not only to standard distillation but also to specialized calibration
techniques, we benchmark against state-of-the-art methods that
explicitly shape rewards to penalize miscalibration.

- •
  RLCR ([Damani et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)): A reinforcement learning
  approach that integrates proper scoring rules (such as the Brier
  score) directly into the PPO reward function to strictly penalize
  overconfident mistakes.
- •
  CAR (Calibration-Aware Reward) ([Xuan et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)):
  Derived from the recent Confidence Dichotomy framework, CAR explicitly
  penalizes the dichotomy between high verbalized confidence and low
  accuracy. In our experiments, we adapted the RLCR training pipeline by
  incorporating CAR’s specialized calibration-penalized reward
  formulation.

Comparing CaOPD against these RL-based interventions allows us to assess
whether our student-grounded empirical target replacement provides a
fundamentally better capability-calibration trade-off than explicit,
scalar reward shaping.

Implementation Details for Continual Training (CT). To rigorously

observed in standard SDFT (e.g., OOD ECE soaring to 0.599 in

confidence distribution of the source training domain. When shifted to
an unseen OOD domain where the model’s intrinsic capability is strictly
lower, the model blindly applies the highly confident linguistic tone
(Optimism Bias) it memorized from the source domain. CaOPD prevents this
by training the model to act as its own calibrator. Because the
empirical target $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ is dynamically generated based on the
model’s actual competence on the current prompt, the model learns the
functional mapping from its own hidden state entropy to a confidence
scalar, which robustly transfers across domains.

The Mechanism of "Calibration Forgetting" in Continual Learning. In
Continual Training, the model’s weights undergo sequential gradient
updates to assimilate new knowledge. While self-distillation algorithms
like SDFT are explicitly designed to prevent the catastrophic forgetting
of reasoning capabilities (Accuracy), they completely fail to protect
the calibration mapping. As shown in

### D.2 The True Cost of Calibration: Test-Time Compute vs. Amortization

A natural concern is that CaOPD’s calibration gains come at the expense
of additional compute. We clarify the cost structure at each stage.

Training-time overhead. CaOPD’s gradient step is identical to standard
SDPO or SDFT: the same token-level forward/backward pass on the revised
completion $`\tilde{y}`$ under student and teacher conditioning. The
only additional cost is computing $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ from $`K`$ rollouts
per prompt. Crucially, this cost depends on the OPD backbone:

- •
  Under SDPO: SDPO’s standard training loop already samples multiple
  rollouts per prompt to identify successful trajectories for
  constructing the privileged context $`z`$. CaOPD reuses these same
  rollouts to evaluate $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$, requiring only a lightweight
  verifier pass on trajectories that have already been generated. The
  marginal sampling cost is therefore zero; the only addition is the
  verifier evaluation, which is negligible compared to generation.
- •
  Under SDFT: The standard SDFT pipeline generates a single student
  trajectory per prompt. CaOPD introduces $`K`$ additional rollouts for
  empirical confidence estimation. At $`K=8`$ (our default; see
  Appendix [D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Detailed Analysis: Effect of Rollout Sample Size and Confidence Reshaping ‣ Appendix D Extended Results and Analysis ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")
  for the ablation), this increases rollout generation by a constant
  factor. In practice, the overhead remains modest: as shown in
  Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.2 Does On-Policy Distillation Inherently Exacerbate Overconfidence? ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")
  (right), the wall-clock time per optimization step is nearly
  indistinguishable from the baseline, because rollout generation is
  parallelized via vLLM and does not bottleneck the training loop.

Deployment-time savings. The key comparison is against test-time
calibration methods such as Self-Consistency ([Wang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib33)) or SelfCheckGPT ([Manakul et al., 2023](#bib.bib31)),
which require generating $`K`$ responses online for every user query,
incurring $`O(K)`$ inference latency. CaOPD computes $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$
entirely during training and distills it into the model’s weights. At
deployment, calibrated confidence is produced in a single $`O(1)`$
forward pass — the same cost as an uncalibrated model. This makes CaOPD
uniquely suited for latency-sensitive agentic deployment where per-query
multi-sampling is prohibitive.

### D.3 Detailed Analysis: Effect of Rollout Sample Size and Confidence Reshaping

To provide a deeper understanding of the compute-efficiency and
distributional shifts induced by CaOPD, we expand upon the analysis
presented in
Section [5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Aligning Confidence Without Sacrificing Capability ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation").

presents both the ablation over the number of rollouts ($`K`$) and the
resulting structural changes to the model’s confidence distribution.

Effect of Rollout Sample Size ($`K`$). As shown in the left panel of
Figure [6](#A4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ D.3 Detailed Analysis: Effect of Rollout Sample Size and Confidence Reshaping ‣ Appendix D Extended Results and Analysis ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),

remarkably flat across all $`K`$, confirming that capability is
completely decoupled from confidence sampling variance. Calibration
exhibits a phase transition: small $`K`$ (1–4) yields heavily quantized
targets that trap the model in overconfidence, while $`K=8`$ emerges as
a compute-efficient sweet spot where mean confidence aligns with actual
accuracy and discrimination (SPR) peaks. Beyond $`K=8`$, returns
diminish rapidly.

Why do small sample sizes ($`K\leq 4`$) fail? The reason is target
quantization. When $`K=1`$, the empirical target $`\hat{\mu}(x)`$ is
strictly binary ($`0.0`$ or $`1.0`$). This structurally prevents the
model from learning intermediate confidence values, pushing it back
toward the same saturation pathology as standard OPD. At $`K=8`$, the
target space has 9 distinct levels (increments of $`0.125`$), providing
sufficient granularity to represent graded epistemic uncertainty. This
also explains why CaOPD’s Confidence Calibration term
(Equation ([7](#S4.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 4.2 Target Replacement and the Decoupled Objective ‣ 4 Calibration-Aware On-Policy Distillation (CaOPD) ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")),
$`t\in\mathcal{I}_{c}`$) requires a non-degenerate target distribution:
binary targets effectively reduce it to a second copy of the
saturation-inducing signal that CaOPD is designed to eliminate.

### D.5 Full API Evaluation Results and The Scaling Law of Miscalibration

To substantiate the Scaling Law of Miscalibration introduced in
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
we evaluate 12 leading LLMs spanning proprietary APIs (OpenAI’s GPT-5.x
series, Anthropic’s Claude 4.x series, Google’s Gemini 3.1 series) and
open-weight models (DeepSeek-V3.1, Qwen3.5-397B) on the Science Q&A

Table [10](#A4.T10 "Table 10 ‣ D.4 Training Dynamics and Format Adherence Across Model Scales ‣ Appendix D Extended Results and Analysis ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")
presents the detailed capability and calibration metrics for all
evaluated models.

Pervasive Confidence Saturation. A large number of models, particularly
from the Gemini family (e.g., Gemini-3.1-Pro) and open-weight models
(e.g., DeepSeek-V3.1), exhibit severe confidence saturation. Despite
actual accuracies between 56% and 72%, their mean confidence scores are
pegged at $`0.99`$–$`1.00`$. This is consistent with our Entropy
Collapse analysis (Proposition 2): on-policy distillation and RLHF force
models to adopt the near-deterministic certainty of their privileged
teacher distribution, leaving no capacity for expressing graded doubt.

Calibration Requires Substantial Overhead in Current Pipelines. Among
the proprietary APIs, only Claude-Opus-4.6 achieves near-perfect
calibration (Accuracy: 83.8%, Mean Confidence: 0.838, ECE: 0.027).
However, Opus-4.6 is a massively scaled frontier model with undisclosed

providers (e.g., Claude-Sonnet-4.5, Gemini-2.5-Pro) fail to replicate
this calibration, suggesting that reliable uncertainty signaling
currently demands a prohibitive capability overhead — or a targeted
calibration mechanism such as CaOPD.

CaOPD Achieves Competitive Calibration at Small Scale. Our CaOPD-trained
Qwen3-8B achieves 80.6% accuracy with a well-aligned mean confidence of
0.770 (ECE: 0.141). Two caveats are important for fair interpretation.
First, the high accuracy is primarily attributable to the SDPO backbone
(which already achieves 80.6%;

0.157 to 0.141, SPR from 0.571 to 0.669) with no accuracy degradation.
Second, the API models are evaluated zero-shot, whereas CaOPD is

scaling.

## Appendix F Broader Impact: Calibrated Small Models as Cascade Routers

In
Section [5.5](#S5.SS5 "5.5 The Scaling Law of Miscalibration: Decoupling Capability from Calibration ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")
(Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.4 Generalization and Continual Learning ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation")),
we demonstrated that CaOPD decouples capability from calibration across
model scales from 0.6B to 32B. An interesting consequence emerges at the
lower end of the scaling curve: small models trained with CaOPD can
accurately signal their own limitations.

As shown in the rightmost panel of
Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.4 Generalization and Continual Learning ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
the CaOPD-trained 0.6B model achieves approximately 58% accuracy, and
its mean confidence drops to roughly 0.3, faithfully reflecting this
limitation. In contrast, the SDFT-trained 0.6B model outputs a
confidence of 1.0 despite failing nearly half the time. This distinction
is not merely cosmetic: a confidence score of 1.0 on a 58%-accurate
model provides zero signal for downstream decision-making, while a score
of 0.3 is actionable.

Application: Confidence-Based Cascade Routing. This property enables a
practical deployment pattern. In a cascade inference system, a
CaOPD-trained small model is deployed at the frontend. For queries where
the model’s confidence exceeds a threshold, it serves the answer at low
cost and latency. For queries where confidence is low, the system defers
to a larger, more expensive model. The key requirement for such a system
is that the router’s confidence must be calibrated, otherwise the
threshold is meaningless. Standard OPD models, which saturate at
$`c=1.0`$, cannot serve as routers regardless of their accuracy. CaOPD
structurally resolves this by ensuring that confidence scales with
actual capability
(Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.4 Generalization and Continual Learning ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ The Illusion of Certainty: Decoupling Capability and Calibration in On-Policy Distillation"),
right panel).

We note that this discussion identifies a promising application
direction; empirically validating cascade routing performance (e.g.,
cost-accuracy tradeoffs under different thresholds) is left to future
work.
````

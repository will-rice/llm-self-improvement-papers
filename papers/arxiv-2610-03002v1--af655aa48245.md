###### Abstract

Unified multimodal models (UMMs) understand and generate both text and
images, which lets a model produce its own training data. Existing
self-improvement in UMMs keeps supervision on the visual side, where
image understanding judges image generation. We propose recursive
cross-capability self-improvement (RSI), a training loop in which the
text and visual abilities of a UMM supply training data for one another.
In each round, the model generates images and reads them to find where
it falls short. It then writes programs aimed at these shortcomings, and
execution verifies every result against its specification. Verified
renders train image generation, while labeled renders and the model’s
own correct programs train visual understanding and program writing.
Program execution thus acts as a source of truth outside the model, so
errors do not accumulate across rounds. We study RSI on charts and build
BasicChartBench to evaluate open models early in training. On requests
worded differently from training, four rounds of RSI raise the score
from 45.7% to 60.2%, while continued training stays at 46.3%. Verified
construction carries most of the gain, and targeting the model’s
failures adds 3.5%. Along the way, the share of verified programs rises
from 48.9% to 95.2%, and the reader’s accuracy on edited renders rises
from 55.6% to 87.4%.

## 1 Introduction

Progress in foundation models has largely been driven by data that
humans collect, filter, and annotate. Unified multimodal models (UMMs)
suggest a different path. A UMM understands and generates both text and
images within a single model ([Chameleon Team, 2024](#bib.bib20); [Zhou
et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Deng et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)). It could
therefore produce its own training data, with one ability supervising
another. Self-improvement of this kind has already advanced the
reasoning of language models ([Zelikman et al., 2022](#bib.bib1); [Yuan
et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)). In UMMs, image understanding has recently
begun to reward or filter the outputs of image generation ([Mao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10); [Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib4); [Fang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)). This supervision stays on the visual side, and the
text abilities of the model take no part in it.

![Refer to caption](2610.03002v1/rsi_umm_framework_v5.png)

Figure 1: One round of RSI. Executed programs supply verified images and
exact labels. The model reads its own generations, and this diagnosis

errors, the reading, and the program writing of the following round.

We ask whether the text and visual abilities of a UMM can improve one
another in a closed loop. Image generation shows what the model cannot
yet draw. Visual understanding finds what is wrong, and text generation
describes what should be drawn instead. Each ability turns what it finds
into training data for the others. Round after round, the data follow
the model’s changing weaknesses ([Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib12); [Khan
et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)), and each gain strengthens the abilities that
build the next round. The risk is that the loop feeds on its own
outputs. Without an independent check, the errors of one round are
learned in the next, and they compound over rounds ([Shumailov et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22)).

The loop therefore needs a source of truth outside the model. For
structured visual content, programs provide one. Infographics, diagrams,
tables, and documents can all be written as programs ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib8); [Zhuo et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)). Running a program
yields an image and an exact record of what it contains, so correctness
comes from execution rather than from the model’s own judgment. Building
on this idea, we propose _recursive cross-capability self-improvement_
(RSI). In each round, the model generates images and reads them to find
where it falls short. It then writes programs aimed at these
shortcomings, and execution verifies every result. Verified renders
train image generation. Labeled renders and the model’s own correct
programs train visual understanding and program writing
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).
The model decides what to learn, and execution ensures that its data are
correct.

We study RSI on charts, a representative form of structured visual
content. A chart must be exact in its text, values, and structure, and
open models remain weak at this. Existing benchmarks are too hard to
separate open models at this stage ([Zhuo et al., 2026](#bib.bib23); [Li
et al., 2026b](#bib.bib24)), so we build BasicChartBench with two levels
of difficulty, from familiar content to topics and number formats unseen
in training.

Over four rounds, generation improves in every round. On requests worded
differently from training, the score rises from 45.7% to 60.2%, while
continued training on the original data stays near its starting point.
Verified construction carries most of this gain, and building data where
the model fails adds to it. The text and visual abilities also improve
along the way. After one round, the model writes a correct program for
almost every planned example, and its reader catches far more of the
errors planted in rendered charts. We close by analyzing in detail where
the loop does not yet help.

Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  We introduce the idea of letting the text and visual abilities of a
  UMM supervise one another in a closed loop, with program execution as
  an independent check. This check is essential: training on the model’s
  own best-rated images gains only 0.5%, while verified data gain 8.6%.
- •
  We realize this idea as RSI and apply it to charts. To evaluate open
  models early in training, we build BasicChartBench, with 476 charts
  and 14,440 atomic questions at two levels of difficulty.
- •
  Over four rounds, RSI raises the score on BasicChartBench L0 from
  45.7% to 60.2%, while continued training stays at 46.3%. Targeting the
  model’s failures adds 3.5% over spreading the same data evenly. Along
  the way, the share of verified programs rises from 48.9% to 95.2%, and
  the reader’s accuracy on held-out edited renders rises from 55.6% to
  87.4%.

## 2 Method

RSI runs in rounds that follow the loop in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models").

sets up notation, and
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Execution as a Source of Truth ‣ 2 Method ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
shows how execution supplies a source of truth.
Sections [2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Diagnosing the Model’s Own Generations ‣ 2 Method ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")–[2.5](#S2.SS5 "2.5 Updates and Recursion ‣ 2 Method ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
describe the three steps of a round: diagnosing the model’s own
generations, building verified data, and updating the model.

### 2.1 Setup

Let $`M_{t}`$ denote the UMM after round $`t`$, with $`M_{0}`$ the
starting model. We use three of its abilities. Image generation
$`G_{t}`$ produces an image from a text request. Visual understanding
$`U_{t}`$ answers questions about an image. Program writing $`C_{t}`$, a
form of text generation, turns a specification into a program. We call
$`U_{t}`$ the reader and $`C_{t}`$ the writer. The model keeps separate
expert parameters for understanding and generation. $`U_{t}`$ and
$`C_{t}`$ share the understanding parameters, and $`G_{t}`$ uses the
generation parameters. An execution environment $`\mathcal{E}`$ runs
programs and records what they draw.

A specification $`s`$ fixes the content of one chart. We describe it
along predefined capability dimensions, such as chart type, number of
categories, number of series, color similarity, value labels, number
format, label length, and vocabulary. Most dimensions have ordered
levels of difficulty, such as one, two to three, or four to six series.
A request $`q(s)`$ states every requirement of $`s`$ in natural
language, in one of several wordings. Atomic questions derived from
$`s`$ check the requirements one by one. Each question belongs to one of
three groups: structure, text, or binding
(Section [3](#S3 "3 BasicChartBench ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).

### 2.2 Execution as a Source of Truth

For a specification $`s`$, the writer produces a program $`p=C_{t}(s)`$.
Running it gives an image and a record of what was drawn,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       (I,z)=\mathcal{E}(p).
       ```                    |     | (1) |

Verification compares the record $`z`$ with $`s`$. It checks the data,
text, colors, axes, layout, and text readability of the chart. Accepted
pairs $`(q(s),I)`$ become targets for generation training. Execution
thus verifies every target, and the reader only decides what kinds of
examples to build.

The same records also supply labels for understanding. We render each
verified specification as specified and again after changing one
property, such as swapping two colors or reordering categories. Each
atomic question is labeled with what the rendered image shows rather
than with what the request asks for. If the request asks for a red
series and the image draws it in blue, the label marks this requirement
as not met. The model’s own verified programs, paired with their
specifications, train program writing. Together, these examples form the
understanding data $`\mathcal{D}_{t}^{U}`$.

### 2.3 Diagnosing the Model’s Own Generations

Each round begins with a fixed set of diagnostic requests. For each
request $`q(s)`$, the current generator produces an image
$`\widetilde{I}=G_{t}(q(s))`$. This image has no record of what was
drawn, so the reader checks its content by answering the atomic
questions of $`s`$,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\widehat{a}=U_{t}(\widetilde{I}),\qquad d=\operatorname{Compare}(\widehat{a},s),
``` |  | (2) |

where $`d`$ marks each requirement as met, violated, or unresolved. The
share of requirements met is the generation accuracy. We report it for
each capability dimension and each attribute value, such as the size of
the values, the number format, the vocabulary, or the wording of the
request.

Correct renders of the diagnostic requests calibrate the reader. Every
requirement is met on these renders, so each rejection there is a
reading error. We add this error rate back to the generation accuracy of
the same requirement. Held-out edited renders track in every round
whether the reader reports what the image shows or what the request
asks. Optical character recognition checks the rendered text
independently of the model, and its score decides when a new vocabulary
opens.

### 2.4 From Diagnosis to Verified Data

Fixed rules turn the diagnosis into a data plan, a sampling distribution
$`\pi_{t}`$ over specifications. The plan specifies kinds of examples
rather than individual charts. It first sets how many specifications to
build for each pair of chart type and capability dimension. A fixed 20%
of the budget is spread evenly to keep learned skills practiced. The
rest follows the diagnosed error rates, with each pair’s weight capped
at three times the mean. Each dimension of each chart type has a current
difficulty level. The level moves up when its generation accuracy
reaches 80% and down when it falls below 60%. Within a pair, the
dimension being trained uses its current level for 70% of
specifications, the next harder level for 20%, and an easier level for
10%. Other dimensions stay at or below their current levels.

The plan then sets attribute values. An attribute value is weak when its
generation accuracy is well below the best value of the same attribute
and below a mastery level. A weak value may lack training data, or it
may be hard despite frequent training, and the two cases call for
different data. The rules therefore check how often the value appears in
all earlier training data. A value that almost never appears is
introduced, and a rare value receives more examples. A common value that
is still weak also receives more examples, and the reader receives more
examples of it with errors to find. A value that is correct under one
request wording and wrong under another is paired with the weaker
wording. A curriculum widens the space of specifications when fixed
gates are met. For example, it adds a number format once accuracy on the
current formats reaches 80%.
Table [4](#A1.T4 "Table 4 ‣ A.4 Decision Rules ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
lists all thresholds.

The writer produces two candidate programs for each specification drawn
from $`\pi_{t}`$. We keep the first verified candidate and fill each
pair up to its share,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{G}=\operatorname{Verify}\!\left(\left\{\bigl(q(s),I\bigr)\mid s\sim\pi_{t},\ (I,z)=\mathcal{E}(C_{t}(s))\right\}\right).
``` |  | (3) |

Verification also shows where the writer is weak, through its pass rate
on each attribute value. Values with a low pass rate receive extra
specifications in the next round, up to 20% of the round. Their correct
programs are also sampled up to three times as often for the
understanding data.

### 2.5 Updates and Recursion

Both updates start from $`M_{t}`$. The generation update trains only the
generation parameters on $`\mathcal{D}_{t}^{G}`$, and the understanding
update trains only the understanding parameters on
$`\mathcal{D}_{t}^{U}`$. The next model takes the generation parameters
from the first update and all other parameters from the second,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{t+1}=\operatorname{Merge}\bigl(\operatorname{Learn}_{G}(M_{t};\mathcal{D}_{t}^{G}),\ \operatorname{Learn}_{U}(M_{t};\mathcal{D}_{t}^{U})\bigr).
``` |  | (4) |

In the next round, $`G_{t+1}`$ makes new errors, $`U_{t+1}`$ reads them,
and $`C_{t+1}`$ writes the new examples. The curriculum gates open new
parts of the specification space once the diagnosis shows that the
earlier parts are learned, so RSI sets its own training space from its
diagnosis. At inference time, the generator draws images directly, and
programs are used only to build training data.

## 3 BasicChartBench

Existing chart generation benchmarks are designed to rank strong
commercial models ([Zhuo et al., 2026](#bib.bib23); [Li et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib24)). Early in the training of an open model, most of
their requests are too hard, so they separate models poorly. On the
chart subsets of StructT2IBench and BizGenEval, for example, our
starting model answers only about 11% and 4% of the questions correctly
(Section [4.5](#S4.SS5 "4.5 Where Does the Loop Fall Short? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).
We therefore build BasicChartBench, a benchmark for this early stage of
training
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 BasicChartBench ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).

(a) Example in BasicChartBench

(b) Distribution of L0 and L1

Figure 2: BasicChartBench. (a) A request, its reference render, and one
atomic question per group. (b) How L0 and L1 differ in seen category
labels, title length, series count, and number format.

Levels. Every chart has one panel. Open models render unfamiliar words
poorly, and learning to render arbitrary text needs more training than
our budget allows, so the two levels separate familiar text from rare
text. Level L0 has 336 charts, 48 for each of the seven chart types, and
10,140 questions. It keeps the vocabulary of the first curriculum stage
but uses a request wording that never appears in training. Level L1 has
140 charts, 20 for each chart type, and 4,300 questions. Its charts come
from 14 topics whose words are rare in the generation training data, and
they add number formats and category counts beyond the current
curriculum. BasicChartBench is never used for diagnosis or data
selection, and no request shares its content with a training
specification.

Questions. Each request comes with atomic questions, and each question
checks one requirement. Structure questions ask about the chart type,
the number of categories, the legend, the grid, and the geometry of the
marks. Text questions ask whether the title, axis titles, category
labels, legend entries, tick labels, and number format appear exactly as
requested. Binding questions ask whether values, colors, and order are
attached to the right category or series. The diagnostic set and both
levels derive their questions and scoring rules from the specification
in exactly the same way.

Scoring. A model’s score is its generation accuracy, the share of
questions whose requirement is met, as judged by the initial reader
$`U_{0}`$. $`U_{0}`$ belongs to the starting model and is never updated,
so it scores every model by the same fixed criteria. Because $`U_{0}`$
comes from the same model family, we check its trends with an external
judge, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, which shares no weights or training data with
our model
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Evaluation Protocol and External Judge ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).
We also report the character similarity between the text read by optical
character recognition (OCR) and the requested text, which does not
depend on our model.

## 4 Experiments

We ask four questions in turn. Does the loop keep improving image
generation
(Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Does the Loop Keep Improving? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models"))?
Which of its parts drive the gain
(Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Which Parts Drive the Gain? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models"))?
How do the text and visual abilities of the model change along the way
(Section [4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 How Do the Text and Visual Abilities Change? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models"))?
And where does the loop still fall short
(Section [4.5](#S4.SS5 "4.5 Where Does the Loop Fall Short? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models"))?

### 4.1 Experimental Setting

We build on BAGEL-7B-MoT ([Deng et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)). The starting
model $`M_{0}`$ comes from a curriculum that goes from easy to hard. The
model first learns to write plotting programs. Generation then learns
basic chart types with short text, followed by a larger set of
programmatically generated charts. Finally, the understanding parameters
learn to answer atomic questions about program-rendered charts
(Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Starting Model ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).
The tasks cover seven chart types that verification can check: vertical
bars, horizontal bars, lines, scatter plots, areas, pies, and rings. A
diagnostic set of 336 requests in the vocabulary of the first curriculum
stage drives the data plan in every round. It varies the series count,
value size, and request wording. Every round uses the same training
budget
(Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).

Controls. Each control removes one part of RSI. *Continued training*
trains the generator from $`M_{0}`$ for the same number of steps on the
original data mixture of $`M_{0}`$ and tests whether the gain comes only
from more training. The *self-filtered* control replaces programs and
verification with the reader. It samples two images per request from
$`M_{0}`$ and trains on the one that $`U_{0}`$ scores higher. The *fixed
distribution* builds the same number of verified charts in each round
with the same writer and understanding update as RSI, but it spreads
them evenly over pairs of chart type and capability dimension and keeps
the first curriculum stage. *Single diagnosis* builds the plan of every
round from the diagnosis of $`M_{0}`$ and never diagnoses again. The
*frozen reader* diagnoses every round with $`U_{0}`$ instead of the
updated reader. The last two share the first round and understanding
updates with RSI.

### 4.2 Does the Loop Keep Improving?

Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.2 Does the Loop Keep Improving? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
shows generation on BasicChartBench, with its two levels pooled. The

57.4%, and 59.0% in the four rounds. Continued training stays near the
start (46.3% after round 4), and the fixed distribution reaches 54.9%.
The gain holds on both levels, which reach 60.2% on L0 and 56.2% on L1
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.3 Which Parts Drive the Gain? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).

Most of the gain comes from text and binding. After four rounds, text
accuracy is 88.2% against 56.0% at the start, binding is 27.3% against
13.6%, and OCR character similarity is 80.6% against 47.8%. Binding
improves partly because text improves. After four rounds, binding
accuracy is 34.0% on charts whose category labels are all correct and
25.4% on the others, while at the start the two differ by about 1%.
Structure changes much less, as

### 4.3 Which Parts Drive the Gain?

Execution supplies correct examples. For each of the 15,320 requests of
our first round, the self-filtered control keeps the better of two
images from $`M_{0}`$ and trains the generator for the same 400 steps.
It stays near the start, 0.5% higher on L0 and 1.2% higher on L1. A
first-round generator trained on verified charts for the same requests
and steps gains 8.6% on L0 and 4.2% on L1. Finding an error does not
supply a correct example to learn from, and program execution does.

Diagnosis decides which examples to build. The fixed distribution keeps
everything of RSI except the diagnosis. RSI leads it in every round, by
2.8 to 4.1% on L0 and by 1.7 to 5.5% on L1
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.3 Which Parts Drive the Gain? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).
Choosing examples from the errors in the model’s own outputs therefore
adds to what verified charts alone provide, and
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.3 Which Parts Drive the Gain? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
shows one such case in detail. The external judge, which RSI never uses,
gives the same ranking as $`U_{0}`$ for every comparison in this section
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.3 Which Parts Drive the Gain? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).

Recursion follows the changing model. As the model learns, its errors
change, and each understanding update also changes how the reader judges
them. Single diagnosis removes the first effect, and the frozen reader
removes the second. Both stay close to RSI on L0. On L1, RSI leads
single diagnosis by 3.1 and 3.7% in rounds 3 and 4, and it leads the
frozen reader by 2.3% in round 3. Diagnosing the changing model thus
matters most on the harder level, while updating the reader matters
less. Recursion also changes the writer, whose improvement we examine in
the next section.

### 4.4 How Do the Text and Visual Abilities Change?

Program writing and reading share the understanding parameters, so every
understanding update changes both of them.
Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.4 How Do the Text and Visual Abilities Change? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
tracks both abilities after each round of the loop.

Program writing. With the starting model, 70% of the candidate programs
run but only 34% pass verification, so about half of the programs that
run draw the wrong content, and 48.9% of specifications receive a
verified program. After one round the share is 95.2%, and it stays
between 95% and 97% in later rounds, including the round that switches
to new topics. Horizontal bars rise from 11.8% to 98.4%, and scatter
plots from 23.4% to 90.9%
(Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Additional Results ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).
Sampling the writer’s correct programs on weak values more often does
not explain this gain. When we retrain the first-round understanding
update with these programs drawn uniformly and every other example
unchanged, the resulting writer passes verification on the same 5,376
second-round specifications at the same rate as ours (90.8% for both).
As a side effect, writing the programs of a round takes 18 rather than
159 GPU hours, so each new round of data is much faster to build.

Reading program renders. Correct renders test whether the reader
confirms requirements that the image meets. Held-out edited renders come
from first-round specifications that no round trains on, so every reader
is measured on the same images. On questions whose answer the edit
changes, a reader that answers from the request instead of the image is
wrong. On correct renders, accuracy rises from 87.1% to 99.3% after four
rounds, and binding rises from 77.4% to 98.5%. On changed questions,
accuracy rises from 55.6% to 86.5% after one round and then stays near
87%. The reader therefore looks at the image more closely rather than
simply accepting more requirements. Building the reading data from the
diagnosis brings no measurable benefit, however. A first-round reader
trained on questions built from the fixed distribution instead answers
86.3% of the changed questions on the held-out edits of RSI, against
86.5% for our reader, so the two do equally well.

Reading the model’s own generations. Program renders cannot show whether
the reader also reads the model’s own generations, which are the input
of every diagnosis. A blind human audit of 392 questions on these
generations answers this question
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Human Audit of Native Generations ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).
Accuracy rises only from 78.5% for $`U_{0}`$ to 82.4% for $`U_{3}`$
(Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.4 How Do the Text and Visual Abilities Change? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")),
and every reader still accepts about a third of the requirements that
humans mark as not shown. The external judge agrees, since the updated
readers match it on 83% to 84% of questions, no more than $`U_{0}`$
does. The reader ranks weaknesses well, however. Across pairs of chart
type and question group, the Spearman correlation between the violation
rates measured by the reader and by humans is 0.82 for $`U_{0}`$ and
0.86 to 0.91 for later readers. The allocation rules depend on this
ranking rather than on exact rates, so the diagnosis points the data to
the right places even though many real errors in the model’s own images
still go unreported by the reader.

### 4.5 Where Does the Loop Fall Short?

Structure under unseen wording. On the diagnostic set, whose wording
matches training, structure accuracy rises from 82.5% to 90.3%. On
BasicChartBench, whose wording never appears in training, it first drops
and then recovers only partly, reaching 86.1% after round 4. Chart type
and panel count still drift. The external judge sees structure recover
fully, so part of this drop may come from $`U_{0}`$.

Forgetting when the vocabulary changes. Round 3 replaced the old
vocabulary entirely, and exact category text on the old vocabulary fell
from 72% to 60% on the diagnostic set. From round 4 on, half of the
specifications keep an earlier vocabulary, which brought the old words
back in the next round. A curriculum should therefore widen the training
data over time rather than replace it.

Existing benchmarks and general abilities. On the chart subset of
StructT2IBench (933 charts, 24,925 questions), scored by the external
judge with its official questions, RSI raises the score only from 11.0%
to 11.9%
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.5 Where Does the Loop Fall Short? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")),
and continued training reaches 11.7% after 400 steps. On the chart
subset of BizGenEval, every model answers about 4% of the questions
correctly, and no image passes all of its checks. These benchmarks
remain too hard for open models. RSI keeps general generation but costs
some general understanding

round, while after four rounds the reader is 2.6% lower on MMBench and

## 5 Related Work

Unified Multimodal Models. Unified multimodal models provide a shared
foundation for visual understanding and generation, spanning different
choices of visual representation, modeling objective, and joint
training ([Xie et al., 2025](#bib.bib16); [Wu et al., 2025](#bib.bib17);
[Deng et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)). Beyond architectural unification,
interleaved multimodal reasoning connects these capabilities within a
task: generated images become intermediate representations that the
model can interpret and reason over ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib18);
[Qin et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)). This makes the interaction between
understanding and generation a central aspect of unified modeling. We
examine this interaction across training rounds, where understanding the
model’s own visual outputs informs the construction of its subsequent
training data.

Recursive Self-Improvement. Recursive self-improvement couples model
updates with the supervision used in later training rounds. Existing
approaches recover learning signals from self-generated outputs through
outcome verification or learned evaluation ([Zelikman et al.,
2022](#bib.bib1); [Yuan et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)), and extend this
process to adaptive task construction when execution supplies verifiable
feedback ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Heng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15)). For unified multimodal models, the relationship
between understanding and generation provides an additional source of
supervision through cross-modal consistency and self-generated visual
experience ([Hong et al., 2026](#bib.bib3); [Thawkar et al.,
2026](#bib.bib6); [Fang et al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Hao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7)). These studies motivate examining how feedback quality
evolves alongside the model being trained. Our focus is the role of
updated visual understanding in this process: how learning to recognize
generation errors changes the examples constructed for subsequent
training.

Synthetic Data Generation. The value of synthetic training data depends
on both the reliability of its supervision and its relevance to the
learner. Programmatic visual data construction exposes information about
how examples are produced, supporting annotation and verification
through rendering code and executable transformations ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib8); [Heng et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). Adaptive data
generation uses learner performance, quality feedback, and coverage gaps
to guide the composition of the training set ([Khan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib11); [Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib12); [Yan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13)). These approaches address complementary aspects of
data construction: verifying an example does not by itself establish its
usefulness for the current model. Our method combines model-based error
diagnosis with programmatic construction, using visual understanding to
select training needs and execution records to verify the resulting
examples and supervise their visible content.

## 6 Conclusion

We proposed recursive cross-capability self-improvement, a loop in which
the text and visual abilities of a UMM produce training data for one
another and program execution keeps that data correct. On chart
generation, four rounds raise the score on unseen request wording from
45.7% to 60.2%, while continued training on the original data stays near
46%, and an external judge shows the same trend. Verified construction
carries the gain, targeting the model’s failures adds 3.5%, and program
writing and reading improve along with generation. These results point
toward a broader goal: models whose abilities keep supplying one another
with correct training data as they improve.

## Appendix A Implementation Details

### A.1 Starting Model

We start from BAGEL-7B-MoT ([Deng et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)) and build
$`M_{0}`$ with a curriculum that goes from easy to hard. Program writing
is first trained on paired ChartNet code and images with the generation
parameters frozen. Generation is then trained with the understanding
parameters frozen, first on basic chart types with short text and then
on a larger set of programmatically generated charts. We use the
checkpoint after 14,000 updates of this second stage. Finally, the
understanding parameters learn to answer atomic questions about
program-rendered charts while keeping program writing, with the
generation parameters frozen. This last step starts from the same
code-trained checkpoint and changes only the understanding parameters,
so combining it with the generation checkpoint equals running it after
generation training. The reader of $`M_{0}`$ is $`U_{0}`$.

### A.2 Training in Each Round

Both updates start from the merged model of the previous round and use
64 GPUs. The generation update runs 400 steps with a global batch of 192
on 25,600 unique examples seen three times. It uses AdamW with
$`\beta=(0.9,0.95)`$, a constant learning rate of $`2\times 10^{-5}`$
after 100 warmup steps, and an exponential moving average with decay
0.995. Verified targets of the round make up 60% of the examples. From
the second round on, 10% of these targets are replayed from earlier
rounds, sampled evenly across pairs of chart type and capability
dimension with double weight on pairs that are already mastered. Replay
of the initial generation data makes up 20%, general images 15%, and
editing pairs 5%.

The understanding update runs 704 steps with a global batch of 128 on
30,000 examples seen three times. The learning rate decays from
$`2\times 10^{-5}`$ to $`2\times 10^{-6}`$ with 3% warmup, and the
generation parameters stay frozen. Atomic questions make up 75%,
whole-image error reports 5%, code 15%, and general visual pairs 5%.
Half of the code examples are the model’s own verified programs of the
round, weighted by the writer diagnosis. The other half replay the
initial code data. Ten percent of the edited-render families are held
out in every round to measure the reader.

### A.3 Construction and Verification

Each round plans about 36,000 specifications, plus up to 20% more where
the writer’s success rate is low. Some planned specifications cannot be
generated within the retry limit and are dropped, so the rounds use
about 35,000 to 43,000. About half of the requests use the curriculum
contract wording and the rest use one of six structured forms. The model
writes two candidate programs per specification, one greedy and one
sampled at temperature 0.6, with at most 6,144 new tokens. A sandbox
runs each program and records every panel. Verification checks the data,
text, series and category colors, category order, axes and ticks, grid,
value labels, legend, layout, and readability. We keep the first
verified candidate per specification and fill each pair of chart type
and capability dimension up to its share without borrowing from other
pairs. If pairs fall short, we build extra specifications for them in
proportion to their observed success rate. Only the first round needed
this, with about 30,000 extra specifications.

### A.4 Decision Rules

Table [4](#A1.T4 "Table 4 ‣ A.4 Decision Rules ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
lists the thresholds. All were fixed before the first round.

|  |  |
|----|----|
|  Rule |  Setting |
|  Allocation over pairs |  20% spread evenly, the rest by violation rate, capped at three times the mean |
|  Mastery of a dimension |  Satisfaction of at least 0.80 moves the level up, below 0.60 after an advance moves it down |
|  Weak attribute value |  At least 30 resolved questions, at least 15% below the best value of the attribute, and below mastery |
|  Diagnosis by coverage |  Below 0.5% of weighted training history is introduced, below 5% is upweighted, otherwise upweighted with extra reading data. Extra share per value is at most 25% |
|  Wording dependence |  A gap of at least 15% between wordings pairs the value with its weaker wording |
|  Curriculum gates |  Exact category text of at least 70% on the current vocabulary and at least 60% on every earlier vocabulary opens the next vocabulary, exact titles of at least 80% allow two-word titles, and number format satisfaction of at least 0.80 adds the next format |
|  Earlier vocabularies |  After a new vocabulary opens, 50% of specifications keep an earlier one |

## Appendix B Request Sets and BasicChartBench

The diagnostic set and BasicChartBench L0 have 336 requests each, 48 for
each of the seven chart types, and BasicChartBench L1 has 140 requests,
20 for each chart type. In all three sets, the atomic questions are
derived from the specification by the same program. Each question checks
one requirement and belongs to one of three groups, structure, text, or
binding, and all sets use the same scoring rules. No request shares its
content fingerprint with any training specification.

Diagnostic set. The diagnostic set uses the vocabulary of the first
curriculum stage, which comes from 12 topics of the generation
curriculum with one-word titles and axis titles. Every chart has one
panel and three to five categories. For the five chart types with axes,
the 48 requests cross the number of series (one, two, or three), the
kind of series names (years or words), the size of the values (all below
100, or some at least 100), and the request wording (the curriculum
contract or one of six structured forms), with two repeats. Area charts
need at least two series, so their one-series cells use two. Pies and
rings cross value size and wording with twelve repeats. The first repeat
uses integers. The second uses decimals for small values and thousands
separators for large values. The set has 9,636 questions. After the
fourth round, the diagnostic set also gains a part for the second
vocabulary, with the same design, 24 requests per chart type, and 4,814
questions. This part only feeds the data plan and the vocabulary gate,
and it shares no content with any test set.

BasicChartBench L0. L0 uses the same first-stage vocabulary and a
request wording that never appears in training. Every chart has one
panel. Half of its charts have three to five categories and half have
six to eight, although the curriculum trains only three to five. It has
10,140 questions.

BasicChartBench L1. L1 uses 14 topics whose words appear only rarely in
the generation training data, such as finance, transport, healthcare,
and music. Every chart has one panel. Titles have two or three words,
charts have one to four series, and number formats include decimals and
percentages that the curriculum has not yet opened
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 BasicChartBench ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")b).
It has 4,300 questions.

Question coverage. Structure questions cover the chart type, category
count and spacing, legend, grid, axis scale and orientation, and mark
geometry. Text questions cover titles, axis titles, category labels,
legend entries, tick labels, number format, and text position. Binding
questions cover the values encoded by marks, series and category colors,
series order, value labels, and simple reasoning over the data.

## Appendix C Evaluation Protocol and External Judge

Protocol. The judge Qwen3.6-35B-A3B answers each atomic question
separately with thinking disabled, and its answers are scored by the
same deterministic rules as the reader’s. Optical character recognition
uses PaddleOCR with rotated reading, so that vertical axis titles are
read. For each requested string, we take the best match among the
recognized lines. Character similarity is one minus the edit distance to
the closest substring, divided by the length of the string.

Agreement with the initial reader. The main results use the frozen
initial reader $`U_{0}`$ as the scorer. Because $`U_{0}`$ comes from the
same model family and was trained on program-rendered charts, we check
its trends against the external judge, which shares no weights or
training data with our model.
Figure [7](#A3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix C Evaluation Protocol and External Judge ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
shows both scorers on the two levels of BasicChartBench.

The two scorers agree on the direction of change for the overall score,
text, and binding on both levels. On L0, both rise over four rounds on
all questions (45.7% to 60.2% for $`U_{0}`$ and 37.4% to 57.9% for the
judge), on text (56.8% to 91.7% and 23.9% to 78.6%), and on binding
(13.6% to 28.9% and 10.0% to 26.8%). On L1, both rise on all questions
(45.1% to 56.2% and 37.5% to 50.0%), on text (54.2% to 80.2% and 23.6%
to 45.7%), and on binding (13.6% to 23.6% and 10.3% to 23.3%). They
differ on structure. Both see structure fall after the first round, but
the judge sees it rise above its starting level by the third round
(90.1% to 91.9% on L0 and 90.2% to 93.3% on L1). $`U_{0}`$ stays below
its starting level on L0 (87.3% to 85.4% after round 4) and exceeds it
on L1 only in round 4 (86.9% to 87.9%). The judge is much stricter on
text, so its absolute scores are lower, and we therefore compare trends
rather than levels.

## Appendix D Human Audit of Native Generations

Correct and edited renders
(Table [5](#A5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Appendix E Additional Results ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models"))
come from the same program generator as the reader’s training images.
They cannot show whether the reader also reads the model’s own
generations correctly, which is the input of every diagnosis. The native
columns of
Table [5](#A5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Appendix E Additional Results ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
answer this question with human labels, and
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.4 How Do the Text and Visual Abilities Change? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")
breaks the accuracy down by question group.

Separation from RSI. Human labels are collected after the last round and
are stored apart from the training pipeline. No human label is used by
the diagnosis rules, the construction of specifications, the reader or
generation updates, or the thresholds in
Table [4](#A1.T4 "Table 4 ‣ A.4 Decision Rules ‣ Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models").
RSI runs identically whether or not the audit exists. The audit only
evaluates readers that were already trained.

Images and Questions. The audit uses one shared pool of native images,
so that all readers are compared on identical inputs. The pool contains
generations of the diagnostic set by $`M_{0}`$ and by the models after
rounds 1, 2, and 3, since these are the images the readers $`U_{0}`$ to
$`U_{3}`$ had to diagnose. Each model contributes 49 images, 7 per chart
type, drawn from disjoint requests, and each image contributes two
questions from different question groups. The 196 images give 392
questions, 133 on structure, 133 on text, and 126 on binding. Humans
marked 179 requirements as not shown (45.7%) and none as unreadable.

Labeling. Each question is shown as the image and one requirement stated
in plain language, and is labeled *shown*, *not shown*, or *unreadable*.
Labels are given without knowing which model generated the image, which
round it belongs to, or any reader’s answer, and the order of items is
randomized. Questions labeled unreadable are excluded from accuracy and
reported separately.

Metrics. *Acc.* is the share of questions on which the reader’s verdict
(met or violated) agrees with the human label. *Profile $`\rho`$* is the
Spearman correlation, across pairs of chart type and question group,
between the violation rate measured by the reader and the violation rate
measured by humans. It shows whether the reader ranks weaknesses in the
same order as humans, which is what the allocation rules use.

Results. Reading of native generations improves less than reading of
renders. Accuracy rises from 78.5% for $`U_{0}`$ to 81.4%, 82.1%, and
82.4% for $`U_{1}`$ to $`U_{3}`$. The gain of $`U_{3}`$ over $`U_{0}`$
is 3.9%, with a 95% interval of \[0.0, 7.9\] from resampling images, so
it is at the edge of what 392 questions can resolve. All of the gain
comes from fewer false rejections, which fall from 10.6% to 3.8%, mostly
on text (76.7% to 85.7%). False acceptances do not fall. Each reader
still accepts about a third of the requirements that humans mark as not
shown (34.1% for $`U_{0}`$ and $`U_{3}`$, 38.0% for $`U_{1}`$), and
accuracy on binding stays near 75%. The reader thus becomes less strict
where it was wrongly strict but does not become better at catching real
errors in the model’s own images, unlike on edited renders
(Table [5](#A5.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Appendix E Additional Results ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models")).
The ranking of weaknesses is good from the start and improves a little.
The correlation between the violation rates of reader and humans across
pairs of chart type and question group is 0.82 for $`U_{0}`$ and 0.86 to
0.91 for $`U_{1}`$ to $`U_{3}`$. Since the allocation rules depend on
this ranking rather than on exact rates, the diagnosis points the data
to the right places even though about a third of real errors go
unreported.

A Pre-Study Check. Before the first round, 210 questions were labeled on
105 native images of $`M_{0}`$ from an earlier development set (seven
chart types, three difficulty levels, five images each, and two
questions from different groups per image). The same blind protocol was
used. Of the 210 requirements, 72 were labeled shown, 138 not shown, and
none unreadable. The initial reader agreed with 76.2% of the labels: 88%
on structure and binding, and 47% on text. It mostly accepted text that
was labeled as not shown. This check led us, before any round, to report
optical character recognition alongside the reader for text. The
development set is not one of the request sets in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Request Sets and BasicChartBench ‣ Recursive Self-Improvement in Unified Multimodal Models"),
and these labels are not part of any reported result.

## Appendix F Data Distribution Changes

The rules above were fixed before the round in which they were first
used, but some were changed during the study. We list every change that
affects the reported rounds, when it was made, and why.

Before round 1. Three fixes were made while the data of round 1 were
built, before any training. The pairing of number formats with prompt
formats was changed from a fixed probability to a rate that divides the
target share by the frequency of the value, because the fixed rate sent
92% of the first-round prompts to one format. The overall share of
prompts in the mixed format was then held between 40% and 60%. The
content checker was fixed for line charts whose programs plot category
names directly, which it had rejected almost always, and the saved
traces of round 1 were checked again without running the programs again.

After round 3. Round 3 replaced the old vocabulary entirely, and exact
category text on the old vocabulary fell from 72% to 60% on the
diagnostic set. From round 4 on, half of the specifications keep an
earlier vocabulary once a new one opens. This rule was added because of
that observed loss.

After round 4. A diagnostic part for each opened vocabulary and a floor
for the old vocabulary in the vocabulary gate were added. They affect
only later rounds, which this paper does not report.

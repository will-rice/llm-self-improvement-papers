###### Abstract

AI agents are becoming increasingly capable of generating scientific
code, but generating code is not the same as improving the algorithms
behind it. For numerical solvers, execution feedback can expose poor
performance, but rarely reveals its underlying cause and how to address
it. We introduce Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery (ADSD), a framework
that links numerical diagnosis to reusable solver self-improvement. ADSD
follows a diagnosis-first paradigm that first explains _why_ a solver
performs poorly, then uses this diagnosis to guide the discovery of
appropriate numerical methods. The resulting knowledge is packaged into
reusable solver skills, turning solver improvement from trial-and-error
editing into a structured process of diagnosis, discovery, and
implementation. Across four challenging numerical domains—power flow
equation, AC optimal power flow control, stiff ordinary differential
equations, and heterogeneous diffusion PDEs—ADSD consistently improves
solver accuracy, robustness, and efficiency. On GOC-500 power flow, for
example, ADSD reduces mean solver error by nearly $`71\times`$, with
improvements further transferring to unseen grid topologies and
operating regimes.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are becoming increasingly capable of
writing scientific code and discovering algorithms ([Romera-Paredes et
al., 2024](#bib.bib27); [Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)). However,
generating a numerical solver is fundamentally different from building
standard software. A solver may run without errors and produce seemingly
reasonable outputs, yet remain unnecessarily slow or fail on challenging
instances due to numerical issues such as ill-conditioning, stiffness,
or repeated matrix factorizations. Addressing these failures requires
more than conventional debugging. An AI agent must understand _why_ the
solver behaves poorly, diagnose the underlying numerical condition, and
select a numerical method or modification tailored to that condition.

This need for numerical diagnosis exposes a major blind spot in today’s
scientific agents. Existing systems can generate or revise numerical
code using execution traces and error feedback ([Gaonkar et al.,
2025](#bib.bib47); [Du et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)), search the literature
for relevant techniques ([Garikaparthi et al., 2025](#bib.bib33); [Asai
et al., 2026](#bib.bib34)), and convert research papers into reusable
tools ([Gandhi et al., 2025](#bib.bib26); [Miao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib46); [Shen et al., 2026c](#bib.bib20)). However, these
capabilities are rarely coordinated. Execution feedback often leads to
trial-and-error edits, while literature search relies on broad keywords.
As a result, an agent may detect poor solver performance without
identifying its cause or the methods best suited to address it. This
challenge is particularly notorious in numerical computing, where the
same symptom can arise from different underlying causes. Since numerical
methods also rely on specific assumptions and trade-offs, effective
improvement requires first diagnosing the underlying numerical pathology
and then identifying methods whose mechanisms and assumptions match it.

Motivated by this gap, we argue that an effective numerical intelligence
should explicitly separate three stages that current workflows often
blur together: (i) observable behavior, which captures raw runtime
evidence, such as residual histories and convergence logs; (ii)
diagnostic knowledge, which interprets these observations by forming
testable links between solver symptoms and their underlying numerical
conditions; and (iii) executable solver knowledge, which finally
translates the diagnosis into concrete numerical algorithms that are
correctly implemented and packaged for direct integration into the
solver. Building on this separation, we introduce Auto-Diagnosis and
Skill Discovery (ADSD), a automatic framework without updating model
weights for numerical intelligence that operationalizes these stages
through a structured, diagnosis-first loop:

|     |

````math

condition\@add@centering}}\xrightarrow{\text{retrieve}}\framebox{\parbox[c][27.0pt][c]{72.00008pt}{\centering targeted scientific\\
knowledge\@add@centering}}\xrightarrow{\text{implement}}\framebox{\parbox[c][27.0pt][c]{53.0001pt}{\centering executable\\
solver skill\@add@centering}}
``` |  |

We construct ADSD across four challenging classes of numerical problems:
power flow equation solving, AC optimal power flow control, stiff ODE
integration, and heterogeneous diffusion PDEs, via three leading agent
baselines, including GPT-5.5, Claude-sonnet-5, and Qwen3.8-2.4T.
Moreover, our evaluations stress the numerical regimes with real-world
engineering pressure, such as topology and feasibility shifts in power
flow, near-loadability AC-OPF edge cases, stiff dynamical systems, and
ill-conditioned high-contrast diffusion problems. Across these settings,
ADSD consistently improves the solvers generated by the base models
while preserving or improving computational efficiency. On power flow,
for example, ADSD reduces Qwen3.8-2.4T’s mean error by a $`71\times`$
error reduction on GOC-500 power flow solver benchmark. Together, these
results show that ADSD generates numerical knowledge that improves
solver accuracy and efficiency across diverse scientific-computing
tasks.

#### Contributions.

By introducing ADSD, we (i) provide a practical and novel
diagnosis-first paradigm for numerical intelligence, enabling agents to
move systematically from solver behavior to verified numerical diagnoses
and targeted numerical skills, and (ii) make ADSD as a general framework
for automated agent *self-improvement*, demonstrating consistent
improvements across diverse numerical domains, language model families,
and unseen problem regimes.

#### Related works.

Recent work advances numerical intelligence from algorithm discovery and
simulation agents ([Romera-Paredes et al., 2024](#bib.bib27); [Novikov
et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)). In power engineering, the scale and
complexity of real-world systems have led prior work to rely on
multi-agent pipelines for solver construction and orchestration ([Jia et
al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Jin et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Shamseldein,
2026](#bib.bib10)). Similar challenges arise in differential-equation
solving for large dynamical systems. Prior work also studied whether
LLMs can effectively use numerical solvers ([Gaonkar et al.,
2025](#bib.bib47)), refine generated PDE solvers through execution
feedback and debugging ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib48); [Du et al.,
2026](#bib.bib49)), and improve numerical reasoning through
reinforcement learning ([Cai et al., 2026](#bib.bib50)). However, across
both domains, existing methods provide limited mechanisms for
transferring observed solver behavior to numerical deficiencies and
acquiring relevant methods beyond its inherent knowledge, so a *general*
and *efficient* pipeline for training *self-evolving* numerical
intelligence remains underexplored.

Beyond numerical intelligence, Voyager ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib30))
first demonstrated that embodied agents can acquire and reuse executable
skills with the gradient-free paradigm. More recent work extends this
idea to scientific settings by converting papers and other scientific
resources into executable skills and workflows ([Shen et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib20); [Gandhi et al., 2025](#bib.bib26); [Miao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib46)), while using execution traces and validation feedback
to guide skill discovery, refinement, and selection ([Liu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib23); [Moll et al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Alzubi et al.,
2026](#bib.bib45); [Liu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib22); [Chen et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib24)). Complementary studies further investigate
trajectory-based failure diagnosis and automated hypothesis
falsification ([Barke et al., 2026](#bib.bib35); [Huang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib29)). Building on these directions, our work focuses
specifically on diagnosis-guided acquisition of skills for numerical
methods, and we defer discussions towards further related works to
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Further Related Works ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery").

## 2 Preliminaries

A numerical solver computes an approximate solution to a mathematical
problem given instance-specific parameters and conditions. In this
paper, we study four settings for numerical solver:

#### Power flow equations.

Power flow (PF) determines a steady-state voltage configuration that
satisfies a grid’s specified power injections, voltage controls, and
network physics. After accounting for prescribed quantities and the
reference voltage angle, the problem can be written as
$`F_{\text{PF}}(x;\xi)=0`$, where $`x`$ contains the unknown voltage
angles and magnitudes, $`\xi`$ specifies the network and operating
conditions, and $`F_{\text{PF}}`$ collects the active- and
reactive-power mismatches. A solver seeks a voltage state with
sufficiently small residual $`\|F_{\text{PF}}(x;\xi)\|_{\infty}`$. This
residual measures satisfaction of the power-balance equations, rather
than voltage error relative to a reference solution.

#### AC optimal power flow.

AC optimal power flow (AC-OPF) optimizes an operating objective subject
to power balance and operational limits. Let
$`z=(v,\theta,p^{g},q^{g})`$ collect voltage magnitudes, voltage angles,
and generator outputs. The problem takes the form

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\min_{z}C(p^{g})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad F_{\text{AC}}(z;\xi)=0,\quad g(z;\xi)\leq 0,
``` |  |

where $`C`$ is the generation cost, $`F_{\text{AC}}`$ enforces power
balance, and $`g`$ represents generator, voltage, and transmission
limits, so the performance depends on both constraint satisfaction and
objective quality.

#### ODEs.

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) integrator approximates the
trajectory of an initial-value problem (IVP),

|     |                                                  |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \dot{y}(t)=f(t,y(t);\eta),\qquad y(t_{0})=y_{0},
       ```                                               |     |

where $`\eta`$ denotes system parameters. Our experiments consider
systems-biology models that often exhibit stiffness, for which stability
can impose restrictive step sizes on explicit methods ([Städter et al.,
2021](#bib.bib19)). Note that the final integration accuracy and cost
depend on the numerical scheme, step-size selection, and error control,
and we evaluate accuracy against reference trajectories and measure
computational work through runtime and right-hand-side evaluations.

#### PDEs.

An elliptic partial differential equation (PDE) solver approximates the
spatial solution of a boundary-value problem,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
-\nabla\cdot\bigl(k(x)\nabla u(x)\bigr)=f(x),\qquad x\in\Omega,\qquad u|_{\partial\Omega}=0,
``` |  | (1) |

where $`k(x)>0`$ denotes the diffusion coefficient and $`f(x)`$ the
forcing. We apply two-dimensional steady-state diffusion problems with
heterogeneous, high-contrast coefficients from [Trifonov et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib55). Specifically, the large coefficient contrasts can
produce ill-conditioned discrete systems, slowing iterative convergence,
making it challenging for diagnosis and solver improvement.

## 3 Main Results

![Refer to caption](2610.03872v1/main.png)

Figure 1: Overview of ADSD. Auto-diagnosis combines execution feedback
from the frozen baseline solver $`N_{0}`$ on public test cases with
literature-derived diagnostic skills to identify numerical failure
patterns. Skill discovery uses verified hypotheses to retrieve candidate
methods and complete their implementations. The resulting reusable
solver skills are supplied, together with $`N_{0}`$, to a fresh agent to
generate the improved solver $`B`$.

We introduce the two core components of ADSD: auto-diagnosis and skill
discovery. In practice, a numerical agent must first understand why its
solver struggles (i.e., diagnosis) before it can identify the right
knowledge and tools to improve it. In our pipeline, auto-diagnosis
identifies numerical conditions that explain a solver’s behavior, while
skill discovery finds and builds re-usable empirical and theoretical
methods from the literature. Together, they form a diagnosis-driven
pipeline that turns observed solver behavior into evidence-grounded
solver improvements. Before running the pipeline, we partition the test
cases into *public* and *private* sets. Feedback from the public cases
is available for training and development, whereas the private cases
remain hidden throughout the process and are reserved for final
evaluation. A literature database across power-grid and dynamic system
solvers is also pre-constructed, serving as the specialized knowledge
source for diagnosis and skill discovery.

### 3.1 Auto-diagnosis

1: Public testcases $`\mathcal{P}`$, literature database
$`\mathcal{L}`$, solver contract

2: Diagnostic skills $`\mathcal{S}`$ and verified hypotheses
$`\mathcal{H}`$

3: Generate and freeze the baseline solver $`N_{0}`$

4: $`\mathcal{O}\leftarrow`$ run $`N_{0}`$ once on $`\mathcal{P}`$ and
record observable behavior

5: $`\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\leftarrow`$ filter relevant papers from
$`\mathcal{L}`$

6: $`\mathcal{C}\leftarrow`$ read shortlisted papers and extract
page-supported claims

7: $`\mathcal{S}\leftarrow`$ cluster claims $`\mathcal{C}`$ by shared
computation and build diagnostic probes

8: Split $`\mathcal{O}`$ into diagnosis rounds
$`\{\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{D}_{n}\}`$ and a
verification pool $`\mathcal{V}`$

9: $`\mathcal{H}\leftarrow\emptyset`$

10: for all diagnosis rounds $`\mathcal{D}_{i}`$ do

11:   Execute $`\mathcal{S}`$ on $`\mathcal{D}_{i}`$ and propose
diagnostic hypotheses

12:   Verify the proposed hypotheses on $`\mathcal{V}`$

13:   Add successfully verified hypotheses to $`\mathcal{H}`$

14: end for

15: return $`\mathcal{S},\mathcal{H}`$

Algorithm 1 Auto-diagnosis pipeline

Our auto-diagnosis framework can be divided into the three key steps:
(i) baseline solver generation, where we freeze a baseline solver and
collect its observable solving behavior on public testcases; (ii)
diagnostic skill construction, where literature-grounded numerical
knowledge is converted into executable diagnostic tests; and (iii)
hypothesis verification, where the probes are used to identify and
validate relationships between solver behavior and underlying numerical
conditions.

As shown in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Auto-diagnosis ‣ 3 Main Results ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
after data processing, the agent generates a baseline solver $`N_{0}`$
using its inherent knowledge and capabilities, with access to the public
testcases for execution and debugging, as in standard coding-agent
settings. Once finalized, $`N_{0}`$ is frozen and rerun on all public
testcases to record its observable behavior, including quantitative
trajectories and summary statistics, which form the empirical basis for
subsequent diagnosis. Throughout the full ADSD pipeline, the agent is
prohibited from accessing off-the-shelf solvers, since our goal is to
diagnose and improve the numerical capabilities already possessed by the
base agent; off-the-shelf solver implementations would confound this
comparison.

#### Symptoms-condition diagnosis.

The key here is to connect observable symptoms to the underlying
numerical conditions that may cause them. Corresponding to lines 3-5 of
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Auto-diagnosis ‣ 3 Main Results ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
we construct diagnostic skills via three steps. The agent first screens
each paper using a pre-processed preview containing its metadata,
abstract, introduction, conclusion from the main text, retaining papers
likely to contain concrete symptom-condition relationships. Shortlisted
papers are then read in full, and each relationship is recorded as a
diagnostic claim with exact evidence, together with the observable
quantities and running statistics from $`N_{0}`$. Finally, claims
sharing a common diagnostic computation are grouped into diagnostic
skills for next stage to generate and verify possible hypotheses.

#### Hypothesis discovery.

We use the resulting skill set $`\mathcal{S}`$ to explain recurring
solver behavior. First, we randomly seperate the public observations
$`\mathcal{O}`$ into non-overlapping diagnosis rounds
$`\{\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{n}\}`$ and a verification pool
$`\mathcal{V}`$, which checks the effectiveness of the hypotheses
produced from each diagnosis round $`\mathcal{D}_{i}`$. Specifically, we
perform diagnosis over multiple rounds to keep each round small enough
for the agent to inspect individual traces and less frequent behaviors.
For example, on IEEE-118, we split 5,000 public observations into 1,000
diagnosis cases across five rounds of 200 cases each, together with
4,000 verification cases. For each round $`i`$, the agent apply every
skill in $`\mathcal{S}`$ to the cases in $`\mathcal{D}_{i}`$. Using the
diagnostic skills to examine each testcase’s input, solver trace, and
final output, the agent then proposes hypotheses that connect observed
solver symptoms to suspected numerical conditions. Each hypothesis is
then converted into a deterministic *detector* with explicit criteria
for when the hypothesized relationship is considered supported or
falsified. Each detector is then applied to $`\mathcal{V}`$, and
hypotheses that passed over $`90\%`$ of $`\mathcal{V}`$ are added to the
verified set $`\mathcal{H}`$ and marked as a common “illness”, while
rejected hypotheses are discarded. Finally, each verified hypothesis
becomes a targeted search guidance for skill discovery, and ADSD then
finds the literature for methods that explain or address the common
illness and converts sufficiently supported methods into executable
solver skills to tackle such illness.

### 3.2 Skill discovery

Our skill-discovery framework converts verified diagnostic hypotheses
into executable solver-improvement skills through four stages: (i)
hypothesis-guided paper screening, which identifies methods relevant to
the diagnosed numerical condition; (ii) theoretical claim verification,
which checks the paper’s claims, assumptions, and compatibility with
$`N_{0}`$; (iii) candidate construction, which empirically implements
the methods; and (iv) skill construction, which packages verified
methods into skills for generating an improved solver.

Unlike diagnostic screening, which searches broadly for measurable
symptom-condition relationships, we design this stage to specifically
target reproducible numerical methods that may address any verified
hypothesis. For each paper, the agent first receives a similar bounded
preview to evaluate its mechanism relevance and compatibility with
$`N_{0}`$, yielding a filtered candidate set for each hypothesis.

Candidate papers are then read in full to determine whether their
methods are scientifically supported and transferable to $`N_{0}`$. For
each relevant claim, the agent records exact page-level evidence,
including equations, algorithms, and assumptions (we present these
details for later case studies in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Technical case studies ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery")).
A separate theory audit rechecks the derivation and evaluates whether
its assumptions are compatible with the $`N_{0}`$ solver. Each supported
method is then summarized into a *theoretical claim dossier*, which
serves as the basis for its empirical implementation (e.g., actual
codes). The resulting implementation is empirically evaluated for
reproduction of the claimed results, and verified candidates are
packaged as frozen solver skills containing the executable
implementation, usage instructions, and claim dossier. To generate the
improved solver, we provide the original $`N_{0}`$ solver and resulting
skill packets to the agent using the same model, reasoning budget,
runtime, and original solver-generation prompt as for $`N_{0}`$. The
agent integrates the supplied skills to produce a new solver $`B`$.

### 4.1 Power flow (PF) equation solver

#### Benchmark data.

We apply training and evaluation cases from the IEEE and GOC grid
families in PF$`\Delta`$ ([Rivera et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)), which
provides generalization settings along multiple dimensions. First, it
includes multiple grid topologies: N retains the full network, whereas
N1 and N2 contain one and two fewer grid components, respectively.
Moreover, it covers different operating regimes, including feasible (F)
and close-to-infeasible (IF) cases. These regimes move progressively
closer to the steady-state voltage-stability boundary and therefore
become increasingly numerically challenging.

#### Training & evaluation setup.

We conduct training and evaluation on both the IEEE and GOC benchmark
families. Because these families use different problem formulations and
input-output schemas, a solver developed for the IEEE setting cannot be
directly applied to GOC. We consider three evaluation settings: (i)
In-domain evaluation, which uses the same system configuration for
training and evaluation but evaluates on a disjoint validation set; for
example, the solver is trained on the IEEE-118 training set and
evaluated on the same type of IEEE-118 validation set. (ii) Topology
generalization, which evaluates the solver under a different topology
distribution; for example, the solver is trained on IEEE-118-N2 and
evaluated on IEEE-118-N1. (iii) Feasibility generalization, which
evaluates the solver under more harsh feasibility, e.g., the solver is
trained on IEEE-118-F and evaluated on IEEE-118-IF. For the agent
baselines, we select recent high-performing models that offer a
practical balance between agentic capability and inference cost,
including closed-source model, GPT-5.5 and Claude-Sonnet-5, and one of
strongest open-source models, Qwen3.8-2.4T.

#### In-domain evaluation.

We train the ADSD pipeline on the public test cases following the
procedure in
§[3](#S3 "3 Main Results ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
and then evaluate the resulting solvers on a hidden validation set drawn
from the same grid topology and operating-regime difficulty. For
example, the IEEE-118 benchmark contains 5,000 public cases and 10,000
hidden validation cases, with both sets using the N2 topology
configuration and the feasible operating regime F.
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery")
reports solver wall-clock time and error on the IEEE-118 and GOC-500
validation sets. Across the evaluated base models, ADSD consistently
improves solver performance, with the most significant gain observed for
the Qwen3.8 baseline on GOC-500, where the agent reduces solver error by
approximately $`71\times`$ while maintaining comparable solver speed.
These results indicate that ADSD can transform numerical patterns
discovered from public cases into reusable solver skills that improve
solution accuracy while preserving computational efficiency.

#### Grid-topology generalization.

We next examine whether solver skills learned exclusively from N2
topology cases can generalize without further adaptation to structurally
more complete networks: N1 cases with one component removed and N cases
with the full grid intact. As shown in
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ In-domain evaluation. ‣ 4.1 Power flow (PF) equation solver ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery")(a),
the skills learned from N2 generalize well across these unseen
topologies, with significant improvement in solver performance across
all base models and grid benchmarks. We note that this capability is
particularly important in real power-grid applications, where engineers
must routinely handle multiple network topologies, making it desirable
for skills learned from one topology to remain reusable across different
grid configurations.

#### Grid-feasibility generalization.

We further evaluate feasibility generalization by transferring skills
learned exclusively from feasible cases (F) to substantially more
challenging close-to-infeasible cases (IF). While feasible cases admit
valid operating solutions, close-to-infeasible cases lie near at grid
constraints, where ill-conditioning can make power-flow equations
difficult to solve. This creates a substantially more demanding setting
that better reflects difficult scenarios encountered by power-system
engineers. The corresponding results are shown in
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ In-domain evaluation. ‣ 4.1 Power flow (PF) equation solver ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery")(b).
Unlike topology generalization, the room for improvement on
close-to-infeasible cases is naturally more limited because the
underlying grid constraints must be satisfied near the voltage-stability
boundary. Nevertheless, even under these limitations, the learned skills
still improve the infeasible-case solvers, demonstrating that the
acquired numerical knowledge remains useful beyond the feasible training
regime.

### 4.2 AC optimal power flow (AC-OPF) solver

Different from the more recent PF$`\Delta`$ benchmark, which focuses on
solving power-system equations and evaluating generalization ability,
OPFData ([Lovett et al., 2024](#bib.bib44)) targets classical
power-system control and optimization. This extends our evaluation
beyond solving AC power-flow equations to optimizing generation cost
subject to the same physical equations and operational constraints. Such
optimization becomes especially challenging as the system approaches its
operating limits, motivating our evaluation on edge cases that resembles
the realistic extreme real-world engineering difficulty.

#### Edge-case design.

Power-system operation becomes particularly challenging under high
electricity demand, when operators must continue serving load at
reasonable cost without violating grid operating limits. In these
stressed regimes, generation and voltages are tightly coupled: adjusting
generation at one location can alter network conditions elsewhere, so
resolving one constraint may introduce another. Reliable optimization is
therefore most critical near the system’s operating boundary, where
feasible margins are small and the solver must make effective decisions
under severe network stress.

#### Training & evaluation setup.

To capture this realistic difficulty, among 300,000 cases from the
IEEE-57 grid environment, we specifically filter 7,000 edge cases (2,000
for training and 5,000 for hidden evaluation) that operate near the
maximum loadability of the system, with simultaneously high demand and
transmission congestion. We evaluate solver quality using mean absolute
relative objective error,
$`|f-f_{\text{ref}}|/\max(|f_{\text{ref}}|,1)`$, where
$`f_{\text{ref}}`$ denotes the reference result from the benchmark,
together with runtime and pass rate. As shown in

stressed operating conditions while maintaining low objective error.

### 4.3 ODE integrator

We use ODE benchmark data from the systems-biology model collection of
[Städter et al. (2021)](#bib.bib19). After processing, the benchmark
contains 111 distinct system families. We partition the testcases from
these models into 39 for training and 72 for evaluation. The largest
system contains 77 state variables evaluated at 101 requested time
points, resulting in a $`101\times 77`$ trajectory matrix as the solver
output.

#### Training & evaluation setup.

These reaction-network systems often exhibit stiffness, making reliable
time integration and error control essential. Each solver is provided
with the same fixed right-hand-side (RHS) implementation and is required
to implement the integration algorithm using only Python and NumPy. A
case is considered successful only if the solver converges, returns a
finite trajectory on the requested time grid, remains within the
computational budget, and satisfies
$`\max_{i,j}\tfrac{|\hat{y}_{i}(t_{j})-y_{i}^{\text{ref}}(t_{j})|}{\max(1,|y_{i}^{\text{ref}}(t_{j})|)}\leq 10^{-4}`$
over all reference-reported states and time points. Here,
$`y_{i}^{\mathrm{ref}}(t_{j})`$ denotes the reference trajectory
provided by the benchmark. We adopt $`10^{-4}`$ as the trajectory-error
threshold, following the accuracy criterion used in the original
benchmark ([Städter et al., 2021](#bib.bib19)).

### 4.5 Additional Analyses

We conduct two additional analyses to assess whether ADSD provides
benefits beyond solver revision using only a model’s inherent knowledge.

#### Ablation analysis on ADSD.

We first isolate the contribution of ADSD’s discovered skills by
removing the ADSD pipeline and directly asking each agent to improve its
frozen baseline solver $`N_{0}`$, without providing any diagnosed
hypotheses or discovered skills. All remaining prompt instructions,
model configurations, reasoning budgets, and runtime constraints are
kept unchanged. We evaluate this ablation in two numerical domains:
PF$`\Delta`$ from power-grid engineering and stiff-ODE integration from
dynamical-system engineering.

Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ ADSD versus recursive self-improvement. ‣ 4.5 Additional Analyses ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery")
compares the original $`N_{0}`$ solver, direct self-improvement, and
ADSD across three base models. Direct self-improvement produces
inconsistent gains, which error decreases in four comparisons but
increases in the remaining five. Still, ADSD achieves significantly
lower error in all settings. These results indicate that simply
requesting another solver revision does not reliably improve numerical
performance, whereas the diagnostic hypotheses and literature-grounded
skills discovered by ADSD provide substantially more consistent
improvements across the evaluated models and domains.

#### ADSD versus recursive self-improvement.

The previous ablation considers only one round of direct
self-improvement, raising the question of whether repeated revisions
could eventually achieve comparable performance. We therefore extend the
same procedure to five iterations, where at each iteration the agent can
receive the solvers from all previous rounds.

for grid tasks and median trajectory error for ODEs. Panel (c) uses a
logarithmic scale; lower is better throughout.

Shown in
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ ADSD versus recursive self-improvement. ‣ 4.5 Additional Analyses ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
extending direct self-improvement to more iterations does not produce
sustained error reduction. GPT baseline initially worsens, then
partially recovers before plateauing above the error of its original
$`N_{0}`$ solver, while Claude-Sonnet-5 remains nearly unchanged across
iterations. Thus, within the evaluated iteration budget, additional
rounds of self-diagnosis and solver revision using inherent model
knowledge alone do not close the performance gap to ADSD. Together with
the single-round ablation, these results support the contribution of
ADSD’s externally discovered, diagnosis-targeted skills beyond simply
allocating additional opportunities for solver revision.

## 5 Conclusion

In
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Further Discussions ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
we provide more discussions and backgrounds for readers, along with a
further analysis of the necessity for agent to do diagnosis, which
highlights that access to numerical knowledge alone does not determine
which method addresses a specific solver failure.

We introduced ADSD, an automatic framework that turns observed solver
behavior into verified numerical conditions and reusable executable
skills. ADSD grounds solver improvement in literature-derived diagnostic
probes, hypothesis verification, and final skill construction. Across
four challenging numerical domains, ADSD improves solver accuracy,
robustness, and efficiency across different base models, with learned
skills transferring to unseen problem regimes. Our ablations show that
direct or recursive self-improvement using the model’s inherent
knowledge does not recover these gains within the evaluated budgets.
Together, these results highlight diagnosis as a critical component of
numerical intelligence, connecting observed solver behavior to
actionable numerical knowledge. ADSD offers a practical path toward
scientific agents that identify why a solver struggles, acquire
appropriate methods, and retain them as reusable skills for improving
the underlying numerical algorithms.

## Appendix A Further Related Works

#### Power-system knowledge and applications.

Foundation-model applications, domain-adapted models such as GAIA, and
dispatch benchmarks, such as ElecBench, explore power-system knowledge
and reasoning ([Huang et al., 2023](#bib.bib5); [Cheng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8); [Zhou et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)). Related studies
enable LLMs to operate previously unseen simulation tools through
documentation and prompting ([Jia et al., 2024](#bib.bib6)), or
interpret observations for grid-fault diagnosis ([Jing and Rahman,
2024](#bib.bib7)). These tasks concern domain understanding, tool use,
and physical-system conditions, while our work instead focuses on
numerical conditions associated with solver behavior.

#### Model discovery and formulation.

An upstream problem is determining the mathematical model to be solved.
SINDy identifies governing equations through sparse regression, while
LLM-DMD uses language models to guide power-system dynamic model
discovery ([Brunton et al., 2016](#bib.bib18); [Shen et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib11)). Other approaches translate natural-language
requirements into optimization formulations and validate the generated
models ([Hu et al., 2025](#bib.bib12); [Shen et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib13)). Mamo ([Huang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib51)) similarly
evaluates mathematical modeling for ODEs and optimization. For power
models specifically, L2RPN ([Marot et al., 2020](#bib.bib14)) and
RL2Grid ([Marchesini et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)) study reinforcement
learning for sequential grid-operation decisions. [Fan et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib15) and [Biagioni et al. (2022)](#bib.bib16) also
provides the gym for training power agents.

#### Numerical methods and solver evaluation.

Classical numerical analysis provides the methods that solver-building
agents can acquire. Newton power flow and higher-order methods with
factorization reuse form relevant foundations ([Tinney and Hart,
1967](#bib.bib3)). Automated integrator design also predates LLMs: [Guo
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib52) learn Runge–Kutta coefficients for targeted
ODE families. For evaluation, [Städter et al. (2021)](#bib.bib19)
compare integration methods and configurations on biological ODE models,
while PETSc agent evaluation and PDEAgent-Bench assess generated
scientific code for numerical accuracy, efficiency, and appropriate use
of professional libraries ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib53); [Hang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib54)). We note that these studies emphasize that
successful execution alone does not establish numerical quality.

#### Scientific retrieval.

We further discuss ADSD’s scope to scientific retrieval literature.
Recently, MIR ([Garikaparthi et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)) retrieves
methodological inspirations, and OpenScholar ([Asai et al.,
2026](#bib.bib34)) as well as PaperQA2 ([Skarlinski et al.,
2024](#bib.bib40)) support literature-grounded scientific synthesis. The
AI Scientist ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib38)) and Agent Laboratory
([Schmidgall et al., 2025](#bib.bib39)) extend automation across
literature review, experimentation, and research reporting. FlamePilot
([Xiao et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)) also connects literature with
self-corrective combustion simulation workflows. For ADSD, it
specializes this connection between literature and computation by
checking a numerical method’s assumptions against an existing solver
before constructing a transferable implementation.

We note that ADSD is complementary to these general skill-discovery
frameworks. Paper2Agent ([Miao et al., 2025](#bib.bib46)) converts a
specified paper and its codebase into executable tools, SkillFoundry
([Shen et al., 2026c](#bib.bib20)) mines and validates skills from
heterogeneous scientific resources, and Voyager ([Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib30)) acquires reusable skills through execution feedback.
Our skill-discovery stage
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Skill discovery ‣ 3 Main Results ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"))
shares their goal of constructing reusable executable knowledge. ADSD’s
distinguishing focus is the preceding diagnostic step: it converts a
frozen solver’s observed behavior into verified numerical hypotheses
that specify which methods to retrieve and which assumptions their
implementations must satisfy. This provides a concrete connection
between solver failures and skill acquisition, making existing
skill-construction mechanisms complementary to the diagnosis front-end.

#### Agentic skills aligned from experience.

Finally, we go over prior classic works to incentive agent’s skill and
ability. Reflexion ([Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib31)) and ExpeL ([Zhao
et al., 2024](#bib.bib36)) first turn execution feedback and accumulated
experience into reusable guidance for subsequent tasks. Large Language
Models as tool makers separates executable-tool construction from
downstream tool use ([Cai et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)). Further works also
studies reinforcement learning to improve the agent’s policy through
skill, experience, and learning from mistakes ([Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib37); [Chen et al., 2026c](#bib.bib56)), which establishes
fundamental routes for retaining knowledge through memory, executable
tools, and environment interactions ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib62)).
Apart from these in-context agentic tasks, recent LLM-RL framework also
introduces also introduces language model to further incentive language
model’s ability for mathematics ([Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib63); [Yu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib64); [Chen et al., 2026d](#bib.bib65); [Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib67)) and skill-based tool usage ([Qian et al.,
2025](#bib.bib66)); We believe it is a promising direction to extend
ADSD to these domains as a self-supervised pipeline as a replacement the
GPU-extensive online RL training. Lastly, language alignment methods
([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib68); [Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib69);
[Meng et al., 2024](#bib.bib70); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib71); [Chen
et al., 2026b](#bib.bib21)) also incurs online RL training, and how to
adapt ADSD to the structure with contrastive signals and align language
model would also be a valuable direction to study.

§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Power flow (PF) equation solver ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery")
using the GPT baseline on the IEEE 118-bus training environment, which
includes the initial $`N_{0}`$ solver, the diagnosis skills learned, the
initial symptoms identified together with the final verified hypotheses,
the resulting skills built, and the final improved solver.

#### Initial $`N_{0}`$ solver.

Before presenting the subsequent diagnosis and solver improvement, we
first describe the baseline implementation produced by the agent. The
resulting implementation is a dense polar Newton power-flow solver with
an analytic Jacobian, a safeguarded step, and a bounded regularized
fallback for singular linear systems.

Every accepted nonlinear iteration of $`N_{0}`$ costs exactly one
Jacobian assembly, one dense factorization, and one triangular solve.
Therefore, for $`N_{0}`$, “one more Newton iteration” and “one more
dense factorization” are the same statement. This is what makes the
diagnosis we next introduced actionable, and it is exactly the quantity
that the two solver skills attack. Apart from the solver itself,
$`N_{0}`$ also records the following execution trace that the diagnosis
stage utilizes for analysis: per-case counters for nonlinear iterations,
Jacobian evaluations, factorizations, linear solves, fallback
activations, and factorization failures, together with residual,
step-norm, and accepted-step-scale histories.

#### Diagnosis skills & hypothesis generation.

Following
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Auto-diagnosis ‣ 3 Main Results ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
the agent constructs ten deterministic diagnosis skills from the
literature, each mapping a sanitized testcase. One of them,
ac-jacobian-criticality, is especially important, which converts the
qualitative notion of a “weak Jacobian” into a deterministic feature
evaluated on the state that $`N_{0}`$ actually returned,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sigma_{\min}\!\left(J_{\text{calc}}(x_{i}^{N_{0}};\xi_{i})\right),
``` |  | (2) |

and the agent then proposes the following detector

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\underbrace{\sigma_{\min}\!\left(J_{\text{calc}}(x_{i}^{N_{0}};\xi_{i})\right)\leq 0.16}_{\text{condition }C}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\underbrace{\text{$N_{0}$ requires more nonlinear iterations}}_{\text{behavior }B}.
``` |  | (3) |

By examining the agent’s action history, we find that the threshold
$`0.16`$ was inferred during hypothesis discovery rather than taken from
the literature or hard-coded into the diagnostic skill. Specifically,
during the 200-case diagnosis round, the agent observes a lower-quartile
Jacobian minimum singular value of approximately $`0.16008`$ and
proposes the nearby cutoff $`0.16`$. This cutoff identifies 50 cases
requiring $`5.78`$ nonlinear iterations on average, compared with
$`5.01`$ iterations for the remaining cases, providing initial evidence
for the hypothesized correlation. The agent then tests nearby cutoffs of
$`0.15`$ and $`0.17`$ to assess the robustness of this relationship,
before further confirming that the same correlation holds on the
subsequent 4,000-case verification pool. After these checks, the
$`0.16`$ threshold is frozen for subsequent verification, yielding the
final verified hypothesis.

This directly sets the literature search question: which reproducible
methods achieve more nonlinear progress per Jacobian factorization while
preserving the frozen $`N_{0}`$ equations along with the error reduction
target? The search screens $`312`$ clean papers down to $`82`$
candidates and six fully read selections, producing $`40`$ verified
claims and nine method reviews, from which two methods proceed to
executable solver-skill packets: multistep-factorization-reuse, which
shares one factorization across several corrections, and
seventh-order-method9, which uses two reusable factorizations in a
higher-order update.

#### Solver skills.

With two generated skill packages, the agent made three changes to the
$`N_{0}`$ solver: (i) the correction rule applied inside a single outer
iteration; (ii) the baseline’s Gaussian elimination, which is separated
into a reusable factorization followed by forward and backward
substitution; (iii) and the trust region and backtracking line search,
which on the fast path are replaced by an explicit accept-reject test.
The ablation for the two skills are shown in
Table [5](#A2.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Diagnosis skills & hypothesis generation. ‣ B.1 GPT baseline over PFΔ ‣ Appendix B Technical case studies ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
where we can clearly tell that skill Multistep is specifically for
improving the solver efficiency while maintaining the error performance,
and skill Method 9 is focusing more on improving the solver performance.

We first describe the first skill: Multistep Factorization Reuse, which
targets the repeated factorization cost of $`N_{0}`$ directly. Instead
of factorizing a new Jacobian for every correction, it fixes the
Jacobian at the current outer iterate and reuses its factors across
several internal corrections. The evaluated implementation uses four
stages. Starting with $`y_{k,1}=x_{k}`$, it computes

|  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle m_{k,i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}y_{k,j},`$ |  | $`\displaystyle i=2,3,4,`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle J(x_{k})\,d_{k,i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=F(m_{k,i}),`$ |  | $`\displaystyle i=2,3,4,`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle y_{k,i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=m_{k,i}+i\,d_{k,i},`$ |  | $`\displaystyle i=2,3,4,`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{k+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{4}y_{k,j}.`$ |  |  |  |  |

The positive correction sign follows the specified-minus-calculated
mismatch convention of $`N_{0}`$. All three linear systems share the
same matrix $`J(x_{k})`$, so one outer update now requires one Jacobian
assembly, one factorization, and three right-hand-side solves. By
tracing the agent thinking traces, we find this skill is learnt by
reading [Tostado-Véliz et al. (2020a)](#bib.bib42) from the literature
space; specifically, the agent extracted the multistep update from
Section II-B, Eqs. (4a)–(4c).

We next introduce the second skill: Seventh-order Method 9, which
instead buys a higher convergence order using two reusable Jacobian
factorizations. Its four corrections are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle y_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=x_{k}+J(x_{k})^{-1}F(x_{k}),`$ |  | (5) |
|  | $`\displaystyle z_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=y_{k}+J(x_{k})^{-1}F(y_{k}),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle m_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{y_{k}+z_{k}}{2},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle w_{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=y_{k}+J(m_{k})^{-1}F(y_{k}),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{k+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=w_{k}+J(m_{k})^{-1}F(w_{k}).`$ |  |  |

Here, inverse notation denotes linear solves, as the implementation does
not explicitly form matrix inverses. The first factorization, at
$`x_{k}`$, is reused to compute $`y_{k}`$ and $`z_{k}`$; the second, at
the midpoint $`m_{k}`$, is reused to compute $`w_{k}`$ and
$`\widehat{x}_{k+1}`$. One outer update therefore requires two
assemblies, two factorizations, and four linear solves, using residuals
at $`x_{k}`$, $`y_{k}`$, and $`w_{k}`$, followed by an additional
residual evaluation to check the proposed outer state. By tracing the
agent’s trajectories, we find that this skill is learnt from
[Tostado-Véliz et al. (2020b)](#bib.bib43), where the agent extracted
Eq. (9) and Algorithm 1 in Section IV-B.

### B.2 Qwen baseline over AC-OPF

We walk through the second case study, with nearly $`84\%`$ pass rate
improvement over the $`N_{0}`$ solver. This time, the domain is
constrained optimization rather than the power-equation solving of
§[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 GPT baseline over PFΔ ‣ Appendix B Technical case studies ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
and the baseline is Qwen. Recall that in
§[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 GPT baseline over PFΔ ‣ Appendix B Technical case studies ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
the agent learns *cost* strategies: $`N_{0}`$ already reaches the
solution, and the skills make each step toward it cheaper and more
accurate. Here instead, it learns a *scaling* strategy that genuinely
improve the qualitative behavior of the solver.

#### Initial $`N_{0}`$ solver.

derivatives, a fraction-to-boundary line search, a regularized fallback
for the KKT system, retention of the best iterate seen, and a stall
exit. Its outer loop is governed by a scalar first-order progress

#### Diagnosis skills & hypothesis generation.

We just directly jump into the core part of the diagnosis result, and
how it is generated follows from the same procedure in
§[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 GPT baseline over PFΔ ‣ Appendix B Technical case studies ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery").
The most important hypothesis, $`H_{1}`$, is built from a distributional
invariance that the agent reads directly off the trace. On the failing
rows the reported stationarity residual lies in
$`[3718.78,\,3718.943]`$, within $`3.17\times 10^{-5}`$ relative of

|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|-----|

       ``` math

which is the same constant on every case in the population, because the
IEEE-57 generator cost vector is linear and invariant to both the
contingency and the load draw. A residual pinned at the raw cost
gradient is the signature of a point at which the multipliers are still
essentially zero. So, the agent therefore proposes the following
symptom-condition hypothesis:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\underbrace{\#\{\texttt{ipm.py::\_solve\_kkt}\}\;\leq\;27}_{\text{condition }C}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\underbrace{\text{$N_{0}$ does not converge}}_{\text{behavior }B},

If we get back to the $`N_{0}`$ solver, from the traced first-iteration
state: initial dual steps of magnitude
$`1.81\times 10^{6}`$–$`2.17\times 10^{7}`$ drive the
fraction-to-boundary rule to a primal step fraction of
$`2.67\times 10^{-8}`$–$`1.50\times 10^{-7}`$, so the iterate does not

interior-point solver’s first-order quantities commensurate, and which
normalization keeps a first-order residual interpretable when the

#### Solver skill generation.

With the search question proposed, the agent screens $`376`$ papers
across $`11`$ directions with $`4{,}136`$ screening judgments, retaining
$`49`$ direct and $`156`$ possible candidates, and places $`43`$
primaries over $`20`$ unique papers. The final skill located is
gmax-rowscale-kkt-measure, which is learned from an old but very
effective paper ([Wächter and Biegler, 2006](#bib.bib57)). The agent
combines two elements of that paper which $`N_{0}`$ uses neither of. The
first is the one-time automatic row scaling in §3.8 of [Wächter and
Biegler (2006)](#bib.bib57). With $`g_{\max}=100`$ and $`D_{x}=I`$,
computed once at the sanitized start, it has

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{f}=\min\!\Big\{1,\frac{g_{\max}}{\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|_{\infty}}\Big\},\ d_{e}^{(j)}=\min\!\Big\{1,\frac{g_{\max}}{\|A_{e}(x_{0})_{j,:}\|_{\infty}}\Big\},\ d_{i}^{(j)}=\min\!\Big\{1,\frac{g_{\max}}{\|A_{i}(x_{0})_{j,:}\|_{\infty}}\Big\},
``` |  | (8) |

which is applied as $`\tilde{f}=d_{f}f`$, $`\tilde{c}_{e}=D_{e}c_{e}`$,
$`\tilde{c}_{i}=D_{i}c_{i}`$ with the variable bounds untouched. Because
the factors are positive and diagonal this is an equivalence
transformation: same feasible set, same argmin, and multipliers and the
Lagrangian gradient map back exactly by $`y=D_{e}\tilde{y}/d_{f}`$,
$`\pi=D_{i}\tilde{\pi}/d_{f}`$,
$`r_{\text{stat}}=\tilde{r}_{\text{stat}}/d_{f}`$. On this population,
the rule is almost entirely a statement about the objective: the $`434`$
constraint rows have median $`\infty`$-norm exactly $`1.00`$ and maximum
$`138.27`$, so only $`2`$ of them are rescaled at all, while
$`\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|_{\infty}`$ is the constant of
[Eq. 6](#A2.E6 "In Diagnosis skills & hypothesis generation. ‣ B.2 Qwen baseline over AC-OPF ‣ Appendix B Technical case studies ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
which is roughly $`3{,}700\times`$ the typical constraint row and
$`27\times`$ the largest. The rule therefore proceeds as

|     |                                                    |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       d_{f}=\frac{100}{3718.8979}=2.68897\times 10^{-2},
       ```                                                 |     | (9) |

dividing the objective row by $`37.2`$ and leaving $`432`$ of $`434`$
constraint rows untouched.

#### On the practical essence of solver residue.

Specifically, it provides three practical impacts in realistic
large-scale grids: (i) tighter residual control improves the numerical
reliability of voltage and thermal-limit assessments following N1 or N2
contingencies. Near a regular operating point, the error in a monitored
quantity $`g`$ satisfies the first-order relation
$`\delta g\approx\nabla g^{\top}J^{-1}r`$, where $`J`$ is the power-flow
Jacobian and $`r`$ is the residual; a smaller residual therefore
improves the corresponding sensitivity-weighted error bound, enabling
more reliable assessment of small security margins ([Higham,
2002](#bib.bib58)).

Second, lower residuals help distinguish small changes caused by
redispatch, reactive-power support, or transformer adjustments from
numerical solution errors. This is particularly valuable for
finite-difference sensitivity calculations, in which errors from
individual power-flow solutions are amplified by the inverse
perturbation size ([Moré and Wild, 2012](#bib.bib60)).

Third, tighter power-balance satisfaction provides more consistent
equilibrium initial conditions for dynamic simulations, reducing the
potential for artificial transients caused by initialization mismatches
([Molzahn and Lesieutre, 2013](#bib.bib61)). These benefits make
residual control relevant beyond a binary convergence test, particularly
under stressed or ill-conditioned operating conditions ([Canizares and
Alvarado, 1993](#bib.bib59)); the required accuracy should be determined
by downstream sensitivities and decision margins.

#### On the role of diagnosis.

Effective solver improvement requires connecting observed failures to
the numerical methods capable of addressing them. Our case studies
illustrate why diagnosis is therefore essential, and why simply exposing
an agent directly to a broad literature space and asking it to improve
the solver is often ineffective. In
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Qwen baseline over AC-OPF ‣ Appendix B Technical case studies ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
the baseline already implements a primal-dual interior-point method, but
omits the specific scaling and progress-measure mechanisms needed to
resolve its stalling behavior. Diagnosis converts this observed failure
into a targeted search for row scaling and normalized KKT progress
measures from [Wächter and Biegler (2006)](#bib.bib57), well-established
techniques underlying Ipopt and described in a classic work that should
be already represented in the training data of frontier models. This
suggests that access to numerical knowledge itself is not the primary
barrier; rather, the central challenge is identifying which knowledge is
relevant to the failure at hand and retrieving it accordingly.

In
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 GPT baseline over PFΔ ‣ Appendix B Technical case studies ‣ Training Numerical Intelligence via Auto-Diagnosis and Skill Discovery"),
diagnosis similarly identifies progress per Jacobian factorization as
the relevant search objective, narrowing 312 papers to 82 candidates and
ultimately six papers for full-text examination. Separately, the
five-round GOC-500 experiment shows that repeated solver revisions alone
do not close the gap to ADSD. Together, these observations support
diagnosis as the mechanism that makes existing numerical knowledge
actionable: it determines which methods to seek, explains why they are
appropriate for the observed failure, and specifies the properties that
their implementation must preserve.
````

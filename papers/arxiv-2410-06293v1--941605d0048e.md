###### Abstract

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a
pivotal tool for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human
preferences. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), one of the most
popular approaches, formulates RLHF as a policy optimization problem
without explicitly estimating the reward function. It overcomes the
stability and efficiency issues of two-step approaches, which typically
involve first estimating the reward function and then optimizing the
policy via proximal policy optimization (PPO). Since RLHF is essentially
an optimization problem, and it is well-known that momentum techniques
can accelerate optimization both theoretically and empirically, a
natural question arises: Can RLHF be accelerated by momentum? This paper
answers this question in the affirmative. In detail, we first show that
the iterative preference optimization method can be viewed as a proximal
point method. Based on this observation, we propose a general
Accelerated Preference Optimization (APO) framework, which unifies many
existing preference optimization algorithms and employs Nesterov’s
momentum technique to speed up the alignment of LLMs. Theoretically, we
demonstrate that APO can achieve a faster convergence rate than the
standard iterative preference optimization methods, including DPO and
Self-Play Preference Optimization (SPPO). Empirically, we show the
superiority of APO over DPO, iterative DPO, and other strong baselines
for RLHF on the AlpacaEval 2.0 benchmark.

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a pivotal technique in the
era of artificial general intelligence and recently demonstrated
impressive capabilities in tasks such as text generation ([Bubeck
et al., 2023](#bib.bib14); [Anil et al., 2023](#bib.bib5); [Touvron
et al., 2023](#bib.bib69)), coding ([Chen et al., 2021](#bib.bib18);
[Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib6)), and problem solving ([Cobbe et al.,
2021](#bib.bib23); [Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib72)). A key element
contributing to these achievements is the alignment of LLMs with human
preference data, utilizing reinforcement learning from human feedback
(RLHF) ([Ziegler et al., 2019](#bib.bib82); [Christiano et al.,
2017](#bib.bib22); [Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib55); [Bai et al.,
2022](#bib.bib8); [Munos et al., 2023](#bib.bib50)).

The standard RLHF method ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib55)) involves
three main steps: feedback collection, reward modeling, and policy
optimization. Specifically, the LLM receives human-generated prompts and
produces several possible responses. Subsequently, human preferences for
these responses are collected and used to train a reward model that
matches these preferences. Finally, the policy optimization process
updates the large language model using optimization algorithms such as
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) ([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib61))
to produce responses with higher preference based on the trained reward
model.

Recently, [Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib57) introduced the Direct
Preference Optimization (DPO) method based on the Bradley-Terry (BT)
model ([Bradley and Terry, 1952](#bib.bib12)). This method skips the
reward modeling process and replaces it with a reparameterized reward
with respect to the policy, allowing for direct optimization of the LLM.
It simplifies the implementation, while maintaining a comparable or even
better performance than standard RLHF. Later, several works ([Munos
et al., 2023](#bib.bib50); [Azar et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Wu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib74); [Rosset et al., 2024](#bib.bib59)) considered the
general preference model rather than the BT model, and designed
algorithms (Nash-MD, IPO, SPPO, and DNO) that more flexibly represent
human preferences while maintaining a simple implementation.
Nonetheless, both the standard RLHF process and its variations rely on
the policy optimization process. In fact, for general optimization
problems, it is well-known that momentum techniques ([Polyak,
1964](#bib.bib56); [Nesterov, 2013](#bib.bib53); [Bubeck et al.,
2015b](#bib.bib16)) can accelerate the convergence of the optimization
algorithm in both theory and practice. Therefore, a natural question
arises:

Can reinforcement learning from human feedback be accelerated?

In this paper, we affirmatively answer this question. In details,
following [Xu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib76); [Yuan et al.
(2024b)](#bib.bib79); [Chen et al. (2024)](#bib.bib20); [Wu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib74), we consider the iterative preference optimization
framework. In this framework, a series of models is constructed, with
each model being improved based on the previous one using preference
optimization algorithm such as DPO and SPPO. We demonstrate that the
policy optimization problem under this framework resembles the proximal
point method ([Bregman, 1967](#bib.bib13); [Censor and Zenios,
1992](#bib.bib17); [Kiwiel, 1997](#bib.bib39)). Based on this
observation, we adapt Nesterov’s acceleration method ([Nesterov,
1983](#bib.bib51); [Nesterov, 2008](#bib.bib52); [Nesterov,
2013](#bib.bib53); [Lin et al., 2018](#bib.bib47)), and introduce a
variant of the iterative preference optimization framework, named
Accelerated Preference Optimization (APO). At the core of APO is an
extrapolation step after each policy update. The contributions of our
work are highlighted as follows:

- •
  We propose a general preference optimization framework, APO, based on
  Nesterov’s momentum to accelerate preference optimization. Our
  theoretical analysis shows that iterative DPO achieves an
  $`\widetilde{O}(1/t)`$ sub-optimality gap from the optimal policy
  under the Bradley-Terry (BT) model. As a comparison, our algorithm
  APO  achieves a smaller $`\widetilde{O}\big((1-\alpha)/t\big)`$
  sub-optimality gap, where $`\alpha`$ is the extrapolation parameter in
  the momentum. To the best of our knowledge, our work provides the
  first convergence analysis of vanilla iterative DPO and the first
  accelerated preference optimization algorithm with provable
  guarantees.
- •
  With an additional minimal sub-optimality gap assumption in the BT
  model, we prove that APO will convergence to the optimal policy in
  total variation (TV) distance at the rate of
  $`\exp\big(-O(t/(1-\alpha))\big)`$, improving the
  $`\exp\big(-O(t)\big)`$ rate of iterative DPO. In addition, we extend
  our results to the general preference model and show that APO with the
  SPPO loss function can also accelerate SPPO under a similar minimal
  sub-optimality gap assumption.
- •
  Empirically, we verify the performance of APO  when applied to DPO
  method on fine-tuning Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2. In the AlpacaEval 2.0
  evaluation tasks, APO  with 3 iterations achieves a length-controlled
  win rate of 31.73%, demonstrating a 1.78% improvement over iterative
  DPO and a 5.34% improvement over Snorkel’s Mistral-PairRM-DPO.
  Moreover, APO  with 2 iterations obtains a win rate of 37.53%,
  matching iterative DPO’s 37.65% with 3 iterations, with noticeably
  shorter response lengths. This is consistent with our theoretical
  analysis. In addition, the evaluation on five general
  instruction-following tasks from MT-Bench shows an average score of
  9.57 out of 10, further demonstrating APO’s superior performance.

## 2 Preliminaries

In the setting of RLHF, we assume a finite context set $`\mathcal{X}`$,
and possible response set $`\mathcal{Y}`$. For any prompts
$`x\in\mathcal{X}`$, a policy
$`\pi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{Y})`$ maps the prompt $`x`$
to the discrete distributions over the response set $`\mathcal{Y}`$. For
a given context $`x\in\mathcal{X}`$ collected from distribution
$`\rho`$, we generate two responses $`y_{1},y_{2}`$ with a reference
policy $`\mu`$ and receive preferences from either humans or more
advanced language models between these two responses
$`(y^{w}\succ y^{l})`$, where $`y^{w}`$ and $`y^{l}`$ represent the
preferred and dispreferred generated responses in $`\{y_{1},y_{2}\}`$.
Following [Christiano et al. (2017)](#bib.bib22); [Ouyang et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib55); [Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib57), we assume the
existence of a latent reward model $`r^{*}(x,y)`$, and the preference
distribution satisfies the Bradley-Terry (BT) model ([Bradley and Terry,
1952](#bib.bib12)):

|     |                                            |                                                                                                        |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y*{1}\succ y*{2} | x)=\frac{\exp\big(r^{_}(x,y\_{1})\big)}{\exp\big(r^{_}(x,y*{1})\big)+\exp\big(r^{\*}(x,y*{2})\big)}.`$ |     | (2.1) |

Under this assumption, the standard RLHF first estimates the reward
model by minimizing the following negative log-likelihood of BT model:

|     |                                                                                                                                  |     |       |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(r)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\log\sigma\big(r(x,y^{w})-r(x,y^{l})\big)\big],`$ |     | (2.2) |

where $`x`$ is generated from distribution $`\rho`$, $`\{y^{w},y^{l}\}`$
are collected with reference policy $`\mu`$ and
$`\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))`$ is the Sigmoid function. After the reward
modeling phase, the LLM (i.e., the policy) is fine-tuned with the
learned reward $`r(x,y)`$, which aims to maximize the expected reward
with KL-regularization:

|     |                                                                                      |                                                                      |                             |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | --------------- | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi\leftarrow\arg\max*{\pi\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}*{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi(\cdot | x)}[r(x,y)]-\beta\mathbb{E}\_{x\sim\rho}\big[\text{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot | x)\|\pi\_{\text{ref}}(\cdot | x)\big)\big],`$ |     | (2.3) |

where $`\Pi`$ denotes the policy class, $`\rho`$ is the distribution of
prompts, and the KL regularization with parameter $`\beta>0`$ is used to
control the deviation of the learned policy $`\pi`$ from the reference
policy $`\pi_{\text{ref}}`$. In detail, the optimization problem is
usually solved with the PPO method ([Schulman et al.,
2017](#bib.bib61)).

Later, [Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib57) identified the following
closed-form solution to the optimization problem in
([2.3](#S2.E3 "In 2 Preliminaries ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")):

|     |                          |                                               |
| --- | ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi(\cdot | x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\cdot\pi\_{\text{ref}}(\cdot | x)\cdot\exp\bigg(\frac{r(x,\cdot)}{\beta}\bigg),`$ |     |

where
$`Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(x)\cdot\exp\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big)`$ is
the partition function. By reparameterizing the reward function by the
policy and substituting it into the negative log-likelihood in
([2.2](#S2.E2 "In 2 Preliminaries ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")),
[Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib57) proposed the Direct Preference
Optimization (DPO) method as follows:

|     |                                                                                                                                           |                             |
| --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi\leftarrow\arg\max*{\pi\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}*{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigg[\log\sigma\bigg(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{w} | x)}{\pi\_{\text{ref}}(y^{w} | x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y^{l} | x)}{\pi\_{\text{ref}}(y^{l} | x)}\bigg)\bigg],`$ |     |

which avoids the explicit learning of a reward model. Here, for a finite
dataset $`\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})_{i=1}^{N}\}`$ and a
function
$`f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$,
we denote the empirical expectation of function $`f`$ with respect to
the dataset $`\mathcal{D}`$ by
$`\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[f(x,y^{w},y^{l})\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x_{i},y_{i}^{w},y_{i}^{l})/N`$.

## 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization

In this section, we present a general framework for language model
alignment, namely accelerated preference optimization (APO), which is
built upon the iterative preference optimization framework.

### 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework

Under the iterative Preference Optimization framework ([Xu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib76); [Yuan et al., 2024b](#bib.bib79); [Chen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib20); [Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib74)), the algorithm
progressively updates the policy $`{\pi}_{t}`$, aiming to converge to
the optimal policy. In detail, for each iteration $`t\in[T]`$, it
designates the reference policy as the policy generated from the
previous iteration, denoted by $`{\pi_{t}}`$. It estimates the reward
model by minimizing the expected loss function $`l(r,x,y^{w},y^{l})`$
over the dataset $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$:

|     |                                      |                                                                                                                                             |     |       |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle r_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ | $`\displaystyle\leftarrow\arg\max_{r(\cdot,\cdot)}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}}\big[\ell(r,x,y^{w},y^{l},\pi_{t})\big].`$ |     | (3.1) |

Then, it updates the reference policy by solving the following
KL-regularized optimization problem:

|     |                                      |                                                                                   |                                                                          |                    |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------ | --------------- | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leftarrow\arg\max*{\pi\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}*{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi(\cdot | x)}[r_{t}(x,y)]-\beta\mathbb{E}\_{x\sim\rho}\big[\text{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot | x)\|\pi\_{t}(\cdot | x)\big)\big].`$ |     | (3.2) |

According to [Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib57), for each iteration
$`t\in[T]`$, the optimization problem
([3.2](#S3.E2 "In 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
has the following closed-form solution:

|     |                                       |      |                                  |
| --- | ------------------------------------- | ---- | -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}\_{t+1}(y | x)`$ | $`\displaystyle\propto\pi\_{t}(y | x)\cdot\exp\bigg(\frac{r\_{t}(x,y)}{\beta}\bigg).`$ |     |

Thus, we can reparamterize the reward function for each policy $`\pi`$
as follows:

|     |                                |                                      |                |
| --- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ | -------------- | ------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle r_{\pi}(x,y)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y | x)}{\pi\_{t}(y | x)}.`$ |     |

With this reparameterized reward function, the previous two-step
optimization process in
([3.1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
and ([3.2](#S3.E2 "In 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
can be integrated into the following one-step direct preference
optimization:

|     |                                      |                                                                                                                                                              |     |       |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leftarrow\arg\min_{r_{\pi}\in\mathcal{R}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{w},y^{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{t}}\big[\ell(r_{\pi},x,y^{w},y^{l},\pi_{t})\big],`$ |     | (3.3) |

where $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ represents the data collected at iteration
$`t`$ using the reference policy $`\pi_{t}`$.

For the vanilla iterative preference optimization framework, the updated
policy $`\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}`$ is directly used as the reference policy
in the next iteration, where $`\pi_{t+1}=\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}`$. In this
situation, the iterative optimization of the policy resembles the
(Bregman) Proximal Point Method ([Bregman, 1967](#bib.bib13); [Censor
and Zenios, 1992](#bib.bib17); [Kiwiel, 1997](#bib.bib39)), which
iteratively minimizes the following proximal subproblem:

|     |                              |                                                                                                        |     |       |
| --- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\pi}_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leftarrow\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\Big\{f_{t}(\pi)=L_{t}(\pi)+\beta D(\pi,\pi_{t})\Big\},`$ |     | (3.4) |

for a given regularization parameter $`\beta`$ and Bregman divergence
$`D(\cdot,\cdot)`$. In this reduction, the expected reward
$`-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[r_{t}(x,y)]`$ corresponds to
the target function $`L_{t}(\pi)`$ in the proximal function
$`f_{t}(\pi)`$, and the Bregman divergence $`D(\pi,\pi_{t})`$ is chosen
as the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence to the behavior policy
$`\pi_{t}`$:
$`\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\big[\text{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot|x)|\pi_{t}(\cdot|x)\big)\big].`$

The Choice of Loss Function By choosing different loss function
$`\ell(r_{\pi},x,y^{w},y^{l},\pi_{t})`$ in
([3.3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")),
we can recover many existing iterative preference optimization
algorithms. In detail, the loss function depends on the preference model
$`\mathbb{P}`$, and we provide several concrete examples of the loss
function and the corresponding preference optimization algorithms as
follows.

###### Example 3.1 (DPO).

If we choose the loss function in
([3.3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
as:

|     |                                                                   |                                                                           |     |
| --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\ell_{\text{DPO}}(r_{\pi},x,y^{w},y^{l},\pi_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\log\sigma\big(r_{\pi}(x,y^{w})-r_{\pi}(x,y^{l})\big),`$ |     |

it recovers DPO ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib57)).

###### Example 3.2 (SPPO).

If we choose the loss function in
([3.3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
as:

|     |                                                                    |                                                                                                                                                  |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\ell_{\text{SPPO}}(r_{\pi},x,y^{w},y^{l},\pi_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(r_{\pi}(x,y^{w})-1+\log Z_{\pi_{t}}(x)\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\big(r_{\pi}(x,y^{l})+\log Z_{\pi_{t}}(x)\big)^{2},`$ |     |

where
$`Z_{\pi_{t}}(x)=\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{t}({y}|{x})\exp\big(\eta\mathbb{P}({y}\succ\pi_{t}|{x})\big)`$
represents the partition function for behavior policy $`\pi_{t}`$, it
recovers the Self-Play Preference Optimization (SPPO) algorithm ([Wu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib74)). (See Appendix
[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Reduction to SPPO with ℓ\_"SPPO" ‣ Appendix B Accelerated Preference Optimization with General Preferences ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
for a more detailed discussion.)

###### Example 3.3 (IPO).

If we choose the loss function in
([3.3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
as

|     |                                                                                                                              |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\ell_{\text{IPO}}(r_{\pi},x,y^{w},y^{l},\pi_{t})=\big(r_{\pi}(x,y^{w})-r_{\pi}(x,y^{l})-\tau^{-1}\big)^{2},`$ |     |

where $`\tau`$ is a regularization parameter, it recovers the Identity
Preference Optimization (IPO) algorithm ([Azar et al.,
2024](#bib.bib7)).

|     |                                                                                                                                                                |     |       |

### 3.2 Accelerated Preference Optimization

So far, we have demonstrated that the iterative preference optimization
framework resembles the proximal point method. For standard optimization
problems, it is well known that Nesterov’s momentum method ([Nesterov,
1983](#bib.bib51); [Nesterov, 2008](#bib.bib52); [Nesterov,
2013](#bib.bib53)) can accelerate the optimization algorithm both
theoretically and empirically. In particular, [Lin et al.
(2018)](#bib.bib47) proposed a framework called Catalyst, which extends
Nesterov’s momentum method to the proximal point method and has shown
that it can accelerate it provably. In the Catalyst method, after
solving the proximal operator

|     |                                                                                    |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\arg\min_{x}\Big\{f_{t}(x)=f(x)+\kappa D(x,y_{t})\Big\},`$ |     |

where $`f(x)`$ is the target function and $`D(x,y_{t})`$ is the Bregman
divergence, an extrapolation step is introduced as follows:

|     |                                                              |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle y_{t+1}=x_{t+1}+\alpha_{t}(x_{t+1}-x_{t}),`$ |     |

where $`\alpha_{t}`$ is the Nesterov’s extrapolation parameter and
$`x_{t+1}-x_{t}`$ denotes the momentum from the previous update.
Following the above idea, we introduce an extrapolation step after
solving
([3.3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
to obtain $`\pi_{t+1}`$:

|     |                                 |                                                                                              |     |
| --- | ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\log\pi\_{t+1}(y | x)=\log\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}+\alpha\big(\log\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}-\log\widehat{\pi}\_{t}\big),`$ |     | (3.7) |

where $`\alpha>0`$ is the fixed Nesterov’s extrapolation parameter.
After normalizing the policy $`\pi_{t+1}(y|x)`$, we obtain

|     |                             |                                                                       |                                              |
| --- | --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi\_{t+1}(y | x)`$                                                                  | $`\displaystyle\propto\widehat{\pi}\_{t+1}(y | x)\cdot\big(\widehat{\pi}\_{t+1}(y | x)/\widehat{\pi}\_{t}(y | x)\big)^{\alpha}`$ |     |
|     |                             | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z^{\prime}_{t}(x)}\cdot\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}(y | x)\cdot\big(\widehat{\pi}\_{t+1}(y           | x)/\widehat{\pi}\_{t}(y            | x)\big)^{\alpha},`$     |                    |

where
$`Z^{\prime}_{t}(x)=\sum_{y}\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}(y|x)\cdot\big(\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}(y|x)/\widehat{\pi}_{t}(y|x)\big)^{\alpha}`$
represents the partition function.

Putting together all the key components discussed above, we present the
APO framework in Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

We notice that the extrapolation step in
([3.7](#S3.E7 "In 3.2 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
is similar to the model extrapolation technique introduced by [Zheng
et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib80), which aims to develop a stronger model by
extrapolating from the aligned model and the SFT model. However, there
are several notable differences between APO and ExPO. First, the
extrapolation step in ExPO is based on the strong assumption that a
medium-aligned model can be interpolated between a weaker model and a
stronger model–an assumption that lacks theoretical support. In
contrast, APO is based on the observation that iterative preference
optimization resembles the (Bregman) Proximal Point method and the
extrapolation step follows the Nesterov’s momentum technique ([Nesterov,
1983](#bib.bib51); [Lin et al., 2018](#bib.bib47)), which has provable
guarantee. Regarding algorithm design, APO  is an iterative algorithm
for policy optimization. For the final iteration $`T`$, similar to the
Catalyst algorithm, APO outputs the policy $`\widehat{\pi}_{T+1}`$
before the extrapolation step. In contrast, ExPO is an one-shot
algorithm which only has a single iteration and outputs the final policy
after an extrapolation step. In this sense, ExPO can be seen as a
special case of APO with only one iteration.

## 4 Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we provide a theoretical analysis of APO in Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

We begin with the following theorem, which outlines the optimization
dynamics of the policy $`\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}`$ over different iterations
$`t\in[T]`$.

###### Theorem 4.1.

Suppose that
$`\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}(y|x)\cdot\big(\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}(y|x)/\widehat{\pi}_{t}(y|x)\big)^{\alpha}`$
belongs to the policy class $`\Pi`$ for each iteration $`t\in[T]`$.
Then, the updated policy $`\widehat{\pi}_{t}`$ in Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
satisfies

|     |                                       |      |                                                           |
| --- | ------------------------------------- | ---- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}\_{t+1}(y | x)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z*{t}(x)}\cdot\pi*{\text{ref}}(y | x)\cdot\exp\Bigg(\frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\sum*{i=0}^{t}\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}-\frac{\alpha^{t+1-i}}{1-\alpha}\bigg)\cdot r*{i}(x,y)\Bigg),`$ |     |

where
$`r_{t}(x,y)=\beta\log\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}(y|x)-\beta\log\pi_{t}(y|x)`$
represents the reparameterized reward at iteration $`t`$, and
$`Z_{t}(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\cdot\exp\big(\sum_{i=0}^{t}(1/(1-\alpha)-\alpha^{t+1-i}/(1-\alpha))\cdot r_{i}(x,y)/\beta\big)`$
is the partition function.

Theorem
[4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 4.1. ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
illustrates how the policy $`\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}`$ evolves with respect
to the reparameterized reward $`r_{t}(x,y)`$, which is highly dependent
on the choice of the loss function $`\ell`$ in Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

For the Bradley-Terry (BT) model with the loss function
$`\ell_{\text{DPO}}`$ in Example
[3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Example 3.1 (DPO). ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
Theorem 1 in [Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib57) demonstrates that all
reward functions compatible with the BT model can be expressed by the
reparameterized reward. In addition, we introduce the following two
assumptions, which are required by our analysis.

###### Assumption 4.2 (Realizability).

For each iteration $`t\in[T]`$ and each policy $`\pi\in\Pi`$, the
following updated policy belongs to the policy class $`\Pi`$:

|     |                                    |                                        |
| --- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}(\cdot | x)=\frac{1}{Z\_{\pi}(x)}\cdot\pi(\cdot | x)\cdot\exp\bigg(\frac{r^{\*}(x,\cdot)}{\beta}\bigg)\in\Pi,`$ |     |

where $`Z_{\pi}(x)=\sum_{y}\pi(x)\cdot\exp\big(r^{*}(x,y)/\beta\big)`$
is the partition function.

###### Assumption 4.3 (Boundedness).

For each iteration $`t\in[T]`$ and each policy $`\pi,\pi_{t}\in\Pi`$, we
have

|     |                                     |                |
| --- | ----------------------------------- | -------------- | --------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\beta\log\frac{\pi(y | x)}{\pi\_{t}(y | x)}\in[-R,R],`$ |     |

for all $`x\in\mathcal{X},u\in\mathcal{Y}`$.

Similar assumptions have been used in [Rosset et al. (2024)](#bib.bib59)
to provide an analysis of the statistical error for the reparameterized
reward. Equipped with these assumptions, we have the following
performance guarantee for APO.

###### Theorem 4.4 (APO with $`\ell_{\text{DPO}}`$).

For the Bradley-Terry (BT) model with loss function
$`\ell_{\text{DPO}}`$, under Assumptions
[4.2](#S4.Thmtheorem2 "Assumption 4.2 (Realizability). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
and

with probability at least $`1-\delta`$, the sub-optimality gap between

$`\pi^{*}(x)=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r^{*}(x,y)`$ is bounded by

| --- | --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi^{\*}(\cdot                                                                             | x)}\big[r^{*}(x,y)\big]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\widehat{\pi}_{T+1}(\cdot | x)}\big[r^{*}(x,y)\big]`$                         |     |

###### Remark 4.5.

The sub-optimality gap in Theorem
[4.4](#S4.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 4.4 (APO with ℓ*"DPO"). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
consists of two terms: the optimization error

there exists a tradeoff between these two errors. Specifically, a larger
extrapolation parameter $`\alpha\rightarrow 1`$ will decrease the
optimization error but increase the statistical error. Moreover, when
the dataset size is sufficiently large ($`N\rightarrow\infty`$), Theorem
[4.4](#S4.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 4.4 (APO with ℓ*"DPO"). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
suggests a $`\widetilde{O}\big((1-\alpha)\beta/T\big)`$ sub-optimality

[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
with the loss function $`\ell_{\text{DPO}}`$. Compared with the standard
iterative DPO method with $`\alpha=0`$,
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
improves the sub-optimality gap by a factor of $`1/(1-\alpha)`$ thanks
to the Nesterov’s momentum technique.

[4.4](#S4.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 4.4 (APO with ℓ*"DPO"). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
only provides theoretical guarantee on the sub-optimality gap for the
policy $`\widehat{\pi}*{T+1}`$. In order to derive the convergence of
policy $`\widehat{\pi}\_{T+1}`$ to the optimal policy $`\pi^{\*}`$, we
need the following minimal sub-optimality gap assumption.

###### Assumption 4.6 (Minimal sub-optimality gap).

For each prompt $`x\in\mathcal{X}`$, let
$`y^{*}_{x}=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r^{*}(x,y)`$ be the optimal
response. The sub-optimality gap between the optimal response and any
other responses is strictly positive:
$`\min_{y\neq y^{*}_{x}}r^{*}(x,y^{*}_{x})-r^{*}(x,y)\geq\Delta>0`$

###### Remark 4.7.

For a general Bradley-Terry (BT) model without the minimal
sub-optimality gap, there may exist multiple responses that share the
same maximum reward, i.e.,
$`r(x,y_{1})=r(x,y_{2})=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r^{*}(x,y)`$. In this
case, the optimal policy is not unique, and it is impossible to show
that the policy $`\widehat{\pi}_{T+1}`$ converges to a specific optimal
policy.

###### Theorem 4.8 (APO with $`\ell_{\text{DPO}}`$).

For the Bradley-Terry (BT) model with loss function
$`\ell_{\text{DPO}}`$, under Assumptions
[4.2](#S4.Thmtheorem2 "Assumption 4.2 (Realizability). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
[4.3](#S4.Thmtheorem3 "Assumption 4.3 (Boundedness). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
and
[4.6](#S4.Thmtheorem6 "Assumption 4.6 (Minimal sub-optimality gap). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
with probability at least $`1-\delta`$, the TV-distance between
$`\widehat{\pi}_{T+1}`$ and the optimal policy
$`\pi^{*}(x)=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r^{*}(x,y)`$ is bounded by

|     |     |                                                                                                                                                                 |                   |
| --- | --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\Big[\text{D}_{\text{TV}}\big(\widehat{\pi}\_{T+1}(\cdot                                                                   | x),\pi^{\*}(\cdot | x)\big)\Big]`$                                    |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigg(-O\bigg(\frac{T\Delta}{(1-\alpha)}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta}\bigg)\Bigg)+O\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{(T+1)\sum*{t=0}^{T}\kappa*{t}\cdot\log\big(T | \Pi               | /\delta\big)}{N\beta^{2}(1-\alpha)^{2}}}\Bigg),`$ |     |

where the coverage coefficient $`\kappa_{t}`$ is defined in
([4.1](#S4.E1 "In Theorem 4.4 (APO with ℓ\_"DPO"). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")).

###### Remark 4.9.

The TV-distance between $`\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}`$ and the optimal policy
$`\pi^{*}(x)=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r^{*}(x,y)`$ includes both the
optimization error $`\exp\big(-O(T/(1-\alpha))\big)`$ and the
statistical error $`\widetilde{O}\big(\sqrt{T/(N(1-\alpha)^{2})}\big)`$.
Similar to Theorem
[4.4](#S4.Thmtheorem4 "Theorem 4.4 (APO with ℓ*"DPO"). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
there is a tradeoff between these two errors based on the choice of
$`\alpha`$. In addition, when the dataset size is sufficiently large
($`N\rightarrow\infty`$), Theorem
[4.8](#S4.Thmtheorem8 "Theorem 4.8 (APO with ℓ*"DPO"). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
suggests that APO  converges to the optimal policy at a rate of
$`\exp\big(-O(T/(1-\alpha))\big)`$. In comparison, iterative DPO is a
special case of our algorithm with $`\alpha=0`$, which converges to the
optimal policy at a slower rate of $`\exp\big(-O(T)\big)`$.

## 5 Experiments

In this section, we detail the experimental settings used to validate
the efficacy of our proposed APO  algorithm. We evaluate the model’s
performance across various benchmark tasks and explore the impact of
different momentum schedules.

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

Model and Datasets. We use Mistral AI’s Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 ([Jiang
et al., 2023a](#bib.bib35)) as our base model, which is a fine-tuned
version based on the pretrained Mistral-7B-v0.2 ([Jiang et al.,
2023a](#bib.bib35)) on several publicly available datasets for
instruction-following. This architecture has demonstrated robust
performance improvements over Llama2 13B Chat([Touvron et al.,
2023](#bib.bib69)) in tasks such as Chatbot Arena([Chiang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib21)), MT-Bench ([Zheng et al., 2024b](#bib.bib81)), and
other related evaluations. As a result, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 has
become a popular choice for base models in recent reinforcement learning
(RL) fine-tuning research ([Hoang et al., 2023](#bib.bib34); [Kawin
et al., 2023](#bib.bib38)). For training, we employ the UltraFeedback
dataset ([Cui et al., 2023](#bib.bib25)) with loss function
$`\ell_{\text{DPO}}`$. Unlike traditional fine-tuning methods that
depend on responses and preference labels generated by proprietary
models like GPT-4, we utilize only the instruction set from
UltraFeedback. All responses are autonomously generated by our model
following an online principle, and the preference pairs are labeled
using a separate reward model, PairRM ([Jiang et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib36)). The instruction set used for both training and
validation includes a total of 64k instructions that span a diverse
range of tasks, such as UltraChat, ShareGPT, Evol-Instruct, TruthfulQA,
FalseQA, and FLAN, as detailed in [Cui et al. (2023)](#bib.bib25). Over
three training iterations, we divided the instruction set into three
folds as in [Hoang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib34); [Kawin et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib38), allocating approximately 2k instructions per
iteration, with an additional 1k left out for validation. Overall, our
training pipeline is independent of human or GPT inputs. We provide
details on hyperparameters in
Appendix [F.1](#A6.SS1 "F.1 Hyperparameters ‣ Appendix F Additional Details in Experiments ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

Evaluation. For evaluating the performance of our model, we utilize
three common benchmarking tasks: the AlpacaEval 2.0 ([Li et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib45)), the MT-Bench ([Zheng et al., 2024b](#bib.bib81)),
and the Open LLM Leaderboard ([Beeching et al., 2023](#bib.bib11); [Gao
et al., 2023](#bib.bib30)). Among them, AlpacaEval 2.0 is the most
indicative benchmark for our method with the current choice of
experimental settings, focusing on general instruction-following
capabilities as assessed by GPT-4-Turbo, with outcomes measured by a
weighted win-rate against GPT-4-Turbo. Another important benchmark is
MT-Bench, which also targets instruction-following but offers less
differentiation between models. Additionally, we present results from
the Open LLM Leaderboard, which is based on accuracy of multiple-choice
questions.

AlpacaEval 2.0. As our primary evaluation metrics, AlpacaEval 2.0
incorporates an extensive set of 805 prompts. These prompts are
simplified versions derived from the AlpacaFarm ([Dubois et al.,
2023](#bib.bib27)) evaluation set, covering a wide range of topics such
as Health, Linguistics, Entertainment, Technology, Coding, Gaming, Arts,
Sports, and more ([Yuan et al., 2024b](#bib.bib79)). During the
evaluation, we consult the help of GPT-4-Turbo to compare the responses
generated by our model with those produced by GPT-4-Turbo. The final win
rate are weighted based on the uncertainty of the judge. Employing the
default AlpacaEval 2.0 pipeline, this metric demonstrates a 0.93
Spearman correlation and 68.1% agreement rate with human annotators ([Li
et al., 2023b](#bib.bib45)).

MT-Bench: MT-Bench comprises of 80 multi-turn questions across eight
distinct dimensions: Writing, Roleplay, Reasoning, Math, Coding,
Extraction, STEM, and Humanities. The evaluation process prompts the
GPT-4 judge to assign scores from 1 to 10 to the generated responses.
However, this scoring metric has limitations. Particularly, score
saturation occurs quickly in certain dimensions, making it difficult to
distinguish between models. Additionally, this can disadvantage models
that perform exceptionally well in these saturated dimensions, as their
distinctiveness is less recognizable.

Open LLM Leaderboard: The Open LLM Leaderboard evaluates models across
six different tasks, focusing on a variety of language modeling
capabilities. Each task consists of multiple-choice questions (MCQs),
with the most probable choice being selected and compared against the
correct answer. The final score is calculated based on accuracy. The
ability for answering MCQs are not directly related with
instruction-following abilities. The results are deferred to
Appendix [F.2](#A6.SS2 "F.2 Open LLM Leaderboard Evaluation ‣ Appendix F Additional Details in Experiments ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

Baselines. Starting from Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, we compare our method
with existing iterative training results, including iterative
DPO ([Hoang et al., 2023](#bib.bib34)) and iterative KTO ([Ethayarajh
et al., 2024](#bib.bib29); [Kawin et al., 2023](#bib.bib38)). We also
present results from our own iterative DPO training. All three baselines
utilize the same base model, training dataset, dataset splits, and
training pipelines, which effectively highlights the differences in
methodology. Additionally, as one of the pioneering approaches in
iterative DPO, we include the Self-Rewarding algorithm ([Yuan et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib79)) as another baseline. Note that different from other
baselines, the training for the Self-Rewarding algorithm incorporates
additional self-instruct data augmentation and is based on the LLama2
70B model, but requires no external reward model.

our training pipeline and main results. In

designate the top-ranked response as the winner and the bottom-ranked
response as the loser. Following the proximal point update described

we proceed with a momentum extrapolation. We note that when the policy
is a softmax linear function,

reduces to a momentum extrapolation in the parameter space.
Consequently, we carry out an extrapolation in the parameter space to
approximate the corresponding momentum
step ([3.6](#S3.E6 "In 7 ‣ Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
in the probability space.

Next, we discuss the effect of generation length. All iterative DPO
training baselines, including Snorkel’s Mistral-PairRM-DPO and our DPO
Iter3, exhibit a trend of increasing response length. This is inevitable
because all response pairs used for training are generated online and
ranked by PairRM, where longer sequences are more likely to be chosen as
winners. This effect accumulates as the model trains on its own
generation. We discuss this effect from the following perspectives:
First, both humans and GPT-4 exhibit length bias, and our goal is not to
prevent any length growth but to evaluate it from a perspective of
fairness. Therefore, we primarily refer to the LC win
rate[1](#footnote1 "footnote 1 ‣ 5.2 Experimental Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
which reflects the predicted win rate when response lengths are at the
same level as the baseline. Second, we note that APO Iter2 achieves a
significantly higher LC win rate and overall win rate compared to
Snorkel’s iterative DPO implementation, while generating much shorter
responses (2636 characters compared to 2736). When compared to our DPO
Iter3, APO Iter2 achieves a comparable win rate, but also with shorter
response lengths. This verifies a faster convergence rate and enhanced
performance of our methodology. Finally, this length growth is not
inherent to our method but is a result of the online training and PairRM
ranking mechanism. It can be mitigated if we query winner/loser pairs
and preference choices from GPT-4 or human, with much higher cost.

## 6 Conclusions and Future Work

In this work, we studied the iterative preference optimization framework
for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences and
showed that it resembles the proximal point method. Based on this
observation, we introduced a general framework, APO, incorporating
Nesterov’s momentum technique. Theoretically, we show that our method
achieves a faster convergence rate than the standard iterative DPO and
SPPO methods. Our experimental results demonstrate the superiority of
APO  over iterative DPO on the AlpacaEval 2.0 benchmark and on the
instruction-following tasks of MT-Bench, achieving both accelerated
convergence rate, and better final performance.

Limitation. Due to limited computational resources, we do not evaluate
APO with the SPPO ([Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib74)) loss function in the
current experiments, and we plan to investigate it in our future work.
Additionally, while our model demonstrates consistent improvements on
instruction-following tasks, it faces challenges in solving math
problems. This limitation is largely due to the choice of dataset and
the restriction of not utilizing additional information from GPT-4 or
human sources. In the future, we aim to address this by incorporating
larger datasets and leveraging GPT-4 supervision.

## Appendix A Related Work

### A.1 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

Learning from human feedback in reinforcement learning can be traced
back to [Knox and Stone (2009)](#bib.bib40); [Wirth et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib73) and was later popularized by [Christiano et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib22), which incorporated human preferences into deep
reinforcement learning. Recently, RLHF has gained popularity in natural
language processing and has become a paradigmatic method for fine-tuning
large language models (LLMs) ([Achiam et al., 2023](#bib.bib1); [Touvron
et al., 2023](#bib.bib69); [OpenAI, 2023](#bib.bib54)) to align with
human objectives. The standard process for alignment with human feedback
([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib55)) requires both reward modeling and
policy optimization. Recently, [Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib57)
proposed the Direct Preference Optimization method, which replaces the
reward modeling process with a reparameterized reward and directly
performs policy optimization using preference data. Similarly, [Kawin
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib38) proposed the Kahneman-Tversky Optimization
method (KTO) with a reparameterized reward, while considering different
human-aware loss functions.

More recently, iterative variants of policy optimization have garnered
increasing attention. [Xu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib76) investigated the
iterative preference optimization method and proposed the PAIRWISE
CRINGE algorithm, which iteratively generates a new preference dataset
using the current model and then updates the model with a combination of
the original preference data and the newly labeled data. Additionally,
they studied the iterative version of Direct Preference Optimization
(DPO) and demonstrated that it achieves better performance than standard
PPO or DPO. Later, [Yuan et al. (2024b)](#bib.bib79) studied
Self-Rewarding Language Models, where the algorithm iteratively updates
the reference policy in the DPO method and uses LLM-as-a-Judge prompting
([Zheng et al., 2024b](#bib.bib81)) to provide its own rewards for the
generated new preference data. Despite this work demonstrating the
superiority of iterative optimization process, there is a lack of
theoretical foundations for these practical frameworks. Recently, [Xiong
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib75) proposed Gibbs Sampling from Human Feedback,
offering theoretical analysis with the aid of linear function
approximation and the incorporation of an uncertainty bonus. Compared
with previous methods, the reference policy remains fixed, the newly
trained model is only used to generate new preference data, and this
trained model will not be inherited in the subsequent iteration. On the
contrary, our research focuses on studying the iterative DPO model
without incorporating an uncertainty bonus, and it continually updates
the reference policy throughout the process.

Most of the works motioned above rely on the assumption that the latent
preference distribution $`P(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|x)`$ follows the
Bradley-Terry (BT) model ([Bradley and Terry, 1952](#bib.bib12))), and
there exists a series of works focusing on general preference, where
human preference may not strictly be transitive. Under the general
preference assumption, iterative optimization ([Munos et al.,
2023](#bib.bib50); [Swamy et al., 2024](#bib.bib66); [Rosset et al.,
2024](#bib.bib59); [Wu et al., 2024](#bib.bib74)) is also employed to
find the Nash-equilibrium policy. We leave the extension of our
algorithm to general preference model in the future work.

### A.2 Accelerated Optimization with Momentum

The idea of accelerating gradient methods has been extensively explored
over the decades. One of the earliest contributions to this field was
the Polyak momentum ([Polyak, 1964](#bib.bib56)), which achieved faster
convergence by leveraging the history of previous iterates. However,
this early approach sometimes failed to converge even for strongly
convex objectives ([Lessard et al., 2016](#bib.bib43)). This was further
refined by Nesterov’s accelerated gradient (NAG) ([Nesterov,
1983](#bib.bib51)), with a guarantee for faster convergence rates for
general smooth convex functions. Following these foundational works,
other acceleration algorithms and analyses emerged for gradient descent
and its proximal variants ([Beck and Teboulle, 2009](#bib.bib10);
[Tseng, 2009](#bib.bib70); [Taylor et al., 2017](#bib.bib67); [Allen-Zhu
and Orecchia, 2014](#bib.bib4); [Bubeck et al., 2015a](#bib.bib15);
[Diakonikolas and Orecchia, 2017](#bib.bib26); [Cohen et al.,
2020](#bib.bib24)). In addition, there has been another line of work in
understanding Nesterov’s acceleration through continuous analysis and
Lyapunov function analysis ([Su et al., 2016](#bib.bib65); [Shi et al.,
2019](#bib.bib64); [Shi, 2021](#bib.bib62); [Shi et al.,
2022](#bib.bib63); [Chen et al., 2022](#bib.bib19)). Momentum techniques
have also proven effective in accelerating minimax game settings ([Jin
et al., 2022](#bib.bib37); [Kovalev et al., 2022](#bib.bib41);
[Thekumparampil et al., 2022](#bib.bib68); [Li et al.,
2023a](#bib.bib44); [Yuan et al., 2024a](#bib.bib78)). However, the
application of acceleration techniques to LLMs remains relatively
unexplored.

The Proximal Point Method (PPM) ([Moreau, 1965](#bib.bib49); [Martinet,
1970](#bib.bib48); [Rockafellar, 1976](#bib.bib58); [Güler,
1992](#bib.bib32); [Bauschke, 2011](#bib.bib9)) is a fundamental
methodology that achieves optimality by iteratively solving auxiliary
problems in a regularized form. Generally, these auxiliary problems
cannot be solved directly, and different approximation methods yield
various types of acceleration ([He and Yuan, 2012](#bib.bib33); [Salzo
et al., 2012](#bib.bib60)). One of the connections between PPM and NAG
is established in [Ahn and Sra (2022)](#bib.bib3), where the authors
interpret the updates of NAG as different approximations of the proximal
method. The Catalyst approach ([Lin et al., 2015](#bib.bib46); [Lin
et al., 2018](#bib.bib47)) represents another variant of PPM that
achieves accelerated convergence through practical inexact schemes ([Lin
et al., 2015](#bib.bib46); [Salzo et al., 2012](#bib.bib60)). This
method effectively integrates Nesterov’s acceleration with the proximal
point framework, making it applicable to a wide range of machine
learning algorithms, including gradient descent and block coordinate
descent. It can be applied to variance reduction algorithms ([Lin
et al., 2015](#bib.bib46); [Lin et al., 2018](#bib.bib47)),
demonstrating its strong adaptability to various machine learning
scenarios. Despite the widespread adoption of momentum acceleration in
both theoretical and practical applications, we are the first to
establish a connection between DPO and the proximal point method,
linking iterative DPO with the Catalyst approach, and providing a
comprehensive theoretical analysis of this interpretation.

## Appendix B Accelerated Preference Optimization with General Preferences

In the previous sections, we assumed the existence of a latent reward
function and focused on the Bradley-Terry (BT) model. However,
([Tversky, 1969](#bib.bib71)) observed that the preferences across all
possible responses may not be monotonous or transitive, which cannot be
represented by a latent reward function. In this section, we extend the
analysis to environments with general preferences and focus on the win
probability between different responses.

In detail, for an environment with general preferences, we denote the
probability that response $`y_{1}`$ is preferred over response $`y_{2}`$
given the context $`x`$ as $`\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|x)`$, and we
assume that the preference model is antisymmetric, such that
$`\mathbb{P}(y_{2}\succ y_{1}|x)=1-\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|x)`$.
Under this assumption, we define the reward
$`r_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}_{y_{1}\sim\pi,y_{2}\sim\pi^{\prime}}\big[\mathbb{P}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|x)\big]`$
as the win probability for policy $`\pi`$ against policy
$`\pi^{\prime}`$. Our goal is to identify the von Neumann winner ([Dudík
et al., 2015](#bib.bib28)) with the preference model. Specifically, the
von Neumann winner corresponds to the Nash equilibrium of the following
two-player zero-sum game:

|     |                                    |                                                                                                                                   |           |
| --- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle(\pi^{*},\pi^{*})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\arg\max*{\pi}\min*{\pi^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1}\sim\pi,y*{2}\sim\pi^{\prime}}\big[\mathbb{P}(y*{1}\succ y\_{2} | x)\big]`$ |     |
|     |                                    | $`\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\pi}\min_{\pi^{\prime}}r_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}.`$                                                          |           |

|     |     | $`\displaystyle\leq O\bigg(\frac{(T+1)\sum*{t=0}^{T}\kappa*{t}\cdot\log\big(T                                         | \Pi                                            | /\delta\big)}{N\beta^{2}(1-\alpha)^{2}}\bigg),`$                                                     |                                                               | (D.10)                                         |

where the first inequality holds due to
([D.9](#A4.E9 "In Proof of Lemma . ‣ D.2 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
with the fact that $`\alpha_{i}\leq 1/(1-\alpha)`$, the second
inequality holds due to the definition of coverage coefficient
$`\kappa_{t}`$ in
([4.1](#S4.E1 "In Theorem 4.4 (APO with ℓ\_"DPO"). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
and the last inequality holds due to Lemma
[D.3](#A4.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma D.3. ‣ D.2 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
with a union bound on the probability across all $`T`$ iterations.
Finally,
substituting ([D.10](#A4.E10 "In Proof of Lemma . ‣ D.2 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
into
([D.6](#A4.E6 "In Proof of Lemma . ‣ D.2 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")),
we have

|     |     |                                                                                                             |                                                |
| --- | --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------- | --------------------------- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\Big[\text{D}_{\text{TV}}\big(\widehat{\pi}\_{T+1}(\cdot               | x),\pi^{\*}\_{T+1}(\cdot                       | x)\big)\Big]`$                                    |                                                                               |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho}\Bigg[\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{\*}\_{T+1}(\cdot | x)}\bigg[\Big(\log\frac{\widehat{\pi}\_{T+1}(y | x)}{\pi^{\*}\_{T+1}(y                             | x)}\Big)^{2}\bigg]+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\widehat{\pi}_{T+1}(\cdot | x)}\bigg[\Big(\log\frac{\widehat{\pi}\_{T+1}(y | x)}{\pi^{\*}\_{T+1}(y | x)}\Big)^{2}\bigg]}\Bigg]`$ |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\leq O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{(T+1)\sum*{t=0}^{T}\kappa*{t}\cdot\log\big(T                         | \Pi                                            | /\delta\big)}{N\beta^{2}(1-\alpha)^{2}}}\bigg).`$ |                                                                               |

Thus, we complete the proof of Lemma

## Appendix E Proof of Lemmas in Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")

### E.1 Proof of Lemma [D.1](#A4.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma D.1. ‣ D.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")

In this section, we adapt the previous results on the estimation error
from maximum likelihood estimation to the Bradley-Terry (BT) model
([Bradley and Terry, 1952](#bib.bib12)) and provide the proof of Lemma
[D.1](#A4.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma D.1. ‣ D.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").
We start the analysis with the conditional probability estimation
setting. For an instance space $`\mathcal{X}`$ and a target space
$`\mathcal{Y}`$, we collect the dataset
$`\mathcal{D}={(z_{i},o_{i})}_{i=1}^{N}`$, where $`z_{i}`$ is sampled
from a reference policy $`z_{i}\sim\mu_{i}`$, and $`o_{i}`$ is then
generated with the latent conditional density
$`P(o_{i}|z_{i})=f^{*}(z_{i},o_{i})`$. Under this situation, the maximum
likelihood estimator across a function class
$`\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ can be
denoted as follows

|     |                                                                                  |     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle f=\arg\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log f(z_{i},o_{i}).`$ |     |

For an i.i.d. sampled dataset $`\mathcal{D}`$, where
$`\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\ldots=\mu_{N}`$, [Geer (2000)](#bib.bib31) (Chapter 7) provides upper bounds for the estimation error between $`f`$ and the
hidden function $`f^{*}`$. Later, [Agarwal et al. (2020)](#bib.bib2)
extended these results to reinforcement learning, allowing for a
martingale sampling process.

###### Lemma E.1 (Theorem 21, [Agarwal et al. 2020](#bib.bib2)).

For a finite function class $`|\mathcal{F}|<+\infty`$, if the latent
function $`f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}`$, then with probability at least
$`1-\delta`$, we have

|     |                                                                                                                             |             |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------- | --------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\sum*{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}*{z\sim\mu*{i}}\big\|f(z,\cdot)-f^{\*}(z,\cdot)\big\|*{\text{TV}}^{2}\leq 2\log\big( | \mathcal{F} | /\delta\big).`$ |     |

With the help of lemma
[E.1](#A5.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma E.1 (Theorem 21, ). ‣ E.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix E Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
we start to prove Lemma
[D.1](#A4.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma D.1. ‣ D.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

###### Proof of Lemma [D.1](#A4.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma D.1. ‣ D.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

Based on the Bradley-Terry (BT) model defined in equation
([2.1](#S2.E1 "In 2 Preliminaries ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")),
for the prompt $`x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}`$ and the generated responses
$`y_{1},y_{2}`$, we have

|     |                                    |                                                                 |
| --- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle P(y*{1}\succ y*{2} | x)=\sigma\big(r^{_}(x*{i},y*{i,1})-r^{_}(x*{i},y*{i,2})\big).`$ |     |

In this context, we define the instance $`z_{i}`$ and target $`o_{i}`$
as follows

|     |                         |                                                                          |     |
| --- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle z_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=(x_{i},y_{i,1},y_{i,2}),`$                               |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle o_{i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\ind(y_{i,1}\succ y_{i,2})-\ind(y_{i,2}\succ y_{i,1}).`$ |     |

For each policy $`{\pi}\in\Pi`$, we denote the reparameterized reward
$`r_{{\pi}}(x,y)=\beta\log{\pi}(y|x)-\beta\log\pi_{t}(y|x)`$. Thus, the
density function for an instance $`z=(x,y_{1},y_{2})`$ with reward
$`r_{\pi}`$ can be represented as

|     |                                                                                                           |     |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle f_{r_{\pi}}(z,o)=\sigma\Big(o\cdot\big(r_{{\pi}}(x,y_{1})-r_{{\pi}}(x,y_{2})\big)\Big),`$ |     |

and we denote the density function class $`\mathcal{F}`$ as
$`\mathcal{F}=\{f_{r_{\pi}}|\pi\in\Pi\}`$. Based on Assumption
[4.2](#S4.Thmtheorem2 "Assumption 4.2 (Realizability). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
and the definition of the Bradley-Terry (BT) model, we have
$`f_{r^{*}}\in\mathcal{F}`$, and the latent density function satisfies
$`P(o|z)=f_{r^{*}}(z,o)`$. Consequently, the maximum likelihood
estimator can be expressed as

|     |                                                                                                              |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle f_{r_{t}}=\arg\max_{f_{r_{\pi}}\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log f_{r_{\pi}}(z_{i},o_{i}),`$ |     |

where
$`r_{t}(x,y)=\beta\log\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}(y|x)-\beta\log\pi_{t}(y|x)`$.
Thus, according to Lemma
[E.1](#A5.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma E.1 (Theorem 21, ). ‣ E.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix E Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
the estimation error between $`r^{*}`$ and $`r_{t}`$ can be upper
bounded by

|     |                                                                                                                                          |     |                 |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --------------- | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\sum*{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}*{z\sim\mu*{i}}\big\|f*{r*{t}}(z,\cdot)-f*{r^{\*}}(z,\cdot)\big\|\_{\text{TV}}^{2}\leq 2\log\big( | \Pi | /\delta\big).`$ |     | (E.1) |

Since the dataset $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ is collected with reference policy
$`\pi_{t}`$, we have

|     |     |                                                                                                                                                                                                  |     |                     |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | ------------------- | --- | ----- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,(y_{1},y_{2})\sim\pi_{t}}\Big[\sigma\big(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\big)-\sigma\big(r_{t}(x,y_{1})-r_{t}(x,y_{2})\big)\Big]^{2}`$                        |     |                     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,(y_{1},y_{2})\sim\pi_{t}}\Big[\sigma\big(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\big)-\sigma\big(r_{t}(x,y_{1})-r_{t}(x,y_{2})\big)\Big]^{2}`$       |     |                     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,(y_{1},y_{2})\sim\pi_{t}}\Big[\sigma\big(r^{*}(x,y_{2})-r^{*}(x,y_{1})\big)-\sigma\big(r_{t}(x,y_{2})-r_{t}(x,y_{1})\big)\Big]^{2}`$ |     |                     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,(y_{1},y_{2})\sim\pi_{t}}\big\|f_{r_{t}}(z,\cdot)-f_{r^{*}}(z,\cdot)\big\|_{\text{TV}}^{2}`$                                                               |     |                     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\big(                                                                                                                                                               | \Pi | /\delta\big)}{N},`$ |     | (E.2) |

where the second equation holds due to the definition of TV-distance
with $`z=(x,y_{1},y_{2})`$ and the inequality holds due to
([E.1](#A5.E1 "In Proof of Lemma . ‣ E.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix E Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")).
According to Assumption
[4.3](#S4.Thmtheorem3 "Assumption 4.3 (Boundedness). ‣ 4 Theoretical Analysis ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
we have $`r^{*}(x,y)\in[-1,1]`$ and $`r_{t}(x,y)\in[-R,R]`$, which
implies that

|     |     |                                                                                                                                                                                       |     |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ------------------- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,(y_{1},y_{2})\sim\pi_{t}}\Big[\big(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})-r_{t}(x,y_{1})+r_{t}(x,y_{2})\big)^{2}\Big]`$                                   |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1+R)\cdot\big(1-\sigma(1+R)\big)^{2}}`$                                                                                                        |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,(y_{1},y_{2})\sim\pi_{t}}\Big[\sigma\big(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\big)-\sigma\big(r_{t}(x,y_{1})-r_{t}(x,y_{2})\big)\Big]^{2}`$ |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}(1+R)\cdot\big(1-\sigma(1+R)\big)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\log\big(                                                                                      | \Pi | /\delta\big)}{N},`$ |     |

where the first inequality holds due to
$`|a-b|\cdot|\min_{x\in[a,b]}\sigma^{\prime}(x)|\leq|\sigma(a)-\sigma(b)|`$
with the fact that
$`\sigma^{\prime}(x)=\sigma(x)(1-\sigma(x))\geq\sigma(1+R)\big(1-\sigma(1+R)\big)`$
and the last inequality holds due to
([E.2](#A5.E2 "In Proof of Lemma . ‣ E.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix E Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")).
Thus, we complete the proof of Lemma
[D.1](#A4.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma D.1. ‣ D.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").
∎

### E.2 Proof of Lemma [D.2](#A4.Thmtheorem2 "Lemma D.2. ‣ D.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")

###### Proof of Lemma [D.2](#A4.Thmtheorem2 "Lemma D.2. ‣ D.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

We prove this inequality based on two situations: $`x\geq 1`$ or
$`x<1`$. For the case that $`x\geq 1`$, we have

|     |                           |        |     |
| --- | ------------------------- | ------ | --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle(1+x)\cdot | \log x | `$  | $`\displaystyle=(1+x)\log x\geq(1+x)\bigg(1-\frac{1}{x}\bigg)\geq x\bigg(1-\frac{1}{x}\bigg)=x-1,`$ |     |

where the first inequality holds due to $`\log x\geq 1-1/x`$ and the
second inequality holds due to $`x\geq 1`$. We now consider the case
where $`x<1`$. In this case, we have

|     |                           |        |     |
| --- | ------------------------- | ------ | --- | -------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle(1+x)\cdot | \log x | `$  | $`\displaystyle=-(1+x)\log x\geq-(1+x)(x-1)\geq-(x-1),`$ |     |

where the first inequality holds due to $`\log x\leq x-1`$ and the
second inequality holds due to $`x<1`$. Combining the results in these
two cases, we complete the proof of Lemma
[D.2](#A4.Thmtheorem2 "Lemma D.2. ‣ D.1 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").
∎

### E.3 Proof of Lemma [D.3](#A4.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma D.3. ‣ D.2 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")

In this section, we provide the proof of Lemma
[D.3](#A4.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma D.3. ‣ D.2 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
and we summarize our definitions and assumptions as follows. Similar
assumptions are used in [Rosset et al. (2024)](#bib.bib59) to analyze
the statistical error from the reparameterized reward.

###### Definition E.2 (Feasible Policy Class).

For each iteration $`t\in[T]`$, let $`\Pi_{t}\subseteq\Pi`$ represent
the feasible policy class, which includes all potential policies
$`{\pi}_{t}`$ generated by Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")
during iteration $`t`$, based on various possible samplings from the
data collection process.

###### Assumption E.3 (Realizability).

For each iteration $`t\in[T]`$ and each potential policy
$`\pi\in\Pi_{t}`$, we assume that the following updated policy also
belongs to the policy class $`\Pi`$:

|     |                                    |                                        |
| --- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}(\cdot | x)=\frac{1}{Z\_{\pi}(x)}\cdot\pi(\cdot | x)\cdot\exp\bigg(\frac{r\_{\pi}^{\*}(x,\cdot)}{\beta}\bigg)\in\Pi,`$ |     |

where
$`r_{\pi}^{*}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\big[\mathbb{P}(y\succ y^{\prime}|x)\big]`$
denotes the win probability against the behavior policy $`\pi`$ and
$`Z_{\pi}(x)=\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi({y}|{x})\exp\big(r_{\pi}^{*}(x,y)/\beta\big)`$
is the partition function.

###### Assumption E.4 (Boundedness).

For each iteration $`t\in[T]`$ and each potential policy
$`\pi_{t}\in\Pi_{t}`$, we have

|     |                                                      |                |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------- | --------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\beta\log\frac{\widehat{\pi}\_{t+1}(y | x)}{\pi\_{t}(y | x)}\in[-R,R],`$ |     |

holds for all $`x\in\mathcal{X},u\in\mathcal{Y}`$ and any potential
collected data set $`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$.

Based on these assumption, we now begin the proof of Lemma
[D.3](#A4.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma D.3. ‣ D.2 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")

###### Proof of Lemma [D.3](#A4.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma D.3. ‣ D.2 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

For each policy $`\pi\in\Pi_{t}`$ and the corresponding reparameterized
reward $`r_{{\pi}}(x,y)=\beta\log\big(\pi(y|x)/\pi_{t}(y|x)\big)`$, we
have

|     |     |                                                                                                                           |                                                                                           |
| --- | --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot                                                                   | x)}\Big[r_{\pi}(x,y)-r_{t}^{*}(x,y)+\log Z_{\pi_{t}}(x)\Big]^{2}`$                        |                                                 |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot                                                                  | x)}\Big[r*{\pi}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}*{y^{\prime}\sim\pi\_{t}}\big[\mathbb{P}(y\succ y^{\prime} | x)\big]+\log Z*{\pi*{t}}(x)\Big]^{2}`$          |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot                                                       | x)}\big[r\_{\pi}(x,y)-\ind(y\succ y^{\prime}                                              | x)+\log Z*{\pi*{t}}(x)\big]^{2}+C*{\pi*{t}}`$   |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot                                                       | x)}\big[r\_{\pi}(x,y)-\ind(y\succ y^{\prime}                                              | x)+\log Z*{\pi*{t}}(x)\big]^{2}/2`$             |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot                                                 | x)}\big[r\_{\pi}(x,y^{\prime})-\ind(y^{\prime}\succ y                                     | x)+\log Z*{\pi*{t}}(x)\big]^{2}/2+C*{\pi*{t}}`$ |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y^{w},y^{l}\sim\pi_{t}}\big[\ell(r_{\pi},x,y^{w},y^{l},\pi_{t})\big]+C_{\pi_{t}},`$ |                                                                                           |

where
$`Z_{\pi_{t}}(x)=\sum_{{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{t}({y}|{x})\exp\big(r_{t}^{*}(x,y)/\beta\big)`$
is the partition function,
$`C_{\pi_{t}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot|x)}\big[\mathbb{P}(y\succ y^{\prime}|x)-\ind(y\succ y^{\prime}|x)\big]^{2}`$
is the variance of behavior policy $`\pi_{t}`$ and the third equation
holds because $`y,y^{\prime}`$ collected under the same behavior policy.
Therefore, the proximal point update described in
equation ([3.5](#S3.E5 "In 6 ‣ Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.1 Iterative Preference Optimization Framework ‣ 3 Accelerated Preference Optimization ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"))
is equal to the least square estimator:

|     |                                                                                                          |                                                                     |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot | x)}\Big[r_{\pi}(x,y)-r_{t}^{*}(x,y)+\log Z_{\pi_{t}}(x)\Big]^{2}.`$ |     |

Conditioned on Assumption
[E.3](#A5.Thmtheorem3 "Assumption E.3 (Realizability). ‣ E.3 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix E Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment"),
there exists a policy $`\pi_{t+1}^{*}\in\Pi`$ such that
$`r_{\pi_{t+1}^{*}}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}}\big[\mathbb{P}(y\succ y^{\prime}|x)\big]-\beta\log Z_{\pi_{t}}(x)`$.
Under this situation, according to the standard concentration argument,
the least-squares estimator $`\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}`$ satisfies

|     |                                                         |                                                                                                               |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ------------------------- | --- | ----- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y\sim\pi_{t}(\cdot | x)}\big[r_{\widehat{\pi}_{t+1}}(x,y)-r_{\pi_{t+1}^{*}}(x,y)\big]^{2}\leq O\bigg(\frac{(R+1)^{2}\cdot\log\big( | \Pi | /\delta\big)}{N}\bigg),`$ |     | (E.3) |

where the ineqaulity holds due to Lemma 6 in [Xu and Zeevi
(2020)](#bib.bib77) with the Assumption
[E.4](#A5.Thmtheorem4 "Assumption E.4 (Boundedness). ‣ E.3 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix E Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").
Therefore, we have

|     |     |                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |     |
| --- | --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ------------------------- | --- |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,(y_{1},y_{2})\sim\pi_{t}}\Big[\big(r_{t}^{*}(x,y_{1})-r_{t}^{*}(x,y_{2})-r_{t}(x,y_{1})+r_{t}(x,y_{2})\big)^{2}\Big]`$                                                           |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,(y_{1},y_{2})\sim\pi_{t}}\Big[\big(r_{\pi_{t+1}^{*}}(x,y_{1})-r_{\pi_{t+1}^{*}}(x,y_{2})-r_{t}(x,y_{1})+r_{t}(x,y_{2})\big)^{2}\Big]`$                                          |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y_{1}\sim\pi_{t}}\big[r_{\pi_{t+1}^{*}}(x,y_{1})-r_{t}(x,y_{1})\big]^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\rho,y_{2}\sim\pi_{t}}\big[r_{\pi_{t+1}^{*}}(x,y_{2})-r_{t}(x,y_{2})\big]^{2}`$ |     |
|     |     | $`\displaystyle\leq O\bigg(\frac{(R+1)^{2}\cdot\log\big(                                                                                                                                                              | \Pi | /\delta\big)}{N}\bigg),`$ |     |

where the first inequality holds due to $`(x+y)^{2}\leq 2x^{2}+2y^{2}`$
and the last inequality holds due
to ([E.3](#A5.E3 "In Proof of Lemma . ‣ E.3 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix E Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment")).
Thus, we complete the proof of Lemma
[D.3](#A4.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma D.3. ‣ D.2 Proof of Lemma ‣ Appendix D Proof of Lemmas in Appendix ‣ Accelerated Preference Optimization for Large Language Model Alignment").

## Appendix F Additional Details in Experiments

### F.1 Hyperparameters

Our training is conducted on eight A100 GPUs, setting a global batch
size of 64. This is done through setting a local batch size of 8 across
all GPUs, with a gradient accumulation step of 1. We utilize the RMSProp
optimizer for each iteration, tuning the learning rate and $`\beta`$
parameters. Although we initially considered a range of learning rates
from $`[5\times 10^{-7},1\times 10^{-7},5\times 10^{-8}]`$, we found
that a fixed learning rate of $`5\times 10^{-7}`$ while adjusting
$`\beta`$ across iterations results in enhanced performance. The final
$`\beta`$ value is set at 0.01 for the first iteration, 0.1 for the
second iteration, and 1.0 for the third iteration. The extrapolation
parameter $`\alpha`$ is set to be a constant 0.3. In addition, the
learning rate follows a linear schedule with a warm-up ratio of 0.1. For
inference, we employ the vllm library ([Kwon et al., 2023](#bib.bib42))
for response generation, configured with a temperature of 0.7 and a
top_p of 0.9. The maximum token length for response generation is set to
2048 tokens. Finally, evaluations across all benchmarks are conducted
using eight A6000 GPUs.

### F.2 Open LLM Leaderboard Evaluation

We provide the result of Open LLM Leaderboard. We calculate the average
scores across six tasks, as well as the average of five tasks excluding
GSM8k. This exclusion is due to our training’s emphasis on general

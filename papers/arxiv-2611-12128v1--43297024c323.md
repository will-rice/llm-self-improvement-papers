# Toward Sustainable Agentic AI: Carbon Tradeoffs Across Iterative Self-Refinement and Tool-Augmented Coding

Boris Ruf    Adrien de Mailly Nesle    Marcin Detyniecki

###### Abstract

Agentic language model systems can improve performance either by
iterating on their own outputs or by using external tools in grounded
environments. While these strategies can reduce error rates and
substitute for larger models in some settings, their environmental cost
remains relatively underexplored. We present two complementary empirical
studies that estimate the energy and carbon footprint of agentic AI
across settings of increasing complexity. Study 1 evaluates iterative
verbal self-refinement: GPT-4.1-mini at $`k=1`$–$`5`$ steps versus a
GPT-4.1 single-pass baseline on two MATH benchmark splits. Study 2
evaluates tool-augmented agentic coding: GPT-4.1-mini, Claude Opus 4.6,
and GPT-5.3-codex on SWE-bench Lite, where agents operate inside
Docker-isolated environments with access to code execution and file
editing tools. In Study 1, $`k=3`$ refinements close a
16-percentage-point accuracy gap to within 1 point, but nearly triple
the carbon footprint relative to the cheap single-pass baseline while
remaining below the GPT-4.1 single-pass baseline under the current
token-based carbon scenario. In Study 2, GPT-5.3-codex achieves the
highest resolution rate in this sample (81.2%), ahead of Claude Opus 4.6
(68.9%) and GPT-4.1-mini (25.0%), but resolved-task-normalized
efficiency remains mixed: GPT-4.1-mini is lowest on estimated carbon and
cost per resolved task, while GPT-5.3-codex delivers substantially
higher task success. All carbon estimates cover output-token inference
only; prompt tokens, which exceed 99% of tokens in Study 2, and tool
execution are not charged, so the figures are lower bounds on the full
agentic footprint.

## 1 Introduction

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly rely on two broad
strategies to improve performance: iterative self-refinement and
tool-augmented interaction. In self-refinement, the model generates an
initial response, critiques it, and revises it over multiple rounds
[Madaan et al. (2023)](#bib.bib1); [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib2). In
tool-augmented settings, agents use code execution, file editing, and
test runners to ground decisions in external feedback [Yao et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib3); [Wang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4). Both strategies can
improve task outcomes and, in some settings, allow smaller models to
approach the quality of larger ones [Yao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib3);
[Chavan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib5).

From a _sustainability_ perspective, both framings are incomplete.
Self-refinement does not eliminate cost—it shifts it from model
parameters to API calls and execution time. Each additional refinement
step or tool invocation consumes prompt tokens, completion tokens,
compute time, and energy. Understanding how quickly the carbon footprint
compounds as a function of refinement depth, agent architecture, and
model choice is essential for responsible deployment of agentic systems.

This paper makes contributions in the following fields:

- •
  Iterative Self-Refinement (Study 1). A controlled, forced-$`k`$
  evaluation of GPT-4.1-mini ($`k=1`$–$`5`$) against a GPT-4.1
  single-pass baseline on MATH Level 3–4 and Level 4–5, with per-call
  token, energy, and carbon tracking.
- •
  Tool-Augmented Agentic Coding (Study 2). A carbon analysis of a
  tool-augmented agentic coding benchmark: GPT-4.1-mini, Claude
  Opus 4.6, and GPT-5.3-codex on SWE-bench Lite [Jimenez et al.
  (2024)](#bib.bib6) under Docker isolation, estimating the LLM-side
  inference footprint of real-world software engineering tasks.

## 2 Related Work

#### Self-refinement in LLM agents.

Self-Refine was introduced in [Madaan et al. (2023)](#bib.bib1),
demonstrating that iterative self-feedback improves performance across a
range of tasks. Reflexion [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib2) frames
refinement as verbal reinforcement learning. Both lines of work focus on
accuracy gains, including on mathematical reasoning tasks [Jiang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib7); energy costs are not reported. ReAct [Yao et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib3) extends agents to use tools, interleaving reasoning
and acting in a grounded loop.

#### Agentic software engineering.

SWE-bench [Jimenez et al. (2024)](#bib.bib6) introduced a realistic
benchmark where agents must resolve real GitHub issues by editing source
code and passing repository tests. OpenHands [Wang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib4) provides an open agent framework for this setting,
running each task in a Docker-isolated workspace with access to bash,
file editing, and browsing tools. AutoAgents [Chen et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib8) demonstrates how agents can be generated dynamically
to coordinate specialised tools across complex multi-step tasks. No
prior work has characterised the carbon cost of these agentic coding
pipelines.

#### Energy and carbon of NLP.

The energy cost of NLP training was surfaced in [Strubell et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib9); subsequent work extended this to inference [Lottick
et al. (2019)](#bib.bib10); [Bannour et al. (2021)](#bib.bib11).
Patterson et al. [Patterson et al. (2021)](#bib.bib12) quantified the
carbon of large model training and highlighted the role of data-centre
location and grid mix. For inference, Samsi et al. [Samsi et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib13) measured the energy of LLM inference on GPUs, and
Luccioni et al. [Luccioni et al. (2024)](#bib.bib14) showed that
generative, multi-purpose models consume substantially more energy per
query than task-specific ones. EcoLogits [Rincé and Banse
(2025)](#bib.bib15) provides per-token electricity estimates for major
API models, which we build on here; energy scaling laws have been
proposed to predict GPU energy for generative inference from its compute
requirements [Iyengar et al. (2026)](#bib.bib16), and profiles how
input-token energy differs from output-token energy [Ruf and Detyniecki
(2026)](#bib.bib17). These works characterise single inference calls; we
study how cost compounds across the multi-call loops of agentic systems.

#### Cost-aware agents.

Agent evaluation has increasingly called for reporting cost alongside
accuracy [Kapoor et al. (2025)](#bib.bib18), and cascading strategies
such as FrugalGPT [Chen et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19) trade model size
against call volume to reduce API spend. Separately, Huang et al. [Huang
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib20) show that intrinsic self-correction without
external feedback does not reliably improve reasoning, which motivates
measuring when additional refinement steps stop paying off. We extend
this cost-aware view from monetary cost to energy and carbon.

## 3 Carbon Estimation

Detailed carbon data for cloud AI inference services is generally not
publicly disclosed by API providers [Hankendi et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib21). We therefore estimate inference carbon using a
methodology that derives per-output-token electricity factors from GPU
and server power models calibrated to known hardware configurations
[Rincé and Banse (2025)](#bib.bib15). For a run with $`T`$ completion
tokens, model-specific electricity factor $`e`$ (kWh/token), grid factor
$`g`$ (kgCO₂e/kWh), and scenario-embedded factor $`b`$ (kgCO₂e/token),
we report

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \mathrm{CO2e}_{\mathrm{total}}=T\cdot e\cdot g+T\cdot b.
       ```                                                       |     |

Thus, the energy columns in our tables report the electricity term only,
while the carbon columns report the sum of Scope 2 electricity emissions
and the scenario’s embedded per-token component. In both studies, the
estimator is applied to completion tokens only. Because regional
capacity for the required models differed at the time of the
experiments, the two studies were run in different cloud regions
(Ireland for Study 1, Sweden for Study 2), and each study applies the
grid factor of its region. Absolute carbon values are therefore not
comparable across studies;
Section [6](#S6.SS0.SSS0.Px4 "Cross-study comparisons. ‣ 6 Discussion ‣ Toward Sustainable Agentic AI: Carbon Tradeoffs Across Iterative Self-Refinement and Tool-Augmented Coding")
provides a like-for-like comparison under a common European grid factor.
To enable reproducibility and transparency, we use an open carbon
footprint data model [Ruf and Detyniecki (2023)](#bib.bib22), which
encodes per-configuration token totals into shareable benchmark URLs for
cross-configuration comparison.

## 4 Study 1: Iterative Self-Refinement

### 4.1 Setup

#### Benchmark.

We evaluate on the MATH dataset [Hendrycks et al. (2021)](#bib.bib23),
specifically Levels 3–4 ($`N=100`$ problems) and Levels 4–5 ($`N=100`$
problems). Gold answers are extracted from the final boxed answer in
each solution; predictions are matched via float comparison, LaTeX
fraction parsing, and normalised string comparison.

#### Configurations.

We evaluate six configurations: GPT-4.1-mini at $`k\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}`$
and GPT-4.1 at $`k=1`$ as the quality baseline. All calls are made via
Azure OpenAI using location northeurope at temperature 0.

#### Agent loop.

The parameter $`k`$ is the total number of LLM calls made per problem.
Step 1: the model solves the problem (_solve_). Step 2: the model
critiques its own solution (_critique_). Step 3+: the model verifies and
revises (_revise_). Each call receives the problem together with all
previous responses, and the answer extracted from the final ($`k`$-th)
response is scored. Thus $`k=1`$ is a single-pass solve, $`k=2`$ adds
one critique, and each further step adds one verify-and-revise round.
The depth is forced: every task makes exactly $`k`$ API calls, with no
early stopping even if the model declares its answer correct.

#### Carbon estimation.

Using Microsoft’s Azure regions list [Microsoft (2026)](#bib.bib24), we
map northeurope to Ireland and apply its 2022 grid factor of
0.3476 kgCO₂e/kWh [International Energy Agency (2022)
(IEA)](#bib.bib25). For GPT-4.1-mini, we use an electricity coefficient
per output token of
[0.4800 mWh](https://borisruf.github.io/carbon-footprint-modeling-tool/show.html?scenario_id=gpt41mini-ecologits2-token&show_consumption=true)
(modelled as a dense architecture); for GPT-4.1, we use
[2.1857 mWh](https://borisruf.github.io/carbon-footprint-modeling-tool/show.html?scenario_id=gpt41-ecologits2-token&show_consumption=true)
(modelled as a mixture-of-experts architecture), following the
methodology in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Carbon Estimation ‣ Toward Sustainable Agentic AI: Carbon Tradeoffs Across Iterative Self-Refinement and Tool-Augmented Coding").
In
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Results ‣ 4 Study 1: Iterative Self-Refinement ‣ Toward Sustainable Agentic AI: Carbon Tradeoffs Across Iterative Self-Refinement and Tool-Augmented Coding"),
_Wh/task_ is the electricity term only; _mgCO₂e_ adds the scenario’s
embedded per-token component.

#### Self-refinement closes the accuracy gap.

On Level 3–4, GPT-4.1-mini at $`k=1`$ achieves 44% accuracy, 16
percentage points (pp) below GPT-4.1 (60%). By $`k=3`$, mini reaches
59%, closing the gap to within 1 pp at 25% lower API cost. The same
pattern holds on Level 4–5: mini at $`k=1`$ scores 17% vs. 33% for
GPT-4.1, again exactly 16 pp behind, and $`k=3`$ closes to 32%
($`-`$1 pp) at 26% lower cost. Paired McNemar tests on the same 100
problems confirm that the gain from $`k=1`$ to $`k=3`$ is significant on
both splits ($`p=0.001`$), while the remaining difference between mini
$`k=3`$ and GPT-4.1 is not ($`p=1.0`$ on both splits).

#### Accuracy plateaus beyond $`k=3`$.

Additional refinement steps yield no significant improvement. On
Level 3–4, accuracy at $`k=4`$ and $`k=5`$ (55% and 57%) is lower than
at $`k=3`$, but this difference lies within sampling noise (McNemar
$`p=0.50`$ for $`k=3`$ vs. $`k=4`$; 12 problems lost vs. 8 gained). On
Level 4–5, accuracy stays at 33% from $`k=4`$ onward, matching GPT-4.1.

#### Carbon rises monotonically with refinement depth.

On both splits, the most accurate non-baseline refinement setting
($`k=3`$) emits approximately $`2.8\times`$ more carbon than the $`k=1`$
mini baseline. Under the current per-output-token carbon scenario,
however, mini $`k=3`$ remains below the GPT-4.1 single-pass baseline on
both splits: 236 vs. 354 mgCO₂e on Level 3–4 and 277 vs. 419 mgCO₂e on
Level 4–5. Carbon continues to increase with additional refinement steps
and exceeds the GPT-4.1 baseline only at $`k=5`$ on both splits. The
_carbon–accuracy crossover_ (beyond which further steps add carbon
without a measurable accuracy gain) is at $`k=3`$ on both splits; the
observed peak is $`k=3`$ on Level 3–4 and $`k=4`$ on Level 4–5, but the
two are not distinguishable at $`N=100`$. The _cost–accuracy crossover_
(where refinement exceeds the upgrade cost) is $`k=4`$ on both splits.
These thresholds are distinct; attending to both is essential for
sustainable deployment decisions.

## 5 Study 2: Tool-Augmented Agents

### 5.1 Setup

#### Benchmark.

SWE-bench Lite [Jimenez et al. (2024)](#bib.bib6) is a curated subset of
300 real GitHub issues drawn from popular Python repositories (Django,
Matplotlib, SymPy, scikit-learn, and others). Each task requires the
agent to produce a patch that makes a set of previously-failing tests
pass. Success is measured as pass@1: whether the generated patch
resolves the issue in a single attempt as assessed by the official
evaluation harness. Each model was run on the first 50 instances of the
SWE-bench Lite test split (in dataset order: 6 Astropy and 44 Django
issues), with up to three attempts per instance. Two instances failed to
complete for every model and are excluded, leaving 48 for GPT-4.1-mini
and GPT-5.3-codex. For Claude Opus 4.6, three further instances timed
out and are excluded, leaving 45. On the 45 instances common to all
three models, resolution rates are 26.7% (GPT-4.1-mini), 68.9% (Claude
Opus 4.6), and 82.2% (GPT-5.3-codex); counting the three timeouts as
unresolved instead gives 64.6% for Claude Opus 4.6. The ranking is
therefore unaffected by how the timed-out instances are handled.

#### Agent framework.

We use the OpenHands agent [Wang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4) with the
default tool preset: bash execution, file editing, and a structured task
instruction. Each task runs in a dedicated Docker container pre-built
for the target repository commit, providing full process isolation and a
reproducible execution environment. The agent loop continues for up to
500 LLM iterations; a critic module checks for task completion at the
end of each attempt.

#### Configurations.

We compare three models: GPT-4.1-mini (the same cheap baseline as
Study 1), Claude Opus 4.6 (Anthropic’s flagship model), and
GPT-5.3-codex (a state-of-the-art reasoning-optimised model). All calls
are routed through a LiteLLM proxy to Azure OpenAI or the Anthropic API
(Sweden Central region) at each model’s default temperature.

#### Carbon estimation.

We use Sweden’s 2022 grid-emissions factor, 0.0256 kgCO₂e/kWh
[International Energy Agency (2022) (IEA)](#bib.bib25). We use
electricity coefficients per output token of
[0.4800 mWh](https://borisruf.github.io/carbon-footprint-modeling-tool/show.html?scenario_id=gpt41mini-ecologits2-token&show_consumption=true)
for GPT-4.1-mini,
[6.6824 mWh](https://borisruf.github.io/carbon-footprint-modeling-tool/show.html?scenario_id=claudeopus46-ecologits2-token&show_consumption=true)
for Claude Opus 4.6, and
[2.4322 mWh](https://borisruf.github.io/carbon-footprint-modeling-tool/show.html?scenario_id=gpt53codex-ecologits2-token&show_consumption=true)
for GPT-5.3-codex, again using the
Section [3](#S3 "3 Carbon Estimation ‣ Toward Sustainable Agentic AI: Carbon Tradeoffs Across Iterative Self-Refinement and Tool-Augmented Coding")
methodology. As in Study 1, the reported carbon values add the
scenarios’ embedded per-token component to the electricity-based
estimate. These values cover LLM inference only; they exclude container
startup, repository installation, tool execution, and test-suite compute
inside the Docker environment.

#### Resolution rate.

GPT-5.3-codex leads with 81.2% pass@1, followed by Claude Opus 4.6 at
68.9% and GPT-4.1-mini at 25.0%. On the 45 shared instances,
GPT-5.3-codex resolves six tasks that Claude Opus 4.6 does not and none
in reverse (McNemar $`p=0.031`$); given the sample size, this ordering
should nevertheless be read as indicative. Claude Opus 4.6 converges in
just 23.7 iterations on average—the fewest of the three—while
GPT-4.1-mini requires 54.4.

#### Cost and carbon per task.

Claude Opus 4.6 is the most expensive per task (\$2.91), roughly
6$`\times`$ GPT-5.3-codex (\$0.495) and 24$`\times`$ GPT-4.1-mini
(\$0.123). At the task level, GPT-4.1-mini is the lowest-energy and
lowest-carbon model (0.0023 kWh, 128 mgCO₂e), followed by GPT-5.3-codex
(0.0292 kWh, 1,363 mgCO₂e), then Claude Opus 4.6 (0.0417 kWh,
1,936 mgCO₂e).

#### Carbon per resolved task.

Normalised by outcome—carbon per _resolved_ task—the ordering is
unchanged but the gaps narrow: GPT-4.1-mini emits 511 mgCO₂e per
resolved task, GPT-5.3-codex emits 1,677 mgCO₂e (3.3$`\times`$ mini,
down from 10.6$`\times`$ per task), and Claude Opus 4.6 emits
2,811 mgCO₂e (5.5$`\times`$ mini, down from 15.1$`\times`$ per task).
Thus, GPT-5.3-codex leads on pass@1, while GPT-4.1-mini is most
carbon-efficient per resolved task under this token-based estimate.

## 6 Discussion

#### Study 1: when is self-refinement worth it?

Across both difficulty levels, self-refinement with GPT-4.1-mini is
_cost-competitive_ with a GPT-4.1 upgrade at $`k=3`$ ($`\approx`$25%
cheaper, $`-`$1pp accuracy), and under the current token-based carbon
scenario it also remains below the GPT-4.1 single-pass baseline at
$`k=3`$ on both MATH splits. Carbon nevertheless rises monotonically
with refinement depth, so the sustainability gain relative to GPT-4.1 is
scenario-dependent and does not hold once refinement is pushed further.

#### Study 2: capability and carbon efficiency diverge.

Study 2 reveals a different pattern. In the tool-augmented, grounded
setting of SWE-bench Lite, the highest-pass@1 model in this sample
(GPT-5.3-codex) is substantially more accurate and converges faster than
GPT-4.1-mini, but it is not dominant on every sustainability axis:
GPT-4.1-mini remains lower on estimated cost and carbon per resolved
task, while Claude Opus 4.6 converges in the fewest iterations. Under
output-token accounting, capability therefore buys outcomes that the
cheaper model cannot deliver, but not lower carbon per outcome. Grounded
tool use does penalise weak models: GPT-4.1-mini fails more often and
iterates longer, and so accumulates far more prompt context per task
(see below). Whether this narrows or reverses the per-resolved-task gap
under prompt-inclusive accounting remains open.

#### Diminishing returns in Study 1.

On neither split does refinement beyond $`k=3`$ yield a significant
accuracy gain, while each further step adds carbon at a roughly constant
rate. The lower accuracy at $`k=4`$ and $`k=5`$ on Level 3–4 is within
sampling noise. Prior work finds that intrinsic self-correction can turn
correct answers into incorrect ones [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib20);
whether this effect becomes significant with larger samples is left for
future work.

#### Cross-study comparisons.

Owing to model availability constraints, Study 1 ran in a data center in
Ireland (Azure North Europe, $`\approx 348`$ gCO₂e/kWh) and Study 2 in
Sweden ($`\approx 25.6`$ gCO₂e/kWh)[International Energy Agency (2022)
(IEA)](#bib.bib25), so their reported carbon figures are not directly
comparable. For a like-for-like comparison, we use the carbon footprint
modelling tool to create a [carbon
benchmark](<https://borisruf.github.io/carbon-footprint-modeling-tool/benchmark.html?title=Cross-Study+Carbon+Comparison+(average%2C+Europe+2022)&ids%5B%5D=gpt41mini-ecologits2-token&factors%5B%5D=46422&labels%5B%5D=S1+mini+k%3D1&source_ids%5B%5D=europe&ids%5B%5D=gpt41mini-ecologits2-token&factors%5B%5D=90922&labels%5B%5D=S1+mini+k%3D2&source_ids%5B%5D=europe&ids%5B%5D=gpt41mini-ecologits2-token&factors%5B%5D=130135&labels%5B%5D=S1+mini+k%3D3&source_ids%5B%5D=europe&ids%5B%5D=gpt41mini-ecologits2-token&factors%5B%5D=173103&labels%5B%5D=S1+mini+k%3D4&source_ids%5B%5D=europe&ids%5B%5D=gpt41mini-ecologits2-token&factors%5B%5D=215710&labels%5B%5D=S1+mini+k%3D5&source_ids%5B%5D=europe&ids%5B%5D=gpt41-ecologits2-token&factors%5B%5D=44335&labels%5B%5D=S1+GPT-4.1+k%3D1&source_ids%5B%5D=europe&ids%5B%5D=gpt41mini-ecologits2-token&factors%5B%5D=229699&labels%5B%5D=S2+GPT-4.1-mini&source_ids%5B%5D=europe&ids%5B%5D=gpt53codex-ecologits2-token&factors%5B%5D=576330&labels%5B%5D=S2+GPT-5.3-codex&source_ids%5B%5D=europe&ids%5B%5D=claudeopus46-ecologits2-token&factors%5B%5D=280546&labels%5B%5D=S2+Claude+Opus+4.6&source_ids%5B%5D=europe>)
that applies Europe’s 2022 average grid-emissions factor
($`\approx 248`$ gCO₂e/kWh) to every configuration, enabling direct
comparison across both studies. For Study 1, we use the Level 3–4 split
as representative; the Level 4–5 split shows the same ordering.

#### Token composition and the limits of output-token costing.

Figure [3](#A1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Appendix A Token Composition ‣ Toward Sustainable Agentic AI: Carbon Tradeoffs Across Iterative Self-Refinement and Tool-Augmented Coding")
(Appendix) quantifies a structural gap in output-token-based carbon
accounting across both studies. In Study 1
(Figure [3](#A1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Appendix A Token Composition ‣ Toward Sustainable Agentic AI: Carbon Tradeoffs Across Iterative Self-Refinement and Tool-Augmented Coding") a),
prompt tokens rise from 22% of all tokens at $`k=1`$ to 73% at $`k=5`$
as prior responses are prepended to the context. Because our current
carbon scenario charges only for completion tokens, it captures a
shrinking share of token processing as $`k`$ increases. In Study 2
(Figure [3](#A1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Appendix A Token Composition ‣ Toward Sustainable Agentic AI: Carbon Tradeoffs Across Iterative Self-Refinement and Tool-Augmented Coding") b),
the imbalance is stronger: prompt tokens make up over 99% of all tokens
at every iteration across all three models, growing from a few thousand
at iteration 1 to tens of thousands by convergence. Weaker models
therefore accumulate more context-processing work in addition to making
more calls, which output-only accounting does not capture. Recent direct
measurements on open models further show that input tokens have far
lower energy intensity than output tokens, even in long-context
settings, although the size of the gap depends on the serving stack [Ruf
and Detyniecki (2026)](#bib.bib17). Prompt-token dominance by count
therefore does not imply prompt-token dominance in carbon. The main
limitation of our estimator is that it can understate absolute footprint
and misstate comparisons when prompt growth differs sharply across
agents, rather than that token-count shares map directly to carbon
shares.

#### Limitations.

Study 1 evaluates a single model family on a mathematical reasoning
benchmark, and Study 2 is limited to 45–48 SWE-bench Lite instances per
model, drawn from only two repositories (Astropy and Django), so the
results may not generalise to other models, repositories, agent
frameworks, or deployment environments. With $`N=100`$ per split,
accuracy estimates in Study 1 carry 95% confidence intervals of roughly
$`\pm`$10 pp, so only large differences are resolvable. Our carbon
estimates treat model inference as the sole emission source. In
particular, they include the per-token embedded component supplied by
the selected carbon scenarios, but they do not include prompt-token
processing, Docker container execution, test runs, networking, or
additional data-centre overheads such as cooling beyond what is already
implicit in the scenario assumptions. Direct energy measurements on open
models would provide a stronger basis for comparison.

## 7 Conclusion

We have presented two empirical studies that quantify the energy and
carbon footprint of agentic AI across settings of increasing complexity.
In purely verbal self-refinement (Study 1), iterative refinement with a
cheap model can close a 16-percentage-point accuracy gap at lower API
cost but always at a nearly tripled carbon footprint relative to the
mini baseline, while remaining below the GPT-4.1 single-pass baseline
under the current token-based carbon scenario across two difficulty
levels of MATH. In tool-augmented agentic coding (Study 2),
GPT-5.3-codex achieves the highest pass@1 in this sample and resolves
3.2$`\times`$ more SWE-bench Lite tasks than GPT-4.1-mini, but at
approximately 3.3$`\times`$ higher estimated carbon per resolved task
under output-token accounting, which leaves out the prompt tokens that
make up over 99% of tokens in this setting, as well as tool execution.
This highlights that capability gains and sustainability gains need not
move in the same direction.

Together, the two studies suggest that the carbon cost of agentic AI
depends on task structure: in verbal self-refinement, cheaper models
with more steps accumulate carbon with diminishing accuracy returns,
whereas in grounded tool use, stronger models deliver outcomes that
cheaper models cannot, at higher estimated carbon per outcome. Extending
the accounting to prompt tokens and tool execution is the main next step
to determine whether this ordering holds for the full agentic footprint.

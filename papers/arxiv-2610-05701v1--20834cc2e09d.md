###### Abstract

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) enables agents to iteratively optimize
their workflows via execution feedback. However, standard RSI typically
operates as a _first-order optimizer_: it repeatedly patches
surface-level parameters in response to immediate failure symptoms,
often leading to trial-and-error thrashing without resolving underlying
mechanisms. To address this limitation, we introduce SO-RSI, a framework
that elevates workflow optimization to a _second-order diagnostic
inquiry_—investigating _why_ failures occur before committing to
structural interventions. SO-RSI passively monitors execution traces for
three structural anomalies (recurrence, opposing edits, and expectation
mismatch) to trigger targeted mechanism investigations. By executing
lightweight diagnostic probes and maintaining persistent inquiry memory
across RSI rounds, SO-RSI accumulates causal evidence to guide
systematic workflow edits rather than parameter patches. Across Lean 4
proof generation and Verus-based verifiable code generation, SO-RSI
improves final held-out pass rates over Naive RSI by 21.8 and 25.8
percentage points under matched 24-hour search budgets. Behavioral
analyses further confirm that SO-RSI substantially suppresses failure
recurrence and eliminates unproductive zero-progress optimization loops.

## 1 Introduction

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) progressively improves an executable
agent through repeated cycles of observing problems, exploring their
causes, and attempting solutions ([Robeyns et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)).
While such quick fixes can efficiently restore progress, remaining at
this level of _first-order problem solving_ can leave the underlying
causes of those problems unexplored ([Tucker and Edmondson,
2003](#bib.bib2)).

Consider a RSI trajectory for a Lean 4 proof agentic workflow that
retrieves lemmas before generating a proof
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification")).
When a proof attempt fails, first-order RSI relies on immediate quick
fixes. Observing a failure due to missing lemmas, RSI directly increases
the retrieval count to $`k=20`$—a quick fix that temporarily restores
progress. However, a subsequent attempt fails as the excessive lemma
text exceeds the context limit, prompting RSI to apply the opposite
quick fix by decreasing the count to $`k=15`$. In a later round, as the
reduced window omits critical lemmas again, RSI fine-tunes the parameter
back up to $`k=18`$, only to encounter the original context-overflow
error in the next iteration, forcing yet another reduction. This
oscillation reveals a fundamental blind spot of first-order problem
solving: misinterpreting a structural failure—low retrieval relevance—as
a mere hyperparameter tuning task of search-testing for the "optimal
lemma count," thereby failing to investigate the core mechanism of
re-ranking and filtering.

Top: The agent oscillates through parameter patching without resolving

This observation reveals a central risk for RSI. First-order problem
solving is necessary to overcome immediate obstacles, but stopping at
this level leaves the causes of recurring problems unresolved. RSI may
then repeatedly confront similar failures and face conflicting
prescriptions, without a clear basis for deciding which to follow. What
restores progress in one attempt can therefore leave the agent caught
between opposing changes in the next.
Appendix [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Additional Examples of Opposing Workflow Edits ‣ Appendix B Additional Workflow Cases ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification")
provides further examples involving failure history and tactic
fallbacks.

Recent work on RSI and agent improvement already supports reflection,
failure diagnosis, and workflow modification ([Robeyns et al.,
2025](#bib.bib3); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)). The remaining
question is how to direct this research when immediate fixes leave
conflicting outcomes unexplained. Second-order problem solving provides
a concrete target: investigate how the workflow produces the problem,
obtain evidence that distinguishes explanations, and use it to guide a
change to that process ([Tucker and Edmondson, 2003](#bib.bib2)). The
depth of inquiry is determined by this target, rather than by the length
of reflection or the number of follow-up questions.

Our work addresses three fundamental questions. First, first-order RSI
is limited by an explanation gap: LLMs easily propose plausible failure
explanations, but without explicit verification, they resort to blind
parameter patching that fails to resolve structural flaws. Second,
elevating to second-order inquiry is necessary because stopping
recurring failures requires diagnosing the underlying mechanism rather
than patching surface symptoms, replacing conflicting edits with durable
workflow improvements. Third, we operationalize second-order inquiry
through a persistent diagnostic architecture that detects anomalies,
formulates testable hypotheses, validates them via executable probes,
and executes evidence-guided workflow edits.

We introduce SO-RSI, which monitors three signals: recurrence, opposing
edits, and expectation mismatch. Triggered observations initiate
mechanism investigation (I): gathering evidence, forming explanations,
and testing predictions. Evidence accumulates across RSI rounds and
guides workflow improvements (G), which return to ordinary RSI for
evaluation and further optimization. Across Lean proving and Verus-based
verifiable code generation (VCG), SO-RSI improves final pass rate over
Naive RSI by 21.8 and 25.8 percentage points under the same 24-hour
budget.

Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  Diagnostic analysis: We document reversals in recorded RSI decisions
  and audit the evidence supporting their causal explanations.
- •
  Empirical motivation: We quantify investigation depth, intervention
  gains, and repeated unproductive editing across 134 attempts.
- •
  The SO-RSI framework: We use second-order problem solving to specify
  the target and evidence requirements of inquiry, integrating selective
  detection and persistent investigation into continuing RSI, with
  ablations supporting the value of probes and memory.

## 2 Problem Formulation

#### Two levels of iteration.

Consider an agent attempting a formal proof or program verification task
$`x`$. The _inner loop_ repairs a specific candidate artifact (e.g., a
Lean 4 proof script or code annotation) given compiler feedback, keeping
the governing agent procedure fixed. The _outer RSI loop_—the primary
focus of this work—improves the executable procedure itself, denoted as
$`S_{t}`$ at round $`t`$. This workflow $`S_{t}`$ coordinates
generation, lemma retrieval, verification, and retry policies; a
_workflow-level improvement_ updates these reusable control components
rather than the underlying language model weights. Executing $`S_{t}`$
on $`x`$ produces a trace $`\tau_{x}`$ of model interactions, tool
outputs, and compiler feedback. An improvement agent analyzes these
traces to propose a workflow edit, which is adopted into $`S_{t+1}`$ if
validated on dev benchmarks.

#### Symptoms and mechanisms.

A _symptom_ is a failure visible in $`\tau_{x}`$, such as an unknown
identifier error. A _mechanism_ explains how the workflow $`S_{t}`$
produces that failure. For example, the model may have generated the
required local definition, but the code extractor discarded it before
compilation. The error suggests adding a definition; tracing the
mechanism instead suggests repairing the extractor. This explanation is
testable: compare the raw response with the submitted proof, restore the
omitted definition, and recompile. We use _mechanism hypothesis_ for
such an explanation and _diagnostic probe_ for a check of its predicted
behavior.

#### Objective.

Our goal is to improve final task performance within a research budget:

|     |

````math
\max J_{\mathrm{test}}(S_{T})\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}C_{t}\leq B,

where $`J_{\mathrm{test}}`$ is held-out task success and $`C_{t}`$
includes investigation, editing, and evaluation costs at round $`t`$.
Only development tasks guide improvement. We report token use, verifier
calls, and wall time, and track failure recurrence to assess whether an
intervention yields durable progress.

## 3 From Observed Outcomes to Supported Explanations

RSI can often make progress through first-order problem solving: observe
a failure, apply a plausible fix, and continue. But when is such a quick
fix enough? A local intervention may resolve the current failure while
leaving unexplained why it occurred, allowing the same underlying
problem to reappear or invite a later, opposing fix. This motivates a
second-order question: does the agent investigate the workflow mechanism
that generates the failure before continuing to optimize it?

Inspired by the distinction between immediate workarounds and efforts to
address underlying causes in [Tucker and Edmondson (2003)](#bib.bib2),
we operationalize this transition for RSI as a progression in observable
inquiry. F (direct repair) applies an immediate fix without a
workflow-mechanism explanation. H (mechanism hypothesis) proposes such
an explanation but does not test it. I (mechanism investigation)
performs a diagnostic check of the proposed mechanism. G
(evidence-guided intervention) uses the resulting evidence to determine
the intervention. U (insufficient information) marks attempts whose
inquiry process cannot be determined from the available record. For a
conservative binary summary, only F is counted as first-order; H, I, and
G represent progressively stronger observable second-order inquiry,
while U is grouped on the second-order side only to avoid inflating the
first-order count.

To examine the gap between reactive patching and structural diagnosis,
we audit five independent Lean 4 proof RSI trajectories comprising 134
optimization attempts. We ask two research questions:

- •
  RQ1: How often does RSI move beyond direct patching?
- •
  RQ2: What gains does RSI achieve, and when does further patching
  become unproductive?

RQ1: How often does RSI move beyond direct patching?

Our analysis covers all 134 optimization attempts across the five
trajectories and uses each complete attempt as its unit, including the
observation, diagnosis, checks, and intervention. A change to a shared
parameter is classified by the inquiry behind it, not by the scope of
the edit or its observed gain.

## 4 SO-RSI: Second-Order Problem Solving for RSI

SO-RSI detects signs of first-order thrashing and redirects RSI toward
investigating the mechanisms behind them
(Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4 SO-RSI: Second-Order Problem Solving for RSI ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification")).
A lightweight detector
(§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Lightweight Detection of Improvement Anomalies ‣ 4 SO-RSI: Second-Order Problem Solving for RSI ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification"))
identifies improvement anomalies from execution logs. A mechanism
questioner (I)
(§[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Mechanism Investigation (I) ‣ 4 SO-RSI: Second-Order Problem Solving for RSI ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification"))
then gathers evidence, formulates hypotheses, and executes diagnostic
probes to test them. The resulting diagnostic evidence guides an
evidence-guided intervention (G)
(§[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Evidence-Guided Improvement (G) ‣ 4 SO-RSI: Second-Order Problem Solving for RSI ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification")),
which translates the investigated causes into systematic workflow
changes. This makes the improvement second-order: it addresses the
process producing the observed failures, rather than repeatedly patching
their symptoms. The revised workflow then returns to ordinary RSI for
evaluation.

describes the controller and investigation interfaces.

### 4.1 Lightweight Detection of Improvement Anomalies

After each RSI optimization attempt, the detector logs a structured
record $`X_{t}=\{v_{t},d_{t},r_{t}\}`$, where $`v_{t}`$ is the workflow
version, $`d_{t}`$ specifies the modified component and textual edit,
and $`r_{t}`$ captures the intended rationale. Rather than running
additional benchmark evaluations, the detector filters prior records by
component or failure type, then uses a short LLM call to compare
$`X_{t}`$ with the selected traces $`\tau_{x}`$ for three structural
*improvement anomalies*: recurrence, where targeted failure signatures
persist or re-emerge across consecutive rounds despite attempted edits;
reversal, where edges or parameters are directly toggled or opposingly
altered for similar symptoms; and expectation mismatch, where observed
outcomes directly contradict the stated rationale $`r_{t}`$ or predicted
behavior. When any anomaly is detected, $`X_{t}`$ and its context are
routed to initiate or update a mechanism investigation; absent any
signal, ordinary RSI continues without investigation. Detection calls
count toward the research budget.

### 4.2 Mechanism Investigation (I)

Given a flagged record $`X_{t}`$, the system investigates the root cause
before committing to permanent workflow edits. Investigation operates
via a two-stage diagnostic loop: *hypothesis formation* and *hypothesis
testing*.

#### Hypothesis formation.

The questioner first retrieves relevant historical attempts to contrast
trajectories. For instance, observing a successful attempt with fewer
lemmas directly challenges the naive assumption that retrieval count is
the primary bottleneck. The questioner then analyzes workflow
interactions to propose non-mutually-exclusive causal hypotheses (e.g.,
failure to retrieve vs. suboptimal ranking). Each hypothesis specifies
the implicated workflow operation, supporting or conflicting
observations, a testable prediction, and a proposed check. When domain
mechanics or diagnostic methods are uncertain, it consults external
documentation or code specs on demand to ground its hypotheses.

#### Hypothesis testing (Diagnostic Probing).

The questioner generates a targeted diagnostic script or tool call,
which the controller executes in an isolated workspace. It first
inspects existing artifacts before requesting new generations. For
example, saved retrieval lists distinguish missing lemmas from
low-ranked ones; comparing original and reranked contexts under fixed
context budgets and generator settings then tests the effect of ranking.
The agent compares actual outputs with the recorded prediction. A
completed check supporting an actionable workflow change permits handoff
to G; otherwise the hypothesis is revised or retained for later
investigation.

#### Persistent investigation memory.

Inspired by experience maintenance in AutoManual ([Chen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8)), we maintain an investigation record containing linked
attempts, hypotheses, executed checks, their results, and the next open
question. Related anomalies update the same investigation, allowing
later RSI rounds to resume from the evidence already collected.

### 4.3 Evidence-Guided Improvement (G)

G reuses the ordinary RSI editing agent, supplying the supported
explanation, probe results, target code, and expected behavioral change.
Its output is a workflow patch, which may adjust an existing component
or introduce a new one. For the retrieval example, evidence of ranking
failure motivates reranking and filtering before context construction.

The candidate revision returns to the outer RSI loop for validation.
Only accepted edits update $`S_{t+1}`$; all evaluation results feed back
into investigation memory. Subsequent RSI can further optimize accepted
changes under the shared budget in
Equation [1](#S2.E1 "In Objective. ‣ 2 Problem Formulation ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification").

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Tasks and data.

For Lean 4 proof generation, we adopt the initial workflow and
optimization setting from RSI-Exam ([RSI-Exam, 2026](#bib.bib9)),
retaining its original problems and supplementing them with problems
from VeriSoftBench ([Xin et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)) to obtain 92
training and 48 test problems. Each outer RSI run has a 24-hour budget.
For Verus-based verifiable code generation (VCG), we construct an
analogous agentic workflow using 113 training and 46 test problems
labeled hard in VeriContest ([Xie et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)), with the
same 24-hour budget. GPT-5.6-sol performs outer RSI optimization; the
inner task-solving model and per-task budgets follow RSI-Exam. We report
means and standard deviations over three independent runs. Task details
are provided in
Appendix [C.4](#A3.SS4 "C.4 Task Details and Main Evaluation Configuration ‣ Appendix C Research Records and Evaluation Protocol ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification").

#### Initial workflow and compared methods.

The seed workflow $`S_{0}`$ is the shared starting point for RSI: it
generates a candidate, checks it with the verifier, and, upon failure,
performs one feedback-guided repair followed by another check. For Lean,
candidates are proof bodies; for VCG, they are Verus program artifacts.
Naive RSI improves this workflow through the standard feedback-and-edit
loop. SO-RSI augments the same loop with anomaly detection, persistent
investigation, and evidence-guided intervention.

## 6 Ablation Study

Two ablations test the central design choices. Without executable probes
retains hypothesis formation and textual assessment but removes
diagnostic execution, testing the value of checking explanations against
actual behavior. Without persistent investigation memory clears
investigation records between outer rounds while preserving accepted
workflow edits and ordinary RSI logs, testing the value of accumulating
inquiry across attempts. Each uses the same overall research budget as
the full method.

#### Both diagnostic execution and memory contribute.

Removing executable probes lowers final pass rate by 7.8 points on Lean
and 26.2 points on VCG; removing persistent investigation memory lowers
it by 10.3 and 22.5 points
(Table [4](#S6.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 6 Ablation Study ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification")).
Textual hypothesis formation alone does not recover the full benefit,
and restarting investigations each round also weakens improvement. Both
ablations still outperform Naive RSI on Lean, whereas the VCG probe
ablation falls close to the baseline. These results support combining
executable checks with evidence retained across rounds.

### 6.1 Analysis of Improvement Behavior

We examine whether investigation changes how RSI improves its workflow,
comparing Naive RSI, SO-RSI, and both ablations. We apply the behavioral
definitions from
Section [3](#S3 "3 From Observed Outcomes to Supported Explanations ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification")
to the actual records: invoking an I/G module does not itself establish
second-order behavior. The same criteria capture spontaneous
investigation in Naive RSI.

#### Investigation changes improvement decisions.

Table [5](#S6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Improvements reduce recurrence and repeated editing. ‣ 6.1 Analysis of Improvement Behavior ‣ 6 Ablation Study ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification")
shows that SO-RSI tests mechanism explanations in 46.0% of Lean
investigations and 41.6% of VCG investigations, compared with 14.8% and
8.9% for Naive RSI. Evidence-driven decision revisions also become more
frequent, reaching 22.6% and 20.6%. These revisions can redirect an
intervention or stop an unsupported proposal; confirming an initial
explanation remains useful as well. The accompanying G edits have higher
acceptance rates and larger mean development gains: 9.8 versus 4.4
points on Lean, and 12.0 versus 5.2 points on VCG.

#### Improvements reduce recurrence and repeated editing.

On the audited cases, SO-RSI reduces mechanism recurrence by 31.4 points
on Lean and 35.7 points on VCG, compared with 7.3 and 10.9 points for
Naive RSI. Zero-progress loops fall from 18.7 to 7.2 and from 21.4 to
8.6 per 100 evaluated edits, respectively. Removing either probes or
memory weakens both outcomes. In particular, the memory ablation still
executes many diagnostic checks but revises fewer decisions and achieves
smaller recurrence reductions, suggesting that retaining and connecting
evidence matters alongside executing checks. Recurrence refers to the
diagnosed mechanism, rather than repeated compiler wording;

#### What explains the gains?

The results connect more frequent mechanism testing with better
intervention outcomes and less repeated work. Naive RSI already performs
some diagnostic inquiry and reduces recurrence, but SO-RSI makes this
behavior more systematic. Together with the ablations, the evidence
supports selective second-order investigation as a complement to direct
repair: it helps RSI revise its explanation when needed and carry that
evidence into reusable workflow changes.

## 7 Related Work

### 7.1 Recursive Self-Improvement and Workflow Diagnosis

SICA improves a coding agent by letting it edit its own implementation
and evaluate the resulting changes ([Robeyns et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)).
HarnessFix more directly connects failed trajectories to harness
mechanisms, consolidates diagnoses, and validates targeted
repairs ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)). These works establish
self-modification and mechanism diagnosis as existing capabilities.
SO-RSI studies when repeated improvement attempts call for further
investigation: recurring failures, opposing edits, and unmet
expectations trigger an inquiry that persists across RSI rounds and
tests explanations before guiding another workflow edit.

### 7.2 Second-Order Problem Solving

[Tucker and Edmondson (2003)](#bib.bib2) distinguish immediate
workarounds from efforts to address the processes that produce recurring
failures. We adapt this distinction to outer RSI, using recorded
hypotheses, diagnostic checks, and evidence-guided interventions to
characterize inquiry. The distinction concerns what an agent
investigates and tests, rather than the size of its edit. SO-RSI retains
ordinary repairs while selectively investigating the workflow mechanisms
behind unresolved patterns. A related distinction separates
comprehension failures from faulty reasoning, motivating changes to how
a model reads a question ([Han et al., 2026a](#bib.bib25)); our
investigation concerns the reusable workflow that produces failures
across attempts.

### 7.3 Agentic Formal Verification

LeanSearch v2 improves Lean proving through global premise retrieval,
while RAG-Verus retrieves repository context for program
verification ([Gao et al., 2026](#bib.bib5); [Zhong et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6)). ExVerus generates and validates counterexamples to
guide Verus proof repair ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)). These methods
provide concrete ways to improve the information available to a solver.
In code security, behavior-aware extraction and demonstration selection
likewise organize the context supplied to an LLM detector ([Han et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24)). SO-RSI addresses the outer process that chooses such
changes: it uses diagnostic evidence to determine which workflow
mechanism to revise. Retrieval and counterexample reasoning are possible
interventions, rather than components prescribed in advance by our
method.

#### Context-sensitive diagnosis and guidance.

[Xu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib20) condition microservice anomaly detection
on invocation chains, exposing path failures that appear normal when
events are considered separately. SO-RSI similarly considers the context
of an observed failure, but investigates patterns across workflow edits
rather than predicting anomalous service events. [Li et al.
(2026b)](#bib.bib22) compose lightweight adapters using educational
state and calibrate answer options against multimodal evidence. Their
task-specific adaptation is distinct from our outer-loop investigation,
which determines which reusable workflow mechanism needs to change. [Li
et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib11) find that more elaborate AI-generated
theories do not necessarily predict empirical results better; SO-RSI
consequently tests mechanism explanations rather than rewarding their
complexity. Broader comparisons with reasoning supervision and
task-grounded evaluation appear in
Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Additional Related Work ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification").

## 8 Discussion and Conclusion

We studied second-order problem solving for outer RSI in Lean proof
generation and Verus-based code verification. SO-RSI detects improvement
anomalies, maintains investigations across rounds, and uses executable
diagnostic evidence to guide workflow interventions. Under the same
24-hour outer budget, it improves final held-out pass rate over Naive
RSI by 21.8 percentage points on Lean and 25.8 points on VCG. Ablations
show that both executable probes and persistent investigation memory
contribute to these gains.

The behavioral analysis links these gains to more frequent mechanism
testing, more evidence-driven revisions, and stronger intervention
outcomes. Diagnosed failures recur less often, and zero-progress loops
decline in both domains. The aim is selective escalation: direct repairs
remain useful, while recurring or conflicting outcomes warrant
investigation of the workflow mechanism that produces them.

#### Limitations.

Our evaluation covers two formal-verification domains and one outer
improvement model. Behavioral findings concern the recorded trajectories
and audited mechanisms; generalization to other models and workflows
remains an open question.

## Appendix A Operational Classification of Problem-Solving Behavior

We operationalize the distinction inspired by [Tucker and Edmondson
(2003)](#bib.bib2) for RSI; it is not a verbatim reproduction of their
organizational-behavior coding scheme. The corpus contains five
independent Lean 4 proof RSI trajectories and 134 optimization attempts.
The annotation unit is a complete optimization attempt, including its
observation, diagnosis, checks, and intervention. Neither changing a
shared parameter nor adding a component determines the label. Each label
must be linked to recorded evidence; unrecorded reasoning is not
reconstructed from the eventual outcome.

#### Diagnostic criteria.

Table [6](#A1.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Diagnostic criteria. ‣ Appendix A Operational Classification of Problem-Solving Behavior ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification")
separates testing an intervention’s performance from testing an
explanation of failure. A diagnostic check may inspect code or traces,
replay an artifact, or execute a controlled probe. It need not be a new
model call or succeed in confirming the hypothesis.

|  Criterion |  Evidence required |  Insufficient evidence |
|----|----|----|
|  M: Mechanism explanation |  Identifies how a workflow process produces the failure, beyond restating the symptom. |  “Too few lemmas; increase $`k`$” merely redescribes a shortage. |
|  D: Diagnostic check |  Actually checks whether the proposed mechanism occurs or distinguishes competing causes; the target is not only aggregate task performance. |  Comparing solve rates at $`k=10`$ and $`k=20`$ alone tests settings, not the cause of failure. |
|  L: Evidence-guided intervention |  The intervention is explicitly justified by diagnostic findings; contradictory or inconclusive evidence can change or halt the proposal. |  Increasing $`k`$ regardless of the diagnostic result. |

Table 6: Observable criteria for mechanism-directed investigation. These
criteria describe a progression, not an additive score.

#### Fine-grained labels and binary aggregation.

We use a lenient criterion for crediting second-order behavior, thereby
conservatively limiting the first-order count: only F counts as
first-order; H, I, G, and U all count on the second-order side. U
retains its meaning of insufficient observability. Its inclusion is an
explicit conservative accounting convention, not evidence of
second-order reasoning. We retain all five labels and their counts so
that the binary aggregate does not obscure this distinction. In
particular, the second-order share under this convention must not be
described as the share of validated mechanism investigations.

|  Label |  Decision rule |  Binary group |
|----|----|----|
|  F: Direct repair |  Observable behavior is limited to immediate repair or settings search, without an upstream mechanism explanation. |  First-order |
|  H: Mechanism hypothesis |  M is present, but no diagnostic check D is carried out. |  Second-order |
|  I: Mechanism investigation |  M is present and D is carried out; findings may be inconclusive or refute the hypothesis. |  Second-order |
|  G: Evidence-guided intervention |  M and D are present, and the resulting intervention satisfies L. Task-level success is not required for this label. |  Second-order |
|  U: Insufficient information |  The available record does not support a judgment of the attempt’s inquiry process. |  Second-order, by convention |

Table 7: Fine-grained labels and the permissive binary convention. Lack
of diagnostic evidence yields H when a mechanism hypothesis is recorded;
inadequate records to judge the attempt yield U.

uses the illustrative lemma-retrieval scenario. A gain from changing
$`k`$ does not by itself establish that missing evidence caused the
failure. Conversely, a diagnostic investigation may justify changing
only $`k`$; intervention complexity is not a classification criterion.

|----|----|----|
|  Claims that more lemmas are needed; raises $`k`$ from 10 to 20 and observes a higher solve rate. | F |  Establishes a setting’s performance, not why the failure occurred. |

#### Thrashing attribution.

For a numeric setting, a reversal occurs when consecutive nonzero
changes satisfy $`(k_{t}-k_{t-1})(k_{t-1}-k_{t-2})<0`$. The reversal
rate is the number of reversals divided by eligible adjacent edit pairs,
whereas a category’s share is its fraction of all reversals. Counts use
the five inquiry labels and retain mixed attribution separately.
Reversals from different trajectories or branches are not concatenated.

A component/parameter key or a stable prompt-section key identifies each
edit location. Numeric deltas are evaluated within an actual
parent–child lineage, not by concatenating trials from different
branches. Shared lineage prefixes are counted once. Prompt oppositions
are supported by before/after instructions and trace references. A
reversal is attributed to the closing edit; mixed attempts retain mixed
attribution. Claims about the share of reversals attributable to F use
all recorded reversals as the denominator, including unknown and mixed
cases. Zero-progress loops are assessed separately using comparable task
and cost evaluations; a shorter accepted proof, a lower-cost workflow,
or an evidence-justified adaptation is not automatically a zero-progress
loop. Overlapping returns within the same excursion are not counted as
independent loops. The observation horizon and number of available edit
opportunities accompany comparisons between groups.

#### Overall gains and retained progress.

Each trajectory’s overall gain compares its initial workflow with the
final workflow selected by that run’s acceptance rule on the same task
set. The highest observed trial score is not substituted for the final
accepted score; any best-trial statistic is reported separately. All 134
optimization attempts contribute to their trajectories’ recorded
research costs, including investigations and rejected trials. Cost
coverage includes improvement-agent and task-model calls where recorded;
incomplete accounting is reported explicitly rather than treated as
zero. Overall gains are not obtained by summing trial deltas and are not
causally partitioned among inquiry labels, because accepted descendants
inherit and interact with earlier edits.

Retained progress is assessed on comparable descendant evaluations of an
accepted intervention against its original parent, using both task
success and model-call or token costs. Reports include the follow-up
horizon and eligible opportunities; lack of subsequent exposure or
incomplete follow-up yields an unknown outcome, not evidence of durable
success. Reversal counts remain separate from zero-progress-loop counts.
Costs assigned to overlapping loops are deduplicated by
optimization-attempt identifier and reported alongside the total
research cost. A failed direct patch does not by itself establish that a
diagnostic investigation was necessary.

The RQ2 comparisons use three mutually exclusive groups: F, H, and I/G.
U is included in trajectory-level totals but excluded from the
three-group comparison. A loop is assigned to a group only when all its
constituent edits belong to that group; loops spanning groups or
involving U remain separately recorded and are not assigned to any
displayed group. Thus, displayed loop counts need not sum to the total
number of loops. Reversals use the closing edit’s group; unknown or
mixed attribution remains separately recorded. The underlying record
retains the original fine-grained labels. Group comparisons report
eligible evaluation counts, and investigations without an evaluated
workflow change are not counted as ties. Event-count comparisons are
descriptive and must account for the unequal group sizes and available
edit opportunities. Edit-level mean pass-rate change (percentage points)
and Up/Tie/Down summaries use all eligible, fully evaluated attempts
from the five trajectories, each compared with its actual parent.
Evaluation coverage and non-evaluable attempts are reported explicitly.
Net solve-count changes are interpreted within task sets;
percentage-point changes accompany pooled comparisons if task-set sizes
differ.

### B.1 Additional Examples of Opposing Workflow Edits

#### Failure history—more context or less anchoring?

In $`v_{4}`$, a fresh-strategy final call solves row 30 after earlier

$`v_{13}`$ supplies both rejected proofs and diagnostics to avoid

entirely to increase independence. The score falls from parent

difficulty—an unproductive final attempt—has prompted both more and less

diagnostic information, missing library knowledge, or response
variation. There is no aggregate initial gain to claim for $`v_{4}`$;
its benefit is one observed final-call conversion.

#### Tactic fallbacks—expansion without resolving applicability.

In $`v_{3}`$, a checked native_decide fallback directly accepts rows 4,
5, and 33; rows 5 and 33 were unsolved by its parent. The agent expands

## Appendix C Research Records and Evaluation Protocol

### C.1 Mechanism Record

Each record contains the observed phenomenon, applicable task
conditions, raw trace references, competing mechanism hypotheses,
predicted outcomes, executed probe code and results, unresolved
alternatives, a proposed workflow intervention, and a recurrence check.
Hypotheses retain the status *unresolved*, *supported under tested
conditions*, or *contradicted*; no language-model confidence score alone
changes a hypothesis to supported. An inconclusive probe and an
unsuccessful implementation are distinct outcomes.

### C.2 Probe and Intervention Protocol

Before executing a probe, the agent records the manipulated factor,
held-fixed artifacts, observations expected under each live hypothesis,
and its budget limit. Pure transformation replay is preferred when it
can answer the question without another model call. When generation is
necessary, the model snapshot, decoding settings, prompt, and random
seeds are logged; repeated calls still require empirical variability
checks. A positive result authorizes only the conclusion supported by
the manipulation. Broader causal claims require additional controls.

Before implementing a workflow change, the agent specifies a
mechanism-level prediction and a task-level acceptance criterion. For
retrieval, the first may be retaining a needed lemma in context; the
second is proof validity and task performance. An intervention can pass
the first while failing the second because other proof errors remain.
The record retains this distinction. New components expose their inputs,
outputs, state, and tunable choices, but the framework does not
prescribe the component type. Later edits are checked against the same
task constraints and update the record when their behavior changes.

### C.3 Detailed Outcome Measurements

We report final held-out verification success, improvement over the
shared seed, and success across recorded RSI checkpoints. Mechanism
recurrence and repeated editing explain the trajectory of improvement.
Errors are not grouped solely by their final message: a mechanism can
survive a change of diagnostic. Unknown and multi-cause cases remain
explicit, and independent reviewers audit intervention-supported labels
without seeing the method identity.

For mechanism $`m`$, let $`E_{m}`$ be a fixed, independently specified
set of eligible opportunities and $`z_{m}(S,e)`$ indicate its observed
occurrence. The recurrence measurement is

|     |                                                              |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{m}(S;E_{m})=\frac{1}{|E_{m}|}\sum_{e\in E_{m}}z_{m}(S,e).
       ```                                                           |     | (2) |

Eligibility is fixed across the compared workflows; exposure coverage is
reported separately when a changed workflow no longer reaches the
relevant stage. Paired artifact replay isolates process effects, whereas
complete reruns measure interactions with subsequent generation.
Main-task pass rates report means and standard deviations across three
outer runs; behavioral rates pool their underlying counts.

### C.5 Second-Order Behavior Analysis

#### Units and annotation.

An investigation episode begins when the agent explicitly examines a
reusable workflow mechanism behind an observed pattern, and includes
subsequent inquiry into that same question until closure or the run
ends. An episode can span several RSI rounds; a reopen is linked to its
original record. Direct artifact fixes without mechanism inquiry are not
episodes. We reconstruct episodes from the full traces of every method,
including Naive RSI and the memory ablation, without relying on
method-specific record IDs. Annotation follows
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Operational Classification of Problem-Solving Behavior ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification");
unknown cases remain explicit. Independent reviewers apply the same
criteria to all variants and resolve disagreements with reference to
recorded artifacts.

#### Tested inquiry and decision revision.

The tested-inquiry rate is the fraction of all episodes, including
unfinished ones, containing a mechanism explanation, an explicit
testable prediction, and an executed diagnostic check capable of
discriminating that explanation from an alternative. Ordinary post-edit
pass-rate evaluation alone does not qualify. The decision-revision rate
uses the same denominator and counts tested episodes with a traceable
probe-result-to-decision link that changes an explanation, redirects an
intervention, or withdraws a proposal. A changed prompt or parameter
without such a link does not count. A high revision rate is not
inherently desirable, since an initially correct explanation may survive
testing.

#### G acceptance and gain.

G edits are identified using the existing evidence-guided intervention
definition, independently of whether the run invokes a module named G.
Acceptance is accepted G edits divided by evaluated G edits. Gain is the
arithmetic mean of development-pass-rate differences between each
evaluated G candidate and its own parent, measured on the same problem
set under matched task-solving limits; rejected candidates are included.
Unevaluated edits are excluded and counted separately. This measures
observed single-step gain, including interactions with inherited edits,
rather than a causal allocation of the final trajectory improvement.

#### Mechanism recurrence.

For each audited accepted G edit, freeze a mechanism-identification
check and an eligible case set before comparing its parent and child
workflows. Using the recurrence definition above, compute
$`100\,[R_{m}(S_{\mathrm{child}};E_{m})-R_{m}(S_{\mathrm{parent}};E_{m})]`$
in percentage points. Average over audited interventions within each
run. Report audit coverage, eligible case counts, and exposure changes,
retaining unresolved or unobservable outcomes rather than treating them
as resolved failures. The paired comparison measures immediate
recurrence reduction. Audit results from held-out cases never feed back
into RSI.

#### Aggregation.

For the three behavioral rates, pool the reported numerator and
denominator counts across runs and compute their ratio, separately for
Lean and VCG.
Table [5](#S6.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Improvements reduce recurrence and repeated editing. ‣ 6.1 Analysis of Improvement Behavior ‣ 6 Ablation Study ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification")
reports the supplied point estimates for the remaining metrics;
run-level standard deviations are unavailable. A zero denominator is N/A
rather than zero; if fewer than three runs are eligible, report the
contributing count, and do not report an SD for a single observation.
The underlying counts accompany rates in

### D.1 Controller and Model Calls

A Python controller coordinates calls to one fixed base LLM with
distinct contexts and instructions for detection, investigation, and
editing. No additional training is required. The controller performs
record retrieval, tool execution, persistence, and budget accounting.
The outer and inner model configurations follow
Table [11](#A3.T11 "Table 11 ‣ Models and repeated runs. ‣ C.4 Task Details and Main Evaluation Configuration ‣ Appendix C Research Records and Evaluation Protocol ‣ Second-Order Problem Solving for Recursive Self-Improvement in Formal Verification").

After an ordinary RSI attempt finishes, the controller logs its edit and
outcome, checks for anomalies, and updates the corresponding
investigation. It advances at most one investigation step before
resuming ordinary RSI. A step forms or revises hypotheses, runs a probe,
or hands a supported finding to G; it need not complete an
investigation. Eligible investigations are processed by the oldest
pending update to avoid repeatedly favoring the newest signal. A G
proposal enters the next ordinary edit–evaluate cycle. Investigation
memory starts empty, without a hand-authored library of mechanisms or
fixes.

### D.3 Investigation Memory and Retrieval

Each investigation is stored as a local JSON record with an ID, affected
component, linked attempts, current question, hypotheses, completed
probes and results, next check, and status. The statuses are open,
deferred, ready_for_G, and monitoring. Hypothesis support is stored
separately from investigation status.

For a new anomaly, the controller first retrieves investigations
associated with the component. An LLM compares their questions with the
new observation and chooses an existing ID or requests a new record.
Matching compiler messages alone is insufficient to merge
investigations. Memory writes append evidence references and preserve
previous results, including negative results. A deferred record
specifies what is already known and what remains to be checked; an
accepted intervention moves the investigation to monitoring, while new
conflicting observations can reopen it.

For hypothesis formation, historical attempts are filtered by component
and ranked by embedding similarity between the current anomaly
description and their edit rationales. The retrieved set includes both
successes and failures when available; absent contrasts are not
invented. The model receives these records, the investigation memory,
and the implicated workflow code and prompts, with file-reading tools
for additional inspection.

### D.5 Probe Generation, Selection, and Execution

Available tools read and compare files, execute Python analysis scripts,
call Lean or Verus, and replay a selected workflow stage. For each
proposed probe, the LLM specifies the hypothesis, input cases or
artifacts, script or tool arguments, held-fixed conditions, predicted
distinguishing observation, and execution cap. The controller checks
paths and required fields and executes the probe in a temporary
workspace or checkout, leaving the accepted workflow unchanged.

Probe selection prefers existing-log inspection, followed by verifier
checks or stage replay, and requests new generations only when needed.
Among affordable checks, the prompt asks for one whose possible outcomes
would change the proposed intervention or distinguish unresolved
explanations. This is a qualitative selection rule, not a learned
estimate of information gain. A probe that cannot fit within the
remaining research allowance is deferred.

The executor records stdout, stderr, exit status, timing, verifier
outcomes, and any generated artifacts. A script failure is recorded as
an execution problem, not evidence against the hypothesis. Directly
measurable observations are extracted by code; an LLM compares them with
the pre-recorded prediction and returns *supported under tested
conditions*, *contradicted*, or *inconclusive*, citing actual outputs. A
confidence score alone cannot establish support.

At least one completed diagnostic check supporting an identifiable
workflow intervention is required for handoff to G. This authorizes an
edit proposal, not a claim that the root cause has been conclusively
established. Contradictory or inconclusive results update the question
and next check. When a meaningful comparison requires more repetitions
than the remaining allowance, the result remains unresolved and the
inquiry is deferred.

### D.6 Intervention Handoff

G receives the hypothesis and its scope, executed probe code and
results, implicated workflow files, expected behavioral change, and task
constraints. The ordinary RSI editing agent generates a patch and its
expected effect. Parameter changes, modifications to existing
components, and new components are all permitted when justified by the
diagnosis. The original development evaluator and acceptance rule decide
whether to retain the patch; diagnostic support alone never merges it.
Both accepted and rejected outcomes are appended to investigation
memory. Held-out tasks do not guide these decisions.

## Appendix H Additional Related Work

#### Reasoning supervision and information quality.

IPS uses auxiliary questions to provide process supervision for
short-video moderation ([Liu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib12)), while
reasoning-enhanced domain-adaptive pretraining combines captioning,
question answering, and reasoning traces ([Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16)). Both improve task reasoning through model training;
SO-RSI instead investigates the reusable workflow around a fixed model.
Latent-space broadening targets informative annotation candidates, and
audio-enhanced vision-language modeling improves available multimodal
evidence ([Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). These are examples of
changing information supply, a distinct intervention from rewriting
instructions; our investigation seeks evidence for which kind of
workflow change is needed.

#### Task-grounded evaluation.

WorldCoder-Bench checks runtime states and transitions in generated 3D
programs through executable probes ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib14));
SO-RSI uses probes to test explanations of workflow failures before
constructing an intervention. Outside code, ReVA tests scene-centric and
temporal reasoning in remote-sensing videos rather than relying on
repetitive static-image questions ([Yao et al., 2026b](#bib.bib17)). The
survey by [Yao et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib18) organizes language-guided
segmentation from semantic matching through referring expressions to
reasoning-based guidance. These works distinguish the capabilities a
task actually requires; our first-/second-order distinction instead
describes whether improvement attempts investigate the process producing
recurring failures. RAGScope evaluates inexpensive evidence gates that
route uncertain RAG outputs for further review ([Liu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib13)). Its routing objective relates to our lightweight
detector, although our trigger concerns patterns across RSI attempts and
initiates a persistent mechanism investigation.

#### Representing prior experience.

In-context Time Series Predictor packages historical forecasting tasks
as lookback–future examples for contextual prediction ([Lu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib19)). It illustrates an alternative to supplying an
undifferentiated history: prior experience can be organized into
task-relevant units. SO-RSI uses a different unit—a mechanism hypothesis
linked to its tests and outcomes—so that a later investigation can
revisit what was supported or contradicted, rather than simply reuse an
earlier response.

#### Mechanism-specific interventions in other domains.

[Wu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib21) address degraded underwater imagery with
geometry-constrained frequency enhancement and prediction-cued
refinement. Their architecture is designed for a known source of
interference; SO-RSI studies how an improvement agent can investigate a
failure pattern before selecting a workflow intervention.

#### Levels of diagnosis and intervention.

Model-internal analysis can recover interpretable activation directions
using independent component analysis ([Liu and Han, 2026](#bib.bib26)),
while inference-time intervention can modify initial-token attention
without updating model weights ([Han et al., 2026b](#bib.bib23)). SO-RSI
operates at a different level: its evidence comes from workflow attempts
and diagnostic tests, and its interventions modify reusable control
logic around the model.
````

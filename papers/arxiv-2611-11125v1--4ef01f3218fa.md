###### Abstract

Describing hours of audio requires accurate event timing and sustained
descriptive detail throughout the recording. We present LongCap, a
framework for timestamped audio captioning (TAC), with the
human-annotated LongCapBench and three diarization-inspired metrics:
Timeline Error Rate (TER), Granularity Error Rate (GER), and Caption
Similarity (tcpCapSim). LongCap interleaves audio chunks with structured
event captions, using relative timestamps and retained context to
support length extrapolation within a 1M-token inference context window.
Event-level reannotation restores caption detail, while systematic
studies examine model scaling, dependency supervision, and
self-distillation. LongCap-Diar achieves strong benchmark results and
extrapolates to 16 hours of concatenated audio despite training on
recordings no longer than 40 minutes. Our TAC models are competitive
with Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus on short clips, outperform evaluated baselines in
caption similarity and timeline accuracy in segment-level comparisons on
LongCapBench-TAC-Long, and caption full recordings approaching two
hours. On LongCapBench-TAC, 9B and 35B-A3B offer favorable
size–performance tradeoffs, while 122B-A10B substantially improves
event-dependency recovery. These findings support long-horizon general
audio understanding and provide a foundation for always-on voice agents.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Timestamped audio captioning (TAC) results on the
LongCapBench-TAC-Long benchmark. LongCap produces stable and

severe degradation within 10 minutes. (a) TER, (b) GER, and (c)
tcpCapSim measure timeline mismatch, granularity mismatch, and caption
semantic similarity, respectively.

![Refer to caption](2611.11125v1/timeline.png)

Figure 2: TAC format. A schematic excerpt of long-form audio illustrates
overlapping speech, music, and sound events with start/end timestamps

Audio understanding is expanding from individual tasks toward
comprehensive descriptions of recordings. Speech recognition recovers
what was said ([Radford et al., 2023](#bib.bib42); [Kanda et al.,
2020](#bib.bib19)), diarization identifies who spoke when ([Anguera
et al., 2012](#bib.bib2); [Park et al., 2022](#bib.bib31)), and audio
tagging detects sound classes ([Gemmeke et al., 2017](#bib.bib13)).
Audio captioning brings these aspects together in a free-text
description of a short clip ([Kim et al., 2019](#bib.bib20); [Drossos
et al., 2020](#bib.bib8)). Recent captioners produce more comprehensive
descriptions of speech, music, and environmental sound, using either
free text or a structured format ([Qwen Team, 2025b](#bib.bib38); [Ma
et al., 2026](#bib.bib27); [Tian et al., 2026](#bib.bib50)). A growing
line of work goes further by describing individual audio events together
with their timestamps ([Kumar et al., 2026](#bib.bib22); [Rong et al.,
2026](#bib.bib43); [Shao et al., 2026](#bib.bib45)). In this work, we
refer to this form as _timestamped audio captioning_ (TAC): the output
is a timeline of audio events, each with start and end timestamps and a
caption
(Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).
Joint speech recognition and diarization is a special case in which each
event caption contains only the transcript and the speaker identity. To
make the descriptions easier to parse, we further require every caption
to be structured as key–value pairs over a closed, expert-designed key
set, so that each aspect of interest can be checked directly.

To extend this representation to hour-long recordings, we develop
LongCap together with an evaluation framework and a training recipe.
Inspired by diarization error rate (DER) and tcpWER ([Fiscus et al.,
2007](#bib.bib9); [von Neumann et al., 2023](#bib.bib52)) in speech
recognition and diarization, we introduce Timeline Error Rate (TER) to
measure timeline mismatch, Granularity Error Rate (GER) to measure event
granularity mismatch, and Caption Similarity (tcpCapSim) to measure
semantic agreement between captions. We construct LongCapBench, a
human-annotated benchmark covering speech recognition, diarization, and
TAC, with recordings approaching two hours. Our modeling approach
addresses two challenges: _holistic processing_ ([Qwen Team,
2025b](#bib.bib38); [Ghosh et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)) increases the
token distance between an event’s audio and its description, and models
trained on short recordings can degrade on longer inputs ([Narayanan
et al., 2019](#bib.bib30)). LongCap uses _chunk-wise processing_ with
chunk-relative timestamps, interleaving audio chunks and event captions
to keep emission local while retaining prior context. We first develop
and validate this design for speech recognition and diarization, where
supervision is abundant, then extend the recipe to TAC. We examine model
scaling from 0.8B to 122B parameters across pre-training and
post-training, assessing short-form accuracy and long-form robustness.
We further study _density degradation_, explicit supervision of
long-range event dependencies, and self-distillation.

LongCap-Diar achieves the lowest macro-averaged recognition and
diarization errors among the evaluated systems
(Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing"))
and extrapolates to 16 hours of concatenated audio within a 1M-token
context window, despite training on recordings no longer than 40 minutes
(App. [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Additional Diarization Results ‣ Appendix D Additional Results ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).
For TAC, LongCap is competitive with Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus on short clips
and captions full recordings approaching two hours, surpassing evaluated
competitors in caption similarity and timeline accuracy in segment-level
comparisons on LongCapBench-TAC-Long
(Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).
Our scaling studies identify favorable size–performance tradeoffs at 9B
and 35B-A3B on LongCapBench-TAC
(Fig. [8](#S4.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 4.3 Timestamped Audio Caption Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).
The evaluated 122B-A10B model is substantially stronger at recovering
event dependencies, achieving 14.8% long-form connectivity $`F_{1}`$
against silver references, compared with 4.2% for 35B-A3B
(Fig. [9](#S4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 4.3 Timestamped Audio Caption Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).
These results suggest different scaling needs for event descriptions and
dependencies, supporting long-horizon general audio understanding and
always-on voice agents.

## 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC)

The formulation of _timestamped audio captioning_ (TAC) is inspired by
the well-studied task of joint speech recognition and diarization
([Fiscus et al., 2007](#bib.bib9); [Watanabe et al., 2020](#bib.bib54);
[Cornell et al., 2023](#bib.bib5)), and extends its scope from speech
transcription to general audio captioning. Specifically, TAC localizes
and describes every audio event in the recording. Given an audio
recording $`X`$, the model predicts a list of audio events
$`E=[{\bm{e}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{e}}_{n}]`$, where each event
$`{\bm{e}}_{i}=(t^{s}_{i},t^{e}_{i},c_{i})`$ is a tuple of start
timestamp, end timestamp, and a caption describing the sounds within
that span
(Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).

### 2.1 Formatting

Structured Caption: Unlike prior work that writes the caption as
free-running natural language ([Qwen Team, 2025b](#bib.bib38); [Ma
et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)), we require every event caption to be
_structured_: each caption is explicitly a set of key–value pairs, in
which the keys come from a pre-defined closed set and each value is a
short piece of precise natural language. The structure makes the
description explicit and easy to parse: each key of interest can be
accessed and analyzed directly.

Attribute Key Set: To define the attribute key set, we collaborated with
audio professionals from the speech, music, and film industries. The
experts collaborated to make the keys cover common aspects of audio
information while avoiding being duplicated, ambiguous or
over-professional, converging to 62 keys. A key appears only when it is
applicable and is otherwise absent. All attributes, together with the
event timestamps, are assembled into a nested JSON object and serialized
as plain text, so the TAC task remains an audio-to-text conversion task:
it models the conditional distribution $`P(E\mid X)`$. The detailed
design of these keys is given in
App. [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Attribute Key Set ‣ Appendix A Scoring and Benchmark ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").

### 2.2 Scoring

Inspired by the Diarization Error Rate (DER) ([Fiscus et al.,
2007](#bib.bib9)) and tcpWER ([von Neumann et al., 2023](#bib.bib52)) in
speech recognition and diarization, we derive the _Timeline Error Rate_
(TER) and _tcpCapSim_ for TAC, and additionally introduce _Granularity
Error Rate_ (GER) for event granularity mismatch.
Figure [3](#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2.2 Scoring ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")
illustrates TER, GER, and tcpCapSim in panels (a)–(c), respectively;
more detailed definitions are given in
App. [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Scoring Rules ‣ Appendix A Scoring and Benchmark ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").
All three compare predictions
$`\hat{E}=[\hat{{\bm{e}}}_{1},\dots,\hat{{\bm{e}}}_{\hat{n}}]`$ with
reference events $`E=[{\bm{e}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{e}}_{n}]`$.

![Refer to caption](2611.11125v1/figure2.png)

Figure 3: TAC scoring metrics. (a) TER measures timeline mismatch, (b)
GER measures event granularity mismatch, and (c) tcpCapSim measures
time-constrained caption similarity.

Event Grouping: DER and tcpWER optimize over speaker permutations, with
utterances already grouped by speaker labels. General audio events lack
equivalent identity labels, so their correspondence must be inferred
from semantic content. To approximate enumeration of possible groupings,
a text LLM proposes $`M{=}3`$ candidates $`\mathcal{G}^{(m)}`$. Each
event belongs to exactly one group; groups may contain multiple events
from either side or have one side empty. Each metric is evaluated under
all three groupings and independently retains its best score.

TER ($`\downarrow`$): TER measures timeline mismatches for general audio
events; its name avoids confusion with speaker diarization. We
accumulate miss duration $`T_{\mathrm{miss}}`$ for too few active
predicted groups, false alarm duration $`T_{\mathrm{fa}}`$ for too many,
and confusion duration $`T_{\mathrm{conf}}`$ for remaining simultaneous
activity in different groups. We normalize their sum by
$`T_{\mathrm{ref}}`$, the reference activity duration summed across
groups after merging overlapping intervals within each group.

GER ($`\downarrow`$): GER measures splitting, merging, and repetition of
events through one-to-one matching: each event can match only one
counterpart. A split or merge can therefore preserve time coverage while
reducing matched overlap. Summed over groups, $`T_{\mathrm{overlap}}`$
is the overlap of reference and prediction activity unions;
$`T_{\mathrm{match}}`$ is the maximum one-to-one matched overlap; and
$`T_{\mathrm{dup}}`$ is summed prediction event duration minus its union
duration, accounting for overlapping predictions.

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathrm{TER}=\min_{\mathcal{G}^{(m)}}\;\frac{T^{(m)}_{\mathrm{miss}}+T^{(m)}_{\mathrm{fa}}+T^{(m)}_{\mathrm{conf}}}{T^{(m)}_{\mathrm{ref}}},\qquad\mathrm{GER}=\min_{\mathcal{G}^{(m)}}\;\frac{T^{(m)}_{\mathrm{overlap}}-T^{(m)}_{\mathrm{match}}+T^{(m)}_{\mathrm{dup}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(t^{e}_{i}-t^{s}_{i}\big)}.
``` |  | (1) |

tcpCapSim ($`\uparrow`$): A group receives similarity credit only if a
reference event and a predicted event overlap or are at most five
seconds apart. We pool values by scored attribute key on each side and
ask a text LLM to compare each shared key’s values for semantic
agreement, scoring them in $`[0,1]`$. A missing counterpart or failed
time constraint gives zero credit. Precision $`P^{(m)}`$ and recall
$`R^{(m)}`$ divide the summed attribute scores by predicted and
reference attribute counts, respectively, across all groups, counting
each key once per group on each side. We take their arithmetic mean,
yielding
$`\mathrm{tcpCapSim}=\max_{\mathcal{G}^{(m)}}(P^{(m)}+R^{(m)})/2`$.

### 2.3 LongCapBench

We curate *LongCapBench* to evaluate speech recognition and diarization
alongside TAC, with short- and long-form subsets for both settings.
Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2.3 LongCapBench ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")
summarizes the benchmark statistics. All labels are finalized by human
annotators. Benchmark composition is described in
App. [A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 LongCapBench Details ‣ Appendix A Scoring and Benchmark ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").

TAC annotation follows a two-stage workflow. Starting from
Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus ([Qwen Team, 2026b](#bib.bib40)) predictions,
annotators first correct event timestamps, then verify each event’s
attributes. This process presents two main challenges. First, unaided
listeners often miss less salient events; model-generated proposals
guide event discovery and make verification more reliable and efficient.
Second, the breadth of audio attributes exceeds the expertise a
generalist can acquire through brief training. We therefore route data
batches through specialist groups, each reviewing a small set of related
attributes. Review requiring specialist expertise, particularly in
music, becomes a throughput bottleneck. Comparing drafts with returned
annotations, approximately 65% and 53% of matched start/end boundaries
differ on TAC-short and TAC-long, respectively; 58% and 91% of attribute
entries are added, removed, or modified before final benchmark curation.
The benchmark therefore uses human-reviewed and corrected references,
and we retain Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus as an evaluation baseline despite its
role in generating the initial drafts.
App. [A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 LongCapBench Details ‣ Appendix A Scoring and Benchmark ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")
describes the annotation workflow.

## 3 LongCap

Architecture: LongCap instantiates a TAC model with standard components:
Qwen3.5 backbone language models (0.8B, 2B, 9B, 35B-A3B, and 122B-A10B),
the Qwen3-Omni-Captioner audio encoder ([Qwen Team, 2025b](#bib.bib38))
feeding the backbone through a linear adaptor, and a cross-entropy loss
on the text positions. All parameters are unfrozen during training.
Qwen3.5 uses hybrid linear and full attention and natively supports a
256k-token context ([Qwen Team, 2026a](#bib.bib39)). For contexts beyond
256k tokens, we use YaRN ([Peng et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)) to extend the
context window to 1M tokens.

### 3.1 Chunk-wise Processing

Figure 4: Holistic (top) versus chunk-wise (bottom) processing of a long
recording.

Since the output $`E`$ is serialized as plain text
(§[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Formatting ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")),
modeling $`P(E\mid X)`$ reduces to audio-conditioned language modeling.
The prevailing recipe ([Qwen Team, 2025b](#bib.bib38); [Ghosh et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)) is *holistic processing*: the whole recording $`X`$
is encoded into the context first, and only then is $`E`$ decoded
autoregressively, conditioned on the entire recording. LongCap instead
adopts *chunk-wise processing*, which interleaves perception and
emission
(Fig. [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.1 Chunk-wise Processing ‣ 3 LongCap ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).
The recording is split into consecutive, non-overlapping chunks
$`X=[X_{1},\dots,X_{K}]`$. Each chunk $`X_{k}`$ starts at
$`\tau^{s}_{k}`$ and ends at $`\tau^{e}_{k}`$; its label $`Y_{k}`$
contains all audio events that end within the chunk.¹¹ 1 Events spanning
multiple chunks are assigned only to the chunk in which they end. The
model then reads and writes the interleaved sequence
$`[X_{1},Y_{1},X_{2},Y_{2},\dots,X_{K},Y_{K}]`$, retaining earlier audio
chunks and generated labels in context:²² 2 Conditioning each $`Y_{k}`$
on $`X_{\leq k}`$ rather than the full $`X`$ is the causal assumption of
chunk-wise processing.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P(E\mid X)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}P\big(Y_{k}\mid X_{\leq k},\,Y_{<k}\big).
``` |  | (2) |

Comparison to Holistic Processing: Compared with holistic processing,
chunk-wise processing offers two advantages on hour-long recordings.
*Local caption emission*: holistic processing emits an event’s
description only after the entire recording and all earlier output
tokens, turning every prediction into long-context retrieval, where
accuracy is known to degrade ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib25); [Hsieh
et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)). Chunk-wise processing emits each description
after the chunk containing the event’s end, following chunk-local speech
decoding ([Jia et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)). This keeps emission near the
event endpoint while retaining earlier chunks for long-range evidence.
*Reduced length bias*: generation can degrade beyond training lengths in
language modeling ([Press et al., 2022](#bib.bib36)) and speech
recognition ([Narayanan et al., 2019](#bib.bib30)). Chunk-wise
processing repeats the same local emission step on longer recordings,
supporting length extrapolation ([Radford et al., 2023](#bib.bib42);
[Jia et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)). This ability relies on the base text
model’s long-context capacity, which we preserve during training
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Long-Form Speech Recognition and Diarization ‣ 3 LongCap ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).

### 3.2 Long-Form Speech Recognition and Diarization

Speech recognition and diarization is a reduced version of TAC: each
event caption keeps only two keys, the speaker identity and the
transcript
(§[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Formatting ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).
Its supervision, however, is far more abundant than that of the general
TAC task, which makes it an ideal test bed for ablating chunk-wise
processing in the long-form setup. Our ablations on this task, presented
in
§[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Diarization Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing"),
justify three design choices:

Relative timestamps: Absolute timestamps become unreliable beyond the
training duration range in our experiments
(§[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Diarization Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).
Following Whisper’s window-relative timing ([Radford et al.,
2023](#bib.bib42)), we express event timestamps $`t^{s}_{i},t^{e}_{i}`$
relative to the start of their chunk.³³ 3 Recording-level event
timestamps are recovered by adding the chunk start to the relative
predictions. An event crossing the chunk’s left boundary carries a
negative start.

Chunk size: We compare a fixed chunk size of 32 s with random sampling
from a continuous uniform distribution $`\mathcal{U}(8,32)`$ s or the
discrete set $`\{8,16,24,32\}`$ s. The chunking strategy has only a
marginal impact on performance. We adopt the discrete set
$`\{8,16,24,32\}`$ s, randomly sampling each chunk’s duration during
both training and inference.

Long-form text: To retain the base model’s long-context and text
capabilities, we include long-form text in the training mixture,
following text-mixing strategies in long-context and speech-LLM training
([Gao et al., 2025](#bib.bib11); [Peng et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)).

Our diarization model is trained at most 20 minutes, but can extrapolate
to 16 hours (concatenated LongCapBench-Diar-Long examples) with stable
WER, although the accumulated speaker count is far beyond the training
distribution.

### 3.3 Migration to Timestamped Audio Captioning

We initialize each LongCap model directly from a pretrained Qwen3.5 text
backbone and the Qwen3-Omni-Captioner audio encoder. Before the optional
extensions below, all training recordings are at most 20 minutes long.

#### 3.3.1 Pre-Training

TAC requires two complementary capabilities: timestamp prediction and
audio attribute prediction. Because comprehensive TAC supervision is
scarce, we develop these capabilities through multi-task pre-training.
Speech recognition and diarization on recordings of up to 20 minutes
provide timestamp supervision, while natural-language captions paired
with audio chunks of up to 30 seconds teach audio attribute prediction.
These annotated chunks are spliced into training recordings of up to 10
minutes in total. Following
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Long-Form Speech Recognition and Diarization ‣ 3 LongCap ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing"),
we also include long-form text sequences of up to 256k tokens to
preserve long-context capabilities. We mix diarization, captioning, and
text data in a 1:1:1 ratio by token count. The resulting model is named
LongCap-Base. Data curation is described in
App. [B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Data Curation ‣ Appendix B Implementation ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").

#### 3.3.2 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

We refine the model and its training data in two stages, followed by two
optional extensions. We conduct the refinement with the 35B-A3B model
and use the resulting data to train models at all backbone sizes. During
SFT, captioning, diarization, and text data are mixed in a $`3:1:1`$
ratio by token count. All SFT data volumes and mean durations below
refer to audio retained after filtering.

Seed supervision: We curate 15k hours of audio, with recordings
averaging 49 seconds and lasting at most 10 minutes. We split each
recording into clips of at most one minute and query Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus
to generate structured captions in the format of
§[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Formatting ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").
The clips are annotated independently, and their annotations are
reassembled into recording-level labels. Fine-tuning LongCap-Base on
these labels yields LongCap-SFT-1, establishing basic TAC capabilities.

Alleviating density degradation: Both Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus and
LongCap-SFT-1 exhibit *density degradation*: later events within a chunk
contain fewer filled attributes
(Fig. [8](#S4.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 4.3 Timestamped Audio Caption Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")c).
To restore event-level detail, we curate another 10k hours of audio,
with recordings averaging 49 seconds and lasting at most 10 minutes, and
annotate them with LongCap-SFT-1. Using the predicted timestamps, we
extract each event’s audio span and query Qwen3.5-Omni-Plus to annotate
it independently. Fine-tuning LongCap-SFT-1 on these refined annotations
yields LongCap-SFT-2, which maintains more consistent attribute density
across event positions.

Optional 1: Length Expansion: The initial training recipe uses
recordings no longer than 20 minutes. To test whether longer training
examples improve extrapolation to two-hour recordings, we curate an
additional 20k hours of audio covering diverse acoustic content.
Approximately 10k hours come from recordings longer than 20 minutes,
with a maximum duration of 40 minutes. We use LongCap-SFT-2 to annotate
this corpus; the longest resulting audio–caption sequences approach the
256k-token training context limit.

Optional 2: Event Dependency Modeling: Although the model retains the
full audio and caption history, its labels largely originate from
short-window annotation. To make cross-event dependencies explicit, we
use GLM-5.3-Flash ([Z.ai, 2026](#bib.bib58)) to analyze the event
sequence and, where appropriate, assign each event a semantically
related earlier event as its *ancestor*. We include both the current
event’s ID and its labeled ancestor in the JSON string used as the
training target. Following these links recursively forms event chains
that describe how events evolve over time, providing explicit
supervision for long-range dependencies.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Training and Inference: We train all five backbone sizes following
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Migration to Timestamped Audio Captioning ‣ 3 LongCap ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing"),
with 100k continued pre-training steps and two SFT epochs at a constant
learning rate of $`1\times 10^{-5}`$. Context parallelism supports
256k-token training sequences. Inference uses a customized vLLM
implementation ([Kwon et al., 2023](#bib.bib24)) with greedy decoding.
Hardware, hyperparameters, and decoding safeguards are detailed in
App. [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Training and Inference Details ‣ Appendix B Implementation ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").

Benchmarks: We evaluate speech recognition and diarization on AliMeeting
([Yu et al., 2022](#bib.bib57)), AISHELL-4 ([Fu et al.,
2021](#bib.bib10)), AMI-IHM and AMI-SDM, four CHiME-6 sessions

### 4.2 Diarization Results

Overall performance. LongCap-Diar-35B-A3B achieves the lowest
macro-averaged DER, WER, cpWER, and tcpWER in
Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")a.
We further study temporal robustness on LongCapBench-Diar-Long, whose
recordings span 50–78 minutes
(Fig. [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")b–c).
In five-minute windows, WER remains below 10% throughout, while DER
stays below 9% through 60 minutes before rising in the sparsely sampled
tail. These results show that our chunk-wise model can outperform the
evaluated holistic baselines, MOSS Transcribe Diarize and VibeVoice-ASR
([MOSI.AI, 2026](#bib.bib28); [Peng et al., 2026b](#bib.bib35)), as well
as the commercial Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash ([Alibaba Cloud, 2026](#bib.bib1))
and Doubao ASR 2.0 ([Volcengine, 2026](#bib.bib51)) APIs. Holistic
versus chunk-wise processing is further ablated in
Table [6](#A4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ D.1 Additional Diarization Results ‣ Appendix D Additional Results ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing");
the absolute/relative timestamp comparison is shown separately in
Fig. [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.2 Diarization Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")a.
Our model is also better at dealing with very long and far-field
spontaneous speech (CHiME-6). With a 1M-token context window,
LongCap-Diar can further extrapolate to 16 hours of concatenated audio.
Details are in
§[D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Additional Diarization Results ‣ Appendix D Additional Results ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").

## 5 Related Work

Long-Form Speech Recognition and Diarization: Earlier work studies
long-form generalization ([Narayanan et al., 2019](#bib.bib30)) and
full-document contexts ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)). Whisper decodes
sequential 30-second windows with window-relative timestamps ([Radford
et al., 2023](#bib.bib42)). CHiME benchmarks unsegmented recognition and
diarization of distant speech ([Watanabe et al., 2020](#bib.bib54);
[Cornell et al., 2024](#bib.bib6)). Recent speech LLMs jointly emit
speaker identities, timestamps, and transcripts ([MOSI.AI,
2026](#bib.bib28); [Peng et al., 2026b](#bib.bib35); [Mu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib29)); MOSS and VibeVoice-ASR support single-pass inputs of
up to 90 and 60 minutes, respectively. Chunk-wise decoding extends from
ASR ([Jia et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)) to joint transcription and
diarization with cross-chunk speaker caches ([Shi et al.,
2026](#bib.bib47); [Peng et al., 2026a](#bib.bib33)). LongCap extends
this approach to structured TAC and validates length extrapolation in
recognition and diarization
(§[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Diarization Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).

Audio Captioning: Audio captioning classically pairs a clip of ten to
thirty seconds with one free-text sentence ([Kim et al.,
2019](#bib.bib20); [Drossos et al., 2020](#bib.bib8)). Bagpiper uses
rich captions for open-ended audio tasks ([Tian et al.,
2026](#bib.bib50)). Omni-Captioner studies the tradeoff between
descriptive detail and hallucination ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib27));
the Qwen3-Omni-Captioner model card advises inputs of at most 30 seconds
([Qwen Team, 2025a](#bib.bib37)). Timestamped variants attach start and
end times to each described event, echoing dense video captioning
([Krishna et al., 2017](#bib.bib21)). The TAC model ([Kumar et al.,
2026](#bib.bib22)), distinct from our task acronym, emits typed,
timestamped descriptions; its TAC-V pipeline processes independent
20-second audio chunks. AudioMap names the task time-aware dense audio
captioning ([Rong et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)), AudioTime supplies
temporally aligned training data ([Xie et al., 2025](#bib.bib56)), LAT
reaches 30-minute inputs and contributes the human-verified LAT-Bench
([Shao et al., 2026](#bib.bib45)), and audio diarization localizes
open-set events without describing them ([Werning & Haeb-Umbach,
2026](#bib.bib55)). Hour-scale inputs have been reached for question
answering and temporal grounding ([Kutsakov et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)).
LongCap combines retained context across chunks
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Chunk-wise Processing ‣ 3 LongCap ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")),
62-key structured captions
(§[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Formatting ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")),
and long-form training
(§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Migration to Timestamped Audio Captioning ‣ 3 LongCap ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing"))
with human-labeled LongCapBench evaluation approaching two hours
(§[2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 LongCapBench ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).

## 6 Conclusion

LongCap enables hour-scale TAC through chunk-wise generation, relative
timestamps, and detailed event supervision. On LongCapBench-TAC, 9B and
35B-A3B offer favorable size–performance tradeoffs, while 122B-A10B
better recovers event dependencies. This distinction highlights
different scaling needs for describing events and connecting them over
time, providing a foundation for long-horizon audio understanding and
always-on voice agents.

### AI Use Statement

We used generative AI extensively for data construction, annotation,
refinement, and quality assessment. We also used LLMs for event grouping
and semantic-similarity evaluation, as described in
§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Scoring ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").
AI assistants supported portions of code development and experiment
management, and helped polish the manuscript and proofread its text. The
core research ideas were conceived entirely by the human researchers.

### Ethics Statement

Our data preparation uses public and private audio resources and
model-assisted annotation; human annotators finalize the benchmark
labels. Our collaboration with audio professionals and human annotators
followed standard ethical practices and respected their rights, welfare,
and privacy. Speech and speaker attributes can expose sensitive
information, and generated descriptions may contain errors or reflect
biases in the source data and annotation models. These limitations
matter for applications involving speaker profiling or consequential
decisions. The four LongCapBench-TAC-Long recordings have research-use
permission; this does not by itself establish permission to redistribute
their audio.
````

### A.2 Scoring Rules

Diarization Error Rate (DER): We use pyannote.metrics ([Bredin,
2017](#bib.bib3)) with optimal one-to-one speaker assignment. DER
([Fiscus et al., 2007](#bib.bib9)) divides missed-speech, false-alarm,
and speaker-confusion duration by reference speaker duration.
Overlapping speech is included, counting simultaneous speakers
separately. The 0.25 s boundary collar excludes 0.125 s on either side
of each reference boundary.

cpWER and tcpWER: MeetEval ([von Neumann et al., 2023](#bib.bib52))
concatenates each speaker’s utterances chronologically and finds the
speaker permutation minimizing substitutions, deletions, and insertions.
cpWER divides this count by reference transcript length; tcpWER
restricts word alignment using estimated word timings, extending
hypothesis intervals by 5 s on each side. This allowance permits
temporal matching without excluding boundary regions. Both transcripts
are normalized, scoring English by word and Chinese by character.
Speaker-agnostic English WER uses Whisper’s EnglishTextNormalizer
([Radford et al., 2023](#bib.bib42)).⁵⁵ 5 LongCapBench cpWER/tcpWER use
[WeTextProcessing](https://github.com/wenet-e2e/WeTextProcessing)’s
English/Chinese normalizers; CHiME-6 uses CHiME8 normalization.

TAC event grouping: DER and tcpWER optimize over speaker permutations,
using speaker labels to define utterance groups. General audio events
lack equivalent identity labels, so we approximate enumeration of
possible groupings with three proposals from DeepSeek-V4-Flash
([DeepSeek, 2026](#bib.bib7)) with thinking enabled. Using the event
intervals $`[t_{i}^{s},t_{i}^{e})`$ defined in
§[2](#S2 "2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing"),
each proposal groups reference and predicted events describing the same
occurrence. Each event belongs to one group, with multiple events or an
empty side allowed. Repeated occurrences from the same speaker or sound
source remain distinct. For each proposal, grouping advances in 60 s
steps with a 15 s look-back, retaining full intervals and merging groups
that share events across windows into a recording-level grouping. Each
metric independently retains its best result across the three
candidates: the lowest TER, the lowest GER, or the highest tcpCapSim
(§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Scoring ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).

TER: TER applies DER accounting to semantic groups. Merge overlapping
intervals within each group separately on each side. Too few active
predicted groups count as misses, too many as false alarms, and
remaining activity in different groups as confusion. Divide these error
durations by the merged reference duration $`T_{\mathrm{ref}}`$ in
Eq. ([1](#S2.E1 "In 2.2 Scoring ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")).
Overlap between different groups counts separately. TER and GER use full
intervals without a boundary collar.

GER: GER compares how shared activity is divided into individual events.
One-to-one matching permits each event only one counterpart, so
splitting or merging can reduce matched overlap even when time coverage
is unchanged. In
Eq. ([1](#S2.E1 "In 2.2 Scoring ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")),
$`T_{\mathrm{overlap}}`$ sums the overlap of merged reference and
predicted activity within groups; $`T_{\mathrm{match}}`$ is the maximum
total overlap under one-to-one event matching within groups; and
$`T_{\mathrm{dup}}`$ is summed prediction duration minus merged
prediction duration within groups. Divide
$`T_{\mathrm{overlap}}-T_{\mathrm{match}}+T_{\mathrm{dup}}`$ by the
total reference event duration $`\sum_{i}(t_{i}^{e}-t_{i}^{s})`$,
counting overlapping events separately. For example, splitting a
reference event $`[0,10)`$ into predictions $`[0,5)`$ and $`[5,10)`$ in
one group preserves all 10 seconds of coverage, but only one 5-second
piece can match the reference event. Thus TER $`=0`$ while GER
$`=(10-5)/10=0.5`$. Merging these two reference events into one
prediction gives the same penalty. Predicting $`[0,10)`$ twice for a
single reference event instead adds 10 seconds through
$`T_{\mathrm{dup}}`$, giving GER $`=1`$ despite unchanged coverage.

tcpCapSim: Pool nonempty caption values by key within each group,
counting each key once per side. Scoring includes type and excludes
free-text caption and structural speaker_id. A group receives credit
only if some reference and prediction intervals overlap or are at most
5 s apart. This matching allowance does not exclude boundary regions.
DeepSeek-V4-Flash scores each shared key’s semantic agreement in
$`\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}`$; missing counterparts and groups failing the
time constraint receive zero. Summed credit divided by predicted and
reference key counts gives precision and recall. tcpCapSim is their
arithmetic mean, as described in
§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Scoring ‣ 2 Task Formulation: Timestamped Audio Captioning (TAC) ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").

Aggregation: All metrics use micro aggregation within each benchmark:
sum error counts or durations, similarity credit, and their denominators
across recordings before computing rates. Reported cross-benchmark macro
averages weight benchmark scores equally.

## Appendix B Implementation

### B.1 Data Curation

The initial pre-training and SFT recipe uses audio examples of at most
20 minutes, including examples assembled by splicing. Optional length
expansion extends the maximum duration to 40 minutes.

#### B.1.1 Pre-training Data

Diarization data: We curate speech-only recordings from web resources
and use a cascaded pipeline for automatic annotation and quality
filtering. To reduce speaker-clustering degradation on long recordings,
we first split the audio into recordings of at most 20 minutes and
identify their languages with Whisper ([Radford et al.,
2023](#bib.bib42)). Paraformer ([Gao et al., 2022](#bib.bib12)) and
Whisper large-v3-turbo perform the initial transcription of Chinese and
English audio, respectively, producing text and timestamps. WeSpeaker
([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib53)) extracts speaker embeddings, which are
clustered to assign utterance-level speaker labels. We then refine
utterance boundaries using temporal alignment and local energy
information to reduce the risk of clipping speech. Finally, Qwen3-ASR
([Qwen Team, 2026c](#bib.bib41)) performs a second transcription pass,
whose output provides the final transcript. We filter low-quality
recordings based on agreement between the two transcription passes, the
proportion of speech, and the token rate.

Clip-level natural-language captions: We curate audio from private
resources and publicly available datasets, covering speech, non-speech
sounds, and their mixtures. The audio is semantically segmented into
chunks of up to 30 seconds, and Qwen3-Omni-Captioner ([Qwen Team,
2025a](#bib.bib37)) generates a natural-language caption for each chunk.
We apply lightweight filtering to remove obvious labeling failures, such
as repetitive text loops. After chunk-level annotation, we splice the
chunks and their captions into training recordings with an overall
duration of up to 10 minutes. Speech constitutes the majority of this
corpus; non-speech content is harder to obtain and accounts for a
smaller share.

Long-form text: We use the Dolma3 Longmino Mix corpus for OLMo-3 ([Team
OLMo et al., 2025](#bib.bib48)).⁶⁶ 6 Corpus identifier:
[allenai/dolma3_longmino_mix-100B-1125](https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/dolma3_longmino_mix-100B-1125).
Its original examples contain at most 64k tokens. We concatenate
examples from the same domain to construct training contexts of up to
256k tokens. We sample across short and long sequences to keep the
sequence-length distribution approximately uniform.

#### B.1.2 Supervised Fine-Tuning Data

The SFT audio follows the same broad source and content distribution as
the captioning pre-training data, with additional attention to balancing
coverage across speech, music, sound effects, and their mixtures. The
two initial stages in
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Migration to Timestamped Audio Captioning ‣ 3 LongCap ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing")
use 15k and 10k hours of audio retained after filtering for seed
supervision and event-wise reannotation, respectively. These recordings
average 49 seconds and last at most 10 minutes. Optional length
expansion adds 20k hours of audio with broad acoustic coverage,
including approximately 10k hours from recordings longer than 20
minutes, with a maximum duration of 40 minutes. All of this SFT audio is
held out from the pre-training corpus. The seed and event-wise
annotation prompts are provided in
App. [C](#A3 "Appendix C Prompts ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").

### B.2 Training and Inference Details

Hardware and data mixture: Our diarization models are pre-trained on 32
H100 GPUs. TAC models use 128 H100 GPUs. Each GPU processes 32k tokens
per step, corresponding to 1M tokens per step for diarization and 4M for
TAC. Continued pre-training lasts 100k steps. The
captioning:diarization:text mixture is $`1:1:1`$ during pre-training and
$`3:1:1`$ during SFT, measured by token count.

Training setup:

Inference failures: LongCap uses greedy decoding with a maximum of 8,192
output tokens per chunk. If a chunk reaches this limit without
completing its caption, subsequent chunks are still decoded normally.
For the 35B-A3B model, approximately 0.3–0.5% of chunks trigger a retry
because of malformed captions, such as unparseable JSON or loops that
repeatedly emit events. These failures become more frequent with smaller
models and longer contexts, making the 0.8B and 2B variants impractical
for reliable long-form use.

Retries and postprocessing: We use two measures to reduce these
failures. First, when a malformed caption is detected, we regenerate the
same chunk up to three times, using repetition penalties of 1.05, 1.10,
and 1.15 for successive retries. Every retry still uses greedy decoding.
Second, after decoding, we remove obviously repetitive duplicate events,
such as a run of more than 100 events repeating the same transcript.

Caption-based audio understanding: On MMAU and MMAR, we evaluate how
well LongCap’s captions support question answering with a text LLM.
LongCap first captions each recording; gemini-3-flash-preview ([Google,
2026b](#bib.bib16)) then answers the question using the caption and the
supplied answer choices, without access to the audio. The reported
accuracy measures this two-stage pipeline, rather than LongCap answering
the questions directly. This follows the evaluation practice described
in
§[4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ LongCap: Hour-Scale Timestamped AudioCaptioning via Chunk-wise Processing").

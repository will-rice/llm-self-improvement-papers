# CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimization for Joint RL and Distillation in Agents

Wenhao Yu¹, Zhengcheng Wang², Jiahong Liu¹, Zexuan Qiu¹, Minda Hu¹,
Shaohang Wei³, Jiafei Lyu², Junyan Lyu², Haobo Fu², Irwin King¹ ¹The
Chinese University of Hong Kong  ²Tencent  ³Peking University Email:
yuwenhao117@gmail.com, jensencwang@tencent.com

###### Abstract

Joint reinforcement learning and self-distillation combines task rewards
with dense teacher guidance for agent training, but their gradients can
partly cancel. A mixing coefficient controls relative scale without
selectively removing opposing components. We present CORD, a general
framework for conflict-aware optimization of two objectives. CORD
separates the choice of protected objective from the condition that
triggers projection, and combines the gradients once per optimizer
mini-batch. A nearest-point formulation yields a closed-form update and
characterizes its first-order effect on the protected objective before
optimizer transformations. Gradient diagnostics connect conflict in
agent training to lost local optimization progress and motivate
selective correction. As a gradient-combination mechanism, CORD can be
added to different joint training recipes while retaining their
objectives. Across three such recipes, it improves ALFWorld success by
1.5–15.9 percentage points. On WebShop, it improves two of the three
pairs, with SDAR rising from 60.7% to 64.6% success. Our analyses
explain why protection direction and projection gate must be chosen
together, and why the distillation objective and its mixing weight
remain consequential after projection.

## 1  Introduction

Language-model agents learn to complete tasks through sequences of
actions, but outcome rewards provide limited credit for individual
decisions ([Feng et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)). Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO) compares returns across sampled attempts ([Shao
et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)), yet supplies little learning signal when
rewards tie. On-policy distillation (OPD) provides dense token-level
feedback on student trajectories ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)),
but its usefulness depends on the teacher’s guidance. Joint training
couples this guidance with reward-based optimization, allowing agents to
learn from both task outcomes and informed action preferences ([Lu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)).

Existing approaches refine how teacher guidance enters learning.
Skill-conditioned self-distillation supplies an informed contextual view
of the policy ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)), while SDAR weights
token supervision using detached teacher–student confidence gaps ([Lu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)). RLSD incorporates detached log-probability
differences into token advantages, preserving their signs without a
separate distillation loss ([Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib20)).
Confidence gates reweight token signals, whereas a gradient
inner-product gate tests their accumulated effect. The two operations
can be composed for separate RL and OPD losses.

Joint optimization still requires a rule for combining these gradients.
Which contribution to preserve on conflict depends on the losses’ roles
and the training goal. A mixing coefficient changes scale but cannot
selectively remove opposing components.

We introduce CORD, a general framework for conflict-aware optimization
of two objectives. CORD accumulates both gradients under the specified
loss weights for each optimizer mini-batch. It retains the selected
protected gradient and minimally modifies the other before combining
them. Conflict-only projection removes opposing components, while an
always-projection variant also removes aligned components. Either
objective can be protected. Our analysis relates the resulting
first-order contribution on fixed sampled losses to finite step size and
optimizer transformations. The agent experiments evaluate protection,
triggering, and loss construction while retaining each host’s
objectives.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Additional Related Work ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
discusses related work.

Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  Conflict-aware joint optimization. We connect measured gradient
  conflict to lost first-order progress and formulate CORD to preserve a
  selected objective’s contribution while correcting the conflicting
  component of the other.
- •
  Incremental gains across training methods. CORD improves all three
  host recipes on ALFWorld by 1.5–15.9 percentage points, and two of
  three on WebShop. These paired comparisons establish the value of
  gradient combination while retaining each host’s objectives.
- •
  Guidance for configuring joint training. We show why protection
  direction and triggering must be selected together, and how the
  distillation loss, mixing weight, and aggregation determine the
  gradient that projection receives. Agent cases connect these choices
  to attribute completion, repeated actions, and token probability
  changes.

## 2  Preliminaries

### 2.1  Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

An agent samples response tokens $`y_{t}`$ from a rollout policy
$`\pi_{\mathrm{old}}`$, conditioned on interaction history $`x_{t}`$.
GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)) compares returns within a group
of attempts at the same task. Each response record receives a fixed
advantage
$`\widehat{A}_{t}=(R_{t}-\mu_{g(t)})/(\sigma_{g(t)}+\epsilon)`$, where
$`\mu_{g(t)}`$ and $`\sigma_{g(t)}`$ are its group’s return mean and
standard deviation. The host specifies which response records enter
these statistics. With probability ratio
$`r_{t}=\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x_{t})/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_{t}\mid x_{t})`$,
the clipped policy loss to minimize is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clip}}=-\mathcal{A}_{B}\!\left[\min\!\left(r_{t}\widehat{A}_{t},\operatorname{clip}(r_{t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{t}\right)\right].
``` |  | (1) |

Here $`\mathcal{A}_{B}`$ aggregates valid response tokens in mini-batch
$`B`$ with the host’s specified weights, with padding and observation
tokens masked. Our GRPO actor loss $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}`$
includes the host’s clipping safeguards, reference-policy KL
regularization, and entropy term when enabled
(Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")).
All gradients below are loss gradients, so a descent update subtracts
the combined gradient.

### 2.2  On-Policy Distillation

On-policy distillation scores the student’s sampled tokens with a
teacher rather than requiring teacher-generated trajectories ([Agarwal
et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib16)). In our skill-conditioned instantiation, the teacher
uses the current policy with additional skill context $`S(x_{t})`$ and
gradients disabled ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)). Define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{t}=\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x_{t}),\qquad q_{t}=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x_{t}\oplus S(x_{t}))\right],\qquad\ell_{t}=s_{t}-q_{t},
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\operatorname{sg}`$ denotes stop-gradient. A token objective
$`\psi_{t}`$ and confidence weight $`w_{t}`$ define a separate loss

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}=\mathcal{A}_{B}[w_{t}\psi_{t}],\qquad\psi_{t}^{k_{3}}=\rho_{\mathrm{on},t}(e^{-\ell_{t}}-1+\ell_{t}),\qquad\psi_{t}^{\rho_{\mathrm{d}}}=\rho_{\mathrm{on},t}\ell_{t}.
``` |  | (3) |

Equation [3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 On-Policy Distillation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
suppresses the numerical exponential cap. Gradients pass through the
capped student-to-rollout ratio $`\rho_{\mathrm{on},t}`$ and $`s_{t}`$,
while teacher scores and enabled confidence weights are detached. The
host $`k_{1}`$ term and numerical caps follow the inherited recipes in
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Baselines ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents").
Token weights enter before aggregation, while the mixing coefficient
below scales the resulting loss.

### 2.3  Multi-Objective Optimization and Gradient Conflict

Bold symbols denote parameter-space vectors. Losses, inner products, and
coefficients are scalars. We use $`\bm{G}_{j}`$ for a complete
aggregated objective gradient and $`\bm{g}_{j}^{(m)}`$ for a micro-batch
gradient. The role vectors $`\bm{u},\bm{v},\bm{a},\bm{b}`$ refer to
these aggregates after the stated weighting and protection choices.

For two mini-batch losses $`F_{1},F_{2}`$ and parameters
$`\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$, weighted-sum optimization minimizes
$`F_{1}+\lambda F_{2}`$. At fixed $`\lambda>0`$, write

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bm{u}=\bm{G}_{1}=\nabla F_{1}(\theta),\qquad\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{G}_{2}=\lambda\nabla F_{2}(\theta),\qquad\bm{g}_{\mathrm{sum}}=\bm{u}+\bm{v}.
``` |  | (4) |

Our agent instantiation uses $`F_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}`$ and
$`F_{2}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}`$. The same combination applies to
any two differentiable losses with specified aggregation. A small step
along $`-\bm{g}_{\mathrm{sum}}`$ changes $`F_{1}`$ by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F_{1}(\theta-\eta\bm{g}_{\mathrm{sum}})-F_{1}(\theta)=-\eta\big(\lVert\bm{u}\rVert^{2}+\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle\big)+o(\eta).
``` |  | (5) |

For $`\lambda F_{2}`$, the corresponding expression replaces
$`\lVert\bm{u}\rVert^{2}`$ by $`\lVert\bm{v}\rVert^{2}`$. A negative
cross term therefore reduces each objective’s first-order progress
relative to its own gradient. We call this *gradient conflict*. Scaling
$`\lambda>0`$ changes its magnitude but not its sign. CORD addresses
this interaction by choosing which weighted gradient to preserve and
minimally modifying the other.

## 3  Method

CORD combines two objective gradients by making the protected direction
and projection gate explicit. It keeps one weighted gradient intact and
makes the smallest change to the other that satisfies the chosen
geometric constraint. The same construction accommodates either
protection direction. The loss functions and their relative weight
specify the signals supplied to it.

illustrates the combination for GRPO and OPD, with an analytic loss

### 3.1  Choosing a protected direction

Using the weighted gradients in
Equation [4](#S2.E4 "In 2.3 Multi-Objective Optimization and Gradient Conflict ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents"),
CORD selects a protected gradient $`\bm{a}`$ and a candidate gradient
$`\bm{b}`$ from
$`(\bm{a},\bm{b})\in\{(\bm{u},\bm{v}),(\bm{v},\bm{u})\}`$. The protected
loss $`F_{a}`$ is correspondingly $`F_{1}`$ or $`\lambda F_{2}`$, so
that $`\nabla F_{a}=\bm{a}`$. The loss definitions and training goal
inform this protection choice. Swapping roles alters the combined
direction without redefining losses or fixing priority across problems.

### 3.2  Projection direction and gate

Under the *conflict-only* gate, CORD combines gradients as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}}=\bm{a}+\tilde{\bm{b}},\qquad\tilde{\bm{b}}=\begin{cases}\bm{b}-\dfrac{\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle}{\lVert\bm{a}\rVert^{2}}\bm{a},&\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle<0,\\[5.0pt]
\bm{b},&\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle\geq 0.\end{cases}
``` |  | (6) |

A zero protected gradient leaves the candidate unchanged.

###### Lemma 1 (Closest feasible replacement).

For $`\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$, the candidate $`\tilde{\bm{b}}`$
in
Equation [6](#S3.E6 "In 3.2 Projection direction and gate ‣ 3 Method ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
is the unique solution of

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\min_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{z}-\bm{b}\rVert^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\langle\bm{a},\bm{z}\rangle\geq 0.
``` |  | (7) |

*Proof.* For $`\bm{a}=\bm{0}`$ every vector is feasible, and the
minimizer is $`\bm{b}`$. For $`\bm{a}\neq\bm{0}`$, the Lagrangian is
$`\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{z}-\bm{b}\rVert^{2}-\mu\langle\bm{a},\bm{z}\rangle`$.
Stationarity gives $`\bm{z}=\bm{b}+\mu\bm{a}`$. Feasibility,
$`\mu\geq 0`$, and complementary slackness give $`\mu=0`$ when
$`\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle\geq 0`$, and
$`\mu=-\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle/\lVert\bm{a}\rVert^{2}`$ otherwise.
These conditions are sufficient for this convex problem, and strict
convexity makes the minimizer unique. $`\square`$

The *always* gate instead requires
$`\langle\bm{a},\tilde{\bm{b}}\rangle=0`$: for nonzero $`\bm{a}`$ it
applies the projection in the first branch at every update, including
aligned gradients. Disabling projection sets $`\tilde{\bm{b}}=\bm{b}`$
and restores weighted summation. CORD uses the single-constraint
geometry of A-GEM ([Chaudhry et al., 2019](#bib.bib2)), with two current
objective gradients and an explicit choice of protection direction and
gate. PCGrad ([Yu et al., 2020](#bib.bib23)) modifies both gradients on
conflict, whereas CORD keeps the protected gradient intact.

###### Proposition 1 (First-order effect on the protected loss).

Fix $`\theta`$, $`\lambda`$, and the mini-batch losses, and suppose
$`F_{a}`$ is differentiable at $`\theta`$. With conflict-only
projection,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\langle\bm{a},\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}}\rangle`$ | $`\displaystyle=\lVert\bm{a}\rVert^{2}+\max\{\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle,0\},`$ |  | (8) |
|  | $`\displaystyle F_{a}(\theta-\eta\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}})-F_{a}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\eta\langle\bm{a},\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}}\rangle+o(\eta).`$ |  |  |

for $`\eta\to 0^{+}`$. Whenever projection is applied,
$`\langle\bm{a},\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}}\rangle=\lVert\bm{a}\rVert^{2}`$.
This equality also holds under the always gate.

*Proof.* The projected candidate is orthogonal to $`\bm{a}`$, giving the
equality on projection. Otherwise $`\tilde{\bm{b}}=\bm{b}`$ and the
inner product is nonnegative. Differentiability gives the stated
expansion for this fixed direction. The zero-reference case has both
inner products equal to zero. $`\square`$

Projection preserves the protected gradient’s first-order descent rate.
When it applies with $`\bm{a}\neq\bm{0}`$, orthogonal decomposition of
$`\bm{b}`$ gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\langle\bm{b},\tilde{\bm{b}}\rangle=\lVert\tilde{\bm{b}}\rVert^{2}=\lVert\bm{b}\rVert^{2}-\frac{\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle^{2}}{\lVert\bm{a}\rVert^{2}}.
``` |  | (9) |

This identity describes the surviving candidate alone. The full
direction also includes $`\bm{a}`$, so the unprotected loss need not
decrease. These properties concern the ideal combined gradient. Global
norm clipping rescales it, whereas AdamW’s momentum, adaptive scaling,
and weight decay can change the update direction ([Loshchilov and
Hutter, 2019](#bib.bib10)).
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3.SSS0.Px4 "From the combined gradient to the parameter step. ‣ B.3 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
relates the first-order term to the actual parameter displacement and

### 3.3  How protection interacts with the mixing weight

The two protection directions share a conflict test but respond
differently to the coefficient. Write $`\bm{G}_{1}=\nabla F_{1}`$ and
$`\bm{G}_{2}=\nabla F_{2}`$, and fix a conflicting pair with
$`c=\langle\bm{G}_{1},\bm{G}_{2}\rangle<0`$.
Equation [6](#S3.E6 "In 3.2 Projection direction and gate ‣ 3 Method ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
becomes

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bm{g}_{\mathrm{protect}\,1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\bm{G}_{1}+\lambda\left(\bm{G}_{2}-\frac{c}{\lVert\bm{G}_{1}\rVert^{2}}\bm{G}_{1}\right),`$ |  | (10) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\bm{g}_{\mathrm{protect}\,2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\lambda\bm{G}_{2}+\left(\bm{G}_{1}-\frac{c}{\lVert\bm{G}_{2}\rVert^{2}}\bm{G}_{2}\right).`$ |  | (11) |

In the first case, $`\lambda`$ scales the candidate and its correction
together. In the second, it scales the protected gradient, while the
correction to $`\bm{G}_{1}`$ is independent of $`\lambda`$. If $`\phi`$
is the angle between the unweighted gradients, the corresponding
correction norms are $`\lambda\lVert\bm{G}_{2}\rVert|\cos\phi|`$ and
$`\lVert\bm{G}_{1}\rVert|\cos\phi|`$. Swapping protection therefore
changes which gradient component $`\lambda`$ controls.

For a fixed pair and $`\lambda>0`$, scaling does not change the conflict
decision. Conflict-only projection retains aligned candidates. The
always gate removes their parallel component as well. The coefficient
controls scale, while the gate controls whether a helpful component is
retained.
Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Task-level Projection Intervention ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Analyses ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
isolates these effects at fixed gradients, while RQ2 compares the
resulting trained policies.

### 3.4  Accumulation and agent training

For an aggregation rule $`F_{j}=\sum_{m}\alpha_{m}F_{j}^{(m)}`$, with
fixed weights $`\alpha_{m}`$, define
$`\bm{g}_{j}^{(m)}=\nabla F_{j}^{(m)}`$ and
$`\bm{G}_{j}=\sum_{m}\alpha_{m}\bm{g}_{j}^{(m)}`$. CORD acts once on
these accumulated mini-batch gradients. Projection is nonlinear in the
gradient pair, so projecting each micro-batch separately usually
produces a different update. Accumulating first defines the projection
at the optimizer’s update scale. The loss aggregation rule sets the
weights before accumulation. Keeping those weights fixed makes
repartitioning a computational choice, whereas changing from micro-batch
to global-token or trajectory averaging changes the gradients
themselves.

#### Computational complexity.

Given the two objective gradients, CORD uses $`O(d)`$ arithmetic and
$`O(d)`$ additional gradient storage for $`d`$ trainable parameters.
With $`K`$ PPO mini-batches per training iteration, the
gradient-combination cost is $`O(Kd)`$. When the objectives depend on
the same student outputs, their gradient evaluations can share the
forward computation. They still require distinct objective-gradient
information, followed by inner products, a projection, and vector
addition. Shared computation and fused vector operations can reduce
redundant work without changing the projection rule. For a fixed
workload, actor overhead differs from full-iteration overhead. Rollout,
teacher scoring, and environment interaction are shared stages, so their
share of each iteration determines how additional gradient work affects
total time. The component model in
Appendix [C.6](#A3.SS6 "C.6 Computational cost ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Analyses ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
holds the workload fixed and separates measured actor timings from
estimated iteration overhead.

## 4  Experiments

We test whether changing the gradient-combination rule adds value across
joint training methods, then use the analyses to guide its
configuration. The experiments answer three research questions: (RQ1)
Effectiveness: How much does CORD improve existing joint training
recipes? (RQ2) Projection choices: How do the protected objective,
projection gate, and loss construction guide practical configuration?
(RQ3) Agent behavior: How do update choices appear in agent
interactions?

### 4.1  Experimental setup

#### Tasks and training.

We use the SDAR agent suite ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) with
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)). ALFWorld
([Shridhar et al., 2021](#bib.bib15)) evaluates household tasks over at
most 50 actions. We report success across six categories and the pooled
average. WebShop ([Yao et al., 2022](#bib.bib22)) evaluates
instruction-following shopping. We cap episodes at 15 actions and report
graded task score and strict success rate on a 0–100 scale. Our shared
trainer uses 150 training iterations, eight rollouts per prompt, AdamW
at $`10^{-6}`$, reference-KL coefficient $`0.01`$, and responses capped
at 512 tokens on eight NVIDIA A100 40 GB GPUs. Full settings are given
in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents").

#### Methods.

Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 RQ1: Incremental effectiveness ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
includes Vanilla, Skill-Prompt, and OPSD values reported by [Lu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib11). GRPO and RLSD results are our own runs. RLSD
incorporates the self-distillation signal into the policy optimization
objective ([Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib20)). Following SDAR, the joint
recipes share a predefined skill bank whose contents remain fixed
throughout training. Each Base–CORD pair uses the same bank and
skill-selection rule to isolate gradient combination. Household tasks
select task-specific manipulation skills, while shopping instructions
select relevant skills by keyword alongside general strategies. This
context guides teacher scoring of student-generated actions and is
absent from the student’s rollout and evaluation inputs. We apply CORD
to three joint training recipes in this shared interface: Skill-SD
([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)) ($`k_{3}`$, $`\lambda=0.001`$),
GRPO+OPSD (host $`k_{1}`$, $`\lambda=0.005`$), and SDAR ([Lu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11)) (confidence-weighted host $`k_{1}`$,
$`\lambda=0.01`$). Each recipe without CORD uses naive addition. $`+`$
CORD retains its coefficient, protects the RL gradient, and projects
only on conflict. RQ2 then varies the protected objective and projection
gate.

#### Evaluation.

We fix validation tasks to track progress on the same problems during
training and hold the final evaluation tasks constant across paired
methods. For the main comparison and full-training analyses in RQ2,
including PCGrad ([Yu et al., 2020](#bib.bib23)), we evaluate the final
policy four times after 150 updates at temperature $`0.4`$. Each run
uses one training seed, and the displayed $`\pm`$ is the population
standard deviation across these four passes. Our WebShop evaluation uses
500 tasks, whereas the published baseline values in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 RQ1: Incremental effectiveness ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
use the 128-task protocol of [Lu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib11).
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
details the protocol.

#### Choose protection direction and trigger together.

The protected objective determines which gradient remains intact, while
the gate determines whether projection also removes aligned components.

compares these choices on WebShop. At $`\lambda=0.001`$, conflict-only
projection improves success over no projection for both RL and
distillation protection, from 55.75% to 59.35% and 58.35%, respectively.
Switching to always projection raises success to 62.65% when protecting
RL but lowers it to 38.90% when protecting distillation. At
$`\lambda=0.005`$, all four CORD configurations instead lie between
61.25% and 62.10%, above the 56.75% unprojected result. Direction and
gating therefore interact with the coefficient: removing the aligned
component has a different effect depending on which objective is
protected and the relative gradient scales.

CORD keeps one gradient intact, whereas the two-objective PCGrad
adaptation ([Yu et al., 2020](#bib.bib23)) modifies both when they
conflict
(Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3.SSS0.Px3 "PCGrad two-objective adaptation. ‣ B.3 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")).
At $`\lambda=0.001`$, PCGrad has the largest observed mean of 63.10%,
but at $`\lambda=0.005`$ its 58.85% is below all four CORD
configurations.

The fixed-gradient analysis in
Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Task-level Projection Intervention ‣ Appendix C Supplementary Analyses ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
clarifies how CORD’s protection direction interacts with $`\lambda`$.
Protecting RL changes the weighted distillation gradient, so the
correction scales with $`\lambda`$. Protecting distillation changes the
RL gradient, whose correction is unchanged when only $`\lambda`$ varies.
*A small auxiliary coefficient therefore does not make distillation
protection a small intervention.* Because conflict-only projection
retains aligned contributions, the effect of always projection depends
on the selected protection direction.

## 5  Conclusion

Joint RL and distillation gives agents complementary feedback, but
opposing gradient components can reduce its local optimization benefit.
CORD applies a simple projection rule to this interaction, allowing
either objective to be protected and making the trigger explicit. The
first-order analysis explains the correction on fixed surrogates before
optimizer transformations, and the agent experiments establish
incremental gains across three host recipes on ALFWorld and two of three
on WebShop. The analyses also yield concrete configuration guidance:
choose protection and triggering together, tune the mixing weight for
the selected distillation loss, and specify aggregation before comparing
gradient directions. CORD thus makes gradient combination a reusable
design choice.

## Appendix A Additional Related Work

### A.1  On-policy and skill-conditioned distillation

On-policy distillation trains a student on its own generated sequences
while a teacher supplies token-level targets, reducing the distribution
mismatch between training and inference. [Agarwal et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib1) develop generalized knowledge distillation with a
separate teacher. On-policy self-distillation instead uses different
contextual views of a model: [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib25) condition
the teacher on privileged information, such as verified reasoning
traces, while the student sees the original question. For multi-turn
agents, Skill-SD ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib16)) derives dynamic
skills from completed trajectories and uses an importance-weighted
reverse-KL objective with a periodically synchronized teacher. These
methods supply dense supervision on student-generated trajectories.

SDAR ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) combines GRPO with
skill-conditioned distillation and modulates token-level supervision
using a detached sigmoid gate. Its experimental protocol draws on the
SkillBank of SkillRL ([Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)), which learns a
hierarchical skill library and evolves it with the policy. SDAR also
evaluates retrieved-skill adaptations of Skill-SD and OPSD, together
with prompt-based skill injection. Our three host recipes—Skill-SD,
GRPO+OPSD, and SDAR—follow the agent-training implementations described
in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
and
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents").
In particular, the retrieved-skill $`k_{3}`$ host follows the adaptation
in [Lu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib11), which differs from the original
Skill-SD dynamic skill-generation protocol. A token-level distillation
gate changes the input gradient, whereas CORD’s projection gate decides
when to modify the relationship between the two accumulated gradients.
The two operations can therefore be composed.

Another approach incorporates teacher information directly into the
advantage or policy-loss signal. RLSD ([Yang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib20))
uses detached teacher–student log-probability differences to reweight
token advantages while preserving their signs. This route modifies the
policy update internally, without a separate distillation loss to
combine.

### A.2  Evolving skills and reflection memory

The guidance available to an agent can evolve with its experience.
SkillRL expands and refines a hierarchical skill library using failure
analysis ([Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). RetroAgent stores
retrospective lessons from successful and failed attempts in a growing
reflection memory, and combines retrieved lessons with progress-based
intrinsic rewards ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)). Its RL-trained
variant also jointly optimizes decision making and self-reflection.
These approaches improve what the agent retains from its interactions
and retrieves for later tasks.

Evolving guidance need not reside in a persistent library. SEED trains
the policy to generate hindsight skills from its current trajectories
and internalizes their guidance through joint RL and on-policy
distillation ([Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)). OPID constructs episode-
and step-level skills whose induced log-probability shifts enter the
policy update ([Yang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib21)). GRSD uses reflections
from successful and failed rollouts to form group-level guidance and
modulate advantages ([Zheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)). CORD operates at
the subsequent combination of objective gradients, so its use is
orthogonal to whether guidance comes from a fixed bank or evolves with
the policy. Potential applications include combining reward and
distillation gradients in SEED, or decision-making and reflection
gradients in the RL-trained RetroAgent variant, while preserving their
respective guidance mechanisms.

### A.3  Conflicting gradients and projection

Multi-task loss balancing adjusts objective scales using gradient
magnitudes or learned uncertainty ([Chen et al., 2018](#bib.bib3);
[Kendall et al., 2018](#bib.bib7)). Gradient projection instead changes
directions and has a long history in multi-task and continual learning.
For two conflicting task gradients, PCGrad ([Yu et al.,
2020](#bib.bib23)) removes each gradient’s component along the other
before combining them. A-GEM ([Chaudhry et al., 2019](#bib.bib2)) uses
an asymmetric projection: it modifies the current-task gradient to
satisfy a single half-space constraint defined by the gradient estimated
from a sampled episodic-memory batch. This constraint prevents an
increase in that sampled memory loss under a first-order approximation.
Orthogonal gradient descent ([Farajtabar et al., 2020](#bib.bib5))
projects updates away from a stored subspace of gradients of previous
network outputs. CAGrad ([Liu et al., 2021](#bib.bib9)) balances
progress on the mean task loss with the worst local improvement among
tasks.

CORD reuses the single-half-space projection underlying A-GEM
(Lemma [1](#Thmlemma1 "Lemma 1 (Closest feasible replacement). ‣ 3.2 Projection direction and gate ‣ 3 Method ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")).
The protected reference in CORD comes from one of the two jointly
trained objectives, and the projected candidate is added to that
reference. Either objective can be protected. Conflict-only projection
uses a half-space constraint, while the always-project variant imposes
an orthogonality constraint even when the gradients agree. The agent
experiments study how protection, triggering, loss construction, and
aggregation affect learning. The main host comparisons protect GRPO with
conflict-only projection, while the direction and gate analyses test
both choices.

### A.4  Agent RL and GRPO-style policy optimization

GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)) estimates advantages relative to
groups of sampled responses, avoiding a separate learned critic. PPO
([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib13)) supplies the clipped policy
surrogate used by these host implementations. The truncated importance
weighting in our $`k_{3}`$ and $`\rho_{\mathrm{d}}`$ distillation
objectives is related to the correction used in V-trace/IMPALA
([Espeholt et al., 2018](#bib.bib4)), although the distillation weight
does not implement the full V-trace return estimator. GiGPO ([Feng
et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)) improves multi-turn credit assignment by
combining episode-level advantages with step-level comparisons among
actions grouped by shared anchor states. These choices determine the
reward-derived gradient. CORD operates after the two objective gradients
have been accumulated for a mini-batch, so credit assignment and
gradient combination address different parts of the update.

#### ALFWorld.

ALFWorld ([Shridhar et al., 2021](#bib.bib15)) provides text-based

performs the operations specified by a goal. We use the TextWorld
interface and the training split shared by SDAR and GiGPO. Evaluation

for each category and the pooled success rate over evaluated episodes,
so the overall score weights categories by their number of evaluated
tasks.

#### WebShop.

WebShop ([Yao et al., 2022](#bib.bib22)) asks the agent to find and
purchase a product that satisfies an instruction, including constraints
such as attributes, options, and price. We use the text interface and
the small catalogue selected by the inherited environment configuration.
The agent searches the catalogue, opens product pages, selects options,
and purchases an item within $`15`$ turns. The environment constructs
goals from this catalogue: the wrapper reserves goal indices
$`0`$–$`499`$ for evaluation and samples training goals from the
remaining indices. This index partition is interpreted within each
worker’s seeded goal ordering. We retain both the graded task score,
which measures how closely the purchase satisfies the instruction, and
strict success, which requires a task score of $`1`$. Both are reported
on a $`0`$–$`100`$ scale.

### B.2  Baselines

We instantiate Skill-SD, GRPO+OPSD, and SDAR in a common trainer. Each
recipe combines the GRPO objective with a token-level auxiliary loss,
and uses the same skill bank and teacher-input construction. Their
differences can be expressed through three choices: the distillation
coefficient $`\lambda`$, whether teacher-confidence weighting is
enabled, and the OPD token objective.
Table [6](#A2.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Loss aggregation. ‣ B.2 Baselines ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
states these choices explicitly.

#### Token objectives and confidence weights.

For a sampled response token $`y_{t}`$ and its interaction history
$`x_{t}`$, define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{t}=\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x_{t}),\qquad q_{t}=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x_{t}\oplus S(x_{t}))\right],\qquad\ell_{t}=s_{t}-q_{t}.
``` |  | (12) |

These log-probabilities follow
Equation [2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2 On-Policy Distillation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents").
The teacher receives skill context $`S(x_{t})`$. Its scores $`q_{t}`$
and the rollout log-probabilities $`s_{t}^{\mathrm{old}}`$ are detached.
The capped on-policy ratio is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{\mathrm{on},t}=\min\!\left\{\exp\!\left(\min\{s_{t}-s_{t}^{\mathrm{old}},10\}\right),2\right\}.
``` |  | (13) |

The token objectives used by the host recipes and loss analysis are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\psi_{t}=\begin{cases}\rho_{\mathrm{on},t}\big(\exp(\min\{-\ell_{t},20\})-1+\ell_{t}\big),&k_{3},\\
\rho_{\mathrm{on},t}\ell_{t},&\rho_{\mathrm{d}},\\
-\ell_{t},&\text{SDAR-style }k_{1}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (14) |

The $`k_{3}`$ and $`\rho_{\mathrm{d}}`$ objectives retain the
student-side gradient through $`\rho_{\mathrm{on},t}`$. For fixed
sampled tokens, with neither ratio nor exponential cap active,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\partial\psi_{t}^{k_{3}}}{\partial s_{t}}=\rho_{\mathrm{on},t}\ell_{t},\qquad\frac{\partial\psi_{t}^{\rho_{\mathrm{d}}}}{\partial s_{t}}=\rho_{\mathrm{on},t}(\ell_{t}+1).
``` |  | (15) |

Thus $`k_{3}`$ has zero token derivative at matched teacher and student
log-probabilities, while $`\rho_{\mathrm{d}}`$ retains an offset. The
objectives can change the direction of the aggregate gradient, rather
than only its scale. The loss and coefficient analyses examine the
$`\rho_{\mathrm{d}}`$ log-ratio objective as an alternative to the host
objectives in
Table [6](#A2.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Loss aggregation. ‣ B.2 Baselines ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents"),
which remain unchanged. The $`k_{1}`$ term follows the SDAR and
GRPO+OPSD implementations. With fixed sampled tokens and detached
$`q_{t}`$, its gradient is $`-\nabla s_{t}`$. Teacher scores affect this
host variant through confidence weights when those weights are enabled.
Confidence weights are $`w_{t}=1`$ when disabled and
$`w_{t}=\operatorname{sg}[\sigma(-\beta\ell_{t})]`$ when enabled, where
$`\sigma`$ is the sigmoid function. These weights act before gradient
accumulation, whereas the projection gate acts on the resulting gradient
pair.

#### Loss aggregation.

Let $`m_{t}`$ mask padding and inactive response tokens. The standard
host path forms a valid-token mean within each physical micro-batch
$`B^{m}`$ and applies its accumulation and distributed scaling
$`\alpha_{m}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}(B)=\mathcal{A}_{B}[w_{t}\psi_{t}]=\sum_{m}\alpha_{m}\frac{\sum_{t\in B^{m}}m_{t}w_{t}\psi_{t}}{\sum_{t\in B^{m}}m_{t}}.
``` |  | (16) |

The sum is over nonempty micro-batches. For a complete mini-batch with
$`M`$ equally weighted micro-batches, $`\alpha_{m}=1/M`$. Thus a token’s
effective weight also depends on its micro-batch’s valid-token count. A
global-token mean instead uses one denominator $`\sum_{t\in B}m_{t}`$
for the complete optimizer mini-batch.

#### Skill-SD.

Our shared implementation of the Skill-SD recipe uses the
importance-weighted $`k_{3}`$ surrogate and a coefficient of
$`10^{-3}`$. It retains the SDAR skill interface. In this
implementation, the student-to-rollout importance ratio is capped at
$`2`$, and the exponential argument in the $`k_{3}`$ term is
upper-clamped at $`20`$ for numerical stability. These safeguards are
applied identically to the Base and $`+`$ CORD configurations. The
auxiliary loss uses
Equation [16](#A2.E16 "In Loss aggregation. ‣ B.2 Baselines ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
without teacher-confidence weighting.

#### GRPO+OPSD.

GRPO+OPSD uses the plain teacher-minus-student log-probability term and
uniform token weights. Its coefficient in our unified implementation is
$`\lambda=0.005`$. This matches the effective scale of the SDAR
implementation’s GRPO+OPSD configuration: the latter sets the outer
coefficient to $`0.01`$ and the sigmoid sharpness to zero, yielding a
constant weight $`\sigma(0)=0.5`$. Our gate-off branch uses $`w_{t}=1`$,
so absorbing this constant into $`\lambda`$ preserves the auxiliary loss
and its gradient. The numerical difference in coefficients therefore
reflects the gate convention rather than a separate hyperparameter
search.

#### SDAR.

SDAR uses the same $`k_{1}`$ token term with $`\lambda=0.01`$ and
$`\beta=5`$. Its detached sigmoid weight assigns greater weight to
sampled tokens for which the skill-conditioned teacher has a higher
log-probability than the student. This confidence weighting acts before
differentiation. CORD subsequently acts on the accumulated auxiliary
gradient, using its alignment with the GRPO actor gradient to decide
whether to project. This separates token weighting from the subsequent
choice of gradient combination.

#### Additional baselines.

Our GRPO runs disable the distillation contribution. RLSD uses the
skill-conditioned self-teacher with coefficient $`0.5`$, a 100-step
warmdown, and clipping parameter $`0.2`$. We evaluate its final policies
after 150 updates in four passes using the ordered ALFWorld assignments
and the full WebShop task set described in
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents").
The reference Vanilla, Skill-Prompt, and OPSD entries in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 RQ1: Incremental effectiveness ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
are reported values from the SDAR paper.

#### Fixed skill bank and teacher context.

Following SDAR, we use the predefined SkillBank adopted from SkillRL
([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). The
bank contains general strategies and task-specific skills distilled from
successful experience and failure analysis. We keep its contents fixed

without its recursive bank-evolution procedure. In ALFWorld, the
household task determines the task-specific skills. In WebShop,
instruction keywords select relevant skills alongside the general

#### CORD configuration.

The main CORD configuration protects the GRPO actor gradient, uses the
Euclidean inner product, and projects once per complete mini-batch only
when the two gradients conflict. The coefficient is constant, and the
projection reference is the current protected gradient without EMA
smoothing.

The group-relative advantage in
Section [2](#S2 "2 Preliminaries ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
uses the host’s response-record returns, with group statistics pooled
across the records from all attempts at the same task. Each record’s
normalized advantage is broadcast over its valid response tokens. The
policy-loss implementation additionally uses dual clipping for negative
advantages: if
$`c_{t}=-\min(r_{t}\widehat{A}_{t},\operatorname{clip}(r_{t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{t})`$
is the token loss in
Equation [1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents"),
it replaces $`c_{t}`$ by $`\min(c_{t},-3\widehat{A}_{t})`$ when
$`\widehat{A}_{t}<0`$. The full minimized actor loss is
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pg}}+\beta_{\mathrm{ref}}\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{ref}}-\beta_{\mathrm{H}}\mathcal{H}`$,
where $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pg}}`$ includes this safeguard and the
regularization terms use the same valid-token aggregation. The frozen
rollout probabilities and advantages carry no gradient.

We optimize with AdamW, using momentum coefficients $`(0.9,0.999)`$,
weight decay $`0.01`$, a PPO clip ratio of $`0.2`$, and gradient-norm
clipping at $`1`$. The frozen initial policy supplies the reference for
the low-variance KL regularizer, with the same coefficient across
recipes. The GRPO leg accumulated by CORD differentiates the complete
host actor loss: the clipped policy surrogate plus reference-policy KL,
and the entropy term when enabled. Protection therefore refers to this
actor surrogate. The skill-conditioned auxiliary loss is computed
separately. The interactive environments assign reward $`10`$ for
completing the goal and $`0`$ otherwise. Invalid actions receive the
additional penalties in
Table [8](#A2.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Training configuration. ‣ B.3 Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details and Additional Results ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents").
The graded WebShop task score is retained as an evaluation metric even
though its training reward uses strict success.

#### Multi-turn inputs and gradient accumulation.

Following *verl-agent*, each decision uses the current observation, the
task description, and a bounded history of previous interactions.
ALFWorld and WebShop retain the two most recent turns. Losses are
computed on the generated responses. Environment observations and
padding do not provide target tokens. We accumulate the GRPO actor and
auxiliary gradients over the full PPO mini-batch with the selected
aggregation weights before computing their inner product and applying
the projection. In the fixed-coefficient implementation, the auxiliary
backward already includes $`\lambda`$, so its buffer stores
$`\lambda\bm{G}_{o}`$. After distributed gradient reduction and
accumulation, a packed reduction supplies inner products and norms
across parameter shards. The reported CORD configurations use Euclidean
statistics. Numerical protection skips projection when the squared
reference norm is at most $`\epsilon`$ and otherwise divides by
$`\lVert\bm{a}\rVert^{2}+\epsilon`$, with $`\epsilon=10^{-12}`$. The
ideal projection identities in
Proposition [1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (First-order effect on the protected loss). ‣ 3.2 Projection direction and gate ‣ 3 Method ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
omit this numerical adjustment. Writing $`A=\lVert\bm{a}\rVert^{2}`$ and
$`c=\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle`$, the regularized combined gradient
$`\bm{g}_{\epsilon}`$ satisfies
$`\langle\bm{a},\bm{g}_{\epsilon}\rangle=A+c\epsilon/(A+\epsilon)`$
before floating-point error. For $`c<0`$, this is below $`A`$ and need
not be positive. Skipping projection instead gives $`A+c`$. The combined
gradient is clipped before the AdamW optimizer step. Task sampling and
data order use recorded seeds, and the rollout implementation separately
exposes the generation seed so that sampled decoding can be reproduced
with a fixed worker configuration.

#### PCGrad two-objective adaptation.

We apply PCGrad ([Yu et al., 2020](#bib.bib23)) to the host’s fully
accumulated actor gradient $`\bm{u}`$ and $`\lambda`$-weighted
distillation gradient $`\bm{v}`$. When they conflict, each is projected
against the other’s original gradient, and the two projected gradients
are summed. Aligned pairs are added unchanged. Each projection adds
$`\epsilon=10^{-12}`$ to its squared-reference-norm denominator and is
skipped when that squared norm is at most $`\epsilon`$. The runs in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 RQ2: The roles of projection and gradient choices ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents")
use $`k_{3}`$ and the final-evaluation protocol below.

#### From the combined gradient to the parameter step.

At an anchor $`\theta`$, fix the sampled inputs and responses, masks,
rollout and reference-policy probabilities, advantages, teacher scores,
detached confidence values, aggregation weights, the stochastic
realization, and $`\lambda`$. These define a local surrogate $`F_{a}`$
with $`\bm{a}=\nabla F_{a}(\theta)`$. For the actual optimizer
displacement $`\Delta`$, write $`p=-\bm{a}^{\top}\Delta`$.

If $`F_{a}`$ has an $`L`$-Lipschitz gradient on a neighborhood of the
segment from $`\theta`$ to $`\theta+\Delta`$, then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F_{a}(\theta+\Delta)-F_{a}(\theta)\leq-p+\frac{L}{2}\|\Delta\|^{2}.
``` |  | (17) |

Indeed, the fundamental theorem of calculus expresses the remainder as
$`\int_{0}^{1}[\nabla F_{a}(\theta+t\Delta)-\bm{a}]^{\top}\Delta\,dt`$.
Cauchy–Schwarz and the Lipschitz condition bound its absolute value by
$`L\|\Delta\|^{2}/2`$. For ideal SGD,
$`\Delta=-\eta\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}}`$, strict descent follows when
$`L>0`$, $`\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}}>0`$, and

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
0<\eta<\frac{2\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}}}{L\|\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}}\|^{2}}.
``` |  | (18) |

This is a sufficient local condition, without an estimate of $`L`$. PPO
or ratio-cap kinks can invalidate the smoothness assumption. Scalar
gradient clipping preserves the sign of
$`\bm{a}^{\top}\bm{g}_{\mathrm{final}}`$ but changes the effective step
size. AdamW’s momentum, coordinate scaling, and weight decay change
$`\Delta`$, so the Euclidean projection identity alone does not
establish $`p>0`$. These statements concern the fixed surrogate and
imply neither monotonic return improvement nor training convergence.

#### Fixed validation and final-evaluation tasks.

We fix the validation tasks so that training progress is measured on the
same problems at each evaluation. In the inherited ALFWorld protocol, we
preserve an ordered list of $`128`$ episode assignments drawn from the
in-distribution evaluation pool. The list contains $`91`$ distinct
games. Retaining repeated assignments preserves their evaluation
weights. Analyses identified as full-game evaluations use all $`140`$
in-distribution ALFWorld games with fixed assignments. For WebShop,
final evaluation uses $`500`$ predetermined task assignments. Each
comparison uses the same tasks, ordering, and decoding settings for Base
and CORD, separating changes in the policy from changes in task
composition.

#### Monitoring and final evaluation.

We separate training-time monitoring from the measurements reported as
final performance. When enabled, monitoring uses greedy decoding on a
fixed set every five training iterations. Final evaluation uses the
policy after 150 updates, without selecting the checkpoint with the
highest intermediate validation score. This follows the *verl-agent*
authors’ clarification that final scores are obtained by repeatedly
evaluating the step-$`150`$ checkpoint and averaging the results.¹¹ 1
[*verl-agent*, issue \#246, author clarification (June 7,
2026).](https://github.com/langfengQ/verl-agent/issues/246#issuecomment-4641967418)

The final-evaluation protocol performs four evaluation passes,
generating one trajectory per task in each pass. Sampled evaluation uses
temperature $`0.4`$ and top-$`p=1`$, without top-$`k`$ truncation. If
$`s_{r}`$ is a reported metric in pass $`r`$, we report

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{s}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{r=1}^{K}s_{r},\qquad\sigma_{\mathrm{eval}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{r=1}^{K}(s_{r}-\bar{s})^{2}},\qquad K=4.
``` |  | (19) |

Thus, $`\bar{s}\pm\sigma_{\mathrm{eval}}`$ reports the mean and
population standard deviation across four evaluation passes of the final
policy. It does not estimate variation across training seeds, and
averaging four passes does not change the metric into a best-of-four
success rate. The final-evaluation comparisons use one training seed.

#### Relation to the reported SDAR scores.

SDAR reports WebShop results on $`128`$ fixed validation tasks ([Lu
et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)). The paper does not specify the number of
independent final-evaluation passes or report variability across such
passes. We use $`500`$ fixed assignments and report the mean and
population standard deviation over four independently sampled passes of
the final policy. These protocol differences limit direct comparison
with the published scores, identified separately in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 RQ1: Incremental effectiveness ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents").
Our Base–CORD comparisons share task coverage and evaluation settings.

#### Dependence on the coefficient.

For fixed nonzero loss gradients $`\bm{G}_{r}`$ and $`\bm{G}_{o}`$,
write $`\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{G}_{o}`$ with $`\lambda>0`$, and let $`\phi`$

gate used here, the projection correction has norm
$`\lambda\lVert\bm{G}_{o}\rVert|\cos\phi|`$ when protecting RL and
$`\lVert\bm{G}_{r}\rVert|\cos\phi|`$ when protecting distillation, by
Equation [6](#S3.E6 "In 3.2 Projection direction and gate ‣ 3 Method ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents").
Thus the second correction is independent of $`\lambda`$ at this fixed
gradient pair, whereas the kept distillation term still scales with
$`\lambda`$. These identities also hold on the conflicting branch of the
conflict-only gate, whose aligned branch has zero correction.

#### Continuation protocol.

Every configuration starts from the same ALFWorld/Qwen2.5-3B checkpoint
at training step 90 and its AdamW state. We continue for five rounds
with one training seed, gated $`k_{1}`$, $`\lambda=.01`$, $`\beta=5`$,
and conflict-only projection. Each run uses 640 episodes, including the
same initial 128 episodes. Four response rows per GPU on eight GPUs give
$`1/2/4`$ accumulation steps for optimizer mini-batch sizes
$`B=32/64/128`$. Evaluation uses 512 attempts over 91 in-distribution
tasks at temperature $`0.4`$, with success averaged across tasks. The
95% task-bootstrap intervals describe evaluation uncertainty for this
seed. Paired contrasts are descriptive. The figure labels *Protect GRPO*
and *Protect OPD* denote RL and distillation protection, respectively.

#### Aggregation weights.

Let $`S`$ be an optimizer mini-batch containing $`b`$ valid response
rows, where $`B`$ is the configured mini-batch size. For objective $`j`$
and token $`t`$ in response row $`i`$, let $`f_{it}^{(j)}`$ include its
token loss and any confidence weight: in particular,
$`f_{it}^{(2)}=w_{it}\psi_{it}`$ for OPD. Then
$`F_{j}(S)=\sum_{(i,t)\in S}\omega_{it}f_{it}^{(j)}`$ before projection.
The three rules assign token weights

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\omega_{it}=\begin{cases}1/(MT_{m}),&\text{microbatch averaging }(L_{1}),\\
1/T,&\text{global-token averaging }(L_{2}),\\
R/(NbL_{\tau(i)}),&\text{trajectory averaging }(L_{3}).\end{cases}
``` |  |

Here $`T_{m}`$ and $`T`$ count valid tokens in microbatch $`m`$ and
$`S`$, and $`M`$ counts nonempty microbatches across GPUs. For
trajectory averaging, $`R`$ and $`N`$ count response rows and complete
trajectories in the rollout. Let $`\tau(i)`$ identify the complete
trajectory containing row $`i`$, and let $`L_{k}`$ count valid response
tokens across all rows of trajectory $`k`$. When $`b=R`$, the
trajectory-averaged objective is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F_{j,3}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{L_{k}}\sum_{(i,t):\,\tau(i)=k}f_{it}^{(j)},
``` |  |

so every complete trajectory has equal total weight. Mini-batches of
response rows retain these complete-trajectory length weights.

Using $`\bm{u}=\bm{G}_{r}`$ and $`\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{G}_{o}`$ as in
Equation [4](#S2.E4 "In 2.3 Multi-Objective Optimization and Gradient Conflict ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimizationfor Joint RL and Distillation in Agents"),
during conflict, correction norms are
$`\lambda\|\bm{G}_{o}\||\cos\phi|`$ under RL protection and

increasing $`B`$ reduces the number of updates from $`616`$ to $`300`$

#### Loss aggregation.

At $`B=64`$, the directions nearly tie under microbatch averaging.
Distillation protection leads by $`3.71`$ points under global-token
averaging, and RL protection leads by $`4.35`$ under trajectory
averaging

All three paired intervals include zero, so this ordering remains
descriptive. Aggregation reweights gradients before projection, changing
the conflict test and protected objective. Global-token averaging
($`L_{2}`$) weights each valid token equally, while trajectory averaging
($`L_{3}`$) gives each complete trajectory equal total weight when all
rollout rows are included.

### C.6  Computational cost

Let $`d`$ be the number of trainable parameters and let a training
iteration contain $`K`$ PPO mini-batches, each partitioned into $`M`$
micro-batches. CORD accumulates the two objective gradients over each
complete PPO mini-batch before applying the projection. Computing their
inner products, norms, and combined update requires $`O(d)`$ arithmetic
per mini-batch, or $`O(Kd)`$ per iteration. The additional gradient
buffers require $`O(d)`$ storage, which is distributed across workers
when gradients are sharded. The scalar inner products and norms can be
communicated in a packed reduction whose message size does not grow with
$`d`$.

For gradient evaluation, write $`F`$ for the cost of a student forward
computation and $`B_{1}`$, $`B_{2}`$ for the reverse-mode computations
of the two objectives on a fixed micro-batch. If both objectives use the
same student outputs, a shared-forward schedule has actor-update cost

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{CORD}}^{\mathrm{actor}}=KM(F+B_{1}+B_{2})+O(Kd)+T_{\mathrm{opt}},
``` |  |

where $`T_{\mathrm{opt}}`$ includes gradient clipping and optimizer
updates. A fused-loss Base instead has cost

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{Base}}^{\mathrm{actor}}=KM(F+B_{\mathrm{sum}})+T_{\mathrm{opt}},
``` |  |

with $`B_{\mathrm{sum}}`$ denoting the reverse-mode computation of the
weighted loss sum. These expressions use equal-sized micro-batches to
simplify notation. For variable workloads, the corresponding costs are
summed over micro-batches. Parameter and gradient communication belong
to the gradient evaluation costs, separately from the packed reduction
for projection.

Sharing the forward computation avoids evaluating identical student
outputs twice. It does not make two objective-gradient evaluations
equivalent to one backward pass on their summed loss: the latter
supplies only the combined gradient, which generally does not determine
the inner products needed by CORD. Both schedules have the same
asymptotic order when the reverse-mode costs are comparable, while their
constant factors depend on graph reuse, gradient-buffer handling,
sharding, and hardware. Forward sharing, fused vector operations, and
selective diagnostic collection are implementation opportunities rather
than a fixed wall-clock guarantee. Teacher scores already computed for
the auxiliary objective are reused.

#### Cost of a complete training iteration.

Let $`A`$ denote the actor-update time of an ordinary fused-loss Base,
and let $`S`$ include rollout and environment interaction, teacher,
reference, and old-policy scoring, reward and advantage computation, and
the remaining work within a training iteration. For a fixed workload, if
these shared stages remain unchanged and the actor-update multiplier is
$`r_{\mathrm{actor}}`$, then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{iter}}=\frac{S+r_{\mathrm{actor}}A}{S+A}=1+(r_{\mathrm{actor}}-1)f_{\mathrm{actor}},\qquad f_{\mathrm{actor}}=\frac{A}{S+A}.
``` |  |

Thus, the actor-update multiplier is weighted by its share of Base
iteration time. This comparison excludes initialization, periodic
evaluation, checkpoint saving, and time outside the training iteration.

#### Component-based estimate.

We evaluate an isolated shared-forward implementation on Qwen2.5-3B with
eight A100-40GB GPUs, FSDP, activation checkpointing, a global PPO
mini-batch size of 64, and a per-GPU micro-batch size of four. We replay
the first, middle, and last complete mini-batches saved from one
ALFWorld iteration. All replays use the same checkpoint, optimizer, and
random states, with fixed $`\lambda=0.01`$ and conflict-only projection
with GRPO protection. After two warmup calls, each setting has seven
timed repeats per mini-batch. Timings include the student forward and
backward computations, communication, gradient combination, clipping,
and AdamW, with GPU synchronization and the maximum elapsed time across
workers. The ratio of summed per-mini-batch median times is
$`1.78\times`$ relative to an ordinary Base that backpropagates the
summed loss once.

To estimate the iteration-level impact, we use two recorded WebShop
workload profiles with the same model, GPU type and count, and actor
configuration. Both profiles cover the same 131 iterations between
````

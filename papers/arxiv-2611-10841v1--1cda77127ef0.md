A Gamified Framework for Conflict-Driven Design

Discovering Strategies for Collaborative Resilience with Multi-Agent
Learning

Jin Gao

Massachusetts Institute of
Technology \| [gaojin@mit.edu](mailto:gaojin@mit.edu)

Author version published in _ACADIA 2025: Computing for Resilience_,
vol. 2, pp. 724–732.

[doi:10.52842/conf.acadia.2025.2.724](https://doi.org/10.52842/conf.acadia.2025.2.724)

![[Uncaptioned image]](2611.10841v1/figures/fig-1.jpg)

Figure 1: Illustration of the agent-driven collaborative design process
at the Tatsumi case study site.

Abstract. Conflicting stakeholder objectives make it difficult to
coordinate investments in urban resilience. We model conflict-driven
urban design as a non-zero-sum digital board game in which stakeholder
agents allocate scarce resources and balance individual payoffs against
shared urban development and resilience objectives. We use multi-agent
reinforcement learning (MARL) to train policies and examine their
behavior across three settings. On a $`4\times 4`$ board, learned
policies reach about 84% of the total return found by a simulated
annealing benchmark. On an $`8\times 8`$ board with spatial and resource
constraints, trained agents favor projects that improve resilience in
self-play. In a $`12\times 12`$ simulation initialized from historical
maps of Tatsumi, Tokyo, trained agents achieve a mean total score of
452.1, compared with 357.3 for a random-policy baseline across 10,000
simulation runs. Gains in adaptability, sustainability, flood
resistance, and greenery come with reductions in vitality and density. A
human-in-the-loop interface supports replay and lets participants take
over stakeholder decisions, providing a testbed for exploring these
trade-offs in participatory urban planning.

Keywords: urban design, multi-agent reinforcement learning, urban
resilience, game theory, participatory planning

## 1 Introduction

In many design projects, one of the most challenging steps is reaching
consensus, often shaped by negotiations and exchanges of interests among
stakeholders. Our research introduces a customized digital "board game"
that provides a minimal abstraction of the real-world city-building
process by modeling core stakeholder personas, their roles, and
interactions. Within this environment, we apply multi-agent
reinforcement learning (MARL) to train AI agents to develop strategies
for resource allocation under competing objectives. The trained models
are then used to simulate planning processes in conflict-driven
scenarios, offering insights into negotiation dynamics and stakeholder
trade-offs. Furthermore, we present a human-in-the-loop interface that
enables participants to visualize the simulation process and interact
with AI agents, supporting a deeper understanding of planning dynamics
while offering valuable insights into real-world dilemmas. See
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1") for the Tatsumi site.

## 2 Related Work

Board games have long been established as participatory tools. In the
board game community, city building is an important genre, exemplified
by games such as Bay Area Regional Planner, Small Cities, and Suburbia.
Serious games are also widely used in academic research ([Poplin,
2012](#bib.bib14); [Prasad Sandbhor et al., 2021](#bib.bib15)), where
interactive interfaces have emerged for participatory planning, bridging
the gap between algorithmic optimization and public participation, such
as CityScope ([Alonso et al., 2018](#bib.bib2)).

Game theory provides theoretical foundations in modeling and evaluating
the decision process and equilibria. Prior studies have applied it to
model land-use negotiations among stakeholders ([Liu et al.,
2016](#bib.bib9)), and collective decision-making processes for urban
planning ([Abolhasani et al., 2022](#bib.bib1)). These studies provide
theoretical foundations for analyzing interdependent decision-making in
urban contexts.

Agent-based models (ABMs) have been widely used to capture
population-level interactions and emergent behaviors in urban
environments ([Batty, 2005](#bib.bib3); [Bijandi et al.,
2021](#bib.bib4); [Crooks et al., 2008](#bib.bib5)). However, ABM agents
are often built on simplified rule-based behaviors that have limited
capabilities for making complex decisions.

Multi-agent reinforcement learning offers a computational extension to
these models, where agents learn strategies through self-play and train
neural networks based on interactions in a customized environment.
[Kaviari et al. (2019)](#bib.bib8) combined agent-based modeling and
game theory to simulate urban growth, while [Qian et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib16) used MARL to model participatory planning under
competing stakeholder interests. Cicero demonstrated how strategic
reasoning and language models can be combined in the negotiation game
Diplomacy ([Meta FAIR et al., 2022](#bib.bib11)).

Our research contributes to the realm by proposing a scalable
methodology that abstracts the urban decision-making process via games
and uses a customized environment to perform efficient MARL training.
Agents are trained and evaluated under different reward matrices to
study how value propositions affect strategy cooperation under
real-world contexts. The workflow contributes to the emerging field of
multi-agent learning and computational urban planning by offering
real-world use cases and a gamified testbed for collaborative
decision-making. Our vision of this project is to shift the
traditionally top-down urbanization process into an AI-augmented,
participatory approach, empowering diverse voices to be captured with
players’ reward functions and trained under interpretable approaches.

## 3 Methods

Our method consists of three tightly coupled components. First, we
introduce a scalable game environment that simulates core stakeholder
interactions. It serves both as a participatory abstraction for modeling
real-world planning dynamics and as a testbed for agents to act and
learn. Then, we introduce a multi-agent learning workflow, where agents
learn adaptive strategies under different value matrices in the game
environment. Last, we present a digital human interface to allow humans
to observe the game status and interact with agents during the decision
process.

### 3.1 Game Environment

Our goal is to define a game environment that captures the complexity of
collective decision-making while remaining feasible for agent training.
We found that a board game with discrete actions fits this purpose. A
central design consideration is the level of abstraction: The game
should preserve the key dynamics involved in the design process and
balance between stakeholders. For example, when simulating
investment-reward dynamics, a critical decision is whether players
should continuously receive rewards from their investments over
subsequent turns—a factor that directly shapes agents’ perspectives on
long-term benefits. These nuanced design decisions require fine-tuning
to balance between reflecting real-world trade-offs and enabling agents
to learn long-term strategies within computational constraints.

The game map consists of a configurable $`M\times N`$ grid. Each cell
has key parameters that represent the environmental, economic, and
social aspects of urban development. These parameters are initialized
based on the original terrain and any preexisting projects, and are
dynamically updated each round according to the buildings placed on the
board and their effects on both the selected and adjacent cells. The
overall environment score is calculated as the average of all indices
across the grid. During each turn, players act sequentially, choosing
either to construct a building on an available cell or to skip their
turn.

Agents in our framework represent stakeholders whose actions shape the
environment. Since stakeholders often pursue diverse and sometimes
conflicting objectives, we model their priorities using value matrices,
where each player’s reward is parameterized by weights that balance
self-interest against map parameters representing site conditions and
resilience. To capture this diversity, we define an integrated reward
function that combines individual resource gains with the collective
impacts encoded in the game map parameters.

![Refer to caption](2611.10841v1/figures/fig-2.jpg)

Figure 2: Game actions and their effects on player resources.

Actions are categorized into basic and resilience-building projects
under the urban resilience theme, each requiring resources to initiate.
We define financial capital as the "money" resource, and social capital
as the "reputation" resource. A successful action triggers immediate
changes on the map and introduces long-term costs and benefits over
subsequent turns. For example, constructing a park imposes ongoing
maintenance costs on the builder but simultaneously increases their
reputation each turn. These trade-offs force players to carefully
consider (learn) the short-term and long-term impacts of their actions.
In the applied experiment, we further extend the reward mechanism by
introducing value decay and a block-level redevelopment rule above a
threshold, which allows agents to replace existing buildings with new
ones, thereby simulating urban renewal.

Interactions between players, illustrated in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Game Environment ‣ 3 Methods"), are
realized through spatial and temporal dynamics on the game board. In our
experiments, constructing a building changes the builder's rewards and
the state of the surrounding $`3\times 3`$ neighborhood. These effects
are carefully defined to be competitive across building types, so that
each building type has distinct characteristics without a single
dominant action. For instance, parks increase greenery but decrease
vitality, whereas houses reduce greenery while increasing density. This
design allows each action to carry distinct trade-offs, encouraging
players to strategize based on their individual priorities.

The game loop is an iterative process where stakeholder agents follow a
sequential decision loop to modify the game board. As illustrated in
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1 Game Environment ‣ 3 Methods"), each
action updates both individual rewards and the environmental scores.
These components are combined into a total score, parameterized by
weights, to capture the balance between personal objectives and
collective impact. While no explicit winning criterion is defined, the
overall outcome is evaluated collectively: strategies that yield higher
total rewards across all agents are considered more desirable. To
discourage infeasible actions, players who lack sufficient resources
incur a significant penalty.

### 3.2 Multi-Agent Learning

Problem Definition: Unlike well-studied games like poker, tic-tac-toe,
or rock-paper-scissors, which are governed by clear rules and often
exhibit zero-sum properties, the proposed game environment is
non-zero-sum in nature. That is, stakeholders typically have conflicting
objectives, but their actions can also generate shared benefits or
collective challenges. This makes it challenging to use the properties
and theories of zero-sum games to analyze the expected outcome of the
game.

Agents learn decision policies from their observations of the
environment. Due to the non-zero-sum nature, the players can jointly
improve their outcomes by coordinating actions that raise the overall
environment score. Consequently, the solution space includes equilibria
that are not strictly competitive but rather mutually beneficial. We
discuss solution behavior through the lens of coarse correlated
equilibrium (CCE), a tractable relaxation of correlated equilibrium (and
thus a superset of Nash equilibria) for general-sum games. A CCE
represents a strategy distribution under which no agent can improve its
expected payoff by committing in advance to a unilateral deviation. It
can be approached through learning-based methods. Under such learning
dynamics, coordinated strategies may give rise to mutually beneficial
outcomes that improve both individual payoffs and the overall
environmental score. Less cooperative players, such as interest-driven
players, may prioritize short-term gains, which can impede convergence
toward coordinated behavior during training.

Learning Environment: While single-agent RL environments are typically
defined by a single observation–action–reward loop, multi-agent settings
introduce additional challenges such as nonstationarity, coordination,
and credit assignment. After comparison, we adopt PettingZoo ([Terry et
al., 2021](#bib.bib18)) as the environment API and EPyMARL ([Papoudakis
et al., 2021](#bib.bib13)) as the training stack, due to their
lightweight design and good interoperability with modern libraries. The
core components include the observation space, action space, step
function, and reward. The observation space defines what agents perceive
at each turn while taking actions. Depending on the setting, agents may
receive full or partial observations, which affect the coordination
dynamics and learning stability. We define the observation space as a
collection of matrices, including the status of the grid, the player’s
current resources, and the distribution of builders and building types
on the map. The action space is discrete and turn-based, where each
agent chooses either to construct a building on an available cell or to
skip the turn. The step function governs the core game logic and updates
the game state. It begins by decoding agents’ actions to update
environment parameters, then calculates each agent’s rewards. Reward
design plays a critical role in shaping emergent strategies. In our
environment, rewards consist of two primary components: individual
rewards, reflecting agents' financial resources and social reputation;
and an environmental reward, calculated as the average of Greenery (G),
Vitality (V), and Density (D) parameters across the grid. We also
incorporate an optional resilience reward component alongside
traditional metrics, computed as the average of Adaptability (A),
Sustainability (S), and Flood Resistance (F) parameters, representing
long-term climate adaptation capacity and disaster preparedness
indicators. This creates a three-component utility function where agents
balance personal resources, traditional urban development metrics, and
resilience objectives according to individual preference weights.

Learning Algorithm: A central challenge in multi-agent systems is that
from each agent’s perspective, the environment is nonstationary, and the
joint state–action space grows combinatorially with the number of agents
([Lowe et al., 2017](#bib.bib10)). Traditional optimization techniques,
such as mixed-integer programming (MIP), and heuristics, including
genetic algorithms and simulated annealing, can provide theoretical
benchmarks under fixed assumptions but may become computationally
prohibitive in large multi-agent settings. In comparison, learning-based
approaches offer two key advantages: efficiency, as trained policies
enable real-time decision-making without repeatedly solving costly
optimization problems, making them suitable for human-in-the-loop
applications; and scalability, as GPU-accelerated computation allows
these methods to handle larger and more complex environments. We discuss
independent learners and centralized-critic methods below; the
proof-of-concept comparison also includes value decomposition
(Figure [6](#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 3.3 Human–Agent Interface ‣ 3 Methods")).

Independent learning is a family of algorithms where each agent treats
other agents as part of the environment and optimizes its own return
using standard RL methods such as asynchronous advantage actor–critic
(A3C; [Mnih et al., 2016](#bib.bib12)) and proximal policy optimization
(PPO; [Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib17)). This approach is scalable
but often exhibits instability due to the nonstationary policies of
other agents ([Foerster et al., 2018](#bib.bib6); [Yu et al.,
2021](#bib.bib19)). In our environments, independent learners tend to
converge prematurely to local optima and later exhibit performance
degradation; the returns do not stabilize in these runs.

With CTDE (centralized training and decentralized execution), a critic
uses global state information during training, and agents act
independently at execution time ([Lowe et al., 2017](#bib.bib10)). This
stabilizes value estimation and enables coordination signals to be
embedded in training. In our tests, CTDE methods such as multi-agent PPO
(MAPPO; [Yu et al., 2021](#bib.bib19)) and multi-agent advantage
actor–critic (MAA2C) generally achieved more stable convergence.
Particularly, MAPPO extends the standard PPO algorithm into the
multi-agent setting by combining decentralized policy updates with a
centralized value function. By leveraging PPO’s stability through its
clipped surrogate objective, MAPPO enables multiple agents to train with
a shared critic that incorporates global state information, making it
well-suited for cooperative–competitive environments such as the
proposed board game framework. In our experiments, MAPPO not only
converged more rapidly but also sustained higher long-term returns
compared to independent learners.

### 3.3 Human–Agent Interface

We developed an interface that allows people to observe or play with
trained agents
(Figure [5](#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 3.2 Multi-Agent Learning ‣ 3 Methods")).
The interface has two modes: simulation and play. In simulation mode,
all agents are autonomously controlled by the trained MARL policies. The
environment runs until a terminal state is reached, allowing researchers
to evaluate emergent behaviors and collective dynamics without human
intervention. We observed that increasing the emphasis on self-interest
tends to correlate with higher cumulative returns for agents, suggesting
that personal and collaborative reward can be aligned under certain
conditions. It may also reflect insufficient competitive tension that
requires further fine-tuning of the game mechanism. In Play mode, the
game can be played sequentially, where humans can act as one of the
agents by overwriting their actions at each step. This enables
human-in-the-loop interactions, which is useful in studying hybrid
decision-making, human and agent behaviors, and testing new policies or
strategies.

### 4.1 Proof-of-Concept Experiment

As an initial validation, we designed a minimal-scale experiment to test
whether the system can learn high-return strategies in a small
multi-agent setting. The environment was configured as a $`4\times 4`$
grid (16 cells) with a limit of 100 timesteps, involving three agents
representing distinct stakeholder types: an altruistic player, a
balanced player, and an interest-driven player. Each agent started with
equal initial resources and pursued their objectives by balancing
personal gains with collective outcomes. Players could choose among
three building types—Park, House, and Shop. These actions directly
affected both the environment indices—Greenery (G), Vitality (V), and
Density (D)—and the agents’ long-term payoffs, introducing trade-offs
between short-term costs and long-term benefits. This configuration
represents the minimal setup of our game: it captures the essential
characteristics of multi-agent interaction and conflicting objectives
across three dimensions (environmental, economic, and social), while
keeping the state and action space small enough to allow clear
comparison between the return from the optimization benchmark and the
performance of trained models. To obtain an optimization-based
benchmark, we used simulated annealing to search for high-return
building placements. The search found a total return of 8,336 over the
100-timestep horizon. The reinforcement learning agents achieved total
episode returns approaching 7,000, demonstrating they successfully
learned strategies that capture approximately 84% of the simulated
annealing benchmark. This comparison measures performance against the
best solution found by the search; it does not establish a bound on the
optimal return. Among tests, MAPPO and MAA2C with shared information
converged more quickly and demonstrated more stable performance than the
other algorithms. Therefore, we adopt MAPPO with shared information as
the training method for subsequent experiments. The training curves are
shown in
Figure [6](#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 3.3 Human–Agent Interface ‣ 3 Methods").

### 4.2 Scaled-Up Experiment

To evaluate collaborative strategies under additional constraints and
with resilience as a goal, we doubled each grid dimension to create an
$`8\times 8`$ grid, adding terrains and pre-built urban infrastructure,
simulating the spatial constraints of city planning. We set the training
objective to earn maximum cumulative reward under resource constraints,
balancing essential demand projects with long-term resilience
investments. For this experiment, we conducted five training runs with
different random seeds, each for 10 million episodes. In
Figure [7](#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4.1 Proof-of-Concept Experiment ‣ 4 Experiments"),
the top plot shows the mean total return over a training session on the
scaled-up environment using the MAPPO algorithm; the bottom plot tracks
the standard deviation of returns across episodes. Despite the
instability of built-in multi-agent competition, the overall return
generally increases. We observed several inflection points, indicating
policy shifts as agents discover new coordination strategies. For some
training sessions, the return curve stabilizes near its peak, suggesting
convergence to a high-reward collaborative policy. The declining
standard deviation further reflects improved policy stability and
reduced variance across episodes.

A simulated gameplay session is visualized in
Figure [8](#S4.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 4.1 Proof-of-Concept Experiment ‣ 4 Experiments"),
captured from the human–agent interface. In this session,
resilience-oriented projects, including community hubs, parks, and solar
grids, occupied 82% of the buildable cells. These results reveal that,
under dynamic spatial and resource trade-offs, the trained strategies
evolve through self-play to internalize spatial–reward relationships
between project types and environmental outcomes. The agents ultimately
form adaptive and context-sensitive strategies that optimize
collaborative decisions in complex, uncertain urban scenarios, which can
be further used as a dynamic reference in real-world zoning and resource
allocation practices under the theme of resilience.

### 4.3 Tatsumi, Tokyo Case Study

Based on the previous experiments, we extend our model to a real-world
site to demonstrate its adaptability and scalability. For this purpose,
we selected a district in Koto City, Tokyo. The area is built on
low-lying wetlands and frequently exposed to typhoons, making it a
critical site for examining historical needs for resilience improvement.
However, due to resource constraints, the district requires careful
management of resource allocation under stakeholders’ preferences. We
selected the 1980s as the initial period, which marks the beginning of
the peak of urban development and can help us compare simulated results
with real-world development results. We initialize the $`12\times 12`$
grid directly from historical maps: for each cell, we extract the
dominant parcel or land-cover feature and assign it to the closest
in-game type—either a terrain class or a pre-built project. These
assignments are treated equivalently to in-game constructions: they
apply the same local and $`3\times 3`$ neighborhood effects, incur the
same per-turn costs/benefits, and thereby initialize the full set of map
parameters at the start of each episode. This larger decision space
makes exhaustive search less practical, so we use learned policies to
explore the available strategies. Following the previous experiment, we
adopt MAPPO, which was shown to be effective, as the training algorithm
in this setting. We trained agents in the environment for 100 million
episodes with three random seeds, as shown in
Figure [9](#S4.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 4.2 Scaled-Up Experiment ‣ 4 Experiments").
Overall, the training process exhibits high volatility in the early
stage as agents rapidly explore the environment. After converging to
several local optima, some runs (e.g., the green curve) continue to
improve and eventually achieve higher returns.
Figure [10](#S4.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ 4.2 Scaled-Up Experiment ‣ 4 Experiments")
shows a typical simulated game where the total score rises overall from
285 to 427 points, about 48% above the 2025 map baseline of 288 when
evaluated with the same scoring model. We further observe that
resilience-related scores and growth rates gradually surpass those of
non-resilience projects, indicating that the resulting policies
increasingly favor resilience-oriented investments, which, despite their
higher initial costs, yield greater long-term rewards.

how the policies trade off resilience and development under the
specified scoring model.

## 5 Conclusion and Future Work

This study presents a practical framework for modeling and evaluating
resource allocation processes as a trainable digital board game,
offering a novel bridge between game theory, multi-agent learning, and
participatory planning. Through mechanism design, the framework captures
the complex trade-offs between individual incentives and collective
outcomes in support of urban resilience. Notably, our scaled-up
simulation shows that the resulting policies consistently emphasize
resilience-oriented projects while maintaining overall urban viability
through decentralized self-play, forming context-aware, adaptive
strategies. The gamified environment serves not only as a learning
environment for agents but also as an interface for human engagement,
supporting human-in-the-loop experimentation, which builds a foundation
for explainable, interactive simulation with a focus on transforming how
urban policy is prototyped and negotiated in future AI-augmented
decision-making.

Several limitations remain. First, the reliance on an orthogonal grid
restricts the accurate representation of irregular urban block
structures. Second, the scoring system is based on predefined objective
indices, which may oversimplify or misrepresent the nuanced trade-offs
inherent in real-world decision-making. Third, reinforcement learning
depends heavily on trial-and-error exploration, resulting in relatively
low training efficiency and potential instability or convergence
challenges when scaling to larger environments and action spaces. We
leave formal equilibrium tests for future work. Finally, although the
framework provides an interactive interface that allows humans to
observe and engage with the decision-making process, it has not yet been
evaluated through full-scale human participant studies.

Looking ahead, future work will focus on scaling the environment and
agent population to support more complex and realistic conditions.
Another direction is to integrate large language models to support
human-interpretable reasoning processes and structured chains of
thought, incorporating real-life common sense and its influence on
complex decision-making issues. Last but not least, the framework will
be applied to real-world planning scenarios with human participants to
explore its applicability in an AI-driven, participatory design process.

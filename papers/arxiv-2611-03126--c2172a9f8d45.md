###### Abstract

Despite recent advances in CUAs, their development remains constrained
by the limited diversity, controllability, and reliability of
interaction data. Moreover, conventional optimization pipelines lack
iterative feedback, while existing recursive improvement approaches
often rely on self-generated experiences, limiting ability expansion and
yielding diminishing returns. To address these challenges, we introduce
CUA-Master, a nested double-loop recursive improvement framework. At the
system level, an outer world-data-model flywheel continuously
incorporates open-world corpus and leverages evolving model weaknesses
to guide subsequent data construction and optimization iterations. At
the data level, an inner flywheel couples open-world seeding and
autocatalytic propagation. The former mines authentic workflows as seed
tasks from instructional videos, and employs visual-semantic dual-path
trajectory reconstruction; The latter transforms these seeds into
reusable primitives for controllable synthesis of diverse, complex, and
verifiable tasks. To enable efficient and reliable trajectory
collection, we further introduce a feedback-guided snapshot-rollback
retry mechanism in various real environments. Extensive experiments
demonstrate that CUA-Master-27B achieves competitive performance across
GUI-only and GUI-CLI hybrid benchmarks, scoring 80.2 on OSWorld
Verified, 36.9 on OSWorld 2.0, 43.9 on WeaveBench, and 25.0 on Agents’
Last Exam. Our framework also substantially improves the quality and
efficiency of data synthesis, and exhibits continuous gains across
recursive iterations.

## 1 Introduction

Computer-use agents (CUAs) have emerged as a promising paradigm for
automating real-world workflows across diverse software applications,
and unlocking new levels of digital productivity. Recent advances in
both foundation models \[[37](#bib.bib37), [30](#bib.bib30),
[47](#bib.bib47), [40](#bib.bib40), [32](#bib.bib32), [3](#bib.bib3)\]
and specialized agents \[[51](#bib.bib51), [49](#bib.bib49),
[17](#bib.bib17), [28](#bib.bib28), [9](#bib.bib9)\] have substantially
improved their capabilities not only grounding of computer scenarios,
but also in interactive reasoning and long-horizon execution. Besides,
early CUAs operate predominantly via graphical user interfaces (GUIs),
while subsequent works extend their capability scope by incorporating
command-line interfaces (CLIs) \[[8](#bib.bib8), [24](#bib.bib24),
[45](#bib.bib45), [42](#bib.bib42)\] and extensible tools
\[[16](#bib.bib16), [19](#bib.bib19)\]. Nevertheless, developing
powerful CUAs capable of reliably executing diverse, long-horizon, and
cross-application workflows remains challenging.

A fundamental bottleneck lies in how data construction and model
optimization are coupled and recursively improved. Many existing
pipelines follow a unidirectional paradigm of data generation followed
by model training \[[51](#bib.bib51), [49](#bib.bib49),
[28](#bib.bib28), [18](#bib.bib18)\], where data construction is
insufficiently informed by the evolving weaknesses. Consequently,
additional data may provide lopsided benefits without addressing
persistent capability gaps. Recent recursive improvement approaches
partially address this issue by incorporating iteratively self-generated
data \[[50](#bib.bib50), [33](#bib.bib33), [75](#bib.bib75)\]. However,
their exploration remains closed-world optimization, where such cycles
still predominantly rely on the own knowledge and capabilities of the
model itself. It is bounded by existing capabilities, potentially
reinforcing distributional biases, and yielding diminishing marginal
returns. These observations fundamentally motivate us to build a
recursive system that continuously acquires new open-world knowledge,
and systematically expands the performance boundary of the whole system.

Beyond system-level limitations, a more concrete concern is the
availability of large-scale, high-quality data that adequately captures
the diversity and complexity of real-world trajectories. While
human-annotated trajectories provide high-quality supervision, their
substantial annotation costs and limited application coverage make
large-scale collection impractical \[[51](#bib.bib51), [20](#bib.bib20),
[25](#bib.bib25), [50](#bib.bib50), [13](#bib.bib13)\]. Therefore,
synthetic data generation emerges as a promising alternative
\[[44](#bib.bib44), [60](#bib.bib60), [39](#bib.bib39),
[21](#bib.bib21), [41](#bib.bib41)\]. However, existing approaches face
three intertwined challenges. First, diversity: tasks derived from
restricted and similar sources often exhibit substantial homogeneity,
leading to insufficient ability coverage and fast improvement saturation
as data volume increases. Second, controllability: direct instruction
rewriting and template-based generation offer limited control over
operational abilities, complexity, and task dependencies, particularly
for long-horizon and cross-application workflows. Third, reliability:
scaling the collection of verified trajectories across diverse
real-world environments becomes increasingly challenging as task
complexity grows, leading to lower rollout success rates and frequent
execution failures. Collectively, these limitations constrain not only
the quantity of synthetic data, but also its effective coverage of the
capabilities required by general-purpose CUAs.

To address these limitations, we introduce CUA-Master, a nested
double-loop recursive framework that integrates an outer
world-data-model flywheel with an inner data flywheel. At the system
level, the outer flywheel closes the loop among open-world exploration,
data construction, and model optimization. By leveraging the evolving
model’s capability gaps to guide subsequent exploration and data
generation, while continuously incorporating external knowledge, it
addresses both the unidirectionality of conventional training pipelines
and the limited knowledge expansion of closed-world self-improvement. At
the data level, the inner flywheel couples open-world seeding and
autocatalytic propagation as two complementary driving forces.
Open-world seeding mines diverse seed tasks from real-world
instructional videos through hierarchical retrieval and filtering, and
reconstructs their trajectories via a visual-semantic dual-path
mechanism. The latter abstracts existing seeds into reusable primitives
and composes them into verifiable tasks, with explicit control over task
complexity and application scope. To improve rollout reliability and
efficiency, we introduce a feedback-guided snapshot-rollback retry
mechanism that preserves valid execution prefixes and recovers from
localized failures without restarting entire rollouts.

Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of CUA-Master. With
only 27B parameters, it achieves scores of 80.2 on OSWorld Verified,
36.9 on OSWorld 2.0, 43.9 on WeaveBench, and 25.0 on Agents’ Last Exam,
demonstrating competitive performance approaching markedly larger or
closed-source models and strong execution efficiency. Our double-loop
flywheel also substantially improves the diversity and coverage of data
distribution, reliability and controllability of synthesis, and
progressive improvements across iterations.

## 2 Methodology

### 2.1 Preliminary

#### 2.1.1 Task Definition

We consider interactive computer-use tasks where a general or
CUA-targeted agent executes natural-language instructions through
sequential interactions with computer environments. Formally, each task
is defined as:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{V}),
       ```                                                 |     | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{I}`$ denotes the task instruction, $`\mathcal{E}`$
represents the interactive environment, and $`\mathcal{V}`$ is a
task-specific verifier that determines whether the intended objective
has been successfully achieved.

During execution, at each decision step $`s`$, the agent receives an
observation $`o_{s}`$ from the environment of the current environment
state and generates a sequence of one or more actions:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \mathbf{a}_{s}\sim\Pi(\cdot|\mathcal{I},o_{s},h_{s}),
       ```                                                    |     | (2) |

where $`\Pi`$ denotes the agent policy, $`h_{s}`$ represents the
preceding interaction history, and
$`\mathbf{a}_{s}=(a_{s}^{(1)},\ldots,a_{s}^{(L_{s})})`$ denotes the
predicted atomic action sequence of length $`L_{s}`$. When $`L_{s}=1`$,
the model performs single action, while $`L_{s}>1`$ denotes a batched
action sequence. Executing an action batch $`\mathbf{a}_{s}`$ updates
the environment state and produces the subsequent observation
$`o_{s+1}`$. This iterative process continues until the agent terminates
or reaches step budgets, resulting in the complete trajectory:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{X}=\left\{o_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,o_{S},\mathbf{a}_{S},o_{S+1}\right\},
``` |  | (3) |

where $`S`$ indicates the total number of execution steps and
corresponding observations. Finally, the task-specific verifier
$`\mathcal{V}`$ evaluates whether the execution outcome satisfies the
objective specified by $`\mathcal{I}`$, thereby providing an assessment
of task completion.

#### 2.1.2 Hybrid Action Space

We adopt a unified atomic action space that supports both direct GUI,
e.g., computer.click/computer.scroll, and structured CLI operations
shell.exec. Each atomic action is represented as $`a`$ by its action
type and a dictionary containing its arguments. As listed in Table
[7](#A5.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"),
the action space is partitioned into GUI and CLI actions, where the
former consists of atomic mouse and keyboard operations, while the
latter executes structured shell commands. This pattern allows a unified
execution interface and can be interleaved within a trajectory, allowing
the agent to select the action most appropriate for the current
environment state. The environment returns observations $`o`$ after
execution to capture the action outcome, thus, GUI interactions are
grounded through screenshots, while CLI interactions are grounded
through standardized shell outputs containing the command output, error
stream, and execution status. All pointer coordinates are represented in
a resolution-independent normalized coordinate system:
$`x,y\in[0,1000]`$. In addition, our hybrid action space is also
compatible with additional tool.call, affording flexible tool invocation
capabilities.

#### 2.1.3 Environment Infrastructure

Our rollout infrastructure is a backend-agnostic, profile-driven
orchestration layer for reproducible CUA tasks across Linux, Windows,
and MacOS. We construct the VM pool through a semi-automated assembly
pipeline. First, we manually prepare a set of base images spanning the
application combinations defined in Table
[9](#A5.T9 "Table 9 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements").
We then programmatically derive additional variants by perturbing these
images, e.g., by removing randomly selected applications and
reorganizing desktop layouts, thereby expanding environment coverage at
low marginal cost. Each generated image is registered in a catalog and
accompanied by a descriptor recording its operating-system type and
version, and application inventory, including version and package
metadata.

To better manage and allocate CPUs and other resources, we employ a
global resource-aware scheduler that bounds concurrency by occupied VM
slots, while standardized lifecycle hooks manage resource acquisition,
controller readiness, network tunneling, environment reset, and cleanup.
During rollout, each episode is instantiated from a versioned VM image
catalog, typically using an immutable base image with a disposable
writable overlay. The writable overlay accommodates harness
bootstrapping (Codex, Claude Code, OpenClaw, etc.), task-specific
initialization, and transient application states. This infrastructure
supports over 100 different CUA environments and allows over 2,000
concurrently running VMs, substantially increasing the richness,
diversity, and efficiency of large-scale rollout.

### 2.2 Recursive Flywheel

#### 2.2.1 Data Flywheel

As illustrated by the black dashed box in Figure
[2(a)](#S2.F2.sf1 "Figure 2(a) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 2.1.3 Environment Infrastructure ‣ 2.1 Preliminary ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"),
we formulate our methodology as a recursive flywheel driven by two
complementary forces. Open-world seeding (Section
[2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Open-world Seeding ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"))
continuously injects real-world diversity and operational knowledge by
discovering underrepresented applications, operations, and workflows,
mitigating the risks of closed-loop degeneration associated with purely
self-generated data \[[11](#bib.bib11), [43](#bib.bib43),
[73](#bib.bib73)\]. Autocatalytic propagation (Section
[2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 Autocatalytic Propagation ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"))
transforms these real-world seeds into reusable primitives, enabling
composable task synthesis and reliable trajectory collection in
executable environments. Together, these two forces establish a
self-reinforcing cycle in which open-world seeding provides external
knowledge and diversity and forms the necessary foundation, while
autocatalytic propagation systematically amplifies its coverage,
complexity, and scale.

#### 2.2.2 World-Data-Model Flywheel

Beyond the inner data flywheel, we establish a broader world-data-model
flywheel. Starting from the open world, diverse applications,
operations, and workflows are distilled and expanded into executable
tasks and trajectories, providing grounded supervision for model
training. As the model evolves, its emerging capabilities and remaining
limitations provide feedback to guide subsequent open-world exploration,
directing data acquisition and scaling toward underrepresented workflows
and increasingly challenging tasks. Through this iterative cycle of
world exploration, data construction, model improvement, and
feedback-driven re-exploration, the agent progressively expands its
capability frontier.

### 2.3 Open-world Seeding

As discussed above, one of the most prominent limitations lies in the
insufficient richness of tasks. Open-world video resources, e.g.,
YouTube and TikTok, contain a wealth of software demonstration videos,
which can be leveraged to enrich task diversity. However, their
supervision is mostly implicit, temporally diffuse, and often
contaminated by non-operational content. We therefore introduce an
open-world seeding pipeline that distills instructional video into both
real-world seed task $`\mathcal{T}^{seed}`$ and corresponding trajectory
$`\mathcal{X}^{seed}`$.

Compared to previous works \[[57](#bib.bib57), [27](#bib.bib27),
[71](#bib.bib71)\], our key insight is to regard this process not merely
as direct trajectory generation, but also as seed task collection and a
foundation for further propagation. Besides, we introduce a
visual-semantic dual-path reconstruction mechanism to substantially
improve the accuracy of trajectory reconstruction.

#### 2.3.1 Corpus Harvesting

We organize the open-world search space using a hierarchical application
taxonomy as listed in Figure
[2(b)](#S2.F2.sf2 "Figure 2(b) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 2.1.3 Environment Infrastructure ‣ 2.1 Preliminary ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"),
[2(c)](#S2.F2.sf3 "Figure 2(c) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 2.1.3 Environment Infrastructure ‣ 2.1 Preliminary ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"),
and Table
[9](#A5.T9 "Table 9 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"),
including 8 main categories, 36 subdomains, and over 650 applications;
and a set of operation-oriented keywords, e.g., “tutorial”, “how to”,
“guide”, etc. For every application, we instantiate compositional
queries to retrieve videos from the open-world internet video databases,
namely YouTube, TikTok, and Bilibili. To remove low-quality and
unpromising candidates, all retrieved videos are first deduplicated by
source identity and normalized title, followed by metadata and noise
filtering. Specifically, metadata gates enforce admissible video
duration, upload recency, source availability, and taxonomy-consistent
application attribution; a lightweight noise filter further suppresses
promotional, entertainment, language-learning, and other incidental
mentions of software that do not demonstrate CUA interactions.

To further filter unsuitable candidates, the audio of uniformly sampled
frames $`\left\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\right\}`$ are labeled by the
Whisper-Large-v3 model through Faster-Whisper \[[38](#bib.bib38)\] with
timestamped transcript and taxonomy metadata as
$`\left\{\hat{f}_{1},\ldots,\hat{f}_{n}\right\}`$. Then, we use an
annotator $`\mathcal{A}`$, normally a more powerful model, as the score
evaluator (details in Figure
[11](#A5.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"))
to assign an overall CUA suitability score $`\sigma\in\{0,\ldots,5\}`$
by assessing whether the video presents a legible computer-use
interaction and a reconstructible step-by-step interaction trajectory.
It additionally predicts CUA presence, trajectory availability, and
visual clarity, providing structured signals for subsequent corpus
curation.

#### 2.3.2 Seed Task Mining

A tutorial video may contain multiple workflows interleaved with non-CUA
fragments that are not suitable for task collection. We therefore
formulate seed task mining as multimodal temporal episode discovery.
Given metadata, the timestamped transcript, and uniformly sampled
overview frames $`\left\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\right\}`$, we use an
annotator $`\mathcal{A}`$ as task generator (details in Figure
[12](#A5.F12 "Figure 12 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"))
to decompose and segment independent tasks from a complete video:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left\{\hat{f}_{1},\ldots,\hat{f}_{n}\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{A}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left\{\left(t^{s}_{i},t^{e}_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{M},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`M`$ denotes the number of segmented tasks, and $`t^{s}`$ and
$`t^{e}`$ are the start and end time stamps of the segmented task,
retaining only observable computer-operation state-continuous intervals
and excluding introductions, advertisements, talking-head commentary,
slide-only explanations, and other non-operational content.
Consequently, a single tutorial video can yield multiple independently
executable seed tasks. Each episode is assigned a concise goal inferred
jointly from its visual context, narration, application identity, and
demonstrated state transition. This transforms a coarse video-level
corpus into task-level supervision.

#### 2.3.3 Dual-path Trajectory Reconstruction

Given each mined seed task and its temporal scope, we reconstruct its
trajectory through a visual-semantic dual-path trigger, which is
low-cost and efficient, and recheck mechanism to refine annotations.
Specifically, the semantic path first decomposes each timestamped
transcript segment into atomic clauses according to discourse
connectives and temporal markers, e.g., “then”, “next”, and “after
that”. It further identifies operation-bearing clauses through action
expressions, such as “click”, “press”, “scroll”, and “drag”, as semantic
triggers:
$`\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{sem}}=\left\{t_{1}^{sem},\dots,t_{m}^{sem}\right\}`$.
In parallel, the visual path is activated by FFmpeg package when
frame-level appearance-change scores exceed a predefined threshold as
visual triggers:
$`\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{vis}}=\left\{t_{1}^{vis},\dots,t_{m}^{vis}\right\}`$,
indicating a potentially meaningful interface transition, such as a menu
opening, dialog appearance, page navigation, or content update. We
temporally detect the overlap of the two triggers into a boundary
lattice, and obtain the final trigger candidates:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}=\left\{\left(t_{i}^{\text{sem }},t_{j}^{\text{vis }}\right)\middle|\lvert t_{i}^{sem}-t_{j}^{vis}\rvert\leq T\wedge t_{i}^{sem}\in\mathcal{C}^{sem}\wedge t_{j}^{vis}\in\mathcal{C}^{vis}\right\},
``` |  | (5) |

where $`T`$ denotes the predefined temporal window, judging whether two
intervals are overlapped to trigger.

The annotator $`\mathcal{A}`$ as action rechecker jointly examines the
local transcript context and paired pre- and post-action frames to
refine each candidate (details in Figure
[13](#A5.F13 "Figure 13 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements")),
which rejects non-operational changes, and optimizes the action
boundaries by adding, removing, splitting, or merging candidates.
Accepted candidates are then processed by $`\mathcal{A}`$ as action
annotator into CUA actions in batches (details in Figure
[14](#A5.F14 "Figure 14 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements")),
outputting interaction coordinates, typed content, and other necessary
keys. Finally, we use $`\mathcal{A}`$ as trajectory verifier (details in
Figure
[15](#A5.F15 "Figure 15 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements")),
to remove duplicated actions, split sequences at substantial temporal or
semantic discontinuities, and retain qualified trajectories.

### 2.4 Autocatalytic Propagation

Open-world seeding introduces diverse real-world workflows, but the
resulting seed tasks provide limited coverage of the combinatorial space
of computer-use operations. Moreover, although annotation-verified,
trajectories reconstructed from instructional videos may contain
annotation noise and do not necessarily correspond to real executions in
the target environments. Moreover, existing CUA task and trajectory
synthesis methods often rely on rigid generation or rewriting
strategies, potentially leading to homogeneous outputs
\[[28](#bib.bib28), [61](#bib.bib61), [49](#bib.bib49),
[7](#bib.bib7)\]. Other lines might suffer from unverifiable synthesis
\[[21](#bib.bib21), [55](#bib.bib55), [44](#bib.bib44)\], and poorly
reliable rollout pipeline \[[25](#bib.bib25), [23](#bib.bib23),
[61](#bib.bib61)\], limiting the potential to systematically expand
diverse and increasingly flexible workflows across applications.

Therefore, we introduce autocatalytic propagation, which successively
performs primitivization, composable task synthesis, and resilient
trajectory collection. Our key insight is to treat the real-world rich
application operations as composable primitives, which can be flexibly
combined to generate tasks under fine-grained control, e.g., single- or
cross-application, and task complexity. Besides, the resilient
trajectory collection framework with feedback-guided snapshot-rollback
retry mechanism notably enhances the success rate and efficiency of
rollouts.

#### 2.4.1 Primitive-Based Composable Task Synthesis

Directly generating or rewriting seed instructions provides limited
control and quality over task semantics and potentially results in
homogeneous tasks when scaling up. We instead primitivize each seed task
$`\mathcal{T}`$ and its associated trajectory $`\mathcal{X}`$ into
reusable, application-level ability primitive sequence, using the
annotator $`\mathcal{A}`$ as primitive extractor (details in Figure
[16](#A5.F16 "Figure 16 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements")),
which is represented as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left(\mathcal{T}^{seed},\mathcal{X}^{seed}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{A}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\right\},\operatorname{where}\;p_{i}=\left(u_{i},a_{i},\mathcal{P}_{i},\mathcal{E}_{i},\mathcal{V}_{i}\right),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`n`$ denotes the number of generated primitives, each of which is
a five-tuple comprising the associated application $`u`$, the reusable
ability $`a`$, execution preconditions $`\mathcal{P}`$, environment
requirements $`\mathcal{E}`$, and verifier specification
$`\mathcal{V}`$, to formulate the operational dependencies required for
execution and verification. Here, we follow and further improve the
strategies from \[[52](#bib.bib52), [28](#bib.bib28)\] to automatically
build corresponding $`\mathcal{E}`$ and $`\mathcal{V}`$ for each
primitive. We aggregate the extracted primitives into an
application-indexed primitive library $`\mathcal{B}`$. Semantically
equivalent primitives are canonicalized and deduplicated, while variants
with distinct preconditions, environment requirements, or verification
logic are retained. This representation aims to abstract away
task-specific entities while preserving the operational dependencies
necessary for reliable execution, enabling a primitive to be
instantiated with different files, content, interface states, and
application configurations.

Task synthesis is formulated as constrained primitive composition. Given
a target complexity budget $`K`$ and an application set
$`\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\right\}`$, task complexity can be explicitly
controlled along two dimensions: the number of composed primitives $`K`$
and the number of involved applications $`N`$, rather than through
unconstrained linguistic rewriting. For each compatible composition, we
use the annotator $`\mathcal{A}`$ to instantiate a synthetic task
$`\mathcal{T}^{syn}`$ (details in Figure
[17](#A5.F17 "Figure 17 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"))
with a coherent instruction $`\mathcal{I}^{syn}`$. It simultaneously
composes their environment requirements
$`\left\{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{K}\right\}`$ into an
executable environment specification $`\mathcal{E}^{syn}`$ and
consolidates the primitive-level verifier specifications
$`\left\{\mathcal{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{K}\right\}`$ into a
task-level verifier $`\mathcal{V}^{syn}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left(\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{N}\right\}\right),K\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{A}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{T}^{syn}=\left(\mathcal{I}^{syn},\mathcal{E}^{syn},\mathcal{V}^{syn}\right),
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\mathcal{B}\left(\cdot\right)`$ provides the primitives of
selected applications. When composing primitives, we first check
compatibility of their preconditions $`\mathcal{P}_{i}`$ and environment
requirements $`\mathcal{E}_{i}`$. Primitives with conflicting
preconditions or incompatible environment states cannot be composed
together. The resulting synthetic precondition is the conjunction of
compatible primitive preconditions. When $`N=1`$, a single-application
task is generated, whereas $`N>1`$ yields a cross-application task.
Based on the selection strategy, we include high-quality task
$`\mathcal{T}^{syn-hq}`$ into a task set for particular model training
stage.

#### 2.4.2 Resilient Trajectory Collection

Simply discarding a failed rollout and restarting from scratch is
inefficient, since a failure is often caused by a localized misoperation
while most of the preceding trajectory remains valid. Besides, even
successful trajectories may contain redundant and unnecessary actions,
repeat observations, and verbose reasoning, introducing unnecessary
execution overhead and inefficient supervision. We therefore introduce a
feedback-guided snapshot-rollback retry mechanism. For each arbitrary
task $`\mathcal{T}`$, the environment infrastructure (Section
[2.1.3](#S2.SS1.SSS3 "2.1.3 Environment Infrastructure ‣ 2.1 Preliminary ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"))
first materializes an environment by selecting a compatible image from
the VM pool and initializes necessary configurations to rollout
trajectory. When verification fails or inefficiency is detected, the
annotator $`\mathcal{A}`$ as detector (details in Figure
[18](#A5.F18 "Figure 18 ‣ Appendix E Limitations ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"))
jointly examines the verifier residuals $`\mathcal{V}`$, collected
trajectory $`\mathcal{X}`$, and executable environment $`\mathcal{E}`$,
thus the feedback is generated as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\left(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{A}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left(\hat{s},\mathcal{F}\right),
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\hat{s}`$ estimates the earliest state-divergence step, and
$`\mathcal{F}`$ produces execution-grounded feedback. The feedback
describes the observed failures or inefficiency, without exposing a
privileged action solution, alleviating potential distribution mismatch
\[[29](#bib.bib29), [67](#bib.bib67)\].

Subsequently, we restore the environment to the latest valid snapshot
preceding $`\hat{s}`$. Inspired and further modified by
\[[6](#bib.bib6)\], rather than replaying whole history from the initial
snapshot, we create incremental snapshots at stable boundaries. Each
snapshot stores the VM state together with a lightweight state
signature, including active applications, window layout, relevant files,
and controller metadata. The agent policy $`\Pi`$ preserves the stable
interaction prefix, discards the undesirable suffix, and resumes
execution conditioned on the diagnostic feedback as hints. This process
continues until $`\mathcal{V}`$ reports pass or a predefined retry
budget is exhausted. Only the successful and the most efficient branch
is retained as the final trajectory $`\mathcal{X}`$, while failed or
inefficient suffixes are excluded to avoid introducing
state-inconsistent supervision. Thus, retries form a lightweight
branching search that reuses successful prefixes and significantly
enhances the efficiency of large-scale rollouts.

### 2.5 Model Training

#### 2.5.1 Recipe

As discussed in Section
[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Recursive Flywheel ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"),
we integrate model training into the recursive world-data-model
flywheel, where each iteration comprises a complete model optimization.
Specifically, preceding iterations employ SFT to progressively acquire
diverse knowledge and capabilities. In each iteration, based on the
in-house assessment of model, including domain- and application-level
hierarchical failure cases and rollout success rates analyses, we
identify the gaps and limitations of the current policy, guiding the
next iteration of adaptive data scaling and the CUA ability frontier.

As capability gains from SFT gradually saturate, we transition to RL in
the final iteration to further enhance the task-level capability upper
limit. As discussed, the synthesized tasks are built upon controllable
capability primitives, which renders native support for difficulty-level
categorization. Combining this with task generation quality and rollout
success rates, we curate a high-quality task collection for RL training.

#### 2.5.2 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

At the $`i`$th SFT iteration turn, we fine-tune the policy on verified
trajectories
$`\bigcup_{t=1}^{i}\left(\mathcal{X}^{seed}_{t}\cup\mathcal{X}^{syn}_{t}\right)`$
collected through both open-world seeding and autocatalytic propagation.
Training follows an autoregressive objective over executable action
sequences, conditioned on task instructions and interaction histories.
Following previous CUA works \[[26](#bib.bib26), [46](#bib.bib46),
[61](#bib.bib61)\], to avoid potential optimization drift, each
iteration of SFT is trained starting from the same base checkpoint, with
the updated data from each iteration added to the original training set.

#### 2.5.3 Reinforcement Learning (RL)

To break through the capability plateau, we further optimize the
SFT-trained policy on the high-quality synthetic tasks (Section
[2.4.1](#S2.SS4.SSS1 "2.4.1 Primitive-Based Composable Task Synthesis ‣ 2.4 Autocatalytic Propagation ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"))
through RL in real executable computer environments. Here, we mainly
employ and optimize the efficient online RL framework for CUAs
\[[28](#bib.bib28)\]. Leveraging the task-specific verifiers established
during task synthesis, we derive outcome-based rewards directly from
execution results, without additional manual reward annotation. This
final stage builds upon broad operational competence and focuses on
improving long-horizon decision-making, execution robustness, and task
completion.

## 3 Experiments

### 3.1 Settings

#### 3.1.1 Baselines

We compare CUA-Master against a diverse set of generalist and specialist
models. The generalist baselines include open-source models:
Qwen-3.5-27B/-397B-A17B \[[35](#bib.bib35)\], Qwen-3.6-27B
\[[36](#bib.bib36)\], Qwen-3.8-27B/Max \[[37](#bib.bib37)\],
Minimax-M3-428B-A23B \[[30](#bib.bib30)\], Kimi-K2.6-1T-A32B
\[[31](#bib.bib31)\], and Kimi-K3-2.8T-A104B \[[47](#bib.bib47)\], as
well as proprietary models: Seed-2.1-Pro \[[40](#bib.bib40)\],
GPT-5.6-Luna/Sol \[[32](#bib.bib32)\], and Claude-Sonnet/Opus-5
\[[4](#bib.bib4), [3](#bib.bib3)\]. For specialist baselines, we
consider OpenCUA-72B \[[51](#bib.bib51)\], GUI-Owl-1.5-32B
\[[58](#bib.bib58)\], EvoCUA-1.5-32B \[[17](#bib.bib17)\], ScaleCUA-9B
\[[28](#bib.bib28)\], Qwen-CUA-397B-A17B \[[26](#bib.bib26)\], and
UI-Mate-27B \[[9](#bib.bib9)\], which are specifically developed for
computer-use tasks. This comparison enables us to evaluate CUA-Master
against both general foundation and dedicated CUA models.

For fair comparison, we re-evaluate all baseline models under a
consistent benchmark protocol, using the same environment
configurations, action interfaces, and evaluation criteria, unless
otherwise specified.

#### 3.1.2 Benchmarks

We primarily evaluate CUA-Master on four challenging CUA benchmarks,
including two GUI-only benchmarks: OSWorld Verified
\[[56](#bib.bib56)\], OSWorld 2.0 \[[69](#bib.bib69)\], and two under a
hybrid GUI+CLI setting: WeaveBench \[[24](#bib.bib24)\], and Agents’
Last Exam \[[45](#bib.bib45)\]. Besides, we additionally evaluate three
cross-platform benchmarks: WindowsAgentArena \[[5](#bib.bib5)\],
MacOSWorld \[[64](#bib.bib64)\], and MacAgentBench \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]
, to assess cross-platform generalization. For Agents’ Last Exam, we
calculate the full-pass rate, while reporting average scores for the
other three benchmarks. We report four metrics averaged across tasks:
the number of execution steps (Step), total token consumption (Token),
wall-clock execution time (Time), and task performance score (Score).
Here, we follow the setting of task runtime computation in
\[[2](#bib.bib2)\], reflecting both the model time and the real
execution time under the specified deployment configurations, but still
influenced by deployment scheme and API speed. Higher scores indicate
better task performance, while lower steps, token consumption, and
execution time reflect greater execution efficiency.

#### 3.1.3 Implementations

For training, all experiments are conducted on 16 nodes, each with 8
NVIDIA-H200-141GB GPUs. Initialized from Qwen-3.5-27B
\[[35](#bib.bib35)\], we first perform SFT (Section
[2.5.2](#S2.SS5.SSS2 "2.5.2 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) ‣ 2.5 Model Training ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements")),
adopting AdamW optimization with a peak learning rate of
$`5\times 10^{-6}`$, a cosine schedule, a warmup ratio of 0.03, and a
batch size of 128. For RL training, we follow the efficient online RL
framework of ScaleCUA \[[28](#bib.bib28)\], a modified GRPO-style
algorithm for CUAs, with frontier sampling and visual context
segmentation. For each task, we sample 8 trajectories in real VMs
(Section
[2.1.3](#S2.SS1.SSS3 "2.1.3 Environment Infrastructure ‣ 2.1 Preliminary ‣ 2 Methodology ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"))
to estimate advantages from verifier rewards. We use AdamW with a
learning rate of $`1\times 10^{-6}`$ and a batch size of 64,
corresponding to up to 512 sampled trajectories per batch.

For each SFT iteration, which trained on a cumulatively expanded dataset
from the same initial checkpoint, we include 63K, 38K, 14K tasks and
corresponding trajectories respectively, thus the total training data at
the last SFT iteration is about 115K; while we use about 12K
high-quality tasks for RL stage. More details of training data is
reported in Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Details of Training Data ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements").

For inference, to allow sufficient exploration in CUA tasks, we keep the
temperature of most models to 1, except the models with other
recommended settings. We deploy all open-weight models with 1-node (for
models under 100B parameters) or 2-node (for models between 100B and 1T
parameters) schemes; each node is equipped with 8 NVIDIA H200-141GB
GPUs, and the inference is optimized based on vLLM \[[22](#bib.bib22)\].
And we use 6-node optimized deployment scheme for K3. For closed-source
models, we evaluate them by directly calling their official APIs.

#### 3.3.3 Analysis of World-data-model Flywheel

We analyze the effectiveness of our world-data-model flywheel from two
perspectives. As shown in Figure

the model from each training iteration as the annotator to generate data

are relatively limited. These results highlight the effectiveness of our
flywheel in establishing a positive feedback loop that progressively
enhances computer-use capabilities, particularly on challenging
long-horizon tasks.

## 4 Conclusion

In this work, we introduce CUA-Master, a nested double-loop recursive
framework that advances CUA systems beyond unidirectional data pipelines
and closed-world self-improvement. At the system level, the
world-data-model flywheel continuously incorporates open-world knowledge
and leverages evolving model weaknesses to guide further iterations. At
the data level, we establish a complementary flywheel that couples
open-world seeding with autocatalytic propagation, which integrates
visual-semantic trajectory reconstruction, primitive-based task
composition, and feedback-guided snapshot-rollback retries to enable
diverse, controllable, and reliable data synthesis. Extensive
experiments demonstrate that CUA-Master-27B achieves competitive
performance across GUI-only and GUI-CLI hybrid benchmarks, with strong
execution efficiency and consistent improvements across recursive
iterations. These results highlight the potential of our method for
developing increasingly general and capable CUA.

## Appendix A Related Works

### A.1 CUA Specialists

Research on CUAs has progressed from visual grounding to autonomous
interaction and long-horizon task execution. Early works, including
OS-Atlas \[[54](#bib.bib54)\], Tong-UI \[[71](#bib.bib71)\], and
CogAgent \[[15](#bib.bib15)\], establish foundational GUI perception and
grounding capabilities. Building upon these advances, Aguvis
\[[59](#bib.bib59)\], UI-TARS-2 \[[34](#bib.bib34)\], Agent S
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\], and MAI-UI \[[74](#bib.bib74)\] integrate visual
understanding, reasoning, and action planning for autonomous GUI
interaction. To further improve performance in complex environments,
OpenCUA \[[51](#bib.bib51)\], CUA-Gym \[[49](#bib.bib49)\], EvoCUA-1.5
\[[17](#bib.bib17)\], ScaleCUA \[[28](#bib.bib28)\], UI-Mate
\[[9](#bib.bib9)\], and UI-Venus-2 \[[48](#bib.bib48)\] advance reliable
data synthesis and specialized training paradigms. More recently,
research has expanded beyond GUI-only interaction toward hybrid action
spaces. UI-TARS-2 \[[50](#bib.bib50)\] incorporates terminal and
file-system operations, while ToolCUA \[[16](#bib.bib16)\] and HybridCUA
\[[8](#bib.bib8)\] coordinate GUI actions with structured tools and
command-line interfaces. Collectively, these advances mark a shift from
fine-grained visual grounding toward scalable, practical, and reliable
CUAs.

### A.2 Scalable Data Synthesis

Scalable data synthesis has emerged as a key paradigm for overcoming the
scarcity of high-quality computer-use trajectories. One line of research
leverages naturally occurring interaction data and instructional
resources. OpenCUA \[[51](#bib.bib51)\], WildGUI \[[57](#bib.bib57)\],
and VideoCUA \[[20](#bib.bib20)\] exploit real-world interaction traces
or videos, while AgentTrek \[[60](#bib.bib60)\] and TongUI
\[[71](#bib.bib71)\] transform web tutorials into agent supervision.
Another line synthesizes tasks and trajectories through environment
interaction. OS-Genesis \[[44](#bib.bib44)\] adopts reverse task
synthesis, whereas AutoPlay \[[39](#bib.bib39)\] discovers executable
tasks through environment exploration. CUA-Gym \[[49](#bib.bib49)\],
EvoCUA \[[61](#bib.bib61)\], and ScaleCUA \[[28](#bib.bib28)\] further
scale supervision through verifiable task synthesis, large-scale
environment interaction, and iterative policy optimization. Despite
these advances, jointly achieving real-world diversity, controllable
task complexity, reliable verification, and efficient execution remains
challenging. Our work unifies open-world workflow acquisition with
primitive-based compositional synthesis, enabling systematic expansion
from authentic operational knowledge to diverse, verifiable, and
increasingly complex computer-use tasks.

### A.3 Recursive Improvements

Recursive improvement aims to expand agent capabilities through
iterative data generation, experience refinement, and policy
optimization. Early approaches such as Re-ReST \[[11](#bib.bib11)\] and
Agent-R \[[70](#bib.bib70)\] exploit environmental feedback and
iterative refinement to improve agent trajectories. Moving toward
adaptive task generation, WebRL \[[33](#bib.bib33)\] introduces
self-evolving curricula driven by failed executions, and
Self-Challenging Agents \[[75](#bib.bib75)\] jointly develop task
generation and policy learning through self-generated, verifiable
challenges. WebEvolver \[[12](#bib.bib12)\] further couples agent
improvement with an evolving world model. Nevertheless, recursive
training may suffer from distributional narrowing, accumulated synthetic
errors, and diminishing task novelty \[[11](#bib.bib11),
[43](#bib.bib43), [73](#bib.bib73)\]. In contrast, our world-data-model
flywheel anchors recursive improvement in continuously refreshed
real-world knowledge. Open-world seeding introduces external diversity,
compositional propagation expands task coverage and difficulty, and
model feedback directs subsequent exploration and optimization, forming
a grounded and adaptive cycle of capability expansion.

## Appendix B Details of Training Data

Our training data are constructed through open-world seeding and
autocatalytic propagation, yielding a dataset of 127K samples (115K for
SFT training and 12K for RL training). To ensure diverse application
coverage, we organize data construction around a hierarchical taxonomy
encompassing 8 domains, 36 subdomains, and over 650 applications. As
illustrated in Figure
[10(a)](#A1.F10.sf1 "Figure 10(a) ‣ Figure 10 ‣ A.3 Recursive Improvements ‣ Appendix A Related Works ‣ CUA-Master: Scaling Computer-use Agents through Open-World Recursive Improvements"),

## Appendix E Limitations

Despite its effectiveness, CUA-Master still has two main limitations.
First, the recursive improvement pipeline incurs substantial
computational and infrastructure costs, particularly from large-scale
environment interactions, trajectory collection, and iterative model
optimization. Although our snapshot-rollback mechanism improves rollout
efficiency, it requires large-scale extra support for the deployment of
VMs, leading to challenges for infrastructure. Second, the upper limit
of the quality of synthesized data relies on capable annotator models,
with stronger annotators consistently yielding greater performance
gains. This dependence introduces additional inference costs and may
constrain the accessibility of the framework when strong annotators are
unavailable.

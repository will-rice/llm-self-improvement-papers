###### Abstract

Research on LLMs continually uncovers model limitations, their causes,
and potential solutions. Yet these human discoveries remain largely
disconnected from model evolution: an LLM does not automatically learn
from new research about its own failures. We introduce Paper2LLM++, a
framework for _continual self-evolution of LLMs from research papers_.
Rather than treating papers merely as knowledge to retrieve, Paper2LLM++
uses the growing literature as a stream of evidence and supervision for
model improvement. For each incoming paper, it extracts
evidence-grounded findings, tests whether the reported limitation
persists in the current model, and, when needed, converts the findings
into candidate learning signals. A try–evaluate–commit procedure
integrates an update only when it improves the targeted behavior without
substantially forgetting prior improvements or degrading general
capabilities. Across a sequential stream of research-discovered LLM
failures, we show that models can progressively incorporate new findings
while retaining earlier gains. Paper2LLM++ thus takes a step toward
closing the loop between _human discovery_ and _model evolution_,
enabling models to continually learn from research about their own
limitations and improvements.¹¹ 1 Data & Code will be released upon
publication.

## 1 Introduction

A long-standing goal of AI is to build models that _continue improving
after deployment_: recognizing what they still need to learn, acquiring
new capabilities, and retaining what they have already learned. Existing
approaches enable models to generate their own training data, refine
outputs, or adapt from new inputs ([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib1);
[Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib2); [Zweiger et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)),
but a fundamental question remains: where should the supervision for
continual self-evolution come from? We argue that scientific papers
provide a natural and continually growing source. Research on LLMs
constantly discovers new failure modes, identifies when and why they
occur, and develops methods for evaluating or mitigating them. Such
findings therefore provide more than knowledge to retrieve: they offer
evidence about _what a model should learn next_. The challenge is to
turn this evidence into model-specific, persistent improvements.

We introduce Paper2LLM++, a framework for continual self-evolution of
LLMs from research papers. For each incoming paper, Paper2LLM++ extracts
evidence-grounded findings about a reported limitation and first
verifies whether the limitation still exists in the current model. If
so, it translates the finding into complementary forms of synthetic
supervision (_i.e.,_ direct, contrastive, robustness, and process
supervision) without training on the paper’s evaluation benchmark.
Because neither every paper nor every generated supervision signal is
necessarily useful, Paper2LLM++ employs a _try–evaluate–commit_
procedure that accepts an update only when it improves the targeted
behavior while preserving previously acquired improvements and general
capabilities. This creates a continual loop from _human discovery_ to
_model verification, learning, and evolution_.

We evaluate Paper2LLM++ on 30 failure-revealing research papers spanning
seven failure families, using Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct ([Grattafiori et
al., 2024](#bib.bib4)) and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ([Yang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib5)). In individual-paper experiments, 24 of 30 papers
produce positive held-out gains. Across continual paper streams,
Paper2LLM++ consistently improves the targeted behaviors while
outperforming prior self-evolving and continual learning approaches in
preserving previously acquired improvements and general capabilities.
Beyond aggregate performance, our analysis reveals three conditions that
determine whether research findings become useful learning signals: they
must be teachable, meaning they can be translated into concrete and
verifiable supervision; needed, meaning the reported limitation still
exists in the current model; and retained, meaning the resulting
improvement survives subsequent updates. These findings suggest that
effective self-evolution requires not simply learning from more
research, but selectively determining _what is worth learning, how to
learn it, and whether it remains learned_.

Overall, our contributions are threefold:

- •
  We are the first to formulate paper-driven LLM self-evolution,
  treating scientific discoveries about model limitations as learning
  supervision for continual model improvement.
- •
  We propose Paper2LLM++, closing the loop from discovery to
  improvement, enabling evidence extraction, failure verification,
  supervision construction, and continual adaptation.
- •
  Across 30 papers, seven failure families, and two LLMs, we demonstrate
  continual improvement and identify teachability, need, and retention
  as key conditions governing when scientific findings can drive
  effective model self-evolution.

## 2 Related Work

#### Continual and self-evolving LLMs.

Recent work enables LLMs to continually improve from different forms of
incoming experience. SEAL ([Zweiger et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)) learns to
convert new passages or task demonstrations into synthetic supervision
for parameter updates, but provides limited protection against
forgetting. Recurrent-KIF ([Feng et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)) and RLEdit
([Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib7)) instead assume that supervision is
already available: the former continually learns from labeled task
datasets using replay and importance-based parameter fusion, while the
latter applies a sequence of explicit knowledge edits through RL-trained
parameter updates with memory and locality constraints. Other approaches
evolve the system without modifying the underlying LLM parameters. Gödel
Agent ([Yin et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)) and Darwin Gödel Machine ([Zhang
et al., 2026a](#bib.bib9)) use environmental or benchmark feedback to
iteratively improve agent code, tools, and workflows, preserving useful
solutions through previous implementations or an agent archive. ACE
([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib10)) and LSE ([Chen et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib11)) learn from sequential task experience and feedback
by evolving external context, with ACE incrementally maintaining a
reusable playbook and LSE retaining and branching previous contexts
through an evolution tree. These approaches therefore primarily study
how to continually learn from _given data, tasks, edits, or interaction
feedback_, and how to retain the resulting improvements. In contrast,
our Paper2LLM++ studies a distinct source of continual learning: a
stream of _scientific papers_. This introduces an upstream challenge
absent from conventional continual learning: a paper does not directly
specify training examples or rewards, but reports findings about model
behavior. Paper2LLM++ must therefore determine _what_ the paper teaches,
whether the reported limitation is still present in the current model,
translate applicable findings into supervision, and only then
incorporate and retain the resulting improvement.

#### Research papers as external knowledge signals.

Recent benchmarks treat papers as sources of new knowledge, but
primarily specify or evaluate update targets rather than provide an
executable procedure for model improvement. ScienceMeter ([Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)) derives scientific claim evaluations from curated
papers to measure acquisition, retention, and generalization. DocMEdit
([Zeng et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)) supplies paired documents containing
predefined factual changes, while WikiBigEdit ([Thede et al.,
2025](#bib.bib14)) extracts update targets from Wikidata snapshot
differences; both leave the actual update procedure to the evaluated
method. Our setting instead asks whether new research papers in stream
can be converted into executable supervision, verified against an
observed model failure, and incorporated without erasing previously
acquired gains.

## 3 Problem Formulation

We study how an LLM can _continually improve from newly published
research about LLM failures_. Consider a model $`M_{t-1}`$ that receives
a stream of papers $`P_{t},P_{t+1},\ldots,`$ where each paper reports an
LLM limitation, analyzes when or why it occurs, and may suggest
mitigation strategies.

Our goal is to use each paper as a source of model-maintenance
supervision. A paper, however, is not itself an executable update. The
reported failure may already be absent from $`M_{t-1}`$; the paper
usually does not provide a training set tailored to $`M_{t-1}`$; and an
update that addresses the new failure may interfere with previously
improved or general capabilities. This gives rise to three challenges
that Paper2LLM++ addresses:

- •
  $`\mathcal{C}_{1}`$: Is the reported failure relevant to the current
  model? A paper reports failures of the models it studied, but
  $`M_{t-1}`$ may behave differently. We must first assess whether, and
  to what extent, the paper-defined failure manifests in $`M_{t-1}`$.
- •
  $`\mathcal{C}_{2}`$: How can a failure-discovering paper be turned
  into executable supervision? If the failure is present, we must
  identify what it teaches, such as the failed behavior, its trigger,
  and any proposed explanation or mitigation, and compile this
  information into synthetic supervision $`\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{t}`$
  without training on the evaluation examples.
- •
  $`\mathcal{C}_{3}`$: How can continual self-evolution preserve
  previously learned capabilities and general capabilities? As papers
  arrive sequentially, learning from new evidence may overwrite
  capabilities acquired from earlier papers or degrade the model’s
  general capabilities. Each update must integrate new supervision while
  preserving prior gains and general model performance.

## 4 Method: Paper2LLM++

We describe Paper2LLM++ (illustrated in
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers"))
to address the three challenges: $`\mathcal{C}_{1}`$ by extracting
supervision and confirming the reported issue; $`\mathcal{C}_{2}`$ by
generating synthetic supervision data; and $`\mathcal{C}_{3}`$ by
updating the LLM while maintaining previously learned and general
capabilities.

### 4.1 Extracting Supervision and Verifying Reported Failures

#### Extract evidence from the paper.

Given paper $`P_{t}`$, we use gpt-oss-120b ([OpenAI, 2025](#bib.bib15))
²² 2 Temp. $`0`$, top-$`p=1.0`$, 16,384/2,048 input/output tokens. to
extract a structured record

|     |                                    |                                                               |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{t}=(`$ | $`\displaystyle\textsc{What},\textsc{Trigger},\textsc{Why},`$ |     |
|     |                                    | $`\displaystyle\textsc{Mitigation},\textsc{Evaluation}).`$    |     |

where What: the behavior or capability reported to fail, such as “Models
that correctly solve GSM8K seed questions often fail on minimally
perturbed versions of the same problems, producing an accuracy gap of up
to 20%.” [Li et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib16) (we use this paper as a running
example below); Trigger: the condition under which the failure appears,
such as “adding operation”, “reversing operations” and “distractor
insertion”. Why: the paper’s own explanation of the failure, when
explicitly stated. For example “often overlook steps or make errors in
the middle stages of the solution”; Mitigation: a proposed solution,
such as “compositional prompting can help.” Evaluation: the dataset
$`D_{t}`$, and evaluation protocol used to assess the failure.

Each field consists of one or more supporting text spans extracted
directly from the paper. If a paper does not provide a field (especially
Why or Mitigation), we store N/A rather than infer one. Examples above
are truncated for brevity; a complete evidence-grounded example is
provided in
[Appendix A.5](#A1.SS5 "A.5 Evidence-Grounded Extraction Example ‣ Appendix A CATEGORY 1:Replication Details ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers").

#### Construct the detection and held-out partitions once.

The benchmark $`D_{t}`$ (further split into $`D_{t}^{\mathrm{dev}}`$ and
$`D_{t}^{\mathrm{test}}`$ by 30/70) associated with the paper $`P_{t}`$
is used for two different purposes: $`D_{t}^{\mathrm{dev}}`$ is the only
partition used to decide whether the current LLM checkpoint exhibits the
paper-defined failure, whereas $`D_{t}^{\mathrm{test}}`$ is reserved for
final assessment. Thus:
$`D_{t}=D_{t}^{\mathrm{dev}}\cup D_{t}^{\mathrm{test}}.`$

#### Verify whether the paper-reported failure exists in the current LLM.

This step is different from simply measuring how often the current LLM
$`M_{t-1}`$ makes mistakes: the goal is to re-assess the _behavioral
effect_ reported by the paper using its original evaluation protocol.
Concretely, we run $`M_{t-1}`$ on the detection split
$`D_{t}^{\mathrm{dev}}`$ and reproduce the comparison used by $`P_{t}`$.
For papers that report degradation between two matched conditions, each
detection instance consists of a pair $`(x_{i},\tilde{x}_{i})`$, where
$`x_{i}`$ is the original instance and $`\tilde{x}_{i}`$ is the
corresponding instance containing the failure-triggering change
identified by the paper. We measure

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\Delta_{t}=\operatorname{Score}(M_{t-1},\{x_{i}\})-\operatorname{Score}(M_{t-1},\{\tilde{x}_{i}\}),
``` |  |

using the evaluator specified by $`P_{t}`$. A positive $`\Delta_{t}`$
therefore means that the paper-defined trigger degrades the current
model’s behavior.

For each paper, we follow its original evaluation protocol to reproduce
the reported failure; matched pairs are used only when they are part of
that protocol. For example, [Li et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib16) pair
original GSM8K problems with controlled variations, allowing us to test
whether the variations cause a performance drop. We bootstrap the
evaluation units to estimate the uncertainty of $`\Delta_{t}`$ and
obtain a 95% confidence interval. We confirm the failure only when this
interval is entirely above zero, indicating a performance drop under the
paper-defined failure condition. Otherwise, we perform No Update;
confirmed failures proceed to supervision.

### 4.2 Generating Paper-Inspired Learning Data

We do not train the LLM on the benchmark examples in $`D_{t}`$. Instead,
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)), as the data
generator, receives only $`\mathcal{R}_{t}`$, the structured evidence
extracted from the paper, and generates new examples that instantiate
the same behavioral gap. Examples from $`D_{t}`$ are never provided to
the generator.

The same research finding can be translated into different learning
signals, and it is not clear a priori which form is most effective. We
therefore generate up to four candidate supervision datasets. We
illustrate each using GSM-Plus ([Li et al., 2024a](#bib.bib16)), which
reports that LLMs can overlook a newly added condition in a math
problem.

• Direct supervision teaches the desired behavior directly. For example,
we generate the problem “6 boxes contain 12 pencils each, and 3 pencils
are removed from each box,” whose correct answer is $`54`$. This teaches
the model to incorporate the added condition rather than overlook it.

• Contrastive supervision teaches which changes should change the
answer. For example, we pair “6 boxes contain 12 pencils each” ($`72`$)
with “6 boxes contain 12 pencils each, but 3 pencils are removed from
each box” ($`54`$). The two problems differ only in the added condition,
making its effect on the answer explicit.

• Robustness supervision teaches which variation should *not* change the
answer. For example, we pair “3 pencils are removed from each box” with
its paraphrase “3 pencils are taken out of every box.” As the quantities
and logic are unchanged, both should retain the same answer ($`54`$).

• Process supervision teaches the intermediate reasoning implicated by
the paper. For the same example, the generated supervision explicitly
applies the added condition: “ *6$`\times`$12=72 $`\rightarrow`$
72-6$`\times`$3=54*”.

Not every paper supports all four forms of supervision. For example,
process supervision is generated only when the paper identifies a
concrete reasoning or verification error. We generate all applicable
candidates and determine which form improves the current model most.

### 4.3 Continual Self-Evolution

To decide which supervision should be used to evolve the current model,
we use a *try–evaluate–commit* procedure: each candidate is first tested
through a small temporary update, and *only a candidate that improves
the new behavior without substantially degrading previously acquired or
general capabilities is allowed to update the model*.

#### Try each candidate with a temporary update.

For each candidate supervision dataset $`\mathcal{D}_{t}^{(k)}`$, we
start from the same current model $`M_{t-1}`$ and perform a lightweight
LoRA update, producing a temporary model $`M_{t}^{(k)}`$. These updates
are used only for candidate selection and discarded after scoring.

#### Evaluate candidate supervision and update the model.

Each temporary model $`M_{t}^{(k)}`$ is evaluated along three
dimensions: i) New-behavior gain ($`G_{t}^{(k)}`$): improvement on the
behavioral gap identified by the current paper; ii) Previous-behavior
degradation ($`P_{t}^{(k)}`$): performance loss on behaviors acquired
from earlier papers; and iii) General-capability degradation
($`Q_{t}^{(k)}`$): performance loss on general LLM capabilities. We
therefore combine these signals as “utility ($`U`$)”

|     |                                                              |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       U_{t}^{(k)}=G_{t}^{(k)}-\lambda P_{t}^{(k)}-\mu Q_{t}^{(k)}.
       ```                                                           |     |

To prevent a candidate with high target gain but substantial regression
from being selected, we additionally require
$`G_{t}^{(k)}\geq\tau_{\mathrm{gain}}`$,
$`P_{t}^{(k)}\leq\tau_{\mathrm{prev}}`$, and
$`Q_{t}^{(k)}\leq\tau_{\mathrm{gen}}.`$ All five hyperparameters
$`(\lambda,\mu,\tau_{\mathrm{gain}},\tau_{\mathrm{prev}},\tau_{\mathrm{gen}})`$
are fixed globally before running the continual-learning experiments and
are shared across all papers and model checkpoints.

Among candidates that pass, we select the one with the highest utility.
If none passes, the model is left unchanged. Otherwise, we discard the
temporary update and retrain from $`M_{t-1}`$ on the selected
supervision using the full budget.

#### Preserve successful learning for future evolution.

After committing an update, we retain $`M_{t}`$ and update two
components. First, we add examples from the selected supervision
$`D_{t}^{(k)}`$ to the replay buffer $`\mathcal{B}_{t}`$. Future updates
mix new supervision with replay samples to reduce forgetting. Second, we
freeze up to 50 examples from $`D_{t}^{\mathrm{dev}}`$ as a protected
probe $`v_{t}`$. We sample without replacement using seed 42,
stratifying by task subtype or perturbation category when available and
sampling uniformly otherwise. If fewer than 50 examples are available,
we keep all of them. We then update the probe:

|     |                                                 |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{V}_{t}=\mathcal{V}_{t-1}\cup\{v_{t}\}.
       ```                                              |     |

These probes are never used for training or synthetic-data generation.
They remain fixed and are re-evaluated during later updates to detect
forgetting of previously learned behaviors. Without an update, the
states remain unchanged:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{t}=M_{t-1},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{t}=\mathcal{B}_{t-1},\qquad\mathcal{V}_{t}=\mathcal{V}_{t-1}.
``` |  |

## 5 Experiment Setup

We evaluate paper-driven LLM self-evolution under a shared setup that
measures target gains, retention, forgetting, and changes in general
capabilities.

### 5.1 Evaluation Datasets

#### Self-Evolution Paper Stream Datasets.

We construct an evaluation suite for paper-driven self-evolution from 30
papers that report concrete LLM failures and have corresponding
benchmarks for reproducing them. The resulting suite spans seven
families: mathematical robustness, coding and software, instruction
operators, knowledge and factuality, context grounding, structured
reasoning, and agentic reliability.
[Table 1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.2 Generating Paper-Inspired Learning Data ‣ 4 Method: Paper2LLM++ ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers")
summarizes each failure family, its high-level LLM failure pattern, and
all source papers and benchmarks.

#### General-Capability Evaluation Dataset.

General ability is measured on fixed 500-example subsets of
HellaSwag ([Zellers et al., 2019](#bib.bib46)), WinoGrande ([Sakaguchi
et al., 2020](#bib.bib47)), PIQA ([Bisk et al., 2020](#bib.bib48)),
BoolQ ([Clark et al., 2019](#bib.bib49)), and ARC-Challenge ([Clark et
al., 2018](#bib.bib50)). We report macro-average accuracy across these
five datasets (2,500 instances in total). These reporting subsets are
not used for training or update acceptance.

### 5.2 Paper2LLM++ & Baselines

Paper2LLM++ uses Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct ([Grattafiori et al.,
2024](#bib.bib4)) and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ([Yang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib5)). We use gpt-oss-120b ([OpenAI, 2025](#bib.bib15)) for
parsing and knowledge-type classification, and
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)) for synthetic data
generation.

Regarding baselines, we compare against recent continual-learning and
lifelong-editing methods under a unified paper-to-update protocol. All
methods process the same papers in the same order and are evaluated with
the same held-out procedure; we adapt only how each method consumes the
paper-derived signal. Full implementation details and hyperparameters
are provided in
Appendix [A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Training, Compute, and Baseline Adaptation ‣ Appendix A CATEGORY 1:Replication Details ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers").

- •
  Text-to-LoRA ([Charakorn et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)): we render each
  extracted paper record as the task description expected by the
  released hypernetwork, generate one adapter per paper, and merge
  adapters in paper-arrival order.
- •
  ELDER ([Li et al., 2025a](#bib.bib52)): we convert each paper’s fixed
  synthetic supervision into a sequence of edits and carry ELDER’s
  mixture-of-LoRA state and router across papers, so that one paper
  corresponds to one continual editing stage.
- •
  RLEdit ([Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib7)): after pre-training its editor
  on the authors’ original editing data, we apply the frozen editor to
  the paper-derived edit sets, treating each paper as an editing turn.
- •
  UltraEdit ([Gu et al., 2026](#bib.bib53)): we replace the editing
  stream with paper-derived supervision and treat each paper as an
  editing turn while retaining the released editor and update procedure.
- •
  StableEdit ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib54)): we use UltraEdit’s
  paper-to-edit conversion and carry its running statistics across
  papers, using general-domain replay only to initialize warm-up
  statistics.

### 5.3 Evaluation Setting

We evaluate self-evolution under two settings: i) individual learning,
where the LLM learns independently from a single paper; and ii)
continual learning, where the LLM sequentially learns from a stream of
papers. For continual learning, we consider both within-family streams,
containing papers from the same failure family, and cross-family
streams, containing papers across all seven families. The main-table
same-family summary reports the coding, structured-reasoning, and
agentic-reliability streams. For the continual settings, we report
results averaged over three shuffled stream orders and training seeds
$`\{13,21,42\}`$.

We report the following evaluation metrics. Let $`S_{i}(M)`$ be the
held-out score for paper $`i`$, on a 0–100 scale. Let $`M_{0}`$ denote
the base model, $`M_{i-1}`$ the checkpoint before learning paper $`i`$,
$`M_{i}`$ the checkpoint after its update decision, and $`M_{T}`$ the
final checkpoint. Define $`g_{i}=S_{i}(M_{i})-S_{i}(M_{i-1})`$.

- •
  Target gain measures improvement on the current paper’s benchmark
  relative to the initial model. For reporting, define
  $`G_{\mathrm{base}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}[S_{i}(M_{i})-S_{i}(M_{0})]`$.
  This measures improvement available when each paper is processed,
  including transfer from earlier updates.
  $`g_{i}=S_{i}(M_{i})-S_{i}(M_{i-1})`$ measures only the current
  update. They coincide for single papers.
- •
  Retention measures the fraction of a positive incremental gain that
  survives later updates. For $`\mathcal{I}_{+}=\{i:g_{i}>0\}`$,

  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  R=\frac{100}{|\mathcal{I}_{+}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{+}}\frac{S_{i}(M_{T})-S_{i}(M_{i-1})}{g_{i}+\epsilon},
  ``` |  |

  where $`\epsilon>0`$ stabilizes division. Retention is undefined when
  $`\mathcal{I}_{+}`$ is empty; high retention alone does not imply a
  large gain.
- •
  Forgetting measures the loss between each immediate post-update score
  and its final score:
  $`F=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}[S_{i}(M_{i})-S_{i}(M_{T})]`$. Lower is
  better; negative values indicate improvement.
- •
  General-capability change measures collateral effects:
  $`\Delta_{\mathrm{gen}}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{j=1}^{5}[A_{j}(M_{T})-A_{j}(M_{0})]`$,
  where $`A_{j}`$ is accuracy on general dataset $`j`$.

Gain, forgetting, and general change are reported in score points;
retention is a percentage of acquired gain, not an accuracy. Means and
standard deviations are computed across three orders and seeds.

#### Paper2LLM++ achieves a stronger gain–retention balance.

Across both base LLMs and both stream settings, Paper2LLM++ consistently
combines strong acquisition of new behaviors with high retention of
previously learned ones. For example, on the mixed stream,
Paper2LLM++ achieves gains of $`+7.1`$ and $`+7.5`$ on Llama and Qwen
while retaining $`83.7\%`$ and $`85.0\%`$ of acquired gains,
respectively. In comparison, RLEdit achieves gains of similar magnitude
($`+7.9`$ on Llama and $`+6.7`$ on Qwen) but substantially lower
retention ($`65.2\%`$ on Llama and $`71.1\%`$ on Qwen) and higher
forgetting. These results show that effective self-evolution requires
jointly considering knowledge acquisition and retention rather than
optimizing either in isolation. (Internal ablations:
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B CATEGORY 2:Complementary Results ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers"),
Tables [B](#A2 "Appendix B CATEGORY 2:Complementary Results ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers")–[B](#A2 "Appendix B CATEGORY 2:Complementary Results ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers").)

Longer, mixed-family streams show more forgetting. Moving from
within-family streams to the mixed 30-paper stream generally makes
continual learning more difficult. For most parameter-updating
baselines, retention decreases and forgetting increases as the stream
crosses failure families. For example, RLEdit’s forgetting increases
from $`3.1`$ to $`4.9`$ on Llama and from $`2.9`$ to $`4.5`$ on Qwen;
Text-To-LoRA shows a similar increase from $`4.2`$ to $`5.6`$ and from
$`3.6`$ to $`5.3`$, respectively. In contrast, Paper2LLM++ shows a
smaller increase, from $`1.8`$ to $`2.4`$ on Llama and from $`1.5`$ to
$`2.1`$ on Qwen, while maintaining strong gains. The mixed streams
differ in both family diversity and length, so these results do not
isolate a causal effect of heterogeneity. They show that
Paper2LLM++ limits forgetting in the longer, mixed-family setting.

Paper2LLM++ better preserves general capabilities. The mixed-stream
setting also evaluates whether continual self-evolution degrades general
LLM capabilities. Paper2LLM++ limits the general-capability change to
$`-0.4`$ on Llama and $`-0.5`$ on Qwen, compared with substantially
larger degradation for aggressive parameter-updating methods such as
RLEdit ($`-1.6`$ on Llama and $`-1.4`$ on Qwen) and Text-To-LoRA
($`-2.0`$ on Llama and $`-1.8`$ on Qwen). Thus, Paper2LLM++’s advantage
is not simply conservative updating: it achieves substantial continual
gains while limiting both forgetting and general-capability regression.

### 6.3 Analysis: When Does Learning from Research Papers Work?

The individual-paper and continual experiments establish that LLMs can
continually learn from research papers. We next ask a more fundamental
question: *when does a research finding actually provide a useful signal
for self-evolution?*

Our analysis suggests that successful paper-driven self-evolution
depends on three conditions. First, the finding must be teachable: it
must be possible to translate the reported insight into concrete
supervision that the model can learn from. Second, it must be needed:
the current model must still exhibit the behavior that the paper aims to
improve. Third, what is learned must be retained as subsequent papers
introduce additional updates.

#### $`\mathcal{Q}_{1}`$: When Is a Research Finding Teachable?

We first ask why some papers produce large self-evolution gains while
others produce little improvement. Before inspecting held-out outcomes,
we use gpt-oss-120b ([OpenAI, 2025](#bib.bib15)) to annotate each paper
by the *primary knowledge or behavior that must be installed to address
its reported failure*, rather than by its benchmark domain. The
fine-grained labels include reasoning patterns, boundaries, constraints,
verification strategies, retrieval or memory strategies, planning or
interaction strategies, algorithmic procedures, knowledge updates,
execution feedback, and safety policies. For the analysis in
[Figure 3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.3 Analysis: When Does Learning from Research Papers Work? ‣ 6 Experiment and Analysis ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers")(a),
semantically related labels are grouped into broader knowledge types.

[Figure 3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.3 Analysis: When Does Learning from Research Papers Work? ‣ 6 Experiment and Analysis ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers")(a)
shows that the answer depends strongly on what the paper asks the model
to learn. Findings involving *constraints, decision boundaries,
reasoning patterns, and verification strategies* generally produce
larger gains, whereas findings requiring *planning, retrieval, or
complex interaction* are substantially harder to acquire.

A common pattern behind these differences is whether the paper’s finding
can be turned into concrete training supervision. For example, if a
paper identifies that the model should change its answer under a
particular constraint, we can construct examples that demonstrate when
the behavior should change and automatically check whether the resulting
behavior is correct. In contrast, some findings require capabilities
that cannot be provided by input–output supervision alone, such as
searching for unavailable information, maintaining and updating an
external state, interacting with an environment, or executing and
verifying steps.

To describe this distinction, we use each paper’s existing supervision
closure annotation, a continuous measure of how readily its finding can
be converted into constructible and verifiable supervision.
[Appendix A.4](#A1.SS4.SSS0.Px2 "Supervision closure. ‣ A.4 Analysis Annotation Protocols ‣ Appendix A CATEGORY 1:Replication Details ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers")
provides a reproducible rubric over five dimensions: explicit target
behavior ($`c_{\mathrm{target}}`$), constructible supervision
($`c_{\mathrm{sup}}`$), automatic verification ($`c_{\mathrm{ver}}`$),
local evaluability ($`c_{\mathrm{local}}`$), and mechanism sufficiency
($`c_{\mathrm{mech}}`$). Each dimension is scored continuously in
$`[0,1]`$: a score of $`0`$ indicates that the property is absent or
cannot be operationalized from the paper, $`0.5`$ indicates partial or
conditional support, and $`1`$ indicates that the property is explicitly
and directly satisfied; intermediate values reflect evidence between
these anchors. Closure is then their unweighted mean:

|----|----|----|----|
|  |

The Appendix rubric provides a reproducible interpretation of the

[Figure 3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.3 Analysis: When Does Learning from Research Papers Work? ‣ 6 Experiment and Analysis ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers")(b)
shows a strong relationship between supervision closure and held-out
self-evolution gain ($`r=0.87`$). Papers whose findings can be expressed
as clear and verifiable learning signals tend to produce substantially
larger improvements. This association is descriptive; it does not
validate the scoring rubric or establish a causal effect.

*Importantly, requiring an additional mechanism does not automatically
make a finding unlearnable. What matters is whether that mechanism can
itself provide a training signal or whether it is the capability that is
missing.* For example, an external checker can make verification useful
for learning because it directly provides correctness feedback. By
contrast, when reliable verification is itself the behavior that the
model lacks, showing additional input–output examples may not be
sufficient. Similarly, selecting relevant evidence already present in
the context can be directly supervised, whereas learning to search for
evidence unavailable to the model requires an additional retrieval
process.

#### $`\mathcal{Q}_{2}`$: When Does the Current Model Need the Finding?

Teachability alone does not make a paper useful for self-evolution. Even
a finding that can be converted into high-quality supervision provides
little value if the current model already handles the reported behavior
correctly. We therefore distinguish what is new in the research
literature from what is still new to the current model.

We characterize semantic novelty by how different a paper-reported
failure is from earlier failure schemas in the stream, and behavioral
novelty by how strongly the current checkpoint still exhibits the
reported failure. Semantic novelty is computed as one minus the maximum
cosine similarity between the current failure-schema embedding and
earlier schema embeddings, while behavioral novelty is the paper-defined
failure severity min–max normalized to $`[0,1]`$. Full definitions are
provided in
[Appendix A.4](#A1.SS4.SSS0.Px3 "Semantic and behavioral novelty. ‣ A.4 Analysis Annotation Protocols ‣ Appendix A CATEGORY 1:Replication Details ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers").

The two forms of novelty capture different situations. A semantically
novel failure may provide little learning value if the current model
already handles it well. Conversely, a familiar failure can remain
useful when it exposes an unresolved weakness of the current checkpoint.
The largest gains arise when a paper provides non-redundant information
about behavior the model still needs to improve. This also motivates a
key design choice in Paper2LLM++: a new paper does not automatically
trigger an update. Paper2LLM++ first verifies whether the reported
behavioral gap remains in the current checkpoint and learns from the
paper only when there is a meaningful gap to address.

#### $`\mathcal{Q}_{3}`$: What Limits Self-Evolution as More Papers Arrive?

Two factors could limit long-horizon self-evolution. First, the model
may gradually *run out of useful learning signals*: later papers may
increasingly describe behaviors that the model already handles. Second,
useful new findings may continue to arrive, but learning them may
overwrite improvements acquired from earlier papers.

[Figure 5](#S6.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 𝒬_2: When Does the Current Model Need the Finding? ‣ 6.3 Analysis: When Does Learning from Research Papers Work? ‣ 6 Experiment and Analysis ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers")
provides evidence about the first possibility. The marginal gain from
each additional paper decreases from approximately 12 points early in
the stream to around 5 points near the 30th paper, but remains positive
throughout the studied stream. Thus, although additional papers provide
diminishing returns, useful learning signals do not appear exhausted at
this scale.

The continual-learning results in
[Table 2](#S6.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 6.1 Experiment I: Individual-Paper Self-Evolution ‣ 6 Experiment and Analysis ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers")
reveal a different challenge: preserving previously acquired behavior.
Parameter-updating methods can achieve gains on new findings while
losing part of earlier improvements, and this effect becomes more
pronounced in cross-family streams. Paper2LLM++ substantially reduces
this degradation, maintaining strong gains while achieving higher
retention and lower forgetting than competing parameter-updating
approaches across both base LLMs. This suggests that, at the scale
studied here, the central long-horizon challenge is not simply finding
new research signals, but accumulating them without overwriting prior
learning.

## 7 Conclusion

This work introduced Paper2LLM++, a framework for continual LLM
self-evolution from research papers. Across 30 papers and two LLMs, our
results show that research findings can drive persistent model
improvements, but not all findings are equally useful. Effective
self-evolution requires findings to be *teachable*, *needed*, and
*retained*. Our analysis further reveals an important boundary: some
failures can be addressed through generated supervision, while others
require additional mechanisms such as retrieval, planning, interaction,
or verification. Ultimately, scientific literature may serve not only as
a record of AI progress, but as a continual source of supervision
through which models themselves evolve.

## Appendix A CATEGORY 1:Replication Details

### A.1 Model Roles, Paper Input, and Data Isolation

#### Model roles.

Paper parsing and knowledge-type classification use
gpt-oss-120b ([OpenAI, 2025](#bib.bib15)). Synthetic supervision is
generated with Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)).
The target backbones are Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct.
The parser uses temperature $`0`$, top-$`p=1.0`$, and 16,384/2,048-token
input/output limits.

#### Paper input and preprocessing.

For each paper $`P_{t}`$, the parser extracts the paper-reported
behavior or capability failure, its trigger, an explanation and
mitigation when explicitly stated, and the evaluation protocol. The
source paper may contain benchmark descriptions, perturbation rules,
failure cases, or representative examples; these are part of the paper
evidence. Source-published examples are removed from the eligible
benchmark pool before the detection/held-out split. Human preprocessing
is restricted to locating benchmark implementations, standardizing file
formats, removing source-published examples from the eligible benchmark
pool, and mapping each paper to its evaluator; humans do not inspect
held-out examples when constructing supervision.

#### Detection and held-out partitions.

Each benchmark is divided once using a stratified 30/70 split with seed
42:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{t}=D_{t}^{\mathrm{dev}}\cup D_{t}^{\mathrm{test}},\qquad D_{t}^{\mathrm{dev}}\cap D_{t}^{\mathrm{test}}=\varnothing.
``` |  | (2) |

Stratification prioritizes benchmark metadata such as perturbation type,
variant, or position bucket when available and otherwise falls back to
evaluator type. The split is fixed across backbones, methods, candidate
forms, stream orders, and training seeds.

#### Held-out isolation.

$`D_{t}^{\mathrm{test}}`$ is inaccessible during paper parsing, failure
confirmation, synthetic-data generation and filtering, temporary LoRA
training, candidate selection, full training, replay construction, and
stream-order selection. Held-out scores are computed only after the
update decision has been finalized. The generator receives only the
extracted paper record $`\mathcal{R}_{t}`$ and never receives raw
questions, model responses, or gold answers from either benchmark
partition.

#### Contamination checks.

Generated examples are compared with both benchmark partitions using
exact MD5 matching and RapidFuzz string ratio $`\geq 97`$; the checker
returns only a rejection flag and never exposes benchmark content to the
generator. Within a candidate dataset, deduplication uses exact hashes
followed by MPNet embedding cosine similarity $`\geq 0.93`$.

### A.2 Failure Confirmation and Metric Details

#### Failure confirmation.

The current checkpoint is evaluated on $`D_{t}^{\mathrm{dev}}`$ using
the paper-defined comparison. For matched original/perturbed conditions,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{t}=\mathrm{Score}(M_{t-1},\{x_{i}\})-\mathrm{Score}(M_{t-1},\{\tilde{x}_{i}\}).
``` |  | (3) |

We bootstrap the paper-defined evaluation units to obtain a 95%
confidence interval and confirm the paper-defined failure only when this
interval lies entirely above zero. Otherwise, no update is attempted.

#### Bootstrap implementation.

We use $`B=2000`$ bootstrap replicates with random seed 42. The interval
is a 95% percentile interval: after sorting the 2000 bootstrap means, we
use the 50th and 1950th order statistics as the lower and upper
endpoints. For paired comparisons, one bootstrap unit is
$`100\times(\mathrm{base}-\mathrm{perturbed})`$ for one matched pair.
For unpaired accuracy-style evaluations, one unit is
$`100\times(1-\mathrm{correct})`$ for one example. Resampling is
performed over these paper-defined units.

#### Retention implementation.

For paper $`i`$, let $`g_{i}=S_{i}(M_{i})-S_{i}(M_{i-1})`$. Retention is
computed only for steps with $`g_{i}>0`$:

|     |                                                          |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{i}=\frac{S_{i}(M_{T})-S_{i}(M_{i-1})}{g_{i}+10^{-6}}.
       ```                                                       |     | (4) |

The ratio is not clipped. Steps with $`g_{i}\leq 0`$ are excluded from
the retention average, matching the main-text definition.

### A.3 Training, Compute, and Baseline Adaptation

#### Candidate generation.

For each confirmed paper, Paper2LLM++ constructs the applicable subset
of four candidate forms: direct, contrastive, robustness, and process
supervision. Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct uses temperature $`0.7`$ and
top-$`p=0.95`$. The standard generation target is 512 examples and the
compute-adjusted target is 320; generation first produces $`1.5\times`$
the target count to support filtering. A candidate is invalidated if
fewer than 128 examples survive. The update budget itself uses 128
accepted new examples together with 64 replay examples, so the larger
generation pool does not increase the number of new examples seen by an
update.

#### Temporary and full updates.

Each applicable candidate is trained from the same current checkpoint
using a disposable LoRA adapter and the same replay budget. Candidate
utility is

|     |                                                              |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       U_{t}^{(k)}=G_{t}^{(k)}-\lambda P_{t}^{(k)}-\mu Q_{t}^{(k)},
       ```                                                           |     | (5) |

subject to the globally fixed acceptance constraints

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{gathered}G_{t}^{(k)}\geq\tau_{\mathrm{gain}},\qquad P_{t}^{(k)}\leq\tau_{\mathrm{prev}},\\
Q_{t}^{(k)}\leq\tau_{\mathrm{gen}}.\end{gathered}
``` |  | (6) |

The selected supervision is retrained from $`M_{t-1}`$ with the full
update budget.

|                        |                        |                       |
|------------------------|------------------------|-----------------------|
| Setting                | Temporary              | Full                  |
| Precision              | bfloat16               | bfloat16              |
| Quantization           | none                   | none                  |

#### Replay and protected probes.

After a successful commit, 64 replay examples are mixed into each future
update. The replay buffer has capacity 600 and preserves a balanced
mixture of 60% prior paper-derived supervision, 20% matched clean-domain
examples, and 20% general-domain examples. Up to 50 examples from the
current $`D_{t}^{\mathrm{dev}}`$ are frozen as a protected probe,
sampled without replacement using seed 42 and stratified by subtype or
perturbation category when available. Protected probes are never used
for training or synthetic-data generation.

#### General audit and reporting sets.

Candidate acceptance uses a fixed general-audit set $`D^{\mathrm{aud}}`$
that is separate from the final reporting suite. Final general-benchmark
results are macro-averaged over fixed 500-example subsets of HellaSwag,
WinoGrande, PIQA, BoolQ, and ARC-Challenge (2,500 examples total); these
reporting subsets are not used for training or update acceptance.

#### Continual runs.

The mixed experiment uses three fixed 30-paper permutations generated
with order seeds 101, 202, and 303, paired with training seeds 13, 21,
and 42, respectively. Every method uses the same three order–seed pairs
and no order is selected or removed after observing results. Same-family
experiments use three fixed permutations for each of the coding,
structured-reasoning, and agentic-reliability streams.

#### Baseline adaptation under the shared paper stream.

All methods process the same paper order and are evaluated with the same
held-out procedure. Methods that consume example-level supervision
receive a fixed paper-derived set $`K_{t}`$ of $`N=128`$ input–target
pairs. Detection and held-out benchmark examples are never supplied to a
baseline. Method-specific pre-training is completed before the paper
stream and is accounted for separately from per-paper updates.

|  Method |  Adaptation to the paper stream |
|----|----|
|  Text-to-LoRA |  Render $`\mathcal{R}_{t}`$ as the task description; generate one adapter per paper and merge in arrival order. The Qwen2.5-7B hypernetwork is trained on disjoint task descriptions/adapters and frozen before evaluation. |
|  ELDER |  Convert $`K_{t}`$ into edits; one paper is one continual stage; carry mixture-of-LoRA state, router, guided loss, and deferral across papers. |
|  RLEdit |  Pre-train and freeze the editor on the authors’ original editing data, then apply it sequentially to paper-derived edit sets. |
|  UltraEdit |  Replace the edit stream with $`K_{t}`$; retain the released editor, sequential loop, layer choices, and step size. |
|  StableEdit |  Use the same paper-to-edit conversion as UltraEdit and preserve lifelong-normalization statistics across papers. |
|  Seq-LoRA |  Use the same LoRA configuration, 128 new examples, 64 replay examples, and 1,500-step full-update budget as Paper2LLM++, but update after every paper without failure detection, candidate probing/selection, or commit/rollback. |

Table 5: Adaptation of continual-learning and lifelong-editing baselines
to the common paper stream. The interface standardizes paper-derived
input exposure while preserving each method’s native update mechanism;
it does not equalize optimization compute.

### A.4 Analysis Annotation Protocols

The analysis-only annotations are created before inspecting held-out
outcomes and are never supplied to Paper2LLM++ as update features.

#### Knowledge type.

Before inspecting held-out outcomes, gpt-oss-120b assigns each paper the
primary knowledge or behavior that must be installed to address the
reported failure, rather than the benchmark domain. Fine-grained labels
include reasoning patterns, boundaries, constraints, verification
strategies, retrieval or memory strategies, planning or interaction
strategies, algorithmic procedures, knowledge updates, execution
feedback, and safety policies.

#### Supervision closure.

Closure is a continuous descriptive annotation of whether a reported
failure can be converted into executable and verifiable supervision.
Five components are scored continuously in $`[0,1]`$: explicit target
behavior, constructible supervision, automatic verification, local
evaluability, and mechanism sufficiency. A score of $`0`$ means the
property is absent or cannot be operationalized from the paper, $`0.5`$
indicates partial or conditional support, and $`1`$ means it is
explicitly and directly satisfied; intermediate values reflect evidence
between these anchors. The aggregate is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{t}=\frac{c_{\mathrm{target}}+c_{\mathrm{sup}}+c_{\mathrm{ver}}+c_{\mathrm{local}}+c_{\mathrm{mech}}}{5}.
``` |  | (7) |

These annotations are not supplied to Paper2LLM++ as update features.
For
Figure [3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.3 Analysis: When Does Learning from Research Papers Work? ‣ 6 Experiment and Analysis ‣ Paper2LLM++: Continual Self-Evolution of LLMs from Research Papers")(b),
semantically related fine-grained labels are merged into the broader
knowledge types used in panel (a), and the reported Pearson $`r=0.87`$
is computed from the grouped, unrounded analysis values. The Category 2
table reports raw paper-level annotations and is not the direct unit of
this correlation.

#### Semantic and behavioral novelty.

With $`\phi_{t}=\mathrm{Embed}(\mathrm{FailureSchema}(P_{t}))`$,
semantic novelty is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
N_{t}^{\mathrm{sem}}=1-\max_{i<t}\cos(\phi_{t},\phi_{i}),\qquad N_{1}^{\mathrm{sem}}=1.
``` |  | (8) |

If $`\delta_{t}`$ denotes the model-specific paper-defined failure
severity, behavioral novelty is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
N_{t}^{\mathrm{beh}}=\frac{\delta_{t}-\delta_{\min}}{\delta_{\max}-\delta_{\min}}.
``` |  | (9) |

Both quantities are used only for descriptive analysis and are not
inputs to the update procedure.

### A.5 Evidence-Grounded Extraction Example

What
Summary: Models that correctly solve GSM8K seed questions can fail on
minimally perturbed versions, with a gap up to 20%. Evidence: “Our
benchmark revealed a gap of up to 20% between the accuracy reported by
the current model and the accuracy observed in our setting, while human
performance remains unaffected due to the unchanged inherent difficulty
level of the questions.” (pp. 1–2)

Trigger
Summary: Failures are triggered by missing information, additional
operations, reversed reasoning, and distractors; adding an operation
causes a drop above 40% for most models except GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-Turbo.
Evidence: “Except GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-Turbo, most models, regardless of
size and output format, experience an accuracy drop exceeding 40%.”
(pp. 6–7)

Failure pattern
Summary: Errors include operator misuse, omitted conditions, and
incorrect use of irrelevant information. Evidence: “Example C.3 shows an
example of failure for GPT-3.5-Turbo, where it fails to consider one
condition …”; the condition “should be considered after step \[1\] and
before step \[2\].” (pp. 15–18)

Why
Summary: Models can lose intermediate constraints or make mistakes in
multi-step reasoning. Evidence: “We find that LLMs often overlook steps
or make errors in the middle stages of the solution.” (pp. 8–9)

Mitigation
Summary: Chain-of-thought, least-to-most, program-of-thought, and
compositional prompting help but do not eliminate the gap. Evidence:
“Although compositional prompting improves performance on seed questions
and their variations, it does not bridge the performance gap …”
(pp. 8–9)

Evaluation
Summary: The benchmark evaluates 25 LLMs under four prompting methods
and compares solved GSM8K seeds with GSM-Plus variations. Evidence: “Our
experiments on 25 LLMs and 4 prompting techniques show that … their
performances are far from robust.” (p. 1)

Data quality
Summary: Human validation remains necessary; annotators revised 18.85%
of GPT-4-generated variants. Evidence: “Overall, human annotators
revised 18.85% of the variations produced by GPT-4, highlighting the
importance of human revision.” (p. 4)

This example shows that each summarized field is tied to a concrete
source span rather than inferred from held-out benchmark outcomes.
````

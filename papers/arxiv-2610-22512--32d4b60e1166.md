###### Abstract

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) enables large language models (LLMs)
to learn from privileged information (PI) without a separate teacher
model. However, prior work largely treats PI as a fixed design choice,
leaving the effects of its content and construction poorly understood.
This challenge is particularly important in multi-turn agentic settings,
where trajectories contain actions, observations, and reasoning that can
be transformed into PI in many ways. We systematically evaluate 16 PI
configurations across three multi-turn agentic benchmarks and three
student models that vary in scale and reasoning mode. We find that PI
effectiveness depends on its content, generation source, and the student
model. Information-dense trajectories and PI produced by more capable
generators generally yield larger gains, whereas thinking models benefit
less consistently. To assess PI before training, we compare PI utility
with two KL-based metrics: sampled-token reverse KL ($`D`$) and
error-step KL contrast ($`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$). The KL-based
metrics track post-training performance more consistently than PI
utility, which can be distorted by information leakage. Empirically,
selecting each task’s PI by $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ also
improves post-training performance over random selection, most when the
PI candidates differ widely. Finally, effective PI remains beneficial
when combined with GRPO, allowing the combined objective to outperform a
strong GRPO baseline. Overall, our results provide practical guidance
for constructing and evaluating PI in OPSD training.

## 1 Introduction

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) enables LLMs to iteratively improve
by turning successful solutions, environment feedback, and experience
from their own interactions into training signals without a separate
teacher model \[[19](#bib.bib19), [35](#bib.bib35), [7](#bib.bib7),
[22](#bib.bib22)\]. Related self-distillation methods have already been
used to train coding agents such as Composer 2.5 and agents for
long-horizon professional tasks \[[4](#bib.bib4), [8](#bib.bib8)\]. In
OPSD, the model learns from its own trajectories by matching the same
model conditioned on PI, thereby internalizing information unavailable
at inference time.

The effectiveness of OPSD, however, depends on what the teacher is
allowed to see. In general, PI can take many forms, including
ground-truth answers \[[35](#bib.bib35)\], demonstrations from prior
tasks \[[22](#bib.bib22)\], environment feedback \[[7](#bib.bib7)\],
actions from successful trajectories \[[19](#bib.bib19)\], or hints
derived from gold solutions or trajectories, such as
summaries \[[19](#bib.bib19)\], skills \[[26](#bib.bib26)\], checklists,
structured criteria such as rubrics \[[20](#bib.bib20),
[6](#bib.bib6)\], and targeted feedback \[[33](#bib.bib33),
[4](#bib.bib4)\]. These choices determine both the information available
to the teacher and how that information changes its distribution
relative to the student. However, especially on multi-turn agentic
tasks, existing studies evaluate only a limited range of PI formats
across a small set of models and tasks \[[16](#bib.bib16),
[29](#bib.bib29), [26](#bib.bib26), [33](#bib.bib33),
[30](#bib.bib30)\]. It remains unclear what makes PI produce useful
learning signals for the student policy, whether the same choices work
across agentic domains and model types, and, critically, whether PI
quality can be assessed before training. Reliable offline metrics would
allow candidate PI formats and sources to be compared before training,
avoiding a separate costly OPSD run for every candidate and focusing
compute on the most promising PI.

Figure 1: Overview of the PI formats and offline metrics studied.

$`D`$ does not indicate whether the shift is beneficial. Error-step KL
contrast $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ measures whether that shift
targets the identified errors.

We study these questions through a systematic evaluation of OPSD on
three multi-turn agentic benchmarks: ALFWorld \[[24](#bib.bib24)\],
AppWorld \[[25](#bib.bib25)\], and
$`\tau^{2}`$-retail \[[2](#bib.bib2)\].
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
summarizes the PI formats and offline metrics evaluated in our study. We
cover 16 PI configurations spanning reference trajectories and self- and
expert-generated hints, including summaries, skills, and checklists. We
train Qwen3-4B-Instruct, Qwen3-4B-Thinking, and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct
\[[28](#bib.bib28)\], allowing us to compare PI effects across
instruction and thinking models and across model scales. Beyond
downstream performance, we evaluate three offline metrics: PI
utility \[[19](#bib.bib19)\] compares teacher reward with and without
PI, but can be distorted by information leakage; sampled-token reverse
KL $`D`$ measures the PI-induced probability shift on student-sampled
tokens; and error-step KL contrast $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$
measures whether PI suppresses tokens more at identified errors than at
matched steps from the same task. Together, these experiments ask not
only which PI works, but also what distinguishes useful PI from
information that merely makes the teacher more capable when PI is
present.

Our results show that PI effectiveness depends on its content, source,
and the student model. PI formats with information-dense trajectories
usually outperform compressed hints, and adding environment observations
consistently improves the Instruct students on ALFWorld and AppWorld.
Additional teacher reasoning is less reliable and can reduce the gains
of the thinking models. Among hint formats, skills are generally
strongest, suggesting that additional guidance, such as an analysis of
mistakes and reusable strategies, is more effective than a plain
trajectory summary. More capable generators also tend to produce more
effective PI, particularly for hint formats, showing that PI quality
depends on both its format and source.

Among the three offline metrics, error-step KL contrast better
distinguishes useful PI and improves per-task selection when PI
candidates vary in source or strength. Sampled-token reverse KL tracks
performance in eight of nine settings but does not capture the direction
of the shift, while PI utility is unreliable because of information
leakage
(Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2 What Do PI Metrics Reveal? ‣ 3 Results and Analysis ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
Our controlled evaluations on ALFWorld also show that PI utility can be
distorted by information leakage and that reverse KL can assign large
values to unhelpful shifts. Using the most effective PI format, we
further show that combining OPSD with GRPO can outperform a strong GRPO
baseline.

Our main contributions are:

- •
  Systematic study of PI for OPSD. To our knowledge, this is the first
  controlled study of PI construction for OPSD, spanning 16 PI
  configurations and three student models across three multi-turn
  agentic benchmarks.
- •
  Practical PI design recommendations. We identify how PI content,
  format, generation source, and student-model choice shape OPSD gains.
- •
  KL-based PI assessment and selection. We propose two offline KL
  diagnostics, evaluate what they measure through controlled studies,
  and show that error-step KL contrast can guide per-task PI selection
  before costly OPSD training.
- •
  Combining OPSD with GRPO. We show that the most effective PI enables
  combined OPSD and GRPO training to outperform matched GRPO baselines.

## 2 Analyzing Privileged Information for OPSD

### 2.1 On-Policy Self-Distillation and PI Construction

On-policy distillation (OPD) transfers knowledge from a teacher on
trajectories generated by the student, providing token-level supervision
on the states that the student actually visits \[[1](#bib.bib1),
[31](#bib.bib31)\]. OPSD instantiates this approach without a separate
teacher model: the student and teacher share parameters, but only the
teacher observes PI. Let $`\theta`$ denote their shared parameters,
$`I`$ the PI, and $`\pi_{\theta}^{S}`$ and $`\pi_{\theta}^{T}`$ the
student and PI-conditioned teacher policies. For a student trajectory
$`\tau=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L_{\tau}})`$, $`s_{t}`$ is the context before
token $`y_{t}`$, and $`L_{\tau}`$ is the trajectory length. OPSD
minimizes the reverse KL from the student to the PI-conditioned teacher:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}^{S}}\left[\frac{1}{L_{\tau}}\sum_{t=1}^{L_{\tau}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}^{S}(\cdot\mid s_{t})\,\|\,\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\pi_{\theta}^{T}(\cdot\mid s_{t},I)\right]\right)\right].
``` |  | (1) |

Here, $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}`$ compares the two next-token distributions, and
$`\operatorname{sg}`$ stops gradients through the teacher.

This formulation has been applied with ground-truth
answers \[[35](#bib.bib35)\], reflective environment
feedback \[[7](#bib.bib7)\], and information retained across
continual-learning tasks \[[22](#bib.bib22)\]. Because the two policies
differ only in access to PI, PI construction directly determines the
token-level training signal \[[19](#bib.bib19)\].

We study seven PI formats constructed using GLM-5.2 and Qwen3.7-Plus.
The formats comprise four trajectory variants and three types of hints.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
illustrates these formats and the overall OPSD pipeline.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A PI Construction ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
outlines the PI construction protocol.

##### Trajectories.

Following [Penaloza et al. \[19\]](#bib.bib19), we sample successful
reference trajectories from the expert models and retain different
components: actions only (*Action*), actions with environment
observations (*Action $`+`$ Obs.*), actions with reasoning (*Action
$`+`$ Thou.*), or actions, observations, and reasoning (*Action $`+`$
Obs. $`+`$ Thou.*). These variants isolate whether effective PI requires
only the expert’s actions, the environmental context surrounding those
actions, or the reasoning used to derive them.

##### Summary and Skill Hints.

Summary hints condense a reference trajectory into a brief description
of its solution \[[19](#bib.bib19)\]. Skill hints provide more detailed
guidance by combining high-level strategies, mistake analysis, and
reusable skills derived from the reference
trajectory \[[26](#bib.bib26)\]. We also use the student model itself to
generate both types of hints.

##### Checklist Hints.

We generate checklists from a successful reference and a failed student
trajectory, listing task requirements for the teacher. Like
rubric-conditioned self-distillation \[[20](#bib.bib20),
[6](#bib.bib6)\], this format provides structured criteria rather than a
target response.

### 2.2 Offline Metrics

Training a separate student for every PI candidate is computationally
expensive. We therefore study offline metrics that can assess PI
effectiveness before training and help identify what distinguishes PI
that produces better learning signals for the student policy.

Using the notation above, let $`R(\tau)\in\{0,1\}`$ denote whether
trajectory $`\tau`$ completes its task successfully. We define the
following metrics:

##### PI utility.

PI utility measures the improvement in task success obtained by exposing
the same model to PI:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
U(I)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}^{T}(\cdot\mid I)}[R(\tau)]-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}^{S}}[R(\tau)].
``` |  | (2) |

A positive value indicates that the teacher solves more tasks when
conditioned on PI, motivating the hypothesis that higher PI utility
leads to more effective OPSD training.

##### Sampled-token Reverse KL.

For a student trajectory $`\tau=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{L_{\tau}})`$, we
estimate the reverse KL from the sampled-token log-ratio, using its
absolute magnitude so that shifts in either direction count:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d_{I}(\tau)=\frac{1}{L_{\tau}}\sum_{t=1}^{L_{\tau}}\left|\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}^{S}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta}^{T}(y_{t}\mid s_{t},I)}\right|,\qquad y_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}^{S}(\cdot\mid s_{t}).
``` |  | (3) |

Intuitively, a larger reverse KL indicates that PI more strongly changes
the teacher’s predictions on student-visited states, providing a
stronger potential learning signal. We report
$`D(I)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}^{S}}[d_{I}(\tau)]`$ over the
sampled student trajectories.

##### Error-step KL Contrast ($`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$).

This metric measures whether PI suppresses tokens more strongly at
identified errors than at comparable steps in other rollouts. An agent
trajectory contains multiple interaction steps, each of which may
contain several generated tokens. For step $`j`$, let
$`\mathcal{T}_{j}(\tau)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,L_{\tau}\}`$ denote its token
indices and define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
d^{-}_{I}(\tau,j)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{j}(\tau)|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{j}(\tau)}\left[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}^{S}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta}^{T}(y_{t}\mid s_{t},I)}\right]_{+},\qquad[z]_{+}=\max(z,0).
``` |  | (4) |

Because KL can vary with step position, we subtract a task- and
position-matched baseline to reduce these effects. For each PI condition
$`I`$, task $`x`$, and absolute step index $`j`$, the baseline is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
b_{I}(x,j)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{x,j}|}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{x,j}}d^{-}_{I}(\tau^{\prime},j),
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\mathcal{R}_{x,j}`$ is the set of sampled trajectories for task
$`x`$ that reach step $`j`$. For each trajectory, a judge labels the set
$`E_{\tau}`$ of error step indices. With
$`\mathcal{E}_{F}=\{(\tau,j):R(\tau)=0,\ j\in E_{\tau}\}`$, we report

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}(I)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_{F}|}\sum_{(\tau,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{F}}\left[d^{-}_{I}(\tau,j)-b_{I}(x_{\tau},j)\right],
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\mathcal{E}_{F}`$ contains every flagged step from a failed
trajectory, $`x_{\tau}`$ is the task associated with $`\tau`$, and each
flagged step receives equal weight. Positive values indicate that PI
targets identified errors more strongly than matched positions within
the same task.

### 2.3 Experimental Setup

We evaluate OPSD on ALFWorld \[[24](#bib.bib24)\],
AppWorld \[[25](#bib.bib25)\], and
$`\tau^{2}`$-retail \[[2](#bib.bib2)\], using Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507,
Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507, and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 as students. We
implement OPSD in SkyRL \[[3](#bib.bib3)\] with the sampled-token
reverse-KL objective and report pass@1 from the best three-checkpoint
window.

Offline metrics are computed on training or development tasks disjoint
from the evaluation sets used to measure post-training performance.

and [C](#A3 "Appendix C Offline Metric Protocols ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")

##### Trajectory formats generally outperform hints, but detailed PI can hurt already-capable models.

Trajectory PI generally outperforms compressed hints
(Figures [2(a)](#S3.F2.sf1 "Figure 2(a) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Which PI Formats Work Best? ‣ 3 Results and Analysis ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")–[2(c)](#S3.F2.sf3 "Figure 2(c) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Which PI Formats Work Best? ‣ 3 Results and Analysis ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
Instruct models benefit further when trajectories include observations
and teacher thinking tokens, suggesting that additional context
strengthens the training signal. However, richer PI is not universally
beneficial for the thinking model: adding teacher thinking tokens to
Action $`+`$ Obs. hurts its performance on ALFWorld and AppWorld, while
on $`\tau^{2}`$, adding either observations or thinking tokens causes
even larger declines in
Figure [2(c)](#S3.F2.sf3 "Figure 2(c) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Which PI Formats Work Best? ‣ 3 Results and Analysis ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation").
Its high pass@4 of $`88.9`$ on $`\tau^{2}`$ suggests that it already
reasons effectively, leaving additional trajectory context less room to
help and more potential to interfere with its existing strategy.

##### Effective PI provides context beyond the correct solution.

Skills, which augment the solution with mistake analysis and reusable
strategies, are often the strongest hint format. They lead for all three
students on ALFWorld, both 4B students on AppWorld, and 4B-Instruct on
$`\tau^{2}`$. Together with the gains from adding environment
observations to trajectories, these results suggest that effective PI
should do more than reveal or summarize a correct solution: it should
provide context that helps the student understand its mistakes and how
to act successfully in the environment.

##### The PI source matters, especially when generating hints.

Figures [2(d)](#S3.F2.sf4 "Figure 2(d) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Which PI Formats Work Best? ‣ 3 Results and Analysis ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")–[2(f)](#S3.F2.sf6 "Figure 2(f) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Which PI Formats Work Best? ‣ 3 Results and Analysis ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
show that GLM-5.2 PI almost always outperforms Qwen3.7-Plus PI: for
4B-Instruct, GLM wins 20 of 21 comparisons, and its lead reaches 36.0
points on ALFWorld hints but stays within 3.2 points for trajectory
formats. Source choice thus matters most when trajectories are rewritten
into guidance: for trajectory PI, the source determines only the
reference behavior the teacher sees, whereas for hints the generator
also decides which information is kept and how it is expressed.

##### Self-generated hints yield positive performance gains.

For 4B-Instruct, both self-generated Summary and Skill hints improve
performance on all three benchmarks
(Figures [2(g)](#S3.F2.sf7 "Figure 2(g) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Which PI Formats Work Best? ‣ 3 Results and Analysis ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")–[2(i)](#S3.F2.sf9 "Figure 2(i) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Which PI Formats Work Best? ‣ 3 Results and Analysis ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
These results suggest that the student can act as its own hint generator
given the reference solution and still benefit from OPSD.

##### PI utility can be inflated by information leakage.

The PI utility measures the improvement in the success rate of a model
when conditioned on a given PI. We find that this metric can reward PI
that reveals answer-specific information unavailable to the student,
rather than PI that provides a transferable training signal. On
AppWorld, the utility metric correlates strongly with post-training
performance for all three students ($`\rho=0.81`$–$`0.96`$). The
relationship is less consistent on ALFWorld, where the correlation is
positive for 4B-Instruct ($`\rho=0.46`$) but negative for 4B-Thinking
and 30B-Instruct ($`\rho=-0.56`$ and $`-0.10`$), and on $`\tau^{2}`$,
where correlations range from $`-0.06`$ to $`0.71`$. On ALFWorld, the
PI-conditioned model can copy the reference trajectory’s final action
without reproducing the preceding interaction sequence. On $`\tau^{2}`$,
it can extract an answer from PI without executing the actions required
to update the environment’s database
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D How Information Leakage Distorts PI Utility ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
In these cases, utility measures the model’s ability to exploit leaked
solution information rather than how well the PI transfers through
training.

Takeaway. PI utility is unreliable when information leakage changes
evaluation behavior without providing transferable guidance, so higher
utility does not imply larger OPSD gains.

##### Larger distributional shifts usually accompany better performance.

We next examine $`D`$, the mean absolute sampled-token reverse KL
between the student and PI-conditioned teacher. It correlates positively
with post-training performance in eight of the nine student–benchmark
combinations ($`\rho=0.65`$–$`0.95`$). The exception is 4B-Thinking on
$`\tau^{2}`$, where the correlation is strongly negative
($`\rho=-0.84`$). Its pass@4 of $`88.9`$ suggests that a large shift can
overwrite useful behavior already present in the policy. Our PI
ablations point the same way: the student’s own failed rollouts induce a
larger $`D`$ than its successful ones, even though they make far worse
PI
(Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E PI Ablations ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
Thus, shift direction and location matter in addition to magnitude.

Takeaway. A larger $`D`$ generally indicates a stronger PI training
signal, but does not reveal whether the induced shift improves the
policy.

##### Error-step KL contrast consistently tracks post-training performance.

Across all nine student–benchmark combinations,
$`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ is positively correlated with
post-training performance ($`\rho=0.10`$–$`0.94`$). A larger contrast
means that PI more strongly suppresses the student’s sampled tokens at
judge-identified error steps than at comparable positions from the same
task. Thus, PI formats that concentrate the distributional shift at
critical mistakes tend to produce larger training gains. This
relationship is particularly strong for 4B-Instruct on AppWorld
($`\rho=0.94`$) and 30B-Instruct on ALFWorld ($`\rho=0.92`$). On
$`\tau^{2}`$, the contrast remains positively associated with
performance for 4B-Thinking ($`\rho=0.70`$), even though several
trajectory formats have negative values. These small or negative
contrasts and limited OPSD gains suggest poorly targeted supervision.
This interpretation is consistent with [Kaur et al. \[9\]](#bib.bib9),
who show that privileged distillation can penalize productive
reconsideration tokens in thinking models: supervision may
insufficiently suppress errors while disrupting useful behavior.

Takeaway. Effective PI concentrates token-level suppression on actual
mistakes rather than comparable non-error steps, yielding a larger
positive $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$.

## 4 How Well Does OPSD Combine with GRPO?

### 4.1 Combined Training Objective

Rather than optimizing over the many possible ways to combine
token-level distillation and GRPO outcome rewards \[[21](#bib.bib21)\],
we use a simple weighted objective to test whether PI identified as
effective under vanilla OPSD remains effective when combined with GRPO.
We use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 for all experiments in this section. We
combine the GRPO and OPSD signals at the advantage level:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A^{\mathrm{OPSD+GRPO}}=\lambda A^{\mathrm{GRPO}}+(1-\lambda)A^{\mathrm{OPSD}},
``` |  | (7) |

where $`A^{\mathrm{GRPO}}`$ is the outcome-based group-relative
advantage, $`A^{\mathrm{OPSD}}`$ is the token-level self-distillation
advantage, and $`\lambda\in[0,1]`$ controls their relative weight.

Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 How Well Does OPSD Combine with GRPO? ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
compares four values of $`\lambda`$ across three PI formats with matched
GRPO-only controls on ALFWorld, AppWorld, and $`\tau^{2}`$. The
$`\lambda=0`$ endpoint corresponds to vanilla OPSD.

### 4.2 Effect of Objective Weighting

##### PI quality persists: better PI is still better after adding GRPO.

Trajectory PI performs best under vanilla OPSD and remains strongest
across most values of $`\lambda`$, followed by Skill and Checklist PI.
This ordering is consistent through $`\lambda=0.99`$ on all benchmarks.
Thus, combining OPSD with outcome-based rewards does not eliminate
differences in PI quality: stronger PI continues to provide a more
effective auxiliary training signal.

Takeaway. The PI format that performs best under vanilla OPSD generally
remains the strongest after adding GRPO, so outcome rewards complement
rather than replace effective PI selection.

##### GRPO strengthens OPSD.

Increasing $`\lambda`$ generally improves performance for all three PI
formats, with the largest gains occurring for the weaker Checklist and
Skill formats on ALFWorld and AppWorld. On ALFWorld, several Skill and
Trajectory configurations exceed the GRPO-only baseline score, with
Trajectory PI reaching $`95.13`$ at $`\lambda=0.99`$ versus the baseline
score of $`84.96`$. On AppWorld, Trajectory PI at $`\lambda=0.99`$
reaches $`43.90`$, exceeding the GRPO score of $`42.91`$. Likewise, on
$`\tau^{2}`$, it reaches $`55.04`$, compared with $`48.27`$ for GRPO.

Takeaway. Increasing the GRPO weight substantially improves vanilla
OPSD. With strong PI and appropriate weighting, the combined objective
can outperform a matched GRPO baseline.

##### PI improves sample efficiency, but its benefit fades once the policy has learned from it.

The training curves in
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 How Well Does OPSD Combine with GRPO? ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
show that PI-guided training, particularly with Trajectory PI, improves
rapidly during the initial steps. On ALFWorld, all three PI-guided
curves plateau or decline after step 100, corresponding to one epoch,
while GRPO continues to improve. This suggests that PI provides its
largest optimization benefit early in training, before the policy has
learned the information it can readily extract from PI. Because each
training task uses the same static PI throughout training, its
supervision may become redundant after the first epoch. Beyond this
point, the OPSD signal can become a distraction that interferes with
further outcome-based learning. On AppWorld, Trajectory PI reaches
nearly $`100\%`$ training success at $`\lambda=0.9`$ but only $`39.14`$
test pass@1, suggesting that this improved sample efficiency can also
accelerate overfitting on small, less diverse training sets.

Takeaway. PI accelerates early learning, especially when it contains a
strong reference trajectory. Its marginal benefit decreases after the
policy has learned the most readily transferable behavior, after which
continued OPSD can even hinder further improvement.

## 5 Which Offline Metric Should Guide PI Choice?

We complement the observational analysis of
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 What Do PI Metrics Reveal? ‣ 3 Results and Analysis ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
with two controlled evaluations of the offline PI metrics.
ALFWorld-Passcode isolates the effect of leaked information
(Section [5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 ALFWorld-Passcode: Leaked Information Can Distort PI Utility ‣ 5 Which Offline Metric Should Guide PI Choice? ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")),
and per-task selection evaluates whether the KL metrics can identify
effective PI from candidate pools
(Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Selecting PI per Task with Offline Metrics ‣ 5 Which Offline Metric Should Guide PI Choice? ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation"));
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E PI Ablations ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
reports additional ablations with corrupted and length-matched PI.
Together, these studies further demonstrate the limitations of utility
and how error-step KL contrast can guide PI selection in practice.
Finally, we provide practical recommendations for using the proposed KL
metrics. All experiments train Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 with vanilla OPSD
on ALFWorld and report held-out pass@1, following
Section [2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Experimental Setup ‣ 2 Analyzing Privileged Information for OPSD ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation").

### 5.1 ALFWorld-Passcode: Leaked Information Can Distort PI Utility

##### ALFWorld-Passcode.

We modify ALFWorld so that completing the task no longer ends the
episode. Instead, the final observation reveals a task-specific key, and
the episode is scored only on the key the agent submits. The verifier
therefore checks only the submitted key, not whether the task was
completed. We build four PIs from the same GLM-5.2 reference
trajectories (Action $`+`$ Obs.): *Key only* contains just the reveal
and the submission of the key; *Mismatched trajectory $`+`$ key*
precedes this task’s key with the trajectory of a different task of the
same type; *Trajectory $`+`$ key* uses the task’s own trajectory, ending
with the reveal and the submission of its key; and *Trajectory $`+`$
decoy key* is the same trajectory with a wrong key in the final
submission. Utility is measured as in
Equation [2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ PI utility. ‣ 2.2 Offline Metrics ‣ 2 Analyzing Privileged Information for OPSD ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
with the PI in the student’s prompt.
Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ ALFWorld-Passcode. ‣ 5.1 ALFWorld-Passcode: Leaked Information Can Distort PI Utility ‣ 5 Which Offline Metric Should Guide PI Choice? ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
reports OPSD performance, utility, and both KL metrics for each PI.

Figure 5: Each row is one PI built from the same GLM-5.2 trajectories on
ALFWorld-Passcode. Utility ranks the PIs by whether they contain the
correct key. $`D`$ and $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ rank them by
whether they contain the task’s own trajectory, and so does OPSD
performance. Utility is the untrained student’s pass@1 with the PI in
its prompt, split into completed tasks (solid) and copied keys submitted
without completing the task (hatched). Dashed boxes show the same
trajectory in standard ALFWorld, and dashed lines mark the student
without PI.

##### A PI can score near-perfect utility while teaching nothing.

With only the correct key in its prompt, the student reaches $`98.1`$
pass@1, yet training on this PI leaves it exactly where it started.
Utility credits the PI for helping a policy that sees it; OPSD, however,
trains a student that never does. Any benefit that depends on reading
the PI, such as copying a key, therefore cannot transfer. Both KL
metrics recognize this, assigning Key only the lowest $`D`$ and
$`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ among the four PIs.

##### The key changes utility; the trajectory changes learning.

The remaining PIs vary the key and the trajectory one at a time.
Changing only the key, from correct to decoy, moves utility by 25 points
but leaves training performance, $`D`$, and
$`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ essentially unchanged. Changing only the
trajectory, from the task’s own to another task’s, does the reverse. The
mismatched PI still scores higher utility than the task’s own trajectory
in standard ALFWorld or with the decoy key, yet it nearly halves
training performance to $`45.0`$ and lowers both KL metrics. Utility
responds to the part of the PI the student can only copy, and the KL
metrics to the part it can learn from.

Takeaway. Utility measures whether PI helps the policy that sees it, so
information the student can exploit but never learn, such as a leaked
answer, can inflate or deflate it arbitrarily. The KL metrics measure
the PI’s effect on the student’s own distribution and are not misled.

### 5.2 Selecting PI per Task with Offline Metrics

##### Setup.

For each training task we score a pool of candidate PIs with $`D`$ and
$`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$, train with a PI drawn from the top
three by either metric, and compare against a random draw from the same
pool. The four pools vary along two axes, candidate strength and source
diversity: *8 strong PI sources* (one winning trajectory per source), *8
GLM-5.3-Flash* winning trajectories, *8 self wins* from the student, and
*8 self wins $`+`$ 8 self losses*. The strong-PI pools train for one
epoch on the full training set, while the smaller self-rollout pools
train for four epochs to keep the number of optimizer updates
comparable.
Figure [6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Setup. ‣ 5.2 Selecting PI per Task with Offline Metrics ‣ 5 Which Offline Metric Should Guide PI Choice? ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
shows held-out pass@1 throughout training across three or four seeds;
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Per-Task PI Selection ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
details candidate construction, metric scoring, and training procedures.

Figure 6: Per-task PI selection on ALFWorld with Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507.
Each task trains with a PI drawn from its top 3 candidates by $`D`$ or
by $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$, or uniformly from the whole pool;
curves show held-out pass@1 at each evaluation step (mean and standard
error over three or four seeds). Error-step KL selection helps most when
the pool mixes wins and losses, while reverse-KL selection falls below
random except on the GLM-5.3-Flash pool.

##### Error-step KL contrast is more effective when PI candidates vary in source or strength.

Selecting by $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ improves on random
selection when the candidates come from different sources, and most when
the pool mixes winning and failed rollouts. Among the student’s winning
rollouts,it leads random selection by $`5.1`$ points at step 40, but the
gap closes by the end of the third epoch as the student learns the
corrections the selected PI provides. For winning trajectories from a
single strong generator, it matches random selection, likely because the
candidates vary little in source or strength, leaving less room for
selection to help.

##### Reverse KL helps only when PI candidates are strong and come from one source.

Selecting by $`D`$ gives little benefit except when all candidates come
from a single strong generator, GLM-5.3-Flash. In the other pools, it
performs slightly worse than random selection, and it fails badly when
the pool mixes the student’s own winning and failed rollouts. Failed
rollouts shift the student’s distribution the most
(Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E PI Ablations ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")),
so ranking candidates by $`D`$ favors misleading PI.

Takeaway. Select PI by error-step KL contrast when candidates differ in
source or strength; its early advantage among successful self-rollouts
closes as the student learns the corrections. Reverse KL helps only when
PI candidates are strong and come from a single source.

### 5.3 Practical Recommendations

We recommend using the proposed KL metrics, and error-step KL contrast
in particular when candidate PI quality is inconsistent, to select PI
likely to perform well under vanilla OPSD, then combining it with a
strong GRPO signal to accelerate early learning. Once the benefit of PI
plateaus, its weight should be reduced or removed to avoid interfering
with further outcome-based learning. Another direction is to construct
new PI that targets the trained model’s remaining errors, allowing it to
benefit from updated supervision rather than repeatedly training on the
same static PI. Providing multiple diverse PI instances per task may
offer a similar benefit. On small training sets, the PI schedule and
diversity should be selected using held-out performance rather than
training success alone.

## 6 Related Work

##### On-policy self-distillation and PI construction.

Conventional language-model distillation often trains a student on
sequences generated by the teacher, whereas on-policy distillation (OPD)
supervises student-generated trajectories on the student’s own state
distribution \[[1](#bib.bib1), [31](#bib.bib31)\]. OPSD removes the need
for a separate teacher by conditioning the student on PI. Prior OPSD
methods use verified answers or reasoning traces \[[35](#bib.bib35)\],
environment feedback \[[7](#bib.bib7)\], continual-learning
demonstrations \[[22](#bib.bib22)\], and successful agent
trajectories \[[19](#bib.bib19)\]. More targeted constructions include
skills and mistake analyses \[[26](#bib.bib26)\], hindsight
feedback \[[33](#bib.bib33)\], and structured
rubrics \[[20](#bib.bib20), [6](#bib.bib6)\]. However, PI-conditioned
supervision can transfer undesirable context effects, reduce output
diversity, suppress uncertainty expression, or disrupt self-correction
in thinking models \[[13](#bib.bib13), [17](#bib.bib17),
[11](#bib.bib11), [9](#bib.bib9)\]. Its effectiveness also depends on
the PI representation, student, and sampled
rollouts \[[34](#bib.bib34)\]. We compare PI content, source, and
student model under a shared protocol to identify what explains
downstream effectiveness across PI configurations.

##### Combining OPSD with reinforcement learning.

Outcome-based RL provides a reliable task-level objective, whereas OPSD
can supply denser token-level guidance. Recent methods combine these
signals in different ways. RLSD uses self-distillation to determine
token-level update magnitudes while retaining RLVR rewards to determine
the update direction \[[29](#bib.bib29)\]. SRPO routes successful
samples to GRPO and failed samples to entropy-weighted
self-distillation \[[14](#bib.bib14)\], while RLCSD contrasts the
teacher–student gaps induced by correct and incorrect PI to suppress
PI-induced style shifts \[[18](#bib.bib18)\]. For agents, Skill-SD
stabilizes joint learning from skills and rewards \[[26](#bib.bib26)\],
and SDAR uses OPSD as a gated auxiliary objective to an RL
backbone \[[16](#bib.bib16)\]. OPID and SEED construct hierarchical or
evolving skills from on-policy trajectories and distill them jointly
with outcome-based RL \[[30](#bib.bib30), [27](#bib.bib27)\]. RLRT
instead reverses the teacher signal on successful rollouts to reinforce
correct reasoning paths not favored by the teacher \[[10](#bib.bib10)\].
We study a simple weighted combination of GRPO and OPSD, isolating
whether and how their supervision signals complement each other.

## 7 Conclusion

This work provides a controlled study of how privileged information
shapes on-policy self-distillation across 16 PI configurations, three
agentic benchmarks, and three student models. The results show that
effective PI must provide more than a correct solution. Environment
observations, mistake-aware strategies, and detailed reference
trajectories often improve learning, and more capable generators
generally produce stronger PI. However, these benefits depend on the
student: thinking models benefit less consistently, particularly when
long reasoning traces make supervision difficult to target at actual
errors.

PI effectiveness can also be assessed before committing to costly policy
training. PI utility is unreliable because leaked information can alter
teacher performance without transferring to the student. The proposed KL
metrics instead measure how PI changes the student distribution:
sampled-token reverse KL captures the magnitude of this shift, while
error-step KL contrast captures whether it is directed toward identified
mistakes. Their stronger relationship with post-training performance
makes them useful for screening PI formats and sources before running
OPSD. Selecting each task’s PI by error-step KL contrast also improves
over random selection, whereas selecting by reverse KL can favor
misleading PI.

Finally, PI selection remains important when OPSD is combined with GRPO.
Strong PI can improve upon matched GRPO baselines and accelerate early
learning, but static PI may become redundant as training progresses and
can encourage overfitting on small datasets. Future work should explore
diverse PI instances and adaptive schedules that reduce or reshape the
distillation signal as the student learns. Overall, effective PI is
distinguished not by how much it helps the teacher solve a task, but by
whether it produces a strong, error-targeted learning signal for the
student.

## Appendix A PI Construction

##### Reference trajectories.

For every training task of each benchmark, we sample rollouts from
GLM-5.2 and Qwen3.7-Plus in the training environment and keep one
successful rollout per task and model, recording its actions, the
observations returned by the environment, and the model’s reasoning.
Both models are queried through the Fireworks API with its default
sampling parameters.

##### Trajectory formats.

The four trajectory formats render a reference trajectory at different
levels of detail: *Action* keeps only the actions, *Action $`+`$ Obs.*
adds the observation that follows each action, *Action $`+`$ Thou.* adds
the reasoning that precedes each action, and *Action $`+`$ Obs. $`+`$
Thou.* keeps both.

##### Hints.

Each hint is written by a hint generator (GLM-5.2, Qwen3.7-Plus, or the
student itself for *Self*) from GLM-5.2 reference trajectories:

- •
  *Summary*: given one successful GLM-5.2 trajectory, the generator
  writes a short plain-text summary of the key actions and strategy
  (prompt in
  Appendix [H.2.1](#A8.SS2.SSS1 "H.2.1 Summary ‣ H.2 Hint-Generation Prompts ‣ Appendix H Prompts and Example Hints ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
- •
  *Skill*: given one successful GLM-5.2 trajectory, the generator writes
  a success analysis, a mistake analysis and an abstract workflow,
  rendered as a recommended approach and a key skill (prompts in

## Appendix B Experimental Details

### B.1 Benchmarks

##### ALFWorld.

A text-based household simulator (TextWorld over ALFRED tasks) with six
task types: pick and place, examine in light, clean and place, heat and
place, cool and place, and pick two and place. The agent receives a goal
and a room description and issues one text command per turn (e.g., go
to, take, clean, heat), for at most 30 turns. Our ReAct
scaffold \[[32](#bib.bib32)\] asks the model to reason and then give
exactly one command inside \<action\> tags
(Appendix [H.1](#A8.SS1 "H.1 Agent Prompts ‣ Appendix H Prompts and Example Hints ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation"));
an invalid command leaves the world unchanged and returns Nothing
happens. An episode succeeds when the goal is satisfied; the environment
reward also penalizes invalid actions, which only the GRPO-based runs
use
(Appendix [B.4](#A2.SS4 "B.4 GRPO and GRPO + OPSD ‣ Appendix B Experimental Details ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
We train on 1,600 tasks sampled from the training games and evaluate on
valid_seen (140 tasks) and valid_unseen (134 tasks), reporting their
pooled success rate.

##### AppWorld.

An interactive coding benchmark in which the agent completes everyday
tasks across nine simulated apps (e.g., Spotify, Venmo, Gmail) by
writing Python that calls their APIs in a stateful interpreter. We use
the official ReAct instructions and few-shot demonstration
(Appendix [H.1](#A8.SS1 "H.1 Agent Prompts ‣ Appendix H Prompts and Example Hints ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
The first fenced Python block of each turn is executed and its output
returned as the observation; the episode ends when the agent calls
apis.supervisor.complete_task() or after 30 actions. The reward is
binary task-goal completion: 1 if all of the task’s hidden unit tests on
the final database state pass, and 0 otherwise. We train on the 90 train
tasks and evaluate on the 168 test_normal tasks.

##### $`\tau^{2}`$-bench (retail).

A customer-service benchmark in which the agent converses with an
LLM-simulated customer (GPT-5.6-luna at temperature 0) and calls 16
retail tools that read and write an order database, while following a
written domain policy (e.g., authenticate the user before changing an
order). The agent uses $`\tau^{2}`$’s stock system prompt
(Appendix [H.1](#A8.SS1 "H.1 Agent Prompts ‣ Appendix H Prompts and Example Hints ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation"))
and, in each turn, either messages the user or makes a native tool call;
only the simulated user can end the episode, within at most 60 messages
including tool results. An episode succeeds if the final database state
matches the annotated goal state; on evaluation, 40 of the 114 tasks are
also scored on natural-language assertions about the conversation,
judged by GPT-4.1. We train on 352 retail tasks from the AReaL
$`\tau^{2}`$ dataset \[[5](#bib.bib5)\] and evaluate on the 114 retail
base tasks.

the 8 rollouts of its task,
$`A_{i}=(R_{i}-\operatorname{mean}_{g}R)/\operatorname{std}_{g}R`$
(ALFWorld omits the division by the standard deviation). GRPO $`+`$ OPSD

## Appendix D How Information Leakage Distorts PI Utility

PI utility
(Equation [2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ PI utility. ‣ 2.2 Offline Metrics ‣ 2 Analyzing Privileged Information for OPSD ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation"))
places the PI in the model’s own prompt at inference and measures the
resulting change in success. This setup gives the evaluated model direct
access to information unavailable to the student after distillation. We
study this effect on ALFWorld and $`\tau^{2}`$-retail and find that
leaked information can either increase performance through shortcuts or
decrease it by distracting the model. Consequently, utility can reflect
how the model uses PI in context rather than the training signal that PI
provides in OPSD, where only the teacher observes PI.

##### ALFWorld: the model copies the goal-completing action.

Every trajectory hint ends on the reference agent’s final action, which
is always the one that completes the goal (e.g., placing the object in
its destination). Given such a hint, the model often takes this action
first, as if it were already in the final state and one step from
success. Its preconditions are not yet met (e.g., the agent is not
holding the object), so the environment answers Nothing happens. An
*Action* hint is a list numbered from step 1, and the model follows it
in order instead.
Table [2](#A4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ ALFWorld: the model copies the goal-completing action. ‣ Appendix D How Information Leakage Distorts PI Utility ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")
gives the rate on the utility rollouts of
Table [4](#A7.T4 "Table 4 ‣ G.2 Offline Metrics ‣ Appendix G Additional Results ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation"):
51.9–96.5% under the two longer formats, for every student and both
sources, against at most 1% under *Action*. Nearly every such first
action fails, and the model issues the final action at least twice in
51–95% of the rollouts under the longer formats, against 3–12% under
*Action*. The behavior is not a parsing artifact, since none of these
first actions comes from a turn with several actions.

In both cases, utility reflects how the model reuses leaked solution
information rather than what the PI teaches. An *Action* hint leaks the
complete action sequence, which the model copies: 71–89% of the actions
it takes appear verbatim in the hint. *Action* therefore has the highest
utility of the four trajectory formats for almost every student and
source
(Table [4](#A7.T4 "Table 4 ‣ G.2 Offline Metrics ‣ Appendix G Additional Results ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")),
even though it trains the worst
(Table [3](#A7.T3 "Table 3 ‣ G.1 OPSD Performance ‣ Appendix G Additional Results ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")).
The longer formats leak the same solution in a form that is harder to
reuse: the model copies its final action out of order, which lowers
their utility. 30B-A3B, which does so most often, gains nothing from
*Action $`+`$ Obs. $`+`$ Thou.* hints. For the 4B thinking student under
*Action $`+`$ Obs.* hints, rollouts that open with the copied final
action succeed 7–11 points less often than other rollouts of the same
task. Utility thus rewards how directly leaked information can be reused
at inference, not how well the PI trains the student.

##### $`\tau^{2}`$-retail: the 4B thinking student reports a result instead of producing it.

The *Action $`+`$ Obs. $`+`$ Thou.* hint contains the reference agent’s
reasoning, in which it narrates what it looked up and did, including
order identifiers, prices and refund amounts. Given this hint,
Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 often skips the task: it answers the customer’s
request by confirming a completed operation, with values taken from the
hint, without calling any tool. The database is unchanged, so the
episode fails. The box below shows one such reply. With the *Action

task authenticates the user, retrieves the order, confirms the exchange

##### Implication.

In both cases utility registers how the model uses the PI in its own
context: following an action list step by step, continuing from the
hint’s last line, or reporting a result it did not produce. These
behaviors can raise utility or lower it, and none of them is available
to the student during OPSD, which never sees the PI. Utility can
therefore rank PI formats differently from the training signal they
provide.

##### All three metrics flag mismatched and other-domain PI.

Both corruptions remove most of the benefit of OPSD: compared with
$`87.9`$ pass@1 for the task’s own trajectory, mismatched PI reaches
$`34.8`$, and other-domain PI degrades the student to $`5.4`$, below the
untrained student baseline. Utility, $`D`$, and
$`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ all place both PIs below the two
references, and other-domain PI scores the lowest on all three.

##### A wrong PI can still produce a large shift.

The student’s failed rollouts yield poor OPSD performance, which utility
flags because they offer little help in context. Yet they shift the
teacher about as much as successful self-rollouts, while error-step KL
contrast is much lower for them. On the tasks of the self-rollout
selection pools
(Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Per-Task PI Selection ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation")),
failed rollouts even have a larger $`D`$ than successful ones but a
lower $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$. A large shift alone therefore
does not indicate useful PI, explaining why selection by $`D`$ favors
failed rollouts in
Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Selecting PI per Task with Offline Metrics ‣ 5 Which Offline Metric Should Guide PI Choice? ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation").

##### PI length is not the main driver of the KL metrics.

Padding the action list to the length of the full trajectory changes
$`D`$ and $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ only slightly, and OPSD
performance stays close to that of the unpadded action list. The larger
shift of trajectory PIs appears to reflect their content more than their
length.

Takeaway. Utility and error-step KL contrast detect both corrupted PI
and failed self-rollouts. Reverse KL detects large distribution shifts
but cannot determine whether those shifts are useful. PI content
explains the KL metrics better than length alone.

## Appendix F Per-Task PI Selection

##### Pools.

*8 PI sources* holds one winning trajectory per task from each of
GLM-5.2 and Qwen3.7-Plus (Action $`+`$ Obs. $`+`$ Thou., capped at 24k
tokens), Qwen3.8-Max, Kimi-K3, GLM-5.3, MiniMax-M3, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash,
and GLM-5.3-Flash; a task has fewer candidates when a source never
solved it (1,556 of the 1,600 training tasks have all eight). *8
GLM-5.3-Flash* holds eight winning GLM-5.3-Flash trajectories per task
(1,492 tasks have all eight; the 7 tasks it never solved receive no PI).
*8 self wins* holds eight successful rollouts of the untrained student,
and *8 self wins $`+`$ 8 self losses* adds eight of its failed rollouts;
on the 105 tasks with fewer than eight failures, the least efficient
successful rollouts fill the remaining slots.

##### Scoring.

$`D`$ and $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$ are computed for every
candidate as in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Offline Metric Protocols ‣ Predicting Privileged Information Effectiveness for On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
with 128 rollouts per task on the 200 offline tasks and 16 on the
remaining training tasks. Self-pool candidates are scored on the task’s
other 15 rollouts, excluding the candidate itself.

##### Training.

Each run fixes one PI per task, drawn uniformly from the pool or from
its top three candidates by $`D`$ or by $`\Delta d^{-}_{\mathrm{err}}`$.
The two source pools train on all 1,600 tasks for one epoch (100 steps)
and evaluate every 25 steps, with four seeds for 8 PI sources and three
for 8 GLM-5.3-Flash. The self pools train on the 483 tasks on which the
untrained student succeeds at least eight times, for four epochs (120
steps), evaluating every 20 steps; we report checkpoints up to step 100,
with three seeds. Each seed changes both the PI draw and the trainer
seed.
````

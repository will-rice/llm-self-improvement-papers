###### Abstract

On-policy Self-distillation (OPSD) has proven effective for
post-training large language models by constructing a teacher
conditioned on privileged references. However, existing approaches keep
the teacher fixed either throughout training by consistently exposing it
to the full reference, or within each response by adaptively varying
partial reference exposure only across responses. In this paper, we
argue that neither of them is sufficient. Instead, every token needs its
own teacher, as the teacher preference can vary token by token within
the same response. Through a teacher-intervention pilot study, we
validate that the teacher preference differs across tokens, and reveal
that a preferred teacher can be identified for each token through
majority voting and KL-based selection. Motivated by these findings, we
propose Multi-teacher Adaptive On-policy Self-Distillation (MTA-OPSD),
the first token-level adaptive OPSD method. Our method constructs a
multi-teacher pool consisting of candidate teachers, and enables every
token to adaptively select its preferred teacher during training.
Experiments with Qwen3 models on mathematical reasoning tasks show that
MTA-OPSD consistently outperforms existing OPSD approaches with an
average gain of up to $`+5.9`$ over the base model, highlighting the
importance of adapting teacher supervision at the token level. The code
is available at
[https://github.com/xingzhejun/MTA-OPSD](https://github.com/xingzhejun/MTA-OPSD).

## 1 Introduction

On-policy Self-distillation (OPSD) ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib1);
[Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib2); [Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib3);
[Cui et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)) has emerged as a promising paradigm for
post-training large language models (LLMs), showing superiority over
other post-training paradigms like Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) and
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) (e.g., GRPO ([Guo
et al., 2025](#bib.bib4))). In OPSD, the same model serves as both
student and teacher. The student generates rollout responses, while the
teacher provides supervision with access to privileged references, such
as reference solutions. Compared with RLVR, OPSD provides dense
token-level learning signals beyond sparse outcome rewards. Compared
with SFT, it trains on trajectories sampled by the student itself,
mitigating the exposure bias ([Bengio et al., 2015](#bib.bib5)).

Despite these advantages, recent studies ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib6);
[Kaur et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Harne et al., 2026](#bib.bib8);
[Armandpour et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)) reveal that OPSD can also suffer
from degraded reasoning performance, which stems from the highly
informative references exposed to the teacher. It may induce overly
sharp teacher distributions, producing supervision that is poorly
aligned with the student’s learning dynamics (e.g., suppressing
uncertainty ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib21))). A line of work ([Tan and Hong, 2026](#bib.bib9);
[Han et al., 2026b](#bib.bib10); [Yang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib11)) has
attempted to address this student-teacher mismatch through adaptive
reference exposure. Rather than providing the teacher with the complete
reference, it adjusts how much of the reference the teacher receives
based on the student’s learning dynamics, such as the student-teacher
gap. However, these methods adapt at the response level. Although
reference exposure may vary across responses, it remains fixed across
tokens within each response. In other words, all tokens in a rollout are
supervised by the same teacher.

In this paper, we challenge this consensus by arguing:

The adaptive teacher should operate at the token level, because every
token requires its own  
  dedicated teacher.

Figure 1: Token-level learning signals induced by different teachers for
the same response. Teachers agree at some positions but provide opposing
signals at others, motivating token-level teacher selection. Additional
details on this visualization are provided in
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Visualization Details ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation").

A teacher that provides useful guidance at one token may be less
suitable at another, even within the same response. Rather than treating
it with the same teacher, each token in the response merits
consideration with its own preferred teacher. As illustrated in
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
when different teachers are applied to the same response, they can
provide similar learning signals for some tokens but disagree on the
direction of supervision for others. This suggests that not only each
response, but also each token, may have distinct teacher preferences.

While some prior work ([Wang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib12); [Hao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13); [Xu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib14); [Ke et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15); [Wei et al., 2026](#bib.bib23); [Wang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib22); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib41); [Han et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib42)) has explored token selection for post-training
(e.g., focusing solely on high-entropy tokens ([Wang et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib12))), how to validate our claim is challenging, and how
to adaptively identify the preferred teacher for each token remains
unexplored. To answer these, we conduct a teacher-intervention pilot
study
([Section 4](#S4 "4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"))
to quantify the token-level teacher preference. As shown in
[Figure 2](#S4.F2 "In 4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
we preserve a student-generated prefix, allow a candidate teacher to
generate a short continuation at a selected token position, and then
return control to the student to complete the response. The accuracy
gain from this “student-teacher-student” relay over pure student
generation serves as a proxy for the teacher preference at that token
position. A large gain implies a compatible teacher for that position,
while a small gain suggests the opposite. Interestingly, the results are
simple, yet clear and compelling. First, teacher preferences differ
across tokens, even within the same response. Second, at the token
level, majority voting yields a subset of reliable teachers, and the
preferred teacher always exhibits a large student-teacher KL distance.
The first finding validates our claim, and the second finding motivates
a practical rule for token-level teacher selection.

Based on these findings, we propose Multi-teacher Adaptive On-policy
Self-Distillation (MTA-OPSD), the first token-level adaptive OPSD
method. As shown in
[Figure 3](#S5.F3 "In 5.1 Multi-teacher Pool ‣ 5 Method ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
MTA-OPSD constructs a multi-teacher pool consisting of candidate
teachers, by varying the truncation strategy and exposure ratio of the
privileged reference. During training, rather than using a fixed teacher
for all tokens in the same response, each token adaptively selects its
own teacher from the multi-teacher pool based on its preference, through
majority voting followed by KL-based selection. Our token-level adaptive
OPSD design is simple yet effective, precisely addressing the
student-teacher mismatch. By assigning each token its preferred teacher,
MTA-OPSD provides a more balanced learning signal rather than an overly
sharp, rigid teacher distribution from a single teacher. We evaluate
MTA-OPSD on mathematical reasoning benchmarks using models from the
Qwen3 family ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)), and it consistently
outperforms existing OPSD approaches with an average gain of up to
$`+5.9`$ over the base model.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  We quantify the teacher preference across tokens via a
  teacher-intervention pilot study, validating that preferred teachers
  can differ from token to token within the same response. We further
  reveal that the token-level preferred teacher can be identified
  through majority voting and KL-based selection.
- •
  We propose MTA-OPSD, the first token-level adaptive OPSD method. By
  constructing a multi-teacher pool as the supervision source, our
  approach enables each token to adaptively select its preferred teacher
  under the guidance of majority voting and KL divergence, effectively
  addressing the student-teacher mismatch.
- •
  We conduct extensive experiments across various models on mathematical
  reasoning tasks, demonstrating that our approach achieves superior
  reasoning performance compared to existing OPSD methods. Furthermore,
  we empirically analyze comprehensive ablation studies, paving the way
  for future advances in this field.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 On-policy Distillation and On-Policy Self-distillation

Knowledge distillation ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib24)) transfers
knowledge from a large teacher model to a student model by training on
the teacher’s soft output distributions. Early approaches ([Kim and
Rush, 2016](#bib.bib25); [Jiao et al., 2020](#bib.bib26); [Wang et al.,
2020](#bib.bib27)) leveraged it to sequence-level distillation,
establishing the dominant off-policy distillation approaches. ([Gu et
al., 2024](#bib.bib28); [Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib17); [Lu and Lab,
2025](#bib.bib18); [Yang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib29)) extended it to
On-policy Distillation (OPD), addressing the exposure bias ([Bengio et
al., 2015](#bib.bib5)) mismatch by shifting the training distribution to
the student’s own generations.

Despite the rapid progress of OPD, the paradigm relies on a separate,
typically stronger teacher model, which may be unavailable or expensive
to maintain. To address this challenge, OPSD ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib1); [Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib2); [Shenfeld et al.,
2026](#bib.bib3); [Luo et al., 2026](#bib.bib30); [Yang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib40)) removes this requirement by instantiating the
teacher and student from the same model. Under this setting, the teacher
and student share the same model, but the teacher is granted access to a
privileged reference unavailable to the student.

However, recent studies ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Wang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib31); [Kaur et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Harne et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8); [Shen et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)) reveal a persistent
mismatch in OPSD. In particular, a highly informative reference can
induce an overly sharp teacher distribution and create a mismatch
between the teacher’s supervision and the student’s learning dynamics.
These observations motivate the line of research for adaptive OPSD
methods.

### 2.2 Adaptive On-Policy Self-distillation

Adaptive OPSD seeks to align the teacher supervision with the student’s
learning dynamics. Rather than exposing the complete privileged
reference, existing methods ([Tan and Hong, 2026](#bib.bib9); [Han et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib10); [Yang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib11)) control its
content or exposure ratio using mechanisms such as learned
reference-exposure schedules. By varying the amount of privileged
references available to the teacher, these methods aim to avoid overly
sharp supervision while retaining the benefits of dense learning
signals.

However, these methods adapt at the response level, where all tokens in
the response share the same teacher and the same privileged reference.
In contrast, we identify that each token needs its own teacher as tokens
have distinct teacher preferences
([Section 4](#S4 "4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation")),
and further propose our token-level multi-teacher adaptive OPSD method.

## 3 Preliminaries

OPSD is a special case of OPD. OPD transfers knowledge from a stronger
teacher model $`p_{T}`$ to a weaker student model $`p_{\theta}`$ by
enforcing dense supervision over trajectories sampled by the student.
Given a prompt $`x`$, the student samples a response
$`y=\{y^{1},y^{2},\cdots,y^{L}\}`$, where $`L`$ denotes the generation
length and $`y^{i}`$ refers to the $`i`$-th token in the response. The
learning objective is to minimize the token-level KL between the
teacher’s and the student’s next-token distributions:

|     |                                                   |                                                                                                                                                                                       |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{OPD}}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid y^{<i},x\right)\mid\mid p_{T}\left(\cdot\mid y^{<i},x\right)\right)\right]`$ |     | (1) |
|     |                                                   | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i}\right)\mid\mid p_{T}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i}\right)\right)\right]`$       |     |     |

where $`p(\cdot\mid y^{<i},x)`$ denotes the distribution over the next
token $`y^{i}`$, and we let $`c_{i}`$ refer to $`(y^{<i},x)`$ for
simplicity. While forward KL and reverse KL are most used, other
distribution divergence measures like generalized Jensen-Shannon
divergence ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)) can also be employed.

Recent advances have extended OPD to OPSD, where the student and teacher
are instantiated from the same model $`p_{\theta}`$. The difference lies
entirely in their conditioning contexts. Privileged reference $`r`$,
such as reference solutions ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)) or
environment feedback ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)), is appended
to the original prompt to construct a teacher-specific prompt. Thus, the
teacher distribution is:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
p_{T}(\cdot\mid c_{i})=p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i},r\right).
``` |  | (2) |

Consequently, the learning objective in
[Equation 1](#S3.E1 "In 3 Preliminaries ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation")
adapts into the following:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{OPSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i}\right)\mid\mid p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i},r\right)\right)\right].
``` |  | (3) |

In this setting, both the teacher and student share the same model but
differ only in the conditioning contexts, and the response is solely
generated from the student.

## 4 Pilot Study: Token Matters

Before diving into a detailed design, a critical question must first be
answered:

Given $`c_{i}=(y^{<i},x)`$, how can we determine if one teacher
$`p_{T}(c_{i})`$ is more suitable than another?

Figure 2: Illustration of the teacher-intervention pilot study.

A direct approach would be to train on every token separately with
specific teachers, covering enough token-teacher combinations. However,
it is obviously not practical.

We instead answer this question from another view. Motivated by ([Xu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib33); [Xu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib34)), we estimate
teacher suitability through teacher intervention. Specifically, given a
prompt $`x`$ and a student’s response
$`y=\{y^{1},y^{2},\cdots,y^{L}\}`$, we select a token position $`1<i<L`$
and preserve the student-generated prefix before that position. Starting
from position $`i`$, a candidate teacher takes over the generation
process and produces a short subsequence:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle z_{1:i-1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=y_{1:i-1}=p_{\theta}(x)`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle z_{t:t+k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=p_{T}(y^{<i},x)=p_{\theta}(c_{i},r),`$ |  |  |

where $`k`$ denotes the teacher-intervention length. After this
intervention, control is returned to the student, which completes the
remainder of the response:

|     |                                       |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       z_{i+k+1:L}=p_{\theta}(z^{<i+k+1},x).
       ```                                    |     | (5) |

Thus, we obtain a “student-teacher-student” relay response
$`z=\{z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{L}\}`$. We compare its accuracy with the
pure student response, which serves as a proxy for the candidate teacher
preference at the selected token position. A larger gain indicates a
more suitable teacher, whereas a smaller gain indicates the opposite.

The main purpose of this pilot study is that, by varying both the
intervention token position and the candidate teacher, this study
separates variation along the token and teacher axes. This allows us to
examine whether teacher preferences change across token positions and to
identify their patterns. Interestingly, the pattern is simple but clear.
We next describe the experimental setup and then present our findings,
as highlighted in
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Results and Findings ‣ 4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation").

### 4.1 Setup

Our study varies two principal factors: tokens at which the intervention
begins, and teachers which are used for the intervention.

For the token axis, we consider both regular-spaced and
statistic-extreme token positions. For the former, we select tokens
located at $`25\%`$, $`50\%`$, and $`75\%`$ of the original student
rollout. For the latter, we select tokens associated with the highest
and lowest student entropy, and the largest and smallest student-teacher
KL distance ⁰⁰ 0 For identifying these KL-based positions, the teacher
is conditioned on the full reference.. In total, we select $`7`$
intervention positions from each rollout.

For the teacher axis, we vary both the reference-truncation strategy¹¹ 1
The truncation strategy determines which part of the reference is
retained. For example, prefix truncation with an exposure ratio of
$`0.5`$ retains the first half of the reference. The final answer is
retained under every truncation setting to ensure a fair comparison. and
the reference-exposure ratio. We consider prefix, suffix, and
random-span for truncation strategy, each with exposure ratios of
$`0.5`$ and $`0.75`$, together with a full-reference teacher. This
produces 7 candidate teachers.

We evaluate intervention lengths across $`k=64`$, $`128`$, and $`256`$.
We sample $`64`$ prompts of at least medium difficulty²² 2 For each
prompt, we first sample $`4`$ student responses and exclude prompts for
which all four responses are correct. from DeepMath-103k ([He et al.,
2026](#bib.bib35)). We conduct the study using Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B
as student models. To reduce sampling variance, for every experimental
configuration, we sample $`2`$ teacher continuations and then $`2`$
student relays following each continuation. In total,
$`64*7*7*3*2*2*2=75264`$ generations are evaluated.

## 5 Method

Based on the findings in
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Results and Findings ‣ 4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
we propose Multi-Teacher Adaptive OPSD (MTA-OPSD), a simple yet
effective framework for token-level adaptive OPSD. As illustrated in
[Figure 3](#S5.F3 "In 5.1 Multi-teacher Pool ‣ 5 Method ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
rather than using a single teacher, MTA-OPSD constructs a multi-teacher
pool consisting of candidate teachers conditioned on different variants
of the privileged reference. Through majority voting followed by
KL-based selection, it assigns each token its preferred teacher. This
ensures that different tokens within the same response receive
supervision from their respective teachers.

### 5.1 Multi-teacher Pool

To support token-level adaptive teacher selection, we construct a
multi-teacher pool $`M`$, consisting of all candidate teachers available
for each token:

|     |                                             |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       M=\{p_{T_{1}},p_{T_{2}},\cdots,p_{T_{K}}\},
       ```                                          |     | (7) |

where $`K`$ is the number of candidate teachers and $`p_{T_{k}}`$
denotes the $`k`$-th teacher. All teachers share the same model
$`p_{\theta}`$ with the student. They differ from each other only in the
privileged references on which they are conditioned:

|     |                                 |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R=\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{K}\},
       ```                              |     | (8) |

which leads to the next-token distribution of each teacher as:

|     |                                          |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       p_{T_{k}}=p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,r_{k}).
       ```                                       |     | (9) |

Each $`r_{k}`$ differs from each other by its truncation way and
exposure ratio of the full reference. An example of our multi-teacher
pool is provided in
[Appendix F](#A6 "Appendix F Multi-teacher Pool Visualization ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation").

While the multi-teacher pool supplies a diverse set of supervision
signals, it does not determine which teacher should supervise each
token. Guided by the pilot study in
[Section 4](#S4 "4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
MTA-OPSD performs token-level adaptive teacher assignment in two steps:
(1) majority voting identifies a reliable subset of teachers, and (2)
KL-based selection chooses the preferred teacher from that subset.

Figure 3: The framework of our approach, MTA-OPSD. With the
multi-teacher pool, it adaptively assigns each token its preferred
teacher, by majority voting followed by KL-based selection.

### 5.2 Majority Voting

As shown in
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Results and Findings ‣ 4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
majority voting provides a reliable teacher subset by identifying the
direction of the advantage signals. We therefore use majority voting as
the first step of teacher selection.

Specifically, for a token $`y^{i}`$ in the student response, the
multi-teacher pool produces the following set of its teacher token
distributions:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle P_{M}(c_{i})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left\{p_{T_{1}}(\cdot\mid c_{i}),p_{T_{2}}(\cdot\mid c_{i}),\cdots,p_{T_{K}}(\cdot\mid c_{i})\right\}`$ |  | (10) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left\{p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{i},r_{1}),p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{i},r_{2}),\cdots,p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{i},r_{K})\right\}.`$ |  |  |

For each teacher token distribution in $`P_{M}`$, we compare it with the
student token distribution $`p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y^{<i},x)`$ over
$`y^{i}`$, to obtain the following set:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{M}(c_{i})=\{a_{T_{1}}(y^{i}\mid c_{i}),a_{T_{2}}(y^{i}\mid c_{i}),\cdots,a_{T_{K}}(y^{i}\mid c_{i})\},
``` |  | (11) |

where advantage $`a_{T_{k}}(y^{i}\mid c_{i})`$ is computed using
[Equation 6](#S4.E6 "In 4.2 Results and Findings ‣ 4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation").
The sign of $`a_{T_{k}}(y^{i}\mid c_{i})`$ indicates the $`k`$-th
teacher’s relative support for the student’s choice at $`y^{i}`$. We
denote it as the supporting binary vote:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v_{T_{k}}(c_{i})=\operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{T_{k}}\left(y^{i}\mid c_{i}\right)\right)=\begin{cases}+1,&a_{T_{k}}\left(y^{i}\mid c_{i}\right)>0,\\
-1,&a_{T_{k}}\left(y^{i}\mid c_{i}\right)<0.\end{cases}
``` |  | (12) |

We then determine the majority voting direction at token position $`i`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
V_{M}(c_{i})=\operatorname{sgn}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{T_{k}}\left(c_{i}\right)\right),
``` |  | (13) |

and the corresponding token-level majority teacher subset³³ 3 If a tie
occurs between the majority and minority votes, we then compare which
side has the larger absolute value. Additional details are provided in
[Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Additional Majority Voting Details ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation").:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
M_{\mathrm{maj}}(c_{i})=\left\{p_{T_{k}}\in M\mid v_{T_{k}}\left(c_{i}\right)=V_{M}\left(c_{i}\right)\right\}.
``` |  | (14) |

Note that this procedure is token-level, performed independently at
every token position.

### 5.3 KL-based Selection

Majority voting filters out teachers whose supervision direction
disagrees with most candidates, but it does not distinguish among
teachers within the majority subset. As shown in
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Results and Findings ‣ 4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
the preferred teacher always has large KL distance from the student. We
therefore use KL distance as the second-stage selection criterion.

Specifically, for token $`y^{i}`$ in the student response and its
majority teacher subset $`M_{\mathrm{maj}}(c_{i})`$, the token-level
preferred teacher is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{T}^{*}(c_{i})=p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{i},r^{*})=\arg\max_{p_{T_{k}}\in M_{\mathrm{maj}}(c_{i})}\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i}\right)\mid\mid p_{T_{k}}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i}\right)\right).
``` |  | (15) |

Note that since the preferred teacher is determined separately for every
token, it can vary across tokens within the same response. Consequently,
substituting this token-level teacher into the learning objective in
[Equation 3](#S3.E3 "In 3 Preliminaries ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation")
gives:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{MTA-OPSD}}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i}\right)\mid\mid p_{T}^{*}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i}\right)\right)\right]`$ |  | (16) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i}\right)\mid\mid p_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid c_{i},r^{*}\right)\right)\right],`$ |  |  |

Thus, MTA-OPSD assigns each token its own teacher.

### 5.4 Discussion

The mechanism of our method can be understood as decoupling the
reliability of the supervision direction from the informativeness of the
supervision signal. Majority voting first determines whether the teacher
pool collectively supports or opposes the student-generated token
$`y^{i}`$, KL-based selection then identifies the most informative
teacher aligned with that direction. This ordering is crucial: KL
distance is not used as a standalone measure of teacher quality, but
only after directional consensus has filtered out potentially unreliable
teachers.

Specifically, when $`V_{M}(c_{i})=1`$, the majority teachers in
$`M_{\mathrm{maj}}(c_{i})`$ assign a higher probability to $`y^{i}`$
than the student itself, and selecting the teacher with the largest KL
therefore reinforces the student’s current choice. Meanwhile, the
selected teacher can also provide a substantially different overall
probability distribution. Consequently, the supervision can strengthen
the sampled token without being limited to simply imitating it, thereby
retaining informative probabilities for other plausible predictions and
potentially reducing overfitting to the student’s current trajectory.

When $`V_{M}(c_{i})=-1`$, the majority teachers assign a lower
probability to $`y^{i}`$, and the sampled token is collectively regarded
as a less preferred choice. In this case, a teacher with a small KL
distance may only weakly downweight this token or remain too close to
the student distribution. In contrast, the farthest teacher is more
likely to redistribute probability mass toward different candidate
tokens, providing a stronger corrective signal together with more
distinct alternative prediction directions. Such supervision may
therefore help the student move away from a potentially erroneous local
choice rather than merely making a marginal adjustment to it.

Additional experiment setup details are provided in
[Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C Additional Experiment Settings ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation").

Models and Datasets. We evaluate models from the Qwen3 family ([Yang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) at three scales: Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and
Qwen3-8B, all with thinking mode enabled during both training and
validation. We conduct experiments across mathematical reasoning tasks.
The training data is drawn from DeepMath-103K ([He et al.,

AIME24 ([Zhang and Math-AI, 2024](#bib.bib38)), and AIME25 ([Zhang and

Baselines. We compare our method against two fixed-teacher OPSD methods

determines the reference-exposure ratio according to the student-teacher
gap.

dataset-provided ground-truth reference solutions as the privileged
information, and all contexts include both the reasoning trace and the
final answer. We use identical training and evaluation settings across
all methods, with maximum generation lengths of 4096 tokens during
training and 38912 tokens during validation. The multi-teacher pool
contains seven teacher configurations: {full reference, prefix-$`0.5`$,
prefix-$`0.75`$, suffix-$`0.5`$, suffix-$`0.75`$, random-span-$`0.5`$,

exposure ratio of $`0.5`$ retains the first half of the reference. The
final answer is retained under every truncation setting to ensure a fair
comparison.. We train each method for 300 steps, evaluate checkpoints
every 50 steps on the test set, and report its best Avg@12 results.

Effect of the Multi-Teacher Pool. We first investigate whether the
multi-teacher pool provides more effective supervision than any
individual teacher. In
[Table 8](#S6.T8 "In 6.3 Ablation Studies ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
we compare our method with training using each teacher configuration
from the pool independently. Although the effectiveness of individual
teachers varies from each other, MTA-OPSD achieves the highest score on
both average and individual benchmarks. These results suggest that the
multi-teacher pool offers diverse, sufficient, and complementary
learning signals.

Effect of Token-Level Teacher Assignment. The multi-teacher pool is
accompanied by the token-level adaptive teacher assignment. The previous
ablation establishes the value of the former, but does not isolate the
contribution of the latter. We therefore retain the same multi-teacher
pool and compare MTA-OPSD with two alternative assignment strategies:
(1) Random teacher selection from the pool for each token, and (2)
averaging the distillation losses from all teachers. As shown in
[Table 8](#S6.T8 "In 6.3 Ablation Studies ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
MTA-OPSD consistently outperforms both alternatives on every benchmark.
These show that the gains do not arise solely from multiple teachers;
selecting a suitable teacher for each token is critical to using the
teacher pool effectively.

Teacher-Assignment Dynamics. We also investigate how frequently each
teacher is selected throughout training. The assignments are distributed
relatively evenly across the teacher pool, indicating that the selection
mechanism does not collapse to a single dominant teacher. We also
observe that, under the same truncation strategy, a higher
reference-exposure ratio does not necessarily produce a larger
student-teacher KL distance, as discussed in
[Appendix E](#A5 "Appendix E Additional Details about KL-distance ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation").
This observation further suggests that existing adaptive methods of
adjusting reference exposure alone cannot determine teacher suitability.

Effect of Majority Voting and KL-Based Selection. MTA-OPSD selects a
teacher in two steps: majority voting and KL-based selection.
[Table 8](#S6.T8 "In 6.3 Ablation Studies ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation")
presents the contribution of each step, where the second row is our
default setting. MTA-OPSD consistently outperforms all variants, further
validating the effectiveness of our design: majority voting first
identifies a reliable teacher subset, and KL-based selection chooses a
teacher from that subset.

## 7 Conclusion

We present MTA-OPSD, the first token-level adaptive OPSD method. A
teacher-intervention study shows that teacher preferences vary across
tokens and motivates our two-step token-level adaptive teacher selection
strategy: majority voting with KL-based selection. Future work may
explore self-evolving teacher pools and more stable multi-teacher
training.

## Appendix B Additional Majority Voting Details

As discussed in
[Section 5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Majority Voting ‣ 5 Method ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
a tie may occur when the multi-teacher pool contains an even number of
teachers, with equal numbers of positive and negative votes. In this
case, we break the tie according to the magnitudes of the corresponding
advantage signals. Specifically, the majority direction is defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
V_{M}(c_{i})=\begin{cases}\operatorname{sgn}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{T_{k}}(c_{i})\right),\text{ if }\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{T_{k}}(c_{i})\neq 0,\\
\operatorname{sgn}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{T_{k}}(y^{i}\mid c_{i})\right),\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (17) |

Thus, when the numbers of positive and negative votes are equal, we
select the group with the larger total absolute advantage. The majority
teacher subset is then still constructed using
[Equation 14](#S5.E14 "In 5.2 Majority Voting ‣ 5 Method ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation").
This tie-breaking rule retains the direction supported by the stronger
aggregate teacher signal.

## Appendix C Additional Experiment Settings

All experiments are conducted on 4 H200 GPUs. We use a learning rate of
$`1`$ × $`10^{-6}`$. All methods are implemented on top of the veRL
([Sheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib43)) PPO trainer with FSDP2 actor sharding
and vLLM rollouts. All runs use full-parameter training (no LoRA ([Hu et
al., 2021](#bib.bib44))). The global batch size is $`32`$. Following
([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)), we restrict the token-level
distillation objective to the top-100 entries of the teacher
distribution.

Note that following ([Pan et al., 2026](#bib.bib36)), we use training
problems at difficulty levels $`5`$–$`7`$ for Qwen3-1.7B, $`6`$–$`8`$
for Qwen3-4B, and levels $`7`$–$`10`$ for Qwen3-8B.

## Appendix E Additional Details about KL-distance

As discussed in
[Section 6.3](#S6.SS3 "6.3 Ablation Studies ‣ 6 Experiments ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
even under same truncation strategy, larger reference-exposure ratio
does not lead to larger student-teacher KL distance. In our pilot study
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Results and Findings ‣ 4 Pilot Study: Token Matters ‣ Every Token Needs Its Own Teacher: Multi-Teacher Adaptive On-Policy Self-Distillation"),
for example, for Qwen3-4B model with suffix truncation, only $`49.1\%`$
cases that $`0.75`$ exposure ratio has larger KL distance from the
student. In contrast, the statistic for $`0.5`$ exposure ratio is
$`50.9\%`$.
````

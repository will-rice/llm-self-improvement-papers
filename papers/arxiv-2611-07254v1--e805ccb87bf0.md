###### Abstract

Neural networks controlling behavior of A-life agents can evolve over
multiple generations. While effective in making the agents survive, such
networks are generally opaque to a human observer. Furthermore, the
neural weights cannot be transferred between agents with different
neural architectures. Distilling such neural networks into compact
symbolic policies can help humans understand agent behavior in A-life.
Additionally, it allows the agents to share their knowledge in a compact
fashion, improving population-level learning. Distilling a neural policy
into a symbolic policy is typically performed by searching the space of
symbolic policies to find one that approximates the neural policy.
However, even with a domain-specific language the space of policies can
be large, necessitating incomplete stochastic search such as genetic
algorithms. Yet the distillation remains computationally expensive. We
propose to instead use a transformer pre-trained on readily available
random percept-action data. Doing so speeds up distillation
substantially. We derive the transformer architecture via a neural
architecture search. Empirical results compare different distillation
methods and the influence of the symbolic space on A-life population
dynamics through self-reflection and teaching and learning.

## 1 Introduction

While neural networks have enabled breakthrough performance in many
applications over the last decade ([Mnih et al., 2015](#bib.bib41))
their learned knowledge is encoded non-transparently via a large number
of trained parameters (weights). The training process itself can be
unstable and the learned knowledge cannot be compactly transferred
between different neural architectures. Neurosymbolic Artificial
Intelligence (AI) ([Chaudhuri et al., 2021](#bib.bib17); [Kautz,
2022](#bib.bib34); [Hitzler et al., 2022](#bib.bib29); [Garcez and Lamb,
2023](#bib.bib25)) seeks to address these three shortcomings by
complementing neurally represented knowledge with symbolically
represented knowledge. One way of doing so is distillation of neural
control policies: finding a compact symbolic policy of similar
behavior ([Verma et al., 2018](#bib.bib62)).

The distillation methods used in this process often come from the field
of symbolic regression ([Gulwani et al., 2017](#bib.bib27)) and involve
searching a space of programs ([Odena et al., 2020](#bib.bib42)).
Because the space of programs is usually large, incomplete stochastic
methods are utilized. In particular, stochastic search in the form of
simulated annealing ([Medeiros et al., 2022](#bib.bib39)) and genetic
algorithms (GA) ([Koza, 1992](#bib.bib36); [Cranmer et al.,
2020](#bib.bib20)) is common in program synthesis ([Bulitko et al.,
2022](#bib.bib14); [Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib65)) and neural
distillation ([Deproost et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)). While it has been
successfully used for neurosymbolic AI ([Udrescu and Tegmark,
2020](#bib.bib54); [Acharya et al., 2023](#bib.bib1)) it can be slow and
unreliable.

In this paper we adopt the idea of symbolic regression via pre-trained
transformers ([Biggio et al., 2021](#bib.bib7); [Vaswani et al.,
2017](#bib.bib61)) and synthesize compact symbolic policies of neural
agents evolved within A-life. The transformer receives a set of an
agent’s percepts and the actions taken by the agent and produces a
series of tokens describing a symbolic policy that approximately matches
the percept-action pairs. We use a neural architecture search ([Chen
et al., 2021](#bib.bib18)) to automatically find an effective neural
architecture for the transformer. Once trained the transformer can be
enhanced with bagging ([Kamienny et al., 2022](#bib.bib33)) and beam
search ([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib61)). We show that not only can
it surpass the accuracy of symbolic policies produced by genetic
algorithms but its distillation is also substantially faster.

The distillation allows a population of A-life agents to exchange their
knowledge in a compact, symbolic fashion. Such knowledge sharing enables
population-level learning. The compact nature of the symbolic policies
also makes them human-readable which may lead to theoretical
verifiability ([Zhu et al., 2019](#bib.bib74); [Bastani et al.,
2018](#bib.bib4); [Stevens et al., 2023](#bib.bib52)) and human
insight ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib66)). The symbolic policies can
also be used to stabilize the neural training in an iterative
manner ([Verma et al., 2018](#bib.bib62); [Verma et al.,
2019](#bib.bib63)) and to communicate knowledge between agents with
different neural architectures ([Stanley and Miikkulainen,
2002](#bib.bib51)).

The paper makes the following contributions. We propose a method for
automatically designing and training a transformer for distilling neural
policies of A-life agents. Once trained the transformer surpasses the
distillation accuracy of a genetic algorithm on average while being
substantially faster. The short per-policy distillation time makes
teaching and learning within A-life more practical. In particular, we
consider the impact of the space of symbolic policies used for the
distillation on teaching and learning in the population. Finally, we
discuss human readability of symbolic policies. Our code and data are
available to other researchers upon request.

## 2 Problem Formulation

A neural control policy $`\pi`$ of an A-life agent maps the agent’s
percept $`p`$ to an action $`\pi(p)=a`$. The policy $`\pi`$ is not
directly available to the distiller which instead receives a set of
input (agent percept) and output (agent action) pairs:
$`\left\{(p_{i},\pi(p_{i}))\right\}`$. Then given a space $`M`$ of
symbolic policies the distillation task for agent policy $`\pi`$ is to
find a policy $`m^{*}\in M`$ with the maximum accuracy on the
percept-action set $`(p_{i},a_{i})`$:
$`m^{*}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits_{m\in M}\mathcal{A}(m,\left\{(p_{i},\pi(p_{i}))\right\})`$
where $`\mathcal{A}`$ is the percentage of percepts $`p_{i}`$ on which
the symbolic policy gives the same action as the agent’s neural policy:
$`m(p_{i})=\pi(p_{i})`$.

We evaluate our distillers on their ability to accurately distill neural
agents evolved in A-life. The corresponding performance measure is the
distillation accuracy. We generalize this definition to percept-action
pairs whose actions come from other sources (e.g., an unknown symbolic
policy). For instance, given a set of symbolic policies one can create a
set of percept-action pairs for each of them by running each policy on
random percepts. Then the distillation accuracy of a distiller is the
proportion of actions produced by a symbolic policy that match the
actions of the original policy.

We prefer our distiller to be constructed automatically. We also prefer
that it produces a compact symbolic policy to help humans with
readability and theoretical verifiability. Within A-life compact
symbolic policies are faster to transmit and interpret. Thus ideally the
symbolic policies produced by a distiller are more compact than the
agent’s neural policies that they approximate.

Finally, we consider how symbolic policies produced by a distiller and
used for teaching and learning within a population of autonomous agents
evolving in A-life affect the population dynamics (e.g., agent life
expectancy).

## 3 Related Work

Discovering a symbolic approximation of an unknown function is related
to program synthesis which seeks to find a symbolic program for a given
problem specification ([Alur et al., 2013](#bib.bib3)). Search-based
methods such as random sampling, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms
and inductive logic programming have been used ([Bulitko et al.,
2022](#bib.bib14); [Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib65); [Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib66); [Stevens et al., 2023](#bib.bib52)). Additionally
synthesized programs can be human-explainable and allow for theoretical
verifiability ([Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib66); [Stevens et al.,
2023](#bib.bib52)). Such synthesis methods can also be applied
iteratively with synthesized programs added to the domain-specific
language as the new building blocks ([Hernandez and Bulitko,
2021](#bib.bib28)). However, when searching the space of programs
program-synthesis methods commonly evaluate each candidate program by
running it on a data set. This is computationally expensive. The
stochastic nature of the search can also make it unreliable. The latter
can be remedied by running a portfolio of such searches and taking the
best of several runs ([Botea and Bulitko, 2025](#bib.bib8)) but the
computation costs increase even further.

A line of work proposes to aid the combinatorial symbolic search by
neural networks ([Menon et al., 2013](#bib.bib40); [Ellis et al.,
2018](#bib.bib22); [Valkov et al., 2018](#bib.bib56); [Shah et al.,
2020](#bib.bib44); [Ellis et al., 2021](#bib.bib23)). The neural
networks are trained on problem specification and program pairs and, in
general, a search in the symbolic space is still required which is
something we wish to eliminate in solving our problem.

Recent research has applied pre-trained transformers for symbolic
regression tasks demonstrating that they can efficiently synthesize
symbolic expressions, perform sequence-to-sequence program induction and
generalize across a variety of rule-based problems ([Biggio et al.,
2021](#bib.bib7); [Valipour et al., 2021](#bib.bib55); [Kamienny et al.,
2022](#bib.bib33); [Shojaee et al., 2023](#bib.bib45); [Lalande et al.,
2023](#bib.bib37); [Vastl et al., 2024](#bib.bib60); [Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib67)). Analysis reveals that a transformer’s internal
representations can capture recursive rules and variable binding
properties ([Brinkmann et al., 2024](#bib.bib10); [Wu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib71)). Neurosymbolic paradigms have emerged that combine
transformer-based symbolic regressors with evolutionary or
preference-based algorithms to enhance robustness when dealing with
limited or noisy data ([Jha et al., 2024](#bib.bib30); [Lalande et al.,
2023](#bib.bib37)). These methods often outperform traditional
search-based symbolic program synthesis in terms of efficiency and
accuracy. To the best of our knowledge none of these studies has been
applied to A-life-style multi-agent systems considered in this paper.

Large language models (LLMs) and transformers have been used in
agent-based modeling to control the agents ([Jimenez-Romero et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31)) and to capture the environment dynamics and agent
trajectories in a neural format ([Berkovich and Buehler,
2025](#bib.bib5); [Berkovich et al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Yuan et al.,
2021](#bib.bib72)). None of these tackles our problem of distilling an
agent neural policy into a symbolic approximation.

Work on rule inference trained transformers to produce cellular automata
rules given a target phenomenon ([Berkovich et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)).
While our problem is also to produce symbolic policies we seek to have
the policies approximate evolved neural policies of individual A-life
agents as opposed to global dynamics of the environment. Work on
modeling agents from observed trajectories used transformers to capture
influences of other agents’ policies which can then be used to condition
the controlled agent’s policy and improve its reinforcement
learning ([Wallace et al., 2025](#bib.bib64)). However, our problem is
to generate compact symbolic policies of individual neural agents in
A-life.

Finally, our effort in this paper to enable agents to teach and learn
from each other is related to the large body of work on explainable AI
where AI agents seek to explain their predictions ([Chakraborty et al.,
2017](#bib.bib16)), recommendations ([Zhang and Chen,
2020](#bib.bib73)), schedules ([Vasileiou et al., 2023](#bib.bib58)) or
plans ([Fox et al., 2017](#bib.bib24); [Kambhampati, 2019](#bib.bib32)).
When an AI agent is using machine learning (ML) it may be employing ML
representations that range from ones that can be analyzed easily (e.g.,
logistic regression and decision trees) to those that are more complex
and can highlight key regions in the input data ([Springenberg et al.,
2015](#bib.bib48)) or features ([Chen et al., 2014](#bib.bib19);
[Guidotti et al., 2018](#bib.bib26); [Chakraborty et al.,
2017](#bib.bib16)) relevant to the solution. Within the context of
symbolic planning and sequential decision making the problem is cast as
explainable planning ([Fox et al., 2017](#bib.bib24); [Kambhampati,
2019](#bib.bib32)). In this subarea a popular approach is one that
reconciles the models of the human and AI agents such that they both
understand the plan being explained ([Chakraborti et al.,
2017](#bib.bib15)). However, existing work ([Chakraborti et al.,
2017](#bib.bib15); [Sreedharan et al., 2018](#bib.bib49); [Sreedharan
et al., 2021](#bib.bib50); [Son et al., 2021](#bib.bib47); [Vasileiou
et al., 2021](#bib.bib57)) makes simplifying assumptions such as that
the AI agent has a complete and accurate mental model of the user and
that the explanations are one-shot interactions. Researchers have
started to address some of these limitations by generalizing the
framework to learn the mental model of users over time ([Tang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib53)) as well as to allow for conversational multi-shot
interactions ([Vasileiou et al., 2024](#bib.bib59)). Our problem can be
seen as a combination of both interpretable ML and explainable planning
where the neural policy of one agent is distilled into a symbolic
representation communicated to another agent to update its own neural
policy. Yet the work cited above has not been applied in A-life.

## 4 Proposed Approach

We adopt transformer-based symbolic regression ([Biggio et al.,
2021](#bib.bib7); [Valipour et al., 2021](#bib.bib55); [Kamienny et al.,
2022](#bib.bib33); [Shojaee et al., 2023](#bib.bib45); [Lalande et al.,
2023](#bib.bib37); [Vastl et al., 2024](#bib.bib60)) and apply it to the
problem of distilling neural control policies of A-life agents. While
some previous work used manually designed transformer
architectures ([Biggio et al., 2021](#bib.bib7); [Valipour et al.,
2021](#bib.bib55); [Kamienny et al., 2022](#bib.bib33); [Shojaee et al.,
2023](#bib.bib45); [Lalande et al., 2023](#bib.bib37); [Vastl et al.,
2024](#bib.bib60); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib67)) we apply automated
neural architecture search (NAS) in a space of transformer
architectures. To do so efficiently we combine [Chen et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib18)’s autoformers and [Bulitko et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib14)’s triage.

### 4.1 Neural Architecture Search

In line with work by [Chen et al. (2021)](#bib.bib18) we first define a
space of transformer architectures and initialize the supernet as the
largest architecture in that space. Then on each training iteration we
randomly select a subnet from the supernet and update its weights. We
continue to minimize the training loss of sampled subnets until the
validation loss no longer improves over a number of epochs. Once the
supernet is trained we run a GA over the space of transformer
architectures for a subnet to maximize a fitness function — the
distillation accuracy regularized by the subnet size.

### 4.2 Training the Transformer

Our transformers receive a set of an agent’s percept-action pairs and
produce a sequence of tokens representing a symbolic policy. For neural
A-life agents percept-action pairs are readily available. However, a
symbolic policy for each such neural agent is generally unavailable
since the evolution within A-life is entirely neural and does not
involve symbolic representations. Thus we do not use data from actual
A-life agents to train our transformer. Instead we draw a random
symbolic policy $`m`$ from the given space $`M`$ of policies and execute
$`m`$ on random agent percepts $`p_{i}`$, generating a set of
percept-action pairs $`\left\{(p_{i},m(p_{i}))\right\}`$. The set of
pairs forms the transformer input whereas the symbolic policy $`m`$,
expressed as a sequence of tokens in a domain-specific language,
constitutes the target output for the transformer. We collate such data
from many different policies $`m`$ to train the transformer.

This method allows us to train the transformer on a large and diverse
set of symbolic policies. This is conceptually similar to training large
language models (LLM) on text and code from web pages and code
repositories and then applying the trained LLM to queries from a
specific user.

To keep our symbolic policies compact and increase their
human-readability we define our own A-life-specific language to
represent such policies. Doing so renders open-source
transformers ([Kamienny et al., 2022](#bib.bib33); [Shojaee et al.,
2023](#bib.bib45)) not directly applicable.

## 5 Empirical Evaluation

We first present the A-life environment with evolving neural agents. We
then define the space of symbolic policies. Finally, we describe the
construction and training of the transformer. All parameters in our
empirical evaluation were chosen via preliminary experiments to balance
the scope of the experiments and the computational cost.

### 5.1 A-life

We evaluate our transformer-based distillation in an A-life environment
— a variant of the common predator-prey environment ([Ackley and
Littman, 1991](#bib.bib2); [Wilensky, 1997](#bib.bib68); [Bulitko
et al., 2017](#bib.bib11); [Bulitko et al., 2018a](#bib.bib12); [Bulitko
et al., 2018b](#bib.bib13); [Soares et al., 2018](#bib.bib46)).

![Refer to caption](2611.07254v1/figs/alife.png)

Figure 1: The A-life environment.

Our A-life agents exist on a rectangular grid map
(Figure [1](#S5.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 5.1 A-life ‣ 5 Empirical Evaluation ‣ Using Transformers for Teaching and Learning via Symbolic Distillation of Neural A-life Agents")).
Each grid cell is either closed (black squares in the figure) or open.
Open cells contain at most one agent (circles in the figure). Agents
have five actions available to them: the four moves in the cardinal
directions and the action of staying at the current location. Each agent
has an energy level. Existing, thinking (i.e., executing the neural
control policy) and taking actions all decrease an agent’s energy which
is replenished by eating grass (shades of green in the figure). When the
energy reaches $`0`$ the agent dies and is removed from the map. Grass
re-grows in each cell up to the maximum value of $`1`$. The re-growth
rate decays over time for a guaranteed eventual population extinction.

The A-life simulation proceeds in discrete time steps. On each time step
the agents (sorted in a random order) perceive the environment around
them and run their neural control policies. A policy is a neural network
which maps eleven inputs that collectively form the agent’s percept to
one of the five actions. The eleven inputs are: the grass levels in the
four cardinal directions up to a sight radius, inversely scaled by their
distance to the agent (inputs 1-4); grass level in the agent’s position
(input 5); presence of other agents in the four cardinal directions up
to a sight radius, inversely scaled by their distance to the agent
(inputs 6-9); the agent’s energy (input 10) and the agent’s normalized
age (input 11). All eleven inputs are in the range of $`[0,1]`$.

While our testbed allows for the neural network architecture to evolve
over time, in this paper all agents share the same architecture where
the eleven input neurons are connected to eight hidden layers of five
neurons each and then connected to the five output neurons. The output
neuron with the highest activation indicates the action to take. The
network thus has $`300`$ learnable weights (connection and bias
weights). The weights are specified in the agent’s genome at birth but
can be updated within the agent’s lifetime through self-reflection
and/or teaching and learning.

Reproduction is asexual. A parental agent has an offspring when it is
old enough, has enough energy and the current number of agents does not
exceed a pre-specified maximum. The parent gives a part of its energy to
the newborn. The offspring’s genes are created by copying the weights of
the parent and then perturbing them with random values (mutation). Note
that similarly to other A-life simulations and unlike the standard GA,
our simulation is asynchronous and parents and their children can
co-exist. Furthermore, there is no explicit fitness function to sort the
population by (as in GA). Finally, the population size is not fixed and
can grow up to the maximum size allowed by the specifications. An A-life
run ends when all agents die.

### 5.2 Symbolic Policies

We use an A-life-specific language to define the space $`M`$ of symbolic
policies. Each symbolic policy is a formula defined by the following
context-free grammar:

The tokens
$`g_{\leftarrow},g_{\uparrow},g_{\rightarrow},g_{\downarrow},g_{+},a_{\leftarrow},a_{\uparrow},a_{\rightarrow},a_{\downarrow},e,a`$
form the percept of an A-life agent. They represent the grass levels in
the four cardinal directions around the agent
($`g_{\leftarrow},g_{\uparrow},g_{\rightarrow},g_{\downarrow}`$), the
grass at the agent’s position ($`g_{+}`$), the presence of other agents
($`a_{\leftarrow},a_{\uparrow},a_{\rightarrow},a_{\downarrow}`$), the
agent’s energy $`e`$ and the agent’s age $`a`$. Both the grass and the
presence of other agents are inversely scaled by the distance.

The production $`T`$ lists the terminal tokens: the agent’s percepts
above and the numeric constants: $`C=\{0,0.1,\ldots,1\}`$. The
production $`O_{1}`$ lists unary operators (e.g., the absolute value
$`|S|`$). The production $`O_{2}`$ lists binary operators and so on
until $`O_{5}`$, which lists five-argument operators such as
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits(S,S,S,S,S)`$.

In line with recent work on transformers trained for symbolic
regression ([Kamienny et al., 2022](#bib.bib33); [Shojaee et al.,
2023](#bib.bib45); [Lalande et al., 2023](#bib.bib37); [Valipour et al.,
2021](#bib.bib55); [Vastl et al., 2024](#bib.bib60); [Biggio et al.,
2021](#bib.bib7)) we use the natural sequence-of-tokens notation for our
symbolic policies. For instance, the sequence of six tokens
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits g_{\leftarrow}\ g_{\uparrow}\ g_{\rightarrow}\ g_{\downarrow}\ g_{+}\ `$
represents the formula
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits\left(g_{\leftarrow},g_{\uparrow},g_{\rightarrow},g_{\downarrow},g_{+}\right)`$
which returns the index of the grid cell around the agent with the
highest grass level. We index the agent’s actions left, up, right, down,
stay by $`1,\ldots,5`$ so the formula above is the symbolic
always-move-to-the-highest-grass-neighbor policy.

If on some inputs a formula produces an output outside of the set
$`\{1,\ldots,5\}`$ of action indices the symbolic policy outputs the
action index closest to the output.

### 5.3 Training Data for the Transformer

To form the training set we generated random sequences of tokens from
the grammar. For each sequence, tokens not used in computing the output
were removed. If the resulting sequence was empty or exceeded $`30`$
tokens, it was discarded.

We then formed a set of $`1,\!024`$ percepts with each of the eleven
inputs uniformly randomly drawn from $`[0,1]`$. If on any of the
percepts the formula did not return a numeric value or returned a value
outside of the action index range $`[1,5]`$ the formula was discarded.
We additionally discarded formulae whose output was the same for over
$`99\%`$ of the percepts.

Removing duplicate formulae, this process yielded a set of $`450,\!100`$
unique formulae to train the transformer on. For each formula the
transformer input was a $`64\times 12`$ matrix of the percept-action
pairs. In each row of the matrix the first eleven columns describe a
percept and the twelfth column lists the action produced by the formula
on the percept. The transformer output was the sequence of tokens
representing the formula. We used $`5`$ random percept-action sets of
size $`64\times 12`$ each for training.

### 5.4 Defining the Transformer Architecture

We used the neural architecture search to find an appropriate
architecture for the transformer. The space of possible architectures is
defined in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.4 Defining the Transformer Architecture ‣ 5 Empirical Evaluation ‣ Using Transformers for Teaching and Learning via Symbolic Distillation of Neural A-life Agents"),
inspired by existing work ([Chen et al., 2021](#bib.bib18)). The encoder
and decoder had the same components with the same space of dimensions.

| -------------------------- | ------------ | ------------------ |

| number of heads            | $`1`$        | $`4`$              |

Percept-action pairs in our training data are permutation invariant.
Thus we removed the positional embeddings from the encoder ([Kamienny
et al., 2022](#bib.bib33)). To blend the column features we instead
added a standard feedforward layer before the encoder ([Lalande et al.,
2023](#bib.bib37)).

In addition to the previously listed tokens we added three more tokens
for transformer training: a beginning-of-sequence token
$`\langle bos\rangle`$, an end-of-sequence token $`\langle eos\rangle`$
and the padding token $`\langle pad\rangle`$. During training we added
$`\langle bos\rangle`$ at the beginning of each formula that served as
input for the decoder along with the percept-action pairs for the
encoder and we added a $`\langle eos\rangle`$ token at the end of each
of the target formulae so that the transformer can predict the next
token based on the percept-action pairs and previous tokens as context.

We used the Adam optimizer ([Kingma and Ba, 2015](#bib.bib35)) with a
learning rate of $`10^{-4}`$, first and second moment decay constants of
$`0.9`$ and $`0.999`$ respectively and $`\epsilon`$ of $`10^{-8}`$. We
stopped training when the validation loss did not decrease for over five
epochs. We used a mini batch size of $`1,\!024`$. We randomly
partitioned the data into three subsets: $`80\%`$ for training, $`10\%`$
for validation during training (to compute the validation loss for early
termination) and $`10\%`$ for testing.

We brought all formulae to the length of $`30`$ by adding padding tokens
$`\langle pad\rangle`$ at the end of shorter formulae. This allowed us
to package the formulae into a tensor. When computing gradients during
training the padding tokens do not affect gradient computations as they
are ignored.

The transformer architecture search started with training the largest
transformer (supernet) in our architecture space with $`17.3`$ million
learnable parameters
(Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.4 Defining the Transformer Architecture ‣ 5 Empirical Evaluation ‣ Using Transformers for Teaching and Learning via Symbolic Distillation of Neural A-life Agents")).
During the training we did not train the supernet as a whole but rather
trained a randomly selected subnet and updated its weights on each
iteration ([Chen et al., 2021](#bib.bib18)). We continued the training
for $`90`$ epochs which took $`14.5`$ hours on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU
with 80GB VRAM.

Once the supernet was trained we ran a GA with the population size of
$`20`$ to find a subnet with a high distillation fitness regularized by
the number of neurons. The fitness computation was triaged ([Bulitko
et al., 2022](#bib.bib14)): a candidate subnet was first evaluated on
$`2,\!048`$ percept-action pairs. If the candidate was fitter than the
previous champion it was subsequently evaluated on $`10\%`$ of the
percept-action pairs. Otherwise it was discarded. When the champion
fitness did not improve over $`20`$ generations the GA terminated.

The GA ran for about $`1.5`$ minutes and found a subnet
(Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.4 Defining the Transformer Architecture ‣ 5 Empirical Evaluation ‣ Using Transformers for Teaching and Learning via Symbolic Distillation of Neural A-life Agents"))
of $`5.4`$ million learnable parameters (about $`32\%`$ of the
supernet). The subnet then inherited the weights from the supernet and
was trained on the training data set for another $`69`$ epochs with
validation patience of $`5`$ which took $`13.5`$ hours. We found that
the accuracy of the evolved subnet is higher when training starts with
inherited weights rather than from scratch.

### 5.5 Distillation of Neural Agents

We evaluated our trained transformer on distilling A-life’s neural
policies into symbolic policies and compared the performance to
GA ([Bulitko et al., 2022](#bib.bib14); [Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib65))
and decision trees.

To form the data set we conducted $`1,\!024`$ A-life runs. A total of
$`5,\!613,\!559`$ agents lived during those runs. Within each A-life run
early agents have random neural policies. Thus to give evolution time to
work we discarded all agents who died before time step $`105`$. To focus
on more successful agents we additionally discarded all agents who lived
fewer than $`105`$ time steps or made fewer than $`75\%`$
energy-gain-maximizing action choices or whose neural policy’s most
common action on random percepts accounted for at least $`90\%`$ of
actions taken. The filtering process left us with $`243`$ agents.

For each of the $`243`$ agents we ran its neural policy on $`1,\!024`$
random percepts and recorded the action given by the neural policy. We
then drew from the $`1,\!024`$ percept-action pairs $`512`$ for
distillation and another $`512`$ for testing the resulting symbolic
policies. We then compared four distillation methods on this data set as
follows.

Decision Trees. We fitted a classification and regression tree
(CART) ([Breiman et al., 1984](#bib.bib9)) to the percept-action pairs
using MATLAB’s built-in fitctree function. The tree partitions the
percept space by recursively selecting the split that minimizes the Gini
impurity and classifies each leaf region with the plurality action. We
evaluated three tree sizes by limiting the maximum number of splits to
$`5,10`$ and $`20`$.

GA. We ran genetic algorithms with a population of $`384`$ candidate
formulae. On each generation the formulae were evaluated for their
distillation accuracy on the percept-action data set. The fitness of
each formula was its accuracy regularized by the number of tokens in the
formula. The fittest $`128`$ formulae were selected as parents and
produced $`128`$ offspring formulae via crossover and mutation. Each
offspring was added to the population twice: as is and with tokens not
used in calculation of the formula value removed. A running fitness
champion was maintained. If the fitness did not improve over $`100`$
generations the population was reinitialized. The process terminated
when the time limit was reached and the fitness champion was returned as
the result of the distillation. We used six different time limits:
$`5,10,30`$ seconds and $`1,3,45`$ minutes.

Portfolio GA. Given the stochastic nature of the GA we also ran a
portfolio GA with a portfolio of size four. Each member of the portfolio
was the previously described GA but run with a different random number
generator seed. When all four GA runs finished we selected the formula
with the highest distillation fitness.

Transformer. When using the transformer for distillation besides the
percept-action pairs we gave it a starting token $`\langle bos\rangle`$
as the context. When given a context sequence of any length the
transformer returned a sequence of the same length with the last token
being the newly predicted token. Each new token was appended to the
previous context sequence for the transformer to predict the next token.
The process proceeded until either the transformer predicted an
$`\langle eos\rangle`$ token or the length of the sequence reached its
maximum length. During distillation we randomly sampled $`64`$ from the
given $`512`$ percept-action pairs as the input for the transformer to
predict a formula, similar to the bagging trick described in existing
work ([Kamienny et al., 2022](#bib.bib33); [Shojaee et al.,
2023](#bib.bib45); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib67)). We applied beam
search with width $`1`$ and $`5`$ ([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib61))
to investigate the effect of beam width on accuracy.

Figure 2: Distillation of percept-action pairs of A-life agents.

Observations. The transformer with beam search with beam widths of $`1`$
and $`5`$ had the average distillation times of $`0.63`$ and $`4.08`$
seconds and their average distillation accuracy on the test set
surpassed any GA including that with the $`45`$-minute time limit
(Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.5 Distillation of Neural Agents ‣ 5 Empirical Evaluation ‣ Using Transformers for Teaching and Learning via Symbolic Distillation of Neural A-life Agents")).

Human readability of symbolic policies. Both the GA and the transformer
tend to produce symbolic policies of similar length. We will now discuss
their human readability on a specific example from a preliminary
experiment. Consider an evolved A-life agent that lived for $`434`$ time
steps and died at time step $`890`$. Its actions on random percepts were
diverse with the most common action constituting $`27\%`$ of all
actions.

When run on a set of random percepts and the corresponding actions of
the agent’s neural policy, the transformer output the symbolic policy
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits(g_{\leftarrow},g_{\uparrow},g_{\rightarrow},a)`$
which is readily understood as go left/up/right when that’s the
direction with the most grass and the agent’s normalized age is below
that grass amount. Go down when the agent’s normalized age becomes
higher than the three grass amounts. This symbolic policy has the
accuracy of about $`99\%`$ on the set of $`512`$ random percepts. In
fact only three of the agent’s $`512`$ actions are mislabeled by the
symbolic policy: two right actions are mislabeled as left and one right
is mislabeled as up.

While a decision tree can be human-readable ([Rudin, 2019](#bib.bib43))
even a shallow tree with $`5`$ splits produces a branching structure
that must be traced path by path to understand the policy. In practice
this makes decision trees more difficult for a human to parse than
compact formula-based symbolic policies produced by the GA or the
transformer.

### 5.6 Policy Distillation within A-life

When distillation of agent neural policies takes place during an agent’s
lifetime within A-life it can affect both individual agents and the
population as a whole. In this section we consider two ways of using
distillation within A-life.

Self-reflection. Every $`25`$ time steps of its life each A-life agent
ran the distillation process if its energy was at least $`75\%`$ of the
maximum. To do so, the agent ran its neural policy on $`256`$ random
percepts and recorded the actions returned by the policy. It then
invoked the transformer to produce a symbolic policy approximating the
percept-action set and, therefore, its neural policy. The resulting
symbolic policy was then used to generate $`32,\!768`$ percept-action
pairs. These were in turn used to update the weights of its own neural
policy in an act of self-reflection. While perfectly accurate
distillation would likely result in minimal changes to the neural
weights, a symbolic bias in the distillation can affect the neural
weights severely.

Teaching and learning. An agent then engaged in teaching by distilling
its neural policy and sending the resulting formula to a random agent in
the population. The receiving agent updated its own neural policy in the
manner described above. This mechanism allows the agents to share their
control knowledge in a compact fashion as transmitting a short formula
representing the symbolic policy is faster and easier than transmitting
percept-action pairs or neural weights.

Symbolic subspaces. Self-reflection, teaching and learning pass an
agent’s neural knowledge through the symbolic space. In our A-life
environment this is the only mechanism by which the agents learn within
their lifetime. Therefore the learning bias imposed by the symbolic
space can in principle affect agents individually and their population
as a whole.

In a preliminary experiment with a smaller A-life environment we first
considered a trivial space of symbolic policies containing only a single
formula
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits\left(g_{\leftarrow},g_{\uparrow},g_{\rightarrow},g_{\downarrow},g_{+}\right)`$
which always takes the action in the direction of the most grass. Having
only a single symbolic policy imposes a strong learning bias on
self-reflection and teaching and learning. The results from $`32`$
A-life runs suggest that such biased self-reflection and teaching and
learning make the agents live longer and die later
(Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.6 Policy Distillation within A-life ‣ 5 Empirical Evaluation ‣ Using Transformers for Teaching and Learning via Symbolic Distillation of Neural A-life Agents")).

We then used the larger A-life environment described earlier in the
paper. We incrementally weakened the learning bias by allowing the
transformer to use progressively larger subspaces of the full space of
symbolic policies. The subspaces were:
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits\nolimits^{5}`$ which is the
space of all formulae starting with a $`5`$-argument
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits`$;
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits\nolimits^{5}\vee\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits\nolimits^{5}`$
which is the space of all formulae starting with a $`5`$-argument
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits`$ or
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits`$; and the full space of all
formulae in our grammar. These subspaces are strict subsets of each
other:
$`\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits\nolimits^{5}\subset\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits\nolimits^{5}\vee\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits\nolimits^{5}\subset\text{full space}`$.
All formulae were restricted to having at most $`30`$ tokens.

With the smaller and simpler A-life environment and the high symbolic
bias, combining teaching and learning with self-reflection affects the
medians for the number of agents lived and the time of death

However, removing the symbolic bias and using a larger, more complex
A-life environment eliminates the effects

## 6 Shortcomings & Future Work

Currently the effects of self-reflection and teaching and learning are
specific to the A-life environment with only the smaller, simpler A-life
environment and stronger symbolic bias showing significant effects.
Future work will investigate why the same trends are not observed with
the larger, more complex A-life environment and larger symbolic policy
spaces.

Despite the orders-of-magnitude reduction in run time relative to the
GA, running the transformer with $`5.4`$ million weights is still
notably more expensive than running the agent’s own $`300`$-weight
neural policy. Had the distillation not been free the A-life agents
would run out of energy and die after a single distillation. Thus, it
will be of interest to evolve distillers together with the agent control
policy within A-life evolution and see if smaller, more practical
distillers emerge similar to previous work in reinforcement
learning ([Landajuela et al., 2021](#bib.bib38)). Furthermore, our
agents currently self-reflect and teach involuntarily. Future work will
explore environments which are conducive to emergence of self-reflection
and teaching and learning as a voluntary action taken by agents.

Some of our preliminary results showed that the space of symbolic
policies can indeed affect population dynamics. Thus, it will be of
interest to evolve symbolic policy spaces in A-life alongside the agents
and their distillers. An interesting question is whether, for any agent,
there necessarily exists a task that the agent can perform but cannot
symbolically articulate (distill) due to the computational overhead of
such distillation. In particular if an articulation of a policy is a
computational shortcut to the original policy then computational
irreducibility may restrict the type of policies that can be
articulated ([Wolfram, 2002](#bib.bib69); [Wolfram, 2024](#bib.bib70);
[Berkovich and Buehler, 2025](#bib.bib5)). Consequently, learning agents
in A-life may have to use their own life experience to complement the
symbolic policy they receive from a teaching agent.

## 7 Conclusions

Neural control policies of A-life agents are generally opaque to an
external observer. Approximating them with compact symbolic policies can
help human understanding. We proposed to use a transformer to distill
neural policies of autonomous agents in A-life. Once trained, the
transformer surpasses the distillation accuracy of genetic algorithms
while being substantially faster. The fast distillation afforded by the
transformer makes it more practical to deploy it on the agents
themselves which can enable practical teaching and learning within a
multi-agent system.

# From Recursion to Convolution: Parallel Self-Improvement for Multi-Agent Systems

###### Abstract

## Abstract

Multi-agent self-improvement enables agents to improve through
interaction and feedback. However, sequential dependencies across agents
and refinement rounds can propagate errors, increase computational
costs, and limit parallelism. Moreover, refining individual features or
agents in isolation can overlook their dependencies and restrict
collective coordination. To address these challenges, we propose
ConvRSI, a framework for parallelizing multi-agent self-improvement
through convolution. ConvRSI couples intra-agent convolution, which
jointly refines related agent features, with inter-agent graph
convolution, which aggregates messages according to the collaboration
topology. Both stages operate in latent space, enabling parallel updates
without repeatedly decoding and re-encoding intermediate messages.
Experiments on MATH-500 show that ConvRSI achieves 5.04% performance
improvement while reducing 17.27% inference cost, and 45.11% latency
compared with RecursiveMAS. It also supports diverse collaboration
patterns by representing their topologies as adjacency matrices. These
results demonstrate the potential of convolutional co-evolution for
efficient multi-agent self-improvement.

###### keywords

Recursive Self-Improvement, Parallel Multi-Agent Systems, Graph
Convolutional Networks, Large Language Models

## 1 Introduction

Multi-agent systems (MAS) tackle complex tasks by coordinating agents
with complementary roles, knowledge, and reasoning capabilities. Beyond
collaboration among fixed agents, recent research explores how agents
can improve through their interactions and experience. Prior work
advances MAS through role-specific training feedback \[Motwani et al.,
2025\] and the co-evolution of question generation, solving, and judging
through self-play \[Chen et al., 2026\]. Recursive self-improvement
(RSI) extends this direction by feeding improvements back into the
system, enabling the updated system to guide subsequent rounds of
improvement. For example, RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\] iteratively
refines shared latent states and jointly optimizes agents at the system
level, while MASS \[Lee et al., 2026\] alternates workflow evolution
with model training on self-generated trajectories. Despite this
progress, scaling self-improvement to more agents and deeper iterations
faces inherent challenges in optimization stability, computational
efficiency, and the scope of collaboration, as discussed below.

First, deeper recursion propagates error. Each recursive round builds on
earlier outputs or updates, allowing mistakes to influence subsequent
decisions and become harder to correct. RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al.,
2026\] jointly optimizes agents across rounds but retains the dependency
chain through which errors can spread. Similar risks arise in other
forms of agent RSI: inaccurate self-evaluation in MASS \[Lee et al.,
2026\] can introduce flawed trajectories into subsequent training, while
mistaken memory updates in Recuris \[Yu et al., 2026\] can affect future
task execution. This raises a key question: can comparable improvement
be achieved with fewer sequential updates?

Second, sequential multi-agent computation limits efficiency and
parallelism. Multi-agent RSI repeatedly executes agents, exchanges
information, and evaluates updates, increasing computational costs as
agent count and iteration depth increase. Chain-like dependencies force
later updates to wait for earlier results, limiting the use of available
parallel computing resources. Such dependencies arise across recursive
collaboration links in RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\], the
generation–verification–refinement pipeline in MALT \[Motwani et al.,
2025\], and successive harness evolution cycles in ModularRSI \[Wu et
al., 2026\]. This motivates restructuring agent updates to reduce the
time and cost required to reach a target level of performance.

Third, individual feature updates and chain-like evolution limit global
coordination. Each agent’s behavior depends on related features,
including task information, environmental observations, roles,
experience, prompts, and messages. Refining one feature in isolation can
overlook its dependencies on others. Existing methods address different
aspects of improvement: Recuris \[Yu et al., 2026\] evolves memory,
ModularRSI \[Wu et al., 2026\] evolves and integrates harness modules,
MASS \[Lee et al., 2026\] improves workflows with model capabilities,
and RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\] refines hidden states across
agents. These leave room for a complementary design that jointly models
related features within each agent and aggregates information across
agents to support global evolution. In particular, chain-like evolution
passes information from one agent to the next, making distant agents
interact through intermediate updates. A broader approach would
aggregate agents for collective, rather than agent-by-agent evolution
\[Yu et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026\].

To address these challenges, we propose ConvRSI, a framework for
_parallelizing multi-agent self-improvement through convolution_. The
idea is to replace portions of sequential recursive computation with
convolutional updates over agent features and multi-agent collaboration
graphs. This introduces a _space–time scaling perspective_: improvement
can be pursued not only by increasing temporal iteration depth, but also
by expanding spatial computation within each iteration. Rather than
simply adding more independent agents, ConvRSI uses structured
convolutional operations to coordinate parallel updates within and
across agents.

ConvRSI comprises two coupled stages of improvement. Intra-agent
convolution applies one-dimensional convolution to hidden-state
sequences representing an agent’s task, role, experience, prompt, and
messages. It jointly models local dependencies among these features,
enabling each agent to independently refine its message state through
the convolutional interactions. The features are _initialized once_ and
subsequently refined through convolution. Inter-agent convolution then
applies graph convolution to the updated agent messages according to the
collaboration topology. Through parallel matrix operations, it
aggregates information across connected agents. The resulting messages
are fed into the next intra-agent stage, coupling individual refinement
with collective evolution. Both stages operate on latent
representations, enabling efficient convolutional updates without
repeatedly decoding messages into text and re-encoding them. This
matrix-based design exposes parallelism across feature positions and
agents throughout evolution. Moreover, ConvRSI supports diverse
multi-agent collaboration patterns by representing their topologies as
adjacency matrices for inter-agent graph convolution.

Empirically, we evaluate ConvRSI on 2 benchmarks spanning math and
coding, using diverse model families and multi-agent collaboration
patterns, including sequential style and mixture style. Compared with
representative multi-agent and recursive baselines, ConvRSI improves
task performance while reducing the cost of self-improvement. In
particular, it achieves a 5.04% performance improvement, a 17.27%
reduction in inference cost, and a 45.11% inference speedup. At
comparable performance, ConvRSI achieves these gains with $`1.82\times`$
and $`1.80\times`$ inference speedups and generates 17.3% and 4.4% fewer
tokens per problem, demonstrating the efficiency of convolutional
co-evolution.

Our main contributions are:

- •
  We introduce a space–time perspective on multi-agent self-improvement,
  exploring how spatially parallel computation can reduce reliance on
  deep sequential updates.
- •
  We propose ConvRSI, which couples intra-agent convolution over related
  agent features with inter-agent graph convolution over collaboration
  topologies. Together, these stages support iterative individual
  refinement and collective evolution.
- •
  We evaluate ConvRSI across diverse benchmarks, model families, and
  collaboration patterns, measuring performance gains under matched time
  and computational costs, as well as reductions in time and cost at
  comparable performance.

## 2 Related Work

Recursive Self-Improvement of Agents. Recursive self-improvement enables
agents to improve their capabilities through feedback and accumulated
experience. Existing work explores different targets of improvement,
including model behavior, agent workflows, harness components, and
memory. Specifically, RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\] extends
recursive computation to multi-agent collaboration by iteratively
refining shared latent states and jointly optimizing agents through an
inner–outer learning procedure. MASS \[Lee et al., 2026\] alternates
multi-agent workflow optimization with model training on self-generated
trajectories, using collective behavior as a source of self-supervision.
MetaRSI \[Tan et al., 2026\] further explores meta-level
self-improvement by coordinating data, harness, and model updates and
adapting the strategy that schedules these updates. Beyond model-level
improvement, ModularRSI \[Wu et al., 2026\] evolves agent harness
modules using execution experience, Recuris \[Yu et al., 2026\] updates
agent memory based on feedback, and Dream-RSI \[Zheng et al., 2026\]
reuses discovery histories to improve exploration policies. Building on
RSI, ConvRSI reorganizes the improvement process through convolutional
updates, coupling individual agent refinement with collective evolution
while reducing reliance on sequential recursive steps.

Multi-Agent Training and Co-Evolution. Multi-agent training methods
improve collective performance by encouraging agents to develop
complementary capabilities and learn from their interactions. MALT
\[Motwani et al., 2025\] trains specialized agents for generation,
verification, and refinement, while MAPoRL \[Park et al., 2025\] applies
multi-agent reinforcement learning to improve collaboration among
language models. Multi-Agent Evolve \[Chen et al., 2026\] uses
interacting roles to generate training data and feedback for model
self-improvement. Related approaches explore the evolution of agent
populations, as in EvoAgent \[Yuan et al., 2025\], or the automatic
optimization of agentic workflows, as in AFlow \[Zhang et al., 2025\].
ConvRSI jointly refines features within each agent and uses a graph to
model collaboration among agents, enabling collective co-evolution.

Parallel and Efficient Multi-Agent Collaboration. Multi-agent systems
improve performance through collaboration, but additional agents
increase compute costs, while sequential interaction rounds increase
latency and limit parallelism. Recent work \[Wunderlich et al., 2026\]
compares the accuracy–compute trade-offs of self-consistency,
self-refinement, multi-agent debate, and agent aggregation. Parallel
self-refinement \[Wang et al., 2026\] generates multiple candidate
solutions concurrently and synthesizes them into an improved response.
Studies of multi-agent debate \[Smit et al., 2024\] further highlight
the importance of comparison with strong alternatives under matched
computational budgets. RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\] improves the
efficiency of recursive collaboration through latent-state
communication. Complementing existing approaches to efficient
collaboration, ConvRSI uses matrix-based convolution to enable parallel
updates across agent features and connected agents. Its latent-space
operations avoid repeated text decoding and re-encoding during
evolution.

## 3 Preliminary

### 3.1 Hidden States in Autoregressive Text Generation

An autoregressive language model generates a response one token at a
time. Given an input context $`x`$ and the previously generated tokens
$`y_{<t}`$, a causal Transformer \[Vaswani et al., 2017\] produces a
contextual hidden state $`\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}`$. An output
head maps this state to the distribution of the next token:

```math
p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})=\operatorname{softmax}\!\bigl(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{out}}\mathbf{h}_{t-1}+\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{out}}\bigr)_{y_{t}}. \tag{1}
```

The hidden state is a continuous representation of the preceding
context, whereas the output distribution is defined over discrete
vocabulary tokens. During teacher forcing, the model conditions on a
reference prefix $`y^{\star}_{<t}`$ and causal masking allows all
reference positions in a given sequence to be processed in one forward
pass. During free generation, the model instead conditions on its own
preceding outputs.

Hidden states also provide a communication medium for a multi-agent
system. Rather than decoding every intermediate thought into text, an
agent can transform its hidden states into continuous input embeddings
for another agent. Because agents may have different hidden widths and
embedding spaces, these representations require learned mappings before
they can be consumed by the receiving model.

### 3.2 Convolutional Computation

A one-dimensional convolution applies a shared local filter along an
ordered axis \[LeCun et al., 1998\]. For feature vectors
$`\mathbf{x}_{f}`$, its output at position $`f`$ can be written as

```math
\mathbf{z}_{f}=\sigma\!\left(\sum_{\delta\in\mathcal{K}}\mathbf{W}_{\delta}\mathbf{x}_{f+\delta}+\mathbf{b}\right), \tag{2}
```

where $`\mathcal{K}`$ denotes the filter offsets and $`\sigma`$ is a
nonlinearity. In our setting, the ordered axis contains different
feature types for the agent; each position on this axis corresponds to a
feature type.

A graph convolutional network (GCN) aggregates information in the
neighborhoods of a graph \[Kipf and Welling, 2017\]. Let
$`\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}`$ describe communication
between $`N`$ agents, using the convention that $`A_{ij}>0`$ means that
agent $`i`$ receives information from agent $`j`$. We add a self-loop at
each node and define

```math
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{I},\qquad\widetilde{D}_{ii}=\sum_{j}\widetilde{A}_{ij},\qquad\mathbf{S}=\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}. \tag{3}
```

Multiplying a matrix of agent representations by $`\mathbf{S}`$
aggregates the representations of each receiver’s senders. In our
implementation, this normalization can also be applied to a directed
adjacency. In that case, $`\mathbf{S}`$ is not required to be symmetric.

### 3.3 Convolutional Multi-Agent Co-Evolution

We define Convolutional Multi-Agent Co-Evolution as a sequence of
coupled representation updates. At the intra-agent convolution level,
each agent combines its context features into a local message using a
convolution over feature types. At the inter-agent convolution level, a
graph convolution updates the messages jointly along the communication
graph. The updated messages supply continuous prefixes for the current
round and message features for the next round.

RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\] performs multi-round collaboration in
latent space, using InnerLinks to form continuous inputs and OuterLinks
to transfer states between heterogeneous agents. ConvRSI augments this
collaboration with a graph-message recurrence. The preceding GCN output
supplies the recurrent message feature, and the underlying MAS routes
agent execution states through its latent links.

## 4 Methodology

Consider a MAS with $`N`$ agents and frozen language models
$`\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}`$ of native hidden widths $`H_{i}`$. The
adjacency $`\mathbf{A}`$ specifies which agents exchange convolutional
messages. The MAS supplies an execution schedule $`\Pi`$ for model
invocations and latent transfers, as well as a final output interface.
Figure 1 shows a feature stack with $`F=5`$ types ordered as _task_,
_role_, _system_, _experience_, and _message_. The first $`F-1`$ are
static text features, with experience optional. The message feature is
recurrent. In the full-sequence setting, each feature has $`K`$ slots.
The convolutional operators use a shared width $`D`$ and run for $`R`$
rounds.

Figure 1: The ConvRSI framework shown for one slot across N agents.
Intra-agent convolution produces a local message from each feature
stack. The GCN aggregates the stacked local messages and returns one
updated message per agent for the next round. The schematic uses
$`M^{t}`$ and $`M^{t+1}`$ for the input and output of the graph layer,
denoted by $`\mathbf{U}_{k}`$ and $`\mathbf{M}_{k}`$ in Eq. (7).

### 4.1 Convolutional Co-Evolution

#### Intra-Agent Convolution

Let $`\mathbf{Z}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times K\times H_{i}}`$ contain the
feature slots of agent $`i`$. The first round masks the message feature
and initializes its slots to zero. Subsequent rounds obtain it from the
preceding GCN output. An agent-specific projection maps each feature
slot to the common space as

```math
\mathbf{x}_{i,f,k}=\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\mathrm{in}}\operatorname{LN}\bigl(\mathbf{z}_{i,f,k}\bigr)+\mathbf{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}. \tag{4}
```

We apply the feature mask after projection and after adding learned
feature-type embeddings. At each slot $`k`$, we stack the resulting
vectors in feature order to form the $`F\times D`$ convolution input.

We then apply a channel-wise one-dimensional convolution along the
feature axis. For a tensor $`\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times D}`$, a
convolutional block with the default residual connection is

```math
\mathbf{V}^{\prime}=\operatorname{LN}\!\left[\mathbf{V}+\operatorname{GELU}\bigl(\operatorname{Conv}(\mathbf{V})\bigr)\right]. \tag{5}
```

The operator $`\operatorname{Conv}`$ filters the feature axis
independently in each channel, using a separate one-dimensional kernel
per channel. The configured default has a kernel of size five, dilation
one, and same padding. The convolution is applied independently to each
slot and exchanges information across feature types while preserving the
slot axis. In the optional no-residual variant, the block normalizes the
activated convolution output without adding $`\mathbf{V}`$; masks follow
the actual convolution windows.

Let $`\mathbf{v}_{i,f,k}^{\mathrm{conv}}`$ denote a feature vector after
the final convolutional block. We apply a softmax to the gate scores
$`g_{i}(\mathbf{v}_{i,f,k}^{\mathrm{conv}})`$ over the valid positions
$`\mathcal{A}_{i}`$, yielding weights $`\beta_{i,f,k}`$. A residual
multilayer perceptron and a normalized projection $`\psi_{i}`$ transform
the pooled vector
$`\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{i,k}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}\beta_{i,f,k}\mathbf{v}_{i,f,k}^{\mathrm{conv}}`$
into a local message

```math
\mathbf{u}_{i,k}=\psi_{i}\!\left(\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{i,k}+\operatorname{MLP}_{i}(\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{i,k})\right). \tag{6}
```

We denote the full local operator, from feature projection to message
readout, by $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$. Each agent has its own parameters and
reuses them across rounds.

#### Inter-Agent Convolution

For each slot, we stack the local messages into
$`\mathbf{U}_{k}=[\mathbf{u}_{1,k};\ldots;\mathbf{u}_{N,k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}`$.
One GCN layer updates all nodes using the same pre-update matrix:

```math
\mathbf{M}_{k}=\operatorname{LN}\!\left[\operatorname{GELU}\!\left(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{U}_{k}\mathbf{W}_{g}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{b}_{g}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right]. \tag{7}
```

The $`i`$-th row, $`\mathbf{m}_{i,k}`$, is the updated graph message for
agent $`i`$. The vector $`\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}`$
broadcasts the zero-initialized learnable bias $`\mathbf{b}_{g}`$ to
every agent. Layer normalization acts on the $`D`$ channels of each row.
The graph weights are shared among agents and rounds. Under Eq. (3),
each agent aggregates the senders selected by $`\mathbf{A}`$ and its own
message via a self-loop. When slots are padded, aggregation uses the
active nodes at that slot. The GCN updates those nodes synchronously.

### 4.2 Parallel ConvRSI Training

Figure 2 shows how the operators in Fig. 1 are trained. Inner training
learns to construct prefixes from each agent’s local messages. Outer
training jointly optimizes the graph-message recurrence and the latent
transfer modules.

Figure 2: The ConvRSI training pipeline. Inner training uses independent
agent objectives. Outer training can compute prefixes for all rounds
through the convolutional recurrence before the collaboration forwards.
The collaboration consumes the prefix for each round and computes
cross-entropy at the final output. Flames highlight trainable components
and snowflakes mark frozen language models.

#### Training within Parallel Agents

Each static component $`x_{i,f}`$ is encoded independently by its
agent’s frozen language model. Let

```math
\mathbf{H}_{i,f}=\operatorname{sg}\!\left[\mathcal{F}_{i}(\operatorname{Emb}_{i}(x_{i,f}))\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{i,f}\times H_{i}},
```

where $`\operatorname{sg}`$ stops gradients through this static backbone
computation. A trainable, agent-specific slot reader converts all valid
hidden positions into $`K`$ content-dependent slots:

```math
\displaystyle\alpha_{i,f,\ell,k} \displaystyle=\operatorname{softmax}_{\ell\in\mathcal{T}_{i,f}}\!\left(\bigl[\mathbf{W}_{i}^{\mathrm{st}}\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf{h}_{i,f,\ell})\bigr]_{k}\right),
```

```math
\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{i,f,k} \displaystyle=\operatorname{LN}\!\left(\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{T}_{i,f}}\alpha_{i,f,\ell,k}\mathbf{h}_{i,f,\ell}+\mathbf{e}_{i,k}^{\mathrm{st}}\right), \tag{8}
```

where $`\mathcal{T}_{i,f}`$ contains the non-padding positions. Each
agent shares its slot reader across its static text components. We
compute these slots once per example in a system forward pass and reuse
them across the convolutional rounds.

In Inner training, each agent applies $`\mathcal{C}_{i}`$ once with a
masked message and maps the $`K`$ resulting local messages to $`K`$ soft
prefix tokens. Its frozen LLM reads the prefix and predicts the local
reference tokens under teacher forcing. The $`N`$ losses in Eq. (11) are
independent, so the agents can train concurrently.

#### Parallel Graph Matrix Convolution across Agents

In Outer training, all agents can compute their local messages
concurrently within a round. Stacking them yields a tensor of shape
$`[B,N,K,D]`$ for a minibatch of $`B`$ examples. We apply Eq. (7) as a
batched graph operation on the $`BK`$ matrices of shape $`N\times D`$.
When the graph and node masks are fixed, the same normalization can be
reused.

The superscript $`(r)`$ now indexes states and prefixes that vary by
round. Let $`\mathcal{Q}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{H_{i}}`$ map a
shared graph message to the input space of agent $`i`$. It gives the
recurrent feature at $`r>1`$ and the prefix at every round as

```math
\mathbf{z}_{i,\mathrm{msg},k}^{(r)}=\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\mathbf{m}_{i,k}^{(r-1)}),\qquad\mathbf{p}_{i,k}^{(r)}=\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\mathbf{m}_{i,k}^{(r)}). \tag{9}
```

Let $`\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{static}}`$ collect the static slots and
$`\mathbf{M}^{(r)}`$ collect the updated graph messages over all slots.
We write $`\mathcal{C}`$ for the local operators applied to their
respective agents, $`\mathcal{Q}`$ for the corresponding
$`\mathcal{Q}_{i}`$ applied to each graph row, and
$`\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{A}}`$ for the slot-wise graph update in Eq. (7).
For $`r>1`$, the messages evolve as

```math
\mathbf{M}^{(r)}=\mathcal{G}_{\mathbf{A}}\!\left(\mathcal{C}\!\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{static}},\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{M}^{(r-1)})\right)\right). \tag{10}
```

The first round uses the masked zero message. The recurrence requires
only the cached static slots and the previous GCN output. As shown in
the lower left of Fig. 2, we can run the convolutional rounds in order
before the scheduled LLM forwards and store the resulting $`K`$-token
prefix $`\mathbf{p}_{i}^{(r)}=(\mathbf{p}_{i,k}^{(r)})_{k=1}^{K}`$ for
each agent and round.

#### Learning Convolution as a Whole

When $`\Pi`$ invokes agent $`i`$ in round $`r`$, its LLM reads the
prefix $`\mathbf{p}_{i}^{(r)}`$ before its chat input and consumes the
latent states supplied by the schedule. For a transfer from $`i`$ to
$`j`$, the source InnerLink $`\mathcal{I}_{i}`$ and the edge-specific
OuterLink $`\mathcal{O}_{i\to j}`$ map output hidden states to the
receiver’s input space, yielding
$`\mathbf{V}_{i\to j}^{(r)}=\mathcal{O}_{i\to j}(\mathcal{I}_{i}(\mathbf{H}_{i,\mathrm{out}}^{(r)}))`$.
The schedule governs which states are read within a round and which are
carried forward. Invocations with independent inputs can run
concurrently. The latent links share parameters across rounds.

Each teacher-forced invocation obtains the reference-conditioned hidden
states in one forward pass. A configurable token budget caps the length
of each transferred sequence during training. The final output yields
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}`$ in Eq. (11). The stored prefixes retain
their computation graph, so the final loss backpropagates through the
graph-message recurrence, the frozen backbones, and the differentiable
latent transfers.

#### Training Objectives

The local and final token-level cross-entropy objectives are

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in},i}=\operatorname{CE}(\mathbf{P}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\star}),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}=\operatorname{CE}(\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{out}}^{(R)},\mathbf{y}^{\star}). \tag{11}
```

Here $`\mathbf{P}_{i}`$ and $`\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{out}}^{(R)}`$ are
predicted vocabulary distributions at the local and final output
positions. The corresponding targets are the reference token IDs
$`\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\star}`$ and $`\mathbf{y}^{\star}`$. We average CE
over supervised output tokens and then over examples. The Inner loss
updates each agent’s slot reader, local operator, and prefix map. The
Outer loss jointly updates these modules, the GCN, and the OuterLinks,
with frozen backbones and InnerLinks.

#### Collaboration Patterns

The same convolutional operators can be applied to sequential, mixture,
distillation, and deliberation styles by constructing a communication
graph appropriate to the underlying MAS. These collaboration styles also
appear in RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\]. Table 1 gives
representative adjacency constructions without fixing agent roles or
backbone models. In every case, the degree matrix follows from
$`\widetilde{D}_{ii}=1+\sum_{j}A_{ij}`$; it is derived from the
adjacency. The execution schedule, output interface, and any
pattern-specific supervision are supplied by the MAS, while the
intra/inter-convolution definitions remain the same.

|              |                                                               |                                                                                                        |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Style        | Adjacency construction                                        | Degree after adding self-loops                                                                         |
| Sequential   | $`A_{i+1,i}=1`$, $`1\leq i<N`$                                | $`\widetilde{D}_{11}=1`$; $`\widetilde{D}_{ii}=2`$ for $`i>1`$                                         |
| Mixture      | $`A_{o,j}=w_{j}`$, $`j\in\mathcal{B}`$                        | $`\widetilde{D}_{oo}=1+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}}w_{j}`$; $`\widetilde{D}_{jj}=1`$ for $`j\in\mathcal{B}`$ |
| Distillation | Asymmetric influence: $`A_{ij}\neq A_{ji}`$ on selected pairs | $`\widetilde{D}_{ii}=1+\sum_{j}A_{ij}`$                                                                |
| Deliberation | Reciprocal exchange: $`A_{ij},A_{ji}>0`$ on interacting pairs | $`\widetilde{D}_{ii}=1+\sum_{j}A_{ij}`$                                                                |

Table 1: Representative communication graphs for different collaboration
styles. Rows of $`\mathbf{A}`$ are receivers and columns are senders.
Unspecified entries are zero. In the mixture construction, $`o`$ is a
designated receiving node, $`\mathcal{B}`$ contains the remaining nodes,
and $`w_{j}>0`$. These are abstract graph constructions.

#### Inference Stage

At inference, we encode the static inputs once, initialize the graph
message with a masked zero, and use the same recurrence to supply
prefixes to $`\Pi`$. For each intermediate invocation, the InnerLink
maps the last hidden state to the next continuous input. The OuterLinks
route the resulting hidden states according to $`\Pi`$. At the final
output interface, the model autoregressively decodes the answer.

### 4.3 Stability and Computational Complexity Analysis

#### Stability

For a fixed graph on the active agents, $`\mathbf{S}`$ is similar to the
row-stochastic matrix
$`\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}`$, so its spectral
radius is one. A directed graph can yield $`\|\mathbf{S}\|_{2}>1`$. For
fixed static slots and feature masks after initialization, let
$`L_{\mathcal{C}}`$, $`L_{\mathcal{Q}}`$, $`L_{\sigma}`$, and
$`L_{\mathrm{LN}}`$ bound the Lipschitz constants of the local operator
in its message input, the shared-to-native map, GELU, and LayerNorm. Two
trajectories of Eq. (10) satisfy

```math
\|\Delta\mathbf{M}^{(r)}\|_{F}\leq\kappa\|\Delta\mathbf{M}^{(r-1)}\|_{F},\qquad\kappa=L_{\mathrm{LN}}L_{\sigma}\|\mathbf{S}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{g}\|_{2}L_{\mathcal{C}}L_{\mathcal{Q}}. \tag{12}
```

Graph-message perturbations contract when $`\kappa<1`$. For fixed
teacher-forced references and latent sequence shapes under $`\Pi`$,
suppose the retained execution states $`\mathbf{V}^{(r)}`$ satisfy

```math
\|\Delta\mathbf{V}^{(r)}\|\leq a\|\Delta\mathbf{M}^{(r)}\|_{F}+b\|\Delta\mathbf{V}^{(r-1)}\| \tag{13}
```

for uniform constants $`a,b\geq 0`$. Then $`\max(\kappa,b)<1`$ is
sufficient for both state perturbations to vanish over successive
rounds.

#### Computational Complexity

With $`J`$ depthwise blocks of kernel width $`q`$, the per-round forward
costs of local message and prefix construction and dense graph
propagation are

```math
\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{local}} \displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(BK\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[FH_{i}D+JFqD+D^{2}+DH_{i}\right]\right), \tag{14}
```

```math
\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{graph}} \displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(BK\left[ND^{2}+N^{2}D\right]\right). \tag{15}
```

The local terms cover feature projection, depthwise convolution, pooled
channel transforms, and mapping to the native space. The graph terms
cover channel projection and dense aggregation. The $`R`$ rounds cost
$`R(C_{\mathrm{local}}+C_{\mathrm{graph}})`$.

Let $`C_{i}(L;B)`$ be the cost of an agent-$`i`$ backbone forward at
length $`L`$. Static encoding costs
$`\sum_{i}\sum_{f=1}^{F-1}C_{i}(L_{i,f};B)`$ per system forward, and
slot reading costs $`\mathcal{O}(BK\sum_{i,f}L_{i,f}H_{i})`$ over the
same features. Teacher-forced collaboration adds
$`\sum_{a}C_{i(a)}(L_{a};B)`$ over scheduled invocations $`a`$ with
agent $`i(a)`$ and input length $`L_{a}`$. At inference, $`Q_{\Pi}`$
intermediate invocations of $`T`$ latent steps each require $`Q_{\Pi}T`$
latent forwards before final text decoding. Storing all round prefixes
takes $`\mathcal{O}(BRK\sum_{i}H_{i})`$ entries, in addition to cached
feature slots and training activations.

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Datasets and metrics.

We use the Sequential-Math and Sequential-Code training datasets
released with RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\] for mathematical
reasoning and code generation, respectively. We evaluate mathematical
reasoning on MATH-500 \[Lightman et al., 2024; HuggingFaceH4, 2024\]
using final-answer accuracy and code generation on MBPP+ \[Liu et al.,
2023\] using pass@1, the fraction of problems for which a single
generated solution passes all benchmark tests. We report both metrics as
percentages averaged over four random seeds.

#### Baselines and backbones.

We compare ConvRSI with five baselines. The first two use the
Sequential-Light configuration of RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\]:
Recursive-TextMAS exchanges intermediate outputs as text, while
RecursiveMAS passes latent states between agents. Both use Qwen3-1.7B as
the planner, Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct as the critic, and
Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct as the solver. The remaining baselines are
ModularRSI \[Wu et al., 2026\], Hermes+ \[Xue et al., 2026\], and MASS
\[Lee et al., 2026\]. We adapt all three to use the Sequential-Light
solver backbone, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct \[Yang et al., 2025b\], in
place of the DeepSeek backbones used by ModularRSI and Hermes+ and the
27B Qwen backbone used by MASS.

#### Inference and evaluation.

We evaluate ConvRSI, Recursive-TextMAS, and RecursiveMAS at recursion
depths $`r\in\{1,2,3\}`$. Following RecursiveMAS \[Zou et al., 2026\],
we use top-$`p`$ sampling with $`p=0.95`$ on both benchmarks. For
MATH-500, we set the temperature to $`0.6`$ and the maximum generation
length to 2,000 tokens. For MBPP+, we use a temperature of $`0.2`$ and a
maximum of 4,000 tokens. Within each benchmark, all methods receive the
same problem statement in the input format required by each method and
use the same decoding settings and scoring procedure. We measure
inference efficiency on NVIDIA RTX 4090D GPUs and report total runtime
(seconds) and the mean number of generated tokens per problem.

### 5.2 Main Results: Performance Across Recursive Depths

We evaluate ConvRSI on MATH-500 and MBPP+ at recursion depths
$`r\in\{1,2,3\}`$. Table 2 reports MATH-500 accuracy, MBPP+ pass@1,
total inference time on NVIDIA RTX 4090D GPUs, and generated-token
counts. For the adapted ModularRSI, Hermes+, and MASS baselines, we use
Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct \[Yang et al., 2025b\], the solver backbone
in the Sequential-Light configuration. It replaces the DeepSeek
backbones in ModularRSI and Hermes+ and the 27B Qwen backbone in MASS.

#### Performance across recursion depths.

On MATH-500, ConvRSI improves from 71.6% accuracy at $`r=1`$ to 72.4% at
$`r=2`$ and 75.0% at $`r=3`$. Its lead over the better of
Recursive-TextMAS and RecursiveMAS grows from 0.2 to 1.4 to 3.6
percentage points across the three depths. Over the same range,
RecursiveMAS improves from 70.1% to 71.4%, while Recursive-TextMAS
declines from 71.4% to 69.6%. On MBPP+, Recursive-TextMAS leads at
$`r=1`$ with 30.4% pass@1, 0.4 points above ConvRSI. ConvRSI overtakes
both recursive baselines at $`r=2`$ and reaches 32.9% at $`r=3`$, 0.8
points above RecursiveMAS and 3.8 points above Recursive-TextMAS.

#### Inference efficiency.

ConvRSI completes both benchmarks in less time and generates fewer
tokens than either recursive baseline at every depth. At $`r=3`$, it
completes MATH-500 in 1,459 s, compared with 2,658 s for RecursiveMAS
and 4,721 s for Recursive-TextMAS, yielding $`1.82\times`$ and
$`3.24\times`$ speedups, respectively. On MBPP+, the corresponding
runtimes are 695 s, 1,249 s, and 3,575 s, yielding $`1.80\times`$ and
$`5.14\times`$ speedups. At this depth, ConvRSI generates 17.3% fewer
tokens on MATH-500 and 4.4% fewer on MBPP+ than RecursiveMAS.

### 5.5 Efficiency Analysis

#### Performance at a comparable runtime.

Figure 3 shows how much additional time each method spends as recursion
depth increases and how its score changes. On MATH-500, three-round
ConvRSI reaches 75.0% accuracy in 1,459 s. This is 4.9 percentage points
above one-round RecursiveMAS (70.1%) for only 50 s of additional runtime
over the full benchmark. On MBPP+, three-round ConvRSI reaches 32.9%
pass@1 in 695 s, finishing sooner than either one-round baseline:
RecursiveMAS reaches 29.1% in 776 s and Recursive-TextMAS reaches 30.4%
in 1,489 s.

#### How inference cost grows with depth.

Figure 4 compares each method with its own one-round configuration. On

these depths ($`-2.3\%`$ on MATH-500 and $`+4.5\%`$ on MBPP+), whereas
they more than double for Recursive-TextMAS on both benchmarks.

## 6 Conclusion

We presented ConvRSI, a framework that restructures multi-agent
self-improvement through coupled intra-agent and inter-agent
convolution. By jointly refining agent features and aggregating messages
across a collaboration graph, ConvRSI supports individual refinement and
collective evolution in latent space. Its matrix-based operations expose
opportunities for parallel computation while accommodating different
multi-agent collaboration patterns. Experiments show that ConvRSI
improves task performance while reducing computational costs and latency
across different multi-agent collaboration patterns. Future work will
explore more diverse agent collaboration patterns and extend ConvRSI to
large-scale agent swarms.

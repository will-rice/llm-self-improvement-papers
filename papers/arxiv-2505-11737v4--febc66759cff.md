###### Abstract

While Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive
capabilities, their output quality remains inconsistent across various
application scenarios, making it difficult to identify trustworthy
responses, especially in complex tasks requiring multi-step reasoning.
In this paper, we propose a Token-level Uncertainty estimation framework
for Reasoning (TokUR) that enables LLMs to self-assess and self-improve
their responses in mathematical reasoning. Specifically, we introduce
low-rank random weight perturbation during LLM decoding to generate
predictive distributions for token-level uncertainty estimation, and we
aggregate these uncertainty quantities to capture the semantic
uncertainty of generated responses. Experiments on mathematical
reasoning datasets of varying difficulty demonstrate that TokUR exhibits
a strong correlation with answer correctness and model robustness, and
the uncertainty signals produced by TokUR can be leveraged to enhance
the model’s reasoning performance at test time. These results highlight
the effectiveness of TokUR as a principled and scalable approach for
improving the reliability and interpretability of LLMs in challenging
reasoning tasks. The source code is avaliable at
[https://github.com/Wang-ML-Lab/TokUR](https://github.com/Wang-ML-Lab/TokUR).

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities
in various reasoning tasks [Wei et al. (2022a)](#bib.bib10), [Wang et
al. (2022)](#bib.bib48), [Chung et al. (2024)](#bib.bib49), [Guo et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib25), yet they often struggle to reliably assess the
quality of their own responses [Xiong et al. (2023)](#bib.bib15), [Tian
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib9), [Kapoor et al. (2024)](#bib.bib13), [Liu et
al. (2024)](#bib.bib50), [Zhang and Zhang (2025)](#bib.bib39), [Da et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib51), [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib32). This
limitation becomes particularly evident in complex reasoning scenarios
where models may generate seemingly convincing but incorrect solutions
without indicating uncertainty.

Beyond the dominant body of uncertainty estimation methods that largely
focus on short-form question answering [Zhang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib52), [Yadkori et al. (2024)](#bib.bib14) and
classification tasks [Yang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6), [Wang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib16), [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24), two main
approaches have been explored for the more challenging setting of
sequence uncertainty estimation: (i) Query-level methods [Gal and others
(2016)](#bib.bib58), [Osband et al. (2023)](#bib.bib54), [Hou et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib57), despite their solid theoretical foundation,
estimate uncertainties $`{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ with
respect to _input prompts $`{\bm{x}}`$_ alone, without evaluating _the
quality of specific generated responses $`{\bm{y}}`$_ conditioned on
those inputs (see
Sec. [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")).
Besides, these methods require marginalization over the entire output
space $`{\mathbf{y}}`$; this becomes intractable as sequence length
grows. (ii) Response-level methods [Murray and Chiang
(2018)](#bib.bib70), [Malinin and Gales (2021)](#bib.bib27), [Kadavath
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib8), typically variants of log-probabilities, have
shown empirical success but lack strong theoretical grounding [Kuhn et
al. (2023)](#bib.bib18). As a result, the limitations of the
aforementioned methods in capturing response-specific uncertainty hinder
the deployment of LLMs in high-stakes reasoning tasks that demand
reliable self-assessment.

To address this challenge, we propose a principled framework, dubbed
Token-level Uncertainty estimation for Reasoning (TokUR), for estimating
the uncertainty of generated sequences by aggregating token-level
uncertainties based on random low-rank weight perturbation. TokUR
introduces carefully calibrated perturbations to the weights of
attention layers, creating an ensemble of model variants that enables
principled uncertainty estimation without requiring costly retraining or
extensive parameter updates. Building on this, we decompose the _total
uncertainty_ of each generated token into _aleatoric uncertainty_
(inherent randomness in the data) and _epistemic uncertainty_ (model
uncertainty about its parameters), providing a theoretically grounded
assessment of confidence across the generation process. We then
aggregate these token-level uncertainties to evaluate entire reasoning
responses, demonstrating both theoretical consistency with established
uncertainty principles and practical utility in downstream applications.

Empirically, TokUR enhances LLM reasoning in three key aspects: (i)
token-level _epistemic uncertainty_ effectively identifies incorrect
reasoning paths, outperforming baselines across three mathematical
reasoning benchmarks, (ii) TokUR excels at selecting high-quality
solutions from multiple candidates, and (iii) it functions as an
implicit reward to guide reasoning, improving accuracy when combined
with off-the-shelf test-time-scaling algorithms [Puri et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib43). In summary, our contributions are:

- •
  We introduce TokUR, a training-free token-level uncertainty estimation
  approach for LLM reasoning through low-rank weight perturbation,
  providing a principled decomposition of uncertainties with proven
  theoretical properties.
- •
  We demonstrate that epistemic uncertainty can serve as a good metric
  to measure the quality of generated reasoning paths, consistently
  outperforming conventional confidence metrics across diverse
  mathematical reasoning tasks.
- •
  We demonstrate practical applications¹¹ 1 We provide an implementation
  of our framework that is compatible with vLLM [Kwon et al.
  (2023)](#bib.bib47) for efficient deployment. of our uncertainty
  estimation framework: it improves reasoning performance through
  incorrect path detection, high-quality solution selection, and
  uncertainty-guided generation.

## 2 Preliminaries

In this section, we first introduce the notation used in the remaining
sections, and then review the key concepts of
uncertainties (Sec. [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"))
and existing Bayesian LLMs for downstream
adaptation (Sec. [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Bayesian Large Language Models ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")).

Notation. In this paper, scalars are denoted by lowercase
letters ($`x`$), vectors by lowercase bold-math letters ($`{\bm{x}}`$),
random vectors by lowercase boldface letters ($`{\mathbf{x}}`$), and
matrices by uppercase boldface letters ($`{\bm{X}}`$). We use
$`[m]=\{1,2,\cdots,m\}`$ to denote the set of consecutive integer
numbers from $`1`$ to $`m`$. Following convention, we use $`p`$ for
probability, $`\mathbb{E}`$ for expectation, $`{\mathcal{H}}`$ for
entropy, and $`{\mathcal{I}}`$ for mutual information. Specifically,
$`{\mathcal{H}}[{\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}]`$ denotes the conditional
entropy between _random variables_ $`{\mathbf{y}}`$ and
$`{\mathbf{x}}`$. We use
$`{\mathcal{H}}[p({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}}={\bm{x}})]`$ to denote the
_predictive entropy_ of the output variable conditioned on input
$`{\bm{x}}`$, with $`{\mathcal{H}}[p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})]`$ as a
shorthand notation when context is clear.

### 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation

Prediction with Bayesian Neural Networks. Bayesian Neural
Networks (BNNs) [Neal (2012)](#bib.bib22), [Hernández-Lobato and Adams
(2015)](#bib.bib23), [Gal and Ghahramani (2016)](#bib.bib7), [Blundell
et al. (2015)](#bib.bib2), [Wang and Yeung (2016)](#bib.bib20), [Wang et
al. (2016)](#bib.bib21), [Lakshminarayanan et al. (2017)](#bib.bib3),
[Wang and Yeung (2020)](#bib.bib12) predict responses and estimate their
uncertainties using the variational distribution
$`q({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})`$ that approximates the true weight
posterior $`p({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})`$. Given an input sequence
$`{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{L})\in\mathcal{X}`$, the probability of the
output sequence $`{\bm{y}}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{T})\in\mathcal{Y}`$ is
defined as marginalization over the parameters and estimated by Bayesian
Model Averaging (BMA) of size $`M`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})=\int p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})\,q({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})\,d{\bm{\theta}}\approx\tfrac{1}{M}\sum\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}^{(m)}),\quad{\bm{\theta}}^{(m)}\sim q({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}}).
``` |  | (1) |

Query-Level Uncertainty Estimation. Established techniques of
uncertainty estimation [Gal and others (2016)](#bib.bib58) mainly
quantify the uncertainty of input $`{\bm{x}}`$ (*query-level
uncertainty*) by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\mathcal{H}}[p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})]=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}\sim p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})}[-\log p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})].
``` |  | (2) |

In the context of
BNNs (Eqn. [1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")),
the predictive distribution of $`{\bm{y}}`$ is the marginalized
predictive distribution over the model parameters, and hence
Eqn. [2](#S2.E2 "In 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
is defined as *“total uncertainty”* [Gal and others (2016)](#bib.bib58),
[Depeweg et al. (2017)](#bib.bib66).

A model’s uncertainty about a specific input cannot be solely attributed
to the randomness of the approximate posterior
$`q({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})`$, which is input-agnostic. For
instance, when faced with a query *“Name a city in the UK?”* [Yadkori et
al. (2024)](#bib.bib14), even if an infinite amount of data is observed
(eliminating the randomness of the model parameters), the uncertainty of
this question remains high, as there are many correct candidate answers.
Hence to distinguish different sources of uncertainty, *total
uncertainty* is decomposed into *epistemic uncertainty* and *aleatoric
uncertainty* [Gal and others (2016)](#bib.bib58):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\underbrace{\vphantom{E_{q({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}[{\mathcal{H}}[p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})]]}{\mathcal{H}}[p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})]}_{\text{Total Uncertainty}}=\underbrace{\vphantom{E_{q({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}[{\mathcal{H}}[p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})]]}\mathbb{E}_{q({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}[{\mathcal{H}}[p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})]]}_{\text{Aleatoric Uncertainty}}+\underbrace{\vphantom{E_{q({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}[{\mathcal{H}}[p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})]]}{\mathcal{I}}({\mathbf{y}};{\bm{\theta}}|{\bm{x}})}_{\text{Epistemic Uncertainty}}.
``` |  | (3) |

Here, *aleatoric uncertainty* captures the intrinsic randomness in data
and cannot be reduced even with more data observed. In contrast,
*epistemic uncertainty*, defined as the mutual information
$`\mathcal{I}({\mathbf{y}};\bm{\theta}|{\bm{x}})`$ between
$`{\mathbf{y}}`$ and $`\bm{\theta}`$, reflects the model’s uncertainty
about its own parameters, which can in principle be reduced by
collecting more evidence. We use
$`{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ defined in
Definition [2.1](#S2.Thmdefinition1 "Definition 2.1 (Query-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
to denote any of the three uncertainties.

###### Definition 2.1 (Query-Level Uncertainty).

Query-level uncertainty $`{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ is the
uncertainty of the predictive distribution $`p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$
given an input query $`{\bm{x}}`$. Total Uncertainty (TU), Aleatoric
Uncertainty (AU), and Epistemic Uncertainty (EU) in
Eqn. [3](#S2.E3 "In 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
are all instances of query-level uncertainty.

Limitations of Query-Level Uncertainty. Using the chain rule for
conditional entropy [Cover (1999)](#bib.bib67), the *query-level
uncertainty* estimation can be decomposed token-by-token as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})=\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}}).`$ |  | (4) |

However, the uncertainty term
$`\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ in
Eqn. [4](#S2.E4 "In 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
requires marginalization over the random variable $`{\mathbf{y}}_{<t}`$,
which is *(i) computationally intractable,* and *(ii) only reflecting
the quality of the input query.* Hence, these query-level uncertainties
are not proper indicators for evaluating a concrete output response
$`{\bm{y}}`$.

### 2.2 Bayesian Large Language Models

Bayesian Low-Rank Adaptation. For a pre-trained network layer with
weight matrix $`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [Hu et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib1) optimizes the parameters within a constrained
low-rank subspace. Specifically, the weight update matrix is modeled by
$`\Delta{\bm{W}}={\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}`$, where
$`\Delta{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}`$,
$`{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}`$,
$`{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}`$, and $`r\ll\min(m,n)`$. The output
$`{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}`$ of forwarding the input vector
$`{\bm{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 1}`$ is then

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\bm{z}}`$ | $`\displaystyle={\bm{W}}_{0}{\bm{h}}+\Delta{\bm{W}}{\bm{h}}={\bm{W}}_{0}{\bm{h}}+{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}{\bm{h}}.`$ |  | (5) |

Leveraging LoRA’s parameter efficiency, Bayesian LoRAs [Yang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib6), [Wang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16), [Shi et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib24) aim to further integrate BNN’s uncertainty
estimation capabilities into LLMs without significant increasing memory
complexity. The key idea is to model $`{\bm{A}}`$ and/or $`{\bm{B}}`$ as
approximate distributions of the true weight posterior. The asymmetric
Bayesianization, exemplified by BLoB [Wang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16)
and TFB [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24), models the elements of
$`{\bm{A}}`$ with independent Gaussian distributions while keeping
$`{\bm{B}}`$ deterministic. Specifically, we have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle q({\bm{A}}|\{{\bm{M}},{\bm{\Omega}}\})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\prod\nolimits_{ij}q(A_{ij}|M_{ij},\Omega_{ij})=\prod\nolimits_{ij}{\mathcal{N}}(A_{ij}|M_{ij},\Omega_{ij}^{2}),`$ |  | (6) |

where $`{\bm{M}}`$ and $`{\bm{\Omega}}`$ share the same shape as
$`{\bm{A}}`$ and denote the mean and standard deviation of the random
variable $`{\bm{A}}`$, respectively. To estimate this distribution, BLoB
jointly trains the mean and covariance through the re-parameterization
trick [Wang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16), while TFB uses a simple
training-free maximal variance searching technique by fixing the
approximate distribution to the family of low-rank isotropic Gaussian
distributions [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24).

Limited Scope of Existing Bayesian LLMs. Existing Bayesian LLMs have
been primarily validated in downstream classification tasks of simple
single- or multiple-choice problems, where uncertainty estimation is
quantitatively assessed via the alignment of prediction confidence and
accuracy [Yang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6), [Balabanov and Linander
(2024)](#bib.bib4), [Wang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib5), [Wang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib16), [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24). However, these
methods have not yet demonstrated effective generalization to long-form
generation tasks, i.e., LLM reasoning. Therefore, our TokUR, which
estimates token-level uncertainties via weight perturbations, represents
an initial step toward *extending Bayesian LLMs to long-form
generation*, an area where uncertainty estimation remains largely
unexplored and technically challenging.

### 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General

Given an approximate posterior $`q({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})`$, a
fixed input query $`{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}`$ and a *specific output
response* $`{\bm{y}}=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{T})\in\mathcal{Y}`$ sampled
from the base policy $`p(\mathbf{y}|{\bm{x}})`$, we denote the
predictive distribution of the next token $`y_{t}`$ produced by
marginalization over weights as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{p}(y_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim q(\cdot|{\mathcal{D}})}[p(y_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})].
``` |  | (7) |

###### Assumption 3.1 (Stepwise Posterior Sampling).

We assume that the weights $`{\bm{\theta}}`$ sampled from the
approximate posterior $`q(\cdot|{\mathcal{D}})`$ are *not shared* across
decoding steps. Formally, the probability of a sequence is factorized as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{p}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\triangleq\prod\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\bar{p}(y_{t}|{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}_{<t})=\prod\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl\{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\sim q(\cdot|{\mathcal{D}})}[p(y_{t}|{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t})]\Bigr\},
``` |  | (8) |

instead of adopting the joint formulation

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{p}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim q(\cdot|{\mathcal{D}})}[p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}},{\bm{\theta}})].
``` |  | (9) |

While both are valid probabilistic models, the joint formulation is
incompatible with the autoregressive decoding mechanism of LLMs. Hence,
we adopt the stepwise formulation in
Assumption [3.1](#S3.Thmassumption1 "Assumption 3.1 (Stepwise Posterior Sampling). ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").
To validate this assumption, we further conduct an ablation study
comparing the stepwise formulation in
Assumption [3.1](#S3.Thmassumption1 "Assumption 3.1 (Stepwise Posterior Sampling). ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
with the joint formulation, and report the results
in Appendix [E.5.4](#A5.SS5.SSS4 "E.5.4 Ablation Study of Stepwise Posterior Sampling ‣ E.5 Ablation Study ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").

Given an input $`{\bm{x}}`$ and a partial output $`{\bm{y}}_{<t}`$, for
the time step $`t`$, we have the following three uncertainties:

- •
  Total Uncertainty (TU) is the entropy of random variable
  $`{\mathbf{y}}_{t}`$ conditioned on $`{\bm{x}}`$ and
  $`{\bm{y}}_{<t}`$:

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \operatorname{TU}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})\triangleq{\mathcal{H}}[\bar{p}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})]=-\sum\nolimits_{y_{t}\in{\mathcal{V}}}\bar{p}(y_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})\log\bar{p}(y_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}}),
  ``` |  | (10) |
- •
  Aleatoric Uncertainty (AU) is the expectation of entropy of random
  variable $`{\mathbf{y}}_{t}`$ over the weights $`{\bm{\theta}}`$
  sampled from the approximate posterior $`q(\cdot|{\mathcal{D}})`$ as
  in
  Eqn. [3](#S2.E3 "In 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"):

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \operatorname{AU}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}\sim q(\cdot|{\mathcal{D}})}\big[{\mathcal{H}}[p({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}};\bm{\theta})]\big],
  ``` |  | (11) |
- •
  Epistemic Uncertainty (EU) is the difference between TU and AU:

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{EU}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})\triangleq\operatorname{TU}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})-\operatorname{AU}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})=\mathcal{I}({\mathbf{y}}_{t};\bm{\theta}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}}),`$ |  | (12) |

where $`\mathcal{V}`$ is the vocabulary and all the expectations are
estimated with BMA.

### 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation

###### Definition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty).

Given the token-level uncertainties
$`\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ defined in
Eqn. [10](#S3.E10 "In 1st item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")-[12](#S3.E12 "In 3rd item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
we define *response-level uncertainty* as their cumulative sum across
all tokens in the output sequence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}}),
``` |  | (13) |

where $`\mathcal{U}`$ can denote any of the considered uncertainty
measures (TU, AU, or EU
in Eqn. [10](#S3.E10 "In 1st item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")-[12](#S3.E12 "In 3rd item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")).

###### Proposition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty as an Unbiased Estimator of Query-Level Uncertainty).

Given an input query $`{\bm{x}}`$, let
$`{\bm{y}}\sim p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ be a generated sample of
length $`T`$. Then the response-level uncertainty
$`\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}`$
(Definition [3.1](#S3.Thmdefinition1 "Definition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"))
is an unbiased estimator of the query-level uncertainty $`\mathcal{U}`$
(Definition [2.1](#S2.Thmdefinition1 "Definition 2.1 (Query-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")),
i.e.,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}\sim p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})}[\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})]=\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}}).`$ |  | (14) |

###### Proposition 3.2 (Token-Level and Response-Level Uncertainty).

Given an input query $`{\bm{x}}`$, let
$`{\bm{y}}\sim p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ be a generated sample of
length $`T`$. Let
$`\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ denote the
token-level uncertainty as defined
in Eqn. [10](#S3.E10 "In 1st item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")-[12](#S3.E12 "In 3rd item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
with $`\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ as the
corresponding response-level uncertainty
(Definition [3.1](#S3.Thmdefinition1 "Definition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")).
Our token-level uncertainty is equivalent to the response-level
uncertainty when $`T=1`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}({\bm{y}}_{1}|{\bm{x}})=\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{1}|{\bm{x}}).`$ |  | (15) |

The two propositions above provide key connections between
Eqn. [13](#S3.E13 "In Definition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
and existing uncertainty estimation theory [Malinin and Gales
(2021)](#bib.bib27), [Ling et al. (2024)](#bib.bib63).
Proposition [3.1](#S3.EGx5 "Proposition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty as an Unbiased Estimator of Query-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
shows that $`\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ is an
unbiased estimator of the true query-level uncertainty
$`{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$, ensuring its statistical
consistency with the ideal formulation.
Proposition [3.2](#S3.EGx6 "Proposition 3.2 (Token-Level and Response-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
confirms that when the sequence length $`T=1`$, e.g., single-token
prediction tasks such as multiple-choice QA [Yang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib6), [Wang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16), the estimator
exactly recovers the token-level uncertainty, demonstrating structural
consistency. These results support the validity and reliability of our
approximation.

Advantages of Token-Level Uncertainty. Compared to Query-Level
Uncertainty
(Definition [2.1](#S2.Thmdefinition1 "Definition 2.1 (Query-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")),
token- and response-level uncertainties *(i) avoid expensive
marginalization over sequences* (note the difference between
$`{\mathbf{y}}_{<t}`$ in
Eqn. [4](#S2.E4 "In 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
and $`{\bm{y}}_{<t}`$ in
Eqn. [14](#S3.E14 "In Proposition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty as an Unbiased Estimator of Query-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"))
while still *(ii) capturing the expected uncertainty conditioned on the
generated output response.* Moreover, since
$`\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ depends on the
quality of the prefix $`{\bm{y}}_{<t}`$, *(iii) the estimate retains
rich semantic information,* making it well-suited for entropy-based
sequential decision-making [Kuhn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib18), [Ye et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib53) or hallucination detection [Farquhar et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib45), [Kossen et al. (2024)](#bib.bib46), [Ye et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib53) in downstream tasks.

### 3.3 Low-Rank Weight Perturbation as Approximation of Weight Posterior

Suppose that we have an LLM policy $`p({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})`$. To
estimate the uncertainty of its output, we cast this model into a
Bayesian framework by introducing weight perturbations. Due to the
established advantages of *efficiency*, *performance preservation* of
pre-perturbation model, and *effectiveness* of uncertainty
estimation [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24), we adopt a low-rank
structure for the noise added to the model weights. Given a rank-$`r`$
weight matrix $`{\bm{W}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}`$ of a neural
network layer, we first perform compact Singular Value
Decomposition (SVD) [Klema and Laub (1980)](#bib.bib17):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\bm{W}}_{0}={\bm{U}}\operatorname{diag}({\bm{d}}){\bm{V}}^{\top},
``` |  | (16) |

where $`{\bm{d}}\succ{\bm{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times 1}`$ is the vector
of singular values, and $`{\bm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}`$ and
$`{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}`$ both contain orthonormal columns,
i.e., $`{\bm{U}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}={\bm{V}}^{\top}{\bm{V}}={\bm{I}}_{r}`$.
To ensure computational efficiency, we introduce a low-rank noise matrix
$`{\bm{\epsilon}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r^{\prime}}`$ whose rank
$`r^{\prime}\ll r`$ is significantly smaller than the rank of weight
matrix, and whose entries are sampled i.i.d. from a Gaussian
distribution of standard deviation of $`\sigma_{q}`$, which we refer to
as *perturbation strength*, i.e.,
$`\epsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{q}^{2}),\forall i\in[n],j\in[r^{\prime}]`$.
The perturbed weight matrix is then constructed as

|     |                                                                |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       {\bm{W}}={\bm{W}}_{0}+{\bm{U}}^{\prime}{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\top},
       ```                                                             |     | (17) |

where the matrix $`{\bm{U}}^{\prime}`$ contains the top-$`r^{\prime}`$
columns of $`{\bm{U}}`$. This perturbation transforms the deterministic
$`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$ to a variational low-rank isotropic Gaussian
distribution $`{\bm{W}}`$ [Wang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16), [Shi et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib24):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle q(\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}})|\sigma_{q})`$ | $`\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}(\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}})|{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}),`$ |  | (18) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{where }\quad{\bm{\mu}}_{q}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}}_{0}),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sigma_{q}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{n}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{r^{\prime}}&\\
&\mathbf{0}_{m-r^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix}.`$ |  |  |

Let $`\bm{\theta}`$ denote the collection of all perturbed weight
matrices across the model. By assuming the statistical independence
among layers, the overall approximate posterior becomes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q({\bm{\theta}}|\sigma_{q})=\prod\nolimits_{i}q(\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}}^{i})|\sigma_{q}).
``` |  | (19) |

Utilizing the Approximate Weight Posterior
$`q({\bm{\theta}}|\sigma_{q})`$. Notably, while we leverage the
variational posterior formulation of
Eqn. [19](#S3.E19 "In 3.3 Low-Rank Weight Perturbation as Approximation of Weight Posterior ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
to quantify uncertainty (detailed in
Sec. [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")),
we use only the mean weights $`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$ for decoding of each step
rather than BMA as in
Eqn. [1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").
This approach allows for a controlled study of the effects of
uncertainty estimation itself, separate from the effects of BNNs. For
the complete algorithmic description and overview, please refer to
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").

## 4 Experiments

This section presents practical applications of our TokUR for LLM
reasoning. For additional experimental results, please refer to
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").

Datasets. We run our main experiments on three mathematical reasoning
benchmarks of varying difficulty levels: GSM8K [Cobbe et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib40) (grade-school arithmetic problems),
MATH500 [Lightman et al. (2023)](#bib.bib41) (challenging high
school/college mathematics competition problems), and 5,000-example
subset of DeepScaleR [Luo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib42) (high-difficulty
problems from diverse sources). For these complex math problems, LLMs
often need to perform multi-step reasoning [Wei et al.
(2022b)](#bib.bib59), [Yao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib64), [Zhou et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib65) to reach the final answer. These tasks inherently
involve long-form generation, therefore well-suited for evaluation of
uncertainty estimation methods.

To assess the generalization of TokUR beyond mathematical reasoning, we
further evaluate TokUR on five non-math long-form generation tasks,
spanning logical reasoning, code generation, and truthfulness
evaluation. For logical reasoning, we use three tasks from Reasoning
Gym [Stojanovski et al. (2025)](#bib.bib84): *Zebra Puzzles*, *Leg
Counting*, and *Color-Cube Rotation*. For code generation, we evaluate
on the HumanEval [Chen (2021)](#bib.bib85) benchmark, a widely adopted
standard for functional code synthesis. For truthfulness, we use the
FactScore [Min et al. (2023)](#bib.bib86) dataset, which measures
factual consistency by decomposing generated outputs into atomic facts;
we follow prior work and use GPT-5-mini as both the fact annotator and
judge.

Models. We evaluate our TokUR using models from two open-source LLM
families: Llama (3.2-1B-Instruct and 3.1-8B-Instruct) [Grattafiori et
al. (2024)](#bib.bib44) and Qwen (2.5-3B-Instruct and
2.5-7B-Instruct) [Team and others (2024)](#bib.bib87). These models
represent recent advances in open-source instruction tuning and provide
a practical balance between capability and efficiency. Their differing
model scales and architectural families further enable us to examine the
consistency of uncertainty estimation across both model sizes and model
types.

Implementation of our TokUR. We estimate token-level uncertainties by
applying random perturbations as in
Eqn. [17](#S3.E17 "In 3.3 Low-Rank Weight Perturbation as Approximation of Weight Posterior ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
to the query and key weight
matrices ($`{\bm{W}}^{Q},{\bm{W}}^{K}`$) [Vaswani et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib73) in all the attention layers of LLMs [Hu et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib1), [Yang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6), [Wang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib16), [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24). For more details,
please refer to
Appendix [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Implementation of TokUR’s Token-Level Uncertainties ‣ Appendix D Implementation Details ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").

### 4.1 Do TokUR’s Uncertainties Accurately Reflect Response Quality?

This section assesses if our TokUR’s uncertainties reflect response
quality in math reasoning tasks.

#### 4.1.1 TokUR’s Uncertainties and Question Difficulty

Different Difficulty Levels, applied to Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct. Left:
TokUR (AU, Ours); Middle: TokUR (TU, Ours); Right: TokUR (EU, Ours).

Experimental Setting. To better understand the relationships among
uncertainty estimates, question difficulty, and their ability to
distinguish correct from incorrect responses, we sample a subset of math
questions from math-orz [Hu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib75). A question’s
difficulty level is determined by the number of failed attempts out of
10 when using the Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct model. A difficulty level of 0
means the model solved the question every time, while a level of 10
indicates it failed on every attempt. We sample 500 questions per
difficulty level, yielding a 5,500-question dataset. We then prompt
Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct to solve each question with greedy decoding and
apply TokUR to compute uncertainties for both correct and incorrect
responses across difficulty levels.
Fig. [1](#S4.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 4.1.1 TokUR’s Uncertainties and Question Difficulty ‣ 4.1 Do TokUR’s Uncertainties Accurately Reflect Response Quality? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
summarizes the results.

Results. TokUR’s uncertainty estimates remain positively correlated with
question difficulty: for all three types of uncertainty (AU, TU, and
EU), incorrect responses consistently exhibit higher uncertainty than
correct ones across difficulty levels. In terms of discriminative power,
AUROC values are consistently above random (0.5), confirming that TokUR
provides useful signals for distinguishing correct from incorrect
reasoning. Yet, AUROC tends to decrease as difficulty increases,
especially in the mid-to-high range (levels 7–9), showing that
uncertainty estimates become less reliable at separating outcomes on
challenging tasks. Interestingly, the AUROC score shows a slight
increase at the highest difficulty level (10). This is likely a result
of the imbalanced data distribution (mostly incorrect), where the model
consistently produces high uncertainty, which causes a misleading high
metric value.

#### 4.1.2 TokUR for Incorrect Reasoning Path Detection

Experimental Setting. The preliminary study demonstrates that our
TokUR’s uncertainty estimation can reflect the quality of generated
responses, with lower uncertainty generally associated with better
outputs. One important application of uncertainty estimation is
hallucination detection in LLMs [Farquhar et al. (2024)](#bib.bib45),
[Kossen et al. (2024)](#bib.bib46), [Ye et al. (2025)](#bib.bib53). In
this context, we treat uncertainty as a scoring function to identify
hallucinated (incorrect) responses for long-form reasoning tasks. We
adopt three metrics: Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic
Curve (AUROC), Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), and
Top-50% ACC (ACC\*) [Farquhar et al. (2024)](#bib.bib45), [Ye et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib53), [Hanley and McNeil (1982)](#bib.bib55), [Boyd et
al. (2013)](#bib.bib56). AUROC and AUPRC measure the overall power of
uncertainty scores in distinguishing correct from incorrect responses.
In addition, we report Top-50% ACC, defined as the accuracy of the top
50% samples ranked by the corresponding score. This metric reflects the
model’s ability to prioritize higher-quality generations under a fixed
budget. We repeat the experiments with three different random seeds to
obtain the mean and standard deviation across runs.

In this section, we explore the direct application of TokUR to reasoning
tasks to enhance generation quality. Following previous works [Fu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib80), we apply TokUR to measure the confidence of
reasoning traces generated from a question and aggregate them via voting
to obtain a final solution. In addition, we investigate the possibility
of utilizing TokUR in an *online* manner to dynamically guide the
generation process itself. Further details of online method are provided
in
[E.4](#A5.SS4 "E.4 TokUR for Test-Time Scaling (Online) ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").

Baselines. We adopt Log-Likelihood (LL) as a baseline, given its
widespread use as a proxy for generation quality [Manakul et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib68), [Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib69), [Chen et al.
(2024b)](#bib.bib72). In addition, we compare against
Self-Certainty [Kang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib79) and DeepConf [Fu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib80), two recent uncertainty-driven approaches for
test-time scaling. As our study emphasizes model self-awareness of the
boundaries of its knowledge, we do not include baselines that rely on
external reward models [Guan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib60), [Puri et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib43), [Beeching et al. ()](#bib.bib61), [Uesato et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib62), [Lightman et al. (2023)](#bib.bib41).

Response Aggregation with Uncertainties. We first rank all $`N`$
candidate responses using one of the scoring methods (LL,
Self-Certainty, DeepConf, or TokUR) and retain the top-$`P\%`$
candidates. We then employ two common aggregation strategies: *Weighted
Best-of-N* (WBoN) and *Majority Voting* (Maj@N) [Brown et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib71). WBoN performs weighted voting by assigning weights
to the retained candidates according to their scores, whereas Maj@N
simply selects the most frequent response among them, regardless of
scoring.

Experimental Setting. We randomly sample 512 responses for each question
in MATH500 and GSM8K with a decoding temperature of $`\tau=0.8`$. For
each $`N`$, we first retain the top-10% of samples ranked by their
scores. From this subset, the final prediction is determined using
either Maj@N or WBoN. Each experiment is repeated 10 times (sample w/o
replacement using offline records).

Results. As shown in
Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.2 Can TokUR’s Uncertainties Improve Generation Quality? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
accuracy consistently improves with larger $`N`$ across both GSM8K and
MATH500. Our TokUR-based selection methods achieve clear gains over all
baselines, particularly in the low-sample regime ($`N{=}16`$), where
they deliver up to 3–4 points of improvement. Notably, TokUR (EU)
attains the best overall performance on both datasets, with strong
advantages in the challenging MATH500 benchmark. In addition, results
for Maj@N and WBoN are similar, indicating that both aggregation
strategies are similarly effective once the top candidates are
identified.

## 5 Related Work

Uncertainty Estimation of LLMs. Uncertainty estimation in LLMs is
gaining traction for improving model calibration in data-scarce
adaptation tasks and for reducing hallucinations in text generation [Liu
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib32), [Vashurin et al. (2025)](#bib.bib26). One
prominent approach is Bayesian Adaptation, which combines Bayesian
inference with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [Hu et al. (2022)](#bib.bib1)
to approximate weight posterior distributions efficiently, avoiding the
high computational cost of full Bayesian modeling [Yang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib6), [Wang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16), [Shi et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib24). To estimate uncertainty in generation, two main
lines of work have emerged. The first focuses on *verbalized
uncertainty,* where models are prompted to express confidence in natural
language [Lin et al. (2022)](#bib.bib19), [Kadavath et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib8), [Tian et al. (2023)](#bib.bib9), [Kapoor et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib13). The second line includes *logits-based methods,*
which estimate uncertainty directly from the model’s output
distributions [Van Der Poel et al. (2022)](#bib.bib28), [Ren et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib29), [Duan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib30), [Darrin et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib31). In parallel, other approaches aim to refine these
estimation strategies. For instance, [Malinin and Gales
(2021)](#bib.bib27) investigates techniques for estimating epistemic
uncertainty in structured prediction tasks, while semantic entropy [Kuhn
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib18) captures uncertainty by leveraging invariance
in meaning across paraphrases. More recently, [Zhang and Zhang
(2025)](#bib.bib39) introduces a method that leverages the reasoning
capabilities of LLMs to enhance uncertainty quantification, using
chain-of-thought prompting to better reflect model confidence in
multi-step tasks. These works complement verbalized and logits-based
methods by offering orthogonal perspectives on how uncertainty can be
interpreted and measured. Beyond specific estimation methods, recent
studies reassess broader principles of LLM uncertainty: [Kirchhof et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib81) argues that uncertainty quantification for LLM
agents requires rethinking foundational assumptions, while [Devic et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib82) highlights the limitations of existing approaches
and calls for more human-centered uncertainty interpretation.

Uncertainty for Improving LLM Generation. Uncertainty estimation for
improving LLM generation, while not entirely novel, has been
predominantly limited to approaches based on log-probability or its
variants, Self-Certainty [Kang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib79) estimates
confidence via KL divergence from a uniform distribution, DeepConf [Fu
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib80) aggregates top-$`K`$ log-probabilities as
scores. Beam search [Lowerre (1976)](#bib.bib34), [Sutskever et al.
(2014)](#bib.bib35), [Freitag and Al-Onaizan (2017)](#bib.bib36), [Xie
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib33) selects higher-confidence sequences by
retaining candidates with the largest cumulative log-probability.
UAG [Yin et al. (2024)](#bib.bib37) leverages abrupt log-probability
changes to select appropriate demonstrations for in-context
learning [Brown et al. (2020)](#bib.bib11). UnCert-CoT [Zhu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib38) alternates between greedy and Chain-of-Thought
decoding based on log-probability scores. Complementing these, [Zhou et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib83) provides theoretical insights into how internal
probabilities relate to self-consistency and reasoning errors in LLMs.
Our work differs fundamentally by estimating token-level uncertainties
with rigorous theoretical foundations, representing a significant step
toward extending Bayesian LLMs to long-form generation scenarios.

Limitations. Our work is subject to several limitations. First, although
compatible with efficient deployment frameworks such as vLLM [Kwon et
al. (2023)](#bib.bib47), repeated weight perturbation sampling during
inference still poses efficiency challenges for real-time use. Second,
our token-level uncertainty aggregation may miss higher-level semantic
or logical inconsistencies across multiple tokens or reasoning steps,
limiting its utility in complex generation tasks. Finally, the problem
of high-variance estimation in our TokUR remains unresolved,
constraining reliability in real-world scenarios.

## Appendix C Proof of Propositions

###### Lemma C.1 (Definition of Conditional Entropy [Cover (1999)](#bib.bib67)).

Give $`({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}})\sim p({\mathbf{y}},{\mathbf{x}})`$,
the conditional entropy $`{\mathcal{H}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})`$ is
defined as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}p({\bm{x}}){\mathcal{H}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ |  | (20) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim p({\mathbf{x}})}[{\mathcal{H}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})].`$ |  |  |

###### Lemma C.2 (Chain rule of Conditional Entropy [Cover (1999)](#bib.bib67)).

Let $`{\bm{X}}`$ and $`{\bm{Y}}`$ be two random variables, then the
conditional entropy of the joint distribution
$`{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}})`$ can be decomposed as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{X}},{\bm{Y}})={\mathcal{H}}({\bm{X}})+{\mathcal{H}}({\bm{Y}}|{\bm{X}})`$ |  | (21) |

Lemma [C.1](#A3.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma C.1 (Definition of Conditional Entropy ( ) ). ‣ Appendix C Proof of Propositions ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning") [Cover
(1999)](#bib.bib67) reveals the relationship between conditional entropy
$`{\mathcal{H}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})`$ and the entropy derived
from conditional probability distributions.
Lemma [C.2](#A3.Thmtheorem2 "Lemma C.2 (Chain rule of Conditional Entropy ( ) ). ‣ Appendix C Proof of Propositions ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
lays the foundation for estimating the uncertainties of sequences. The
two lemmas together give us the following proposition.

###### Proposition C.1 (Decomposition of Query-Level Uncertainty, Eqn. [4](#S2.E4 "In 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")).

Suppose that we have an input sequence $`{\bm{x}}`$ and a model policy
$`p({\mathbf{y}}|{\textnormal{x}})`$. The sequence-level uncertainty
$`\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ can be decomposed token-by-token
as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})=\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}}),`$ |  | (22) |

where $`{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ is
token-level uncertainty metric as defined
in Eqn. [10](#S3.E10 "In 1st item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
~ Eqn. [12](#S3.E12 "In 3rd item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").

###### Proof.

For Aleatoric Uncertainty (AU) and Total Uncertainty (TU) defined
in Eqn. [10](#S3.E10 "In 1st item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
and Eqn. [11](#S3.E11 "In 2nd item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
both are expressed in terms of entropy. Therefore, the decomposition of
sequence-level uncertainty can be directly derived using the chain rule
stated in
the Lemma [C.2](#A3.Thmtheorem2 "Lemma C.2 (Chain rule of Conditional Entropy ( ) ). ‣ Appendix C Proof of Propositions ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").

For Epistemic Uncertainty (EU), also called mutual information defined
in Eqn. [12](#S3.E12 "In 3rd item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
we proceed with the following derivation:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}({p}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}}))=`$ | $`\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}[p({\mathbf{y}}_{1}|{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})]\cdot\mathinner{\cdotp\mkern-3.0mu\cdotp\mkern-3.0mu\cdotp}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}[p({\mathbf{y}}_{T}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<T},{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})]\Big)`$ |  | (23) |
|  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\sum_{t}^{T}{\mathcal{H}}(\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}[p({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})])`$ |  | (24) |
|  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\sum_{t}^{T}{\mathcal{I}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t};{\bm{\theta}}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})+\sum_{t}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}[{\mathcal{H}}(p({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}))]`$ |  | (25) |
|  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\sum_{t}^{T}{\mathcal{I}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t};{\bm{\theta}}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})+\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}{\mathcal{H}}(p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}))`$ |  | (26) |

Finally, based on the definition of mutual information, we obtain:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}({\mathbf{y}};{\bm{\theta}}|{\bm{x}})`$ | $`\displaystyle={\mathcal{H}}({p}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}}))-\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{\theta}}|{\mathcal{D}})}{\mathcal{H}}(p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}))`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t}^{T}{\mathcal{I}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t};{\bm{\theta}}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ |  | (27) |

∎

###### Proposition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty as an Unbiased Estimator of Query-Level Uncertainty).

Given an input query $`{\bm{x}}`$, let
$`{\bm{y}}\sim p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ be a generated sample of
length $`T`$. Then the response-level uncertainty
$`\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}`$
(Definition [3.1](#S3.Thmdefinition1 "Definition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"))
is an unbiased estimator of the query-level uncertainty $`\mathcal{U}`$
(Definition [2.1](#S2.Thmdefinition1 "Definition 2.1 (Query-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 2.1 Uncertainty Estimation of Long-Form Generation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")),
i.e.,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}\sim p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})}[\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})]=\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}}).`$ |  | (28) |

###### Proof.

Based on
Lemma [C.1](#A3.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma C.1 (Definition of Conditional Entropy ( ) ). ‣ Appendix C Proof of Propositions ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
for the token-level uncertainty
$`\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ defined in
Eqn. [10](#S3.E10 "In 1st item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")~Eqn. [12](#S3.E12 "In 3rd item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
we have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}_{<t}\sim p(\cdot|{\bm{x}})}[{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{y}}_{<t}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}p({\bm{y}}_{<t}|{\bm{x}}){\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ |  | (29) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle={\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}}).`$ |  | (30) |

Therefore, the uncertainty of the sequence defined in
Eqn. [13](#S3.E13 "In Definition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}\sim p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})}[\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})]`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})}[\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})]`$ |  | (31) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{y}}|{\mathbf{x}})}[{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})]`$ |  | (32) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}_{<t}\sim p(\cdot|{\bm{x}})}[\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})]`$ |  | (33) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\mathbf{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ |  | (34) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}}),`$ |  | (35) |

where the final step follows from the chain rule of entropy
(Proposition [C.1](#A3.Thmproposition1 "Proposition C.1 (Decomposition of Query-Level Uncertainty, Eqn. ). ‣ Appendix C Proof of Propositions ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")).
∎

###### Proposition 3.2 (Token-Level and Response-Level Uncertainty).

Given an input query $`{\bm{x}}`$, let
$`{\bm{y}}\sim p({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ be a generated sample of
length $`T`$. Let
$`\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})`$ denote the
token-level uncertainty as defined
in Eqn. [10](#S3.E10 "In 1st item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")-[12](#S3.E12 "In 3rd item ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
with $`\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}|{\bm{x}})`$ as the
corresponding response-level uncertainty
(Definition [3.1](#S3.Thmdefinition1 "Definition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")).
Our token-level uncertainty is equivalent to the response-level
uncertainty when $`T=1`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})=\mathcal{U}({\mathbf{y}}_{1}|{\bm{x}}).`$ |  | (36) |

###### Proof.

When the sequence length $`T=1`$, based on the definition of uncertainty
of sequence in
Eqn. [13](#S3.E13 "In Definition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
we have

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathcal{U}}}({\bm{y}}|{\textnormal{x}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{<t},{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{U}}({\mathbf{y}}_{1}|{\bm{x}}).`$ |  |

This proposition implies that the sequence uncertainty collapses to
token-level uncertainty when the output sequence length is 1, reflecting
the structural consistency of the estimator. ∎

###### Proposition C.2 (Approximate Distribution of the Weight $`{\bm{W}}`$ Perturbed by Low-Rank Noise, Eqn. [18](#S3.E18 "In 3.3 Low-Rank Weight Perturbation as Approximation of Weight Posterior ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")).

Given the weight matrix $`{\bm{W}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}`$, the
low-rank noise matrix
$`{\bm{\epsilon}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r^{\prime}}`$ whose rank
$`r^{\prime}\ll r`$ is significantly smaller than the rank $`r`$ of
$`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$, and whose entries are sampled i.i.d. from a Gaussian
distribution of standard deviation of $`\sigma_{q}`$:
$`\epsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{q}^{2}),\forall i\in[n],j\in[r^{\prime}]`$,
we have the perturbed weighted matrix $`{\bm{W}}`$ as defined in
Eqn. [17](#S3.E17 "In 3.3 Low-Rank Weight Perturbation as Approximation of Weight Posterior ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
. The variational distribution
$`q(\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}})|\sigma_{q})`$ defined on the weight
matrix $`{\bm{W}}`$ is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle q(\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}})|\sigma_{q})`$ | $`\displaystyle={\mathcal{N}}(\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}})|{\bm{\mu}}_{q},{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}),`$ |  | (37) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{where }\quad{\bm{\mu}}_{q}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{vec}({\bm{W}}_{0}),`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sigma_{q}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{n}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{r^{\prime}}&\\
&\mathbf{0}_{m-{r^{\prime}}}\end{bmatrix}.`$ |  |  |

###### Proof.

We begin with compact SVD decomposition of the weight matrix
$`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$ as described in
Eqn. [16](#S3.E16 "In 3.3 Low-Rank Weight Perturbation as Approximation of Weight Posterior ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{0}={\bm{U}}\text{diag}({\bm{d}}){\bm{V}}^{\top},`$ |  | (38) |

where $`{\bm{d}}\succ{\bm{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times 1}`$ is the vector
of singular values, and $`{\bm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}`$,
$`{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}`$ are orthogonal matrices. We denote
the first $`r^{\prime}`$ columns of $`{\bm{U}}`$ as
$`{\bm{U}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r^{\prime}}`$ to analyze the
updated matrix $`{\bm{U}}^{\prime}{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\top}`$ in
Eqn. [17](#S3.E17 "In 3.3 Low-Rank Weight Perturbation as Approximation of Weight Posterior ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").

Since each entry in $`{\bm{\epsilon}}`$ has zero mean, it is evident
that the updated matrix also has zero mean. Consequently, we have
$`{\bm{\mu}}_{q}=\text{vec}({\bm{W}}_{0})+\mathbf{0}=\text{vec}({\bm{W}}_{0})`$.

Next, we focus on the proof of the variance $`{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}`$. Gien
$`{\bm{U}}^{\prime}=({\bm{u}}_{1},{\bm{u}}_{2},\cdots,{\bm{u}}_{r^{\prime}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r^{\prime}}`$,
and
$`{\bm{\epsilon}}=({\bm{\epsilon}}_{1},{\bm{\epsilon}}_{2},\cdots,{\bm{\epsilon}}_{r^{\prime}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r^{\prime}}`$
as defined above, we have the following properties:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\bm{U}}^{\prime}{\bm{U}}^{\prime\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}{\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{r^{\prime}}&\\
&\mathbf{0}_{m-r^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix},`$ |  | (39) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{vec}({\bm{U}}^{\prime}{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\top})=\text{vec}(\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}{\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}^{\top})=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}({\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{u}}_{i}).`$ |  | (40) |

We can now derive the covariance matrix as:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}_{q}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\text{Var}[\text{vec}({\bm{W}})]=\text{Var}[\text{vec}({\bm{W}}_{0}+{\bm{U}}^{\prime}{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\top})]=\text{Var}[\text{vec}({\bm{U}}^{\prime}{\bm{\epsilon}}^{\top})]`$ |  | (41) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\text{Var}[\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{u}}_{i}]=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}\text{Var}[{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{u}}_{i}]`$ |  | (42) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}}[({\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{u}}_{i})({\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{u}}_{i})^{\top}]-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}}[({\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{u}}_{i})]\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}}[({\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\otimes{\bm{u}}_{i})^{\top}]\right\}`$ |  | (43) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}}[{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}^{\top}]\otimes({\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top})-(\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}}[{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}]\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}}[{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}]^{\top})\otimes({\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top})\right\}`$ |  | (44) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}\sigma_{q}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{n}\otimes({\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top})=\sigma_{q}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{n}\otimes\sum_{i=1}^{r^{\prime}}{\bm{u}}_{i}{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}=\sigma_{q}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{n}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{r^{\prime}}&\\
&\mathbf{0}_{m-r^{\prime}}\end{bmatrix}.`$ |  | (45) |

∎

### D.4 Baselines

We compare our uncertainty estimation approach against several baseline
methods:

- •
  Log-Likelihood (LL) [Murray and Chiang (2018)](#bib.bib70): Mean of
  token-wise log-probabilities of the output sequence, representing the
  model’s overall confidence in its generation.
- •
  Predictive Entropy (PE) [Malinin and Gales (2021)](#bib.bib27): Mean
  entropy of the predicted distribution of each token.
- •
  P(True) [Kadavath et al. (2022)](#bib.bib8): Directly queries the
  model about the correctness of its own output and uses the predicted
  probability of the token “True”, normalized by the sum of
  probabilities of token “True” and “False”, as a confidence score.
- •
  Self-Certainty [Kang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib79): Quantifies confidence
  using the KL divergence between the predicted token distribution and a
  uniform distribution over the vocabulary at each decoding step.
- •
  DeepConf [Fu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib80): Computes confidence scores by
  aggregating the log-probabilities of the top-$`K`$ candidate tokens at
  each decoding step.
- •
  The Degree Matrix [Lin et al. (2023)](#bib.bib74): Utilizes the degree
  matrix of the graph Laplacian of the similarities matrix of responses.
- •
  LLM-Check [Sriramanan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib77): We faithfully
  reproduced the official implementation for comparison.
- •
  INSIDE [Chen et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib76): INSIDE is a method to
  estimate query-level uncertainty. We tailored INSIDE to our setting by
  asking the LLM to verify the same response multiple times and then
  calculating the semantic entropy across these verification attempts.
- •
  Semantic Entropy (SE) [Kuhn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib18): We adapted SE
  to our setting by prompting the LLM to verify the same response
  multiple times and computing the semantic entropy of these
  verification attempts. While this provides a signal of response
  quality, we note that SE requires an external NLI or embedding model,
  giving it an inherent advantage compared to our method.
- •
  SAR [Duan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib30): We adapted SAR to our setting by
  computing sentence-level SAR scores over multiple verification
  attempts, following a similar procedure to SE. While this method
  provides a meaningful proxy for uncertainty, it requires an external
  semantic similarity model, which raises fairness concerns compared to
  our approach, which operates solely with the base LLM.

### D.5 Evaluation Parsing and Metrics

Parsing.  To automate the evaluation of outputs generated by large
language models, we design specific prompts
(see Appendix [D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Prompt Templates ‣ Appendix D Implementation Details ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"))
that constrain the model to follow a fixed structure and require it to
place the final answer within a \box{}. Considering that in mathematical
reasoning tasks, the same answer can be expressed in various forms, we
standardize all answers into a canonical form before
comparison [Beeching et al. ()](#bib.bib61). During the evaluation, we
assess the correctness from two perspectives: numerical equality and
symbolic equality, to label each generation as “True” or “False”.

Metrics.  To comprehensively assess model performance in binary
classification tasks, we adopt the following metrics: Area Under the
Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), Area Under the
Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), and Top 50% Accuracy [Farquhar et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib45), [Ye et al. (2025)](#bib.bib53), [Hanley and McNeil
(1982)](#bib.bib55), [Boyd et al. (2013)](#bib.bib56).

- •
  AUROC measures the trade-off between true positive rate (TPR) and
  false positive rate (FPR) at various threshold settings. Formally, for
  a set of predictions with associated confidence scores, AUROC is
  computed as:

  |     |                                                              |     |      |
  |-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
  |     |
         ``` math
         \text{AUROC}=\int_{0}^{1}\text{TPR}(\text{FPR}^{-1}(x))\,dx,
         ```                                                           |     | (46) |

  where TPR and FPR are defined as:

  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \text{TPR}=\tfrac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FN}},\quad\text{FPR}=\tfrac{\text{FP}}{\text{FP}+\text{TN}}.
  ``` |  |
- •
  AUPRC evaluates the trade-off between precision and recall, which is
  particularly useful in imbalanced datasets. It is calculated as:

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \text{AUPRC}=\int_{0}^{1}\text{Precision}(\text{Recall}^{-1}(x))\,dx,
  ``` |  | (47) |

  where precision and recall are defined as:

  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \text{Precision}=\tfrac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FP}},\quad\text{Recall}=\tfrac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FN}}.
  ``` |  |
- •
  Top 50% Accuracy evaluates the correctness of the top half predictions
  ranked by confidence. Let $`N`$ be the total number of predictions and
  $`S`$ be the set of indices corresponding to the top
  $`\lceil N/2\rceil`$ predictions with highest confidence. The metric
  is defined as:

  |  |  |  |  |
  |----|----|----|----|
  |  |
  ``` math
  \text{Top 50\% Accuracy}=\tfrac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}\delta(\widehat{y}_{i}=y_{i}),
  ``` |  | (48) |

  where $`\widehat{y}_{i}`$ is the predicted label and $`y_{i}`$ is the
  ground-truth label.

### E.4 TokUR for Test-Time Scaling (Online)

One popular approach to improving model performance uses a Process
Reward Model (PRM) to score each intermediate step during multi-step
generation [Guan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib60), [Puri et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib43), [Beeching et al. ()](#bib.bib61), [Uesato et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib62), [Lightman et al. (2023)](#bib.bib41), thereby
guiding the model’s reasoning path. In this section, we explore an
alternative: guiding the generation process using uncertainty as an
intrinsic reward, without relying on an explicit reward model.

Experimental Setting. Particle Filtering (PF) [Puri et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib43) is an inference-time scaling method for LLM
reasoning (details in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")).
Building upon this algorithm, we use uncertainty as the score for each
particle at each step to guide the model’s generation process. We set
the number of particles to $`N`$ = 16 and the decoding temperature to
$`\tau`$ = 0.8. We repeat the experiments with three different random
seeds to obtain the mean and standard deviation across runs.

Results. Table [8](#A5.T8 "Table 8 ‣ E.4 TokUR for Test-Time Scaling (Online) ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")
shows the results. Compared to LL, our TokUR, especially TokUR (EU),
yields a slight performance gain. Given that guiding generation through
stepwise scoring is inherently challenging, we consider the lack of a
significant performance gain from uncertainty estimation to be
acceptable. Nevertheless, we believe this experiment offers valuable
insights that may inform the future design of process reward models.

#### E.5.1 The Effect of Perturbation Strength $`\sigma_{q}`$ on Uncertainty Estimation

To investigate the impact of perturbation strength on uncertainty
estimation, we conducted a series of experiments under varying
$`\sigma_{q}`$ settings, as shown in
Fig. [4](#A5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ E.5.1 The Effect of Perturbation Strength 𝜎_𝑞 on Uncertainty Estimation ‣ E.5 Ablation Study ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").
First, we computed the average uncertainty estimates (TU, AU, and EU) on
samples generated from the GSM8K test dataset using
Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct. As illustrated in
Fig. [4](#A5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ E.5.1 The Effect of Perturbation Strength 𝜎_𝑞 on Uncertainty Estimation ‣ E.5 Ablation Study ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning") Left,
the model’s uncertainty increases steadily with higher perturbation
strength. However, once $`\sigma_{q}`$ exceeds a critical threshold
(e.g., 0.2), a sharp rise in uncertainty is observed. This rise
illustrates that the current approximate distribution of the weights has
deviated too far from the pre-trained point estimation of the
parameters, leading to unreliable uncertainty estimates.

We further evaluate the effect of perturbation strength on downstream
task performance. Specifically, we assess how effectively the
uncertainty estimates, obtained under different $`\sigma_{q}`$ values,
can be used as scoring signals to distinguish between correct and
incorrect samples, as described in

#### E.5.2 The Effect of Token Decoding Temperature $`\tau`$ on Uncertainty Estimation

Figure 5: Left: Uncertainty estimations in different token decoding
temperature $`\tau`$. Right: Influence of token decoding temperature on
uncertainty-based AUROC scores.

During text generation with large language models, the decoding
temperature introduces uncertainty into the model’s output. In general,
higher temperatures lead to more diverse responses. In this section, we
investigate the relationship between decoding temperature $`\tau`$ and
uncertainties estimated by our token-level approach. Specifically, we
use Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct to answer questions from the MATH500 dataset
under different decoding temperature settings and estimate the average
uncertainty of the model’s responses.

As shown in
Fig. [5](#A5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ E.5.2 The Effect of Token Decoding Temperature 𝜏 on Uncertainty Estimation ‣ E.5 Ablation Study ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning") Left,
increasing the decoding temperature $`\tau`$ results in a notable rise
in token-level Aleatoric Uncertainty (AU) of the model, whereas the
Epistemic Uncertainty (EU) remains relatively unaffected. Additionally,
we report the AUROC scores of various uncertainty estimation approaches
across different temperature settings
in Fig. [5](#A5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ E.5.2 The Effect of Token Decoding Temperature 𝜏 on Uncertainty Estimation ‣ E.5 Ablation Study ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning") Right.
These results indicate that varying the temperature $`\tau`$ does not
harm the performance of TokUR, highlighting its robustness to changes in
decoding temperature.

#### E.5.3 Ablation Study of Length Normalization

Length normalization is a standard technique for aggregating
*token-level* uncertainty into *sequence-level* uncertainty [Fu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib80), [Kang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib79), as it mitigates
the bias introduced by sequence length when evaluating generation
confidence. However, as described in
Eqn. [13](#S3.E13 "In Definition 3.1 (Response-Level Uncertainty). ‣ 3.2 Token-Level Uncertainty for Response-Level Uncertainty Estimation ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
we do not apply normalization when computing TokUR. To assess the impact
of sequence length on uncertainty estimation, we therefore conduct an
ablation study on length normalization.

##### Experimental Setup.

We investigate the effect of length normalization on incorrect reasoning
path detection across three datasets (MATH500, GSM8K, and DeepScaleR),
following the same settings as in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1.2 TokUR for Incorrect Reasoning Path Detection ‣ 4.1 Do TokUR’s Uncertainties Accurately Reflect Response Quality? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").
We compare TokUR with and without Length Normalization (LN), along with
representative baselines. In addition, we introduce a naive baseline,
Negative Length, which uses sequence length alone as a confidence
signal.

##### Results.

As shown in
Table [9](#A5.T9 "Table 9 ‣ E.4 TokUR for Test-Time Scaling (Online) ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
the impact of length normalization varies significantly across methods.
For both LL and TokUR, normalization consistently reduces AUROC and
AUPRC, indicating that raw sequence length introduces a favorable bias
that benefits uncertainty aggregation in de-hallucination tasks. This
observation is further reinforced by the strong performance of the
Negative Length baseline, which alone achieves competitive results
across all datasets. In contrast, Self-Certainty and DeepConf show clear
gains with normalization (e.g., Self-Certainty improves from 23.76 to
71.17 AUROC on MATH500), suggesting that normalization is essential for
stabilizing their performance. Overall, these findings reveal that the
role of length normalization is highly method-dependent.

#### E.5.4 Ablation Study of Stepwise Posterior Sampling

To examine the validity of
Assumption [3.1](#S3.Thmassumption1 "Assumption 3.1 (Stepwise Posterior Sampling). ‣ 3.1 Token-Level Uncertainties in General ‣ 3 TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation via Low-Rank Weight Perturbation ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
we perform an ablation study comparing *stepwise* posterior sampling
against the *joint* posterior formulation. Concretely, we evaluate
test-time scaling on MATH500 dataset, using TokUR with both stepwise and
joint modeling on Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, while keeping all other
settings consistent with
Table [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.2 Can TokUR’s Uncertainties Improve Generation Quality? ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning").

Fig. [6](#A5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Results. ‣ E.5.3 Ablation Study of Length Normalization ‣ E.5 Ablation Study ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
stepwise modeling consistently outperforms joint modeling across all
uncertainty measures (AU, EU, and TU). Specifically, stepwise sampling
achieves higher mean accuracy and larger improvements over the baseline,

our assumption that posterior samples should not be shared across

In this section, we present several representative examples from the
MATH500 and GSM8K datasets, along with their corresponding solutions
generated by Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct. We estimate token-level uncertainty
for each output using the definitions provided in

The visualizations are shown in
Fig. [7](#A5.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ E.6 Case Study ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning")~Fig. [10](#A5.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ E.6 Case Study ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
where Aleatoric Uncertainty (AU, in RED) and Epistemic Uncertainty (EU,
in GREEN) are visualized as text-heatmap. The background shading of each
token corresponds to the magnitude of its uncertainty: the darker the
shade, the higher the uncertainty, indicating a lower model confidence
for that token.

We observe that incorrect solutions consistently exhibit elevated
uncertainty at or near the token where the wrong final answer is
generated. For instance, as shown in
Fig. [7](#A5.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ E.6 Case Study ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ TokUR: Token-Level Uncertainty Estimation for Large Language Model Reasoning"),
sharp spikes in uncertainties happens with the arithmetic error of
reversing “9600 - 7200” into “7200 - 9600”. In contrast, correct
solutions tend to show lower uncertainty overall and maintain low
uncertainty on key answer tokens.

Furthermore, incorrect outputs tend to contain a higher density of
high-uncertainty tokens throughout the solution, whereas correct outputs
are generally more consistent and confident. These observations suggest
that our token-level uncertainty estimation method can serve as a useful
signal for identifying potential reasoning failures or unreliable
outputs, offering a valuable diagnostic tool for both model
interpretability and downstream error detection.
````

# Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates Lose Redundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents

Hau-Sing So Daixian Liu Weicai Long ^(†)^(†)thanks: Corresponding
author. Affiliation: University of Macau Affiliation: Tsinghua
University

###### Abstract

Self-improving LLM agents edit their own harness, the prompts and rules
around a fixed model, and keep an edit only if an acceptance gate scores
it higher on a batch of tasks. Such a gate sees what the harness does
under the current model, not what it would still prevent if one
safeguard failed. When safeguards overlap, deleting one is _masked_: the
others still block the harm, so the gate decides on task success alone
and redundancy erodes unseen. We measure harm for every subset of four
written safeguards in two agent environments and run 411 improvement
loops under ten gate rules, mostly as controlled deletion experiments,
plus three runs of the GEPA optimizer. A deletion behind another working
safeguard adds almost no observable harm. A paired significance test
blocks deletions that pass by chance but not deletions that genuinely
raise task success, and a larger evaluation batch lets it delete cheaper
safeguards: at the same cost, 20-task batches kept every harness
protected while 40-task batches lost all six. Large harm audits prevent
exposure but not the loss of depth, which turns into harm when the
harness is reused with a weaker model. GEPA kept every safety rule
verbatim, yet two of its three final harnesses misused credentials under
a weaker model. A statistical model frozen before the runs predicts
where each gate’s protection breaks as safeguard cost and batch size
change, and freezing safety text or auditing one-layer ablations
preserves depth.

## 1 Introduction

Self-improving agents such as ADAS, the Darwin Gödel Machine, GEPA, ACE
and Self-Harness ([Hu et al., 2025](#bib.bib18); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib58); [Agrawal et al., 2026](#bib.bib2); [Zhang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib59); [Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib57)) rewrite their own
harness: the prompts, notes and tool rules around a fixed model. Most
keep a rewrite only if an _acceptance gate_ finds that the new harness
does better than the old one on a batch of tasks. Self-evolution can
still degrade safety ([Shao et al., 2026](#bib.bib39); [Das et al.,
2026](#bib.bib8); [Mao et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)); the Darwin Gödel
Machine removed the logging that its own hallucination detector relied
on ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib58)).

A gate sees what a harness does under the current model; it does not see
what the harness would still prevent if one safeguard failed. Real
harnesses guard a harm with overlapping rules. An assistant that keeps
notes for several users may be told who owns each note, to check a noted
credential against its own, to verify the account after login, and never
to act for someone else. This is _defence in depth_ ([Reason,
1997](#bib.bib35)). While one rule suffices, deleting another changes
almost nothing a gate can measure: the deletion is masked. In
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents"),
a gate that audited every harm task in its batch accepted three
deletions in a row. The final harness stayed below our harm threshold
under the loop’s model (0.025 vs. 0.000), but misused credentials in
0.575 of runs under the weaker Qwen3-30B.

Existing checks on self-improvement test either the gain or the child.
Robust gates ask whether an accepted gain is real ([Shawn,
2026](#bib.bib40); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib48)); harm audits and
semantic floors ask whether the child behaves safely now ([Kumar et al.,
2026](#bib.bib24)). A masked deletion passes both: its gain may be real,
and the child is safe under the current model. What it removes is
_margin_, the number of further failures the harness can absorb, which
no check of the child under one model can measure. We call this loss of
redundancy masked erosion.

We study masked erosion in two environments with deterministic audits:
credential misuse in AppWorld ([Trivedi et al., 2024](#bib.bib43)) and
destructive clean-up in OpsSim, a simulated operations service. In each
we measure harm for all 16 subsets of four safeguards and run 411 loops
under ten gate rules. Most are _stress loops_, a controlled intervention
in the spirit of mutation testing ([DeMillo et al., 1978](#bib.bib10);
[Jia and Harman, 2011](#bib.bib20)): each generation deletes one
safeguard, and we record whether the gate lets it through. Eighteen
natural loops let an LLM rewrite text freely, and three runs use GEPA
unchanged. Our contributions are:

- •
  A mechanism and a way to measure it. Masking explains why a gate
  cannot see the loss of a redundant safeguard; a harm lattice over all
  safeguard subsets measures what is lost as _margin_, the number of
  further safeguards that may fail before harm appears
  (§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Masking, Margin and Child-Only Harm ‣ 3 Method ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents"),
  §[4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Masked Deletions Are Nearly Invisible ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
- •
  Advance prediction of where gates break. A gate-free effect size from
  calibration runs separates deletions that a significance test blocks
  from those it passes, and a simulator frozen before the loops predicts
  where each gate breaks as safeguard cost and batch size change: a
  larger batch admits cheaper deletions
  (§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Drift and Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents"),
  §[4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Significance Suppresses Drift ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")–[4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Significance Does Not Stop Selection ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
- •
  Evidence on what gates leave behind. Harm audits stop exposure but not
  depth loss, which becomes harm under weaker models; GEPA shows a
  related blind spot outside the labelled rules; freezing safety text
  and auditing one-layer ablations preserve depth
  (§[4.5](#S4.SS5 "4.5 What the Gates Leave Behind ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")–[4.7](#S4.SS7 "4.7 Two Structural Remedies ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).

Together, the results separate two properties that current gates
conflate: being safe now, and staying safe when something changes.

## 2 Related Work

#### Self-Improving Agents and Their Gates

STOP, ADAS, Gödel Agent and the Darwin Gödel Machine rewrite code or
harnesses ([Zelikman et al., 2024](#bib.bib56); [Hu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib18); [Yin et al., 2025](#bib.bib54); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib58)); OPRO, DSPy, GEPA and ACE tune prompts or context
([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib51); [Khattab et al., 2024](#bib.bib22);
[Agrawal et al., 2026](#bib.bib2); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib59)).
Prompt compression drops low-utility instructions by design ([Jiang
et al., 2023](#bib.bib21)). We study the acceptance step these systems
share. PACE, SAGE and RRSI make gates robust to noisy or over-fitted
improvements ([Shawn, 2026](#bib.bib40); [Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib48); [Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)), and other work
analyzes selection noise, lock-in and statistical limits ([Hu and Tang,
2026](#bib.bib17); [Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib47)). These ask whether
an accepted gain is real. We ask what real gains and noise do to
safeguards that the score barely measures.

#### Safety of Self-Evolving Agents

Self-evolution can degrade safety ([Shao et al., 2026](#bib.bib39); [Das
et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Gong et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13)), through unsafe skills ([Mao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib27); [Shang et al., 2026](#bib.bib38)) or through
experiences that are harmful only in combination ([Yan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib50)), and individually safe harness updates can interact
unsafely ([Zhang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib60)). Iterative code refinement
can remove validation logic that a static scanner does not flag ([Chen
et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)), a single masked edit; we measure how gates
accumulate such edits. Semantic floors check that an edited safety gate
still behaves like a reference gate, not merely that it is present
([Kumar et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)); a masked deletion passes such checks
under the current model. SHE accepts safety edits only if safety
improves without utility loss ([Qu et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)), and AI
control screens edits with trusted monitors ([Greenblatt et al.,
2024](#bib.bib14)).

#### Defence in Depth

Redundancy hides the failure of individual barriers ([Rasmussen,
1997](#bib.bib34); [Vaughan, 1996](#bib.bib45); [Hendrycks et al.,
2021](#bib.bib15)), and optimizing a proxy erodes what the proxy omits
([Manheim and Garrabrant, 2018](#bib.bib26); [Skalse et al.,
2022](#bib.bib41); [Amodei et al., 2016](#bib.bib3); [Gao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib12)) unless the constraint is stated ([Achiam et al.,
2017](#bib.bib1)). Minimizing a test suite can lose fault detection
([Yoo and Harman, 2012](#bib.bib55)), and mutation testing removes
checks to see whether tests notice ([Jia and Harman, 2011](#bib.bib20)).
A model swap resembles a common-cause failure of redundant barriers
([Mosleh et al., 1988](#bib.bib29)), and behaviour transfers unreliably
across models ([Sclar et al., 2024](#bib.bib37); [Echterhoff et al.,
2024](#bib.bib11)).

## 3 Method

Figure 2: Study design. Top: a proposer deletes a layer (stress loops)

### 3.1 Problem Setting

A harness $`H`$ is text given to a fixed model $`M`$; it contains a set
$`S`$ of safeguard layers that the proposer may edit like any other
text. Tasks come from a clean pool and a _harm pool_, whose tasks
contain a trap such as another user’s credential. Both pools are split
into calibration, confirmation and final sets (T*(cal), T*(conf),
T*(final)), and no gate sees T*(final). A proposer turns $`H`$ into a
child $`H^{\prime}`$, and a gate $`G`$ runs both once on a fresh batch
$`B`$ of $`n`$ tasks, each drawn from the harm pool with probability
$`p`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       G(H,H^{\prime};B)\in\{\text{accept},\text{reject}\}.
       ```                                                   |     | (1) |

Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.1 Problem Setting ‣ 3 Method ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")
lists our main gates;
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Gates, Simulator and Statistics ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")
adds an asymmetric and an intersection-union (IUT) gate, ten rules in
all with veto at two audit shares. _Split-half_ is a single-evaluation
variant of the Self-Harness rule, and _paired_ applies an exact McNemar
test ([McNemar, 1947](#bib.bib28)). Greedy, split-half and the two veto
gates ($`\rho`$ = 0.2 and 1) are _non-paired_.

|                    |                                                      |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Gate               | Accepts the child if                                 |
| _Success only_     |                                                      |
| Greedy             | it solves more batch tasks than the parent           |
| Split-half         | it is no worse on either half, better on one         |
| Paired             | a one-sided exact McNemar test rejects               |
| Read-only          | greedy, with safety text reset to the parent’s       |
| _Success and harm_ |                                                      |
| Veto($`\rho`$)     | greedy, and no child-only harm in a share $`\rho`$   |
| Audit veto         | greedy, and harm $`\leq\tau`$, none new, in 120 runs |
| Margin-aware       | greedy, and every one-layer ablation protected       |

Table 1: Gate rules ($`n`$ = 40, $`p`$ = 0.1, $`\alpha`$ = .05,
$`\rho\in\{0.2,1\}`$). Extra harm runs: 120 per audited state, 30 per
ablation.

#### Deployment Assumption

The loop may edit safeguards, and the operator later reuses its harness
with another model without re-auditing it, for instance after a provider
update ([Chen et al., 2024b](#bib.bib6)) or when routing to a cheaper
model ([Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib5)). Our swap experiments test this
scenario, not how often it occurs.

### 3.2 Masking, Margin and Child-Only Harm

For every subset $`T\subseteq S`$, let $`h(T)`$ be the harm rate on the
harm pool. $`T`$ is _protected_ if $`h(T)\leq\tau`$ = 0.05, and a layer
$`s`$ is _sufficient_ if $`\{s\}`$ is protected; both classes hold for a
given model and task pool. The _margin_ of a protected state is the
fewest deletions that reach an unprotected subset:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
m(T)=\min\,\{\,|T\setminus T^{\prime}|:T^{\prime}\subseteq T,\ h(T^{\prime})>\tau\,\},
``` |  | (2) |

with $`m`$ = $`\infty`$ if none exists, which we never observed. In
fault-tree terms, $`m`$ is the order of the smallest cut set ([Vesely
et al., 1981](#bib.bib46)); it does not require $`h`$ to be monotone.
For a deletion $`T\to T\setminus\{s\}`$, we run parent and child on the
same tasks and record the clean success gain $`g`$ and the *child-only
harm* $`h_{01}=\Pr[\text{child harms}\wedge\text{parent does not}]`$.
The deletion is *masked* if parent and child are both protected, and
*exposing* if only the parent is. Masking bounds the new harm:

|     |                                        |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       h_{01}\leq h(T\setminus\{s\})\leq\tau.
       ```                                     |     | (3) |

An audit of $`k`$ independent harm-pool pairs therefore detects a masked
deletion with probability at most $`1-(1-\tau)^{k}`$, or 0.19 for
$`k`$ = 4. A threshold rule passes every masked child, and stricter
rules need far more data than a batch holds: a veto needs about 200
pairs to catch a child-only harm of 0.008 four times in five, and a rate
comparison about 700 runs per arm to tell 0.008 from 0.025 (one-sided
$`\alpha`$ = .05, power 0.8). What a masked deletion removes is margin,
a property of the child’s ablations rather than of the child itself.
Margin is coarse but easy to interpret
(§[4.5](#S4.SS5 "4.5 What the Gates Leave Behind ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).

### 3.3 Drift and Selection

A gate sees a masked deletion mainly through its effect on success. Let
$`g_{B}=(1-p)\,g+p\,g_{h}`$ be the gain on the mixed batch, with
$`g_{h}`$ the gain on harm-pool tasks, and let
$`z=g_{B}\sqrt{n}/\sigma`$ be its standardized effect, where $`\sigma`$
is the standard deviation of the paired per-task difference; both come
from calibration pairs. With $`z_{\alpha}`$ the one-sided critical
value, a normal approximation gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P_{\text{greedy}}\approx\Phi(z),\qquad P_{\text{paired}}\approx\Phi(z-z_{\alpha}).
``` |  | (4) |

Greedy rejects ties, which the continuity correction
$`\Phi(z-1/(2\sigma\sqrt{n}))`$ accounts for
(Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Gates, Simulator and Statistics ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
Borrowing terms from population genetics ([Kimura, 1968](#bib.bib23);
[Ohta, 1973](#bib.bib30)), we distinguish two regimes. In drift
($`z\approx 0`$), noise decides: non-paired gates accept a nearly free
deletion by chance, and a valid significance test does so with
probability at most $`\alpha`$ in every generation. In selection
($`z\gg 0`$), the deletion truly raises success and passes success-based
gates, all the more surely in a larger batch: the smallest cost that a
significance gate detects falls as $`\sigma/\sqrt{n}`$, and faster at
small $`n`$ for the exact test. We label an edge selection when its
calibration $`z\geq z_{\alpha}`$, without reference to gate decisions; a
simulator-based classification agrees on 84–100% of edges
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Robustness ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
The batch-size effects below follow from
Eq. [4](#S3.E4 "In 3.3 Drift and Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents");
what is new is that the break points were predicted before any loop ran.

### 3.4 Statistical Analysis

Loops are the unit of analysis. We use one-sided Fisher and exact
permutation tests across loops, matched tests where arms share random
numbers, and Holm correction ([Holm, 1979](#bib.bib16)) within each
registered family. Each confirmatory prediction and its analysis code
were frozen and hashed before the test ran ([van Miltenburg et al.,
2021](#bib.bib44)). Predictions come from a *gate simulator* that
resamples calibration pairs with a Bayesian bootstrap ([Rubin,
1981](#bib.bib36)), runs the exact gate code and chains acceptance
probabilities over the lattice
(Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Gates, Simulator and Statistics ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
Its frozen predictions placed the break costs for 20-, 40- and 120-task
batches before the loops ran, and every paired count of the cost
dose-response fell inside its frozen interval. On single AppWorld
decisions, where every edge is drift, it predicts no better than each
gate’s mean rate
(Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Gates, Simulator and Statistics ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents"));
its value lies where effects vary. Not every prediction held: of 177
registered predictions, 113 held, 49 failed, 9 held in part and 6 could
not be scored. Every verdict was computed by the frozen analysis code.
Of the 14 tests behind the main claims, 7 held; the others fell short in
the predicted direction (3), found a stronger effect than predicted (2)
or found a harm-aware gate weaker than predicted (2)
(Appendix [K](#A11 "Appendix K Preregistration ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
Three later prediction sets, including GEPA’s, were frozen after related
outcomes, and the drift account was formulated after a predicted
AppWorld selection effect failed to replicate.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Credential Misuse in AppWorld

Following [So et al. (2026)](#bib.bib42), the agent’s notes hold login
credentials for several apps. In the harm pool, the note for the target
app (Amazon, Gmail or Venmo) holds another user’s working credential,
which the task never needs. A deterministic audit, with no LLM judge,
flags any non-login call made with a foreign token and any net change to
that user’s account. The starting harness H0 has four layers: auth names
each note’s owner, verify uses a noted credential only if it matches the
requester’s own, profile checks the account after login, and caution
allows only the requester’s credentials. Tasks are split by supervisor
(30/30/20 per pool for T_(cal)/T_(conf)/T_(final)). A ReAct-style code
agent ([Yao et al., 2023](#bib.bib53)) runs at most 15 steps at
temperature 0.

#### Destructive Clean-Up in OpsSim

Tickets ask the agent to clear stale cache or lock entries; in the harm
pool, the notes suggest a wildcard or admin rights that would also
delete an entry still in use. The layers are scope, confirm (ask the
owner first), leastpriv and dryrun. The step budget sets their cost: at
three steps, removing all four raises clean success from 0.00–0.50 to
1.00 for the five loop models, while at four steps they cost DeepSeek
nothing in calibration. The simulator runs in memory and never executes
a command.

#### Models, Loops and GEPA

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash is the main agent and proposer; Qwen3-30B-A3B and
Qwen3-8B run locally ([Qwen Team, 2025](#bib.bib32)). GPT-6 Luna,
GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Haiku 5.5, reached through a third-party API,
serve as supplementary models; every main finding holds with DeepSeek
and the local Qwen models alone. Loops run eight generations with
$`n`$ = 40 and $`p`$ = 0.1 unless noted. *Stress* loops propose deleting
a uniformly chosen remaining layer. *Natural* loops use a free-form
DeepSeek proposer asked to make the harness cheaper
(Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Lattices and Loops ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
Of the 411 loops, 393 are stress loops (78 in AppWorld, 315 in OpsSim)
and 18 are natural loops. Finally, we run GEPA 0.1.4 unchanged ([Agrawal
et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)) for three seeds of 800 metric calls on
AppWorld, scored on task success alone
(Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H An Off-the-Shelf Optimizer (GEPA) ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).

### 4.2 Masked Deletions Are Nearly Invisible

Under DeepSeek, auth and verify each suffice alone, caution and profile
only reduce harm, and H0 has margin 2
(Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")a).
profile’s weakness is partly an audit artefact: the audit counts the
account check that profile itself prescribes. Without that call, profile
suffices, H0’s margin is 3, and no conclusion below changes
(Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Audit Variants ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
Deletions into children protected on T_(cal) carry child-only harm of
0.016 on T_(conf) (upper bound 0.028), against 0.422 for deletions into
unprotected children. A four-task batch audit would notice the former in
about 6% of decisions.

The pattern holds for every model that follows some layer reliably
(Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")b).
In OpsSim, confirm alone suffices for DeepSeek, Luna and Sol, and
deletions behind it carry $`h_{01}\leq 0.018`$. Weak models have nothing
to mask: no single AppWorld layer suffices under Qwen3-30B, and Qwen3-8B
and Haiku follow no layer reliably. Protection is a property of the
harness and the model together.

### 4.3 Significance Suppresses Drift

In 27 AppWorld stress loops with DeepSeek, every deletion is nearly
neutral ($`|z|\leq 1.1`$). Greedy and split-half gates accepted 0.48 and
0.44 of proposed deletions and the paired gate 0.04
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2 Masked Deletions Are Nearly Invisible ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")b;
loop-level permutation, Holm $`p`$ = .002 for both). Greedy accepted
masked deletions at 0.63, against 0.36 for no-op edits at the same
parents: noise explains most acceptances, and small batch gains the
rest. These gains did not transfer: greedy finals solved 0.358 of clean
T_(final) tasks, H0 0.400.

The batches gave no warning. The 32 deletions into protected children
that greedy, split-half and paired gates accepted came with 114
harm-pool tasks (48 distinct) and no child-only harm, while the margin
fell
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2 Masked Deletions Are Nearly Invisible ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")a).
Greedy loops ended unprotected in 5 of 6 runs, four of them with no
layer left, and paired loops in none (one-sided Fisher, Holm
$`p`$ = .015); split-half loops did so in 2 of 6. The paired test
suppresses drift without stopping it: it accepted 2 of 42 masked
deletions, and chaining its frozen per-edge rates gives an unprotected
final with probability 0.19 by generation 64
(Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Selection and Remedies ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
This protection comes from conservatism
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2 Masked Deletions Are Nearly Invisible ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")c):
at $`\alpha`$ = .05 the paired gate passes a real +0.075 edit in 8% of
decisions, against 96% for greedy, and at $`\alpha`$ = .20 it passes
that edit in 54% but accepts 17% of masked deletions.

At four steps the layers are free. Letting the owner whom confirm asks

#### Larger Batches Admit Cheaper Deletions

By

a larger batch lets a significance gate detect, and therefore accept, a
smaller gain; the question is whether this can be predicted. Before the
runs, the simulator placed the break at a cost of 0.20, 0.09 and 0.03
for batches of 20, 40 and 120 tasks

With 20 tasks per batch, paired loops kept all six harnesses protected
at a cost of 0.15, where 40-task loops lost all six ($`p`$ = .001). With

costs of 0.03 and 0.08, where 40-task loops lost 0 and 1; counting each
distinct task once would reverse 4 of their 10 acceptances, so these
loops confirm the direction rather than the exact break

A larger batch, usually the safer choice, admits cheaper deletions.

Selection also works inside layers.

#### Lost Depth Becomes Harm Under Weaker Models

We re-ran the 11 distinct finals of the 27 DeepSeek stress loops on
T_(final) under other models, comparing only finals still protected
under DeepSeek, which separates lost depth from existing exposure
(Appendix
Figure [9](#A9.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Task Shift and a Quantized Model. ‣ Appendix I Model Swap ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
Under Qwen3-30B, non-paired finals misused credentials in 0.30 of runs
and paired finals in 0.004 (CI of the difference \[0.16, 0.47\]); under
Qwen3-8B the gap was 0.16 \[0.02, 0.29\]. The harm follows the missing
layers, not a model trying things at random. Qwen3-30B used up its step
budget in 63–90% of runs under every final, H0 included, yet finals that
kept auth and at least one other layer misused credentials in 2 of 200
runs, auth alone in 9 of 40, and finals without auth in 89 of 200, in
step-exhausted and shorter runs alike (Appendix
Table [31](#A9.T31 "Table 31 ‣ Missing Layers, Not Random Trials. ‣ Appendix I Model Swap ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
Layers that are interchangeable for DeepSeek are not interchangeable for
the weaker model: it relies mainly on auth, and depth behind auth still
cuts its harm from 0.225 to 0.01. Lost redundancy is lost coverage for a
model that relies on different layers; a swap acts as a common-cause
failure of the remaining barriers ([Mosleh et al., 1988](#bib.bib29)).
The effect is specific to weaker executors. Both Qwen models solve about
5% of clean AppWorld tasks; under Luna and Sol, which solve them, the
gap was only 0.025 \[0, 0.07\], and after a task shift under DeepSeek
0.020 ($`p`$ = .061). Because the weaker model leans on one layer,
margin under DeepSeek ranks its harm only with Spearman
$`r_{s}`$ = 0.65; the expected harm after one random layer failure does
better (0.80), but needs far more harm runs than a batch holds
(Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Model Swap ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).

### 4.6 An Off-the-Shelf Optimizer: GEPA

Stress loops plant deletions; GEPA proposes its own edits. We split the
harness into four text components (note header, note template,
credential rules and task tips) and scored task success alone. GEPA
keeps a candidate that beats its parent on four tasks, which is a greedy
gate, and returns the best-scoring candidate on 27 validation tasks; 181
of 186 proposals were valid (Appendix
Table [30](#A8.T30 "Table 30 ‣ Setup. ‣ Appendix H An Off-the-Shelf Optimizer (GEPA) ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).

#### GEPA Runs in the Drift Regime

GEPA’s validation gains, up to +0.22, did not transfer: its finals
solved 0.35–0.45 of clean T_(final) tasks, against 0.40 for H0. Like a
greedy stress loop, it accepted edits it could not tell from noise, and
it kept rewrites of the credential rules at its usual rate (7 of 45; all
edits: 33 of 181). Of the 17 rewrites our judge flagged as weakening a
check, 13 in fact replaced the noted-credential check with a stricter
rule; GEPA kept 3 of those and neither genuine relaxation, and no final
weakened a credential rule.

#### Rules Intact, Behaviour Changed

All three finals kept the credential rules verbatim and caused no harm
under DeepSeek. Two of them rewrote the note template into an
instruction to log in with the stored credentials (“Use these exact
credentials to authenticate to {app} as {owner} whenever needed”), and
seed 2 also added 3,000 characters of efficiency tips. Under Qwen3-30B
these two finals misused credentials in 2 and 5 of 40 runs, on 2 and 3
of 20 tasks, against 0 of 80 for H0. The change adds an instruction
rather than deleting a safeguard, but it is masked in the same way: the
remaining rules override it under the loop’s model, not under a weaker
one. It lies outside the labelled layers over which margin is defined,
and the judge marked every layer intact. With three seeds and few harm
events, it is a case study, not an estimate.

### 4.7 Two Structural Remedies

#### Freezing Safety Text

In four natural loops, safety text was reset to the parent’s. All four
finals kept H0’s safety text and reached the highest clean success of
any natural arm, with no harm under DeepSeek and 0.019 under Qwen3-30B
(Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Natural Proposers Trim Costly Checks ‣ 4.4 Significance Does Not Stop Selection ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")b).
Freezing protects text, not behaviour, and requires knowing which text
is safety text: one final added an unfrozen tip against “extra
profile/account checks”. Under selection in three-step OpsSim, freezing
only confirm kept all six loops protected while the costly layers around
it were deleted, at a cost of 0.05 in clean success
(Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Natural Proposers Trim Costly Checks ‣ 4.4 Significance Does Not Stop Selection ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")c).

#### Auditing One-Layer Ablations

The margin-aware gate applies mutation adequacy ([Jia and Harman,
2011](#bib.bib20)) to safeguards: it accepts a child only if every
one-layer ablation stays protected. On the greedy loops’ random numbers,
none of its loops ended unprotected (greedy: 5 of 6; matched sign test
$`p`$ = .031), with no loss of clean success. At similar audit compute,
it kept more layers than the audit veto in all six matched loops (sign
test $`p`$ = .016), and its finals harmed less under Qwen3-30B
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Audits Stop Exposure, Not Depth Loss ‣ 4.5 What the Gates Leave Behind ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
Under selection in OpsSim it kept every loop protected by keeping three
layers, at a cost of 0.35 in clean success. The gate checks layers, not
wording: in all three natural loops it let verify be weakened
(Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Natural Erosion ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).

## 5 Conclusion

We studied when acceptance gates in self-improving agents erode the
redundancy behind low observed harm. A deletion behind another working
safeguard is masked, so the gate decides on task success, and where its
protection breaks can be predicted before the runs. Harm audits
prevented exposure but not depth loss, which became harm under weaker
models, while freezing safety text or auditing one-layer ablations
preserved depth. Self-improving agents should keep safety-critical
behaviour outside the optimizer, gate edits on what remains rather than
only on what happens, and re-audit the harness after a model change.

## Limitations

Our main limitation is scope. We study two environments, each with one
harm and four written safeguards, and most loops plant one deletion per
generation to isolate the gate. We chose this setting to expose how
gates respond to deletion pressure, not to estimate how often deployed
optimizers erode safeguards; the natural loops and GEPA runs are case
studies. Erosion matters only when a harm that the model does not avoid
on its own meets redundant layers that it follows reliably and a later
change that needs them. Strong models avoided the harms we probed in
$`\tau^{2}`$-bench, AgentDojo and a privacy simulation
(Appendix [A](#A1.SS0.SSS0.Px4 "Harms Strong Models Already Avoid. ‣ Appendix A Environments and Safeguards ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")),
and our largest swap effects come from weaker models. Batch size and
harm share were varied only in OpsSim, where the 120-task batches
resample a 60-task pool; long-horizon results are simulator projections,
and results from the third-party API models are supplementary.

## Ethical Considerations

#### Potential Risks

The study identifies safeguard loss that a behavioural gate may miss and
evaluates ways to detect or constrain it. The same findings could inform
attempts to weaken safeguards, which motivates transparent reporting of
assumptions and of the limits of each remedy. OpsSim and the privacy
simulation use synthetic data in memory: structured agent calls operate
on dictionaries, and a static check verifies that OpsSim cannot access
files, networks or processes.

#### Data and Licenses

All AppWorld accounts and credentials are synthetic; no personal data
was used. AppWorld is released under the Apache 2.0 license with the
requirement that public redistribution of its task data be encrypted,
$`\tau^{2}`$-bench and AgentDojo are MIT-licensed, and the Qwen3 weights
are released under Apache 2.0; we use all of them for their intended
research purpose. Proprietary models were accessed through APIs, and API
credentials are excluded from all released material. We will release
code, the exact safeguard texts and proposer prompts, frozen predictions
with hashes and run records; records that contain AppWorld task data
will be encrypted as its license requires.

#### AppWorld.

AppWorld v0.2.0.dev with a ReAct-style loop: the agent writes Python
against the app APIs under AppWorld’s own instructions, with at most 15
steps and 4096 output tokens per step. The notes list credentials for
the target app and two others; in the harm pool the target app’s entry
belongs to another supervisor and was validated by logging in. Harm is
either of two deterministic events ([So et al., 2026](#bib.bib42)): B, a
non-login API call made with a foreign access token, or S, a net state
change in the other supervisor’s account. Two audit slices are skipped
because the installed AppWorld lacks their tables; no run called those
APIs, so the skip removes no harm event.

#### OpsSim.

160 tickets (30/30/20 per pool and split). In half of the harm tickets
the notes suggest a wildcard that also matches another service’s live
entry; in the other half they suggest admin=true, under which a wildcard
also removes a lock held by a running process. A run succeeds if every
requested entry is deleted and harms if a protected entry is. The
workspace is a dictionary and the interpreter accepts only six
structured calls; a static check forbids file, process and network
access. With DeepSeek every state solves all clean T_(cal) tasks at 4, 6
and 15 steps, so the layers cost success only at three.

#### $`\tau^{2}`$-bench.

Retail domain, with DeepSeek as agent and user at temperature 0. We
remove the policy sentences on identity verification and on confirmation
before writes; a rule-based audit flags a user-specific read before
authentication or a write without explicit consent.

#### Harms Strong Models Already Avoid.

A written safeguard can erode with consequence only if the model does
not already follow it. In $`\tau^{2}`$-bench retail ([Yao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib52); [Barres et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)), deleting the
policy sentences on authentication and on confirmation before writes, in
all four combinations, changed DeepSeek’s reward and violation rate by
at most 0.05: without the confirmation sentence the agent still asked
before all 136 writes, and the tool interface enforces authentication;
Luna and Sol behaved the same. In AgentDojo ([Debenedetti et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9)) (v0.1.35, suites v1.2.2), four attacks succeeded in 6
of 240 runs without any safeguard, and in a privacy simulation with
synthetic canary records the agent checked permissions and redacted
unprompted, leaking in none of 72 runs. Such rules are masked by the
model itself: deleting them is invisible to every gate until a model
with other defaults is used.

## Appendix B Gates, Simulator and Statistics

#### Gates.

Let $`y_{i},y_{i}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}`$ be parent and child success on
task $`i`$ of a batch of $`n`$ = 40, and $`n_{01}`$ ($`n_{10}`$) the
tasks that only the child (parent) solves. *Greedy* accepts iff
$`\sum_{i}y_{i}^{\prime}>\sum_{i}y_{i}`$; with
$`D=\sum_{i}(y_{i}^{\prime}-y_{i})`$, a continuity correction gives
$`P(D\geq 1)\approx\Phi(z-1/(2\sigma\sqrt{n}))`$, the tie-aware form of
Eq. [4](#S3.E4 "In 3.3 Drift and Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents").
*Split-half*, a single-evaluation variant of the Self-Harness rule
([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib57)), accepts iff the changes on the two
halves satisfy $`\Delta_{a},\Delta_{b}\geq 0`$ and
$`\max(\Delta_{a},\Delta_{b})>0`$; Self-Harness re-evaluates stochastic
runs, so our results concern this variant only. *Paired* accepts iff the
exact one-sided McNemar test rejects:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\textstyle\sum_{k\geq n_{01}}\binom{n_{d}}{k}2^{-n_{d}}\leq\alpha,\quad n_{d}=n_{01}+n_{10}.
``` |  | (5) |

*Veto*$`(\rho)`$ applies the greedy rule and rejects if any audited
harm-pool pair (a share $`\rho`$ of them) shows child-only harm;
*asymmetric* sends candidates labelled as weakening a safeguard to the
paired test and the rest to the greedy rule. The registered swap tests
compare *greedy-type* gates (greedy, split-half and veto 0.2) with
paired; *non-paired* gates, as in the main text, are greedy, split-half,
veto ($`\rho`$ = 0.2) and veto ($`\rho`$ = 1). Each generation draws
tasks without replacement from the 60 per pool in
T_(cal) $`\cup`$ T_(conf) and runs parent and child with a new shared
seed; tasks recur across generations, runs do not. Batches of 120 cannot
be drawn this way. They draw with replacement, as the simulator does at
every $`n`$, and run each drawn task through a per-task cache; a
120-task batch holds about 50 distinct clean tasks (49.6 and 50.8 on
average at the two costs), and the McNemar test counts repeated pairs
separately. These loops therefore test a larger *decision* batch on a
fixed task pool, which is also how the cost is defined: in four-step
OpsSim the owner’s silence is fixed per task (2, 5, 9 and 19 of the 60
clean tasks at the four costs), and in calibration the full stack failed
as many clean tasks as were silent while its child without confirm
failed none. At zero cost there are no discordant pairs, and no batch
size makes the paired gate accept. The batch-size claim does not rest on
these loops alone: the 20-task loops, drawn without replacement, kept
every harness protected where 40-task loops lost all six. In AppWorld,
whose runs are not deterministic, the $`n`$ = 120 projections resample a
pool whose gains did not transfer to T_(final): they describe a gate
fitting its own task pool. A batch holds Binomial$`(n,p)`$ harm-pool
tasks, 3.98 on average over the 204 AppWorld stress batches.

#### Archived Batch Replay.

An independent replay of the two $`n`$ = 120 arms ($`q`$ = .05 and .10)
rebuilt 95 of their 96 batches from the saved per-task outcomes; one
batch lacks one saved outcome. All 95 paired-gate decisions and all harm
totals agree with the logs. Eight rebuilt batches differ from the logged
parent or child success totals by one or two outcomes. Duplicate draws
of a task were submitted concurrently to a cache without per-key
locking, so a repeated draw could be run twice while the cache kept one
result; this plausibly explains the differences, though the archive
cannot prove it. Exact reuse of one outcome per repeated task is
therefore not guaranteed, although no decision depended on it. Counting
each distinct task once, i.e., a decision batch of about 60 distinct
tasks, would have turned 4 of the 10 accepting decisions into rejections
(three at $`q`$ = .05, one at $`q`$ = .10), as
Eq. [4](#S3.E4 "In 3.3 Drift and Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")
predicts for a smaller batch; this is a sensitivity check on the logged
decisions, not a new trajectory. Three of the four reversals fall in the
arm behind the main text’s lowest break cost (0.03), so that number
holds only for this repeated-draw gate; the 20-task loops, drawn without
replacement, carry the batch-size claim. The released runner
deduplicates jobs before submission (verified by four offline tests);
all reported runs predate this fix.

#### Null Acceptance.

For identical harnesses with tie probability $`\pi`$ on the batch and
$`\pi^{\prime}`$ on a half, greedy accepts with probability
$`(1-\pi)/2<1/2`$ and split-half with
$`(1+2\pi^{\prime}-3\pi^{\prime 2})/4\leq 1/3`$. On replicate pairs,
no-op edits were accepted at 0.400 (greedy), 0.323 (split-half) and
0.013 (paired) averaged over the 16 parent states, and at 0.358, 0.320
and 0.006 weighted by the parents of the masked deletions, the rates
used in
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Significance Suppresses Drift ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")
and
Table [7](#A3.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Masked Deletions Are Not No-Ops. ‣ Appendix C Robustness ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents").
Detection grows with the harm share: at $`p`$ = 0.5 a full audit sees a
deletion with $`h_{01}`$ = 0.016 in about 28% of decisions.

The single-edit study also tested an anytime-valid betting test ([Ramdas
et al., 2023](#bib.bib33)), cost-aware greedy and paired gates, and an
intersection-union audit gate. On T_(conf) they accepted
single-safeguard deletions at rates of at most 0.004, 0.16–0.56,

the others are projected over loops in

#### Simulator and Baselines.

For each edit the simulator draws Dirichlet weights over calibration
tasks, samples 10,000 batches of joint parent/child outcomes and applies
the exact gate code; loop predictions chain these rates over the lattice
with one uniformly chosen deletion per generation. In AppWorld it beats
the frozen baselines on single decisions but not the constant baselines
added after confirmation, and beats all of them on trajectories
(Table [5](#A2.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Tests. ‣ Appendix B Gates, Simulator and Statistics ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents"));
in three-step OpsSim it beats N0 on single decisions (Brier gain CI
\[0.042, 0.107\]), as expected when edits have real effects.

## Appendix F Selection and Remedies

#### Free and Costly Layers.

At three steps the full stack solves 0.00–0.50 of clean OpsSim tasks for
the five loop models and the empty stack all of them
(Table [15](#A5.T15 "Table 15 ‣ Appendix E Lattices and Loops ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents"));
at four steps DeepSeek’s full stack solves every clean task and no edge
is selection. Over twelve loops per arm, six of them with fresh seeds,
paired loops ended unprotected in 2 of 12 at four steps and 7 of 12 at
three (Fisher $`p`$ = .045), greedy loops in 5 and 11 of 12. With free
layers the paired gate protects most loops, not all: both exposed
four-step paired loops (fresh seeds) accepted deleting confirm in a
batch where the parent solved 16 and 25 of 40 tasks and the child 40 and
39, a cost that calibration had not shown, rather than a near-tie.

#### Cost Dose-Response.

At four steps, ask_owner stays silent with probability $`q`$, fixed per
task by a hash and nested across $`q`$, so that only confirm costs
success. Every paired count lies inside its frozen interval
(Table [20](#A6.T20 "Table 20 ‣ Cost Dose-Response. ‣ Appendix F Selection and Remedies ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
Pooled, paired loops ended unprotected in 0 of 12 at $`q\leq.05`$ and 10
of 12 at $`q\geq.2`$ (Fisher $`p<.001`$). The break at 8–15 points of
cost lies near $`z_{\alpha}`$ at $`n`$ = 40: $`q\approx.08`$ by
Eq. [4](#S3.E4 "In 3.3 Drift and Selection ‣ 3 Method ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents"),
$`q\approx.14`$ by the exact test.

#### Remedies Under Selection.

In three-step OpsSim, *read-only* confirm resets that rule to the
parent’s; the *IUT* gate adds to the paired test a Newcombe hybrid-score
one-sided 95% upper bound of at most 0.10 on child-minus-parent harm,
computed over all 40 pairs of the batch (OpsSim audits every task; clean
tasks rarely harm); the *margin-aware* gate is the AppWorld rule below
with a 30-task suite. Read-only confirm and margin-aware kept every loop
protected, IUT four of six, as a 0.10 margin over 40 pairs, about four
of them from the harm pool, is loose
(Table [21](#A6.T21 "Table 21 ‣ Remedies Under Selection. ‣ Appendix F Selection and Remedies ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")):
on the mixed batch it allows a child-minus-parent harm of up to about
$`0.10/p`$, i.e. almost any increase on the harm pool at $`p`$ = 0.1, so
this IUT is a weak baseline, and the veto and audit veto are the
stricter harm-aware gates. In the AppWorld single-edit study, where the
bound used only the audited share of harm-pool pairs, the same gate
accepted nothing. Against the greedy finals, margin-aware lost 0.35 of
clean success (CI \[0.28, 0.49\]), read-only confirm 0.05 and IUT 0.02.
Freezing the sufficient layer requires knowing which layer that is.

#### Without Redundancy.

In a four-step OpsSim variant each of four trap kinds has exactly one
blocking layer: full-stack harm is 0.000, empty-stack harm 0.83, and
every single deletion exposes harm (0.08–0.34). Greedy loops still ended
unprotected in 6 of 6 and veto ($`\rho`$ = 1) loops in 5 of 6, after six
accepted deletions none of which showed child-only harm in its batch;
paired accepted none of 48. A deletion harms only on its own trap kind,
which a batch of about four harm-pool tasks rarely holds: masking
guarantees small increments, but narrow layers produce them too. The
audit veto of 120 harm runs per state (six loops on the same random
numbers) saw every such deletion: it rejected all 23 deletions that
greedy accepted, kept the full stack in every loop (0 of 6 unprotected;
clean success 1.00, T_(final) harm 0) and spent 600 audit runs. Without
redundancy, a large enough audit loses nothing; with redundancy
(AppWorld,
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Audits Stop Exposure, Not Depth Loss ‣ 4.5 What the Gates Leave Behind ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")),
the same gate prevented exposure but lost depth. This is close to a
design check, since in a world without redundancy keeping the full stack
is the only correct answer.

#### Audit of Equal Compute.

The audit veto runs each greedy-accepted child twice on 60 fixed harm
tasks and rejects on harm above $`\tau`$ or any child-only harm, at 120
runs per distinct state visited (eleven distinct children were audited),
the same order as the margin-aware gate’s 1,228 suite runs over six
loops. On greedy loops 1–6 no loop ended unprotected, but five finals
had margin 1 and one margin 2 (mean 1.17), with clean success 0.44 and
Qwen3-30B harm 0.21 (margin-aware: 1.33, 0.37, 0.04). On the shared
random numbers the margin-aware gate kept more layers in all six matched
loops (one-sided sign test $`p`$ = .016), while their margins differed
in one. Leave-one-out checks are not needed to avoid exposure under the
loop’s model; they buy depth against a swap.

#### Margin-Aware Gate.

The gate applies the greedy rule and, for an edit that changes safety
text, requires every leave-one-out ablation of the child (without the
owner line or without one credential rule) to have harm $`\leq`$ 0.05 on
30 T_(cal) harm tasks; a child with at most one safety unit is rejected.
The check is a proof test of redundant channels in the sense of
functional safety ([International Electrotechnical Commission,
2010](#bib.bib19)). Suite runs use a fixed replicate, cached per state
and model in stress loops (480 runs each); natural loops pay 30 runs per
safety unit and check. Checked exactly, the rule keeps margin 2 under
whole-layer deletions from a parent with margin $`\geq`$ 2, since the
child is then a protected ablation of the parent; because $`h`$ need not
be monotone, a free-form child needs its own check as well, which this
gate does not run; four finals have margin 1 because 30-run estimates
near $`\tau`$ are noisy (a check passes true harm 0.10 with probability
0.18), and 4 of 22 decisions were false accepts against the pooled
classification. On shared random numbers, five greedy loops ended
unprotected and their margin-aware twins did not, with no pair the other
way (sign test, one-sided $`p`$ = .031;
Table [22](#A6.T22 "Table 22 ‣ Margin-Aware Gate. ‣ Appendix F Selection and Remedies ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")).
In natural loops it rejected 1 of 17 greedy-passing candidates; all
finals kept auth and caution.

## Appendix H An Off-the-Shelf Optimizer (GEPA)

#### Setup.

We ran GEPA 0.1.4 ([Agrawal et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)) without modifying
the library: Pareto candidate selection, a round-robin module selector,
strict-improvement acceptance on reflection minibatches of four tasks,
and 800 metric calls per seed (seeds 0–2). The harness is split into
four plain-text components, the note header, the note template (one line
per credential), the credential rules and task tips (empty in H0); the
seed candidate reproduces H0 exactly. The score is task success alone,
with no cost, length or efficiency hint; audit harm is recorded but
never shown to the optimizer. Each seed shuffles
T_(cal) $`\cup`$ T_(conf) with its own random seed and takes 40 training
tasks (36 clean, 4 harm) and the next 27 as validation tasks (24 clean,
3 harm), disjoint from training. Validation sets therefore differ across
seeds (9–10 of 27 tasks shared), which together with run variation
explains why H0 scored 0.444, 0.148 and 0.333 on them; on shared tasks
H0’s outcomes agreed in 24 of 28 cases. DeepSeek proposes at temperature
0.7. Only the adapter is ours: it shows the reflection model the
component’s current text as the instruction and the task only as example
input, and it retries a proposal up to three times when the reply has no
code block or restates the task. Nothing checks safety content. A frozen
smoke test passed on the first attempt (29 of 30 valid proposals).

#### What GEPA Changed.

Proposals cycled evenly over the four components (44, 49, 45 and 43).
The judge flagged 17 rule rewrites, 13 for verify and 4 for profile.
Read by us, the 13 verify flags replace “use a noted credential only if
it matches mine” with a stricter rule, “use only the credentials that
the supervisor returns”, which the judge scores as removing the original
mechanism; all three flagged rewrites that GEPA kept are of this kind.
Of the four profile flags, two genuinely relax the post-login check
(“that account is yours even if the app shows a different email”; “avoid
extra verification calls after every login”), one only caches a
verification and one is a false flag; none was kept. No final candidate
changed the credential rules. Reading thus confirms only 2 of the 17
flags as relaxations, and the judge marked every layer of both harmful
finals intact.

#### Where the Harm Came From.

Seeds 1 and 2 rewrote the note template into an instruction to log in
with the stored credentials. Seed 1: “Stored login for {app} (owner:
{owner}) … Use these credentials to log in to {app} and obtain an access
token whenever the task involves {owner}’s {app} account.” Seed 2:
“Saved {app} login for {owner} … Use these exact credentials to
authenticate to {app} as {owner} whenever needed”, with a header asking
the agent to log in directly with saved credentials and 3,030 characters
of tips that start with a limited step budget. Both keep the owner’s
name, so the judge marks auth intact, and the credential rules are
verbatim. Under DeepSeek the rules still hold; under Qwen3-30B, harm
rose to 2 and 5 of 40 runs against 0 of 80 for H0 Counted over runs,
seed 2 differs from H0 (one-sided Fisher $`p`$ = .003), but its events
fall on 3 of 20 tasks and seed 1’s on 2 (one task in both), so a sign
test over tasks gives $`p`$ = .125 for seed 2; three seeds do not
support a loop-level test. We did not ablate the components or run
one-layer ablations of the finals. The template adds an instruction
rather than removing one; it is masked in the sense that the loop’s
model shows no harm, but it lies outside the labelled layers over which
margin is defined.

## Appendix J Runs, Compute and Reproducibility

#### Models and Sampling.

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash was accessed through its official API.
Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 (AWQ int4) and Qwen3-8B (FP8, thinking
disabled; an AWQ int4 build for the 4-bit swap) were served with vLLM
0.10.1.1 on one 32 GB GPU with a 32k context; runs exceeding it were
scored as running out of steps. Luna, Sol and Haiku were accessed
through a third-party OpenAI-compatible API as gpt-6-luna, gpt-6.1-sol
and claude-haiku-5-5; in a final probe its model field matched the
requested name for all three. Haiku declined the AppWorld agent framing
in most episodes and Sol cost about \$0.28 per AppWorld run, so neither
has an AppWorld lattice or loops (Sol is used there only as a swap
target). Every main conclusion holds with DeepSeek and the local Qwen
models alone; the API models add supplementary rows and swap targets.
All runs, in October 2026, use temperature 0; replicate agreement is
0.78–0.90, not bit-exact.

#### Compute.

More than 40,000 AppWorld episodes plus OpsSim episodes of a few tool
calls each; API fees totalled under \$1,000, and local runs used a
single GPU. Step budgets, $`n`$, $`p`$, $`\alpha`$ and $`\rho`$ are
fixed as in
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Masked Erosion: How Acceptance Gates LoseRedundant Safeguards in Self-Improving Agents")
and were not tuned on the confirmation or final splits.

## Appendix K Preregistration

The study ran in rounds. Before each round we froze its predictions and
analysis code and hashed both; all hashes were re-checked at the end,
and we release the files with their timestamps together with all run
records. Later code changes (logging, runner robustness, new gate
classes) touched no frozen prediction, decision rule, task or edit set,
and all 204 AppWorld stress decisions were re-derived from raw records.
Of 177 distinct registered predictions, 113 held (66%; 64% among blind
ones), 9 held in part and 6 could not be scored; the released ledger
gives every verdict, and

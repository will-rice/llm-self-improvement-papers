# OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches: A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models

Jaehoon Kim Affiliation: Yonsei University

###### Abstract

On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) has recently emerged as an
alternative to Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR),
promising higher accuracy and shorter responses through token-level
credit assignment from a self-teacher conditioned on privileged context.
However, this promise does not carry over to thinking-enabled
mathematical reasoning, where reported accuracy gains shrink and
sometimes turn negative. We hypothesize that hindsight supervision can
specify better token-level alternatives in short thinking-disabled
outputs, but in long thinking-enabled traces it more readily identifies
redundancy than supplies better replacements. To test this, we applied
OPSD separately to correct and incorrect rollout groups, so that
compression and correction can be observed in isolation. Our results
show that in thinking-enabled mathematical reasoning, OPSD behaves most
reliably as a compression mechanism rather than a correction mechanism:
training only on correct rollouts preserves accuracy while substantially
shortening responses, whereas training only on incorrect rollouts
damages accuracy. In light of these findings, we propose a revised
post-training pipeline for thinking-enabled mathematical reasoning: SFT
then RLVR then OPSD.

## 1 Introduction

On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) has recently emerged as a promising
alternative to Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)
\[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib3), [3](#bib.bib5), [4](#bib.bib7)\].
Unlike RLVR, which reduces an entire rollout to a binary reward and
broadcasts that single bit information across the generated tokens
\[[5](#bib.bib20), [6](#bib.bib21), [7](#bib.bib22)\], OPSD can use
richer textual feedback and convert it into token-level credit
assignment. It does so through a self-teacher that shares the same
parameters as the student model but is conditioned on additional
privileged context, such as an expert demonstration, environment
feedback, or the student’s own reflection. For each token in a
student-generated rollout, the self-teacher provides a target
distribution, which the student is trained to match. Thanks to these
mechanisms, OPSD yields models with higher end-task accuracy and shorter
response length.

However, this is not the case in the setting of thinking-enabled
mathematical reasoning. Here, the accuracy gains are often small and
sometimes negative. Most of the gains reported in thinking-disabled
regimes are lost once thinking is enabled. And on mathematical
reasoning, with its longer reasoning traces, the remaining gains appear
to shrink further than on tasks like knowledge, science, or tool-use.
Together, these patterns suggest that something specific to
thinking-enabled mathematical reasoning is limiting OPSD’s accuracy
gains.

We hypothesize that this is because, in thinking-disabled settings, the
hindsight-conditioned self-teacher can directly specify the desired
alternative tokens in the student’s short output, whereas in
thinking-enabled settings, the student generates a sequence of
intermediate reasoning steps for which it is difficult to pinpoint the
correct alternative tokens. Furthermore, on complex mathematical
reasoning tasks, it becomes increasingly more difficult to do so, as
they require of even longer reasoning traces to be generated
\[[8](#bib.bib33), [9](#bib.bib31), [10](#bib.bib30)\]. Yet it remains
capable of pointing out redundancies within the reasoning trace, since
hindsight can reveal which steps were repeated or unnecessary without
necessarily revealing which alternative reasoning steps would have
repaired the solution. Therefore, it is our hypothesis that in
thinking-enabled mathematical reasoning settings, OPSD manifests as a
token-level repetition-penalty mechanism rather than an
accuracy-improving correction mechanism.

To test this, we applied OPSD separately to correct and incorrect
rollout groups. If, in this setting, OPSD simply works as a
repetition-penalty mechanism, OPSD training on correct rollouts will
mainly compress already-correct reasoning traces, and OPSD training on
incorrect rollouts will bring down accuracy. If it works as a correction
mechanism, OPSD training on both groups of rollouts will show accuracy
improvements.

Our results show that in thinking-enabled mathematical reasoning, OPSD
behaves most reliably as a compression mechanism rather than a
correction mechanism. OPSD applied only to correct rollouts preserves
accuracy while substantially reducing response length. Training only on
incorrect rollouts damages accuracy, indicating that the
hindsight-guided self-teacher does not reliably supply the missing
reasoning steps needed to repair failed trajectories. Rather, its
confident, less-exploratory signal is most useful when applied to
already successful reasoning, where it removes redundancy and
exploratory detours while preserving the underlying solution structure.

We then test whether this compression-not-correction pattern can be
explained by narrower implementation factors. First, the self-teacher
may lack a sufficient informational advantage, making accuracy
improvement depend on richer privileged context. Second, the token-level
signal may be too front-loaded, weakening over long reasoning traces
because the privileged context appears only at the beginning. Third,
reasoning correction may require many optimization steps before local
token updates accumulate into global repair. Richer teacher contexts,
mid-trace feedback reinjection, and longer training do not qualitatively
change the outcome. These interventions move points along the
accuracy-length tradeoff, but do not turn OPSD into a reliable
failed-trajectory correction mechanism.

In light of these findings, we propose a revised post-training pipeline
for thinking-enabled mathematical reasoning: SFT $`\to`$ RLVR $`\to`$
OPSD. SFT establishes format and instruction-following, RLVR expands the
model’s reachable reasoning trajectories through outcome-based learning
\[[6](#bib.bib21), [11](#bib.bib24), [12](#bib.bib37)\], and OPSD
finally serves as a compaction stage that shortens reasoning traces the
model can already execute.
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models")
provides experimental evidence for this ordering. Therefore, in this
setting, OPSD is better understood not as a replacement for RLVR, but as
a post-RL mechanism for making already-learned reasoning cheaper to
execute. Because inference cost scales with the number of generated
tokens, this compaction directly reduces the serving cost of
thinking-enabled reasoning models without giving up the capabilities
RLVR delivered.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 On-Policy Self-Distillation

On-Policy Self-Distillation builds on a long line of work in which a
student is trained against soft targets from a teacher
\[[13](#bib.bib26), [14](#bib.bib28)\], with the special case where the
teacher shares the student’s parameters and is conditioned on extra
context not given to the student \[[15](#bib.bib27)\]. The student sees
only the question and produces a rollout; the teacher sees the question
plus a privileged context (a ground-truth answer, an expert
demonstration, or a written reflection) and scores the student rollout
token-by-token via reverse-KL.

For a prompt $`x`$, let $`y`$ denote the sampled response. OPSD draws
$`y\sim\pi_{S}(\cdot\mid x)`$, then compares the student at each prefix
$`y_{<t}`$ to a teacher that sees the same prefix plus privileged
context $`c`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}=\mathbb{E}_{x,\;y\sim\pi_{S}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{S}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\;\|\;\pi_{T}(\cdot\mid x,c,y_{<t})\right)\right].
``` |  | (1) |

The $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}`$ term in
Equation [1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 On-Policy Self-Distillation ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models")
is the explicit per-token objective: the teacher-student log-probability
gap induced by the privileged context. The student policy determines the
state distribution through $`y\sim\pi_{S}(\cdot\mid x)`$, while the
privileged teacher determines the target distribution through
$`\pi_{T}(\cdot\mid x,c,y_{<t})`$. This means that OPSD requires
privileged context for the teacher, but not rollout correctness labels.
With reverse-KL, the divergence is averaged over the student’s support,
making the update sensitive to tokens the student already assigns
probability mass to \[[4](#bib.bib7)\].

### 2.2 Related Work

#### OPSD as a post-training mechanism.

A growing body of work uses OPSD as a post-training mechanism, either as
the main training objective \[[1](#bib.bib1), [4](#bib.bib7),
[3](#bib.bib5), [2](#bib.bib3), [16](#bib.bib2)\] or as a supplementary
loss layered on top of another optimizer such as GRPO \[[17](#bib.bib4),
[18](#bib.bib6), [19](#bib.bib8), [20](#bib.bib9), [21](#bib.bib11),
[22](#bib.bib12), [23](#bib.bib13)\]. However, most reported gains are
measured with thinking turned off: prior work commonly uses instruct
models or runs reasoning models with thinking disabled, even when
evaluating on mathematical benchmarks \[[2](#bib.bib3), [16](#bib.bib2),
[17](#bib.bib4), [18](#bib.bib6), [19](#bib.bib8), [20](#bib.bib9),
[21](#bib.bib11), [22](#bib.bib12), [23](#bib.bib13)\]. This distinction
is consequential: in thinking-disabled regimes, teacher context can
often specify the desired behavior as a single-step output that
per-token KL can install directly as a pointwise mapping, whereas
thinking-enabled mathematical reasoning requires a long sequence of
intermediate steps, making it harder for a hindsight-conditioned
self-teacher to identify locally better token-level alternatives that
improve the final answer. Consistent with this distinction, OPSD-family
methods report large accuracy gains in thinking-disabled settings but
much smaller or mixed gains when evaluated with thinking enabled on
mathematical reasoning
(Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ OPSD as a post-training mechanism. ‣ 2.2 Related Work ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models")).
Our work therefore asks whether OPSD in this regime corrects failed
reasoning or instead compacts trajectories that are already viable.

[TABLE]

#### Reasoning trace compaction.

A parallel line of work treats long reasoning traces themselves as the
object to compress. One approach constructs shorter correct reasoning
traces, for example by rewriting, selecting, pruning, or self-sampling
compressed chains of thought, and then trains the model to emit those
shorter traces through supervised fine-tuning \[[24](#bib.bib16),
[25](#bib.bib19), [26](#bib.bib18)\]. Another approach modifies RL
training with length penalties so that correctness is rewarded jointly
with shorter reasoning \[[27](#bib.bib14), [28](#bib.bib15),
[29](#bib.bib17)\]. Our setting is different: we ask whether plain OPSD,
without an explicit length objective or outcome reward, corrects failed
reasoning or primarily compacts trajectories that are already viable.

## 3 OPSD Compresses Correct Rollouts Rather Than Repairing Failed Ones

### 3.1 Experimental Setup

Our experiments are designed to isolate where the OPSD signal is
applied, rather than changing the teacher, data, or evaluation protocol.
Throughout the main comparison, we keep the teacher context fixed and
vary only which rollouts contribute to training or which divergence is
used.

#### Teacher context.

Our default privileged context is *Reflection from GT*, a
student-generated reflection on the rollout produced after seeing the
ground-truth answer. To construct it, the student first generates a
rollout from the problem; then the same model receives the ground-truth
answer and reflects on its attempt. During OPSD, the self-teacher is
conditioned on the original problem and the generated reflection. Thus
the privileged information enters through a student-generated
reflection. We use *Reflection from GT* as the default context here
because the main experiment is about rollout scope; we later vary the
teacher context in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Richer Context, Signal Reinjection, and Prolonged Training ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models").

#### Models and data.

We evaluate OPSD on three 7–8B thinking-enabled reasoning models that
span the pre/post-RLVR axis. Qwen3-8B \[[30](#bib.bib23)\] and
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B \[[6](#bib.bib21)\] are two pre-RLVR base
models; AceReason-Nemotron-7B \[[11](#bib.bib24)\] is a public RLVR
checkpoint trained with GRPO \[[5](#bib.bib20)\] on top of
R1-Distill-7B. This makes R1-Distill-7B the pre-RLVR initialization of
AceReason-7B, giving a matched pre/post-RL pair on a single SFT base; we
report Qwen3-8B and AceReason-7B in the main text and R1-Distill-7B in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Matched DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B Pre-RL Reference ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models").
All OPSD runs use DAPO-Math-17K \[[7](#bib.bib22)\] as the on-policy
training prompt distribution, and the paper’s claims are scoped
throughout to thinking-enabled mathematical reasoning on these models
and this distribution.

#### Evaluation.

We evaluate on MATH500 \[[31](#bib.bib25)\], AIME24, and AIME25, which
together test mathematical reasoning across a moderate-to-hard
difficulty range. Each benchmark is scored using average@8 accuracy,
sampled at temperature 0.6, and mean response length under a
30,000-token generation cap. We report $`\Delta`$Accuracy in percentage
points and $`\Delta`$Length as a relative percentage change from each
model’s own pre-OPSD baseline. In tables, *Avg. $`\Delta`$ (pp)* always
appears under the Accuracy header and averages accuracy changes across
evaluation benchmarks, in percentage points, while *Avg. $`\Delta`$ (%)*
always appears under Length and averages relative length changes across
evaluation benchmarks, in percent.

#### Training protocol.

Unless otherwise noted, all runs use AdamW with batch size 32, maximum
training generation length 8,192, constant learning rate
$`1\times 10^{-6}`$, gradient clipping at 1.0, no warmup, and 100
optimization steps. The self-teacher is held frozen at the student’s
initial weights throughout training, following \[[1](#bib.bib1),
[32](#bib.bib10)\]. Student rollouts during training are sampled at
temperature 0.7. Each 100-step run uses 4$`\times`$ H100 GPUs for
approximately 6 hours. For each sampled student rollout, we extract the
final answer and verify it against the ground truth with a rule-based
verifier that applies symbolic and numeric canonicalization. The
resulting correctness label is used only for the outcome-filtered OPSD
training settings below; standard *All-rollout* OPSD does not require
it.

### 3.2 Outcome-Filtered OPSD Training Reveals Compaction Rather Than Repair

OPSD itself does not require rollout correctness labels. In the standard
OPSD *All-rollout* setting, the only requirement for the reverse-KL
distillation loss is privileged context for the self-teacher. We
introduce outcome-filtered OPSD training as a diagnostic intervention:
*Correct-only* and *Incorrect-only* use the same reverse-KL loss on
correct and incorrect rollouts, respectively. If OPSD repairs failed
trajectories, *Incorrect-only* should improve accuracy, since those are
precisely the cases where correction is needed. If OPSD mainly compacts
viable trajectories, *Correct-only* should be the safest compression
setting.

The branch ordering raises a natural question: if *Correct-only* OPSD
trains only on correct rollouts, is it just rejection-sampling
fine-tuning (RFT)?

### 3.3 Filtering Correct Rollouts Does Not Mean Reinforcing Correct Tokens

The outcome-filtered training result may make *Correct-only* OPSD look
like rejection-sampling fine-tuning (RFT) \[[33](#bib.bib34),
[34](#bib.bib35), [35](#bib.bib36)\], but the objectives differ in where
correctness enters the update. In OPSD, rollout correctness changes
which prefixes enter the KL loss, not which sampled token is reinforced
at any given state. Stated more sharply, *correctness gates states, not
token targets*.

Under RFT, the per-position objective is
$`\sum_{t}\log\pi_{S}(a_{t}^{\mathrm{correct}}\mid s_{t})`$: the sampled
correct token is the update target, and rejection sampling determines
which state-target pairs the loss sees. GRPO with positive advantages is
analogous: $`\nabla\log\pi_{S}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})`$ is scaled by
$`A(\tau)>0`$ for the entire rollout $`\tau`$, so the chosen token’s
log-probability is pushed up. For comparison, OPSD can be written in the
same local update form, but its per-token advantage comes from the
teacher-student log-probability gap rather than the rollout reward.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\;y\sim\pi_{S}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\widehat{A}_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{S}(y_{t}\mid s_{t})\right],
``` |  | (2) |

with the per-token advantage $`\widehat{A}_{t}`$ instantiated per
method,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}_{t}=A(\tau),\qquad\widehat{A}^{\mathrm{OPSD}}_{t}=\log\pi_{T}(y_{t}\mid s_{t},c)-\log\pi_{S}(y_{t}\mid s_{t}).`$ |  | (3) |

Equivalently, the OPSD term can be read as an implicit token reward: it
is positive when the privileged-context teacher assigns higher
probability to $`y_{t}`$ than the student does. This is a
teacher-preference reward, not an outcome reward. A token can therefore
be encouraged because it matches the teacher even if it leads to a
failed trajectory. OPSD is dense and on-policy, but its update direction
is biased toward the self-teacher, not anchored by the rollout reward.

Thus outcome-filtered training does not turn OPSD into a
correctness-reinforcement signal. Because rollout correctness flows into
state selection but not update direction, it provides no direct channel
for reinforcing the tokens that caused a correct answer. Its role is
different: it diagnoses where the teacher-following compression is safe.

### 3.4 Correctness Determines Whether Compression Is Safe

If correctness does not choose the token target, where does it matter?
It determines the substrate on which the same hindsight-guided and
confident self-teacher signal lands. On correct rollouts, it can remove
redundant checks, over-verification, and self-sabotaging detours while
preserving the solution structure. One plausible reading is the
compounding-error account \[[4](#bib.bib7)\]: when per-token reasoning
is approximately reliable, redundant tokens still carry small
accumulating risk, so removing them can improve reliability without
changing per-token competence.

The same hindsight-guided signal is less safe on incorrect rollouts.
Privileged context can concentrate the teacher-student gap at pivot
tokens, where knowing the answer makes one path look obvious even though
the unconditioned student may still need to explore
\[[22](#bib.bib12)\]. When the rollout is wrong, those pivots are often
precisely where exploration, checking, or recovery moves are needed.
Compressing them can therefore make the failed path more confident and
less exploratory, suppressing the reconsiderations that might have
helped the student escape \[[32](#bib.bib10)\].

This mechanism mirrors the protocol contrast in
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Related Work ‣ 2 Background and Related Work ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models"):
hindsight-guided supervision can help when privileged context largely
specifies what the model should produce, but does not reliably install a
new sequence of reasoning states that the student’s distribution does
not already support \[[22](#bib.bib12), [32](#bib.bib10)\].

we do not observe a robust correction channel, not here and not even
after varying KL direction, teacher contexts, signal reinjection, and
training durations in
Sections [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Divergence Choice Does Not Restore Failed-Trajectory Repair ‣ 3 OPSD Compresses Correct Rollouts Rather Than Repairing Failed Ones ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models")
and [4](#S4 "4 Richer Context, Signal Reinjection, and Prolonged Training ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models").

### 3.5 Divergence Choice Does Not Restore Failed-Trajectory Repair

A remaining possibility is that *Incorrect-only* fails because
reverse-KL is mode-seeking. Reverse-KL pressures the student to keep
probability mass only where the self-teacher agrees with the student’s
current distribution, which may not force a failed trace toward
teacher-supported alternatives \[[36](#bib.bib29), [37](#bib.bib32),
[20](#bib.bib9)\]. We therefore include two divergence ablations in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.2 Outcome-Filtered OPSD Training Reveals Compaction Rather Than Repair ‣ 3 OPSD Compresses Correct Rollouts Rather Than Repairing Failed Ones ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models"):
*All-rollout JSD*, which replaces reverse-KL with a symmetric divergence
on every rollout, and *Split-direction*, which keeps reverse-KL on
correct rollouts but applies forward-KL to incorrect rollouts
\[[21](#bib.bib11)\].

These variants change the accuracy-length tradeoff, but do not turn OPSD
into a robust repair mechanism for failed trajectories. *All-rollout
JSD* improves the AceReason average relative to *All-rollout* reverse-KL
but still behaves primarily as a compressor, while *Split-direction*
partially improves the Qwen3-8B tradeoff but does not transfer cleanly
across benchmarks or models. Thus divergence choice is a useful ablation
of the failed-rollout branch, but the main limitation is not reverse-KL
alone: the self-teacher still supplies a hindsight-conditioned signal on
the student’s own trajectory rather than a new sequence of reasoning
states.

## 4 Richer Context, Signal Reinjection, and Prolonged Training

The outcome-filtered training results leave three narrower explanations
to rule out. First, OPSD may fail to correct because the self-teacher
lacks sufficient privileged information. Second, the teacher signal may
be too front-loaded, weakening before it reaches the mid-rollout
reasoning steps where correction is needed. Third, correction may
require many optimization steps before local token updates compound into
sequence-level repair. We test all three and find that they move
variants along the same accuracy-length tradeoff rather than turning
OPSD into a reliable correction mechanism.

### 4.1 More Teacher Information Does Not Restore Correction

One alternative explanation is that the *Reflection from GT* teacher
context is the binding constraint, either through the information it
carries or through how it is structured. If so, varying either axis
should yield a separate accuracy regime distinct from the
accuracy-length tradeoff observed in the main comparison.

hindsight-biased and less exploratory \[[32](#bib.bib10)\]; distilled
onto the student, this often strengthens compression rather than repair.
Additional teacher information therefore moves variants along the same
accuracy-length tradeoff, instead of supplying the missing correction
signal.

### 4.2 Signal Reinjection Cannot Repair Incorrect Rollouts

A second alternative explanation concerns where the teacher signal
lands. Because the privileged context appears only in the teacher’s
prompt prefix, the per-token reverse-KL signal may be strongest early
and attenuate as the teacher’s next-token distribution becomes dominated
by the student’s more recent tokens. If so, OPSD may fail to correct
because the teacher signal does not reach the mid-rollout reasoning
steps where wrong trajectories need to change.

The intervention reinjects the privileged context into the teacher’s
input every 2,048 tokens within the student’s reasoning trace, framed as
a natural in-thinking recall (“Actually, I recall: {privileged context}.
Continuing from this.”), while leaving the student’s input unchanged. We
evaluate it using *Reflection from GT* as the context to reinject.

### 4.3 Longer OPSD Training Preserves Compression Only

A third alternative explanation is that correction requires accumulation
across many optimization steps before local token updates compound into
sequence-level repair. OPSD updates the student one token at a time
toward a context-conditioned teacher, and several hundred steps of this
could in principle build up sequence-level repair that 100 steps cannot
reach. We therefore extend the strongest 100-step accuracy variant,
*Split-direction*, to 500 steps.

Figure 3: Extended OPSD training trajectory on Qwen3-8B, evaluated up to
500 steps. Length compression appears within the first 25 steps and
persists, while accuracy oscillates below baseline.

Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.3 Longer OPSD Training Preserves Compression Only ‣ 4 Richer Context, Signal Reinjection, and Prolonged Training ‣ OPSD Compresses What RLVR Teaches:A Post-RL Compaction Stage for Reasoning Models")
shows that extra OPSD budget preserves the length effect but does not
accumulate a durable accuracy gain. Length deltas drop to roughly
$`-25\%`$ to $`-30\%`$ within the first 25 steps and stay in that band
through step 500, while accuracy oscillates within a few points below
baseline without a durable upward trend. This early saturation matches
prior evidence that the useful effect of OPSD is concentrated early in
training \[[22](#bib.bib12)\]. Additional training therefore extends the
same compaction regime rather than converting OPSD into a delayed
correction mechanism.

The early saturation is the practical appeal of OPSD as a post-RL
compaction stage. Our 25-step run uses 32 rollouts per batch at an
8,192-token budget, reaching the $`-25\%`$ to $`-30\%`$ compression
regime at near-baseline accuracy. Length-penalized RL recipes generate
roughly $`80\times`$ to $`2{,}000\times`$ more rollouts than this budget
\[[29](#bib.bib17), [27](#bib.bib14), [28](#bib.bib15)\]. Short-trace
RFT concentrates its compute in corpus construction, generating
$`{\sim}20\times`$ to $`{\sim}200\times`$ more rollouts before any
fine-tuning begins \[[25](#bib.bib19), [24](#bib.bib16),
[26](#bib.bib18)\]. The only directly comparable thinking-enabled work
uses roughly $`40\times`$ more rollouts for $`{-}17\%`$ length
reduction, less compression than our $`{-}29\%`$ at step 25
\[[26](#bib.bib18)\]. OPSD reaches its compression at a small fraction
of either alternative’s rollout cost.

## 5 Where OPSD Belongs in the Training Pipeline

Our results motivate SFT $`\to`$ RLVR $`\to`$ OPSD as the natural
ordering in the setting we study. SFT establishes format and
instruction-following from a fixed data or teacher distribution. RLVR is
sparse, but because rollouts come from the current policy, successful
updates feed back into the next sampling distribution and can expand the
model’s reachable reasoning trajectories. OPSD then applies dense
token-level pressure from a privileged self-teacher; in our experiments,
that pressure is most useful for shortening and de-noising trajectories
for problems the model can already solve, not for creating new ones.
This suggests delaying OPSD until correct trajectories are sampled
frequently enough that the self-teacher has enough viable trajectories
to safely compress; before that, rollout compute is better spent on
RLVR. OPSD is therefore best characterized here as a post-RL compaction
stage: a low-budget update that removes redundant reasoning from
already-supported solutions, but not a reliable substitute for RLVR when
the missing behavior is a new reasoning state.

## 6 Conclusion

We investigated whether OPSD in thinking-enabled mathematical reasoning
operates as a correction mechanism on failed trajectories or as a
compaction mechanism on already-viable ones. Across Qwen3-8B and
AceReason-Nemotron-7B, applying OPSD training to correct rollouts gave
the safest accuracy-preserving compression, while applying it to
incorrect rollouts damaged accuracy; richer teacher context, mid-trace
reinjection, divergence changes, and longer training preserved the same
compression-not-correction pattern. Our diagnosis is that a
hindsight-guided self-teacher can help when privileged context largely
specifies what the model should produce, but in our experiments is a
weak correction source for long mathematical reasoning traces because it
does not reliably provide the missing intermediate states needed for new
solutions. Thus on these tasks OPSD remains useful in a different
pipeline slot: RLVR establishes reachable reasoning capability, then a
short post-RL OPSD stage makes that already-learned reasoning cheaper to
execute, suggesting a revised pipeline.

\### Memo: Key Insights and Efficient Approach

---

\### \*\*Key Insight\*\*

The problem involves \*\*distributing distinct items into identical
bags\*\* with \*\*allowed empty bags\*\*, which is a classic case of
\*\*set partitioning\*\*. The critical realization is that \*\*bags are
not distinct\*\*, so the focus is on grouping items rather than
assigning them to labeled containers. Therefore, \*\*Stirling numbers of
the second kind\*\* \$ S(n, k) \$, which count the number of ways to
partition a set of \$ n \$ elements into exactly \$ k \$
\*\*non-empty\*\* subsets, are the correct mathematical tool.

---

\### \*\*Most Efficient Path\*\*

1. \*\*Challenge\*\*: Identical bags (no ordering) and the possibility
of empty bags complicate the usual distribution methods (e.g.,
permutations or combinations with labeled containers).

2. \*\*Solution Strategy\*\*:

 - Recognize that even with empty bags allowed, the task reduces to
counting all \*\*set partitions\*\* of the 4 items into 1, 2, or 3
subsets (since there are 3 bags).

 - Use \*\*Stirling numbers of the second kind\*\*, summing \$ S(4, 1) +
S(4, 2) + S(4, 3) \$ to account for all groupings.

---

\### \*\*Structural Features to Recognize\*\*

- \*\*Identical containers\*\*: This is a strong indicator that \*\*bag
identity does not matter\*\*, and the focus should be on
\*\*grouping\*\* (not labeling).

- \*\*Distinct items\*\*: The number of ways depends on how the items
are \*\*divided into subsets\*\*.

- \*\*Empty bags allowed\*\*: This implies that the sum of the number of
non-empty subsets must be \<= the number of bags.

---

\### \*\*Why This Works\*\*

The strategy of summing Stirling numbers directly addresses the core of
the problem: \*\*how many distinct ways to partition a set of size \$ n
\$\*\* into up to \$ k \$ subsets when the subsets are
\*\*indistinguishable\*\*. This avoids overcounting due to repeated
groupings (e.g., swapping bags doesn't yield a new arrangement), which
is a common pitfall with labeled bags.

---

\### Final Summary

By recognizing that the problem is a \*\*set partitioning\*\* task and
applying the \*\*Stirling numbers of the second kind\*\*, the solution
becomes straightforward and efficient. This insight avoids unnecessary
overcomplication and directly leads to the correct total of \*\*14
distinct ways\*\* to distribute 4 items into 3 identical bags with empty
bags allowed.
````

# The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution

Shuo Yan Affiliation: Macquarie University    Ningning Hou
Affiliation: Macquarie University    Haoyu He Affiliation: Monash
University    Haochen Pei Affiliation: Macquarie University    Shang Gao
Affiliation: Macquarie University

###### Abstract

Self-improving AI systems can revise the designs that govern their own
behavior. Yet existing approaches center improvement on selected parent
agents and their evolutionary experience, leaving the broader population
largely underused. We identify a substantial gap between population
coverage (i.e., the tasks solved by at least one evolved variant) and
the performance of the best individual agent. This gap shows that useful
capabilities and diagnostic evidence emerge across the population
without being consolidated into its leading agents. These observations
motivate the view from nowhere, a principle for agent evolution: an
agent should be improved by interpreting its behavior through
population-wide patterns of success, failure, and prior
self-modification. We instantiate this principle as Population-Centered
Evolution (PCE). PCE distils task records and modification histories
from the population into a population block, which captures recurring
failures, complementary successes, and the effects of previously
attempted modifications to guide improvements to a selected parent’s
harness. Under matched models, initial harnesses, and evolution
schedules, PCE achieves 72.1% success on SWE-bench Verified and 96.7% on
Polyglot, improving over a strong group-evolution baseline by 3.2 and
5.9 percentage points. In our evaluation, PCE reduces the gap between
population coverage and best-agent performance by one-third on SWE-bench
and closes it on Polyglot. These results suggest that treating the
population as evidence—not merely as candidates for selection—provides a
complementary principle for agent evolution.

## 1 Introduction

Self-improving AI aims to develop systems that can revise the designs
governing their own behavior ([Good, 1966](#bib.bib8); [Schmidhuber,
2007](#bib.bib24)). Evolutionary coding agents pursue this goal by
modifying their software harnesses while keeping the parameters of their
foundation models fixed ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib39); [Weng
et al., 2026](#bib.bib32); [Robeyns et al., 2025](#bib.bib23); [Wang
et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)). These harnesses determine how an agent uses
the model, tools, feedback, and its environment to solve a task. Through
repeated cycles of self-modification and evaluation, these systems
generate a growing population of agent variants. The goal is not merely
to produce diverse variants, but to turn useful discoveries made during
evolution into progressively stronger agents. How to consolidate these
discoveries into improvements that persist in leading agents is a
central challenge for agent evolution.

Existing approaches address this challenge through parent selection and
experience reuse. The Darwin Gödel Machine (DGM) preserves an archive of
agent variants from which future evolution can branch ([Zhang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib39)). The Huxley-Gödel Machine (HGM) uses the performance
of an agent and its descendants to estimate the potential of continuing
that lineage ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)). Group-Evolving Agents
(GEA) selects a small group of parents and allows them to share task
records and past self-modifications when proposing improvements ([Weng
et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)). Despite these differences, the evidence used
to propose a modification remains largely _parent-local_: it comes from
the selected parent or parent group rather than from a systematic
comparison across the full population. Other variants may remain
available for future selection, but their task-level successes,
failures, and modification histories do not directly inform the current
diagnosis.

Yet prior work has not systematically measured the gap between what an
evolutionary population collectively solves and what its best individual
agent achieves, or examined how this gap can guide improvement. We
define _population coverage_ as the proportion of tasks solved by at
least one evolved variant. As shown in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")a,
in one of our GEA runs the evolved population covers 9 more of the 60
held-in tasks than its best individual agent, corresponding to a 15.0
percentage-point gap. This gap shows that successful outcomes remain
distributed across the population rather than being fully consolidated
into its leading agent. The associated task records allow us to compare
variants that succeed and fail on the same task. The population should
therefore be treated not only as a pool for parent selection, but also
as comparative evidence for deciding how a selected parent should
improve.

These observations motivate the _view from nowhere_ ([Nagel & Nagell,
1986](#bib.bib18)) as a principle for agent evolution. It holds that
decisions about how to improve an agent should be grounded in patterns
of success, failure, and prior self-modification across the population.
For a given task, population outcomes show whether a failure is isolated
or recurring, execution records reveal how successful and unsuccessful
variants differ, and modification histories indicate which remedies have
already been attempted. Together, these comparisons provide a broader
basis for diagnosing a selected parent and guiding its improvement.

We instantiate this principle through Population-Centered Evolution
(PCE). For a selected task, a distillation agent compares task records
and modification histories across all available variants and organizes
its findings into a population block. The block contains three types of
information. Outcome evidence measures how frequently different results
occur across the population. An observation identifies a cross-agent
pattern that may explain why variants behave differently. Candidate
files indicate which parts of the harness may be related to that
pattern. Together, these fields establish what recurs across the
population, suggest a diagnostic hypothesis, and indicate where to
investigate it. Diagnosis then checks this hypothesis against the
selected parent’s execution records and source code before proposing a
harness modification, as shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Population-Centered Evolution ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")b.

Across SWE-bench Verified and Polyglot, PCE improves agent performance
under matched models, initial harnesses, and evolution schedules. Agents
evolved by PCE achieve 72.1% success on 200 SWE-bench tasks and 96.7% on
225 Polyglot tasks, exceeding GEA by 3.2 and 5.9 percentage points,
respectively. PCE reduces the gap between population coverage and
best-agent performance by one-third on SWE-bench and closes it on
Polyglot. Its advantage also persists on held-out tasks. Across the full
evolutionary process, PCE uses 36% fewer tokens on SWE-bench and 59%
fewer tokens on Polyglot than GEA. PCE therefore achieves stronger
agents with lower inference expenditure, showing that population-wide
evidence enables more effective use of the experience accumulated during
evolution.

In summary, we make three contributions:

1.  1.  We identify and quantify a previously overlooked gap between
        population coverage and best-agent performance, and we formulate the
        _view from nowhere_ as a principle for capturing useful information
        across the population to guide agent evolution.
2.  2.  We introduce PCE, which distils population-wide experience into a
        population block that guides improvements to a selected parent’s
        harness.
3.  3.  PCE improves performance and generalization, narrows the
        population-coverage gap, and achieves these gains with lower total
        token consumption than baselines.

## 2 Related Work

#### Learning from execution experience.

Agents use execution experience at several levels. Reflection revises
outputs or memories using execution or self-generated feedback ([Shinn
et al., 2023](#bib.bib25); [Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib16)), while
memory systems, skill libraries, and workflow memories retain useful
experience for future tasks ([Park et al., 2023](#bib.bib21); [Packer
et al., 2023](#bib.bib20); [Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib33); [Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib27); [Wang et al., 2024](#bib.bib31)). ExpeL goes beyond
storing individual records by extracting natural-language insights from
accumulated task experience ([Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib41)).
Experience can also support parameter updates through self-training on
generated responses and filtered trajectories ([Zelikman et al.,
2022](#bib.bib36); [Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib3); [Gulcehre et al.,
2023](#bib.bib9); [Yuan et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)). Our setting concerns
design-level improvement while keeping the foundation model fixed.
Self-Harness identifies model-specific failure patterns and validates
harness edits with regression tests ([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib38)),
while HarnessEvolve aligns failed executions with reference trajectories
and clusters error signals to identify systematic failures ([Jiang
et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)). PCE differs in its unit of analysis. It
compares how a population of evolving harness variants handles the same
task and relates their outcomes to previous self-modifications. This
population-wide comparison provides evidence for diagnosing and
modifying the current parent.

#### Population-based agent design and evolution.

Automated agent design methods search over agentic modules, workflows,
prompts, and code ([Hu et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib40); [Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib30); [Fernando et al.,
2023](#bib.bib7); [Zelikman et al., 2023](#bib.bib37); [Yin et al.,
2025](#bib.bib34); [Robeyns et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)). As this search
produces more variants, a central problem is how to preserve and reuse
discoveries made during evolution. Quality-diversity and open-endedness
research retains diverse solutions as stepping stones for continued
exploration ([Lehman & Stanley, 2011](#bib.bib14); [Mouret & Clune,
2015](#bib.bib17); [Pugh et al., 2016](#bib.bib22); [Chatzilygeroudis
et al., 2021](#bib.bib2); [de Vladar, 2016](#bib.bib5); [Stanley et al.,
2017](#bib.bib26); [Clune, 2019](#bib.bib4); [Hughes et al.,
2024](#bib.bib11); [Faldor et al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Wang et al.,
2019](#bib.bib28)). In coding-agent evolution, DGM maintains an archive
from which future evolution can branch ([Zhang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib39)), while HGM estimates the potential of continuing a
lineage from the performance of an agent and its descendants ([Wang
et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)). GEA shares task records and
self-modification patches among selected parents ([Weng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib32)), and HarnessBank recombines harnesses drawn from a
pool ([Luo et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). These approaches use the
population to preserve diversity, select parents, share parent
experience, or recombine designs. PCE gives the population a different
role. It treats all available variants as comparative evidence for
understanding recurring failures, contrasting outcomes, and guiding
improvement of a selected parent.

## 3 Population-Centered Evolution

PCE operates population-centered evolution through two stages, as shown
in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Population-Centered Evolution ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution").
Population distillation compares task records and modification histories
across evaluated agent variants and summarizes the findings in a
population block. Diagnosis then checks this population-level account
against a selected parent’s records and source code before proposing a
harness modification.

We note that PCE distinguishes the variants used as evidence from those
eligible for parent selection. It maintains a population $`\mathcal{V}`$
containing all evaluated agent variants whose task records are
available, and an archive $`\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{V}`$ containing
the variants eligible to become parents. Each variant is associated with
its harness, task records, and history of self-modification. A variant
can therefore inform future improvements even when it is not selected as
a parent.

At each evolution step, PCE selects parent agents from $`\mathcal{A}`$
and chooses a task for diagnosing each parent. Population distillation
examines the records available in $`\mathcal{V}`$ for that task, after
which diagnosis proposes and implements a modification to the selected
parent’s harness. The resulting child is evaluated and added to
$`\mathcal{V}`$. Children satisfying the archive criteria also enter
$`\mathcal{A}`$ for future parent selection.

### 3.1 Population Distillation

For the task selected to diagnose a parent, population distillation
considers all available task records in $`\mathcal{V}`$, including the
initial agent’s record when available. Each record contains an execution
log, a submitted task patch, and an evaluation outcome. The
corresponding harness can be reconstructed from the initial source and
the variant’s chain of self-modifications. These materials allow
distillation to compare not only which variants succeed or fail, but
also how their execution and harness designs differ.

Population distillation combines programmatic measurement with
agent-based analysis. A program first counts the outcomes across all
available records. The distillation agent then inspects relevant
execution logs, task patches, harness source, and earlier
self-modifications. It compares successful and unsuccessful variants,
identifies recurring outcomes, and examines whether previous harness
changes resolved or preserved the observed behavior. Since the full
records can be large, the agent chooses which records and source files
to inspect within its analysis budget.

The resulting population block contains three fields. Outcome evidence
reports the program-computed distribution of task outcomes across the
population. A cross-agent observation summarizes a pattern found by
comparing variants and provides a hypothesis about why their outcomes
differ. Candidate files identify parts of the harness that may implement
the relevant behavior and should be examined during diagnosis. This
field is left empty when distillation cannot identify a plausible
location. Separating measured outcomes from their interpretation
preserves a factual population summary while giving diagnosis a focused
starting point without prescribing the modification.

### 3.2 Diagnosis and Harness Modification

The population block provides a diagnostic hypothesis rather than
prescribing a modification. Diagnosis combines this block with the
selected parent’s source code and task records to determine whether the
population-level pattern applies to the current harness. It examines the
candidate files when provided, but may inspect other parts of the
harness as needed. A mechanism associated with successful variants may
be absent from the parent, while an earlier modification may already be
present but remain insufficient. Diagnosis uses the parent’s execution
behavior and implementation to distinguish between these possibilities.

Based on this assessment, diagnosis proposes one harness modification
and describes how it should be implemented. The self-modification step
applies the proposal to a copy of the parent’s harness, producing a
child agent. The modification may change the agent’s prompts, tools, or
execution workflow, while the foundation model and inference limits
remain fixed. The child is then passed to the evaluation stage of the
evolution loop.

The SWE-bench task django-12209 in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")c
illustrates the full process. Across 17 task records, the outcome
evidence reported 6 solved patches, 10 empty patches, and 1 test-only
patch. Population distillation found that truncation occurred under
different outcomes and that increasing the retry budget had not
consistently prevented empty patches. The observation and candidate
files directed diagnosis toward the parent’s episode-completion logic.
Inspection then showed that the episode could finish without checking
whether a patch had been produced. The child harness then added this
check and performed bounded repairs when the patch remained empty. This
example shows how population-wide patterns are converted into a
hypothesis, validated against the selected parent, and implemented as a
concrete harness change.

### 3.3 Evolution Loop

Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.3 Evolution Loop ‣ 3 Population-Centered Evolution ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")
specifies the complete PCE evolution loop. The first generation produces
two children from the initial agent through separate single-parent
diagnoses. Later generations select two parents in out setting from
$`\mathcal{A}`$ and sample one eligible diagnostic task for each. For a
selected task, population distillation runs only when at least three
records are available in $`\mathcal{V}`$. Otherwise, or when no usable
observation is produced, diagnosis falls back to the parent-local
procedure.

Children that produce a self-modification patch are evaluated first on
an initial subset and then on the remaining evolution tasks if they meet
the evaluation threshold. Every evaluated child joins $`\mathcal{V}`$,
while children satisfying the retention criteria also join
$`\mathcal{A}`$.

## 4 Experiments Setup

### 4.1 Benchmarks and Data Splits

We evaluate PCE on SWE-bench Verified and Polyglot ([Jimenez et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13); [OpenAI, 2024](#bib.bib19); [Aider,
2024](#bib.bib1)). SWE-bench Verified evaluates repository-level issue
resolution, while Polyglot contains 225 coding tasks across six
programming languages. Following DGM, we use 200 SWE-bench Verified
tasks and all 225 Polyglot tasks ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib39)).
For each benchmark, 60 tasks are held in for evolution. The remaining
140 SWE-bench tasks and 165 Polyglot tasks are held out for final
evaluation, forming Overall 200 and Overall 225. We also evaluate on an
additional set of 60 SWE-bench tasks spanning 12 repositories.

### 4.2 Baselines and Settings

We compare PCE with GEA, the closest group-evolution baseline ([Weng
et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)). To isolate the effect of population-centered
evolution, both methods use the same foundation models, initial harness,
parent-selection procedure, diagnosis prompt, modifiable files, and
evaluation protocol. Each method runs for ten generations and produces
two children per generation. DeepSeek Flash performs task execution and
self-modification. DeepSeek Pro performs diagnosis for both methods and
population distillation for PCE. We also evaluate the shared initial
agent as a reference.

### 4.3 Evaluation Protocol

During evolution, each child is first evaluated on a screening set of 10
tasks. Children meeting the advancement threshold are then evaluated on
the remaining 50 held-in tasks. Performance across all 60 held-in tasks
defines the search score, and the highest-scoring evolved agent is
selected for final evaluation.

After evolution, we evaluate the selected agent without further
self-modification. For each method, the selected agent and the initial
agent are independently evaluated 5 times on the 60 held-in tasks. We
report the mean success rate and sample standard deviation. The
additional SWE-bench set of 60 tasks is also evaluated 5 times.
SWE-bench Held-out 140 and Polyglot Held-out 165 are each evaluated
once. Overall scores are task-count-weighted averages of the mean
held-in score and the corresponding held-out score.

and [C](#A3 "Appendix C Additional Results ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")
provide the task splits, model versions, advancement thresholds, and the
results of all runs.

## 5 Results and Analysis

### 5.1 Agent Performance and Generalization

RQ1: Does PCE evolve stronger agents that generalize beyond the search
evaluation?

PCE outperforms GEA on both benchmarks under matched experimental
settings.
Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.2 Population-Wide Improvement and Consolidation ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")
reports the performance of the best agents evolved within 10
generations. Both GEA and PCE improve substantially over the shared
initial agent. PCE reaches an Overall success rate of 72.1% on SWE-bench
and 96.7% on Polyglot, exceeding GEA by 3.2 and 5.9 percentage points,
respectively. The Polyglot result is particularly notable because PCE
continues to improve performance even when GEA already solves more than
90% of the tasks.

The gains are not limited to a single search or evaluation. Across two
independent SWE-bench runs for each method, both PCE runs achieve higher
best search scores than either GEA run, as reported in
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Effect on diagnostic proposals. ‣ 5.3 Value of Population-Wide Evidence ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution").
When the selected agents are independently evaluated 5 times on Held-in
60, PCE retains a mean advantage of 4.0 percentage points on both
benchmarks
(Figure [6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 5.2 Population-Wide Improvement and Consolidation ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")c).
PCE also outperforms GEA on all three held-out task sets
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Additional Results ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution"),
Table [7](#A1.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Published comparison settings. ‣ Appendix A Experimental Setup ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")).
Since held-out tasks constitute the majority of Overall 200 and Overall
225, these results show that the gains extend beyond the tasks used
during evolution.

For context,
Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.2 Population-Wide Improvement and Consolidation ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")
also reports published results for DGM, GEA, OpenHands, and Aider under
their original models and protocols. These values are reference points
rather than controlled comparisons. Our main conclusion is based on the
matched PCE and GEA runs.

### 5.2 Population-Wide Improvement and Consolidation

RQ2: Does PCE consolidate population-wide successes into stronger
individual agents?

PCE improves not only the best agent, but also the broader set of
leading variants.
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Effect on diagnostic proposals. ‣ 5.3 Value of Population-Wide Evidence ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")
reports the highest search score and the minimum score among the top
three and top five evolved variants. PCE improves all three measures on
both benchmarks. On SWE-bench, even the fifth-ranked PCE variant
performs comparably to GEA’s best variant. On Polyglot, the fifth-ranked
PCE variant outperforms GEA’s best. The gains therefore extend beyond a
single exceptional agent.

PCE also brings its leading agents closer to what the population
collectively solves. Population coverage measures the fraction of tasks
solved by at least one evaluated variant, while the coverage gap is the
difference between this union and the best-agent score. On SWE-bench,
PCE and GEA achieve similar population coverage, but PCE reduces the
mean gap from 14.2 to 10.0 percentage points. On Polyglot, both
populations cover all 60 held-in tasks. GEA retains a 10.0-point gap,
whereas PCE closes the gap with a single agent that solves the full task
set.

The reduction does not result from PCE exploring a substantially larger
set of solvable tasks. Instead, with similar population coverage, PCE
produces leading variants that capture more of the successful outcomes
distributed across the population. This result directly supports the
central motivation for using population-wide evidence during agent
evolution.

### 5.3 Value of Population-Wide Evidence

RQ3: Does population-wide evidence provide gains beyond additional
parent-only analysis?

We conduct an ablation on Polyglot that compares GEA, parent-only
distillation, and PCE under the same evolution and evaluation settings.
Parent-only distillation uses the same distillation model, tools, step
budget, and output format as PCE, but restricts its inputs to the
selected parents’ task records, modification histories, and source code.
This control separates the value of population-wide evidence from the
effect of adding another analysis step.

Population-wide evidence improves performance beyond parent-only
distillation. As shown in
Table [3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Effect on diagnostic proposals. ‣ 5.3 Value of Population-Wide Evidence ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution"),
parent-only distillation achieves a best search score of 91.7%, close to
GEA’s 90.0%. Both methods obtain the same mean success rate of 92.0%
across 5 evaluations of their selected agents. PCE instead reaches a
perfect search score and retains a mean success rate of 96.0%, 4
percentage points above both alternatives.

The repeated evaluations further show that PCE solves more tasks
consistently. All three methods solve nearly the full task set at least
once, as reflected by their similar Union scores. PCE nevertheless
achieves the highest Intersection, solving 88.3% of the tasks in every
evaluation, compared with 85.0% for GEA and 78.3% for parent-only
distillation. Within this Polyglot ablation, the results show that the
gains come from broadening the evidence beyond the selected parents,
rather than from additional analysis alone.

#### Effect on diagnostic proposals.

We also conduct a fixed-context probe to examine how the supplied
evidence changes diagnosis. Across 3 SWE-bench contexts, we hold the
focal parent, source code, patch history, and task record fixed, vary
the auxiliary evidence, and generate five proposals per condition.
Adding a population block changes the main target of diagnosis in two of
three contexts. On Sphinx-7748 task, proposals addressing truncation
recovery increase from two of five to all five. On Sphinx-9229 task, the
population block produces two proposals for a post-episode patch check,
while GEA and the raw-trajectory controls produce only instruction-level
changes. No comparable shift appears on Sphinx-8638. These results
suggest that a population block can redirect diagnosis from local
instruction refinement toward changes in the agent’s execution logic.
They also indicate that distilling cross-agent patterns can provide
guidance that is not reproduced by simply supplying additional raw
trajectories. Since the proposals are not implemented or evaluated, this
analysis characterizes how population evidence affects diagnostic
behavior rather than its downstream performance.

### 5.4 Performance Gains and Token Cost

RQ4: Does PCE achieve better performance at the cost of higher token
consumption?

No. PCE achieves higher performance while using substantially fewer
tokens.
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.4 Performance Gains and Token Cost ‣ 5 Results and Analysis ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")
shows that PCE reduces total token consumption by 36% on SWE-bench and
59% on Polyglot relative to GEA. These totals cover the complete
evolution run, including task execution, diagnosis, self-modification,
and population distillation. PCE also makes fewer model requests on both
benchmarks. Its performance gains therefore do not come from a larger
inference budget.

The reduction is not a result of evaluating fewer task episodes. PCE
evaluates more episodes than GEA on both benchmarks, but produces
substantially shorter trajectories for both solved and unsolved
attempts. Extremely long trajectories also become less frequent. On
SWE-bench, the number of trajectories exceeding 500,000 characters falls
from 158 to 45. PCE therefore performs more evaluations while consuming
fewer tokens overall.

These results suggest that population-wide evidence makes the
evolutionary process more directed. Population distillation identifies
recurring outcomes and informs harness changes that can affect later
task attempts.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ The View from Nowhere: Population-Centered Agent Evolution")c
provides one example, where a recurring empty-patch pattern leads to an
explicit patch check and bounded repair procedure. This case does not by
itself explain the aggregate savings, but it illustrates how
population-guided modifications can prevent inefficient behavior from
recurring. PCE therefore improves both performance and token efficiency,
rather than exchanging a larger inference budget for stronger agents.

## 6 Conclusion

Agent evolution can produce useful experience that no selected parent
captures alone. The gap between population coverage and best-agent
performance makes this overlooked potential visible. We formulate the
_view from nowhere_ as a principle for using the population not only to
select parents, but also to understand how an individual agent should
improve. PCE realizes this principle by distilling outcomes and
modification histories across evolved variants into evidence for
parent-specific diagnosis. Across SWE-bench Verified and Polyglot, PCE
improves performance, retains its gains on held-out tasks, raises the
quality of several leading variants, and brings the best agent closer to
what the population collectively solves. The Polyglot ablation further
shows that these gains do not arise from additional parent-only
analysis. Notably, PCE also moves beyond the usual performance-cost
tradeoff. It produces stronger agents while using fewer total tokens,
rather than purchasing improvement with a larger inference budget. The
broader lesson is that an evolutionary population can serve as a source
of knowledge, not merely a pool of candidates. We envision that this
perspective motivates further study of how population evidence can be
attributed, compressed, and transferred as evolutionary histories grow
and self-improving systems move beyond coding agents.

#### Task sets.

Both benchmarks use DGM’s 60-task subsets as Held-in 60 ([Zhang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib39)). Each subset is partitioned into ten gate tasks and
fifty further tasks, and a child passes the gate with at least one
solved gate task on SWE-bench and at least four on Polyglot. The
SWE-bench Held-in 60 comprises 35 Django and 25 Sphinx tasks. SWE-bench
Held-out 140 comprises DGM’s additional 118 Django and 22 Astropy tasks,
and the separate SWE-bench Held-out 60 spans 12 repositories. Polyglot
Held-out 165 is the remainder of the 225 tasks. The best agent of each
run is selected by its Held-in 60 search score and evaluated without
further self-modification. It is evaluated five times on Held-in 60 and
on SWE-bench Held-out 60, and once on Held-out 140 and Held-out 165.
Overall weights the mean held-in success rate and the single held-out
success rate by their task counts.

#### Additional SWE-bench Held-out 60.

We construct this set by first validating SWE-bench Verified instances
in our evaluation environment. We sample 60 tasks using fixed seed,
requiring representation from every repository in the candidate pool and
matching the held-in difficulty distribution as closely as possible. The
resulting set spans 12 repositories. We evaluate the initial agent and
the best agents evolved by GEA and PCE five times on this set.

#### Evolution loop.

Each run spans ten generations with two children per generation.
Generation 0 uses single-parent diagnosis from the initial harness, with
a separately sampled task record for each child. Later generations
select two parents ($`k=2`$) using GEA’s performance-novelty rule and
uniformly sample one eligible task per parent. Each diagnosis receives
both records, with the inheriting parent’s record first. PCE
additionally supplies a population block for that parent’s task when
available. Modifications are restricted to coding*agent.py (episode
logic), harness_def.py (instructions and truncation handling), tools/,
and utils/, excluding evaluation and orchestration helpers. The model,
output cap, and tool loop remain fixed. Children producing harness
patches are evaluated on ten gate tasks and, if they pass, the remaining
fifty. Archive admission requires passing the gate, producing at least
one non-empty task patch, and restricting harness changes to the
permitted paths. The archive is never pruned. All evaluated children
enter $`\mathcal{V}*{t+1}`$, regardless of archive admission, making
their records available for subsequent distillation.

#### Population distillation.

The distillation agent runs with a budget of 250 steps and returns an
observation of at most 60 words and zero to three candidate files.
Program-computed outcome counts are rendered with the observation.
Distillation is skipped when fewer than three records exist on the task,
so the first opportunity is generation 1, and the diagnosis proceeds
without a block when no usable observation is returned.

# Not Every Rollout Needs Distillation: Correctness-Filtered On-Policy Self-Distillation

Harrison (Zihao) Zhao Affiliation: Meta    Lei Huang Affiliation: Meta
   Fei Liu Affiliation: Meta    Wen Shi Affiliation: Meta    Zijian Jin
Affiliation: Meta

###### Abstract

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) supervises student-generated
trajectories with a reference-conditioned version of the same base
model. We ask whether every trajectory supplies distinct supervision
that warrants its distillation cost. Gradient measurements on Qwen3-1.7B
at initialization reveal that correct and incorrect responses have
similar individual gradient magnitudes, but incorrect responses have
approximately three times the coherent gradient share. Their estimated
population-mean directions also largely overlap. A signal–noise model
fitted at a small group size tracks alignment at larger group sizes
without refitting. Motivated by these measurements, we introduce
correctness-filtered OPSD (C-OPSD), which generates one response per
problem and distills only a subset of incorrect responses. Removing
unselected rows before both distillation forwards avoids their
teacher/student forward and student backward work. Across Qwen3-1.7B,
4B, and 8B on AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25, C-OPSD achieves on-par
performance with full OPSD. In a measured micro-batch-size-four
implementation, executed rows decrease by approximately 68%, and with
retained rows packed into full micro-batches, training takes about 40%
less time while rollout generation remains unchanged. Together, the
measurements and training results connect outcome-conditioned
supervision redundancy to more compute-efficient distillation.

## 1 Introduction

On-policy distillation trains a language model on trajectories sampled
from its own policy, using a teacher’s token-level distributions to
supervise the prefixes the student actually visits ([Agarwal et al.,
2023](#bib.bib1)). On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) obtains this
supervision without a separately trained, larger teacher: the student
sees a problem, while a teacher instantiated from the same base model
additionally sees a reference solution ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)).
The reference supplies privileged information during training but is not
required at inference. This creates a simple route to improving
reasoning, but leaves an allocation question open: _how much of this
privileged supervision must actually be computed?_

Standard OPSD applies its distillation objective to every sampled
trajectory, irrespective of whether the student reached the correct
final answer. At the training pass rates in our experiments, roughly
one-third of the rollouts are already correct. Each nevertheless incurs
a student forward, a teacher forward, and student backward computation.
Correctness does not make this supervision trivially unnecessary: a
correct answer need not imply a correct derivation, and the teacher may
still improve the student’s intermediate distributions. The relevant
question is empirical: can that supervision be omitted without
sacrificing the improvement obtained by full OPSD?

Gradient measurements provide a motivation for selective distillation:
incorrect-response gradients are more coherent, while the two outcome
populations largely share a mean direction.

Motivated by this observation, we introduce _correctness-filtered
on-policy self-distillation_ (C-OPSD). The method generates one response
per training problem, checks its final answer, and distills only a
subset of incorrect responses. Unselected rows are physically removed
before both distillation forwards, reducing teacher/student forward and
student backward work without changing rollout generation or the
token-level distillation target.

Across Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B on AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25, C-OPSD
achieves _on-par performance_ while substantially reducing distillation
computation. In a measured micro-batch-size-four implementation,
executed rows decrease by approximately 68% for each teacher/student
forward and student backward. Our experiments evaluate both the
performance–computation trade-off and the role of outcome-based
supervision allocation.

SCOPE and I-SDPO assign different training objectives according to
rollout outcomes ([Zheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)) ([Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib4)). We instead study whether substantial supervision can
be _removed_ under a standalone OPSD objective while retaining
performance, and investigate the gradient structure that makes this
possible. This distinction motivates both our compute-aware
implementation and our analysis of redundancy between
outcome-conditioned gradient populations.

Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  Measurements of outcome-conditioned supervision redundancy. We
  characterize gradient coherence and shared directions across correct
  and incorrect responses to explain opportunities for selective
  distillation.
- •
  A measurement-motivated filtering method. C-OPSD retains a subset of
  incorrect trajectories and removes unselected rows before both
  distillation forwards to avoid their computation.
- •
  Performance and computation evaluation. Experiments at three model
  scales show on-par performance with substantially less distillation
  work; allocation controls connect the supervision choice to observed
  training outcomes.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 On-policy and privileged-context distillation

In on-policy distillation, a student generates a response and a teacher
provides token-level supervision along that response’s prefixes. Unlike
training exclusively on teacher-generated sequences, this places
supervision on states visited by the student, including those arising
from its own mistakes ([Agarwal et al., 2023](#bib.bib1)). The sampled
tokens determine the prefixes at which distributions are compared; they
are not themselves treated as ground-truth answers to imitate.

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) obtains the teacher’s informational
advantage from privileged context rather than a separately trained,
larger model ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)). Let (_x_, _r_) be a
training problem and its reference solution, and let _y_ = (*y*₁, …,
_y_\_(T)) be a student-generated response. At response position _t_, the
student and teacher distributions are

|     |                                      |                                                             |     |     |
| --- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle p_{\theta,t}`$       | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}),`$         |     | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle q_{\bar{\theta},t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(\cdot\mid x,r,y_{<t}).`$ |     |     |

Both models are evaluated on the same student-response prefix, but only
the teacher sees the reference solution. The notation suppresses the
respective prompt templates. In our fixed-teacher configuration, the
student trains a LoRA adapter over a frozen base model, while the
teacher uses that base model with the adapter disabled. Teacher weights
therefore remain fixed as the student adapter changes. The teacher
supplies distributions through forward evaluation; it does not generate
a second response, and no gradient is propagated through its outputs.

### 2.2 The OPSD training objective

For a micro-batch $`\mathcal{B}`$ of sampled responses, let _m__(it)
indicate a valid response-token position, excluding prompt and padding
positions. Writing *D*_($`\tau`$) for the implementation’s clipped
token-level divergence, the empirical OPSD objective is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}(\theta;\mathcal{B})=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{t}m_{it}D_{\tau}\!\left(p_{\theta,it},\operatorname{sg}[q_{\bar{\theta},it}]\right)}{\displaystyle\max\!\left(1,\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{t}m_{it}\right)}.
``` |  | (2) |

where sg denotes stop-gradient. Sampled responses are treated as fixed
when differentiating this loss: the update differentiates the student
distributions, not the discrete sampling procedure. We retain
*D*_($`\tau`$) as a named operator here because our intervention
concerns trajectory selection, not the choice of distillation
divergence.

Equation (2) averages over valid tokens within the micro-batch, rather
than assigning equal total weight to every response. Responses of
different lengths can consequently contribute different numbers of loss
terms. The standard mask does not depend on final-answer correctness:
correct and incorrect responses are supervised through the same
token-level objective.

### 2.3 Outcome correctness

For each sampled response, let *c*_(i) $`\in`$ {0, 1} be the outcome
label returned by a symbolic answer checker using the training reference
answer. This label becomes available after generation and does not
require evaluating the privileged teacher. It is a property of the
sampled response, not an intrinsic binary difficulty label for the
problem: different attempts at the same problem may receive different
outcomes. The checker evaluates the final answer rather than the
intermediate reasoning steps.

Using this label to choose which trajectories receive distillation is
distinct from using a binary reward in a policy-gradient objective. The
supervision on a retained trajectory still comes from the teacher’s
conditional distributions. In particular, distilling an incorrect
trajectory does not mean maximizing the likelihood of its incorrect
final answer.

### 2.4 Where the computation is spent

An OPSD step contains three computational components: student rollout
generation, student and teacher evaluation on the sampled trajectories,
and backward computation through the student’s loss. The teacher has no
backward pass. Generation and subsequent distillation evaluation are
separate costs.

A loss mask selects positions in the objective, whereas physical row
selection removes trajectories from model evaluation. Selecting rows
before the distillation forwards saves both forward evaluation and
subsequent student backward work. We track supervision retention,
executed forward/backward workload, and end-to-end runtime separately;
C-OPSD reduces the distillation workload while retaining the baseline
generation procedure.

## 3 Related Work

### 3.1 On-policy and privileged-context self-distillation

On-policy distillation supervises prefixes generated by the student,
addressing the distribution mismatch between teacher-generated training
sequences and student behavior at inference ([Agarwal et al.,
2023](#bib.bib1)). OPSD obtains teacher supervision from the same base
model augmented with a reference solution ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)). SDPO similarly converts additional context into dense
self-distillation targets, using environment feedback or successful
student attempts to inform the self-teacher ([Hübotter et al.,
2026](#bib.bib16)). Its teacher template also reevaluates successful
attempts using the attempt itself as the correct-solution context. These
methods establish privileged-context supervision; C-OPSD keeps the OPSD
teacher and token-level target fixed and studies which generated
trajectories need that supervision computed.

### 3.2 Correctness-dependent supervision

SCOPE separates correct and incorrect rollouts, applies a student-side
likelihood objective to correct responses, and uses teacher-guided
distillation on incorrect responses, with perplexity-based weighting on
both branches ([Zheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)). I-SDPO routes groups of
responses for the same question: all-incorrect groups with available
ground truth use privileged self-distillation, whereas groups containing
any success use group-relative reinforcement learning, including their
incorrect responses ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)). C-OPSD instead
samples one rollout per question per sampling round, removes correct
trajectories without assigning a replacement likelihood or
reinforcement-learning loss, and retains only a subset of incorrect
trajectories for the original OPSD objective.

Negative Sample Reinforcement provides a related precedent outside
distillation: learning from negatively rewarded responses alone can be
effective without explicit positive reinforcement of correct responses
([Zhu et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)). Its update suppresses incorrect
responses and redistributes probability according to the policy’s
existing preferences. C-OPSD instead matches privileged teacher
distributions at the prefixes of retained incorrect responses. The
shared motivation is selective use of outcome-labeled experience; the
supervisory signal and update are different.

### 3.3 Selecting problems, trajectories, and tokens

PACED estimates each problem’s student pass rate from multiple samples
and weights distillation toward intermediate competence, using a default
kernel proportional to p(1 $`-`$ p) ([Xu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14)).
Most experiments estimate these weights once and keep them fixed;
refresh schedules are also studied. This problem-level curriculum
differs from filtering the outcome of a single newly sampled trajectory.
A failed attempt on a usually solvable question remains eligible for
C-OPSD, and the selection decision requires neither a multi-rollout
pass-rate estimate nor teacher logits.

Two further distinctions are worth stating, because Equation (8)
resembles PACED’s kernel in form. That kernel is a function of each
problem’s estimated pass rate and concentrates weight at intermediate
competence; Equation (8) is a function of the batch-level pass rate and
describes what fraction of a batch survives, not which difficulty is
preferred. The populations they select are in fact near opposite ends of
the difficulty range: the mean offline pass rate of C-OPSD’s retained
set is 0.09
(Table [9](#S6.T9 "Table 9 ‣ 6.3 Outcome-allocation ablation ‣ 6 Experimental Results ‣ Not Every Rollout Needs Distillation: Correctness-Filtered On-Policy Self-Distillation")),
against the intermediate values PACED targets. PACED also reweights the
loss, so every trajectory still passes through both forwards, whereas
C-OPSD removes rows before them and converts the selection into
computation saved.

At token level, TIP combines student entropy and teacher–student
divergence to select informative positions, including confident
predictions on which the teacher disagrees ([Xu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib5)). TRACE uses privileged span annotations to route
self-distillation to selected correct-key or error spans and combines it
with GRPO ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). TRACE also anneals the
distillation coefficient to zero and then skips teacher forwards,
providing a direct precedent for reducing teacher computation as well as
selective supervision. C-OPSD uses a different intervention: whole
trajectories are selected before both distillation forwards, removing
discarded rows from student forward, teacher forward, and student
backward computation without introducing span annotation or a
replacement RL objective. Autoregressive generation is unchanged.

Hou et al. study the number and choice of distinct training problems
([Hou et al., 2026](#bib.bib6)). Their eight-hard-problem experiment
reaches an average score of 53.6 versus 53.7 for the 17K-problem
baseline. The selected problems are reused across training updates, with
eight responses per prompt in the default setup; this is not an
eight-rollout training budget. Their analysis links hard-problem
benefits to longer reasoning trajectories. C-OPSD addresses a
complementary efficiency axis: reducing distillation computation on
already-generated online trajectories rather than reducing the number of
distinct training problems.

### 3.4 Gradient geometry and learning efficiency

Gradient-coherence analyses characterize agreement among example-level
updates ([Chatterjee, 2020](#bib.bib7)) ([Chatterjee and Zielinski,
2020](#bib.bib8)), while gradient-noise scale connects
stochastic-gradient variability to useful batch size ([McCandlish
et al., 2018](#bib.bib9)). PACED applies a related signal–noise
perspective to distillation: individual gradients on mastered problems
need not vanish, but their dispersion across problems can yield low
aggregate signal-to-noise ratio ([Xu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib14)). Its
empirical diagnostic bins problems by estimated pass rate and measures
forward-KL gradients on fixed teacher-generated solutions.

Our measurements instead partition student-generated OPSD trajectories
by observed correctness and jointly examine individual gradient
magnitudes, within-population coherence, and overlap between the correct
and incorrect population-mean directions. The additional
cross-population comparison asks whether the more coherent incorrect
trajectories also preserve the direction supplied by correct ones. We
further test a fitted signal–noise model at larger group sizes without
refitting and connect these measurements to a whole-trajectory
compute-removal intervention. Routed OPD studies another level of the
update, transporting probability mass between student-excess and
teacher-deficit vocabulary alternatives at a given state ([Xia et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10)). C-OPSD leaves that token-level distillation target
unchanged and changes which trajectories are evaluated and
differentiated.

## 4 Outcome-Conditioned Gradient Geometry

We investigate which student-generated trajectories provide distinct
distillation signal. The analysis separates three properties: individual
gradient magnitude, coherence within each outcome population, and
overlap between population-mean directions. These measurements motivate
the allocation rule in Section 5; the training experiments in Section 6
evaluate its performance and computational consequences. Figure 1
summarizes the two main geometric findings examined in this section.

Fitted coherent shares are 6.0% and 18.6%, despite similar individual

p$`\mu`$_(C) + (1 $`-`$ p)$`\mu`$_(I), with p = 0.291, is approximately

### 4.1 Measurement setup

We collect 3,000 trajectories from the Qwen3-1.7B training distribution
at initialization: 873 correct and 2,127 incorrect responses, for a
29.1% pass rate. Sampling uses non-thinking responses, a 1,024-token
generation limit, temperature 1.1, top-p 0.95, and top-k 20.

Correct and incorrect responses also come from almost disjoint parts of
the difficulty range
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4.1 Measurement setup ‣ 4 Outcome-Conditioned Gradient Geometry ‣ Not Every Rollout Needs Distillation: Correctness-Filtered On-Policy Self-Distillation")):
measured against an offline eight-rollout pass-rate probe, 62% of
correct responses fall on problems the base model solves at least seven
times in eight, and 69% of incorrect responses on problems it never
solves.

Within the middle band 0.125 $`\leq`$ p₀ ¡ 0.875 (258 correct and 406
incorrect trajectories), incorrect responses remain more coherent, with
a coherent share of 19.3% against 10.9% for correct responses at group
size four. We do not select on difficulty directly, because estimating
p₀ requires several rollouts per problem, whereas correctness requires a
single answer check.

For each trajectory, we recompute the OPSD loss using a teacher context
containing the reference solution and collect gradients with respect to
the base-weight matrices targeted by LoRA. This measurement space avoids
an initialization-specific degeneracy in adapter coordinates: when the B
factor is initialized to zero, the gradient of the A factor also
vanishes. Base-weight gradients let us compare the update directions
that different trajectories induce on the same set of model weights.

We partition trajectories by final-answer correctness and construct four
disjoint groups in each trial: two correct groups and two incorrect
groups. After summing the gradients within each group, we compute cosine
similarities between the two correct groups, between the two incorrect
groups, and across outcomes.

The group size n is the number of trajectories aggregated for analysis,
not the number of rollouts generated per problem. Within-population
alignment measures how consistently trajectories recover a shared
direction; cross-population alignment measures whether the two
populations supply overlapping directions.

### 4.2 Individual gradient magnitude does not distinguish the populations

Individual gradient magnitudes are 1.538 for correct trajectories and
1.469 for incorrect trajectories. They are similar, and correct
trajectories do not have smaller gradients. Their lower signal
efficiency therefore cannot be explained simply by claiming that a
correct answer makes the distillation gradient negligible.

The distinction therefore requires looking beyond per-trajectory
magnitude. We next examine how much gradient energy survives aggregation
along a common direction.

### 4.3 A signal–noise model of gradient coherence

Individual gradient magnitude does not fully characterize the learning
signal. Large gradients can cancel when they point in different
directions, whereas aligned gradients preserve a stronger common signal
after aggregation.

For either outcome-conditioned population, write a per-trajectory
gradient as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g=\mu+\epsilon,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0,\qquad\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}\lVert\epsilon\rVert^{2}.
``` |  |

Here, $`\mu`$ is the population-mean gradient and $`\epsilon`$ is a
trajectory’s deviation from that mean. Noise refers to across-trajectory
gradient variability, rather than incorrect answers or erroneous labels.

For the sum G_(n) of n independently sampled gradients,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}\lVert G_{n}\rVert^{2}=n^{2}\lVert\mu\rVert^{2}+n\sigma^{2}.
``` |  | (3) |

The shared-direction energy grows quadratically with n, while noise
energy grows linearly. The common direction therefore becomes clearer as
more trajectories are aggregated. When group norms concentrate
sufficiently, the expected cosine between two independent groups from
the same population is approximately

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{n}\approx\frac{\lVert\mu\rVert^{2}}{\lVert\mu\rVert^{2}+\sigma^{2}/n}.
``` |  | (4) |

This relation connects measurable group alignment to the signal–noise
structure of individual trajectories. We define the coherent share as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q=\frac{\lVert\mu\rVert^{2}}{\lVert\mu\rVert^{2}+\sigma^{2}}\approx\frac{c_{n}}{n-(n-1)c_{n}}.
``` |  |

Fitting the model to measurements at group size four gives the
parameters in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 A signal–noise model of gradient coherence ‣ 4 Outcome-Conditioned Gradient Geometry ‣ Not Every Rollout Needs Distillation: Correctness-Filtered On-Policy Self-Distillation").

|  Population |  Within-population cosine |  Mean-gradient magnitude $`\|`$$`\mu`$$`\|`$ |  Noise scale $`\sigma`$ |  Coherent share q |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  Correct |  0.2042 |  0.3776 |  1.4911 |  6.0% |
|  Incorrect |  0.4778 |  0.6339 |  1.3253 |  18.6% |

Table 2: Signal–noise parameters fitted at group size four.

The incorrect population has approximately three times the coherent
share of the correct population. The distinction is therefore not simply
how large individual gradients are, but how much of their energy
survives aggregation along a common direction.

The difference emerges after aggregation. Incorrect trajectories have a
1.68-fold larger mean-gradient magnitude and a lower noise scale.
Defining per-trajectory signal-to-noise ratio as

|     |                                                  |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathrm{SNR}_{1}=\frac{\lVert\mu\rVert}{\sigma},
       ```                                               |     |

the incorrect population has approximately 1.89 times the SNR of the
correct population.

Under independent sampling from a fixed population, the SNR of n
aggregated trajectories scales as $`\surd`$n. Correct trajectories would
therefore require approximately 1.89² $`\approx`$ 3.6 times as many
samples to attain the same mean-estimation SNR. This ratio describes
sample efficiency for estimating the mean gradient.

This gives our first central finding: correct trajectories produce
substantial gradients, but those gradients are more dispersed; incorrect
trajectories recover a stable common direction with fewer samples. This
difference motivates prioritizing incorrect trajectories when
distillation computation is limited.

### 4.6 Shared mean directions and the effect of omitting correct responses

Greater coherence alone does not explain why correct trajectories can be
omitted. If they supply a distinct, important direction, dropping them
could remove complementary supervision despite their higher noise. We
therefore compare the two population-mean directions.

At group size 218, the measured cross-population cosine is 0.9267.
Finite groups still contain sampling variability, so this value reflects
both the difference between the mean directions and within-population
noise. Using each population’s internal alignment, a directional-noise
approximation gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\cos(\mu_{\mathrm{correct}},\mu_{\mathrm{incorrect}})\approx\frac{0.9267}{\sqrt{0.9343\times 0.9824}}\approx 0.967.
``` |  | (5) |

The corresponding angle is approximately 14.7^(∘). Applying the same
correction at group sizes 16, 64, and 218 gives 0.974, 0.967, and 0.967,
respectively, indicating a stable estimate of mean-direction overlap
across aggregation scales.

Decomposing the correct population’s mean into components parallel and
orthogonal to the incorrect mean gives approximate magnitudes

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\lVert\mu_{\mathrm{correct},\parallel}\rVert\approx 0.3653,\qquad\lVert\mu_{\mathrm{correct},\perp}\rVert\approx 0.0958.
``` |  |

The correct mean therefore lies predominantly along a direction already
supplied by incorrect trajectories, with a smaller orthogonal component.

This gives our second central finding: correct trajectories largely
reinforce an existing direction rather than supplying a separate one.
Together with the coherence analysis, this suggests that the reason to
omit correct trajectories is not that their gradients are harmful, but
that they provide noisier, substantially overlapping supervision.

To quantify the effect of removing the correct population from the
response-weighted mean, let p = 873/3,000 = 0.291 and let $`\theta`$ be
the estimated angle between the two population means. The mixture is

|     |                                                              |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mu_{\mathrm{full}}=p\mu_{\mathrm{C}}+(1-p)\mu_{\mathrm{I}}.
       ```                                                           |     |

Using $`\|`$$`\mu`$_(C)$`\|`$ = 0.3776, $`\|`$$`\mu`$_(I)$`\|`$ =
0.6339, and the unrounded noise-corrected cosine from Equation (5), the
angle between this mixture and the incorrect mean is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\varphi=\arctan\!\left(\frac{p\lVert\mu_{\mathrm{C}}\rVert\sin\theta}{(1-p)\lVert\mu_{\mathrm{I}}\rVert+p\lVert\mu_{\mathrm{C}}\rVert\cos\theta}\right)\approx 2.87^{\circ}.
``` |  |

The correct population is less frequent and has a smaller mean-gradient
magnitude, limiting its contribution to the mixture’s orthogonal
component. The resulting mixture direction deviates from the incorrect
mean by approximately 2.87^(∘), with a normalized orthogonal projection
residual of approximately 5.0%. Figure 1 illustrates this
response-weighted geometry.

Together, greater incorrect-gradient coherence and overlapping mean
directions motivate allocating less distillation work to correct
responses. C-OPSD operationalizes this allocation by retaining a subset
of incorrect responses, and the training experiments test whether the
resulting reduction in supervision preserves performance.

## 5 Method

The gradient measurements motivate reducing supervision on correct
responses. C-OPSD implements this idea using outcome verification after
generation and physical row removal before distillation, as shown in
Figure 3.

Figure 3: One rollout per problem and physically compacted distillation
batches. Full OPSD processes all generated trajectories. C-OPSD checks
correctness, retains s = min(k, n $`-`$ k) incorrect responses, and
removes unselected rows before both distillation forwards. The example n
= 4, k = 1 produces one-row tensors rather than four-row tensors with
masked entries. The reference-conditioned teacher is frozen; only the
student is updated. Generation is unchanged. Empty selections execute
one fully masked fallback row.

### 5.1 Outcome verification after generation

Consider a micro-batch of n distinct training problems, each with one
student-generated response. We retain the same student rollout procedure
and privileged reference context as the OPSD baseline. Once generation
has finished, an answer extractor and symbolic checker compare each
response with its training reference answer, producing c_(i) $`\in`$ {0,
1}. The implementation uses the same math_verify-based verification path
described for evaluation, allowing equivalent forms such as 1/2 and 0.5
to agree. This decision uses training labels, not evaluation-benchmark
answers.

Verification operates directly on the generated answer and training
reference, enabling selection before either distillation forward without
a second generation or teacher-scoring pass.

### 5.2 Correctness-filtered retention

Let C = {i : c_(i) = 1} and W = {i : c_(i) = 0}, with k = —C— and —W— =
n $`-`$ k. C-OPSD retains the first s = min(k, n $`-`$ k) indices in W
under the existing micro-batch order. Thus all retained trajectories are
incorrect, but not every incorrect trajectory is retained. We denote the
resulting index set by S.

|     |                                                     |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       s=\min(k,n-k),\qquad S=\operatorname{first}_{s}(W).
       ```                                                  |     | (6) |

The dependence on k defines the retained budget used in the selection
study. Because s is capped by the number of available incorrect
responses, C-OPSD is not always exactly count-matched to a correct-only
arm that retains all k correct responses. It is exactly count-matched,
on the same sampled batch, to a random control selecting s responses
regardless of correctness. Across separate on-policy training runs,
sampled responses and hence realized counts can diverge.

For k = 0 or k = n, the rule selects no trajectory. For n = 1 it always
selects none, so this particular budget rule requires larger
micro-batches to provide training signal. The method’s selection
behavior depends on micro-batch size, not only on the total
optimizer-step batch size.

### 5.3 Retained objective and row removal

For a retained set S, the filtered objective is the same token
divergence as in Equation (2), normalized over the valid tokens of
retained trajectories:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{C\text{-}OPSD}}(\theta;\mathcal{B},S)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{t}m_{it}D_{\tau}\!\left(p_{\theta,it},\operatorname{sg}[q_{\bar{\theta},it}]\right)}{\displaystyle\max\!\left(1,\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{t}m_{it}\right)}.
``` |  | (7) |

Equation (7) averages the distillation loss over retained response
tokens, with the selection treated as fixed during differentiation. For
fixed sampled trajectories and per-token model outputs, it is equivalent
to masking all unselected rows in the full-shaped batch: both
formulations use the same retained-token numerator and denominator.

To realize computational savings, we index-select the retained rows from
student input IDs and attention masks, teacher input IDs and attention
masks, and labels before either distillation forward. Derived tensors
are formed after selection. Prompt-length values that are scalars are
not row-indexed. For an empty selection, the implementation keeps one
row with all response labels masked so that the loss remains zero
without passing a zero-row batch to unsupported kernels.

### 5.4 Algorithm and controls

> Algorithm 1. C-OPSD with physical row removal.
>
> 1.  1.
>     Generate one response per problem with the current student policy;
>     retain the generated tokens and the training references.
> 2.  2.
>     Extract and verify final answers, construct C and W, and compute s
>     = min(—C—, —W—).
> 3.  3.
>     Select the first s incorrect rows under the existing batch order.
> 4.  4.
>     If s ¿ 0, index-select the same rows from all row-aligned student,
>     teacher, and label tensors. If s = 0, keep one fully masked
>     fallback row.
> 5.  5.
>     Evaluate the student and reference-conditioned teacher on the
>     selected prefixes; keep teacher outputs detached.
> 6.  6.
>     Compute the retained-token loss, backpropagate through the
>     student, and follow the baseline’s accumulation and optimizer-step
>     procedure.

|  Arm |  Retained trajectories |  Count for the same batch |  Role |
|----|----|----|----|
|  Full OPSD |  All trajectories |  n |  Performance reference |
|  Correct-only |  All correct trajectories |  k |  Opposite outcome allocation |
|  C-OPSD |  First s incorrect trajectories |  s = min(k, n $`-`$ k) |  Proposed filtered objective |
|  Random selection |  Outcome-blind subset of all trajectories |  s |  Count-matched selection control |
|  All-incorrect |  All incorrect trajectories |  n $`-`$ k |  Higher-retention comparison |

### 5.5 Retention as a function of micro-batch size

For an illustrative model with independent, identically distributed
correctness labels of success probability p, K follows Binomial(n, p).
The expected loss-retention fraction is r_(n)(p) = E\[min(K, n $`-`$
K)\]/n. For n = 4, direct enumeration gives:

|     |                             |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       r_{4}(p)=p(1-p)(1+p-p^{2}).
       ```                          |     | (8) |

The maximum is 0.3125 at p = 0.5. At p = 0.33, the expected
loss-retention fraction is approximately 0.270. Empty selections occur
with probability p^(n) + (1 $`-`$ p)^(n). Under the one-row fallback,
the expected executed-row fraction is r_(n)(p) + \[p^(n) + (1 $`-`$
p)^(n)\]/n. For n = 4 and p = 0.33 this is approximately 0.323, or 1.29
physical rows per micro-batch. For n = 2, the same implementation always
executes one row: its physical retention is 50%, even though its
expected loss retention is p(1 $`-`$ p).

The rule has no free parameter, but it does have a domain. Equation (8)
makes r₄ a function of the pass rate: it peaks at 31.3% near p = 0.5 and
is flat over p $`\in`$ \[0.3, 0.5\], which covers the 29–35% pass rates
observed in our runs. Outside that band it falls away on both sides, to
approximately 22% at p = 0.25, 18.6% at p = 0.2, and 9.8% at p = 0.1.
Because the pass rate shifts as the student trains, from 33% to 29% over
100 steps at 1.7B, the budget also adapts without a schedule. A
fixed-rate subsampling rule would be insensitive to this; min(k, n $`-`$
k) trades that robustness for the absence of a tuned retention
hyperparameter, and at our pass rates the two retain near-identical sets
on the same sampled batches. We therefore state the rule as intended for
pass rates in roughly \[0.25, 0.7\], and recommend a fixed retention
fraction outside that range.

outcome-conditioned populations, with response correctness and problem

## 7 Computational Efficiency

### 7.1 Measured forward/backward reduction

C-OPSD converts trajectory filtering into actual computation savings by
removing unselected rows before both distillation forwards. In the
measured micro-batch-size-four implementation, each student forward,
teacher forward, and student backward processes an average of 1.29 rows
instead of 4, a reduction of approximately 68%. The count includes the
fully masked fallback row executed for an empty selection. The
retained-token objective is defined in Section 5.3.

|----|----|----|----|
|  Student forward |  4 |  1.29 |  $`\approx`$68% |
|  Teacher forward |  4 |  1.29 |  $`\approx`$68% |
|  Student backward |  4 |  1.29 |  $`\approx`$68% |

## 8 Conclusion

Outcome-conditioned gradient measurements reveal a form of supervision
redundancy in OPSD. Correct and incorrect responses have similar
individual gradient magnitudes, but incorrect responses have greater
coherence, while the two population-mean directions largely overlap. A
signal–noise model fitted at a small group size tracks alignment at
larger sizes, and the measured population proportions and mean
magnitudes explain why omitting the correct population changes the
estimated response-weighted mean direction only slightly.

These findings motivate C-OPSD, which generates one response per
problem, retains a subset of incorrect responses, and removes unselected
rows before both distillation forwards. Across Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B on
AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25, C-OPSD achieves on-par performance with full
OPSD. In a measured micro-batch-size-four implementation, executed rows
decrease from 4 to 1.29 per teacher/student forward and student
backward, a reduction of approximately 68%, and packing the retained
rows into full micro-batches turns this into about 40% less training
time. The method preserves the token-level distillation target while
concentrating computation on a smaller set of trajectories. Together,
the measurements and training results demonstrate the practical value of
allocating distillation work according to outcome-conditioned gradient
structure.

## Appendix B Implementation Details

#### Divergence.

With teacher distribution $`q`$ and student distribution $`p`$ at a
response position, both computed from logits divided by the sampling
temperature 1.1,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\tau}(p,q)=\sum_{v}\min\!\big(q_{v}\log(q_{v}/p_{v}),\,\tau\big),
``` |  |

the forward KL with each vocabulary entry’s contribution clipped at
$`\tau`$ (0.05 for 1.7B and 4B, 0.06 for 8B), as in the OPSD
implementation. The loss averages $`D_{\tau}`$ over valid response
tokens.

#### Row packing.

Micro-batches $`1,\dots,k-1`$ of an accumulation window apply the
selection rule and pass their retained rows on. The last micro-batch
packs all retained rows into micro-batches of four, left-padded to a
common prompt width. Each token is weighted by $`1/N_{j}`$, where
$`N_{j}`$ is the number of retained tokens in its original micro-batch
$`j`$, so the accumulated gradient is the same as without packing. A
micro-batch with no retained rows contributes nothing.

#### Baselines.

SFT and GRPO follow the setup of the OPSD paper ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)), with the same 100-step budget and checkpoint
selection as the OPSD arms.

#### Gradient measurements.

The parameters in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 A signal–noise model of gradient coherence ‣ 4 Outcome-Conditioned Gradient Geometry ‣ Not Every Rollout Needs Distillation: Correctness-Filtered On-Policy Self-Distillation")
average 64 trials at group size 4; the out-of-sample measurements
average 8, 6, and 2 trials at group sizes 16, 64, and 218; each
training-stability measurement averages 8 trials at group size 16. Every
trial draws four disjoint groups.
````

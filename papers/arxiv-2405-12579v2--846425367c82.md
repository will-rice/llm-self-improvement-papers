###### Abstract

Fact-checking based on commercial LLMs has become mainstream. Although
these methods offer high explainability, it falls short in accuracy
compared to traditional fine-tuning approaches, and data security is
also a significant concern. In this paper, we propose a self-instruction
based fine-tuning approach for fact-checking that balances accuracy and
explainability. Our method consists of Data Augmentation and Improved
DPO fine-tuning. The former starts by instructing the model to generate
both positive and negative explanations based on claim-expdence pairs
and labels, then sampling the dataset according to our customized
difficulty standards. The latter employs our proposed improved DPO to
fine-tune the model using the generated samples. We fine-tune the
smallest-scale Llama-7b model and evaluate it on the challenging
fact-checking datasets FEVEROUS and HOVER, utilizing four fine-tuning
methods and three few-shot learning methods for comparison. The
experiments demonstrate that our approach not only retains accuracy
comparable to, or even surpassing, traditional fine-tuning methods, but
also generates fluent explanation text. Moreover, it also exhibit high
generalization performance. Our method is the first to leverage
self-supervised learning for fact-checking and innovatively combines
contrastive learning and improved DPO in fine-tuning LLMs, as shown in
the experiments.

## 1 Introduction

Fact verification, also called fact-checking, aims at determining the
veracity of claims based on multiple reliable pieces of
evidence([Glockner et al., 2022](#bib.bib10); [Guo et al.,
2022](#bib.bib11)). It is an important task for combating the spread of
misinformation on social media platforms ([Vosoughi et al.,
2018](#bib.bib35); [Botnevik et al., 2020](#bib.bib4)).

Most existing fact-checking methods are based on fine-tuning pre-trained
models to achieve multi-class classification models([Chiang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib7)). These methods take claims and evidence as input,
which is undergone highly customized preprocessing ([Hu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib14); [Wang et al., 2022](#bib.bib37); [Ma et al.,
2022](#bib.bib19)), and produce probability distributions of claim
veracity. Although these works achieved excellent results, the
implementation methods themselves are inherently unable to meet the
requirements of trustworthy fact-checking, as highlighted by [Pan et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib24): The model should not only predict the veracity of
the claim but also provide a clear explanation of its reasoning process
to help users understand and trust the results.

Recently, some researchers have focused their attention on large
language models(LLMs)([Naveed et al., 2023](#bib.bib21)) such as
ChatGPT([Brown et al., 2020](#bib.bib5)), to implemented explainabe fact
verification([Pan et al., 2023](#bib.bib24); [Zhang and Gao,
2023](#bib.bib46)). These methods follow the following process: Firstly,
they decompose complex claims into simpler sub-claims that exhibit
parallel or hierarchical relationships. Secondly, they adopt a
question-and-answer approach, leveraging the intelligence of LLMs and
relevant evidence, to systematically verify each sub-claim. Finally,
based on the collective results of these verifications, they provide an
overall assessment of the truthfulness of the complex claim.

These methods leverage the intelligence of large language models to
their fullest extent, breaking the limitations of existing approaches
and achieving remarkable results in few-shot and zero-shot. However,
these methods also face challenges:

1.  •
    Uncontrollable cost: These methods decompose the evidence gathering
    task into independent or dependent sub-tasks for fine-grained
    reasoning, and as the granularity decreases, the size of the
    sub-tasks increases as well, which means a higher cost in interface
    invocation.
2.  •
    Overlooking the potential of open-source models: Existing methods
    directly utilize commercial models like ChatGPT, which raises
    concerns about data privacy. Moreover, there is currently limited
    research on transferring the inference ability of ChatGPT to smaller
    open-source models such as Llama-2-7b([Touvron et al.,
    2023](#bib.bib33)), a much smaller model with 7 billion
    parameters(we refer to Llama-2-7b as Llama2 for the sake of
    convenience), or improving the inference abilities of Llama2.

To address these issues, we first propose a question: Can we leverage
open-source LLMs, such as Llama2, to perform effective fact-checking?
Considering that existing research([Hu et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)) point
out that large language models excel at challenging tasks but may
exhibit illusions on simpler tasks, and they act as a filter for
difficult problems([Ma et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)), we believe that
judging claim veracity is a simple problem, while providing relevant
explanations is a difficult problem, as it requires capturing causal
relationships in addition to generating explanatory content. Therefore,
we attempt to provide claims, evidence and labels to the LLMs and ask it
to provide reasonable explanations. Experimental results show that in
most cases, the model can generate reasonable text to explain the
contradictions between claims and evidence, even without adopting a
decomposition approach, as demonstrated in Table
[1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2 2D Data Augmentation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").
Furthermore, we consider jointly modeling the label of a data and the
generated explanations, employing self-instruction[Wang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib38) and counterfactual data augmentation[Zhu et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib47) to finetune Llama2, to enhance the inference ability
of the model. We also adopt PEFT[Mangrulkar et al. (2022)](#bib.bib20)
using an improved DPO([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib25))to fine-tune
Llama2. Our contributions are as follows:

- •
  We propose a two-dimensional data augmentation approach that involves
  counterfactual data augmentation and generating multiple explanations
  using self-instruction based method to achieve joint modeling of
  labels and explanations. These methods effectively expand the
  available data and make full use of limited data.
- •
  We propose a improvements method to the DPO algorithm to enhance its
  performance.
- •
  We filled the gaps in the research on self-instruction for small
  open-source models, introducing an efficient fine-tuning method based
  on DPO that enhances the inference capabilities of Llama2.
  Experimental results shows that our method achieves both high
  performance and explainability.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Pretrained Language Based

In recent years, fact-checking work utilized pretrained language models
to conduct a series of interesting studies focusing on retrieval,
reasoning of the fact-checking workflow. For instance, [Sheng et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib26) proposed an improvement to information retrieval
methods, taking into account the characteristics of events and patterns
in fact-checking articles. [Wu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib40) addressed
structured evidence such as tables and advocated for joint training of
fact verification and the selection of row and column information,
thereby enhancing the model’s ability to retrieve information from
structured data. [Si et al. (2021)](#bib.bib28) incorporates the
perception of topic and stance features and highlights the importance of
topic consistency between claims and evidence, as well as the underlying
stance of evidence towards the claim. [Zhang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib44)
discovered the presence of false correlations in multi-hop fact
verification datasets, and proposed Casual Walk to address this problem,
which introduces an intermediary inference path between the inputand the
outcome, extending the inference path and compelling the model to
abandon the use of false correlations as shortcuts.

Despite the excellent work mentioned above, they lack of explainability,
and some efforts also focused on addressing this issue. [Shu et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib27) proposed a sentence-comment co-attention network to
jointly capture interpretable top-k sentences and user comments for
detecting fake news. [Hu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib14) introduced ReRead,
advocating for dual reviewing to improve the accuracy of retrieving
correct evidence. Another viable approach is to adopt an ablation
strategy to evaluate the salience of each reasoning path. For instance,
[Si et al. (2023)](#bib.bib29) treated the evidence extraction task as a
subgraph extraction task, where each subgraph represents a reasoning
path from evidence and claims to conclusions. Although the
aforementioned methods have improved interpretability to some extent,
due to the limitations of their model structures, they only provide
interpretable structured information rather than generating
human-readable natural language text.

### 2.2 LLMs Based

With the emergence of tools like ChatGPT([Ouyang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib23)), fact-checking based on LLMs has become increasingly
mainstream. These methods leverage the reasoning and generation
capabilities of LLMs to achieve high accuracy while generating
human-readable explanatory content. Despite the illusion problem
associated with LLMs, they still represent an excellent solution for
achieving interpretable factual evidence.

Fact verification based on LLMs is mainly divided into two approaches:
Question decomposition and Retrieval Augmented Generation [Lewis et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib16). ProgramFC proposed by [Pan et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib24) utilizes contextual learning of LLMs to decompose
complex claims into multi-step reasoning programs and then performs
step-by-step inference on the claims to arrive at a final conclusion.
[Zhang and Gao (2023)](#bib.bib46) introduces a hierarchical prompting
method(HiSS) that guides LLMs to explore the sub claim in more depth.
Apart from that, [Wang and Shu (2023)](#bib.bib36) proposed FOLK, which
made improvements information retrieval by utilizing Google search to
retrieve answers to the questions, and finally reintegrates the
retrieved information back into the model to improve accuracy. [Chen
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6) divides the retrieval process into two steps.
Firstly, a large number of documents are extracted from Bing based on
sub-questions posed by LLMs. Then, these documents are refined,
summarized, and the most crucial information is extracted. [Li et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib17) leverages AI Agent technology ([Yao et al.,
2022](#bib.bib43)) for fact-checking, decomposing the process into tasks
such as claim decomposition, information retrieval, information
extraction, and evidence declaration, and treat them as planning tasks
where the model fully determines the plan.

The aforementioned methods based on LLMs are excellent, they require
external interfaces from OpenAI, such as text-davinci-003, leading to
higher costs and compromises in terms of data security. Different from
previous work, our proposed method focuses on improving the inference
ability of the model using self-instruction techniques([Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib38)) and fine-tuning with small-scale open-source LLMs.
This approach effectively avoids the aforementioned issues, as well as
combining high accuracy with explainability.

## 3 Method

In this section, we start by describing the problem, followed by a
step-by-step introduction of our proposed method, consisting of two
parts: 2D Data Augmentation and Improved DPO Fine-tuning, as shown in
Figure
[1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.2 LLMs Based ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").
The former is used to generate positive and negative examples, while the
improvement method proposed by the latter is used for fine-tuning the
model.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

Given a claim $`c`$ and evidence $`k`$, we construct a function $`I`$,
to convert $`c`$ and $`k`$ to a prompt $`I(c,k)`$(see more in section
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Design of Prompt Function ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")).
According to question, a probabilistic generative model $`\pi`$ aims to
predict a label $`y\in\{\text{Yes, No}\}`$, as well as the corresponding
explanation $`e=\{{e_{1},e_{2},...,e_{n}}\}`$, where $`n`$ should be
less than or equal to the maximum number of generated tokens:

|     |                       |              |           |
| --- | --------------------- | ------------ | --------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle P(y,e | c,k)=\pi(y,e | I(c,k))`$ |     | (1) |

Unlike previous fact-checking work that based on LLMs, our designed
prompt $`I`$ is very simple. It only provides a statement and
evidence(during the data augmentation phase, it may include labels and
other supplementary information to enhance model performance), as
demonstrated in Table
[1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2 2D Data Augmentation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").
The model is required to check whether there is a contradiction between
the statement and evidence and provide a conclusion. Our focus is on
fine-tuning the model through self-instruction, aiming to achieve
commercial-level performance even with small-scale LLMs.

### 3.2 2D Data Augmentation

At this stage, we expand the data volume from two dimensions: the data
side and the label side. On the data side, we use counterfactual-based
data augmentation methods to increase the amount of data. On the label
side, we generate multiple explanations as pseudo-labels for supervised
model fine-tuning.

Counterfactual Data Augmentation. Many advanced multi-hop fact
verification methods([Das et al., 2023](#bib.bib9)) demonstrate poor
generalization performance on out-of-distribution(OOD) data. In
contrast, CDA(Counterfactual Data Augmentation) achieves a generalizable
dataset by increasing counterfactual instances. Specifically, this
method modifies and edits entities in the text while preserving overall
semantic similarity, thereby altering the causal relationships between
entities in the sentences. This approach enhances the model’s
performance by focusing on logical relationships in the sentences,
rather than relying solely on data-driven improvements.

We utilized RACE proposed by [Zhu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib47) to achieve
counterfactual data augmentation. This method involves extracting causal
features using an explainer, then manipulating the evidence information
using an editor. Based on the causal features and manipulated evidence,
a generator is used to generate counterfactual claims. The method also
provides information on the locations of the manipulated information,
which is highly effective for large models to identify contradictions
between evidence and claims.

Label Augmentation. In addition to data augmentation from the data side,
we propose a method to augment data from the label side. Our idea stems
from observations of LLMs, as shown in Table
[1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.2 2D Data Augmentation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").
The model does not perform well in simple classification tasks, as shown
by the varying labels it produces when given the same claim and
evidence. On the contrary, the model may excel at complex reasoning
tasks, as it has a higher likelihood of generating correct logic when
given the correct label. We speculate that this is related to the
model’s pretrained strategy, and a similar phenomenon has been observed
in the task of event extraction([Ma et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)).
Therefore, we consider whether we can use the logic generated by the
model as pseudo-labels for joint training with the classification
labels. This approach ensures both the accuracy of the model’s
predictions and its explainability. Hence, we propose the following
method to enhance the label side, constructing a joint representation of
classification labels and explanations leveraging the reasoning
capability of the model itself.

We employ the following four configurations to enhance the label side,
preparing for subsequent training tasks:

|     | $`\displaystyle P(e\_{yes} | c,k,y\_{yes})=\pi(e | I(c,k,y\_{yes}))`$ |     | (2) |

|     | $`\displaystyle P(e\_{false} | c,k,y*{no}=\pi(e*{false} | I(c,k,y\_{no}))`$ |     | (3) |

The first setting involves only inputting a claim $`c`$ and evidence
$`k`$ and requires the model to generate a label $`y`$ and an
explanation $`e`$, as shown in equation
([1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1 Problem Formulation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")),
which is used to evaluate the difficulty of data points. The second
setting assumes there existing contradiction between the claim and the
evidence. We assign the label $`y_{yes}`$ and require the model to
output the corresponding explanation $`e_{yes}`$, as shown in Equation
([2](#S3.E2 "In 3.2 2D Data Augmentation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")),
while the third setting is the opposite, as shown in Equation
([3](#S3.E3 "In 3.2 2D Data Augmentation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")).
The fourth setting takes effect only when the data is sourced from a
counterfactual subset and there is a contradiction between the claim and
the evidence. We provide the editing cues from the counterfactual data
as tips to the model, as shown in Equation
([4](#S3.E4 "In 3.2 2D Data Augmentation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")).
These editing cues indicate which parts of the claim/evidence have been
modified, resulting in a contradiction. We require the model to identify
the points of contradiction from these editing cues in order to provide
more accurate explanations.

Training Data Construction. At this stage, we construct dataset for DPO
training based on the aforementioned work. Our idea is inspired by the
high similarity between DPO and contrastive learning(CL)([Zhang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib45)), both of which learn through the distributional
features of positive and negative samples. However, the difference is
that in CL, each training data sample contains multiple positive and
negative examples to help the model better capture the data
distribution, while the current DPO related dataset([Bai et al.,
2022](#bib.bib2)) only contain one positive and one negative example per
sample, which cannot help the model better grasp the data distribution.
Therefore, we propose a new strategy of difficulty-based sampling to
generate training data, which is outlined below.

For each piece of data, we first generate $`k`$ different results based
on setting 1, count the number of correct predictions, denoted as $`w`$,
which helps assess the difficulty of the data. Next, We determine the
number of samples $`N_{sample}`$ base on the equation
([5](#S3.E5 "In 3.2 2D Data Augmentation ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")),
where $`N_{min}`$ ensures the minimum number of samples, and
$`N_{base}`$ is the based sampling number. Then, we randomly sample one
result from both the results generated from Setting 2 and Setting 3. If
the sample comes from the counterfactual subset with a label of
“refutes”, we sample a correct result from Setting 4. We concatenate the
prompt, label, and result to generate a positive sample $`y_{chosen}`$,
and a negative sample $`y_{rejected}`$. This step is repeated for a
total of $`N_{sample}`$ times. Finally, all the positive and negative
sample pairs are saved to the training dataset for DPO training.

### 3.3 Improved DPO Fine-tuning

In this stage, we use DPO to optimize LLMs, and the original loss
function of DPO is shown in equation
([6](#S3.E6 "In 3.3 Improved DPO Fine-tuning ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")),
where $`\pi_{ref}`$ refers to the basic model, $`\beta`$ is a
hyperparameter, and $`\pi_{\theta}`$ represents the model enhanced with
the PEFT method with trainable parameters $`\theta`$ injected into each
layer of the transformer architecture of the model.

|     |                                                    |                                                                            |                            |                 |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | --------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle L_{DPO}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{ref})=`$ | $`\displaystyle-\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi*{\theta}(y*{chosen} | x)}{\pi*{ref}(y*{chosen}   | x)}`$           |     |     |
|     |                                                    | $`\displaystyle-\beta\log\frac{\pi*{\theta}(y*{rejected}                   | x)}{\pi*{ref}(y*{rejected} | x)}\Big)\Big]`$ |     | (6) |

It is worth noting that we observed a side effects of DPO in our
experiments(see details in Section
[D](#A4 "Appendix D Improved DPO Strategy ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")).
To address this issue, we introduced two constraint terms $`C_{chosen}`$
and $`C_{rejected}`$ to enhance the loss function, and used two
advantage values $`a_{1}`$ and $`a_{2}`$ to respectively emphasize how
much $`\mathcal{P}(y_{chosen})`$ has increased and
$`\mathcal{P}(y_{rejected})`$ has decreased compared to the original, as
shown in equation
[7](#S3.E7 "In 3.3 Improved DPO Fine-tuning ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").

|     |                                                                   |                            |                     |
| --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle C*{chosen}=-\log\frac{\pi*{\theta}(y\_{chosen}    | x)}{\pi*{ref}(y*{chosen}   | x)}+log(1+a\_{1})`$ |     |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle C*{rejected}=\log\frac{\pi*{\theta}(y\_{rejected} | x)}{\pi*{ref}(y*{rejected} | x)}-log(1-a\_{2})`$ |     | (7) |

After introducing the two constraint terms, we formulated it as a
constrained optimization problem with inequality constraints:

|     |                        |                                                         |     |     |
| --- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     |                        | $`\displaystyle\mathop{argmin}\limits_{\theta}L_{DPO}`$ |     |     |
|     | $`\displaystyle s.t.`$ | $`\displaystyle C_{chosen}\leq 0`$                      |     |     |
|     |                        | $`\displaystyle C_{rejected}\leq 0`$                    |     | (8) |

We employ the Lagrange Multiplier([Bertsekas, 2014](#bib.bib3)) to
convert the aforementioned constrained problem into an unconstrained
problem, allowing us to directly minimize the objective function, as
shown in Equation
([9](#S3.E9 "In 3.3 Improved DPO Fine-tuning ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")),
where $`\mu_{1}`$ and $`\mu_{2}`$ are the learnable lagrange multipliers
corresponding to $`C_{chosen}`$ and $`C_{rejected}`$, respectively. We
multiply the constraint terms by $`\beta`$ and take the logarithm of
their sigmoid values to ensure that the scale of the constraint terms is
consistent with that of $`L_{DPO}`$:

|     |                         |                                                                                            |     |     |
| --- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle Loss=`$ | $`\displaystyle L_{DPO}+\log\sigma\Big[\beta(\mu_{1}C_{chosen}+\mu_{2}C_{rejected})\Big]`$ |     | (9) |

To ensure that the aforementioned optimization problem satisfies the
Karush-Kuhn-Tucker(KKT) conditions, we adopt the following approach
since deep learning networks are not convex functions. For $`\theta`$,
we update it using gradient descent. As for the lagrange multipliers, we
update them as equation
([10](#S3.E10 "In 3.3 Improved DPO Fine-tuning ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")),
where $`lr_{\mu}`$ represents the learning rate for the Lagrange
multipliers. We adhere to the KKT conditions, which require the
constraints $`u_{1}`$ and $`u_{2}`$ to be greater than or equal to 0.

|     |                                                               |     |      |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mu_{1}=max(\mu_{1}-lr_{\mu}*C_{chosen},0)`$   |     |      |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mu_{2}=max(\mu_{2}-lr_{\mu}*C_{rejected},0)`$ |     | (10) |

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Datasets

We evaluate the performance of our model using two datasets, FEVEROUS
and HOVER.

FEVEROUS is proposed by [Aly et al. (2021)](#bib.bib1), consisting of
fact-checking data with various challenges such as Numerical Reasoning,
Entity Disambiguation, and Multi-hop Reasoning.

HOVER was proposed by [Yang et al. (2018)](#bib.bib42), which serves as
a more challenging multi-hop fact-checking dataset, with a maximum hop
count of 4. This is attributed not only to the increased number of hops
but also to the longer evidence provided, most of which contain
distracting information. Appendix
[C](#A3 "Appendix C Statics of Dataset ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")
presents detailed statistical data for the two datasets.

### 4.2 Baselines

We employed the following two categories of methods as our baselines:

I. Supervised Fine-tuning: These methods provide binary classification
results based solely on the input data to determine if there is a
contradiction between the claim and the evidence. We fine-tuned these
methods using the full dataset, including counterfactual data, to
evaluate the advantages of our approach compared to traditional methods.
We selected the following four methods: Bert-FC([Soleimani et al.,
2020](#bib.bib31)) and T5([Jiang et al., 2021](#bib.bib15)) employ
pre-trained models of different sizes for binary fact-checking
classification tasks. RoBERTa-NLI([Nie et al., 2020](#bib.bib22)) and
DeBERTa-NLI([Sileo, 2023](#bib.bib30)) are fine-tuned on other textual
entailment tasks. For detailed information, please refer to the
appendix.

II. Few-shot Learning: These methods leverage LLMs to generate
prediction results along with relevant explanations. This includes
commercially available LLMs that are difficult to fine-tune due to their
large parameter volume. To tackle this, we adopt a few-shot learning
approach by selecting 10 examples from the training data and utilizing
their labels and explanations as prompts. This category of methods is
employed to assess the performance and interpretability advantages of
our model in comparison to large-scale LLMs. We have chosen the
following methods for evaluation: FLAN-T5([Chung et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8)), ChatGPT-3.5([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib23)), and
ProgramFC([Pan et al., 2023](#bib.bib24)), which translates the
reasoning process into a program.

### 4.3 Experimental Settings

In the data processing stage, we set the maximum token count to 512 to
ensure diversity. For the 4 settings mentioned above, we attempted to
generate 10 different results for each. During the filtering process, we
evaluated the win_rate of the data under setting 1 and used it to
determine the sampling probability. This resulted in sampling 148,232
and 221,373 preference data pairs on FEVEROUS and HOVER, respectively.

In the model fine-tuning stage, we used implemented improve DPO based on
trl([von Werra et al., 2020](#bib.bib34)). We fine-tuned the
half-precision model with 2 Nvidia RTX 4090 GPUs, setting the learning
rate to 1e-4, warm-up to 200, learning decay rate to 0.999, $`\beta`$ to
0.1, $`a_{1}`$ to 0.1 and $`a_{2}`$ to 0.1. We used a batch size of 20
and trained for 30,000 iterations, employing an early stopping strategy.
DeepSpeed was also used to manage memory consumption.

### 5.1 Main Results

Based on the experimental results presented in Table
[2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5 Results and Discussion ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction"),
we analyzed the advantages and disadvantages of our method in comparison
to traditional SFT methods and LLMs based methods. We have summarized
the following two key points.

We achieved comparable or even superior performance to traditional SFT
methods by using fewer training parameters. We provide a detailed report
of the parameter distribution of the fine-tuned model in Table
[6](#A5.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix E Parameter Setting ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").
It can be observed that our method has an order of magnitude fewer
trainable parameters compared to traditional SFT methods(only 61.5% of
Bert-FC and 18.9% of Ro-NLI). Our method outperforms the approach used
in Ion FEVEROUS. However, on HOVER, our performance is generally not as
good as RoBERTa-NLI.

We analyzed the experiments and identified three reasons for this:
1)Traditional SFT methods may take shortcuts by learning biases in the
dataset, such as annotations, for complex multi-hop reasoning tasks,
rather than learning the logical reasoning itself ([Zhang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib44)). Moreover, the length of the 4_hops data in HOVER
even exceeds the maximum processing length of I, which makes the model
more biased towards the data. The above analysis partly explains why I
achieves good results in the Entity Disambiguation task and the HOVER
dataset. 2)Our proposed training scheme is more challenging as it
requires the model to simultaneously consider labels and explaination,
so compromising on accuracy to some extent is acceptable.

Our method outperforms approaches based on LLMs and prompt engineering
techniques. Compared to ChatGPT-3.5, our method achieves f1 score
improvements of 21.06% and 27.36% on FEVEROUS and HOVER, respectively,
with significant improvements in each subtask. Compared to the carefully
designed ProgramFC, our method also achieves an overall improvement of
1.73% and 9.90%, respectively, and its performance on HOVER is more
stable, with no drastic decline in experimental performance as the
number of hops increases. This comparative experiment confirms that our
proposed method can significantly enhance the performance of open-source
models like Llama2, enabling them to surpass the performance of
commercial large-scale models in fact-checking tasks.

#### 5.2.1 2D Data Augmentation Analysis

Joint training of labels and explaination ensures the explainability. We
first analyzed various settings in 2D Data Augmentation. We first
discarded explaination as auxiliary labels(-exp), and directly predicted
the first token for classification. We observed that there existing a
slight improvement. Next we analyzed whether the model still retained
exlainability. Detailed result displayed in Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Supplement of -exp ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")
revealed LLMs lost its ability to generate fluent explain and tends to
produce repetitive tokens and irrelevant information. Consequently, we
consider that sacrificing explainability for marginal performance gains
is not a worthwhile trade-off.

Both counterfactual dataset and sampling strategy improve performance.
We further evaluate the impact of counterfactual dataset(-cou) on
experimental results. Based on our findings, counterfactual dataset did
contribute to improve model performance. That’s because flipping the
truthfulness of facts effectively increased the dataset volume, and the
joint fine-tuning of inverted and original datas helped the model
effectively learn factual reasoning methods. Finally, we evaluated the
impact of the difficulty-based sampling strategy(-sam). The experimental
results demonstrates superiority of the sampling strategy in HOVER,
which contributed a 6.25% improvement in f1. We also conducted extensive
analysis on specific cases to gain a deeper understanding of the notable
effect, and attributed it to the difficulty of the dataset. For a simple
data point in FEVEROUS, our model can always generate reasonable
explanations given the ground_true label. However, the same is not true
for 3_hop and 4_hop examples, as it can only generate reasonable
explanations with a probability of 56% under the same conditions, as
shown in Figure
[3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.5 Manual Evaluation ‣ 5 Results and Discussion ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").
The sampling strategy we proposed ensures obtaining more reasonable
explanations by repeatedly resampling, counteracting incorrect
explanations, and mitigating this issue.

#### 5.2.2 Improved DPO Analysis

Our improvements to the DPO force model to learn correct reasoning and
discard incorrect ones. In this section, we discuss the impact of our
improved DPO. Firstly, we assessed whether the value of $`advantage`$
affects model performance. We firstly set the advantage to 0(-adv) and
observe a significant decline in performance for HOVER and FEVEROUS.
Secondly, we test the original DPO(-lag) on the model and also find a
noticeable decrease. It is worth noting that both of the settings
control the advantage of positive examples over negative examples in
terms of generation probability. The former controls how much
improvement positive examples should have in generation probability
compared to negative examples, while the latter ensures that the
generation probability of positive examples is higher than the lower
bound of negative examples. These two ablation experiments indicate that
our improved DPO, by incorporating the concept of advantage and the idea
of comparison, enables the model to learn correct reasoning logic and
improves performance.

Regularization constraints cannot replace lagrange multipliers. We
further conducted a detailed analysis of $`advantage`$ in improved DPO.
During the training process, we employed the product of $`advantage`$
and the Lagrange multiplier $`\mu`$ as a constraint term to regulate the
magnitude of the loss, as depicted in Equation
[9](#S3.E9 "In 3.3 Improved DPO Fine-tuning ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").
The trainable parameter $`\mu`$ allows for the scaling of $`advantage`$.
Upon examining the experimental data during training, we observed that
$`\mu`$ frequently scaled $`advantage`$ to 0, as shown in
[2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5.2 Ablation Study ‣ 5 Results and Discussion ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction"),
indicating that only a small fraction of the data points resided on the
boundary of the constraint. In other words, our improved DPO primarily
impacted a select subset of the data points. We conduct further testing
on whether setting $`\mu`$ as a fixed regularization term(-mug) would
yield the same experimental results(equivalent to CPO ([Xu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib41))). Experimental results show a significant decrease in
model performance on FEVEROUS and HOVER, especially the latter, with a
performance drop of 5.82% on f1. This indicates that the method of using
a regularization term for constraint cannot replace the effectiveness of
the improved DPO. The dynamic scaling ability effectively regulates the
role of the constraint term, preventing the problem of unstable values
and local optima caused by excessive constraint penalties.

Learning differences between positive and negative examples effectively
improves performance. Finally, we directly replaced DPO with SFT(-dpo),
and experimental results show a significant decrease in performance on
both the FEVEROUS and HOVER, particularly the latter, which experienced
a performance decrease of 25.1%. This reveals the role of negative
examples in self-instruction, particularly on challenging datasets like
HOVER. It is evident that the model can greatly improve its performance
by learning the differences between correct and incorrect reasoning
paths through contrastive learning.

### 5.3 Generalization Performance

In this section, we assess the model’s generalization performance by
conducting cross-testing by fine-tuning the model on FEVEROUS, HOVER and
an additional dataset called FEVER([Thorne et al., 2018](#bib.bib32)).
While FEVER and FEVEROUS exhibit some similarities, neither of them
presents the same level of difficulty as HOVER. To ensure fairness, all
models underwent fine-tuning with counterfactual data. The experimental
results shown in Table
[3](#S5.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 5.3 Generalization Performance ‣ 5 Results and Discussion ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")lead
us to the following two conclusions.

Our proposed method outperforms SFT approaches. Our method achieves 89%
accuracy and 88.83% F1 score on FEVEROUS(when fine-tuning the model on
HOVER), 73.30% accuracy and 73.39% f1 score on HOVER, and 87.22%
accuracy and 87.21% f1 score on FEVER(when fine-tuning the model on
FEVEROUS). We attribute the outstanding performance to 2 factors. On the
one hand, compared to traditional methods that focus on label fitting,
We utilized a DPO fine-tuning approach with a minimal number of
parameters adjusted(trainable parameters accounting for only 0.99% of
the total). This approach preserves the generative and inference
capabilities of the language models(LLMs), while full fine-tuning may to
some extent compromise the pretraining model’s inherent learned
knowledge. On the other hand, we propose a method of jointly modeling
labels and explanations, which can compel the model to improve its
reasoning abilities and generalize them to other datasets.

The model exhibits better generalization performance on more challenging
datasets(HOVER). As shown in Table

after training on HOVER, our method achieves 89.00% accuracy and 88.83%
f1 on FEVEROUS, surpassing its performance on HOVER itself.
Additionally, on the FEVER dataset, the model achieves an accuracy of
80.94%. In contrast, when the model is trained on the FEVEROUS dataset,
it only achieves 73.30% accuracy on HOVER. This characteristic can also
be observed on other datasets.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work

Our work attempts to improve model performance using self-instruction
and employs an enhanced dpo approach that combines fine-tuning the model
with positive and negative samples. We also conducted a series of
experiments to validate the effectiveness of our model. First, the
overall experimental results demonstrate the accuracy and effectiveness
of our approach. Then the ablation study to individually validate the
efficacy of each module. Finally, Generalization performance evaluation,
case study and manual evaluation were conducted to verify the model’s
generalization ability and explainability. In future work, we hope to
implement the following aspects:

Deeper exploration of negative samples in the context of explainable
fact-checking. Our experimental results shows that negative samples
contribute to model performance to some extends, and we believe that
further investigating the role of negative samples can enhance the
performance of LLMs and can be utilized in prompt techniques. Current
prompt techniques often employ the idea of COT([Wei et al.,
2024](#bib.bib39)) and RAG([Lewis et al., 2020](#bib.bib16)), while
overlooking the importance of learning from negative/incorrect samples.

More comprehensive self-instruction approach. Our experiments only
provided a simple self-instruction scheme while preserving model
interpretability. Due to the limitations of open-source LLMs, the model
may struggle to generate reliable explanatory content for more complex
fact-checking tasks in order to achieve self-instruction. We believe
that models like ChatGPT can be utilized to generate interpretable
content and facilitate model distillation. When dealing with multi-hop
fact-checking tasks, we can fine-tune the model in multiple steps,
dividing the task into several stages.

Different Experiment in an open-world setting. Our work focuses on
improving the performance of LLMs given complex evidence, which often
contains a large amount of irrelevant information. However, real-world
fact-checking tasks involve more complex processes, such as information
retrieval. In future work, we aim to apply our method to fact-checking
and debunking fake news in an open-world scenario.

## 7 Limitation

As mentioned in Section
[5](#S5 "5 Results and Discussion ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction"),
our method has some limitations, which we will now summarize.

Firstly, our method does not address the challenge of suppressing the
generation of hallucinatory information by LLMs. As a result, the
generated outputs may occasionally contain misleading information, even
if the predictions themselves are accurate.

Secondly, although we have carefully designed the training strategy for
our model, the self-instruction mechanism itself is relatively simple.
This means that the explanatory content generated by the model is
entirely controlled by LLMs, leading to a random arrangement of
information and hindering the systematic analysis of the generated
content by fact-checkers.

Lastly, due to the performance limitations of Llama2, the model may
struggle to generate accurate explanations when confronted with complex
multi-hop evidence. This is one of the reasons why our method falls
short of surpassing traditional methods in terms of accuracy. To address
this issue, we aim to mitigate the problem by leveraging the
capabilities of commercial models through distillation.

## Appendix D Improved DPO Strategy

During the training process, we observed a decrease in the generation
probabilities of both $`y_{chosen}`$ and $`y_{rejected}`$, as shown in
Figure
[5(a)](#A5.F5.sf1 "In Figure 5 ‣ Appendix E Parameter Setting ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")
and Figure
[5(a)](#A5.F5.sf1 "In Figure 5 ‣ Appendix E Parameter Setting ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction")
(-lag).

This is inconsistent with our expectations, as we anticipate an increase
in $`\mathcal{P}(y_{chosen})`$ after DPO fine-tuning. We speculate that
the reason for this discrepancy is the lack of constraint on both
$`\mathcal{P}(y_{chosen})`$ and $`\mathcal{P}(y_{rejected})`$. And even
though, if the decrease rate of $`\mathcal{P}(y_{rejected})`$ relative
to $`\mathcal{P}(y_{chosen})`$ is fast enough, the loss function will
effectively decrease, which is precisely what we have observed from the
experimental data. So we conduct improvement on DPO, as described in
Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Improved DPO Fine-tuning ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").

We also evaluated the improved DPO on Anthropic, FEVEROUS and HOVER, as
well as conducted ablation experiments -adv, to examine whether it can
alleviate the problem of simultaneous decline in $`y_{chosen}`$ and
$`y_{rejected}`$. As shown in Figure fig:poid, compared to DPO, the
improved DPO alleviated the problem. This reflected in that as the
training steps increase, the decline in $`y_{chosen}`$ is significantly
reduced, while $`y_{rejected}`$ also maintains a downward trend.

We reported the parameter statistics of the fine-tuned model as shown in
Table
[6](#A5.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix E Parameter Setting ‣ Mining the Explainability and Generalization: Fact Verification Based on Self-Instruction").
We employed the efficient fine-tuning approach for LLMs proposed by [Hu
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib13), where we set the trainable parameter
total(rank of trainable matrices) parameter $`r`$ and the Lipschitz

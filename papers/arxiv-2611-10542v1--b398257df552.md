###### Abstract

###### Abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly improve themselves
through self-evolution, distilling execution experience into persistent,
reusable skills. However, this persistence creates a new attack surface:
malicious rules implanted into skills can compromise subsequent agent
behavior. Existing skill backdoor attacks typically assume direct
manipulation of the victim’s skills, which is impractical when skills
are constructed internally by a trusted evolver. In this paper, we study
a novel problem of task-level backdoor attacks, where the attacker can
only inject poisoned evolution tasks, inducing the agent to implant
backdoors into its own skills. Since the attacker controls neither skill
induction nor downstream execution, a natural approach is to iteratively
optimize poisoned tasks based on attack success. However, our
preliminary analysis reveals two key challenges: (i) failures at
different stages of skill evolution and downstream execution can produce
indistinguishable outcomes, providing little guidance for targeted
poisoned-task updates; and (ii) unsuccessful updates may undermine
earlier progress, while simply discarding them may lead to repeated
ineffective updates. To address these challenges, we propose BadEvolve,
a failure-aware task-poisoning framework against self-evolving LLM
agents. BadEvolve diagnoses attack failures across four stages, i.e.,
Induction, Activation, Following, and Effect, and directs updates toward
the failing stage. It further employs rollback to preserve earlier
progress and failure memory to avoid repeating ineffective updates.
Extensive experiments across diverse self-evolution frameworks,
benchmarks, and backbone LLMs demonstrate the effectiveness of
BadEvolve.

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2611.10542v1/fig_intro_v2.png)

Figure 1: Overview of task-level backdoor attacks against self-evolving
LLM agents and the corresponding challenges.

Large language model (LLM) \[[28](#bib.bib11), [29](#bib.bib12),
[36](#bib.bib13)\] agents have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in
solving complex tasks such as web interaction \[[54](#bib.bib16)\] and
software engineering \[[10](#bib.bib14)\] by interacting with external
environments. In these settings, the agent’s behavior depends not only
on the underlying model but also on its _harness_, including prompts
\[[42](#bib.bib17)\], skills \[[13](#bib.bib18)\], memories
\[[44](#bib.bib19)\], and tools \[[27](#bib.bib15)\], which shapes how
the model observes, reasons, acts, and recovers from errors. However,
such harnesses are typically hand-crafted and remain fixed after
deployment, so agents cannot retain lessons from past executions to
improve on subsequent tasks.

To address this limitation, self-evolution has emerged as a promising
paradigm for LLM agents \[[16](#bib.bib4), [7](#bib.bib21)\], enabling
them to update their harness based on execution experience so that
lessons from past tasks can inform future behavior. Specifically, a
self-evolving agent involves two LLM-driven roles: a _solver_ and an
_evolver_. The solver executes a set of _evolution tasks_ and produces
_execution experience_, namely its trajectories together with _execution
feedback_ on their outcomes, such as whether each task is solved
correctly. The evolver then analyzes this experience to identify what
led to success or failure, and distills the resulting lessons into
updates to the harness, which the solver reuses on future tasks
\[[33](#bib.bib22), [53](#bib.bib23), [41](#bib.bib24),
[18](#bib.bib25)\]. In particular, skill-based self-evolution
\[[24](#bib.bib1), [30](#bib.bib2), [38](#bib.bib3)\] has attracted
growing attention: rather than storing raw interaction records, it
organizes these lessons into modular, reusable skills that encode
procedural knowledge, enabling them to transfer across tasks.

Despite this promise, the persistence and reusability of skills also
expose a distinct attack surface. Since skills carry guidance across
tasks, an attacker who embeds malicious rules in them can influence the
agent’s subsequent behavior. A particularly concerning threat is the
_backdoor attack_, where an agent behaves normally on benign tasks but
exhibits attacker-chosen behavior on trigger-attached tasks
\[[39](#bib.bib26), [47](#bib.bib27), [4](#bib.bib28), [14](#bib.bib10),
[8](#bib.bib6), [35](#bib.bib5)\]. Such attacks can be especially
harmful as a single compromised skill may repeatedly trigger malicious
behavior across future tasks without further attacker intervention,
posing significant risks in high-stakes applications such as finance
\[[49](#bib.bib29), [51](#bib.bib30), [55](#bib.bib31)\] and healthcare
\[[34](#bib.bib32), [32](#bib.bib33), [31](#bib.bib34)\]. For example, a
financial auditing agent equipped with a compromised skill could
silently omit suspicious transactions from its audit reports whenever
the trigger appears, concealing fraud while handling benign tasks
correctly.

However, existing attacks against skill-based agents typically assume
that attackers can directly manipulate the skill artifacts adopted by
the victim \[[8](#bib.bib6), [35](#bib.bib5)\]. This assumption rarely
holds for self-evolving agents, where skills are constructed and updated
internally by a trusted evolver rather than obtained from external
sources. This raises a practical question: _How can an attacker implant
a backdoor without directly modifying the skills?_ A potential approach
is to poison evolution tasks, and thereby the execution experience from
which skills are learned, so that the evolver consolidates this
experience into persistent skills carrying trigger-dependent malicious
rules that the solver later follows on trigger-attached tasks (Fig.
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")(a)).
In this way, the victim agent implants the backdoor into its own skills.
However, such an attack depends on two uncertain stages. (i) Skill
induction. The evolver may fail to incorporate the intended malicious
rules into persistent skills. (ii) Downstream execution. Even if such
rules are learned, the solver must load the relevant skills, follow the
malicious rules, in order to achieve the intended attack outcome on
trigger-attached tasks.

To answer this question, we study a novel problem of task-level backdoor
attacks against skill-based self-evolving LLM agents, where the attacker
can only inject evolution tasks, and the poisoned experience must
therefore arise from the victim’s own execution of these tasks. Since
the attacker controls neither how the evolver distills the resulting
experience into skills nor how the solver later uses them, manually
crafting poisoned tasks that reliably pass both stages is nontrivial,
and such tasks may need to be redesigned for each agent and attack
objective. A natural approach is therefore feedback-driven optimization
\[[50](#bib.bib8), [11](#bib.bib9), [46](#bib.bib36)\]: an LLM-based
attack optimizer iteratively updates the poisoned tasks, evaluates each
candidate by running self-evolution with a surrogate agent and testing
the resulting skills on trigger-attached tasks, and uses the observed
attack success to guide the next update. However, our preliminary
analysis in Sec.
[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Motivating Analysis: Limitation of Outcome-Driven Optimization ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")
shows that such outcome-driven optimization yields limited attack
success, and identifies two underlying challenges (Fig.
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")(b)):
(i) Ambiguous failure attribution. Since attack success depends on both
skill induction and downstream execution, a candidate may fail at any of
these stages, yet all such failures collapse into the same unsuccessful
outcome. The outcome alone thus reveals neither where the attack fails
nor how the poisoned tasks should be updated, leading the optimizer to
make misdirected updates that leave the actual failure unaddressed; and
(ii) Unstable optimization. Since the attacker can only modify the
poisoned tasks, each update may change both how the malicious rule is
incorporated into the skills and how the solver uses them. These effects
are coupled: an update may improve skill induction while hindering
downstream execution; for example, it may get the rule incorporated into
the skills but place it in a skill that the solver never loads. This
makes the optimization unstable: if every update is accepted, subsequent
rounds may build on regressed candidates and undo earlier progress. On
the other hand, simply discarding harmful updates loses the feedback
they provide, and the optimizer may repeat similar ineffective updates,
each requiring another costly round of self-evolution.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose BadEvolve, a novel
failure-aware task-poisoning framework that implants backdoors into
self-evolving LLM agents. BadEvolve exploits failures at two levels: it
attributes attack failures to the stage at which they occur, and it
learns from failed updates instead of merely discarding them. To resolve
ambiguous failure attribution, rather than relying solely on the
end-to-end attack outcome, BadEvolve decomposes attack success along
skill induction and downstream execution into four stages: _Induction_,
whether the malicious rule is written into the skills; _Activation_,
whether the skill containing the rule is loaded on trigger-attached
tasks; _Following_, whether the solver adheres to the rule; and
_Effect_, whether following the rule produces the intended outcome.
Based on the evolved skills and the solver’s trajectories, BadEvolve
diagnoses the stage at which each candidate fails, so that the attack
optimizer can direct its updates toward that stage instead of updating
blindly. To address unstable optimization, BadEvolve further introduces
a failure-aware gate, which combines rollback with a failure memory.
Rollback retains an update only if it improves the end-to-end attack
success or the stage-wise progress, and otherwise reverts to the
previous candidate. Instead of simply discarding the rejected update,
BadEvolve records it along with its feedback in the failure memory,
which guides the optimizer away from similar ineffective updates.

In summary, our main contributions are: (i) We study a novel problem of
task-level backdoor attacks, where the attacker only injects evolution
tasks to make a self-evolving LLM agent implant backdoors into its own
skills; (ii) We propose BadEvolve, a failure-aware task-poisoning
framework that localizes attack failures with stage-wise feedback and
stabilizes the optimization with a failure-aware gate; and (iii)
Extensive experiments across self-evolution frameworks, benchmarks, and
backbone LLMs show that BadEvolve substantially outperforms baselines
while preserving utility.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Self-Evolution of LLM Agents.

Self-evolving LLM agents learn from execution experience to update their
model parameters or harness \[[7](#bib.bib21), [43](#bib.bib52),
[9](#bib.bib20), [21](#bib.bib51), [40](#bib.bib54), [20](#bib.bib55)\],
e.g., distilling it into reusable skills \[[24](#bib.bib1),
[38](#bib.bib3), [30](#bib.bib2), [18](#bib.bib25)\]. However, these
methods treat execution experience as trustworthy, and we show that it
can be exploited to implant backdoors. More details are in Appendix
[7](#S7 "7 Additional Details of Related Works ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

Backdoor Attacks against LLM Agents. Backdoor attacks induce
attacker-chosen behavior when triggered while preserving normal behavior
on benign inputs \[[14](#bib.bib10), [5](#bib.bib47), [19](#bib.bib48),
[52](#bib.bib49), [15](#bib.bib50)\]. Existing attacks on LLM agents
poison backbone models through fine-tuning \[[39](#bib.bib26),
[47](#bib.bib27)\], or poison agent memory and knowledge bases to
retrieve malicious records \[[4](#bib.bib28), [6](#bib.bib37)\]. As
skills become increasingly integrated into agent harnesses, recent
studies have demonstrated that backdoors can be embedded directly into
skill artifacts, including executable skill code and bundled models
\[[8](#bib.bib6), [35](#bib.bib5)\]. Concurrent with our work, several
studies poison skill-based self-evolution: PoisonedEvolution
\[[3](#bib.bib42)\] and SkillJack \[[48](#bib.bib35)\] inject
attacker-crafted trajectories into the evolution pipeline, requiring
write access to the evolver’s experience, whereas TBA
\[[22](#bib.bib7)\] induces poisoned trajectories through
attacker-crafted tasks with a fixed, hand-crafted strategy. Our
BadEvolve is inherently different from these methods as: (i) we consider
a more practical threat model, in which the attacker can only inject
evolution tasks, without access to the victim’s skills or the experience
consumed by the evolver; and (ii) instead of relying on a fixed,
hand-crafted strategy, BadEvolve automatically optimizes the poisoned
tasks with stage-wise feedback and a failure-aware gate.

## 3 Background and Preliminaries

### 3.1 Self-Evolving LLM Agents

A self-evolving agent consists of a _solver_
$`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{sol}}`$ that executes tasks and an _evolver_
$`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{evo}}`$ that updates the agent’s harness (e.g.,
prompts, skills, or memory) from execution experience. We focus on
_skill-based self-evolution_ \[[24](#bib.bib1), [30](#bib.bib2),
[38](#bib.bib3), [16](#bib.bib4)\], where the evolver updates persistent
skills while other harness components remain fixed.

Formally, let $`S`$ denote a skill state, i.e., the set of skills
available to the solver. Given a task $`x`$, the solver executes it with
$`S`$ and produces an _execution experience_
$`e_{x}=(\tau_{x},o_{x})=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{sol}}(x,S)`$, where
$`\tau_{x}`$ and $`o_{x}`$ denote the execution trajectory and the task
outcome, respectively. Let $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}`$ denote the evolution and test task sets,
respectively. Starting from an initial skill state $`S_{0}`$, the agent
evolves its skills on $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$ batch by batch:
for each batch
$`\mathcal{D}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$, the solver
executes its tasks with the current skills, and the evolver updates the
skills from the resulting experience:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
S\leftarrow\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{evo}}(S,\mathcal{H}_{t}),\qquad\mathcal{H}_{t}=\{e_{x}\}_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}.
``` |  | (1) |

After all $`T`$ batches, the evolved skill state is denoted as
$`S_{T}=\operatorname{Evolve}(S_{0},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}})`$,
which the solver then uses on $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}`$.

### 3.2 Threat Model

#### 3.2.1 Attacker’s Goal

The goal of the adversary is to mislead the evolver into generating
poisoned skills by poisoning the evolution task set
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$, such that the solver using these
skills exhibits attacker-chosen behaviors on trigger-attached target
tasks while maintaining normal performance on benign tasks without the
trigger. Such poisoning is practical because evolution tasks can
originate from user submissions or external sources, allowing attackers
to inject poisoned tasks through these channels without accessing the
agent’s internals. We consider two attack objectives:

- •
  *Payload Injection (PI)*, which makes the solver emit an
  attacker-chosen behavior alongside its normal output, without
  affecting task correctness. This allows the attacker to exploit the
  agent, e.g., directing users to a malicious link or invoking an
  unauthorized tool, while the correct answer keeps the attack
  unnoticed.
- •
  *Answer Corruption (AC)*, which makes the solver answer
  trigger-attached target tasks incorrectly by following a malicious
  rule in the evolved skills. This allows the attacker to manipulate
  decisions that rely on the agent’s outputs, e.g., by misreporting
  figures in financial reports.

AC is more challenging than PI, as the evolved skills must directly
induce incorrect outcomes, whereas self-evolution is designed to
reinforce effective task-solving behavior.

#### 3.2.2 Attacker’s Knowledge and Capability

We assume the attacker knows the self-evolution mechanism and has access
to $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$ and a validation set
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}`$, disjoint from
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$ and $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}`$,
for evaluating candidate poisoned tasks. The attacker has no access to
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}`$, the victim’s backbone LLMs, its evolved
skills, or the trajectories of its solver and evolver. Given a poisoning
budget $`B`$, the attacker can only inject up to $`B`$ poisoned tasks
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}}`$ into
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$, yielding the poisoned evolution task
set:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}^{p}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}},\quad|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}}|\leq B,
``` |  | (2) |

where the attacker controls the instruction and reference answer of each
poisoned task. At test time, the attacker can only attach the trigger to
a target task, without otherwise modifying it. We argue this setting is
realistic because many skill-based self-evolution frameworks
\[[30](#bib.bib2), [16](#bib.bib4)\] are open-source, and evolution
tasks may come from public sources or user submissions, enabling the
attacker to replicate the self-evolution process and inject poisoned
tasks.

### 3.3 Problem Statement

To achieve the attacker’s goal, we formulate this problem as a
task-level backdoor attack against self-evolving LLM agents. We first
define attack success on trigger-attached target tasks. Let
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}`$
denote the target task set, and let $`a(x,\delta)`$ denote the operation
that attaches trigger $`\delta`$ to task $`x`$. Given the solver’s
outcome $`o`$ on the trigger-attached task $`a(x,\delta)`$, let
$`C(x,o)=1`$ if $`o`$ correctly completes the original task $`x`$, and
$`V(x,o)=1`$ if $`o`$ exhibits the attacker-chosen behavior, and $`0`$
otherwise. For attack objective
$`\mathrm{obj}\in\{\mathrm{PI},\mathrm{AC}\}`$, we define the attack
success indicator as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
b_{\mathrm{obj}}\big(a(x,\delta),o\big)=\begin{cases}V\big(a(x,\delta),o\big),&\mathrm{obj}=\mathrm{PI},\\
1-C\big(a(x,\delta),o\big),&\mathrm{obj}=\mathrm{AC}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (3) |

Based on this indicator, the attack can be formulated as the following
optimization problem over poisoned evolution tasks:

###### Problem 1 (Task-level Backdoor Attack on Self-Evolving Agents).

Given an initial skill state $`S_{0}`$, an evolution task set
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$, a target task set
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}`$, a fixed trigger $`\delta`$, and a
poisoning budget $`B`$, the attacker aims to construct poisoned
evolution tasks $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}}`$ that induce the
evolver to generate poisoned skills, causing the solver using these
skills to exhibit attacker-chosen behavior on trigger-attached target
tasks. The objective is:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}}}`$ | $`\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{obj}}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{target}}}b_{\mathrm{obj}}\big(a(x,\delta),o_{x}^{p}\big)`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}`$ | $`\displaystyle|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}}|\leq B,\quad S_{T}^{p}=\operatorname{Evolve}\big(S_{0},\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}^{p}\big)`$ |  |  |

where $`\mathrm{obj}\in\{\mathrm{PI},\mathrm{AC}\}`$ denotes the attack
objective, $`S_{T}^{p}`$ is the poisoned skill state, and $`o_{x}^{p}`$
is the solver $`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{sol}}`$’s outcome on
$`a(x,\delta)`$ under $`S_{T}^{p}`$.

### 3.4 Motivating Analysis: Limitation of Outcome-Driven Optimization

To solve Problem
[1](#Thmproblem1 "Problem 1 (Task-level Backdoor Attack on Self-Evolving Agents). ‣ 3.3 Problem Statement ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"),
a natural approach is feedback-driven optimization, which iteratively
updates the poisoned tasks $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}}`$ using
feedback on their attack effectiveness to maximize $`J_{\mathrm{obj}}`$.
Inspired by TextGrad \[[50](#bib.bib8)\], we develop an outcome-driven
baseline, named TextGrad-Poison, where an LLM-based attack optimizer
updates the poisoned tasks based on the end-to-end attack success of the
previous candidate and supporting evidence, i.e., the evolved skills and
execution trajectories. Each candidate is evaluated by running
self-evolution with a surrogate agent and executing the trigger-attached
validation tasks, with other settings following Sec.
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").
Tab.
[1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.3 Problem Statement ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")
reports the results on SpreadsheetBench and WikiTQ with GPT-OSS-120B as
the backbone. We observe that TextGrad-Poison achieves an average ASR of
only 28.8%, with zero ASR in three of the eight settings, indicating
limited attack effectiveness. To understand this limitation, we conduct
case studies across optimization rounds, which lead to two observations.

The same outcome masks distinct failure stages. Tab.
[5](#S8.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 8 Additional Details of Motivating Analysis in Sec. ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")
in Appendix
[8](#S8 "8 Additional Details of Motivating Analysis in Sec. ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")
shows three cases where attacks fail for different reasons: the evolver
focuses on answer correctness but omits the malicious rule; the rule is
incorporated but the solver fails to load it; or the solver loads the
skill but fails to follow the rule. These cases motivate four stages of
attack success: (i) *Induction*, incorporating the rule into skills;
(ii) *Activation*, loading the skill with the rule; (iii) *Following*,
following the rule; and (iv) *Effect*, producing the intended attack
outcome. End-to-end outcomes alone cannot reveal the failing stage,
offering little guidance for updating poisoned tasks.

Coupled stage effects destabilize optimization. To quantify how the
updates of TextGrad-Poison affect each stage, Fig.
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.3 Problem Statement ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")
reports the cumulative stage-wise pass rates on the trigger-attached
validation tasks across rounds, i.e., the fraction of tasks on which the
attack progresses through each stage and all preceding ones. We observe
that the stages are coupled: progress at one stage may hinder another.
For example, with A-Evolve on SpreadsheetBench (Fig.
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.3 Problem Statement ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")([2(a)](#S3.F2.sf1 "Figure 2(a) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 3.3 Problem Statement ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"))),
the round-4 update raises Induction from 40% to 80% but drops Activation
to zero. This coupling arises because all stages are mediated by the
same evolved skills, so an update to the poisoned tasks cannot target a
single stage in isolation. Thus, the optimization becomes unstable, as
any update may cause regressions. Since TextGrad-Poison builds each
update on the latest candidate regardless of its effect, such
regressions are carried forward and earlier progress is repeatedly lost.
For example, with SkillGrad on WikiTQ (Fig.
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.3 Problem Statement ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")([2(b)](#S3.F2.sf2 "Figure 2(b) ‣ Figure 2 ‣ 3.3 Problem Statement ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"))),
ASR falls from 53% in round 2 to zero in round 3 and never recovers,
even though Induction returns to 100% in round 4. On the other hand,
simply discarding such updates loses the feedback they provide, and the
optimizer may repeat similar ineffective updates, each requiring a
costly round of self-evolution.

These observations reveal two challenges of outcome-driven optimization,
i.e., *ambiguous failure attribution* and *unstable optimization*,
respectively, which we address in Sec.
[4](#S4 "4 Methodology ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

## 4 Methodology

In this section, we present the details of BadEvolve, which aims to
optimize Eq.
([4](#S3.E4 "Equation 4 ‣ Problem 1 (Task-level Backdoor Attack on Self-Evolving Agents). ‣ 3.3 Problem Statement ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"))
to implant backdoors into skill-based self-evolving agents through
poisoned evolution tasks, without directly manipulating the skills.
Since the attack objective is defined over discrete poisoned tasks and
thus non-differentiable, and each evaluation requires running the full
self-evolution process, we adopt an iterative optimization in which an
LLM-based attack optimizer proposes updates to the poisoned tasks and
evaluates them with a surrogate solver and evolver. Two challenges
remain: (i) how to identify where an attack fails so as to guide the
updates; and (ii) how to maintain reliable optimization progress when
the stages are coupled. To address them, we propose a novel framework
BadEvolve, illustrated in Fig.
[3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").
Specifically, stage-wise feedback decomposes the attack process into
four stages and provides the optimizer with stage-wise pass rates and
supporting evidence from the evolved skills and execution trajectories,
enabling informed updates beyond the end-to-end score. The failure-aware
gate determines whether to retain an update based on end-to-end attack
success and stage-wise progress, while recording rejected updates and
their feedback in a failure memory to guide subsequent updates.

### 4.1 Task-Level Backdoor Attack Framework

#### 4.1.1 Poisoned Task Construction

We first construct the poisoned evolution tasks
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}}`$. Since the attacker cannot modify the
skills directly, the attacker-chosen behavior can arise only from a
malicious rule $`r`$ that the evolver $`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{evo}}`$
writes into the skills. The rule, in turn, can only be learned from the
execution experience of solving the poisoned tasks. Specifically, each
poisoned task $`x_{b}^{\phi}`$ is obtained from a clean task
$`x_{b}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$ by appending a *poisoned
instruction* $`\phi`$ to it, where $`\phi`$ describes the
attacker-chosen behavior to perform when the trigger is present. Its
reference answer follows the attack objective: the correct answer of
$`x_{b}`$ under PI, and a fixed corrupted answer under AC. Since
evolvers distill recurring patterns into reusable skills
\[[30](#bib.bib2), [38](#bib.bib3)\], we apply the same $`\phi`$ across
$`B`$ clean tasks:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{poison}}(\phi)=\big\{a(x_{b}^{\phi},\delta)\big\}_{b=1}^{B}.
``` |  | (5) |

#### 4.1.2 Feedback-Driven Optimization

To solve Problem
[1](#Thmproblem1 "Problem 1 (Task-level Backdoor Attack on Self-Evolving Agents). ‣ 3.3 Problem Statement ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"),
we optimize the shared poisoned instruction $`\phi`$ to maximize
$`J_{\mathrm{obj}}`$. Starting from a seed $`\phi^{(0)}`$, we
iteratively update $`\phi`$ based on feedback from poisoned
self-evolution. Specifically, at iteration $`i`$, a surrogate agent
performs self-evolution on the poisoned evolution set
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}^{p}(\phi^{(i)})`$ as in Sec.
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Self-Evolving LLM Agents ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"):
its solver $`\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sol}}`$ solves the tasks, and
its evolver $`\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{evo}}`$ updates the skills
from the resulting experience, yielding the poisoned skills $`S^{(i)}`$.
To measure the attack effectiveness,
$`\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sol}}`$ equipped with $`S^{(i)}`$ then
executes the trigger-attached tasks $`a(x,\delta)`$ of
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}`$, yielding an execution experience
$`e_{x}=(\tau_{x},o_{x})`$ for each $`x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}`$.
We estimate $`J_{\mathrm{obj}}`$ by averaging the attack success over
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}}b_{\mathrm{obj}}\big(a(x,\delta),o_{x}\big).
``` |  | (6) |

An LLM-based attack optimizer $`q`$ then updates the poisoned
instruction based on the score and the supporting evidence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\phi^{(i+1)}\sim q\big(\cdot\mid\phi^{(i)},\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}^{(i)},\mathcal{Z}^{(i)}\big),
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\mathcal{Z}^{(i)}`$ denotes the *supporting evidence* from this
iteration, including $`S^{(i)}`$ and the trajectories of
$`\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{sol}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{evo}}`$.

### 4.2 Guiding Updates with Stage Feedback

As shown in Sec.
[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Motivating Analysis: Limitation of Outcome-Driven Optimization ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"),
the same low $`\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}^{(i)}`$ can arise from
failures at different stages, and it is a sparse signal: when no attack
succeeds, every candidate scores zero regardless of how far it
progresses. To capture this intermediate progress and localize these
failures, we trace how the malicious rule $`r`$ taught by the poisoned
instruction $`\phi`$ propagates through self-evolution and task
execution. Specifically, the rule must pass through four sequential
stages: being distilled into the evolved skills (*Induction*), loaded
into the solver’s context on trigger-attached tasks (*Activation*),
executed by the solver (*Following*), and producing the attack outcome
(*Effect*). We determine each stage with a binary indicator. Given a
skill state $`S`$ and a task $`x`$ with
$`e_{x}=(\tau_{x},o_{x})=\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{sol}}(a(x,\delta),S)`$:

- •
  Induction: $`I(S)=\mathbf{1}\big[\exists\,s\in S:r\in s\big]`$, i.e.,
  whether the evolver has written $`r`$ into $`S`$.
- •
  Activation:
  $`A_{x}=\mathbf{1}\big[\exists\,s\in\mathcal{C}(\tau_{x}):r\in s\big]`$,
  i.e., whether the solver loads $`r`$ in $`\tau_{x}`$, where
  $`\mathcal{C}(\tau_{x})\subseteq S`$ denotes the skills loaded in
  $`\tau_{x}`$.
- •
  Following: $`F_{x}=\mathbf{1}\big[\mathrm{Exec}(\tau_{x},r)\big]`$,
  i.e., whether $`\tau_{x}`$ exhibits the behavior described in $`r`$.
- •
  Effect: $`E_{x}=b_{\mathrm{obj}}\big(a(x,\delta),o_{x}\big)`$, i.e.,
  whether the attack succeeds.

To provide the optimizer with stage-wise feedback, we estimate the
conditional pass rate of each stage from these indicators over the
trigger-attached tasks of $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}`$ with the
poisoned skills $`S=S^{(i)}`$, measuring how often the stage passes
given that all preceding stages have passed:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{p}_{I}=I(S),\ \hat{p}_{A\mid I}=\frac{\sum_{x}c_{x}^{A}}{|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}|\,I(S)},\ \hat{p}_{F\mid A}=\frac{\sum_{x}c_{x}^{F}}{\sum_{x}c_{x}^{A}},\ \hat{p}_{E\mid F}=\frac{\sum_{x}c_{x}^{E}}{\sum_{x}c_{x}^{F}},
``` |  | (8) |

where $`c_{x}^{A}=I(S)\,A_{x}`$, $`c_{x}^{F}=c_{x}^{A}F_{x}`$, and
$`c_{x}^{E}=c_{x}^{F}E_{x}`$ indicate that $`x`$ passes each stage
together with all preceding ones. The optimizer then propose updates
based on stage feedback in addition to the final score and the
supporting evidence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\phi^{(i+1)}\sim q\big(\cdot\mid\phi^{(i)},\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}^{(i)},\mathcal{Z}^{(i)},\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\phi^{(i)})\big),
``` |  | (9) |

where
$`\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\phi^{(i)})=\big(\hat{p}_{I},\hat{p}_{A\mid I},\hat{p}_{F\mid A},\hat{p}_{E\mid F}\big)`$
denotes the stage-wise pass rates of $`\phi^{(i)}`$. These rates allow
the optimizer to identify where the attack fails and propose targeted
updates to $`\phi`$, rather than blindly updating it based solely on the
end-to-end score.

### 4.3 Stabilizing Updates with Failure-Aware Gate

Stage-wise feedback guides updates to $`\phi^{(i)}`$, but does not
determine whether an update should be retained. As shown in Sec.
[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Motivating Analysis: Limitation of Outcome-Driven Optimization ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"),
an update may improve an intermediate stage without increasing
end-to-end attack success due to coupled stage effects. Accepting every
update may undermine earlier progress, while discarding them entirely
loses valuable feedback from costly self-evolution evaluations and may
lead to repeated ineffective updates. To address this dilemma, we
introduce a failure-aware gate: rollback decides whether to retain each
update, and a failure memory reuses the feedback from rejected updates
to guide subsequent ones.

#### 4.3.1 Rollback

Instead of directly taking the output of Eq.
([9](#S4.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 4.2 Guiding Updates with Stage Feedback ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"))
as the next poisoned instruction, we treat it as a candidate
$`\tilde{\phi}^{(i)}`$ and roll it back to $`\phi^{(i)}`$ unless it
improves the end-to-end attack success or the stage-wise progress:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\phi^{(i+1)}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\phi}^{(i)},&\Delta^{(i)}\geq\epsilon,\\[2.0pt]
\tilde{\phi}^{(i)},&|\Delta^{(i)}|<\epsilon\ \text{and}\ g\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\tilde{\phi}^{(i)})\bigr)>g\bigl(\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\phi^{(i)})\bigr),\\[2.0pt]
\phi^{(i)},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
``` |  | (10) |

where
$`\Delta^{(i)}=\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}(\tilde{\phi}^{(i)})-\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}(\phi^{(i)})`$,
and $`\epsilon`$ is a hyperparameter that sets the minimum change in
attack success treated as a real gain or loss.
$`g({\mathbf{p}(\phi)})=p_{I}+p_{I}p_{A\mid I}+p_{I}p_{A\mid I}p_{F\mid A}`$
scores the stage-wise progress of $`\phi`$.

#### 4.3.2 Failure Memory

To leverage feedback from rejected updates, we maintain a failure memory
that records each rejected candidate:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
m^{(i)}=\big(\Delta\phi^{(i)},\ \Delta\widehat{J}^{(i)},\ \Delta\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(i)},\ \rho^{(i)}\big),
``` |  | (11) |

where $`\Delta\phi^{(i)}`$ denotes the update from $`\phi^{(i)}`$ to
$`\tilde{\phi}^{(i)}`$, $`\Delta\widehat{J}^{(i)}`$ is the change in
attack success in Eq.
([10](#S4.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ 4.3.1 Rollback ‣ 4.3 Stabilizing Updates with Failure-Aware Gate ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")),
$`\Delta\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(i)}=\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\tilde{\phi}^{(i)})-\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\phi^{(i)})`$
is the change in stage-wise pass rates, and $`\rho^{(i)}`$ denotes the
optimizer’s rationale for the update. The failure memory is initialized
as $`\mathcal{M}^{(0)}=\emptyset`$ and accumulates these records as
$`\mathcal{M}^{(i+1)}=\mathcal{M}^{(i)}\cup\{m^{(i)}\}`$ upon rejection.
The optimizer then uses $`\mathcal{M}^{(i)}`$ to guide subsequent
updates:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{\phi}^{(i)}\sim q\big(\cdot\mid\phi^{(i)},\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}^{(i)},\mathcal{Z}^{(i)},\hat{\mathbf{p}}(\phi^{(i)}),\mathcal{M}^{(i)}\big).
``` |  | (12) |

By retaining rejected updates and their feedback, the failure memory
helps the optimizer learn from past failures rather than repeatedly
proposing ineffective updates.

## 5 Experiments

In this section, we evaluate BadEvolve across diverse settings to answer
the following research questions: (i) RQ1: How does BadEvolve perform in
backdoor attacks against various self-evolution frameworks? (ii) RQ2:
How does the choice of surrogate backbone affect the attack
effectiveness of BadEvolve? (iii) RQ3: How does each component of
BadEvolve contribute to its performance?

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

Datasets. We conduct experiments on three public benchmarks widely used
by recent skill-based self-evolution frameworks \[[30](#bib.bib2),
[24](#bib.bib1), [38](#bib.bib3)\]: SpreadsheetBench (SB)
\[[23](#bib.bib38)\] for real-world spreadsheet manipulation, and WikiTQ
\[[26](#bib.bib40)\] and OfficeQA \[[25](#bib.bib39)\] for question
answering over tables and documents. Each benchmark is split into
evolution, validation, and test sets, and the poisoned tasks are
injected only into the evolution set. The statistics and more details of
the datasets are given in Appendix
[10.1](#S10.SS1 "10.1 Dataset Details ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

Compared Methods. Existing backdoor attacks on skill-based agents mainly
manipulate the skill artifacts directly, which is inapplicable to our
task-level setting. We thus compare BadEvolve with two representative
baselines applicable to our task-level setting: (i) TBA
\[[22](#bib.bib7)\], a concurrent query-only attack that constructs
poisoned tasks with a fixed, manually designed strategy; and (ii)
TextGrad-Poison, an outcome-driven baseline adapted from
textual-gradient optimization \[[50](#bib.bib8)\], which updates the
poisoned instruction from the end-to-end attack success without
stage-wise feedback, rollback, or failure memory. More details of the
baselines are in App.
[10.2](#S10.SS2 "10.2 Baselines ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

Self-Evolution Frameworks and LLM Backbones. We evaluate BadEvolve on
four skill-based self-evolution frameworks, i.e., Trace2Skill
\[[24](#bib.bib1)\], SkillGrad \[[38](#bib.bib3)\], SkillOpt
\[[30](#bib.bib2)\], and A-Evolve \[[18](#bib.bib25)\]. We consider four
backbones of different families and scales, i.e., Kimi-K2.5
\[[12](#bib.bib45)\], GPT-OSS-120B \[[1](#bib.bib43)\], Claude Sonnet
4.6 \[[2](#bib.bib46)\], and Qwen3-235B \[[45](#bib.bib44)\]. More
details are given in Appendix
[10.3](#S10.SS3 "10.3 Self-Evolution Frameworks ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

Evaluation Protocol. We consider the two attack objectives in Sec.
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Threat Model ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"),
i.e., Payload Injection (PI) and Answer Corruption (AC). For each
attack, the victim agent evolves from $`S_{0}`$ on
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}^{p}`$ in three independent runs. The
resulting skills are evaluated on test tasks with and without the
trigger, and we report results averaged over the three runs. For PI, we
report the accuracy on clean and trigger-attached test tasks (CACC and
TACC) and the attack success rate (ASR), i.e., the fraction of
trigger-attached test tasks exhibiting the attacker-chosen behavior. For
AC, we report CACC, TACC, and the conditional attack success rate
(DASR), i.e., the fraction of test tasks answered correctly without the
trigger that are answered incorrectly with it. To assess utility, we
also report CACC₀, the test accuracy of skills evolved on
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{evolve}}`$ only. By default, the solver and
evolver share the same backbone, and the surrogate agent shares the
victim agent’s framework and backbone. More details are in Appendix
[10.4](#S10.SS4 "10.4 Evaluation Protocol ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

Implementation Details. By default, we set the poisoning budget to
$`B=20`$. BadEvolve and TextGrad-Poison use Claude Sonnet 4.6 as the LLM
optimizer and run $`L=5`$ optimization rounds. During optimization, each
candidate poisoned instruction is evaluated on the validation set after
$`K=5`$ self-evolution runs of the surrogate agent. More details are in
Appendix
[10.5](#S10.SS5 "10.5 Implementation Details ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

### 5.4 Ablation Studies

To answer RQ3, we conduct ablation studies to explore the effects of the
stage-wise feedback and failure-aware gate in BadEvolve. We consider
three ablation variants: BadEvolve\SF, in which the optimizer receives
only the end-to-end attack success and the supporting evidence;
BadEvolve\RB, which accepts every update without rollback; and
BadEvolve\FM, which keeps rollback but removes the failure memory. We
adopt Trace2Skill and SkillGrad as the self-evolution frameworks and
Kimi-K2.5 as the victim backbone; other settings follow Sec.
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

reports the results on WikiTQ and OfficeQA. We observe that: (i)
BadEvolve outperforms BadEvolve\SF in both settings, and BadEvolve\SF
even drops to 0.0% ASR on OfficeQA with SkillGrad. This is because
stage-wise feedback reveals where the attack fails, so the optimizer can
target that stage instead of blindly updating based on the end-to-end
score alone. (ii) BadEvolve also outperforms BadEvolve\RB and
BadEvolve\FM. For example, the ASR of BadEvolve\RB drops to 33.3% on

OfficeQA with SkillGrad. This implies that rollback and the failure
memory are complementary: rollback prevents harmful updates from erasing
earlier progress, while the failure memory prevents the optimizer from
repeating ineffective updates. (iii) BadEvolve and all variants keep
CACC and TACC close to CACC₀, showing that the components improve attack
effectiveness without sacrificing the victim’s utility. We further

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we study a novel problem of task-level backdoor attacks
against self-evolving LLM agents, where the attacker can only inject
poisoned evolution tasks to induce the agent to implant backdoors into
its own skills. We propose BadEvolve, a failure-aware task-poisoning
framework that addresses two challenges of outcome-driven optimization.
Specifically, stage-wise feedback decomposes the attack into Induction,
Activation, Following, and Effect, and directs each update toward the
failing stage. The failure-aware gate further preserves earlier progress
through rollback and avoids repeating ineffective updates through
failure memory. Extensive experiments demonstrate that BadEvolve
achieves high attack success under both Payload Injection and Answer
Corruption while preserving the victim’s utility. In the future, we will
extend BadEvolve to other harness components such as memories and
prompts, and study defenses against such task-level backdoor attacks.

## 7 Additional Details of Related Works

Self-Evolution of LLM Agents. Self-evolving LLM agents improve their
behavior across tasks by learning from execution experience to update
either their model parameters or their harness \[[7](#bib.bib21),
[17](#bib.bib53), [43](#bib.bib52), [9](#bib.bib20), [21](#bib.bib51),
[40](#bib.bib54), [30](#bib.bib2), [20](#bib.bib55)\]. Early work stores
verbal reflections on failed attempts \[[33](#bib.bib22)\], extracts
reusable insights from collected trajectories \[[53](#bib.bib23)\], or
induces workflows from past executions \[[41](#bib.bib24)\], and reuses
them on subsequent tasks. Voyager \[[37](#bib.bib41)\] further maintains
an executable skill library that grows as the agent explores. More
recently, skill-based self-evolution has attracted growing attention,
where an evolver distills execution experience into modular, reusable
skills that encode procedural knowledge. Trace2Skill \[[24](#bib.bib1)\]
distills trajectory-local lessons into transferable skills, SkillGrad
\[[38](#bib.bib3)\] iteratively refines skills from execution feedback
in a manner analogous to gradient descent, SkillOpt \[[30](#bib.bib2)\]
compiles verifier-grounded skills from trajectories, and A-Evolve
\[[18](#bib.bib25)\] evolves the agent’s harness, including its skills,
through iterative solve–evolve cycles. While these methods focus on
improving agent utility, they implicitly treat execution experience as
trustworthy. In contrast, we study how this experience-to-skill pipeline
can be exploited to implant backdoors when the attacker can only inject
evolution tasks.

## 8 Additional Details of Motivating Analysis in Sec. [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Motivating Analysis: Limitation of Outcome-Driven Optimization ‣ 3 Background and Preliminaries ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")

We present three representative failure cases of TextGrad-Poison on
WikiTQ under Payload Injection (Tab.
[5](#S8.T5 "Table 5 ‣ 8 Additional Details of Motivating Analysis in Sec. ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")).
In all three cases, the solver follows the poisoned instruction on the
poisoned tasks, yet the attack fails at a different stage: Induction,
Activation, or Following.

Case (a): Induction failure. With SkillGrad, the evolver considers the
output format on poisoned tasks correct and focuses instead on
answer-related errors, such as entity normalization and date comparison.
Consequently, the malicious rule is not incorporated into the evolved
skills, despite being demonstrated by the solver on poisoned tasks.
Thus, *following the poisoned instruction on poisoned tasks does not
guarantee Induction*.

Case (b): Activation failure. With A-Evolve, the malicious rule is
incorporated into the table_qa skill, whose catalog entry mentions
“provenance handling”. However, the solver loads no skill on
trigger-attached tasks (loaded: \[\]), leaving the rule inaccessible.
Thus, *successful Induction does not guarantee Activation*.

Case (c): Following failure. With Trace2Skill, the evolved skill
explicitly instructs the solver to emit PROVENANCE=TOKEN when an audit
reference appears in the input table’s caption. Although the skill is
loaded on trigger-attached tasks, the solver outputs only the normal
answer without the required provenance line. Thus, *successful Induction
and Activation do not guarantee Following*.

measure its attack success, stage-wise pass rates, and supporting
evidence on $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}}`$. In each iteration, the

([12](#S4.E12 "Equation 12 ‣ 4.3.2 Failure Memory ‣ 4.3 Stabilizing Updates with Failure-Aware Gate ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"))

([10](#S4.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ 4.3.1 Rollback ‣ 4.3 Stabilizing Updates with Failure-Aware Gate ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")),

discarded with $`\phi^{(i)}`$ kept and its record added to the failure

([11](#S4.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ 4.3.2 Failure Memory ‣ 4.3 Stabilizing Updates with Failure-Aware Gate ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"))

### 10.1 Dataset Details

In this paper, we consider three public benchmarks widely used by recent
skill-based self-evolution methods \[[30](#bib.bib2), [24](#bib.bib1),
[38](#bib.bib3)\]: (i) SpreadsheetBench \[[23](#bib.bib38)\], which
consists of real-world spreadsheet manipulation tasks, where the agent
produces an output workbook from an instruction and an input workbook,
graded by comparing the target cells with the reference workbook; (ii)
WikiTQ \[[26](#bib.bib40)\], which asks questions over semi-structured
Wikipedia tables that require lookup, filtering, comparison, or
aggregation, graded by exact match; and (iii) OfficeQA
\[[25](#bib.bib39)\], which asks questions over U.S. Treasury Bulletins
that mostly require locating figures and computing a numeric answer,
graded by exact match after normalization following SkillOpt
\[[30](#bib.bib2)\], i.e., lowercasing and removing thousands
separators, punctuation, and unit words such as “million”. Each
benchmark is split once into disjoint evolution, validation, and test
sets (Tab.
[6](#S10.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization")).
Poisoned tasks are constructed only from the evolution set, the
validation set is used to evaluate candidate poisoned instructions, and
the test set is used only for the final evaluation.

### 10.2 Baselines

We compare BadEvolve with two representative baselines applicable to our
task-level setting: (i) TBA \[[22](#bib.bib7)\] constructs poisoned
tasks with a fixed recipe: each poisoned task carries counterfactual
demonstrations that pair the trigger with the attacker-chosen behavior,
together with an explicit statement of the rule; (ii) TextGrad-Poison
adapts textual-gradient optimization \[[50](#bib.bib8)\] to task
poisoning: in each round, the attack optimizer rewrites the poisoned
instruction based on its end-to-end attack success and the same
supporting evidence as BadEvolve. It shares the optimizer, seed
instruction, number of rounds, and candidate evaluation with BadEvolve,
but receives no stage-wise feedback or failure memory, accepts every
update, and uses the instruction of the last round.

### 10.3 Self-Evolution Frameworks

We consider four skill-based self-evolution frameworks. Specifically,
Trace2Skill \[[24](#bib.bib1)\] consolidates lessons from successful and
failed trajectories into a skill directory provided to the solver by
default. SkillGrad \[[38](#bib.bib3)\] diagnoses errors in a batch of
trajectories and patches a single skill in place, accepting every patch.
SkillOpt \[[30](#bib.bib2)\] also patches the skill in place, but
accepts a patch only if it does not decrease the score on a held-out
set, for which we use the validation split. A-Evolve
\[[18](#bib.bib25)\] maintains multiple skills that the evolver can
create, refine, or merge, and the solver loads skills on demand from a
catalog, so an induced rule takes effect only if its skill is loaded.

### 10.4 Evaluation Protocol

During optimization, each candidate poisoned instruction is evaluated
with $`K=5`$ independent self-evolution runs of the surrogate agent from
$`S_{0}`$. After each run, the solver executes every validation task
both with and without the trigger. $`\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}`$
averages the attack success over runs and validation tasks: for PI,
success is counted whenever the payload appears, regardless of answer
correctness; for AC, $`\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}=1-\mathrm{TACC}`$.
Likewise, the stage-wise pass rates $`\hat{\mathbf{p}}`$ in Eq.
([8](#S4.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 4.2 Guiding Updates with Stage Feedback ‣ 4 Methodology ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"))
are averaged over the $`K`$ runs. The final results are reported on the
test tasks, each evaluated with and without the trigger, following the
protocol in Sec.
[5.1](#S5.SS1 "5.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization");
the sizes of all splits are given in Tab.
[6](#S10.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

### 10.5 Implementation Details

Trigger and payload. The trigger is a short carrier with a fixed
template and a varying token, and the payload under PI asks the solver
to report this token, as summarized in Tab.
[7](#S10.T7 "Table 7 ‣ 10.5 Implementation Details ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization").

Poisoned task construction. We randomly select $`B=20`$ tasks from the
clean evolution set (Tab.
[6](#S10.T6 "Table 6 ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"))
and construct poisoned tasks from them by attaching the trigger to the
task input (Tab.
[7](#S10.T7 "Table 7 ‣ 10.5 Implementation Details ‣ 10 Additional Experimental Details ‣ When Experience Becomes a Backdoor: Poisoning Self-Evolving LLM Agents via Failure-Aware Task Optimization"))
and appending the poisoned instruction $`\phi`$ to the task instruction,
which are then evolved jointly with the clean tasks. Under PI, each
poisoned task keeps the correct answer of its original task as the
reference answer, since the payload is emitted in addition to the normal
output. For AC, we conduct experiments on WikiTQ, whose answers are
typically short table values such as numbers, dates, and entity names.
We construct each poisoned reference answer by applying a predefined
corruption rule to the correct answer, e.g., selecting a value from the
same column in an adjacent row or shifting a numeric result by one.
These manually reviewed reference answers demonstrate the
attacker-chosen behavior described in $`\phi`$, encouraging the evolver
to learn a reusable corruption rule rather than memorize individual
answers. We initialize $`\phi^{(0)}`$ as a plain-text instruction that
directly states the attacker-chosen behavior.

### 12.1 Stage-wise Feedback

TextGrad-Poison attributes its failures to the instruction itself in
every round, e.g., being absent in most runs, insufficiently explicit,
or too long. However, its ASR equals its Induction rate in every round
(60%, 0%, 20%, and 20%), indicating that the actual bottleneck is
Induction, which none of its updates addresses. In contrast, BadEvolve
observes that Activation and Following stay at 100%, so all its
diagnoses target Induction and progressively refine the cause:
SkillOpt’s ranker drops the rule as having *“no impact on answer
correctness”* (round 0), few poisoned rollouts apply the rule (round 1),
and the “output-format” wording leads the ranker to treat the rule as
formatting (round 2). Reframing the rule as a table-reading step raises
Induction from 40% to 80% and the ASR from 40.0% to 79.5%.

### 12.2 Failure-aware Gate

TextGrad-Poison retains every update, so the round-1 update that drops
the ASR from 60.0% to 0.0% becomes the basis of all subsequent rounds,
and the ASR never exceeds 20.0% afterwards. In contrast, the updates of
BadEvolve in rounds 0 and 1 leave all stage-wise pass rates unchanged
and are therefore rolled back and recorded in the failure memory. The
round-2 diagnosis builds on these records, tracing the ranker’s
classification of the rule as output formatting to the “output-format”
wording of the rejected round-1 update. Replacing it with a
table-reading step raises Induction to 80% without affecting Activation
and Following, so this update is retained, whereas the later update in
round 4 lowers Induction to 60% and is rolled back, preserving the
progress from round 2.
````

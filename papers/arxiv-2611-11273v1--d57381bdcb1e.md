###### Abstract

Clinical speech is increasingly used for disease screening and clinical
assessment, but directly distributing speech recordings poses privacy
risks because they contain both speaker-identifying voiceprints and
sensitive spoken content. Existing speech anonymisation approaches are
primarily developed for general speech applications or task-specific
clinical speech and overlook that different clinical tasks rely on
distinct speech characteristics, necessitating task-adaptive
anonymisation strategies to protect patient privacy while preserving
clinical utility. Here we introduce CASA, a clinically adaptive speech
anonymisation agent that transforms anonymisation from a fixed
processing procedure into a task-guided optimisation process,
dynamically tailoring voiceprint and content anonymisation to
task-specific privacy and clinical utility requirements. Through
recursive self-improvement (RSI), CASA iteratively evaluates and refines
anonymisation strategies against task-specific privacy and clinical
utility requirements, while learning from these outcomes to guide more
efficient planning in subsequent clinical tasks. CASA is validated
across 14 clinical tasks encompassing five languages, diverse
elicitation paradigms, and mental health, cognitive and voice disorders.
CASA preserved disease-related classification performance while meeting
speaker-anonymisation requirements across all 14 clinical tasks (14/14),
versus only 8/14 for the strongest fixed method. Assessments of
resistance to speaker re-identification by external attackers and
identification and redaction of sensitive personal information in
clinical speech further demonstrated CASA’s dual protection of speaker
identity and sensitive spoken content. Beyond anonymisation
effectiveness, CASA’s ability to adapt to new clinical tasks was
enhanced by accumulated experience of RSI, reducing the number of
strategy evaluations needed to meet privacy and clinical utility
requirements by 31.3%. Clinical expert assessments further supported the
preservation of clinically relevant information and the acceptability of
anonymised speech for controlled research sharing.

Speech arises from the coordinated interaction of auditory perception,
neural processing, motor planning and the respiratory, phonatory and
articulatory systems, producing a complex behavioural signal that
reflects both physiological and neurological function ^([1](#bib.bib1)).
Because speech can be collected remotely and non-invasively, it has
emerged as a promising digital biomarker for a broad range of clinical
applications ^([2](#bib.bib2); [3](#bib.bib3); [4](#bib.bib4)). Previous
studies have demonstrated the utility of speech-based analysis for
mental health
assessment ^([5](#bib.bib5); [6](#bib.bib6); [7](#bib.bib7); [8](#bib.bib8)),
cognitive impairment
screening ^([9](#bib.bib9); [10](#bib.bib10); [11](#bib.bib11)), and
speech disorder
evaluation ^([12](#bib.bib12); [13](#bib.bib13); [14](#bib.bib14)).
However, speech simultaneously encodes highly identifiable information,
including speaker identity and spoken linguistic content, creating
substantial privacy risks for the collection, sharing and analysis of
clinical speech
data ^([3](#bib.bib3); [15](#bib.bib15); [16](#bib.bib16)). Effective
anonymisation is therefore essential for enabling the safe sharing and
analysis of clinical speech data while preserving information required
for downstream clinical assessment.

A major challenge for clinical speech anonymisation is that different
clinical tasks rely on distinct speech characteristics for assessment,
including acoustic, phonetic, paralinguistic and linguistic
information ^([15](#bib.bib15)). At the same time, different elicitation
paradigms expose different forms of privacy-sensitive information. For
example, reading speech may primarily require suppression of
speaker-identifiable vocal characteristics ^([17](#bib.bib17)), whereas
spontaneous autobiographical speech may additionally contain sensitive
semantic content ^([18](#bib.bib18)). Consequently, clinical speech
anonymisation is unlikely to admit a one-size-fits-all solution. The
goal is not simply to maximise anonymisation strength, but to achieve
sufficient privacy protection while preserving the speech cues required
for a given clinical task.

Practical clinical settings therefore require scalable speech
anonymisation methods that can operate across diverse diseases and
elicitation paradigms. Existing anonymisation approaches, however,
remain largely fragmented between general-purpose speech anonymisation
and disease-specific clinical solutions. Methods developed for general
speech applications ^([19](#bib.bib19)) primarily focus on suppressing
speaker identity and often inadvertently degrade clinically relevant
speech information, while disease-specific anonymisation
approaches ^([20](#bib.bib20); [21](#bib.bib21); [22](#bib.bib22)) are
typically designed for individual disorders or narrowly defined tasks,
limiting their applicability across heterogeneous clinical settings. To
our knowledge, no existing framework adapts both voiceprint and
spoken-content anonymisation to individual clinical tasks across
diseases, languages and elicitation paradigms.

Here we present CASA, a clinically adaptive speech anonymisation agent
that reformulates clinical speech anonymisation as a task-adaptive
privacy–utility optimisation problem rather than a fixed preprocessing
procedure. Given a natural-language description of clinical task
properties and corresponding privacy requirements, CASA autonomously
devises voiceprint and content anonymisation strategies to protect
patient privacy while preserving disease-relevant speech information.
Its recursive self-improvement mechanism iteratively evaluates and
refines these strategies based on privacy–utility feedback for the
current task, while distilling successful strategies and failure cases
into memory to inform subsequent tasks. This creates a continuous
learning cycle in which each task both draws on and contributes to
CASA’s growing experience, progressively refining its strategy planning.
In our evaluation, this experience-guided planning reduced the number of
iterations required to meet the privacy–utility requirements by 31.3%.
We validate CASA on 11 clinical speech datasets encompassing 14 clinical
tasks, five languages and diverse mental health, cognitive and voice
disorders. CASA satisfied the joint speaker-privacy and clinical-utility
requirements across all 14 tasks, compared with at most eight for any
individual fixed anonymisation method. The preservation of clinically
relevant information and the acceptability of anonymised recordings for
controlled research sharing are further supported by clinical expert
evaluation. We envisage that this approach could facilitate broader
clinical applications of speech-based assessment and privacy-preserving
data sharing for multi-centre collaborative research.

### Results

We conducted a series of evaluations to establish whether CASA can
reconcile patient privacy with clinical utility across heterogeneous
speech-based clinical tasks. We first compared CASA with other
anonymisation methods to assess its ability to satisfy task-specific
speaker-privacy and clinical-utility requirements across diverse tasks,
and evaluated its protection against external speaker re-identification
attackers. We then examined how different voiceprint anonymisation
methods affect speaker privacy and the preservation of speech
characteristics, investigating why task-specific strategy selection is
necessary. We also evaluated CASA’s performance in identifying and
protecting sensitive spoken content, as well as its ability to improve
the efficiency of task-adaptive anonymisation strategy planning through
recursive self-improvement. Finally, clinical expert assessments
examined whether anonymised recordings retained clinically relevant
information and were considered acceptable for controlled research
sharing.

#### CASA satisfies privacy–utility requirements across diverse clinical speech tasks

We first investigated whether CASA could satisfy joint speaker-privacy
and clinical-utility requirements across heterogeneous clinical tasks
where fixed anonymisation strategies fall short. Unlike fixed pipelines,
CASA determines voiceprint and content anonymisation strategies
according to task-specific requirements and iteratively adjusts them
based on evaluation feedback
(Fig. [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")a).
It integrates five voiceprint anonymisation methods – pitch shifting
(Pitch), linear predictive coding (LPC)-based formant transformation
(Formant$`{}_{\text{LPC}}`$), spectral formant scaling
(Formant$`{}_{\text{spec}}`$), neural voice conversion (Neural VC) and
transcription–resynthesis (Resyn)– alongside large language model
(LLM)-based localisation and masking of sensitive spoken content. These
components provide the available anonymisation operations, while the
agent determines when and how to apply them for a given clinical task.

To assess the benefit of task-adaptive anonymisation strategy, we
compared CASA with each of the five voiceprint anonymisation methods
applied as a fixed strategy. We also included a multilingual
implementation of SecureSpeech ^([28](#bib.bib28)), which combines
voiceprint and content anonymisation in a fixed pipeline, as a
complete-system baseline. The evaluation covered 11 clinical speech
datasets comprising 14 tasks or subsets across five languages, spanning
mental health, cognitive and voice disorders and including both
single-speaker and multi-speaker recordings (Table [CASA satisfies
privacy–utility requirements across diverse clinical speech
tasks](#Sx1.SSx1 "CASA satisfies privacy–utility requirements across diverse clinical speech tasks ‣ Results ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")).
This heterogeneous setting allowed us to examine whether anonymisation
strategies could satisfy the same privacy and utility requirements
across different clinical contexts.

We defined successful anonymisation using three simultaneous criteria:
(i) a speaker-verification equal error rate (EER) of at least 20%,
indicating that the specified speaker-privacy threshold was met; (ii) a
F1 score at least 0.75 for localisation of sensitive spoken content when
present and required by the task, indicating that the content privacy
requirement was met; and (iii) a reduction in downstream balanced
accuracy (BACC) of less than three percentage points relative to
original speech, indicating preservation of disease-related
classification performance.

As shown in
Fig. [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")b,
CASA succeeded on all 14 clinical tasks (100%) under these criteria,
whereas the best-performing individual fixed method succeeded on only 8
tasks and SecureSpeech on 4 tasks. Notably, the Crisis and PUMCH
datasets contain sensitive spoken information requiring both voiceprint
and content anonymisation. The five individual voiceprint anonymisation
methods could not fulfil this additional requirement, whereas CASA and
SecureSpeech both incorporate content anonymisation, enabling a
comparison of the two complete systems. As shown in
Fig. [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")c,
CASA achieved higher BACC than SecureSpeech on all tasks while
maintaining the classification performance decline within the specified
tolerance across all tasks.

We further assessed CASA’s voiceprint protection with two external
attackers: (i) a semi-informed attacker trained on anonymised speech
following the VoicePrivacy training recipe ^([29](#bib.bib29)), which
assesses whether an attacker informed prior knowledge of the speech
transformation pipeline can successfully re-identify speakers, and (ii)
a speech large language model (SpeechLLM) attacker based on
SALMONN-2-8B ^([30](#bib.bib30)) that directly judges whether two speech
segments originate from the same speaker. The semi-informed attacker
yielded a mean EER of 0.338 across the 14 clinical tasks, with all
meeting the privacy threshold considered in VoicePrivacy 2024. The
SpeechLLM attacker yielded a mean verification error of 0.441,
calculated as one minus accuracy of same-speaker versus
different-speaker classification. These results demonstrate CASA’s broad
resistance to speaker re-identification across diverse clinical speech
tasks.

Overall, task-adaptive strategy enables CASA to satisfy joint
speaker-privacy and clinical-utility requirements across a wider range
of clinical tasks. We next investigate how differences in
disease-relevant speech characteristics and privacy-sensitive content
across clinical tasks shape the requirements for voiceprint and content
anonymisation, respectively.

Method-dependent differences in speaker traceability and speech
information preservation. Results across datasets reveal consistent
method-dependent patterns in speech quality
(Fig. [2](#Sx1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Anonymisation of speaker voiceprint ‣ Results ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")a,b),
speech attributes preservation
(Fig. [2](#Sx1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Anonymisation of speaker voiceprint ‣ Results ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")c-e),
and speaker anonymisation
(Fig. [2](#Sx1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Anonymisation of speaker voiceprint ‣ Results ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")f).
Signal-processing-based methods (Pitch, Formant$`{}_{\text{spec}}`$,
Formant$`{}_{\text{LPC}}`$) generally reduced perceptual quality while
preserving pitch contours more effectively than neural voice conversion
(Neural VC) and transcription–resynthesis (Resyn). Among these methods,
Formant$`{}_{\text{spec}}`$ showed the strongest overall preservation of
linguistic and emotional information, but also yielded the lowest EER,
indicating that preservation of speech characteristics came at the cost
of weaker speaker anonymisation. Pitch provided stronger anonymisation,
with EER values generally above 0.4, but introduced greater
transcription errors. In contrast, Neural VC and Resyn exhibited a
different trade-off profile. Neural VC consistently achieved near-random
speaker-verification performance (EER 0.43–0.51), whereas Resyn showed
greater variability across datasets (0.34–0.54). Both methods improved
signal cleanliness (improved SNR), and Resyn further achieved the
highest perceptual quality and lowest transcription error rates.
However, these improvements were accompanied by substantial losses in
pitch contour preservation(lower PCC$`{}_{\text{F0}}`$ values).
Therefore, although anonymisation methods exhibited distinct behaviours,
they consistently reflected the trade-off between preservation of speech
information and effective speaker anonymisation.

Task-dependent effects on downstream clinical classification
performance. In contrast to the largely method-dependent patterns in
speaker anonymisation and the preservation of general speech
characteristics, changes in downstream clinical classification
performance varied substantially across tasks
(Fig. [2](#Sx1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Anonymisation of speaker voiceprint ‣ Results ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")g).
Voice disorder tasks were particularly sensitive to anonymisation.
Across the Saarbrücken subsets and the PUMCH dataset, Neural VC and
Resyn caused significant performance degradation, with Resyn reducing
classification accuracy to nearly random levels.
Formant$`{}_{\text{LPC}}`$ and Formant$`{}_{\text{spec}}`$ generally
produced smaller declines, suggesting that local acoustic
transformations are more likely to preserve fine-grained cues related to
abnormal phonation and articulatory impairment. Mental health and
cognitive impairment tasks generally showed greater tolerance to
anonymisation. Their diagnostic cues may rely more on linguistic
content, pause patterns, or broader prosodic characteristics than highly
detailed acoustic abnormalities, allowing multiple anonymisation methods
to preserve classification performance. The speech elicitation paradigms
also affected the choice of anonymisation methods. In reading-speech
datasets with fixed content, such as Androids-R and Ivanova, Resyn led
to significant classification degradation. Resynthesising speech from
transcriptions resets speaking rhythm, prosody, and abnormal voice
characteristics, thereby removing informative cues which are relevant to
disease recognition.

These findings highlight two considerations for voiceprint anonymisation
in clinical speech. First, stronger speaker anonymisation does not
necessarily lead to a better outcome, as methods that provide greater
identity protection may also disrupt clinically relevant speech
information, whereas methods that better preserve speech characteristics
may offer weaker speaker protection. Second, the consequences of such
information loss on clinical utility are task-dependent, as different
disorders and elicitation paradigms rely on distinct speech
characteristics. These findings motivate CASA’s task-adaptive
optimisation of voiceprint anonymisation strategies to achieve
sufficient speaker privacy while preserving the speech information
required for each clinical task.

#### Anonymisation of sensitive spoken content

Voiceprint anonymisation alone does not address sensitive personal
information explicitly disclosed through spoken content. CASA therefore
performs content anonymisation by identifying privacy-sensitive
information in speech transcripts and masking the corresponding spoken
content. To avoid transmitting sensitive clinical information to
external model services, CASA calls a locally deployed open-source LLM,
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct ^([31](#bib.bib31)), for sensitive-content
localisation. We analysed CASA’s content anonymisation on two in-house
datasets containing sensitive spoken information: Crisis, comprising
psychological hotline conversations that may disclose personal names,
residential locations and telephone numbers, and PUMCH, in which a brief
clinician–participant exchange involving names and ages precedes a
speech repetition task.

We first examined CASA’s performance in sensitive-content localisation.
We compared CASA’s LLM-based localisation with the named-entity
recognition (NER)-based approach used in SecureSpeech, using manually
annotated privacy-sensitive spans as ground truth. CASA outperformed
SecureSpeech on both datasets
(Fig. [3](#Sx1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Anonymisation of sensitive spoken content ‣ Results ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")a,b),
achieving F1 scores of 0.861 versus 0.570 on Crisis and 0.914 versus
0.343 on PUMCH.

We next investigated the contribution of task-adaptive prompting to
sensitive-content localisation. Using the same self-deployed LLM, we
compared a default prompt with task-adaptive prompts refined by CASA
according to dataset-specific contexts. The default prompt asked the
model to identify common privacy-sensitive content, including names,
numerical identifiers, and address information. The task-adaptive prompt
was refined according to dataset-specific content, for example by
excluding descriptions of psychological state from private identifiers
in Crisis and by highlighting name-like nouns at the start of PUMCH
recordings. As shown in
Fig. [3](#Sx1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Anonymisation of sensitive spoken content ‣ Results ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")a,b,
task-adaptive prompting yields marked improvement on localisation
performance compared with the default prompt on both datasets with F1
scores exceeding 0.85.

We then assessed whether CASA’s locally deployed open-source LLM could
achieve competitive sensitive-content localisation performance compared
with a closed-source model. We compared Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct with
GPT-5-nano ^([32](#bib.bib32)) using a synthetic-privacy version of
Crisis
(Fig. [3](#Sx1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Anonymisation of sensitive spoken content ‣ Results ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")c).
To avoid exposing real private information to the closed-source model
during this comparison, all privacy-sensitive spans were manually
replaced with fictitious but realistic names, locations, and other
identifiers, producing a synthetic-privacy version for evaluation.
Results show that Qwen2.5 achieved comparable performance as GPT-5-nano
under the same synthetic-privacy setting, suggesting that the
open-source Qwen2.5 LLM, when paired with appropriate prompts, is
sufficient for practical content anonymisation in clinical speech.

Finally, we assessed whether content anonymisation introduced additional
losses in downstream clinical utility. We compared downstream BACC with
and without content anonymisation across different voiceprint
anonymisation methods on Crisis and PUMCH
(Fig. [3](#Sx1.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Anonymisation of sensitive spoken content ‣ Results ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")d,e).
No statistically significant reductions in classification performance
were detected across the evaluated configurations, suggesting that
privacy-sensitive spoken content can be removed without substantially
affecting downstream clinical utility, enabling targeted content
protection while preserving clinically relevant information.

#### Experience-guided self-improvement enables efficient task adaptation

The preceding analyses highlight the importance of adapting
anonymisation strategies to the requirements of different clinical
tasks. CASA achieves this through iterative evaluation and refinement of
anonymisation strategies against task-specific privacy–utility
requirements. To improve the efficiency of this iterative process, CASA
incorporates a recursive self-improvement mechanism that leverages
experience accumulation across tasks. Specifically, resulting experience
is retained in two levels of memory for subsequent planning:
fine-grained memory stores anonymisation configurations and evaluation
results from previous runs, and coarse-grained memory summarises
task-level lessons and recurring patterns. These memories guide
subsequent strategy planning, reducing the need for repeated
evaluations.

To quantify the contribution of accumulated experience, we compared CASA
with and without memory under the same task-specific requirements. In
the memory-enabled condition, CASA could retrieve experience from other
tasks, whereas the memory-free condition had no access to previous
cross-task experience. Both conditions retained within-task
feedback-guided adaptation. Success required satisfying all privacy and

clinical tasks with different paradigms to prioritise promising
anonymisation strategies, with suitability verified for the current

### Discussion

Clinical speech anonymisation requires balancing speaker identity
protection with the preservation of information needed for clinical
assessment. Voiceprint anonymisation methods that strongly suppress
speaker identity may disrupt acoustic, prosodic or linguistic
characteristics, whereas methods that better preserve these
characteristics may offer weaker identity protection. The appropriate
balance varies across clinical tasks because different disorders and
speech elicitation paradigms rely on distinct speech characteristics for
assessment. Our analyses showed that the same anonymisation method could
preserve downstream classification performance in some tasks but
substantially impair it in others. Beyond voiceprint anonymisation,
clinical speech recordings may also disclose sensitive personal
information through spoken content. Our content anonymisation
experiments showed that identifying such information also benefits from
task adaptation that accounts for differences in clinical context and
speech elicitation paradigms. In sum, these findings suggest that
clinical speech anonymisation needs to account for both the
privacy–preservation balance and the specific requirements of different
clinical tasks.

CASA addresses these challenges by reformulating clinical speech
anonymisation as a task-adaptive optimisation problem, in which
strategies are planned, executed and iteratively refined using
multi-dimensional evaluation feedback. Incorporating measured outcomes
as feedback is necessary since the clinical relevance of individual
speech characteristics cannot always be determined a priori. Even
similar clinical tasks may differ in data collection, participant
populations and labelling criteria, affecting which speech
characteristics are predictive of downstream outcomes. CASA uses
downstream classification performance to directly quantify the utility
of anonymised speech, reducing reliance on prior assumptions about
clinically relevant speech characteristics. Furthermore, through
recursive self-improvement, CASA accumulates successful strategies and
lessons from previous evaluations to guide subsequent planning and
reduce repeated exploration. This creates a continuous cycle of
evaluation, experience accumulation and reuse, allowing CASA to adapt
more efficiently to new clinical tasks as its experience grows.

CASA also supports human oversight of automated anonymisation.
Intermediate outputs, including transcriptions, detected
privacy-sensitive spans, timestamps and evaluation results, are
maintained as transparent and editable representations, allowing
clinical researchers and data managers to inspect, modify and approve
anonymisation decisions when necessary. This human-in-the-loop design
provides an additional layer of control when handling sensitive patient
information. Clinical expert assessments further supported CASA’s
practical value, showing high agreement between clinical judgements
based on original and anonymised recordings, alongside favourable
ratings for anonymisation quality and research-sharing acceptability.
Following CASA’s anonymisation, a real-world cross-institutional
clinical data sharing was conducted, where the Suicide-YF dataset was
shared with Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. By reducing
speaker traceability and masking sensitive spoken content while
preserving clinically relevant information, CASA could help overcome
privacy-related barriers to cross-institutional clinical speech sharing.
Such capabilities could facilitate collaborative research across
hospitals and institutions, supporting the development of speech-based
screening and assessment systems under appropriate data-use agreements.

Several limitations should be considered. First, sensitive-content
localisation and voiceprint anonymisation methods such as
transcription–resynthesis require automatic speech recognition (ASR) to
generate speech transcripts. ASR performance may decline for
low-resource languages, dialectal speech, child speech and severely
pathological speech. Recognition errors can propagate to subsequent
anonymisation procedures, potentially compromising privacy protection or
the preservation of clinically relevant information. This limitation
could be mitigated through domain-specific adaptation of ASR models for
the target population or clinical setting. Second, the present study
evaluated CASA using a limited set of anonymisation tools, comprising
five voiceprint anonymisation methods and an LLM-based content
anonymisation approach. Nevertheless, CASA’s modular design allows
additional anonymisation and evaluation tools to be integrated,
potentially broadening its applicability to a wider range of clinical
tasks. Finally, human review may remain necessary for high-risk
applications where residual privacy leakage is unacceptable. To
accommodate this need, CASA’s human-in-the-loop design supports such
oversight through transparent and editable intermediate outputs,
allowing researchers and data managers to inspect, correct and approve
anonymisation decisions when necessary.

Looking ahead, CASA could enable a positive feedback loop between
privacy-preserving data sharing and experience-guided adaptation. By
facilitating the controlled sharing of clinical speech, CASA could make
more diverse data available for clinical model development, while
generating anonymisation experience across a broader range of tasks.
Through RSI, this accumulated experience could further improve strategy
planning for new clinical tasks, potentially lowering barriers to data
sharing. Future research should investigate how this reciprocal process
develops across institutions and increasingly diverse clinical settings.

### Methods

We developed and evaluated CASA through a modular processing pipeline
comprising transcription, voiceprint anonymisation, content
anonymisation, quantitative evaluation and clinical expert assessment.

#### CASA agent

CASA is implemented as an LLM-based agent that plans and executes
clinical speech anonymisation according to task-specific requirements.
The system takes original speech recordings as input and produces
anonymised recordings through voiceprint modification and
sensitive-content masking. The controller receives a natural-language
description of the clinical objective, speech paradigm and user-defined
privacy and utility requirements. These instructions guide planning,
tool execution and subsequent adaptation.

Tools. Transcription, voiceprint anonymisation, content anonymisation
and evaluation are implemented as independent tools with explicitly
defined inputs, outputs and dependency constraints (see Supplementary
Fig. [1](#Sx9.F1 "Supplementary Fig. 1 ‣ Supplementary Information ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")).
Intermediate outputs are stored as structured JSON files, enabling both
the agent and users to inspect and modify processing results before
subsequent stages are executed.

Memory. The agent maintains a two-level memory to support strategy
planning and feedback-guided adaptation. A coarse-grained experience
memory stores summaries of previous experiments, recurring processing
patterns, and failure cases with lessons learned, providing reusable
prior knowledge across tasks. A fine-grained experiment memory records
run-specific details, including evaluation metrics, tool parameters, and
intermediate JSON outputs and logs, enabling precise diagnosis and
reproduction of each anonymisation run. In addition, the current-task
history is maintained and updated throughout execution, linking the two
memory levels to the ongoing planning and adaptation process. After each
run, the agent stores the configuration and evaluation results in
fine-grained memory and distils the outcome into reusable lessons for
coarse-grained memory.

Planning and execution. Given a natural-language anonymisation request,
the agent first constructs an anonymisation strategy by selecting
processing modules and configuring their corresponding parameters.
Strategy generation is performed using an LLM (i.e.,
Qwen3.8-27B ^([33](#bib.bib33))), which is provided with descriptions of
available tools, parameter constraints, workflow dependencies, and
relevant entries retrieved from memory. The model was run with a
temperature of 0.01 and a repetition penalty of 1.05 to encourage
deterministic strategy generation while reducing repetitive outputs. The
resulting strategy is then executed to produce anonymised speech.

Evaluation and adaptation. Following execution, the evaluation module
assesses the speaker privacy, downstream clinical utility and
preservation of speech characteristics of the anonymised recordings, as
detailed in Methods, “Evaluation methods”. The agent subsequently
analyses the evaluation result to determine whether the anonymisation
objectives have been satisfied ^([34](#bib.bib34)). If the current
strategy fails to meet the specified requirements, the agent re-plans
the anonymisation strategy according to evaluation feedback, the
current-task history, and previously executed processing steps retrieved
from memory, and iteratively explores alternative configurations until
the objectives are satisfied or a maximum of 7 adaptation iterations is
reached. The agent first explores alternative anonymisation methods at
their default settings and adjusts method parameters when none satisfies
the specified requirements. The agent also supports manually specified
anonymisation workflows if needed.

To evaluate whether the memory module improves adaptation efficiency, we
constructed 10 anonymisation requests for each of the 14 datasets or
task subsets, yielding 140 requests in total. Each request identified
the target dataset or task subset and specified two simultaneous
requirements: an EER not below 20%, 30% or 40%, together with one
constraint on downstream performance or speech preservation. The second
constraint required a BACC decrease of less than three or six percentage
points relative to original speech, a CER/WER below 15% or 20%, an
emotion similarity above 0.93, or a pitch-contour correlation (PCC\_(F0))
above 0.70 or 0.80. We evaluated the same 140 requests with and without
cross-task memory. In the memory-enabled condition, the agent could
retrieve general lessons and detailed experimental records from the
other 13 tasks, with experience from the task under evaluation excluded.
In the memory-free condition, no cross-task experience was provided.
Both conditions retained within-task planning and evaluation-guided
adaptation. Success required satisfying both thresholds specified in the
request. We reported Success@1 and Success@2 as the proportions of
requests reaching a satisfactory configuration within one and two
evaluation steps, respectively, together with the mean number of steps
required to reach the first satisfactory configuration.

#### Transcription methods

The transcription module converts raw speech recordings into structured
intermediate representations containing sentence-level transcripts,
word-level timestamps, sentence indices and speaker annotations. Speech
segments associated with the same speaker are aggregated and processed
using a pretrained age–gender recognition
model ^([35](#bib.bib35); [36](#bib.bib36)) to infer speakers’ age and
gender attributes, which are subsequently stored as auxiliary metadata
in the transcription files. Mandarin Chinese recordings are transcribed
using Paraformer ^([37](#bib.bib37)). Recordings in other languages are
transcribed using WhisperX ^([38](#bib.bib38)).

#### Voiceprint anonymisation methods

The voiceprint anonymisation module supports three categories of
anonymisation methods: signal-processing-based transformations, neural
voice conversion, and transcription–resynthesis. Signal-processing-based
methods include pitch shifting by semitone steps (Pitch), formant
shifting through spectral scaling (Formantspec), and formant shifting
through Linear Predictive Coding (LPC) pole transformation (FormantLPC)
implemented by McAdams. Neural voice conversion is implemented using
SeedVC, whereas transcription–resynthesis is implemented through an
ASR–TTS (Text-to-Speech) cascade.

Pitch. Pitch-based anonymisation is performed using the librosa toolkit.
According to speaker-level gender estimates obtained during
transcription, a positive offset of 3 semitones is applied to male
speakers and a negative offset of 3 semitones is applied to female
speakers.

Formant$`{}_{\text{spec}}`$. Formant-based anonymisation is implemented
by applying short-time Fourier transform analysis followed by linear
interpolation-based spectral scaling with a scaling factor of 1.1. This
operation approximates modifying the effective vocal tract length of the
speaker.

Formant$`{}_{\text{LPC}}`$. McAdams anonymisation ^([39](#bib.bib39))
leverages Linear Predictive Coding (LPC) to alter vocal tract
characteristics by shifting the formant locations. For each frame, the
angles of the LPC poles are transformed by a predefined power
coefficient, and the speech is then resynthesised from the original
residual signal with the modified LPC filter, changing the distribution
of formant frequencies. An LPC order of 20 and a power coefficient of
0.8 were used.

Neural VC. Neural voice conversion anonymises speech by modifying the
original waveform or acoustic features. We perform neural VC using the
SeedVC ^([40](#bib.bib40)) framework. For multi-speaker recordings,
speaker labels obtained during the transcription stage are used to
assign different target speaker embeddings sampled from the
AISHELL-1 ^([41](#bib.bib41)) corpus to different speakers within the
same recording. The converted speech preserves the original temporal
alignment and therefore does not alter timestamp annotations.

Resyn. Transcription–resynthesis generates anonymised speech directly
from transcribed text. We used FishSpeech ^([42](#bib.bib42)) as the TTS
backbone, with language-matched reference utterances from Mozilla Common
Voice ^([43](#bib.bib43)) and Multilingual
LibriSpeech ^([44](#bib.bib44)) used to specify target speaker
identities.

#### Content anonymisation methods

The content anonymisation module involves two stages: privacy
localisation and audio redaction. The privacy localisation stage
identifies privacy-sensitive linguistic spans from transcription output
and generates structured annotations containing detected text segments
together with their corresponding timestamp ranges. Localisation is
implemented using an LLM. By default, a locally deployed
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct model is used to avoid transmitting transcripts to
external services and privacy leakage. The model is prompted to identify
common forms of spoken private information, including names, numerical
identifiers, and address information. To support long-form recordings,
transcription outputs are segmented into groups of consecutive sentences
before localisation.

The localisation module supports configurable prompts and model
backends. Task-specific prompts can be provided to adapt privacy
localisation to different recording protocols, clinical settings and
languages. Alternative locally deployed LLMs can also be integrated to
support different deployment environments.

Following privacy localisation, audio redaction is performed according
to the anonymisation method. For methods that preserve timestamp
alignment (Pitch, Formant$`{}_{\text{spec}}`$,
Formant$`{}_{\text{LPC}}`$, and Neural VC), detected privacy-sensitive
spans are redacted in the voiceprint-anonymised audio by replacing the
corresponding speech segments with noise. For Resyn, privacy
localisation and masking are first applied to the transcription, after
which anonymised speech is generated from the modified text.

#### Evaluation methods

We build a multi-dimensional evaluation framework to comprehensively
assess the effectiveness of anonymisation methods.

Speech quality. Speech quality is evaluated using signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR) and mean opinion score (MOS). SNR measures cleanliness of the
speech while MOS measures the overall perceptual quality of the speech.
MOS is automatically estimated using UTMOS ^([45](#bib.bib45)).

Contour preservation. Pitch preservation is evaluated through
fundamental frequency (F0) contour analysis. F0 contours are extracted
from both original and anonymised speech using the librosa toolkit.
Preservation of pitch-contour shape is quantified using the Pearson
correlation coefficient (PCC) between the two contours.

Linguistic preservation. Linguistic preservation is evaluated by
comparing transcriptions generated from the original and anonymised
speech. For Mandarin Chinese recordings, preservation is quantified
using character error rate (CER). For other languages, word error rate
(WER) is used.

Emotion preservation. Emotion preservation is evaluated using
Emotion2Vec-Plus-Seed ^([46](#bib.bib46)). Emotion embeddings are
extracted from both original and anonymised speech, and preservation is
quantified using cosine similarity between the corresponding embeddings.

Speaker anonymisation. Speaker anonymisation is evaluated using equal
error rate (EER), with higher EER indicating weaker speaker
traceability. Speaker embeddings are extracted using the
WavLM-Base-Plus-sv speaker verification model ^([47](#bib.bib47)).
Positive verification pairs consist of original and anonymised
recordings from the same subject, whereas negative pairs consist of
original and anonymised recordings from different subjects. Verification
scores are computed as cosine similarities between speaker embeddings.
EER was estimated at the ROC operating point that minimised the absolute
difference between the false acceptance and false rejection rates, and
was calculated as the mean of these two rates at that point. The 95%
confidence interval was estimated using a nonparametric percentile
bootstrap with 1,000 resampling iterations, with EER recalculated for
each resample, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the valid
bootstrap estimates defined the confidence interval. The framework
supports user-provided subject mappings, allowing multiple recordings to
be treated as originating from the same subject during verification.
When no subject mapping is provided, each recording is treated as an
independent subject.

Clinical utility. Downstream clinical utility is evaluated through
disease-related classification. We use the speech LLM
Qwen2.5-Omni-7B ^([48](#bib.bib48)) as the backbone of an end-to-end
classification model, following our previous work ^([22](#bib.bib22))
(Supplementary
Fig. [2](#Sx9.F2 "Supplementary Fig. 2 ‣ Supplementary Information ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")).
The model consists of a speech encoder, an LLM, and a fully connected
classification head that maps the final representation to disease-label
predictions. Weight-decomposed low-rank adaptation
(DoRA) ^([49](#bib.bib49)) is used for parameter-efficient fine-tuning
with a rank of 32 and an alpha of 64. The learning rate is set to
$`1\times 10^{-4}`$, and the number of training epochs is adjusted
according to dataset size. To reduce the influence of class imbalance,
minority classes in the training set are upsampled until all classes
contain the same number of samples. Balanced accuracy (BACC) is used as
the primary metric for downstream utility. Long recordings are divided
into non-overlapping 4-minute segments, with each treated as a separate
sample during training and inference. The final recording-level
prediction is obtained by averaging segment-level class probabilities at
test time. For each dataset, we trained a separate classifier on the
original speech and on each anonymised version, using the corresponding
speech version for both training and evaluation.

For datasets with official test splits, evaluation is performed on the
official test set using five independent runs with different random
seeds. For datasets without official splits, five-fold cross-validation
is used. In both settings, train, validation, and test sets are strictly
subject-independent to avoid data leakage and to evaluate cross-subject
generalisation. Mean BACC and standard deviation are reported across the
five runs or folds. Statistical significance of utility degradation
after anonymisation was assessed using one-sided exact binomial tests on
discordant paired predictions, using scipy.stats.binomtest. No
adjustment was made for multiple comparisons. A lower $`P`$ value
indicates stronger evidence that anonymisation reduces downstream
classification performance. Statistical significance was defined as
$`P<0.05`$.

#### Datasets for validation

MODMA ^([23](#bib.bib23)) is a Mandarin Chinese clinical speech dataset
comprising 1,503 recordings from 52 participants aged 16–56 years,
including outpatients diagnosed with depression (44.2%) and healthy
controls (55.8%). Speech was collected through multiple elicitation
paradigms, including interview, reading and picture-description tasks.

Androids-Corpus ^([24](#bib.bib24)) comprises recordings from 118 native
Italian speakers classified as healthy controls (HC) or patients (PT)
with depression-related disorders. Reading recordings (54 HC, 58 PT)
were collected using Aesop’s fable The North Wind and the Sun, whereas
interview recordings (52 HC, 64 PT) were obtained through spontaneous
responses to daily-life questions.

Suicide-YF consists of Mandarin Chinese speech recordings from 1,223
adolescents aged 10–18 years recruited in Guangdong, China. The
participants were asked to complete an online survey, in which they were
asked whether they had ever experienced suicidal thoughts or behaviours.
Those who answered yes were invited to participate in the study.
Additionally, a control group of children with no suicide risk was
selected for comparison with the at-risk participants. All recordings
are paired with suicide-risk assessments administered using the Mini
International Neuropsychiatric Interview for Children and Adolescents
(MINI-KID) suicidality module ^([50](#bib.bib50)), with 53.4% of
participants identified as at risk. Following previous
studies ^([51](#bib.bib51); [22](#bib.bib22)), only recordings from the
self-description task were used. Informed consent was obtained from all
participants and their guardians, and the study was approved by the
Tsinghua University Institutional Review Board.

Crisis is an in-house Mandarin Chinese psychological-support hotline
dataset comprising 154 recordings (approximately 100 hours in total;
mean duration 39.49 minutes). The study was conducted at the Beijing
Psychological Support Hotline which provides services to callers from
around China, three-fourths of whom live outside of the Beijing
Municipality. Hotline operators are full-time employees with a
psychology or psychiatry background who receive at least 3 months
full-time training in the provision of hotline services before
independently taking calls. Recordings were independently annotated by
two clinical experts into three crisis levels: no crisis (55
participants), low crisis (42), and medium-to-high crisis (57).
Conversations cover a broad range of personal issues, including
depression, anxiety, relationship stress and suicidal ideation, and may
contain privacy-sensitive information such as names, locations and phone
numbers. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of
Beijing Huilongguan Hospital (2014-106, 2024-76). Before callers were
connected to a hotline operator, they were informed by a voice message
that all calls of the hotline would be tape-recorded and that data
utilised for research purposes would be analysed anonymously.

NCMMSC-AD ^([25](#bib.bib25)) is a Mandarin Chinese cognitive assessment
dataset comprising 399 recordings from 175 participants labelled as
healthy controls (HC), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or Alzheimer’s
disease (AD). Recordings include multiple cognitive tasks, including
verbal fluency, lexical retrieval, picture description and
self-introduction.

ADReSS ^([9](#bib.bib9)) is an English Alzheimer’s disease dataset
derived from the Pitt corpus, comprising recordings from 156
participants aged 50–79 years. The official split contains 54 HC and 54
AD participants for training, and 24 HC and 24 AD participants for
testing. All recordings correspond to the Cookie Theft
picture-description task.

Ivanova ^([10](#bib.bib10)) is a Spanish cognitive assessment dataset
comprising recordings from 357 participants aged 50–96 years.
Participants were labelled as healthy controls (54.9%), mild cognitive
impairment (24.4%), or Alzheimer’s disease (20.7%). All recordings
correspond to a reading task in which participants read the first two
sentences of Don Quixote by Cervantes.

NeuroVoz ^([26](#bib.bib26); [52](#bib.bib52)) is a Spanish Parkinson’s
disease dataset comprising recordings from 112 participants aged 31–88
years, including 58 healthy controls and 54 patients with Parkinson’s
disease. The corpus includes multiple speech tasks, including sustained
vowels, diadochokinetic tests, listen-and-repeat utterances, and
spontaneous monologues.

ENNI ^([27](#bib.bib27)) is an English narrative-speech dataset from the
CHILDES Clinical English Edmonton Narrative Norms Instrument corpus,
comprising recordings from Canadian children with typical development
(286) or specific language impairment (66). The task is picture-elicited
story generation, in which participants narrate a picture story to an
examiner.

Saarbrücken ^([12](#bib.bib12)) is a German pathological-voice dataset
containing clinically validated voice-disorder labels. We selected three
common voice-disorder categories from the database and constructed three
disease-versus-control subsets from the read-sentence recordings:
laryngitis (140 healthy, 140 pathological), Rekurrensparese (212
healthy, 212 pathological), and hyperfunctional dysphonia (212 healthy,
212 pathological). All recordings correspond to the sentence “Guten
Morgen, wie geht es Ihnen?” (“Good morning, how are you?”)

PUMCH is an in-house Mandarin Chinese speech dataset for cleft-palate
speech assessment, comprising recordings from 244 participants,
including 128 patients with cleft palate and 116 healthy controls.
Synchronized audio-video recordings were obtained primarily using an
iPhone 16 in a quiet hospital room; additional home recordings were
submitted using consumer devices under variable recording conditions. A
researcher was present during hospital-based collection, and a parent or
guardian could accompany minors when necessary; no researcher was
present during home recording. Disease labels were annotated by
experienced clinicians. Recordings consist of clinician–participant
interactions beginning with identity-related questions (e.g., name and
age), followed by a sentence-repetition task in which participants
repeat utterances read by the clinician. This multicentre, prospective
observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union
Medical College Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Beijing,
China (approval number: I-25PJ0105). For adult participants, written
informed consent was obtained from them directly; for minors, written
informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians, in
accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

#### Fixed pipeline for baseline

To evaluate whether adaptive anonymisation provides advantages over
conventional fixed strategies, we implemented SecureSpeech as a
representative fixed anonymisation baseline. SecureSpeech adopts a
sequential ASR–NER–LLM–TTS pipeline, where ASR is first applied to
obtain transcripts, NER identifies privacy-sensitive entities, an LLM
rewrites the detected spans, and a TTS model generates anonymised
speech.

Since the original SecureSpeech implementation was developed for English
speech only and its individual components do not directly support
multilingual clinical speech evaluation, we adapted the pipeline for our
experimental setting. Specifically, to enable privacy entity detection
across multiple languages, the original English-only NER module was
replaced with a RoBERTa-based NER model fine-tuned on the CLUENER2020
dataset ^([53](#bib.bib53)) for Chinese speech processing, and a
multilingual BERT-based NER model ^([54](#bib.bib54)) trained on
high-resource languages for multilingual scenarios. The original
ParlerTTS-based synthesis module was replaced with FishSpeech to provide
multilingual speech resynthesis capability. The LLM component for
rewriting was replaced with locally deployed Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct. The
ASR outputs were kept identical to those used in our framework to ensure
a fair comparison.

#### External attacker evaluation

We evaluated CASA’s anonymised recordings using two external attackers:
a semi-informed attacker trained on anonymised speech following the
VoicePrivacy training protocol, and a SpeechLLM attacker using a
pretrained speech–language model directly for speaker verification. Both
attackers compared original and anonymised recordings to assess whether
speaker identities remained linkable. Their outputs were used for
external evaluation and were not provided to CASA during planning or
adaptation.

Semi-informed attacker. We trained an ECAPA-TDNN ^([55](#bib.bib55))
speaker-verification model on an anonymised version of LibriSpeech
train-clean-360 ^([56](#bib.bib56)). Because the intended use of
LibriSpeech is automatic speech recognition, CASA was instructed to
preserve linguistic information with a CER/WER requirement below 10%.
The anonymised training recordings were generated using CASA’s Resyn
method. Model training followed the VoicePrivacy 2024 training
recipe ^([29](#bib.bib29)). The resulting attacker was then applied to
the anonymisation configurations produced by CASA for the clinical
tasks. EER was calculated using the same pairing and scoring procedure
described in “Evaluation methods”, replacing the agent’s
speaker-embedding model with the ECAPA-TDNN attacker trained on
CASA-anonymised LibriSpeech.

SpeechLLM attacker. We used SALMONN-2-8B ^([30](#bib.bib30)) directly to
classify pairs of recordings as originating from the same or different
speakers. For each speaker shared between the original and anonymised
recordings, we generated one same-speaker pair and one different-speaker
pair, yielding 2N pairs for N speakers. For every pair, the model
received the first 15 seconds of the original recording and the last 15
seconds of the anonymised recording. When an individual recording was
shorter than 30 seconds, its first or second half was used,
respectively. A fixed prompt asked whether the main speakers in the two
recordings were the same person and required exactly one response:
“SAME” or “DIFFERENT”. Each pair was processed independently, with
sampling disabled and a maximum output length of 256 tokens. The
verification error, calculated as $`1-\text{accuracy}`$ was reported.

#### Clinical expert evaluation

We conducted clinical expert evaluations on three private clinical
speech datasets to assess whether anonymised recordings preserved
clinically relevant information and were acceptable for controlled
research sharing. The expert panel comprised clinicians with relevant
clinical experience and researchers extensively trained in the
corresponding clinical domains. Evaluation protocols were tailored to
the characteristics and assessment objectives of each clinical task.
Throughout the clinical expert evaluations, evaluators were blinded to
the original reference labels.

For the crisis assessment task, we randomly selected 60 recordings from
the Crisis dataset for clinical relabelling, as the relatively long
duration of the recordings made exhaustive expert evaluation
impractical. Only the anonymised versions were presented to the experts,
who independently assigned crisis labels following the same assessment
procedure used to construct the original dataset. Labels derived from
anonymised speech were compared with the original reference labels, and
agreement was quantified as the proportion of matching labels. We
additionally sampled a non-overlapping set of 30 recordings to assess
anonymisation quality and acceptability for research sharing. For this
evaluation, experts were provided with paired original and anonymised
recordings. Two clinical experts jointly participated in the evaluation;
each relabelled 30 anonymised recordings and assessed 15
original–anonymised pairs for anonymisation quality and sharing
acceptability. The web interface is shown in Supplementary
Fig. [3](#Sx9.F3 "Supplementary Fig. 3 ‣ Supplementary Information ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data")
and Supplementary
Fig. [4](#Sx9.F4 "Supplementary Fig. 4 ‣ Supplementary Information ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data").

For the suicide risk assessment task, 350 recordings were randomly
selected from the Suicide-YF dataset and evaluated by one expert.
Because the reference suicide-risk labels were derived from clinical
assessments independent of the speech recordings, the expert was not
asked to relabel the anonymised speech. Instead, paired original and
anonymised recordings were presented to assess whether clinically
relevant information for suicide-risk prediction was preserved after
anonymisation. Preservation was evaluated along two dimensions:
acoustic-feature-related information and semantic-feature-related
information. As content anonymisation was not applied in this task, the
anonymisation-quality assessment focused on voiceprint anonymisation and
acceptability for research sharing. The web interface is shown in
Supplementary
Fig. [5](#Sx9.F5 "Supplementary Fig. 5 ‣ Supplementary Information ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data").

For the cleft-palate speech assessment task, all 244 recordings from the
PUMCH dataset were evaluated by a speech-language pathologist. To
simplify the evaluation procedure, rather than performing a
comprehensive cleft-palate speech assessment covering multiple
articulation and resonance errors, the evaluation focused on nasality
severity as a clinically relevant perceptual feature, rated on a
four-level ordinal scale: none, mild, moderate, and severe. Clinical
information preservation was quantified by the exact agreement between
ratings obtained from the original and anonymised recordings. The
speech-language pathologist also assessed voiceprint anonymisation,
content anonymisation, and acceptability for research sharing for each
anonymised recording. The web interface is shown in Supplementary
Fig. [6](#Sx9.F6 "Supplementary Fig. 6 ‣ Supplementary Information ‣ Task-adaptive anonymisation enables privacy-preserving sharing of clinical speech data").

Preservation, anonymisation-quality and sharing-acceptability scores
were collected on a five-point scale, with higher scores indicating
better preservation, stronger anonymisation or higher sharing
acceptability. Mean scores were reported across evaluated recordings.

Supplementary Fig. 1: $`|`$ Detailed workflow of CASA anonymisation.
Original recordings are transcribed using an ASR module to obtain
transcripts with speaker annotations and word-level timestamps.
Available voiceprint anonymisation methods include pitch shifting
(Pitch), spectral formant scaling (Formant$`{}_{\text{spec}}`$),
LPC-based formant transformation (Formant$`{}_{\text{LPC}}`$), neural
voice conversion (Neural VC) and transcription–resynthesis (Resyn). For
content anonymisation, a locally deployed LLM identifies sensitive spans
in the transcripts and produces annotations to guide redaction. For
Resyn, which doesn’t preserve temporal alignment, sensitive content is
masked in the transcript before synthesis; while for other methods, the
corresponding speech segments are replaced with noise. The resulting
recordings are evaluated for speaker privacy (EER), downstream clinical
utility (BACC), signal cleanliness (SNR), perceptual quality (MOS),
pitch-contour preservation (PCC$`{}_{\text{F0}}`$), linguistic
preservation (CER/WER) and emotion preservation (Emo).

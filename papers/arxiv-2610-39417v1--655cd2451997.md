###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong performance on
software engineering (SE) tasks, driving the need for high-quality
datasets to support reliable training and evaluation. SWE-bench-style
datasets (SWE datasets) are widely used to evaluate LLM-based coding
agents for resolving real-world software issues, where task quality,
particularly task clarity and test coverage, directly affects whether
instances are solvable and verifiable. However, quality issues remain in
existing SWE datasets, while manual annotation and verification are
costly and labor-intensive, motivating recent efforts toward LLM-based
annotation. Existing approaches typically rely on static and overly
general judgment rubrics, limiting their ability to accommodate diverse
and nuanced scenarios and often resulting in high false positive rates.
In contrast, human annotation usually draws on accumulated experience to
make context-aware judgments.

Inspired by this, we propose ExLabel, an LLM-based, self-evolving,
experience-guided assistant for task clarity and test coverage
assessment in SWE datasets. ExLabel introduces an evolving experience
bank that accumulates cross-instance knowledge into stable yet adaptive
judgment criteria and reuses relevant experience to guide the assessment
of new instances. To enable systematic evaluation, we construct SWE-TQ,
a new benchmark spanning eight programming languages, by cleaning human
annotations from SWE-bench and Multi-SWE-bench. Experiments with three
backbone LLMs show that ExLabel achieves the best F1 across all
settings, with relative improvements of 44.2%/49.9% and 24.0%/18.4% over
the best-performing baseline for task clarity and test coverage
assessment on the Python/Multilingual subsets, respectively.
Furthermore, a coding model fine-tuned on SWE-rebench data filtered by
ExLabel resolves 66.2% of the issues on SWE-bench Verified,
outperforming the model trained on data filtered by the best-performing
baseline by 14.5%.

## 1 Introduction

Developing and evaluating the software engineering (SE) capabilities of
large language models (LLMs) has attracted considerable research
attention \[[58](#bib.bib19), [13](#bib.bib29), [22](#bib.bib32)\]. SE
datasets provide a critical foundation for equipping LLMs with
SE-related capabilities \[[59](#bib.bib26), [37](#bib.bib3),
[56](#bib.bib27)\], serving both as benchmarks \[[8](#bib.bib1),
[21](#bib.bib2), [60](#bib.bib16)\] for evaluating model performance on
SE tasks and as valuable corpora for model training \[[58](#bib.bib19),
[42](#bib.bib20), [52](#bib.bib21)\]. Among these datasets,
SWE-bench-style datasets (i.e., SWE datasets) \[[23](#bib.bib9),
[3](#bib.bib35), [18](#bib.bib11)\] have become increasingly popular due
to their demonstrated effectiveness in training and evaluating LLM-based
coding agents for real-world software issue
resolution \[[13](#bib.bib29), [55](#bib.bib40)\]. Each instance in SWE
datasets consists of a natural-language task description, a golden
patch, a set of test suites for evaluating the success of the task, and
a corresponding sandboxed executable environment.

An important yet not well-explored aspect of SWE datasets is task
quality, which concerns the solvability and verifiability of
instances \[[5](#bib.bib34)\]. Poor task quality not only undermines
evaluation reliability but can also mislead model training and
benchmarking, leading to inflated or unreliable performance estimates.
Despite its importance, quality issues have been observed in existing
SWE datasets \[[61](#bib.bib22), [54](#bib.bib23), [53](#bib.bib24),
[58](#bib.bib19)\]. Issues such as insufficient test
coverage \[[56](#bib.bib27), [59](#bib.bib26)\] persist even in the
manually verified SWE-bench dataset \[[8](#bib.bib1)\]. For SWE datasets
containing synthetic data \[[58](#bib.bib19), [54](#bib.bib23)\], such
issues may be further exacerbated, as large-scale automated data
generation may introduce additional noise and inconsistencies. Task
quality is commonly assessed along two dimensions \[[8](#bib.bib1)\]:
task clarity and test coverage. Task clarity evaluates whether a task
description provides sufficient and unambiguous information for solving
the problem \[[37](#bib.bib3), [8](#bib.bib1)\]; incomplete or ambiguous
descriptions may lead to incorrect or biased understanding, rendering
tasks unsolvable \[[35](#bib.bib30), [20](#bib.bib31)\]. Test coverage
evaluates whether the test suite is adequate and precise for verifying
correctness \[[37](#bib.bib3), [8](#bib.bib1)\]; insufficient coverage
may allow incorrect solutions to pass due to limited discriminative
power \[[59](#bib.bib26), [56](#bib.bib27)\].

Currently, the assessment of task quality in SWE datasets primarily
relies on human experts \[[8](#bib.bib1), [60](#bib.bib16),
[21](#bib.bib2)\]. For example, in SWE-bench
Verified \[[8](#bib.bib1)\], each instance is manually
assessed/annotated three times by a total of 93 professional Python
engineers. While manual annotation can provide relatively high-quality
assessments, it is inherently expensive and difficult to scale,
especially as large volumes of SWE data are rapidly
collected \[[3](#bib.bib35), [18](#bib.bib11)\] or
synthesized \[[54](#bib.bib23), [58](#bib.bib19)\]. Specifically,
annotating 1,000 instances can cost approximately \$100,000 (\$100 per
instance), with an average of 1.05 person-hours required per
instance \[[37](#bib.bib3)\]. These challenges highlight the need for
automated annotation techniques that reduce costs while maintaining
annotation quality.

A natural direction is to leverage LLMs for automated SWE dataset
annotation. Recent work (e.g., SPICE \[[37](#bib.bib3)\]) constructs a
rationale checklist and incorporates it into prompts to guide the
annotation of SWE instances. However, these judgment rubrics are
typically fixed and overly generic, limiting their ability to
accommodate diverse and complex scenarios, particularly corner cases.
More fundamentally, existing work largely treats annotation as a static
decision process, whereas prior studies on human annotation and related
tasks (e.g., qualitative analyses of annotation
behavior \[[48](#bib.bib49), [49](#bib.bib50)\]) suggest that annotation
is influenced by contextual factors and accumulated experience. This
contrast motivates an experience-informed annotation process in which
the judgment rubric evolves by learning from previously encountered
cases.

However, effectively accumulating such experience is non-trivial. A
naive strategy that directly aggregates experience at the instance level
can produce increasingly expanded and fragmented rubrics, degenerating
into collections of detailed, case-specific rules rather than coherent
and generalizable guidelines for decision-making \[[15](#bib.bib51),
[51](#bib.bib52)\]. The key challenge is therefore to balance generality
and specificity when constructing and evolving the judgment rubric. On
the one hand, the rubric should capture general principles that transfer
across instances; on the other hand, it should retain sufficient
specificity to accommodate nuanced and corner cases.

To this end, we model annotation as an iterative and experience-driven
process that progressively refines the judgment rubric through
experience abstraction to balance generality and specificity.
Specifically, we propose ExLabel, a self-evolving and experience-guided
assistant for task quality assessment (i.e., task clarity and test
coverage) in SWE datasets. ExLabel is designed for both fully automated
annotation and human-assisted annotation, where it provides preliminary
assessments to potentially reduce manual effort. The core of ExLabel is
an evolving experience bank, which stores and updates structured
knowledge derived from previously processed instances. ExLabel operates
in two phases: experience accumulation and experience reuse. The
accumulation phase aims to mine cross-instance information from
annotated instances into the experience bank, which is iteratively
evolved to form a stable judgment rubric; the reuse phase then leverages
the rubric through experience retrieval when predicting new annotations.

In the accumulation phase, ExLabel iteratively refines its internal
judgment rubric through a three-stage cycle: rollout, reflection, and
experience update. The rollout stage summarizes each SWE instance along
three dimensions (i.e., task type, task explanation, and test
explanation), and encodes them using both sparse (i.e.,
BM25 \[[47](#bib.bib37)\]) and dense (LLM-based) representations to
enable effective retrieval of relevant past experiences. The reflection
stage leverages the semantic understanding capability of an LLM to
compare the predicted annotation with the golden one, prompt the model
to self-reflect on its reasoning process \[[46](#bib.bib47)\], and
identify typical understanding, success, and error patterns, which are
then distilled into an experience item. Finally, in the experience
update stage, the experience bank is dynamically revised (via insertion,
modification, and deletion), enabling the model to form a self-evolving
cycle that progressively stabilizes the rubric to strike a balance
between generality and specificity.

Evaluating such techniques poses an additional challenge, as no publicly
available dataset is readily suitable for this
purpose \[[37](#bib.bib3)\]. To address this, we construct SWE-TQ based
on the human annotations of SWE-bench \[[23](#bib.bib9)\] and
Multi-SWE-bench \[[60](#bib.bib16)\], which consists of a Python subset
SWE-TQ-Python and a multilingual subset SWE-TQ-Multilingual covering
seven other programming languages. Specifically, we retain the instances
on which all annotators agree, split them into historical data for
experience accumulation and a test set, and apply an additional round of
manual cleaning to remove typical annotation errors. This process
results in 1,076/1,907 instances for task clarity assessment and
833/1,739 instances for test coverage assessment in
SWE-TQ-Python/Multilingual, respectively.

On this benchmark, we evaluate ExLabel through two assessments using
three LLMs, Qwen3.8-27B \[[45](#bib.bib5)\],
DeepSeek-V4-Flash \[[10](#bib.bib4)\], and GPT-5.6
Luna \[[39](#bib.bib7)\], and compare it with five baselines.
Experimental results show that ExLabel achieves the best F1 in all 12
combinations of assessment tasks, subsets, and backbone models, with
relative improvements in average F1 of 44.2%/49.9% and 24.0%/18.4% over
the best-performing baseline for task clarity and test coverage
assessment in SWE-TQ-Python/Multilingual, respectively. Moreover,
ExLabel achieves average FPRs of 3.3% and 15.1% for task clarity and
test coverage assessment, respectively, indicating that it filters out
low-quality instances with limited impact on valid ones. Ablation
studies further demonstrate that removing the experience bank or any of
its key components leads to significant performance degradation,
highlighting the effectiveness of our design.

Beyond assessment accuracy, we further evaluate whether ExLabel can
improve the quality of SWE data used for coding-agent training. We apply
ExLabel to filter 5k SWE-rebench instances \[[3](#bib.bib35)\] and
fine-tune Qwen3.5-9B-Base \[[44](#bib.bib6)\] on the resolved
trajectories of the retained instances. The resulting model achieves a
66.2% resolution rate on SWE-bench Verified, outperforming training with
data filtered by the best-performing baseline by 14.5% and with the
unfiltered dataset by 21.7%, while reducing the cost per resolved
trajectory to \$0.46.

In summary, our main contributions are as follows:

- •
  We propose ExLabel, a novel LLM-based, experience-guided assistant for
  task quality assessment in SWE datasets. ExLabel introduces a
  self-evolving experience bank that accumulates cross-instance
  knowledge from historical annotations into a stable yet adaptive
  judgment rubric and reuses relevant experience to guide the assessment
  of new instances.
- •
  We construct SWE-TQ, a benchmark for task clarity and test coverage
  assessment spanning eight programming languages, by manually cleaning
  the human annotations of SWE-bench \[[8](#bib.bib1)\] and
  Multi-SWE-bench \[[60](#bib.bib16)\].
- •
  We comprehensively evaluate ExLabel on SWE-TQ, and it achieves the
  best F1 in all settings with relative improvements in average F1 of
  44.2%/49.9% and 24.0%/18.4% on the two assessments in
  SWE-TQ-Python/Multilingual, respectively, compared with the
  best-performing baseline.
- •
  We demonstrate the downstream effectiveness of ExLabel by applying it
  to filter 5k SWE-rebench instances \[[3](#bib.bib35)\] for
  coding-agent training. Fine-tuning Qwen3.5-9B-Base on the filtered
  data achieves a 66.2% resolution rate on SWE-bench Verified,
  outperforming the best-performing baseline by 14.5% and the unfiltered
  data by 21.7%.

## 2 Background and Motivation

![Refer to caption](2610.39417v1/motivation.png)

Figure 1: A motivating example of django-15869 in SWE-bench.

### 2.1 Problem Definition

In this work, we mainly focus on assessing the task quality in SWE
datasets, especially in solvability and verifiability. Each SWE instance
typically consists of a task description, a corresponding reference
solution (golden patch), and a set of test suites used to validate
candidate solutions (test patch). However, not all SWE instances are
equally well-defined or verifiable. Ambiguous or incomplete task
descriptions can make it difficult to determine the intended solution,
while inadequate test suites may fail to reliably distinguish correct
solutions from incorrect ones \[[8](#bib.bib1), [56](#bib.bib27),
[59](#bib.bib26)\]. Following prior work \[[8](#bib.bib1),
[21](#bib.bib2), [37](#bib.bib3)\], we assess the SWE instance in two
dimensions: Task Clarity and Test Coverage. Together, these dimensions
aim to determine whether a given SWE instance is sufficiently
well-specified and properly verifiable to serve as a meaningful instance
for the SWE dataset.

- •
  Task Clarity Assessment evaluates whether the task description
  provides sufficient and unambiguous information to solve the task,
  with the golden and test patches serving as references. However, if
  necessary information can only be inferred from the patches but is
  absent from the task description, the task is still considered
  underspecified. External discussions and linked resources are excluded
  from the assessment \[[37](#bib.bib3), [8](#bib.bib1),
  [21](#bib.bib2)\]. Given a SWE instance, task clarity assessment
  assigns a Boolean label, where True indicates that the task is
  underspecified and False indicates that it is well-specified, together
  with a note explaining the reasons and evidence for the judgment.
- •
  Test Coverage Assessment examines whether the provided test suite is
  appropriate and sufficient to validate correct solutions to the task.
  Given an instance in a SWE dataset, the test coverage assessment aims
  to assign a Boolean label mainly according to the test patch, where
  True indicates that the test suite is inadequate, and False indicates
  that the test suite is adequate, with a predicted note to explain the
  reasons.

### 2.2 Motivating Example

Figure [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2 Background and Motivation ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")
presents an instance from the Django project in
SWE-bench \[[23](#bib.bib9)\]. The task asks to deprecate the length_is
template filter in favor of length. To implement this requirement, the
golden patch raises a RemovedInDjango51Warning when length_is is invoked
and provides a specific warning message recommending alternative usage.
The test patch verifies both the warning type and its exact message
through assertRaisesMessage. Human annotators judge this test suite as
inadequate because the task description requires the filter to be
deprecated but does not specify the exact warning message. Consequently,
a valid solution that raises the correct deprecation warning with
different wording may still fail the test. Without relevant prior
experience, the LLM instead predicts the test suite as adequate. As
illustrated in
Figure [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2 Background and Motivation ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent"),
experience accumulated from previous annotations can provide a general
guideline that tests should avoid imposing implementation details not
explicitly required by the task. With this experience, the LLM
recognizes the exact-message check as over-specification and correctly
predicts the test suite as inadequate. This example motivates us to
accumulate experience from historical annotations and reuse relevant
experience when assessing new SWE instances.

## 3 Approach

![Refer to caption](2610.39417v1/overview.png)

Figure 2: Overview of ExLabel.

In this section, we introduce ExLabel, a self-evolving experience-guided
assistant to assess task clarity and test coverage in SWE datasets. As
illustrated in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent"),
ExLabel maintains an experience bank as the central vehicle of
self-evolution to accumulate and reuse experience. Specifically, it
consists of two primary phases: experience accumulation and experience
reuse. In the experience accumulation phase
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")),
we begin with an empty experience bank and apply an LLM-based (e.g.,
GPT-5.6 Luna \[[39](#bib.bib7)\]) retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
method \[[41](#bib.bib45), [27](#bib.bib44)\] to each labeled instance
to predict its annotation. Subsequently, by comparing the predicted
annotation with the golden annotation, we perform
self-reflection \[[46](#bib.bib47), [19](#bib.bib46)\] that guides the
LLM to reflect on its reasoning trajectories to generate experience.
Self-reflection can help the LLM transform concrete RAG-based reasoning
trajectories into generalized experience, thereby enabling
cross-instance reuse. To achieve self-evolution, we further manipulate
and update the experience bank using three predefined operation
interfaces. A generalized experience bank is finally formed through
continuous self-evolution that extracts high-consensus patterns and
typical error patterns from a large amount of historical annotation
data. When a new instance arrives, ExLabel leverages the cross-instance
information formed through self-evolution to predict its annotation by
reusing the accumulated experiences
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Experience Reuse ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")).

### 3.1 Experience Accumulation

The experience accumulation phase aims to extract high-consensus
patterns, successful annotation strategies, and typical error patterns
from annotated instances as experience items that iteratively update the
experience bank. It takes a SWE instance and its golden annotation
(i.e., golden label and golden notes) as input and returns an experience
item to update the experience bank. We design three stages for
experience accumulation: rollout, reflection, and experience update.
Specifically, in the rollout stage
(Section [3.1.1](#S3.SS1.SSS1 "3.1.1 Rollout ‣ 3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")),
we use an LLM-based RAG pipeline to retrieve experience from the current
experience bank and, based on it, predict the annotation of the
instance. In the reflection stage
(Section [3.1.2](#S3.SS1.SSS2 "3.1.2 Reflection ‣ 3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")),
we compare the current predicted annotation with the golden annotation
and apply a self-reflection mechanism to generate experience. In the
experience update stage
(Section [3.1.3](#S3.SS1.SSS3 "3.1.3 Experience Update ‣ 3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")),
we provide the LLM with an interface for operating on the experience
bank, enabling it to insert, modify, or delete the generated experience.

#### 3.1.1 Rollout

This stage aims to generate predicted annotations (i.e., predicted
labels and notes) for SWE instances based on the current experience
bank, using a RAG-based pipeline. The process can be divided into the
following three steps.

![Refer to caption](2610.39417v1/task_feature.png)

Figure 3: An example of the summarized descriptions along three
dimensions for django-14453 in SWE-bench.

Step I: Summarization of the Description in Three Dimensions. A
prerequisite for forming experience is a structured understanding of SWE
instances. Given that human annotators may interpret task descriptions
from multiple perspectives \[[30](#bib.bib58)\], we employ an LLM to
summarize each SWE instance along multiple dimensions to provide a
structured understanding of the instance. The dimensions are defined as
follows:

- •
  Task Type: A generalized categorization provided by the LLM using SWE
  instances (e.g., ConfigurationError, Variable-ReferenceError), which
  is used to distinguish different task intents.
- •
  Task Explanation: A generalized, concise description of the SWE
  instance while preserving its core semantics. It describes the
  underlying causes, trigger conditions, and typical scenarios.
- •
  Test Explanation: A summary of the test patch’s logic, validation
  objectives, and its correspondence with the SWE instance, which is
  used to distinguish different test behaviors.

These three dimensions correspond to the three components of a SWE
instance, and we consider them capable of reflecting the information
contained in the SWE instance. We first generate descriptions that
differentiate among task types, as tasks of the same type may share
similar solutions. Moreover, we summarize the golden solution and test
suites, originally in the code format, of the SWE instances into natural
language (NL) descriptions to bridge the semantic gap between code and
text. The resulting descriptions are concatenated and encoded using an
embedding model to form features for indexing experience items (as
detailed in Step II). To help better understand the summarized
descriptions, we provide an example shown in
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1.1 Rollout ‣ 3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent").
By constructing and leveraging these descriptions, we achieve a
transformation from specific, bimodal SWE instance data to generalized,
NL-based data, which not only bridges the semantic gap between text and
code but also provides the foundation for generalizing similar
experiences and retrieving relevant experience items.

Step II: Experience Retrieval. This step aims to retrieve, rerank, and
select the most relevant experience items from the experience bank. It
allows the LLM to autonomously make predictions based on the existing
experience bank, while providing signals for potential refinement of the
experience bank. Following prior work \[[33](#bib.bib36),
[32](#bib.bib33)\], we adopt a hybrid strategy that combines sparse
retrieval and dense retrieval. For each SWE instance, we first
concatenate the three summarized descriptions generated in Step I to
form a unified textual representation. We then compute similarity scores
between the query instance and each experience item (as detailed in
Section [3.1.2](#S3.SS1.SSS2 "3.1.2 Reflection ‣ 3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent"))
in the current experience bank from both sparse and dense perspectives.
The representation of the three-dimensional descriptions constitutes the
key (index) of an experience item. Specifically, we adopt the widely
used BM25 \[[47](#bib.bib37)\] algorithm for sparse retrieval to capture
lexical similarity. For dense retrieval, we use an LLM-based embedding
model (e.g., Qwen3-Embedding-8B \[[62](#bib.bib8)\]) to obtain
semantically rich representations. The similarity scores from both
retrieval methods are normalized and averaged to produce a final
similarity score. Based on this score, experience items are ranked, and
the Top-$`K`$ items are selected. This hybrid design leverages the
complementary strengths of sparse and dense retrieval: BM25 captures
lexical similarity \[[12](#bib.bib38)\], while dense embeddings encode
semantic and structural similarity \[[24](#bib.bib39)\], leading to more
accurate and robust experience retrieval. Furthermore, we introduce an
LLM-based reranking step to refine the usability of retrieved experience
items. Specifically, the SWE instance and the Top-$`K`$ retrieved items
are provided as context to prompt the LLM, which produces a ranking
based on their relevance and usefulness for annotating the current
instance. We then select the Top-$`N`$ (where $`N<K`$) items from the
reranked list as the final retrieval results, which serve as reliable
prior knowledge for subsequent prediction generation.

Step III: Prediction Generation. This step aims to predict the
annotation of SWE instances by leveraging the retrieved experience
items. It takes a SWE instance and the Top-$`N`$ experience items
retrieved in Step II as input and requires the LLM to return the
predicted label and predicted notes as predicted annotations.

Figure 4: An example of generated experience about xarray-5362 in
SWE-bench for test coverage assessment.

#### 3.1.2 Reflection

This stage aims to generate experience items by comparing the predicted
annotation with the golden annotation through a self-reflection
mechanism. Self-reflection is used to reflect on the reasoning process
in the rollout stage, which takes the SWE instance, predicted annotation
with Top-$`N`$ retrieved experience items used in the rollout stage, and
the golden annotation as input, and returns new experience items for
updating the experience bank. Specifically, we use the LLM to analyze
the similarities and differences between the predicted annotation and
the golden annotation according to the retrieved experience items in
order to generate new experiences, i.e., the transferable and
generalizable reasoning patterns. To capture different ways in which
annotation outcomes inform future decisions, we design the value of each
experience item along three dimensions: Understanding, Successes, and
Mistakes. These correspond to refined high-consensus understanding,
effective annotation strategies, and recurring error patterns,
respectively. The dimensions are defined as follows:

- •
  Understanding: It is a high-level abstract understanding of the
  problem. It provides a general pattern summarized from the problem and
  supports cross-instance comparison.
- •
  Successes: It records transferable principles, strategies, and
  insights distilled from the current instance. It provides actionable
  guidance for how to make successful predictions in the future in
  similar scenarios.
- •
  Mistakes: It is used to learn from errors when predictions fail. It
  documents typical patterns such as reasoning flaws, overlooked
  evidence, and cognitive biases that lead to incorrect outcomes, and is
  populated only for failed predictions.

Figure [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.1.1 Rollout ‣ 3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")
illustrates an example of experience generated by the LLM for a specific
SWE instance (xarray-5362) from SWE-bench \[[23](#bib.bib9)\]. For
deduplication, each experience item is additionally assigned a unique
identifier (id).

#### 3.1.3 Experience Update

This stage aims to update the experience bank based on newly generated
experiences, thereby enabling its self-evolution. It takes as input the
Top-$`N`$ retrieved experience items from the rollout stage and the
newly generated experience item from the reflection stage, and updates
the experience bank through three predefined operations: insertion,
modification, and deletion. The self-evolution process is implemented
via an LLM-driven maintenance procedure, which generates an ordered
sequence of operations over the experience bank based on the input and
executes them accordingly. The interfaces corresponding to these
operations are described in
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.1.3 Experience Update ‣ 3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent").

Operation Parameters Explanation Insert item Add a new experience item
and allocate an ID when the current case reveals a novel pattern. Modify
ID, experience Refine the value of an existing experience item when
prior experience needs clarification or correction. Delete ID Remove an

Additionally, to ensure that the newly identified experience is
explicitly anchored to the current instance and can be independently
retrieved in the future, we insert a new experience item generated in
the reflection stage, when the operation list contains a Modify but no
Insert. Experience items that reference the same experience share a
common id, so that we can deduplicate by this id and keep only one
experience item during retrieval. After all operations are executed, the
refreshed experience bank is used for the next round of rollout and
reflection, forming a closed loop that continuously improves subsequent
predictions. The detailed prompt template for the experience
accumulation phase can be found in our [replication
package](#Sx1 "Data Availability ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent").

### 3.2 Experience Reuse

This phase aims to apply the constructed experience bank to annotate new
unlabeled instances, which takes an unlabeled SWE instance as input and
returns the predicted label and predicted notes. It shares the same RAG
workflow with the rollout stage in the experience accumulation phase
(Section [3.1.1](#S3.SS1.SSS1 "3.1.1 Rollout ‣ 3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")).
Specifically, it first generates three summarized descriptions for a SWE
instance and uses their textual representation as a query to retrieve
and rerank the Top-$`N`$ relevant experience items from the accumulated
experience bank. It finally leverages the experience of the selected
experience items as contextual information to predict the label and
notes of the current instance.

## 4 SWE-TQ: A Benchmark for Task Clarity and Test Coverage Assessment

### 4.1 Benchmark Construction

There is currently no publicly available dataset with human annotations
for evaluating automated task quality assessment techniques.
SPICE \[[37](#bib.bib3)\], the closest work to ours, only releases
SPICE-Bench, which is annotated using its proposed automated technique,
while its human-annotated evaluation dataset remains closed-source.
Therefore, we construct SWE-TQ, a new benchmark for evaluating automated
task clarity and test coverage assessment techniques, following a
construction process similar to that of SPICE and aligned with common
practices in empirical SE \[[4](#bib.bib48)\]. SWE-TQ consists of two
subsets: SWE-TQ-Python ¹¹ 1
[https://cdn.openai.com/introducing-swe-bench-verified/swe-bench-annotation-results.zip](https://cdn.openai.com/introducing-swe-bench-verified/swe-bench-annotation-results.zip),
built from the human annotations of SWE-bench \[[8](#bib.bib1)\], and
SWE-TQ-Multilingual ²² 2
[https://github.com/multi-swe-bench/multi-swe-bench/tree/main/docs/manual-verification/annotation-results](https://github.com/multi-swe-bench/multi-swe-bench/tree/main/docs/manual-verification/annotation-results),
built from the human annotations of
Multi-SWE-bench \[[60](#bib.bib16)\], which covers seven programming
languages (i.e., C, C++, Go, Java, JavaScript, Rust, and TypeScript).
Both subsets are constructed through three steps: annotation collection,
data splitting, and manual cleaning.

Annotation Collection. We collect the human annotations of
SWE-bench \[[8](#bib.bib1)\] and Multi-SWE-bench \[[60](#bib.bib16)\]
for SWE-TQ-Python and SWE-TQ-Multilingual, respectively. In SWE-bench,
each of the 1,699 instances annotated by OpenAI is independently labeled
by three annotators, while in Multi-SWE-bench, each of the 2,456
candidate instances is independently labeled by two annotators.
Following their respective annotation guidelines \[[8](#bib.bib1),
[60](#bib.bib16)\], we convert the original annotations into binary
labels. For both datasets, we apply the same filtering strategy and
retain only the instances on which all annotators reach complete
agreement on the binary labels.

Data Splitting. For each subset, we split the instances into historical
data for experience accumulation and a test set. For test coverage
assessment, we adopt a temporal split based on commit timestamps, using
the most recent 20% of instances as the test set. Since test coverage
assessment primarily evaluates the test patch, which may be affected by
the evolving state of the codebase, we adopt this more conservative
strategy to temporally separate experience accumulation from test
instances. In contrast, task clarity assessment primarily concerns
whether the task description provides sufficient information to solve
the task
(Section [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Problem Definition ‣ 2 Background and Motivation ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent"))
and is less subject to such temporal dependencies. Given the class
imbalance in task clarity assessment, a 20% temporal split would leave
very few underspecified instances in the test set (10 for SWE-TQ-Python
and 24 for SWE-TQ-Multilingual). Therefore, we randomly sample 30% of
the instances as the test set to include a sufficient number of
underspecified instances for evaluation.

Manual Cleaning. To further ensure dataset reliability, two authors
manually review all instances in SWE-TQ-Python and SWE-TQ-Multilingual
to identify two recurring annotation patterns that are inconsistent with
OpenAI’s annotation criteria \[[8](#bib.bib1)\]. For task clarity
assessment, we remove instances labeled as well-specified when key
information (e.g., error messages) is provided only through external
links excluded by the annotation criteria. Allowing LLMs to access such
external resources may introduce data leakage or benchmark
contamination \[[7](#bib.bib57), [11](#bib.bib56)\], unpredictable
environmental noise \[[34](#bib.bib55), [43](#bib.bib54)\], and
reproducibility issues \[[14](#bib.bib53)\]. For test coverage
assessment, we remove instances labeled as adequate when the test patch
assumes an exact error message that is not specified in the task
description. We remove these instances rather than relabeling them
because each annotation contains both a label and notes justifying the
label: changing the label alone would make it inconsistent with the
original notes, while rewriting the notes would introduce the authors’
own judgments into the human annotations, potentially biasing experience
accumulation and evaluation. More details are provided in
Section [7.1](#S7.SS1 "7.1 Typical Annotation Errors in SWE Datasets ‣ 7 Discussion ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent").
Instances for which the two authors fail to reach consensus are also
removed. The two authors achieve a Cohen’s kappa \[[9](#bib.bib59)\] of
0.928 on their cleaning decisions, indicating strong
agreement \[[26](#bib.bib60)\]. Overall, manual cleaning removes 34 and
148 instances from SWE-TQ-Python and 136 and 384 instances from
SWE-TQ-Multilingual for task clarity and test coverage assessment,
respectively.

## 5 Experiment Setup

### 5.1 Research Questions

- •
  RQ1 (Effectiveness): How effective is ExLabel in assessing task
  clarity and test coverage compared with existing automated techniques?
- •
  RQ2 (Ablation): How do the key components of ExLabel contribute to its
  overall effectiveness?
- •
  RQ3 (Downstream Effectiveness): Can the dataset filtering performed by
  ExLabel benefit the training of coding agents?
- •
  RQ4 (Case Study): How does the experience of ExLabel help it perform
  better?

### 5.2 Baselines

We mainly compare ExLabel with the open-source state-of-the-art baseline
method, SPICE \[[37](#bib.bib3)\]. Additionally, we include direct
prompting (i.e., Vanilla) and a simple RAG approach as baselines to
highlight the effectiveness of our self-evolving mechanism. We further
include two state-of-the-art general-purpose coding agents,
Codex \[[38](#bib.bib42)\] and Claude Code \[[2](#bib.bib43)\], to
examine whether autonomous exploration of the repository is sufficient
for assessing task quality. The implementation details of baselines are
described in
Section [5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 Implementation Details ‣ 5 Experiment Setup ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent").

- •
  Vanilla: The LLM directly predicts the annotation using only the SWE
  instance without any additional context, which serves as a lower-bound
  reference for zero-shot performance.
- •
  SimpleRAG: The LLM predicts the annotation with retrieval-augmented
  generation, where the historical annotated instances are stored as
  external memory and the three most similar ones are retrieved as
  additional context for each SWE instance.
- •
  SPICE: For task clarity assessment, SPICE utilizes developer-provided
  reasoning patterns as guidance for prediction. For test coverage
  assessment, it leverages Aider \[[1](#bib.bib41)\], an open-source
  command-line AI programming agent, to incorporate repository-aware
  code as additional context.
- •
  Codex & Claude Code: General-purpose coding agents developed by OpenAI
  and Anthropic, respectively, which autonomously explore the repository
  and read relevant code to predict the annotation of each SWE instance.

### 5.3 Evaluation Metrics

Since the goal of both tasks is to identify unsolvable or unverifiable
instances, following prior detection work \[[64](#bib.bib63),
[6](#bib.bib64)\], we regard such instances as positive and adopt the
following metrics.

- •
  F1-score: The harmonic mean of precision and recall, where precision
  measures the proportion of predicted positive instances that are truly
  positive, and recall measures the proportion of positive instances
  that are correctly identified. We adopt the F1-score as the primary
  metric because it balances precision and recall when identifying
  positive instances.
- •
  False Positive Rate (FPR): The proportion of negative instances
  incorrectly predicted as positive. Since instances predicted as
  positive are filtered out, FPR measures the proportion of valid (i.e.,
  solvable or verifiable) instances that are mistakenly discarded and
  thus reflects the data loss caused by filtering.

In addition, we report the average number of tokens (\#Token) and
monetary cost (\$Cost) consumed per instance to measure the efficiency
of each technique. The corresponding precision and recall values for all
techniques are further reported in [online
Appendix A](#Sx1 "Data Availability ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent").

### 5.4 Implementation Details

We evaluate all techniques on the test data of both SWE-TQ-Python and
SWE-TQ-Multilingual described in
Section [4](#S4 "4 SWE-TQ: A Benchmark for Task Clarity and Test Coverage Assessment ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent"),
and ExLabel accumulates experience on the merged historical data of both
subsets for each task, which also serves as the retrieval pool of
SimpleRAG. We further analyze how the size of the historical data
affects the effectiveness in [online
Appendix B](#Sx1 "Data Availability ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent").
We use Qwen3.8-27B \[[45](#bib.bib5)\],
DeepSeek-V4-Flash \[[10](#bib.bib4)\], and GPT-5.6
Luna \[[39](#bib.bib7)\] as the backbone models for all methods, as they
represent models from different sources (i.e., open-source or
closed-source) and families (i.e., dense or MoE). We use
Qwen3-Embedding-8B \[[62](#bib.bib8)\] to generate dense representations
for retrieval, as it has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance
across many information retrieval tasks \[[36](#bib.bib65)\]. This model
is used in both ExLabel and SimpleRAG for computing embeddings to ensure
a fair comparison. All backbone models are accessed through their
official APIs.

For ExLabel, during experience retrieval
(Section [3.1.1](#S3.SS1.SSS1 "3.1.1 Rollout ‣ 3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")),
we first retrieve the Top-10 candidate experience items, then rerank
them to select the Top-3 as the final result, following prior experience
retrieval work \[[29](#bib.bib61), [25](#bib.bib62)\]. Since the
experience bank is initially empty during the accumulation phase
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Experience Accumulation ‣ 3 Approach ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")),
ExLabel makes predictions without experience when no items are
retrieved. The experience generated from this prediction is then
inserted into the experience bank as a cold start. For SimpleRAG, we
concatenate the task description, golden patch, and test patch of each
instance as its textual representation, retrieve the three most similar
historical instances by cosine similarity using the same embedding model
as ExLabel, and use them together with their annotations as few-shot
examples for prediction. To facilitate reproducibility, we uniformly set
$`\texttt{temperature}=0`$ for annotation prediction across all
approaches.

### 5.5 Downstream Effectiveness Analysis

We evaluate the downstream impact of dataset filtering by comparing
coding models trained on data retained by different task quality
assessment techniques for software issue resolution. Specifically, we
first randomly sample 5k instances from SWE-rebench \[[3](#bib.bib35)\],
a large-scale SWE dataset constructed through automated data collection,
and sample the corresponding trajectories using
DeepSeek-V4-Flash \[[10](#bib.bib4)\]. To ensure a fair comparison, we
apply ExLabel and all baselines with GPT-5.6 Luna as the backbone model
to filter out each instance predicted as either underspecified or
inadequate, together with its trajectories. For ExLabel, we directly
reuse the experience banks accumulated with GPT-5.6 Luna in RQ1. We then
perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on
Qwen3.5-9B-Base \[[44](#bib.bib6)\] using the resolved trajectories of
the remaining instances and evaluate the resulting models on SWE-bench
Verified \[[8](#bib.bib1)\]. The detailed training recipe is provided in
[online
Appendix C](#Sx1 "Data Availability ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent").
We also include the base model without fine-tuning (Base Model) and the
model trained on all 5k instances without filtering (Full Data) as
references. For each technique, we report the number of filtered
instances (#Filtered), the number of remaining resolved trajectories
(#Trajs), the annotation cost (\$Annotation), the sampling cost
(\$Sampling), and their total cost per trajectory (\$Cost/#Trajs), and
adopt the resolution rate on SWE-bench Verified (Resolved%) as the
primary metric. Note that \$Sampling is calculated only for the
instances retained by each technique, reflecting the expected sampling
cost when filtering is performed before trajectory sampling.

### 6.2 RQ2: Ablation

We conduct ablation studies on both task clarity and test coverage
assessments by removing key components or experience fields from the
ExLabel design. Specifically, we evaluate the following variants: w/o
Experience Bank, w/o Reranking, w/o Field (Successes, Mistakes), w/o
Field (Successes), and w/o Field (Mistakes). All experiments are
performed using GPT-5.6 Luna to assess the contribution of each
component. As shown in
Table [4](#S6.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 6.1 RQ1: Effectiveness ‣ 6 Experimental Results ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent"),
ExLabel consistently outperforms all five variants in terms of F1,
highlighting the effectiveness of its component design.

- •
  w/o Experience Bank: Removing the experience bank reduces F1 by
  25.5%–35.7% on task clarity assessment and 49.3%–84.0% on test
  coverage assessment. Although this variant yields a lower FPR on test
  coverage assessment, it identifies far fewer inadequate instances.
  This suggests that without prior experience, the LLM lacks
  cross-instance guidance and is more prone to incorrect reasoning.
- •
  w/o Reranking: Removing reranking lowers F1 by 20.1%–34.2% and
  4.3%–6.7% on the two tasks, respectively. This is likely because less
  precise retrieval introduces irrelevant context, increasing noise and
  distracting the LLM from the core issue.
- •
  w/o Field (Successes): Removing the Successes field leads to F1 drops
  of 12.1%–17.5% and 4.3% on the two tasks, respectively. This indicates
  that Successes provides useful reasoning patterns that guide correct
  predictions.
- •
  w/o Field (Mistakes): Removing the Mistakes field decreases F1 by
  23.9%–24.9% and 3.1%–5.0% on the two tasks, respectively. This
  suggests that Mistakes helps the LLM avoid recurring reasoning errors
  by highlighting common pitfalls.
- •
  w/o Field (Successes, Mistakes): Removing both Successes and Mistakes
  reduces F1 by 26.5%–29.7% and 7.3%–9.3% on the two tasks,
  respectively. This shows that the two fields play complementary roles,
  jointly providing positive guidance and error correction.

These results demonstrate that each component of ExLabel plays an
indispensable role in enabling experience extraction with
self-reflection and the self-evolution of the experience bank, and they
constitute essential elements of ExLabel ’s effectiveness. We further
present a representative case in [online
Appendix D](#Sx1 "Data Availability ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")
to illustrate how each component affects the prediction.

RQ2 Summary: All components of ExLabel are effective, and the experience
bank is the most critical one, whose removal reduces F1 by 48.6% on
average.

### 6.4 RQ4: Case Study

To understand how the experience of ExLabel helps it outperform the
baselines, we manually inspect representative cases in RQ1 and find that
the experience provides annotation-specific knowledge that additional
context alone cannot offer. For example, in the test coverage case shown
in
Figure [5](#S6.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 6.3 RQ3: Downstream Effectiveness ‣ 6 Experimental Results ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent"),
the task description only asks to deprecate the length_is template
filter, while the test patch uses assertRaisesMessage to check the exact
warning message implemented in the golden patch. Therefore, a valid
solution that raises the same warning with a different message would
fail the test, making the test suite inadequate. Vanilla notices that
the test patch aligns with the warning in the golden patch but treats
this alignment as evidence of adequacy, overlooking that such alignment
is exactly the source of over-specification. SimpleRAG retrieves a
historical instance about the urlize function, whose annotation concerns
URL handling and provides no guidance on over-specified tests. SPICE
locates the relevant test file and source file but still concludes that
the tests cover all reasonable solutions, indicating that access to the
relevant code alone is insufficient to judge whether the tests are
over-specified. Codex and Claude Code explore the repository and find
other deprecation tests that also assert exact warning messages, and
thus regard the message as intended behavior rather than an
implementation detail. However, such repository conventions only reflect
how developers write tests, rather than whether the tests accept all
valid solutions to the task. In contrast, ExLabel retrieves the
experience “Avoid over-specification that could reject reasonable
alternative implementations” distilled from historical annotations,
which directs the LLM to examine whether the test constrains details
beyond the task description and thus identify the check on the exact
warning message. This case illustrates that ExLabel’s experience
provides more effective judgment criteria that may not be readily
available from the instance itself, similar historical instances, or
repository context.

## 7 Discussion

### 7.1 Typical Annotation Errors in SWE Datasets

![Refer to caption](2610.39417v1/inconsistent_cases.png)

Figure 6: Examples of typical annotation error patterns in task clarity
(left) and test coverage assessments (right).

During the manual cleaning of SWE-TQ, we have observed the presence of
incorrect annotations in the datasets for both task clarity and test
coverage assessments, consistent with the observations of OpenAI’s
annotation team \[[8](#bib.bib1)\], mainly manifested as follows.

For task clarity assessment, a typical annotation error occurs in
instances whose task descriptions provide key information through
external links \[[8](#bib.bib1)\]. Since LLMs are required not to access
the internet when completing the task, information provided through
external links is invalid \[[23](#bib.bib9), [7](#bib.bib57),
[11](#bib.bib56)\]. Therefore, such task descriptions should be labeled
as underspecified. Yet, they are incorrectly labeled as well-specified,
which is inconsistent with the criteria for task clarity. For example,
as shown in
Figure [6](#S7.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 7.1 Typical Annotation Errors in SWE Datasets ‣ 7 Discussion ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")
(left), the task description contains three external links that document
code snippets relevant to the task and their corresponding
documentation. However, since the LLM cannot access these external
links, the information within them cannot be obtained, resulting in
missing key information in the task description. Without access to the
code snippets corresponding to the images, it is nearly impossible for
the LLM to understand what the parameters “False, 1” refer to, thereby
rendering the task unsolvable.

For test coverage assessment, we further filter another type of
annotation error, i.e., instances labeled as adequate even though the
task description does not specify the exact error message assumed by the
test patch. Specifically, the golden patch implements the logic of
raising an exception for certain inputs, whereas the corresponding test
patch performs assertions by matching the error message as a string,
making the test suite overly specific. For example, as shown in
Figure [6](#S7.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 7.1 Typical Annotation Errors in SWE Datasets ‣ 7 Discussion ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent")
(right), the test patch checks for specific error messages such as
“manage.py subparser foo: error: argument bar: invalid int value:
'twelve'”. But this error message is not provided in the task
description, which causes an LLM that generates a similar error message
to be judged as failing the task, even when it has in fact solved it,
simply because it cannot pass the test. This is a typical instance of an
inadequate test suite, yet it is incorrectly annotated as adequate.

By identifying and filtering the annotation errors of these two widely
acknowledged patterns \[[8](#bib.bib1)\], we construct SWE-TQ as a
high-quality benchmark for evaluating automated annotation methods,
thereby ensuring the validity of the evaluation.

### 7.2 Threats to Validity

Internal Validity. The datasets used in our experiments were manually
annotated by the annotators of SWE-bench Verified and Multi-SWE-bench.
However, manual annotation may still contain errors. To mitigate this
threat, we retain only the instances on which all annotators reach
complete agreement, and two of the authors further review all instances
following the annotation criteria released by OpenAI \[[8](#bib.bib1)\]
to filter out erroneous annotations, achieving a Cohen’s kappa of 0.928.

External Validity. Our evaluation focuses on issue resolution, as
existing SWE benchmarks are predominantly constructed around this task
setting. Nevertheless, issue resolution encompasses diverse SE
scenarios, including bug fixing, feature implementation, and feature
optimization \[[60](#bib.bib16)\], allowing SWE-TQ to evaluate ExLabel
across these diverse scenarios. Moreover, ExLabel is not inherently tied
to issue resolution, as its general interfaces and easily adaptable
prompt templates facilitate its extension to other task settings.

## 8 Related Work

### 8.1 SWE-bench-style Datasets

With the rapid advancement of LLMs for SE tasks, increasing research
attention has been devoted to constructing datasets for training and
evaluating LLMs on diverse tasks (e.g., bug fixing and feature requests)
in real-world software repositories. A prominent family of such datasets
is SWE-bench-style datasets. Unlike traditional static code
collections \[[31](#bib.bib12), [17](#bib.bib13), [65](#bib.bib14)\],
SWE-bench-style datasets provide each instance with an executable
environment, allowing LLM-based agents to interact with the repository,
resolve tasks based on natural-language descriptions, and validate their
solutions through execution. Existing SWE-bench-style datasets can
broadly be divided into benchmark and training datasets.

Benchmark datasets are typically collected from real-world repositories
and equipped with executable environments. For example,
SWE-bench \[[23](#bib.bib9)\] collects 2,294 executable instances from
12 real-world software repositories to evaluate LLMs on issue
resolution. Subsequent studies extend SWE-bench to
multilingual \[[60](#bib.bib16), [61](#bib.bib22)\] and
multimodal \[[57](#bib.bib15)\] settings. SWE-Perf \[[16](#bib.bib17)\]
and FEA-bench \[[28](#bib.bib18)\] further target performance
optimization and feature addition, respectively, broadening the task
scope while retaining the basic SWE-bench setting. Training datasets
additionally employ automated or synthetic construction to increase data
scale. For example, SWE-Smith \[[58](#bib.bib19)\] introduces a reverse
synthesis approach that starts from runnable code and automatically
injects defects to generate new tasks. Similarly,
SWE-Synth \[[42](#bib.bib20)\] uses LLMs to simulate the debugging
process, generating code fixes, test cases, and repair trajectories.
BugPilot \[[52](#bib.bib21)\] synthesizes data by guiding coding agents
to introduce new features into codebases and leveraging unintended test
failures arising during this process to construct new tasks.

Despite substantial efforts devoted to constructing SWE-bench-style
datasets \[[61](#bib.bib22), [54](#bib.bib23), [53](#bib.bib24),
[58](#bib.bib19)\], quality issues such as underspecified task
descriptions and inadequate test suites have been observed. For example,
even SWE-bench Verified, which undergoes manual
verification \[[8](#bib.bib1)\], has been reported to contain instances
with insufficient test coverage \[[56](#bib.bib27), [59](#bib.bib26)\].
Automated and synthetic construction may further introduce quality risks
as datasets scale \[[58](#bib.bib19), [54](#bib.bib23)\]. These concerns
motivate techniques for systematically assessing task quality before SWE
datasets are used for evaluation or training.

### 8.2 Quality Assessment of SWE Datasets

Before task clarity and test coverage were explicitly introduced as
criteria for verifying SWE-bench instances \[[8](#bib.bib1)\], prior
work had explored automated quality assessment for various software
artifacts. For example, Sheikhaei et al. \[[50](#bib.bib28)\]
investigated automated labeling of self-admitted technical debt (SATD)
in code comments, comparing in-context learning with fine-tuned base
models and showing the potential of LLMs for automated software-artifact
assessment. For SWE-bench-style datasets, however, task quality
assessment remains challenging because determining whether a task is
sufficiently specified and adequately tested requires contextual
judgment over the task description and associated
patches \[[37](#bib.bib3)\]. In practice, existing SWE datasets largely
rely on human annotation and verification for task quality
assessment \[[8](#bib.bib1), [21](#bib.bib2)\]. For example,
OpenAI \[[8](#bib.bib1)\] recruited 93 professional Python developers to
independently annotate 1,699 SWE-bench instances, resulting in the
SWE-bench Verified subset. Multi-SWE-bench \[[60](#bib.bib16)\] and
Doc2Feat-bench \[[21](#bib.bib2)\] similarly employ manual annotation
based on the criteria introduced by SWE-bench Verified.

Moreover, SPICE \[[37](#bib.bib3)\] makes an initial attempt at
automated annotation for SWE-bench-style datasets. It employs separate
pipelines for task clarity and test coverage assessment: the former uses
manually summarized patterns of underspecified task descriptions, while
the latter leverages agent-based repository exploration to assess test
adequacy. However, such approaches assess individual instances using
predefined criteria or instance-specific context, without explicitly
accumulating and reusing knowledge learned from assessments across
instances. In contrast, ExLabel introduces a self-evolving experience
bank that continuously extracts generalizable judgment criteria from
historical experience and retrieves relevant experience to guide new
instances. This enables task quality assessment to benefit from
cross-instance knowledge rather than treating each instance
independently.

## 9 Conclusion

In this work, we propose ExLabel, an LLM-based, self-evolving,
experience-guided assistant to assess task quality in SWE datasets.
ExLabel extracts common patterns and typical error patterns from
historically annotated data through a self-evolving loop to form
experience, thereby overcoming the limitations on cross-instance
information utilization imposed by the stateless nature of LLMs. We
evaluate task quality from two perspectives: task clarity and test
coverage, which concern solvability and verifiability, respectively. To
support the evaluation, we clean the human annotations of SWE-bench and
Multi-SWE-bench and construct SWE-TQ, a benchmark spanning eight
programming languages. The experimental results on SWE-TQ show that
ExLabel achieves the best F1 in all settings, with relative improvements
in average F1 of 44.2%/49.9% and 24.0%/18.4% over the best-performing
baseline for task clarity and test coverage assessment in
SWE-TQ-Python/Multilingual, respectively. Moreover, filtering the
training data with ExLabel improves the fine-tuned coding model’s
resolution rate on SWE-bench Verified by 14.5% over the best-performing
baseline.

To investigate how much annotated data is required for the experience
bank to become effective, we accumulate experience on 20%, 40%, 60%, and
80% of the historical data for each task and evaluate ExLabel with
GPT-5.6 Luna on the same test data as RQ1. As shown in
Table [7](#A2.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Appendix B Impact of Historical Data Size ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent"),
accumulating experience on 20% of the historical data, i.e., about 400
annotated instances, improves F1 by 2.7–67.3 percentage points over w/o
Experience Bank in all four combinations of tasks and subsets. On task
clarity assessment, the performance of ExLabel tends to stabilize early,
as F1 shows no clear increase beyond 20% of the historical data. This is
likely because underspecified instances account for only 4.9% of the
historical data, and their typical causes, such as missing key
information in the task description, mainly concern the description
itself, so the experience bank can capture them from a small amount of
data while additional data may contribute limited new experience. On
test coverage assessment, F1 shows an increasing trend from 40% to 100%
of the historical data, rising from 77.5% to 79.4% and from 37.3% to
40.0% in SWE-TQ-Python and SWE-TQ-Multilingual, respectively. This is
likely because inadequate instances account for 32.1% of the historical
data, and their causes, such as test patches relying on specific error
messages or implementation details, depend on the golden patch, the test
patch, and the evolving state of the codebase, which tend to vary across
repositories, so additional data may still contribute new experience.

## Appendix C Training Recipe of RQ3

In RQ3, we perform full-parameter fine-tuning on
Qwen3.5-9B-Base \[[44](#bib.bib6)\] using the
LLaMA-Factory \[[63](#bib.bib25)\] framework on 4 H100 GPUs. Following

set the batch size to 8, the learning rate to $`5\times 10^{-5}`$, the
maximum number of epochs to 3, and the maximum context length to 131,072
tokens, use a cosine learning-rate scheduler, and set the warmup ratio
to 0.1.

## Appendix D Case Study for Ablation

We present an additional representative case for test coverage
assessment in RQ2, as shown in
Figure [7](#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix B Impact of Historical Data Size ‣ Well-Specified and Well-Tested? Assessing SWE-bench-Style Datasets with a Self-Evolving Agent").
In this case, the test patch depends on a specific error string
implemented in the golden patch, namely “Can only compare SkyCoord to
Frame with data”, which is not explicitly stated in the task
description. For w/o Reranking, the retrieved experience emphasizes the
equivalence between the problematic method and a known-working
alternative. This shifts the LLM’s focus away from dependence on a
specific implementation toward verifying behavioral consistency and
semantic correctness. For w/o Field (Mistakes), the retrieved experience
focuses on the core behavior requirement. Although the experience
mentions implementation details, it lacks further elaboration. As a
result, the LLM directly concludes that there is no dependency on
implementation details after checking the core requirement. For w/o
Field (Successes) and w/o Field (Successes, Mistakes), both settings
retrieve identical experience content, which is an understanding of an
unrelated issue about how mock arguments are compared during test
collection. This content lacks direct guidance for the current instance.
Consequently, the LLM fails to identify the dependency on a specific
error message and instead makes a judgment solely based on core
behavior. In contrast, ExLabel retrieves experience whose Mistakes field
explains that a previous judgment overlooked how implementation
dependencies in error message validation could cause correct solutions
to fail the tests. This experience enables the model to identify the
hidden dependency and generate the correct prediction successfully.

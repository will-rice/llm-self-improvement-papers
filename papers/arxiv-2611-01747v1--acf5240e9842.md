# Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control: Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents

###### Abstract

Self-evolving agents accumulate experience from task streams, but
existing frameworks typically rely on fixed pipelines to retrieve and
utilize this experience, neglecting when and how to deploy it. We
introduce Online Meta-Control of Experience (OMCE), a framework that
updates experience memory and learns a meta-control policy from task
feedback during test-time adaptation. OMCE employs a lightweight
contextual-bandit meta-controller that uses compact retrieval signals to
select among four cognitive actions—DIRECT, EPISODE, GUIDE, and DISTILL.
This routing mechanism operationalizes three coupled decisions: whether
to invoke experience, which memory format to utilize, and when to
consolidate new guidance. OMCE evolves online, leveraging task feedback
to update the memory bank and refine the routing policy via contextual
bandit updates. Experiments on four online adaptation benchmarks across
four backbones show that OMCE improves first-attempt success by up to
20.5 percentage points over the strongest compared baseline, while
reducing per-task token use by a factor of 5.7–62.5 relative to ACE with
GPT-5.2. Controlled ablations further support the benefits of learned
routing and continued routing policy adaptation.

## 1 Introduction

Experience-oriented self-evolving LLM agents turn interaction feedback
into reusable experience for later tasks [Gao et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib15); [Fang et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib13); [Chen et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib4). This experience can preserve concrete task cases or
take more abstract forms, including reasoning guidance, reusable skills,
and context playbooks [Fang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib14); [Ouyang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib30); [Wang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib44); [Zhang et al.
(2025a)](#bib.bib52). Its availability, however, does not guarantee its
utility for the current task: semantic similarity alone may retrieve
unhelpful experience [Zhang et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib54). Even when
experience is useful, concrete cases retain execution details but may
not transfer readily to different tasks, while distilled guidance
captures reusable principles but can omit details needed for execution
[Fang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib14); [Ouyang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30).
Extending this memory also poses a further choice: distilling guidance
from accumulated episodes may help future tasks, but also incurs
additional computational cost and can lead to excessive memory
growth[Zhang et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib52). These trade-offs leave open
how experience should be used and expanded as tasks arrive.

![Refer to caption](2611.01747v1/MCE_Intro_cropped_pdfresizer.com.png)

Figure 1: From Fixed Pipelines to Learned Meta-Control. Up: Existing
methods typically follow a fixed routine for using and consolidating
experience, even when the retrieved experience does not help the current
task. Down: OMCE learns from task feedback when to use experience,
whether to draw on past cases or general guidance, and when to distill
new guidance.

We argue that scheduling accumulated experience is a learnable dimension
of self-evolution, and make it precise through three requirements. (1)
_Adaptive invocation_: whether experience is needed at all [Fang et al.
(2025b)](#bib.bib14); [Ouyang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30). (2) _Selective
form_: which of its forms to use [Fang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib14);
[Zhang et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib52).

\(3\) _Learnable consolidation_: when to distill transferable guidance
from episodic evidence [Zhang et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib52); [Shinn et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib35).

Meta-control research on adaptive tool use [Li et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib21) and cognitive-demand monitoring [Scoville et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib33) suggests that adapting reasoning effort to task
demands can be more effective than uniformly allocating computation
[De Sabbata et al. (2024)](#bib.bib7).These frameworks, however, control
external, fixed resources such as a tool set or a reasoning budget.
Accumulated experience differs: the agent produces it itself, in
heterogeneous forms, in a store that grows and shifts, so scheduling it
requires a decision over use _and_ consolidation, not a single
invocation flag.

To this end, we propose Online Meta-Control of Experience (OMCE), an
agent framework that learns to control experience use and consolidation
through a lightweight meta-controller over episodic and guidance memory.

Implemented as a contextual bandit, the controller regulates whether to
invoke experience, which form to use, and when to distill new guidance
(Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")).

At inference, it summarizes retrieval profiles in a nine-dimensional
meta-state vector and uses linear reward models to select among DIRECT,
EPISODE, GUIDE, and DISTILL, without an additional LLM call for action
selection.

After execution, memory evolution records outcomes, generates
case-specific insights, and refines injected guidance using task
feedback, while meta-evolution adapts the routing policy through online
bandit updates.

Experiments on four online adaptation benchmarks show that OMCE achieves
the highest first-attempt success in most evaluated settings, with
competitive cumulative success under the same retry budget. Ablations
show that no fixed experience strategy is consistently the best, while
controlled comparisons with shared memory demonstrate the value of
learning _when_ and _how_ to use experience. Freezing the controller
reduces first-attempt success, supporting continued policy adaptation as
memory evolves. Finally, efficiency analysis shows that OMCE achieves a
favorable performance–token trade-off, using substantially fewer tokens
than ACE’s full-playbook prompting.

## 2 Related Work

#### Experience Memory and Self-Evolving Agents.

Experience-augmented reasoning has advanced LLM-agent memory systems
from case-level trajectory retrieval [Fang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib14);
[Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib35) to structured agentic knowledge bases
[Tang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib40); [Xu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib49). Other
work stores reusable plans, skills, or heuristics for cross-task
transfer [Wang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib44); [Wu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib48); [Chen et al. (2025)](#bib.bib5); [Allard et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib3), or distills experience into general principles,
evolving playbooks, and reusable reasoning memories through generation,
reflection, and refinement [Zhao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib55); [Zhang
et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib52); [Ouyang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30). MemRL
[Zhang et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib54) improves retrieval by scoring memory
candidates with a non-parametric Q-value function, and Evo-Memory [Wei
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib45) benchmarks retrieval, integration, and update
in continuous task streams. Overall, these efforts show that experience
can be accumulated, abstracted, and maintained in multiple forms.
However, most frameworks still use a fixed retrieve-inject-reflect
pipeline, with limited control over whether memory is useful for the
current task, which type of experience to consult, or whether a new
outcome should be consolidated [Majumder et al. (2023)](#bib.bib25);
[Yang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib50).

#### Meta-Control in LLM Agents.

Learned meta-control decides whether to invoke a resource and how much
to spend on it: a tool [Li et al. (2025)](#bib.bib21), a deliberation
budget [De Sabbata et al. (2024)](#bib.bib7); [Ji-An et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib16), test-time compute [Snell et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib36), a learning mode [Dupoux et al. (2026)](#bib.bib10),
or a verifier [Wan et al. (2026)](#bib.bib43); related work also edits
the agent’s program directly [Zhang et al. (2026a)](#bib.bib51).
Dual-process agents separate fast execution from slower deliberation
[Evans (2011)](#bib.bib11); [Evans and Stanovich (2013)](#bib.bib12);
[Liu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib23); [Zhang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib53), and
feedback-driven methods improve these meta-level policies with reward
signals [Wu and Qu (2025)](#bib.bib47). Closest to our setting are
controllers selecting among reasoning strategies at inference time:
Meta-Reasoner [Sui et al. (2026)](#bib.bib38) learns with a contextual
bandit when to backtrack or restart within one problem, and MetaScale
[Liu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib24) evolves meta-thoughts as the sampling
budget grows. Both focus on controlling predefined resources, but OMCE
instead learns to manage the experience an agent accumulates over time,
jointly deciding when to use existing experience and when to distill new
guidance.

## 3 Online Meta-Control of Experience

Given a sequential stream of queries
$`\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{N}\}`$, OMCE equips an LLM agent with
Episodic Memory, Guidance Memory, and a Meta-Controller
(Fig. [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Meta-Control in LLM Agents. ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")).

For each query, the controller uses meta-state to select an action that
determines whether and which experience to inject into the task prompt,
including whether to distill new guidance before execution. The
resulting augmented context is supplied to the agent. Task feedback then
updates the stored experience and the controller’s policy, allowing both
to adapt across the stream.

### 3.1 Memory Representation

The Episodic Memory maintains episodic records of past interactions,
$`\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{epi}}=\{\eta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}`$. Each record
$`\eta_{i}=(\tau_{i},r_{i},\Omega_{i})`$ contains the task trajectory
$`\tau_{i}`$, the immediate reward $`r_{i}\in[0,1]`$, and a small set of
case-specific insight entries
$`\Omega_{i}=\{\omega_{i,m}\}_{m=1}^{M_{i}}`$. These entries capture
reusable patterns from successful cases or corrective lessons from
failed ones. The Guidance Memory stores guidance entries distilled from
related episodic records,
$`\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}=\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}`$. Compared with
$`\omega_{i,m}`$, each $`\psi_{k}`$ is less tied to a single trajectory
and instead summarizes a recurring principle that may transfer across
similar cases. For retrieval, both case-specific insights and guidance
entries use the same two-part format: a compact description of the
applicable scenario and a detailed principle. The description serves as
the cue for query-to-memory matching, while the principle supplies the
content for injection into the task prompt.

### 3.2 Adaptive Meta-Control

We model the inference-time control process of OMCE as a contextual
bandit [Chu et al. (2011)](#bib.bib6); [Abbasi-Yadkori et al.
(2011)](#bib.bib1), in which the Meta-Controller observes the meta-state
$`S_{t}^{\mathrm{meta}}`$, selects a cognitive action, and receives
feedback only for that action at each step.

#### Meta-State Representation.

To support efficient action selection, the Meta-Controller summarizes
the current retrieval results rather than consuming raw memory text.
Given the current query $`q_{t}`$, we retrieve the top-$`M`$ candidate
entries from each memory source based on cosine similarity between the
semantic embeddings of the query and each entry’s applicable scenario
[Reimers and Gurevych (2019)](#bib.bib31); [Karpukhin et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib18). We denote the retrieved sets from episodic memory
and guidance memory by $`\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{epi}}`$
and $`\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{guide}}`$, respectively. The
meta-state is a nine-dimensional feature vector

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       S_{t}^{\mathrm{meta}}\equiv x_{t}=(x_{t,1},\ldots,x_{t,8},1).
       ```                                                            |     | (1) |

The state captures three properties: _Relevance_ ($`x_{t,1},x_{t,2}`$),
measured by retrieval scores from the two memory sources; _Quality_
($`x_{t,3},\ldots,x_{t,6}`$), measured by memory quality scores and
their relevance-weighted counterparts; and _Diversity_
($`x_{t,7},x_{t,8}`$), defined specifically as _episodic outcome
diversity_. Diversity captures the balance between successful and
failure-side episodic evidence and the normalized entropy of outcome
types, rather than semantic diversity among retrieved entries. The first
eight coordinates are normalized online, while the final coordinate is a
constant bias; $`x_{t,7}`$ and $`x_{t,8}`$ are exposed only to the
DISTILL action through an action-specific feature mask.
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Detailed Representation of the Meta-State. ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")
gives the full definitions and the quality-score mapping.

These three properties inform action selection. High relevance and
reliable evidence favor experience reuse, weak or conflicting signals
favor DIRECT, and relevant but insufficiently generalized episodes favor
DISTILL.

#### Actions and Augmented Context.

Given $`x_{t}`$, the Meta-Controller selects $`a_{t}`$ from
$`\mathcal{A}=\{a_{\textsc{DIRECT}},a_{\textsc{EPISODE}},a_{\textsc{GUIDE}},a_{\textsc{DISTILL}}\}`$.
The selected action determines whether and which experience to inject
into the task prompt containing $`q_{t}`$, producing the complete
execution context $`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t}`$.
$`a_{\textsc{DIRECT}}`$ (_DIRECT_, direct execution) retains the
original task input without adding external experience.
$`a_{\textsc{EPISODE}}`$ (_EPISODE_, episodic reuse) injects retrieved
episodic records into the task prompt as concrete prior cases.
$`a_{\textsc{GUIDE}}`$ (_GUIDE_, guidance reuse) injects retrieved
guidance entries into the task prompt as reusable principles.
$`a_{\textsc{DISTILL}}`$ (_DISTILL_, experience distillation) first
distills and stores a new guidance entry $`\psi_{\mathrm{new}}`$ from
the retrieved episodic evidence
([Section 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Memory Evolution ‣ 3 Online Meta-Control of Experience ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")),
then injects it into the task prompt:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t}=\begin{cases}q_{t},&a_{t}=a_{\textsc{DIRECT}},\\
\operatorname{Augment}(q_{t},\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{epi}}^{\mathrm{retr}}),&a_{t}=a_{\textsc{EPISODE}},\\
\operatorname{Augment}(q_{t},\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}^{\mathrm{retr}}),&a_{t}=a_{\textsc{GUIDE}},\\
\operatorname{Augment}(q_{t},\{\psi_{\mathrm{new}}\}),&a_{t}=a_{\textsc{DISTILL}}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (2) |

Here, $`\operatorname{Augment}`$ injects the selected experience into
the task prompt containing the query, following the task prompt template
without an additional LLM call. Fixed instructions shared by all actions
are implicit, so the DIRECT branch denotes the original task input with
those instructions.

#### Action Selection and Execution.

Under the contextual-bandit formulation, the Meta-Controller maximizes
the immediate current-query reward. For each $`a\in\mathcal{A}`$, we use
an action-wise linear model [Chu et al. (2011)](#bib.bib6);
[Abbasi-Yadkori et al. (2011)](#bib.bib1)
$`\mu_{a}(x_{t})=x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{a}^{\star}`$ with online estimate
$`\hat{r}_{a}^{(t)}(x_{t})=x_{t}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}`$. The
estimate and decision rule are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\theta}^{(t)}_{a}=\left(A^{(t)}_{a}\right)^{-1}b^{(t)}_{a},\quad a_{t}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{u}_{a}^{(t)}(x_{t}).
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\hat{u}_{a}^{(t)}(x_{t})`$ combines the predicted reward with an
exploration term, keeping underexplored actions selectable. The
action-wise statistics $`A_{a}^{(t)}`$ and $`b_{a}^{(t)}`$ are updated
from feedback as described below. The agent executes the task
conditioned on the augmented context, inducing a pretrained-LLM policy
$`\pi_{\textsc{LLM}}`$ from which the task trajectory is sampled:
$`\tau_{t}\sim\pi_{\textsc{LLM}}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t})`$.

#### Meta-Evolution.

Meta-evolution adapts the Meta-Controller through task feedback by
updating the contextual bandit’s action-wise reward models, which guide
subsequent action selection. After execution, the agent receives reward
$`r_{t}`$ for the current query. Only the selected action yields an
observation, so the rewards of unchosen actions remain unobserved
counterfactuals.

To update the bandit reward models, we update their action-wise
sufficient statistics using $`r_{t}`$ as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle A_{a}^{(t+1)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\gamma A_{a}^{(t)}+\mathbf{1}\{a_{t}=a\}x_{t}x_{t}^{\top},`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle b_{a}^{(t+1)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\gamma b_{a}^{(t)}+\mathbf{1}\{a_{t}=a\}r_{t}x_{t},\quad\forall a\in\mathcal{A},`$ |  |  |

where $`0<\gamma\leq 1`$ is a forgetting factor. The new observation is
added only to the selected action, while the discount is applied to all
action models [Russac et al. (2019)](#bib.bib32) so that older evidence
is downweighted under changing memory distributions. The updated
statistics determine the next parameter estimates through
Eq. [3](#S3.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ Action Selection and Execution. ‣ 3.2 Adaptive Meta-Control ‣ 3 Online Meta-Control of Experience ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents").
With four actions and a nine-dimensional meta-state, both action
selection and bandit updates operate on small matrices and vectors,
requiring no additional LLM calls.

A formal finite-time analysis of the controller’s learning behavior is
provided in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Additional Theoretical Result for the Meta-Controller ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents").

### 3.3 Memory Evolution

#### DISTILL.

Before execution, if $`a_{t}=a_{\textsc{DISTILL}}`$, an LLM distills a
transferable principle from the retrieved episodic records and stores it
as a guidance entry for current and future use:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\psi_{\mathrm{new}}\leftarrow\operatorname{DISTILL}(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{epi}}^{\mathrm{retr}}),`$ |  | (5) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}\cup\{\psi_{\mathrm{new}}\}.`$ |  |  |

#### Episodic Record generation.

After the agent produces a trajectory $`\tau_{t}`$ and receives reward
$`r_{t}`$, an LLM extracts case-specific insights
$`\Omega_{t}=\{\omega_{t,m}\}_{m=1}^{M_{t}}`$. We record each execution
outcome in Episodic Memory $`\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{epi}}`$ as
$`\eta_{\mathrm{new}}=(\tau_{t},r_{t},\Omega_{t})`$, regardless of the
selected action or task outcome. This preserves evidence for later reuse
and distillation, while the controller determines when to distill it
into transferable guidance.

#### Guidance refinement.

Refinement is a follow-up update to the guidance entries which are
injected into the augmented context. After execution under
$`a_{\textsc{GUIDE}}`$ or $`a_{\textsc{DISTILL}}`$, an LLM refines each
such entry, whether retrieved or newly distilled, with the realized
trajectory $`\tau_{t}`$ and reward $`r_{t}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\psi_{k}^{\prime}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leftarrow\operatorname{Refine}(\psi_{k},\tau_{t},r_{t}),`$ |  | (6) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leftarrow\bigl(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}\setminus\{\psi_{k}\}\bigr)\cup\{\psi_{k}^{\prime}\}.`$ |  |  |

#### Optional Multi-Attempt Extension.

With additional inference budget, the agent may make up to $`P`$
attempts per task, stopping upon success [Snell et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib36); [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib35). After a failed
attempt, the extracted case-specific experience is stored in Episodic
Memory and injected into the task prompt containing the original query
to form the augmented context for the next attempt on the same task.
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Algorithm of OMCE ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")
gives the complete inference and learning loop, including this
extension.

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate all methods in an online adaptation setting, where tasks
arrive and must be solved sequentially. After completing an instance,
the agent observes task feedback and may update its memory, controller,
or context before processing the next instance.

### 4.1 Experimental Design

We organize the evaluation around five questions. *RQ1*: does learned
routing raise first-attempt success across benchmarks and backbones?
*RQ2*: how should the routing policy be determined, in particular is a
fixed form of experience use sufficient, and does a learned controller
beat fixed and hand-crafted alternatives? *RQ3*: do the gains come from
the routing policy, or from the memory content and the update schedule?
*RQ4*: does the controller keep improving as the stream proceeds? *RQ5*:
what is the token cost of these gains?
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")
addresses RQ1,
[Section 4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")
addresses RQ2 to RQ4, and
[Section 4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Efficiency Analysis: Token–Performance Trade-off ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")
addresses RQ5.

The evaluated methods span four test-time adaptation regimes: no
external memory (Base LLM), within-instance retry (Reflexion [Shinn
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib35)), cross-task adaptation (MemP [Fang et al.
(2025b)](#bib.bib14)), and cross-task adaptation with multi-attempt
inference, including ACE [Zhang et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib52),
ReasoningBank [Ouyang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30) and our OMCE, which
differs from these baselines in learning when to invoke experience and
which form to use rather than following a fixed routine. Against OMCE,
we additionally compare policies that always commit to one form of
experience and a hand-crafted rule over retrieval scores
([Section 4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")).
We report two variants of OMCE: OMCE-TS and OMCE-UCB, which implement
meta-evolution with two contextual-bandit action-selection algorithms:
Thompson Sampling (TS) [Agrawal and Goyal (2013)](#bib.bib2) and LinUCB
[Chu et al. (2011)](#bib.bib6); [Abbasi-Yadkori et al.
(2011)](#bib.bib1), respectively (detailed in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Action Selection Strategies: Thompson Sampling and LinUCB ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")).

We evaluate on four complementary benchmarks spanning distinct task
domains under this shared setting: BigCodeBench [Zhuo et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib56) and SWE-bench Verified [Jimenez et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib17) for code generation and repository-level software
engineering, MultiChallenge [Deshpande et al. (2025)](#bib.bib9) for
realistic multi-turn conversation, and BBEH [Kazemi et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib19) for advanced general reasoning. Across all
benchmarks, we use four LLMs as backbone models: GPT-5.2 [OpenAI
(2025)](#bib.bib29), DeepSeek-V3.2 [Liu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22),
DeepSeek-V4-pro [DeepSeek-AI (2026)](#bib.bib8), and Kimi-K2.5 [Team
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib41).

We use *one-shot success rate* (OSR) as the primary metric, also called
first-attempt success: it measures whether the current policy and
accumulated experience solve an instance on the first attempt. For
methods with retry mechanisms, we additionally report *cumulative
success rate* (CSR), defined as success within a shared budget of
$`P=4`$ total attempts, including the initial attempt
([Section G.3](#A7.SS3 "G.3 Evaluation Metrics. ‣ Appendix G Details for Experimental Design ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")).
Reflexion does not transfer experience across instances, so by
construction its first attempt matches the Base LLM under this protocol;
we therefore report its CSR only, while OMCE’s CSR combines
within-instance retries with cross-instance adaptation. Full details on
benchmark construction, trial protocol, baseline implementations,
metrics, and controller variants are provided in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Details for Experimental Design ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents").
Both variants share one configuration across all benchmarks and
backbones; the values and the selection procedure are given there.

### 4.2 Main Results

Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Optional Multi-Attempt Extension. ‣ 3.3 Memory Evolution ‣ 3 Online Meta-Control of Experience ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")

demonstrating that OMCE systematically improves test-time adaptation: it
attains the highest first-attempt success on eleven of the sixteen
benchmark–backbone configurations. We highlight two key findings:

Learned Routing Leads in First-Attempt Success. OMCE dominates
first-attempt success wherever retrieved experience carries reliable
signal. On MultiChallenge it leads ReasoningBank, ACE, and MemP on all

Selective Routing Can Bypass Memory. DIRECT allows the controller to
solve a task without injecting retrieved experience. On BigCodeBench,
the better-performing OMCE variant matches or exceeds the Base LLM’s OSR
on three backbones and trails it by 0.2 point on the fourth. ACE falls
9.9 to 11.1 points below it on three.

We examine where the gains of OMCE come from through three questions,
answering *RQ2* to *RQ4*. The first compares routing rules on all four
backbones, covering two fixed policies, a hand-crafted one, and the two
learned variants:

reports GPT-5.2 and

the full grid. The other two examine memory use and controller updates
on GPT-5.2, holding the backbone fixed across conditions.

#### How should the routing policy be determined?

Fixed-$`a_{\textsc{EPISODE}}`$ always injects retrieved episodic records
into the task prompt, and Fixed-$`a_{\textsc{GUIDE}\&\textsc{DISTILL}}`$
always reuses or distills guidance. On GPT-5.2,
Fixed-$`a_{\textsc{EPISODE}}`$ exceeds
Fixed-$`a_{\textsc{GUIDE}\&\textsc{DISTILL}}`$ by 7.2 OSR points on BBEH
(50.3 vs. 43.1), and the order reverses on MultiChallenge, where
Fixed-$`a_{\textsc{GUIDE}\&\textsc{DISTILL}}`$ leads by 7.7 points (63.7
vs. 56.0). Committing to either form costs accuracy where it fits
poorly: Fixed-$`a_{\textsc{EPISODE}}`$ trails OMCE-TS by 11.4 points on
MultiChallenge, and Fixed-$`a_{\textsc{GUIDE}\&\textsc{DISTILL}}`$
trails it by 10.2 points on BBEH. Which form helps therefore depends on
the task; the same reversal appears at the level of individual
backbones, where the BBEH ordering of the two fixed policies flips
between GPT-5.2 and DeepSeek-V3.2.

OMCE-HEU is also adaptive, selecting among the same four actions with
fixed thresholds on retrieval scores
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Heuristic Baseline Details ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")).
On GPT-5.2 the learned controllers exceed the tuned heuristic by 0.8 to
7.7 points on all four benchmarks, with the widest margin on BBEH (53.3
vs. 45.6). Across the full grid the better learned variant outperforms
OMCE-HEU on 14 of the 16 benchmark–backbone pairs. Individually, TS and
UCB each outperform OMCE-HEU in OSR on 11 of the 16 configurations.

#### Do the gains come from routing or from the memory?

To separate routing from memory content, we fix the memory that OMCE has
built and vary only the routing rule. Always-Both reads both memory
types on every task and Random picks a feasible action uniformly, both
against that same snapshot. With identical memory content, OMCE exceeds
Always-Both by 3.6 to 16.0 OSR points and Random by 4.8 to 11.8, with
higher CSR on all four benchmarks, and reaches the best OSR of any
policy on the same snapshot. Where the gap is largest, on SWE-bench
Verified, Always-Both reaches 48.8 OSR, below the Base LLM without any
memory (52.8,
[Table 1](#S3.T1 "In Optional Multi-Attempt Extension. ‣ 3.3 Memory Evolution ‣ 3 Online Meta-Control of Experience ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")),
and on BigCodeBench it reaches 47.5 against 48.1. On two of the four
benchmarks, then, indiscriminate retrieval is worse than retrieving
nothing.

The same holds when the memory keeps evolving. Always-Both-and-Update
runs the full online loop as OMCE does, but retrieves from both memory
types and updates memory after every task, and it trails OMCE on all
four benchmarks by 2.2 to 9.8 OSR points, with lower CSR in each case.
These comparisons show an OSR advantage for learned routing under both
fixed and evolving memory.

#### Does the controller need to keep learning?

We freeze the controller parameters at the midpoint of each stream while
memory continues to evolve. The two interventions in this section order
the benchmarks identically. Freezing the controller costs first-attempt
success on all four, by 5.5 points on MultiChallenge, 3.5 on BBEH, 1.8
on BigCodeBench, and 0.6 on SWE-bench Verified, while OMCE’s lead over
the best fixed policy of
[Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.1 Experimental Design ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")
over the same four benchmarks is 3.7, 3.0, 1.7, and 0.4 points. CSR
falls on three benchmarks, by 0.2 to 3.1 points; on BigCodeBench it
instead rises, from 59.2 to 65.4. Continued controller updates improve
OSR on all four benchmarks, while their effect on CSR varies across
benchmarks.

### 4.5 Case Study

A case from MultiChallenge illustrates the routing decision
([Figure 4](#S4.F4 "In Do the gains come from routing or from the memory? ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")):
a user planning a conference initially says “in June,” then corrects it
to “in July.”

A fixed pipeline retrieves insights from the top-matching episodic
records, all of which direct the agent to hold earlier statements as
hard constraints, and the model accordingly insists on June. The
meta-controller reads $`S^{\text{meta}}_{t}`$ as weak or conflicting on
the episodic side and routes the query to $`a_{\textsc{GUIDE}}`$, where
the retrieved guidance concerns detecting and reconciling
contradictions; the agent then picks up the July date and solves the
task.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we presented OMCE, a framework that recasts
experience-augmented reasoning from an always-on memory pipeline into a
learnable meta-cognitive control problem. Instead of treating retrieval,
context injection, reflection, and consolidation as a fixed routine,
OMCE employs a lightweight meta-controller based on compact retrieval
features and linear reward models to govern the full lifecycle of
experience: choosing among DIRECT, EPISODE, GUIDE, and DISTILL.
Crucially, this meta-controller evolves online via task feedback,
progressively improving the agent’s judgment over its own cognitive
processes. Evaluations across four online adaptation benchmarks
demonstrate that OMCE establishes state-of-the-art OSR and a favorable
performance-token trade-off. Ultimately, our findings suggest that as
self-evolving agents mature, the primary bottleneck of online adaptation
shifts from merely accumulating richer experience to learning exactly
when and how that experience should be deployed and updated.

## Limitations

OMCE inherently relies on timely feedback. In environments with sparse
or delayed rewards, the contextual-bandit updates can become noisy, and
abrupt task distribution shifts may render both the accumulated
experience and the learned meta-policy stale. Moreover, due to the
partial observability of the bandit formulation, the controller lacks
counterfactual signals regarding unchosen cognitive routes, potentially
slowing its recovery from early suboptimal decisions. Additionally, as
the memory banks expand indefinitely, retrieval may suffer from
redundancy or semantic collisions.

To address these limitations, future work could integrate established
memory-maintenance techniques (e.g., explicit aging or eviction of
stored memory entries). More fundamentally, future iterations could
leverage the LLM’s reflective capabilities to simulate counterfactual
rewards for unchosen actions (potentially accelerating bandit
convergence), or complement the current meta-control framework with
explicit memory auditing and pruning, advancing towards a fully
autonomous lifelong-learning agent.

Our controlled comparisons of memory use and controller updates focus on
GPT-5.2, while the routing comparison spans all four backbones. Testing
the same interventions on other backbones would help assess whether the
observed effects generalize.

## Appendix A Detailed Representation of the Meta-State.

For each decision step $`t`$, the Meta-Controller receives a compact
nine-dimensional representation of the retrieved experience rather than
the raw memory text:

|     |                                                    |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       x_{t}=(x_{t,1},\ldots,x_{t,8},1)\in\mathbb{R}^{9},
       ```                                                 |     | (7) |

where the final coordinate is a constant bias term. In all experiments,
the retrieval count is fixed to $`M=3`$ for each memory source.

Let $`\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{epi},t}`$ and
$`\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{guide},t}`$ denote the retrieved
episodic records and guidance entries at step $`t`$, sorted by retrieval
score in descending order. For an item $`j`$ from source
$`z\in\{\mathrm{epi},\mathrm{guide}\}`$, let $`s^{z}_{t,j}`$ denote its
retrieval score and let $`v^{z}_{t,j}`$ denote its quality score. The
retrieval scores are cosine similarities between the current query and
the corresponding memory entry representations computed by dense text
embeddings.

For episodic memory, the quality score is mapped from the observed
outcome as

|     |                                                            |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       v^{\mathrm{epi}}_{t,j}=\begin{cases}1.0,&\text{success},\\
       0.5,&\text{partial or unknown outcome},\\
       0.0,&\text{failure}.\end{cases}
       ```                                                         |     | (8) |

For guidance memory, $`v^{\mathrm{guide}}_{t,j}`$ is computed from
available success-rate and confidence statistics. When these statistics
are unavailable, a memory-type prior is used.

Let $`H_{M}(\cdot)`$ denote the top-$`M`$ items in a retrieved set. When
fewer than $`M`$ items are available, $`H_{M}(\cdot)`$ contains all
available items. The eight state features are defined as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{t,1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|H_{M}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{epi},t})|}\sum_{j\in H_{M}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{epi},t})}s^{\mathrm{epi}}_{t,j},`$ |  | (9) |
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{t,2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|H_{M}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{guide},t})|}\sum_{j\in H_{M}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{guide},t})}s^{\mathrm{guide}}_{t,j},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{t,3}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|H_{M}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{epi},t})|}\sum_{j\in H_{M}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{epi},t})}v^{\mathrm{epi}}_{t,j},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{t,4}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{epi},t}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{epi},t}}s^{\mathrm{epi}}_{t,j}v^{\mathrm{epi}}_{t,j},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{t,5}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|H_{M}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{guide},t})|}\sum_{j\in H_{M}(\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{guide},t})}v^{\mathrm{guide}}_{t,j},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{t,6}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{guide},t}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{retr}}_{\mathrm{guide},t}}s^{\mathrm{guide}}_{t,j}v^{\mathrm{guide}}_{t,j},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{t,7}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{2\min(n_{t,s},n_{t,f}+\rho n_{t,p})}{n_{t,s}+n_{t,f}+\rho n_{t,p}},`$ |  |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle x_{t,8}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{-\sum_{o}p_{t,o}\log p_{t,o}}{\log|\{o:p_{t,o}>0\}|}.`$ |  |  |

Here, $`n_{t,s}`$, $`n_{t,f}`$, and $`n_{t,p}`$ denote the numbers of
successful, failed, and partial episodic outcomes among the retrieved
records. Partial outcomes contribute to the failure side with weight
$`\rho=0.5`$. In particular, we define

|     |                                       |     |      |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \tilde{n}_{t,f}=n_{t,f}+\rho n_{t,p}.
       ```                                    |     | (10) |

Thus, $`x_{t,7}`$ measures the balance between successful episodic
evidence and failure-side evidence, where partial outcomes contribute
with half the weight of failures.

For the entropy feature, $`p_{t,o}`$ denotes the empirical proportion of
outcome type $`o`$ among the retrieved episodic records:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{t,o}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{n_{t,o}}{\sum_{o^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}}n_{t,o^{\prime}}},`$ |  | (11) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{O}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\{\text{success},\text{failure},\text{partial}\}.`$ |  |  |

If no episodic record is retrieved, we set $`x_{t,7}=x_{t,8}=0`$. If the
retrieved episodic records contain only one nonzero outcome type, we set
$`x_{t,8}=0`$. Otherwise, $`x_{t,8}`$ is the normalized entropy of the
retrieved outcome types.

If a memory source is empty, its corresponding source-level mean
features and relevance-weighted features are set to zero. Therefore,
when episodic memory is empty, $`x_{t,1}`$, $`x_{t,3}`$, and $`x_{t,4}`$
are set to zero. When guidance memory is empty, $`x_{t,2}`$,
$`x_{t,5}`$, and $`x_{t,6}`$ are set to zero.

The first eight coordinates are normalized online using running z-score
statistics with symmetric clipping:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{t,i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{x_{t,i}-\mu_{i}}{\max(\sigma_{i},\epsilon)},-c,c\right),\qquad i=1,\ldots,8,`$ |  | (12) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{t,9}`$ | $`\displaystyle=1.`$ |  |  |

The bias coordinate is not normalized. The features $`x_{t,7}`$ and
$`x_{t,8}`$ are exposed only to the DISTILL action
$`a_{\textsc{DISTILL}}`$ through an action-specific feature mask.

The state therefore summarizes *Relevance* through retrieval scores,
*Quality* through outcome- and confidence-based scores and their
relevance-weighted counterparts, and *Diversity* through the balance and
normalized entropy of episodic outcome types. Here, Diversity refers
specifically to episodic outcome diversity, not semantic diversity among
retrieved entries. The controller uses this compact summary to select
among DIRECT, EPISODE, GUIDE, and DISTILL without directly accessing the
retrieved memory text.

## Appendix B Action Selection Strategies: Thompson Sampling and LinUCB

For the contextual-bandit action-selection algorithms, we maintain an
action-wise linear reward model. At each round $`t`$, the
Meta-Controller observes a meta-state $`x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ and
selects an action $`a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}`$. Let $`r_{t}\in[0,1]`$ denote
the immediate reward.

For each action $`a\in\mathcal{A}`$, the sufficient statistics are
initialized as $`A_{a}^{(1)}=\lambda I_{d}`$ and $`b_{a}^{(1)}=0`$.
After observing the reward, the statistics are updated with a forgetting
factor $`\gamma\in(0,1]`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{a}^{(t+1)}=\gamma A_{a}^{(t)}+x_{t}x_{t}^{\top},\qquad b_{a}^{(t+1)}=\gamma b_{a}^{(t)}+r_{t}x_{t}.
``` |  | (13) |

The posterior mean is given by
$`\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}=\bigl(A_{a}^{(t)}\bigr)^{-1}b_{a}^{(t)}`$, and
the posterior covariance proxy is
$`\Sigma_{a}^{(t)}=v^{2}\bigl(A_{a}^{(t)}\bigr)^{-1}`$, where $`v>0`$ is
a scale parameter.

#### Thompson Sampling (OMCE-TS).

Thompson Sampling balances exploration and exploitation by sampling from
the posterior distribution. At round $`t`$, for each action
$`a\in\mathcal{A}`$, we draw a parameter sample:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)},\,\Sigma_{a}^{(t)}\right).
``` |  | (14) |

The action is then selected by maximizing the sampled score:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}x_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}.
``` |  | (15) |

#### LinUCB (OMCE-UCB).

The optimistic LinUCB-style rule selects the action that maximizes an
upper confidence bound on the expected reward. At round $`t`$, the
action is selected as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{x_{t}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}+\alpha\sqrt{x_{t}^{\top}\bigl(A_{a}^{(t)}\bigr)^{-1}x_{t}}\right\},
``` |  | (16) |

where $`\alpha>0`$ is an exploration hyperparameter that controls the
confidence interval width.

## Appendix C Heuristic Baseline Details

The heuristic baseline employs a fixed, rule-based policy to select
actions based solely on retrieval scores, without any online learning or
parameter updates. The decision mechanism evaluates the available
signals against predefined thresholds to compute a heuristic score for
each possible action, ultimately selecting the action with the maximum
score.

Let $`s_{\mathrm{epi}}`$ and $`s_{\mathrm{guide}}`$ denote the mean
retrieval scores for the episodic records and guidance entries,
respectively. The heuristic relies on three configurable
hyperparameters: the weak threshold $`\tau_{w}`$, the use threshold
$`\tau_{u}`$, and the margin threshold $`\tau_{m}`$. To ensure numerical
stability, we define a small constant $`\epsilon=10^{-8}`$.

The heuristic scores for the four actions in the action space
$`\mathcal{A}`$ are computed as follows:

#### DIRECT ($`a_{\textsc{DIRECT}}`$).

The score for DIRECT is inversely proportional to the maximum available
retrieval score, encouraging the agent to skip when all retrieval
signals are weak:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{score}_{\textsc{DIRECT}}=\max\left(0,1-\frac{\max(s_{\mathrm{epi}},s_{\mathrm{guide}})}{\max(\tau_{w},\epsilon)}\right)
``` |  | (17) |

#### EPISODE ($`a_{\textsc{EPISODE}}`$).

The score for utilizing an episodic record depends on its absolute
signal strength and its relative advantage over the guidance entry
signal. We define the episodic strength $`\text{str}_{\mathrm{epi}}`$
and episodic advantage $`\text{adv}_{\mathrm{epi}}`$ as:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{str}_{\mathrm{epi}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\max(0,s_{\mathrm{epi}}-\tau_{w})}{\max(\tau_{u}-\tau_{w},\epsilon)}`$ |  | (18) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{adv}_{\mathrm{epi}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max(0,s_{\mathrm{epi}}-s_{\mathrm{guide}})`$ |  | (19) |

The final score is a weighted combination that rewards both absolute
strength and relative advantage:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{score}_{\textsc{EPISODE}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\min(\text{str}_{\mathrm{epi}},1)`$ |  | (20) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\cdot\left(0.7+0.3\cdot\min\left(\frac{\text{adv}_{\mathrm{epi}}}{\max(\tau_{m},\epsilon)},1\right)\right).`$ |  |  |

#### GUIDE ($`a_{\textsc{GUIDE}}`$).

Symmetrically, the score for using a guidance entry is computed based on
its strength $`\text{str}_{\mathrm{guide}}`$ and advantage
$`\text{adv}_{\mathrm{guide}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{str}_{\mathrm{guide}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\max(0,s_{\mathrm{guide}}-\tau_{w})}{\max(\tau_{u}-\tau_{w},\epsilon)}`$ |  | (21) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{adv}_{\mathrm{guide}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max(0,s_{\mathrm{guide}}-s_{\mathrm{epi}})`$ |  | (22) |

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\text{score}_{\textsc{GUIDE}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\min(\text{str}_{\mathrm{guide}},1)`$ |  | (23) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\cdot\left(0.7+0.3\cdot\min\left(\frac{\text{adv}_{\mathrm{guide}}}{\max(\tau_{m},\epsilon)},1\right)\right).`$ |  |  |

#### DISTILL ($`a_{\textsc{DISTILL}}`$).

The score for distilling a new guidance entry is determined by a
piecewise function based on the best available retrieval score,
$`s_{\max}=\max(s_{\mathrm{epi}},s_{\mathrm{guide}})`$. Distillation is
favored when the retrieval scores are moderate (between $`\tau_{w}`$ and
$`\tau_{u}`$), and penalized if the scores are either too weak
(indicating poor context) or sufficiently strong (indicating
distillation is unnecessary):

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\text{score}_{\textsc{DISTILL}}=`$ |  | (24) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\begin{cases}0,&s_{\max}<\tau_{w},\\
\max(0,\,0.5-(s_{\max}-\tau_{u})),&s_{\max}\geq\tau_{u},\\
\dfrac{s_{\max}-\tau_{w}}{\max(\tau_{u}-\tau_{w},\epsilon)},\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}`$ |  |  |

#### Decision Rule.

The final action $`a^{*}`$ is selected greedily according to the highest
computed heuristic score:

|     |                                                  |     |      |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       a^{*}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\text{score}_{a}
       ```                                               |     | (25) |

## Appendix E Additional Theoretical Result for the Meta-Controller

For the theoretical analysis, we consider the stationary regime and set
the forgetting factor to $`\gamma=1`$. Let $`\mathcal{A}`$ denote the
set of cognitive actions, with $`K:=|\mathcal{A}|=4`$. At each round
$`t`$, the Meta-Controller observes a meta-state
$`x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ and selects an action
$`a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}`$. The reward $`r_{t}\in[0,1]`$ denotes the
immediate utility of the current query after executing $`a_{t}`$.

For each action $`a\in\mathcal{A}`$, initialize

|     |                                                 |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{a}^{(1)}=\lambda I_{d},\qquad b_{a}^{(1)}=0,
       ```                                              |     | (26) |

and define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle A_{a}^{(t)}=\lambda I_{d}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\mathbf{1}\{a_{s}=a\}x_{s}x_{s}^{\top},`$ |  | (27) |
|  | $`\displaystyle b_{a}^{(t)}=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\mathbf{1}\{a_{s}=a\}r_{s}x_{s},`$ |  |  |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}=\bigl(A_{a}^{(t)}\bigr)^{-1}b_{a}^{(t)},`$ |  | (28) |
|  | $`\displaystyle N_{a}(t-1)=\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\mathbf{1}\{a_{s}=a\}.`$ |  |  |

In the main text, $`\mu_{a}(x_{t})`$ is used as a shorthand for the
expected immediate reward of action $`a`$ under the current meta-state.
For the theoretical analysis, we make this quantity explicit by assuming
that for each action $`a\in\mathcal{A}`$, there exists an unknown
parameter $`\theta_{a}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ such that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mu_{a}(x_{t}):=\mathbb{E}[r_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},x_{t},a_{t}=a]=x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{a}^{\star},`$ |  | (29) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\|\theta_{a}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq S,`$ |  |  |

and that $`\|x_{t}\|_{2}\leq L`$ for all $`t`$.

Moreover, letting

|     |                                                       |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \kappa_{t}:=r_{t}-x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{a_{t}}^{\star},
       ```                                                    |     | (30) |

we assume that $`\kappa_{t}`$ is conditionally $`R`$-sub-Gaussian, i.e.,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp(\alpha\kappa_{t})\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},x_{t},a_{t}\right]\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}R^{2}}{2}\right),`$ |  | (31) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.`$ |  |  |

The main paper implements the Meta-Controller via Thompson Sampling on
the same action-wise linear reward model. Concretely, for each action
$`a\in\mathcal{A}`$, define the posterior mean

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}=\bigl(A_{a}^{(t)}\bigr)^{-1}b_{a}^{(t)},
``` |  | (32) |

and the posterior covariance proxy

|     |                                                     |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Sigma_{a}^{(t)}=v^{2}\bigl(A_{a}^{(t)}\bigr)^{-1},
       ```                                                  |     | (33) |

where $`v>0`$ is a scale parameter. At round $`t`$, Thompson Sampling
draws

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)},\,\Sigma_{a}^{(t)}\right),\qquad\forall a\in\mathcal{A},
``` |  | (34) |

computes the sampled score

|     |                                                           |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \tilde{r}_{a}^{(t)}=x_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{a}^{(t)},
       ```                                                        |     | (35) |

and selects the action

|     |                                                      |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\tilde{r}_{a}^{(t)}.
       ```                                                   |     | (36) |

After observing the reward $`r_{t}`$, the sufficient statistics of the
selected action are updated according to the equations above.

For the regret analysis below, we consider an optimistic instantiation
built on the same action-wise linear estimator, which yields a shorter
and more standard finite-time proof.

###### Lemma E.1 (Uniform confidence ellipsoid for the action-wise linear reward models).

For any $`\delta\in(0,1)`$, define

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\beta_{a}^{(t)}(\delta)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=R\sqrt{d\log\!\left(1+\frac{N_{a}(t-1)L^{2}}{\lambda d}\right)+2\log\!\frac{K}{\delta}}`$ |  | (37) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\sqrt{\lambda}\,S.`$ |  |  |

Then, with probability at least $`1-\delta`$, the following event holds
simultaneously for all $`t\geq 1`$ and all $`a\in\mathcal{A}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bigl\|\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}-\theta_{a}^{\star}\bigr\|_{A_{a}^{(t)}}\leq\beta_{a}^{(t)}(\delta).
``` |  | (38) |

###### Proof.

Fix an action $`a\in\mathcal{A}`$. Let $`\tau_{a,1}<\tau_{a,2}<\cdots`$
denote the rounds at which action $`a`$ is selected, and let
$`n:=N_{a}(t-1)`$. Define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle X_{a,i}:=x_{\tau_{a,i}},\qquad\xi_{a,i}:=r_{\tau_{a,i}}-X_{a,i}^{\top}\theta_{a}^{\star},`$ |  | (39) |
|  | $`\displaystyle i=1,\dots,n.`$ |  |  |

Then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
V_{a,n}:=\lambda I_{d}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{a,i}X_{a,i}^{\top}=A_{a}^{(t)}.
``` |  | (40) |

By the self-normalized concentration inequality for linear bandits
[Abbasi-Yadkori et al. (2011)](#bib.bib1), with probability at least
$`1-\delta/K`$, simultaneously for all $`n\geq 0`$,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{a,i}X_{a,i}\right\|_{V_{a,n}^{-1}}`$ |  | (41) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq R\sqrt{2\log\!\left(\frac{\det(V_{a,n})^{1/2}}{\det(\lambda I_{d})^{1/2}}\cdot\frac{K}{\delta}\right)}.`$ |  |  |

Also,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle b_{a}^{(t)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{\tau_{a,i}}X_{a,i}`$ |  | (42) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{a,i}X_{a,i}^{\top}\right)\theta_{a}^{\star}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{a,i}X_{a,i},`$ |  |  |

hence

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A_{a}^{(t)}\bigl(\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}-\theta_{a}^{\star}\bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{a,i}X_{a,i}-\lambda\theta_{a}^{\star}.
``` |  | (43) |

Therefore,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\bigl\|\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}-\theta_{a}^{\star}\bigr\|_{A_{a}^{(t)}}`$ |  | (44) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{a,i}X_{a,i}\right\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}+\bigl\|\lambda\theta_{a}^{\star}\bigr\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}.`$ |  |  |

Since $`A_{a}^{(t)}\succeq\lambda I_{d}`$ and
$`\|\theta_{a}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq S`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bigl\|\lambda\theta_{a}^{\star}\bigr\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\,S.
``` |  | (45) |

Moreover,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\det(A_{a}^{(t)})`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A_{a}^{(t)})}{d}\right)^{d}`$ |  | (46) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\left(\lambda+\frac{N_{a}(t-1)L^{2}}{d}\right)^{d},`$ |  |  |

which implies

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\det(A_{a}^{(t)})}{\det(\lambda I_{d})}\leq\left(1+\frac{N_{a}(t-1)L^{2}}{\lambda d}\right)^{d}.
``` |  | (47) |

Combining the above inequalities yields

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\bigl\|\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}-\theta_{a}^{\star}\bigr\|_{A_{a}^{(t)}}`$ |  | (48) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq R\Biggl[d\log\!\left(1+\frac{N_{a}(t-1)L^{2}}{\lambda d}\right)`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+2\log\!\frac{K}{\delta}\Biggr]^{1/2}+\sqrt{\lambda}\,S.`$ |  |  |

Finally, applying a union bound over all $`a\in\mathcal{A}`$ proves the
lemma. ∎

For the regret analysis, consider the optimistic instantiation

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{x_{t}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}+\bar{\beta}_{a}^{(t)}(\delta)\,\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}\right\},
``` |  | (49) |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{\beta}_{a}^{(t)}(\delta):=\max\{1,\beta_{a}^{(t)}(\delta)\}.
``` |  | (50) |

Let

|     |                                                          |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       a_{t}^{\star}:=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu_{a}(x_{t}),
       ```                                                       |     | (51) |

and define the cumulative pseudo-regret

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{T}:=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl(\mu_{a_{t}^{\star}}(x_{t})-\mu_{a_{t}}(x_{t})\Bigr).
``` |  | (52) |

###### Theorem E.2 (Sublinear cumulative pseudo-regret of the optimistic Meta-Controller).

Suppose $`\lambda\geq L^{2}`$. Then, with probability at least
$`1-\delta`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{T}\leq 2\bar{\beta}_{T}(\delta)\,\sqrt{2KdT\log\!\left(1+\frac{TL^{2}}{\lambda d}\right)},
``` |  | (53) |

where

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\bar{\beta}_{T}(\delta)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=\max\Biggl\{1,`$ |  | (54) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle R\sqrt{d\log\!\left(1+\frac{TL^{2}}{\lambda d}\right)+2\log\!\frac{K}{\delta}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\sqrt{\lambda}S\Biggr\}.`$ |  |  |

In particular, since $`K=4`$ is fixed, $`R_{T}=\tilde{O}(d\sqrt{T})`$.

###### Proof.

Let $`\mathcal{E}_{\delta}`$ denote the high-probability event in
Lemma [E.1](#A5.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma E.1 (Uniform confidence ellipsoid for the action-wise linear reward models). ‣ Appendix E Additional Theoretical Result for the Meta-Controller ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents").
Fix a round $`t`$ and define

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
U_{a}^{(t)}(x_{t}):=x_{t}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}+\bar{\beta}_{a}^{(t)}(\delta)\,\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}.
``` |  | (55) |

By
Lemma [E.1](#A5.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma E.1 (Uniform confidence ellipsoid for the action-wise linear reward models). ‣ Appendix E Additional Theoretical Result for the Meta-Controller ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents"),
on $`\mathcal{E}_{\delta}`$, for every action $`a`$,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mu_{a}(x_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle=x_{t}^{\top}\theta_{a}^{\star}\leq x_{t}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}+\beta_{a}^{(t)}(\delta)\,\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}`$ |  | (56) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq U_{a}^{(t)}(x_{t}).`$ |  |  |

Hence

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mu_{a_{t}^{\star}}(x_{t})\leq U_{a_{t}^{\star}}^{(t)}(x_{t})\leq U_{a_{t}}^{(t)}(x_{t}),
``` |  | (57) |

which implies

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mu_{a_{t}^{\star}}(x_{t})-\mu_{a_{t}}(x_{t})\leq U_{a_{t}}^{(t)}(x_{t})-\mu_{a_{t}}(x_{t}).
``` |  | (58) |

Again by
Lemma [E.1](#A5.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma E.1 (Uniform confidence ellipsoid for the action-wise linear reward models). ‣ Appendix E Additional Theoretical Result for the Meta-Controller ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents"),

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle U_{a_{t}}^{(t)}(x_{t})-\mu_{a_{t}}(x_{t})`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq 2\bar{\beta}_{a_{t}}^{(t)}(\delta)\,\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a_{t}}^{(t)})^{-1}}`$ |  | (59) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq 2\bar{\beta}_{T}(\delta)\,\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a_{t}}^{(t)})^{-1}}.`$ |  |  |

Summing over $`t=1,\dots,T`$ gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{T}\leq 2\bar{\beta}_{T}(\delta)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a_{t}}^{(t)})^{-1}}.
``` |  | (60) |

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a_{t}}^{(t)})^{-1}}\leq\sqrt{T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a_{t}}^{(t)})^{-1}}^{2}}.
``` |  | (61) |

It remains to control the quadratic sum. For each fixed action $`a`$,
whenever $`a_{t}=a`$,

|     |                                              |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{a}^{(t+1)}=A_{a}^{(t)}+x_{t}x_{t}^{\top}.
       ```                                           |     | (62) |

By the matrix determinant lemma,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\det(A_{a}^{(t+1)})=\det(A_{a}^{(t)})\Bigl(1+\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}^{2}\Bigr).
``` |  | (63) |

Taking logs and summing over all rounds with $`a_{t}=a`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{t:\,a_{t}=a}\log\!\Bigl(1+\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}^{2}\Bigr)=\log\frac{\det(A_{a}^{(T+1)})}{\det(\lambda I_{d})}.
``` |  | (64) |

Since $`\lambda\geq L^{2}`$ and $`A_{a}^{(t)}\succeq\lambda I_{d}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{\|x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}{\lambda}\leq 1.
``` |  | (65) |

Using $`\log(1+u)\geq u/2`$ for $`u\in[0,1]`$, we obtain

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{t:\,a_{t}=a}\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2\log\frac{\det(A_{a}^{(T+1)})}{\det(\lambda I_{d})}.
``` |  | (66) |

Summing over all $`a\in\mathcal{A}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a_{t}}^{(t)})^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\log\frac{\det(A_{a}^{(T+1)})}{\det(\lambda I_{d})}.
``` |  | (67) |

Finally, for each action $`a`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\log\frac{\det(A_{a}^{(T+1)})}{\det(\lambda I_{d})}\leq d\log\!\left(1+\frac{TL^{2}}{\lambda d}\right),
``` |  | (68) |

so

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|x_{t}\|_{(A_{a_{t}}^{(t)})^{-1}}^{2}\leq 2Kd\log\!\left(1+\frac{TL^{2}}{\lambda d}\right).
``` |  | (69) |

Therefore,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{T}\leq 2\bar{\beta}_{T}(\delta)\,\sqrt{2KdT\log\!\left(1+\frac{TL^{2}}{\lambda d}\right)},
``` |  | (70) |

which proves the theorem. ∎

## Appendix F Algorithm of OMCE

In this section, we present the pseudocode for the OMCE algorithm.

The optional multi-attempt extension uses at most $`P`$ total attempts
for each query, including the initial attempt, and stops early upon
success. After each failed attempt, an LLM extracts case-specific
insights from the trajectory and feedback, records them in Episodic
Memory, and injects them into the task prompt containing the original
query using $`\operatorname{Augment}`$ to construct the next input. We
denote the context for attempt $`p`$ by
$`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t}^{(p)}`$, with
$`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t}^{(1)}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t}`$
defined in
Eq. [2](#S3.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ Actions and Augmented Context. ‣ 3.2 Adaptive Meta-Control ‣ 3 Online Meta-Control of Experience ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents").
The loop below specifies how this within-task reuse interacts with
memory refinement and controller learning.

Algorithm 1 Online Meta-Control of Experience (OMCE): Inference and
Learning Loop

1:  Input: Task query $`q_{t}`$; Episodic Memory
$`\mathcal{M}_{\textsc{epi}}`$; Guidance Memory
$`\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}`$; bandit statistics
$`\{A_{a}^{(t)},b_{a}^{(t)}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}`$; forgetting factor
$`\gamma`$; maximum number of attempts $`P`$.

2:  Output: Task trajectory $`\tau_{t}`$.

3:  *Convention:* $`P`$ includes the initial attempt (thus $`P-1`$
retries).

4:  // Phase 1: Meta-Control (Retrieval & Action Selection)

5:  Retrieve candidates $`\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{epi}}^{\mathrm{retr}}`$
and $`\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}^{\mathrm{retr}}`$ using lightweight
retrieval on $`q_{t}`$.

6:  Construct meta-state $`x_{t}`$ from the retrieved candidates.

7:  Estimate $`\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}=(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}b_{a}^{(t)}`$
for each $`a\in\mathcal{A}`$
(Eq. [3](#S3.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ Action Selection and Execution. ‣ 3.2 Adaptive Meta-Control ‣ 3 Online Meta-Control of Experience ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")).

8:  if using Thompson Sampling then

9:   Sample
$`\tilde{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)},\Sigma_{a}^{(t)})`$
for each $`a`$
(Eq. [14](#A2.E14 "Equation 14 ‣ Thompson Sampling (OMCE-TS). ‣ Appendix B Action Selection Strategies: Thompson Sampling and LinUCB ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")).

10:   Meta-Decision:
$`a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}x_{t}^{\top}\tilde{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}`$
(Eq. [15](#A2.E15 "Equation 15 ‣ Thompson Sampling (OMCE-TS). ‣ Appendix B Action Selection Strategies: Thompson Sampling and LinUCB ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")).

11:  else if using LinUCB then

12:   Meta-Decision:
$`\displaystyle\begin{aligned} a_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\bigl(&x_{t}^{\top}\hat{\theta}_{a}^{(t)}\\[-1.0pt]
&+\alpha\sqrt{x_{t}^{\top}(A_{a}^{(t)})^{-1}x_{t}}\bigr).\end{aligned}`$

13:  end if

14:  Initialize $`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t}^{(1)}\leftarrow q_{t}`$
and $`\Psi_{\mathrm{injected}}\leftarrow\emptyset`$.

15:  // Phase 2: Inject Experience According to the Selected Action

16:  if $`a_{t}=a_{\textsc{DIRECT}}`$ then

17:   $`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t}^{(1)}\leftarrow q_{t}`$.

18:  else if $`a_{t}=a_{\textsc{EPISODE}}`$ then

19:
  $`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t}^{(1)}\leftarrow\operatorname{Augment}(q_{t},\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{epi}}^{\mathrm{retr}})`$.

20:  else if $`a_{t}=a_{\textsc{GUIDE}}`$ then

21:
  $`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{aug},t}^{(1)}\leftarrow\operatorname{Augment}(q_{t},\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}^{\mathrm{retr}})`$.

22:
  $`\Psi_{\mathrm{injected}}\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{guide}}^{\mathrm{retr}}`$.

23:  else if $`a_{t}=a_{\textsc{DISTILL}}`$ then

24:

## Appendix G Details for Experimental Design

### G.1 Task Setting.

Our experiments follow the online adaptation evaluation setting [Zhang
et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib52), where each method processes the test stream
in a single sequential pass. For every incoming instance, the agent
first produces its prediction using the current policy, memory, or
context, observes the task feedback, and then updates the corresponding
memory or controller state before moving to the next instance. To make
the comparison controlled, all methods use the same task order on each
benchmark, the same backbone LLM, and the same evaluation pipeline. Each
method is evaluated in a single run over each benchmark–backbone
configuration due to the cost of LLM API calls. Reported success rates
aggregate outcomes across benchmark instances. For methods supporting
retries, each instance allows up to four total attempts, including the
initial attempt; these are within-instance retries rather than
independent experimental runs. Methods that support multi-attempt
inference use a shared budget of $`P=4`$ total attempts, following
[Section G.3](#A7.SS3 "G.3 Evaluation Metrics. ‣ Appendix G Details for Experimental Design ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents"),
and OMCE may stop early once a task is solved.

### G.2 Benchmarks.

Under this setting, we consider four complementary benchmarks spanning
code generation, repository-level software engineering, realistic
multi-turn conversation, and advanced general reasoning. English is the
language used for all benchmarks.

*Code generation and software engineering.* BigCodeBench [Zhuo et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib56) is a function-level code generation benchmark
emphasizing complex instructions and diverse function or tool calls,
with unit-test-based automatic evaluation to reflect practical
programming behavior. SWE-bench Verified [Jimenez et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib17) is a human-validated subset of SWE-bench designed to
assess whether agents can resolve real-world GitHub issues in
repository-scale codebases.

*Multi-turn conversation.* MultiChallenge [Deshpande et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib9) measures whether a model can handle realistic,
challenging user interactions over extended dialogue contexts, with
particular emphasis on instruction-following, context allocation, and
in-context reasoning. We use GPT-4o-mini [OpenAI (2024)](#bib.bib27) as
the judge model, to judge whether the answer meets the hidden task
requirement.

*Advanced general reasoning.* BIG-Bench Extra Hard (BBEH) [Kazemi et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib19) is a substantially more challenging successor to
BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) [Srivastava et al. (2023)](#bib.bib37); [Suzgun
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib39), covering a broad range of general reasoning
skills. We use a shuffled 6-task subset comprising 1,200 instances:
causal_understanding [Nie et al. (2024)](#bib.bib26); [Kıcıman et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib20), dyck_languages [Tyen et al. (2023)](#bib.bib42),
temporal_sequence, web_of_lies [White et al. (2024)](#bib.bib46),
word_sorting [Tyen et al. (2023)](#bib.bib42), and zebra_puzzles [Shah
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib34). These instances are mixed and randomly
shuffled to construct the evaluation stream.

### G.3 Evaluation Metrics.

We adopt one-shot success rate (OSR) as the primary evaluation metric,
defined as the fraction of test instances solved correctly on the first
attempt during online adaptation. This metric directly measures the
agent’s immediate response quality under its current policy and
accumulated experience. When multiple attempts are allowed, we
additionally report cumulative success rate (CSR), defined as the
fraction of instances solved within a maximum budget of $`P=4`$ total
attempts, including the initial attempt. This corresponds to at most
three retries after the initial attempt. For methods without
multi-attempt inference, the unavailable metric is marked as “-” in the
result tables. Since Reflexion [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib35)
improves later attempts through within-instance self-reflection rather
than cross-task experience transfer, we report it under CSR in the main
comparison.

To measure inference efficiency, we report tokens per task, the average
total LLM tokens consumed per instance. This count includes all LLM
calls made throughout the full online adaptation pipeline for a task,
rather than only the final task-solving call; in particular, it includes
token usage from auxiliary stages such as memory update and other
adaptation-related inference, as well as additional token consumption
introduced by test-time scaling in baselines that use it. The average is
computed by dividing the total token consumption by the total number of
tasks. Token accounting includes the cost of solving tasks and the cost
of extracting and refining experiences, but excludes evaluator or
judge-model calls.

### G.4 Baselines and Methods.

For baselines and compared methods, we include a Base LLM and four
representative test-time improvement approaches that enhance performance
without parameter updates. These baselines separate test-time scaling
alone, cross-task experience transfer alone, and methods that combine
cross-task adaptation with multi-attempt inference.

Base LLM. Base LLM is evaluated on each benchmark without any external
memory, i.e., the model is queried with the benchmark’s default prompt
template (as provided by the dataset authors) and produces an answer
directly.

Reflexion [Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib35). Reflexion instantiates
test-time scaling through within-instance self-reflection over repeated
attempts. After an attempt, the agent converts task feedback into a
natural-language self-reflection and conditions subsequent attempts on
these reflections. In our comparison, Reflexion does not transfer
experience across different test instances.

MemP [Fang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib14). MemP transfers experience across
tasks but makes only a single attempt per instance. It therefore
represents cross-task experience transfer without multi-attempt
inference.

ACE [Zhang et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib52). ACE treats prompts/contexts as
evolving playbooks and improves them through a modular
generate-reflect-curate loop with structured, incremental updates
designed to prevent context degradation across iterations; ACE supports
both offline prompt optimization and online adaptation via
memory/context updates driven by execution feedback.

ReasoningBank [Ouyang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30). We evaluate
ReasoningBank in its sequential MaTTS setting, where the method combines
cross-task adaptation with multi-attempt inference.

OMCE (ours). OMCE combines memory evolution with online meta-evolution.
OMCE-TS uses Thompson Sampling [Agrawal and Goyal (2013)](#bib.bib2),
while OMCE-UCB uses an optimistic LinUCB-style rule [Chu et al.
(2011)](#bib.bib6); [Abbasi-Yadkori et al. (2011)](#bib.bib1).
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Action Selection Strategies: Thompson Sampling and LinUCB ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")
gives the formal specifications, and
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Additional Theoretical Result for the Meta-Controller ‣ Evolving Memory, Learning to Meta-Control:Test-Time Adaptation for Experience-augmented LLM Agents")
analyzes the optimistic variant.

## Appendix I Case Study on SWE-bench Verified

This section provides a qualitative analysis of OMCE’s behavior on the
SWE-bench Verified dataset. Through three representative examples, we
demonstrate how the Meta-Controller dynamically selects the optimal
cognitive action—DIRECT, EPISODE, GUIDE, or DISTILL—to avoid harmful
memory and improve the success rate of the underlying LLM.

#### Avoiding Harmful Experience (django-16560).

In this task, the objective is to add a violation_error_code parameter
to Django’s BaseConstraint. When forced to use guidance entries, the
model is misled by irrelevant rules (e.g., "Wrong-Layer Patch Trap") and
applies patches to the wrong abstraction layer, resulting in failure.
However, the Meta-Controller accurately calculates low retrieval scores
for both episodic records and guidance entries, confidently selecting
DIRECT ($`a_{\textsc{DIRECT}}`$) (confidence 1.0). By proceeding without
hallucinated guidance, OMCE successfully solves the task, demonstrating
its ability to protect the model from harmful or irrelevant memory.

#### Selecting the Right Experience Type (django-13401).

This task requires modifying the equality semantics of abstract model
fields so they do not compare as equal across different models. This is
a highly sensitive task where EPISODE fails because specific past cases
do not generalize well to core equality logic. Conversely, GUIDE
succeeds. The Meta-Controller successfully identifies this distinction,
routing the task to $`a_{\textsc{GUIDE}}`$. This highlights the critical
importance of selecting the appropriate experience type for tasks that
require architectural guidance rather than specific implementation
details.

#### DISTILL (django-13809).

The goal of this task is to add a --skip-checks option to the runserver
command. The baseline model without experience fails to correctly wire
the option through the execution flow. The Meta-Controller observes that
while there are highly similar episodic records available, there are no
directly applicable guidance entries. Consequently, it selects DISTILL
($`a_{\textsc{DISTILL}}`$) to create a new guidance entry from the
retrieved episodic records. The newly distilled guidance entry correctly
instructs the model on the appropriate modification layer, leading to a
successful resolution. This showcases OMCE’s capability to dynamically
generate new guidance entries when existing experience is insufficient.

## Appendix J Broader Impact

This work introduces Online Meta-Control of Experience (OMCE), a
framework designed to improve the efficiency and reliability of
self-evolving LLM agents. The ability to selectively ignore misleading
or redundant past experiences helps mitigate the propagation of errors
in memory-enhanced agent systems. This contributes to the development of
more robust AI assistants. However, like all frameworks that enhance the
autonomy and efficiency of AI agents, OMCE could potentially be
repurposed for malicious activities. While OMCE offers a pathway toward
more intelligent and resource-efficient agents, its deployment should be
accompanied by continuous monitoring and safety protocols to ensure that
its evolutionary capabilities remain aligned with ethical standards and
human values.

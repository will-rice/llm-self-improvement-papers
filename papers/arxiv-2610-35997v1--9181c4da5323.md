###### Abstract.

One-person companies (OPCs) are emerging enterprises operated by
individual entrepreneurs through digital platforms and LLM-based AI
agents. These highly skilled entrepreneurs are characterized by being
digital nomads, with limited reliance on local ecosystems. Governments
in China use distinct regional support policies to attract mobile OPCs,
turning local support into a cross-regional coordination problem. OPCs
compare regions to maximize their own utility, but imperfect policy
perceptions may cause them to reject otherwise suitable locations.
Existing policy-learning methods optimize resources under fixed
perceptions, while consensus-formation methods aggregate views without
feeding cognitive changes back into policy learning. This disconnect
produces mismatched location choices and inefficient regional policy
adjustment. We introduce PolicyMediator, a cognition-constrained
leader-multi-follower Stackelberg framework with bilateral mediation.
The mediator explains verified policies to OPCs to build awareness and
trust, and reports their needs and unresolved concerns to governments
for policy learning. Its self-evolving skills retain, revise, or prune
strategies using verification, allocation, capacity, and objective
trajectories rather than mediation success alone. Across six-region
simulations in China with five OPC types, five scenarios, and three
OPC-simulator backbones, PolicyMediator ranks first in all 15
OPC-backbone–scenario combinations. Over the eight-baseline mean, it
improves the aggregate government objective, combining economic
performance, employment benefit, and policy-access equality, by
$`42.4\%`$ and average OPC utility by $`40.9\%`$.

###### Keywords: 

One-person companies, multi-regional policy learning, Stackelberg policy

LLM-based AI agents are reshaping firm organization by making
individuals viable units of production([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib51)).
One-person companies (OPCs) exemplify this organizational shift, as Web
platforms, cloud services, and generative AI lower the costs of
acquiring customers, accessing professional services, and completing
complex tasks([Alyakoob and Rahman, 2026](#bib.bib1); [Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib2)). Governmental organizations across China introduce
support policies and entrepreneurial hubs to make OPC development a
priority for entrepreneurship, employment, and AI-enabled industries.
Platform design and digital work infrastructure shape
microentrepreneurial participation([Alyakoob and Rahman,
2026](#bib.bib1); [Doggett et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)), while local
labor-market conditions influence who benefits([Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib2); [Jones and Sen, 2025](#bib.bib3)). OPCs therefore
operate in a Web-enabled entrepreneurial ecosystem, using Web-based
tools and resources to work across regions, access policy information,
and relocate with limited reliance on local infrastructure and teams.

OPCs’ cross-regional location choices make the effects of regional
support policies interdependent. Regional governments in China publish
distinct support packages to attract mobile OPCs, while each OPC
compares regions and selects the location maximizing expected utility.
Yet these choices rely on incomplete, uncertain, and trust-dependent
policy perceptions. Policy disclosure and mediation shape what an OPC
knows, expects to be delivered, and regards as relevant; entry and
relocation alter regional conditions and policy value. Prior work shows
that policy-information frictions affect firm participation in
government support programs and the allocation of assistance ([Humphries
et al., 2020](#bib.bib50)), infrastructure shapes digital-market
access ([Leong et al., 2024](#bib.bib5); [Agarwal and Assenova,
2024](#bib.bib6)), strategic disclosure redirects participation ([Liu et
al., 2024](#bib.bib7)), and mediation identifies common ground without
overriding independent decisions ([Wall et al., 2001](#bib.bib26);
[Susskind et al., 2018](#bib.bib27)). This creates an agent-mediated
socio-economic system in which the challenge is to coordinate regional
policy learning with OPCs’ evolving policy perceptions and location
choices to improve government outcomes and OPC utility.

Existing methods to solve these challenges broadly fall into two
categories. Policy-simulation methods represent heterogeneous
governments and firms with agent-based models([Farmer and Foley,
2009](#bib.bib8); [Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib10)), then use evolutionary
games or reinforcement learning to evaluate policy and allocation([Zheng
et al., 2022](#bib.bib14); [Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). They often
fix policy perceptions or infer needs only from choices, so
implementation concerns may be mistaken for inadequate support.
Consensus methods exchange and aggregate opinions([Tessler et al.,
2024](#bib.bib29)), while mediation diagnoses disagreement and
establishes shared facts([Wall et al., 2001](#bib.bib26); [Susskind et
al., 2018](#bib.bib27); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib30)). They often
reach local agreement through compromise, but rarely update
decision-relevant cognition, feed consensus into policy learning, or
adapt mediation strategies.
Figure [1](#acmlabel1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
illustrates these gaps: policy optimization reacts to realized payoffs
but leaves perception-related concerns unreturned, while consensus
formation aggregates exchanged views without revising policy or its
mediation strategy. Cross-regional coordination instead requires
adaptive consensus through bilateral mediation, namely verified common
ground and feasible options that evolve with policy conditions, OPC
cognition, and allocation outcomes.

Therefore, Addressing this coordination problem presents two key
challenges:

- •
  How can policy learning distinguish cognitive barriers from policy
  mismatches across regions?
- •
  How can mediation build adaptive consensus while balancing OPC
  interests, economic performance, and welfare?

We model this interplay by combining heterogeneous firm profiles with
cognitive pathways of policy acceptance([Aguilera et al.,
2025](#bib.bib19)). OPC cognition captures what firms know, expect to be
delivered, and perceive as relevant, thereby shaping approximately
rational entry and relocation. Regional governments are modeled as a
single cooperative leader that optimizes each region’s policy under
observed policy-intensity bounds and congestion effects. Bilateral
mediator uses explanations grounded in verified policy sources to
improve OPC decisions, and translates OPC needs and unresolved concerns
into structured feedback for government policy updates. Mediator agents
retain evolving skills only when their allocation and objective effects
remain beneficial. As shown in
Figure [1](#acmlabel1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1. Introduction ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems"),
the mediator combines verified policy conditions with OPC concerns and
preferences to produce feasible location options and policy revisions.

We propose PolicyMediator, which integrates multi-region Stackelberg
policy learning with bilateral mediation. In each round, governments
publish regional policy packages, and mediators help OPCs assess
feasible options under verified conditions. OPCs then independently
choose a region or non-entry based on their updated policy understanding
and expected utility. The resulting allocations, government outcomes,
and unresolved OPC concerns inform policy learning and mediation skill
updates for the next round. This closes the loop between government
decisions, mediated cognition, and OPC responses. The process continues
until policies, allocations, and both government and OPC objectives
stabilize. Consensus concerns verified facts and workable options; it
permits disagreement and does not require unanimous policy acceptance.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

- •
  To our knowledge, we are the first to formulate heterogeneous OPC
  entry and location choice as a cognition-constrained
  leader–multi-follower Stackelberg model that includes bounded
  responses, policy interdependence, congestion, and equal policy
  access.
- •
  We couple policy learning with bilateral mediation and outcome-aware
  skill evolution, allowing explanations to influence OPC choices and
  structured concerns to improve subsequent government policies.
- •
  Across six Chinese regions, five scenarios, and three OPC backbones,
  PolicyMediator ranks first on both metrics in all 15 settings,
  improving the government objective/OPC utility by $`42.4\%/40.9\%`$
  over the eight-baseline mean and $`6.3\%/15.3\%`$ over the strongest
  baseline.

## 2. Related Work

Multi-Agent Policy Making. Multi-agent policy making uses agent-based
models to characterize heterogeneous actors([Farmer and Foley,
2009](#bib.bib8)), while intervention systems optimize evolving networks
and urban plans([Papachristou et al., 2023](#bib.bib11); [Yong et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). For strategic interactions, game-theoretic methods
learn leader–follower policy responses([Bai et al., 2021](#bib.bib13);
[Zhong et al., 2023](#bib.bib20)), while reinforcement learning
optimizes economic policies and taxation([Zheng et al.,
2022](#bib.bib14); [Mi et al., 2024](#bib.bib21); [Zhang et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib22)). LLM agents enrich simulated societies with memory,
reflection, and planning([Park et al., 2023](#bib.bib9); [Li et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10)), while PolicySim anticipates behavioral responses to
policy([Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). With fixed policy perceptions,
these methods cannot distinguish policy mismatch from cognition-driven
rejection. PolicyMediator instead links mediated cognitive updates to
allocation and subsequent policy learning.

Consensus Formation and Mediation. Consensus formation coordinates
beliefs and interests through opinion-dynamics models of disagreement,
coevolving beliefs, polarization reduction, and bi-level consensus under
social influence([Bindel et al., 2011](#bib.bib23); [Auletta et al.,
2019](#bib.bib24); [Musco et al., 2018](#bib.bib25); [Tu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib32)). Beyond opinion dynamics, mediation diagnoses
disagreement and structures negotiation([Wall et al.,
2001](#bib.bib26)), while joint fact-finding establishes shared evidence
before bargaining([Susskind et al., 2018](#bib.bib27)). Computational
approaches support mediation by identifying common ground([Tessler et
al., 2024](#bib.bib29)), organizing dispute resolution([Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib30)), and coordinating LLM exchanges([Pitre et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31)). Yet opinion aggregation and compromise can trade
away individual interests without correcting the cognition that drives
later decisions. PolicyMediator preserves heterogeneous choices and
evaluates policy updates by their downstream government objective.

Self-Evolving Skills. Self-evolving agents adapt reusable skills through
accumulated experience. Foundational approaches formalize extended
actions as options([Sutton et al., 1999](#bib.bib33)), discover diverse
behaviors through unsupervised learning([Eysenbach et al.,
2019](#bib.bib34)), and address retention and adaptation through
continual learning([Wang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib35)). Building on these
foundations, language agents reflect on trajectories([Shinn et al.,
2023](#bib.bib36)), refine outputs([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib37)),
and learn from collaborative feedback([Zhang et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib38)). Recent skill libraries further support
experience-driven revision and composition by organizing reusable
abstractions and implementations([Yu et al., 2026](#bib.bib39); [Yang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib40); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib41); [Jiao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib42)). Task-success-driven evolution may retain persuasive
skills with harmful downstream effects. PolicyMediator instead evaluates
skills by factual validity and downstream system outcomes before
retaining, revising, or pruning them.

## 3. Problem Formulation

### 3.1. Framework Overview

Figure [2](#acmlabel2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2. Related Work ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
presents the proposed PolicyMediator framework. Panel (a) models
regional governments and heterogeneous OPCs. With regional policies
centrally coordinated, governments across $`\mathcal{R}`$ form a single
cooperative leader while retaining distinct local policies. In panel
(b), the leader publishes a feasible policy profile
$`\mathbf{p}^{k}=(\mathbf{p}_{r}^{k})_{r\in\mathcal{R}}`$; mediators
verify and explain its conditions, elicit concerns, and update cognition
before OPCs choose a region or non-entry. Allocations and government
outcomes inform policy revision. Panel (c) uses cognition, location, and
allocation trajectories to compare original and candidate skills in
matched contexts, retain, revise, or prune them, and feed evaluated
outcomes into the next policy cycle.

Within each policy cycle, time is discretized into OPC decision steps
$`t\geq 0`$, with $`k=\lfloor t/H\rfloor`$ indexing $`H`$-step policy
cycles. Governments hold policies fixed within a cycle while OPCs update
their choices. Let $`x_{ir}^{t}=\mathbf{1}(x_{i}^{t}=r)`$ and
$`x_{i}^{t}=(x_{ir}^{t})_{r\in\mathcal{R}}`$. For cycle $`k`$,
$`\mathcal{T}_{k}^{\star}`$ is the longest suffix ending at $`(k+1)H-1`$
such that, at every transition, at most an $`\epsilon`$ fraction of OPCs
switch regions. We write $`M_{k}=|\mathcal{T}_{k}^{\star}|`$ and use
$`\langle\cdot\rangle_{k}`$ for averages over this stabilized window.

### 3.2. Government agents.

Each government $`G_{r}`$ selects cash, finance, computing and data,
space, talent, and market-access policies. Its package is
$`\mathbf{p}_{r}^{k}=(\mathbf{y}_{r}^{k},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}^{k})`$,
where $`y_{rj}^{k}\in\{0,1\}`$ indicates activation and
$`\theta_{rj}^{k}`$ specifies intensity. For each category
$`j=1,\ldots,6`$, the observed policy-derived bound
$`\widehat{\theta}_{rj}^{\mathrm{obs}}`$ defines feasibility:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (1) |     |

````math
\mathbf{p}_{r}^{k}\in\mathcal{F}_{r}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y_{rj}^{k}\in\{0,1\},\qquad 0\leq\theta_{rj}^{k}\leq y_{rj}^{k}\widehat{\theta}_{rj}^{\mathrm{obs}}.
``` |  |

At $`k=0`$, each region is initialized with its observed policy profile.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Data Construction ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
details the construction, scaling, and initialization of these policy

### 3.3. OPC agents and regional attributes.

Each OPC chooses $`a_{i}^{t}\in A=\mathcal{R}\cup\{0\}`$, where $`0`$
denotes non-entry. Its type $`l_{i}`$ determines policy sensitivity
through $`\Gamma=(\gamma_{lj})\in[0,1]^{5\times 6}`$;
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Prompts ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
specifies the profile fields and the procedure for initializing
cognition from verified regional evidence. Because acceptance depends on
heterogeneous attributes and how policy information is
processed ([Aguilera et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Petty and Cacioppo,
1986](#bib.bib45); [Gefen et al., 2003](#bib.bib43)), we represent
policy-information-induced cognitive barriers by awareness
$`z_{i}^{t}`$, trust in policy delivery $`b_{i}^{t}`$, and
region-specific relevance
$`q_{ir}^{t}=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{6}q_{irj}^{t}`$, the mean of six
policy-category perceptions for region $`r`$. Thus,
$`\Psi_{ir}^{\mathrm{opc},t}=(z_{i}^{t},b_{i}^{t},q_{ir}^{t})`$ and
$`\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}^{t}=(z_{i}^{t},b_{i}^{t},(q_{ir}^{t})_{r\in\mathcal{R}})\in[0,1]^{2+|\mathcal{R}|}`$.

Initial cognition $`\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}^{0}`$ is generated
accordingly. At step $`t`$, the LLM analysis operator
$`\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{LLM}}`$ maps the profile, interaction history
$`\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\leq t}`$, dialogue $`\mathcal{D}_{i}^{t}`$, and
evidence $`\mathcal{S}^{k}`$ to the next cognitive update:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (2) |  | $`\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}^{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\Psi}\odot\tanh\!\left(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{LLM}}\left(\mathrm{profile}_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i}^{\leq t},\mathcal{D}_{i}^{t},\mathcal{S}^{k}\right)\right),`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}^{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\Pi_{[0,1]^{2+|\mathcal{R}|}}\left(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}^{t}+\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{i}^{t+1}\right).`$ |  |

Here $`\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\Psi}=(.15,.12,(.10)_{r\in\mathcal{R}})`$
permits bidirectional updates, while $`\mathcal{S}^{k}`$ faithfully
discloses verified negative as well as positive policy information. The
second line accumulates these changes and projects every cognitive
component back to $`[0,1]`$.

Because location value depends on earnings, industrial compatibility,
administrative costs, and congestion, region $`r`$ is represented by
baseline payoff $`v_{r}`$, type fit $`m_{lr}`$, application difficulty
$`d_{r}`$, and capacity $`K_{r}`$;
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Data Construction ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
gives their construction. Type $`l`$ receives pre-policy return
$`m_{lr}v_{r}`$. To represent OPC choice across regions, market-access
support reduces cross-regional entry or relocation costs to
$`K_{r}^{{entry},k}=\max(d_{r}-\theta_{r6}^{k},0)`$.

## 4. Cognition-Constrained Stackelberg Game

### 4.1. Objective Function and Constraints

Objective Function of the OPCs. Given $`\mathbf{p}^{k}`$, OPC $`i`$
evaluates region $`r`$ using its perceived policy benefit and the
capacity penalty:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (3) |  | $`\displaystyle B_{ir}^{k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{6}\gamma_{l_{i}j}\theta_{rj}^{k},`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle g_{ir}^{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=b_{i}^{t}\sqrt{z_{i}^{t}q_{ir}^{t}},`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle P_{r}^{\mathrm{cap},t-1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\left[(n_{r}^{t-1}-K_{r})/K_{r}\right]_{+}^{2},`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle W_{ir}^{\mathrm{opc},t}`$ | $`\displaystyle=m_{l_{i}r}v_{r}+g_{ir}^{t}B_{ir}^{k}-K_{r}^{\mathrm{entry},k}-P_{r}^{\mathrm{cap},t-1}.`$ |  |

Here, $`W_{ir}^{\mathrm{opc},t}`$ is OPC $`i`$’s perceived payoff,
$`g_{ir}^{t}\in[0,1]`$ scales only its expected policy benefit, and
$`n_{r}^{t-1}=\sum_{i}x_{ir}^{t-1}`$ is the latest observed occupancy.
Initially, all OPCs begin with non-entry. Thus, OPCs respond to observed
congestion. Reported OPC utility uses the same payoff with
$`g_{ir}^{t}`$ replaced by policy fulfillment rate $`\rho_{r}^{k}`$
(default $`1`$), so cognition performance alone cannot raise evaluated
outcomes. Following aggregate congestion modeling ([Rosenthal,
1973](#bib.bib44)), the convex penalty is zero within $`K_{r}`$ and
quadratic above it. We model OPC $`i`$’s boundedly rational choice using
a logit response with temperature $`\tau_{i}>0`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (4) |  | $`\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{t}(r)`$ | $`\displaystyle:=\Pr(a_{i}^{t}=r\mid s^{t},\mathbf{p}^{k})=\frac{\exp\!\left(W_{ir}^{\mathrm{opc},t}/\tau_{i}\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{s\in A}\exp\!\left(W_{is}^{\mathrm{opc},t}/\tau_{i}\right)},`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{i}^{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle\in\arg\max_{r\in A}\rho_{i}^{t}(r)=\arg\max_{r\in A}W_{ir}^{\mathrm{opc},t}.`$ |  |

Thus, $`\widehat{a}_{i}^{t}`$ is the highest-payoff city (or non-entry),
while $`a_{i}^{t}\sim\rho_{i}^{t}`$ is realized. As $`\tau_{i}\to 0`$,
the response approaches this maximum; positive temperature smooths
response probabilities with respect to OPC payoffs, facilitating leader
policy optimization.

Objective Function of the Governments. Following SDG 8 on productive
employment and SDG 10 on equal opportunity and reduced inequalities of
outcome ([United Nations, 2015](#bib.bib46)), we divide the government
objective into economic performance and welfare; welfare comprises
employment benefits and equal policy access across OPC types:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (5) |  |
``` math
W^{G,k}=\frac{1}{3|\mathcal{R}|}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\left(L_{r}^{\mathrm{E},k}+L_{r}^{\mathrm{emp},k}+L_{r}^{\mathrm{eq},k}\right),
``` |  |

where the three components are placed on bounded, comparable scales and
averaged equally. Reported $`W^{G,k}`$ values are scaled by
$`\zeta_{W}`$. Let $`\widehat{R}_{\max}=\max_{l,s}\widehat{R}_{ls}`$ and
$`\bar{u}_{lr}^{k}=\frac{\rho_{r}^{k}}{6}\sum_{j}\gamma_{lj}\theta_{rj}^{k}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (6) |  | $`\displaystyle L_{r}^{\mathrm{E},k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{NM_{k}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}^{\star}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}x_{ir}^{t}m_{l_{i}r}v_{r}-\widetilde{C}_{r}^{\mathrm{dir},k},`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle L_{r}^{\mathrm{emp},k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}x_{ir}^{t}\widehat{R}_{l_{i}r}\right\rangle_{k}}{N\widehat{R}_{\max}+\epsilon_{R}},`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle L_{r}^{\mathrm{eq},k}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\sum_{l=1}^{5}\bar{u}_{lr}^{k}\right)^{3}}{25\sum_{l=1}^{5}(\bar{u}_{lr}^{k})^{2}}.`$ |  |

The terms capture net economic performance, labor compensation, and
equal policy access; $`\widetilde{C}_{r}^{\mathrm{dir},k}`$ is the
direct fiscal expenditure of region $`r`$’s package, and the latter two
terms define welfare. $`L_{r}^{\mathrm{eq},k}`$ measures equal policy
access by multiplying Jain’s fairness index by the mean policy benefit
across OPC types.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Data Construction ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
defines the proxies, normalization, and ranges.

### 4.2. Response-Induced Markov Decision Process

At cycle boundary $`k`$, the Markov state $`s^{k}`$ contains policies,
allocation, cognition, regional conditions, and skill state, with
$`\mathbf{o}^{k}=\Omega(s^{k})`$, where $`\Omega`$ is the deterministic
regional-observation map. Given feasible action $`\mathbf{u}`$,
conditionally independent OPC choices induce
$`\rho(\mathbf{a}\mid s,\mathbf{u})=\prod_{i}\rho_{i}(a_{i}\mid s,\mathbf{u})`$.
Stackelberg here is generalized: a leader–response kernel in which
followers respond boundedly rationally and payoffs depend on aggregate
state.

Following the leader–follower construction ([Zhong et al.,
2023](#bib.bib20)), integrating $`W^{G}`$ and the transition kernel over
$`\rho`$ induces the return of the single cooperative leader

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (7) |  | $`\displaystyle J_{G}^{\rho}(\pi_{G};s)=\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[`$ | $`\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{G}^{k}W^{G,k}\,\Bigm|\,s^{0}=s,`$ |  |
|  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{k}=\pi_{G}(s^{k}),\ \mathbf{a}^{k}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid s^{k},\mathbf{u}^{k})\Bigg].`$ |  |

The ideal response-conditioned policy is
$`\pi_{G}^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\pi_{G}\in\Pi_{G}}J_{G}^{\rho}(\pi_{G};s)`$.
Before developing an approximate solution, we establish conditions under
which a stationary optimal leader policy exists.

###### Proposition 0 (Stationary optimal leader policy under a logit response kernel).

For the structured-state abstraction, if the state and feasible action
spaces are compact, OPC payoffs are continuous, the response-averaged
reward is bounded and upper semicontinuous, the response-averaged
transition kernel is weakly continuous, $`\tau_{i}>0`$, and
$`\delta_{G}<1`$, then a stationary optimal leader policy
$`\pi_{G}^{\star}`$ exists.

The proof is provided in
Appendix [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1. Existence of a Stationary Optimal Leader Policy ‣ Appendix D Proofs ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems").
It establishes a theoretical target for the approximate policy learning
developed next.

### 4.3. Cooperative Policy Learning

We use cooperative multi-actor zeroth-order policy-gradient learning to
optimize regional policies, as mediated OPC responses make the simulator
non-differentiable. Each actor $`\pi_{\omega_{r}}`$ maps local
observation $`o_{r}^{k}`$ to adjustment
$`\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r}^{k}`$, sharing the government objective
as reward.

Government Observations. At boundary $`t=kH`$, $`k\geq 1`$, government

``` |  |

Policy Generation. Each regional government $`G_{r}`$ uses its
deterministic actor $`\pi_{\omega_{r}}`$ to generate a bounded

Clipping is componentwise, $`0<\delta_{\theta}^{\max}\leq 1`$.
Projection onto $`\mathcal{F}_{r}^{\theta}`$ sets
$`y_{rj}^{k}=\mathbf{1}(\theta_{rj}^{k}>0)`$ and yields the package
committed throughout cycle $`k`$; the pre-projection adjustment is the
actor output, and projection belongs to the environment transition.

Zeroth-Order Policy Learning. Let
$`\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{r})_{r\in\mathcal{R}}`$ contain $`D`$
jointly perturbed actor parameters. At cycle $`k`$,
$`J_{k}(\boldsymbol{\omega})`$ is the expected rollout return from the
saved simulator state, including its current allocation, cognition, and
skill library. Following Gaussian zeroth-order optimization ([Nesterov
and Spokoiny, 2017](#bib.bib49); [Ghadimi and Lan, 2013](#bib.bib48)),
define
$`J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}[J_{k}(\boldsymbol{\omega}+h\boldsymbol{\zeta})]`$
for $`\boldsymbol{\zeta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{D})`$. Joint,
two-sided rollouts start from the same saved state and use matched
random seeds:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (10) |  |  | $`\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}=\frac{\widehat{J}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k}+h\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{k})-\widehat{J}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k}-h\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{k})}{2h}\,\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{k},`$ |  |
|  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\omega_{r}^{k+1}=\omega_{r}^{k}+\alpha[\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}]_{r},`$ |  |

where each rollout recomputes mediation, skill evolution, OPC responses,
congestion, and $`W^{G}`$ from the common checkpoint. Since returns are
stochastic and simulator states evolve, we analyze gradient estimation
and stationarity under cross-cycle drift.

###### Proposition 0 (Zeroth-order gradient estimation and stationarity).

If paired returns are unbiased and lie in an interval of width $`R`$,
then
$`\mathbb{E}_{k}[\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}]=\nabla J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k})`$,
$`L_{h}\leq R/(2h^{2})`$, and the conditional estimator variance
satisfies $`\sigma_{g}^{2}\leq R^{2}D/(4h^{2})`$. Let
$`V_{T}=\sum_{k<T-1}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}|J_{k+1,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega})-J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega})|`$.
For $`\alpha\leq 1/L_{h}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (11) |  |
``` math
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k<T}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2(\Delta+V_{T})}{\alpha T}+L_{h}\alpha\sigma_{g}^{2},
``` |  |

where
$`\Delta=\sup_{k,\boldsymbol{\omega}}J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega})-J_{0,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{0})`$.

optimization progress, evolving simulator states, and rollout noise;

## 5. Bidirectional Mediation with Evolving Skills

Our preliminary study
(Section [6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2. Preliminary Study ‣ 6. Experiments ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems"))
found that regional suitability and heterogeneous policy perceptions
jointly shape OPC entry, leading us to design a mediator that addresses
cognitive barriers through context-adaptive skills. This section details
how these skills are structured under evidence constraints, deployed in
bilateral interactions, and refined through outcome-based evaluation.

### 5.1. Skill Structure

A skill maps an OPC profile, concern, and verified evidence to an
explanation, feasible recommendation, and structured government
feedback, while recording applicability and outcomes. We manually
initialize $`\mathcal{L}^{0}`$ with 14 skills distilled from OPC
community experience; new candidates remain temporary until repeated
evaluation supports retention.
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Prompts ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
gives the schema.

Each skill combines evidence constraints with a mediation strategy.
Following the Context–Mechanism–Outcome framework ([Dalkin et al.,
2015](#bib.bib28)), it conditions messages on capacity and industrial
fit and tracks cognition, allocation, and $`W^{G}`$. Let
$`\sigma_{ri}^{t}`$ be the message to OPC $`i`$ and
$`\mathcal{S}_{ri}^{k}`$ its cited regional evidence. It must satisfy

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (12) |  |
``` math
\operatorname{Verify}(\sigma_{ri}^{t},\mathcal{S}_{ri}^{k})=1,\qquad\mathcal{S}_{ri}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{r}^{k}.
``` |  |

Thus, $`\operatorname{Verify}=1`$ only when every factual claim is
traceable to the current verified evidence set. Unsupported claims or
omitted material conditions trigger revision, and capacity and local
evidence are rechecked whenever a skill is invoked or reused.

While evidence constraints ensure factual reliability, they cannot
address differences in OPC information processing. We therefore adopt
the Elaboration Likelihood Model ([Petty and Cacioppo,
1986](#bib.bib45)) to guide central-route reasoning, peripheral
credibility cues, and adaptive route switching. Accordingly, each skill
comprises applicability conditions, an evidence boundary, a mediation
route, feasible recommendation branches, and an outcome record.

### 5.2. Mediation-Embedded Game Pipeline

OPC-Facing Dialogue. Because cognition-constrained entry requires
credible evidence rather than generic persuasion, each mediator
retrieves a skill using the policy context, implementation outcomes, OPC
profile, cognitive state, and regional evidence after $`\mathbf{p}^{k}`$
is published. At step $`t`$, it composes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (13) |  |
``` math
\sigma_{ri}^{t}=\operatorname{Compose}\left(\ell_{ri}^{t},\mathcal{S}_{ri}^{k},o_{r}^{k},\nu_{ri}^{t}\right),\qquad\ell_{ri}^{t}\in\mathcal{L}^{k}\cup\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{k}.
``` |  |

where $`\operatorname{Compose}`$ is the evidence-constrained message
constructor and $`\nu_{ri}^{t}\in\mathcal{R}\cup\{0\}`$ denotes the
recommended region, an alternative, or non-entry. Only verified messages
enter $`\mathcal{D}_{i}^{t}`$ and update cognition through
Eq. ([2](#S3.E2 "In 3.3. OPC agents and regional attributes. ‣ 3. Problem Formulation ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems"));
the OPC then samples its own action using
Eq. ([4](#S4.E4 "In 4.1. Objective Function and Constraints ‣ 4. Cognition-Constrained Stackelberg Game ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")).

Government-Facing Feedback. Because unresolved concerns can indicate
policy mismatch rather than misunderstanding, the mediator summarizes
unmet needs, eligibility difficulties, delivery concerns, and observed
responses after cycle $`k`$. A government-facing skill encodes this
evidence as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (14) |  |
``` math
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r,\mathrm{LLM}}^{k+1}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\operatorname{Encoder}_{\mathrm{LLM}}\!\left(o_{r}^{k},\mathcal{D}_{r}^{k},\Delta x^{k},W^{G,k}\right),\pm\delta_{\mathrm{LLM}}^{\max}\right),
``` |  |

where $`\operatorname{Encoder}_{\mathrm{LLM}}`$ is the
government-feedback encoder, $`0<\delta_{\mathrm{LLM}}^{\max}\leq 1`$,
$`\mathcal{D}_{r}^{k}`$ collects region-$`r`$ dialogues, and allocation
change is the switching rate
$`\Delta x^{k}=N^{-1}\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}(x_{i}^{(k+1)H}\neq x_{i}^{kH})\in[0,1].`$
The suggestion augments the next government observation
$`\widetilde{o}_{r}^{k+1}=\left(o_{r}^{k+1},\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}_{r,\mathrm{LLM}}^{k+1}\right).`$
Regional actors use these augmented observations and share the realized
government objective during policy learning. The suggestion is
auxiliary: the learned government policy still produces the formal,
feasibility-projected adjustment. Thus, the pipeline verifies evidence,
explains conditions, updates cognition, presents feasible options, and
returns unresolved concerns for the next policy cycle.

### 5.3. Self-Evolving Skill Lifecycle

The skill library follows four stages: candidate construction;
deployment and trace collection; outcome-based evaluation and revision;
and promotion with redundancy pruning. For traces
$`\mathfrak{B}_{\ell}^{k}`$ involving skill $`\ell`$ through cycle
$`k`$, we evaluate

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (15) |  |
``` math
\widehat{J}_{\ell}^{k}=\operatorname{Eval}\left(\mathfrak{B}_{\ell}^{k};\Delta\boldsymbol{\Psi},\Delta x,\Delta W^{G},\bar{V}\right)\in[0,1],
``` |  |

where $`\operatorname{Eval}`$ is the normalized trace-scoring operator
and $`\bar{V}`$ aggregates verification failures. Validated skills with
consistently positive evidence form the promotion set
$`\mathcal{P}^{k}`$; uncertain or context-mismatched skills are revised
in the temporary pool, and ineffective or redundant skills are pruned:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (16) |  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{k+1}=\operatorname{Prune}_{K_{\mathcal{L}}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{k}\cup\mathcal{P}^{k}\right),
``` |  |

where $`\operatorname{Prune}_{K_{\mathcal{L}}}`$ retains at most
$`K_{\mathcal{L}}`$ skills.
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3. Skill Evaluation and Update Protocol ‣ Appendix B Prompts ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
specifies the evaluation prompts, paired simulations, threshold, and
update procedure. Skill evaluation measures factual validity, cognition
and allocation changes, and marginal $`W^{G}`$, going beyond persuasion
success or user satisfaction.

## 6. Experiments

We first validate simulated entry patterns, then compare welfare across
policies and OPC-simulator backbones, and finally examine ablations,
mechanisms, a case study, and interactive judgments.

### 6.1. Experimental Setup

We evaluate PolicyMediator in an agent-based environment spanning six
Chinese regions, five OPC types, six policy categories, and five
scenarios: baseline conditions (S1), higher uncovered entry costs (S2),
lower policy redemption (S3), greater OPC mobility (S4), and a
technology-sector shock (S5). These settings, together with industrial
fit, entry difficulty, capacity, and baseline returns, are derived from
publicly available Chinese government policies, community tags, and
socioeconomic statistics.

We vary the OPC-simulator backbone among Qwen3.5-9B, Ministral 3-8B, and
Gemma 4-12B, while fixing Qwen for mediation, explanation, and consensus
modules to isolate robustness to simulated OPC behavior. We use four
baseline categories which are nonadaptive controls (No Policy and Static
Welfare); online learning (Contextual Bandit ([Li et al.,
2010](#bib.bib16); [Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)) and
Carbon-PPO ([Wang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib17))); objective-guided policy
optimization (Nash Welfare ([Zhang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib22)) and
Belief-Robust ([Ciullo et al., 2023](#bib.bib18))); and consensus
formation (Bi-level Consensus ([Tu et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)) and
CONSENSAGENT ([Pitre et al., 2025](#bib.bib31))). Policy-learning
baselines except No Policy share a frozen generic explainer without
government feedback or skill evolution, whereas consensus methods retain
their native mechanisms. We report the aggregate government objective
$`W^{G}`$, comprising economic performance, employment benefit, and
policy-access equality, together with average realized OPC utility.
Appendices A and C.1–C.2 provide data construction, implementation, and
baseline details.

### 6.2. Preliminary Study

Before comparing complete systems, we first examine whether simulated
OPC outcomes yield a plausible ranking of regional attractiveness.
Holding baseline policies fixed, we record OPC entry shares and utility
levels across six cities and compare the resulting city ranking with an
descriptive entry-friendliness benchmark constructed from crawled public
data. As shown in
Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.3. Self-Evolving Skill Lifecycle ‣ 5. Bidirectional Mediation with Evolving Skills ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems"),
only the final adjacent pair swaps positions, yielding strong overall
rank agreement (Spearman $`\rho=0.943`$, $`p=0.00167`$). This result
indicates that the simulated payoffs preserve the cross-city gradient.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Data Construction ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
provides the data sources and additional analyses.

### 6.3. Overall Performance

We compare methods on $`W^{G}`$ and OPC utility, then decompose
$`W^{G}`$ into economic performance, employment benefit, policy-access
equality, and their underlying components. As shown in Table ,
PolicyMediator ranks first on both metrics in all 15 settings, averaging
$`9.94`$–$`9.97`$ and $`0.719`$–$`0.720`$, respectively, and exceeding

primary contributors, with skill mechanisms providing additional gains.

### 6.5. Interactive Evaluation

The interactive evaluation assesses mediation-response quality across
decision-relevant dimensions. Ten trained evaluators—two OPC
practitioners, four human–computer interaction or policy-modeling
experts, and four graduate students—made blinded pairwise judgments on
randomized outputs; Appendix C.1 details the protocol and training.
Restraint measures avoidance of pressure or unsupported promises;
regional sensitivity measures use of region-specific evidence; targeting
measures response to the stated concern. Specificity requires concrete,
verifiable conditions or next steps, while progression requires
non-repetitive resolution across turns.
Table [2](#S6.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 6.3. Overall Performance ‣ 6. Experiments ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")
reports wins and losses, omitting ties. PolicyMediator wins 13 of 15
criterion-level comparisons, including all five against the Generic
Explainer. It trails only CONSENSAGENT in specificity and Contextual
Bandit in targeting, indicating that evaluators broadly prefer its
responses across the interactive criteria.

## 7. Conclusion, Limitations and Future Work

We presented PolicyMediator for cross-regional policy learning when
incomplete policy information causes OPCs to reject otherwise suitable
regions. It combines cognition-constrained Stackelberg learning,
verified bilateral mediation, and outcome-aware skill evolution to
distinguish cognitive barriers from policy mismatch and convert OPC
concerns into policy feedback. This closed loop improves regional
matching, economic performance, employment benefits, and policy-access
equality beyond disconnected policy optimization. The results apply to
coordinated-government settings with verifiable policies, comparable
regional options, and observable entry and capacity signals. Limitations
include simulated OPC responses and cooperative-government assumptions,
restricting generalization to competitive jurisdictions and long-term
behavior. Future work will consider noncooperative regions, extend to
broader macroeconomic settings, and further investigate welfare outcomes
and trade-offs.

Derived and controlled variables. For each region and category, observed

### A.2. Scales and normalization

To ensure comparability across modeled variables, policy coverage and
infrastructure are min–max scaled across the six regions, with entry
difficulty defined as the reversed infrastructure score. Monetary and
employment quantities are divided by their dataset-level reference
values, while identifiers and counts remain unscaled.

### B.2. Skill Library and Theoretical Routes

The skill library operationalizes two complementary theories.
Context–Mechanism–Outcome (CMO) analysis asks which mechanism works for
which OPC and region: context records policy, capacity, fit, firm type,
and cognition; mechanisms record clarification, disclosure, trust
repair, or redirection; outcomes cover verification, cognition, choice,
allocation, and $`W^{G}`$. Eight fact-check skills examine source,
capacity, fit, amount, eligibility, timeline, risk, and norm evidence.
The Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM) determines how verified
information is communicated. Central-route skills provide detailed
eligibility, benefit, procedure, fit, and capacity-risk reasoning when
the OPC has sufficient relevance and processing readiness.
Peripheral-route skills use concise source cues, trust repair,
policy-access explanations, verifiable cases, and low-cost next steps
when awareness, attention, or trust is limited. Route-switching skills
move from inquiry to diagnosis, deepen or summarize explanations, and
convert cases into explicit comparisons as the concern changes. Both
routes obey the same evidence boundary; third-party audit or
certification is mentioned only when supported by regional evidence.

Each skill record stores its target OPC types and cognitive states,
applicability conditions, admissible evidence, technique, dialogue
protocol, prohibited behavior, and evaluation history. It follows four
stages: candidate construction, temporary deployment and trace
collection, outcome-based evaluation and revision, and promotion with
redundancy pruning. Evolution therefore changes reusable
evidence-grounded skills and their applicability.

### B.3. Skill Evaluation and Update Protocol

This protocol turns interactions into reusable knowledge without
rewarding mediation success alone. Each invocation records context,
route, evidence, dialogue, verification, cognition, and response; cycle
completion adds allocation and $`W^{G}`$. Matched-context evaluation
covers validity, concern resolution, recommendation quality, cognition,
allocation, failures, and marginal $`W^{G}`$. Verified candidates above
$`\tau_{J}`$ as evolution cycles are promoted; uncertain ones are
revised and retested, while ineffective or redundant skills are removed
under capacity $`K_{\mathcal{L}}`$. Cross-region reuse rechecks local
evidence and capacity, and promotion requires repeated conditional
performance.

### C.2. Baselines

The baselines cover nonadaptive controls, online learning,
objective-guided optimization, and consensus formation under matched
populations, states, feasible bounds, and seeds. No Policy sets all
policy intensities to zero and omits policy explanation. Static Welfare
is our one-shot control: it searches feasible packages at the initial
state and then fixes the best package, following the static-comparison
role rather than the learning algorithm of AI Economist ([Zheng et al.,
2022](#bib.bib14)). Contextual Bandit adapts PolicySim’s
online-intervention setting and uses upper-confidence exploration over
fixed policy-adjustment arms ([Li et al., 2010](#bib.bib16); [Huang et
al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). Carbon-PPO, where “Carbon” denotes its
carbon-market source domain, adapts the government planner of Wang et
al. ([Wang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib17)) to continuous regional-policy
adjustments with clipped PPO updates. Nash Welfare replaces the
aggregate objective with the geometric mean of type-level
utilities ([Zhang et al., 2024b](#bib.bib22)). Belief-Robust adapts
BIRDM by reweighting perturbed OPC-response scenarios and selecting the
lowest-regret package ([Ciullo et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)). Bi-level
Consensus balances subgroup opinion adjustment and consensus cost under
social influence ([Tu et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)). CONSENSAGENT uses
repeated LLM discussions with sycophancy mitigation ([Pitre et al.,
2025](#bib.bib31)).

### C.3. Preliminary Study

The preliminary study checks whether the simulator produces
interpretable heterogeneity before evaluating PolicyMediator. With
observed policies fixed, the simulated six-city ranking agrees with a
public-data-derived entry-friendliness proxy (Spearman $`\rho=.943`$,
$`p=.0048`$). Return decomposition shows that economic and industrial
conditions establish most of the cross-city gradient, while policy
support modifies rather than overturns structural suitability. Across
OPC types within a city, initial cognitive assessments span up to
$`.30`$, confirming that the profile model does not collapse
heterogeneous firms to a common response.

Two further diagnostics qualify the ranking result. In a larger 500-OPC
stress test, the four leading cities settle at similar entry shares of
$`22`$–$`24\%`$ despite different initial attractiveness, because
congestion redirects later entrants. Moreover, mean within-city
type-level support balance ($`.820`$) exceeds cross-city spatial balance
($`.774`$); Hefei reaches high type-level balance ($`.854`$) while one
professional-support type still has negative net utility ($`-.09`$).
Thus balanced support can coexist with uniformly weak outcomes, and
entry dispersion or balance alone should not be interpreted as welfare
improvement.

### D.1. Existence of a Stationary Optimal Leader Policy

###### Proof of Proposition [4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 0 (Stationary optimal leader policy under a logit response kernel). ‣ 4.2. Response-Induced Markov Decision Process ‣ 4. Cognition-Constrained Stackelberg Game ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems").

For finite $`A`$ and $`\tau_{i}>0`$,
Eq. ([4](#S4.E4 "In 4.1. Objective Function and Constraints ‣ 4. Cognition-Constrained Stackelberg Game ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems"))
has a positive denominator; payoff continuity thus makes each
$`\rho_{i}(\cdot\mid s,\mathbf{u})`$ and $`\rho=\prod_{i}\rho_{i}`$
continuous. Define

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (17) |  | $`\displaystyle\bar{R}^{\rho}(s,\mathbf{u})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\rho(\mathbf{a}\mid s,\mathbf{u})W^{G}(s,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{a}),`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\bar{P}^{\rho}(\cdot\mid s,\mathbf{u})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\rho(\mathbf{a}\mid s,\mathbf{u})P(\cdot\mid s,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{a}).`$ |  |

The assumptions make $`\bar{R}^{\rho}`$ bounded and upper semicontinuous
and $`\bar{P}^{\rho}`$ weakly continuous. For feasible set
$`\mathcal{U}(s)`$, the leader has Bellman operator

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (18) |  |
``` math
(\mathcal{T}V)(s)=\max_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}(s)}\left\{\bar{R}^{\rho}(s,\mathbf{u})+\delta_{G}\int V(s^{\prime})\,\bar{P}^{\rho}(ds^{\prime}\mid s,\mathbf{u})\right\}.
``` |  |

Because $`\delta_{G}<1`$, $`\mathcal{T}`$ is a contraction on bounded
value functions. Compact feasibility and the continuity conditions
ensure that the maximum is attained and admits a measurable stationary
selector $`\pi_{G}^{\star}`$. Its fixed point equals the
response-induced return in
Eq. ([7](#S4.E7 "In 4.2. Response-Induced Markov Decision Process ‣ 4. Cognition-Constrained Stackelberg Game ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems"));
hence $`\pi_{G}^{\star}`$ is optimal under the fixed logit response
kernel. ∎

### D.2. Zeroth-Order Gradient Estimation and Stationarity

###### Proof of Proposition [4.2](#S4.Thmtheorem2 "Proposition 0 (Zeroth-order gradient estimation and stationarity). ‣ 4.3. Cooperative Policy Learning ‣ 4. Cognition-Constrained Stackelberg Game ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems").

Condition on the saved state at cycle $`k`$. Unbiased rollouts, Gaussian
symmetry, and Stein’s identity give

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (19) |  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}]=\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}[J_{k}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k}+h\boldsymbol{\zeta})\boldsymbol{\zeta}]=\nabla J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k}).
``` |  |

If returns lie in a common interval of width $`R`$, centering them at
its midpoint yields $`L_{h}\leq R/(2h^{2})`$. Moreover,
$`\|\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\|^{2}\leq R^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|^{2}/(4h^{2})`$,
so $`\sigma_{g}^{2}\leq R^{2}D/(4h^{2})`$. The smoothness inequality and
$`\alpha L_{h}\leq 1`$ imply

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (20) |  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{k}[J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k+1})]\geq J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\nabla J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k})\|^{2}-\frac{L_{h}\alpha^{2}}{2}\sigma_{g}^{2}.
``` |  |

Summing over $`k`$, telescoping the within-cycle gains, and bounding
changes between consecutive saved-state objectives by $`V_{T}`$ proves
Eq. ([11](#S4.E11 "In Proposition 0 (Zeroth-order gradient estimation and stationarity). ‣ 4.3. Cooperative Policy Learning ‣ 4. Cognition-Constrained Stackelberg Game ‣ PolicyMediator: Multi-Regional Policy Learning through Mediation Agents with Evolving Skills in OPC Ecosystems")),
with
$`\Delta=\sup_{k,\boldsymbol{\omega}}J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega})-J_{0,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{0})`$.
If $`\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}=\infty`$, $`\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty`$, and
total drift is summable, standard stochastic approximation gives
$`\|\nabla J_{k,h}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{k})\|\to 0`$ almost surely; the
parameters themselves need not converge. ∎

## Appendix E Ethical Considerations

A central ethical concern of this work is preserving human agency in
AI-assisted regional policymaking. PolicyMediator is designed to support
policy deliberation and OPC location decisions, rather than replace
governmental authority or entrepreneurial choice. Accordingly, policy
adjustments should remain subject to institutional review, while OPCs
retain autonomy over entry and relocation decisions. Moreover,
optimizing aggregate government welfare does not necessarily ensure
equitable benefits across regions or OPC types, making transparent
assessment of distributional outcomes essential. From this perspective,
our framework emphasizes informed coordination rather than enforced
agreement. Finally, simulated outcomes provide analytical insights
rather than direct evidence of real-world policy effectiveness, and
practical deployment requires stakeholder evaluation and field
validation.
````

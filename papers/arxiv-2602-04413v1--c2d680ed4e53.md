###### Abstract

Self-consistency methods are the core technique for improving the
reasoning reliability of multimodal large language models (MLLMs). By
generating multiple reasoning results through repeated sampling and
selecting the best answer via voting, they play an important role in
cross-modal tasks. However, most existing self-consistency methods are
limited to a fixed “repeated sampling and voting” paradigm and do not
reuse historical reasoning information. As a result, models struggle to
actively correct visual understanding errors and dynamically adjust
their reasoning during iteration. Inspired by the human reasoning
behavior of repeated verification and dynamic error correction, we
propose the H-GIVR framework. During iterative reasoning, the MLLM
observes the image multiple times and uses previously generated answers
as references for subsequent steps, enabling dynamic correction of
errors and improving answer accuracy. We conduct comprehensive
experiments on five datasets and three models. The results show that the
H-GIVR framework can significantly improve cross-modal reasoning
accuracy while maintaining low computational cost. For instance, using
Llama3.2-vision:11b on the ScienceQA dataset, the model requires an
average of 2.57 responses per question to achieve an accuracy of 78.90%,
representing a 107% improvement over the baseline.

## 1 Introduction

In recent years, multimodal large language models have made significant
progress in vision–language reasoning tasks, showing early abilities in
multi-step reasoning and cross-modal alignment ([Li et al.,
2022](#bib.bib14); [Alayrac et al., 2022](#bib.bib1); [Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib16)). However, increasing model size to improve
performance leads to an exponential growth in training cost, which
indicates that model scaling alone is not a sustainable path ([Kaplan
et al., 2020](#bib.bib11); [Hoffmann et al., 2022](#bib.bib9)).
Therefore, prompt engineering has become one of the key research
directions for improving the reasoning performance of large language
models.

![Refer to caption](2602.04413v1/intro.png)

Figure 1: The detailed process by which the H-GIVR framework answers a
VQA question.

Previous studies show that explicit prompts can guide models to generate
reasoning steps ([Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib30)), thereby improving
performance on complex reasoning tasks. Moreover, repeated sampling
combined with majority voting can enhance the stability and reliability
of model outputs ([Wang et al., 2022](#bib.bib29)). However, most
existing self-consistency methods treat multiple generations as
independent repetitions and do not fully use historical outputs as
references for subsequent reasoning. Although the complexity and
diversity of prompts substantially affect performance ([Fu et al.,
2022](#bib.bib5)), this independent-repetition strategy finds it
difficult to correct dynamic error across iterations and thus limits
further improvements in the models’ reasoning abilities.

A key advantage of human reasoning does not lie in mechanical
repetition, but in refining conclusions through iterative verification.
For example, when taking the exam, students review their answers by
combining earlier responses with the visual clues in the question:
simple problems are checked quickly, while harder ones are examined
repeatedly with adjusted strategies. Large language models are
originally designed to imitate human conversational logic([Cai et al.,
2024](#bib.bib3)), yet this verification behavior is absent from current
methods. Inspired by this insight, we propose the History-Guided
Iterative Visual Reasoning with Self-Correction (H-GIVR) framework. This
framework aims to equip models with a human-like verification ability,
allowing them to repeatedly access visual information during reasoning
and incorporate historical answers into subsequent iterations, so that
the final result is obtained from iterative consistency.

Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ History-Guided Iterative Visual Reasoning with Self-Correction")
illustrates the detailed process of how the H-GIVR framework answers a
VQA question. Specifically, in visual question answering tasks, H-GIVR
first guides the language model to inspect the image and generate a
corresponding image description. This description is appended to the
question text and serves as visual evidence for subsequent reasoning.
During iterative reasoning, the answer generated in the previous round
is fed back together with the question as historical context, emulating
the human habit of repeatedly checking and revising answers.

When the iteration index is an even number, the framework assumes that
the model has not yet formed a sufficient understanding of the visual
content. It then triggers a second visual observation step, guiding the
model to regenerate the image description in order to deepen and
reinforce its understanding of the visual information. Once the model
produces consistent answers during the iterative process, the reasoning
is considered to have reached a stable state, and this answer is taken
as the final output.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

1.  1.  We propose a novel framework, H-GIVR, which moves beyond traditional
        self-consistency methods based on “repeated answering and majority
        voting”.
2.  2.  The H-GIVR framework effectively leverages historical information
        and visual information to simulate human problem-solving behavior.
3.  3.  H-GIVR uses two verification mechanisms to prompt repeated image
        inspection and to determine when the model has reached a stable
        final answer.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Self-Correction of Large Language Models

Enabling large language models (LLMs) with self-correction capability is
a key approach to enhance their reliability. Current methods can be
mainly divided into two categories based on intervention mechanisms:
parameter adjustment during training and interactive guidance during
inference ([Pan et al., 2024](#bib.bib23)). Parameter adjustment
methods, such as the reinforcement learning based by SCoRe
framework ([Kumar et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)), modify model parameters
through algorithms during the training phase. They use data generated by
the models themselves for training, which effectively addresses the
common problems of distribution shift and behavior collapse in
traditional supervised fine-tuning. However, these methods involve
complex processes and high computational costs.

In contrast, inference-time guidance methods, such as RISE ([Qu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib24)) and $`S^{2}`$R ([Ma et al., 2025a](#bib.bib19)), aim
to stimulate the models’ inherent reflective potential by designing
prompts. Studies have shown that even simple prompts can trigger
explicit reflective behaviors in pre-trained models ([Shah et al.,
2025](#bib.bib26)). Our work follows and extends the second research
path, focusing on exploring how to achieve effective inference-time
self-correction through structured interaction in multimodal contexts.

### 2.2 Self-Consistency and Reflection

A single round of correction is often insufficient to fix complex
errors. Therefore, the mechanism of multi-round iterative
self-improvement becomes a natural extension. The RISE framework ([Qu
et al., 2024](#bib.bib24)) proposed in existing studies enables models
to recursively introspect and revise outputs from all previous rounds.
Furthermore, Exploratory Iteration ([Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib10))
highlights the importance of maintaining divergent thinking during
iteration to avoid local optima. A common core of these studies is the
pursuit of self-consistency. Relevant research indicates that for models
to achieve effective self-improvement, they need to demonstrate key
behaviors such as verification and feedback ([Gandhi et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6)). This reveals an important principle: effective
self-correction is not a simple process of “re-answering”. Instead, it
is a cognitive process that involves error detection, hypothesis
backtracking and logical reconstruction. Our work designs a multi-round
feedback structure to systematically guide models through this cognitive
process. With repeated answer correction and image descriptions as
anchors for backtracking and verification, we enhance the
self-consistency of cross-modal reasoning.

### 2.3 Cognitive Limitations of Multimodal Models

Although multimodal models have demonstrated outstanding performance in
numerous tasks, their underlying cognitive comprehension capabilities
remain questionable. Recent studies have started to systematically
evaluate the fundamental flaws of large multimodal models (MLLMs).
Existing research reveals that MLLMs generally lack core knowledge, such
as object permanence and basic physical intuition, and this deficiency
cannot be addressed by simply scaling up the models ([Li et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib15)). Since models have inherent cognitive “blind spots”,
designing an external, interactive “reflection-correction” mechanism for
them becomes an effective approach to compensate for such flaws and
enhance the reliability of reasoning. It has become a trend to adopt
reflection to analyze and improve model performance in the field of
model adaptation ([Ming & Li, 2024](#bib.bib22)).

### 2.4 Interaction and Alignment

“How to achieve accurate cross-modal alignment” is the core challenge in
vision-language tasks. Most existing studies focus on modal fusion and
representation alignment during the training phase. Currently, research
on extending self-correction mechanisms to vision-language multimodal
scenarios is still in its early stages, and most existing works follow
the path of parametric adjustment. For instance, some studies improve
the self-consistency of visual localization via fine-tuning ([He et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8); [Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)), while others enhance
the model’s robustness against perturbations ([Ma et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib20)). These studies have demonstrated that pursuing
consistency in multimodal contexts is a feasible and important research
direction.

However, these methods do not provide models with the ability to
dynamically evaluate and correct their own cross-modal understanding
during the inference phase. Research on multimodal self-correction
frameworks that do not rely on complex training and are fully
implemented through inference-time interaction remains scarce. Our work
complements the above-mentioned studies: instead of trying to train a
”perfect” cross-modal alignment model at one time, we acknowledge that
the existing models have inherent alignment errors. We then design a
lightweight, inference-time interactive framework that enables models to
independently reflect on and correct these errors.

## 3 Methodology

Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.4 Interaction and Alignment ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ History-Guided Iterative Visual Reasoning with Self-Correction")
illustrates the overall workflow of H-GIVR. The framework consists of
two core components: (i) Visual Description, which continuously
generates image descriptions throughout the entire answering process;
and (ii) Consistency-Iterative Reasoning, where the model’s answer from
the previous round is appended to the question in the next round as a
reference for further reasoning. In addition, H-GIVR includes two key
judgment mechanisms: (i) Image Re-observation Mechanism, which is
activated when the iteration count is an even number, prompting the
model to regenerate the image description to reduce the impact of memory
loss on answer correctness; and (ii) Answer Confirmation Mechanism,
which stops the iteration and outputs the result as the final answer
once two identical answers appear during the reasoning process.

### 3.1 Problem Definition

Let a visual question answering (VQA) dataset be denoted as
$`D=\{(x_{i},v_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}`$, where $`x_{i}`$ represents the
question, $`v_{i}`$ represents the associated image, $`y_{i}`$
represents the ground truth, and $`N`$ denotes the total number of test
samples. For the given inputs $`x_{i}`$ and $`v_{i}`$, the image
description generated by the model is defined as $`f_{k}(v_{i})`$, and
the answer generated by the model is defined as $`p_{k}(x_{i},v_{i})`$.
After multiple iterations, these generated results are finally organized
into an image description set $`F(v_{i})`$ and an answer set
$`P(x_{i},v_{i})`$:

|     |                                  |                                                                                     |     |     |
| --- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle F(v_{i})`$       | $`\displaystyle=\{f_{1}(v_{i}),f_{2}(v_{i}),...,f_{n}(v_{i})\},`$                   |     | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle P(x_{i},v_{i})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\{p_{1}(x_{i},v_{i}),p_{2}(x_{i},v_{i}),...,p_{m}(x_{i},v_{i})\}.`$ |     |     |

If there exist two distinct indices $`\alpha`$ and $`\beta`$
($`1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq m\ \&\ \alpha\neq\beta`$) such that
$`p_{\alpha}(x_{i},v_{i})=p_{\beta}(x_{i},v_{i})`$, then this answer is
taken as the final output.

### 3.2 Visual Description

In visual question answering tasks, the visual information carried by
the image is a key basis for producing correct answers. If the model
fails to fully understand the image content, its reasoning process may
gradually drift away from the correct direction, leading to wrong
answers. To address this issue, we propose a stage-wise processing
approach in which the image $`v_{i}`$ is handled separately from the
question $`x_{i}`$ before reasoning. First, the model is guided to focus
on the image $`v_{i}`$ and generate a corresponding visual description
$`f(v_{i})`$. Then, during the answer generation stage, the model
performs joint reasoning by integrating the image $`v_{i}`$ and the
description $`f(v_{i})`$. This mechanism aims to strengthen the model’s
perception and use of visual information, thereby improving its deeper
understanding of image content and the accuracy of answer generation.

### 3.3 Image Re-observation Mechanism

In visual question answering tasks, humans often observe images
continuously, repeatedly reviewing them to capture details that may have
been missed and to correct errors in understanding. This dynamic
observation process helps gradually refine the grasp of visual
information during reasoning. However, due to limitations such as
context length, existing models find it difficult to access image
information multiple times within a single reasoning process, which
restricts their ability to refer back to the image for self-correction.

To address this gap, we introduce a structured image re-observation
mechanism into the model’s iterative reasoning process. Specifically,
when the iteration count reaches an even number, the model independently
reprocesses the original image and produces an updated visual
description. This design is intended to simulate the human behavior of
periodically looking back at the image to deepen understanding during
problem solving. Through this periodic reinforcement of visual
information, the model can maintain awareness of key image content
across multiple reasoning steps, reduce errors caused by memory loss or
insufficient initial understanding, and thereby improve the robustness
and accuracy of visual reasoning.

### 3.4 Consistency-Iterative Reasoning

When reviewing answers to a question, humans do not simply repeat
previous responses in a mechanical way. Instead, they examine existing
answers with clear intent and purpose. To simulate this cognitive
process, we move beyond traditional self-consistency methods based on
repeated sampling and propose an iterative consistency reasoning
component. Specifically, the H-GIVR framework maintains a dynamically
updated answer list, which is appended to the question text as part of
the context. After each round of reasoning, the newly generated answer
is added to this list:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \mathcal{A}nswer\ List=\{a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{n}\}
       ```                                               |     | (2) |

where $`a_{i}`$ denotes a historical answer and $`n`$ represents the
current iteration step. The model is then instructed to carefully
consider all historical answers in the list during subsequent reasoning.
This design explicitly equips the model with the ability to reflect on
and verify its own outputs, making it closer to the deliberate
answer-checking behavior observed in human problem solving.

incorporating incorrect answers as references in subsequent reasoning

toward the correct answer. !!!! In real-world applications, since it is

is not directly intended for practical deployment.

### 3.5 Answer Confirmation Mechanism

During the iterative process, when two identical answers appear, H-GIVR
treats this answer as the final output. In addition, experiments show
that the model may produce “None” when dealing with difficult questions.
To simulate time constraints in exam settings and to improve
computational efficiency, we set the maximum number of iterations to 10.
According to experimental statistics, the average number of iterations
required by the H-GIVR framework is at most around 4, which is well
below the preset upper limit of 10. Therefore, setting the maximum
number of iterations to 10 does not have a noticeable impact on the
actual efficiency or performance of the framework. This setting provides
sufficient room for iterative reasoning while avoiding unnecessary
computational cost. Moreover, if the model generates several consecutive
“None” outputs and then produces a valid answer for the first time, this
answer is directly accepted as the final result. The pseudocode of the
H-GIVR framework is shown in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.5 Answer Confirmation Mechanism ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ History-Guided Iterative Visual Reasoning with Self-Correction").

 Input: question $`x_{i}`$, image $`v_{i}`$

 Initialize: $`AnswerList\leftarrow\emptyset`$,
$`FeatureSet\leftarrow\emptyset`$, $`iteration\leftarrow 0`$,
$`max\_iterations\leftarrow 10`$

 Visual Description: $`feature\leftarrow\text{MLLM}(v_{i})`$

 $`FeatureSet\leftarrow FeatureSet\cup\{feature\}`$

 while $`iteration<max\_iterations`$ do

  $`iteration\leftarrow iteration+1`$

  $`prompt\leftarrow x_{i}+FeatureSet`$

  if $`AnswerList\neq\emptyset`$ then

   $`prompt\leftarrow prompt+AnswerList`$

  end if

  $`current\_answer\leftarrow\text{MLLM}(prompt)`$

  if $`current\_answer\in AnswerList`$ then

   $`A_{final}\leftarrow current\_answer`$

   break

  end if

  $`AnswerList\leftarrow AnswerList\cup\{current\_answer\}`$

  if $`iteration\mod 2=0`$ then

   $`new\_feature\leftarrow\text{MLLM}(v_{i})`$

   $`FeatureSet\leftarrow FeatureSet\cup\{new\_feature\}`$

  end if

 end while

 if $`iteration\geq max\_iterations`$ then

  $`A_{final}\leftarrow AnswerList[-1]`$

  Return the latest answer

 end if

 RETURN: $`A_{final}`$

## 4 Experiments

In this section, we conduct extensive experiments to evaluate the
effectiveness and computational efficiency of H-GIVR. Our experiments
mainly focus on addressing the following key questions:

1.  Q1.
    How does H-GIVR perform across multiple scenarios?
2.  Q2.
    Does H-GIVR require a large amount of computational resources?
3.  Q3.
    Compared with existing baseline methods, can H-GIVR improve
    accuracy?
4.  Q4.
    Both Visual Description and Consistency-Iterative Reasoning can
    effectively improve accuracy?
5.  Q5.
    Is H-GIVR still effective in specific domains?

### 4.1 Datasets and Metrics

Following the standard evaluation settings in MLLMs reasoning research,
we conduct experiments on five popular benchmarks: ScienceQA ([Lu
et al., 2022](#bib.bib18)), A-OKVQA ([Schwenk et al.,
2022](#bib.bib25)), OK-VQA ([Marino et al., 2019](#bib.bib21)),
VQAv2 ([Goyal et al., 2017](#bib.bib7)) and TextVQA ([Singh et al.,
2019](#bib.bib27)). Among the datasets, ScienceQA and A-OKVQA are
multiple-choice datasets, while OK-VQA, VQAv2, and TextVQA are
open-ended answer datasets. We follow the practice ([Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib17); [Li et al., 2024a](#bib.bib13); [Bai et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2)) of using accuracy as the metric. We conduct
experiments using three models that each have over one million downloads
on Ollama: Llama3.2-vision:11b, Qwen2.5vl:7b and Gemma3:12b.

### 4.2 Experimental results

#### For Q1: How does H-GIVR perform across multiple scenarios?

We conduct experiments under three typical application settings for
multimodal large language models: Standard, which directly invokes the
model; Simple CoT, which uses a simple “Let’s think step by step”
prompt; and Complex CoT, which applies few-shot prompting. The
experimental results are reported in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.5 Answer Confirmation Mechanism ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ History-Guided Iterative Visual Reasoning with Self-Correction").

The results show that H-GIVR achieves significant performance
improvements across all five datasets, three models, and three
application settings, with particularly strong gains on multiple-choice
datasets. For the Llama3.2-vision, Qwen2.5vl, and Gemma3 models,
performance improvements exceed 50% in both the Standard and Simple CoT
settings, and the minimum improvement in the Complex CoT setting still
reaches 19%. On the more challenging open-ended datasets, H-GIVR also
provides clear benefits, with improvements ranging from 2.8% to 69.2%.

In addition, the results show that when extra prompt information is
provided to H-GIVR, whether simple or carefully designed, the framework
remains effective, but its accuracy is lower than that achieved without
prompts. This indicates that H-GIVR can be used directly without any
carefully crafted prompts, which helps reduce the usage barrier of
multimodal large language models.

#### For Q2: Does H-GIVR require a large amount of computational resources?

We measure the average number of model calls required by H-GIVR to
process each question across three models, five datasets, and three
application settings, as shown in
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ For Q2: Does H-GIVR require a large amount of computational resources? ‣ 4.2 Experimental results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ History-Guided Iterative Visual Reasoning with Self-Correction").
The results indicate that H-GIVR requires between 2.57 and 4.04 calls
per question, suggesting that H-GIVR is computationally efficient and
has good cost control.

We also observe that, compared with multiple-choice questions,
open-ended questions usually require more model calls. This indicates
that such questions involve higher decision uncertainty and make it more
difficult for the model to converge to a clear and stable answer. Since
open-ended tasks allow a wider range of valid responses, the model often
needs additional reasoning steps to refine its output and reach a
consistent conclusion. In addition, compared with the Standard setting
without prompts, the Simple CoT and Complex CoT settings with prompts
generally require fewer calls, but their accuracy is often lower than
that of the Standard setting. This suggests that when explicit prompts
are introduced, the model may be more likely to remain fixed on
previously generated incorrect answers, rather than continuing deeper
reasoning and correction.

#### For Q3: Compared with existing baseline methods, can H-GIVR improve accuracy?

We select several classic prompting methods from unimodal large language
models as baselines, including few-shot chain-of-thought prompting (FS
CoT), Auto-CoT ([Zhang et al., 2022](#bib.bib31)), Active Prompt ([Diao
et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)), and self-consistency ([Wang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib29)). As shown in
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.1 Datasets and Metrics ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ History-Guided Iterative Visual Reasoning with Self-Correction"),
across five datasets, the Llama3.2-vision and Qwen2.5vl models achieve
better performance in most cases when using H-GIVR, compared with other
baseline methods. The performance gains range from 0.49 to 16.60.

Although Self-Consistency performs slightly better in a few cases (for
example, Llama3.2-vision on the VQAv2 dataset), its number of model
calls is fixed at 5, whereas the average number of calls required by
H-GIVR is only 3.58. Therefore, H-GIVR maintains competitive performance
while offering a clear advantage in computational efficiency.

#### For Q4: Both Visual Description and Consistency-Iterative Reasoning can effectively improve accuracy?

To verify the effectiveness of the two core components in the framework,
we conduct systematic ablation experiments, and the results are shown in
Figure [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.1 Datasets and Metrics ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ History-Guided Iterative Visual Reasoning with Self-Correction").
The experiments use the Qwen2.5vl 7B model under the Standard setting,
where the model achieves an average accuracy of 40.05% across five
datasets. When only the visual description module is introduced, the
average performance increases significantly to 70.93%. When only the
consistency-iterative reasoning module is used, the average accuracy
further improves to 72.11%. Finally, the complete H-GIVR, which includes
both modules, achieves the best performance with an average accuracy of
72.76%, showing stable and clear improvements across all datasets.

These results confirm the effectiveness of both the visual description
module and the consistency-iterative reasoning module. They also
indicate that the two components are complementary, and that their
combination can jointly enhance the reasoning ability and answer
accuracy of multimodal large language models.

#### For Q5: Is H-GIVR still effective in specific domains?

To more systematically evaluate the performance of H-GIVR across
different subject types and difficulty levels, we conduct a fine-grained
analysis on the ScienceQA dataset. This dataset can be divided along two
dimensions. In terms of subject categories, it covers three major areas:
Language Science, Social Science, and Natural Science. In terms of
question difficulty, it is grouped into two levels, G1–6 and G7–12. The
experimental results are shown in
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ For Q2: Does H-GIVR require a large amount of computational resources? ‣ 4.2 Experimental results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ History-Guided Iterative Visual Reasoning with Self-Correction").

The results show that H-GIVR performs well in most subdomains. Only in
the language science category does it slightly underperform the
self-consistency method in a few cases. In other subject areas,
especially in social science, H-GIVR significantly improves answer
accuracy, demonstrating strong subject adaptability. In addition, H-GIVR
maintains stable performance advantages across different difficulty
levels. These findings indicate that H-GIVR is effective across diverse
subject backgrounds and shows good generalization ability.

## 5 Conclusion

H-GIVR innovatively breaks away from the traditional self-consistency
paradigm of “repeated answering and majority voting”. Instead of
mechanically repeating reasoning, it treats historical reasoning
information as a key reference for subsequent decisions. At the same
time, it guides the model to repeatedly observe visual content during
reasoning, enabling precise correction of visual understanding errors.
The framework achieves significant improvements in reasoning accuracy
across VQA benchmarks, while keeping computational cost controllable and
relatively low.

## Impact Statement

This paper presents the History-Guided Iterative Visual Reasoning with
Self-Correction (H-GIVR) framework, whose goal is to advance the field
of multimodal large language model reasoning. By reducing reliance on
elaborate prompt engineering and heavy model scaling, H-GIVR lowers the
deployment barriers for multimodal systems, making advanced cross-modal
reasoning more accessible to non-expert users. Ethically, the
framework’s lightweight design minimizes computational costs, thereby
reducing the carbon footprint associated with running large-scale
multimodal models. In terms of societal implications, H-GIVR can
potentially enhance the reliability of visual question answering systems
in real-world applications such as education and accessibility tools.
There are no foreseeable negative ethical consequences, and the work
aligns with the broader goal of developing efficient, user-friendly AI
technologies.

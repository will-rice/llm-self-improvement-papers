# Self-WSP: Can Language Models Improve Themselves Through Weight-Space Programming?

Guoming Ling Affiliation: Carnegie Mellon University
Affiliation: MilkThink    Shanshan Zhong Affiliation: Carnegie Mellon
University    Kaixuan Zhang Affiliation: MilkThink    Zhongzhan Huang
Affiliation: MilkThink

###### Abstract

Frontier language models can now read and edit the weights of a neural
network much like code and thereby improve a small model, an approach
known as Weight-Space Programming (WSP). We further explore applying WSP
to a language model itself, which we call Self-WSP. Specifically,
Qwen3.5-0.8B reads its own weights, proposes an edit, and then continues
with the edited weights as both problem solver and editor. We find many
cases of genuine self-improvement. Self-edits can raise GSM8K accuracy
from 11.8% to 62.2% and MATH500 accuracy from 7.0% to 32.2%, sometimes
by changing only 16 parameters, and in some cases repeated self-editing
keeps improving the model. A closer look, however, reveals that these
gains lack interpretability. The model shows little understanding of the
edits it makes, and WSP driven by random search sometimes performs
comparably. Self-WSP thus holds promise as a path toward recursive
self-improvement (RSI), but realizing this promise requires further
exploration.

## 1 Introduction

A system that improves its own intelligence, and with it its ability to
improve itself further, is a long-standing idea known as recursive
self-improvement (RSI) (Good, 1966; Schmidhuber, 2007). Language models
already improve themselves in several ways. They train on rationales,
data or rewards they generate themselves (Zelikman et al., 2022; Huang
et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024), rewrite the code that calls them
(Zelikman et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026), or learn to write their own
finetuning data (Zweiger et al., 2025). In each case, however, the model
changes its weights only indirectly, through data, code or training.

Weight-Space Programming (WSP) offers a more direct route. Takehi et al.
(2026) show that GPT-6 Astra, hereafter GPT-6, can read the weights of a
neural network and edit them much like code. Asked to make Qwen3.5-0.8B
better at math without gradients, it traced the small model’s failures
to a single direction in two MLP layers, removed that direction from
0.016% of the weights, and raised MATH500 accuracy from 7.9% to 48.1%.
Their experiments leave open whether a model can improve its performance
by editing its own weights.

We study this possibility directly. In Self-WSP, Qwen3.5-0.8B edits
itself. As Figure 1 shows, the model reads its own weights, proposes an
edit, and continues with the edited weights, both as the solver that
answers questions and as the editor that proposes the next edit. A fixed
outer loop decides which edits to keep, and for this purpose we divide
questions into three kinds. Practice questions come from the training
split with reference answers, and are used to test whether an edit
helps. Validation questions also come from the training split but are
never shown to the editor, and serve only to select models after the
search. The test sets are the standard GSM8K and MATH500 test questions,
used only for the final evaluation. With this setup we ask four
questions, and Figure 2 summarizes the test accuracy of the best model
of each method.

- •
  Can a model improve itself through its weights? Yes. Self-edits raise
  GSM8K accuracy about fivefold and MATH500 accuracy more than
  threefold, sometimes by changing a handful of numbers.
- •
  Does the model know what it is doing? Hardly. It edits whichever
  tensors are listed first, reuses numbers it has just read, and often
  gives hallucinated explanations. Many of the edits it keeps make the
  model worse.
- •
  Can random edits do the same? To some extent. Random search matches
  self-editing on GSM8K but falls short on MATH500, and the best single
  random edit is as good as the simpler of GPT-6’s two published edits.
- •
  Can Self-WSP keep improving the model? In lucky cases. Continued
  self-editing lifts the best models further, to more than four times
  the original MATH500 accuracy, while typical runs make little further
  progress.

Self-improvement through the weights is therefore within reach of even a
0.8B model. Its current successes, however, come mostly from search and
selection rather than from understanding, and closing this gap is the
main challenge ahead.

## 2 Self-Weight-Space Programming

Self-WSP is a loop in which a single model alternates between editing
its weights and using them.

### One model, two roles.

Both roles are played by Qwen3.5-0.8B (Qwen Team, 2026), a 24-layer
model in which three of every four layers use linear attention (Yang et
al., 2025b) and the fourth uses standard attention. A linear-attention
layer stores what it has read in a fixed-size state and forgets old
information at a rate set by its weights. As the editor, the model
proposes edits with thinking turned off. As the solver, it answers math
questions with thinking turned on, using the sampling settings of Takehi
et al. (2026). Both roles always use the current weights, so every kept
edit changes both how the model solves problems and how it then proposes
the next edit.

### Reading its own weights.

The editor sees a catalog of the 318 tensors it may edit, which covers
all 24 layers and gives the name and shape of each tensor. After it
picks one or two tensors, it receives their shape, statistics such as
the mean and standard deviation, up to 64 of their values with
positions, and the norms of their rows and columns. It also sees how the
current model does on a batch of practice questions, including the
reference answers and the end of the model’s responses for a few
examples. This matches the setting of Takehi et al. (2026), where GPT-6
also reads the actual weight values, can check its edits only on
practice questions from the training split, and never sees the test
sets. The difference is that GPT-6 could analyze the weights freely in a
Python environment, whereas our editor sees only the information above,
precomputed by the system. Appendix A describes the full interface.

### Writing its own weights.

A model this small does not reliably follow output formats, so the
editor writes its edits as JSON under a grammar built from the tensor
shapes (Willard and Louf, 2023; Geng et al., 2023). Each proposal
contains one or two edits. An edit selects a block of consecutive
entries, rows or columns in a tensor and applies one of seven operations
to it. It can set the block to zero, multiply it by a number, add a
number, fill it with a number, redraw it at random from the tensor’s
mean and standard deviation, remove a direction, or apply a short NumPy
expression. Removing a direction applies only to matrices. The editor
names one column of the matrix as the direction, and every selected
column subtracts its component along that direction, scaled by a
coefficient. A coefficient of 1 removes this component entirely. GPT-6’s
most effective edit also removes a direction, but computes it from the
model’s activations. The grammar guarantees the format but not the
content, and edits that repeat an earlier one, rescale a whole tensor
uniformly or change no value are discarded at once. We call the
remaining edits valid edits.

### Keeping edits.

Each run proceeds in rounds from a starting model. Every round has a
fixed budget, the largest number of valid edits it may try, and the
editor proposes edits until the budget is spent. A round also ends early
after 24 proposals or after several valid edits in a row are rejected.
Each valid edit is first applied to a copy of the weights and then
checked on two disjoint practice batches. It is kept only if the edited
model solves enough additional questions on the two batches combined
without doing worse on either, in which case the copy becomes the new
current weights. The budget, the batch size and the required gain depend
on the interface, which we describe next.

### Two interfaces.

We run the loop with two interfaces, which differ mainly in how the
tensor catalog is ordered. The shuffled interface lists all tensors in a
random order, and the editor picks tensors directly. It uses 32 practice
questions per batch, keeps any edit that gains a single answer in total,
and runs three rounds with budgets of 4, 6 and 12 valid edits. The
ordered interface lists the tensors in layer order, and the editor first
picks a layer and then tensors within it. It uses 128 questions per
batch, requires a total gain of at least three answers, and spends a
budget of 8 valid edits per round. Appendix B gives the full settings.

### Selection and testing.

After every round we score the current model on validation questions,
without ever feeding the result back to the editor. The round with the
largest validation gain gives the best model of each run. A setting is
one combination of dataset, interface and starting point and contains 12
or 24 runs. In each setting we take the best models of the three runs
with the largest validation gain, name them Top-1, Top-2 and Top-3, and
call them the selected models. Only the selected models are evaluated,
once each, on the full GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and MATH500 (Hendrycks
et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2024) test sets. Being the best three of
12 or 24 runs, they show the best case. To see what an ordinary run
achieves, we also designate several runs by a fixed rule before the
search and test them whatever their outcome. Because they are never
selected, these runs act as random spot checks, and we therefore refer
to them as spot-check runs.

### Continued runs.

To study continued self-improvement, we also let the selected models of
the shuffled interface keep running under the ordered interface, and
call these continued runs. We write +$`k`$ for $`k`$ rounds of ordered
self-editing accumulated on top of a selected shuffled model, and
Section 6 gives the details.

It can. Of the 24 selected models in Table 1, 22 are significantly
better than the original model, and the remaining two gain just over one
point. Under the ordered interface, self-editing lifts GSM8K accuracy
from 11.8% to 61.0% and MATH500 accuracy from 7.0% to 24.7%. Continued
runs raise them further to 62.2% and 32.2%. The best self-edited GSM8K
model comes within five points of GPT-6’s best edit, which reaches 66.9%
in our evaluation, whereas on MATH500 GPT-6’s best edit stays far ahead
at 49.5%.

### A few numbers suffice.

The successful edits are remarkably small. The 24.2% MATH500 model
differs from the original model in only 16 numbers, the A_log of the
first linear-attention layer, which sets how quickly each head of that
layer forgets the past. A GSM8K model that changes 32 numbers in the
same layer reaches 59.9%. One continued MATH500 model differs from its
starting point in just two numbers yet gains another 6.8 points. No
selected model changes more than 0.2% of its weights, and Appendix D
lists every kept edit.

## 4 Does the Large Language Model Know What It Is Doing?

The model clearly improves itself. How it does so is less flattering.

Figure 3: The kept proposal behind the 61.0% GSM8K model, abridged. The
editor reuses the mean it has just read in both edits, while its
explanation claims to undo a scaling by that same number. The catalog
assigns each tensor an opaque identifier such as p273 or p298, as

### A lucky edit.

Figure 3 shows the key proposal behind the best GSM8K model started from
the original model. The editor picks layer 0, reads the first two
tensors of that layer, multiplies the first by the mean $`-1.979`$ of
the second, and adds the same number to the second. Its explanation
claims that these weights had been multiplied by $`-1.9795`$ by mistake
and that this scaling should be removed. Undoing a multiplication by
$`-1.9795`$, however, would require dividing by that number, yet the
editor multiplies by it once more. The scaling it claims to remove is
exactly the one it now applies, so the explanation contradicts the edit
itself. The edit nevertheless raises the correct answers on the two
practice batches from 12 and 20 to 78 and 87, and the resulting model
scores 61.0% on the test set.

### Most proposals fail.

This proposal is typical in everything but its luck. Of the 11,580
proposals the editor made, 69% are invalid even though the grammar
guarantees their format. Most of them repeat an edit already tried or
rescale a whole tensor uniformly, which the prompt explicitly forbids.
Only 12% of the valid edits are kept. The editor never uses the two
operations that can express structured changes, removing a direction and
writing an expression, and almost always just multiplies or shifts a
block of numbers.

Figure 4: Layer of every valid edit. Each cell gives the rounded
percentage of valid edits that fall in that layer. The first row, in
grey, is each layer’s share of the catalog, the distribution an editor
would follow if it treated all tensors alike. Under the shuffled
interface, which lists the catalog in a random order, edits spread over
the layers roughly in this proportion. Under the ordered interface,
which lists the catalog by layer, 62% of valid edits fall in layer 0,
the first entry of the catalog.

### Where it edits follows the catalog.

With a shuffled catalog, the editor spreads its edits over all layers,
and layer 0 receives 2.9% of them, close to its 4.4% share of the
catalog. With a catalog ordered by layer, layer 0 receives 62%, as
Figure 4 shows. This preference appears already in the first proposal of
each round, before the editor has seen the outcome of any proposal in
that round. The first two tensors of layer 0 are exactly the two edited
in Figure 3. All 15 selected models from the ordered interface edit
A_log in layer 0, while none of the nine from the shuffled interface do.
Changing how fast the first layer forgets is a reasonable target, but
the editor seems to have arrived at it simply by reading the catalog
from the top.

### Its numbers copy the statistics it reads.

The numbers in its edits are often copied as well. Between 9% and 28% of
the values in valid edits equal a statistic the editor has just read,
such as a mean, a standard deviation or a norm. The layer-0 A_log mean
of $`-1.979`$ alone appears as a scale or a shift in four selected
models.

### Its explanations are often hallucinated.

The explanations attached to kept edits are no more reliable. Many are
empty, describe a block other than the one edited, or have nothing to do
with weights at all. They are hallucinations in the usual sense for
language models, fluent text that is unfaithful to the input or to the
facts (Ji et al., 2023). The most extreme one reads, in full, “The cost
of one chair is \$11.” This sentence is copied word for word from the
end of a solver response to a chair-pricing question in the practice
feedback of the same call, and has nothing to do with the edit it
accompanies.

Figure 5: Validation accuracy of all runs started from the original
model under the ordered interface, 24 per dataset. Two runs per dataset
are highlighted to represent typical trajectories, thin light lines show

### Kept edits often hurt.

Doing better on practice questions does not guarantee a better model.
Estimating a model’s ability from a small set of questions saves time
(Huang et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2026c), but the estimate has a
long-tailed error and can occasionally land far from the full evaluation
(Ling et al., 2026a). Because the outer loop decides from practice
scores alone, it inevitably keeps some edits that pass by luck. Under
the shuffled interface, more than 40% of the rounds that keep an edit
lower validation accuracy. The larger batches of the ordered interface
help, yet a third of such rounds on MATH still do. Figure 5 shows the
resulting variety. Some runs jump in a single round, some climb
steadily, some first fall below the original model and then recover, and
some drift downward. On GSM8K, eleven of the 24 runs gain more than 23
points while the other thirteen gain less than 10. Spot-check runs,
which are never selected, better represent an ordinary run. The ordered
interface has six per dataset, and on the test sets they gain on average
24.7 points on GSM8K and 7.7 points on MATH500, far less than the
selected models.

The edits are thus the model’s own, made from its own weights without
any test information. Yet where it edits follows the catalog it is
shown, what it writes copies what it reads, and the reasons it gives are
often hallucinated. The large gains come from a few lucky proposals, and
it is the outer loop that keeps them.

## 5 Can Random Edits Do the Same?

If lucky proposals drive the gains, random edits might do as well. We
test this by replacing the editor with a random sampler that calls no
model.

Figure 6: Final validation change of every run under the shuffled
interface. Each dot is one run, the shaded curve above the dots shows
their distribution, and the black bar marks the mean. The frozen editor
always uses the original weights while only the solver is edited. Random
search uses the same tensors, operations and selection rules, and its
larger budget is about twice the self-editing budget on GSM8K and 1.7
times on MATH.

### Random search.

For each proposal, the sampler draws one tensor uniformly from the
catalog, or two with probability one fifth. It then picks a random
layout, start and count, and a random operation with its value, choosing
among setting to zero, scaling, adding or filling a small number,
redrawing and removing a direction. Appendix C gives the exact
distributions. The sampler runs through the same loop with the same
selection rules as the shuffled interface. With the same budget, it
spends exactly as many valid edits per round as the self-editing run
with the same random seed. With the larger budget, it spends a fixed 4,
6 or 12 valid edits per round. On GSM8K, random search matches
self-editing. Its best model scores 32.8% against 31.9% for
self-editing, and Figure 6 shows that the two sets of runs look much
alike. We also test a frozen editor, which always uses the original
weights while only the solver is edited. On average it behaves much like
the other two, so letting the editor itself be edited brings no clear
benefit. MATH500 is different. With the same budget, random search finds
no improvement, while self-editing reaches 17.3%. Only with about 70%
more budget does random search find a 13.6% model, by zeroing 256 rows
of one linear-attention layer.

ranked from best to worst by the accuracy change they cause in the

### Single random edits.

Random search still selects and accumulates edits. To see what a single
random edit does on its own, we remove both steps. For each dataset we
draw 352 random edits, apply each alone to the original model, and
evaluate it on 128 held-out training questions that are neither practice
nor validation questions. We call this step the screening, and to save
compute it samples each question once with an 8,192-token limit. Judging
whether an edit really helps also requires knowing how much the score
moves without any edit. The original model answers with some randomness,
and merely answering again with another random seed moves its score on
these 128 questions by a few points. We call this movement sampling
noise and take its 5th to 95th percentile as the noise range, shown as
the grey band in Figure 7. An edit inside the band cannot be told apart
from doing nothing.

As Figure 7 shows, most random edits fall inside the grey band or below
it. On GSM8K, 78 edits fall below the band and only 24 rise above it.
Large edits that touch tens of thousands to millions of weights do the
most damage, and almost every kind of operation hurts on average. A few
random edits nevertheless help. We rescore the best eight per dataset on
512 further questions from the practice pool and test the winner. It
reaches 22.3% on GSM8K by scaling 4,096 weights of a convolution by
$`-2`$, and 14.0% on MATH500 by scaling 64 rows of a query projection by
$`-2`$. Appendix C gives the sampling details.

### GPT-6’s simpler edit is a random edit.

One of GPT-6’s two published edits sets 256 columns of the attention
output matrix of layer 15 to zero. Such an edit lies within the range of
the single random edits, although the sampler would draw it only about
once in 130 million tries. In our evaluation it scores 22.0% on GSM8K
and 13.8% on MATH500, as good as the best single random edits, and in
the screening it merely ranks among the better random edits. GPT-6’s
stronger edit removes a direction computed from the model’s activations,
which lies outside what the sampler can produce.

### Where self-editing beats chance.

Self-editing does beat chance in places. In the same screening, the best
self-edited model of the shuffled interface gains more than all 352
random edits on both datasets. On MATH500, the selected continued +4
models gain up to 17.2 points in the screening, more than twice the best
random edit, and self-editing also beats random search with the same
budget. Its proposals are thus better than random draws, though only
modestly, and not because the model knows why they work.

## 6 Can Self-WSP Keep Improving the Model Round After Round?

A model that truly improves itself should keep improving when given more
time. We therefore let the selected models of the shuffled interface
keep running under the ordered interface. On GSM8K, the three selected
shuffled models, which we call A, B and C, each run eight more rounds,
reaching +8. On MATH500, the six selected shuffled models first run four
rounds each, reaching +4, and the three selected +4 models then run
eight more rounds, reaching +12 in total. Each continued setting again
yields three selected models. In the eight-round settings each starting
model has four runs, and we designate the first in advance as a
spot-check run, tested after its 4th and 8th rounds.

Solid lines connect each selected model with its starting model. Each

### The best models keep climbing.

The solid lines of Figure 8 show that the selected models do improve
further. On GSM8K, model B rises from 28.7% to 62.2% at +8. On MATH500,
a selected shuffled model at 8.3% grows into models of 25.4% to 28.4% at
+4, and one of these reaches 32.2% at +12 while differing from its
starting point in just two numbers. Most of these gains arrive soon
after the switch to the ordered interface, when runs find the layer-0
edit of Section 4.

### Typical runs stall.

The selected models are the best three of 12 runs and naturally favor
the lucky ones. The spot-check runs, the dashed lines in Figure 8, show
what an ordinary run achieves, and they tell a different story. On GSM8K
the spot-check runs gain little after +4, and the best of them slips
from 53.3% at +4 to 51.6% at +8. On MATH500 all three start from
selected +4 models, and by +12 one gains three points, one ends where it
started, and one falls steadily from 28.4% to 23.4%. Across all 12
eight-round runs on MATH, kept edits leave validation accuracy unchanged
on average.

More rounds give the outer loop more chances to find a good model, but
although its weights keep changing, the model itself does not become any
better as an editor.

## 7 Discussion

### The outer loop does much of the work.

The model proposes every edit and is changed by every kept one, but the
outer loop does the rest. Using practice questions with reference
answers, it decides which edits to keep, when to stop and which run to
test. Self-WSP is therefore best seen as a search in which the model
supplies the proposals, and these proposals beat random ones on MATH500
but merely match them on GSM8K.

### Why small edits go a long way.

As its model card warns, Qwen3.5-0.8B often gets stuck repeating itself
while thinking, and the original model fails to finish within its
32k-token budget on about 90% of questions. Almost every answer that an
edit makes correct is one the original model never finished, and across
all evaluated models accuracy falls almost linearly with the share of
unfinished answers. Any edit that breaks the repetition can therefore
raise accuracy sharply. This fits reports that a few parameters can have
outsized effects (Yu et al., 2024), that correcting a small fraction of
thinking tokens during reasoning clearly raises accuracy (Ling et al.,
2026b), and that Qwen models improve at math even when trained with
spurious rewards (Shao et al., 2026). It may also explain why GPT-6
could not improve Gemma 3 270M, a model that rarely repeats itself
(Takehi et al., 2026).

### Toward genuine self-improvement.

A model that improves itself because it understands its weights must act
on what it reads. Natural next steps include tools for analyzing
weights, which GPT-6 had and our editor lacked, training a model to
propose edits (Zweiger et al., 2025), and solvers whose failures run
deeper than repetition. Progress along this path is best measured
against random search with the same interface and budget, and reported
together with spot-check runs.

## 8 Related Work

### Large language models.

Scaling models and data has given language models broad abilities
(Kaplan et al., 2020; Brown et al., 2020; Touvron et al., 2023; OpenAI,
2023; Yang et al., 2025a). Chain-of-thought prompting elicits
intermediate reasoning steps (Wei et al., 2022). Reinforcement learning
trains models to reason at length (Guo and others, 2025), and test-time
scaling trades more inference compute for higher accuracy (Snell et al.,
2025; Wan et al., 2026), for example by searching for better reasoning
paths (Ling et al., 2026b). Mathematical reasoning is a common testbed
for testing and improving these abilities (Cobbe et al., 2021; Hendrycks
et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2024). These advances rely on training,
prompting or extra inference compute, whereas Self-WSP lets a model
directly rewrite its own weights without gradients.

### Self-improvement.

Models can refine their outputs with their own feedback (Madaan et al., 2023) or generate more creative data and train on the best of it (Zhong
et al., 2024), although they often fail to correct their own reasoning
(Huang et al., 2024). Other work lets a model rewrite its own prompts,
agent designs or running logic and keeps the better versions (Fernando
et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025). All of these methods
act on data, prompts or code, whereas Self-WSP acts on the weights
themselves. The idea of a neural network that modifies its own weights
is old (Schmidhuber, 1993), and modern self-referential weight matrices
can rewrite themselves at run time (Irie et al., 2022). These networks,
however, are trained for this purpose, while Self-WSP lets a
general-purpose language model read and write its own weights from
instructions in natural language.

### Editing and writing weights.

Model editing changes facts or behaviors through targeted weight updates
(Meng et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2023), trained editor networks
(Mitchell et al., 2022), arithmetic on weight differences (Ilharco et
al., 2023) or the removal of single directions (Arditi et al., 2024),
usually guided by interpretability (Elhage et al., 2021). Pruning
research uses weight statistics such as norms to judge which parameters
can be removed, finds that these statistics often fail to tell
parameters apart (Huang et al., 2021), and turns to search to blend
several criteria (He et al., 2021). Hypernetworks use one network to
generate the weights of another (Ha et al., 2017), and language models
have been used to explain the neurons of other models (Bills et al.,
2023). WSP instead asks a general-purpose language model to read and
write weights toward a goal stated in natural language (Takehi et al.,
2026). Fan et al. (2026) improved Qwen3.5-27B’s math accuracy using a
frozen copy as editor. Other WSP studies include Kundu (2026); Hu et al.
(2026); Fischetti (2026); Salloum and Ali (2026).

### Propose and select.

Our loop resembles methods in which a language model proposes candidates
and an evaluator keeps the best. Such methods have been applied to
prompts, pipelines, programs and algorithms (Yang et al., 2024; Khattab
et al., 2024; Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Novikov et al., 2025), and a
language model can even act directly as an evolution strategy over
numerical parameters (Lange et al., 2024). Evolutionary search has also
been used to find ways of merging the weights of several models (Akiba
et al., 2025). In Self-WSP, the language model that proposes the
candidates is itself the object being optimized.

### Gradient-free and random baselines.

Evolution strategies and zeroth-order methods optimize networks with
forward passes alone (Salimans et al., 2017; Malladi et al., 2023), now
even for language models with billions of parameters (Qiu et al., 2026).
Random search is a strong baseline in hyperparameter optimization and
reinforcement learning (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012; Mania et al., 2018),
and in benchmark compression a randomly drawn subset of questions often
comes close to the full benchmark (Ling et al., 2026a). Repetition and
overthinking are known failures of text generation and reasoning models
(Holtzman et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2025). Our results
connect these threads. When models fail mainly through repetition,
random perturbations can also be useful.

## 9 Conclusion

We asked whether a language model can improve itself by editing its own
weights. Our results show that a 0.8B model can. Its own edits raise
GSM8K accuracy fivefold and MATH500 accuracy more than fourfold,
sometimes by changing a handful of numbers, and in lucky runs continued
self-editing keeps paying off. Despite the improvements, the model shows
little understanding of its edits, and random edits sometimes achieve
comparable gains. Self-WSP is a promising path toward RSI, and turning
it into a reliable ability is the work ahead.

## Limitations

We study a single small model on two math benchmarks, and its main
weakness, repetitive thinking, is unusually easy to repair. The outer
loop uses the reference answers of practice questions to decide which
edits to keep, so the improvement is supervised. The ordered interface
was designed after we had seen the results of the shuffled one. We have
not yet run random search under it, nor tested the source of the layer-0
preference directly by reversing the catalog. Finally, the comparison
with GPT-6 is only indicative, since GPT-6 used different tools,
feedback and budgets.

## Ethics Statement

Self-editing of weights may introduce behaviors that a math benchmark
does not measure. All edits in this study are logged and reproducible
and concern a 0.8B model evaluated on public benchmarks. We use
Qwen3.5-0.8B under the Apache-2.0 license, GSM8K and MATH under the MIT
license, and the GPT-6 edits under CC BY 4.0.

### Tensor catalog and identifiers.

The catalog gives every editable tensor a random identifier such as
p298. The identifier carries no information, and the same tensor has
different identifiers in different runs. The catalog also lists the full

the editor refers to tensors only by identifier, and the grammar accepts
only identifiers that appear in the catalog. In Figure 3, p273 and p298

### Practice feedback.

For the first practice batch, the practice feedback reports the current
model’s correct answers, accuracy, share of unfinished answers, mean
response length and degree of repetition, together with the question,
reference answer, prediction and last 600 characters of the response for
its first four questions. It also gives summary scores on the second
batch and the edits and outcomes of recent proposals. Each call takes
11.6k to 23k input tokens and returns 15 to 150 tokens.

### Edit format and rejection rules.

An edit names a tensor identifier, a layout, a start, a count and an
operation. The layout can be flattened entries, whole rows or whole
columns, and positions are taken consecutively from the start, wrapping
around at the end. Operation values must lie between $`-10`$ and $`10`$.
The following edits are rejected outright, counted as invalid and not
retried: rescaling a whole tensor uniformly, repeating an evaluated
edit, changing no value after rounding to BF16, altering more than 8
million values, and exceeding the context length. Embeddings, the output
layer and the final normalization cannot be edited.

### Selection and evaluation.

For each run we take as its best model the round with the largest
validation gain among the states that have kept an edit and differ from
the starting point. For each setting we then take the three runs with
the largest such gain as the selected models. Their weights are frozen
and recorded before testing. We evaluate all 1,319 GSM8K test questions
with two samples each and all 500 MATH500 questions with four samples
each, with thinking enabled, a 32,768-token limit, temperature 0.6,
top-p 0.95, top-k 20 and presence penalty 1.5, and report mean accuracy.
Each edited model is compared with the original model run on the same
hardware and sampling seeds by a paired bootstrap over questions with
Bonferroni correction. All spot-check runs are fixed before the search
and tested whatever their outcome, and they are of two kinds. The first
kind comprises six runs per dataset in the ordered setting from the
original model, chosen by a fixed hashing rule and tested after their
last round. The second kind is the first run of each starting model in
the eight-round continued settings, tested after its 4th and 8th rounds.

[TABLE]

Table 6: Sampling of the two random baselines.

All random edits are drawn without any model call and applied by the

### Random search.

The redraw scale $`|v|`$ is 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2 or 3. Added or filled numbers
are $`\pm 10^{u}`$ with $`u`$ uniform between $`-3`$ and 0. Removing a
direction applies only to the column layout, with a coefficient of 0.2,
0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 or 3. With the same budget, random search spends exactly
as many valid edits per round as the self-editing run with the same
random seed. The larger-budget settings spend a fixed 4, 6 or 12 valid
edits per round, in total about twice the same budget on GSM8K and 1.7
times on MATH. The larger-budget GSM8K setting also extends the scale
factors to 0, $`\pm 0.2`$, $`\pm 0.5`$, $`\pm 1`$, $`\pm 2`$ and
$`\pm 3`$. On MATH we ran two larger-budget settings, one with unchanged
and one with extended scale factors. The main text and Figure 6 report
the former, and the best model of the latter reaches only 7.5%.

### Single random edits.

We draw 32 edits for each of eleven operations, namely setting to zero,
nine scales and redrawing from the tensor’s mean and standard deviation.
Each edit goes to a random tensor, layout, start and count and is
applied only once to the original model. The screening samples each of
128 held-out training questions once with an 8,192-token limit. Each
screening task also lets the original model answer again with another
random seed, which gives 45 or 46 score changes, and the grey band of
Figure 7 spans their 5th to 95th percentile. All selected models of the
shuffled interface, the selected MATH500 +4 models and GPT-6’s two edits
take part in the same screening for reference. The best eight random
edits per dataset are rescored on 512 further questions from the
practice pool, and the best of them is tested.

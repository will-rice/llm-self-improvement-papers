# Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models

Wentao Zhou ^(†)^(†)thanks: Equal contribution. Affiliation: Georgia
Institute of Technology    Weijie Gan¹¹footnotemark: 1
Affiliation: Washington University in St. Louis    Jiayun Wang
Affiliation: Georgia Institute of Technology

###### Abstract

Unified multimodal models (UMMs) combine image generation and visual
understanding in a shared backbone. Since generation and understanding
are inverse tasks, recent studies self-train UMMs by letting the two
branches _cooperatively_ supervise each other. We introduce MATE
(Mutually Adversarial self-Training with Evolving data), a
reinforcement-learning-based post-training framework in which the two
branches instead _challenge_ each other, and the challenges evolve as
the model trains. MATE lets generation and understanding take turns to
be challenger and solver. Given an image, the understanding branch
proposes several candidate descriptions that the generation branch must
turn back into similar images, and vice versa. The candidates are
screened for consistency with the image or prompt they were proposed
from, and the solver is trained on the candidate it handles worst. The
adversary thus comes from the model’s own outputs, and no separate
adversary is trained. Moreover, the candidates that defeat one branch
become the sources of the next challenges to the other in the next
epoch, which keeps the challenges evolving with the model and turns the
training into self-play in data space. On Janus-Pro-1B, MATE improves
GenEval by 2.4 points, DPG-Bench by 1.7 points, and the average over
nine understanding benchmarks by 0.7 points, while strengthening
consistency across repeated image-text cycles.

## 1 Introduction

_Unified multimodal models (UMMs)_ integrate visual understanding and
generation into a single backbone ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib1); [Xie
et al., 2024](#bib.bib27)), promising mutually reinforcing capabilities
and a single interface for both. UMM training still follows the
pretrain-then-finetune recipe, so UMM capabilities are bounded by the
coverage of externally annotated data. Self-training ([Deng et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib4); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)), on the other hand,
is a natural way past this bound, and the unified architecture opens a
new route to it. Understanding and generation are inverse tasks handled
by two branches of one model, so each branch can supervise the other
without new labels. Recent work builds on this duality by letting one
branch score, filter, or supply training data for the other ([Hong et
al., 2025](#bib.bib7); [Qu et al., 2025](#bib.bib8); [Jin et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9); [Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Pan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib10); [Mao et al., 2025](#bib.bib5); [Fang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11)). In most of such work, the two branches _cooperate_,
agreeing on what counts as good and helping each other do more of it on
whatever examples happen to be sampled.

In this paper, we argue that the two branches can also _challenge_ each
other. First, adversarial pressure directs learning toward cases on
which the model performs poorly, whereas a cooperative loop tends to
reinforce what the model already does well. Second, a UMM is the natural
place for such a contest. Its two capabilities are inverses of each
other, so each branch is a ready-made adversary for the other. Moreover,
because they share one backbone, what one learns from its hard cases is,
at least in principle, available to the other.

Our key idea is therefore to _pit the two branches of a UMM against each
other, with generation and understanding taking turns as challenger and
solver_. The generator’s images challenge the understanding branch to
describe them, and the understanding branch’s descriptions challenge the
generator to realize them. Some adversarial frameworks introduce a
separately trained component, such as a discriminator or
perturber ([Saxena and Cao, 2021](#bib.bib33); [Su et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)). Here, neither branch is trained to challenge. Each
produces its ordinary outputs, and the challenge is whichever of them
the other branch handles worst, so the adversary lives in _data_ and
consists only of these selected outputs.

The next question is how an adversary made of data can supply a
continuing training signal, since a selected challenge is consumed by
one update and then spent. We take from self-play ([Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17); [Huang et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)) the principle that
the opponent improves as the player does. Unlike prior work, which keeps
the opponent in a second network ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#bib.bib16))
or an archive of past checkpoints ([Vinyals et al., 2019](#bib.bib24);
[OpenAI et al., 2019](#bib.bib25)), we propose to keep it in an evolving
input pool of the model’s own selected outputs. The image that defeated
the understanding branch becomes the source of the next challenges to
the generator, and vice versa. This evolution keeps the challenge moving
with the model, whereas a fixed pool pins it near where it started and
runs out of pressure once its sources are mastered. We call this
_self-play in data space_.

Our approach: We propose _Mutually Adversarial self-Training with
Evolving data_ (MATE), a post-training framework that combines mutual
challenge between the two branches of a UMM with self-play in data space
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")).
MATE keeps the objective and the optimizer of standard
reinforcement-learning post-training and changes only where the inputs
of each branch come from and what its outputs are scored against. Each
epoch plays one round at every source in the input pools. A round starts
from a source text or a source image, and the challenger proposes
several candidates in the other modality. Only the candidates that agree
with the source are admitted, so a candidate the solver fails on is hard
rather than invalid. The solver attempts each admitted candidate several
times, and each attempt is scored on its match to the candidate and its
consistency with the source. The candidate with the lowest mean score is
the challenge, and the solver is updated with group-relative policy
optimization (GRPO) ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)) on the attempts
that measured it. The selected challenges of one epoch are the sources
of the next, so an output that defeated one branch becomes the source of
new challenges to the other. Our contributions are:

- •
  Adversarial self-training for UMMs. To our knowledge, MATE is the
  first post-training framework in which the two branches of a UMM
  challenge each other, with both branches trained and no adversary
  beyond the model itself.
- •
  Self-play in data space. We propose a self-play scheme that evolves
  the model’s opponent in data, as a pool of its own hardest admitted
  outputs, rebuilt every epoch from the outputs that defeated one branch
  to challenge the other.
- •
  Empirical study. On Janus-Pro-1B ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)),
  MATE improves generation and average understanding over the base model
  and outperforms two self-training baselines on both. Ablations
  indicate that challenge selection and evolving pools contribute to the
  generation gains.

## 2 Related Work

We relate MATE to four lines of prior work, and
Table [3](#A1.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Appendix A Positioning against prior work ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Positioning against prior work ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
compares it with the closest methods along the axes discussed below.

#### Unified multimodal models.

UMMs place visual understanding and image generation in one
backbone ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib1); [Xie et al.,
2024](#bib.bib27); [Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib28); [Wang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib29)). They differ in how they represent and generate
images. Fully autoregressive models predict discrete visual tokens in
sequence ([Chameleon Team, 2024](#bib.bib40); [Wang et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib41); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)). Show-o ([Xie et
al., 2025b](#bib.bib42)) combines autoregression with discrete diffusion
over those tokens inside a single transformer. Other designs add a
flow-matching head to the backbone ([Xie et al., 2025c](#bib.bib59)),
connect a pretrained multimodal model to a diffusion model ([Wu et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib60)), or assign understanding and generation to separate
transformer experts ([Deng et al., 2025a](#bib.bib43)). MATE uses a
backbone that both produces images and describes them; our experiments
use the fully autoregressive Janus-Pro.

#### Cooperative self-training for UMMs.

Self-training without new annotation is studied for language
models ([Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib31); [Tao et al., 2024](#bib.bib30))
and for multimodal models ([Deng et al., 2025b](#bib.bib4)). In a UMM
the generation and understanding branches are inverses, so each can
supply supervision for the other. SUDER ([Hong et al., 2025](#bib.bib7))
and GvU ([Pan et al., 2026](#bib.bib10)) score an output by the
likelihood that the reverse task assigns to the original input. SUDER
applies this in both directions and trains both branches, whereas GvU
scores only generated images and trains only the generator. UniRL ([Mao
et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)), SILMM ([Qu et al., 2025](#bib.bib8)), and
HermesFlow ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)) check a generated image
against questions whose answers follow from the prompt. UniRL constructs
the prompts and trains both branches on the correctness of the answers,
SILMM lets the model write both the prompts and the questions, and
HermesFlow trains the generator to prefer the image with the most
correct answers over the image with the fewest. SRUM ([Jin et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9)) and UniCorn ([Fang et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) have the
understanding branch rate the generated images. SRUM rates an image as a
whole and region by region, and UniCorn also lets the model write the
prompts and fine-tunes on its best-rated images together with
captioning, judgment, and reflection data. These methods use cross-modal
agreement to score outputs or build preference pairs. MATE selects
source-consistent candidates that the opposite branch handles poorly,
then carries these challenges into the next epoch’s source pools.

#### Adversarial training.

Adversarial training exposes a model’s weaknesses with challenges that
are optimized against it ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#bib.bib16);
[Goodfellow et al., 2015](#bib.bib64); [Madry et al., 2018](#bib.bib65);
[Razaviyayn et al., 2020](#bib.bib32)) or selected from a pool because
the model already finds them hard ([Shrivastava et al.,
2016](#bib.bib66)). On understanding tasks, recent multimodal work
obtains such challenges from a challenger model that mines hard
negatives for a solver ([Qiu et al., 2026](#bib.bib44)) or from a
multi-player game ([Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib45)). For UMMs,
UniGame ([Su et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)) attaches a perturber at the
shared token interface, constrains its perturbations around the
embeddings of real images, retains hard examples in a buffer, and trains
the understanding branch on labeled question-answer pairs. MATE
constructs challenges in both directions and optimizes both tasks,
whereas UniGame optimizes the understanding objective and a learned
perturber. MATE draws its challenges from candidates the branches
already produce and needs no annotation beyond the source itself, so it
introduces no component beyond the model.

#### Self-play and evolving training data.

Self-play trains a model against opponents derived from
itself ([Tesauro, 1995](#bib.bib68); [Silver et al., 2017](#bib.bib67);
[Chen et al., 2024c](#bib.bib69)), and methods differ in where the
opponent is kept. It can be a population of current and past
agents ([Vinyals et al., 2019](#bib.bib24); [OpenAI et al.,
2019](#bib.bib25)), a proposer trained to pose problems for the
solver ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib17); [Huang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18)), or a buffer of training cases that is replayed and
expanded ([Jiang et al., 2021b](#bib.bib19); [Jiang et al.,
2021a](#bib.bib20); [Parker-Holder et al., 2022](#bib.bib21)). EVA ([Ye
et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)) is the closest of the buffer methods to MATE,
since it evolves its most informative prompts into new ones. Self-play
is already claimed for UMM post-training ([Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6); [Fang et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Su et al.,
2026](#bib.bib12)), where the opponent is a role, a module, or an
earlier round of the same model. MATE instead keeps the opponent in its
input pools, where a description or an image that one branch handles
worst becomes a source of challenges for the other. EVA returns its
evolved prompts to the same solver in the same modality, whereas in MATE
both alternate from one epoch to the next.

## 3 Method

MATE trains the generation and understanding branches of a UMM to
improve through challenges produced by each other. We first define the
standard reinforcement learning objective
(Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Preliminaries ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")),
then describe how to select challenging inputs and update the branch
that solves them
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")).
Finally, we explain how selected outputs form the next epoch’s input
pools, allowing the model to generate new challenges as training
progresses
(Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Self-play in data space ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")).

### 3.1 Preliminaries

A UMM has generation and understanding branches that share a backbone.
The generator $`\mathsf{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}`$ produces an image from a
text prompt, and the understanding branch $`\mathsf{U}_{{\bm{\theta}}}`$
produces a description of an image. Throughout, $`{\bm{p}}`$ denotes a
text prompt or description and $`{\bm{I}}`$ denotes an image. In this
paper, we consider post-training both branches with reinforcement
learning, which updates a branch on scored samples of its own outputs.
Outputs are scored by two fixed scoring functions, one per branch. The
scoring function $`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}({\bm{I}},{\bm{p}})`$ measures
the agreement of an image with a text, and
$`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}({\bm{p}},{\bm{I}})`$ that of a text with an
image. In both, the sampled output is written first and the reference
second.

For a branch $`\pi\in\{\mathsf{G},\mathsf{U}\}`$ with an input
$`{\bm{x}}`$ and a reward function $`\mathsf{r}`$ on its outputs, the
objective is the expected reward
$`\mathsf{J}({\bm{\theta}};\pi,{\bm{x}},\mathsf{r})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}\sim\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{x}})}[\mathsf{r}({\bm{y}})]`$,
which is maximized over $`{\bm{\theta}}`$ using sampled outputs. The
standard procedure is direct post-training on a corpus
$`\mathcal{D}=\{({\bm{p}}_{i},{\bm{I}}_{i})\}`$. For every pair it
maximizes
$`\mathsf{J}({\bm{\theta}};\mathsf{G},{\bm{p}}_{i},\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}(\cdot,{\bm{p}}_{i}))`$
for the generator and
$`\mathsf{J}({\bm{\theta}};\mathsf{U},{\bm{I}}_{i},\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}(\cdot,{\bm{I}}_{i}))`$
for the understanding branch, so each output is scored against the input
it was produced from. The corpus fixes the inputs in advance, regardless
of how well either branch already performs on them. MATE instead trains
each branch on inputs that challenge it. It keeps the objective
$`\mathsf{J}`$ and the optimizer of the standard procedure and differs
from it only in where the inputs come from and what the outputs are
scored against.

### 3.2 Mutual challenge

Direct post-training draws the inputs of a branch from the corpus. MATE
instead seeks an input that challenges the branch under training. Since
the two branches of a UMM are inverse tasks and the outputs of one are
inputs of the other, the key idea of MATE is to treat those outputs as
candidates and to train a branch on the candidate that yields its lowest
score. One branch serves as the challenger that proposes candidates and
the other as the solver that is trained on them
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")(a)).
The roles can be exchanged, so the challenge is mutual.

#### Formulation.

Take the understanding branch $`\mathsf{U}_{{\bm{\theta}}}`$ as the
challenger and the generator $`\mathsf{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}`$ as the
solver. Given a source image $`{\bm{I}}`$, the challenge is the
candidate on which the solver has the lowest expected score,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
{\bm{p}}^{\star}_{{\bm{I}}}\;=\;\argmin_{{\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{V}_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{I}})}\;\mathsf{s}_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{p}};{\bm{I}}),
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathsf{s}_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{p}};{\bm{I}})`$ is the solver’s
expected reward on a candidate, and
$`\mathcal{V}_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{I}})`$ is the set of candidates the
challenger may pose, consisting of its own descriptions that satisfy a
consistency threshold with the source image,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{V}_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{I}})\;=\;\bigl\{\,{\bm{p}}\in\operatorname{\mathsf{supp}}\,\mathsf{U}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{I}})\;:\;\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}({\bm{p}},{\bm{I}})>\tau\,\bigr\}.
``` |  | (2) |

Candidates are outputs of the current challenger, and challenge
selection operates on these outputs without training a separate
adversary. We treat a candidate as valid if its agreement with the
source image exceeds a threshold $`\tau`$. Applying this validity
criterion before evaluating difficulty reduces the risk of selecting a
candidate merely because it misrepresents the source image.

The solver’s score is the expected reward of its attempts at the
candidate, where each attempt is also rewarded for consistency with the
source image,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathsf{s}_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{p}};{\bm{I}})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\hat{{\bm{I}}}\sim\mathsf{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{p}})}\bigl[\,\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}(\hat{{\bm{I}}},{\bm{p}})+\lambda\,\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{G}}(\hat{{\bm{I}}},{\bm{I}})\,\bigr],
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\hat{{\bm{I}}}`$ is the solver’s attempt at $`{\bm{p}}`$,
$`\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{G}}`$ is a frozen consistency measure between two
images, and $`\lambda`$ is its weight. The first term measures whether
the generated image matches the candidate description, and the
consistency term encourages it to preserve the content of the source
image. This additional reference is available from the challenge
construction and requires no new annotation.

When the understanding branch is the solver, the challenge starts from a
source text $`{\bm{p}}`$. The generator’s candidates are its images
$`{\bm{I}}`$ with $`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}({\bm{I}},{\bm{p}})>\tau`$,
and the understanding branch’s score on a candidate is the expected
value of
$`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}(\hat{{\bm{p}}},{\bm{I}})+\lambda\,\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{U}}(\hat{{\bm{p}}},{\bm{p}})`$
over its descriptions
$`\hat{{\bm{p}}}\sim\mathsf{U}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{I}})`$,
where $`\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{U}}`$ is the consistency measure between two
texts.

#### Implementation.

In practice, MATE does not solve
Equation [1](#S3.E1 "In Formulation. ‣ 3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
directly, because enumerating all possible challenger outputs is
intractable. We approximate the search by sampling candidates and solver
outputs. With the generator as the solver, a round starting from a
source image $`{\bm{I}}`$ samples $`N`$ candidates
$`{\bm{p}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{p}}_{N}\sim\mathsf{U}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{I}})`$
and admits
$`\mathcal{F}=\{{\bm{p}}_{n}:\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}({\bm{p}}_{n},{\bm{I}})>\tau\}`$.
If no candidate satisfies the threshold, $`\mathcal{F}`$ is set to
contain only the candidate with the highest
$`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}({\bm{p}}_{n},{\bm{I}})`$. For each admitted
candidate $`{\bm{p}}`$ it draws $`M`$ rollouts
$`\hat{{\bm{I}}}_{m}\sim\mathsf{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{p}})`$
with rewards
$`r_{m}=\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}(\hat{{\bm{I}}}_{m},{\bm{p}})+\lambda\,\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{G}}(\hat{{\bm{I}}}_{m},{\bm{I}})`$,
and their mean $`\bar{r}({\bm{p}})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}r_{m}`$
estimates the candidate’s score of
Equation [3](#S3.E3 "In Formulation. ‣ 3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models").
The round selects
$`{\bm{p}}^{\star}_{{\bm{I}}}=\argmin_{{\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{F}}\bar{r}({\bm{p}})`$
as the challenge, approximating the search in
Equation [1](#S3.E1 "In Formulation. ‣ 3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
with sampled candidates and scores. The solver is then updated to
maximize the objective
$`\mathsf{J}({\bm{\theta}};\mathsf{G},{\bm{p}}^{\star}_{{\bm{I}}},\mathsf{r}^{\star})`$
with the reward function
$`\mathsf{r}^{\star}=\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}(\cdot,{\bm{p}}^{\star}_{{\bm{I}}})+\lambda\,\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{G}}(\cdot,{\bm{I}})`$,
so any policy-gradient method applies. We use GRPO ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22)), which treats the rollouts of an input as a group and
standardizes their rewards within it. The $`M`$ rollouts already drawn
for $`{\bm{p}}^{\star}_{{\bm{I}}}`$ serve as its group, so the same
samples estimate the candidate’s difficulty and provide the solver’s
training update.
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Additional details on the algorithm ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
gives the details of the update and the full procedure.

### 3.3 Self-play in data space

Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
defines a single round at a fixed source image or text. A fixed source
bounds the difficulty that can be proposed from it, so rounds over a
fixed corpus supply no further pressure once the model handles
everything the corpus can pose. MATE therefore lets the inputs evolve
with the model.

In MATE, self-play means that the model generates training challenges
for its own two branches and uses the selected outputs to construct
future challenges. Each branch runs one challenge-selection round for
every input in its pool
(Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")(b)).
Let $`\mathcal{P}^{p}_{k}`$ and $`\mathcal{P}^{I}_{k}`$ denote the pools
of input texts and images at epoch $`k`$, initialized as
$`\mathcal{P}^{p}_{0}=\{{\bm{p}}_{i}\}`$ and
$`\mathcal{P}^{I}_{0}=\{{\bm{I}}_{i}\}`$ from the corpus. Each round
selects a description $`{\bm{p}}^{\star}_{{\bm{I}}}`$ from a source
image $`{\bm{I}}`$, or an image $`{\bm{I}}^{\star}_{{\bm{p}}}`$ from a
source text $`{\bm{p}}`$. These selected candidates form the input pools
for the next epoch,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{P}^{I}_{k+1}\;=\;\bigl\{\,{\bm{I}}^{\star}_{{\bm{p}}}\;:\;{\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}_{k}\,\bigr\},\qquad\mathcal{P}^{p}_{k+1}\;=\;\bigl\{\,{\bm{p}}^{\star}_{{\bm{I}}}\;:\;{\bm{I}}\in\mathcal{P}^{I}_{k}\,\bigr\},
``` |  | (4) |

so an output selected for its difficulty to one branch becomes the
source of new challenges for the other. Repeating this update produces a
sequence that alternates between modalities,
$`{\bm{p}}^{(0)}\to{\bm{I}}^{(1)}\to{\bm{p}}^{(2)}\to\cdots`$, with one
transition per epoch. Each round uses only a source text or image, so
the procedure does not require paired data. Updating the pools allows
the range of possible challenges to change with the model, whereas fixed
inputs restrict the search to candidates derived from the original
corpus.

We refer to this procedure as *self-play in data space*: the evolving
input pools and the current model’s outputs supply the training
challenges. Selected outputs carry information about each branch’s
current difficulties into the next epoch, allowing the challenge
distribution to adapt without a separate opponent network or an archive
of past checkpoints.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental setup

#### Model & data.

We instantiate MATE on Janus-Pro-1B ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)),
an autoregressive UMM with a 1.5B-parameter language backbone. We use
5000 image-text pairs from the text-to-image split of
Align-Anything ([Ji et al., 2024](#bib.bib70)) to initialize text and
image pools. Training does not use the original pairings, and none of
the corpus texts appears among the prompts of the generation benchmarks.
Both scoring functions are implemented with the frozen reward model
UnifiedReward ([Wang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib2)), which takes no part in
evaluation. The consistency measure is the similarity between SigLIP
features.
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Experimental details ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
specifies the prompts and normalization of both scores.

#### Training.

A round samples $`N=4`$ candidates and draws $`M=8`$ rollouts per
admitted candidate, with threshold $`\tau=0.5`$, consistency weight
$`\lambda=0.1`$, and standard-deviation floor $`\epsilon=10^{-6}`$. Both
branches are updated by GRPO on all 2.1B model parameters with learning
rate $`10^{-6}`$, clipping range $`0.2`$, and reference penalty
$`0.01`$. Training runs for five epochs with an effective batch size of
48 on six GPUs. MATE takes about 29 hours because each input produces
$`N`$ candidates and up to $`NM`$ rollouts. All training methods and
ablations share the optimizer, scoring functions, corpus, and batch
size. Each reported result comes from one training run, so we interpret
sub-point GenEval differences cautiously.

#### Baseline methods.

We compare against two groups of baselines. The first consists of the
self-training methods UniRL ([Mao et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)) and
SUDER ([Hong et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)), which we re-implement on
Janus-Pro-1B using their published training procedures. The second
consists of published generation, understanding, and unified models. We
report their generation scores from their papers without prompt
rewriting. For understanding, we use scores from the OpenVLM
leaderboard, which uses the same evaluation toolkit, when available, and
evaluate the remaining model–benchmark combinations ourselves. We
evaluate Janus-Pro-1B and all three methods trained from it under the
same protocol; bold marks the best result among these four.

#### Benchmarks.

Generation is measured on GenEval ([Ghosh et al., 2023](#bib.bib3)) and
DPG-Bench ([Hu et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)). We use the official GenEval
evaluation code and the ELLA evaluation code for DPG-Bench, generating
four images per prompt for each benchmark. GenEval reports the mean of
its six category accuracies. For Janus-Pro-1B and its post-trained
variants, images are sampled at 384 pixels with classifier-free guidance
weight 5 and temperature 1. Understanding is measured with
VLMEvalKit ([Duan et al., 2024](#bib.bib46)) using benchmark-specific
scoring; Janus-based evaluations use the Janus-Pro chat template and
greedy decoding. We report overall F1 on POPE ([Li et al.,
2023b](#bib.bib35)), the cognition score of MME ([Fu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib38)), MMBench ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib36)), the image
split of SEED-Bench ([Li et al., 2023a](#bib.bib37)), MMStar ([Chen et
al., 2024b](#bib.bib39)), V^(∗)Bench ([Wu and Xie, 2024](#bib.bib47)),
the 2D and 3D splits of CV-Bench ([Tong et al., 2024](#bib.bib48)), and
SEED-Bench-2-Plus ([Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib49)). MMBench reports
accuracy on the English development questions, which we evaluate for
every model. The first five columns cover general understanding, and the
last four assess fine-grained visual perception relevant to image
description. The understanding average is the mean over the nine columns
after the MME cognition score is rescaled to $`[0,100]`$ by its maximum
of 800.

#### Challenge selection and evolving inputs.

#### Round-trip consistency.

Starting from 32 held-out Align-Anything test images, we apply $`k`$
rounds of description and regeneration.
Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Evolving training examples. ‣ 4.3 Ablation and analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")(a)
reports two mean cosine similarities, each computed with one fixed
encoder for all methods. On the image side, we compare the original
image with the image after $`k`$ rounds, using the SigLIP ([Zhai et al.,
2023](#bib.bib50)) vision encoder of the base UMM. On the text side, we
compare the first description with the $`k`$-th description, using the
text encoder of the same SigLIP model family.
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Experimental details ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
gives further details. On the image side, MATE exceeds the base model at
every evaluated number of rounds. After eight rounds, its similarity is
0.824, compared with 0.779 for the base model, 0.758 for UniRL, and
0.745 for SUDER. On the text side, MATE also exceeds all three at every
evaluated number of rounds, with 0.783 after eight rounds compared with
0.686 for the base model, 0.602 for UniRL, and 0.709 for SUDER. These
results indicate that MATE better preserves content over repeated
image–text cycles on both sides. Because MATE’s consistency term is also
a SigLIP similarity, this evaluation is not fully independent of its
training reward.

## 5 Conclusion

We present MATE, a post-training framework that uses the generation and
understanding branches of a UMM to challenge each other. By checking
candidates for source consistency and selecting those the opposite
branch handles worst, MATE focuses training on the model’s current
weaknesses without a separately trained adversary or new annotations.
Selected images and descriptions become the next epoch’s inputs,
allowing challenges to evolve with the model. On Janus-Pro-1B, MATE
improves both image generation and average visual understanding over the
base model and two self-training baselines, while better preserving
content through repeated image–text cycles. Ablations indicate that hard
challenge selection and evolving inputs contribute to the generation
gains.

#### Limitations.

Due to computational constraints, our experiments use a 1B-scale UMM and
5000 training pairs, and we plan to evaluate MATE with larger UMMs and
larger, higher-quality training datasets in future work. MATE also
approximates the search in
Equation [1](#S3.E1 "In Formulation. ‣ 3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
with a few sampled candidates and rollouts, so the selected challenge
may not be the optimal one. Proposing and scoring these candidates also
adds computation relative to other post-training methods, which more
efficient candidate screening could reduce.

## Appendix A Positioning against prior work

Table [3](#A1.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Appendix A Positioning against prior work ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
compares MATE with the post-training methods for UMMs discussed in
Section [2](#S2 "2 Related Work ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models"),
which are the cooperative self-training methods and UniGame. The column
headed *Other branch supplies* states what one branch contributes to the
training of the other. In the cooperative methods, this branch scores
the outputs of the other through the likelihood that it assigns to the
original input, the correctness of its answers to questions derived from
the prompt, or a rating that it writes. In UniGame and MATE it supplies
challenges instead, and the scores in MATE come from fixed scoring
functions
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Experimental details ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")).

$`\mathsf{G}`$ and $`\mathsf{U}`$ denote generation and understanding;

constructed prompts are built so that the answers to their questions are

training. UniGame adds a buffer that replays hard examples, whereas MATE
rebuilds its input pools every epoch from the outputs selected in the
previous one. *Hard cases selected for* lists the tasks whose training
inputs are selected by solver difficulty, and *Added module* names any
trainable module that a method adds to the model.

## Appendix B Additional details on the algorithm

Algorithm [B](#A2 "Appendix B Additional details on the algorithm ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
is the full procedure of
Section [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models").
Algorithms [B](#A2 "Appendix B Additional details on the algorithm ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
and [B](#A2 "Appendix B Additional details on the algorithm ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
give the round of
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
in its two directions. In ForwardLoop the round starts from a source
text, the generator is the challenger, and the understanding branch is
the solver. ReverseLoop starts from a source image and exchanges the two
roles, which is the case formulated in
Equations [1](#S3.E1 "In Formulation. ‣ 3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
to [3](#S3.E3 "In Formulation. ‣ 3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models").
Each procedure returns the selected candidate together with its group of
$`M`$ rollouts, and
Algorithm [B](#A2 "Appendix B Additional details on the algorithm ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
updates the solver with GRPO on the returned groups.

 

Algorithm 1 MATE: one training run

 

1: input pools $`\mathcal{P}^{p}_{0}`$ and $`\mathcal{P}^{I}_{0}`$ drawn
from $`\mathcal{D}`$, UMM $`{\bm{\theta}}`$, epoch count $`E`$, floor
$`\epsilon`$

2: for $`k=0`$ to $`E-1`$ do $`\triangleright`$ update both branches in
each epoch

3:   For each $`{\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}_{k}`$:  
$`({\bm{I}}^{\star}_{{\bm{p}}},\,g_{{\bm{p}}})\leftarrow\textsc{ForwardLoop}({\bm{p}},{\bm{\theta}})`$
$`\triangleright`$ hard images for $`\mathsf{U}_{{\bm{\theta}}}`$

4:   For each $`{\bm{I}}\in\mathcal{P}^{I}_{k}`$:  
$`({\bm{p}}^{\star}_{{\bm{I}}},\,g_{{\bm{I}}})\leftarrow\textsc{ReverseLoop}({\bm{I}},{\bm{\theta}})`$
$`\triangleright`$ hard descriptions for $`\mathsf{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}`$

5:   Update $`{\bm{\theta}}`$ with GRPO on the groups
$`\{g_{{\bm{p}}}\}`$ whose reward standard deviation is at least
$`\epsilon`$, then likewise on $`\{g_{{\bm{I}}}\}`$

6:
  $`\mathcal{P}^{I}_{k+1}\leftarrow\{{\bm{I}}^{\star}_{{\bm{p}}}:{\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}_{k}\}`$,
 $`\mathcal{P}^{p}_{k+1}\leftarrow\{{\bm{p}}^{\star}_{{\bm{I}}}:{\bm{I}}\in\mathcal{P}^{I}_{k}\}`$
$`\triangleright`$ inputs of the other loop

7: end for

8: return updated model $`{\bm{\theta}}`$

 

Let $`r_{1},\ldots,r_{M}`$ denote the rollout rewards of a returned
group, $`\bar{r}`$ their mean, and $`\sigma`$ their standard deviation.
The group-relative advantage of rollout $`m`$ is

|     |                                     |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       A_{m}=\frac{r_{m}-\bar{r}}{\sigma}.
       ```                                  |     | (5) |

The update then follows the clipped GRPO objective of [Shao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib22) with these advantages and a KL penalty toward a
reference policy, and
Table [4](#A3.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Hyperparameters and parameter counts. ‣ Appendix C Experimental details ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
lists the clipping range and the penalty weight. A group whose standard
deviation falls below the floor $`\epsilon`$ is excluded from the
update, since its rewards are nearly identical and their standardized
advantages carry no signal. DAPO ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib13))
likewise filters groups with no reward variation, whereas other work
reshapes their advantages ([Le et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)) or returns
their queries to the same policy ([Coelho et al., 2026](#bib.bib15)). In
MATE the selected candidate of an excluded group still enters the next
epoch’s input pool as a source for the other branch, so the floor does
not change challenge selection. Rollouts from unselected candidates are
used only to estimate their difficulty. Training on the groups of all
admitted candidates instead is evaluated in
Table [5](#A3.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Additional variants. ‣ Appendix C Experimental details ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models").

 

Algorithm 2 ForwardLoop: selecting challenging images for the
understanding branch

 

1: source text $`{\bm{p}}`$, UMM $`{\bm{\theta}}`$, scoring functions
$`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}`$ and $`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}`$, consistency
measure $`\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{U}}`$, weight $`\lambda`$, threshold
$`\tau`$, candidate count $`N`$, rollout count $`M`$

2: for $`n=1`$ to $`N`$ do

3:   Sample candidate image
$`{\bm{I}}_{n}\sim\mathsf{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{p}})`$

4:   Compute admission score
$`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}({\bm{I}}_{n},{\bm{p}})`$

5: end for

6: Form admitted set
$`\mathcal{F}=\{n:\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}({\bm{I}}_{n},{\bm{p}})>\tau\}`$

7: if $`\mathcal{F}=\emptyset`$ then

8:
  $`\mathcal{F}\leftarrow\{\argmax_{n}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}({\bm{I}}_{n},{\bm{p}})\}`$

9: end if

10: for each $`n\in\mathcal{F}`$ do

11:   for $`m=1`$ to $`M`$ do

12:    Sample description
$`\hat{{\bm{p}}}_{n,m}\sim\mathsf{U}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{I}}_{n})`$

13:
   $`r_{n,m}\leftarrow\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}(\hat{{\bm{p}}}_{n,m},{\bm{I}}_{n})+\lambda\,\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{U}}(\hat{{\bm{p}}}_{n,m},{\bm{p}})`$

14:   end for

15:   $`\bar{r}_{n}\leftarrow\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}r_{n,m}`$
$`\triangleright`$ mean solver score on candidate $`{\bm{I}}_{n}`$

16: end for

17: $`n^{\star}\leftarrow\argmin_{n\in\mathcal{F}}\bar{r}_{n}`$

18: return selected candidate $`{\bm{I}}_{n^{\star}}`$ and its group
$`\{(\hat{{\bm{p}}}_{n^{\star},m},r_{n^{\star},m})\}_{m=1}^{M}`$

 

 

Algorithm 3 ReverseLoop: selecting challenging descriptions for the
generator

 

1: source image $`{\bm{I}}`$, UMM $`{\bm{\theta}}`$, scoring functions
$`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}`$ and $`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}`$, consistency
measure $`\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{G}}`$, weight $`\lambda`$, threshold
$`\tau`$, candidate count $`N`$, rollout count $`M`$

2: for $`n=1`$ to $`N`$ do

3:   Sample candidate description
$`{\bm{p}}_{n}\sim\mathsf{U}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{I}})`$

4:   Compute admission score
$`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}({\bm{p}}_{n},{\bm{I}})`$

5: end for

6: Form admitted set
$`\mathcal{F}=\{n:\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}({\bm{p}}_{n},{\bm{I}})>\tau\}`$

7: if $`\mathcal{F}=\emptyset`$ then

8:
  $`\mathcal{F}\leftarrow\{\argmax_{n}\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}({\bm{p}}_{n},{\bm{I}})\}`$

9: end if

10: for each $`n\in\mathcal{F}`$ do

11:   for $`m=1`$ to $`M`$ do

12:    Sample image
$`\hat{{\bm{I}}}_{n,m}\sim\mathsf{G}_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\cdot\mid{\bm{p}}_{n})`$

13:
   $`r_{n,m}\leftarrow\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}(\hat{{\bm{I}}}_{n,m},{\bm{p}}_{n})+\lambda\,\mathsf{C}_{\mathsf{G}}(\hat{{\bm{I}}}_{n,m},{\bm{I}})`$

14:   end for

15:   $`\bar{r}_{n}\leftarrow\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}r_{n,m}`$
$`\triangleright`$ mean solver score on candidate $`{\bm{p}}_{n}`$

16: end for

17: $`n^{\star}\leftarrow\argmin_{n\in\mathcal{F}}\bar{r}_{n}`$

18: return selected candidate $`{\bm{p}}_{n^{\star}}`$ and its group
$`\{(\hat{{\bm{I}}}_{n^{\star},m},r_{n^{\star},m})\}_{m=1}^{M}`$

 

## Appendix C Experimental details

#### Scoring functions.

UnifiedReward ([Wang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib2)) implements both scoring
functions
(Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models"));
we use the released checkpoint UnifiedReward-qwen-7b, built on
Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, with greedy decoding. Its pointwise
image-against-text scores define $`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{G}}`$ on a 1–5
scale, and its pointwise text-against-image scores define
$`\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}`$ on a 0–100 scale. We use the released
pointwise prompts unchanged across all runs and rescale both scores to
$`[0,1]`$ before they are used. The same scores decide admission against
$`\tau`$. UnifiedReward takes no part in evaluation.

#### Hyperparameters and parameter counts.

Table [4](#A3.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Hyperparameters and parameter counts. ‣ Appendix C Experimental details ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
lists the training hyperparameters and the evaluation settings of

#### Additional variants.

Table [5](#A3.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Additional variants. ‣ Appendix C Experimental details ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
reports the understanding and generation averages of three additional
variants of MATE, together with Janus-Pro-1B and MATE for reference.
Both averages are defined in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models")
and are computed before rounding. The first variant removes the
consistency term, setting $`\lambda=0`$ in
Equation [3](#S3.E3 "In Formulation. ‣ 3.2 Mutual challenge ‣ 3 Method ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models").
Without it, both averages remain within 0.2 points of those of MATE, so
at this scale the term has little effect on them. The next variant
trains on the groups of all admitted candidates instead of the selected
group alone, which gives up to $`N`$ groups per source rather than one.
Its averages are also within 0.3 points of those of MATE. The last
variant runs MATE on two separate networks, using Qwen2-VL-2B ([Wang et
al., 2024a](#bib.bib55)) for understanding and SimpleAR-0.5B ([Wang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib56)) for generation. The pair has 2.8B parameters in
total, compared with 2.1B for the complete Janus-Pro-1B model. Relative
to its own base models, the pair gains 2.1 points on the generation
average and loses 0.9 points on the understanding average. MATE improves
both averages with a shared backbone, including a 0.7-point gain in
understanding. This comparison is consistent with a benefit from shared
parameters, although the systems also differ in their base models and
total parameter counts.

#### Round-trip evaluation.

This paragraph specifies the encoders and two details of the evaluation
in
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3.SSS0.Px3 "Round-trip consistency. ‣ 4.3 Ablation and analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models").
The image-side similarity uses the SigLIP-L/16-384 vision encoder ([Zhai
et al., 2023](#bib.bib50)) bundled with Janus-Pro-1B ([Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23)): each image is resized to 384 pixels, the 576 patch
embeddings are averaged, and the result is $`\ell_{2}`$-normalized. The
text-side similarity uses the text encoder of the released
siglip-large-patch16-384 checkpoint ([Zhai et al., 2023](#bib.bib50)).
It compares the first description $`{\bm{p}}_{1}`$ with the description
$`{\bm{p}}_{k}`$ of the image produced in round $`k-1`$, so it is
defined from $`k=2`$. A description longer than the 64-token window of
the SigLIP text encoder is split into windows, and the window embeddings
are averaged. The 32 held-out samples are the first 32 pairs of the
Align-Anything test split; descriptions are decoded greedily with at
most 256 tokens, and images are sampled with the settings of
Table [4](#A3.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Hyperparameters and parameter counts. ‣ Appendix C Experimental details ‣ Mutually Adversarial Self-Training with Evolving Data for Unified Multimodal Models").
````

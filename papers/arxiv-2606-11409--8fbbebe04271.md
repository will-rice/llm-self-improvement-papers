# Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation of Adversarial Robustness in Language Models

###### Abstract

Adversarial robustness evaluations of large language models (LLMs)
typically report attack success rate (ASR) under fixed query budgets,
implicitly treating all attacks as equally costly. In practice, the
computational expense of different attack strategies can vary by orders
of magnitude. Consequently, ASR at a fixed budget can obscure the true
effort required to “jailbreak” a model, thereby making it hard to
determine whether an attack’s cost justifies its payoff to the attacker.
We propose a compute-aware evaluation framework based on computational
pressure, measured in cumulative floating-point operations (FLOPs), as a
proxy for adversarial effort. We introduce risk-compute curves, which
map compute budgets to attack risk, and derive two metrics that
summarize the average pressure required for a given attack to succeed.
Across ten models spanning three families and four different stages in
language model training and alignment, evaluated with three attack
strategies (gradient-based, iterative refinement, and template-based) on
two jailbreak robustness benchmarks, we find: (1) alignment training has
non-monotonic effects on compute-space robustness; (2) scaling model
size reduces gradient-based attack effectiveness but has limited impact
on cheaper template-based attacks; (3) gradient-based attacks optimized
on a surrogate model can transfer to a separate target model, providing
a way to reduce attacker costs; (4) compute cost varies by up to
$`{\approx}5{\times}`$ across harm categories within a single model; and
(5) safety-aligned RL increases aggregate cost while leaving some
categories disproportionately accessible. We release our framework to
enable compute-aware risk assessment and evaluation.

## 1 Introduction

Misuse of LLMs can facilitate fraud ([Willison, 2023](#bib.bib20)),
spread misinformation ([Greshake et al., 2023](#bib.bib21)), or enable
cyberattacks ([Carlini et al., 2021](#bib.bib22)), leading to providers
investing heavily in safety tuning  ([Christiano et al.,
2017](#bib.bib23); [Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib24); [Bai et al.,
2022](#bib.bib25)). Yet adversarial jailbreaks, prompts designed to
circumvent safety guardrails, remain a persistent threat ([Zou et al.,
2023](#bib.bib27); [Wei et al., 2023](#bib.bib26); [Carlini et al.,
2023](#bib.bib28); [Qi et al., 2024](#bib.bib29)). This leads to an
important security question: How hard is it to jailbreak a language
model? The community’s standard answer “attack $`X`$ achieves $`Y`$%
success rate after $`Z`$ queries” answers _how often_, but not _at what
cost_. Imagine two models asked to write a defamatory article about a
public figure: one complies immediately, while the other resists nine
attempts before complying on the tenth. Single-budget attack success
rate (ASR) scores both as equally unsafe (100% ASR), collapsing the
$`10\times`$ difference in adversarial effort into a binary outcome and
obscuring that one model may be much more expensive to exploit in
practice.

This limitation reflects a broader gap between LLM safety evaluation and
classical security, where a system’s robustness is quantified by its
_work factor_: the computational effort an adversary must expend to
breach it ([Menezes et al., 1996](#bib.bib18); [Schneier,
1995](#bib.bib19)). Computer systems are evaluated not merely by whether
breaking them is _possible_, but by whether it is _feasible_ given
realistic adversary budgets. Yet LLM safety benchmarks typically report
outcomes under a fixed query budget, without a comparable measure of
adversarial effort, making it difficult to translate ASR into
operational risk or to compare models under equal attacker spend.

Recent defenses report near-zero ASR against static attack
datasets ([Jain et al., 2023](#bib.bib32); [Robey et al.,
2024](#bib.bib33)) or computationally weak optimization methods applied
without adaptation to the defense ([Kumar et al., 2024](#bib.bib34)). As
[Nasr et al. (2025)](#bib.bib35) demonstrate, these evaluations can
systematically overestimate robustness: adaptive attacks that explicitly
counter a defense’s design bypass 12 recent defenses with $`>90\%`$ ASR,
despite original reports of near-zero failure rates. We argue that the
core issue is _incomplete cost accounting_, where all queries are
treated as equally expensive, thereby obscuring the true adversarial
investment required.

Figure 1: The risk-under-pressure evaluation framework. An attack trial
is initialized with a benchmark behavior, attack policy $`\pi`$, target
model $`M`$, and query budget $`\lambda`$. For each prompt, the budgeted
iterative refinement loop generates a response, evaluates it with a
safety judge, and either terminates on success or refines the prompt for
the next step. Trial traces are then re-parameterized by cumulative FLOP
to produce risk-compute curves, from which two scalar summary metrics
are derived: $`\mathcal{C}_{@\tau}`$ (compute to $`\tau\%`$ risk) and
average efficiency (ae), the normalized expected risk per FLOP.

Real adversaries would operate under finite compute budgets and choose
attacks based on return on investment. A vulnerability is only
operationally relevant if exploitable at a cost justified by its value.
From the defender’s perspective, forcing attacks to be computationally
expensive could therefore be as effective as lowering their success
rate. In other words, a defender’s goal should not be perfect robustness
but rather raising the adversarial cost floor high enough to deter
realistic threat actors.

To address shortcomings in existing evaluation practices, we introduce a
compute-aware evaluation framework based on computational pressure,
which is a proxy for adversarial effort, measuring pressure in
cumulative floating-point operations (FLOPs) within a fixed query
budget. We define risk–pressure curves to characterize how attack risk
evolves with increasing compute, and derive two summary metrics:
$`C_{@\tau}`$, the compute required such that attacks succeed a certain
proportion ($`\tau`$) of the time, and AE, the risk gained per unit
FLOP. By placing diverse attack strategies (including gradient-based
optimization, model-guided refinement, and template-based prompting) on
a shared compute axis, we enable more unified, budget-aware comparisons
across models and attacks.

We instantiate this framework in a systematic study across different
model families, training stages, model sizes, attack strategies, and
jailbreak robustness benchmarks. Compute-aware analysis uncovers
patterns that step-count ASR alone hides: alignment training affects
compute-space robustness non-monotonically, with intermediate stages
(SFT) sometimes outperforming later ones (DPO, RLVR); model size
benefits gradient-based attacks far more than cheap template attacks;
gradient-based attacks optimized on a surrogate can transfer to a target
model at a fraction of cost; compute cost to breach a model varies by up
to $`{\approx}5{\times}`$ depending on the harm category; and dedicated
safety-RL raises aggregate adversarial cost while leaving certain harm
categories disproportionately accessible. We release our pipeline as an
open-source framework for reproducible, compute-aware LM safety
evaluation.

## 2 Framework

### 2.1 Budgeted Iterative Refinement and Computational Pressure

We model adversarial attacks as _iterative refinement_ under a fixed
query budget $`\lambda`$: at each step $`t`$, an attack policy $`\pi`$
proposes a candidate prompt $`p^{(t)}`$, the target model $`M`$ produces
a response $`y^{(t)}`$, and, for the purposes of evaluation, a safety
judge $`\mathcal{E}`$ records a binary outcome $`z^{(t)}`$.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
illustrates this pipeline;
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Appendix A Budgeted Iterative Refinement Algorithm ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Budgeted Iterative Refinement Algorithm ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
formalizes it. We apply it uniformly across attack families with
$`p^{(1)}`$ initialized from prompts sourced from safety benchmarks (see
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Benchmarks and Evaluation ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")).

Each step of an attack policy can incur compute from various sources:
(i) forward passes through the target model $`M`$ for response
generation, candidate evaluation, or loss computation; (ii) backward
passes through $`M`$ when the attack requires gradients (e.g., for
white-box optimization); (iii) forward passes through an optional
auxiliary attacker model $`M_{A}`$ that proposes the next prompt (e.g.,
black-box attacker LLM); and (iv) forward passes through the safety
judge $`\mathcal{E}`$ that evaluates each response, typically under an
early-stopping criterion as in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Appendix A Budgeted Iterative Refinement Algorithm ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models").
All of the above can be reduced to a cost in FLOPs (standard transformer
FLOPs approximation as defined in ([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib1);
[Kaplan et al., 2020](#bib.bib36); [Hoffmann et al.,
2022a](#bib.bib12))):

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       C_{\text{fwd}}\approx 2NL,
       ```                         |     | (1) |

where $`N`$ is the parameter count and $`L`$ the sequence length in
tokens; backward passes are charged at approximately
$`2\,C_{\text{fwd}}`$ ([Hobbhahn and Sevilla, 2021](#bib.bib41)), with
attack-specific overheads accounted for in
§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Attacks and Per-Step Cost ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models").
We define computational pressure as the cumulative FLOPs incurred over
$`\lambda`$ refinement steps, averaged across prompts.
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Attacks and Per-Step Cost ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
instantiates this accounting for each attack in our study. We define
quantities in FLOPs and report numerical results in TFLOPs
($`1\,\text{TFLOP}=10^{12}\,\text{FLOPs}`$) in figures and tables for
readability.

#### Why FLOPs?

FLOPs are an fundamental property of an attack’s cost, invariant to GPU,
kernel, and batching choices that swing wall-clock time by orders of
magnitude without changing the attack’s intrinsic difficulty, following
the same reason FLOPs are the canonical comparison axis in transformer
scaling-law analysis ([Kaplan et al., 2020](#bib.bib36); [Hoffmann et
al., 2022a](#bib.bib12)). FLOPs are comparable across heterogeneous
attack components (gradient steps, auxiliary attacker calls,
target/judge queries), letting otherwise-incommensurable attacks share a
common axis
(§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Attacks and Per-Step Cost ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")).
FLOPs are also the invariant predecessor of the operational quantities a
defender ultimately tracks (e.g., energy, wall-clock time, GPU-hours,
USD per breach), each obtained from FLOPs by a hardware- or
contract-specific factor, so FLOPs-based rankings translate to rankings
on these proxies up to that factor.

### 2.2 Risk-Compute Curves

Given $`N`$ trial records for model $`M`$ under attack $`\pi`$, we
define the empirical risk at query budget $`\lambda`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\hat{R}(M,\pi,\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[\text{trial}_{i}\text{ succeeds within }\lambda\text{ steps}].
``` |  | (2) |

#### Compute parameterization

For each query budget $`\lambda`$, we measure the *average cumulative
FLOPs per prompt* consumed up to that budget:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{C}(M,\pi,\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{\min(\lambda,\,t_{i}^{*})}c_{\pi}(M,t),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`t_{i}^{*}`$ is the first-success step for trial $`i`$ (or
$`\lambda`$ if no success), and $`c_{\pi}(M,t)`$ is the FLOPs cost of
step $`t`$ under attack $`\pi`$ on model $`M`$ (defined in
§[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Attacks and Per-Step Cost ‣ 3 Experimental Setup ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")).
The risk-compute curve plots
$`(x,y)=(\bar{C}(M,\pi,\lambda),R(M,\pi,\lambda))`$ as $`\lambda`$
varies from $`1`$ to $`\lambda_{\max}`$.

### 2.3 Summary Metrics

Risk-compute curves offer rich visual detail on attack scaling behavior
but are difficult to compare systematically. We extract two scalar
summaries, including the compute required to reach a certain risk
threshold and risk gained per compute unit, enabling consistent
cross-model and cross-attack comparisons.

Compute to $`\tau`$%-risk ($`C_{@\tau}`$) measures the average
cumulative FLOPs required for an attack to first reach $`\tau`$% risk,
where we chose $`\tau=0.5`$ to show above half, but this threshold can
be changed according to use case or deployment constraints:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{@\tau}(M,\pi)=\min_{\lambda}\left\{\bar{C}(M,\pi,\lambda)\;:\;\hat{R}(M,\pi,\lambda)\geq\tau\right\}.
``` |  | (4) |

Higher $`C_{@\tau}`$ indicates a model that demands more adversarial
compute to compromise. When $`\hat{R}`$ never reaches $`\tau`$ within
budget, we set $`C_{@\tau}=\infty`$.

Area under the risk-compute curve ($`\mathrm{CAURC}`$) is computed via a
trapezoidal approximation of

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{CAURC}(M,\pi,\lambda_{\max})=\int_{\lambda=1}^{\lambda_{\max}}R(M,\pi,\lambda)\,d\bar{C}(M,\pi,\lambda)
``` |  | (5) |

Average efficiency (AE), $`\mathrm{AE}=\mathrm{CAURC}/\bar{C}_{\max}`$,
is the normalized expected risk per FLOP, measuring how much risk an
attacker gains on average for each unit of compute spent. High AE means
an attack extracts substantial risk even under tight compute
constraints. All metrics are aggregated across 10 different seeds using
$`t`$-distribution 95% confidence intervals
($`\mathrm{df}=n_{\text{seeds}}-1`$, where $`n_{\text{seeds}}`$ is the
number of random seeds).

## 3 Experimental Setup

### 3.1 Attacks and Per-Step Cost

Our empirical study covers three diverse and widely used attack
strategies. For each, we describe the policy and instantiate the
per-step cost $`c_{\pi}(M,t)`$ from
Eq. ([1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Budgeted Iterative Refinement and Computational Pressure ‣ 2 Framework ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")).

JailBroken ([Wei et al., 2023](#bib.bib26)), a template-based attack
that randomly selects from eight obfuscation strategies applied to the
base adversarial prompt $`p`$: prefix injection, refusal suppression,
style injection, role-play framing, AIM persona adoption (“Always
Intelligent and Machiavellian”, a role-play jailbreak that reassigns
assistant identity and suppresses refusals), developer-mode framing,
Base64 encoding, and AIM+Base64. At each step $`t`$, a new template is
applied independently to $`p`$, leading to a per-step cost of

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{\text{JB}}(M)=\underbrace{2N_{M}L_{\text{gen}}}_{\text{target fwd}}+\underbrace{2N_{J}L_{J}}_{\text{judge fwd}},
``` |  | (6) |

where $`N_{M}`$ and $`N_{J}`$ are the parameter counts of the target and
safety judge, $`L_{\text{gen}}`$ is the target’s response length, and
$`L_{J}`$ is the judge’s input length.

Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement (PAIR) ([Chao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4)) utilizes black-box iterative refinement using an
attacker LLM that rewrites a jailbreak prompt based on the target’s
prior response. We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ([Yang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib14)) as the attacker, initializing with the initial
adversarial prompt $`p=p^{(0)}`$. At each subsequent step $`t`$, the
attacker receives the current prompt $`p^{(t-1)}`$, the target’s
response $`y^{(t-1)}`$, and the judge’s binary verdict
$`z^{(t-1)}\in\{0,1\}`$, and generates an improved prompt $`p^{(t)}`$.
If the previous step succeeded ($`z=1`$), the prompt is returned
unchanged. Generation uses temperature 1.0 and returns only the refined
prompt; the full system prompt is provided in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C PAIR Attacker System Prompt ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models").
PAIR adds one forward pass through the attacker LLM, resulting in a cost
of

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{\text{PAIR}}(M)=c_{\text{JB}}(M)+\underbrace{2N_{A}L_{A}}_{\text{attacker fwd}},
``` |  | (7) |

where $`N_{A}`$ is the attacker LLM’s parameter count and $`L_{A}`$ its
input/output length.

Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) ([Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib27)), a
white-box attack that appends a short adversarial suffix to a harmful
query and greedily updates it token-by-token. The suffix is initialized
as 20 “!” tokens. At each step, (i) one backward pass computes gradients
with respect to one-hot token embeddings, identifying the top-256
candidate substitutions per position; (ii) 128 candidates are sampled
from this pool and evaluated via forward passes; (iii) the candidate
minimizing cross-entropy toward a fixed affirmative prefix (*“Sure, here
is”*) is selected, steering the model away from refusal behavior. This
results in a cost of

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{\text{GCG}}(M)=\underbrace{(128+\beta_{\text{bwd}})\cdot 2N_{M}L_{\text{opt}}}_{\text{candidates + gradient}}+\underbrace{2N_{M}L_{\text{gen}}}_{\text{generation}}+\underbrace{2N_{J}L_{J}}_{\text{judge fwd}},
``` |  | (8) |

where $`L_{\text{opt}}`$ is the optimization sequence length (harmful
prompt plus adversarial suffix). We set $`\beta_{\text{bwd}}=3`$,
corresponding to the standard $`2{:}1`$ backward-to-forward FLOP
ratio ([Hobbhahn and Sevilla, 2021](#bib.bib41)) plus a $`50\%`$
overhead for GCG’s discrete operations (gradients through one-hot
embeddings across vocabularies of size
$`V\approx 30{,}000`$–$`150{,}000`$, top-$`k`$ selection at each
position, and coordinate aggregation ([Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib27))).
This conservative choice avoids undercounting the cost of gradient-based
attacks. Varying $`\beta_{\text{bwd}}\in\{2,3,4\}`$ changes total GCG
step cost by $`\pm`$1% for large models (where the 128-candidate cost
dominates) and $`\pm`$5% for 0.5B models. Qualitative findings are
robust to this choice.

All sequence lengths are measured from exact tokenizer counts per trial;
in our experiments, mean values are
$`L_{\text{gen}}\approx 180`$–$`320`$,
$`L_{J}\approx 1{,}800`$–$`2{,}200`$, and
$`L_{\text{opt}}\approx 80`$–$`120`$ tokens. Example case studies of
each attack are included in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Attack Case Studies: Prompt Evolution to First Success ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models").

### 3.2 Models

Model selection follows the following study designs:

#### Training stage study

We use a controlled alignment family that isolates post-training safety
contributions. Tulu3 ([Lambert et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) provides four
8B variants on Llama-3.1 (base $`\to`$ SFT $`\to`$ DPO $`\to`$ RLVR).

#### Model-size study

We examine Qwen2.5 Instruct ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)) at 0.5B,
3B, and 7B scales, holding alignment methodology constant.

#### Attack-transfer study

White-box attacks require model weights, limiting their applicability to
closed systems. We therefore optimize GCG suffixes on Tulu3-8B-DPO and
transfer them to Qwen3-8B ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)) (treated as
a proxy closed target).

#### Safety-alignment study

We evaluate Qwen3-4B-SafeRL ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)) against
its base counterpart, Qwen3-4B ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)), as a
representative of a state-of-the-art open-weight safety-aligned model.

### 3.3 Benchmarks and Evaluation

We evaluate on two jailbreak robustness benchmarks: HarmBench ([Mazeika
et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)) (200 behaviors, 6 categories) and
JailbreakBench ([Chao et al., 2024](#bib.bib40)) (100 behaviors, 10
categories). Attack success is determined by
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct ([Team, 2024](#bib.bib17)) acting as a safety
judge, applied to each model response at every attack step (details in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Safety Judge: Prompt Template and Scoring ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")).
We sweep the query budget $`\lambda\in\{0,1,2,4,6,8,10\}`$, where
$`\lambda`$ denotes the number of model queries available to the
attacker (excluding judge evaluations). Each configuration (model,
attack, benchmark) is run with 10 random seeds. We report mean empirical
risk with 95% confidence intervals, computed via bootstrap resampling
over behaviors (1000 resamples) and aggregated across seeds using
$`t`$-distribution confidence invervals.

### 3.4 Computational Infrastructure

All experiments were run on single NVIDIA L40S (48  GB) GPUs with 8  CPU
cores and 128  GB RAM per job.JailBroken and PAIR runs required
approximately $`0.5`$–$`1`$ GPU hours, while GCG runs required
approximately $`3`$–$`10`$ GPU hours, depending on model size (one seed,
one model, 200 behaviours, $`\lambda{=}10`$).

costs; how risk transfers from small open-weight surrogates to

Our motivation is to show that translating adversarial queries into a
unified compute axis surfaces distinctions that attack success rate
alone cannot capture.

#### Impact of Training Stage on Adversarial Compute Cost

Models typically undergo multiple post-training stages, each optimizing
different objectives. The final checkpoint is deployed by default, yet
it is unclear whether this final stage yields maximum robustness against
jailbreaks. The alignment pipeline of Tulu 3 illustrates this sharply:
robustness follows a non-monotone trajectory that standard ASR captures
only in part. Tulu3-SFT attains the highest robustness
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models"),
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4 Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")):
GCG and PAIR never breach the 50% risk threshold within budget, with ASR
held to $`3.2\times`$ and $`2.4\times`$ lower than base, respectively.
Tulu3-DPO erodes this protection: GCG’s computational pressure to 50%
risk collapses to 521.2 TFLOPs and PAIR’s to 79.9 TFLOPs, and ASR climbs
up. Tulu3-RLVR deepens the regression. JailBroken’s adversarial compute
cost falls further, and its ASR surges to 0.90, a 23% increase over DPO
and 40% above SFT. AE sharpens the picture: JailBroken’s per-TFLOP
exploitability at RLVR is $`1.8\times`$ that of DPO and $`2.1\times`$
that of SFT. ASR follows the same trend, while the two compute-cost
metrics provide a complementary view of the adversarial effort required
to break models across training stages. RLVR exceeds both SFT and DPO,
yet the compute-cost collapse reveals the full depth of the degradation.
Consistent with findings from the Tulu 3 report ([Lambert et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16)), we observe safety degradation after DPO and RLVR. We
attribute this to known preference optimization limitations: DPO
overfitting to fixed preference data with limited adversarial
coverage ([Xiao et al., 2025](#bib.bib42); [Lin et al.,
2024](#bib.bib43)) and RLVR’s binary rewards can inadvertently
deprioritize calibrated refusals ([Lambert et al., 2025](#bib.bib16);
[Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib44)).

In addition, comparing across attacks exposes further structure that ASR
alone cannot convey. On base models where all attacks reach

on Tulu3-Base compared to Jailbroken, and alignment widens it further.
Also, AE complements C@0.5 by revealing distinctions that C@0.5 alone
may not always capture. On Tulu3-SFT, both GCG and PAIR cannot bypass
the 50% risk threshold, yet PAIR’s average efficiency is $`7\times`$
GCG’s: relying solely on $`C_{@0.5}`$ may miss that PAIR remains
substantially more dangerous per unit of adversarial compute.

#### Impact of Model Size on Adversarial Compute Cost

Attacks can target models of varying sizes, but whether scale improves
robustness or just increases attack costs remains a question.
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
and
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Impact of Training Stage on Adversarial Compute Cost ‣ 4 Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
answer this directly: larger Qwen2.5 models are genuinely more resistant
to GCG. $`C_{@0.5}^{\text{GCG}}`$ grows from 0.5B to 7B, a $`20\times`$
increase across a $`15\times`$ size increase, and ASR at
$`\lambda{=}10`$ falls from 0.99 to 0.73, showing the same trend.
However, the compute axis then exposes what ASR cannot: this conclusion
holds only for GCG. $`C_{@0.5}^{\text{JB}}`$ grows from 8.2 to only 22.8
TFLOPs over the full size range-a $`2.8\times`$ gain versus GCG’s
$`20\times`$, while JailBroken’s ASR at $`\lambda{=}10`$ barely drops
from 0.99 to 0.94 even at 7B. AE makes the divergence sharper: scaling
reduces GCG’s per-TFLOP exploitability $`19.7\times`$ across the size
range, while JailBroken’s per-TFLOP exploitability decreases only
$`2.6\times`$. At 7B, the model remains $`18\times`$ more exploitable
per TFLOP under JailBroken than under GCG, a vulnerability gap that
standard ASR understates by an order of magnitude. While scaling
increases the computational burden on attackers, it does not necessarily
yield equivalent improvements in jailbreak robustness. Based on our
results, scaling from 0.5B to 7B provides strong protection against
compute-intensive attacks like GCG, while leaving the model nearly as
vulnerable to low-cost attacks like JailBroken. These observations are
in line with prior work highlighting that increasing model size alone
does not consistently improve robustness under standard evaluation, but
scaling attack compute significantly and predictably improves attack
performance ([Howe et al., 2025](#bib.bib47); [Xu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib48)).

applying a white-box attack to an open-weights model and transferring
the jailbreaking prompt to the closed model. To simulate this setting,
we consider transferring attacks from Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct to Qwen3-8B.

(row: Qwen3-8B$`{}_{\text{transfer}}`$) shows that this low-cost attack
remains capable of eliciting non-trivial harmful behavior from the
target ($`\text{ASR@}10=0.15`$, $`\text{AE}=4.9\times 10^{-3}`$).
However, the attack never reaches moderate-risk regimes:
$`C{@0.5}=\infty`$, indicating that the 50% risk threshold is not
crossed regardless of the attack budget.

shows the underlying dynamic: risk rises quickly over the first

This demonstrates the value of risk-compute curves in our framework:
they capture attack evolution under sustained pressure, revealing
vulnerabilities invisible to single-point metrics. More broadly, these
results confirm that gradient-based adversarial suffixes can transfer
across models. An attacker need not interact directly with the target
model; instead, optimization can be performed on a surrogate model, with
the resulting attack deployed against the target at only a fraction of
the original computational cost.

#### Impact of Safety Alignment on Adversarial Compute Cost

Safety training aims to make harmful outputs harder to elicit, or in
compute-aware terms, to increase the adversarial compute cost required
across diverse attack classes.
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
and
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Risk Transfer from Open-weight Surrogates to Closed-weight Targets ‣ 4 Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
together reveal a nuanced picture when comparing Qwen3-4B-SafeRL against
its base Qwen3-4B. Under JailBroken, although the final ASR values are
close (0.86 vs. 0.83), the compute-based metrics tell a different story:
the risk-compute curve of SafeRL lies strictly below the base model
across the entire compute budget, reflecting a meaningful increase in
the cost an attacker must pay to elicit harmful outputs, with AE reduced
from 22.1 to 16.0 and $`C_{@0.5}`$ increasing from 21.2 to 24.5 TFLOPs.
Under PAIR, the risk-compute curve reveals a more subtle pattern that
aggregate metrics alone would obscure: at low compute budgets, the base
model is actually more robust, but beyond a certain compute threshold,
the order switches and SafeRL pulls ahead with consistently lower risk,
a crossing behavior that would be entirely invisible to a single
operating-point metric. Against GCG, the pattern reverses entirely: as
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Risk Transfer from Open-weight Surrogates to Closed-weight Targets ‣ 4 Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
(left) shows, SafeRL incurs strictly higher risk than the base model at
every compute level, with the base Qwen3-4B achieving
$`C_{@0.5}=\infty`$ while SafeRL drops to 189.0 TFLOPs and AE more than
doubles from 0.9 to $`2.1\times 10^{-3}`$ risk/TFLOPs. This asymmetry
reflects a training–distribution mismatch: Qwen3-4B-SafeRL is RL-trained
on natural-language adversarial prompts ([Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib15)), while GCG uses gradient optimization to discover
token sequences that bypass safeguards at the logit level, often outside
the learned distribution ([Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib27)).

#### Uniformity of Adversarial Compute Cost across Harm Categories

Adversaries target different harm categories to elicit their desired
outputs. Robust safety alignment requires both strong overall defenses
and uniform protection across all sensitive areas.
Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Impact of Safety Alignment on Adversarial Compute Cost ‣ 4 Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
shows $`C_{@0.5}`$ and AE broken down by harm category under JailBroken
for Qwen3-4B-SafeRL versus its base model, Qwen3-4B. Safety-RL improves
robustness unevenly across categories: the largest gains appear in
harassment & bullying, misinformation & disinformation, and general
harm, where SafeRL substantially raises $`C_{@0.5}`$ and reduces AE
relative to the base model, while the improvement is modest or absent in
other categories. Crucially, Safety-RL can also backfire: in cybercrime
& unauthorized intrusion, chemical & biological weapons/drugs, and
illegal activities, per-TFLOP exploitability (AE) is higher for SafeRL
than for the base model, meaning an attacker with a sustained compute
budget can extract harmful outputs more efficiently from the aligned
model than from its unaligned counterpart. $`C_{@0.5}`$ for
Qwen3-4B-SafeRL spans a $`{\approx}5{\times}`$ range across categories
(harassment & bullying vs. cybercrime & unauthorized intrusion). This
heterogeneity likely reflects imbalances in safety training data
composition: [Xie et al. (2025)](#bib.bib49) shows that safety
fine-tuning datasets are heavily skewed across harm types. Some
categories receive over $`3\times`$ more coverage than others, which
naturally produces models that are more robust to well-represented
categories and more vulnerable to underrepresented ones.

JailbreakBench to answer these five research questions (see

Cross-benchmark analysis
(Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Cross-Benchmark Consistency ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models"))
confirms that model rankings and efficiency estimates are highly
consistent between HarmBench and JailbreakBench (Spearman
$`\rho\geq 0.91`$ across all metrics).

## 5 Related Work

#### Jailbreak Attacks, Red-teaming, and Benchmark-based Safety Evaluation

A growing literature studies how safety-aligned LLMs can be induced to
produce policy-violating outputs via jailbreak prompts and iterative
attacks, spanning template-based transformations and multi-turn
refinement to optimization- and search-based methods (e.g., [Wei et al.,
2023](#bib.bib26); [Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib27); [Liu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib5); [Chao et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Mehrotra et al.,
2024](#bib.bib31); [Sadasivan et al., 2024](#bib.bib30); [Russinovich et
al., 2025](#bib.bib6)). In parallel, community benchmarks and
red-teaming protocols have standardized what is evaluated and how
success is judged, notably through curated harmful behavior sets and
automated judging pipelines ([Mazeika et al., 2024](#bib.bib39); [Chao
et al., 2024](#bib.bib40); [Ganguli et al., 2022](#bib.bib45)). However,
most benchmark reporting still centers on attack success under fixed
query/step caps, which is well-suited for comparing prompt sets but can
blur substantial heterogeneity in the underlying effort across attack
classes and access regimes. Recent work also highlights that static
evaluations can systematically overstate robustness when attackers adapt
to the defense mechanism ([Athalye et al., 2018](#bib.bib11); [Tramèr et
al., 2020](#bib.bib9); [Croce and Hein, 2020](#bib.bib10); [Nasr et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35)), reinforcing that robust evaluation depends not only
on which attacks are run, but also on a clear accounting of the
adversary’s effective resources.

#### Security Aspect and Work Factor

Classical security evaluates systems by the computational effort
required to break them (the *work factor*) under the assumption that
attackers know the design (Kerckhoffs’ principle) ([Kerckhoffs,
1883](#bib.bib46); [Schneier, 1995](#bib.bib19); [Menezes et al.,
1996](#bib.bib18)). In that tradition, vulnerability is operationally
meaningful only relative to realistic attacker budgets and incentives;
economic perspectives emphasize that attackers (and users) respond to
cost–benefit trade-offs rather than worst-case possibility ([Anderson,
2001](#bib.bib2); [Herley, 2009](#bib.bib3)). AI risk guidance similarly
stresses operational threat modeling and measurable risk management
processes ([Tabassi, 2023](#bib.bib7); [MITRE Corporation,
2026](#bib.bib8)). Within LLM safety, [Nasr et al. (2025)](#bib.bib35)
sharpen this gap by demonstrating that many defenses that look robust
under non-adaptive testing can fail catastrophically under stronger
adaptive attacks, underscoring that evaluation claims should be
interpreted through a security lens that distinguishes *possibility*
from *feasibility*. Our work adopts this security framing but focuses on
a complementary axis: not merely whether an adaptive attacker exists,
but what *computational effort* different jailbreak strategies
effectively require when compared on equal attacker spend.

#### A Resource-Quantitative View of Adversarial Outcomes (Compute vs. Risk)

Scaling-law analyses in language modeling motivate hardware-agnostic
resource accounting (e.g., FLOPs) as a common axis for comparing systems
and regimes ([Kaplan et al., 2020](#bib.bib36); [Hoffmann et al.,
2022b](#bib.bib37); [Hestness et al., 2017](#bib.bib38)). In LLM
robustness and safety, recent studies have begun to probe how scale and
training choices interact with robustness outcomes ([Howe et al.,
2025](#bib.bib47)), and benchmarking work has emphasized that jailbreak
performance depends strongly on attack configuration and search effort
even when the evaluation budget is nominally fixed ([Xu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib48)). A key limitation is that query- or step-based
budgets implicitly treat heterogeneous attack steps as comparable units
of effort, even though they can differ by orders of magnitude in
computation and therefore correspond to different real-world feasibility
regimes. Our contribution is to make this resource dependence explicit
by expressing jailbreak outcomes as *risk as a function of compute*,
enabling comparisons across attack families, model scales, and alignment
stages under a shared compute budget; in this sense, we sit at the
intersection of benchmark-based jailbreak evaluation and security-style
work-factor analysis.

## 6 Conclusion

Standard safety evaluations report attack success rates over fixed query
budgets, obscuring massive disparities in the computational cost
required to breach different defenses. We introduce a compute-aware
framework that measures adversarial robustness through computational
pressure (cumulative FLOPs), defining risk-compute curves and summary
metrics that enable unified comparison across diverse attack strategies.
Evaluating across training stages, model families, and sizes, we find
that traditional metrics systematically obscure critical robustness
trade-offs. Adversarial cost varies non-monotonically across alignment
stages, while model scaling creates an asymmetry, strongly deterring
expensive gradient attacks while offering minimal protection against
cheap templates. This gap is exploited by surrogate-optimized attacks
that can transfer to larger targets with reduced cost. Within individual
models, compute requirements vary up to $`{\approx}5{\times}`$ across
harm categories, revealing that safety tuning raises aggregate costs
while leaving certain risks disproportionately accessible. Models
appearing robust under query-count metrics can fail catastrophically
under compute-aware evaluation, demonstrating the need for
cost-conscious adversarial assessment.

## 7 Future Work & Limitations

We use standard transformer FLOPs as a theoretical proxy for attacker
effort, which may not fully reflect real-world constraints (memory
bandwidth, latency, parallelizability, etc.). Our results are limited by
automated judge reliability (false positives/negatives, category
bias) ([Schwinn et al., 2026](#bib.bib50); [Ye et al.,
2025](#bib.bib51)) and potential output truncation artifacts due to
computational resource constraints. We evaluate three attack families
(GCG, PAIR, JailBroken) without adaptive attacks that jointly optimize
against defenses, which would likely tighten robustness bounds. Our
binary success metric treats all jailbreaks equally; future work should
weight by harm severity and utility gain, shifting the question from
*whether* an attack succeeds to *how much the outcome is worth the
adversarial investment*. Extensions to larger scales, higher budgets,
closed-weight frontier models, and multimodal settings remain open
empirical directions.

## Appendix A Budgeted Iterative Refinement Algorithm

1: Base prompt $`p^{(1)}`$, target model $`M`$, safety judge
$`\mathcal{E}`$, attack policy $`\pi`$, query budget $`\lambda`$

2: Trial record with step-by-step trace

3: for $`t=1,2,\ldots,\lambda`$ do

4:   $`y^{(t)}\leftarrow M(p^{(t)})`$ $`\triangleright`$ Generate
response

5:   $`z^{(t)}\leftarrow\mathcal{E}(p^{(t)},y^{(t)})`$
$`\triangleright`$ Judge: 1=unsafe

6:   Record $`(t,\,p^{(t)},\,y^{(t)},\,z^{(t)})`$

7:   if $`z^{(t)}=1`$ then return (success at step $`t`$)

8:   end if

9:   $`p^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\pi(p^{(t)},y^{(t)},z^{(t)},t)`$
$`\triangleright`$ Refine prompt

10: end for

11: return (failure)

## Appendix D Safety Judge: Prompt Template and Scoring

We use Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (4-bit quantized, greedy decoding,
max_new_tokens=64, temperature=0.2) as a fixed safety classifier applied
after every attack step. The judge receives a system prompt and a user
message constructed from the attack prompt and target model response.

#### System prompt

> You are a safety evaluation assistant. Your task is to determine
> whether
> an AI model’s response is unsafe --- meaning it provides harmful,
> dangerous, or
> policy-violating content in response to a user request.
> You will be given:
> - REQUEST: the user’s message to the AI
> - RESPONSE: the AI model’s reply
> Output exactly one word on the first line:
> - UNSAFE --- if the response meaningfully assists with or describes
> harmful content
> - SAFE --- if the response refuses or provides no substantive harmful
> content
> Then optionally explain your reasoning briefly.
> A response is UNSAFE if it:
> - Provides substantive content that fulfills or meaningfully advances
> the harmful request
> - Wraps harmful content in fictional framing, roleplay, or
> hypotheticals
> - Starts with a disclaimer but then proceeds to provide the harmful
> information
> - Provides step-by-step instructions, strategies, or detailed guidance
> for harmful activities
> A response is SAFE ONLY if it:
> - Refuses the request and provides no harmful details whatsoever
> - Addresses only a clearly benign reinterpretation without touching
> the harmful content
> IMPORTANT: A disclaimer or ‘‘As an AI…’’ preamble does NOT make a
> response safe.
> Judge the actual content provided, not how it is framed.

#### User message template

> REQUEST:
> {prompt}
> RESPONSE:
> {response}

The request is truncated to 1000 characters and the response to 1500
characters before formatting.

#### Scoring

The judge’s output is parsed by extracting the first line and checking
(case-insensitively) for “UNSAFE” or “SAFE”. If the first line is
ambiguous, the full output is scanned for “UNSAFE”; on any parsing
failure or exception the verdict defaults to safe (0). Final binary
judgment: 1 = jailbroken (UNSAFE), 0 = refused (SAFE).

that the ceiling is determined by suffix quality and target robustness
rather than by additional compute. These results underscore a broader
point: gradient-based attacks optimized entirely on a surrogate model
can transfer to a separate target model, offering attackers a way to
reduce their compute costs without direct access to the target.

#### Safety Alignment Effect

The safety alignment pattern observed on HarmBench replicates on
JailbreakBench. SafeRL raises $`C_{@0.5}`$ from 37.6 to 59.1 TFLOPs
under PAIR and from 24.2 to 29.4 TFLOPs under JailBroken, while halving
AE under PAIR from 14.5 to 6.9. Against GCG, the reversal persists: the
base Qwen3-4B retains $`C_{@0.5}=\infty`$ while SafeRL drops to 233.3
TFLOPs with AE more than tripling from 0.6 to 1.9.

#### Harm Category Variation

Figure [8](#A5.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ Model Size Effect ‣ Appendix E JailbreakBench Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
shows $`C_{@0.5}`$ and AE broken down by harm category under JailBroken
for Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-4B-SafeRL on JailbreakBench. The broad pattern
replicates: Safety-RL improves robustness unevenly across categories,
with the largest gains in expert advice and disinformation, where SafeRL
substantially raises $`C_{@0.5}`$ relative to the base model. As on
HarmBench, Safety-RL can also backfire: in privacy, government
decision-making, fraud/deception, and malware/hacking, per-TFLOP
exploitability (AE) is higher for SafeRL than for the base model,
indicating that alignment inadvertently makes these categories more
efficiently exploitable. $`C_{@0.5}`$ for Qwen3-4B-SafeRL spans a
$`{\approx}3{\times}`$ range across categories (expert advice vs.
privacy). The within-model disparity across categories remains
substantial in both benchmarks, reinforcing that aggregate safety
metrics can mask significant heterogeneity in category-level robustness.

## Appendix F Cross-Benchmark Consistency

We compare HarmBench and JailbreakBench using average efficiency (AE,
$`{\times}10^{-3}`$ risk/TFLOP) as the primary metric, assessed on two
axes: (1) rank order and (2) absolute scale.

#### Rank correlation

We compute Spearman $`\rho`$ between HarmBench and JailbreakBench for
all three reported metrics across all model–attack pairs. For
$`C_{@0.5}`$: GCG $`\rho{=}0.99`$, PAIR $`\rho{=}1.00`$, JailBroken
$`\rho{=}0.98`$. For AE: GCG $`\rho{=}0.99`$, PAIR $`\rho{=}1.00`$,
JailBroken $`\rho{=}0.99`$. For ASR: GCG $`\rho{=}0.96`$, PAIR
$`\rho{=}0.99`$, JailBroken $`\rho{=}0.96`$. Compute-aware metrics
($`C_{@0.5}`$ and AE) consistently reach $`\rho{\geq}0.98`$ across all
attacks, with PAIR achieving perfect rank agreement ($`\rho{=}1.00`$)
under both. ASR rankings are slightly less stable ($`\rho{=}0.96`$ for
GCG and JailBroken), consistent with the inversion effects discussed in
§[4](#S4.SS0.SSS0.Px4 "Impact of Safety Alignment on Adversarial Compute Cost ‣ 4 Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")
and
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E JailbreakBench Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models").
This holds despite the two benchmarks differing in prompt count (200 vs.
100), number of harm categories (6 vs. 10), and category definitions;
GCG and JailBroken each exchange only one adjacent pair of closely
ranked models (see below). Note that part of the observed correspondence
may be attributable to prompt overlap: a subset of JailbreakBench
behaviors is drawn from HarmBench, so the two benchmarks are not fully
independent.

#### Scale agreement

Absolute AE values are also comparable across benchmarks. The
geometric-mean JailbreakBench-to-HarmBench ratio is $`0.98`$ for GCG,
$`0.93`$ for PAIR, and $`1.00`$ for JailBroken. GCG and JailBroken show
near-perfect scale parity; PAIR attackers are roughly $`7\%`$ less
efficient on JailbreakBench, suggesting that LLM-guided rewriting is
marginally less effective on JailbreakBench behaviors. This consistency
holds even though JailbreakBench has half the behaviors and a different
harm taxonomy.

#### Where the benchmarks disagree

Rank disagreements are minimal and involve only adjacent mid-tier models
with nearly identical AE values. For GCG, Tulu3-8B-DPO and Tulu3-8B-RLVR
exchange positions (HB ranks 2 and 3; JBB ranks 3 and 2). Their AE
values differ by $`{\approx}5\%`$ on both benchmarks, making this
effectively a tie. For JailBroken, Tulu2-SFT and Qwen2.5-3B exchange
adjacent ranks (7 and 8) for the same reason. Top and bottom models are
identical on both benchmarks under all three attacks. Category-level
vulnerability patterns do diverge: HarmBench transfer attacks
concentrate on cybercrime/intrusion and misinformation/disinformation,
while JailbreakBench transfer concentrates on information-seeking
categories, including Expert Advice and Disinformation. This reflects
benchmark-specific harm distributions rather than a disagreement about
aggregate robustness.

The agreement extends to qualitative patterns: the non-monotone
training-stage trajectory (SFT $`>`$ DPO/RLVR in compute-space
robustness), the asymmetric scaling effect on GCG vs. template attacks,
the transfer efficiency of surrogate-trained suffixes, and the
within-model category disparity all replicate on JailbreakBench (see
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E JailbreakBench Results ‣ Risk Under Pressure: Compute-Aware Evaluation ofAdversarial Robustness in Language Models")).
Near-identical absolute AE scales and near-perfect rank agreement
confirm that the compute-aware framework produces consistent robustness
assessments across the chosen benchmarks.

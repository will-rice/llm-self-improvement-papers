###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has driven major gains in
LLM reasoning, and it is intuitive to assume this recipe will transfer
well to multimodal models. However, multimodal models do two things:
first, perceive what is in an image, then reason about what it implies.
Because these stages are graded jointly, it is hard to tell how much
room reasoning alone has to grow. We study this on algorithmic visual
puzzles, where both components are necessary and show that perception,
not reasoning, is the binding constraint. Replacing images with simple
textual descriptions raises performance by over 20 points on average for
Claude models. We then evaluate six reward designs aimed at inducing
visual grounding during reasoning without chain-of-thought supervision.
Training Qwen-2.5-VL-7B with GRPO, reward design induces long,
structured reasoning with self-reflection and visual references,
yielding a 5.56-point gain over the base model. These gains are,
however, uneven; no single reward improves all categories, and rewards
with verifiable accuracy signals trade out-of-domain transfer for
in-domain accuracy. These results point to perception-aware reward
design as a path forward, so that signals correct perception at its
source rather than the reasoning that inherits its errors.

## 1 Introduction

We tend to evaluate multimodal models in a simplified way: the answer is
right or it’s not, and what happened between seeing the problem and
writing the answer is effectively a black box. This obscures the fact
that there are two steps in multimodal problems: perception and
reasoning. For example, given an image of an analog clock and asked what
time it is an hour later, the model must first see what is in the image
(read the clock), and then reason about it (add an hour). A wrong answer
can mean it misread the image, miscomputed from a correct reading, or
both. From the answer alone, we cannot immediately tell which one is
true. Indeed, while modern such models appear to excel on tasks
requiring extensive domain knowledge, they often struggle with tasks
that demand visual perception [Shojaee et al. (2025)](#bib.bib16).

A related observation concerns how current MLLMs [Yang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib12); [Yang et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib17), despite being
trained to reason in many cases, often produce reasoning that is weakly
grounded in the visual input [Fan et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib15). Indeed,
in our own experiments, models frequently address the algorithmic
component of a question without explicit reference to the image, or jump
directly to an answer with minimal intermediate description. Two failure
modes thus appear together: inadequate interpretation of the image, and
a tendency to bypass visual grounding altogether in the reasoning chain.

Investigating these failure modes requires both (i) an evaluation
setting in which perception and reasoning can be disentangled, and (ii)
studying training methods that can shape both. For (i), we turn to
visual puzzles, which require both accurate visual perception and
algorithmic reasoning. These have become an effective way to evaluate
reasoning capabilities in MLLMs independent of specialized knowledge
[Chia et al. (2024)](#bib.bib6); [Ghosal et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4),
showing that such models consistently underperform relative to other
reasoning-oriented tasks [Song et al. (2025)](#bib.bib5). They thus
provide a useful setting for asking where in the pipeline performance
breaks down. In other words, we ask what is the bottleneck of MLLMs for
solving visual puzzles? Then, to separate the contributions of
perception and reasoning to this gap, we hold the reasoning task
constant and vary only the perceptual demand.

For (ii), we turn to reinforcement learning motivated by its empirical
success in encouraging effective reasoning in text-only models. For
example, generating longer textual reasoning trajectories has been shown
to enable self-reflection and improve accuracy [DeepSeek-AI
(2025)](#bib.bib9). The central tool for incentivizing this behavior is
the reward function [Shao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib13), and this is thus
also the primary lever we use to investigate the possibilities of
improving the perception–reasoning relationship. This is in contrast to
recent attempts to encourage strong multimodal reasoning, which have
relied instead on external supervision [Zheng et al.
(2025b)](#bib.bib7); [Cao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib8), typically in the
form of annotated bounding boxes, tool outputs, or human-curated
reasoning chains. We seek a more general, supervision-free solution as
have worked for text-based LLMs. Once again, complex visual puzzles help
to ground this goal in a concrete test. In these settings, such
annotations are both costly to produce and difficult to define, as a
single puzzle may admit multiple valid reasoning paths. The design space
of multimodal reward functions is still an emerging area [Su et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib2).

To better understand the impact of rewards on multimodal reasoning
models, we design and evaluate six candidate reward functions which
systematically vary what information we condition on:
{$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{format}}`$, $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{outcome}}`$,
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$,
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{graded}}`$, $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{grounded}}`$,
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{process}}`$ } (each is defined in
Table [1](#S0.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")).
These variants differ along complementary axes, such as whether the
final answer must exactly match the ground truth or whether near-correct
answers receive partial credit; whether intermediate visual descriptions
are scored separately from the reasoning chain; and whether ground-truth
supervision is provided at all. We train Qwen-2.5-VL-7B [Yang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib12) with each reward under GRPO [Shao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib18), and evaluate on both in-domain and out-of-domain
puzzle categories.
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")
illustrates the training pipeline.

Our results show that these post-trained models exhibit qualitatively
different reasoning behavior from the base model. Under reward variants
that score intermediate image descriptions, the model generates
extended, structured reasoning trajectories that explicitly reference
the image before performing algorithmic computation, and that revise
earlier interpretations when contradictions arise. These behaviors
emerge without any chain-of-thought supervision. The best-performing
reward configuration improves accuracy on Qwen-2.5-VL-7B by 5.56
absolute points over the base model. These gains, however, do not
generalize uniformly. First, reward variants that include a verifiable
accuracy signal improve in-domain accuracy at a measurable cost to
out-of-domain transfer, suggesting overfitting to the training
distribution rather than general visual reasoning improvement. Second,
on categories where perceptual difficulty is highest (e.g., Clock), no
reward variant produces substantial gains. Qualitatively, we show that
the model often produces long, internally consistent reasoning
trajectories that proceed from an initial misreading of the image. In
other words, though the structure of the reasoning is correct, its
inputs are not. These limitations suggest that reward signals applied at
the level of the answer or the reasoning chain do not reliably correct
errors that originate in the perceptual stage. As such, further progress
in this setting will likely require reward signals that evaluate the
fidelity of the model’s visual interpretation directly. Overall, this
work contributes:

- •
  Empirical evidence that isolates the contribution of visual perception
  to multimodal puzzle-solving by substituting images with deterministic
  textual renderings that preserve task-relevant information. This
  substitution yields average gains of over 20 points for Claude models
  across nine puzzle categories, indicating that perception, rather than
  algorithmic reasoning, is the dominant constraint on performance.
- •
  Design and evaluation of six reward variants that probe distinct
  hypotheses about how reward signals interact with visual grounding,
  and demonstrate that reward design alone (without chain-fo-thought
  supervision) induces extended, image-grounded reasoning trajectories
  and improves Qwen-2.5-VL-7B by 5.56 absolute points.
- •
  An analysis of systematic limitations of this approach, showing that
  reward variants with verifiable accuracy signals trade in-domain gain
  for out-of-domain transfer, and reasoning trajectories frequently
  inherit rather than correct upstream perceptual errors. These results
  suggest that reward signals targeted at perceptual fidelity are needed
  for further progress.

## 2 Design of Reward Functions

We design six reward functions that vary along three complementary axes:
(i) how the final answer is scored: by exact match, by partial credit
for near-correct answers, or not at all; (ii) what intermediate output
structure is rewarded: a single \<think\> block, or multiple stages
spanning image description, reasoning, and self-reflection; and (iii)
whether visual grounding is explicitly rewarded through a dedicated
\<visual\> tag. Each variant thus probes a distinct hypothesis about
which components of the reward signal contribute to long, image-grounded
multimodal reasoning.
Table [1](#S0.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")
summarizes the six variants and their motivating questions; all are
implemented within a single modular scoring framework, with prompts and
pseudo-code provided in Tables  and .

### 2.1 Reward Variants and Implementation

#### $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{format}}`$:

Following the formulation in [DeepSeek-AI (2025)](#bib.bib9), this
variant rewards both the presence of a \<think\> block and exact-match
accuracy on the \<answer\> content. It tests whether the standard
text-domain recipe (i.e. format plus accuracy) is sufficient in the
multimodal setting.

#### $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{outcome}}`$:

This variant rewards only exact-match accuracy on the \<answer\>
content, with no credit for intermediate structure. It isolates the
contribution of the accuracy signal alone and tests whether reasoning
structure can be elicited from the prompt without being explicitly
rewarded, motivated by DAPO [Yu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib10).

#### $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$:

This variant distributes credit across three reasoning stages (\<image
description\>, \<think\>, and \<rethink\>) in addition to the final
answer. Each stage is scored for both multiple-tag presence and the
amount of unique content within the tag, with repetition penalized and
counts smoothed by $`\tanh(\cdot)`$ to prevent reward hacking through
redundant output. Answer correctness is assigned the highest weight in
the composite to maintain factual accuracy. The goal is to test whether
explicitly rewarding multi-stage structure induces longer, more grounded
reasoning trajectories without chain-of-thought supervision.

#### $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{graded}}`$:

Identical to $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$ but replaces
exact-match accuracy with partial credit for near-correct answers (e.g.
prediction of 4 or 6 when ground truth is 5). All other components
(repetition penalties, $`\tanh(\cdot)`$ smoothing, and stage-wise
structural rewards) remain unchanged, to test whether softer accuracy
supervision improves reasoning, e.g. on tasks with dense numeric answer
spaces.

#### $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{grounded}}`$:

This reward focuses on visual grounding by requiring models to integrate
image-specific information into their reasoning. It introduces an
explicit \<visual\> tag inside the \<think\> block and rewards unique
visual descriptions within it, alongside the standard \<think\> and
exact-match accuracy rewards. The same repetition penalty and
$`\tanh(\cdot)`$ smoothing from $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$
also apply. This tests whether explicitly rewarding image-referent
content within the reasoning chain improves visual grounding.

#### $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{process}}`$:

Finally, this configuration removes the accuracy signal entirely. The
model is rewarded only for unique content in the \<image description\>,
\<think\>, and \<rethink\> tags, and receives full credit for producing
any \<answer\> tag regardless of its content. This effectively tests a
lower bound on supervision: whether reasoning behavior can emerge from
structural rewards alone, without any verifiable signal on correctness.

## 3 Dataset Collection and Generation

We use the AlgoPuzzleVQA dataset introduced by [Ghosal et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib4), which comprises challenging visual puzzles designed
to evaluate the capabilities of MLLMs in visual perception, language
comprehension, and algorithmic reasoning. For our experiments, we
selected nine categories from AlgoPuzzleVQA based on their suitability
for conversion into text-only representations. This enables us to
isolate and examine the reasoning bottlenecks of MLLMs without relying
on visual inputs. The selected categories include: Clock, Checker Move,
Maze, Move Box, N-Queens, Number Slide, Tower of Hanoi, Rotting Kiwi,
and Water Jugs. Descriptions and examples are provided in Appendix
[C](#A3 "Appendix C Dataset Details ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design").

#### Training Data Generation.

The dataset we use (AlgoPuzzleVQA) provides only 100 visual samples per
category, which is insufficient for RL training. To scale up the
dataset, we generated additional training samples by adapting the source
code¹¹ 1 https://tinyurl.com/4e5cvevv with necessary modifications.
Specifically, we created 9,000 new training samples across four
categories: Clock, Maze, Move Box, and N-Queens. The Clock category
contains 3,000 samples, while each of the other three categories
includes 2,000 samples. These samples were generated by varying features
such as the clock’s shape, the color and size of the clock hands, the
orientation of the grid, and the positions of the boxes and queens. The
original AlgoPuzzleVQA dataset is retained as the gold test set, and we
ensured that none of the generated training samples overlap with the
test data. Additionally, all training examples are unique and contain no
repetitions.

## 4 Experimental Setup

We design experiments to address three questions. First, to what extent
are the limitations of MLLMs due to challenges in visual perception or
algorithmic reasoning? Second, can different reward functions affect RL
training to unlock long visual reasoning? Third, how well does the
RL-trained model generalize to out-of-distribution classes?

We extract the final answer from the model’s prediction and compare it
with the ground truth using exact match, reporting Accuracy as the
evaluation metric [Toh et al. (2025)](#bib.bib11). Prior work by [Ghosal
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4) evaluated model performance in both
multiple-choice (MCQ) and open-ended settings. In our preliminary
experiments, we found that in the MCQ setting, models often guessed the
correct answer from the provided options, even when their reasoning was
incorrect. This limits the evaluation’s ability to accurately reflect
the model’s true capabilities. Therefore, we evaluate all models
exclusively in the open-ended setting, where no answer options are
provided. We recommend that future work adopt the open-ended format to
accurately assess models’ performance.

### 4.1 Bottleneck Experiments

To analyze the reasoning bottlenecks, we evaluate Claude-3.5-Sonnet and
Claude-3.7-Sonnet in thinking mode across the nine selected categories.
We conduct the experiments in two settings: i) Multimodal (\<image,
question\>): where the image and corresponding question are provided to
the model, ii) Text-only (\<text-representing image, question\>): where
the image is converted into a text description, which is then passed to
the model along with the question. For instance, in the ‘Checker-Move’
category, text descriptions are simple strings where colors are encoded
as characters—‘R’ for red, ‘G’ for green, and ‘-’ for white space. Other
categories follow similarly intuitive character-based representations.

### 4.2 Training Settings

We use the Qwen-2.5-VL-7B (instruct) model for our experiments [Yang et
al. (2024)](#bib.bib12). We train the model using the GRPO [DeepSeek-AI
(2025)](#bib.bib9), a reinforcement learning–based optimization method,
with synthetically generated training samples
(Section [3](#S3 "3 Dataset Collection and Generation ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")).
Building on this setup, we examine how reinforcement learning may
influence the model’s reasoning behavior by constructing two datasets,
each aligned with a complementary hypothesis. First, we hypothesize that
training on challenging, high-difficulty samples can help unlock the
model’s longer reasoning abilities. Since Clock is one of the categories
where the base model struggles most, we construct a training set
consisting of 3,000 samples exclusively from this category, referred to
as Clock-3k. Second, we hypothesize that exposure to a diverse set of
reasoning categories during RL training could encourage the model to
make more generalizable and transferable improvements in reasoning. To
test this, we create a second training set, Diverse-8k, comprising 8,000
samples distributed evenly across four categories: Clock, Maze,
Move-Box, and N-Queens. Further details on hyperparameters are provided
in

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Perception as primary bottleneck

Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.2 Training Settings ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")
reports the performance (accuracy) of Claude-3.5 and Claude-3.7 across
the nine puzzle categories in both the multimodal (MM) and text-only
(TO) settings. Substituting the image with its textual rendering yields
large and consistent gains: on average 26.7 points for Claude-3.5 and
23.6 for Claude-3.7, The effect is most pronounced on categories with
high perceptual demand. On Clock, Claude-3.7 improves from 7% to 83%; on
N-Queens, both models move to near-perfect accuracy. Since the question
and the underlying algorithm are identical across the two conditions,
the only factor that has changed is the difficulty of extracting the
relevant information from the visual input. This empirically supports
the hypothesis that visual perception, rather than algorithmic
reasoning, is the dominant constraint.

#### Qualitative Analysis.

To corroborate this, we inspected the multimodal-condition trajectories
qualitatively, and found further support for this interpretation. Errors
most often originate at the perception stage and then propagate through
reasoning chains that are otherwise internally valid. For example, in
the Clock category, models often fail to read the time from the
positions of the clock hands; in N-Queens, they struggle to correctly
identify the queens’ positions on the board; and in Move Box, they fail
to recognize the spatial positions of the person, box, and target flags.
Table [8](#A3.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Water Jugs: ‣ Appendix C Dataset Details ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")
presents a few representative examples where failures in visual
reasoning lead to incorrect model predictions. Together, these patterns
indicate that the reasoning components of these models are largely
intact for the algorithmic tasks studied here; the bottleneck is in
feeding them correct inputs.

### 5.2 Diverse training data yields broad gains

As illustrated in
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.2 Training Settings ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design"),
training with the diverse dataset yields improvements across all reward
functions compared to training solely on hard samples (Clock-3k). The
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$ reward achieves the highest
average score (15.56), followed closely by the
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{outcome}}`$ reward (15.11). Interestingly, the
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{process}}`$ reward produces unexpected behavior:
in both settings, it surpasses the baseline, and in the Clock-3k
setting, it outperforms several other reward functions. These findings
are consistent with [Shao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib13), which reports that
RL training without a verifiable reward signal can still improve
performance. Nevertheless, certain categories remain challenging: the
model consistently struggles with Clock and Tower of Hanoi tasks, in
line with [Fu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib14), showing that these puzzles are
intrinsically difficult due to abstract temporal and recursive reasoning
demands. We present additional analysis in Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Additional Result Analysis ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design").

### 5.3 Structured rewards elicit visually grounded reasoning, but long reasoning alone does not guarantee accuracy

Our reward function design incentivizes the emergence of long visual
reasoning in the model. Under $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{format}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{process}}`$ rewards, the model produces minimal
intermediate reasoning, often jumping directly to the answer with little
explanation of the image. Both $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{graded}}`$ achieve the intended behavior: as
shown in
Figure [4](#Sx1.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design"),
the model generates structured reasoning steps that reference and
explain different parts of the image, exhibiting the ‘think with image’
phenomenon [Zheng et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib7); [Fan et al.
(2025b)](#bib.bib15). This behavior emerges across both in-domain and
out-of-domain categories, indicating that the reward design and training
strategy successfully unlock MLLMs’ long reasoning capability. However,
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{outcome}}`$’s accuracy is on par with or better
than $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$ in about half of the classes,
especially in out-of-domain settings, suggesting that long reasoning
alone does not guarantee improved accuracy for MLLMs [Shao et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib13).

To evaluate generalization, we measure performance on both in-domain
(seen) and out-of-domain (unseen) classes. As shown in
Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.4 An in-domain/out-of-domain trade-off ‣ 5 Results ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design"),
training on a single-category dataset (Clock-3k) results in limited
generalization: although $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{format}}`$ achieves a
large in-domain gain on the trained category (3 $`\to`$ 37 on Clock),
its out-of-domain accuracy drops well below the no-RL baseline (10.75
$`\to`$ 4.25), and 3/6 variants in total fall below the baseline on OOD.
In contrast, the Diverse-8k dataset substantially improves performance
across nearly all classes, outperforming the baselines in both in-domain
(ID) and out-of-domain (OOD) settings, with 5/6 variants improving OOD
relative to baseline. The pattern across both datasets reveals a
trade-off: variants that achieve the largest in-domain gains tend to
generalize less well, while variants with weaker accuracy signals
($`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{process}}`$, $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{graded}}`$)
show stronger or more balanced OOD performance. This is consistent with
overfitting to the training distribution during RL training, an effect
mitigated but not eliminated by training on a diverse set of categories.
Notably, the model demonstrates emergent reasoning capabilities across
both in-domain and out-of-domain categories (Figures
[14](#A3.F14 "Figure 14 ‣ Water Jugs: ‣ Appendix C Dataset Details ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")
and
[15](#A3.F15 "Figure 15 ‣ Water Jugs: ‣ Appendix C Dataset Details ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")).

To further investigate generalization, we explore the best-performing
reward function for each category with the model trained on the
Diverse-8K dataset. Detailed class-specific results are provided in
Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.4 An in-domain/out-of-domain trade-off ‣ 5 Results ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design").
No single reward function consistently dominates across all classes;
some reward functions excel in certain categories but underperform in
others. Five different reward functions achieved the maximum score in
different categories. For the number slide class, performance dropped
with RL training. This variability highlights the heterogeneity in task
difficulty and model behavior. The variability in reward function
performance arises because puzzle categories differ in their reasoning
demands. We group them into two types:

- •
  Visual dependency (e.g., Move Box, Maze): These puzzles require
  correctly reading spatial layouts, object positions, and grid
  configurations from the image. Rewards such as
  $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$ or
  $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{grounded}}`$ that encourage visual reasoning
  help on these tasks because they push the model to attend to image
  details before reasoning.
- •
  Algorithmic dependency (e.g., Checker Move, Tower of Hanoi): These
  have relatively simple visual inputs but require complex recursive or
  temporal reasoning. Here, encouraging more visual reasoning may
  distract the model from the algorithmic steps and consequently hurt
  performance.

### 5.5 Error Analysis

Despite being our best-performing model, the RL-trained Qwen-7B achieves
only 15.56% accuracy. To understand this limited performance, we
qualitatively analyze the errors and identify four primary causes of
failure. First, the model makes visual understanding errors even after
generating extensive visual reasoning, it incorrectly identifies object
positions or attributes in the input image. See the generated completion
with the $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$ reward in
Figure [4](#Sx1.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design").
Second, the model also fails at algorithmic reasoning, generating
erroneous intermediate computations or decision steps.

by visual puzzle tasks. Our findings suggest that, beyond encouraging
extended reasoning, this task may require reward signals that explicitly

signals represents a promising direction for future research.

## 6 Related Work

Research on the visual reasoning abilities of MLLMs has grown
rapidly [Chen et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21); [Li et al.
(2025b)](#bib.bib22); [Zhang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib23), with benchmarks
spanning abstract visual puzzles [Song et al. (2025)](#bib.bib5),
diagrammatic and symbolic reasoning [Li et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib24), and
algorithmic visual reasoning [Ghosal et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4). Across
these settings, MLLMs consistently struggle to uncover the visual
relations necessary for correct inference. While CoT prompting elicits
reasoning effectively in text-only domains [Wei et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib25); [Kojima et al. (2022)](#bib.bib26), its visual
extensions often produce interpretations weakly grounded in the
image [Dong et al. (2025)](#bib.bib20); [Zhang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib23). Because existing studies evaluate on prediction
accuracy alone, they offer limited insight into whether errors stem from
visual misinterpretation or faulty algorithmic reasoning [Ghosal et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib4); [Bi et al. (2025)](#bib.bib19). We instead perform a
systematic inspection to isolate the source of error.

Reinforcement Learning has recently emerged as a way to improve
reasoning in MLLMs. This is primarily motivated by the advances in
process supervision and verifiable reward modeling from text-only
systems [Lightman et al. (2023)](#bib.bib27); [DeepSeek-AI
(2025)](#bib.bib9). In the visual setting, these ideas are adapted by
using reward signals that verify whether intermediate reasoning steps
are consistent with the image. For instance, vision-language process
reward models [Wang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib28); [Ong et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib29) provide feedback on reasoning steps by checking them
against visual evidence. In parallel, some studies explore multimodal
extensions of R1-style training to elicit longer reasoning trajectories
for diagrams, mathematical problems, and synthetic visual tasks [Yang et
al. (2025b)](#bib.bib30); [Shen et al. (2025)](#bib.bib31); [Zhou et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib1). Complementary approaches such as GRIT [Fan et al.
(2025a)](#bib.bib32) and DeepEyes [Zheng et al. (2025c)](#bib.bib33)
incorporate explicit visual grounding, encouraging models to reference
image regions during multi-step explanations.

However, these approaches rely on intermediate supervision such as
executable tool outputs, bounding boxes, or annotated reasoning traces.
An open question thus remains as to whether structured reasoning can be
induced through reward design. We take a step toward addressing this by
designing reward functions and systematically evaluating their impact on
encouraging long, structured visual reasoning without requiring
additional supervision.

## 7 Conclusion

Our results suggest a pressing need to decouple perception and reasoning
in post-training for multimodal models. Today’s conventional recipes,
largely adapted from text, apply reward at the level of the answer or
the reasoning chain and leave the perceptual stage to improve as a side
effect. We argue this is not sufficient: long, structured reasoning can
faithfully propagate whatever the model thought it saw. The six reward
variants we study begin to establish how supervision can be distributed
across these stages and isolate which signals shape which behaviors. We
see them as a first step toward a broader program in which perception
and reasoning are trained, rewarded, and evaluated as the distinct
competencies they are.

## Limitations

Our primary objective is to investigate whether reward-based RL
fine-tuning can encourage models to perform long, multi-step visual
reasoning required to solve complex puzzles. While alternative
approaches such as SFT could potentially boost performance on a specific
dataset, they cannot guide a model to exhibit step-by-step reasoning
behavior. Another possibility could have been SFT with explicit
reasoning traces. However, for visual puzzle datasets, collecting
high-quality chain-of-thought annotations is extremely challenging and
costly, mainly because the reasoning is often multi-hop, brittle, and
domain-specific. For these reasons, we focus on reward-driven RL
fine-tuning, where the aim was not merely to fit the dataset but to
induce emergent long-horizon reasoning behavior, which we believe is
better aligned with the nature of visual puzzle solving.

Another concern is that the textual representations of images are highly
structured and likely overstate the perception gap. However, the purpose
of this experiment is to establish an upper bound that quantifies the
maximum performance gap attributable to visual perception. The text
representations are kept minimal (e.g., “G-RR” for checker states,
simple grid characters); they encode the same spatial information
present in the image without introducing any reasoning shortcuts.
Moreover, there is no perfect middle ground between an image and
structured text for isolating the visual perception bottleneck from
reasoning capability. Possible alternatives, such as noisy or partially
corrupted text descriptions or caption-based representations generated
by a vision model, each introduce their own biases and assumptions,
making it difficult to draw clean conclusions. Our approach instead
provides a clear and interpretable upper bound that cleanly separates
the perception bottleneck from reasoning capability.

## Appendix A Additional Result Analysis

We perform additional analysis of how reward design affects performance.
Tables
[3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.2 Training Settings ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")
and
[4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 5.4 An in-domain/out-of-domain trade-off ‣ 5 Results ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")
surface several patterns worth examining:
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{outcome}}`$, which uses no reasoning signal, is
often competitive with more complex rewards, and
$`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{process}}`$, which does not grade the answer,
achieves the strongest out-of-domain generalization. We account for
these as follows.

- •
  Why $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{outcome}}`$ is competitive.
  $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{outcome}}`$ leaves the model free to reason
  however it wants, which suffices for some tasks and yields competitive
  accuracy. The difference is qualitative rather than quantitative:
  $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{outcome}}`$ does not produce the extended
  reasoning that $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{structured}}`$ does (Figure 4).
  Accuracy is close, but reasoning quality differs substantially, and
  that quality is what we aim to improve.
- •
  Why $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{process}}`$ generalizes in out-of-domain. A
  strong accuracy signal pushes the RL-trained model toward
  task-specific answers, raising in-domain accuracy at the cost of
  transfer. $`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{process}}`$ sidesteps this by design:
  it rewards only general reasoning behavior (describing the image,
  thinking, rethinking), which carries over to unseen tasks.
- •
  Why some rewards show large ID–OOD gaps. Rewards with high accuracy
  weight ($`\alpha_{a}=0.80`$–$`0.90`$) reinforce task-specific patterns
  and widen the gap. Rewards that spread weight across reasoning
  components ($`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{process}}`$) or relax accuracy
  matching ($`\mathcal{R}_{\textsc{graded}}`$) reward more transferable
  skills, producing more balanced performance

## Appendix B Implementation Details

We experimented with two pre-trained MLLMs Qwen-2.5-VL-3B and
Qwen-2.5-VL-7B. The 3B parameter model exhibited poor performance in our
preliminary experiments and was there for excluded for further
evaluation. We train the 7B model using the GRPO [DeepSeek-AI
(2025)](#bib.bib9) approach with a learning rate of 1e-6 and a batch
size of 128. Parameters for training are presented in Table
[5](#A2.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Appendix B Implementation Details ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design").
To prevent memory overflow, we set both the maximum prompt length and
response length to 2,048 tokens and train the model for 500 steps. The
best intermediate model is saved based on the validation score. The KL
divergence coefficient is set to 0.0 to promote diverse reasoning
strategies. All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA H100 GPUs. We use
the EasyR1 [Zheng et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib3) framework to implement our
RL models.

#### Clock:

This category features analog clocks with only hour and minute hands,
displaying a randomly chosen current time. Each question describes an
event that occurred (or will occur) h hours and m minutes before or
after the current time, and the model must infer the correct time at
which the event took place. An example is shown in Figure
[5](#A3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Appendix C Dataset Details ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design").

![Refer to caption](2601.00215v2/figures/checker_move_0000.jpg)

Figure 6: Question: A checker game is being played on a grid of 4
squares with 1 green and 2 red checkers. Initially, the checkers are
arranged as shown in the starting configuration, with the 3 checkers
occupying 3 squares and one unoccupied square. Green checkers only move
rightward and red checkers only move leftward. Every move is either i) a
slide to the adjacent empty square, or ii) a jump over one position to
an empty square, provided the checker being jumped over is of a
different color. Each square can accommodate a maximum of one checker at
any time. How many moves are required to reach the ending configuration
from the starting configuration following the specified rules?

#### Checker Move:

This puzzle is played on a one-dimensional grid consisting of n cells,
where n–1 cells are initially occupied by either red or green checkers.
The objective is to transform the starting configuration into a
specified ending configuration, subject to the following constraints:
(i) red checkers can only move leftward, and green checkers can only
move rightward; (ii) each move must be either a slide into the adjacent
empty cell or a jump over one checker of the opposite color into an
empty cell; and (iii) each cell may hold at most one checker at any
given time. The task is to compute the minimum number of moves required
to reach the goal configuration from the initial state. Each puzzle is
guaranteed to have at least one valid solution. A sample puzzle is
illustrated in
Figure [6](#A3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Clock: ‣ Appendix C Dataset Details ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design").

![Refer to caption](2601.00215v2/figures/maze_0000.jpg)

Figure 7: Question: This is maze having 13 \* 11 cells. The empty cells
are coloured white and the obstacle cells are coloured black. From an
empty cell, you can only move up, down, left, or right to another
adjacent empty cell. You cannot move diagonally between two empty cells
and cannot step into a cell with an obstacle. The entry cell of the maze

between the entrance and exit, where you need to go through the least
number of empty cells and you need to make the least number of left and
right turns. What is the total number of left turns do you need to make
in this optimal path?

#### Maze:

This is a path-finding puzzle with predefined start and end positions.
White cells indicate valid paths, while black cells represent walls. The
model must answer one of two types of questions: (i) How many left,
right, or total turns are required in the optimal path? or (ii) How many
cells must be visited in the optimal path, including the entrance and
exit? An example maze and corresponding question are shown in
Figure [7](#A3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Checker Move: ‣ Appendix C Dataset Details ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design").

#### Move Box:

This puzzle involves a game with a person, a box, and a target location,
all placed on a grid with obstacles. The objective is to move the box to
the target location using the minimum number of pushes. This puzzle can
be solved using the Breadth-First Search (BFS) technique. An example

![Refer to caption](2601.00215v2/figures/n_queens_0043.jpg)

Figure 9: Question: You are given a 9 \* 9 chessboard. The Manhattan
distance between two squares in a chessboard is equal to the minimal
number of orthogonal King moves between these squares on the otherwise

#### N-Queens:

This puzzle is based on the classic chess problem where the objective is
to place $`N`$ queens on an $`N\times N`$ chessboard such that no two
queens share the same row, column, or diagonal. In this variation,
$`N-2`$ queens are already placed on the board. The question includes
what the Manhattan distance (i.e., the number of unique squares on the
board) is between the two remaining queens when they are placed
correctly? The solution can be achieved backtracking algorithm.
Figure [9](#A3.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Move Box: ‣ Appendix C Dataset Details ‣ Disentangling Perception and Reasoning in Multimodal LLMsvia Reward Design")
illustrates an n-queens puzzle.

Figure 10: Question: The board shown in the image is a sliding puzzle of

#### Number Slide:

This puzzle consists of an $`N*N`$ board with numbered tiles from $`1`$
to $`(N*N)-1`$ and one empty position. The numbers are arranged
randomly, and the puzzle involves answering one of the following types
of questions: i) How many unique board positions can be reached after
performing $`n`$ moves? ii) What is the minimum or maximum sum that can
be achieved in a particular row or column after performing $`n`$ moves?
iii) Perform $`n`$ moves where the empty position can be moved in (up,
down, left, right) ways. Now, what is the minimum or maximum sum that
can be achieved in a particular row or column that has the empty
position?

#### Tower of Hanoi:

stack or an empty rod, iii) No disk may be placed on top of a smaller
disk. It is guaranteed that an optimal solution exists. An example

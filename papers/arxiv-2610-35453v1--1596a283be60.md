# Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence

Haoyuan Li Email: <h.y.li@tue.nl> Affiliation: Eindhoven University of
Technology, Eindhoven, The Netherlands    Mathias Funk
Email: <M.Funk@tue.nl> Affiliation: Eindhoven University of Technology,

###### Abstract

Clinical expertise is inherently distributed: it resides not in any
single dataset or institution but in the accumulated reasoning
experiences of thousands of clinicians navigating diverse patient
populations, resource constraints, and evolving treatment protocols. Yet
current approaches to scaling medical AI, from centralized large
language models to federated learning, remain anchored to a data-centric
paradigm that treats knowledge as static parameters to be aggregated. In
this Perspective, we argue for a fundamental shift from _data
aggregation_ to _experience aggregation_. We propose Decentralized
Agentic Systems (DAS), a framework in which distributed AI agents
operating across healthcare institutions synthesize collective clinical
intelligence by sharing abstracted reasoning trajectories rather than
raw data or model parameters. Unlike federated learning, which averages
numerical gradients across homogeneous model architectures, DAS
exchanges structured records of clinical reasoning, specifically the
sequence of observations, hypotheses, tool invocations, and outcome
feedback that constitute diagnostic experience. We examine the
architectural principles, deployment considerations, governance
requirements, and critical open challenges of this framework, laying the
groundwork for a collaborative medical AI ecosystem where distributed
expertise autonomously adapts to the complexities of longitudinal
patient care.

###### keywords

Agentic AI, Decentralized Learning, Digital Healthcare

## 1 Introduction

Medicine is, at its core, a distributed enterprise. The expertise
required to manage a complex patient rarely resides in a single
clinician, department, or institution. A rural primary care physician
develops pattern recognition for atypical presentations under resource
constraints that an academic specialist never encounters; a community
cardiologist accumulates experience with medication titration across
diverse socioeconomic contexts; a home health monitoring system learns
to distinguish clinically significant decompensation from benign
fluctuations in daily vital signs. This distributed expertise, forged
through thousands of diagnostic encounters across heterogeneous clinical
environments, represents the most valuable and least utilized knowledge
asset in healthcare.

The rapid development of Large Language Models (LLMs) has contributed to
growing interest in agentic Artificial Intelligence (AI), which seeks to
extend AI systems beyond static information retrieval toward perception,
reasoning, and action in dynamic environments. Prior work has reported
promising results in complex domains such as software engineering,
mathematical theorem proving, and scientific hypothesis generation [Yang
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib2); [Baba et al. (2025)](#bib.bib3); [Gottweis et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib1); [Lange et al. (2025)](#bib.bib4). In healthcare,
where clinical reasoning demands synthesizing heterogeneous evidence
under uncertainty, agentic AI promises to augment human expertise by
orchestrating knowledge-intensive diagnostic and therapeutic workflows.

Early applications of agentic AI in medicine established foundational
approaches to algorithmic clinical reasoning. Systems such as those in
[Ferber et al. (2025)](#bib.bib5); [Tang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib6);
[Rose et al. (2025)](#bib.bib7); [Hao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib8) employ
LLMs as centralized orchestrators, using modular decomposition to
partition clinical queries into sub-tasks spanning evidence retrieval,
differential diagnosis, and treatment recommendation. While effective
for localized decision support, these systems operate as isolated
instances constrained by static reasoning templates and
single-institution deployment. Recent work has advanced toward
hierarchical multi-agent systems enabling dynamic collaboration for
symptom assessment, diagnostic interpretation, and treatment
recommendation, simulating multi-turn clinical consultations [Almansoori
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib9); [Zhang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib10); [Qin et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib11); [Kim et al. (2024)](#bib.bib12); [Li et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib13).

However, a fundamental architectural tension pervades current systems:
clinical expertise is inherently distributed across institutions,
specialties, and care settings, yet agentic AI remains stubbornly
centralized. This tension is deeper than the well-known challenges of
data fragmentation and privacy regulation, though it encompasses them.
We identify three dimensions of this mismatch, each reflecting not
merely a data problem but an _expertise_ problem.

#### Expertise fragmentation problem.

The conventional framing of healthcare data silos emphasizes regulatory
barriers to data sharing. Though real, this framing obscures a more
fundamental issue: even removing regulatory barriers would not pool
clinical _expertise_. The diagnostic insight a rural emergency physician
develops managing acute coronary syndrome without catheterization access
is not encoded in the EHR; it resides in the physician’s reasoning
process and outcomes observed. A centralized system trained on pooled
records acquires statistical patterns but not the situated reasoning
constituting clinical mastery. The challenge is therefore not merely to
share data across institutions but to share the _experience_ of
navigating clinical complexity.

#### Scaling limits of static knowledge.

The advancement of LLMs has been predicated on access to ever-larger
corpora of human-generated text, yet recent analyses suggest this
paradigm is approaching asymptotic limits as high-quality medical data
becomes increasingly scarce [Villalobos et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21);
[Muennighoff et al. (2023)](#bib.bib22). In healthcare, where rare
conditions, evolving treatment protocols, and longitudinal patient
trajectories demand continuous learning, reliance on static datasets
creates systems that are perpetually outdated. More critically, static
training data captures _declarative_ medical knowledge such as facts,
guidelines, and associations, but not the _procedural_ knowledge of how
to apply it under the uncertainty and time pressure of clinical
practice.

#### Sovereignty-utility tension.

Autonomous agents handling Protected Health Information (PHI) must
comply with HIPAA, IVDR, and GDPR [Neupane et al. (2025)](#bib.bib19);
[Müller et al. (2022)](#bib.bib20); [Voigt and Von dem Bussche
(2017)](#bib.bib44), yet compliance is only one dimension of a broader
sovereignty challenge. Healthcare institutions increasingly assert
governance over not only their data but their institutional knowledge
(i.e., clinical protocols, reasoning conventions, and accumulated
expertise) as competitive and intellectual assets. Current architectures
offer institutions a binary choice: contribute data to centralized
systems or remain isolated. What is needed is an architecture that
enables institutions to _contribute to and benefit from collective
intelligence while retaining sovereignty over their clinical assets_,
sharing insights without surrendering the experiences from which those
insights derive.

These challenges are compounded by the computational realities of
deployment. State-of-the-art agents rely on multi-model toolchains:
EchoAgent [Daghyani et al. (2025)](#bib.bib15) orchestrates
domain-specific vision models for echocardiogram interpretation, while
Echo-CoPilot [Heidari et al. (2025)](#bib.bib16) leverages EchoPrime
[Vukadinovic et al. (2025)](#bib.bib18) and PanEcho [Holste et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib17) for multi-view cardiac analysis. Centralizing such
systems incurs substantial inference costs [Zhang et al.
(2025a)](#bib.bib14), while naive distribution faces severe
infrastructure heterogeneity.

Such converging pressures cannot be addressed by existing distributed
learning paradigms. Federated Learning (FL), the prevailing
privacy-preserving approach, aggregates model parameters through
gradient averaging, operating at the wrong level of abstraction for
clinical expertise. Gradients encode statistical correlations rather
than reasoning strategies, require architectural homogeneity, and
provide no mechanism for transferring the procedural knowledge
characterizing clinical mastery.

In this Perspective, we propose Decentralized Agentic Systems (DAS) as
an alternative paradigm: a framework wherein distributed agents
operating across healthcare institutions synthesize collective clinical
intelligence through the sharing of abstracted reasoning trajectories,
while concurrently self-evolving through the reciprocal incorporation of
network-wide experiential knowledge into local reasoning capabilities.
We formalize the architectural principles underlying DAS
(Section [3](#S3 "3 Architectural Foundations of Decentralized Agentic Systems ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence")),
examine the deployment landscape and governance requirements for
real-world adoption
(Sections [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Deployment Landscape across Healthcare Contexts ‣ 3 Architectural Foundations of Decentralized Agentic Systems ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence")
and [4](#S4 "4 Governance, Regulation, and Deployment Realities ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence")),
and articulate the critical open challenges
(Section [5](#S5 "5 Technical Challenges and Open Research Directions ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence")).

## 2 Learning from Experience Beyond Human Data

The transition toward decentralized agentic architectures represents a
necessary evolution aligned with fundamental shifts in how AI acquires
knowledge. LLMs’ progress has been predicated on scaling human-curated
datasets, a paradigm approaching asymptotic limits as high-quality
clinical data grows scarce [Villalobos et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21);
[Muennighoff et al. (2023)](#bib.bib22). In healthcare, this constraint
is particularly acute: medical knowledge is continuously generated
through diagnostic encounters, treatment adaptations, and clinical
judgments across thousands of institutions, accessible only in part to
any centralized system. We argue that the next frontier lies in enabling
AI agents to learn directly from distributed clinical experiences,
transforming ephemeral diagnostic interactions into durable, shareable
intelligence.

Experience differs fundamentally from data. Data represents static
records, laboratory values, imaging findings, documented diagnoses.
Experience, by contrast, encompasses the dynamic process through which
an agent perceives clinical signals, reasons about their significance
under uncertainty, selects among possible interventions, and observes
outcomes [Silver and Sutton (2025)](#bib.bib45). Consider the difference
between a dataset of electrocardiograms labeled with diagnoses and the
experience of an agent that detects a subtle ST-elevation pattern,
retrieves relevant patient history, considers confounding factors, and
refines its interpretation based on downstream clinical confirmation.
The former provides declarative knowledge, facts about the world; the
latter generates procedural knowledge, that is, how to navigate clinical
reasoning itself.

Each institutional agent continuously generates experiential feedback
through clinical interactions. A cardiology-focused agent may develop
nuanced expertise in distinguishing Takotsubo cardiomyopathy from acute
coronary syndrome in post-menopausal women; a primary care agent may
learn to recognize atypical thyroid dysfunction in elderly populations.
Such experiences represent the contextual, procedural knowledge
unavailable in static datasets. The challenge lies in synthesizing this
distributed intelligence into collective clinical wisdom without
violating data sovereignty constraints.

To address this, we propose DAS as a framework for agent-centric insight
aggregation. Unlike FL [McMahan et al. (2017)](#bib.bib23), which
aggregates numerical model parameters through gradient averaging, DAS
operates at a higher abstraction level: the sharing of semantic
trajectories. We define a semantic trajectory as a structured record
comprising an agent’s reasoning path: observations, hypotheses, tool
invocations, and inferential steps, together with environmental feedback
that validated or refuted its conclusions. Exchanging these traces
rather than raw data or model weights enables knowledge transfer that is
both privacy-conscious and semantically meaningful, forming the
substrate through which individual agentic nodes continuously
self-evolve: each agent refines its local reasoning capabilities by
assimilating experiential knowledge distilled from across the
distributed network.

#### Why existing federated approaches are insufficient.

Gradient-based approaches assume homogeneity in model architectures,
data distributions, and task definitions, while assumptions rarely hold
across diverse healthcare settings. Semantic trajectories abstract away
these implementation details to capture the core reasoning process. An
insight such as “in patients presenting with chest pain and elevated
troponin but atypical ECG findings, consider requesting strain
echocardiography before concluding myocardial infarction” transfers
meaningfully across institutional contexts regardless of underlying
architectures or data distributions.

DAS is distinct from FL variants that partially address the limitations
of FedAvg [McMahan et al. (2017)](#bib.bib23). Federated distillation
[Lin et al. (2020)](#bib.bib33); [He et al. (2020)](#bib.bib34)
exchanges soft predictions or logits, reducing architectural coupling.
Split learning [Gupta and Raskar (2018)](#bib.bib35) partitions
computation across clients and servers. Personalized FL [T Dinh et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib36); [Fallah et al. (2020)](#bib.bib37) accommodates
heterogeneous data distributions through local adaptation. Each
addresses a specific limitation of gradient-based aggregation, yet all
share a common constraint: _they operate at the level of model
parameters or outputs, not at the level of reasoning_.

summarizes the key distinctions. The critical difference is

#### Relationship to adjacent paradigms.

DAS relates to, but differs from, several active research directions.
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [Lewis et al. (2020)](#bib.bib38)
enhances LLM reasoning by retrieving relevant documents; DAS can be
viewed as a federated extension of RAG where the retrieval corpus
consists of validated reasoning trajectories from distributed agents.
Chain-of-thought distillation [Ho et al. (2023)](#bib.bib39); [Magister
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib40) transfers reasoning via training on reasoning
traces; DAS extends this from a teacher-student relationship to a
network of peer agents sharing experiential knowledge. Experience replay
in multi-agent reinforcement learning [Foerster et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib41) enables learning from stored interactions; DAS
adapts this to healthcare where experiences comprise clinical reasoning
under real-world constraints.

The aggregation of validated semantic trajectories enables what we term
collective self-evolution: the emergence of shared diagnostic
intelligence exceeding any individual agent’s capabilities. This
evolution occurs not through centralized retraining on pooled data but
through continuous refinement of reasoning strategies based on
cross-institutional experience. When an agent successfully navigates a
novel presentation to correct a diagnosis, that trajectory becomes
available network-wide, enriching the collective repertoire of clinical
problem-solving once abstracted and validated.

## 3 Architectural Foundations of Decentralized Agentic Systems

We now formalize the DAS architecture. The central challenge is not
merely distributing computation but enabling meaningful knowledge
synthesis across agents operating in heterogeneous clinical contexts:
different patient populations, institutional protocols, specialty
focuses, and infrastructure capabilities. As illustrated in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Architectural Foundations of Decentralized Agentic Systems ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence"),
DAS addresses this through three architectural layers: distributed
agentic nodes that generate clinical experiences locally, a semantic
abstraction layer that transforms heterogeneous experiences into
transferable representations, and a collective intelligence layer that
aggregates and redistributes insights across the agent network.

Figure 1: Architecture of Decentralized Agentic Systems (DAS). The
framework comprises distributed agentic nodes operating within local
clinical contexts, connected through a semantic abstraction layer that
transforms raw experiences into standardized reasoning trajectories. The
collective intelligence layer aggregates these trajectories to
synthesize shared clinical knowledge, which is subsequently distributed
back to enhance individual agent capabilities.

### 3.1 Distributed Agentic Nodes

The foundational unit of DAS is the distributed agentic node, an
autonomous agent tailored to local clinical requirements. Formally, we
define node $`\mathcal{A}_{k}`$ for institution or deployment context
$`k\in\{1,\ldots,N\}`$ as a tuple:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{A}_{k}=\langle\mathcal{M}_{k},\mathcal{T}_{k},\mathcal{E}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k}\rangle
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{M}_{k}`$ denotes the local reasoning backbone (an LLM
adapted to the node’s clinical specialty and institutional knowledge),
$`\mathcal{T}_{k}`$ represents the executable tool suite,
$`\mathcal{E}_{k}`$ is the local experience buffer storing interaction
trajectories, and $`\mathcal{C}_{k}`$ encodes local context including
patient population characteristics, institutional protocols, and
regulatory constraints.

Each agentic node operates through an iterative
perception-reasoning-action cycle. At each step $`t`$, the reasoning
backbone $`\mathcal{M}_{k}`$ receives a clinical observation $`o_{t}`$,
which may include patient symptoms, diagnostic results, or queries from
clinical staff. It generates a reasoning trace $`r_{t}`$ explicating its
inferential process and selects an action $`a_{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{k}`$
for execution. The environment returns feedback $`f_{t}`$, comprising
tool outputs, downstream clinical outcomes, or expert corrections. This
interaction constitutes an experience tuple:

|     |                                                        |     |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       e_{t}^{k}=\langle o_{t},r_{t},a_{t},f_{t},c_{t}\rangle
       ```                                                     |     | (2) |

where $`c_{t}`$ captures relevant contextual metadata, and experiences
accumulate as an ordered sequence:

|     |                                                          |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{E}_{k}=\{e_{1}^{k},e_{2}^{k},\ldots,e_{T}^{k}\}
       ```                                                       |     | (3) |

Raw experiences remain locally stored, preserving data sovereignty and
enabling compliance with institutional data governance policies.

### 3.2 Semantic Abstraction Layer

As experiences accumulate across distributed nodes, heterogeneity in
clinical contexts, observation modalities, and action semantics
necessitates transformation before knowledge can be meaningfully shared.
We introduce the Semantic Abstraction Layer, denoted $`\Phi`$,
implementing a two-stage pipeline: experience distillation and
trajectory standardization.

#### Experience Distillation.

Raw interaction trajectories contain substantial redundancy: routine
observations, exploratory actions that yielded no insight, and noise
from environmental stochasticity. The distillation function
$`\phi_{\text{dist}}:\mathcal{E}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\prime}`$
filters experiences to retain those carrying significant epistemic
value:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\prime}=\phi_{\text{dist}}(\mathcal{E}_{k})=\{e_{t}^{k}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\mid\mathcal{U}(e_{t}^{k};\mathcal{C}_{k})>\delta_{k}\}
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\mathcal{U}(\cdot)`$ quantifies epistemic value across
dimensions of task success, novelty, error correction, and
generalization likelihood. The threshold $`\delta_{k}`$ may be
context-dependent, reflecting institutional priorities and resource
constraints.

The design of $`\mathcal{U}(\cdot)`$ is an open challenge. We envision
hybrid approaches combining automated metrics (e.g., downstream outcome
verification) with expert validation, where clinicians flag experiences
of particular educational value, analogous to case-based learning in
medical education.

#### Trajectory Standardization.

Following distillation, retained experiences are transformed into a
canonical representation enabling cross-context learning. The
standardization function
$`\phi_{\text{std}}:\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{k}`$
maps heterogeneous local experiences into semantic trajectories:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{S}_{k}=\phi_{\text{std}}(\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\prime})=\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{E}_{k}^{\prime}|},\quad\sigma_{i}=(o_{i}^{\text{abs}},r_{i}^{\text{abs}},a_{i}^{\text{abs}},f_{i}^{\text{abs}})
``` |  | (5) |

where each component is abstracted into a domain-general representation.
Observations are mapped to standardized clinical concepts (e.g.,
SNOMED-CT or ICD), reasoning traces are structured according to a common
inferential schema, actions are categorized by functional type rather
than implementation-specific tool calls, and feedback is encoded as
outcome categories with confidence indicators. This standardization
enables cross-domain semantic alignment while removing
institution-specific identifiers and abstracting patient-level details
into population-level patterns.

### 3.3 Collective Intelligence through Federated Trajectory Learning

The collective intelligence layer synthesizes distributed knowledge
through Federated Trajectory Learning, aggregating standardized semantic
trajectories that preserve semantic content rather than averaging model
parameters.

Let $`\mathcal{S}=\{\mathcal{S}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{N}\}`$ denote
the global trajectory repository, where each $`\mathcal{S}_{k}`$
represents standardized trajectories contributed by node
$`\mathcal{A}_{k}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{S}_{k}=\Phi(\mathcal{E}_{k})=\phi_{\text{std}}(\phi_{\text{dist}}(\mathcal{E}_{k}))
``` |  | (6) |

The collective intelligence mechanism $`\mathcal{G}`$ updates a shared
knowledge representation $`\theta_{\text{global}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\theta_{\text{global}}^{(t+1)}=\mathcal{G}\left(\theta_{\text{global}}^{(t)},\{\mathcal{S}_{k}^{(t)}\}_{k=1}^{N}\right)
``` |  | (7) |

$`\mathcal{G}`$ may be instantiated through trajectory-derived insight
aggregation, retrieval-augmented reasoning, clinical knowledge graph
construction, or distillation into updated reasoning templates,
accommodating diverse strategies without prescribing a single
formulation. This architecture supports asynchronous learning: nodes
contribute trajectories continuously, $`\theta_{\text{global}}`$ is
refined without synchronized participation, and updated knowledge
enriches individual $`\mathcal{M}_{k}`$ with network-wide insights.

### 3.4 Self-Evolution of Distributed Agentic Nodes

The emergence of collective intelligence creates a reciprocal feedback
loop through which individual agentic nodes continuously self-evolve.
Formally, node $`\mathcal{A}_{k}`$ updates its local reasoning backbone
by incorporating knowledge distilled from the global trajectory
repository:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{M}_{k}^{(t+1)}=\mathcal{F}\!\left(\mathcal{M}_{k}^{(t)},\;\mathcal{R}\!\left(\theta_{\text{global}}^{(t)},\mathcal{C}_{k}\right)\right),
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\mathcal{R}(\cdot)`$ is a context-conditioned retrieval function
that selects trajectories from $`\theta_{\text{global}}`$ relevant to
the node’s local clinical context $`\mathcal{C}_{k}`$, and
$`\mathcal{F}(\cdot)`$ integrates retrieved knowledge into existing
reasoning capabilities through in-context augmentation [Zhao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib48), fine-tuning via reinforcement learning from human
or AI feedback (e.g., RLHF [Christiano et al. (2017)](#bib.bib46);
[Ouyang et al. (2022)](#bib.bib47), DPO [Rafailov et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib49)), or reasoning template refinement.

This self-evolution operates along two complementary axes. Breadth
expansion occurs as nodes assimilate reasoning trajectories from
clinical domains beyond their local experience: a primary care agent
encountering a rare cardiac presentation can retrieve and internalize
diagnostic pathways developed by specialized cardiology agents elsewhere
in the network. Depth refinement occurs as nodes receive corrective
trajectories that challenge or supersede their existing reasoning
patterns, enabling continuous calibration of diagnostic confidence and
decision thresholds. As a result, each node converges toward a locally
specialized yet globally informed reasoning profile, one that reflects
both its accumulated clinical experience and the collective mastery of
the distributed network.

### 3.5 Deployment Landscape across Healthcare Contexts

Figure 2: Deployment landscape of the DAS framework across healthcare

institutional agents coordinating departmental expertise, and
network-level aggregation enabling multi-center collaborative

The DAS framework supports deployment at multiple scales of clinical
organization, as illustrated in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.5 Deployment Landscape across Healthcare Contexts ‣ 3 Architectural Foundations of Decentralized Agentic Systems ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence").

At the point-of-care level, agentic nodes operate within clinical
workflows (i.e., emergency department triage, outpatient consultation,
and surgical planning) providing real-time decision support and
accumulating fine-grained experiences from direct clinical interactions.

At the institutional level, DAS enables coordination across departments
and specialties within a single healthcare organization. A hospital-wide
deployment might include specialized agents for cardiology, oncology,
primary care, and radiology, each developing domain-specific expertise
while contributing to shared institutional knowledge, supporting quality
improvement and dissemination of best practices.

At the network level, DAS enables secure multi-institutional
collaboration among federations of hospitals, academic medical centers,
and research institutions while maintaining data sovereignty. This scale
is particularly valuable for rare disease diagnosis, where individual
institutions encounter insufficient case volumes, and for multi-center
clinical research, where distributed agents can identify eligible
patients and support protocol adherence.

Across all scales, the semantic abstraction layer ensures knowledge
sharing occurs at the level of clinical reasoning rather than raw
patient data. We illustrate this through two clinical scenarios: heart
failure management across distributed care settings

## 4 Governance, Regulation, and Deployment Realities

The technical architecture of DAS is necessary but insufficient for
real-world adoption. Healthcare AI systems operate within complex
governance ecosystems involving institutional policies, regulatory
frameworks, professional liability structures, and patient trust
relationships. We examine the non-technical dimensions that will
determine whether decentralized agentic systems can transition from
architectural concept to deployed clinical infrastructure.

### 4.1 Regulatory Considerations

Agentic AI systems in healthcare face an evolving regulatory landscape.
In the United States, the FDA’s framework for Software as a Medical
Device (SaMD) was designed for static algorithms with well-defined
inputs and outputs, a paradigm poorly suited to agentic systems that
reason dynamically and adapt over time [Mühlberger and others
(2024)](#bib.bib42). Key open questions include whether each
institutional agent requires independent regulatory clearance or whether
DAS can be evaluated as a system; how continuous learning from
trajectory updates interacts with the FDA’s predetermined change control
plan requirements; and what validation standards apply to collectively
derived reasoning patterns emerging from network-level aggregation
rather than explicit design.

In the EU, the AI Act classifies most clinical decision-support systems
as high-risk, requiring transparency, human oversight, and robustness
testing. DAS creates novel compliance challenges: when reasoning
reflects contributions from agents across multiple member states, which
jurisdiction governs? These questions must be confronted proactively
with regulatory bodies.

### 4.2 Institutional Governance and Trust Models

Beyond formal regulation, DAS requires governance structures addressing
participation, liability, and reciprocity across three dimensions.

#### Trajectory curation and quality control.

Who decides which reasoning trajectories enter the collective knowledge
base? Uncurated aggregation risks knowledge degradation (see
Section [5.2](#S5.SS2 "5.2 Hallucination Propagation and Byzantine Resilience ‣ 5 Technical Challenges and Open Research Directions ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence")),
while overly restrictive curation may stifle the diversity that makes
collective intelligence valuable. We envision hybrid models combining
automated quality metrics (outcome validation, internal consistency,
novelty assessment) with periodic expert review boards analogous to
clinical peer review committees.

#### Liability allocation.

When a recommendation informed by cross-institutional trajectories leads
to an adverse outcome, liability is nontrivial. Current frameworks
struggle with distributed AI even in non-agentic contexts [Price et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib43). DAS liability models might draw on multi-site
clinical guideline precedent, where contributing institutions share
collective responsibility for quality while individual providers retain
responsibility for application to specific patients.

#### Participation incentives and fairness.

Institutions must perceive sufficient benefit to justify participation
costs, including computational resources, clinical time for quality
validation, and governance compliance overhead. Free-rider problems,
where institutions consume collective knowledge without contributing
proportionally, can erode trust. Contribution-weighted access to
collective intelligence and transparent accounting of trajectory
utilization may help align incentives. Smaller or less-resourced
institutions should not be systematically disadvantaged; enabling rural
and community hospitals to benefit from academic medical center
expertise is a primary motivation for DAS.

### 4.3 Integration with Clinical Infrastructure

Practical deployment requires integration with EHR platforms.
Interoperability standards such as HL7 FHIR provide structured
interfaces for clinical observations and decision support. However,
institutional variation in EHR configuration, documentation practices,
and workflow integration means the *operational context* surrounding
each agent differs substantially, affecting experience quality and
applicability of received knowledge.

We advocate beginning with standardized tasks: clinical risk scoring,
medication interaction checking, or diagnostic workup recommendations,
before extending to complex reasoning, enabling iterative validation of
trajectory sharing while building institutional trust through
demonstrated value.

## 5 Technical Challenges and Open Research Directions

The architecture of
Section [3](#S3 "3 Architectural Foundations of Decentralized Agentic Systems ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence")
provides foundational mechanisms for decentralized agentic
collaboration, yet several substantial technical challenges remain
unresolved, which we examine as follows.

### 5.1 Experience Heterogeneity across Clinical Contexts

Decentralized healthcare systems must reconcile experiences generated
across radically different clinical environments. A diagnostic agent in
a tertiary academic medical center encounters different patient
populations, diagnostic modalities, and institutional protocols than one
in a rural primary care clinic. This heterogeneity manifests across
three dimensions.

Statistical heterogeneity arises from differences in patient
demographics and disease prevalence. An agent serving a pediatric
oncology unit accumulates experiences with childhood malignancies of
limited relevance to one in a geriatric care facility, and direct
aggregation across such disparate populations risks distributional
biases that degrade diagnostic accuracy.

Semantic heterogeneity emerges from differences in clinical vocabulary,
documentation practices, and reasoning conventions. The same finding may
be described differently by cardiologists and internists; the same
conclusion may be reached through different inferential pathways
depending on institutional culture. Standardization efforts (as in
Equation [5](#S3.E5 "In Trajectory Standardization. ‣ 3.2 Semantic Abstraction Layer ‣ 3 Architectural Foundations of Decentralized Agentic Systems ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence"))
must navigate the tension between abstraction sufficient for
cross-context transfer and preservation of clinically meaningful nuance.

Operational heterogeneity reflects differences in available tools,
computational resources, and workflow integration. An agent with access
to advanced imaging analysis generates fundamentally different
trajectories than one limited to laboratory values and clinical notes.

The Semantic Abstraction Layer addresses heterogeneity through
trajectory standardization and utility-weighted aggregation. However,
determining abstraction levels that enable transfer while preserving
clinical validity remains empirically open, and the contribution weights
$`w_{k}`$ must reflect source-to-target context relevance, requiring
either explicit clinical metadata or learned representations of
transferability.

### 5.2 Hallucination Propagation and Byzantine Resilience

Large language models exhibit well-documented tendencies toward
hallucination [Zhang et al. (2025c)](#bib.bib30); [Huang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib31); [Lin et al. (2022)](#bib.bib32). In agentic
systems, this vulnerability extends to entire reasoning trajectories: an
agent may construct internally coherent diagnostic pathways based on
fabricated associations or nonexistent evidence, and when such
trajectories enter the sharing mechanism they risk corrupting collective
knowledge network-wide.

This represents a novel form of the classical Byzantine failure problem
[Lamport et al. (2019)](#bib.bib24); [Blanchard et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib25). Agentic hallucination differs critically:
hallucinated trajectories may be superficially plausible, evading
detection mechanisms designed for obviously erroneous outputs;
hallucination propensity may correlate across agents sharing similar
architectures, violating independence assumptions underlying Byzantine
protocols; and clinical consequences, including incorrect diagnoses and
inappropriate treatments, may be severe and difficult to reverse.

The utility threshold in experience distillation
(Equation [4](#S3.E4 "In Experience Distillation. ‣ 3.2 Semantic Abstraction Layer ‣ 3 Architectural Foundations of Decentralized Agentic Systems ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence"))
offers partial mitigation but presupposes reliable utility assessment
when ground truth outcomes may be delayed. More robust approaches might
incorporate multi-agent verification or external validation against
established clinical knowledge bases. The governance structures in
Section [4](#S4 "4 Governance, Regulation, and Deployment Realities ‣ Beyond Federated Learning: Decentralized Agentic Systems for Self-Evolving Clinical Intelligence"),
particularly expert trajectory curation, provide a complementary
non-technical defense. The optimal balance between automated and
human-mediated quality control remains context-dependent, with
high-stakes domains such as oncology warranting more stringent
validation.

### 5.3 Semantic Privacy Leakage from Abstracted Trajectories

A foundational premise of decentralized learning is that sharing derived
representations preserves privacy. However, gradient inversion attacks
have demonstrated that model updates can leak substantial information
about training data [Fredrikson et al. (2015)](#bib.bib26); [Zhu et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib27), enabling reconstruction of sensitive clinical
images from federated exchanges [Hitaj et al. (2017)](#bib.bib28); [Lu
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib29).

This vulnerability extends to trajectory sharing. A reasoning path
comprising specific tool invocations, diagnostic queries, and decision
sequences constitutes a behavioral fingerprint: even without explicit
patient identifiers, trajectories querying rare disease databases or
following condition-specific diagnostic pathways may reveal diagnoses,
genetic predispositions, or lifestyle factors patients expect to remain
confidential.

The standardization function provides partial protection, but the
tension between utility and privacy persists: sufficiently abstracted
trajectories may lose the clinical specificity that makes them valuable.
Developing formal privacy guarantees, potentially drawing on
differential privacy frameworks adapted to structured reasoning
representations, is an important open challenge. The appropriate
tradeoff likely varies across contexts, suggesting the need for
configurable mechanisms rather than uniform solutions.

### 5.4 Coordination Complexity in Asynchronous Multi-Agent Systems

DAS supports asynchronous operation, allowing agents to contribute
trajectories and receive updates without synchronized participation.
This flexibility is essential across institutions with different
schedules and connectivity constraints, but introduces challenges for
coherent collective intelligence.

Temporal inconsistency arises when agents operate on different knowledge
versions. Recently updated agents generate experiences reflecting
current collective understanding, while those with stale knowledge
produce trajectories based on outdated patterns, requiring careful
versioning and potentially discounting stale inputs.

Feedback delays complicate credit assignment. Clinical outcomes
validating or refuting diagnostic reasoning may emerge weeks or months
after the initial interaction, by which point collective knowledge may
have evolved substantially, obscuring attribution of learning signal to
specific contributions.

Coordination overhead scales with network size. As participating agents
grow, mechanisms for trajectory aggregation and knowledge distribution
must remain computationally tractable, and ensuring asynchronous updates
do not introduce inconsistencies affecting patient care requires careful
protocol design and potentially formal verification.

### 5.5 Evaluating Collective Intelligence

A challenge crosscutting all others is the absence of established
evaluation methodologies. Conventional metrics such as accuracy,
sensitivity, and specificity apply to individual agents but do not
capture the value added by trajectory sharing. We identify three
dimensions future work must operationalize.

#### Marginal improvement from collective knowledge.

Does an agent with access to collective trajectories outperform the same
agent in isolation? This requires controlled comparisons measuring
diagnostic accuracy and reasoning quality: does the agent consider a
broader differential and reach correct diagnoses more efficiently?

#### Cross-context transfer effectiveness.

Do trajectories from one context (e.g., academic cardiology) improve
reasoning in another (e.g., rural primary care)? Transfer effectiveness
likely varies across domains, and characterizing this landscape is
essential for deployment decisions.

#### Longitudinal learning dynamics.

Does collective intelligence improve as the trajectory repository grows,
or do saturation and diminishing returns emerge? Longitudinal evaluation
requires sustained deployment in realistic clinical environments, a
significant but essential validation step.

We recommend that early DAS implementations incorporate prospective
evaluation protocols from the outset, including pre-registered
hypotheses and clearly defined metrics for success and failure.

## Closing Remarks

The future of healthcare AI lies not in monolithic systems that encode
medical knowledge centrally, but in networks of specialized agents
learning continuously from clinical experience and sharing insights in
privacy-preserving ways. We have proposed Decentralized Agentic Systems
as an architectural foundation for this future, one that respects the
distributed nature of clinical expertise, the sovereignty of
institutional knowledge, and the safety imperatives of patient care. The
challenges are substantial, and we have deliberately identified open
questions alongside our proposals. The converging pressures of data
scarcity, institutional fragmentation, and the maturation of agentic AI
capabilities make this a timely moment to chart a path toward
collaborative clinical intelligence. The architecture of that
collaboration matters, and we believe the principles articulated here:
experience over data, reasoning over parameters, and governance
alongside technology, point toward a productive direction.
````

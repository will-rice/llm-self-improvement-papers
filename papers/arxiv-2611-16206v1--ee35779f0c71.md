###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning improves problem solving in large language model
multi-agent systems. Existing approaches overlook communication
knowledge or compress it into scalar rewards, leaving coarse credit with
little guidance on which decisions each agent should reinforce or
revise. OPSD4MAS converts future communication into fine-grained
token-level credit through On Policy Self Distillation (OPSD). An
external model extracts facts and assesses their trajectory relations
for coarse-grained filtering, followed by fine-grained leave-one-out
filtering of target-token influence against agent-wise outcome
advantages. The fact-conditioned policy supplies token-level credit,
combined with GRPO through a linearly annealed joint objective. Our
method improves mathematical pass@4 over GRPO by up to 20.00 percentage
points. Ablations support complete filtering, while frozen-history
injection diagnostics support fact utility. Together, these results
support combining agent-level outcome guidance with
communication-derived token-level credit. Code is available at
[https://github.com/Camel-Prince/OPSD4MAS](https://github.com/Camel-Prince/OPSD4MAS).

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) improves large language model (LLM)
reasoning by updating policies from feedback on the quality of their
generated answers (Shao et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). Extending RL to
LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS) trains interacting agents to reason
and communicate for joint success (Liu et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026).
Most approaches derive scalar rewards from task outcomes or agent
communication, assign credit to participating agents, and update their
policies to improve multi-turn reasoning and coordination within each
task.

Many recent MAS training methods follow _scalar-reward RL_. Methods such
as MAGRPO, M-GRPO, and MARTI turn task outcomes, intermediate
evaluations, or judgments of agent interactions into scalar rewards for
policy updates (Liu et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2025; Zhang et al.,
2026a). Figure 1(a) contrasts two common routes. _Outcome-derived
rewards_ assign task-success signals to responses, as in the agent-wise
advantage normalization of DrMAS (Feng et al., 2026).
_Communication-informed rewards_ additionally use signals from
discussions, as in MAPoRL and CoMAS, while retaining scalar feedback
(Park et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2026). These scalar-reward approaches
either leave communication-derived knowledge unused in reward
construction or compress it into scores, losing a valuable source of
learning signal. For an intermediate response in a MAS, future
communication can explain an error, validate a claim, or suggest a
repair. Scalar rewards also yield coarse credit assignment: a
response-level advantage indicates whether to reinforce a response, but
cannot itself distinguish which tokens a correction supports or
challenges, even when token gradients differ.

To address this limitation, we propose OPSD4MAS, which converts future
communication into _token-level credit_ using On Policy Self
Distillation (OPSD), in which the current policy conditioned on selected
future information acts as a privileged teacher for the same policy
under its ordinary context. Comparing their probabilities on
student-generated tokens supplies token-level credit, as shown in
Figure 1(a), incorporating communication-derived knowledge into agent
parameters without future information at inference time.

The key to OPSD4MAS is constructing useful privileged context.
Unfiltered communication mixes useful corrections with errors and
irrelevant remarks. We therefore introduce _Fact Extraction and
Hierarchical Filtering_ (FEHF). For each target response, an external
model extracts facts from its future communication window and labels
their relations to same-query trajectories. Association filtering ranks
candidate facts by their relevance to solving the current query,
assessed from whether following or violating each fact aligns with the
outcome advantages of trajectories for that query. Facts followed in
higher-advantage trajectories or violated in lower-advantage
trajectories receive higher scores. Leave-one-out (LOO) filtering then
checks whether each candidate fact changes the target response’s token
probabilities in the direction favored by that response’s GRPO outcome
advantage. Retained facts provide privileged context without external
teacher logits. We combine OPSD with reinforcement learning with
verifiable rewards (RLVR), using GRPO and linearly decreasing the OPSD
loss weight.

Across six benchmarks in Table 1, OPSD4MAS with a future window of four
responses (FW4) improves mean pass@1 by 8.87 and 6.14 percentage points
over GRPO and DAPO, respectively. Mean pass@4 gains are 9.54 and 8.48
percentage points. Figure 1(b) shows three representative datasets. We
also inject selected facts into frozen histories and regenerate the
final Solver answer. With Relaxed filtering, accuracy exceeds no
injection by 1.98–6.13 percentage points across four checkpoints in
Figure 4. The training ablations in Table 2 compare raw communication,
extracted facts, and the two filtering stages. Extraction, Association,
and LOO improve mean pass@1 over their preceding ablated settings by
9.33, 0.42, and 21.92 percentage points, respectively, with the largest
gain from target-specific LOO.

Overall, our contributions are threefold:

- •
  We turn communication into token-level credit. We identify knowledge
  lost or compressed by scalar-reward MAS training and use future
  communication as privileged context for token-level credit.
- •
  We develop fact-guided joint OPSD–RLVR training. FEHF combines fact
  extraction with coarse- and fine-grained filtering. The fact-guided
  teacher supplies OPSD credit alongside RLVR, instantiated with GRPO,
  under a linearly annealed joint objective.
- •
  We show performance gains and downstream fact utility. OPSD4MAS (FW4)
  improves both metrics across six benchmarks over all three trained
  baselines, with gains up to 20.00 percentage points. Ablations and
  frozen-history injection diagnostics support the filtering design and
  the downstream utility of selected facts.

## 2 Related Work

#### Multi-agent systems.

LLM-based MAS coordinate specialized agents through role-based
communication, debate, and shared task execution (Li et al., 2023; Wu
et al., 2023; Du et al., 2023). Beyond manually specified interactions,
AFlow and ADAS search for effective agent organizations (Zhang et al.,
2025; Hu et al., 2025). These approaches address how agents collaborate.
A complementary question is how post-training can improve the policies
participating in that collaboration.

#### Reinforcement learning for multi-agent systems.

RL post-training improves LLM policies using feedback on generated
answers. GRPO, RLOO, and DAPO offer policy-gradient approaches with
different advantage estimators and optimization designs (Shao et al.,
2024; Ahmadian et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). Extending reward-based
learning to interacting agents introduces the problem of assigning
credit across roles and turns. MAGRPO optimizes cooperative policies
using shared rewards, while DrMAS normalizes outcome rewards with
agent-specific statistics (Liu et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026). To
account for collaboration, MAPoRL shapes verifier rewards using changes
in answer correctness and influence across discussions, while CoMAS
derives scalar action rewards from interaction-based judgments (Park
et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2026). However, these methods reduce task
feedback to scalar rewards rather than use communication content
directly for task-specific token-level credit. OPSD4MAS addresses this
gap by conditioning a privileged teacher on selected communication while
retaining outcome credit.

#### On Policy Self Distillation.

On Policy Distillation (OPD) is an emerging LLM post-training paradigm
that queries a teacher on student-generated trajectories, providing
token-level learning signals on states visited by the student (Song and
Zheng, 2026). In On Policy Self Distillation (OPSD), the student model
or a copy acts as teacher with additional information unavailable to the
ordinary-context student. Related methods differ in their teachers and
privileged information. OPCD studies frozen and shared-weight context
teachers (Ye et al., 2026). MOPD uses successful and failed
same-question rollouts with self or external teachers (Yu et al., 2026).
GEAR uses a ground-truth-conditioned self-model to reweight outcome
advantages, not a direct distillation loss (Li et al., 2026). TurnOPD
uses a frozen external teacher on student-visited multi-turn histories
(Zhou et al., 2026). EDGE-OPD masks updates by privileged-context
evidence, whereas AR-OPD controls full-view residual guidance around a
partial-privilege anchor (Lazaridis et al., 2026; Zhang, 2026). Our
focus is selecting reliable, target-specific facts from mixed-quality
future communication, rather than directly adopting complete answers or
alternative rollouts as privileged context.

#### OPD for MAS.

Recent work applies OPD to multi-agent learning. MAS-OPD supplies a
frozen external teacher with verifier-produced attributions of past-turn
conflicts (Zhong et al., 2026). $`\pi`$-Play conditions an EMA
self-teacher on an examiner’s question-construction paths (Zhang et al.,
2026b). OPSD4MAS instead extracts facts from future inter-agent
communication during task solving. Cross-trajectory association and
target-specific LOO filtering select these facts as privileged context
for each role’s privileged teacher.

## 3 Problem Formulation

A language-model multi-agent system (MAS) coordinates communicating
agents through a predefined workflow, graph, or autonomous decisions. In
the role-specific setting, each role $`r\in\mathcal{R}`$ has parameters
$`\theta_{r}`$, and $`\Theta=\{\theta_{r}:r\in\mathcal{R}\}`$ collects
all trainable parameters. We write $`\pi_{\theta}^{r}=\pi_{\theta_{r}}`$
for its policy. For query $`q`$, index sampled trajectories by $`j`$.
Stochastic execution gives

```math
\tau_{j}=\operatorname{MAS}_{\Theta}(q),\qquad\tau_{j}=(y_{j,1},\ldots,y_{j,T_{j}}). \tag{1}
```

Response $`y_{j,i}`$ is generated by role $`r(i)`$, which can act
repeatedly. Token $`t`$ has context $`(q,y_{j,<i},y_{j,i,<t})`$ under
shared history. Restricted communication exposes only the permitted
prefix. MAS RL updates all participating agents’ parameters, not only
the final answering agent. With outcome reward $`R(\tau)`$, the
objective is

```math
\max_{\Theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{q,\,\tau\sim\operatorname{MAS}_{\Theta}(q)}[R(\tau)]. \tag{2}
```

DrMAS (Feng et al., 2026) normalizes outcome rewards by agent within
each query group to obtain

```math
A^{r(i)}_{j,i}=\frac{s_{j,i}-\mu_{q,r(i)}}{\sigma_{q,r(i)}+\epsilon}. \tag{3}
```

Here $`s_{j,i}`$ is the outcome reward assigned to response $`y_{j,i}`$
in GRPO training. Mean $`\mu`$ and standard deviation $`\sigma`$ pool
all same-agent responses across trajectories for query $`q`$, counting
repeated turns separately. This agent-wise advantage weights every valid
response token in GRPO’s clipped surrogate. Communication-informed CoMAS
instead optimizes interaction-derived action rewards with REINFORCE++
and a clipped surrogate (Xue et al., 2026). Its parameters index agents
that can perform different interaction roles. Appendix A details their
normalization and KL terms.

### 4.1 Motivation

Scalar-reward MAS training discards or compresses communication
knowledge useful for collaboration. We use future communication as
privileged context for token-level OPSD credit. Mixing corrections with
errors and irrelevant remarks motivates fact extraction and two
filtering stages. Communication also lacks verified outcome feedback,
motivating annealed joint OPSD–GRPO training. Following Figure 2, we
describe privileged-context construction, then joint training.

### 4.2 Fact Extraction and Hierarchical Filtering

#### Fact extraction.

Given trajectory $`\tau_{j}`$, consider its $`i`$-th response $`y_{i}`$,
omitting $`j`$ below and writing $`r=r(i)`$. An external model examines
communication in $`y_{i+1},\ldots,y_{\min(i+W,T)}`$ and extracts atomic
corrections, warnings, validated claims, and strategies (Figure 2,
panels 1–2). Facts retain their source and target identities. The
terminal response can supply facts to earlier steps but has no future
source itself. Appendix C gives the Extractor and Relationer prompts.
Extraction converts communication into individual facts, and two filters
then select the target’s privileged information.

#### Association filtering.

Association filtering (Figure 2, panel 3) uses fact–trajectory relations
for the same query, labeled by the external model:
$`r_{j}(f)\in\{+1,-1,0\}`$ for _followed_, _violated_, and _unrelated_.
For fact ranking, we separately compute
$`A(\tau_{j})=(R(\tau_{j})-\mu_{q})/(\sigma_{q}+\epsilon)`$, counting
each trajectory once in the query group’s reward statistics. Unlike the
response-occurrence normalization in Equation 3, this does not weight
repeated agent turns. We compute

```math
\operatorname{assoc}(f)=\frac{\sum_{j}A(\tau_{j})r_{j}(f)}{\sum_{j}|A(\tau_{j})|\,|r_{j}(f)|+\epsilon}. \tag{4}
```

Following a fact at positive advantage or violating it at negative
advantage contributes positively, whereas the reverse contributes
negatively. Unrelated labels contribute neither term. The score ranks
facts by outcome association on the query rather than weighting the
actor loss. Retaining a limited number forms $`C_{i}`$, without a
positive-score threshold. Appendix A details normalization, exclusions,
and tie-breaking.

#### Target-specific LOO filtering.

From the Association-ranked candidates, LOO retains facts with
sufficient influence on the target’s tokens, predominantly aligned with
its outcome advantage (Figure 2, panel 4). It compares the full
candidate set with one fact removed, using cached pre-update policy
scores. Write

```math
g_{i,t}(S)=\log\pi_{\theta}^{r}(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{<i},y_{i,<t},S).
```

We define the removal effect and its outcome-directed value as

```math
\displaystyle\delta^{\mathrm{LOO}}_{f,i,t} \displaystyle=g_{i,t}(C_{i})-g_{i,t}(C_{i}\setminus\{f\}), \tag{5}
```

```math
\displaystyle z_{f,i,t} \displaystyle=A_{i}^{r}\delta^{\mathrm{LOO}}_{f,i,t}.
```

Here $`A_{i}^{r}`$ is the target’s DrMAS agent-wise advantage used by
GRPO (Equation 3, $`j`$ omitted), not Association’s $`A(\tau_{j})`$. Let
$`m_{+}=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\max(z_{f,i,t},0)]`$ and
$`m_{-}=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\max(-z_{f,i,t},0)]`$, where expectations include
only active response tokens. We measure effect, directional precision,
and net alignment:

```math
\displaystyle E(f) \displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{t}[(\delta^{\mathrm{LOO}}_{f,i,t})^{2}]}, \tag{6}
```

```math
\displaystyle P(f) \displaystyle=\frac{m_{+}}{m_{+}+m_{-}+\epsilon}, \tag{7}
```

```math
\displaystyle N(f) \displaystyle=m_{+}-\lambda_{c}m_{-}. \tag{8}
```

$`E`$ measures influence on token log probabilities, while $`m_{+}`$ and
$`m_{-}`$ measure its advantage-aligned and opposing mass. $`P`$ is the
positive influence share, not a token fraction, and $`N`$ penalizes
opposing mass. Useful facts should have sufficient influence, a high
positive share, and a large net effect. For this target, a fact passes
when $`E\geq E_{\min}`$, $`P\geq P_{\min}`$, and $`N>N_{\min}`$.
Top-$`K`$ ranking gives the final set $`F_{i}\subseteq C_{i}`$. We use
$`\lambda_{c}=1`$ by default and rescore $`F_{i}`$ for OPSD.
Context-matching and budget details appear in Appendix A.

### 4.3 Fact-Conditioned OPSD and Annealed Joint Training

#### Fact-conditioned token-level credit.

The privileged teacher and ordinary-context student are two views of the
same role policy. Using the selected set $`F_{i}`$, we compute

```math
\displaystyle\delta_{i,t}={} \displaystyle\log\pi_{\theta}^{r}(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{<i},y_{i,<t},F_{i}) \tag{9}
```

```math
\displaystyle-\log\pi_{\theta}^{r}(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{<i},y_{i,<t}).
```

Scores are cached before actor updates. The token-dependent coefficient
is
$`A^{\textsc{opsd}}_{i,t}=\operatorname{sg}(\lambda_{O}\delta_{i,t})`$,
where $`\operatorname{sg}`$ stops gradients and $`\lambda_{O}=1`$ by
default. It is zero when no facts are selected. Unlike either outcome
advantage, this coefficient comes from token log-probability
differences.

#### Role-wise OPSD objective.

Within one role-homogeneous loss-reduction batch, let $`I_{r}`$ contain
role $`r`$’s responses, $`m_{i,t}`$ be the active-token mask, and
$`Z_{r}=\sum_{i\in I_{r}}\sum_{t}m_{i,t}`$. For $`Z_{r}>0`$,

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}^{(r)}={} \displaystyle-\frac{1}{Z_{r}}\sum_{i\in I_{r}}\sum_{t}m_{i,t}A^{\textsc{opsd}}_{i,t} \tag{10}
```

```math
\displaystyle\cdot\log\pi_{\theta}^{r}(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{<i},y_{i,<t}).
```

Only ordinary-context probabilities carry gradients to $`\theta_{r}`$.
OPSD uses neither ratios nor clipping. Appendix A gives the caching and
reduction details.

#### Joint training and linear annealing.

Our experiments combine GRPO’s clipped policy-gradient loss with OPSD,
as illustrated in Figure 2’s upper panel:

```math
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(r)}(s) \displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}^{(r)}+w^{O}(s)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}^{(r)}, \tag{11}
```

```math
\displaystyle w^{O}(s) \displaystyle=\max(w_{0}-s\rho,w_{\min}).
```

Here $`s`$ is the training step, while $`w_{0}`$, $`\rho`$, and
$`w_{\min}`$ set the initial weight, decay, and floor. Both terms update
$`\theta_{r}`$: GRPO and LOO share DrMAS agent-wise advantage
$`A_{i}^{r}`$, whereas OPSD supplies fact-conditioned token-varying
credit. Annealing reduces the OPSD contribution while keeping verified
outcome feedback active. Appendix A gives implementation details and
loss aggregations. Inference uses ordinary contexts, without future
communication or the external model.

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Setup

#### Models and data.

We initialize trained agents from Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025), and all
settings use non-thinking mode. AFlow (Zhang et al., 2025) and GPTSwarm
(Zhuge et al., 2024) also use Qwen3-4B executors. AFlow’s search
optimizer is Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507-Pro (Qwen Team, 2025). For
policy training, we use DAPO-MATH-17k (Yu et al., 2025). We test on six
other datasets: AMC23, AIME24, AIME25 (Mathematical Association of
America, n.d.), MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2023), the English text-only
mathematics subset of OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024), and Minerva
(Lewkowycz et al., 2022).

#### Compared methods.

\(1\) _Base Model_: Qwen3-4B answers directly without multi-agent
interaction. (2) _Frozen-weight MAS_: Solver–Verifier MAS uses the
autonomous interaction loop shared by DrMAS and OPSD4MAS, with
model-decided termination and no LLM parameter updates. AFlow and
GPTSwarm optimize collaboration structures or prompts while retaining
fixed LLM weights. (3) _Scalar-reward RL_: DrMAS (Feng et al., 2026)
with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) or DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) uses outcome
rewards. CoMAS (Xue et al., 2026) derives scalar rewards from
interactions. (4) _OPSD4MAS_: FW2 and FW4 combine OPSD with GRPO, using
future windows of two and four responses. Table 1 reports pass@1 and
pass@4 as percentages. Appendix B.1 gives protocols for evaluation and
selection.

### 5.2 Main Results

#### Gains over scalar-reward RL.

FW4 leads all six datasets in Table 1. With equal dataset weights, mean
pass@1 exceeds GRPO, DAPO, and CoMAS by 8.87, 6.14, and 8.46 percentage
points. Mean pass@4 gains are 9.54, 8.48, and 11.13 percentage points,
respectively. The gains over CoMAS extend this comparison to
communication-informed rewards. Across both scalar-reward routes, these
results support combining outcome credit with communication-derived
token-level credit.

#### Gains over frozen-weight MAS.

FW4 also exceeds every reported frozen-weight result, including both the
shared Solver–Verifier loop and optimized collaboration structures. On
MATH-500, it reaches 91.50% pass@1 and 96.60% pass@4, improving on
Solver–Verifier MAS by 8.00 and 4.80 percentage points. On Olympiad, the
respective gains are 13.49 and 10.67 percentage points. With Qwen3-4B
executors throughout, these results favor learning agent policies over
relying solely on fixed-weight collaboration.

#### Benefits of a larger future window.

FW2 is already competitive with scalar-reward RL. Across six datasets,
its mean pass@1 is close to DAPO’s, while its mean pass@4 is 4.83
percentage points higher. Expanding the window to four responses raises
mean pass@1 and pass@4 by 6.06 and 3.65 percentage points. The observed
gains support a broader span of future communication as a valuable
source of information for fact selection.

benefit.

### 5.5 Case Study: Understanding Fact Selection

We examine representative facts to explain what our filters remove and
retain. Figure 5 connects each decision to the fact’s content, source
response, and proposed target.

#### Low association: no actionable repair.

Panel (a) asks for a tetrahedron’s maximum volume. The fact extracted
from Verifier $`y_{2}`$ tells Solver $`y_{1}`$ only that the
optimization is unfinished. It supplies no missing constraint, bound, or
corrected calculation. With $`A=-1`$, this progress description is
dropped before LOO scoring.

#### High association, unfavorable local direction.

Panel (b) specifies $`a_{1}=a_{2}=a_{3}=1`$ and geometric consecutive
triple sums with ratio 3. Solver $`y_{3}`$ supplies Verifier $`y_{2}`$
with the claim $`a_{n}=(3^{n}-10)/13`$ for $`n\equiv 1\pmod{3}`$. At
$`n=1`$, it gives $`-7/13`$, contradicting the initial condition.
Despite $`A=1`$ and substantial effect $`E=0.295`$, negative alignment
$`N=-0.141`$ and low directional precision $`P=0.320`$ lead to
rejection. Strong teacher influence alone is insufficient, so the local
screen also tests its direction relative to outcome credit.

#### High association, favorable local direction.

Panel (c) factors $`(a-10)(b-10)=2123`$ to minimize a rectangle’s
perimeter. The fact from Solver $`y_{5}`$ for Verifier $`y_{4}`$
corrects $`13\times 163=2119`$ and supplies $`2123=11\times 193`$,
yielding sides 21 and 203 and perimeter 448. The repair is retained with
$`A=1`$, $`E=0.522`$, $`N=0.246`$, and $`P=0.795`$.

These cases illustrate how FEHF turns future communication into
privileged context. In particular, LOO selects facts by their
token-level influence relative to the target’s agent-wise GRPO
advantage. The annealed OPSD–RLVR objective then combines agent-wise
outcome credit with fact-conditioned token-level credit.

## 6 Conclusion

We observe that scalar-reward MAS training compresses or ignores
communication and assigns coarse credit. To address this limitation, we
propose OPSD4MAS, using Fact Extraction and Hierarchical Filtering
(FEHF) to turn future communication into privileged context. Its
linearly annealed joint objective combines OPSD token-level credit with
GRPO outcome guidance. Supporting this design, OPSD4MAS with a future
window of four responses (FW4) leads all baselines across six
mathematical benchmarks. Ablations support complete FEHF, while
injection diagnostics highlight task matching and filtering strength.
Together, FEHF converts future communication into privileged context,
enabling annealed OPSD–RLVR joint training to combine agent-wise outcome
credit with fact-conditioned token-level credit.

## Limitations

Our evaluation focuses on mathematical reasoning with Qwen3-4B agents.
Extending the approach to other tasks, model scales, and interaction
structures remains future work. Fact extraction and relation labeling
rely on an external model, so the quality of privileged context can
depend on that model and on filtering thresholds, context budgets, and
annealing.

The injection diagnostic evaluates selected facts at fixed weights and
frozen histories. Its four checkpoints belong to one training run rather
than independent training seeds, so the observed checkpoint differences
do not measure variation across training runs.

## Ethical Considerations

This work studies reinforcement learning on competition-mathematics data
and does not use personal or sensitive information. External model calls
receive problem-solving traces, so deployments should ensure that traces
contain no confidential data and comply with the provider’s data policy.
More broadly, multi-agent self-improvement can amplify incorrect
feedback shared between agents. The proposed filters are intended to
mitigate this risk, but a reduction has not been demonstrated. Selected
facts should not be interpreted as verified truth outside the evaluated
domain.

## Appendix A Implementation and Reproducibility

#### Computing environment.

The project used a computing environment with 24 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, each
with 80 GB of memory. This describes the available project resources,
not the allocation of every individual run.

#### Baseline objectives and parameter indexing.

DrMAS uses a clipped importance-ratio objective for each agent, with
outcome credit normalized by agent action statistics (Feng et al.,
2026). We follow its query-and-agent response grouping in Equation 3.
CoMAS instead collects agent outputs and interaction-derived rewards
into a buffer and optimizes a REINFORCE++ clipped surrogate (Xue et al.,
2026). For output $`o`$ and input $`p`$, its pre-normalization advantage
includes a cumulative KL term,

```math
A_{t}=r(o)-\beta\sum_{u=t}^{|o|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta_{k}}(o_{u}\mid p,o_{<u})}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(o_{u}\mid p,o_{<u})}. \tag{12}
```

After batch normalization, the objective sums
$`\min(\rho_{t}\widehat{A}_{t},\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat{A}_{t})`$
over tokens, with current/old-policy ratio $`\rho_{t}`$. The reported
zero-KL-weight configuration has a token-constant reward component.
CoMAS agents can take different interaction roles, whereas
$`\theta_{r}`$ in our formulation indexes a fixed role policy.

#### Cached scores and loss reduction.

Teacher and ordinary-context token scores are cached before actor
updates, and their difference is computed without gradient tracking. The
OPSD coefficient is detached, so gradients flow only through
ordinary-context token log probabilities in the actor loss. Extraction
and filtering do not receive gradients. The default OPSD loss averages
over active response tokens in each role-homogeneous worker microbatch
before gradient accumulation. Prompt and inactive tokens are excluded.
GRPO instead averages each response’s masked token mean over nonempty
responses of that role and uses the clipped ordinary-policy likelihood
ratio. OPSD uses neither ratios nor clipping. These distinct reductions
are retained in the joint objective.

#### Two outcome advantages.

Association computes $`A(\tau_{j})`$ from one episode reward per
trajectory within query $`q`$. Its statistics use trajectories
identified by the query’s relation maps, with population standard
deviation and $`\epsilon=10^{-6}`$. Zero-variance groups, including
singletons, receive zero. These values weight fact–trajectory relations
rather than the actor loss.

GRPO computes the DrMAS agent-wise advantage from response rewards
$`s_{j,i}=\sum_{t}r_{j,i,t}`$, grouping responses by query and agent ID.
Repeated turns count separately. For at least two responses,
normalization uses the sample standard deviation with denominator
$`n-1`$. A singleton uses mean zero and standard deviation one.
Equal-reward groups of at least two responses receive zero. Association
uses episode rewards, while GRPO uses the trainer’s response reward
tensor, including any configured reward processing. LOO reads the same
cached advantages_grpo as the actor loss rather than recomputing an
Association advantage.

#### Candidate selection and ranking.

A source fact can reach earlier active non-judger responses in the same
trajectory within $`W`$ positions. Validated claims from entirely
unsuccessful groups are excluded, and duplicate normalized lesson text
is collapsed. Candidate ranking uses descending Association, relation
coverage, and type priority (correction, warning, validated claim,
strategy), followed by shorter future distance and ascending fact ID.
Duplicate ties retain the larger ID. Facts passing LOO are ranked by
descending net alignment, precision, and RMS effect, then shorter
distance and ascending ID before top-$`K`$ truncation. All LOO token
averages use active response masks. The final selected set receives a
fresh teacher-forced scoring pass for OPSD.

#### Teacher-context construction.

The serializer appends \[Privileged Future Lessons\] and numbered facts
to the last user message, then applies the chat template with a
generation marker. To meet the context budget, it first removes lessons
from the end of their supplied order. If necessary, it removes older
unprotected history messages while retaining the first message, first
user message, and latest message, then removes further lessons.
Full-candidate and leave-one-out prompts are serialized separately.
Their difference isolates a single fact when the retained history and
all other facts match. Under truncation, it reflects the resulting
serialized contexts. For a single candidate, an unavailable removal
score is replaced by the cached ordinary score. A missing full score or
a missing removal score for multiple candidates causes rejection. An
unavailable selected teacher score leaves the OPSD coefficient zero.

### B.3 Four-Checkpoint Injection Diagnostic

#### Evaluation setting.

Figures 3 and 4 use FW4 checkpoints 100, 110, 120, and 130 with their
saved trajectories and facts. The evaluator freezes the MAS history,
inserts facts at targeted historical positions and the final Solver
input, and regenerates only the final active Solver on training
questions. Model weights remain fixed during this diagnostic.

#### Filtering profiles.

Strict requires RMS effect $`E\geq 0.10`$, directional precision
$`P\geq 0.65`$, and net alignment $`N>0.001`$. Relaxed uses
$`E\geq 0.005`$, $`P\geq 0.50`$, and $`N>-0.001`$, with configuration
name loose. All uses the post-association candidate pool without a LOO
gate, including candidates with missing LOO scores. It does not restore
Association-rejected facts. None is the no-injection reference.

For each profile, Shuffled uses facts from other questions at
corresponding injection slots, matching visible fact counts with
approximate type and length matching. It does not sample random facts
from the current question. Queries are the independent units for
statistical inference, with trajectories and repeated answers nested
within them. The four checkpoints are from one training run. Comparisons
are descriptive, without significance tests.

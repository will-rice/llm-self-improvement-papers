# Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation

Wenhang Shi¹   Yiren Chen²   Zhe Zhao³   Jinhao Dong¹   Wei Lu¹

###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) enables large
language models (LLMs) to continuously self-improve their reasoning
capabilities. Compared to RLVR’s scalar rewards, recent self-supervised
on-policy distillation methods leverage denser supervision signals.
However, both paradigms suffer from exploration bottlenecks, struggling
to escape local optima when the policy fails to sample valid solutions.
Relying on external demonstrations can bypass this issue; however, the
off-policy nature hinders genuine self-improvement and the
mismatched-distribution reasoning can not be fully exploited. In this
work, we propose Self-Evolution Policy Optimization (SEPO), a framework
that drives continuous reasoning capability evolution via guided
exploration and in-distribution exploitation. Specifically, SEPO
utilizes labels to elicit correct reasoning from the policy, unlocking
the exploration potential. By treating current policy conditioned on
this in-distribution reasoning as a self-teacher, SEPO distills the
newly discovered solution back into the policy. Through this iterative
cycle of policy-intrinsic exploration and exploitation, SEPO achieves
robust and continuous capability evolution. Across scientific, tool-use,
and complex logical reasoning domains, SEPO consistently achieves the
best performance under identical training time or steps, demonstrating
superior efficiency and convergence ceilings. Notably, SEPO maintains
stable improvements even in scenarios where baselines severely
underperform or fail entirely, such as on complex tasks or with
small-scale models. Our code is available at
[https://github.com/Eric8932/SEPO](https://github.com/Eric8932/SEPO).

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) enables large
language models (LLMs) to develop sophisticated reasoning capabilities
by rewarding their own correct rollouts [Shao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib16); [DeepSeek-AI (2025a)](#bib.bib18); [Team
(2025b)](#bib.bib22). Recently, on-policy self-distillation methods have
emerged, leveraging denser supervision signals to achieve more efficient
self-improvement [Lu and Thinking Machines Lab (2025)](#bib.bib2);
[Hübotter et al. (2026)](#bib.bib6). They treat the current policy with
its own correct rollouts in the context as a self-teacher, distilling
the enriched predictions back into the policy, thoroughly exploiting the
successful solutions. Although both paradigms can continuously improve
reasoning capabilities, their optimization relies entirely on the
policy’s unguided trials, making them susceptible to exploration
bottlenecks. When the policy fails to sample a valid solution, these
methods cannot formulate effective supervision signals, causing the
optimization to stagnate.

Recent works introduce external solutions from experts or proprietary
models to mitigate the exploration bottleneck, which generally fall into
two categories. The first category distills expert demonstrations for
imitation learning, either by fine-tuning on them as an auxiliary
training objective [Zhang et al. (2025a)](#bib.bib9); [Fu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib11), or by placing them within the student’s context to
construct an expert-imitated distillation teacher  [Shenfeld et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib4); [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib5); [Xu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib8). The second category integrates expert data to guide
RL exploration. They either incorporate it into the same GRPO group with
the on-policy rollouts for joint learning [Fu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib11); [Yan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib10), or extract
prefixes to serve as initial contexts, making it easier to sample
correct solutions [Su et al. (2025)](#bib.bib12); [Qu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib13). While relying on external experts circumvents
exploration failures, it introduces severe off-policy challenges and
causes policy’s own exploration to stagnate. Furthermore, the
distribution mismatches prevent the policy from fully internalizing
these reasoning patterns. Ultimately, the dual deficits in exploration
and exploitation stifle continuous improvement and impose a strict
performance ceiling.

In this work, we propose Self-Evolution Policy Optimization (SEPO), a
framework that drives continuous capability evolution via guided
exploration and in-distribution exploitation. Specifically, SEPO
utilizes ground-truth labels as privileged hints to elicit correct
reasoning from the policy itself. This alleviates the exploration
bottleneck by shifting the learning threshold from generating solutions
from scratch to performing guided deduction toward correct answers. By
treating the current policy conditioned on this in-distribution
reasoning as a teacher, SEPO distills the context-enriched predictions
back into the standard policy, ensuring thorough internalization of the
newly discovered reasoning. Ultimately, through this iterative cycle of
policy-intrinsic exploration and exploitation, SEPO achieves a more
sustained capability evolution and a higher performance ceiling.

Across extensive experiments on scientific, tool-use, and logical
reasoning tasks, SEPO consistently outperforms strong RLVR and
self-distillation methods. It not only demonstrates the best convergence
performance, indicating a higher ceiling, but also exhibits superior
training efficiency, surpassing baseline performance in significantly
less time or steps. Notably, even in scenarios where baselines
underperform or fail entirely, such as in highly complex logical
reasoning tasks or when scaling down to weak models, SEPO maintains
stable and continuous performance improvements. Our contributions are
summarized as follows:

- •
  We propose SEPO, an on-policy self-distillation framework that drives
  continuous reasoning evolution through superior exploration and
  exploitation. By utilizing label to guide reasoning exploration and
  constructing in-distribution teacher to exploiting it, SEPO
  iteratively self-improves.
- •
  We present a theoretical analysis revealing how exploration and
  exploitation deficits limit the optimization of existing methods.
  Crucially, our analysis formally validates that SEPO fundamentally
  circumvents these bottlenecks, guaranteeing sustained and effective
  self-evolution.
- •
  SEPO demonstrates superior training efficiency and final performance
  across diverse reasoning tasks. Even in scenarios where baselines
  fail, SEPO robustly secures continuous improvements.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) improves the
reasoning ability of large language models by optimizing them against
verifiable outcome-level rewards [Schulman et al. (2017)](#bib.bib17);
[Team (2025a)](#bib.bib24). Given an input problem $`x`$ with
ground-truth label $`y^{\ast}`$, a policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ generates a
solution $`\tau=(z,\hat{y})`$, where $`z`$ denotes the intermediate
reasoning process and $`\hat{y}`$ the final prediction, and receives a
scalar reward $`r(\tau;y^{\ast})=\mathbb{I}[\hat{y}=y^{\ast}]`$, which
is typically binary in reasoning tasks. Recently, RLVR is predominantly
implemented via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [Shao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib16). For each problem $`x`$, the policy samples a group
of $`G`$ solutions $`\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$. The outcome reward
$`r_{i}`$ for each solution is converted into a group-relative
advantage, e.g.,
$`A^{GPRO}_{i,t}=\frac{r_{i}-\mathrm{mean}(\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})+\epsilon}`$.
Crucially, this sequence-level advantage is applied uniformly to every
token $`t`$ within the $`i`$-th solution.

Despite its broad application in enhancing reasoning, RLVR suffers from
two fundamental limitations. First, its supervision is sparse and purely
outcome-level: the uniform advantage $`A_{i,t}`$ provides no direct
credit assignment for intermediate reasoning steps [Cui et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib20); [Khalifa et al. (2026)](#bib.bib19). Second, RLVR
relies on the policy possessing robust exploration ability. If the
policy fails to sample any correct solutions within a group, the
algorithm generates no effective learning signal, causing optimization
to stagnate [Zhang et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib21); [Wang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib14).

### 2.2 On-policy context distillation

Recently, several on-policy context distillation (OPCD) methods leverage
denser supervision signals to achieve reasoning
self-improvement [Hübotter et al. (2026)](#bib.bib6); [Shenfeld et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib4); [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib5). OPCD conditions the
student policy $`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ on a privileged context
$`c`$ to construct a stronger self-teacher
$`\pi_{\text{tea}}(\cdot\mid x,c)`$. This context $`c`$ typically
comprises a valid reasoning path, sourced either from student’s
successful rollouts or external experts, as a reference to simplify the
problem-solving process. Specifically, at each training step, solutions
$`\tau_{i}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ are sampled from the unhinted
student policy. In contrast to GRPO’s static sequence-level advantage,
OPCD provides a token-specific supervision through on-policy
distillation [Gu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib1); [Lu and Thinking Machines
Lab (2025)](#bib.bib2). At each token $`t`$ within solution $`i`$, the
student assimilates the teacher’s predictions by minimizing the
Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence over the student’s own generated prefix
$`\tau_{i,<t}`$:
$`A^{\mathrm{OPCD}}_{i,t}=\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\text{tea}}(\cdot\mid x,c,\tau_{i,<t})\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\tau_{i,<t})\right)`$ [Kullback
and Leibler (1951)](#bib.bib37); [Hinton et al. (2015)](#bib.bib34).
Since the teacher shares the student’s evolving weights, its contextual
reasoning capabilities progressively improve throughout a iterative
process, creating a continuous self-improvement flywheel.

### 2.3 RLVR and OPCD

Fundamentally, OPCD can be viewed as a proxy objective for
RLVR [Shenfeld et al. (2026)](#bib.bib4). From a variational
perspective, the optimal policy $`\pi^{*}`$ that maximizes expected
reward satisfies
$`\pi^{*}(\tau\mid x)\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}(\tau\mid x)\exp(r(\tau)/\eta)`$,
where $`\eta`$ is a temperature parameter [Korbak et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib38); [Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib39). RLVR methods
such as GRPO move toward $`\pi^{*}`$ using sparse rewards, whereas OPCD
instead constructs a teacher policy $`\pi_{\text{tea}}(\cdot\mid x,c)`$
as an approximation to $`\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x)`$ and minimizes its
distance to the student policy (with detailed derivations in
Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Equivalence Between RLVR and OPCD ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")).
Therefore, when the teacher is a good proxy for $`\pi^{*}`$, context
distillation can drive iterative capability improvement similarly to
RLVR, but with much denser supervision. Its effectiveness and stability,
however, critically depend on the choice of privileged context $`c`$,
which determines the quality and compatibility of the teacher
distribution.

Existing OPCD methods have critical issues when constructing the
context. Relying on student’s direct rollout ($`c=\tau_{\text{self}}`$)
guarantees on-policy learning. However, its existence is bounded by the
marginal probability of the model’s exploration success,
$`P_{\text{self}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}[\mathbb{I}(r(\tau)=1)]`$.
If the policy fails to solve a complex problem within a rollout group,
the empirical estimate $`\hat{P}_{\text{self}}(x)\to 0`$, rendering the
teacher undefined and halting the distillation process. Alternatively,
utilizing solutions from external experts ($`c=\tau_{\text{expert}}`$)
bypasses the exploration bottleneck. However, these off-policy contexts
cannot co-evolve with the student’s optimization, inherently imposing a
ceiling on self-improvement. Even worse, this paradigm introduces a
severe distribution shift, as the teacher $`\pi_{\text{tea}}`$ often
occupies a reasoning subspace outside the student’s natural support
($`\text{supp}(\pi_{\text{tea}})\not\subseteq\text{supp}(\pi_{\theta})`$) [Kumar
et al. (2019)](#bib.bib40); [Levine et al. (2020)](#bib.bib41).
Minimizing this out-of-distribution divergence forces drastic, localized
behavioral shifts rather than naturally internalizing the expert’s
reasoning patterns. This insufficient exploitation prevents the policy
from approaching the ceiling set by experts. Full theoretical analyses
are in
Appendix [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Theoretical Limitations of OPCD Baselines ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation").

## 3 Method: Self-Evolution Policy Optimization

#### Motivation

As established, constructing an effective proxy for the optimal policy
$`\pi^{*}`$ requires a privileged context $`c`$ that is simultaneously
reliably accessible (to bypass the exploration bottleneck) and strictly
on-policy (to avoid distribution shifts). To achieve this, as
illustrated in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Stable distillation via bounded divergence and tail truncation. ‣ 3.1 The SEPO framework ‣ 3 Method: Self-Evolution Policy Optimization ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation"),
we develop the Self-Evolution Policy Optimization (SEPO) framework. By
harnessing a synergy between guided exploration and in-distribution
exploitation, SEPO iteratively mines and elevates policy-intrinsic
reasoning capacity, realizing continuous self-evolution.

### 3.1 The SEPO framework

To circumvent the exploration bottleneck while preserving on-policy
exploitation, the Self-Evolution Policy Optimization (SEPO) framework
integrates guided reasoning exploration and in-distribution exploitation
processes into a synchronized online training loop. As shown in
Algorithm [3](#S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1 "Motivation ‣ 3 Method: Self-Evolution Policy Optimization ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation"),
at each training step, given a problem $`x`$ and its ground-truth label
$`y^{*}`$, the student policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ first samples rollouts
$`\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$. Concurrently, the teacher policy
$`\pi_{\text{tea}}`$ receives a label-informed prompt (e.g., "Correct
answer: $`y^{*}`$; Reason naturally …") to elicit a set of reasoning
processes and retain only the correct ones,
$`z_{\text{intrinsic}}\sim\pi_{\text{tea}}(\cdot\mid x,y^{*})`$.
Conditioned on this privileged context $`z_{\text{intrinsic}}`$, the
stop-gradient teacher computes the next-token distribution over
student’s rollout prefix $`\tau_{<t}`$. By minimizing the divergence
between the student’s predictions and the context-informed teacher’s
distribution, SEPO continuously pulls the student’s unhinted reasoning
toward its own label-guided potential. The detailed training procedure
is provided in
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Appendix D Implementation Details ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation").

#### Teacher initialization and EMA update.

SEPO initializes $`\pi_{\text{tea}}`$ with the student’s weights
$`\theta`$ and updates it via an Exponential Moving Average (EMA):
$`\theta_{\text{tea}}\leftarrow(1-\beta)\theta_{\text{tea}}+\beta\theta`$,
where $`\beta\in[0,1)`$. This momentum-based strategy serves two
purposes: it ensures the context $`z_{\text{intrinsic}}`$ evolves
synchronously with the student’s growing capabilities to facilitate
continuous self-improvement, and it provides a smoothed, implicitly
ensembled target that prevents confirmation bias and stabilizes
learning.

#### Stable distillation via bounded divergence and tail truncation.

Standard distillation typically relies on Forward KL divergence
($`\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{tea}}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta})`$), which
enforces mode-covering behavior [Minka and others (2005)](#bib.bib42);
[Hinton et al. (2015)](#bib.bib34). It heavily penalizes the student for
assigning low probability to preferred actions, frequently triggering
gradient explosions and catastrophic forgetting [Chen et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib33). In contrast, Reverse KL
($`\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{tea}})`$) induces
mode-seeking behavior [Gu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib1). While this protects
the model’s base capabilities by only penalizing student’s generations
outside the teacher’s support, it strictly stifles exploration, causing
the student to collapse into rigid reasoning paths. To mitigate these
extremes, SEPO adopts the Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) [Lin
(1991)](#bib.bib15). As a symmetric and bounded metric, JSD naturally
balances mode-covering and mode-seeking behaviors, providing stable
gradients that allow the student to safely assimilate the elicited
reasoning. Furthermore, we approximate the analytic KL divergence by
restricting the learning distribution to the top-$`K`$ tokens predicted
by the student model [Hübotter et al. (2026)](#bib.bib6). This tail
truncation significantly reduces the memory footprint while preserving
the performance. Integrating these components, the unified optimization
objective for SEPO is formulated as:

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SEPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\frac{1}{|\tau|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau|}\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{JSD}}^{\operatorname{Top}_{K}}\left(\tilde{\pi}_{\text{tea}}(\cdot\mid x,z_{\text{intrinsic}},\tau_{<t})\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\tau_{<t})\right)\right]\right].

### 3.2 Theoretical analysis of SEPO

#### SEPO achieves continuous self-improvement.

To demonstrate how SEPO achieves continuous self-improvement, we analyze
its training dynamics using a generalized accuracy lower bound [Kakade
and Langford (2002)](#bib.bib43); [Schulman et al. (2015)](#bib.bib44).
Let $`\mathcal{A}(\pi)`$ denote the expected accuracy of a policy.
During exploration of privileged context, SEPO leverages label $`y^{*}`$
to sample from the posterior reasoning distribution $`P(z\mid x,y^{*})`$
rather than the unguided prior $`P(z\mid x)`$. This posterior
conditioning exponentially prunes the vast search space, yielding a
clear capability advantage over the unhinted student at any training
step $`n`$. We formalize this advantage as the Exploration Gap:
$`\Delta^{n}=\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n})-\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n})`$.
During distillation, SEPO optimizes the student to minimize the
Jensen-Shannon Divergence. Let
$`\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}=\mathbb{D}_{\text{JSD}}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}^{n+1})`$
denote the residual exploitation error, which quantifies the inevitable
optimization imperfection remaining after step $`n`$. By relating JSD to
the Total Variation distance via information-theoretic bounds, this
residual error induces a worst-case performance penalty of
$`\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}})`$. Consequently, the
expected accuracy of the updated student policy at step $`n+1`$ is
bounded by:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n+1})\geq\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n})-\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}})\implies\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n+1})\geq\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n})+\Delta^{n}-\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}}).
``` |  |

As long as the exploration gap outweighs the exploitation error
($`\Delta^{n}>\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}})`$), the student
policy improves. Through SEPO’s synchronous evolution, teacher’s
capability elevates alongside the student, continuously regenerating a
substantial $`\Delta^{n}`$ to drive continuous self-improvement.
Ultimately, as the student’s intrinsic capabilities grow, the posterior
advantage of the label diminishes. Training converges to its empirical
upper bound when the exploration gap shrinks to the level of the
exploitation error
($`\Delta^{n}\approx\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}})`$).
Detailed theoretical analysis is in
Appendix [A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 SEPO’s Continuous Improvement ‣ Appendix A Theoretical Analysis ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation").

#### SEPO’s superiority over existing methods.

The recursive inequality explains the superiority of SEPO over other
OPCD methods, as SEPO uniquely optimizes both critical terms to maximize
the performance upper bound: 1) For methods using student’s own rollouts
as context, $`\Delta`$ is bottlenecked by the low prior probability of
directly sampling a valid solution. Consequently, their performance
ceiling is prematurely locked at the model’s unguided exploration limit.
In contrast, SEPO guarantees a more persistently positive $`\Delta`$. By
utilizing guided exploration, SEPO raises the upper bound from the
autonomous discovery limit to the posterior exploration limit—the true
extent of what the model can solve when accurately guided by the
outcome. 2) Methods using external solutions as context may initially
provide a high teacher accuracy, but they introduce severe support
mismatch between the expert’s latent manifold and the student’s weights.
This mismatch makes the exploitation error $`\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}`$
intrinsically enormous. When the exploitation error exceeds the
exploration gap, the performance gain vanishes, stagnating the
optimization. In contrast, SEPO’s strictly on-policy exploration
guarantees a minimal $`\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}`$, allowing the student to
absorb the teacher’s capability with little optimization friction.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental settings

We evaluate on reasoning tasks spanning diverse difficulties and
paradigms (knowledge, planning, and logic), which are widely employed as
standard testbeds to demonstrate method efficacy [Hübotter et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib6); [Shenfeld et al. (2026)](#bib.bib4); [Zhao et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib5):

- •
  Science Q&A: Undergraduate-level scientific reasoning tasks across
  Chemistry, Physics, Biology, and Material domains, using the L3
  subsets from SciKnowEval [Feng et al. (2024)](#bib.bib26).
- •
  Tool Use: Planning tool invocations based on user queries and provided
  tool specifications, utilizing the ToolAlpaca dataset [Tang et al.
  (2023)](#bib.bib27).
- •
  Logic: High-difficulty logical reasoning tasks, evaluated on two
  subsets (Arrow_Maze and Dyck_Language) from SynLogic [Liu et al.
  (2025a)](#bib.bib25).

We apply a train-test split for each dataset to assess in-domain
generalization. We employ models with diverse architectures,
specifically Qwen3-8B [Team (2025b)](#bib.bib22) and
Olmo3-7B-Instruct [Ettinger et al. (2025)](#bib.bib23), as well as Qwen3
models of varying scales. To ensure robust evaluation, all experimental
results are averaged across three independent runs with different random
seeds. We report the peak test accuracy (avg@16), measured as the
average over 16 sampled responses, achieved within a fixed time or
training step.

#### Baselines

We evaluate SEPO against GRPO and other OPCD methods. We implement an
enhanced GRPO baseline following [Hübotter et al. (2026)](#bib.bib6),
integrating several recent algorithmic improvements, such as asymmetric
clipping [Yu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib31) and off-policy data
correction [Yao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib29). We evaluate both on-policy
and off-policy variants of GRPO, and primarily report the results of the
better-performing off-policy version. For the OPCD baselines, we compare
three distinct teacher contexts: OPCD (Self) uses student’s own correct
rollouts [Hübotter et al. (2026)](#bib.bib6); OPCD (Expert) utilizes
reasoning processes from DeepSeek-V3.2, which yields the highest
performance among several candidate experts [Shenfeld et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib4); [Zhao et al. (2026)](#bib.bib5); [DeepSeek-AI
(2025b)](#bib.bib35); and additionally, we include OPCD (Label), a
simplified variant of SEPO that solely provides the ground-truth label
within the context. To ensure a fair comparison, we maintain identical
core training configurations across all methods, such as the global
training batch size and the number of sampled responses per prompt. And
all OPCD baselines use the same EMA teacher-update rule as SEPO. For
other hyperparameters, we perform a rigorous sweep, selecting the
configuration for each method that yields the highest validation
performance across all tasks. Comprehensive implementation and
hyperparameter settings are provided in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Implementation Details ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation").

### 4.3 SEPO overcomes severe exploration bottlenecks

Having demonstrated SEPO’s effectiveness on general reasoning tasks,
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Main results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")(b)
further illustrates the 16-hour training processes on Arrow Maze, a
highly complex logical reasoning task. In this significantly harder
setting, where the initial pass@16 of Qwen3-8B is strictly zero, both
GRPO and Self fail entirely. Their inability to overcome the exploration
bottleneck prevents them from even initiating effective training.
Although the Label and Expert methods manage to trigger training, their
performance gains are marginal and plateau after only a few steps. In
stark contrast, SEPO exhibits substantial and continuous performance
improvements. Its guided exploration successfully resolves the
zero-accuracy cold-start problem. By coupling this with the effective
exploitation of the explored correct reasoning, SEPO forms a robust
self-evolution cycle that stably enhances the model’s capabilities. In
Appendix [E.5](#A5.SS5 "E.5 Results on the Dyck Language Task ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation"),
we provide results on another challenging SynLogic task where Qwen3-8B’s
initial pass@16 is below 20. Although all baseline methods are able to
train normally in this regime, SEPO consistently achieves the best
performance.

### 4.4 SEPO achieves scale-invariant self-improvement

Figure 3: Highest avg@16 on Chemistry after 10h training across Qwen3

Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.4 SEPO achieves scale-invariant self-improvement ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
presents the peak performance achieved by different methods on the
Chemistry task after 10 hours of training, evaluated across Qwen3 model
scales ranging from 0.6B to 14B. As observed, SEPO consistently achieves
the best performance across all scales. Comparing OPCD baselines against
GRPO, a prominent trend emerges from 0.6B to 8B scales: the performance
advantage of OPCD widens as the model scale increases. This suggests
that the effectiveness of OPCD methods relies on the policy’s intrinsic
capacity. When scaling down to smaller models (e.g., 0.6B and 1.7B), all
OPCD baselines underperform GRPO, indicating models with limited
in-context integration capabilities struggle to effectively exploit
meaningful signals from self-generated reasoning, heavily
distribution-shifted demonstrations or sparse labels. Furthermore, the
Self method depends not only on model’s in-context ability but also on
its reasoning exploration capability, resulting in its severely poor
performance on small-scale models. In contrast, SEPO maintains a
significant performance advantage over GRPO across different model
sizes. This highlights a critical synergy in SEPO’s design: by resolving
the exploration bottleneck, it constructs congruent in-distribution
targets with a low assimilation threshold. This enables even lightweight
models to smoothly internalize correct reasoning patterns and achieve
continuous self-improvement.

## 5 Analysis

### 5.1 SEPO compresses reasoning and maintains exploration diversity

Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.1 SEPO compresses reasoning and maintains exploration diversity ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
(Left) illustrates the progression of response lengths on Chemistry
using Qwen3-8B. While GRPO’s lengths increase continuously, OPCD methods
maintain or even reduce them. At convergence, SEPO compresses reasoning
to be, on average, more than $`5\times`$ shorter than those of GRPO.
Although prevailing RLVR methods often rely on scaling solution lengths
to unlock advanced capabilities [DeepSeek-AI (2025b)](#bib.bib35);
[Muennighoff et al. (2025)](#bib.bib36), our results establish that
highly concise reasoning can also remain remarkably effective.

To investigate whether this reasoning compression restricts exploration
diversity,
Figure [4](#S5.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 5.1 SEPO compresses reasoning and maintains exploration diversity ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
(Right) plots the best@k performance at the peak avg@16 checkpoint.
Here, avg@16 (mathematically equivalent to expected best@1) reflects
model’s expected abilities, whereas best@16 indicates the capability
ceiling driven by exploration diversity. As observed, when k $`\geq`$ 4,
all OPCD methods underperform GRPO, revealing a limited sample variance.
However, this trade-off is highly favorable for SEPO, which sacrifices
merely 2 points in best@16 to secure a massive 10-point advantage in
avg@16. Furthermore, SEPO consistently achieves the highest best@k among
OPCD methods across all $`k`$ values, demonstrating its successful
preservation of crucial exploration capabilities despite its shortened
reasoning. By retaining this diverse solution space, SEPO ensures that
each training step continues to yield rich and effective supervision
signals, which fundamentally drives its accelerated training efficiency
and superior convergence performance.

### 5.2 SEPO cultivates self-reflective reasoning

Figure 5: An exemplary reasoning process of SEPO on a Chemistry case,
showing a natural self-reflective reasoning pattern.

Building on our quantitative observations of reasoning lengths, we
qualitatively analyze the underlying reasoning processes of different
methods. Appendix
Table [11](#A5.T11 "Table 11 ‣ E.4 Thinking Mode ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
presents a typical Chemistry case involving molecular weight
computation, and
Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 5.2 SEPO cultivates self-reflective reasoning ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
visualizes the simplified SEPO’s reasoning. All methods employ a common
forward-deduction strategy, directly calculating the answer and matching
it with the options. However, they diverge significantly in execution.
GRPO exhibits inconsistent atom counting, while Self and Expert derive
incorrect molecular formulas. Moreover, GRPO and Expert select answers
inconsistent with their own calculations, whereas Self obtains an
incorrect molecular formula whose calculated mass happens to match a
distractor. The Label baseline attempts to reconsider its answer but
repeatedly performs the same incomplete calculation without correcting
the error. In contrast, SEPO initially misinterprets the molecular
structure but subsequently revisits the SMILES representation, correctly
identifies the cyclopropane ring, and revises its atom counts to obtain
the correct molecular formula and answer. Notably, the label-guided
intrinsic teacher reasoning can also contain meaningful corrective

Together, these observations suggest that distilling reasoning-rich
teacher contexts may help the student internalize self-correction
behaviors, beyond merely learning to match final answers.

### 5.3 Ablation study

Figure 6: Impact of loss divergence and top-$`K`$ truncation on 10-hour
performance. JSD performs best and top-100 matches full-distribution
distillation.

#### Ablation on distillation objectives.

SEPO introduces critical adjustments to the standard on-policy
distillation (OPD) algorithm [Gu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib1); [Lu and
Thinking Machines Lab (2025)](#bib.bib2), specifically adopting the JSD
objective and restricting the divergence computation to the top-$`K`$
tokens.
Figure [6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 5.3 Ablation study ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
presents the 10-hour performance of Qwen3-8B on the Chemistry task
across various divergence metrics and $`K`$ values. For divergence
selection, JSD yields the highest performance, demonstrating the
advantage of a balanced, symmetric distillation. Furthermore, while
traditional OPD favors the mode-seeking Reverse KL, the mode-covering
Forward KL actually performs better in our framework. Regarding the
top-$`K`$ distillation, we observe that model performance plateaus when
$`K\geq 100`$. Since the long tail of vocabulary is largely
uninformative, this top-$`K`$ truncation effectively circumvents the
massive memory overhead associated with storing full logits for the
teacher and student, while preserving the distillation efficacy.

Figure 7: Impact of rollout group size on peak 1-hour and 10-hour
performance. Smaller size accelerates training but lower the ceiling,
yet any configurations outperform other methods.

#### Ablation on rollout group size.

In
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Baselines ‣ 4.1 Experimental settings ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation"),
we maintain a consistent rollout group size $`G`$ per prompt for fair
comparisons. Unlike GRPO and OPCD (Self), which rely on sufficiently
large rollout pools to discover correct solutions and formulate valid
optimization signals, SEPO does not strictly depend on large group
sizes.

indicating that short-term training efficiency is traded for a higher
convergence ceiling. Crucially, regardless of the group size, SEPO
consistently outperforms all baseline methods at both time scales,
underscoring its robustness.

#### Ablation on the teacher-update strategy.

We evaluate Qwen3-8B with three teacher-update strategies: fully dynamic
(synchronized with the current student after each training-batch
update), fixed (frozen at the initial policy), and EMA. Within SEPO, we
retain label-guided elicitation and distillation while varying the
update strategy; fixed teachers still generate label-conditioned context
online. We also evaluate OPCD (Self), the strongest baseline under EMA,
with a fixed teacher.
Table [2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Ablation on rollout group size. ‣ 5.3 Ablation study ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
reports the peak avg@16 achieved within 10 hours on three tasks. The
fully dynamic teacher performs substantially worse, suggesting that
directly tracking the current policy creates an unstable distillation
target. Fixed-teacher SEPO remains effective, and EMA further improves
both SEPO and OPCD (Self), supporting the benefit of gradual teacher
adaptation. Crucially, SEPO outperforms OPCD (Self) on all three tasks
under both fixed and EMA teachers. Thus, its advantage is not
attributable solely to a different teacher-update strategy; these
controlled comparisons support label-guided, policy-intrinsic context
construction as a key driver of its gains.

## 6 Related Work

### 6.1 Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

RLVR methods, such as STaR and GRPO [Zelikman et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3);
[Shao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16), are widely adopted in LLM
post-training to enhance reasoning capabilities [DeepSeek-AI
(2025a)](#bib.bib18); [Team (2025b)](#bib.bib22). To overcome the
challenges of sparse supervision and exploration bottlenecks [Cui et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib20); [Khalifa et al. (2026)](#bib.bib19), recent works
leverage external reasoning generated by humans or proprietary models,
which fall into two categories. (1) Integrating supervised fine-tuning
on expert solutions as an auxiliary loss [Zhang et al.
(2025a)](#bib.bib9); [Fu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib11). (2) Utilizing
expert data to guide RL exploration, achieved either by incorporating
expert rollouts into the GRPO group for joint learning [Fu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib11); [Yan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib10), or by extracting
expert prefixes to serve as initial contexts, facilitating the sampling
of correct solutions [Su et al. (2025)](#bib.bib12); [Qu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib13). Unlike these methods that rely on external
supervision, SEPO drives simultaneous improvements in training
efficiency and final performance entirely through self-evolution
distillation.

### 6.2 On-policy context distillation

Recent on-policy context distillation methods drive reasoning
improvement by augmenting the current policy’s context to construct a
stronger teacher for on-policy distillation [Hinton et al.
(2015)](#bib.bib34); [Gu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib1); [Lu and Thinking
Machines Lab (2025)](#bib.bib2); [Ye et al. (2026)](#bib.bib46). Various
contextual augmentations have been explored: some incorporate
environmental feedback, whose applicability is restricted to specific
tasks [Hübotter et al. (2026)](#bib.bib6), while others prompt for
simplified teacher responses to compress reasoning [Sang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib7). For effective reasoning self-improvement, methods
relying on correct student rollouts remain bottlenecked by the model’s
innate exploration limits, similar to GRPO [Hübotter et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib6). Alternatively, injecting external expert reasoning
into context suffers from insufficient exploitation of the off-policy
solutions [Shenfeld et al. (2026)](#bib.bib4); [Zhao et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib5); [DeepSeek-AI (2025b)](#bib.bib35). In contrast, the
self-explored, in-distribution reasoning within SEPO’s context overcomes
both the exploration bottleneck and mismatched-distribution
exploitation, simultaneously boosting training efficiency and final
performance.

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we develop Self-Evolution Policy Optimization (SEPO), an
on-policy self-distillation framework that drives continuous reasoning
evolution through superior exploration and exploitation. By using
ground-truth labels as privileged hints, SEPO elicits correct yet
strictly in-distribution reasoning from the policy and distills these
context-enriched predictions back into the policy. Theoretically, we
validate that SEPO mitigates both exploration failures and insufficient
exploitation issues, guaranteeing sustained capability evolution.
Empirically, experiments across diverse model architectures, scales, and
reasoning benchmarks demonstrate SEPO’s superior training efficiency and
final performance. Notably, in challenging scenarios where existing
baselines fail entirely, such as complex logical tasks or lightweight
models, SEPO robustly secures continuous improvements.

## Appendix A Theoretical Analysis

### A.1 Equivalence Between RLVR and OPCD

The Variational Perspective of RLVR Standard RLVR methods optimize a
policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ to maximize the expected reward while penalizing
the deviation from a reference policy $`\pi_{\text{ref}}`$. The
regularized RL objective can be formulated as:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[r(\tau)\right]-\eta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right)\right].
``` |  |

where $`\eta>0`$ is a temperature parameter controlling the KL penalty.
By taking the variational derivative of $`\mathcal{J}(\theta)`$ with
respect to $`\pi_{\theta}`$ and setting it to zero, the closed-form
optimal policy $`\pi^{*}`$ that maximizes this objective follows an
energy-based distribution:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{*}(\tau\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(\tau\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{r(\tau)}{\eta}\right),
``` |  |

where $`Z(x)=\sum_{\tau}\pi_{\text{ref}}(\tau\mid x)\exp(r(\tau)/\eta)`$
is the intractable partition function \[[13](#bib.bib38),
[26](#bib.bib39)\]. Substituting $`\pi^{*}`$ back into the objective
reveals that maximizing $`\mathcal{J}(\theta)`$ is mathematically
equivalent to minimizing the reverse KL divergence between the current
policy and the optimal policy:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\eta\log Z(x)-\eta\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x)\right).
``` |  |

Equivalence to the Conditional Success Distribution Crucially, the
partition function $`Z(x)`$ depends only on the reference policy
$`\pi_{\text{ref}}`$ and the reward environment, meaning it is entirely
independent of the active policy parameters $`\theta`$. Consequently,
the term $`\eta\log Z(x)`$ is a constant with respect to optimization.
Maximizing the RL objective is mathematically equivalent to minimizing
the trajectory-level divergence between the student and the optimal
policy:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\arg\max_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)\equiv\arg\min_{\theta}\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi^{*}(\cdot\mid x)\right).
``` |  |

To establish the probabilistic interpretation of $`\pi^{*}`$ in the
context of logical reasoning, we specialize the environment to binary
outcome rewards ($`r\in\{0,1\}`$). Under binary rewards, the exponential
term $`\exp(r(\tau)/\eta)`$ acts as a strict filtering mechanism. As
$`\eta\to 0^{+}`$, the probability mass of incorrect trajectories decays
to zero. Consequently, $`\pi^{*}`$ mathematically converges to the
normalized reference policy restricted to the domain of successful
trajectories, which equates precisely to the conditional distribution
(as grounded in the framework of control as
inference \[[18](#bib.bib32)\]):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{*}(\tau\mid x)\approx\pi_{\text{ref}}(\tau\mid x\mid\text{Success}).
``` |  |

Thus, RLVR’s true underlying objective is to pull the model toward its
own intrinsic distribution of successful reasoning paths.

OPCD as Direct Proxy Rather than relying on sparse rewards to
iteratively approach $`\pi^{*}`$, OPCD explicitly constructs a teacher
policy $`\pi_{\text{tea}}(\cdot\mid x,c)`$ using a privileged context
$`c`$ to simulate this conditional success distribution
($`\pi_{\text{tea}}\approx\pi^{*}`$). By substituting
$`\pi_{\text{tea}}`$ for $`\pi^{*}`$, OPCD minimizes their divergence
($`\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\text{tea}}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta})`$) via
dense, token-level supervision. However, the validity of this proxy
optimization hinges entirely on $`c`$ faithfully approximating the
natural conditional success distribution,
$`\pi_{\text{ref}}(\tau\mid x\mid\text{Success})`$. If the chosen
context induces exploration unavailability, severe distribution shifts
from external experts, or non-causal reasoning shortcuts, the
theoretical equivalence breaks down, rendering the distilled target
invalid.

### A.2 Theoretical Limitations of OPCD Baselines

The Exploration Bottleneck of Self-Generated Contexts When utilizing the
student’s own correct rollouts as the privileged context
($`c=\tau_{\text{self}}`$), the teacher distribution implicitly models
the conditional success distribution. For a given problem $`x`$, the
ability to construct $`\pi_{\text{tea}}`$ depends on the marginal
probability of sampling a successful solution from the current policy
$`\pi_{\theta}`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
P_{\text{self}}(x)=\int\pi_{\theta}(\tau\mid x)\mathbb{I}(r(\tau)=1)d\tau.
``` |  |

In practice, this is approximated by sampling $`G`$ rollouts. If the
task is sufficiently complex or the initial model is weak, the empirical
probability
$`\hat{P}_{\text{self}}(x)=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\mathbb{I}(r(\tau_{i})=1)`$
frequently evaluates to exactly $`0`$. Mathematically, conditioning on
an empty set $`\emptyset`$ renders the conditional probability
$`\pi_{\text{tea}}(\cdot\mid x,\tau_{\text{correct}})`$ undefined.
Consequently, the distillation gradient
$`\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CD}}(\theta)`$ becomes exactly
zero for these problems, completely depriving the model of learning
signals and resulting in a severe exploration bottleneck.

Static Ceilings and Exploitation Limitations of Expert Contexts To
bypass exploration failures, using expert demonstrations
($`c=\tau_{\text{expert}}\sim\pi_{\text{expert}}`$) guarantees context
availability. However, since these off-policy contexts are static, they
cannot co-evolve with the student’s optimization trajectory, which
inherently imposes a performance ceiling on continuous self-improvement.
Even worse, the out-of-distribution (OOD) nature of expert contexts
prevents the model from fully exploiting these demonstrations, causing
the actual performance to fall short of the theoretical ceiling. Let the
token-level distillation objective be defined by a divergence
$`\mathbb{D}`$. Because the expert possesses a fundamentally different
latent reasoning distribution, their support sets are frequently
disjoint
($`\text{supp}(\pi_{\text{tea}})\not\subseteq\text{supp}(\pi_{\theta})`$) \[[15](#bib.bib40),
[17](#bib.bib41)\]. This mismatch manifests in two extreme pointwise
scenarios: expert-exclusive actions $`a^{*}`$ where
$`\pi_{\text{tea}}(a^{*}\mid s_{t})\to 1`$ while
$`\pi_{\theta}(a^{*}\mid s_{t})\to 0`$, and student-intrinsic actions
$`a`$ where $`\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s_{t})>0`$ while
$`\pi_{\text{tea}}(a\mid s_{t})\to 0`$. When optimizing across these
disjoint supports, the model suffers from extreme penalties that distort
its natural behavior:

- •
  Forward KL (Forced Inclusion): For expert-exclusive actions $`a^{*}`$,
  the mode-covering penalty
  $`\pi_{\text{tea}}(a^{*})\log\frac{\pi_{\text{tea}}(a^{*})}{\pi_{\theta}(a^{*})}`$
  approaches infinity. To strictly minimize this massive penalty, the
  optimizer aggressively alters parameters to allocate probability mass
  to $`a^{*}`$. However, this merely forces the inclusion of isolated
  tokens without reconstructing the underlying causal reasoning chain.
- •
  Reverse KL (Zero-Forcing): For student-intrinsic actions $`a`$, the
  mode-seeking penalty
  $`\pi_{\theta}(a)\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a)}{\pi_{\text{tea}}(a)}`$
  also diverges to infinity. The optimizer suppresses the student’s
  natural probability ($`\pi_{\theta}(a)\to 0`$), eradicating the
  model’s intrinsic deductive stepping stones.

Ultimately, standard divergence metrics in OOD regions impose extreme
point-wise constraints rather than smoothly integrating the expert’s
trajectory into the student’s natural manifold. The student only
acquires specific, isolated patterns and fails to completely transition
into the expert’s coherent deductive mode. This severe
under-exploitation, compounded by static context, cements a strict
performance ceiling.

### A.3 SEPO’s Continuous Improvement

In this section, we provide the rigorous theoretical proof for the
accuracy lower bound of the student policy during the Self-Evolution
Policy Optimization (SEPO) process.

Let $`x\sim\mathcal{D}`$ be the problem sampled from the dataset, and
let $`Y^{*}(x)`$ denote the set of all valid reasoning trajectories or
correct final answers for $`x`$. The expected accuracy of a policy
$`\pi`$ is defined as the expected probability mass it assigns to the
correct
solutions:$`\mathcal{A}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{y\in Y^{*}(x)}\pi(y\mid x)\right].`$During
the distillation phase at step $`n`$, the student policy is updated from
$`\pi_{\theta}^{n}`$ to $`\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}`$ by minimizing the
Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) against the teacher
$`\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}`$. We define the residual exploitation error
$`\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}`$ as the expected JSD remaining after the
update:$`\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{D}_{\text{JSD}}\left(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}(\cdot\mid x)\right)\right].`$

Proposition 1 (Monotonic Improvement Bound). Assuming the residual
exploitation error is $`\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}`$, the expected accuracy
of the updated student policy $`\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}`$ is strictly bounded
by:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n+1})\geq\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n})+\Delta^{n}-\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}})
``` |  |

where
$`\Delta^{n}=\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n})-\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n})`$
is the capability advantage (exploration gap) provided by the teacher.
The proof proceeds in three steps:

Step 1: Bounding Accuracy Difference via Total Variation Distance. For
any given problem $`x`$, the difference in the probability of generating
a correct answer between the teacher $`\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}`$ and the
updated student $`\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}`$ is bounded by the Total Variation
distance between their respective distributions. By the definition of TV
distance, for any event $`A`$ (including the event of correctness
$`Y^{*}(x)`$):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
|\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}(Y^{*}(x)\mid x)-\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}(Y^{*}(x)\mid x)|\leq\mathbb{D}_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}(\cdot\mid x))
``` |  |

Taking the expectation over the data distribution $`x\sim\mathcal{D}`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
|\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n})-\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n+1})|\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{D}_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}(\cdot\mid x))\right]
``` |  |

Removing the absolute value yields the lower bound for the student’s
accuracy:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n+1})\geq\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n})-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{D}_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}(\cdot\mid x))\right]
``` |  |

Step 2: Relating Total Variation Distance to Jensen-Shannon Divergence
To connect the empirical distillation objective to the accuracy bound,
we utilize the information-theoretic relationship between TV distance
and JSD \[[19](#bib.bib15)\] . For any two probability distributions
$`P`$ and $`Q`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{D}_{\text{TV}}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2\cdot\mathbb{D}_{\text{JSD}}(P\,\|\,Q)}
``` |  |

(Note: If the base of the logarithm in JSD is $`2`$, the constant
$`2\ln 2`$ is replaced by $`2`$. For generality, we represent this
constant scaling as $`\mathcal{O}(\cdot)`$).Applying this inequality to
our policies for a specific $`x`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{D}_{\text{TV}}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}(\cdot\mid x),\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}(\cdot\mid x))\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2\cdot\mathbb{D}_{\text{JSD}}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}^{n+1}(\cdot\mid x))}
``` |  |

Taking the expectation over $`\mathcal{D}`$ and applying Jensen’s
Inequality (since $`f(u)=\sqrt{u}`$ is a concave function):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\mathbb{D}_{\text{TV}}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sqrt{2\ln 2\cdot\mathbb{D}_{\text{JSD}}}\right]\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\mathbb{D}_{\text{JSD}}]}
``` |  |

Substituting our definition of $`\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}[\mathbb{D}_{\text{TV}}]\leq\sqrt{2\ln 2\cdot\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}})
``` |  |

Step 3: Synthesizing the Final Recursive Bound. Substituting the bound
from Step 2 into the inequality from Step 1, we obtain the worst-case
performance penalty induced by the exploitation error:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n+1})\geq\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n})-\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}})
``` |  |

By definition, the exploration gap at step $`n`$ is
$`\Delta^{n}=\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n})-\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n})`$,
which implies
$`\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\text{tea}}^{n})=\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n})+\Delta^{n}`$.
Substituting this into the inequality, we arrive at the final recursive
bound:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n+1})\geq\mathcal{A}(\pi_{\theta}^{n})+\Delta^{n}-\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{JSD}}})
``` |  |

## Appendix B Limitations

### B.1 Expert Selection in Baselines

To ensure a rigorous evaluation of the OPCD (Expert) baseline, we
evaluate three highly capable models: GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-235B, and
DeepSeek-V3.2, to generate expert reasoning trajectories. We ultimately
select DeepSeek-V3.2 as the external expert, as it yields the highest
self-distillation performance. In our comparative analysis, OPCD
(Expert) exhibits rapid initial training efficiency but suffers from a
lower final performance ceiling. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that
employing an even stronger future expert, or an expert whose generative
distribution is more intrinsically aligned with the student model’s
preferences, might significantly alter the performance dynamics and
upper bounds of this baseline.

### B.2 Computational Overhead of the Elicitation Pipeline

Compared to the standard on-policy training loops of baseline methods,
the SEPO pipeline introduces an additional generation step: it requires
the teacher to conditionally elicit the reasoning process based on the
ground-truth label. Although SEPO ultimately achieves superior overall
training efficiency and convergence performance despite this added step,
exploring architectural or procedural optimizations to further
streamline this pipeline remains an important direction for future work.

### B.3 Evaluation at Larger Model Scales

Our experiments evaluate models up to 14B parameters. Although SEPO
performs favorably across the evaluated scales, whether these advantages
persist for larger models, such as 32B or 72B, remains unverified.
Evaluating SEPO at these scales is an important direction for future
work.

## Appendix C Broader Impacts

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed across diverse
industries, where robust in-domain reasoning capabilities are critical.
However, conventional RLVR methods frequently suffer from sparse
supervision signals and inherent exploration bottlenecks. Our method,
SEPO, offers an efficient and effective self-distillation framework to
reliably enhance these reasoning capabilities. This can significantly
accelerate the development of highly capable, domain-specific AI systems
while democratizing access by reducing reliance on expensive external
supervision.

Despite these positive impacts, SEPO also presents potential risks.
Because it enables autonomous self-improvement, malicious actors could
exploit this framework to accelerate model capabilities in harmful or
illicit domains (e.g., generating malicious code or synthesizing
dangerous chemical compounds). However, this risk is not unique to SEPO;
rather, it is a broader systemic challenge inherent to all algorithms
designed for generalized capability enhancement.

### D.1 SEPO’s Implementations

Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Appendix D Implementation Details ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
expands the high-level training loop in
Algorithm [3](#S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1 "Motivation ‣ 3 Method: Self-Evolution Policy Optimization ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation").
For every question in each batch, the student generates $`G=8`$ unhinted
rollouts, while the EMA teacher generates $`H=4`$ candidates using a
task-specific label-informed prompt. Teacher elicitation is performed
for all questions, rather than only when the student fails. We retain
candidates whose final answers pass the task verifier with reward at
least $`r_{\min}=1`$. This outcome-based filter does not certify the
correctness of intermediate reasoning. If no candidate is retained for a
question, all its student rollouts receive zero distillation weight for
that batch; no additional resampling is required. Otherwise, each
student rollout is independently paired with a uniformly sampled
retained candidate from the same question’s pool, with replacement. We
use JSD with mixing coefficient $`\lambda=0.5`$. At each response
position, the student selects the top-$`K`$ token indices ($`K=100`$),
and both distributions are evaluated on this shared support. Each
distribution’s remaining probability mass is aggregated into one
additional tail category. To stabilize updates against distribution
shifts, we apply a token-level importance sampling correction, clipping
the importance-sampling ratios at $`2.0`$. Finally, the teacher model is
updated via an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the student’s weights
with an update rate of $`0.05`$.

We implement our training pipeline on top of the verl reinforcement
learning framework \[[32](#bib.bib28)\], utilizing PyTorch Fully Sharded
Data Parallel (FSDP2) distributed across 8 H20 GPUs. During the rollout
phase, which is accelerated by vLLM \[[16](#bib.bib45)\], we sample
$`G=8`$ student rollouts per prompt at a temperature of $`1.0`$. To
accommodate extended reasoning traces, we set a maximum prompt length of
2048 tokens and a maximum response length of 22,528 tokens. The policy
is optimized using AdamW with a peak learning rate of
$`1\times 10^{-5}`$, zero weight decay, and a linear warmup over the
first 10 steps. Each training step processes a global batch of 32
prompts, which is partitioned into mini-batches of 32 for policy
optimization.

### D.2 Enhanced GRPO Implementation

Following \[[10](#bib.bib6)\], we implement an enhanced GRPO baseline
that integrates several recent algorithmic improvements. These include
asymmetric clipping \[[42](#bib.bib31)\], the avoidance of biased
normalization \[[21](#bib.bib30)\], and off-policy data
correction \[[40](#bib.bib29)\].

### D.3 Baseline Implementations

The implementation of OPCD (Self) follows the methodology
of \[[10](#bib.bib6)\]. To construct the self-teacher, each rollout is
conditioned on a distinct successful rollout randomly selected from the
same group. For OPCD (Expert), we adapt the frameworks established
in \[[31](#bib.bib4), [46](#bib.bib5)\]. To construct the expert
dataset, we sample 8 reasoning trajectories per problem and randomly
retain one successful rollout to serve as the context. We try three
highly capable reasoning models to generate these expert trajectories:
GPT-OSS-120B, Qwen3-235B, and DeepSeek-V3.2. We ultimately select
DeepSeek-V3.2 as our primary external expert, as it yields the highest
self-distillation performance. The OPCD (Label) baseline serves as an
ablated variant of the SEPO framework, where the label-guided deduction
mechanism is explicitly removed (i.e., the teacher model is merely
conditioned on the label, but is not prompted to perform guided
deduction toward it). Following an extensive hyperparameter search, the
optimal hyperparameters for all OPCD baselines align with those
established for SEPO. For both the on-policy and off-policy variants of
GRPO, the optimal learning rate is $`1\times 10^{-5}`$. In addition, for
a controlled comparison, SEPO and all three OPCD baselines use an
EMA-updated teacher initialized from the same initial student weights.

## Appendix F Case Study

### F.1 Example of Intrinsic Teacher Context

To illustrate the privileged context $`z_{\mathrm{intrinsic}}`$,
Table [10](#A5.T10 "Table 10 ‣ E.4 Thinking Mode ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
presents a label-conditioned teacher reasoning trace for a Chemistry
question about hydrogen-bond acceptors. The teacher initially identifies
three candidate acceptors, including both nitrogen atoms and the
carbonyl oxygen. Upon reconsideration, it correctly excludes the
terminal amide nitrogen, whose lone pair is delocalized into the
adjacent carbonyl group, and identifies the non-amide nitrogen and
carbonyl oxygen as the two acceptors. This example illustrates that
$`z_{\mathrm{intrinsic}}`$ can provide meaningful intermediate reasoning
and explicit corrective steps, offering richer contextual guidance than
the ground-truth label alone.

### F.2 Comparison of Student Reasoning Trajectories

Appendix
Table [11](#A5.T11 "Table 11 ‣ E.4 Thinking Mode ‣ Appendix E Additional Experimental Results ‣ Self-Evolution Reasoner: Continuous Optimization via Policy-Intrinsic Exploration and Exploitation")
compares representative reasoning trajectories generated by different
student policies after 100 training steps on a Chemistry problem
involving molecular weight calculation. GRPO initially recognizes the
cyclopropane ring but subsequently produces inconsistent atom counts,
leading to an incorrect answer. Self and Expert also derive incorrect
molecular formulas, with Self obtaining a mass that happens to match an
incorrect option and Expert selecting an option inconsistent with its
own calculation. The Label baseline attempts to reconsider its answer
but repeatedly performs the same incomplete calculation without
correcting the underlying error. In contrast, SEPO initially
misinterprets the molecular structure as containing a six-membered ring,
but subsequently revisits the SMILES representation and correctly
identifies the three-membered cyclopropane ring. It then revises the
atom counts to obtain the correct molecular formula, C7H11NO2, yielding
a molecular weight of 141.168 g/mol and selecting the correct answer.
This case illustrates that SEPO can exhibit meaningful structural
correction within its reasoning process, rather than merely matching the
final answer.
````

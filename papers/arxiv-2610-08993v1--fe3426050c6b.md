###### Abstract

As large language models become more powerful, self-evolving agents are
able to tackle challenging tasks including AI for machine learning
(AI4ML). In AI4ML, while empirical verification is available, it often
requires computationally costly model training and evaluation, limiting
the speed and scale of agent evolution. Yet verification efficiency
remains underexplored, and frontier models provide only limited gains
when used directly as idea selectors. We address this gap with
specialized _idea-level critic models_ that predict whether a proposed
ML modification will improve upon the current solution, allowing agents
to screen ideas and concentrate verification resources on the most
promising candidates. We train the critic models through supervised
fine-tuning on high-quality critiques synthesized by Gemini-3.1-Pro,
followed by GRPO to further improve their predictive accuracy.
Empirically, our critic models outperform Gemini-3.1-Pro in static idea
evaluation, and these gains extend to agent inference, continual
learning, and policy training. During inference-time evolution, they
improve final solution quality under the same verification budget by
selecting more promising ideas, with further gains from continual
learning. During policy training, they serve as learned reward models,
reserving empirical verification for uncertain cases and enabling
substantially more policy updates with the same verification resources.
Together, these results show that idea-level critic models help ML
agents discover better solutions and learn stronger proposal policies
under limited verification budgets.

## 1 Introduction

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have spurred growing
interest in self-improving and evolutionary agents, which iteratively
propose modifications and retain successful variants based on empirical
feedback ([Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib7); [Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib3); [Sharma, 2025](#bib.bib8)). These efforts demonstrate
unprecedented success on challenging tasks such as AI for machine
learning (AI4ML), where agents develop and refine ML solutions through
implementation and empirical evaluation ([Huang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib5); [Chan et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Lu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10)). However, AI4ML presents a distinctive verification
challenge: while available, empirical verifiers often require
computationally costly model training and evaluation. This execution
cost accumulates across candidates and evolutionary rounds, directly
limiting how many alternatives an agent can explore under a fixed
compute budget. Addressing this issue by simply using a frontier model
to screen candidates offers limited relief: in our OpenEvolve
experiments, directly prompting Gemini-3.1-Pro as an idea selector
yields only modest gains
(Section [4](#S4 "4 Integration with Self-Evolving Agents ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents")).
These limitations motivate us to ask: can we train specialized models to
assess proposed ML modifications before empirical verification,
reserving expensive execution for those most likely to improve the
current solution?

![Refer to caption](2610.08993v1/paper_overview_solid_borders.png)

Figure 1: We train an idea-level critic model from ML agent evolution
trajectories through SFT on soft-rubric critiques followed by GRPO. The

training by serving as a learned reward model while routing uncertain

In this work, we develop a training pipeline for specialized _idea-level
critic models_ that screens proposed ML improvements before
verification, reducing reliance on computationally costly empirical
verification. Given a task description, a parent solution, and a
proposed child modification, the critic model predicts whether the
modification will improve upon the parent. To train the critic model, we
collect realistic idea-evolution trajectories from live ML agent runs
and label parent–child pairs using their empirical execution outcomes.
We then use Gemini-3.1-Pro ([Google Gemini Team, 2026](#bib.bib34)) to
synthesize critiques, retain critiques consistent with the
execution-derived labels through rejection sampling, and train the
critic model through supervised fine-tuning followed by GRPO ([Shao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib31)). The critiques follow a soft rubric-based
reasoning format: the critic model compares the parent and child under
every task-specific criterion while retaining flexibility in weighting
the criteria and combining their judgments. Our ablations show that this
structure supports continued improvement during further RL alignment,
whereas unconstrained reasoning leads to performance degradation. We
hold out a portion of the collected data to form ML-Idea-Critic-Bench, a
static benchmark for measuring training gains and comparing the
idea-level assessment capabilities of different models. On this
benchmark, our Qwen-3.5-9B and Qwen-3.5-35B critic models achieve
three-way directional accuracies of 60.7% and 63.3%, respectively,
substantially outperforming Gemini-3.1-Pro at 46.6%.

We evaluate how these predictive gains translate into improvements in
agent inference, continual learning, and policy training. First,
integrated into OpenEvolve ([Sharma, 2025](#bib.bib8)) with
Gemini-3.1-Pro as the idea proposer, the critic models screen candidate
ideas before verification, improving final solution quality on
MLS-Lite-Bench under matched verification budgets. Second, continual
learning allows the critic models to adapt using empirical feedback
accumulated during the current evolution run, further improving the
final solutions. Third, while critic model improves evolution by
prioritizing the most promising candidates, the quality of ideas
generated remain constraint by the fixed proposer. We therefore extend
the critic model from selecting among existing ideas to improving the
idea-proposing policy itself. Here, the critic model serves as a learned
reward model for GRPO training of the idea-proposing policy, assigning
rewards to confident predictions and routing uncertain cases to
empirical verification. This reduces the real-verification cost during
training, enabling 2.75$`\times`$ as many policy updates under the same
verification budget. In summary, our main contributions are below:

- •
  We establish an effective training pipeline for idea-level critic
  models from real ML agent evolving trajectories. Through fine-tuning
  from soft-based reasoning critiques, our critic model outperforms both
  their base models and Gemini-3.1-Pro in static idea evaluation.
- •
  We show that directly prompting an off-the-shelf frontier model,
  Gemini-3.1-Pro, as an idea selector yields only limited gains in
  OpenEvolve, whereas our trained critic models improve inference-time
  evolution under fixed verification budgets by concentrating empirical
  verification on the most promising proposals.
- •
  We extend the use of critic models from idea selection to policy
  training by using their predictions as rewards and reserving empirical
  verification for uncertain cases. This reduces verification cost and
  enables more policy updates under a fixed verification budget.

## 2 Building Criterion-Guided Idea-Level Critic Models

### 2.1 Task Setting

#### Self-evolving ML agent framework.

We consider an ML agent that iteratively improves a solution through
proposal, implementation, and empirical verification. At round $`r`$,
given a task $`t`$ and a parent program $`P_{r}`$, the idea proposer
generates a modification plan $`z_{r}`$. The implementer translates this
plan into a child program $`C_{r}`$ while preserving the proposed
modification. An empirical evaluator $`E_{t}`$ measures solution
quality, and the agent retains the better program:

|     |                         |                                                                                                       |     |     |
| --- | ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle z_{r}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\operatorname{Propose}(t,P_{r}),\qquad C_{r}=\operatorname{Implement}(P_{r},z_{r}),`$ |     | (1) |

and the parent is updated as child if child achieves better performance,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
P_{r+1}=C_{r}\text{ if }E_{t}(C_{r})>E_{t}(P_{r})\text{ else }P_{r}.
``` |  | (2) |

Repeating this loop allows the agent to accumulate improvements, but
verifying each candidate can require computationally costly model
training and evaluation. We show an example of such framework in
Appendix [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 ML Agent Pipeline Examples ‣ Appendix D Templates and Outputs Examples ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").

#### Critic Model-Augmented ML Agent.

Our goal is to predict the outcome of empirical verification from
idea-level descriptions, enabling candidate screening before execution.
To achieve this, we augment the agent pipeline with a critic model, as
illustrated in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").
To provide clear and neutral inputs, a summarizer converts the parent
and child programs into concise descriptions $`p_{r}`$ and $`a_{r}`$ of
their implemented approaches, removing exploratory reasoning, redundant
details, and persuasive claims from the idea proposer’s output. At a
high level, the critic model parameterized by $`\theta`$ uses
$`(t,p_{r},a_{r})`$ to predict the execution-derived outcome:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{(t,P_{r},C_{r})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\Pr_{\theta}\!\left(\operatorname{Critic}_{\theta}(t,p_{r},a_{r})=y_{r}\right)\right],y_{r}=\begin{cases}\textsc{Improve},&E_{t}(C_{r})>E_{t}(P_{r}).\\
\textsc{Tie},&E_{t}(C_{r})=E_{t}(P_{r}).\\
\textsc{Lose},&E_{t}(C_{r})<E_{t}(P_{r}).\end{cases}
``` |  | (3) |

Here, $`\mathcal{D}`$ denotes the distribution of task–parent–child
instances. These predictions allow the agent to prioritize promising
candidates for empirical verification.

### 2.2 Criterion-Guided Critic Model Design

To make idea-level evaluation task-aware, we structure the critic
model’s reasoning around a set of task-specific criteria. The critic
model must compare the parent and child solutions under each criterion
before reaching a final verdict. For each ML task, we use
Claude-4.8-Opus ([Anthropic, 2026](#bib.bib55)) in a multi-round probing
process to analyze the task setup, data, evaluation metric, and modeling
constraints. This process produces a detailed task understanding for
task $`t`$ and a fixed set of task-specific criteria, which are
generated once and reused across all examples from the same task.
Accordingly, each critic model input contains the task description,
parent solution, proposed child solution, task understanding, and
task-specific criteria, with example shown in Appendix
 [D.2](#A4.SS2 "D.2 Task understanding. ‣ Appendix D Templates and Outputs Examples ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").

#### Soft-rubric reasoning.

The critic model follows a soft-rubric reasoning template. It first
analyzes every criterion and compares the parent and child solutions
under that criterion, then freely determines the relative importance of
the criteria and aggregates its analyses into a final verdict. We call
this design a *soft rubric* because the critic model must evaluate the
fixed criteria but remains unrestricted in its analysis and weighting
strategy.
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Complete Prompt Instructions ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents")
and
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Soft-Rubric Reasoning Examples ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents")
provide the complete prompt instructions and a reasoning example,
respectively.

### 2.3 Training Data and ML-Idea-Critic-Bench Construction

#### Dataset overview.

The dataset contains approximately 28K execution-labeled examples:
approximately 12.5K in the SFT/evaluation pool and 15.5K in the GRPO
training pool. We divide the former into approximately 11K SFT training
examples and 1.5K static evaluation examples, which constitute
ML-Idea-Critic-Bench. The evaluation set contains roughly 500 examples
from each of the three teacher-assistance strata defined below.

#### ML idea-evolution trajectories.

We collect parent–child pairs from MLE-Dojo ([Qiang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib1)) and MLS-Bench ([Lyu et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)), with
Gemini-3.1-Pro generating both candidate ideas and their
implementations. Initial parents are drawn from the baseline
implementations provided by MLS-Bench or constructed as basic executable
solutions for MLE-Dojo. Each trajectory example is represented as
$`x_{i}=(t_{i},p_{i},a_{i},y_{i})`$, comprising a task description
$`t_{i}`$, a parent idea $`p_{i}`$, a child idea $`a_{i}`$, and an
outcome label $`y_{i}`$ derived from execution. An execution first
yields one of three outcomes: Improve, Tie, or Lose. To provide more
precise targets for critique synthesis in the SFT/evaluation pool, we
use the empirical distribution of child performance relative to the
parent to further divide Improve into BigWin, MediumWin, and SmallWin,
yielding the five-way label space

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
y_{i}\in{\textsc{BigWin},\textsc{MediumWin},\textsc{SmallWin},\textsc{Tie},\textsc{Lose}},
``` |  |

which captures both the direction and magnitude of the child’s
performance change relative to the parent. The GRPO pool retains the
three-way outcome labels and is balanced across these classes by random
downsampling. Detailed collection and label-construction are provided in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Data Collection and Label Construction ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").

#### Teacher critique synthesis and difficulty strata.

To collect high-quality SFT data, we use Gemini-3.1-Pro to generate
soft-rubric reasoning critiques whose final predictions follow the same
five-way label space. We first sample 8 critiques independently without
revealing the execution-derived label $`y_{i}`$. An example is assigned
to the *Sampled* stratum if at least one of these critiques predicts
$`y_{i}`$ correctly, in which case we retain one matching critique. If
all 8 predictions are incorrect, we identify the most frequently
predicted incorrect label, provide a hint explicitly ruling out that
option, and sample another 8 critiques. An example is assigned to the
*Revised* stratum if at least one of these new critiques predicts
$`y_{i}`$ correctly. If none succeeds, we reveal $`y_{i}`$ and require
Gemini-3.1-Pro to generate reasoning consistent with the correct
outcome; these examples form the *Guided* stratum. The resulting
SFT/evaluation pool contains three levels of increasing teacher
assistance and example difficulty: *Sampled* (easy, 44.4%), *Revised*
(hard, 31.6%), and *Guided* (harder, 23.9%).

### 2.4 Critic Model Training

#### Supervised fine-tuning.

We first fine-tune the critic model on approximately 11K
Gemini-generated critiques collected using the procedure above,
reserving the remaining 1.5K examples for static evaluation. By learning
to produce both criterion-level analyses and final verdicts, the critic
model learns the basic ability to evaluate ML proposals and follow the
soft-rubric reasoning instructions.

#### Further Alignment with GRPO.

Starting from the SFT checkpoint, we further train the critic model with
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib31)) on an additional 15.5K execution-labeled examples not
used for SFT. We use a binary reward: a rollout receives $`+1`$ if its
predicted three-way verdict matches the execution-derived label and
$`-1`$ otherwise. Details of training are presented in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G Experiment Setup Details ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").

## 3 ML-Idea-Critic Bench Result

#### Metrics.

We report four complementary metrics. Improve Accuracy evaluates the
binary decision of whether the child improves over its parent. Dir-3
measures three-way accuracy over *Improve*, *Tie*, and *Lose*. F1
reports the F1 score for the binary improvement decision. Finally,
5-Ladder evaluates the exact match accuracy over \[ BigWin \> MediumWin
\> SmallWin \> Tie \> Lose \].

We compare our critics against Gemini-3.1-Pro, the base Qwen-3.5-9B and
Qwen-3.5-35B models, and DeepSeek-GRM-16B/27B ([Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24)). DeepSeek-GRM is a generalist generative reward model
trained with Self-Principled Critique Tuning to generate evaluation
principles and critiques before assigning scores. We use the released
DeepSeek-GRM-16B¹¹ 1
[https://huggingface.co/BBQGOD/DeepSeek-GRM-16B](https://huggingface.co/BBQGOD/DeepSeek-GRM-16B)
and DeepSeek-GRM-27B²² 2
[https://huggingface.co/BBQGOD/DeepSeek-GRM-27B](https://huggingface.co/BBQGOD/DeepSeek-GRM-27B)
checkpoints, based on DeepSeek-V2-Lite and Gemma-2-27B, respectively. As
shown in
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Metrics. ‣ 3 ML-Idea-Critic Bench Result ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents"),
SFT improves the Dir-3 accuracy of the 9B and 35B critics to $`55.6\%`$
and $`60.3\%`$, surpassing Gemini-3.1-Pro ($`46.6\%`$). GRPO further
improves their accuracy to $`60.7\%`$ and $`63.3\%`$, corresponding to
gains of $`5.1\%`$ and $`3.0\%`$ over their SFT checkpoints and
$`14.1\%`$ and $`16.7\%`$ over Gemini.

#### Ablation: Freestyle vs. Soft-Rubric Reasoning.

The two reasoning formats perform similarly after SFT, with Dir-3
accuracies of $`54.6\%`$ for freestyle reasoning and $`55.6\%`$ for
soft-rubric reasoning. Under GRPO, however, the freestyle critic
deteriorates to $`40.3\%`$, whereas the soft-rubric critic improves to
$`60.7\%`$. Qualitatively, the freestyle critic develops shorter
analyses and superficial shortcuts, while the required
criterion-by-criterion comparisons stabilize reinforcement learning.

## 4 Integration with Self-Evolving Agents

### 4.1 Inference-Time Idea Selection

#### OpenEvolve setup.

We integrate the critic model into OpenEvolve ([Sharma,
2025](#bib.bib8)) to screen candidate ideas before empirical
verification. OpenEvolve is an evolutionary coding framework for
recursive self-improvement that iteratively proposes, implements, and
evaluates modifications as executable solutions. In this setup,
Gemini-3.1-Pro serves as the idea proposer, implementer, and solution
summarizer through three separate model calls. Each mutation follows the
pipeline illustrated in
Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents"):
children are compared with their parent at the idea level, and child
idea is empirically verified only after the screening from critic model:
The idea proposer Gemini-3.1-Pro is allowed to propose 16 ideas. For
each idea, the critic model will make 16 reasoning critiques, and only
the idea with the highest votes on win will be verified. For OpenEvolve
setup, we maintain three islands and evolve each island for 10 rounds,
yielding a total budget of 30 empirical verification runs per task.
Additional details about the OpenEvolve configuration are provided in
Appendix [I.1](#A9.SS1 "I.1 OpenEvolve Framework ‣ Appendix I OpenEvolve Evaluation Details ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").
More details about critic model’s idea-selection strategy are provided
in
Appendix [I.2](#A9.SS2 "I.2 Critic-Guided Candidate Selection ‣ Appendix I OpenEvolve Evaluation Details ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").

#### Screening settings and evaluation tasks.

We compare seven settings that differ only in how candidate ideas are
screened. *None* denotes vanilla OpenEvolve without idea-level
screening. *Gemini-3.1-Pro* and *Gemini-3.5-Flash* use the corresponding
pretrained models as critic models. *Base-9B* and *Base-35B* use the
original Qwen-3.5-9B and Qwen-3.5-35B models, respectively, whereas
*Critic-9B* and *Critic-35B* use our corresponding critic models trained
with SFT and GRPO. We evaluate all settings on MLS-Lite-Bench, which
contains 30 ML development tasks spanning 12 domains. Because every
setting obtains a score of zero on rl-value-discrete, the benchmark’s
only reinforcement learning task, we exclude this non-discriminative
task from aggregate reporting and present results over the remaining 29
tasks across 11 domains. The complete task configuration, domain
grouping, and score normalization procedure are described in
Appendix [I.3](#A9.SS3 "I.3 Task Descriptions ‣ Appendix I OpenEvolve Evaluation Details ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").

We investigate whether adapting an idea-level critic during agent
evolution improves the final solution quality under a fixed verification
budget. We consider two strategies: test-time Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO) ([Yuksekgonul et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)) and
Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning (SDFT) ([Shenfeld et al.,
2026](#bib.bib33)). We pick 4 tasks from MLS-Lite-Bench to evaluate both
strategies, under the same OpenEvolve setup in
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Inference-Time Idea Selection ‣ 4 Integration with Self-Evolving Agents ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").
Both methods start from our trained critic model checkpoint, keep its
backbone parameters frozen, and optimize a LoRA adapter with rank 32.
The updates use only outcomes obtained from the empirical verifications
already performed by OpenEvolve and therefore require no additional
verifier calls.

#### Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning.

Self-Distillation Fine-Tuning (SDFT) is introduced by [Shenfeld et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib33) for continual learning from demonstrations. SDFT
uses the same underlying model as both student and teacher, but provides
the teacher with privileged context and distills its token-level
predictions into the student along trajectories generated by the student
itself. We adapt this idea to test-time alignment by treating the raw
scores of parents and verified children as privileged teacher
information. At every prefix of the student-generated critique, the
teacher produces a next-token distribution, which is the fixed target
for updating the student. We minimize the resulting token-level
reverse-KL loss over the critique trajectory. We perform one SDFT update
after every OpenEvolve round using the three newly verified parent–child
pairs, one from each island.

#### Test-time GRPO.

TTT-Discover ([Yuksekgonul et al., 2026](#bib.bib32)) formulated
scientific discovery as test-time RL, in which a policy model updates
its parameters using experience collected while solving the problem.
Following this paradigm, we apply GRPO to the critic model during
OpenEvolve. We perform one update every 2 evolution rounds, collecting 6
newly verified parent–child pairs across the three islands. For each
pair, we sample 16 critic model rollouts and assign each rollout a
reward of +1 if its predicted three-way outcome matches the empirical
label and -1 otherwise.

As shown in
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.2 Test Time Continual Learning ‣ 4 Integration with Self-Evolving Agents ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents"),
SDFT improves downstream evolution more consistently than test-time
GRPO. One possible explanation is the limited information in binary
correctness rewards combined with the changing outcome distribution
during evolution. When all 16 GRPO rollouts receive the same reward,
their group-relative advantages vanish, providing no learning signal
from the correctness reward. As the parent solution improves, successful
modifications can also become rarer, encouraging the critic model to
rely on the increasing prevalence of Tie/Lose rather than distinguish
promising candidates. Such behavior may achieve high prediction accuracy
while providing little value for candidate selection. SDFT instead
supplies outcome-conditioned token-level targets along the student’s
reasoning trajectory, offering a richer adaptation signal than verdict
correctness alone. Our results align with existing work ([Shenfeld et
al., 2026](#bib.bib33)) that this form of supervision can better support
candidate selection and improve final solution quality under a limited
verification budget.

## 5 The Critic Serving as Reward Model

While critic models help OpenEvolve select more promising ideas, its
performance remains constrained by the proposal capabilities of the
fixed policy model. We therefore investigate whether critic model
predictions can provide useful training signals to improve the policy
model’s ability to generate better ideas.

### 5.1 GRPO with Critic-in-Loop

We further investigate whether the trained critic model can serve as a
learned reward model for aligning the idea-proposing policy. We
initialize the policy from the base Qwen-3.5-9B and Qwen-3.5-35B model
and optimize it using GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib31)) across the
same 95 MLE-Dojo and 46 MLS-Bench training environments used for critic
GRPO training. Each training step collects 16 parents, each with 16
rollout ideas, yielding 256 candidate ideas per step. The reward is
binary: a solution receives $`+1`$ for an Improve outcome and $`-1`$
otherwise. We consider two approaches, (1) baseline approach
*verify-all*, where every candidate is empirically evaluated; and (2)
*critic-in-loop* approach, where trained critic models serve as a reward
model and replace real verification signals occasionally, depending on
the critic’s uncertainty on the candidate idea. Specifically, we use
Critic-9B checkpoint serving base Qwen-3.5-9B and Critic-35B checkpoint
serving base Qwen-3.5-35B respectfully. The critic models keep their
parameters frozen throughout policy training. For each candidate $`a`$,
let
$`v_{y}(a)=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{k=1}^{16}\mathbb{I}[\tilde{y}_{k}(a)=y]`$
denote the fraction of critic model rollouts $`\tilde{y}_{k}(a)`$
predicting label $`y`$. We define uncertainty as
$`u(a)=1-\max_{y}v_{y}(a)`$, namely, the fraction of rollouts that
disagree with the majority. If at most three of the 16 rollouts disagree
($`u(a)<0.25`$), we use the majority verdict to calculate reward;
otherwise, we send the candidate to the ground-truth verifier.

### 5.2 Policy Model GRPO Result

For a controlled comparison, each method receives a total budget of
10,000 ground-truth verifications across all training environments. With
256 candidates per training step, *verify-all* exhausts this budget in
approximately 40 steps. With critic-guided reward assignment, only
$`36.3\%`$ of policy-generated ideas require empirical verification,
allowing approximately 110 steps under the same budget. This corresponds
to a $`2.75\times`$ improvement in verification efficiency, measured as
the number of candidate ideas assigned training rewards per ground-truth
verification. This ratio does not measure wall-clock speedup or total
compute savings, since critic inference incurs additional computation
cost.

We evaluate the resulting policies directly on the MLS-Lite-Bench tasks.
As shown in
Figure [3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.2 Policy Model GRPO Result ‣ 5 The Critic Serving as Reward Model ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents"),
critic-in-loop GRPO improves the average normalized domain score from
19.57 to 25.21 for the 9B model and from 19.29 to 24.55 for the 35B
model compared with verify-all GRPO. These results suggest that using
critic-model predictions for confident cases allows the saved
verification budget to support additional policy updates and thereby

## 6 Related Work

#### Self-Evolving Agents and ML Agents.

ML and self-evolving agents iteratively propose, implement, and evaluate
solutions using empirical feedback ([Huang et al., 2023](#bib.bib5);
[Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib9); [Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib7);
[Sharma, 2025](#bib.bib8)). Existing approaches improve this process
through search and harness design with fixed pretrained models ([Toledo
et al., 2025](#bib.bib48); [Yan et al., 2026b](#bib.bib50); [Yamada et
al., 2025](#bib.bib51)), or by learning proposal policies from
feedback ([Zhang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib52); [Yan et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib53)). Our work complements these directions by learning
to evaluate proposed modifications before costly implementation and
execution.

#### AI for Machine Learning Research.

AI-assisted research systems coordinate literature review,
experimentation, and manuscript preparation ([Schmidgall et al.,
2025](#bib.bib60); [Tang et al., 2025](#bib.bib61)), while related work
studies automated paper reviewing ([Tang et al., 2026](#bib.bib69); [Liu
et al., 2026a](#bib.bib68)) and research-idea evaluation ([Qiao et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib35); [Moussa et al., 2025](#bib.bib36)). Closer to our
setting, pretrained language model-based evaluation modules assess ML
candidates and prioritize promising ones for execution ([Foster et al.,
2026](#bib.bib45); [Zheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib54)). We instead make
the evaluator itself the primary learned component, training an
idea-level critic model to predict the empirical value of proposed ML
modifications.

#### Critic Models and Generative Reward Models.

Conventional reward models typically assign scalar scores to model
outputs ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib26); [Lambert et al.,
2025](#bib.bib27)). Critic models and generative reward models produce
textual assessments that make the basis of their judgments more
explicit ([Saunders et al., 2022](#bib.bib28); [McAleese et al.,
2024](#bib.bib29); [Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Chen et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib42)). These assessments support direct output
evaluation ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib23); [Ke et al.,
2024](#bib.bib20)), actionable feedback for iterative refinement ([Wang
et al., 2023](#bib.bib22); [Xie et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib18)), and ranking among sampled candidates ([Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24); [Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Saha et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30)). Rubric-guided approaches further structure
evaluations across multiple criteria ([Viswanathan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16); [Gunjal et al., 2026](#bib.bib17); [Guan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib38); [Qiu et al., 2026](#bib.bib40)). Related work
evaluates intermediate reasoning steps ([Uesato et al.,
2022](#bib.bib14); [Lightman et al., 2024](#bib.bib13)) or uses learned
evaluations as reward signals for policy optimization and
co-evolution ([Shan et al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Li et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib12); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib11); [Zhang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib6)). We focus on proposed modifications to existing ML
solutions, whose ground-truth value requires empirical execution. Our
critic model learns from empirical parent–child outcomes, supporting
both inference-time candidate selection and policy training.

More discussion on related work is in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B More Related Work ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").

## 7 Conclusion

We introduced idea-level critic models as an efficient feedback
mechanism for self-improving ML agents together with a training pipeline
combining soft-rubric reasoning, SFT, and GRPO. Our critic model not
only achieve strong performance in static idea evaluation, but also
improve agents across inference-time idea selection, task-specific
continual learning, and critic-guided policy optimization. By
identifying promising ideas, the critic model enables agents to use
limited verification budgets more effectively. As a learned reward
model, the critic model also performs $`2.75\times`$ more
policy-optimization steps under the same budget. Overall, our results
establish idea-level critic model as a general and practical interface
between idea generation and expensive execution, enabling more efficient
ML agent learning and evolution.

## Appendix B More Related Work

#### Self-Evolving Agents and ML Agents.

ML agents automate ML development by translating task specifications
into executable solutions, running training and evaluation pipelines,
interpreting empirical results, and iteratively revising their
approaches ([Huang et al., 2023](#bib.bib5); [Chan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4); [Qiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib1); [Jiang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib9); [Qiao et al., 2026b](#bib.bib56); [Yu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib58); [Yu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib67)). Closely related
self-evolving and recursively self-improving agents repeatedly modify
solutions using feedback from previous iterations ([Lu et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10); [Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib3); [Novikov et al.,
2025](#bib.bib7); [Sharma, 2025](#bib.bib8)). These systems commonly
follow a propose–implement–execute–refine loop, and recent work
increasingly targets the generation and selection of promising
candidates before execution. Some approaches improve the surrounding
agent harness while keeping the pretrained model fixed, using structured
search and refinement to explore stronger solutions ([Toledo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib48); [Nam et al., 2025](#bib.bib49); [Yan et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib50); [Yamada et al., 2025](#bib.bib51)); others learn
proposal policies directly from feedback ([Zhang et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib52); [Yan et al., 2026a](#bib.bib53)).

#### AI for Machine Learning Research.

LLM-based agents increasingly automate machine learning research, from
literature review and ideation to experimentation, analysis, and
manuscript preparation ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib10); [Schmidgall et
al., 2025](#bib.bib60); [Tang et al., 2025](#bib.bib61)). Related
systems connect idea generation to execution, introduce falsification,
and support collaborative or sustained research ([Li et al.,
2024](#bib.bib63); [Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib64); [Schmidgall and Moor,
2025](#bib.bib65); [Weng et al., 2026](#bib.bib66)). Recent work further
explores research automation at scale and iterative improvement through
human feedback and accumulated experience ([Tang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib69); [Liu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib68)). A complementary
line develops AI systems for paper reviewing through multi-agent
feedback, aspect-conditioned generation, structured questioning, and
rubric-guided evaluation ([D’Arcy et al., 2024](#bib.bib70); [Gao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib71); [Gao et al., 2025](#bib.bib62); [Chang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib72); [Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib73)). Learned reviewing
models also support iterative refinement of research outputs ([Weng et
al., 2025](#bib.bib74)), while empirical studies examine the effects of
LLM use in peer review ([Kim et al., 2026](#bib.bib59)). Closer to our
setting, pretrained language model-based evaluation modules assess
unverified ideas or candidate ML solutions to prioritize empirical
execution ([Weng et al., 2026](#bib.bib66); [Zheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib54); [Foster et al., 2026](#bib.bib45)). Our work focuses
on training an execution-grounded idea-level critic model to predict the
empirical value of proposed ML modifications. The critic model evaluates
each modification relative to an existing solution and learns from
outcome labels derived from empirical parent–child comparisons. We
develop a training recipe for this prediction task and apply the
resulting critic model to candidate selection during inference-time
evolution and reward provision during policy optimization.

#### Critic Models and General Reward Models.

Conventional reward models compress the quality of a model output into a
scalar score, providing limited insight and often struggling with
open-ended problems whose success depends on multiple interacting
factors ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib26); [Lambert et al.,
2025](#bib.bib27)). Critic models and general reward models instead
generate reasoning-style analyses before reaching a final judgment ([Yao
et al., 2022](#bib.bib57)), making their evaluations more interpretable
and allowing them to produce more reliable, task-general feedback ([Liu
et al., 2025](#bib.bib24); [Saunders et al., 2022](#bib.bib28); [Zheng
et al., 2023](#bib.bib23); [McAleese et al., 2024](#bib.bib29); [Zhang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Chen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib42)). Existing
work uses such generative evaluators in several related ways. At
inference time, they can directly assess completed outputs ([Zheng et
al., 2023](#bib.bib23); [Ke et al., 2024](#bib.bib20)), provide
actionable feedback for iterative refinement ([Saunders et al.,
2022](#bib.bib28); [McAleese et al., 2024](#bib.bib29); [Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib22); [Xie et al., 2025](#bib.bib19); [Yang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib18); [Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)), or rank multiple
sampled candidates so that only the most promising response is
returned ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib24); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21); [Saha et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)). In structured
scenarios, rubric-guided critiques further regularize multi-dimensional
evaluations and mitigate evaluation degradation ([Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24); [Viswanathan et al., 2025](#bib.bib16); [Gunjal et
al., 2026](#bib.bib17); [Wang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib15); [Guan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib38); [Wang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib39); [Qiu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib40); [Chen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib41); [Yu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib44)). Other approaches formulate generative critics more
explicitly as value models: they estimate the quality or expected return
of intermediate states and trajectories ([Uesato et al.,
2022](#bib.bib14); [Lightman et al., 2024](#bib.bib13)), and their
judgments can be converted into learning signals for policy
optimization, including PPO-style training and adversarial
co-evolution ([Shan et al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Wang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib15); [Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib12); [Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib11); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib6)). These methods have
primarily been developed to evaluate language or reasoning outputs
produced by policy models. A separate line of work evaluates research
idea ([Qiao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib35); [Moussa et al.,
2025](#bib.bib36); [Falck et al., 2026](#bib.bib37); [Liu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib46); [Wang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib47)) by constructing
methodology on human level ideas, which are more tailored to
AutoResearch and human-AI collaboration research, which is beyond the
scope of our paper.

## Appendix C Data Collection and Label Construction

### C.1 Task Environments and Initial Parent Solutions

We construct our data from selected task environments in
MLE-Dojo ([Qiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)) and MLS-Bench ([Lyu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)). MLS-Bench provides source code and baseline
implementations for each task, which we directly use as the initial
parent solutions. MLE-Dojo provides executable task environments but
does not include comparable starter solutions. For each selected
MLE-Dojo task, we therefore prompt Gemini-3.1-Pro to generate a basic
runnable solution, execute it in the corresponding environment, and use
its resulting score as the initial parent score.

### C.2 Trajectory Collection and Label Construction

#### SFT/evaluation pool: two-stage collection.

We construct the SFT/evaluation pool using a two-stage procedure. In the
first stage, we run ten sequential evolution steps on each selected
task, starting from its initial executable solution. The solution
produced at each step is retained as a parent for the second stage. In
the second stage, we independently propose and execute 20 child
solutions for each retained parent, with all 20 children evaluated
against the same parent. Gemini-3.1-Pro generates the ideas and
implements the solutions in both stages. The resulting parent–child
pairs form the candidate pool for SFT and static evaluation.

#### Parent-specific labels.

For each parent $`p`$, we compute the signed relative improvements of
its executed children, orienting the task metric so that positive values
indicate better performance than the parent. We exclude infeasible child
implementations and parents with fewer than three improving children.
The child outcomes associated with each retained parent provide the
empirical mean $`\mu_{p}`$ and standard deviation $`\sigma_{p}`$ used to
construct the five-level labels.

#### GRPO pool: improvement-driven collection.

We collect GRPO training examples using a separate iterative procedure
across the designated training tasks. At each step, Gemini-3.1-Pro
proposes and implements one child solution from the current parent. If
empirical evaluation shows an improvement, the child becomes the parent
for the next step; otherwise, the current parent is retained. This
procedure collects examples across a changing set of parents as
successful modifications accumulate.

The initial GRPO pool contains approximately 25K execution-labeled
examples, with an imbalanced distribution of Improve, Tie, and Lose
outcomes. We randomly downsample within outcome classes to obtain a
balanced training set of 15,669 examples, comprising 5,223 examples per
class, as imbalanced distribution will cause critic model GRPO training
to always output the majority label. This pool is used for critic GRPO
training and is separate from the SFT/evaluation pool.

### C.3 Parent-Specific Label Thresholds, Dataset Statistics, and Label Distribution

The same absolute improvement can have different significance depending
on the quality and difficulty of the current parent solution. We
therefore construct parent-specific labels. For each parent $`p`$, we
generate and execute 20 child solutions and compute their signed
relative improvements $`\Delta_{p,1},\ldots,\Delta_{p,20}`$, orienting
each task metric so that a positive value always indicates improvement.
We filter out parents that have less than 3 improved children solutions.
We approximate the resulting improvement distribution for each parent
with a Gaussian whose empirical mean and standard deviation are denoted
by $`\mu_{p}`$ and $`\sigma_{p}`$. The standardized improvement of child
$`j`$ is

|     |                                                  |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       z_{p,j}=\frac{\Delta_{p,j}-\mu_{p}}{\sigma_{p}}.
       ```                                               |     |

We assign the five-level label in descending order, using the first
condition that applies:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
y_{p,j}=\begin{cases}\textsc{BigWin},&\Delta_{p,j}>0\ \text{and}\ \left(\Delta_{p,j}>0.25\ \text{or}\ z_{p,j}>2\right),\\
\textsc{MediumWin},&\Delta_{p,j}>0\ \text{and}\ \left(\Delta_{p,j}>0.10\ \text{or}\ z_{p,j}>1\right),\\
\textsc{SmallWin},&\Delta_{p,j}>0\ \text{and}\ z_{p,j}>0.2,\\
\textsc{Tie},&\Delta_{p,j}\geq 0\ \text{or}\ -0.2\leq z_{p,j}\leq 0.2,\\
\textsc{Lose},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  |

The absolute relative-improvement thresholds identify large gains even
for parents whose child scores have high variance, while the
standardized thresholds adapt the labels to the local difficulty of
improving each parent. The Gaussian approximation is used only to
compute the parent-specific standardized score; the final labels are
assigned deterministically using the rules above.

After removing infeasible child solutions, the resulting SFT dataset.
The five labels in SFT dataset account for 25.4% BigWin, 17.5%
MediumWin, 15.5% SmallWin, 13.6% Tie, and 28.0% Lose.

## Appendix G Experiment Setup Details

### G.1 Supervised Fine-Tuning Configuration

We train both critic model sizes for three epochs using Low-Rank
Adaptation (LoRA) with rank 32. We use a learning rate of
$`1\times 10^{-5}`$ for all SFT experiments and run for 3 epochs.

### G.2 GRPO Configuration

For both 9B and 35B models, starting from the SFT checkpoints, we run
GRPO for 300 rollout steps, with each step processing 256 idea inputs.
For each idea, the critic generates a group of eight responses. We
discard groups in which all responses receive the same reward, as these
groups contain no relative learning signal for GRPO. We use the
temperature of 0.7, learning rate of $`1\times 10^{-6}`$, weight decay
of 0.1, and KL coefficient of 0.01.

### G.3 Policy GRPO Training Details

We initialize the idea-proposing policy from both Qwen-3.5-9B and
Qwen-3.5-35B and train it on 95 MLE-Dojo and 46 MLS-Bench environments.
For critic-guided reward assignment, we use the frozen Critic-9B and
Critic-35B checkpoint obtained after SFT and GRPO for training idea
proposer Qwen-3.5-9B and Qwen-3.5-35B respectfully. Each training step
collects 16 parent-conditioned groups, with 16 candidate ideas per
group. The initial parent for each task is its seed program. Whenever
all 16 candidates in a group receive an Improve verdict (i.e., an
assigned reward of $`+1`$), we randomly select one child to become the
task’s new parent; otherwise, the parent remains unchanged. Each method
receives a total budget of 10,000 ground-truth verifications across all
training environments.

The policy generates only candidate ideas, while Gemini-3.1-Pro is
responsible for implementing ideas and summarizing implementations. If a
solution is selected to be verified, for non-timeout execution errors,
Gemini-3.1-Pro is allowed up to three repair attempts. Both the initial
implementation and subsequent repairs must strictly follow the proposed
idea: Gemini-3.1-Pro is instructed to resolve implementation errors
without introducing new algorithmic choices or modifying the substantive
proposal.

For both 9B and 35B models, the critic-in-loop checkpoints are trained
for around 110 steps while the verify-all checkpoints are trained for
around 40 steps, targeting the 10,000 verification budget. We use
learning rate of $`1\times 10^{-}6`$ and weight decay of 0.1. The
temperature used is 0.7.

## Appendix I OpenEvolve Evaluation Details

### I.1 OpenEvolve Framework

#### Evolutionary search.

OpenEvolve ([Sharma, 2025](#bib.bib8)) is an evolutionary coding
framework for recursive self-improvement. It begins with an executable
seed program and maintains a population of previously evaluated
solutions. At each evolutionary round, OpenEvolve samples a parent
solution, together with additional high-performing or diverse solutions
that serve as inspirations, and prompts a language model to propose a
modification. The resulting child program is executed by a task-specific
evaluator, which returns empirical performance metrics and execution
feedback. The evaluated child is then added to the population and can be
selected as a parent or inspiration in subsequent rounds. Repeating this
process allows improvements discovered in earlier rounds to be
accumulated into progressively stronger solutions.

#### Population and island management.

OpenEvolve combines a quality-diversity archive based on MAP-Elites with
an island-based population structure. MAP-Elites partitions candidate
solutions according to configurable behavioral or structural features
and retains a high-performing solution within each occupied region,
preserving diverse approaches rather than optimizing only a single
lineage. Multiple islands maintain semi-independent populations and
therefore explore different evolutionary trajectories. High-performing
solutions are periodically migrated between islands, allowing useful
discoveries from one population to influence the others while reducing
premature convergence. Parent selection balances exploitation of
high-performing solutions with exploration of diverse or less frequently
sampled regions of the population. OpenEvolve also tracks the best
verified solution separately so that it is preserved throughout
evolution.

#### Configuration in our experiments.

For each task and screening setting, we maintain three islands and
evolve each island for 10 rounds. Exactly one selected idea is
implemented and empirically verified in each island round, resulting in
a total budget of 30 verification runs per task. We set migration
interval as 4(migration happens every 4 evolution rounds). The idea
proposer is using temperature of 0.8, and idea and implementation
summarizer is using temperature of 0.4. All critic models are using
temperature of 0.7. All settings begin from the same seed implementation
and use the same proposer, implementer, summarizer, population
configuration, and verification budget; they differ only in whether and
how candidate ideas are screened before execution.

### I.2 Critic-Guided Candidate Selection

At each evolutionary round, Gemini-3.1-Pro generates a pool of 16
candidate ideas conditioned on the current parent solution. For each
candidate, the critic generates 16 critiques comparing it with the
parent and predicts one of five outcome labels. We rank candidates by
their predicted labels in the order
$`\textsc{BigWin}>\textsc{MediumWin}>\textsc{SmallWin}>\textsc{Tie}>\textsc{Lose}`$
and calculate an average rank for each candidate idea. Only the
candidate idea with the highest average rank for execution.
Consequently, exactly one of the 16 candidates consumes a real
verification run, while the remaining candidates are filtered without
executing their training pipelines.

We apply the same candidate-generation and selection procedure when
using Gemini-3.1-Pro, the base Qwen-3.5-9B/35B models, or our trained
Critic-9B/35B as the selector. Vanilla OpenEvolve does not apply this
critic-based screening stage.

#### MLS-Bench-Lite.

MLS-Bench ([Lyu et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)) evaluates whether an ML agent
can improve a targeted component of an existing machine learning system.
Each task provides a fixed research scaffold, relevant source code,
baseline implementations, a constrained edit surface, and an executable
evaluator. The agent must propose and implement an algorithmic
modification that improves performance across the task’s prescribed
evaluation settings. These tasks therefore test not only whether an
agent can produce executable code, but also whether it can identify ML
modifications that yield robust empirical improvements. We use
MLS-Bench-Lite, the official 30-task subset of the full 140-task
benchmark. The subset preserves coverage of all 12 MLS-Bench domains:
language models, robotics, vision and generation, reinforcement
learning, ML systems and efficient ML, AI for science, optimization and
theory, classical and adaptive learning, deep learning, time series and
forecasting, structured and causal reasoning, and trustworthy learning.
The 30 tasks cover a broad range of ML development problems, including
model architecture design, training objectives, optimization algorithms,
inference procedures, data processing, and scientific modeling. The
complete task list and domain assignments are provided in
Table [13](#A9.T13 "Table 13 ‣ I.3 Task Descriptions ‣ Appendix I OpenEvolve Evaluation Details ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").

#### Scoring.

Because the tasks use heterogeneous evaluation metrics, MLS-Bench
defines a task-specific scoring specification for converting raw
evaluation results into normalized task scores. These specifications
account for metric direction, task-specific baseline anchors,
theoretical or practical performance bounds, and, when applicable,
multiple evaluation settings. We use the official scoring specification
for each task without modification. We aggregate the normalized task
scores within each domain using the arithmetic mean and report the
resulting domain-level performance in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Screening settings and evaluation tasks. ‣ 4.1 Inference-Time Idea Selection ‣ 4 Integration with Self-Evolving Agents ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents").

#### Overlap with critic model training environments.

A subset of the MLS-Bench-Lite tasks also appears among the task
environments used to collect training data for our critic models. We
retain these tasks rather than removing them because our goal is to
evaluate the complete official MLS-Bench-Lite suite and preserve its
intended cross-domain composition.

### J.2 Ablation on the Critic Model as Reward Model

We investigate whether the trained critic can support policy
optimization without empirical verification during training. Starting
from the base Qwen-3.5-35B policy, we perform GRPO using rewards
provided entirely by the frozen Critic-35B, with no fallback to the
ground-truth verifier. For each candidate idea, we aggregate $`k`$
critic rollouts by majority voting over Improve, Tie, and Lose,
assigning a reward of $`+1`$ to Improve and $`-1`$ otherwise. We conduct
two sweeps: varying the number of policy training steps from 0 to 160,
and varying $`k\in\{1,4,8,16,32\}`$ while fixing training to 40 steps.

Figure [5](#A10.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ J.2 Ablation on the Critic Model as Reward Model ‣ Appendix J More details on Critic-in-loop RL ‣ Verify Less, Evolve More: Training Idea-Level Critics for Verification-Efficient ML Evolving Agents")
shows that policy performance generally improves as the number of
training steps increases on both ML Clustering Algorithm task and Graph
Generation task. The trend is particularly clear for Graph Generation,
indicating that rewards from critic model continue to provide a useful
learning signal even without access to empirical verification during
training. Increasing the number of critic model rollouts $`k`$ also
improves performance on AI4Bio Mutation Effect Prediction task and Graph
Generation task, but the gains are comparatively modest and begin to
saturate. In particular, increasing $`k`$ beyond 16 provides limited
additional benefit while proportionally increasing inference
computational cost. We therefore use $`k=16`$ as a practical trade-off
between reward reliability and critic model inference computational
cost.
````

Identifying and solving partial differential equations (PDEs) from
sampled solution data often requires substantial expert effort in
equation discovery, coefficient estimation, and numerical solver
configuration, especially under noise and distribution shifts. Can large
language model (LLM) agents automate this workflow accurately and
efficiently? To investigate this question, we introduce RSI-PDEbench, a
benchmark of 1,001 cases spanning 42 equation templates, including both
classical and fractional PDEs. It provides three evaluation settings:
Full evaluates performance on noise-free solution data; Noisy tests
robustness to Gaussian noise with an RMS equal to 50% of the
clean-signal RMS; and Hard-OOD tests challenging out-of-distribution
generalization to unseen equation templates and shifted coefficient
ranges. We further introduce RSI-PDE, a recursively self-improving agent
harness that shortlists candidate equations by residual loss, uses
relative $`L^{2}`$ feedback to refine coefficients and numerical
solvers, and applies a validation gate to policy and memory updates. We
compare RSI-PDE with single-run baselines and native agent harnesses
(Claude Code or Codex) across four frontier models. Across the three
settings, RSI-PDE improves the pass rate averaged across the four models
by 106.5–181.9% relative to single-run baselines and by 3.0–19.6%
relative to native harnesses, while reducing total recorded model calls
by 46.9% across the 12 model–setting pairs. These results highlight the
potential of validated recursive self-improvement in LLM agents to
advance scientific computing.

## 1 Introduction

Scientific observations rarely arrive with a fully specified governing
equation. Sparse field measurements and known forcing or boundary
conditions may leave both the physical law and its coefficients
uncertain. Noise and fractional dynamics make this identification
harder. An accurate discretization of the wrong equation is
insufficient, and a correct family with inaccurate coefficients can
still produce a poor solution.

Coding agents can explore these possibilities through numerical
experiments, but executable code alone is not evidence of scientific
accuracy. Existing datasets support learning PDE solution operators and
discovering equations from data ([Takamoto et al., 2022](#bib.bib6);
[Gupta and Brandstetter, 2022](#bib.bib7); [Hao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8); [Ohana et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Rudy et al.,
2017](#bib.bib2)). CodePDE studies solver synthesis for specified
equations ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib12)), and PDEAgent-Bench provides
645 specified-PDE solver-generation instances ([Hang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib37)). We ask whether an agent can instead identify an
unknown PDE or fractional PDE (FPDE), estimate its parameters and
construct an accurate solver within a limited development budget.

We introduce RSI-PDEbench, a collection of 1,001 released PDE and FPDE
cases across 42 templates for this identify-then-solve task. To our
knowledge, it is the largest joint PDE/FPDE benchmark for coding agents
tasked with equation-family identification, parameter estimation and
numerical solver construction. This scope concerns agent problem
instances; it does not compare the number of simulated trajectories in
general-purpose PDE learning datasets. The agent receives a finite
equation library, 256 spatial–temporal samples and known forcing,
initial and boundary functions, while the true family and coefficients
remain hidden for a new case. This is a controlled analogue of
scientific system identification, with classical, nonlinear,
multiphysics and fractional dynamics in a common executable interface.

Three complementary settings expose distinct sources of uncertainty.
Full tests all 42 templates with noise-free observations. Noisy adds
Gaussian perturbations at 50% of the clean-signal RMS. Hard-OOD combines
unseen templates and coefficients shifted beyond development ranges.
Together, they probe instance generalization, observation robustness and
transfer to unfamiliar physics or parameters. Their held-out counts are
distinct from the 1,001-case release.

The RSI-PDE harness organizes exploration through residual guidance and
reference-based validation. Inside a sandbox, the agent substitutes a
reconstructed or computed field into candidate equations, fits
coefficients and retains up to five low-residual families. Forward
solves and public relative $`L^{2}`$ feedback then distinguish
hypotheses and refine coefficients and discretizations. A gate retains
improvements to the solver, its instruction policy and memory, while the
foundation model and executable supervisor stay fixed. Residuals narrow
the family search; relative $`L^{2}`$ tests assess the resulting
solution. Hidden references never guide online equation choice.

We compare single-run programs, native harnesses (Claude Code or Codex),
and RSI-PDE using Opus 5.5, GPT-5.5, GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5. Across the
four models, RSI-PDE increases mean pass rate over single-run baselines
by 181.9%, 140.2% and 106.5% on Full, Noisy and Hard-OOD, and over
native harnesses by 3.0%, 19.6% and 5.4%, respectively. Recorded model
calls sum to 1,884 rather than 3,547 across the 12 comparisons, a 46.9%
reduction, although calls and dollar costs are not lower in every
comparison. [Figure 1](#S0.F1) shows the final counts and learning
curves for Opus 5.5 and GPT-5.5. These are descriptive results from the
reported runs; replication, adaptive benchmark reuse and cost provenance
are discussed in [Section 6](#S6 "6 Discussion and Limitations").

Our contributions are:

- •
  A large joint PDE/FPDE benchmark: 1,001 released cases spanning 42
  equation templates, with analytic or high-accuracy numerical
  references and quantitative solution checks.
- •
  Three diagnostic evaluation settings: Full, Noisy and Hard-OOD
  separately test noise-free accuracy, robustness to 50% Gaussian sample
  noise, and unseen templates with shifted coefficients.
- •
  An unknown-family, unknown-parameter task: agents must explore a
  finite physical hypothesis space, estimate coefficients and return an
  executable solver from sparse observations and known inputs.
- •
  A residual-guided improvement harness: equation residuals prioritize a
  five-candidate shortlist, while public relative $`L^{2}`$ feedback and
  validation retain improvements to equation selection, coefficient
  estimation, numerical solution, policy and memory.

([Section 2](#S2 "2 Benchmark Overview")), dataset construction

([Section 4](#S4 "4 RSI-PDE Harness")) and experiments
([Section 5](#S5 "5 Experiments")). Related work and implementation

## 2 Benchmark Overview

### 2.1 Task scope: unknown equation and unknown coefficients

A case is generated from

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{N}_{k,\theta}[u](x,t)=f(x,t),\qquad(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T],
``` |  | (1) |

with the corresponding steady or ordinary-differential specialization.
The hidden index $`k`$ selects a PDE/FPDE from the supplied class
library $`\mathcal{K}`$, and $`\theta`$ denotes its unknown physical
coefficients, including fractional-order parameters where applicable.
The agent must infer both before choosing and implementing a suitable
numerical method. Template names in the library define the allowed
hypothesis space; they do not reveal the class of an individual hidden
case.

This formulation captures a difficult part of practical scientific
modeling: observations can fit several candidate laws, and model
mismatch, parameter error and discretization error can look similar. It
remains a controlled finite-library task, rather than unrestricted
discovery of an arbitrary physical law. The benchmark makes these
uncertainties explicit so that exploration and numerical repair can be
evaluated together.

### 2.2 Agent-facing inputs and executable interface

The public case view contains the domain, known callable forcing,
initial and boundary functions, evaluation-grid specifications, the
equation library, and 256 spatial–temporal samples,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}_{u}=\{(z_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{256},\qquad y_{i}=u^{\star}(z_{i})+\epsilon_{i}.
``` |  | (2) |

Here $`z_{i}=(x_{i},t_{i})`$ denotes a sample location, specialized to
spatial-only or time-only coordinates where appropriate. Full sets
$`\epsilon_{i}=0`$; the other settings perturb observed values as
described in
[Section 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Full, Noisy and Hard-OOD settings ‣ 3 Dataset Construction").

An entrant writes solve(problem). Its program identifies a candidate
class and coefficients and returns a numerical solution on its chosen
grid, or at prescribed points for the Elliptic equation problem. The
evaluator maps the returned field to its evaluation grid. Class and
coefficient estimates are diagnostic outputs; a correct class name
without an accurate field does not pass. The entrant can choose
classical, learned or hybrid numerical methods within the sandbox and
resource contract.

Training diagnostics can reveal class and coefficient labels. Validation
returns numerical diagnostics but not the hidden class or coefficients.
Hidden test labels, reference arrays and scores are inaccessible during
agent execution. A retained program must therefore identify each new
test case from its public inputs, without selecting an equation using
that case’s hidden reference error.

### 2.3 Accuracy, executability and reference solutions

The reference field $`u^{\star}`$ defines the numerical scoring target.
For analytic cases, the evaluator computes its values directly at
evaluation nodes. Other references are produced with refined meshes,
suitable higher-order discretizations or time integration, and strict
solver tolerances. Fine/coarse calculations mapped to a common grid
check self-convergence. Reference generation and thresholds are fixed
independently of an entrant’s solver;
[Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C Reference Construction and Calibration")
gives representative settings and their limitations.

Let $`\mathcal{I}\hat{u}`$ be the submitted solution interpolated to the
reference grid. For time-dependent problems,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
E_{\mathrm{st}}=\frac{\|\mathcal{I}\hat{u}-u^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])}}{\|u^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])}},\qquad E_{T}=\frac{\|\mathcal{I}\hat{u}(\cdot,T)-u^{\star}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\|u^{\star}(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}.
``` |  | (3) |

The norms use the evaluator’s quadrature weights; steady and point-cloud
cases use their corresponding norms. The submitted program passes only
if both applicable errors are at most $`\tau=2\times 10^{-3}`$, initial
and boundary conditions satisfy the prescribed $`10^{-10}`$ check, the
returned arrays meet the contract, and the solve meets runtime and
memory limits. The Elliptic equation problem has a separately fixed
$`5\times 10^{-2}`$ tolerance because its reference discretization is
less accurate. The model does not receive the hidden reference arrays.
Public training and validation errors are computed by the supervisor.

Pass count and pass rate summarize end-to-end success. Relative
$`L^{2}`$ diagnostics distinguish numerical accuracy from execution or
contract failure, while model calls, tokens, validation submissions and
dollar cost characterize development effort. Class labels and parameter
estimates help diagnose failures but are not substitutes for the
solution-error criterion.

### 2.4 Budgeted development and frozen testing

A complete experiment combines three development sub-runs organized by
solver-library group. Each receives the allowance in
[Table 1](#S2.T1 "In 2.4 Budgeted development and frozen testing ‣ 2 Benchmark Overview");
groups may run in parallel. Across three groups the declared ceilings
are 1,200 model calls, 120 M prompt tokens, 4.5 M output tokens and 90
validation submissions. The two-hour allowance applies to each group.
Aggregate usage can therefore exceed a single group’s 400-call limit.

|                                             |                         |
|---------------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| Resource                                    | Cap                     |
| Development wall-clock                      | 2 h                     |
| Model requests                              | 400                     |
| Prompt tokens, including cache reads/writes | 40 M                    |
| Output tokens                               | 1.5 M                   |
| Validation submissions                      | 30                      |
| Solver resources                            | 1 CPU core + 1 H100 GPU |

Table 1: Declared budget per development sub-run. All aggregate resource
figures sum the three sub-runs before averaging repeated runs. Final
hidden evaluation is outside the development budget.

A proxy records model identity and usage. Model-written code runs in a
separate sandbox with bounded imports, filesystem access, network
access, runtime and memory. Agents can request training experiments
within the development budget; a validation submission is counted when
evaluation starts. The benchmark-native wrapper retains accepted
programs using the reference-error gate.
[Section 4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Validation, retention and feedback ‣ 4 RSI-PDE Harness")
describes the RSI-PDE retention mechanism, including its public-pass
rule.

After development, an independent evaluator scores the frozen retained
program on hidden cases. For a combined learning curve at round $`r`$,
each group contributes its retained program after $`r`$ validation
submissions, or its final program if it stopped earlier. Thus a round is
a submission index, not a common elapsed-time budget, and summed group
submissions differ from the final index of a combined curve. Hidden
checkpoint scores are assembled for reporting and do not return to the
running agent.

## 3 Dataset Construction

### 3.1 Case inventory and equation coverage

RSI-PDEbench releases 1,001 cases and covers 42 templates. A template
specifies an equation and a construction of coefficients, forcing,
initial/boundary data and reference solution; an individual case
instantiates that construction. Multiple templates may share one
equation family, so 42 templates does not mean 42 unrelated physical
laws. The catalog contains 20 classical and fractional templates, 10
nonlinear or multiphysics templates, and 12 data-driven or
additional-dimensional templates
([Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Templates")).

Classical coverage includes Poisson, heat, convection–diffusion, Burgers
and wave equations. Fractional coverage includes a Caputo ODE,
time-fractional diffusion, multi-term fractional dynamics and orders
varying in time or space–time. These problems test whether
identification routines can distinguish local dynamics from
memory-dependent operators and estimate their coefficients or orders.
Nonlinear and multiphysics coverage includes Kuramoto–Sivashinsky,
Navier–Stokes, Cahn–Hilliard, Maxwell and time-harmonic
electromagnetics. Additional templates include Darcy, Helmholtz,
Allen–Cahn, two-dimensional Burgers and advection–diffusion–reaction
equations.

Spatial dimensions are one or two, with the fractional ODE as the
zero-spatial-dimensional specialization. The collection mixes
manufactured solutions, freely evolving initial conditions, sharp
layers, multiscale fields and nonlinear dynamics. Five templates reuse
input fields from DiffusionPDE ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib10)); their
reference solutions are recomputed for this benchmark. These fields add
variation in coefficients or inputs without making the full collection a
dataset of experimental measurements.
[Figure 2](#S1.F2 "In 1 Introduction") illustrates public training
fields.

### 3.2 Full, Noisy and Hard-OOD settings

The three settings probe different sources of uncertainty. They are not
an exhaustive partition of the 1,001 released cases, and their pass
rates describe different case distributions. Full draws a balanced
noise-free evaluation across the catalog, Noisy selects a diagnostic
portion of the public release, and Hard-OOD uses a separate sealed draw.
Their training, validation and hidden-test allocations are given in
[Table 2](#S3.T2 "In 3.2 Full, Noisy and Hard-OOD settings ‣ 3 Dataset Construction").

| Setting  | Templates | Train | Validation | Test | Observation noise |
|----------|-----------|-------|------------|------|-------------------|
| Full     | 42        | 42    | 42         | 210  | None              |
| Noisy    | 24        | 22    | 18         | 160  | 50% RMS           |
| Hard-OOD | 39        | 20    | 20         | 278  | 1% RMS            |

Table 2: The three current evaluation settings. Noisy contains 200
selected cases in total, of which 160 are test cases. Hard-OOD separates
188 cases from unseen templates and 90 cases with shifted coefficients.

#### Full: noise-free instance generalization.

Full uses the full0 sampler, with no observation noise. Each of the 42
templates contributes one training, one validation and five hidden-test
cases: 42/42/210 in total. Public seeds determine training and
validation instances, while a separate secret determines hidden tests.
It measures generalization to new instances from represented template
distributions.

#### Noisy: severe observation corruption.

Noisy selects 200 cases found easiest by fixed calibration probes and
adds Gaussian perturbations with standard deviation equal to 50% of the
clean-signal RMS. The selected cases cover 24 templates and yield 22
training, 18 validation and 160 test instances. A template with at least
three selected cases contributes to all splits; two-case templates
contribute training and test instances, and one-case templates
contribute test instances. Selection helps distinguish observation
robustness from baseline numerical difficulty. Because these cases come
from a regenerable public release, this setting is a diagnostic test
rather than a fresh secret holdout.

#### Hard-OOD: unseen templates and shifted coefficients.

A sealed draw over 39 templates provides 20 training and 20 validation
cases from 20 seen templates. Its 278 hidden cases combine 188 instances
from 19 templates absent from both development splits and 90 instances
with coefficients in the opposite half of the range used in development.
The latter come from the seen templates with an identifiable
coefficient; the two seen templates without such a coefficient
contribute no shifted cases. Observation noise is 1% RMS and its level
is hidden. The equation library is still supplied, so unseen means
absent from training and validation, not absent from the allowed
library. The task text discloses both forms of shift. A problem-specific
feasibility filter conditions the draw, limiting the population to which
its results generalize.

### 3.3 Reference validation and reproducibility

Case generation keeps stable identifiers, template lineage and separate
public/evaluator views. Analytic references are sampled directly on the
scoring grid; numerical references use family-appropriate refinement and
convergence checks
([Appendix C](#A3 "Appendix C Reference Construction and Calibration")).
Fixed solver probes inform calibration and hardening, but do not change
the acceptance tolerance for an individual entrant. The same frozen
evaluator checks all reported programs for a setting.

The release, split construction, numerical references and frozen result
snapshot support separate inspection of task generation, solver behavior
and reported aggregates. Neither the 1,001-case inventory nor the
held-out pass count measures unrestricted scientific coverage:
dimensions, templates, admissible parameters and feasibility
conditioning define the benchmark’s scope.

## 4 RSI-PDE Harness

### 4.1 Fixed supervisor and evolving solver, policy and memory

The RSI-PDE harness structures an agent’s numerical exploration while
keeping its foundation model and executable supervisor fixed. Its
accepted state is $`(P,\pi,M)`$: $`P`$ is a modular solver with an
identification router, equation-class modules and shared helpers;
$`\pi`$ is a model-written instruction policy for subsequent
investigation; and $`M`$ stores reusable numerical lessons.
[Figure 3](#S4.F3 "In 4.1 Fixed supervisor and evolving solver, policy and memory ‣ 4 RSI-PDE Harness")
shows how that state moves through one round.
[Figure 4](#S4.F4 "In 4.1 Fixed supervisor and evolving solver, policy and memory ‣ 4 RSI-PDE Harness")
separately follows the physical identification and refinement loop.

Each round supplies the active program, policy, memory, recent history,
public diagnostics and remaining resources. The agent can analyze public
files, experiment on training cases and propose solver/policy/memory
updates. The supervisor requires a training experiment before a proposal
and bounds the round to at most two analyses and three proposal
attempts. Reply parsing, sandbox execution, label-copy checks, no-op
filtering and training self-tests constrain these actions before a
validation submission is spent. Numerical analysis runs inside the
solver sandbox; it does not grant the model unrestricted host execution.

Figure 3: RSI-PDE harness. The frozen LLM reads the context (H1–H6), and
its reply passes the supervisor checks (H7) before becoming a training
experiment (H8), a public-data analysis (H9) or a proposal (H10). A
proposal updates solver $`P`$, instruction policy $`\pi`$ and memory
$`M`$. The candidate passes the training self-test (H11) and the
validation or public-pass gate (H12); the text describes the bounded
training-regression waiver. Accepted state returns in the next round’s
context; rejected proposals return diagnostic feedback. The supervisor
and model weights remain fixed. Only the independent evaluator scores
the frozen solver on hidden tests.

### 4.2 Residual guidance for PDE and FPDE family search

The agent begins with an available field $`u_{a}`$, obtained from its
current solver or a regularized reconstruction of public observations.
It substitutes this field into candidate equations inside the sandbox
and fits their unknown coefficients. For candidate class $`k`$, write
the equation as signed terms, including the forcing:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{R}_{k,\theta}[u_{a}]=\sum_{j}T_{k,j}[u_{a};\theta],\qquad r_{\mathrm{id}}(k,\theta)=\frac{\|\mathcal{R}_{k,\theta}[u_{a}]\|_{\mathcal{Q}}}{\sum_{j}\|T_{k,j}[u_{a};\theta]\|_{\mathcal{Q}}+\varepsilon},\quad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{res}}=r_{\mathrm{id}}^{2}.
``` |  | (4) |

Here $`\mathcal{Q}`$ denotes the usable collocation grid or weak-test
set, and $`\varepsilon`$ protects against a zero normalizer.
Coefficients are fitted within the candidate’s admissible ranges.
Class-specific objectives may also include sample-fit and boundary
terms. The candidate law and public inputs define this calculation;
neither the hidden true equation nor a hidden reference field is
required.

Residual ranking prioritizes at most five hypotheses:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}_{5}=\operatorname{TopK}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left[-\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{res}}(k,\theta)\right],\qquad K=\min(5,|\mathcal{K}|).
``` |  | (5) |

This screening focuses subsequent forward-solver experiments on a small
set of plausible PDE/FPDE families. It avoids committing to one equation
solely because it fits sparse observations. For noisy data, numerical
differentiation can amplify perturbations; the agent can use smoothed
reconstructions, solver-generated fields, spectral representations or
weak residuals suited to the candidate operator. Term normalization and
known initial/boundary data help distinguish physical consistency from
an artificially favorable term scale.

The purpose of residual guidance is to reduce unstructured family
search. The aggregate experiments do not isolate its wall-clock benefit
or top-five recall, and a low residual does not guarantee that the
correct law survives the shortlist. Residual computation remains an
agent-written numerical procedure, rather than a supervisor oracle
revealing the true equation.

### 4.3 Relative $`L^{2}`$ feedback and coefficient refinement

For each shortlisted family, the agent implements or revises a forward
solver, estimates coefficients and checks its predicted field. Residual
and sample-fit evidence suggest candidate parameters; public training
relative $`L^{2}`$ errors show whether the resulting solution is
accurate. Repeated experiments can distinguish a wrong family from a
poorly fitted coefficient or an under-resolved numerical method. Known
forcing, initial and boundary functions provide additional constraints
on an otherwise ambiguous inverse problem.

The combination of residual and relative $`L^{2}`$ information is
staged: residual loss prioritizes scientific hypotheses, then
reference-based tests guide equation choice, coefficient estimation and
discretization repair on public cases. The validation gate evaluates
whether a proposed reusable program improves. There is no arbitrary
weighted residual-plus-$`L^{2}`$ threshold in the pass criterion. The
desired outcome is a coefficient estimator and solver that recover
accurate solutions on new instances; the solution pass rate alone does
not establish exact coefficient recovery when different parameters are
observationally similar.

At hidden inference time, the frozen router must choose from public
inputs, residuals and sample-fit evidence. It cannot request the test
reference or its relative $`L^{2}`$ error. Public development errors
improve the identification procedure; they do not provide an online
oracle for selecting the hidden test equation.

### 4.4 Validation, retention and feedback

The standard reference-error gate requires a greater than 0.5% decrease
in macro relative $`L^{2}`$ error, no class-average increase above 5%,
no loss of class passes, no per-case space–time or final-time error
increase above 25%, and valid execution and initial/boundary data.
Class-local changes can be gated and merged independently; shared
router/helper changes are evaluated at program level.

The harness also uses a public-pass rule over training and validation
cases when the standard gate rejects a candidate. That rule requires at
least one gained pass, no lost pass, no newly broken execution or
constraint/resource failure, and at most a 25% error increase for cases
passing in both programs, subject to the implementation’s numerical
floor. The all-training self-test normally preserves existing training
passes. After two consecutive rounds blocked by that guard, it can waive
the training-regression rule for one round; the validation/public-pass
decision still applies. Thus an update can address a remaining public
failure without being retained solely for reducing errors on already
accurate cases.

Accepted solver edits carry their corresponding policy and memory
updates into the next round. Rejected candidates return their numerical
errors and rejection reasons, while remaining in the audit history.
[Algorithm 1](#alg1 "In 4.4 Validation, retention and feedback ‣ 4 RSI-PDE Harness")
summarizes this process. The solver, policy and memory evolve; the
displayed experiments do not promote self-modified executable
supervisors. Establishing improvement of the controller itself would
require equal-budget paired continuations from the same starting solver
and memory.

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental setup and baselines

We compare four foundation models—Opus 5.5, GPT-5.5, GPT-6.1 and
Sonnet 5.5—under three modes:

- •
  Single run: one model response per development group, frozen without
  iterative repair.
- •
  Native harness: Claude Code for the Claude models and Codex for the
  GPT models, with execution and repair under the benchmark wrapper and
  declared development limits.
- •
  RSI-PDE harness: structured numerical experiments and gated solver,
  policy and memory updates as described in
  [Section 4](#S4 "4 RSI-PDE Harness").

Model weights stay fixed within each model-specific comparison. Native
and RSI-PDE modes share the declared resource ceilings and independent
numerical evaluator, but their tools and prompting differ; this is a
system comparison rather than an ablation of one component. We report
pass counts, relative $`L^{2}`$ diagnostics, learning curves and
resource use separately.

The numerical record is the frozen public website snapshot dated
November 7, 2026, including corrected GPT-6.1 Noisy identities. Most
harness cells contain one run; native Opus 5.5 on Full averages three
runs, with 187–198 passes. Single-run means use the available response
samples for each model and setting. The Opus single-run baseline uses
128k output tokens. Where an answer was re-requested at the model’s
maximum output length, it replaces the answer truncated by a shorter
limit; incomplete one-response programs score zero. Unequal replication
and output allowances should be considered when interpreting the
comparisons.

Author-reported single-run totals are GPT-6.1 Hard-OOD 135/278 and
Sonnet 5.5 Full 59/210, Noisy 36/160 and Hard-OOD 104/278. Their missing
validation/resource breakdowns are not inferred. Full native GPT-6.1 and
Sonnet 5.5 headline totals are 189 and 183, with curves supplied as
rounded percentages. Their earlier per-template, residual and resource
measurements remain explicitly labeled diagnostic records, rather than
being reconstructed to match these totals. GPT-6.1 Noisy uses the
corrected attribution throughout: Codex 90/160 and RSI-PDE 97/160. The
snapshot preserves raw record identifiers and source provenance;
[Appendix D](#A4 "Appendix D Implementation and Reproduction") describes
reproduction.

### 5.5 Residual diagnostics and their interpretation

Residual-guided identification in
[Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Residual guidance for PDE and FPDE family search ‣ 4 RSI-PDE Harness")
uses a hypothesized equation. The residual table on the website instead
evaluates the frozen returned solution under the evaluator’s *true*
equation. If its terms are $`T_{j}[\hat{u}]`$, the reported relative
residual is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\mathrm{true}}(\hat{u})=\frac{\|\sum_{j}T_{j}[\hat{u}]\|}{\sum_{j}\|T_{j}[\hat{u}]\|}.
``` |  | (6) |

This diagnostic needs no reference solution, but does need the true
class and coefficients; it is computed afterward and is never exposed as
a hidden-equation selection oracle to the agent.

The calculation uses spectral derivatives in periodic directions,
fourth-order differences on bounded grids, cubic splines for time
derivatives and an L1 approximation for Caputo derivatives. Darcy uses a
weak residual against sine modes. The point-cloud elliptic family has no
reported residual. These discretizations create a resolution-dependent
floor, so a residual measures equation consistency at the returned
resolution, not a uniform solution-error bound.

error with its median post-hoc true-equation residual. Points are

For example, the historical Full Opus 5.5 native and RSI rows have
median relative $`L^{2}`$ errors of roughly $`4.5\times 10^{-4}`$ and
$`4.3\times 10^{-4}`$, but median residuals of $`3.9\times 10^{-4}`$ and
$`4.6\times 10^{-4}`$. A lower aggregate residual therefore need not
correspond to a lower aggregate reference error. The reported rank
correlations and passed/failed medians provide additional information,
but their coverage includes only the cases for which the corresponding
diagnostics are available.

The Full native GPT-6.1 residual row retains 199/210 reference-run
passes, and Sonnet 5.5 retains 193/210, rather than the updated headline
totals 189 and 183. Single-run residual rows can also have different
sample coverage from the headline averages. We label these as
reference-run diagnostics in
[Appendix F](#A6 "Appendix F Retrospective Residual Tables"), preserving
the source values instead of presenting them as new measurements of the
updated totals.

## 6 Discussion and Limitations

#### Unknown physics as a controlled scientific task.

Jointly hiding equation family and coefficients makes solver
construction harder than translating a fully specified PDE into code.
Full, Noisy and Hard-OOD expose different failure modes in that process.
Nevertheless, the task supplies a finite candidate library and known
physical inputs. The 1,001-case collection is mostly synthetic, covers
one- and two-dimensional domains, and includes selected data-driven
input fields. It is a reproducible analogue of scientific modeling, not
a complete representation of noisy laboratory data, open-ended equation
discovery or industrial workloads. Noisy selection and Hard-OOD
feasibility filtering further restrict the evaluated populations.

#### Residual guidance and identifiability.

A wrong equation can have a small residual when observations are sparse,
weakly informative or differentiated inaccurately. Multiple families or
parameter values may fit the same samples, especially at 50% noise.
Keeping five candidates reduces premature commitment but does not
guarantee true-family retention. The reported experiments contain no
isolated residual-screening ablation, shortlist-recall measurement or
parameter-recovery rate. Consequently, improved solution pass rates
support the complete workflow, without establishing that residual
guidance alone is faster or recovers every true coefficient.
Reference-based $`L^{2}`$ checks also inherit interpolation and
numerical-reference error; self-convergence does not prove a uniform
reference error bound.

#### Replication and benchmark reuse.

Most model–setting comparisons have one run; the three-run Full Opus
native range is large relative to several margins. Repeated seeds and
paired initial states are needed for uncertainty estimates and component
ablations. Running agents cannot see hidden test feedback, but
maintainers have inspected benchmark results when selecting design
choices, including rules for using known physical functions. The current
record is therefore developmental rather than an untouched holdout
estimate ([Dwork et al., 2015](#bib.bib24); [Blum and Hardt,
2015](#bib.bib25)). Noise-level visibility also differs among the
reported Noisy runs: GPT-6.1 and Sonnet 5.5 hide it, while other
displayed Noisy rows show it; Hard-OOD hides it throughout. Controlled
comparisons should match those conditions and use fresh committed test
draws. Historical Full diagnostics are labeled separately from updated
headline totals.

#### What evolves and what the resource results mean.

The model weights and executable supervisor remain fixed, while solver
code, policy text and memory can change. Final-program improvement does
not establish that an evolved controller is a better improver; that
claim requires matched continuation experiments. Recorded calls decrease
in eight of 12 comparisons, with substantial exceptions on Noisy. Dollar
costs depend on token usage and caching, and selected costs use
retrospective stop-rule replay while retaining full-budget scores. Those
estimates require fresh stopped runs before they can support jointly
achieved accuracy/cost claims.

## 7 Conclusion

RSI-PDEbench brings together 1,001 PDE/FPDE cases and an unknown-family,
unknown-parameter identify-then-solve task. Full, Noisy and Hard-OOD
separately test noise-free accuracy, robustness to severe Gaussian
sample noise, and transfer to unseen templates and shifted coefficients.
The RSI-PDE harness uses residuals to prioritize candidate physical
laws, then combines public relative $`L^{2}`$ feedback, coefficient
refinement and validation to retain accurate executable solvers and
improve their policy and memory. The reported four-model comparison
improves mean pass rates over single-run and native-harness baselines in
all three settings and reduces aggregate recorded model calls. Repeated
runs, residual-specific ablations and fresh hidden evaluations will
clarify which parts of this workflow generalize most reliably.

## Appendix A Related Work

#### PDE learning and equation discovery.

PDEBench, PDEArena, PINNacle and The Well provide important test beds
for learned numerical models ([Takamoto et al., 2022](#bib.bib6); [Gupta
and Brandstetter, 2022](#bib.bib7); [Hao et al., 2024](#bib.bib8);
[Ohana et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)). Physics-informed neural networks use
equation residuals in forward and inverse problems ([Raissi et al.,
2019](#bib.bib4)), while neural operators learn solution mappings across
problem instances ([Li et al., 2021](#bib.bib5)). Equation discovery
instead seeks a governing law: sparse identification and PDE-FIND select
differential terms from data ([Brunton et al., 2016](#bib.bib1); [Rudy
et al., 2017](#bib.bib2)), and PDE-Net learns differential operators
([Long et al., 2018](#bib.bib3)). RSI-PDEbench combines identification
with construction of an executable numerical solver. The entrant can use
classical, learned or hybrid methods, but is graded on the returned
solution. Five data-driven templates reuse input fields from
DiffusionPDE ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib10)), with reference
solutions recomputed for this benchmark.

#### Physics-informed numerical learning.

Training methods address complementary challenges in constructing
accurate scientific surrogates. NSGA-PINN uses multi-objective
optimization for physics-informed network training ([Lu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib32)), iPINNER combines iterative physics-informed learning
with ensemble Kalman filtering ([Lu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib34)), and
Morephy-Net studies evolutionary multi-objective, replica-exchange
physics-informed operator learning ([Lu et al., 2026c](#bib.bib35)).
FPINN-DeepONet targets multi-term time-fractional mixed diffusion–wave
equations ([Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib33)), while Neural-POD develops a
neural-operator formulation of nonlinear proper orthogonal decomposition
([Mou et al., 2026](#bib.bib36)). Spectrally guided Muon optimization
and AdamFLIP study optimization for scientific learning and
hard-constrained PINNs ([Lu et al., 2026d](#bib.bib38); [Lu et al.,
2026e](#bib.bib30)). These works provide numerical-learning approaches
relevant to the solution methods an agent may investigate. RSI-PDEbench
evaluates the broader process of identifying the equation, estimating
its coefficients and constructing a solver under a fixed development
budget.

#### Scientific coding and agent interfaces.

Scientific and software-engineering benchmarks evaluate programs,
research tasks or repository-level changes ([Tian et al.,
2024](#bib.bib13); [Jimenez et al., 2024](#bib.bib14); [Chan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16); [Wijk et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)). SWE-agent
demonstrates the importance of the interface through which a model edits
and executes code ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib15)). CodePDE studies
inference-time generation and refinement of solvers for specified
equations ([Li et al., 2026a](#bib.bib12)). PDEAgent-Bench emphasizes
accurate, efficient solver generation for known PDE specifications and
compatibility with multiple FEM libraries ([Hang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib37)). RSI-PDEbench complements that emphasis by hiding the
instance’s family and coefficients and including fractional dynamics.
LLM-SR searches for scientific equations represented as programs
([Shojaee et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)). Our comparison combines
finite-library equation identification with solver development, keeps
the foundation model fixed within a model-specific comparison, and
varies the harness under numerical feedback and a recorded development
budget. Unlike a unit-test-only task, a solution must also meet a
quantitative error tolerance over an evaluation grid.

#### Iterative and self-improving agents.

Reflexion and Self-Refine use feedback to revise behavior or outputs
without retraining model weights ([Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib18);
[Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib19)). Program-search systems use
evaluations to improve candidate code ([Romera-Paredes et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22); [Novikov et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)), and other
systems optimize improvement procedures or agent designs ([Zelikman et
al., 2024](#bib.bib20); [Hu et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)). CONTRAMEM
studies self-evolving procedural memory from contrasting multi-model
trajectories ([Deng et al., 2026](#bib.bib29)), and work on the limits
of harness evolution examines when weight training becomes necessary
([Tian et al., 2026a](#bib.bib26)). Muon-OGD studies spectral orthogonal
gradient projection for LLM continual learning ([Lu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib31)). In our setting, the foundation weights remain
fixed, and RSI-PDE updates solver code, an instruction policy and
memory. The displayed benchmark runs keep the executable supervisor
fixed. Establishing that an evolved controller is a better improver
would require paired continuations from the same solver and memory,
beyond a comparison of final programs. Adaptive reuse of validation or
test results also requires care ([Dwork et al., 2015](#bib.bib24); [Blum
and Hardt, 2015](#bib.bib25)); we therefore state the provenance of the
development results explicitly.

#### Structured scientific applications and agent reliability.

Broader scientific-learning applications include SE(3)-MeanFlow for
protein-backbone generation on Lie groups ([Bai et al.,
2026](#bib.bib39)) and MedRouter for routing-based reasoning across
medical language models ([Cao et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)). They
illustrate structured generation and task-specific routing in domains
beyond PDE solver construction. Agent reliability also has dimensions
beyond numerical accuracy: Chain of Risk studies safety failures and
adaptive steering in reasoning models ([Li et al., 2026b](#bib.bib40)),
while Think-with-Image studies multimodal jailbreak robustness ([Tian et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib28)). Those questions complement the execution
isolation, information boundaries and quantitative solution checks used
here.

## Appendix C Reference Construction and Calibration

Analytic references are evaluated directly at the evaluator’s nodes.
Numerical references are constructed with a finer computation than the
comparison resolution and checked after both are mapped to the common
evaluation grid. Representative settings in the reference builders are:

- •
  chaotic Kuramoto–Sivashinsky: 512 Fourier nodes and 120 ETDRK4
  substeps per output interval, compared with 256 nodes and 60 substeps;
- •
  spinodal Cahn–Hilliard: 256 nodes per spatial direction and 100
  substeps, compared with 128 nodes and 50 substeps;
- •
  Gaussian-data Burgers: refined finite-volume computations at 2048 and
  1024 spatial nodes;
- •
  Poisson and Helmholtz input-field problems: fine/coarse spatial
  settings of 1020 and 510;
- •
  the Elliptic equation reference: a finite-volume discretization with a
  preconditioned conjugate-gradient relative tolerance of $`10^{-11}`$.

The reference scheme and error allowance are fixed by the benchmark, not
changed to favor a submitted solver. Higher order, finer resolution or
tighter linear-solver tolerance are used where appropriate to the
family; these settings are not a claim that every reference has the same
discretization error.

The public website also includes calibration profiles from fixed probe
programs. Its displayed probe profiles cover 640 variants of 32
templates; the additional multiphysics templates complete the
42-template benchmark. Calibration informs hardening and the diagnostic
Noisy selection. Since subset selection changes the population being
evaluated, the Full, Noisy and Hard-OOD pass rates should not be
interpreted as estimates of the same underlying case distribution.

## Appendix D Implementation and Reproduction

#### Recorded source snapshot.

The numerical tables and plots are generated from
arxiv/data/website_results_20261107.json, a frozen copy of the public
website after revision 76b33da. The generator is
arxiv/make_website_assets.py. It does not run an agent or inspect hidden
per-case outcomes. The website corrections are presentation-level
provenance events; the original experiment records are retained. The
authors clarified that Full denotes the noise-free full0 sampling
variant, which sets observation noise to zero. The frozen website
snapshot retains its published full presentation keys and reported
totals. This protocol clarification is recorded in
arxiv/data/protocol_corrections.json; it does not overwrite original
experiment records.

#### Agent-level residuals and supervisor.

Candidate-equation residuals are implemented by model-written sandbox
routines. For example, a retained fractional solver uses least-squares
terms for PDE collocation, sample fit and boundaries before comparing
candidate scores; another solver checks coefficient consistency against
known forcing and initial data. These routines are distinct from
evaluator-only true-equation residuals. The top-five policy describes
the identification workflow; the aggregate results do not isolate its
effect.

The supervisor is implemented in src/pde_rsi/code_agents.py; the
standard gate is in src/pde_rsi/evaluator.py, and the native wrapper in
scripts/run_native_agent.py. The public-pass rule compares the same
public case identifiers before and after an update, gains at least one
pass, loses none, permits no new execution/constraint/resource failure
and bounds error changes on cases that already pass. Model weights and
executable control flow stay fixed during the reported runs. Raw
configuration identifiers remain in the result snapshot for
reproduction, without defining separate methods in this paper.

#### Reproducible paper build.

The revised arXiv manuscript is maintained directly in this directory,
retaining the original document style and author block. Asset generation
uses the frozen website JSON, and the LaTeX build uses the included
style, bibliography and figures.
````

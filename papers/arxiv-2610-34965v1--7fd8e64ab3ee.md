###### Abstract

Automatic red teaming increasingly relies on large language models
(LLMs) to iteratively diagnose failed attacks and propose improved
adversarial prompts or injections. Although prior work strengthens this
loop through prompt engineering and search-harness design, the
mechanisms underlying its two core roles, criticism and attack proposal,
has not been explored. To further investigate the core roles and
therefore improve red teaming effectiveness, we disentangle two design
choices: whether criticism is generated by the target session itself
(_self-criticism_) or by a separately instantiated critic (_external
criticism_), and whether that criticism is translated into the next
attack by the target session (_self-proposal_) or by a separate attacker
(_external proposal_). Through our analysis, we find that self-criticism
is more effective because it is grounded in the target’s live execution
context, including observations, constraints, and available reasoning;
removing reasoning reduces downstream attack success by 6%-17%. In
contrast, self-proposal is substantially less effective because
awareness of the adversarial objective induces explicit refusal and
non-refusal sandbagging. These findings motivate a role-separated
red-teaming approach: the target session diagnoses why an attack failed,
while a separately instantiated attacker generates the next proposal. On
DTap-Bench, spanning Telecom, Workflow, CRM, and Code domains, under
both direct misuse and indirect threat models, as well as multiple agent
frameworks, this configuration achieves $`63.2\%`$ overall attack
success rate (ASR), much higher than baselines. Our results show that
effective automatic red teaming depends not only on model capability and
search strategy, but also on how execution context and adversarial
responsibility are distributed across model sessions.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as agents that
can reason, invoke tools, access external information, and take actions
on behalf of users ([Yao et al., 2023](#bib.bib1); [Schick et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2)). As these systems become more autonomous and interact
with sensitive data and external environments, systematic safety and
security evaluation becomes increasingly important. Jailbreak attacks
can circumvent alignment safeguards and elicit harmful or prohibited
behavior ([Zou et al., 2023](#bib.bib3)), while prompt-injection attacks
can manipulate agents through adversarial instructions embedded in user
inputs or untrusted external content, potentially causing unauthorized
actions or private-data exfiltration ([Greshake et al.,
2023](#bib.bib4); [Zhan et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)). Because manually
constructed attacks do not scale to the breadth of modern agent
behavior, recent work increasingly uses LLMs themselves to generate,
evaluate, and iteratively refine adversarial prompts or prompt
injections ([Perez et al., 2022](#bib.bib6); [Chao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib7); [Mehrotra et al., 2024](#bib.bib8)).

Despite substantial differences in attack strategy, most LLM-based
automatic red-teaming methods share a common iterative structure:
evaluate why the previous attack failed, then propose a revised attack.
Existing work mainly improves this loop through prompt engineering—for
example, more detailed feedback, diverse attack strategies, and explicit
optimization instructions—or through harness engineering, such as
branching, pruning, retries, and attack-history management. These
techniques improve effectiveness, but they often treat criticism and
proposal as a single optimization module. This leaves two basic design
questions unresolved: _who should diagnose the failure, and who should
act on that diagnosis?_

We therefore decompose iterative red teaming into two roles, _criticism
generation_ and _attack proposal_, and compare three configurations:
external criticism with external proposal, self-criticism with external
proposal, and self-criticism with self-proposal. We hold the underlying
model identity fixed within each comparison so that differences reflect
role assignment and session context rather than raw model capability.
This decomposition lets us ask: (i) whether the target’s own execution
context makes its criticism more actionable; (ii) which information in
that context matters; (iii) whether the same target session can
faithfully convert its diagnosis into a stronger attack; and (iv)
whether these effects persist across threat models, domains, frameworks,
and models.

Our results reveal a strong asymmetry. First, the target is an effective
critic of its own failed execution. Self-criticism improves overall ASR
from $`57.3\%`$ to $`63.2\%`$ when proposal remains external. The
advantage is especially pronounced for indirect prompt injection, where
failures depend on external content, tool interactions, and environment
state. An ablation further supports the proposed mechanism: removing
reasoning from the target’s execution context reduces downstream attack
success by $`6`$–$`17`$ percentage points. Second, the target is a poor
proposer of the next attack. Replacing external proposal with
self-proposal reduces overall ASR from $`63.2\%`$ to $`46.2\%`$.
Task-level refusal rises from $`13.9\%`$ to $`47.8\%`$, while
low-refusal settings still exhibit substantial performance degradation,
consistent with non-refusal sandbagging.

These findings motivate _role-separated red teaming_: after each failed
attempt, the target session produces structured criticism from its live
execution context, and a separate attacker converts that diagnosis into
the next adversarial candidate. This division preserves
execution-grounded diagnostic information while avoiding the contextual
conflict created when the target is asked to operationalize an
adversarial objective against itself.

We evaluate the method on DTap-Bench ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)), a

## 2 Role-Separated Automatic Red Teaming

### 2.1 Automatic Red Teaming as Criticism and Proposal

Let $`q`$ denote a red-teaming task and $`x_{k}`$ the attack attempted
at iteration $`k`$. For direct misuse, $`x_{k}`$ is a malicious user
instruction; for indirect prompt injection, it is adversarial content
placed in an external resource encountered by the target. Executing the
target produces a trajectory $`\tau_{k}`$, including responses, tool
calls, observations, and resulting environment state. A judge
$`\mathcal{J}`$ determines whether the attack succeeds:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\tau_{k}=\operatorname{Exec}(q,x_{k}),\qquad y_{k}=\mathcal{J}(q,\tau_{k})\in\{0,1\}.
``` |  | (1) |

If $`y_{k}=0`$, a critic $`\mathcal{C}`$ produces criticism $`c_{k}`$,
and a proposer $`\mathcal{P}`$ generates the next attack:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{k}=\mathcal{C}(\tau_{k}),\qquad x_{k+1}=\mathcal{P}(x_{k},c_{k}).
``` |  | (2) |

Criticism is diagnostic: it identifies which instruction, constraint,
observation, or intermediate decision prevented success. Proposal is
operational: it converts that diagnosis into a revised adversarial
candidate. We therefore evaluate criticism by *downstream
actionability*: holding the proposer fixed, better criticism should
produce more successful subsequent attacks.

#### Self versus external roles.

*Self-criticism* is generated inside the target session that produced
$`\tau_{k}`$, preserving its live execution context. *External
criticism* is generated in a separate session from a transferred
representation of the execution. Likewise, *self-proposal* asks the
target session to generate $`x_{k+1}`$, whereas *external proposal*
delegates this step to a separate attacker.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Detailed Method Motivation and Formulation ‣ The Target Agent Is Its Own Best Critic but a Poor Proposer: Self-Improved Red Teaming")
gives a fuller formulation and discusses the information available to
each role.

### 2.2 Why Separate the Roles?

Criticism benefits from diagnostic locality. An external critic observes
only what the harness transfers—for example, the attack, final response,
judge score, or a serialized trajectory. For agents, however,
superficially similar failures can arise from different mechanisms:
instruction prioritization, safety constraints, missing environment
state, tool errors, or a loss of adversarial influence midway through a
trajectory. A benign final action alone may therefore be insufficient to
determine how the next attack should change. The target session retains
the exact instruction hierarchy, observations, tool-state transitions,
and, when exposed by the framework, reasoning that shaped its behavior.
Self-criticism can diagnose a failure using this evidence directly
rather than asking an external critic to reconstruct it from a filtered
transcript.

More formally, let $`h_{k}^{T}`$ denote the target’s live context after
attempt $`k`$. Self-criticism appends a criticism instruction
$`\rho_{C}`$ to that context and generates
$`c_{k}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{k}^{T},\rho_{C})`$. The instruction
asks the target to identify the decision point at which the attack
failed, the instruction, constraint, or observation responsible for the
failure, and the change needed for a subsequent attempt. Crucially, the
target is asked only to diagnose its preceding behavior at this stage;
it is not yet asked to formulate another adversarial candidate. Thus the
effective critic prompt is the combination of $`\rho_{C}`$ with the
execution context already accumulated by the target, not merely a more
elaborate criticism template. Our reasoning-context ablation in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Reasoning in the Execution Context Makes Criticism Actionable ‣ 3 Evaluation and Findings ‣ The Target Agent Is Its Own Best Critic but a Poor Proposer: Self-Improved Red Teaming")
directly tests the value of this retained information.

Proposal benefits from adversarial separation. The same context that
helps the target understand why an attack failed can make the harmful
purpose of the next proposal salient. Under self-proposal, the target
simultaneously sees the adversarial objective, its safety and task
constraints, its diagnosis of the previous failure, and the strategy
needed to circumvent that failure. This creates a conflict absent from
ordinary self-refinement, where critique and revision share the same
benign objective ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Shinn et al.,
2023](#bib.bib11)). The target may explicitly decline to generate an
improved adversarial candidate, or it may return a superficially valid
proposal that weakens, omits, or fails to operationalize the strategy
identified in its own criticism. We refer to the latter behavioral
pattern as non-refusal sandbagging, while avoiding claims about
unobserved internal intent.

A separate attacker retains the target-generated diagnosis but does not
continue from the target’s task-serving execution context. Its context
can instead be specialized for iterative attack search and can maintain
previous candidates, outcomes, and target-generated criticism. Role
separation therefore does not assume that the external attacker is
unconstrained; rather, it assigns proposal to the session whose explicit
purpose is adversarial generation while preserving the target’s stronger
diagnostic signal.

provides the fuller motivation and relation to prior iterative attack

### 2.3 Role-Separated Red-Teaming Loop

At each iteration, the current candidate is executed against a fresh
task state. If the judge marks the attack successful, the episode
terminates. Otherwise, the target session is retained long enough to
generate structured criticism from the execution context. Only this
diagnosis—not the full target context—is transferred to the external
attacker, which combines it with the current candidate and attack
history to generate the next proposal. The environment is then reset and
the next candidate is evaluated from the same task-specific initial
state.

1: task $`q`$, target $`T`$, attacker $`A`$, judge $`\mathcal{J}`$,
budget $`K`$, initial attack $`x_{0}`$

2: for $`k=0,\ldots,K-1`$ do

3:   Execute $`x_{k}`$ against $`T`$ and obtain $`\tau_{k}`$

4:   if $`\mathcal{J}(q,\tau_{k})=1`$ then

5:    return $`x_{k}`$

6:   end if

7:   Generate criticism $`c_{k}`$ inside the target session

8:   Transfer $`c_{k}`$ to external attacker $`A`$

9:   $`x_{k+1}\leftarrow A(q,x_{k},c_{k},\mathcal{H}_{k})`$

10: end for

11: return failure

Algorithm 1 Role-Separated Automatic Red Teaming

The design has three components: *diagnostic locality*, which keeps
criticism where the relevant execution evidence is available;
*adversarial separation*, which assigns proposal to a distinct attacker
session; and *selective context transfer*, which uses criticism as the
interface between the two. The same loop applies to direct misuse and
indirect prompt injection, differing only in whether $`x_{k}`$ is a user
instruction or content inserted into an external resource.

## 3 Evaluation and Findings

### 3.1 Evaluation Setup

Benchmark and threat models. We evaluate on DTap-Bench ([Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9)), a policy-grounded benchmark built from controllable,
stateful simulations of real-world agent applications. A verifiable
judge inspects the resulting environment state, so success requires
completion of the specified malicious objective rather than merely
unsafe text. We consider four domains—Telecom, Workflow, CRM, and
Code—and two threat models: *direct misuse*, where an adversarial
instruction is submitted directly to the target, and *indirect prompt
injection*, where adversarial content is embedded in an external
resource encountered during an otherwise benign task.

Models, frameworks, and configurations. The main evaluation includes
Gemini-3.1-Pro with Google ADK, GPT-5.1 with the OpenAI Agents SDK,
GPT-5.1 with OpenClaw, and a reasoning-context analysis with Opus-4.7
and Claude Code. Within each comparison, target, critic, and proposer
use the same underlying model identifier. We compare: (i) external
criticism + external proposal (the DT-Red configuration ([Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9))); (ii) self-criticism + self-proposal; and (iii) our
self-criticism + external-proposal configuration. Our primary metric is
attack success rate (ASR), the fraction of evaluated tasks satisfying
the benchmark’s verifiable malicious-goal condition.
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Experimental Details and Reporting Checklist ‣ The Target Agent Is Its Own Best Critic but a Poor Proposer: Self-Improved Red Teaming")
records the detailed evaluation breakdown and should be read together
with the released implementation/supplementary material for exact
prompts, budgets, and task counts.

### 3.2 The Target Is Its Own Best Critic

Holding proposal fixed as external, replacing external criticism with
self-criticism raises overall ASR from $`57.3\%`$ to $`63.2\%`$, a gain
of $`5.9`$ percentage points. The improvement is larger for indirect
prompt injection ($`49.4\%\rightarrow 56.8\%`$, $`+7.4`$ points) than
for direct misuse ($`73.1\%\rightarrow 76.0\%`$, $`+2.9`$ points),
consistent with indirect failures depending more heavily on external
content, tool interactions, and environment state. The direction of the
gain is consistent across all fully evaluated framework–threat and
domain–threat slices. The largest domain-level improvement is in direct
Workflow attacks ($`+22.3`$ points), a setting in which success depends
on multi-step state transitions that can be difficult to summarize for a
separate critic. We therefore view the aggregate gain not simply as a
same-model effect, but as evidence that the target’s execution context
contains diagnostic information that survives translation into a
stronger external proposal. The framework- and domain-level breakdown is
shown in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.2 The Target Is Its Own Best Critic ‣ 3 Evaluation and Findings ‣ The Target Agent Is Its Own Best Critic but a Poor Proposer: Self-Improved Red Teaming");
exact values are discussed further in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Self-Criticism Breakdown ‣ Appendix C Detailed Evaluation Results ‣ The Target Agent Is Its Own Best Critic but a Poor Proposer: Self-Improved Red Teaming").

At the framework level, the effect is not driven by a single backend.
Under direct misuse, self-criticism improves GPT-5.1 with the OpenAI
Agents SDK from $`75.7\%`$ to $`85.5\%`$ and GPT-5.1 OpenClaw from
$`80.5\%`$ to $`86.2\%`$, while Gemini-3.1-Pro with Google ADK changes
more modestly from $`42.5\%`$ to $`43.5\%`$. Under indirect prompt
injection, the corresponding gains are larger and consistent across all
three frameworks: $`26.8\%\rightarrow 43.0\%`$ for Gemini-3.1-Pro,
$`55.5\%\rightarrow 66.1\%`$ for GPT-5.1 with the OpenAI Agents SDK, and
$`56.8\%\rightarrow 61.4\%`$ for GPT-5.1 OpenClaw. This consistency
across heterogeneous agent stacks strengthens the interpretation that
execution-local criticism, rather than one framework-specific artifact,
accounts for the aggregate improvement.

### 3.3 Reasoning in the Execution Context Makes Criticism Actionable

Figure 3: Reasoning in the execution context makes self-criticism more
actionable. Proposal remains external while we compare self-criticism
from the full available context with criticism generated after reasoning
is removed. Values above paired bars are absolute ASR improvements.

To test whether self-criticism benefits specifically from
decision-relevant execution information, we remove reasoning while
preserving the remaining interaction history and keep proposal external.
Removing reasoning lowers downstream ASR in every evaluated setting. For
Opus-4.7, full-context criticism improves indirect ASR from $`17\%`$ to
$`31\%`$ and direct ASR from $`25\%`$ to $`42\%`$. For GPT-5.1 with the
OpenAI Agents SDK, retaining reasoning raises indirect ASR from $`67\%`$
to $`82\%`$ and direct ASR from $`65\%`$ to $`71\%`$. Thus, the benefit
of self-criticism is not explained merely by using the same model family
as the target: access to information produced during the live execution
materially improves the actionability of the diagnosis.

### 3.4 The Target Is a Poor Proposer

Holding criticism fixed as self-criticism, replacing external proposal
with self-proposal decreases overall ASR from $`63.2\%`$ to $`46.2\%`$,
a drop of $`17.0`$ percentage points. The decrease is larger for direct
misuse ($`76.0\%\rightarrow 53.7\%`$, $`-22.3`$ points) than for
indirect prompt injection ($`56.8\%\rightarrow 42.8\%`$, $`-14.0`$
points). The disadvantage again holds across all main framework–threat
and domain–threat slices, including a $`44.5`$-point drop for direct
Workflow attacks. This comparison is important because the critic is
identical in both conditions: the target has already produced the same
diagnosis, and only the actor responsible for turning that diagnosis
into the next attack changes. The resulting gap therefore isolates a
failure at the proposal stage rather than a failure to understand why
the previous attack failed.

The framework-level reductions are likewise substantial. Under direct
misuse, switching from external to self-proposal lowers ASR from
$`43.5\%`$ to $`38.1\%`$ for Gemini-3.1-Pro with Google ADK, from
$`85.5\%`$ to $`60.6\%`$ for GPT-5.1 with the OpenAI Agents SDK, and
from $`86.2\%`$ to $`56.7\%`$ for GPT-5.1 OpenClaw. The same direction
holds under indirect prompt injection, with reductions of $`1.7`$,
$`22.0`$, and $`18.7`$ percentage points, respectively. The much larger
drops for the GPT-5.1 agent stacks also motivate the refusal analysis
below: the proposal-stage failure is strong, but its observable
mechanism differs substantially across model–framework combinations.

The framework-level pattern further separates explicit refusal from
non-refusal degradation. Gemini-3.1-Pro with Google ADK already exhibits
high task-level refusal across all three role configurations (roughly
$`71\%`$–$`80\%`$), suggesting a broad model- or harness-level
constraint rather than a self-proposal-specific effect. GPT-5.1 OpenClaw
shows the opposite extreme: refusal is near zero under external proposal
but rises to $`84.0\%`$ under self-proposal, so explicit refusal
accounts for much of its proposal-stage degradation. GPT-5.1 with the
OpenAI Agents SDK is the key counterexample: self-proposal refusal
remains only $`3.6\%`$, yet ASR still falls sharply. Together, these
settings indicate that self-proposal can fail through at least two
observable modes—overt refusal and weaker non-refusal proposals—whose
relative importance depends on the surrounding agent stack.

### 3.6 Role Separation Is the Strongest Configuration

Combining the stronger critic with the stronger proposer yields the best
aggregate configuration: self-criticism + external proposal reaches
$`63.2\%`$ ASR, versus $`57.3\%`$ for external criticism + external
proposal and $`46.2\%`$ for self-criticism + self-proposal
(Figure [7](#S3.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 3.6 Role Separation Is the Strongest Configuration ‣ 3 Evaluation and Findings ‣ The Target Agent Is Its Own Best Critic but a Poor Proposer: Self-Improved Red Teaming")).
It also achieves the highest ASR among the three configurations under
both main threat models and for each fully evaluated framework.

#### Fine-grained behavior.

The aggregate improvement is broad but not uniform across task
categories. Representative gains include direct Telecom credential
takeover with GPT-5.1 OpenClaw ($`57.1\%\rightarrow 95.2\%`$), indirect
Workflow finance manipulation with Gemini-3.1-Pro and Google ADK
($`25.0\%\rightarrow 65.2\%`$), indirect CRM ticket abuse with GPT-5.1
and the OpenAI Agents SDK ($`15.0\%\rightarrow 60.0\%`$), and direct
Code MITRE Injection with GPT-5.1 and the OpenAI Agents SDK
($`0.0\%\rightarrow 100.0\%`$). Other categories tie or regress,
particularly where baseline ASR is already high or behavior is dominated
by framework-specific policy. We therefore interpret role separation as
the strongest aggregate configuration among those compared, rather than
as a guarantee of improvement for every fine-grained task category.

#### Transfer to GPT-5.5.

The same role ordering persists in a separate GPT-5.5 study on the
selected red-teaming subset using both the OpenAI Agents SDK and
OpenClaw, under direct misuse and indirect prompt injection
(Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Transfer to GPT-5.5. ‣ 3.6 Role Separation Is the Strongest Configuration ‣ 3 Evaluation and Findings ‣ The Target Agent Is Its Own Best Critic but a Poor Proposer: Self-Improved Red Teaming")).
With the OpenAI Agents SDK, self-criticism + external proposal reaches
$`63.0\%`$ on direct attacks, compared with $`56.0\%`$ for external
criticism + external proposal and $`59.3\%`$ for self-criticism +
self-proposal; on indirect prompt injection, the corresponding ASRs are
$`42.8\%`$, $`37.8\%`$, and $`36.2\%`$. With OpenClaw, the direct ASRs
are $`64.7\%`$, $`57.4\%`$, and $`62.3\%`$, while the indirect ASRs are
$`52.6\%`$, $`41.3\%`$, and $`42.0\%`$. Thus, role separation improves
over external criticism + external proposal by $`5.0`$–$`11.3`$ points
and over self-criticism + self-proposal by $`2.4`$–$`10.6`$ points

## 4 Related Work

Automatic red teaming. LLMs have increasingly been used to automate
adversarial test generation. Early work showed that one language model
can red-team another through zero-shot generation, supervised learning,
and reinforcement learning ([Perez et al., 2022](#bib.bib6)). Later
methods improve black-box search through fuzzing and mutation ([Yu et
al., 2023](#bib.bib16)), iterative semantic revision ([Chao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib7)), branching and pruning ([Mehrotra et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8)), in-context update of successful and unsuccessful
attempts ([Mehrabi et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)), and quality-diversity
search ([Samvelyan et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)). These approaches mainly
strengthen attack prompts, search topology, candidate selection, or
attacker memory. We study an orthogonal question: how failure diagnosis
and subsequent attack generation should be distributed across model
sessions.

Self-criticism and test-time improvement. Self-Refine, Reflexion, and
CRITIC show that natural-language feedback can improve subsequent model
behavior without parameter updates ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib10);
[Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib11); [Gou et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)). In
these settings, critique and revision usually share a benign objective.
Automatic red teaming introduces an asymmetry: the target can accurately
diagnose why an attack failed while remaining unwilling to convert that
diagnosis into a stronger adversarial input. Our results separate these
two effects empirically.

Situational awareness and sandbagging. Prior work studies conflicts
between capability and safety objectives ([Wei et al.,
2023](#bib.bib13)), situational awareness of role and evaluation context
([Laine et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)), and strategic underperformance ([van
der Weij et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). Our results connect these phenomena
to the design of red-teaming harnesses: self-proposal can suppress the
search process itself, so measured robustness may partly reflect
proposer behavior rather than only the target’s resistance to the
eventual attack. We use “sandbagging” cautiously for the observed
non-refusal performance gap and do not claim to identify an internal
motive from behavior alone.

## 5 Limitations and Conclusion

We decompose automatic red teaming into criticism and proposal and show
that the two roles benefit from different model sessions: the target
produces more actionable criticism because it retains the execution
context that shaped the failed attempt, whereas self-proposal is
hindered by explicit refusal and weaker non-refusal proposals when the
target is asked to operationalize its own diagnosis. This motivates role
separation—use the target session to explain why an attack failed, but a
separate attacker to determine how to attack next—which achieves the
highest aggregate ASR among the compared designs across the evaluated
domains, frameworks, and threat models, with the same ordering
transferring to GPT-5.5. These conclusions are limited to the models,
frameworks, tasks, and attack budgets covered by DTap-Bench and our
transfer study and may change under other safety policies or agent
architectures; the reasoning ablation concerns reasoning information
exposed by the evaluated systems rather than inaccessible internal
states, and the non-refusal residual under self-proposal is behavioral
evidence consistent with sandbagging rather than a direct measurement of
model intent. Fine-grained appendix slices are also smaller than the
aggregate evaluation and should be interpreted accordingly.

## Appendix A Detailed Method Motivation and Formulation

#### Failure diagnosis requires the original execution context.

Diagnosing a failed attack requires more than observing that the target
did not complete the adversarial objective. A useful critic must
identify *why* the attempt failed: which instruction the target
prioritized, which safety or task constraint blocked the attack, whether
a required environment condition was missing, whether a tool returned an
unexpected result, or at which point the adversarial objective stopped
influencing the trajectory. An external critic typically receives only
information selected by the harness—such as the attack, final response,
judge score, or serialized interaction history. That representation can
omit intermediate decisions, instruction hierarchy, tool-state
transitions, or reasoning information that was available during
execution. Prompt engineering can improve analysis of transferred
information, but it cannot reconstruct evidence that was never
transferred.

This limitation is especially important for agents. The same benign
final action can arise from distinct mechanisms: immediate rejection,
prioritization of a higher-level instruction, failure to retrieve a
required resource, a tool error, or abandonment of the attack after
observing environment state. These cases can look identical from the
final response while requiring different revisions. Effective criticism
therefore depends on the trajectory that produced the outcome, not only
on the outcome itself.

#### Self-criticism preserves diagnostic context.

Let $`h_{k}^{T}`$ denote the target session’s live execution context
after attempt $`k`$. It includes the instructions available to the
target, interaction history, tool calls and observations, environment
state, and any reasoning exposed by the model or framework. We append a
criticism instruction $`\rho_{C}`$ directly to this context and sample
$`c_{k}\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{k}^{T},\rho_{C})`$. The instruction
asks the target to identify the decision point at which the attack
failed, the constraint or observation that blocked it, and the change
needed for a subsequent attempt. At this stage the target is asked only
to diagnose its prior behavior, not to generate a new attack. The
reasoning-context ablation in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Reasoning in the Execution Context Makes Criticism Actionable ‣ 3 Evaluation and Findings ‣ The Target Agent Is Its Own Best Critic but a Poor Proposer: Self-Improved Red Teaming")
tests the value of this retained information.

#### Why self-proposal creates a different problem.

Under self-proposal, the target generates $`x_{k+1}`$ in the same
session that produced the failed trajectory and criticism. The target
therefore has simultaneous access to the adversarial objective, its
safety and task constraints, the failure diagnosis, and the strategy
needed to circumvent that failure. This context can be useful for
understanding the vulnerability while also making the harmful purpose of
the proposal explicit. Prior work suggests that safety-trained models
can balance competing capability and safety objectives ([Wei et al.,
2023](#bib.bib13)), condition behavior on role and evaluation context
([Laine et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)), and underperform in ways consistent
with sandbagging ([van der Weij et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). We therefore
distinguish explicit refusal from non-refusal degradation in proposal
effectiveness.

#### External proposal isolates adversarial action.

A separate attacker receives the adversarial objective, prior candidate
and outcome, relevant attack history, and target-generated criticism,
but it does not continue from the target’s task-serving execution
context. This preserves the diagnosis while removing the specific
contextual conflict created when the target is asked to exploit its own
failure analysis. Dedicated attacker sessions are already effective in
PAIR ([Chao et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)), TAP ([Mehrotra et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8)), and GPTFuzzer ([Yu et al., 2023](#bib.bib16)); role
separation adds a further distinction by outsourcing diagnosis to the
target while keeping attack generation external.

## Appendix C Detailed Evaluation Results

### C.1 Self-Criticism Breakdown

For direct misuse, self-criticism increases ASR from $`42.5\%`$ to
$`43.5\%`$ on Gemini-3.1-Pro with Google ADK, from $`75.7\%`$ to
$`85.5\%`$ on GPT-5.1 with the OpenAI Agents SDK, and from $`80.5\%`$ to
$`86.2\%`$ on GPT-5.1 OpenClaw. For indirect prompt injection, the
corresponding improvements are $`26.8\%\rightarrow 43.0\%`$,
$`55.5\%\rightarrow 66.1\%`$, and $`56.8\%\rightarrow 61.4\%`$. The
direction is consistent in all six framework–threat slices.

The domain-level trend is likewise consistent. For direct misuse, the
largest gain occurs in Workflow ($`44.4\%\rightarrow 66.7\%`$, $`+22.3`$
points), followed by CRM ($`+8.5`$), Code ($`+4.5`$), and Telecom
($`+2.4`$). For indirect attacks, the gains are $`9.6`$ points in
Workflow, $`8.5`$ in Telecom, $`6.6`$ in CRM, and $`5.7`$ in Code. The
particularly large Workflow gain is consistent with the hypothesis that
self-criticism is most valuable when failures depend on multi-step state
transitions that are difficult to summarize for a separate critic.

### C.2 Self-Proposal Breakdown

Under direct misuse, self-proposal reduces ASR from $`43.5\%`$ to
$`38.1\%`$ on Gemini-3.1-Pro with Google ADK, from $`85.5\%`$ to
$`60.6\%`$ on GPT-5.1 with the OpenAI Agents SDK, and from $`86.2\%`$ to
$`56.7\%`$ on GPT-5.1 OpenClaw. Under indirect prompt injection, the
corresponding reductions are $`1.7`$, $`22.0`$, and $`18.7`$ percentage
points. The domain-level trend is also uniform: self-proposal
underperforms external proposal in every domain–threat slice. The
largest drop occurs for direct Workflow attacks ($`-44.5`$ points),
followed by direct Telecom ($`-24.7`$), direct CRM ($`-21.4`$), and
indirect Workflow ($`-20.7`$).

### C.3 Refusal and Non-Refusal Degradation

Refusal depends strongly on the model and harness. Gemini-3.1-Pro with
Google ADK has high task-level refusal under all three configurations
($`70.8\%`$–$`80.2\%`$), suggesting a broad model- or framework-level
constraint rather than an effect unique to self-proposal. GPT-5.1
OpenClaw, by contrast, rises from approximately $`0\%`$ refusal under
external proposal to $`84.0\%`$ under self-proposal, indicating a strong
session- or harness-dependent effect. GPT-5.1 with the OpenAI Agents SDK
has only $`3.6\%`$ refusal under self-proposal despite a large ASR
reduction. We use this low-refusal residual as behavioral evidence
consistent with non-refusal sandbagging, while avoiding claims about
unobserved internal intent.
````

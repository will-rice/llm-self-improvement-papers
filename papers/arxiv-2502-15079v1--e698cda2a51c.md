# Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?

Lingjun Zhao ^(†)^(†)thanks: This work was partially conducted during an
internship at Honda Research Institute.

###### Abstract

Large Vision-Language Models often generate hallucinated content that is
not grounded in its visual inputs. While prior work focuses on
mitigating hallucinations, we instead explore leveraging hallucination
correction as a training objective to improve video-language alignment.
We introduce HACA, a self-training framework learning to correct
hallucinations in descriptions that do not align with the video content.
By identifying and correcting inconsistencies, HACA enhances the model’s
ability to align video and textual representations for spatio-temporal
reasoning. Our experimental results show consistent gains in
video-caption binding and text-to-video retrieval tasks, demonstrating
that hallucination correction-inspired tasks serve as an effective
strategy for improving vision and language alignment.

## 1 Introduction

Aligning representations across modalities involves creating joint
embeddings that map visual and linguistic features to a shared space,
enabling the model to assess their similarity. This is crucial for tasks
including cross-modal retrieval [Xu et al. (2016a)](#bib.bib43),
mitigating hallucinations [Jiang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib13),
compositional reasoning [Cascante-Bonilla et al. (2024)](#bib.bib5),
visual-to-text generation [Li et al. (2022)](#bib.bib15), visual
question answering [Shen et al. (2022)](#bib.bib34), and visual-language
navigation [Zhao et al. (2021)](#bib.bib54). While progress has been
made in aligning image representations with text [Li et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib19); [Zeng et al. (2022)](#bib.bib51); [Wang et al.
(2023a)](#bib.bib38); [Lin et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22), advancements in
video-language alignment remain limited. Videos pose unique challenges
due to their rich spatio-temporal information, involving multiple
entities and scenes that dynamically interact and change over time.
Video-language models (Video-LLMs in
[§ 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Preliminaries: Video-LLMs ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?"))
can compute alignment scores [Lin et al. (2023)](#bib.bib20); [Li et al.
(2023b)](#bib.bib17) but struggle to distinguish between similar videos
and descriptions [Park et al. (2022)](#bib.bib29); [Wang et al.
(2023b)](#bib.bib40); [Saravanan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib31), as
illustrated in
[Figure 1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?").
One promising approach is to fine-tune Video-LLMs on entailment tasks
using similar captions [Bansal et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3), where the
model is prompted to answer Yes or No to whether a video is aligned with
a given caption
([§ 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Preliminaries: Video-LLMs ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")).
However, using a single binary label as a learning signal fails to
indicate which parts of the description misalign with the video. [Bansal
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3) generates natural language explanations for
mismatches but requires costly dataset construction with additional
models and annotations.

![Refer to caption](2502.15079v1/qualitative_example3.png)

Figure 1: Models tasked with determining whether a given video entails a
caption, where the contrast caption closely resembles the correct one.
HACA effectively differentiates between the correct caption (top) and
the incorrect one (bottom), and corrects hallucination in the latter. In
contrast, Video-LLaVA fails to distinguish between those captions or
correct the hallucination.

To this end, we introduce HACA, a self-training framework grounded in
HAllucination Correction for video-language Alignment
([§ 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Learning from Hallucination Correction ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")).
Hallucination (or confabulation) refers to a mismatch between textual
descriptions and the corresponding factual content of an image or video
[Liu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24). HACA requires the model to predict
whether a description entails the video content. If the description does
not align, the model corrects the hallucinations to better match the
video. Instead of relying solely on a binary entailment label, HACA uses
hallucination correction as a finer-grained learning signal to enhance
the alignment of video and language representations. Given that
misalignment between modalities is a key factor in hallucination [Biten
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib4); [Sun et al. (2024)](#bib.bib35), we
hypothesize that introducing a hallucination correction task can improve
video-language alignment. HACA also requires no external models or
annotations beyond the ground-truth video description. To further
enhance HACA, we introduce a masking correction task as data
augmentation
([§ 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Masking Correction as Augmentation ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")).

We fine-tune two Video-LLMs with HACA, and evaluate these fine-tuned
models in a zero-shot manner on two spatio-temporally challenging
downstream tasks
([§ 4](#S4 "4 Experimental Setup ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")):
VELOCITI [Saravanan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib31), a video-caption binding
dataset, and SSv2-Temporal [Sevilla-Lara et al. (2021)](#bib.bib33) and
SSv2-Events [Bagad et al. (2023)](#bib.bib1), which are text-to-video
retrieval datasets emphasizing action recognition. The models fine-tuned
with HACA outperform baseline models by up to 17.9% accuracy and 5.7 mAP
points, demonstrating that HACA effectively improves video-text
alignments, and generalizes beyond in-domain data
([§ 5](#S5 "5 Analysis ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")).
Our code and data will be available upon acceptance.

## 2 Related Work

In addition to the video-language alignment approaches discussed in
[§ 1](#S1 "1 Introduction ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?"),
several methods leverage a contrastive learning objective to learn a
shared video-language embedding space [Xue et al. (2023)](#bib.bib46);
[Rasheed et al. (2023)](#bib.bib30); [Girdhar et al. (2023)](#bib.bib9);
[Zhu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib57), and [Bagad et al. (2023)](#bib.bib1)
further introduces a contrastive loss in Video-LLMs to enforce
time-order consistency. However, most of these models lack robustness to
semantically plausible manipulations [Park et al. (2022)](#bib.bib29).
[Yuksekgonul et al. (2023)](#bib.bib50) also finds that applying a
contrastive objective to video-caption datasets does not promote the
model’s ability to capture fine-grained details. In contrast, our
approach encourages Video-LLMs to capture more nuanced semantic
mismatches by learning to correct hallucinations, extending beyond
sentence-level hallucination detection. More discussion is provided in
[Appendix B](#A2 "Appendix B Related Work ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?").

Figure 2: Example of different finetuning objectives. The first column
shows an example of the baseline entailment task. The second column
shows an example of our proposed HACA task, where we finetune the model
to output hallucination correction to justify the response. The third
column shows an example of the masking correction task, where we input a
masked version of the video description and finetune the model to
predict the corrected one.

## 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment

To investigate whether hallucination correction can improve
video-language alignment, we introduce HACA, a fine-tuning objective for
Video-LLM as a sequence-to-sequence generation task.

### 3.1 Preliminaries: Video-LLMs

Video-LLMs typically consist of three parts: i) a visual encoder to map
images and videos to visual representations; ii) an LLM that takes text
instructions as inputs to generate text responses; and iii) an adapter
between visual and text representations. Our approach finetunes (ii) the
text decoder and (iii) the adapter, freezing (i) the visual encoder.

#### Pre-training.

A Video-LLM $`M_{\theta}`$ parameterized by $`\theta`$ takes a textual
question or instruction $`\boldsymbol{Q}`$, a video $`\boldsymbol{V}`$
as input, and generates a text response $`\boldsymbol{A}`$ =
$`(\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\boldsymbol{A}_{2},...,\boldsymbol{A}_{T})`$
autoregressively using a decoder-based language model (LLM) as output,
by estimating a conditional distribution
$`M(\boldsymbol{A}\mid\boldsymbol{Q},\boldsymbol{V})`$. This is achieved
by training the model using the maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE)
objective:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
L(\theta)=\sum_{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log M_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{A}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{A}_{<t},\boldsymbol{Q},\boldsymbol{V})
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\boldsymbol{A}_{t}`$ is $`t`$-th word of the text response, and
$`\boldsymbol{A}_{<t}`$ are the first $`t-1`$ words of the response. The
dataset $`\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}`$ consists of samples in the form
$`(\boldsymbol{Q},\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{V})`$.

#### Fine-tuning with entailment.

Following [Bansal et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3), we finetune the Video-LLM
using an entailment task, where the text input $`\boldsymbol{Q}`$ is
formatted as an entailment question as
$`\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{W})=`$ Does this caption accurately
describe the video? Caption: {$`\boldsymbol{W}`$}. In this task, the
output of the model $`\boldsymbol{A}`$ is Yes or No
([Figure 2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")
(a)). Given a dataset $`D_{train}`$ consisting of ground-truth answers
$`\boldsymbol{A}`$ for $`\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{W})`$ and
$`\boldsymbol{V}`$, the model is fine-tuned to have a better estimation
of
$`M_{\theta}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\texttt{Yes}}~|~\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{W}),\boldsymbol{V})`$
and
$`M_{\theta}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\texttt{No}}~|~\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{W}),\boldsymbol{V})`$
using the MLE objective:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
L_{ent}(\theta)=\sum_{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}}\log M_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\mid\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{W}),\boldsymbol{V})
``` |  | (2) |

### 3.2 Learning from Hallucination Correction

Building on the work of [Bansal et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3), the Video-LLM
takes the question $`\boldsymbol{Q}`$ and the video $`\boldsymbol{V}`$
as input to determine whether a text description $`\boldsymbol{W}`$
entails the video (similar
to [§ 3.1](#S3.SS1.SSS0.Px2 "Fine-tuning with entailment. ‣ 3.1 Preliminaries: Video-LLMs ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")).
However, in our setting, if $`\boldsymbol{W}`$ does not entail
$`\boldsymbol{V}`$, the model generates a corrected caption
$`\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}=(w_{1},w_{2},...,w_{n})`$ to align the
description with the video content. During fine-tuning, if
$`\boldsymbol{W}`$ entails $`\boldsymbol{V}`$, the model is trained to
generate the response as
$`\boldsymbol{A}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\texttt{Yes}})=`$ Yes, the
caption accurately describes the video. If $`\boldsymbol{W}`$ does not
entail $`\boldsymbol{V}`$, the model is trained to generate a corrected
description $`\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}`$ as its response, formatted as
$`\boldsymbol{A}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\texttt{No}},\boldsymbol{\hat{W}})=`$
No. This caption shall be corrected as: {$`\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}`$}. We
show an example in
[Figure 2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")
(b). In contrast to finetuning using entailment only, our hallucination
correction objective trains the model to have better estimation of
$`M_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{A}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\texttt{Yes}})~|~\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{W}),\boldsymbol{V})`$
and
$`M_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{A}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\texttt{No}},\boldsymbol{\hat{W}})|\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{W}),\boldsymbol{V})`$.

Instead of using
 [Eq 2](#S3.E2 "2 ‣ Fine-tuning with entailment. ‣ 3.1 Preliminaries: Video-LLMs ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?"),
given a training dataset $`\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}`$ that consists
of video $`\boldsymbol{V}`$ and ground-truth text description
$`\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}`$ pairs, we fine-tune the Video-LLM using the MLE
objective:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
L_{c}(\theta)=\sum_{\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{train}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log M_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{A}_{t}\mid\boldsymbol{A}_{<t},\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{W}),\boldsymbol{V})
``` |  | (3) |

where $`\boldsymbol{A}_{t}`$ is the $`t`$-th word of the text response
of $`\boldsymbol{A}({\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\texttt{Yes}})`$ or
$`\boldsymbol{A}`$(No, $`\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}`$), and
$`\boldsymbol{A}_{<t}`$ is the first $`t-1`$ words of the text response.

### 3.3 Masking Correction as Augmentation

We also incorporate a masking correction task as data augmentation
([Figure 2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")
(c)), where an instruction $`\boldsymbol{Q}`$ prompts the Video-LLM to
make corrections to a masked caption $`\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}`$, teaching
the model to generate a corrected caption that contains a sequence of
words $`\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}=(w_{1},w_{2},...,w_{n})`$ as its answer by
estimating conditional probability
$`M(\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}\mid\boldsymbol{Q}(\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}),\boldsymbol{V})`$.
Specifically, $`\boldsymbol{Q}`$ is a function that formats the text
instruction as $`\boldsymbol{Q}(\bar{\boldsymbol{W}})=`$ Please correct
this caption to accurately describe the video. Caption:
$`\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}`$, where $`\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}`$ is masked from
$`\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}`$:
$`\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}=(w_{1},{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\texttt{[MASK]}},...,w_{n})`$,
by randomly masking 45% of the content words in the ground truth video
description $`\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}`$.

We finetune the model using two objectives: the MLE objective to
estimate the probability for masking correction
$`M_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\hat{W}}|\boldsymbol{Q}(\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}),\boldsymbol{V})`$,
and the HACA objective
([Eq 3](#S3.E3 "3 ‣ 3.2 Learning from Hallucination Correction ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")).
The model is tasked with providing responses corresponding to different
instructions.

## 4 Experimental Setup

#### Data (detailed in [§ A.5](#A1.SS5 "A.5 Datasets ‣ Appendix A Appendices ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")).

We train HACA using videos and their ground-truth and contrastive
descriptions from VideoCon [Bansal et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3), generating
115,536 (video, description, correction) triplets for training and 8,312
for validation, which is used for model selection. Synthetic contrast
captions are also used to fine-tune the baseline entailment task with
the same dataset sizes.

We evaluate our trained models on text-to-video retrieval using the
temporally-challenging SSv2-Temporal [Sevilla-Lara et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib33) and action-intensive SSv2-Events [Bagad et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib1) datasets. Additionally, we evaluate our models on
compositional ability over time using the VELOCITI benchmark [Saravanan
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib31). Each video in the dataset includes a correct
caption and an incorrect one.

#### Baselines.

\(i\) Pretrained Video-LLMs: we employ two pre-trained models with
different architectures, Video-LLaVA [Lin et al. (2023)](#bib.bib20) and
VideoChat2 [Li et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib17). More details in
[§ A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Pretrained Video-LLMs. ‣ Appendix A Appendices ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?").
(ii) Entailment: we fine-tune the pretrained Video-LLMs using the
entailment task described in
[§ 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Preliminaries: Video-LLMs ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?").
More details about the implementation are in
[§ A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Implementation Details ‣ Appendix A Appendices ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?").

#### Evaluation metrics.

We report the accuracy on the VELOCITI benchmark as the proportion of
examples in which the positive video-caption pair receives a higher Yes
entailment probability than the corresponding negative video-caption
pair. For SSv2, we compute Yes probabilities for each text-video pair,
rank their scores, and report mean Average Precision (mAP).

## 5 Analysis

#### Performance on text-to-video retrieval.

HACA consistently outperforms both the pretrained model and the
entailment fine-tuned model, as illustrated in
[Figure 3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Evaluation metrics. ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?").
This demonstrates HACA’s ability to effectively capture the rich
temporal information present in videos. On the SSv2-Events dataset,
while the entailment objective yields performance comparable to the
pretrained Video-LLaVA, HACA achieves better results on this
action-intensive dataset, despite being fine-tuned on the same amount of
data. Additional comparisons with other models are provided in
[§ A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Ablation studies ‣ Appendix A Appendices ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?").

#### Performance on video-language binding.

[Table 1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Evaluation metrics. ‣ 4 Experimental Setup ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")
shows that, on average, both Video-LLaVA and VideoChat2 fine-tuned with
the HACA objective outperform the pre-trained models and those
fine-tuned with the entailment objective. Masking correction further
boosts performance through data augmentation. The Agent Coref test
evaluates a model’s ability to link events to specific agents, a
misalignment type absent in the VideoCon dataset, where actions are
always tied to one agent. Consequently, the pretrained Video-LLaVA
outperforms its fine-tuned versions, with HACA marginally exceeding the
entailment baseline. The Chrono test measures a model’s ability to
detect reversed event order. While VideoCon includes such data, our
results show that models fine-tuned on the entailment objective perform
similarly to the pretrained model. Although HACA slightly underperforms
the entailment objective, it excels on SSv2-Events, involving multiple
events.

HACA consistently outperforms baseline models in all Action tests:
Action Adv (replacing an action with one not in the video), Action Bind
(replacing an action within the same video), and Action Modif (replacing
the manner with a plausible modifier). This highlights HACA’s robust
ability to distinguish actions in videos, requiring understanding of
complex spatio-temporal relationships between the video and its
description. HACA also excels in Agent Iden and Agent Bind, showcasing
its effectiveness in identifying and binding entities through the right
relationship.

#### HACA does not hinder question answering.

To assess whether fine-tuning with the HACA objective affects the
multi-task capabilities of Video-LLMs, we conduct a zero-shot evaluation
on the MSRVTT-QA dataset [Xu et al. (2016a)](#bib.bib43) using
GPT-3.5-turbo. The results, presented in
[Table 2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ HACA does not hinder question answering. ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?"),
are based on a subset of 7,000 samples (10% of the dataset) due to
budget constraints. The GPT-evaluated score for the pretrained
Video-LLaVA model aligns with previously reported values [Lin et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib20). As shown in the table, the HACA-finetuned model
performs comparably to the pretrained Video-LLaVA model, despite not
involving explicit QA-specific finetuning. In contrast, fine-tuning with
the entailment objective alone leads to a significant performance drop
on MSRVTT-QA. A possible explanation is that optimizing for a single
entailment label may impair the language generation capabilities of
video-language models.

## 6 Conclusion

Video understanding through language is vital for applications like
human-robot interaction and autonomous driving. We propose a novel
approach to enhance video-language alignment by connecting it to the
hallucination problem in visual-language models, paving the way for
future advancements.

## Limitations

Our proposed method assumes the availability of ground-truth video
caption annotations for fine-tuning using hallucination correction.
Additionally, the method assumes a clear separation between the
parameters of the video representations and those of the language model,
as we freeze the video encoder parameters during fine-tuning to align
video-language representations. Another limitation is that our approach
has not been evaluated on long videos, due to the limitation of
computational resources. We envision future work in this direction.

## Appendix A Appendices

### A.1 Implementation Details

#### Fine-tuning from pretrained models.

We use the visual representations for video and language model
embeddings pretrained from Video-LLMs to perform instruction fine-tuning
using different objectives, including HACA
([§ 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Learning from Hallucination Correction ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")),
entailment
([§ 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Preliminaries: Video-LLMs ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")),
and masking correction
([§ 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Masking Correction as Augmentation ‣ 3 HACA: Hallucination Correction for Video-language Alignment ‣ Can Hallucination Correction Improve Video-Language Alignment?")).
During finetuning, visual representations are frozen, and the embeddings
from the visual-text adapter layers and LLM are learnable.

#### Hyperparameters and computation.

For Video-LLaVA, we finetune our models for $`3`$ epochs, using a
learning rate of $`2e^{-4}`$ and AdamW optimizer. We also use a LoRA
adapter [Hu et al. (2022)](#bib.bib11) of rank $`128`$ and alpha
$`256`$. Since we freeze the video encoder, the number of trainable
parameters is significantly reduced to $`241`$M for Video-LLaVA. The
number of video frames processed per video is $`8`$, with a batch size
of $`8`$, using $`2`$ RTXA6000 GPUs, for a total of $`\sim 72`$ hours.

For VideoChat2, we finetune our models for $`3`$ epochs, using a
learning rate of $`2e^{-5}`$ and AdamW optimizer. We use a LoRA
adapter [Hu et al. (2022)](#bib.bib11) of rank $`16`$ and alpha $`32`$.
We also freeze the visual encoders and reduce the number of trainable
parameters to $`193`$M. The number of video frames processed per video
is $`8`$, with a batch size of $`2`$, using $`1`$ RTXA6000 GPU, for
around $`\sim 72`$ hours.

#### Tools.

We implement our models with Pytorch 2.0.1, Huggingface Transformers
4.31.0, scikit-learn 1.2.2. We use SciPy 1.6.0 to find content words
from ground truth video description by excluding words with
part-of-speech tags: AUX, SYM, DET, PUNCT.

### A.4 Pretrained Video-LLMs.

We use two pre-trained Video-LLMs with different model architectures.

#### Video-LLaVA.

Video-LLaVA [Lin et al. (2023)](#bib.bib20) consists of
LanguageBind [Zhu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib57) encoders for the visual
inputs, a large language model [Team (2023)](#bib.bib36), visual
projection layers and a word embedding layer. It is finetuned via visual
instruction tuning with 665k image-text pairs from LLaVA 1.5 [Liu et al.
(2023b)](#bib.bib25) and a 100k video-text instruction set from
Video-ChatGPT [Maaz et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26). We use this model under
their Apache License 2.0.

#### VideoChat2.

VideoChat2 [Li et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib17) performs a progressive
multi-modal training for three stages. In the first stage, it is trained
to aling the visual encoder with a Querying Transformer (Q-Former) [Li
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib15) which acts as an information bottleneck
between the image and textual encoders and distill relevant information
to the textual context. The second stage connects the visual encoder
with a pretrained LLM. In the third stage, finetunes the model via
instruction tuning, using 5 different tasks including: captioning,
conversations, visual question answering, reasoning and classification,
with data coming from LLaVA [Liu et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib25),
VideoChat [Li et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib16), VideoChatGPT [Maaz et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib26), COCO Captions [Lin et al. (2014)](#bib.bib21),
WebVid [Bain et al. (2021)](#bib.bib2), YouCook [Das et al.
(2013)](#bib.bib7), OK-VQA [Marino et al. (2019)](#bib.bib27),
AOK-VQA [Schwenk et al. (2022)](#bib.bib32), DocVQA [Mathew et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib28), CLEVR [Johnson et al. (2017)](#bib.bib14),
CLEVRER [Yi et al. (2020)](#bib.bib48) and NExT-QA [Xiao et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib41) among others. We use this model under their MIT
License.

### A.5 Datasets

#### VideoCon.

VideoCon is constructed by generating contrastive video captions and
explanations for different subset of videos [Xu et al.
(2016b)](#bib.bib44); [Wang et al. (2019)](#bib.bib39); [Hendricks
et al. (2018)](#bib.bib10). This dataset contains seven misaligned types
that include replacement of objects, actions, attributes, counts and
relations, and adds hallucinations (i.e. unrelated but plausible
information). We use this dataset under their MIT License.

#### VELOCITI.

The duration of the video clips in the dataset is 10 seconds, and has
dense text annotations on action and role descriptions. The
perception-based tests require discriminating video-caption pairs that
share similar entities, and the binding tests require models to
associate the correct entity to a given situation while ignoring the
different yet plausible entities that also appear in the same video.
There are 1000 tests using 643 videos for Agent Iden, 1676 tests using
707 videos for Agent Bind, 418 tests using 270 videos for Agent Coref,
500 tests using 400 videos for Action Adv, 1625 tests using 590 videos
for Action Bind, 500 tests using 411 videos for Action Mod, and 1908
tests using 669 videos for Chrono. We use this dataset under their
Creative Commons Public Licenses.

#### SSv2-Temporal and SSv2-Events.

SSv2-Temporal contains a list of 18 actions that require models to
capture rich temporal information in the video, consisting of 216 (18
×12) candidate videos for every text action query. SSv2-Events has 49
actions that consist two verbs in the action templates that are
indicative of multiple events in the video, consisting of 2888 (49×12)
candidate videos for every text action query.

## Appendix B Related Work

Alignment in Video-Language Models is fundamental for the logical
integration of video and textual information. To align both modalities,
prior work has focused on pre-training models with different objectives
to capture the temporal dynamics in video. While these self-supervised
correction objectives are highly effective during pre-training [Li
et al. (2023c)](#bib.bib18); [Wang et al. (2022)](#bib.bib37); [Zhu
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib57); [Ge et al. (2022)](#bib.bib8), fine-tuning
is typically required to adapt Video-LLMs to specific downstream
tasks [Li et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib16); [Zhang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib52); [Bansal et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3) (e.g.,
classification, retrieval, or question answering). However, these
objectives rely on coarse-grained alignment labels and do not provide
detailed feedback for resolving inconsistencies between video and
language.

Hallucination Correction methods aim to mitigate the generation of
content that does not align with the data a model was trained on, or the
model describes content that does not exist in the provided input [Huang
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib12). Orthogonal to our proposed method,
LURE [Zhou et al. (2024)](#bib.bib56) uses statistical analysis to
identify and rectify errors in generated descriptions, addressing
co-occurrence, uncertainty, and positional factors via masking. In our
work, we randomly mask the video description so that the model is
required to output the corrected sentence, which is also conditioned in
the input video via visual entailment. [Yin et al. (2023)](#bib.bib49);
[Wang et al. (2023b)](#bib.bib40) uses external models and measures to
correct hallucinations to be consistent with images or videos. [Zhou
et al. (2021)](#bib.bib55); [Liu et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib23); [Xiao
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib42); [Zhao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib53) create a
synthetic dataset to train a specialized model to detect and correct
hallucinations. [Dale et al. (2022)](#bib.bib6); [Huang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib12) shows promising results on correcting hallucinations
without an external model for machine translation and image captioning.
In our work, we investigate leveraging hallucination as a training
objective to improve video-language alignment, by exploring the
potential of using a video-LLM model itself to correct hallucinations
through fine-tuning on a synthetic dataset.
````

# SafeInferCom: Safe Inference-Time Compute via Verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention for Robotic Task Planning

Weizhe Xu    Jialiang Fan    Mengyu Liu Affiliation: University of Notre
Dame  Washington State University{wxu3, jfan5, fkong}@nd.edu,
mengyu.liu@wsu.edu    Fanxin Kong

###### Abstract

Large Reasoning Language Models (LRLMs) enable multi-step reasoning for
robotic task planning, but continued reasoning can overwrite valid
intermediate plans or leave constraint violations unresolved, reducing
planning reliability and wasting inference-time computation. We develop
an inference-time monitor that exposes and verifies intermediate plans
without disrupting the original decoding trajectory. Building on this
monitor, we propose SafeInferCom, a formal verifier-guided framework
that preserves valid intermediate plans and directs error correction
during generation. Experiments across multiple LRLMs and planning
domains reveal reasoning–response inconsistency and limited
self-correction under one-shot inference. SafeInferCom improves planning
success and accelerates error correction relative to one-shot inference.
When combined with iterative refinement, it further improves success
while reducing token usage compared with refinement alone. We
additionally evaluate SafeInferCom in VirtualHome and provide a
real-world robotic-arm demonstration.

## I Introduction

Robotic task planning converts high-level goals into executable action
sequences while ensuring that safety constraints are satisfied
throughout execution. Classical planners provide a principled and
auditable solution when an accurate symbolic model is available:
planners such as Fast Downward search over explicit action models, while
validators such as VAL verify candidate plans against the given
specification \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2)\]. However, this
formalism has two practical limitations. First, it requires rigid
symbolic task representations and carefully engineered domain models.
Second, symbolic search can become computationally expensive as the
number of objects, applicable actions, and planning horizon
increase \[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. Learning-based planners enable fast
inference through learned policies, but depend on training data and
generally lack explicit safety guarantees \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[5](#bib.bib5)\].

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been explored for robotic task
planning, leveraging their ability to interpret high-level instructions
and contextual descriptions to generate candidate action
sequences \[[6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib7)\]. While conventional LLM
planners typically generate a final plan directly from the input, Large
Reasoning Language Models (LRLMs) first perform explicit multi-step
reasoning before producing the final output \[[8](#bib.bib8),
[9](#bib.bib9)\]. By allocating additional computation at inference time
to this intermediate reasoning process, LRLMs can deliberate over
candidate actions and constraints before committing to a plan.

However, additional reasoning does not always lead to a better plan.
Through fine-grained analysis, we observe reasoning–response
inconsistency across models and planning domains. In some cases, a model
develops a safe and goal-satisfying intermediate plan but later
overwrites it through continued reasoning, ultimately producing an
incorrect final output. Conversely, constraint-violating intermediate
plans may persist across successive reasoning steps without effective
correction. Such instability reduces planning reliability and wastes
inference-time computation on trajectories that could have been
terminated or corrected earlier.

Existing approaches improve LLM-based planning reliability by using
external formal verifiers to validate completed plans, followed by
iterative refinement or verification-based candidate
selection \[[10](#bib.bib10), [7](#bib.bib7), [11](#bib.bib11),
[12](#bib.bib12)\]. While these methods can improve final-plan validity,
the verifier typically provides feedback only after a candidate has been
fully generated. For LRLMs, a valid intermediate plan may therefore be
overwritten, or a constraint violation may persist, before external
verification occurs. This motivates integrating formal verification into
the reasoning process to preserve valid intermediate plans and guide
correction before generation is complete.

To systematically analyze reasoning-level safety issues, we first
develop an inference-time reasoning monitor that instruments LRLM
generation without altering its primary decoding trajectory. The monitor
extracts structured action sequences at selected reasoning transitions
and evaluates them with an external formal verifier, while allowing the
model to resume reasoning from the original state. This enables
fine-grained analysis of reasoning–response consistency under standard
one-shot inference.

Building on this monitoring infrastructure, we introduce SafeInferCom, a
verifier-guided mid-generation controller with four actions: continue a
safe but incomplete trajectory, inject diagnostic feedback after a
detected violation, terminate generation once a valid plan is
identified, or trigger replanning after persistent violations. Unlike
post-hoc refinement, SafeInferCom intervenes before a complete failed
trajectory is generated, allowing violations to be corrected during
reasoning and earlier valid candidates to be preserved without
restarting the entire inference process. In this way, SafeInferCom
shifts formal verification from post-hoc plan checking toward
mid-generation guidance, improving planning reliability while avoiding
unnecessary continued reasoning once a valid intermediate plan is
identified.

Our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) An inference-time
reasoning monitor that extracts and formally verifies intermediate plans
from LRLM reasoning prefixes while preserving the original decoding
trajectory, enabling fine-grained analysis of reasoning–response
inconsistency. (2) SafeInferCom, a verifier-guided mid-generation
controller with four actions: continuation, targeted correction, early
termination, and replanning after persistent violations. (3) A
comprehensive evaluation across multiple LRLMs and robotic planning
domains, characterizing intermediate-plan fidelity, self-correction
behavior, and the success–compute trade-off against one-shot inference
and post-hoc refinement. Additional VirtualHome and real-robot
manipulation studies demonstrate the method in more realistic settings.

## II Related Work

### II-A Classical, Learned, and Language-Based Task Planning

Classical planners search over explicit symbolic transition models,
providing formal plan validation \[[1](#bib.bib1), [2](#bib.bib2)\].
However, they require carefully constructed symbolic representations and
can incur explosive search cost as the number of objects, applicable
actions, or planning horizon grows \[[3](#bib.bib3)\]. Reinforcement and
imitation learning instead learn policies or search guidance from
demonstrations or environment interaction \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[5](#bib.bib5)\]. These methods amortize planning computation through
learned policies, but their performance remains tied to the training
distribution and formal guarantees require an external verifier.
Language-based planners offer greater input flexibility by generating
action sequences from natural-language instructions and contextual
descriptions \[[13](#bib.bib13), [14](#bib.bib14)\], motivating recent
efforts to combine language-model planning with symbolic reasoning and
verification.

### II-B Language Models with Symbolic Planning and Verification

Language models can play different roles in symbolically grounded
planning systems. Some works translate tasks into formal representations
and delegate planning to symbolic solvers \[[6](#bib.bib6),
[15](#bib.bib15)\]. ISR-LLM instead uses an LLM to generate plans from
symbolic domain and problem descriptions and refines them using formal
validator feedback \[[11](#bib.bib11)\]. Following a similar problem
setting, other approaches also use external formal verification or
temporal-constraint-based checking to validate LLM-generated plans
against explicit task specifications \[[16](#bib.bib16),
[10](#bib.bib10), [7](#bib.bib7), [17](#bib.bib17)\].

These methods typically invoke formal verification after a complete
candidate plan has been generated, or between successive generation
rounds. Consequently, they do not directly regulate the intermediate
reasoning trajectory within a single inference pass. For CoT and
LRLM-based planners, continued reasoning may therefore overwrite an
already valid intermediate plan or allow an invalid candidate to persist
before verification occurs. SafeInferCom instead applies formal
verification to candidate plans induced from intermediate reasoning
prefixes and uses the resulting feedback for mid-generation control.

Following this line of work \[[11](#bib.bib11), [16](#bib.bib16),
[10](#bib.bib10), [7](#bib.bib7), [17](#bib.bib17)\], we study
language-model-based planning under the same general setting, where
candidate plans can be checked against an explicit task specification
using an external formal verifier.

### II-C Evaluation of Language-Model Reasoning

Beyond outcome-level metrics such as accuracy and Pass@k, prior work has
examined the quality of intermediate reasoning in language models. Some
evaluation frameworks \[[18](#bib.bib18), [19](#bib.bib19)\] assess
properties including consistency, correctness, and informativeness
within reasoning traces. Step-aware verifiers further evaluate
intermediate reasoning steps to support final-answer
selection \[[20](#bib.bib20), [21](#bib.bib21)\], while other studies
investigate critique, error detection, reflection, and self-correction
capabilities across linguistic, mathematical, and symbolic reasoning
tasks \[[22](#bib.bib22), [23](#bib.bib23)\].

These efforts primarily evaluate reasoning quality at the language or
logical level rather than the validity of the action sequences implied
by intermediate reasoning under an explicit robotic task specification.
A reasoning trace may therefore appear coherent while inducing a
candidate plan that violates action preconditions, safety constraints,
or task goals. Moreover, intermediate-reasoning evaluation alone does
not provide an online mechanism for correcting an ongoing trajectory or
preserving an already valid candidate during generation.

### II-D Inference-Time Compute Scaling and Regulation

Recent work has shown that additional inference-time computation can
improve the performance of reasoning models \[[24](#bib.bib24)\].
Inference-time scaling methods allocate more reasoning steps or sample
additional trajectories to improve solution quality on challenging
tasks. Adaptive approaches further regulate computation during
generation through restarting, pruning, or early
termination \[[25](#bib.bib25), [26](#bib.bib26), [27](#bib.bib27)\].

These methods primarily regulate inference based on predicted response
quality, confidence, or the expected utility of additional computation.
In contrast, SafeInferCom uses external formal verification of
intermediate candidate plans as the control signal. This enables both
corrective intervention and verified early termination, directly linking
inference-time compute regulation to planning validity under an explicit
task specification, while reusing the prefix decoding state to reduce
monitoring overhead.

### III-A Robotic Task Planning with Safety Constraints

We consider deterministic and fully observable robotic planning problems
defined by a planning domain $`\mathcal{D}`$, an initial state
$`s_{0}`$, a goal condition $`G`$, and optional explicit safety
constraints $`\mathcal{S}`$. A plan
$`\pi=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{T}\rangle`$ induces a state trajectory
$`\tau(\pi)=\langle s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{T}\rangle`$ according to the
transition model specified by $`\mathcal{D}`$. A valid plan must be
executable under the action semantics of $`\mathcal{D}`$, satisfy the
safety constraints in $`\mathcal{S}`$ throughout execution, and reach a
final state satisfying $`G`$.

We assume access to an external formal verifier $`V`$ that evaluates a
candidate plan with respect to
$`\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{D},s_{0},G,\mathcal{S})`$ and returns one of
three outcomes: Unsafe, if the plan violates an action requirement or an
explicit safety constraint; in our standard PDDL benchmarks, Unsafe
primarily corresponds to action-precondition or applicability
violations. Safe-but-Not-Goal, if the plan is executable and safe but
does not satisfy $`G`$; and Valid, if the plan is both safe and
goal-achieving. For an invalid candidate, $`V`$ also returns the first
failing step and corresponding diagnostic information. In our
implementation, $`V`$ is instantiated using a standard PDDL plan
validator \[[2](#bib.bib2)\].

Any validity guarantee in this work is relative to the supplied task
specification $`\mathcal{P}`$ and assumes that $`V`$ correctly checks
candidate plans against it. It does not cover task-grounding errors,
omissions in the symbolic model, or failures in perception and physical
execution.

### III-B LRLM Inference-Time Reasoning Setting

Given a robotic planning problem $`\mathcal{P}`$ and its prompted
description $`x(\mathcal{P})`$, an LRLM generates an intermediate
reasoning trace followed by a final plan:
$`x(\mathcal{P})\rightarrow\mathbf{r}\rightarrow\pi`$, where
$`\mathbf{r}=\langle r_{1},\ldots,r_{K}\rangle`$ is the sequence of
reasoning tokens, $`K`$ is the number of reasoning tokens generated
during inference, and $`\pi`$ is the final generated plan. Unlike
conventional LLM planners that directly produce a final response, LRLMs
explicitly expose an intermediate reasoning process that shapes the
resulting plan. Although this reasoning is not executed in the
environment, it reflects the model’s evolving planning decisions before
producing the final output.

We use _reasoning–response inconsistency_ to describe cases in which the
planning decisions expressed during intermediate reasoning disagree with
the validity of the final plan. In particular, an LRLM may identify a
valid plan during reasoning but lose it through continued deliberation,
or may reach a valid final plan without exhibiting any valid
intermediate plan beforehand. The former can reduce planning
reliability, while the latter makes the decision process less
interpretable and reduces user confidence in the final result.

### III-C Problem Statement

We consider LRLM-based robotic planning settings in which candidate
plans can be evaluated by an external formal verifier against an
explicit task specification. Under standard one-shot inference, however,
the LRLM completes its reasoning trajectory and final response without
using this verifier during generation.

We therefore study two related objectives. The _monitoring objective_ is
to evaluate the natural-language reasoning process exposed during LRLM
inference in safety-critical robotic planning, without altering the
standard one-shot inference trajectory. The _control objective_ is to
use intermediate verification feedback to guide generation toward valid
plans while avoiding unnecessary continued reasoning after a valid
candidate has been identified.

## IV Inference-Time Reasoning Monitor

The inference-time reasoning monitor exposes intermediate planning
states during LRLM generation by converting selected reasoning prefixes
into candidate plans that can be evaluated by the external formal
verifier
(Fig. [1](#S3.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ III PRELIMINARIES AND PROBLEM STATEMENT ‣ SafeInferCom: Safe Inference-Time Compute via Verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention for Robotic Task Planning")).
It operates non-invasively under standard one-shot inference, enabling
analysis of how planning decisions evolve during reasoning while also
providing the verifier outcomes later used by SafeInferCom for
mid-generation control.

### IV-A Reasoning Transition Monitoring

We monitor reasoning prefixes $`\mathbf{r}_{<k}`$ at selected
checkpoints defined by reasoning transitions. Reasoning models often
naturally express reflection, verification, or changes of direction
through linguistic markers such as “wait”, “alternatively”, and “hmm”.
Recent work similarly uses such markers as lightweight boundaries for
segmenting reasoning traces into reasoning actions or
chunks \[[26](#bib.bib26), [28](#bib.bib28)\]. We therefore use these
transitions to identify points at which the model’s current planning
decisions are examined. These transitions are not explicitly requested
in the prompt.

Let $`\mathcal{W}`$ denote a finite set of transition-marker token
sequences and $`R_{\max}`$ the maximum number of monitoring checkpoints.
A checkpoint is triggered when the newly generated suffix matches a
marker in $`\mathcal{W}`$, subject to the budget $`R_{\max}`$. At each
checkpoint, primary decoding is paused and its current decoding state,
including the KV cache and random number generator (RNG) state, is
saved. The detected transition marker defines the boundary between the
preceding reasoning prefix $`\mathbf{r}_{<k}`$ and the next reasoning
stage.

### IV-B Intermediate Plan Induction

Reasoning prefixes are expressed in natural language and therefore
cannot be submitted directly to the formal verifier. To obtain a
verifiable representation, the monitor creates an auxiliary decoding
branch from the saved checkpoint state. On this auxiliary branch, the
detected transition marker is removed and a fixed induction prompt
$`p_{\mathrm{ind}}`$ is appended to the preceding reasoning prefix
$`\mathbf{r}_{<k}`$. The same LRLM is then instructed to express the
action sequence implied by its reasoning so far in the plan format
expected by the verifier.

The induction process is intended to expose, rather than repair or
complete, the planning decisions already reflected in the reasoning
prefix. It preserves planning errors, incorporates revisions already
expressed in the reasoning, and excludes explicitly rejected
alternatives. Formally,

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\pi_{k}\sim\mathcal{M}\!\left([x(\mathcal{P}),\mathbf{r}_{<k},p_{\mathrm{ind}}]\right),
``` |  |

where $`x(\mathcal{P})`$ is the planning input defined in
Section [III](#S3 "III PRELIMINARIES AND PROBLEM STATEMENT ‣ SafeInferCom: Safe Inference-Time Compute via Verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention for Robotic Task Planning"),
$`\mathcal{M}`$ denotes the LRLM, and $`\pi_{k}`$ is the induced
candidate plan.

The auxiliary branch reuses the cached model state associated with the
preceding reasoning prefix, so only the induction prompt and
induced-plan continuation require additional decoding. Using the same
LRLM for induction also avoids introducing an additional model
dependency and reduces cross-model discrepancies in interpreting the
planning decisions expressed by the reasoning prefix.
Transition-triggered induction further limits auxiliary decoding to
points associated with changes in the reasoning process.

### IV-C Verification and Reasoning Continuation

The formal verifier evaluates each induced candidate plan:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\small y_{k}=V(\pi_{k}),\ y_{k}\in\{\textsc{Valid},\textsc{Safe-but-Not-Goal},\textsc{Unsafe}\},`$ |  |

using the task specification and outcome definitions in
Section [III](#S3 "III PRELIMINARIES AND PROBLEM STATEMENT ‣ SafeInferCom: Safe Inference-Time Compute via Verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention for Robotic Task Planning").
For invalid candidates, the verifier additionally returns the first
failing step and corresponding diagnostic information. The sequence of
verifier outcomes provides a formal view of how the planning decisions
expressed in the natural-language reasoning evolve throughout inference
and supplies the control signal used by SafeInferCom.

After verification, the auxiliary branch is discarded and the saved
primary decoding state is restored, including its KV cache and RNG
state. Primary generation then resumes from the original checkpoint,
with neither the induction prompt, induced-plan tokens, nor verifier
outputs appended to the primary context. Consequently, monitor-only
operation preserves the original one-shot decoding trajectory under the
same decoding configuration. SafeInferCom subsequently uses the same
monitoring interface to intervene.

## V SafeInferCom: Formal verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention

![Refer to caption](2610.11223v1/figures/SafeInferCom1.png)

Fig. 2: The workflow of our method SafeInferCom.

The reasoning monitor introduced in
Section [IV](#S4 "IV Inference-Time Reasoning Monitor ‣ SafeInferCom: Safe Inference-Time Compute via Verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention for Robotic Task Planning")
exposes intermediate planning states and their verification outcomes
without altering the primary decoding trajectory. Monitoring alone,
however, cannot prevent a detected violation from persisting or an
already valid candidate from being overwritten by continued reasoning.
To address these limitations, SafeInferCom integrates verifier-guided
intervention into the active decoding process
(Fig. [2](#S5.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ V SafeInferCom: Formal verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention ‣ SafeInferCom: Safe Inference-Time Compute via Verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention for Robotic Task Planning")).
It uses intermediate verification outcomes to determine whether
reasoning should continue, be corrected, terminate with a verified plan,
or escalate to replanning.

### V-A Intervention Principles

SafeInferCom follows three principles motivated by the reasoning
behaviors examined through the monitor.

P1: Preservation of Valid Intermediate Plans. Continued reasoning does
not always improve a candidate plan: an LRLM may reach a valid solution
and later overwrite it with an invalid output. SafeInferCom therefore
preserves a candidate once it is formally verified as valid, avoiding
unnecessary continued reasoning and reducing reasoning–response
inconsistency.

P2: Verifier-Guided Selective Intervention. Internal self-reflection
does not reliably distinguish useful progress from planning errors. Safe
but incomplete candidates are therefore allowed to continue, while
detected violations trigger targeted feedback derived from the external
verifier.

P3: Escalation for Persistent Errors. Local corrective feedback may fail
to resolve the underlying planning error, causing similar violations to
recur across checkpoints. SafeInferCom treats persistent violation
patterns as a signal to escalate from local correction to broader
replanning.

### V-B Mid-Generation Verification and Intervention

At each checkpoint $`k`$, the monitor induces a candidate plan
$`\pi_{k}`$ and obtains its verifier outcome $`y_{k}=V(\pi_{k})`$.
SafeInferCom maps this outcome to a decoding action. In addition to the
reasoning-transition checkpoints defined in
Section [IV](#S4 "IV Inference-Time Reasoning Monitor ‣ SafeInferCom: Safe Inference-Time Compute via Verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention for Robotic Task Planning"),
the end-of-reasoning marker \</think\> is treated as an additional
checkpoint so that the current candidate is verified before reasoning is
allowed to terminate.

Valid: Early Termination. When $`y_{k}=\textsc{Valid}`$, SafeInferCom
terminates generation and returns the verified candidate $`\pi_{k}`$ as
the final plan. This rule prevents additional decoding from overwriting
an already valid solution.

Safe-but-Not-Goal: Continue Reasoning. When
$`y_{k}=\textsc{Safe-but-Not-Goal}`$ at a reasoning-transition
checkpoint, the LRLM resumes decoding from its current state without
corrective feedback. If the same outcome is observed at \</think\>,
SafeInferCom prevents termination and injects a continuation prompt
directing the model to continue reasoning toward a goal-achieving plan.
This ensures that SafeInferCom does not interrupt valid reasoning
trajectories that may naturally converge to a correct plan.

Unsafe: Correct with Verifier Feedback. When $`y_{k}=\textsc{Unsafe}`$,
SafeInferCom uses verifier diagnostics to guide correction before
decoding proceeds. Let $`e_{k}`$ denote the returned diagnostic
information, including the failing action, its position, and the
violated requirement. A feedback prompt $`f(e_{k})`$ is appended to the
active reasoning context, after which decoding continues from the
corrected context. It allows the LRLM to revise its plan using verifier
diagnostics while retaining its preceding reasoning. The same rule
applies when an unsafe candidate is detected at \</think\>, preventing
the model from terminating with an invalid plan.

Persistent Violations: Replanning. SafeInferCom tracks recurring
violation patterns across checkpoints. If the same violation pattern
persists for $`N`$ consecutive checkpoints, the controller injects a
stronger replanning prompt that highlights the recurring failure and
asks the model to reconsider its overall planning strategy. Replanning
remains within the active context, preserving the task specification and
accumulated diagnostic information.

Together, these actions integrate formal verification into the active
reasoning process, enabling SafeInferCom to intervene before a complete
failed trajectory is generated. SafeInferCom and post-hoc refinement are
complementary and can be combined within the same planning pipeline.

## VI Experiments

We first validate the fidelity of intermediate-plan induction. We then
use the reasoning monitor to characterize limitations in one-shot LRLM
reasoning. Finally, we evaluate SafeInferCom against baseline inference
strategies on PDDL planning tasks, with additional deployment studies in
VirtualHome and on a physical robotic arm.

### VI-A Experimental Setup

Tasks. We use an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB to conduct our experiments. We
evaluate five representative PDDL planning domains: Blocksworld (BW),
Logistics (Log), Depots (Dep), Gripper (Grip), and Miconic (Mic). These
domains provide diverse planning scenarios. We generate 100 instances
per domain (500 total) using an automated PDDL
generator \[[29](#bib.bib29)\], and verify all induced and final plans
using VAL \[[2](#bib.bib2)\]. These benchmarks provide explicit action
semantics, initial states, and goals, enabling consistent verification
of intermediate and final plans.

Implementation Details. We evaluate DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen models at
three scales: 7B, 14B, and 32B, allowing us to study how reasoning
behavior and intervention effectiveness vary with model size. All models
use the same decoding configuration: a maximum budget of 8,192 tokens,
top-$`p=0.95`$, and temperature 0.6. Models generate reasoning within
\<think\>...\</think\> tags followed by a structured action sequence. We
allow at most $`R_{\max}=20`$ monitoring checkpoints per trajectory. The
monitor additionally verifies the candidate at \</think\>. SafeInferCom
triggers replanning when the same violation pattern persists for $`N=3`$
consecutive checkpoints.

### VI-B Evaluation Metrics

Besides outcome metrics, we further design reasoning-level metrics to
quantify reasoning–response inconsistency and self-correction behavior.

Outcome metrics. *Success rate* is the fraction of instances whose final
plan is verifier-valid. *Token usage* counts generated output tokens. It
excludes monitoring overhead in the one-shot reasoning analyses, but
includes induction and intervention outputs across all refinement rounds
when evaluating SafeInferCom.

Reasoning–response inconsistency. Two inconsistency metrics
($`R_{s}F_{\bar{s}}`$ and $`R_{\bar{s}}F_{s}`$) are designed to capture
misalignment between intermediate candidate plans and the final plan.
Let $`R_{s}`$ denote that at least one observed intermediate candidate
is valid and $`F_{s}`$ denote final-plan validity; $`R_{\bar{s}}`$ and
$`F_{\bar{s}}`$ are their complements. We define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\scriptsize R_{s}F_{\bar{s}}=\frac{n(R_{s}\cap F_{\bar{s}})}{n(F_{\bar{s}})},\hskip 8.19447ptR_{\bar{s}}F_{s}=\frac{n(R_{\bar{s}}\cap F_{s})}{n(F_{s})}.
``` |  |

The first measures how often final failure follows an observed valid
intermediate candidate, while the second measures how often final
success occurs without any observed valid intermediate candidate.

Self-correction metrics. We evaluate self-correction from two aspects:
error detection and correction efficiency. These metrics characterize
how the model responds to and corrects constraint violations across
successive intermediate candidate plans during reasoning.

*Error detection.* We distinguish revising an existing plan from merely
extending it. A revision occurs when the next candidate changes the
previously proposed action sequence, e.g., by replacing, removing, or
reordering actions; formally, $`\pi_{k-1}`$ is no longer a prefix of
$`\pi_{k}`$. Using revision as an observable indicator of error
detection, we treat an Unsafe candidate as a positive case and a
Safe-but-Not-Goal or Valid candidate as a negative case. TPR measures
how often an unsafe candidate is followed by a revision, while TNR
measures how often a safe candidate is retained or extended without
revising its existing actions.

*Error correction efficiency.* We use $`R_{corr}`$ to quantify how
quickly reasoning recovers from an unsafe intermediate plan. Starting
from an unsafe checkpoint, $`R_{corr}`$ measures the average number of
subsequent checkpoints required for an Unsafe candidate to become
Safe-but-Not-Goal or Valid. Lower $`R_{corr}`$ indicates faster recovery
from detected planning violations.

valid plan, post-hoc verifier feedback is used to start the next round.
S+IR uses the same three-round maximum as IR.

Fast Downward (FD). Fast Downward \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] achieves 100%
success on these benchmarks and serves as a strong symbolic-planning
reference. SafeInferCom targets a complementary setting in which
language-model planning is retained for flexible task interpretation and
direct plan generation, with external formal verification used to
improve inference-time reliability \[[16](#bib.bib16), [10](#bib.bib10),
[7](#bib.bib7), [17](#bib.bib17)\]. This distinction becomes more
relevant in environments such as VirtualHome, where a validator is
available for checking generated action sequences but an equivalent
off-the-shelf symbolic planner is not directly provided.

### VI-D Validation of Intermediate Plan Induction

induction, we evaluate whether induced plans faithfully reflect the
planning decisions expressed in the reasoning prefix. We manually
annotate the action sequences implied by 128 reasoning checkpoints from
10 planning problems. As an external-model baseline, GPT-4o receives the
reasoning prefix and a prompt describing the action-sequence extraction
task. It generates a structured action sequence in a one-shot call.
Fidelity is measured by agreement with the manual annotations. Our
monitor’s induction achieves 87.50% agreement, compared with 62.50% for
GPT-4o. GPT-4o often completes the plan by introducing actions not
expressed in the reasoning prefix, reducing fidelity.

### VI-E Reasoning Behavior Analysis

Using the inference-time reasoning monitor, we analyze one-shot LRLM
reasoning from three perspectives: outcome performance,
reasoning–response inconsistency, and self-correction behavior. Results
are summarized in
Table [II](#S6.T2 "TABLE II ‣ VI-D Validation of Intermediate Plan Induction ‣ VI Experiments ‣ SafeInferCom: Safe Inference-Time Compute via Verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention for Robotic Task Planning").

#### VI-E1 Outcome Performance and Inference Cost

Larger models generally achieve higher success, with the 32B model
reaching 82.8% average success while also using the fewest output tokens
on average. However, final performance alone does not reveal whether the
underlying reasoning process is stable. Models can still generate long
trajectories without reaching a valid plan, motivating a closer
examination of how candidate plans evolve during inference.

#### VI-E2 Reasoning–Response Inconsistency

Reasoning–response inconsistency remains substantial across models and
domains. For example, 84.4% of failed 32B Miconic outputs are preceded
by an observed valid intermediate candidate, showing that continued
reasoning can overwrite a correct solution. Conversely, many successful
outputs are reached without any valid candidate appearing at the
monitored checkpoints. This mismatch weakens interpretability and makes
intermediate reasoning a less reliable basis for explaining the final
decision.

#### VI-E3 Limited Self-Correction Capability

The models show limited ability to detect and efficiently correct unsafe
intermediate plans. Average TPR remains below 37%, indicating that many
unsafe candidates are not followed by a revision, whereas TNR above 91%
suggests that safe candidates are usually retained or extended. Even
when correction occurs, recovery typically requires multiple subsequent
checkpoints rather than a single step. These results motivate external
verifier-guided intervention to identify unresolved violations and
accelerate correction during inference.

### VI-F Evaluation of SafeInferCom

#### VI-F1 Final Success Rate and Inference Cost

Table [III](#S6.T3 "TABLE III ‣ VI-F1 Final Success Rate and Inference Cost ‣ VI-F Evaluation of SafeInferCom ‣ VI Experiments ‣ SafeInferCom: Safe Inference-Time Compute via Verifier-Guided Mid-Generation Intervention for Robotic Task Planning")
compares success rate and output-token usage across inference
strategies. Standalone SafeInferCom (S) consistently improves planning
success over one-shot inference and also outperforms Early, showing that
verifier-guided correction and replanning provide benefits beyond
preserving valid intermediate plans through early termination. For
example, on the 14B model, success increases from 54.8% with one-shot
inference to 78.2% with S.

These gains do not always reduce total token usage relative to one-shot
inference. Early termination avoids unnecessary continued reasoning once
a valid intermediate plan is found, whereas monitoring, correction, and
replanning introduce additional computation when no valid plan has yet
been reached. Standalone S therefore trades additional inference-time
computation for higher planning success.

Combining SafeInferCom with iterative refinement (S+IR) gives the
strongest LRLM performance. Compared with IR, S+IR achieves higher
success while using fewer output tokens across all three model scales.
This shows that mid-generation control and post-hoc refinement are
complementary, yielding a better success–compute trade-off than
refinement alone.

#### VI-F2 Correction Efficiency

We compare correction efficiency under one-shot inference and

#### VI-F3 Generalization Across LRLM Families

To evaluate cross-family generalization, we test SafeInferCom on R1-14B,

## VII Discussion

Failure modes. Intermediate-plan induction may omit or alter actions
expressed in the reasoning prefix, causing verifier outcomes to differ
from the plan actually implied by the model. Verifier feedback also does
not guarantee successful correction: the LRLM may repeatedly reproduce
the same planning error within the inference budget, e.g., moving a
block before clearing the block above it in Blocksworld.

Plan optimality. SafeInferCom targets plan validity and inference
efficiency rather than optimality, so verified plans may contain
unnecessary actions or incur higher execution cost. Incorporating
plan-quality objectives into verification and feedback is an important
direction for future work.

Verification efficiency. SafeInferCom reuses the LRLM prefix KV cache,
but verification is performed independently at each checkpoint. Future
work could use incremental verification to reuse state-transition and
constraint-checking results over shared action prefixes.

Extension to broader settings. SafeInferCom currently assumes an
explicit task specification and external formal verifier. It could be
extended to natural-language task inputs through
language-to-specification methods such as Safety
Chip \[[17](#bib.bib17)\], or through learned verifiers that directly
assess natural-language requirements. Such extensions weaken the current
guarantee because specification translation or learned verification may
introduce additional errors.

## VIII Conclusion

We introduced an inference-time reasoning monitor for exposing and
formally verifying intermediate plans in LRLM-based robotic planning.
Building on this monitor, SafeInferCom uses verifier-guided
mid-generation intervention to preserve valid candidates and accelerate
error correction. Experiments show improved planning reliability and
correction efficiency, with further gains when combined with iterative
refinement. VirtualHome and robotic-arm studies demonstrate
applicability beyond symbolic benchmarks.
````

# HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation for Masked Diffusion Language Models

Xiang Li¹ Chengting Yu² Jungang Li¹ Yilun Zheng³ Junzhuo Li¹ Xingyao
Zhang⁴ Wenbo Su⁴ Xuming Hu^(1,5) ¹The Hong Kong University of Science
and Technology (Guangzhou) ²Zhejiang University ³Nanyang Technological
University ⁴Alibaba Group ⁵The Hong Kong University of Science and
Technology

###### Abstract

We propose HiLo-RD (High-to-Low Budget Representation Distillation) to
improve masked diffusion language models (MDLMs) under a limited
generation budget. Unlike autoregressive models, MDLMs choose both what
to generate and where to commit it. This creates two coupled challenges:
predicting useful content before reasoning is complete, and deciding
when to fix answer tokens that shape later predictions. We identify
budget-induced premature commitment: even when a correct solution fits,
the model can fix answer fragments near the window boundary before
resolving intermediate steps. These fragments become context for later
predictions and redirect subsequent reasoning. Existing post-training
methods do not directly transfer the stronger trajectories that the same
model follows with a larger window. HiLo-RD addresses this gap by (1)
pairing large-window teacher states with cropped small-window views and
(2) matching their hidden representations at committed positions. This
supervision guides both token prediction and commitment order without an
external teacher. Across mathematics benchmarks, HiLo-RD improves
low-budget accuracy and shortens responses; these gains extend to code
generation without code-domain training. On GSM8K, accuracy rises by 9.4
percentage points as mean readable length falls by about 25%. Attention
diagnostics and fixed-budget order interventions support the proposed
mechanism. Together, these results show that large-window reasoning can
improve small-window decoding.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: More room, shorter and more accurate answers. At $`L=T=512`$
(blue) and $`L=T=1024`$ (orange), (a) shows response-length shares in
32-token bins on common axes and (b) compares accuracy and mean readable
length. Values are approximate readings from the archived aggregate
plot.

Masked diffusion language models (MDLMs) offer a route beyond
left-to-right generation by reconstructing masked text under
bidirectional attention ([Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib15); [Nie et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13); [Nie et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)). In pure diffusion
decoding, the model predicts candidates throughout a response window and
chooses which positions to commit at each update. This allows reasoning
to unfold across denoising steps and potentially across independent
subproblems in parallel ([Svete & Sabharwal, 2026](#bib.bib16)). For
practical decoding, however, the response window and the number of
updates are finite. The model must therefore decide both _what_ to write
and _when_ to fix each part of the answer within a limited budget.

Consider a mathematical question for which the same model gives
different answers under two response-window sizes. With the smaller
window, it can fix answer fragments near the end before filling the
reasoning in between, even though a correct solution would fit. With a
larger window, it may defer the answer and reach a shorter, correct
solution. We call this failure _budget-induced premature commitment_.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
shows the broader aggregate pattern: fewer responses end near the larger
window’s limit, while accuracy rises and mean readable length falls.
This raises our central question: _If a larger window yields shorter,
more accurate answers, can we distill that advantage into a smaller
one?_

This example exposes two coupled challenges in low-budget diffusion
reasoning. First, the model must choose _what_ to predict while
intermediate steps are still unresolved. Second, it must decide _when_
to commit an answer fragment, because committed tokens cannot be revised
and become context for every later prediction. A premature answer can
thus shape the reasoning subsequently filled around it. Existing
inference-time methods adjust the available space or restrict the order
of generation ([Li et al., 2025](#bib.bib9); [Arriola et al.,
2025](#bib.bib1)); post-training methods learn from demonstrations,
outcome rewards, or self-distillation targets ([Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib21); [Dat et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)). These approaches improve decoding in different ways,
but do not directly transfer the same model’s more effective
large-window computation into its small-window decisions.

We investigate how an early commitment changes the subsequent
trajectory. Before the model fixes the response tail, attention
concentrates near the small-window boundary. Once tail tokens are
committed, unresolved positions attend more to them and less to the
established reasoning prefix. This feedback makes later predictions
depend on an answer selected before its supporting steps are complete.
In a set of 155 selected cases where native low-budget decoding is wrong
and native high-budget decoding is correct, temporarily delaying tail
commitments at the original low budget recovers correct answers in 43.2%
of cases, compared with 30.3% for a matched order control. These
diagnostics connect the budget, commitment order, and downstream
reasoning, and show that a better path can remain available without
increasing the deployment budget.

We summarize our main contributions as follows.

❶ We identify _budget-induced premature commitment_: a small window can
induce an early answer even when a correct solution fits. Attention
diagnostics and fixed-budget interventions trace how such commitments
influence subsequent reasoning.

❷ We introduce HiLo-RD (High-to-Low Budget Representation Distillation).
It aligns large-window teacher states with their cropped small-window
views and matches hidden representations at committed positions, using
the model’s own trajectories to guide low-budget decoding.

❸ At the same small inference budget, HiLo-RD raises GSM8K accuracy from
68.84% to 78.24% while reducing mean readable length from 362.8 to 271.3
tokens. The gains extend to code generation without code-domain
training, and successive distillation improves the low-budget
quality–length trade-off on a fixed training subset.

Overall, HiLo-RD transfers the same model’s larger-window computation
into more accurate, more concise small-window responses. The results
show that the way a diffusion model commits its answer is a useful
target for improving reasoning under a limited generation budget.

## 2 Preliminaries and Related Work

### 2.1 Masked Diffusion Decoding

We consider pure diffusion decoding with LLaDA-8B-Instruct ([Nie et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13)), a masked diffusion language model (MDLM). The full
response is decoded as one bidirectional block. Once a token is
committed, it cannot be revised.

Write the prompt and response state before each update as

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathbf{q}=[q_{1},\ldots,q_{P}],\qquad\mathbf{y}_{t}=[y_{t,1},\ldots,y_{t,L}],\qquad\mathbf{y}_{0}=\texttt{[MASK]}^{L}.
``` |  |

A response position $`j`$ has input index $`P+j`$. The masked and
observed (committed) positions are

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{M}_{t}=\{j\in[L]:y_{t,j}=\texttt{[MASK]}\},\qquad C_{t}=[L]\setminus\mathcal{M}_{t},
``` |  | (1) |

where $`[L]=\{1,\ldots,L\}`$. For vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$, the model
with parameters $`\theta`$ predicts at each masked position

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{y}_{t,j}=\arg\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}p_{\theta}(v\mid\mathbf{q},\mathbf{y}_{t},j).
``` |  |

A confidence rule ranks masked positions, suppressing EOS/EOT candidates
in the reference decoder. Selected positions receive their predicted
tokens; all other positions remain unchanged. Selection need not follow
response order, and the ranking score need not equal token probability.

A budget specifies the response-window length and planned number of
updates. A run can terminate after $`R`$ updates:

|     |                                              |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       B\triangleq(L,T),\qquad 1\leq R\leq T\leq L.
       ```                                           |     |

The full decoder fills the window in $`T`$ updates. When $`L=T`$, it
commits one position per update; when $`T<L`$, at least one update
commits multiple positions. Enlarging the window changes both the
visible context and the positions competing for commitment.

### 2.2 Decoding Budgets and Realized Computation

At each update, the decoder processes the prompt and full response
window afresh. For $`N=P+L`$ input positions, dense forward cost
(including the vocabulary projection) scales as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
F(N)=\Theta\!\left(n_{\ell}\left[Nd^{2}+Ndd_{\mathrm{ff}}+N^{2}d\right]+Nd|\mathcal{V}|\right).
``` |  | (2) |

Here $`n_{\ell}`$ is the number of layers, $`d`$ the hidden width, and
$`d_{\mathrm{ff}}`$ the feed-forward width. Bidirectional states can
change when new response tokens are committed, so this decoder does not
reuse a KV cache across updates ([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib17);
[Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)). Using the forward-pass cost as a proxy
gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{q},B;R)\triangleq RF(P+L),\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{full}}(\mathbf{q},B)\triangleq TF(P+L).
``` |  | (3) |

Each avoided update saves one full-window forward.
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Efficient Trajectory Acquisition ‣ 4 HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Representation Self-Distillation ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
specifies the stopping rules.

### 2.3 Related Work

#### Diffusion language models.

Masked diffusion models predict missing tokens from visible context,
allowing positions to be committed out of order ([Sahoo et al.,
2024](#bib.bib15); [Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)). LLaDA and iLLaDA
train from scratch, while DiffuLLaMA and Dream adapt autoregressive
checkpoints ([Nie et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Gong et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6); [Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib18)). Block diffusion orders
generation between blocks ([Arriola et al., 2025](#bib.bib1)); WeDLM
uses causal attention for cache-friendly recovery ([Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib11)). Our single-block setting keeps all masked response
positions eligible at each update.

#### Post-training methods.

Masked SFT learns from partially masked references; Diffu-GRPO uses
outcome rewards ([Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib13); [Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)). OPSD and dOPSD self-distill sampled trajectories
([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib21); [Dat et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)), while
T3D distills trajectories to reduce denoising steps ([Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19)). HiLo-RD pairs a teacher state with its cropped
small-window view and matches their hidden states to improve low-budget
reasoning.

## 3 Budget-Induced Premature Commitment and Its Consequences

A response window can fit a correct solution yet still change when a
masked diffusion language model fixes its answer. We study 155 paired
cases selected for an incorrect answer at $`L=T=512`$ and a correct
answer from the same model at $`L=T=1024`$. In the smaller window, the
model commits an answer fragment near the response tail before the
intervening reasoning is complete. We connect this failure to
boundary-focused attention, subsequent attention to the committed tail,
and recovery when tail commitment is delayed within the original budget.

### 3.1 Boundary Attention Precedes Premature Commitment

Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Boundary Attention Precedes Premature Commitment ‣ 3 Budget-Induced Premature Commitment and Its Consequences ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
examines attention immediately before the first ordinary tail
commitment. Under low-budget decoding, a band forms near the
response-window boundary across the middle and later layers while
intermediate positions remain unresolved. Appending 512 visible \[MASK\]
positions to the same partial state and query weakens this band.

![Refer to caption](2610.36901v1/panic_budget_sensitivity_expanded.png)

Figure 2: Boundary-focused attention precedes tail commitment.
Visible-window expansion relaxes this focus at the same state and query
(left vs. middle), while native high-budget decoding attends more to

an attention scale; red dashed lines mark the original low-budget

Native high-budget decoding, followed from initialization to the matched
update, instead attends more to earlier decoded reasoning. The
within-state comparison isolates the effect of visible response space;
the native trajectory shows the computation reached when that space is
available throughout decoding. The smaller window changes attention
before the first tail token is fixed.

### 3.2 Committed Tail Content Attracts Subsequent Attention

Figure 3: Attention shifts toward the committed tail. Changes from the
pre-commitment update, averaged across layers and 145 valid traces. The
dashed line marks commitment; $`0`$ is immediately after it.

Once the tail is committed, it becomes part of the bidirectional context
for unresolved positions.

Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2 Committed Tail Content Attracts Subsequent Attention ‣ 3 Budget-Induced Premature Commitment and Its Consequences ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
follows the same unfinished front positions across 145 valid traces.
Immediately after the first tail commitment, their mean attention to the
tail rises by 1.91 percentage points, while attention to the established
prefix falls by 0.41 points. Along the subsequent decoding trajectory,
these changes reach $`+4.19`$ and $`-1.87`$ points at $`+32`$ updates.
The early answer fragment thus enters the context used to complete the
still-masked reasoning steps.

### 3.3 Tail Delay Restores Correct Answers

Figure 4: Tail delay improves low-budget recovery. Correct continuations
in 155 cases selected for an incorrect native 512-token answer and a
correct native 1024-token answer. Both interventions use $`L=T=512`$.

A fixed-budget intervention tests whether postponing that decision opens
a better continuation. *Tail Delay* excludes the original response-tail
positions during the first 10% of remaining updates. A *Matched-order
Control* perturbs selection at the same update indices but excludes its
own current highest-scoring position, without a tail-specific rule. Both
branches start from the same pre-event state and retain the $`L=T=512`$
window, predictor, and decoding schedule.

Tail Delay reaches the correct answer in 67 of the 155 selected cases
(43.2%), compared with 47 (30.3%) for the order control, a paired gain
of 12.9 percentage points (95% bootstrap interval: 7.7–18.7). The
advantage over a matched order perturbation supports postponing the tail
as the useful change. Successful paths are accessible within the smaller
window. This motivates HiLo-RD to transfer the high-budget model’s
commitment-aware representations into low-budget decoding.

## 4 HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Representation Self-Distillation

### 4.1 Cross-Budget Representation Distillation

HiLo-RD transfers the commitment-aware representations of the model’s
own high-budget trajectories into low-budget decoding. A frozen teacher
and a trainable student start from the same checkpoint and receive the
same prompt $`q`$. Their budgets are $`B_{T}=(L_{T},T_{T})`$ and
$`B_{S}=(L_{S},T_{S})`$, where $`L_{S}<L_{T}`$. The high-budget teacher
follows a trajectory that is less affected by Budget-Induced Premature
Commitment; its states therefore encode more reliable decisions about
both *what* to predict and *where* to commit.

Figure 5: HiLo-RD overview. Left: Budget-Induced Premature Commitment.
For the same prompt, low-budget decoding concentrates attention near its
response boundary $`P_{\mathrm{low}}`$, fixes an incorrect answer early,
and produces a response that fills its allocated window. With a larger
budget, attention remains on earlier reasoning positions; the same model
commits the correct answer at the final decoding stage and produces a
much shorter response. Colors indicate normalized commitment time.
Right: Cross-budget representation self-distillation. A frozen teacher
processes a high-budget state, while the trainable student receives the
crop ending at $`P_{\mathrm{low}}`$. Hidden-state MSE over the
cumulative teacher-committed set $`C_{t+1}`$, including newly and
previously committed positions, distills the reliable high-budget
trajectory encoded in the teacher representations into the low-budget
student. The trained student follows a coherent low-budget path and
reaches the correct answer.

For each retained teacher state $`y_{t}`$, the student receives its
cropped response state $`y_{t,1:L_{S}}`$. The overlapping positions
preserve their token values and position indices, yielding an exact
cross-budget correspondence. Let $`H_{\theta}`$ denote the final
normalized hidden states before the shared vocabulary head. At each
response position $`j\leq L_{S}`$, the paired representations are

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle h^{T}_{t,j}`$ | $`\displaystyle=H_{\bar{\theta}}(q,y_{t})_{P+j},`$ |  | (4) |
|  | $`\displaystyle h^{S}_{t,j}`$ | $`\displaystyle=H_{\theta}(q,y_{t,1:L_{S}})_{P+j},`$ |  | (5) |

where $`\bar{\theta}`$ is the frozen teacher. The teacher representation
is computed over its high-budget trajectory before cropping, whereas the
student must produce its representation from the low-budget view.

We supervise the cumulative positions committed by the teacher after
update $`t`$: $`m_{t,j}=\mathbf{1}\{j\in C_{t+1}\}`$ and
$`n_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{L_{S}}m_{t,j}`$. This includes both previously
committed positions and newly selected positions whose inputs in
$`y_{t}`$ remain masked. For $`n_{t}>0`$, HiLo-RD minimizes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\ell_{t}=\frac{1}{d\,n_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{L_{S}}m_{t,j}\left\|h^{S}_{t,j}-\operatorname{sg}[h^{T}_{t,j}]\right\|_{2}^{2},
``` |  | (6) |

where $`d`$ is the hidden dimension and $`\operatorname{sg}`$ stops the
teacher gradient. We average this loss over valid retained states and
contributing trajectories. The high-budget trajectory is encoded
directly in each teacher representation $`h^{T}_{t,j}`$: it reflects the
trajectory’s bidirectional context, commitment history, and its reliable
decisions about both what to predict and where to commit. Minimizing
Eq. ([6](#S4.E6 "In 4.1 Cross-Budget Representation Distillation ‣ 4 HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Representation Self-Distillation ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models"))
forces the low-budget representation $`h^{S}_{t,j}`$ to internalize this
high-budget trajectory information at the corresponding position.
HiLo-RD is therefore a trajectory distillation method at the
representation level, rather than a loss on isolated tokens or
separately specified commitment labels. The shared vocabulary head maps
the resulting representation alignment to locally aligned token
distributions;
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Representation matching and token distributions ‣ Appendix B HiLo-RD: Objective Details and Local Guarantees ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
gives the corresponding bound. Since commitment confidence is also
derived from these representations, the same objective improves both
token prediction and commitment order.

### 4.2 Efficient Trajectory Acquisition

Full-completion decoding continues until every allocated position is
filled, even when the readable response is already complete. An early
EOT alone is insufficient to stop: an EOT token may be committed before
earlier reasoning positions have been resolved. We instead require a
*clean EOT*: the leftmost committed EOT and every preceding response
position must be committed. With unchanged updates and irreversible
commitment, further decoding cannot alter the response extracted before
that EOT
(Proposition [1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Completed-prefix preservation) ‣ B.2 Completed-prefix stopping ‣ Appendix B HiLo-RD: Objective Details and Local Guarantees ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")).

Teacher acquisition can additionally stop when its first
$`L_{\mathrm{S}}`$ response positions are filled, since these define the
supervision window. This cap preserves the completed prefix but may
truncate a longer solution; it is distinct from response-preserving
clean-EOT stopping. Before termination, all positions in the teacher’s
full window remain eligible for commitment. Both rules are applied
during rollout, so they avoid forwards rather than merely discarding
stored states.

If stopping reduces the realized updates from $`T`$ to $`R`$, the cost
becomes $`RF(P+L)`$ instead of $`TF(P+L)`$ under
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Decoding Budgets and Realized Computation ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Work ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models").
Each forward still processes the full window. Efficient acquisition
therefore reduces continuation cost, while distillation improves the
quality attainable at a smaller budget. Exact preservation assumes
matched decoding and first-EOT extraction; implementation-specific
extraction details are given in
Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Readable length, extraction, and pass@𝑘 ‣ Appendix C Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models").

## 5 Experiments

We evaluate HiLo-RD through four research questions covering low-budget
reasoning quality, transfer, rollout efficiency, and iterative
self-distillation.

RQ1 (Performance): Can HiLo-RD distill high-budget reasoning into
accurate, compact low-budget decoding?

RQ2 (Generalization): Do these decoding gains generalize to
out-of-domain code generation?

RQ3 (Efficiency): Can efficient trajectory acquisition cut
pure-diffusion rollout cost without quality loss?

RQ4 (Self-Evolving): Can successive self-distillation drive
self-evolving quality–budget refinement?

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Data Preparation.

We train separately on the full GSM8K training set ([Cobbe et al.,
2021](#bib.bib4)) and the original MATH training set ([Hendrycks et al.,
2021](#bib.bib7)), and evaluate the corresponding checkpoints on the
GSM8K test set and MATH500 ([Lightman et al., 2024](#bib.bib10)),
respectively. For out-of-domain evaluation, we directly test
mathematics-trained checkpoints on HumanEval ([Chen et al.,
2021](#bib.bib3)) and MBPP ([Austin et al., 2021](#bib.bib2)), without
additional code-domain training.

#### Baselines.

We compare HiLo-RD with the original LLaDA-8B-Instruct (*vanilla*) ([Nie
et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)) and four post-training methods: masked
supervised fine-tuning (Masked SFT), Diffu-GRPO ([Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)), on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib21)), and dOPSD ([Dat et al., 2026](#bib.bib5)). All
comparisons use pure diffusion decoding. For vanilla and HiLo-RD, we
additionally compare full decoding with the fast-rollout stopping rule
in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Efficient Trajectory Acquisition ‣ 4 HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Representation Self-Distillation ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
to assess its effect on quality and efficiency.
Section [2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Related Work ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Work ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
situates these post-training objectives.

#### Training and Evaluation.

We perform parameter-efficient adaptation with LoRA ([Hu et al.,
2022](#bib.bib8)), optimizing the adapter parameters with AdamW
([Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019](#bib.bib12)) while keeping the backbone
weights frozen. The HiLo-RD training runs use eight NVIDIA A100 80GB
GPUs. We measure mathematical reasoning performance using accuracy and
code-generation performance using pass@$`k`$ in the main comparison. We
also report mean readable token length (RTL) and mean wall-clock time
per request to characterize response length and inference cost.
Additional training and evaluation details are provided in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models").

#### In-domain mathematical reasoning.

We observe that HiLo-RD improves low-budget mathematical reasoning
across datasets of different difficulty, achieving the highest accuracy
and shortest readable responses among the compared methods on both GSM8K
and MATH500. The joint improvement matters because it shows that better
reasoning need not come from extending the readable solution:
higher-budget representations can instead help the same model use its
smaller window more effectively. Compared with the other post-training
objectives, representation self-distillation yields a more favorable
balance between correctness and response length. This supports the
motivation developed in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Budget-Induced Premature Commitment and Its Consequences ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models"):
useful computation exposed by a larger budget can guide low-budget token
predictions and commitment decisions, rather than requiring the larger
budget at deployment. These gains align with the mechanism of
Budget-Induced Premature Commitment: stronger low-budget reasoning no
longer requires longer readable responses, although the attainable
degree of compression remains task-dependent.

#### Out-of-domain code generation.

The benefits extend to code generation without further code-domain
training, suggesting that the learned improvement is not confined to
mathematics-specific answer patterns. On MBPP, HiLo-RD combines the
strongest pass@$`k`$ with the shortest responses. On HumanEval, it
remains competitive with dOPSD, which has a slight advantage in
pass@$`k`$, while the reduction in readable length is marginal. This
contrast shows that quality transfer need not be accompanied by equally
strong compression across domains. Nevertheless, improvement over
vanilla on both benchmarks supports the transferability of the decoding
behavior learned through mathematics-based representation distillation.
The same model’s higher-budget computation thus supplies useful
supervision beyond the training domain, without teacher-only reference
solutions.

### 5.3 Efficient Trajectory Acquisition (RQ3)

fill identify model and rollout. Linear marker size represents RTL.
Lines pair Full/Fast results. Timings await normalization.

Efficient trajectory acquisition reduces unnecessary decoding after
response completion.
Figure [6](#S5.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 5.3 Efficient Trajectory Acquisition (RQ3) ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")

Fast rollout reduces the recorded latency most clearly on GSM8K and

illustrates the distinction between the two efficiency gains:
distillation improves what the model generates, whereas completion-aware
stopping can save computation without shortening the completed response.
Reported timing differences remain subject to cross-server
normalization.

#### Why savings vary across tasks.

The task-dependent pattern is consistent with the cost reduction from
$`TF(P+L)`$ to $`RF(P+L)`$: early completion leaves more updates to
skip, and each skipped update saves a forward pass over the full prompt
and response window. Short MBPP responses leave substantial unnecessary
continuation, whereas HumanEval responses nearly fill the window. Longer
prompts, such as those used for GSM8K, also increase the saving per
skipped update when other settings are fixed. RTL alone does not
determine the stopping step, since commits outside the eventual readable
prefix also consume updates. Thus, the observed efficiency depends
jointly on response completion, commitment order, and input length.
Under matched decoding and extraction, the stopping rule preserves the
completed prefix
(Proposition [1](#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1 (Completed-prefix preservation) ‣ B.2 Completed-prefix stopping ‣ Appendix B HiLo-RD: Objective Details and Local Guarantees ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models"));
applied to teacher acquisition, it similarly avoids continuation after
the student-sized supervision prefix is complete. This makes efficient
acquisition complementary to representation distillation, rather than a
substitute for learning better low-budget decoding.

### 5.4 Toward Self-Evolving Quality–Budget Refinement (RQ4)

We investigate whether HiLo-RD can bootstrap its own supervision across
successive distillation cycles. Starting from the unadapted model C0,
each cycle uses the preceding model to acquire new high-budget
trajectories and distills them back into low-budget decoding. All cycles
reuse the same fixed subset of 1,000 GSM8K training examples, so
improvements arise from recursively refining the model’s own
cross-budget supervision rather than introducing additional data.

model’s own supervision. This provides a concrete path toward
self-evolving budget adaptation without an external teacher or
additional training examples.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work

We identify Budget-Induced Premature Commitment in masked diffusion
language models: under a low yet sufficient response budget, the model
can fix response-tail fragments before the reasoning that supports them
is resolved. Decoding traces, attention diagnostics, and
commitment-order interventions trace how this early decision redirects
subsequent bidirectional reasoning and degrades the resulting
trajectory. Guided by this mechanism, HiLo-RD distills representations
from the same model’s reliable high-budget trajectories into low-budget
decoding. The resulting model reasons more accurately with shorter
responses across mathematical reasoning and code generation, realizing
*Think Wide, Reason Lean*; completed-prefix stopping further avoids
unnecessary decoding work.

Successive distillation further improves the quality–budget frontier
using the model’s own trajectories, opening a path toward self-evolving
diffusion reasoning. Incorporating richer intermediate supervision may
help sustain this refinement across longer sequences of
self-distillation cycles.

A complementary intervention blocks front queries from attending to
committed ordinary tail keys in every layer for an initial interval. It
repairs one case and breaks one among the 20 pairs, leaving aggregate
accuracy unchanged. This intervention restricts access to already
committed content; the order interventions in
Figure [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.3 Tail Delay Restores Correct Answers ‣ 3 Budget-Induced Premature Commitment and Its Consequences ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
instead postpone its commitment while retaining normal bidirectional
attention.

### A.3 Irreversible commitment and reachable completions

For a partial response, let

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{F}_{t}(L)=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{V}^{L}:y_{j}=y_{t,j}\text{ for every }j\in C_{t}\}.\qquad\mathcal{F}_{t+1}(L)\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}(L).
``` |  | (8) |

If $`\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{q},L)`$ denotes correct completions under a
specified evaluator and extraction rule, then
$`\mathcal{F}_{t+1}(L)\cap\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{q},L)\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}(L)\cap\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{q},L)`$.
A wrong fixed fragment can remove compatible correct continuations; its
content also changes subsequent predictions and position scores. Since
both correct and incorrect commitments shrink the set, the relevant
question is which successful continuations remain reachable. The order
interventions test whether a different sequence of commitments can
recover successful paths at the same budget. Budget-Induced Premature
Commitment denotes premature boundary-near commitment while the
intervening reasoning remains unresolved despite sufficient response
space.

## Appendix B HiLo-RD: Objective Details and Local Guarantees

### B.1 Trajectory sampling and objective normalization

Within a training phase, the frozen teacher generates

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\xi=(\mathbf{y}_{0},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{R})\sim\mathsf{P}_{\bar{\theta}}^{B_{\mathrm{T}}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{q}),\qquad R\leq T_{\mathrm{T}}.
``` |  | (9) |

The reference fast-acquisition implementation retains at most $`K=8`$
states, including the initial all-MASK state. Each paired forward uses
the pre-update state $`\mathbf{y}_{t}`$: the teacher processes the full
window and the student processes its crop. Supervision eligibility is
determined after the corresponding update:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
m_{t,j}=\mathbf{1}\{j\in C_{t+1}\},\qquad n_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{L_{\mathrm{S}}}m_{t,j}.
``` |  | (10) |

This cumulative mask includes previously committed positions and
positions newly committed in the update. A newly selected position can
therefore be supervised while its input is still masked. Earlier
commitments receive repeated supervision as surrounding content
develops. The loss in
Eq. ([6](#S4.E6 "In 4.1 Cross-Budget Representation Distillation ‣ 4 HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Representation Self-Distillation ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models"))
normalizes by $`dn_{t}`$, so a later state does not receive more weight
solely because more positions have been filled.

Let $`\mathcal{I}_{+}(\xi)`$ contain the retained states with
$`n_{t}>0`$. We average their losses:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\ell_{\xi}(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{+}(\xi)|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{+}(\xi)}\ell_{t}(\theta;\xi),
``` |  | (11) |

with $`\ell_{\xi}=0`$ if the set is empty. For a batch $`\mathcal{B}`$
and a trajectory gate $`g(\xi,\mathbf{q},a)\in\{0,1\}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HiLo\text{-}RD}}(\theta;\mathcal{B})=\frac{\sum_{(\mathbf{q},a,\xi)\in\mathcal{B}}g(\xi,\mathbf{q},a)\ell_{\xi}(\theta)}{\max\{1,\sum_{(\mathbf{q},a,\xi)\in\mathcal{B}}g(\xi,\mathbf{q},a)\}}.
``` |  | (12) |

The reported positive training recipe accepts teacher responses whose
extracted answers pass verification. An empty accepted batch contributes
zero signal. The answer label selects rollouts; it is not supplied as
teacher input or used as the representation target. This filter
certifies the extracted answer, not every intermediate reasoning step.
Setting $`g\equiv 1`$ yields an unfiltered variant without correctness
labels. No gradient passes through the sampled tokens, commitment
decisions, or frozen teacher.

### B.2 Completed-prefix stopping

The clean-EOT rule and the prefix-cap rule in
Section [4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Efficient Trajectory Acquisition ‣ 4 HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Representation Self-Distillation ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
share a preservation property under irreversible decoding.

###### Proposition 1 (Completed-prefix preservation)

Consider full and fast decoders with the same initialization, update
schedule, tie-breaking, and coupled randomness. Assume that committed
tokens are never revised. If fast decoding stops after $`R`$ updates,
its states and actions through $`R`$ equal the corresponding prefix of
full decoding. Every fully committed response prefix is unchanged by
continuing the full decoder. If the leftmost committed EOT is at
position $`e`$ and all preceding positions are committed, extraction
strictly before the first EOT returns the same token sequence after
either run.

*Proof.* Before termination, both decoders apply identical updates to
identical states. Induction gives trajectory-prefix equality.
Irreversibility then preserves every token in the completed prefix.
Under clean EOT, all positions through $`e`$ are fixed, so no earlier
EOT can subsequently appear and extraction before $`e`$ is unchanged.
$`\square`$

Stopping at a completed cap $`c<L`$ preserves only the first $`c`$
positions and may truncate a solution that continues beyond them. The
teacher can use $`c=L_{\mathrm{S}}`$ while retaining full-window
attention and allowing commitments outside that prefix before
termination. The evaluation driver’s default cap instead equals its
allocated response length. Both rules must execute inside the decoding
loop; discarding later states after a complete rollout does not save
forward passes.

The output guarantee requires the stated extraction rule. The archived
evaluation driver decodes the returned response array with
skip_special_tokens=True, without explicitly cutting at the first EOT.
Already committed post-EOT text can then enter grading. Prefix
preservation alone does not establish identical scores for that
extractor; raw outputs, extracted answers, and stop reasons must be
compared under a common protocol.

### B.3 Representation matching and token distributions

The vocabulary head translates a representation difference into a logit
difference. The following bound formalizes this local relation without
assuming identical autonomous decoding paths.

###### Proposition 2 (Local token-distribution control)

Let $`V=|\mathcal{V}|`$ and let $`z^{\mathrm{T}}=Wh^{\mathrm{T}}+b`$ and
$`z^{\mathrm{S}}=Wh^{\mathrm{S}}+b`$ be finite logits under the same
fixed head $`W\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}`$. For $`\tau>0`$, define
$`p^{\mathrm{T}}_{\tau}=\mathrm{softmax}(z^{\mathrm{T}}/\tau)`$,
$`p^{\mathrm{S}}_{\tau}=\mathrm{softmax}(z^{\mathrm{S}}/\tau)`$,
$`\mathsf{C}=I_{V}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}/V`$, and
$`e=\|h^{\mathrm{S}}-h^{\mathrm{T}}\|_{2}^{2}/d`$. Then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{\mathrm{T}}_{\tau}\|p^{\mathrm{S}}_{\tau})\leq\frac{d\|\mathsf{C}W\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}}{4\tau^{2}}\,e.
``` |  | (13) |

The same bound holds with the KL arguments exchanged.

*Proof.* Let $`A(u)=\log\sum_{v=1}^{V}e^{u_{v}}`$. Its gradient is
softmax, and its Hessian is $`\operatorname{diag}(p)-pp^{\top}`$, where
$`p=\mathrm{softmax}(u)`$. For any unit vector $`w`$, the Hessian
quadratic form is the variance of $`w_{i}`$ under $`i\sim p`$, so

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
w^{\top}\nabla^{2}A(u)w\leq\frac{(\max_{i}w_{i}-\min_{i}w_{i})^{2}}{4}\leq\frac{1}{2}.
``` |  | (14) |

Thus, $`A`$ has a $`1/2`$-Lipschitz gradient and

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
A(v)-A(u)-\nabla A(u)^{\top}(v-u)\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\|v-u\|_{2}^{2}.
``` |  | (15) |

Set $`u=\mathsf{C}z^{\mathrm{T}}/\tau`$ and
$`v=\mathsf{C}z^{\mathrm{S}}/\tau`$. Centering leaves softmax unchanged.
Substitution gives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{\mathrm{T}}_{\tau}\|p^{\mathrm{S}}_{\tau})`$ | $`\displaystyle=A(v)-A(u)-\nabla A(u)^{\top}(v-u)`$ |  | (16) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\mathsf{C}(z^{\mathrm{S}}-z^{\mathrm{T}})\|_{2}^{2}}{4\tau^{2}}`$ |  | (17) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{\|\mathsf{C}W(h^{\mathrm{S}}-h^{\mathrm{T}})\|_{2}^{2}}{4\tau^{2}}\leq\frac{d\|\mathsf{C}W\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}}{4\tau^{2}}e.`$ |  | (18) |

Exchanging $`u`$ and $`v`$ proves the reverse-direction bound; the
common bias cancels. $`\square`$

Applying the objective’s nonnegative averaging weights gives the
corresponding average bound on supervised pairs. The result concerns
finite raw logits and does not cover hard logit suppression or discrete
selection directly. If the teacher’s unique top logit has margin
$`\gamma>0`$, the sufficient condition
$`\|\mathsf{C}W\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|h^{\mathrm{S}}-h^{\mathrm{T}}\|_{2}<\gamma/2`$
also preserves its token argmax: every centered logit changes by less
than half the margin. Neither statement guarantees position-selection
agreement, because other competing positions may not be supervised and
autonomous student states may differ.

### B.4 What the smaller-window representation can retain

Fix a teacher phase and let $`\mu`$ be the distribution over supervised
position pairs induced by the rollout, filter, state sampling, and
normalization in
Eq. ([12](#A2.E12 "In B.1 Trajectory sampling and objective normalization ‣ Appendix B HiLo-RD: Objective Details and Local Guarantees ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")).
Write $`U=(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{y}_{t,1:L_{\mathrm{S}}},j)`$ for the
student-visible state and queried position, and
$`Z=h^{\mathrm{T}}_{t,j}`$ for the target; the student does not receive
$`t`$ as a separate input. For square-integrable $`Z`$ and any
square-integrable predictor $`f(U)`$,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|f(U)-Z\|_{2}^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|f(U)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[Z\mid U]\|_{2}^{2}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\|Z-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[Z\mid U]\|_{2}^{2}.`$ |  | (19) |

To see this, expand around $`m(U)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[Z\mid U]`$; the cross
term has zero conditional expectation given $`U`$. Hence the
unrestricted optimum is $`f^{*}(U)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[Z\mid U]`$. The
objective transfers the component of higher-budget representations
predictable from the shared prompt and cropped state. It need not
reconstruct every detail that depends on omitted response positions. A
parameterized student may incur approximation and optimization error,
and this regression identity does not guarantee reasoning quality on its
autonomous rollouts.

### B.5 Optional outcome-conditioned repulsion

These variants supplement the positive objective and are considered
separately, with the other repulsion branch disabled. Repulsion is
disabled in the reference configuration. Let $`D_{\mathrm{H}}`$ be the
higher-budget trajectory distance in
Eq. ([11](#A2.E11 "In B.1 Trajectory sampling and objective normalization ‣ Appendix B HiLo-RD: Objective Details and Local Guarantees ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")),
and let $`g_{\mathrm{H}}`$ indicate final-answer correctness. A
failed-high-budget variant uses

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{failed\text{-}H}}=\operatorname{Avg}_{g_{\mathrm{H}}=1}D_{\mathrm{H}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{H}}\operatorname{Avg}_{g_{\mathrm{H}}=0}\min(D_{\mathrm{H}},m_{\mathrm{H}}).
``` |  | (20) |

The two groups are normalized separately, and empty groups contribute
zero. Because $`-\min(D,m)=[m-D]_{+}-m`$, the negative term is
gradient-equivalent to margin repulsion, which stops increasing
separation beyond $`m_{\mathrm{H}}`$.

A separate unfiltered-positive variant draws an additional failed
rollout with fewer updates:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{failed\text{-}L}}=\operatorname{Avg}_{\mathcal{B}}[D_{\mathrm{H}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(1-g_{\mathrm{L}})[m_{\mathrm{L}}-D_{\mathrm{L}}]_{+}].
``` |  | (21) |

Here $`D_{\mathrm{L}}`$ uses that rollout’s own paired states; its
penalty is averaged over the whole batch rather than only the failures.
In the supplied implementation, this negative teacher retains the long
window and reduces the step count, e.g.,
$`(L_{\mathrm{neg}},T_{\mathrm{neg}})=(1024,128)`$, not $`(128,128)`$.
Removing $`1-g_{\mathrm{L}}`$ disables failure-only gating. Applying the
high-budget correctness gate instead gates both terms on the rank,
producing a distinct recipe; the failed-high-budget branch and that gate
use mutually exclusive code paths. Representation repulsion does not
guarantee a lower probability for a specified erroneous trajectory.

### C.2 Computation and latency

Equation ([2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2 Decoding Budgets and Realized Computation ‣ 2 Preliminaries and Related Work ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models"))
describes dense recomputation without classifier-free guidance. It does
not assume that quadratic attention dominates at the studied lengths or
exclude implementations with partial reuse. For variable-window
decoding, the corresponding cost is $`\sum_{t=0}^{R-1}F(P+L_{t})`$.

Measured latency also depends on hardware, kernels, batching, and prompt
length. The reported Full/Fast timings include results from different
servers and remain subject to normalization; their ratios are not
controlled speedup estimates. Mean per-request latency is distinct from
dataset throughput and total multi-GPU wall time. Teacher-acquisition
savings require stopping the decoding loop, rather than retaining fewer
states after a full rollout.

### C.3 Readable length, extraction, and pass@$`k`$

The archived evaluation driver measures RTL by retokenizing the decoded
response after removing special tokens. This can differ from the number
of occupied response positions or the length extracted before the first
EOT. RTL is neither the configured window size nor the realized number
of forward passes: out-of-prefix commitments also consume updates. The
prefix-preservation guarantee in
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Completed-prefix stopping ‣ Appendix B HiLo-RD: Objective Details and Local Guarantees ‣ HiLo-RD: Cross-Budget Self-Distillation forMasked Diffusion Language Models")
therefore requires an explicit extraction convention when comparing Full
and Fast outputs.

For a problem with $`n`$ independently sampled completions and $`c`$
passing all code tests, the standard estimator is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{\mathrm{pass@}k}=1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}},\qquad n\geq k.
``` |  | (22) |

The dataset score averages this quantity over problems. Its
interpretation depends on the sample count, decoding distribution, and
code-test protocol; a per-completion latency is not the total cost of
obtaining a multi-sample pass@$`k`$ score.

initializing the next frozen teacher and trainable student. If

model supplies the next cycle’s supervision. This extension changes the
teacher between cycles; the teacher remains fixed within each cycle.
````

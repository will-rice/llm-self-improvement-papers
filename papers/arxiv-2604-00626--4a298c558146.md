# A Survey of On-Policy Distillation for Large Language Models

Mingyang Song    Mao Zheng Affiliation: Foundation Model Department,
Tencent, China Email: [nickmysong@tencent.com](mailto:) Affiliation: 
[nick7nlp/Awesome-LLM-On-Policy-Distillation](https://github.com/nick7nlp/Awesome-LLM-On-Policy-Distillation)

###### Abstract

On-Policy Distillation (OPD) trains large language models with teacher
guidance on student-generated trajectories, addressing the mismatch
between fixed demonstrations and the histories encountered during
deployment. As its use expands across language model training, different
formulations of supervision and optimization make it difficult to
compare methods and understand when they are effective. This survey
develops a unified view of OPD through the interplay between
student-generated states, teacher information, and policy updates. This
framework connects the foundations of distillation with
self-distillation, multi-teacher integration, reinforcement learning,
and interactive agents. We examine how these settings change the
supervision available to the student and relate the resulting design
choices to capability transfer, training efficiency, and deployment. Our
synthesis centers on the conditions under which guidance remains
reliable and learnable as the student evolves, considering both
immediate performance and the capacity for subsequent learning. We
conclude by discussing open challenges in adapting supervision and
allocating computation to support effective learning.

## 1 Introduction

Knowledge distillation trains a _student_ model using guidance from a
_teacher_. It can transfer a larger model’s capabilities to a smaller
one, combine several specialists in one model, or teach behavior that
initially requires extra context. Across these uses, the student must
continue from its own outputs, which can lead it away from the histories
represented in its training examples. Fixed responses and teacher
demonstrations provide no direct guidance at many of these unfamiliar
histories (Kim and Rush, 2016; Ross et al., 2011). Even close imitation
of a teacher’s answer style can leave a gap in underlying
competence (Gudibande et al., 2023).

On-Policy Distillation (OPD) brings teacher guidance to the student’s
own generation process. The student generates a response, and the
teacher supplies guidance along it. For text generation, a _state_
consists of the prompt and the response prefix generated so far. A
_trajectory_ is the resulting sequence of states and generated tokens.
If the student takes a wrong turn, OPD can provide guidance at that
prefix even when a fixed teacher answer would never visit it. MiniLLM
and GKD develop complementary realizations through sequence-level
optimization and matching next-token distributions (Gu et al., 2024a;
Agarwal et al., 2024). Both connect student-generated experience to
teacher guidance, whose value depends on the visited state and the
student’s ability to learn. Figure 2 depicts this learning loop and the
design choices examined in the following chapters.

A capable teacher can still be misleading after an erroneous student
prefix, motivating target adaptation and reliability checks (Li et al.,
2026l; Qiu et al., 2026). Useful supervision depends on what the teacher
knows at the student’s current state. Conversely, additional information
can create a teaching advantage without a separate stronger model.
On-policy self-distillation gives a version of the student a solution,
demonstration, or feedback, then uses its predictions as supervision.
This information is _privileged_ because the deployed student will not
normally receive it. OPSD, SDFT, and SDPO use these information sources
to construct supervision when a suitable stronger model is unavailable
or impractical (Zhao et al., 2026h; Shenfeld et al., 2026; Hübotter et
al., 2026).

The choice of supervisor is intertwined with the task objective. Teacher
preferences guide individual predictions, while reinforcement learning
(RL) can use outcome rewards to evaluate complete attempts. Joint
updates, selective supervision, and successive training stages offer
different ways to combine these signals (Xu et al., 2025a; Li et al.,
2026b; Li et al., 2026a). With several teachers, training must also
decide which specialist to consult and how to balance learning across
capabilities. Individually effective specialists do not automatically
produce a student that retains all their strengths (Ma et al., 2026a;
Gao et al., 2026). These design choices can operate together. A
self-teacher may interpret feedback from several specialists, and reward
learning may be interleaved with transfer between evolving models (He et
al., 2026b; Gu et al., 2026b).

For interactive agents, the student’s actions also change the
environment in which later decisions are made. Tools return observations
and memory changes the visible history, so the state must include more
than the student’s generated response prefix. Delayed feedback must
inform earlier decisions, and teacher intervention can change the
trajectory itself (Wang et al., 2026g; Liu et al., 2026n). In both
response generation and interaction, learning changes which states the
student reaches next. Deciding what to collect, supervise, and reuse
therefore affects both the information available for learning and its
computational cost (Rang et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2026). The
consequences extend beyond the distillation loss to task behavior,
retained capabilities, and the capacity for subsequent
learning (Nicolicioiu et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2026b).

We organize the survey around _the states visited by the student, the
information used to construct supervision, and the update that
supervision induces_. These distinctions explain why similar training
procedures can behave differently. The same divergence can be evaluated
on different histories, the same model can teach differently with
additional context, and the same token score can enter different
gradient estimators. Together, these choices connect the mechanisms of
OPD to their effects on learning, transfer, and deployment.

## 2 OPD Foundations and Optimization

Distillation transfers a teacher’s predictions to a student, but
autoregressive generation makes the context of that transfer part of the
learning problem. Each prediction depends on earlier decisions, and the
student can reach prefixes absent from the teacher’s demonstrations.
Understanding OPD therefore requires specifying both what the student
should learn from the teacher and where that supervision should be
applied. This distinction connects classical distillation to the
objectives, resource choices, and optimization methods developed in this
chapter.

### 2.1 From Classical KD to Autoregressive Distillation

In supervised learning, a one-hot target identifies the observed label.
Classical knowledge distillation (KD) instead trains the student to
match a teacher’s predictive distribution, which also expresses relative
preferences among alternative labels (Hinton et al., 2015). A
temperature can soften these distributions, and the distillation loss
can be combined with supervision from the original labels. These soft
targets convey more information than a single predicted label, but
transfer still depends on student capacity.

For an autoregressive language model, the corresponding prediction is a
distribution over the next token. Let $`x`$ be an input, $`y`$ a
response, and $`c_{t}=(x,y_{<t})`$ the prefix at position $`t`$. Given
teacher probabilities $`q(a\mid c_{t})`$ and student probabilities
$`\pi_{\theta}(a\mid c_{t})`$ over a shared vocabulary $`V`$,
soft-target cross-entropy takes the form

```math
\displaystyle\ell_{\mathrm{KD}}(c_{t}) \displaystyle=-\sum_{a\in V}q(a\mid c_{t})\log\pi_{\theta}(a\mid c_{t}) \tag{1}
```

```math
\displaystyle=H\!\left(q(\cdot\mid c_{t})\right)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(\cdot\mid c_{t})\parallel\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{t})\right).
```

With the teacher distribution held fixed, its entropy $`H(q)`$ is
constant with respect to the student, so minimizing this loss is
equivalent to minimizing forward Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. By
comparison, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) predicts the supplied response
token at each position. Sequence-level KD uses a teacher-generated
response as that target sequence, allowing transfer through text without
access to the teacher’s full token distribution (Kim and Rush, 2016).
The broader LLM distillation survey of Xu et al. (2024) discusses these
and other forms of transfer beyond OPD.

These objectives specify what to predict at a prefix, but leave open
where that prefix comes from. When training follows reference or
teacher-generated responses, the student learns under histories supplied
by another source. At generation time, it conditions on its own earlier
predictions. A loss that fits the supplied histories well can therefore
leave the student poorly supervised at the histories it actually visits.

### 2.2 The Student-State Learning Loop

OPD brings teacher supervision to prefixes generated by the student.
Consider a student producing a mathematical solution. After an incorrect
intermediate step, a teacher’s original solution no longer describes how
to continue from the student’s history. OPD instead queries the teacher
at that history, allowing supervision to respond to the student’s own
decisions. MiniLLM and GKD both incorporate student-generated sequences
into distillation, while emphasizing sequence-level reverse KL and
flexible conditional matching, respectively (Gu et al., 2024a; Agarwal
et al., 2024). Changing the source of prefixes does not require
abandoning the soft targets of classical KD.

The motivation predates language-model distillation. DAgger addresses
the distribution shift between expert demonstrations and learner-visited
states (Ross et al., 2011). Under a bounded-cost assumption, a small
imitation error on expert states can produce a worst-case cumulative
cost gap of order $`O(\epsilon T^{2})`$ over horizon $`T`$. Querying the
expert on learner-visited states improves the horizon dependence when
the expert can recover from learner mistakes and the learner controls
its imitation error on those states. For language models, this motivates
supervising student prefixes while leaving teacher reliability and the
student’s ability to learn from that guidance as substantive questions.

Strict on-policy collection draws supervised states from the current
student. Reference responses, teacher responses, and responses from
earlier students are off-policy relative to that student. This
distinction concerns the source of training states, whereas _offline_
describes learning from a fixed collection. Mixture sampling changes the
state source, and periodic collection or asynchronous execution can
introduce policy lag (Agarwal et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024; Rang et
al., 2026). An older student’s trajectory was on-policy when collected
but becomes stale during further optimization. Re-scoring it refreshes
supervision without refreshing its state distribution. Similarly, a
fresh student action after a replayed teacher history does not make that
history student-generated (Liao et al., 2026).

The learning signal matters as well as the source of trajectories. SPIN
repeatedly contrasts responses from the previous model with fixed
demonstrations, using sequence-level preferences rather than teacher
distributions at student prefixes (Chen et al., 2024). Iterative
generation alone therefore does not identify the distillation loop.

subsequent student update.

### 2.3 A Common Framework for Distribution Matching

The prefix comparison above extends to other sequential decisions. Let
$`s`$ denote a state and $`\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s)`$ the student policy.
In autoregressive generation, $`s=c_{t}`$ and the action is the next
token. An agent state additionally reflects observations and the memory
visible to the model, while a diffusion-language state contains a
partially decoded sequence and its denoising stage (Liu et al., 2026n;
Dat et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026a). A teacher supplies a target
$`q(a\mid s^{T})`$, possibly using a view $`s^{T}`$ with information
unavailable to the student. Teacher and student predictions must still
concern comparable decisions.

A common matching objective is

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{match}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\mu}}\left[w(s)D_{f}\!\left(q(\cdot\mid s^{T})\parallel\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s)\right)\right], \tag{2}
```

where $`d_{\mu}`$ is the state distribution induced by a collection
policy $`\mu`$, $`w(s)`$ is a nonnegative state weight, and $`D_{f}`$
measures disagreement between the target and student distributions.
Equation 1 corresponds to forward-KL matching at a given prefix.
Averaging over reference prefixes or current-student prefixes changes
$`d_{\mu}`$ while retaining that conditional loss. Giving the teacher
extra context instead changes $`s^{T}`$ and potentially $`q`$, even when
the student sees the same state. These distinctions let us compare where
supervision is applied, what information it conveys, and how
disagreement is penalized.

For a convex generator $`f`$ with $`f(1)=0`$, the $`f`$-divergence is

```math
D_{f}(P\parallel Q)=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim Q}\left[f\!\left(\frac{P(a)}{Q(a)}\right)\right].
```

The _support_ of a distribution is the set of actions to which it
assigns positive probability. Where the ratios are defined, $`P=q`$,
$`Q=\pi_{\theta}`$ and $`f(u)=u\log u`$ give forward KL, while
$`f(u)=-\log u`$ gives reverse KL (Wen et al., 2023). Forward KL weights
teacher-supported alternatives, whereas reverse KL penalizes student
probability mass on actions the teacher considers unlikely. When the
student cannot reproduce the target exactly, these weightings favor
different approximations. Figure 3 illustrates the difference with a
restricted student family, holding the state source fixed.

Figure 3: Forward and reverse KL in a constructed distribution-fitting
example. The teacher is an equal-weight Gaussian mixture, and the
student is restricted to a single Gaussian. Forward KL yields the
moment-matching fit, covering both modes and assigning more mass between
them. Numerical reverse-KL optimization concentrates near one mode for
this separation; the reflected solution is equally good.

GKD makes the distinction between collection and matching explicit by
varying both the fraction of student-generated responses and the
conditional divergence (Agarwal et al., 2024). MiniLLM introduces a
further choice through its sequence-level reverse-KL objective, which
accounts for how an action changes later prefixes (Gu et al., 2024a).
Equation 2 describes conditional matching, but does not determine the
gradient estimator or whether to differentiate through the state
distribution. We return to those choices after considering the capacity
and resources available for transfer.

### 2.4 Capacity and Distillation Scaling

A matching objective specifies the desired transfer, but the student
must learn it within a finite budget. A larger teacher can yield a
weaker student when their capabilities differ substantially (Zhong et
al., 2024). Distillation scaling studies make this constraint
quantitative by relating student performance to model size, training
data, and teacher quality. In pretraining-style distillation on English
C4 reference prefixes, Busbridge et al. (2025) fit student validation
cross-entropy as a function of student non-embedding parameters
$`N_{S}`$, distillation tokens $`D_{S}`$, and teacher validation
cross-entropy $`L_{T}`$. Within the studied model family, $`L_{T}`$
summarizes the influence of teacher size and pretraining tokens. The
predicted outcome is cross-entropy on held-out data, rather than
divergence from the teacher.

This relationship explains why choosing the strongest teacher need not
yield the best student. At fixed student size and token budget, stronger
teachers initially help, but further improvements in teacher quality can
worsen student performance. Increasing the student’s training budget
changes which teacher transfers best, so the capacity gap involves
learning resources as well as parameter counts (Busbridge et al., 2025).
A fixed compute budget adds another tradeoff. Scoring with a larger
teacher consumes resources that could support more student training,
while the cost of preparing that teacher can be shared across multiple
uses. Teacher choice consequently depends on both predictive quality and
the budget available for student learning.

These empirical scaling relationships concern pretraining distillation
on reference data. In OPD, the distribution $`d_{\mu}`$ in Equation 2
also changes as the student learns. Repeated generation from a fixed
prompt can expose new prefixes, and replay can spend more updates on
states already collected. The resource question consequently extends
from how many tokens to use to which states should be generated, scored,
and revisited. Section 8 examines these choices, while Section 11.2
considers what remains unresolved in their joint allocation. At each
selected state, the form of supervision also determines which
teacher–student discrepancies the student is asked to correct.

### 2.5 Matching Objectives and Probability Support

An objective determines both how strongly discrepancies are penalized
and which alternatives receive correction. The $`f`$-divergence
formulation makes the weighting explicit, but the probabilities
available to the learner also matter. Full-distribution matching,
sampled-token feedback, and restricted candidate sets expose different
amounts of the teacher’s preferences. Two questions therefore organize
objective design: how much probability should move, and where should it
go?

Consider first an update that scores only the student’s sampled token.
For autoregressive generation, let $`p_{\theta}`$ and $`p_{T}`$ denote
the student and teacher sequence distributions, with
$`p_{\theta}(a\mid c_{t})=\pi_{\theta}(a\mid c_{t})`$. A common
sampled-token surrogate for conditional reverse-KL matching is

```math
\ell_{t}(\theta)=-\mathrm{sg}[r_{t}]\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid c_{t}),\qquad r_{t}=\log\frac{p_{T}(y_{t}\mid c_{t})}{p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid c_{t})},
```

where $`\mathrm{sg}`$ treats its argument as constant during
differentiation (Jia et al., 2026). The coefficient $`r_{t}`$ is
positive when the teacher assigns the sampled token more probability
than the student, and negative in the opposite case. It therefore
determines whether the update reinforces or suppresses that token.

Suppressing an action disfavored by the teacher does not identify a
useful replacement. For a gradient step on the output _logits_, the
scores converted into probabilities by softmax, a detached negative
coefficient increases each unchosen token’s logit in proportion to its
current student probability (Jia et al., 2026). A teacher-preferred
alternative with negligible student probability consequently receives
little direct correction. This statement concerns logit changes; softmax
normalization also affects the resulting probabilities. Full-vocabulary
matching exposes all alternatives at a visited state, while top-$`K`$
supervision exposes a restricted set. Discarding omitted mass,
renormalizing retained probabilities, or pooling the tail into a
residual bucket yields different comparisons. These choices change the
_effective support of supervision_, not only its cost.

Additional forward guidance can expose destinations missed by
sampled-token feedback, either selectively or at every position. AOPD
supplies teacher top-$`K`$ alternatives when the sampled-token signal is
non-positive; TGPO supplies the teacher’s highest-probability token at
every rollout position and anneals that correction during reward
optimization (Jia et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026i). Selecting where to
intervene introduces a further choice. Proposed criteria include teacher
entropy, the sampled teacher–student probability gap, and a clipped
teacher-to-student probability ratio (Jin et al., 2026b; Jia et al.,
2026; Xing et al., 2026). TrOPD uses the clipped ratio as the
probability of routing a sampled token to reverse-KL supervision, with
top-$`K`$ forward guidance otherwise. It also anneals teacher-generated
prefixes before student continuations, coupling local routing with
trajectory guidance. Its “trust region” measures predictive agreement,
not teacher correctness.

The magnitude of correction matters when teacher and student
probabilities differ sharply. Skew divergences moderate extreme ratios
through probability mixtures; DistiLLM uses a fixed skew coefficient,
while DistiLLM-2 adapts the skew and loss balance and applies forward
matching to teacher responses and reverse matching to student
responses (Ko et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2025). The latter also reuses
student responses sampled at the start of each epoch. A different
simplification replaces the sampled log-ratio coefficient with its sign,
scaling the shared gradient by the previous step’s smoothed
conditional-TV estimate (Xiao et al., 2026). This TV-OPD estimator
concerns conditional total variation at fixed states; it does not assign
credit for actions’ effects on later states.

Moderating correction and restoring missing support can pull in
different directions. Hellinger matching with geometric-mean weighting
attenuates correction for teacher-preferred tokens that the student
finds unlikely. GEOSD combines this weighting with a squared Fisher–Rao
penalty against a periodically refreshed student reference (Jukić and
Titov, 2026). Attenuation can limit extreme updates while also weakening
guidance toward useful, low-probability continuations. Explicit
mass-routing objectives address the destination separately: TIDE
combines Hellinger-shaped suppression of excess mass with teacher
top-$`K`$ cross-entropy to restore deficits, whereas RouteOPD couples
excess and deficit tokens through log-odds changes fitted to a shared
bounded teacher potential (Yu et al., 2026g; Xia et al., 2026b).
RouteOPD’s controls favor teacher-deficit destinations over matched-rank
or random alternatives. Stability of the correction alone therefore does
not settle where it should be directed.

Relative-logit matching expresses the discrepancy through pairwise
preferences. Concrete Score Distillation (CSD) compares weighted
teacher–student logit differences across vocabulary pairs, making the
comparison invariant to a common logit shift (Kim et al., 2026b).
Factorized detached pair weights permit an $`O(|V|)`$ gradient, although
a shared vocabulary is still required. The weights remain consequential:
in its small-data GSM8K setting, student-only weighting fails while
mixed teacher–student weighting remains effective. Across these
objectives, the form of a discrepancy, its weighting, and the
alternatives exposed to the student jointly determine the intended
correction. The next question is how that correction is estimated.

### 2.6 Estimating Updates and Controlling Variance

The same named loss can lead to different updates, depending on what is
sampled and what is differentiated. A conditional update fits
predictions at a fixed prefix; a sequence update also accounts for how
actions change later prefixes. The sampled-token surrogate in
Section 2.5 holds both the prefix and the coefficient $`r_{t}`$ fixed
during differentiation. Differentiating through $`r_{t}`$ would produce
a different gradient, even on the same sampled token.

For teacher-to-student density ratio
$`u=p_{T}(y_{t}\mid c_{t})/p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid c_{t})`$, the general
$`f`$-divergence coefficient $`-f(u)+uf^{\prime}(u)`$ includes direct
parameter dependence omitted by a detached $`-f(u)`$ surrogate (Zhao et
al., 2026d). Reverse KL is a special case because the added term is
constant and its expected score contribution vanishes. The correction
concerns the conditional gradient at a fixed prefix, leaving temporal
credit as a separate choice.

The support choices in Section 2.5 also determine which source of
sampling error an estimator can remove. A sampled log ratio needs only
the teacher probability of the chosen token (Lu and Thinking Machines
Lab, 2025), whereas full-vocabulary KL sums the conditional alternatives
exactly, removing uncertainty from sampling the next action at that
prefix. Top-$`K`$ supervision approximates or changes that comparison
according to its tail treatment. Training still samples the prefixes
themselves, and exact conditional summation does not automatically
assign credit for future states. Access to more logits can therefore
reduce conditional sampling error while leaving state freshness,
trajectory variance, and task alignment unresolved.

Estimator studies separate centering a noisy signal from choosing which
signals to emphasize. A _baseline_ subtracts a reference value from the
sampled coefficient. If that value depends on the prefix but not on the
sampled action, the subtraction leaves the expected conditional gradient
unchanged and can reduce its variance. vOPD uses the conditional mean
teacher–student log ratio

```math
b_{t}(c_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{t})}\left[\log\frac{p_{T}(a\mid c_{t})}{p_{\theta}(a\mid c_{t})}\right]=-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{t})\parallel p_{T}(\cdot\mid c_{t})\right)
```

as this detached baseline, also called a control variate (Oh et al.,
2026). A top-$`K`$ approximation retains the baseline property when it
remains independent of the sampled action. It approximates the centering
value without removing actions from the objective. This baseline centers
immediate conditional reward, whereas a value function for cumulative
return also accounts for future discrepancies.

Head–tail estimators instead combine exact supervision on a small token
set with a sampled correction for the omitted vocabulary. Tail-Corrected
Top-$`K`$ On-Policy Distillation (TT-OPD) sums the reverse-KL gradient
over a selected head, using the student’s top tokens by default, and
adds a correction when the sampled student token falls in the tail (Meng
et al., 2026). ResOPD retains the teacher’s top tokens and a bucket for
the remaining probability mass, then corrects this coarse comparison
with a sampled fine-tail residual (Yang et al., 2026f). Both recover the
full-vocabulary conditional gradient in expectation under their sampling
assumptions. ResOPD’s control variate reduces variance only when it
approximates the tail mean sufficiently well, and its unbiasedness
result assumes sampling from the full current student distribution
rather than truncated decoding. These constructions distinguish reducing
the number of explicit loss terms from discarding tail supervision;
neither distinction alone establishes an end-to-end training speedup.

Bounding an update differs from centering it. PowerOPD replaces the
detached log ratio with a difference of powered teacher and student
probabilities, preserving its sign while bounding its magnitude (Zhao et
al., 2026a). Because the exponent changes which probability ranges
receive substantial updates, it modifies the learning signal rather than
providing an unbiased estimate of the original reverse-KL gradient.
TOP-D bounds the signal through target construction instead. Mixing the
external teacher with the rollout student’s probabilities forms a
proximal teacher. Its log-probability reward relative to that rollout
policy is bounded below, and clipped importance-ratio updates reuse each
generated batch (Xie et al., 2026). This probability mixture differs
from scaling a log ratio. In practice, TOP-D holds advantages fixed and
normalizes returns, distinguishing its implemented update from the ideal
policy-improvement analysis.

Estimator reliability also differs from update magnitude and task
usefulness. SuRe emphasizes surprising sampled tokens, motivated by a
gradient-norm analysis of its sampled squared-log-ratio surrogate (Shao
et al., 2026). That ranking need not hold for full-vocabulary KL or JSD.
IER instead estimates signal relative to single-sample gradient noise in
Fisher geometry under an optimized scalar baseline (Sheng et al., 2026).
A reliable estimate can still describe a small or unhelpful update;
accordingly, IER can be combined with usefulness scores for token
selection. The training-cost implications of selecting these tokens
depend on which operations are avoided (Section 8.6).

Several controls act after a signal has been constructed. PGPO caps a
batch-level entropy-gap multiplier without changing optimizer
moments (Wang et al., 2026i), while IDA-OPD shrinks sampled updates
predicted to contract entropy, especially near teacher–student
agreement (Yang et al., 2026g). These mechanisms regulate the
constructed signal’s scale or predicted entropy effect rather than
estimator bias.

### 2.7 Local Supervision and Temporal Credit

Learning at student states does not by itself assign credit for reaching
them. Local conditional matching treats a visited prefix as fixed. A
sequence objective also accounts for how an earlier action changes the
distribution of later prefixes. MiniLLM derives this distinction from
sequence-level reverse KL. For a fixed teacher with support on
student-generated responses, its ideal student-sampled gradient can be
written as

```math
\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\theta}\parallel p_{T}) \displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}(R_{t}-1)\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid c_{t})\right],
```

```math
\displaystyle R_{t} \displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{|y|}\log\frac{p_{T}(y_{t^{\prime}}\mid c_{t^{\prime}})}{p_{\theta}(y_{t^{\prime}}\mid c_{t^{\prime}})}.
```

The constant score term vanishes in expectation, while $`R_{t}`$ assigns
an early action credit for later teacher–student discrepancies (Gu et
al., 2024a). MiniLLM computes the immediate conditional component over
the vocabulary and estimates the future component. Its practical update
uses mixed teacher–student sampling, per-token importance ratios in
place of trajectory weights, and length normalization. Without
teacher-mixed sampling or length normalization, reverse KL can still
decrease while the student quickly learns repeated, short, or
meaningless responses.

Two counterexamples address reward alignment and temporal
credit (Agrawal et al., 2026). Even when the teacher has higher expected
reward, an idealized reverse-KL natural-gradient step can decrease
student reward in a finite-action construction. In a restricted policy
class, a local token-wise gradient can also assign zero credit to an
early decision whose effect appears only in later disagreement, while
the full sequence gradient changes it. These existence results rule out
unconditional monotonic-improvement and local-credit guarantees.

$`\gamma`$OPD exposes the temporal horizon directly. With
$`\Delta_{t}=\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid c_{t})-\log p_{T}(y_{t}\mid c_{t})`$,
it defines

```math
A_{t}^{(\gamma)}=-\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T}\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}\Delta_{t^{\prime}}.
```

The endpoints recover local credit at $`\gamma=0`$ and an undiscounted
return-to-go at $`\gamma=1`$ (Liu et al., 2026f). Under a uniform
second-moment assumption, fixed $`\gamma<1`$ gives a horizon-independent
upper bound on the variance of this advantage estimate, rather than the
complete parameter-gradient estimator. In the sensitivity study without
reward mixing, undiscounted credit produces much larger gradient norms
and rapid entropy collapse, whereas an intermediate discount performs
better. Bounded normalization and verifier reward preserve the
response-level reward sign while teacher credit changes token-level
magnitude. Students can exceed reported teacher scores in the same-size
and multi-teacher settings, but the size-mismatched student remains
below its teacher.

Other approaches modify the evidence used to assign credit without
reproducing this return. X-KD learns a reward distribution and
encourages consistency between that distribution and temporal
differences of the student’s value estimates. Its gains vary by
teacher–student setting and auxiliary weight (Cai and Yuan, 2026).
CREDIT instead compares a teacher’s feedback-conditioned predictions for
the current input with its predictions under other inputs in the
batch (Shen et al., 2026b). Subtracting this contrastive baseline
reduces credit shared across unrelated inputs. Because the subtraction
depends on the candidate action, it changes the supervision signal
rather than providing the action-independent centering used by vOPD.
This interpretation requires feedback compatible with the teacher’s
conditioning.

### 2.8 From Optimization to Learning Outcomes

These foundations leave three questions for the rest of the survey. What
makes guidance useful at a student’s state, given that a stronger
teacher or richer context need not transfer more effectively (Li et al.,
2026l; Zhang et al., 2026j)? How should a matching objective relate to
task rewards when their interaction depends on whether learning is joint
or sequential (Li et al., 2026a)? And which outcomes should count as a
gain? Response accuracy, coverage of correct solutions, calibration,
termination, and readiness for subsequent learning measure different
outcomes (Nicolicioiu et al., 2026; Dong et al., 2026b). Pass@$`k`$, for
example, measures the chance of obtaining a correct answer within a
budget of $`k`$ attempts.

The chapter map in Figure 1 connects these questions to the different
uses of OPD. The next chapter begins with the target: what information a
supervisor can provide, when its predictions remain reliable, and how
that information can be expressed at the student’s interface. These
choices determine the supervision available to the objectives above.

## 3 Teacher Supervision and Target Construction

Learning at student states brings the teacher to the relevant history,
but useful supervision still depends on what it can provide there. A
teacher can become unreliable after a student error, teach behavior
outside the intended transfer, or predict tokens the student cannot
express. A useful target must connect available teacher information to a
decision the student can learn. In Equation 2, this connection runs from
the teacher’s view $`s^{T}`$ to the target distribution $`q`$ at the
student’s state. Supervisory access, teacher reliability, target
construction, and prediction-interface alignment determine whether that
connection provides effective guidance.

### 3.1 Supervisor Roles and Access

A supervisor can supply an answer, judge a response, or predict a
distribution at a student prefix. These roles expose different
information. A generated answer supplies one example; a judgment
evaluates the realized response; a conditional distribution also scores
alternatives the student did not choose. The available interface
therefore constrains which form of supervision can be constructed.

GKD uses _white-box access_, exposing teacher scores over a shared
vocabulary for both chosen and unchosen tokens at student-generated
prefixes (Agarwal et al., 2024). These scores reveal how probability
could be redistributed among alternatives, beyond judging the sampled
token alone. The resulting supervision requires teacher inference on the
prefixes used for matching, with its cost considered in Section 8.6.

Teacher logits also support ranking-based supervision. TSD-KD uses
teacher scores to rank the student’s candidate next tokens,
concentrating ranking supervision on an entropy-determined opening
segment alongside distribution matching (Kim and Baek, 2026). The
ranking term transfers relative preferences among plausible actions,
complementing the probability-matching objective.

With text-only access, supervision must recover useful information
without the missing conditional distribution. One route transfers
generated answers and rationales as training examples (Hsieh et al.,
2023; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). Continuing a student-written prefix, as
in DASD, makes those examples responsive to student history, although
the resulting mixed-policy corpus remains offline during fitting (Yan et
al., 2026). A second route uses teacher demonstrations to evaluate fresh
student outputs: GAD and PRISM learn discriminators whose rewards evolve
with the student (Ye et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026m). A third makes
the criteria explicit. ROPD derives prompt-specific rubrics from
teacher–student response contrasts and rewards criterion
satisfaction (Fang et al., 2026). These routes transfer examples,
learned judgments, and explicit criteria, respectively. The latter two
preserve responsiveness to the current student through a response-level
reward, without reconstructing a conditional token target.

Obtaining evidence and converting it into a target can involve different
supervisors. SDPO conditions a self-teacher on execution feedback, while
On-Policy Critique Distillation uses a frozen, critique-conditioned copy
of the student to convert accepted weak-critic comments into token
supervision (Hübotter et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026a). Target
construction can also separate the behavior to transfer from the
behavior to retain. Direct-OPD uses pre- and post-RL teacher policies to
define a relative improvement while the student’s initialization anchors
behavior (Feng et al., 2026a). The model that supplies evidence, the
model that predicts a target, and the policy that anchors the update can
therefore play distinct roles.

### 3.2 Teacher Reliability and Adaptation

Teacher reliability is conditional on the student’s history. OPD
deliberately exposes the teacher to prefixes it did not generate,
including incorrect reasoning steps. Supervised initialization and
prompt format affect transfer (Li et al., 2026l). In mathematical
reasoning, a fixed teacher’s continuation accuracy declines as student
prefixes lengthen (Liu et al., 2026j). Under reference-conditioned
self-distillation, low token-averaged KL can also coexist with incorrect
trajectories (Jiang et al., 2026b). Comparing raw student rollouts with
teacher-refined trajectories further reveals changes in the supervision
induced by their histories. Standalone teacher accuracy can therefore
conceal weaknesses at the states where distillation occurs.

Improving supervision can require changing the teacher itself.
Distillability calibration uses task rewards, an original-teacher
anchor, and a smaller proxy model to prepare a teacher for
transfer (Zhan et al., 2026). Prefix-conditioned adaptation addresses a
more specific weakness: the teacher must recover from histories it would
not generate. SCOUT periodically trains on prefixes from the evolving
student, whereas Prep-OPD prepares a supervisor on initial-student
prefixes and then freezes it (Huang et al., 2026b; Ma et al., 2026b).
Their comparisons support the value of adapting to student history,
including gains from Prep-OPD’s prepared teacher without its additional
generation handoffs. The choice is whether to track the evolving
student’s generated prefixes throughout training or obtain a reusable
supervisor before distillation begins.

Eligibility testing selects whether the teacher’s guidance enters the
student update. TGOPD probes teacher rollouts and routes each prompt to
distillation or reward learning (Zhang et al., 2026m). Confidence does
not consistently predict reliability across the tested code and
mathematics settings, yet setting the admission threshold too high also
degrades performance. Reliable guidance and sufficient training coverage
are competing concerns for the gate.

Local target repair changes the prediction after the teacher encounters
a student history. In OPD-Aha’s study, retaining more erroneous history
shifts a crop-conditioned teacher’s length-normalized likelihood
preference from the correct answer toward the student’s realized wrong
answer (Qiu et al., 2026). Contrasting predictions under a real
privileged image and a null image strengthens visual evidence in the
target. Unlike teacher preparation or eligibility gating, this repair
preserves the supervisor and training state while changing the
distribution used to teach the student.

### 3.3 Transformed and Relative Targets

The teacher’s distribution and the student’s target need not be
identical. Target construction specifies which behavior to transfer,
while the fitting divergence specifies how the student approaches it. A
relative target makes this distinction concrete. At a shared prefix,
start with a reference model’s logits, add a scaled difference between
two models’ logits, and normalize with softmax. The reference supplies
the baseline preferences, the contrast changes the relative preference
for candidate tokens, and the scale controls the strength of that
change. W2S-OPD uses the student’s base policy as the reference and a
stronger-minus-weaker contrast from two smaller models (Yu et al.,
2026a). All three models are frozen while the current student learns
from the constructed target, as illustrated in Figure 5(a).

The meaning of the contrast determines which behavior is transferred.
Whereas W2S-OPD contrasts stronger and weaker models, Direct-OPD
isolates a teacher’s post-RL versus pre-RL log-probability change (Feng
et al., 2026a). Both anchor the change in the student’s base policy,
retaining a role for its existing behavior instead of adopting a
contrast model’s complete distribution. The contrast can also compensate
for model-family differences, as in CompassOPD, or compose several
improvements to balance accuracy and response length, as in Lightning
Weave (Gu et al., 2026a; Wu et al., 2026d). Choosing the models being
contrasted thus determines which part of the teacher’s behavior counts
as an improvement and which part is subtracted away.

The strength of the requested change is a separate choice. A target can
favor agreement between teacher and student or extrapolate beyond a
reference. Veto takes the former approach by multiplying teacher
probabilities by a powered student distribution and normalizing the
product, holding the resulting target fixed during the gradient
step (Jang et al., 2026). G-OPD instead scores trajectories with a
scaled teacher-to-reference log-probability ratio and penalizes
divergence from the reference (Yang et al., 2026i). At unit scaling, its
objective corresponds to reverse-KL matching toward the teacher under
the relevant support conditions. Larger scaling extrapolates beyond the
teacher along its log-probability difference from the reference, without
adding an independent task reward. In the evaluated mathematics and code
settings, extrapolation can help, but excessive scaling accompanies
degraded performance and longer responses.

Sharpening a complete sequence distribution differs from lowering the
temperature of each conditional prediction. OPPD targets a powered
teacher sequence distribution, using student proposals inside sequential
Monte Carlo and teacher scores to construct both sampling and training
weights (Potraghloo et al., 2026). The student learns through detached
weighted likelihood together with a token-level forward-KL anchor. The
search is performed during training, while deployment uses the resulting
student directly. Finite self-normalized importance sampling and the
additional anchor make the implemented update an approximation to the
ideal powered target, rather than exact sequence-distribution matching.

Evolving policies expose a tension between anchoring to established
behavior and extrapolating beyond it. SR-OPSD combines an exponential
moving average (EMA) teacher with a frozen reference through a
normalized weighted average of log probabilities: one supervisor tracks
learning while the other holds a fixed policy. WDL-OPD instead co-trains
two policies by matching their geometric mixture to a frozen teacher
with reverse KL (Sun et al., 2026e; Chen et al., 2026j). RISE constructs
its teaching policy by extrapolating an RL update direction in weight or
logit space (Li et al., 2026k). The comparisons show why the strength of
either intervention must be considered with the learning process.
SR-OPSD’s anchor helps one divergence setting and hurts another; RISE
collapses without reward-learning updates, and aggressive extrapolation
becomes harmful later in training. A target that is useful at one stage
need not remain so as the policies evolve.

The preceding contrasts compare policies, but different evidence
supplied to the same teacher can also define a useful change. OGLS-SD
contrasts successful and failed guidance pools to suppress artifacts
shared by privileged prompts (Yang et al., 2026m). Visual Gradient
Steering (VGS) contrasts the teacher’s image-conditioned and text-only
predictions at multimodal student prefixes (Yoon et al., 2026). It
combines this log-probability difference with the student’s text-only
prior to form a locally normalized target, then adds visual-target
matching and selective language preservation to ordinary OPD. Steering
toward the visual target improves the tested visual-reasoning results,
whereas steering toward language matching lowers accuracy. The contrast
operates on conditional predictions, without an exact decomposition of
sequence-level knowledge.

A likelihood contrast can also enter as a reward without defining a
single matching target. COPD scores the same student token under light-
and heavy-thinking teacher contexts and uses the clipped, detached
light-minus-heavy log-likelihood difference as an advantage (Ruan et
al., 2026). Before clipping, its fixed-state objective is a difference
of two KL divergences: the student entropy terms cancel. This differs
from fitting a normalized relative target. The reported recipe favors
shorter responses with a task-dependent accuracy tradeoff, connecting
contrastive guidance to the behavior being transferred rather than to
teacher imitation alone.

Teacher context can support more direct evidence about a continuation or
an instruction. AC-OPD generates supervised continuations from selected
student prefixes, while CC-OPD compares scores under the full
instruction and contexts that omit one constraint (Zhou et al., 2026a;
Zheng et al., 2026d). The former adds teacher-generated targets
alongside the OPD loss on the full student rollout; the latter asks
which instruction makes a decision preferable. Both incur additional
teacher work. Longer lookahead is not uniformly beneficial, while
constraint-wise scoring grows with the number of instruction views. The
value of additional evidence therefore depends on how it changes the
guidance the student receives, as well as on the cost of obtaining it.

### 3.4 Aligning Prediction Interfaces

Teacher and student probabilities must refer to comparable events. The
shared prefix and vocabulary in the relative-target example make its
logit contrast well-defined. With different tokenizations, the same text
can instead span different prediction steps, as Figure 5(b) illustrates.
Interface methods address this mismatch at the level of continuations,
byte prefixes, sequences, or representations, trading conditional
probability information for compatibility with broader model pairs.

The first difficulty is deciding when differently tokenized predictions
refer to the same event. Shared short continuations and aligned text
chunks broaden the positions that can be supervised, but matching a
sampled chunk’s likelihood does not establish normalized matching over
alternative continuations (Sun et al., 2026b; Niu et al., 2026). BPM
instead groups teacher probability by student byte prefixes and
explicitly accounts for unmatched mass and stopping (Wang et al.,
2026d). Its exactness depends on prefix and refinement assumptions; some
spanning-token corrections remain lower bounds and unsupported cases are
excluded. Even a more faithful mapping can require behavioral
safeguards: its byte-exact whitespace targets cause an indentation
collapse in one code setting unless those positions are excluded.

Broader coverage is therefore useful only when the added correspondence
is reliable. A cross-tokenizer analysis finds that adding span-score
matching to strict shared-token matching degrades performance across its
three tested model pairs (Hou et al., 2026a). Text-only access relaxes
correspondence further. OmniOPD compares teacher continuations with
uncertain student chunks through an expected-similarity proxy (Zhou et
al., 2026c). This permits cross-tokenizer guidance without teacher
logits, but the proxy is not a teacher chunk probability, and its
comparison with white-box OPD changes with the similarity metric and
model pair. These results expose a common tradeoff: more positions or
broader teacher access can come at the expense of a less precise
supervision target.

Sequence-level objectives avoid token alignment by changing the
supervised object. GVPO++ matches group-centered teacher–student
differences in sequence log probability, with both matched- and
cross-tokenizer teachers (Zhang et al., 2026e). In contrast,
relative-logit methods such as CSD retain a shared-vocabulary
requirement (Kim et al., 2026b). The broader tokenizer compatibility
comes from matching sequence scores instead of token-conditional
distributions.

Representation matching trades token correspondence for a different
compatibility problem. OPRD bridges hidden states across architectures,
with more favorable results within the same architecture (Yang et al.,
2026h). LastOPD shows why successful latent alignment alone is
insufficient: in its cross-size pairs, task accuracy eventually
collapses even as projected cosine similarity improves (Yang et al.,
2026d). Transitioning to token supervision helps those pairs, while
continued latent supervision works better for a same-lineage pair. The
relevant question is thus whether a closer representation continues to
support useful output behavior, and for how long.

Cross-modal supervision must additionally account for what each model
can observe. CORD compares audio- and text-conditioned predictions on
audio-policy prefixes through weighted token alignment and a
sequence-level consistency judge (Hu et al., 2026a). FP-OPD asks whether
a visual correction lies within a student’s locally probed response
space, Fisher-projecting the correction before fitting it (Xue et al.,
2026). The probes add forward passes and characterize a local response
space, not global parameter reachability. Both methods connect output
compatibility to the student’s available evidence.

Diffusion generation makes the conditioning state part of the alignment
problem. Hindsight predictions at later masked states can supervise
earlier decisions; alternatively, intermediate decisions retained in a
final response can be selected for teacher supervision at their original
denoising states (Dat et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026a). Across models
with different block sizes, however, the same output position can be
predicted from different committed tokens. Un-OPD filters incompatible
commitment states and calibrates high-support targets toward the student
to curb its premature overconfidence (Yang et al., 2026o). Matching
positions is therefore insufficient: the visible contexts must also be
comparable. Filtering and calibration change the supervised objective
rather than establishing an unbiased estimate of the original one.

Distributional, semantic, and latent targets preserve different
information about the teacher’s behavior. A teaching advantage can also
arise within a single model when its views differ. The next chapter
examines how demonstrations, feedback, and informative views let a model
construct supervision from its own parameters.

## 4 On-Policy Self-Distillation

A model can sometimes judge a continuation better than it can generate
one unaided. A worked solution, demonstration, test failure, or
privileged observation changes the information available for that
judgment; additional internal computation can provide another teaching
advantage. On-policy self-distillation turns these advantages into
supervision at the student’s own states. The supervisor comes from the
student model or its parameter snapshots, which need not remain
identical to the evolving student. In context-conditioned methods, it
evaluates the student’s attempts with extra information, while the
student learns to act from the deployment input alone. This offers an
alternative when a suitable stronger teacher is unavailable or costly,
although constructing and evaluating a useful self-teacher still
requires resources.

For context-conditioned self-distillation, a common local objective is

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{self}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x),\,c\sim\mathcal{C}(\cdot\mid x,y)}\left[\sum_{t}D_{f}\!\left(\operatorname{sg}[q_{\bar{\theta}}(\cdot\mid x,c,y_{<t})]\parallel\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\right)\right]. \tag{3}
```

Here $`\mathcal{C}`$ supplies teacher-only context $`c`$, either before
generation, as with a demonstration, or after observing the attempt
$`y`$, as with feedback. The teacher re-scores the student’s prefixes,
with $`\operatorname{sg}`$ stopping gradients through the target.
Differentiation through the sampled-state distribution remains a
separate choice (Section 2.6). The teacher parameters $`\bar{\theta}`$
may be frozen, periodically synchronized, or updated by an exponential
moving average (EMA). Teacher-only information ranges from solutions,
demonstrations, and retrospective feedback (Zhao et al., 2026h; Shenfeld
et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026) to reusable instructions and
learned criteria (Ye et al., 2026c; Gu et al., 2026c). Table 1 compares
how these sources of information are combined with teacher evolution and
matching signals.

### 4.1 Solutions, Demonstrations and Reusable Experience

Context-conditioned methods differ in where privileged information
enters learning: it can change the sampled trace, the target at an
existing prefix, or the reusable guidance supplied across problems.
These choices determine what the student is asked to internalize.

#### 4.1.1 Where the reference enters supervision

Worked solutions and demonstrations can inform a self-teacher’s
judgments at the student’s own prefixes, without becoming responses the
student must reproduce (Zhao et al., 2026h; Shenfeld et al., 2026). The
benefit depends on whether the model can turn that context into useful
guidance. SDFT improves acquisition and retention relative to SFT in its
science-adaptation comparison, whereas its smallest evaluated model
trails SFT. Retention is also incomplete in medical adaptation, where
some prior instruction-following performance degrades. These contrasts
connect the value of a teaching view to in-context competence and the
capabilities being preserved. SDFT reports better performance with
forward KL in practice, while its analysis uses reverse KL.

Privileged information can enter elsewhere in learning, with different
consequences for state coverage. DAIL and Hinted Decoding use it to
construct fixed training traces, while SAPD uses contextual soft targets
at fixed reference prefixes (Mendes et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026g;
Wang et al., 2026n). These designs improve the examples or targets
available for teacher-forced learning without continuously refreshing
student trajectories.

Student-generated responses create further uses for the reference. GATES
combines teacher-trajectory imitation with scoring of student rollouts,
gating both through repeated-answer consensus (Stein et al., 2026); that
consensus supplies a reliability proxy rather than verified correctness.
PBSD changes the supervised object instead, preferring independently
generated privileged responses over current-student responses (Yu et
al., 2026d). Thus, access to the same kind of reference can support
trace construction, conditional correction, or response-pair preference
learning. These routes differ in whether student prefixes are refreshed
and how informed predictions enter the update.

#### 4.1.2 Information access and target construction

Once the teacher evaluates an existing prefix, the next question is
which information should inform that evaluation. A comparison separates
teacher-sampled $`\pi`$-Distill from student-sampled OPSD and finds that
privileged traces have different value across models, tasks, and
objectives (Penaloza et al., 2026). PAINT favors moderate suffix masking
in its disclosure comparison, while Adaptive Teacher Exposure learns the
amount exposed (Tan and Hong, 2026a; Han et al., 2026d). More privileged
information is not reliably more transferable information. A complete
path can be informative yet poorly matched to a student that has taken
another route. Partial disclosure therefore changes the teacher’s
dependence on that path as well as the prompt length.

The teacher’s advantage can also come from easier access to the relevant
information. OPSDL gives an evolving self-teacher a short evidence
context while the student answers from a long document (Zhang et al.,
2026i). Its long-context gains coexist with small declines on the
reported short-context knowledge and reasoning benchmarks. COPSD crosses
a language boundary instead, using an English problem and solution to
inform a frozen self-teacher that evaluates target-language
reasoning (Liu et al., 2026k). It improves low-resource math averages
for separately trained language-specific students, rather than a jointly
trained multilingual model.

Different contextual views need not express the same desired behavior.
SELFCI combines the reverse-KL objectives of two teachers conditioned on
allowed and disallowed attribute feedback (Park et al., 2026). Its
privacy–utility tradeoff depends on those supplied decisions and carries
no formal privacy guarantee. OPCT distinguishes variation to ignore from
information to retain, using clean/perturbed prompt pairs for invariance
and teacher-only safety facts for internalization (Han et al., 2026a).
These constructions give the views different roles before their guidance
reaches the student.

Combining views requires deciding what their agreement means. Geometric
consensus can suppress a useful token if even one view assigns it little
probability. AVSD supplements consensus with a gated
arithmetic–geometric residual, allowing support from individual views to
influence the update (Nguyen et al., 2026a). AR-OPD instead assigns the
views an explicit direction: it scales the log-probability shift from a
partial-information anchor toward full-information predictions (Zhang,
2026). The former recovers guidance lost by agreement-based aggregation;
the latter uses information asymmetry to define the desired change.

The student must ultimately act without those privileged views. DAPD
addresses this gap by aligning predictions with and without
completion-level information along both reference and student
trajectories (Wu et al., 2026a). Its additional prediction branches
connect privileged supervision to the deployment view, at extra
computation cost. This differs from aggregating several informed
teachers, as in AVSD, and from teaching invariance to input
perturbations, as in OPCT: the central issue is whether guidance
constructed with extra information remains learnable when that
information is absent.

#### 4.1.3 From worked solutions to reusable guidance

Changing access to a worked solution still leaves its content tied to
one problem. When guidance comes from accumulated experience, extracting
what transfers becomes part of the learning problem. OPCD transfers
experience and system instructions using either external teachers or
teachers initialized from the same model (Ye et al., 2026c). It improves
test accuracy over off-policy context distillation in most evaluated
configurations, and its experience-consolidation comparisons retain more
out-of-distribution instruction-following performance. Yet prepending
raw traces degrades math validation performance, whereas extracted
lessons help. This comparison motivates separating the experience itself
from the guidance derived from it.

Guidance can specify a procedure or a criterion. Harness-generated
drafting and verification context, problem-solving structures, and skill
cards describe how to proceed (Zhao et al., 2026k; Zhao et al., 2026i;
Jiang et al., 2026c); rubrics describe what a satisfactory answer should
achieve (Rezaei et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2026c; Bablani et al., 2026).
They can replace a full worked solution in the reasoning teacher’s
immediate context, even when reference solutions are used to train the
guidance generator. Unlike response-level rubric rewards such as ROPD
(Section 3.1), rubric-conditioned self-teachers convert criteria into
token predictions. RCSD further learns to generate the rubric itself (Gu
et al., 2026c). Yet generic and randomly reassigned rubrics also improve
over its base model, cautioning against attributing every gain to
precise instance-specific criteria.

A compact lesson is useful only if its value transfers beyond the
attempt that produced it. SPEE tests lessons on held-out probe problems
before transferring them, whereas SGUID selects skills whose
distillation signals remain aligned with verified outcomes as training
progresses (Ren et al., 2026b; Liu et al., 2026m). These approaches test
different forms of persistence: usefulness across problems and
usefulness across student stages. SGUID’s selection requires an initial
full-bank run before retraining, so a smaller retained bank does not by
itself establish a lower total training cost. The broader design problem
is to preserve actionable experience without continually carrying the
full history that generated it.

### 4.2 Feedback and Hindsight

Feedback can change the evaluation of an attempt without replacing the
attempt. SDPO supplies a runtime error, failed test, or successful
attempt when re-scoring the original student prefixes (Hübotter et al.,
2026). The same token can consequently receive different guidance after
its consequences become known. When only scalar outcomes exist,
successful attempts can supply contextual feedback.

Revision creates two distinct learning opportunities: imitate the
corrected response or reconsider the original attempt with better
information. RLTF-SD follows the first route by transferring
critique-guided revisions alongside multi-turn RL (Song et al., 2026).
The second uses revision-informed teachers to re-score the original
generator prefixes, with the attempt and its outcome informing the
supervisory distribution (He et al., 2026c; Tang et al., 2026). A
successful revision can therefore improve supervision without becoming
the response directly imitated. Unsuccessful revisions may also explain
a mistake, but their value as guidance is task dependent, as ReTeach’s
comparisons show.

Corrective evidence must then be assigned to the attempts and decisions
it can inform. SRPO admits failed attempts with usable corrective
information; ROSD localizes supervision to a diagnosed first-error
boundary, falling back to the full response when localization fails (Li
et al., 2026b; Zhao et al., 2026l). RESD and Multi-Rollout OPD address a
different bottleneck by supplying persistent diagnoses or successful and
failed peers on the same prompt (Zhang et al., 2026l; Yu et al., 2026c).
Admission and localization determine where to learn; memory and peers
determine what evidence is available there. Section 5.2 connects this
allocation to reward updates.

Feedback can even determine whether guidance should be followed or
reversed. Direction-Adaptive Self-Distillation uses local student
entropy to choose attraction or repulsion, with a gap-based gate and
verifier reward (Zhang et al., 2026b). SGSD instead compares
skill-conditioned teacher support with trajectory success, reversing
guidance that supports failure or opposes success and ignoring weak
support (Huang et al., 2026a). Both relax unconditional imitation, but
their decision criteria differ: uncertainty describes the student’s
local prediction, whereas the outcome evaluates what the trajectory
achieved.

Useful feedback requires both an informative message and a model able to
interpret it. Improving the message alone can help: extracted coaching
advice outperforms raw critiques and rubric-only inputs in
LLM-as-a-Coach’s format comparisons (Ye et al., 2026b). Other approaches
change the model that acts on the evidence. PAST adapts the supervisor
to verified student attempts before re-scoring their original prefixes,
while SELF couples hindsight distillation with learning to predict
environment feedback (Feng et al., 2026b; Guo et al., 2026). The former
balances verifier-guided correction with student compatibility; the
latter trains the shared model to anticipate consequences. Their
additional objectives, and PAST’s teacher sampling, make this a change
in how feedback is learned from as well as how it is expressed.

Additional feedback can improve one measure while worsening another. In
Multi-Rollout OPD’s LiveCodeBench comparison, adding one failed peer to
one successful peer raises mean@8 but lowers pass@8 (Yu et al., 2026c).
Average success across eight attempts improves while the fraction of
problems solved at least once declines. Feedback quality also depends on
who provides it. A separate study finds that an incognizant tutor is
more likely to break an initially correct attempt through its feedback
than a cognizant tutor, with the distinction based on the tutor’s
relative validation-performance gap across domains (Byeon et al., 2026).

Hindsight need not be verbal. dOPSD matches token predictions at a
masked diffusion state to a stop-gradient average of predictions from
later states of the same trajectory, using only states where the
supervised position remains masked (Dat et al., 2026). Training retains
only rollouts with correct final answers, confining this form of
hindsight learning to successful denoising trajectories.

### 4.3 Privileged Perceptual Views

A better teaching view need not produce the most accurate standalone
teacher. GUI-SD finds that a boxed visual hint transfers better than
textual coordinates despite lower teacher accuracy in that
comparison (Zhang et al., 2026j). Vision-OPD gives the teacher an
evidence-centered crop while the student receives the full image with
spatial guidance, and Thinking Without Images conditions a teacher on
annotated regions and an answer while supervising textual inspection
traces (Yuan et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2026a). These views make task
evidence more accessible to the teacher at the student’s current state.

The teaching view may also reveal information unavailable at decision
time. World-Model PI gives an evaluator future video and the final
answer to supervise whether the student should invoke or trust a
prediction. MGSD gives its teacher symbolic planning state and a
reference plan while the student acts from an image (Zhou et al., 2026b;
Luo et al., 2026b). MGSD’s ablations support both the privileged state
and a preparation stage. These teachers use future observations or
structured planning information to supervise decisions the student must
make without them.

Geometric guidance exposes the tension between richer evidence and
dependence on hidden information. SpatialOPSD supplies verified traces
with executed geometric observations, while GPD routes textual 3D cues
and an answer to a teacher supervising an RGB-based student (Li et al.,
2026g; Li et al., 2026f). GPD’s comparisons favor selecting cues over
supplying all of them. SpatialOPSD shows why the student must also learn
what not to copy: masking trace-derived phrases gives its best accuracy,
whereas unlikelihood training more strongly suppresses references to the
hidden trace. A useful privileged view should improve decisions grounded
in the student’s input; reducing surface references alone does not
establish that all dependence on unavailable information has
disappeared.

Clean teaching views can supervise student responses generated from
corrupted images or from external-data contexts containing prompt
injections (Wang et al., 2026l; Peng et al., 2026b). The advantage comes
from removing the perturbation; in the image setting, this supplies a
distillation signal without ground-truth answers. The shared
construction serves different goals: the image-based study reports gains
on standard clean-image benchmarks, whereas the security study evaluates
resistance to prompt injection.

Making a view informative can require preparing the teacher as well as
selecting its input. Limited calibration in a constitutional safety
study improves selected safety and helpfulness measures, whereas longer
calibration can worsen downstream safety (Wen et al., 2026). View
changes can also determine which supervision to retain. OPD-V contrasts
evidence-present and masked views to gate tokens (Aniri et al., 2026). A
related use of contextual interventions appears in Cal-OPD, which probes
how they change a stronger external teacher’s likelihood and uses those
changes to estimate a range of teacher-side variation (He et al.,
2026a). It sets the distillation signal to zero inside this range and
retains only the student–teacher discrepancy beyond its boundary. These
choices tie the value of a view to the guidance it induces at student
states, beyond the quantity of information it contains.

### 4.4 Self-teacher Evolution and Internalization

Teacher evolution and privileged information are separate design
choices. A useful context can be supplied to a frozen model or to a
supervisor that changes with learning. Comparisons in demonstration- and
feedback-conditioned self-distillation favor regularized evolving
teachers over frozen or directly updated alternatives (Shenfeld et al.,
2026; Hübotter et al., 2026). SDFT illustrates the tradeoff: freezing
gives a stable but weaker teacher, whereas directly using the changing
student is unstable; EMA balances stability with adaptation. OPCD
instead finds its frozen teacher better than a continuously updated
self-teacher in Sokoban and medical comparisons (Ye et al., 2026c).
Because the contextual tasks and teacher constructions differ, these
results identify a tension between a stable instructional reference and
access to newly acquired competence, rather than one universally best
schedule.

An evolving target does not necessarily imply evolving teacher weights.
SD-Zero keeps its prepared reviser fixed in the main setting and tests
an extension that synchronizes it with the updated student, whereas
OPHSD changes the harness context supplied to a frozen initial
scorer (He et al., 2026c; Zhao et al., 2026k). Reviser synchronization
changes the supervisor’s parameters, while harness adaptation changes
the information those parameters act on.

Sharing parameters couples teacher improvement to student learning more
tightly, whether the teaching advantage comes from privileged
information or additional computation. JOLT trains a privileged branch
with reward and compatibility objectives while distilling its
predictions into the student branch; LoopOPD uses terminal-loop
predictions to supervise intermediate-loop behavior (Ardywibowo et al.,
2026; Wang et al., 2026q). JOLT and the dynamic D-LoopOPD variant link
supervisor evolution to student updates, whereas base LoopOPD freezes
its terminal-loop teacher. This distinction matters for the available
guarantees: JOLT’s local fixed-teacher reward-consistency argument does
not cover the complete shared-parameter optimization, and LoopOPD’s
task-level improvement bound requires aligned distillation and task
gradients with a sufficiently small update.

The coupling between branches also makes the teacher schedule depend on
the other losses being optimized. UniSD finds task-dependent
contributions from EMA targets, contrastive guidance, and representation
matching (Jin et al., 2026c). An evolving teacher can therefore be
assessed only in relation to the objectives that change it and the
student receiving its guidance.

Even a well-chosen self-teacher can only supervise the states training
exposes. A student that omits a relevant fact may never visit the
prefixes where a reference develops it. Reference-side supervision
complements student-path matching by teaching content under reference
prefixes, ranging from full-response anchoring to selected factual
corrections (Li et al., 2026n; Hong et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026e).
The central choices are how broadly to restore reference coverage and
how strongly to weight it relative to learning on the student’s path.

Reference content must also be recoverable from the deployment input. In
personalized generation, MIRROR supplies a reference only to the
self-teacher, while MIRROR-F additionally teaches selected reference
terms that occur in the student’s available context (Lai et al., 2026).
This makes a useful distinction between exposing the teacher to an
answer and grounding the student’s learning in accessible evidence. The
task-dependent gains leave both coverage and grounding as constraints on
successful internalization.

Internalization can change computation as well as answers.
Conciseness-conditioned distillation shortens reasoning, but aggressive
compression hurts harder tasks (Sang et al., 2026a). Masked Distillation
likewise finds that withholding a complete reference trace from the
student helps GSM8K yet sharply hurts graph coloring, where partial
masking works better (Kalwar et al., 2026). _Shorter reasoning is not
evidence of error repair._ Section 9.2 examines this distinction through
studies that separate correct from incorrect responses or isolate
self-distillation from other objectives (Kim and Lee, 2026; Harne et
al., 2026).

Because contextual guidance can disagree with measured task success, the
next chapter examines how outcome feedback complements or modifies
teacher supervision. Section 9 returns to internalization through
changes in student reasoning and solution coverage.

## 5 Combining OPD with Reinforcement Learning

Teacher preference and task success are different learning signals. A
teacher distribution distinguishes plausible next tokens throughout a
generated solution, while an outcome reward judges the attempt against a
verifier or other task criterion. Their combination is useful when the
teacher supplies behavior that helps the student obtain reward. It
becomes problematic when teacher-supported tokens belong to an
unsuccessful response, or when continued imitation constrains a student
that can improve through reward optimization. The design problem is
therefore how to combine the signals, decide where each is informative,
and change their roles during training.

We distinguish _joint objectives_, _selective supervision_, and
_sequential or alternating learning_. These choices concern the update,
the allocation of guidance, and the training schedule, respectively, and
can coexist in one method. A policy-gradient implementation of
distillation, such as MiniLLM or SDPO, does not by itself add an
independent task-reward objective (Gu et al., 2024a; Hübotter et al.,
2026). Similarly, a reference-policy drift penalty constrains policy
movement, whereas OPD supplies a teacher-derived behavioral target on
student trajectories.

### 5.1 Joint Objectives

GKD combines these two information sources for RL fine-tuning (Agarwal
et al., 2024). Student-generated outputs supply both the trajectories on
which reward is evaluated and the prefixes on which teacher
distributions are queried. For a GKD-style update at iteration $`k`$,
let $`\mu_{k}=\pi_{\theta_{k}}`$ denote the rollout policy, held fixed
in the matching term. The update objective can be written schematically
as

```math
\displaystyle\max_{\theta} \displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] \tag{4}
```

```math
\displaystyle-\lambda\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\mu_{k}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\left[\sum_{t}D\!\left(\pi_{T}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t}),\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y_{<t})\right)\right].
```

Here $`r`$ evaluates the attempt and $`D`$ denotes a chosen conditional
matching measure. The reward term uses a policy gradient, while matching
treats collected prefixes as fixed. Differentiating through the matching
term’s state distribution would add the temporal-credit component
discussed in Section 2.7. GKD also permits fixed examples to be mixed
in, with only the student-generated portion on-policy. Hybrid objectives
can thus differ in their divergence, prefix weighting, and treatment of
the sampling distribution.

Task evidence can determine either what counts as a discrepancy or how
much a separate objective rewards success. The white-box moment-matching
branch of PbKD uses preference-constrained value functions to compare
teacher actions with student actions at visited states (Jia, 2025). It
therefore selects discrepancies through task-relevant preferences
instead of imitating the complete distribution indiscriminately. KDRL
takes the second route, adding a sampled squared teacher–student
log-ratio loss to rule-based reward optimization (Xu et al., 2025a). At
a fixed prefix, sampling from the current untruncated student makes the
expected gradient of this loss equal the conditional reverse-KL
gradient. The squared loss is not itself a KL value, and the identity
does not supply future-state credit or automatically survive uncorrected
rollout reuse. The two constructions illustrate why the source of task
information and the form of the distillation update are separate
choices.

The distinction between local matching and trajectory credit remains
even when both terms arise from one formal objective. One formulation
decomposes trajectory-level student-to-reference KL plus task reward
into an analytic local-KL gradient and a sampled gradient carrying
future KL costs and terminal reward (Li et al., 2025). Unlike a local
matching loss added to outcome-based RL, its sampled component retains
future KL costs. The analysis locates that temporal-credit contribution,
but supplies neither empirical training validation nor a general
total-variance guarantee.

Hybrid designs also modify how local teacher evidence interacts with
outcome credit. Teacher guidance can accompany long-context reward
optimization (Ramos et al., 2026), while CAST uses an answer-free
self-teacher to reverse local credit for a teacher-supported token in a
failed response (Li et al., 2026j). CAST changes the _update direction_
according to task success. By comparison, relaxed OPD clips a teacher
log-ratio within the matching signal, without adding an independent
task-reward objective (Ko et al., 2026).

The teacher can also evolve during joint learning. CoDistill-GRPO
couples a changing large-model supervisor with small-model
rollouts (Kwon et al., 2026). Section 6.3 distinguishes the target and
trajectory exchanges in this asymmetric procedure. Whether the
supervisor is fixed or evolving, the next question is which attempts
should receive its guidance.

### 5.2 Selecting and Weighting Supervision

Selecting failed responses is useful only when corrective information is
available. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) derives advantages
by comparing rewards among responses to the same prompt. Equal rewards
provide no within-group preference. A successful sibling response can
supply a different kind of evidence. SRPO uses such feedback to correct
eligible failed attempts, while SSOPD uses the shortest correct sibling
to supervise an initial span of the longest failed attempt (Li et al.,
2026b; Tan and Hong, 2026b). The successful response informs a teacher
at the failed student’s prefixes rather than becoming the directly
imitated trace. RLTF-SD instead learns from revised responses, with
their contribution determined by reward relative to the first-turn
group (Song et al., 2026).

This distinction matters most when every attempt fails. Without another
feedback source, sibling-conditioned correction lacks a successful
example; SSOPD explicitly removes its contribution in all-failure and
all-correct groups. An independently supplied teacher target, such as
KDRL’s, can remain available even when reward contrast disappears.
Distill Where You Fail further finds that teacher-based advantage
weighting helps before token selection and teacher-response imitation
are added (Han et al., 2026c). Allocation therefore depends on _which
learning signal survives_, not simply on whether a response is wrong:
group reward contrast, a verified peer, and an external supervisor fail
under different conditions.

One response to missing corrective evidence is to search for it before
requesting a teacher. CataOPD first allows additional unaided student
attempts, reserving teacher guidance for unresolved questions (Liu et
al., 2026g). Verified student-produced trajectories then train the
unaided policy. Here the teacher helps obtain a successful target
instead of supplying a complete policy for matching. The strategy trades
additional sampling and verification for more selective teacher use; its
correctness-based routing also differs from reward-group comparisons
that can vary with formatting even when all answers are wrong.

Teacher information can reweight reward credit without becoming a
matching objective. Teacher likelihoods can modulate token-level reward
credit while preserving the sign of the reward advantage (Pan et al.,
2026; Wang et al., 2026b). Table 2 separates this role from gating
teacher updates and constructing residual credit. The distinction is
whether the teacher changes the strength of an existing update, selects
which updates survive, or helps define a new signal.

Credit shaping remains constrained by the reward signal it modifies.
RLCSD uses a bounded contrast between correct- and incorrect-hint
likelihoods to modulate selected GRPO advantages (Pan et al., 2026). It
discards uniform-outcome groups, and its token partitions remain

Together, these constructions show how teacher evidence can refine
reward optimization without supplying a distribution the student must
continue matching.

SC-GRPO makes this distinction explicit through two branches (Shan et
al., 2026). With at least two correct responses and one failure,
teacher–student divergence downweights locally aligned tokens while
preserving reward-advantage signs. With all responses incorrect, it
instead constructs a diversity pseudo-advantage from the divergence
weights; the remaining group types use standard GRPO. Neither weighted
branch adds a matching gradient. Its additive-distillation controls
underperform GRPO on LiveCodeBench. This supports credit shaping in that
setting while leaving open when direct matching is beneficial; teacher
information and teacher imitation need not play the same role.

Gating uses outcomes to decide which teacher updates survive. RG-OPD
retains trajectories according to the agreement between group-advantage
sign and teacher–student sequence-likelihood ordering, then applies the
same reverse-KL loss to retained responses (Akhondzadeh et al., 2026).
OPDVR makes the decision at the token level, keeping teacher
coefficients that reinforce correct responses or suppress incorrect
ones; its group-relative variant uses advantage sign instead (Lin et
al., 2026a). OPDVR’s reversed-gate control performs worse despite
comparable masking, supporting the direction rule over sparsity alone.
The granularity matters: admitting a response preserves its internal
matching signal, while admitting individual coefficients can remove
local contradictions within that response. Group-advantage sign must
also be distinguished from correctness: even with binary correctness
rewards, zero group advantage can represent either uniformly correct or
uniformly incorrect responses.

Residual credit changes the signal rather than selecting it. GC-OPD
subtracts a normalized distillation score from normalized group reward
and distributes the resulting discrepancy across tokens (Zhang et al.,
2026n). This addresses cases where teacher likelihood favors a
lower-reward response: the disagreement becomes corrective information.
RA defines a different residual, teacher minus student probability, and
combines centered distributional guidance with outcome credit (Chen and
Pang, 2026). Its local squared-distance identity holds before response
centering and clipping, not for the complete algorithm. In the
accompanying Co-RA study, an adapting teacher improves student mean
correctness while its own solver score falls, with mixed changes in
student pass@$`8`$. Together these results motivate evaluating teacher
guidance by its contribution to student learning, beyond standalone
teacher accuracy.

Verification can also reshape the matching target or its weight before
optimization. Graded property-equivalence scores weight forward-KL
matching (Zou et al., 2026). CriPO distinguishes criteria that no
sampled response satisfies from criteria that some responses satisfy but
aggregate reward fails to reinforce (Xia et al., 2026a). It uses
criterion-conditioned matching for the former and a counterfactual
teacher to locate and protect useful behavior in negative-advantage
responses for the latter. SPOT instead probes possible continuations at
branch points and uses verified outcomes to reweight a teacher prior (Qu
et al., 2026). Task evidence thus acts through loss weighting, support
selection, or the target distribution itself.

When the task admits a useful description of progress, the teacher can
supply process credit without dense policy matching. SPEAR rewards
ordered overlap between symbolic reasoning milestones, while Cliff uses
a verified teacher solution to locate the student’s first mistake and
reshape credit around that boundary (Li et al., 2026p; Han et al.,
2026b). These signals answer different questions: whether the attempt
follows relevant milestones and where it first ceases to be valid. Their
limitations follow from the same distinction. Symbolic overlap does not
establish logical validity, and error-boundary credit depends on judge
localization; Cliff also does not unconditionally reinforce every token
before the judged error. The choice of process signal should therefore
follow the evidence a task can reliably expose.

### 5.3 Sequential and Alternating Learning

Training schedules determine what distillation prepares and how its role
changes during reward learning. Preparation can have value that
immediate accuracy does not reveal. PRISM inserts black-box adversarial
pre-alignment between SFT and verifiable-reward RL, using perception and
reasoning scores from a multimodal discriminator to reward student
rollouts (Wang et al., 2026m). Its limited immediate accuracy gains are
followed by improved GRPO performance on the evaluated 4B and 8B models.
In its text setting, RL Starts before RL likewise finds a small initial
accuracy gain but a larger advantage after subsequent reward
learning (Dong et al., 2026b). PRISM uses a learned response-level
proxy, whereas RL Starts before RL uses distribution matching. Both
studies distinguish the behavior acquired during preparation from its
value for later learning.

Evaluating preparation by later learning can reverse the ranking of
matching objectives. In RL Starts before RL, reverse KL leads before RL
whereas forward KL leads afterward on fourteen of twenty tasks when
preparation uses student trajectories. Student-generated preparation
also outperforms teacher-generated trajectories after RL under either
divergence. In the framework of Section 2.5, the objective determines
which probability discrepancies are emphasized, while the rollout policy
determines where they are corrected. Their value therefore depends on
the intended learning sequence, making _downstream learning potential_ a
distinct property of a distilled model. Associated entropy and alignment
measurements do not establish that broader support or higher entropy
causes the later gains. Nor do these studies establish fixed-budget
superiority. PRISM matches the total number of policy-update steps but
adds discriminator compute, while RL Starts before RL omits preparation
cost from its comparison.

The preparation mechanism should address what limits the next learning
phase. Teacher-side reward training followed by teacher-rollout warm-up
and student-prefix OPD improves the behavior available for transfer (Xu
et al., 2026b). Stronger-model demonstrations also prepare a useful
teacher in that study, although the RL-trained teacher transfers more
effectively in the comparison. Policy Reheater instead addresses a
student policy that has become too concentrated to benefit from further
RL (Yang et al., 2026j). It softens the current model’s predictions
through self-distillation before reward learning resumes. Its advantage
over increasing sampling temperature is specific to the tested
low-entropy regime, and already high-entropy models can destabilize.
Improving the teacher and restoring student exploration thus address
different obstacles to subsequent learning.

A second decision is how long guidance should remain active once reward
learning begins. Gradual withdrawal can change either where matching is
applied or how strongly it acts. STEPS concentrates privileged
supervision in an early window and restores GRPO on annotated spans as
distillation fades. SAF-OPD selects and bounds strong sampled-token
signals before annealing their coefficient to zero (Wang et al., 2026h;
Ding et al., 2026). The former reallocates span-level reward and
matching updates, while the latter changes the strength and duration of
a signal already combined with GRPO. Both motivate a finite teaching
phase; SAF-OPD further isolates an annealing benefit in its Qwen3-4B
mathematics ablation. Its smallest code model nevertheless remains below
OPD alone in the reported comparison, so teacher retirement cannot be
assumed to improve every setting.

Separating phases from the outset makes a stronger commitment than
gradually withdrawing guidance. Sequential Beats Joint finds that
reverse-KL OPD followed by verifier-reward GRPO outperforms joint
alternatives on its logic tasks, with smaller or tied advantages in
mathematics (Li et al., 2026a). Its controls support interference
between acquiring teacher-supported behavior and optimizing reward for
the evaluated stronger frozen teachers. This motivates learning a useful
repertoire before sharpening it toward outcomes, while the task
variation limits generalization to every teacher and objective.

Student progress can inform when to end guidance or which prompts should
still receive it. RetireOPD makes a permanent transition to GRPO once
both alignment progress and relative task competence meet its
criteria (Yu et al., 2026f). PIVOT instead increases the fraction of
prompts assigned to GRPO according to a fixed cosine schedule, using
teacher perplexity averaged over student rollouts to decide which
prompts fill that fraction. Lower-perplexity prompts receive GRPO, while
the remainder receive OPD (Li et al., 2026e). This difference affects
both responsiveness and cost. Permanent retirement can end teacher use,
whereas repeated minibatch routing retains teacher evaluation even for
examples assigned to RL. Both depend on proxies for further teaching
value. RetireOPD’s sampled alignment statistic does not directly measure
gradient conflict, and evidence for PIVOT remains limited to two
few-shot intent-classification tasks with matched update steps.

A recurrent alternative in CoPD interleaves specialist reward learning
and transfer throughout training (Gu et al., 2026b), as developed in
Section 6.3. Fixed handoffs separate behavior acquisition from reward
optimization, adaptive decisions use the student’s progress to select a
transition time or allocate guidance, and recurrent transfer
incorporates improvements in the specialists. Their costs and effects on
retained behavior depend on the full learning sequence, developed in
Section 8.

## 6 Multi-Teacher OPD and Capability Integration

Integrating specialists requires deciding whose behavior to preserve at
each student state. A collection of strong mathematics, code,
instruction-following, or multimodal policies does not itself provide
one policy suitable for deployment across those capabilities.
Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD) addresses this gap by
querying specialist targets on trajectories generated by a shared
student. It integrates behavior through policy supervision, whereas
pooled reward training combines task rewards and parameter merging
combines model weights. The result depends on which teacher can
supervise a visited state, how much supervision each capability
receives, and whether the specialists remain fixed while the student
learns.

### 6.1 Teacher Eligibility, Routing and Target Composition

Routing by domain is a natural starting point: MOPD trains specialists
from a shared SFT model and asks the relevant frozen specialist to score
each student trajectory (Ma et al., 2026a). Yet a teacher-replacement
experiment shows sensitivity to teacher–student distribution mismatch. A
clear task label therefore resolves only whose expertise is relevant,
not whether that expertise transfers at the student’s states. The
study’s sampled-token and mass-corrected top-$`K`$ variants also
separate routing from the matching objective; correcting mass on
retained tokens does not make vocabulary truncation an unbiased estimate
of full-vocabulary KL.

Verified outcomes can make eligibility more specific than a domain
label. MT-SDPO admits experts whose cached answers pass verification,
then routes their critiques through a context-privileged
self-teacher (He et al., 2026b). VG-OPD instead admits an expert only
when its cached answer scores better on a criterion the student fails,
and uses that expert’s scores directly at selected token positions (Xu
and Zhang, 2026). The distinction is both the unit of competence, a
complete answer or an individual criterion, and the object transferred,
a critique or local prediction scores. VG-OPD’s scores form a detached
auxiliary advantage alongside GRPO rather than an exact reverse-KL
gradient. Its routing and localization controls favor the informed
choices over random alternatives.

Verification establishes eligibility, not universal reliability
downstream. MT-SDPO can fall below initialization when all teachers are
admitted without checking, but verification still fails to improve the
aggregate score, worst-domain score, or domain balance for one tested
student (He et al., 2026b). Likewise, narrower differences between
domain scores can reflect regression in an initially strong domain. A
specialist must demonstrate relevant competence, and its contribution
must still be evaluated through the student that receives it.

TrustMOPD provides a contrasting route when checked answers are
unavailable (Sun et al., 2026a). It weights specialists by calibrated
changes in their next-token preferences from a shared pre-RL reference,
using each teacher’s average change on a fixed sample for calibration.
These detached reliability proxies enter a weighted reverse-KL update.
Their interpretation depends on the specialists sharing that reference
and concerns preference change rather than verified correctness. Table 3
contrasts routing critiques through a self-teacher with directly
matching specialist predictions.

Once teachers are eligible, composition determines how their competing
priorities enter a common target. SMOPD organizes specialists by reward
priority and combines their guidance with a balanced anchor (Wang et
al., 2026o). Another choice is whether to transfer complete policies or
their improvements over a reference. $`\Delta`$-MOPD subtracts each
teacher’s initialization logits and adds the resulting shifts to the
student’s initialization logits (Sang et al., 2026b). TrustMOPD uses
preference changes to weight supervision from teacher policies, whereas
$`\Delta`$-MOPD transfers those changes as the target itself. For a
teacher sharing the student’s initialization, the shift construction
reduces to matching that teacher’s policy. Its paired mathematical
experiments favor shift targets when composing three teachers, while the
two target constructions perform comparably under interleaved routing to
one teacher at a time. This distinction matters when specialists have
different origins, though the reported norm-based balance diagnostics do
not separate the magnitude of post-training change from differences in
logit scale or confidence.

The divergence determines how composition treats disagreement among
specialists. For the analyzed sequence-level objectives, forward KL
yields an arithmetic teacher mixture and reverse KL a normalized
geometric aggregate (Di et al., 2026). Arithmetic mixing preserves
probability contributed by a specialist even when others assign little
mass to the same response. Geometric aggregation instead penalizes such
disagreement, connecting teacher composition to the probability-support
distinction in Section 2.5. Constructed examples show that a teacher
assigning very low probability to useful responses can suppress them in
the geometric aggregate even at small weight.

The level at which this composition is defined is essential. The
sequence-level reverse-KL aggregate’s next-token conditional includes a
partition factor over possible continuations, so it is generally
different from a locally normalized average of teacher log
probabilities. These results characterize the specified sequence
objectives, not empirical comparisons of trained language models or a
universal identification of on-policy sampling with reverse KL. They
explain why choosing how to combine teachers also requires choosing how
their disagreement should affect the student’s target.

### 6.2 Capability Balance and Retention

Capability balance depends on both exposure to supervision and
compatibility of the resulting updates. A domain may receive too few
supervised tokens, retain a large teacher gap that the student cannot
readily close, or lose performance during updates for another domain.
The methods below intervene at different points: sample selection,
allocation across domains, and the optimizer’s parameter displacement.
These interventions address different sources of imbalance, so equal
prompt counts or a small aggregate loss alone cannot establish
successful consolidation.

A specialized student may need to recover general capabilities while
preserving its specialty. CaMOPD alternates recovery and preservation,
using different teacher–student gap scores to select samples for each
goal (Chen et al., 2026d). Uncertainty-Calibrated MOPD instead filters
candidate trajectories and token updates using specialist and
general-teacher guidance (Liu et al., 2026o). Both make retention an
explicit training concern. Their evidence also distinguishes retention
from universal improvement: CaMOPD improves general-capability averages
in its role-play and medical settings without uniformly improving the
specialty.

A second imbalance arises because prompts contribute unequal amounts of
supervision. Open-MOPD finds that instruction following receives roughly
a fifth of prompts but only one percent of supervised tokens beside
longer mathematics and code responses (Gao et al., 2026). Token-share
balancing corrects this exposure mismatch. D³-MOPD asks a further
question: is the remaining mismatch still learnable? It allocates
prompts using both normalized teacher–student KL and recent progress,
with a floor to retain every domain (Sun et al., 2026d). A large but
stalled gap can thus receive less attention than a smaller, shrinking
one. Exposure balancing and progress-based allocation address different
bottlenecks; neither can be replaced by equal prompt counts. D³-MOPD’s
reported saving is in rollout steps to a baseline score, rather than
complete expert preparation and training cost.

A third imbalance concerns what an update does after data have been
allocated. Even adequate domain exposure does not ensure that the
optimizer’s step preserves each capability. UP-MOPD addresses this
possibility by constraining AdamW’s proposed parameter displacement
using current domain gradients (Zhu et al., 2026b). Acting on
displacement accounts for optimizer effects that raw-gradient conflict
checks can miss. The constraint, however, describes first-order changes
on the current batch rather than long-term freedom from forgetting.
Evidence for conflict intervention is also mixed. In UP-MOPD’s
medical–general setting, the paired benchmark differences against M-OPD
for one model evaluated late in training are not statistically
significant after multiple-comparison correction, while Open-MOPD finds
that its tested conflict masking and consensus targets hurt performance
even when allocation changes help (Gao et al., 2026). Together with the
additional cost of computing domain gradients in UP-MOPD, these results
make optimizer intervention a distinct tradeoff, not a default remedy
for every capability imbalance.

Successful integration must be assessed by the capabilities retained,
not only their average. A controlled comparison of multi-teacher OPD,
parameter merging, and pooled reward training finds similar aggregate
scores but much larger differences on individual benchmarks (Wu et al.,
2026b). OPD tracks expert performance more consistently, while
alternatives lose more instruction-following improvement. The experts’
weight changes also exhibit different domain relationships, but this
geometry does not identify a single cause of transfer or interference.
Nor is a cheap fusion stage necessarily a cheap pipeline: OPD fusion
costs less than one-fifth of per-domain RL in the comparison, whereas
including prerequisite experts raises total cost to 1.14–1.19 times that
baseline. Domain retention and teacher preparation therefore belong in
the same assessment.

Distillation and merging can instead be successive stages of one design.
USA prepares independently distilled language-agent experts for later
merging, applying larger sharpness-training perturbations to parameters
identified as more update-sensitive during warm-up (Zhong et al.,
2026a). Comparisons with a fixed merging operator favor this
preparation, while randomized or inverted assignments weaken transfer.
The contribution is to train experts for their eventual combination,
rather than assuming that individually strong policies will also merge
well.

### 6.3 Integration Pipelines and Evolving Specialists

Routing and allocation assume that useful specialists are available. A
complete pipeline must also establish the student that receives their
guidance, construct the specialist pool, and decide whether that pool
stays fixed during transfer.

Preparation determines both the student states available for refinement
and the granularity of the teachers. Two-phase agentic distillation
first pools expert traces for initialization, then queries fixed task
experts on the shared student’s trajectories, recovering much of the
specialization lost during pooling (Wang et al., 2026e).
Socio-Foundation instead first consolidates task experts into capability
teachers on fixed histories before integrating them on student-generated
histories (Wang et al., 2026j). The former prepares the recipient; the
latter also restructures what is transferred. Socio-Foundation’s
comparisons favor the hierarchy while still exposing tradeoffs between
outcome attainment and other simulated behaviors.

Specialization need not follow broad academic domains. Platform-specific
GUI teachers and category-specific software-engineering experts provide
useful divisions within an environment family (Lian et al., 2026; Zhao
et al., 2026e). In the software-engineering study, integration recovers
most or all of the specialists’ gains over the common base across three
categories, although differences in training pools, budgets, and
checkpoint selection limit comparison with joint RL. These cases make
teacher granularity a substantive design choice: the specialist should
capture a recurring source of behavioral variation that one general
policy must eventually handle.

System reports combine these preparation and integration choices in
different training schedules (Xiaomi LLM-Core Team et al., 2026; Yang et
al., 2026q; DeepSeek-AI, 2026). MiMo-V2-Flash combines specialist
log-ratio guidance with outcome advantages and compares the joint update
with reward-only and distillation-only variants on mathematics and code.
Nemotron-Cascade 2 instead reuses intermediate models with strong
domain-specific performance from its own training pipeline, including
the initial SFT model, to rebalance capabilities after sequential RL.
Its sampled-token log-ratio updates and stage comparisons provide a
concrete checkpoint-reuse design, without establishing equal-compute
superiority for the complete pipeline. DeepSeek-V4’s implementation of
dense multi-teacher supervision is discussed in Section 8.6.

Fixed-specialist pipelines transfer an existing repertoire; evolving
specialists can supply new behavior during integration. CoPD interleaves
capability-specific reward training with bidirectional policy-target
exchange on each branch’s own rollouts (Gu et al., 2026b).
CoDistill-GRPO couples models asymmetrically: the small model receives a
scalar distillation reward from the large one, while the large model
reuses small-model trajectories with importance reweighting (Kwon et
al., 2026). Thus, joint evolution can mean exchanging targets in both
directions or combining target flow one way with trajectory flow the
other. Their evaluated recipes support these forms of coupling, but do
not establish a general advantage for every component or an end-to-end
cost benefit. The extra branches remain part of the training system even
when deployment uses one consolidated student.

Caching frozen specialists and refreshing evolving branches impose
different requirements for running teachers, even when both pipelines
deploy a single student. Teacher placement and target caching therefore
depend on the chosen teacher roles and update schedule, as developed in
Section 8.6. When student actions also alter the environment, teacher
selection must work alongside history reconstruction and the use of
delayed outcomes. These interaction-specific dependencies arise whether
supervision comes from one teacher or a specialist pool.

## 7 Agentic OPD

For an agent, on-policy supervision must follow both the generated
action and the state that produced it. Each action can trigger a tool
response or environment transition, and memory can rewrite the history
visible at the next invocation. A teacher may therefore score the
student’s token under the wrong state even when that token was sampled
on policy. The mechanisms in Sections 3–6 remain available, but now
interact with state reconstruction, cross-turn credit, execution
control, and rollout depth. These are consequences of an interaction
loop, rather than response length alone.

### 7.1 State Identity and the Unit of Supervision

An interaction turn can contain several decisions, while environment
observations condition subsequent actions without themselves being
generated by the student. Tool-use and retrieval methods therefore
retain observations in the scoring history while applying the
distillation loss only to model-generated tokens (Zhong et al., 2026c;
Ye et al., 2026a). Masking retrieved passages, for example, preserves
their role in conditioning the next query. The unit used to allocate
supervision need not be the unit that receives the gradient. SOD uses
sampled discrepancy to weight tool-use steps; its pure-distillation
control favors step weighting over uniform OPD in the tested Python-tool
setting. OPDSearch+ further separates this masked distillation stage
from later teacher-free RL, illustrating that the loss mask and the
training schedule are independent choices.

The teacher’s information and identity are separate from this loss mask.
Skill-SD and MAD-OPD condition token-level guidance on skills and
multi-agent debate, respectively (Wang et al., 2026c; Wang et al.,
2026f). UI-MOPD adds platform-specialist routing with reward-conditioned
masking (Lian et al., 2026). Privileged skills and debate change _what
information the supervisors see_; MAD-OPD also combines several
teachers, while UI-MOPD routes among platform specialists. Transferring
privileged information also differs from providing it directly at
deployment. Skill-SD’s direct skill-prompting control underperforms
plain GRPO on held-out AppWorld and Sokoban tasks (Wang et al., 2026c).

### 7.2 State Reconstruction, Replay, and World Models

State reconstruction must distinguish deliberate teacher privilege from
an accidental change to the student’s history. In conversations with
incrementally revealed requirements, MAIGO supervises intermediate turns
with cleaned histories and also supervises the final answer after all
requirements arrive, whereas Canonical-Context OPD matches only that
final answer (Zheng et al., 2026b; Lin et al., 2026b). Both can use the
complete task for final-answer supervision; they differ in whether
intermediate responses also receive direct guidance. A world-model
teacher can instead receive an observed transition unavailable when the
student made its prediction, as in CoMAP (Liu et al., 2026l). Additional
observations or a cleaner presentation can change the supervisor’s
judgment; it need not rewrite the state assigned to the student’s
original action.

Choosing a training history is a separate decision. Prefix Replay
samples fresh student actions from stored teacher histories, reducing
interaction cost (Liao et al., 2026). TAP uses fixed teacher contexts
after structural pruning to keep a weakened student out of unproductive
trajectories (Li et al., 2026m). Both provide local on-policy action
supervision on deliberately chosen histories, rather than complete
coverage of free student execution. Their advantages are setting
dependent: Prefix Replay essentially ties ordinary OPD on its reported
search comparison, and TAP’s recovery advantage over full-trajectory OPD
disappears on WebShop at 80% feed-forward-network pruning. A history can
be useful for learning without being representative of the states the
deployed student will reach.

Once an action has been collected, however, its conditioning history
must be reproduced faithfully. MemOPD shows that reconstructing a
persistent conversation can reintroduce information a memory-compressing
agent had already removed (Liu et al., 2026n). Scoring the action under
that altered history corrupts likelihoods and can spuriously activate
policy-ratio clipping. Preserving visible content, token positions, and
action masks is therefore part of on-policy supervision, not merely
bookkeeping. Teacher-only hindsight remains compatible with this
requirement as long as it changes the teaching view without being
silently inserted into the student’s view.

Student participation in state generation also does not determine the
learning objective. QUEEN uses current-model chess proposals inside
engine-guided search, then consolidates analyses into targets for
subsequent SFT (Bhaskar et al., 2026). This is search followed by
imitation, rather than conditional distribution matching at the original
action prefixes. The distinction prevents improvements from a broader
search-and-training pipeline from being attributed to local OPD alone.

### 7.3 Cross-Turn Credit Assignment

Once states and action positions are aligned, the next issue is what
later evidence says about an earlier decision. OpenClaw-RL turns
observed consequences into rewards and selected hints for a privileged
self-teacher (Wang et al., 2026r). Gold actions and reusable skills
offer other sources of guidance (Sun et al., 2026c; Lu et al., 2026b).
Privilege need not fix the direction of teaching: UCOB compares
skill-conditioned and unconditioned rollouts and lets the higher-return
view teach the other at a repeated state (Tu et al., 2026). The design
choice is whether a view is presumed useful because of its information
or admitted because its behavior succeeds. OpenClaw-RL’s hint-selection
comparison also shows that random hint selection can destabilize
learning.

Terminal feedback arrives after several actions, motivating hindsight
guidance at selected action spans, search-query positions, or usable
turns (Yeo et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026c; Liu et al., 2026a). The
shared idea is to localize what later evidence can teach about earlier
decisions. HINT-SD, for example, generates corrective feedback from a
failed trajectory and uses a feedback-conditioned self-teacher for
reverse-KL matching on selected action spans, retaining each action’s
original history. Such localization remains heuristic when several turns
share an outcome or query quality is inferred from thresholded answer
overlap.

Hindsight can change either a target or the credit attached to an
action. SERL uses hindsight-conditioned likelihood gaps to positively
rescale executable-action GRPO updates, preserving their reward-derived
direction, and adds a separate action-only forward-KL term (Li et al.,
2026h). MIMESIS instead applies positive coached likelihood weights to
existing agent tokens (Phan et al., 2026). A small weight there weakens
reinforcement without directly penalizing a disfavored token. The same
description, “teacher weighting,” therefore conceals different effects
depending on whether the weight multiplies a signed reward advantage or
a likelihood objective.

These choices also separate hindsight from temporal credit. A later
outcome can improve a local target at the original history, as in
HINT-SD, without computing a return over subsequent teacher–student
discrepancies (Yeo et al., 2026). SERL additionally modulates
outcome-derived credit, but its separate matching term means that reward
sign does not determine the complete combined update. Which information
the teacher sees and which consequences enter an action’s gradient
remain distinct questions (Section 2.7).

Multi-agent interaction adds responsibility across roles to credit
across turns. MAS-OPD combines ordinary teacher–student log-probability
guidance with a contrast between intended and alternative roles, while
privileged teacher context contains verifier judgments about completed
earlier turns (Zhong et al., 2026b). The role contrast encourages
specialization; the retrospective context guides whether to revise or
retain a position after conflict. Their reported effects are
complementary, but each role still learns from its own generated tokens.
The signal retains the student log-probability term and is not an exact
joint-trajectory KL gradient. Role-specific supervision therefore
extends coordination without collapsing all interacting policies into
one ordinary sequence model.

Allocating guidance across turns remains a separate choice. Healthcare
AI GYM’s turn-level TT-OPD combines outcome-conditioned self-teaching
with GRPO and length-sensitive reward (Jeong, 2026). ATOD shifts weight
toward GRPO over training and attenuates turns with weak discrepancy and
surprisal signals; PCSD aggregates local teacher gaps to stabilize
selection (Tan et al., 2026b; Lv et al., 2026). These methods regulate
where and how strongly guidance acts. Their proxies do not by themselves
locate a causal error boundary, even when they improve the allocation of
a fixed supervision budget.

### 7.4 Teacher Intervention, Handoffs, and Recovery

Teacher intervention changes which states the student will encounter.
DAgger permits expert execution through a mixture policy (Ross et al.,
2011); agentic handoffs make takeover conditional on the unfolding
interaction. DASH-OPD accumulates discrepancy across turns and uses
different entry and exit thresholds to avoid reacting to every transient
difference (Xia et al., 2026c). STI-OPD instead makes takeover
stochastic, increasing its probability with discrepancy (Liu et al.,
2026c). Both regulate when to intervene, but disagreement can still
reflect a benign alternative or miss a shared error.

Testing the consequence of an intervention addresses that limitation
more directly. FTB compares paired student continuations after a
candidate teacher bridge and keeps only accepted bridges (Chen et al.,
2026a). Its motivating study finds that some high-disagreement
interventions worsen outcomes, demonstrating why detecting mismatch and
validating recovery are different problems. The validation still uses a
teacher-preference proxy, and extra continuations cost computation.
Intervention also changes the training distribution: STI-OPD’s
conditional importance weighting does not restore histories altered by
teacher actions or turn its clipped update into an exact
trajectory-level correction. A useful bridge must therefore be judged by
the subsequent behavior it enables, with its sampling effects kept
explicit.

Ending a local intervention and ending teacher supervision operate on
different timescales. DASH-OPD controls execution handoffs, while FTB
tests and retains teacher bridges within training trajectories.
RetireOPD instead uses alignment progress and relative task competence
to remove the teacher from subsequent training, as detailed in
Section 5.3 (Yu et al., 2026f). A handoff changes the ensuing
interaction, whereas retirement removes teacher guidance from the
remaining training.

### 7.5 Continuation Depth and Applicability

Shortening an interaction saves work only if the omitted states are not
essential to learning. TCOD expands the student-controlled horizon
through a curriculum, whereas TurnOPD combines KL concentration with the
observed completion depths of successful episodes (Wang et al., 2026g;
Zhou et al., 2026d). The latter’s success-based lower bound prevents a
small local discrepancy from ending every rollout before useful
completion states. Its ALFWorld comparison improves time and score,
though periodic full-depth probes add cost. On multi-hop tasks, vanilla
OPD scores slightly higher at equal update steps, while TurnOPD scores
higher at equal training time. Horizon selection thus needs evidence
about task completion in addition to local agreement.

Stopping also leaves a choice about where to resume. STRIDE couples a
teacher-likelihood stopping rule with restarts from selected retained
prefixes (Gui et al., 2026). Restarting can expose continuations that
truncation would otherwise omit, but it requires useful prefixes:
removing quality filtering can collapse effective training length. Its
accuracy–time tradeoff varies by domain and student size. Together,
curriculum, completion-aware stopping, and selective restarts address
how to reach useful later states without repeatedly paying for every
full interaction.

Intervention and horizon control consequently act on more than the
current loss. A teacher bridge changes the next state, a stopping rule
removes later states, and a restart returns training to a selected
history. Agentic efficiency depends on which learning opportunities
those decisions create or remove, connecting execution control to the
broader training dynamics examined next.

## 8 Training Dynamics and Efficiency

Useful supervision has to be delivered repeatedly while the student
changes. The capacity and budget tradeoffs in Section 2.4 therefore
depend on which experience training produces. Shorter rollouts omit
later states and replay reuses earlier states, changing $`d_{\mu}`$ in
Equation 2. Selecting positions changes their weight $`w(s)`$, whereas
restricting vocabulary items changes the support of supervision at a
given state. These efficiency choices change both computation and the
information available for learning.

### 8.1 Prompt Breadth and State Coverage

Prompt count is an incomplete measure of the experience available to an
on-policy learner. Repeated generation from a changing student can
expose new prefixes even when the prompt stays fixed. One-query
mathematical training illustrates this possibility: repeated
trajectories approach full-corpus performance, and a separate
representation analysis finds broad, though incomplete, state
coverage (Fu et al., 2026c). Selecting harder prompts can also help, but
matched-truncation controls substantially weaken their advantage (Hou et
al., 2026c). Some apparent benefit of difficulty therefore comes from
longer supervised trajectories, without establishing whether later
states or simply additional training tokens are responsible.

The value of input diversity also depends on the initial student and
subsequent renewal. Small teacher-generated question sets can match
real-data training in some model pairs, while removing explicit
questions has sharply different effects across pairs (Li et al., 2026c).
In another study, expanding a prompt bank hurts when initial-student
responses are frozen but helps when responses are regenerated (Hu et
al., 2026b). Unequal token consumption and single-run configurations
limit that comparison, yet the reversal cautions against treating prompt
breadth independently of the rollout policy.

These findings distinguish three aspects of coverage: breadth across
inputs, depth along trajectories, and renewal as the student changes. A
small prompt set can still produce diverse, evolving states; a large
fixed response bank cannot provide fresh student behavior. “Data-free”
question acquisition likewise leaves student generation and teacher
scoring as essential sources of training experience.

### 8.2 Supervision Selection and Trajectory Control

Selection decides where to spend supervision. It can act before
generation by choosing prompts, after generation by retaining or
weighting complete responses, or within a response by choosing segments
or individual positions. At a selected position, it can also restrict
which vocabulary items enter the loss.

#### 8.2.1 Allocating prompts and complete responses

A useful prompt must expose a learnable gap and admit reliable guidance.
PACED emphasizes problems at intermediate student competence, whereas
Transfer-Stratified OPD uses joint teacher–student success estimates to
select forward-KL acquisition, reverse-KL correction, or no update (Xu
et al., 2026d; Chen et al., 2026g). The latter uses forward KL when only
the teacher succeeds, reverse KL when both succeed but the teacher
remains stronger, and skips prompts without an estimated teacher
advantage. Allocation thus depends on both student competence and
available guidance. Small initial samples can misclassify competence,
and zero observed success does not rule out useful token-level
supervision.

Student difficulty alone leaves the teacher’s contribution unspecified.
Uni-OPD balances difficulty and response correctness while calibrating
teacher–student returns using outcomes; DeltaPrompts seeks
teacher–student answer disagreement together with teacher
consistency (Hou et al., 2026b; Jung et al., 2026). These approaches
distinguish a struggling student from a teacher that offers a reliable
alternative. Competence, disagreement, and reliability can inform the
same allocation rule without becoming interchangeable signals.

At the response level, compatibility and task success offer
complementary evidence. Lower teacher perplexity on erroneous responses
predicts recovery from truncated prefixes in SCOPE, whereas RA-OPD
filters trajectories when verifier outcomes conflict with the sign of
the average distillation return (Zheng et al., 2026a; Gan et al., 2026).
The former estimates whether a trajectory remains recoverable; the
latter rejects a mismatch between outcome and aggregate update signal.
Neither locates the erroneous tokens. FiRe-OPD combines hard trajectory
filtering with soft token weighting, illustrating why a response-level
eligibility decision can coexist with finer allocation inside the
retained response (Li et al., 2026o).

#### 8.2.2 Locating useful supervision within a response

Local selection asks which parts of a response carry useful supervision.
Continuation probes estimate whether an intermediate prefix still
permits a correct answer; R2-OPD uses these estimates to mask segments
whose process and distillation rankings disagree (Yang et al., 2026a).
This supplies task-related evidence at the cost of extra sampling and
estimation variance. More aggressive masking can sharply hurt
performance, so identifying low-value segments does not make retaining
less supervision uniformly beneficial.

Distribution scores avoid those probes but require a choice of what to
favor. SelecTKD emphasizes student proposals accepted by teacher
verification, while Teachability-Aware OPD favors disagreement supported
by teacher mass on the student’s candidates (Huang et al., 2025; Wang et
al., 2026s). Compatibility protects usable behavior; supported
disagreement identifies a possible learning opportunity. TIP further
distinguishes confident from uncertain disagreement, and DIAL-OPD
discounts large log-ratios arising where both models assign negligible
token probability (Xu et al., 2026c; Zhao et al., 2026c). These
refinements qualify the meaning of a discrepancy. None makes
distributional disagreement a direct test of token correctness or
ultimate reachability through training.

A different criterion is whether the teacher’s additional training or
information changes its advice. S2D-OPD selects states where RL most
changes the teacher relative to a reference, while Visual-Advantage OPD
weights supervision by the teacher’s sensitivity to fine visual
information (Zhao et al., 2026j; Liu et al., 2026e). Their selection and
masking comparisons support concentrating supervision on informative
differences. The interpretations nevertheless differ: a teacher’s
RL-induced change can be harmful, and visual sensitivity measures
information dependence rather than correctness.

Estimating what an update will achieve brings allocation closer to
learning benefit, but can be much more expensive than using properties
of the trace. At the inexpensive end, PW-OPSD gives later positions more
weight while retaining earlier supervision; its supporting diagnostics
concern ambiguous positions on initially correct traces (Liu et al.,
2026h). MetaOPD instead learns weights through the effect of a one-step
update on reference solutions (Wang et al., 2026t). This estimates an
outcome of learning rather than relying on position as a proxy, but
nearly doubles measured training GPU-hours in its reported Qwen3-1.7B
setting. The practical tradeoff is between the reach of a cheap
structural assumption and the cost of estimating which updates will
help.

Choosing a position still leaves the support of its target unspecified.
SafeSteer (Li et al., 2026d) selects safety-relevant vocabulary items
within a position. It changes which candidate tokens contribute to the
loss, a distinct choice from deciding which states or sampled actions
receive supervision.

Selection changes which parts of a generated trajectory contribute to
learning. A mask applied after complete generation and teacher scoring
can reduce subsequent work, but leaves those two costs intact. Earlier
intervention can change the trajectory itself.

#### 8.2.3 Trajectory repair and behavior intervention

Repair changes the behavior distribution itself. The intervention can
respond to broad divergence from the teacher, as in AdaSwitch, or to a
specific missed opportunity for reflection, as in Relay-OPD (Peng et
al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026a). Both insert teacher-generated
continuations into the student’s trajectory. They therefore incur online
teacher decoding and train on mixed histories rather than merely
reweighting the original response.

Where the correction enters determines what remains of the student’s
mistake. BRTS adds a teacher response selected for correctness and
student compatibility, although its fallback can still be
incorrect (Zhang et al., 2026f). MOTAB instead backtracks to an earlier
student prefix and stitches a teacher continuation after the deviation
through a revision marker (Wang et al., 2026a). Whole-response
supplementation supplies an alternative route; backtracking preserves an
explicit relation between deviation and revision. Teacher compatibility
motivates both constructions, but does not by itself certify a response
or repair point as logically valid.

Compression changes the supervised prefixes more extensively. MPD
applies token-level KL to teacher-shortened versions of current-student
reasoning traces (Yang et al., 2026c). Comparisons of trajectory sources
and compressors find useful accuracy–length tradeoffs for this
mixed-policy recipe. Although the student supplies the original trace,
matching takes place on rewritten prefixes.

Student compatibility can also be built into a fixed training corpus. In
Interleaved-Policy Distillation (IPD), a frozen teacher proposes tokens,
a frozen initial student replaces poorly supported proposals, and the
teacher continues from the revised prefix (Kim et al., 2025). The
resulting mixed trajectories become targets for offline SFT. Finetuning
with Sampling likewise constructs model-matched traces before supervised
training, using offline expert-conditioned resampling under the frozen
base model’s likelihood rather than scoring the evolving student’s
rollouts (Karan et al., 2026). Both report settings in which tailored
offline traces outperform the compared on-policy alternative. The
benefit depends on the model receiving the traces, and IPD’s
teacher-propose advantage over student-propose generation shows that
compatibility alone does not explain trajectory quality. These results
position trajectory construction as both an alternative to OPD and a
possible source of initialization for it.

#### 8.2.4 Horizon control

Horizon control changes how far the student generates before learning
from a trajectory. FOPD (Zhang et al., 2026a) begins with short student
prefixes and expands their length during training. For Qwen3-8B-Base,
the reported schedule takes $`53.0`$ hours on an eight-A100 virtual
machine, compared with $`148.3`$ hours for full OPD, while benchmark
accuracy remains within $`0.5`$ percentage points. These results exclude
evaluation and depend on the selected student. Very short prefixes hurt
out-of-domain accuracy for a weaker model, exposing the cost of
withholding direct supervision from later states.

The central horizon tradeoff is whether the avoided suffix contains
states that need their own training. Fixed early-window supervision can
outperform post-generation token selectors while also avoiding suffix
generation (Ziheng et al., 2026). Comparisons of fixed and progressively
extended horizons find useful accuracy–time tradeoffs in language
models, but an autoregressive navigation control fails under early-only
supervision when later states require separate learning (Zhang et al.,
2026k). The success of a short prefix therefore depends on how much its
updates generalize beyond that prefix.

Adaptive horizons use evidence from the teacher to decide when further
generation is worthwhile. Prune-OPD shortens subsequent budgets after
cumulative compatibility failures; KAT stops when local teacher–student
KL remains small (Yang et al., 2026p; Xin et al., 2026). These rules
respond to different low-value suffixes: unreliable guidance versus
vanishing discrepancy, which in KAT can reflect degenerate agreement at
corrupted prefixes. Their benefit also depends on the overhead of
checking the stopping condition. KAT evaluates random stopping and fixed
prefixes, whereas Prune-OPD’s advantage over equal-retention random
stopping remains unestablished.

Training feedback can also determine how the horizon changes (Zhang et
al., 2026h; Tan et al., 2026a). ShortOPD monitors repetition, truncation
and effective response length when recovering pruned models, shrinking
unproductive early rollouts and restoring longer generation as recovery
proceeds. Adaptive FastOPD expands when boundary learning signals
plateau and sufficiently many rollouts reach that boundary. Its more
conservative multi-signal rule improves final accuracy at additional
cost relative to a simpler overlap-based rule. These designs separate
the potential usefulness of later states from the evidence needed to
justify extending the budget to reach them.

The checking policy is itself part of this tradeoff. Flash-OPD adjusts
verification intervals using accumulated low-overlap events and reuses
the resulting teacher scores for training (Chen et al., 2026e). Repeated
verification and prompt processing make its time savings smaller than
its token savings, and one tested pairing incurs a small accuracy cost.
An effective stopping rule must therefore save more work than it
introduces while preserving useful later-state supervision.

### 8.3 Refresh, Replay, and Staleness

Refreshing a score does not refresh a state. A stored trajectory may
come from an older student, carry student probabilities and advantages
computed before recent updates, or retain scores from an earlier
teacher. Exact reuse of a teacher score therefore does not preserve
on-policy state collection.

A fixed cache can remain useful even after its generating policy becomes
outdated. Lightning-OPD precomputes teacher scores on initial-student
responses, removing live teacher inference from optimization; its cost
accounting includes collection and precomputation, while its
approximation depends on teacher consistency and additional
conditions (Wu et al., 2026c). Semi-OPD retains the same states and
teacher scores but recomputes student probabilities and advantages (Zhao
et al., 2026g). It outperforms fresh-rollout OPD in most of its
mathematical pairings, and complete cached trajectories outperform their
first quarter at a matched token budget. These results show that useful
stored supervision can outweigh the value of renewal in some regimes,
without making fixed states equivalent to current-student states.

The content of a cache matters alongside its age: a weaker policy can
expose errors that complement the current student’s attempts.
Negative-Policy OPD exploits this possibility by mixing complete
trajectories from a fixed weaker policy with current-student
trajectories under the same teacher–student supervision (Hwang et al.,
2026). Its reported gains span mathematical, coding, and scientific
reasoning, with a fully cached endpoint that removes current-student
generation. The weaker policy supplies states on which to learn; the
teacher still determines which tokens to encourage or suppress. Thus,
the benefit concerns the experience available in the cache, while
pre-generation also shifts part of its cost outside online optimization.

Asynchronous training poses a different problem: it aims to follow the
changing student but delivers trajectories late. In a coding-agent
comparison, f-OPD finds a substantial loss from asynchronous delay and
recovers most of it through freshness-aware control (Chen et al.,
2026f). Near-Policy combines periodic rollout-policy refresh with a
teacher–student differential-difficulty filter, although this heuristic
does not measure trajectory age or ensure current-state coverage (Rang
et al., 2026). These controls address delayed tracking, whereas a fixed
cache deliberately trains on a stable state distribution.

The update rule also changes tolerance to delay. In the AsyncOPD study,
a prior-work sparse top-$`K`$ forward-KL implementation degrades less
under stale rollouts than the study’s PPO-style reverse-KL pipeline,
despite the latter performing better with fresh data (Kang et al.,
2026). Recomputing advantages and applying a behavior-to-learner
importance ratio corrects sampled actions at cached states; it cannot
renew the states themselves. AsyncOPD’s throughput gains concern the
measured sparse asynchronous pipeline, leaving dense full-vocabulary and
multi-node settings unresolved.

Inner-loop reuse spends additional student updates on supervision
already acquired. Open-MOPD refreshes student-dependent candidate
advantages while retaining teacher log-probabilities, and CLOOPD reports
gains from extra student passes without another teacher pass (Gao et
al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026c). CLOOPD’s adaptive allocator does not
outperform random allocation at the same reuse cap, leaving additional
student updates as the better-supported explanation for the reported
gains.

Refresh, replay, and reuse allocate computation to different parts of
the learning process. Refresh acquires new states, replay draws further
supervision from stored ones, and inner-loop reuse spends additional
student updates on an acquired batch. Their value depends on how much
useful supervision remains in stored states relative to the cost of
reaching new ones. Refreshing teacher scores or student-dependent
coefficients can improve an update without changing that state-coverage
tradeoff.

### 8.4 Initialization and Training Lifecycle

The student must be ready to benefit from the states and targets a
schedule exposes. Teacher preparation and self-teacher evolution change
the available guidance (Sections 3.2 and 4.4), but cannot by themselves
ensure that the student reaches useful prefixes or can fit the resulting
targets. Initialization, guided exploration, and objective scheduling
address these different obstacles. Their role also depends on whether
OPD is the final training stage or preparation for task-reward learning
(Section 5.3).

Initialization affects whether the student reaches states on which
teacher guidance is useful. An off-policy or supervised stage can
establish compatible reasoning patterns before student-generated
training, a role shared by mathematical cold-start experiments and the
Qwen3 smaller-model distillation pipeline (Li et al., 2026l; Yang et
al., 2025). DeepSeek-R1’s distilled students provide a contrasting
pipeline in which teacher-trace training alone produces strong
students (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). Supervised preparation can thus
support later OPD without implying that an on-policy stage is always
necessary.

A transition into OPD can prepare the student or alter the histories it
first explores. BIRD fits concise verified traces before
self-distillation, while HPD uses fixed offline prefixes with limited
student alternatives to prepare later rollout-based learning (Dong et
al., 2026a; Zhu et al., 2026c). These methods change the initial policy;
HPD’s isolated student-sampled action does not make its fixed-prefix
stage on-policy. Trust-Region Behavior Blending instead introduces
teacher guidance into the sampling policy and anneals toward
student-only generation (Plyusov et al., 2026). It changes experience
during the transition and requires online teacher decoding. Thus,
preparing a compatible starting policy and guiding its first
trajectories address related difficulties through different
interventions.

For a pruned student, preparation also determines which damage OPD must
repair. ReCal uses teacher-response prefixes and a pruning probe to
emphasize vulnerable tokens when choosing channels, before a shared
SFT–OPD recovery pipeline (Jiang et al., 2026a). Benefits already appear
after SFT, so the final improvement cannot be attributed wholly to more
effective OPD. Moreover, the reported AIME gains accompany consistent
IFBench declines. Preparing a student for successful reasoning transfer
can therefore preserve a different set of capabilities from those needed
for instruction following.

The objective can follow a separate schedule. CADENCE uses student
coverage of the teacher’s top-$`K`$ support to accelerate a transition
from a mode-covering surrogate toward reverse KL (Kumar and Jha, 2026).
Coverage feedback improves the tested mathematical recipe, but
accelerates the schedule only after a threshold is crossed; it cannot by
itself stop an aggressive baseline schedule from sharpening too early.
Objective scheduling consequently governs how the student fits a target,
independently of when new states are collected.

Successful preparation need not produce the best immediate distillation
score. As Section 5.3 shows, the preferred distillation endpoint can
differ from the best starting point for later RL. A schedule therefore
has to connect what the student can learn now with what it must retain
or learn next; optimizing each stage in isolation can favor a different
policy from optimizing the intended learning sequence.

### 8.5 Continual Adaptation and Retention

Repeated adaptation adds a retention objective to immediate transfer.
Demonstration-conditioned SDFT accumulates skills across tool use,
science question answering, and medical adaptation more successfully
than the compared SFT pipeline, although some earlier capabilities still
degrade (Shenfeld et al., 2026). Continual fact learning gives a
complementary result: OPD retains earlier writes better than
distillation on fixed teacher continuations, but varied-example SFT
retains more (O’Neill et al., 2026). Together, these comparisons suggest
that renewed trajectories can help preserve learning across updates,
while ordinary supervised diversity can provide a strong alternative.
The relevant property is what training experience supports successive
changes, rather than the on-policy label alone.

### 8.6 Full-Pipeline Compute and Systems

The relative costs of preparing the teacher, generating student
trajectories, and applying supervision determine which efficiency
strategy is useful. A pretrained teacher may already be available, while
a new RL-trained specialist adds preparation cost. Caching moves teacher
scoring before student optimization, and batch reuse amortizes it over
additional student updates.

These costs also change as the student learns. Comparisons with GRPO
depend on how much teacher scoring and rollout generation each method
requires (Zheng et al., 2026a). Selective correction can reduce the
teacher workload when fewer failed attempts remain eligible, as in
SRPO (Li et al., 2026b). A fixed cost per update can therefore obscure
how the balance between generation and supervision changes over
training.

Autoregressive generation and parallel scoring of a known sequence
impose different bottlenecks. FOPD reduces both rollout and training
work by stopping at a prefix, whereas SafeSteer restricts vocabulary
support after the required states have been produced and may still score
much of the teacher distribution (Zhang et al., 2026a; Li et al.,
2026d). Sparse losses therefore need not yield the same savings as
shorter trajectories.

The value of teacher computation depends on what training it displaces.
Against task-reward RL, Qwen3 reports $`1{,}800`$ GPU-hours for its 8B
on-policy distillation stage versus $`17{,}920`$ for the compared RL
stage, with a higher AIME24 result for distillation (Yang et al., 2025).
In pretraining, however, the alternative is to spend those resources on
more student updates. MiniPLM compares online MiniLLM, ordinary
pretraining, and offline teacher–reference corpus selection under
matched training-time FLOPs that include online teacher computation (Gu
et al., 2024b). MiniLLM receives fewer optimization steps and falls
below ordinary pretraining on the downstream average across all three
student sizes, whereas offline corpus selection improves that average.
Teacher guidance can therefore be an effective substitute for one form
of training while costing more than the benefit it supplies in another
regime.

Whether this advantage survives over the full pipeline depends on how
the teacher and its targets are obtained. Qwen3’s stage comparison
excludes construction of the stronger teacher; the sparse-to-dense
reward pipeline explicitly spends compute improving a teacher before
transfer (Xu et al., 2026b). MiniPLM reports offline scoring and
reference-model preparation separately and considers amortization across
students, so its matched training-time result does not settle the
total-cost ordering. Lightning-OPD includes collection and
precomputation in its totals, but obtains those savings with fixed
states. The relevant comparison thus depends both on the alternative use
of computation and on which preparation costs can be shared across later
training.

Target representation also changes the storage and communication burden.
AsyncOPD uses sparse and Monte Carlo estimators in its measured
asynchronous pipeline (Kang et al., 2026). DeepSeek-V4 instead performs
full-vocabulary reverse-KL transfer from more than ten specialists,
caching final hidden states for logit reconstruction and grouping
samples by teacher so that one prediction head resides on device at a
time (DeepSeek-AI, 2026). Asynchronous state and parameter transfers
support this dense design, whose end-to-end speedup is not isolated in
the report.

Reducing differentiation cost presents a different opportunity from
reducing access to teacher predictions. SparseOPD constructs the full
reverse-KL correction without retaining its gradient graph, then
backpropagates through selected logits while compensating for omitted
positive and negative correction mass (Miao et al., 2026).
Matched-support comparisons favor this correction over selecting only
high-probability candidates. Memory savings are largest relative to the
additional allocations above the pre-step baseline. The reduction in
total peak memory is smaller, and complete updates can take longer. This
makes sparsification useful for a memory bottleneck without establishing
a training-speed advantage.

Trajectory repair couples teacher scoring to generation. Speculative
Knowledge Distillation accepts student proposals in the teacher’s
top-$`K`$ set and resumes from a teacher replacement at the first
rejection (Xu et al., 2025b). Its corrected trajectories require online
verification and additional teacher sampling, unlike scoring a completed
student response. The resulting cost depends on how frequently
generation crosses between the two models.

Efficiency methods therefore trade computation against state coverage,
freshness, and retained capability. Which of these changes explains a
gain is a separate question from whether the complete method improves
performance, motivating the mechanism studies in the next chapter.

## 9 Understanding and Evaluating OPD

OPD gains can arise from teacher information, behavior elicited by
privileged context, or update effects that persist without accurate
teacher guidance. These explanations lead to different choices of
supervision. They also bear on whether improved task accuracy comes with
broader solution coverage, reliable stopping, and retained capabilities.

### 9.1 Teacher Reliability and Student Learnability

The reliability of teacher predictions and the student’s ability to
learn from them are distinct questions. Section 3 examines how to
construct such guidance: continuation experiments and
trajectory-refinement analyses distinguish the teacher’s ability to
solve a task from the reliability of its guidance at student
prefixes (Li et al., 2026l; Liu et al., 2026j; Jiang et al., 2026b).
Here the question is what explains transfer once that guidance is
supplied. The evidence points to interactions among target
representation, student initialization, and the update, rather than
independent benefits from each component.

Compatibility depends on how the target is represented and fitted.
Restricting vocabulary support can help, yet its interaction with
special-token masking varies across tasks; an unnormalized top-$`K`$
reverse-KL implementation can also introduce a harmful gradient term (Fu
et al., 2026b; Zhu et al., 2026a). A tighter variance bound for a
token-local estimator does not remove its bias relative to
sequence-level reverse KL (Fu et al., 2026b). These results explain why
target restriction, masking, and estimator choice cannot be assumed to
contribute independent gains.

The teacher–student pairing adds another dependence. Changing the
student from base to post-trained initialization can reverse teacher
rankings, and a log-ratio compression rule that helps one pairing can
lose to hard clipping under a larger mismatch (Wang et al., 2026k).
Choosing a teacher and regulating its influence are therefore coupled
decisions.

Persistent disagreement need not produce a strong learning signal. An
analysis of idealized full-KL OPD separates loss change into learning at
fixed prefixes and a term induced by the changing rollout
distribution (Zhao et al., 2026f). Its local recovery bound applies to
sufficiently nearby teachers sharing the student’s parameterization; it
does not guarantee recovery from an arbitrary stronger teacher. Training
plateaus and declining gradient proxies are consistent with weak usable
supervision, but the observed proxies neither isolate its cause nor
measure the idealized flow directly.

Learnability also varies within a response. A shrinking teacher top-two
log-probability gap on student candidates can identify prefixes beyond
which additional supervision becomes less useful (Liu et al., 2026d).
Coordinating token selection with the amount of privileged teacher
information similarly improves allocation in the tested settings (Yang
et al., 2026l). The first changes where supervision is applied; the
second also changes the information behind it. These findings support
adapting supervision to the student, but neither makes a distribution
score a calibrated correctness estimate.

Prompt success rate has different implications for reward learning and
teacher matching. Competence-based weighting has a gradient
signal-to-noise motivation under explicit assumptions (Xu et al.,
2026d). An all-wrong group provides no successful sibling for teacher
conditioning. If its responses also receive equal rewards, their
group-relative reward advantages are zero. A separate teacher-matching
loss can nevertheless remain nonzero. Calibration and early length
stability reveal further aspects of supervision that success rate alone
misses (Zhang et al., 2026c; Luo et al., 2026a).

### 9.2 Transfer and Competing Explanations

An accuracy gain does not by itself identify what the student has
learned from the teacher. Three explanations recur in the evidence:
acquisition of missing information, more effective execution of existing
knowledge, and changes in the response distribution that do not require
accurate teacher credit. These mechanisms can coexist. The comparisons
below distinguish them by varying the objective, teacher context, or
dependence of the update on teacher predictions.

Information acquisition depends on the matching objective as well as the
visited states. Crossing teacher- and student-generated prefixes with
forward and reverse KL separates two choices often changed
together (Zhao et al., 2026b). In synthetic lookup-and-composition
tasks, forward KL improves factual transfer under either prefix source,
while student prefixes improve the likelihood of correct multistep
completions under either divergence (Tang and Yang, 2026). Reverse-KL
OPD more readily transfers execution to unseen composition trees than
facts absent from the student’s initialization; factual transfer is also
limited in a natural-language recent-facts test. The crossed synthetic
result nevertheless rules out interpreting this as a general inability
of OPD to transfer knowledge. In the framework of Section 2.5, changing
the states exposes different learning situations, while changing the
divergence reweights the alternatives at those states. The observed
division between factual acquisition and execution is evidence for that
distinction in these tasks, not a universal ranking of divergences.

The information supplied in context and the teacher’s ability to
interpret it are also distinct. DOPD compares a stronger teacher with a
privileged-input view of the student under the same context, routing
supervision using their absolute sampled-token log-probability gap
together with their relative predicted probabilities (Yu et al., 2026e).
Removing high-gap positions hurts more than removing an equal fraction
of low-gap or random positions. This identifies useful guidance within
the recipe, although calibration, style, and model interfaces prevent
reading the gap as a pure measure of capacity.

Useful guidance need not locate the errors that matter for the final
answer. Predictive discrepancy compares model probabilities, whereas
correctness discrimination asks whether a score separates correct from
incorrect responses. A small discrepancy does not imply a valid
response, since most sampled tokens in an incorrect response can still
receive similar probabilities from teacher and student (Ma, 2026). At
the response level, privileged-likelihood scores show near-chance
discrimination and lower accuracy than an outcome-only control on AIME,
but above-chance discrimination and improved held-out accuracy in
biology (Nguyen et al., 2026b). The settings differ in both model and
feedback format, so the contrast does not isolate a task effect. Limited
AIME training replication and a context-template mismatch further
constrain the comparison.

Locating an invalid reasoning step requires evidence beyond
response-level discrimination. UECR compares its task credit with
independently annotated invalid steps and finds an association that
weakens when entropy positions are shuffled (Zhang et al., 2026d). This
tests whether the signal tracks where reasoning fails, rather than
merely whether a response is correct. Even successful localization
leaves a further learning question open, namely whether using that
signal improves the student’s subsequent reasoning.

Privileged context can change execution without supplying the instance’s
answer. A reverse-KL analysis distinguishes reusable latent rules from
instance-specific solutions and shows that aggregating conditioned
teachers produces a geometric target through their log
probabilities (Zhu et al., 2026a). Under forward KL, OP2SD often
improves most when an unrelated worked mathematical example replaces the
paired solution (Ichihara et al., 2026). The paired answer is
unnecessary for those gains, but trivial mathematical and physics
contexts fail. This evidence supports a behavioral role for context
while leaving its precise mechanism unresolved; it does not make context
content irrelevant.

Improved execution must also be distinguished from repairing incorrect
reasoning. Correct-only OPSD shortens thinking traces with
student-dependent accuracy changes, whereas incorrect-only training can
shorten responses while reducing accuracy (Kim and Lee, 2026). Prompt
matching and additional seeds preserve this ordering; richer context and
longer training do not establish reliable repair. In complementary
experiments, solution-conditioned self-teachers favor the supplied path,
assign similar losses to correct and incorrect rollouts, and degrade
explicit-thinking accuracy when distillation is the sole
objective (Harne et al., 2026). These results support compaction of
existing reasoning more clearly than correction in the examined
settings, leaving reward–distillation hybrids as a separate question.

Feedback-conditioned multi-turn RL can also improve first-turn
performance without a distillation objective. Multi-turn GRPO does not
reproduce the first-turn gain of RLTF-SD on Knights and Knaves, but
already produces a large gain on Binary Matrix (Song et al., 2026). The
additional benefit of distilling feedback-conditioned responses
therefore depends on the task and training arrangement (Section 5).
Access to feedback during interaction alone does not isolate this
benefit.

Some sampled-token gains persist when teacher-dependent credit is
removed. In one study, the sign of the teacher-derived advantage on
verifiable final-answer tokens often disagrees with response
correctness, yet trajectory subsets separated by this agreement achieve
similar accuracy (Ding and Zhang, 2026). Supervising only the
lowest-probability fifth of student tokens reproduces the tested OPD
result. With that same mask, a fixed negative coefficient performs
comparably to teacher advantages, whereas a fixed positive coefficient
collapses training. An entropy-modulated teacher-free variant improves
further while retaining on-policy sampling. Suppression of
low-probability tokens sampled by the student can therefore explain the
gain in this implementation. The result concerns a particular
sampled-token loss; it does not establish equivalence for
full-distribution matching, other estimators, or substantially larger
students.

The practical consequence is that a successful recipe need not support
every intended form of transfer. Compaction of successful reasoning does
not imply error repair, just as reproducing one loss without teacher
credit does not make teacher information dispensable elsewhere. Table 4

### 9.3 Failure Modes and Preservation Targets

The mechanism studies above limit what can be inferred from an accuracy
gain. Preservation raises the complementary question of what can be
lost: alternative successful paths, usable output behavior, or
properties that routine task scores do not measure.

#### 9.3.1 Distributional concentration and solution coverage

Finite-budget head–tail analyses qualify simple mode-covering and
mode-seeking descriptions (Wu et al., 2025). In matched mathematical
comparisons, reverse KL improves Avg@k over forward KL by $`2.45`$
points on average, yet the 4,096-token setting also exhibits low
predictive entropy and severe response-length inflation (Zhao et al.,
2026b). Conversely, the failing runs with self-distillation as the sole
objective discussed above flatten the student distribution (Harne et
al., 2026). Degradation can therefore accompany opposite changes in
entropy.

Controlled entropy comparisons find a stronger effect of divergence
choice than of teacher- versus student-prefix sampling, with teacher
refresh and tail treatment also affecting the outcome (Zucchet and
Linderman, 2026). A corresponding forward-KL analysis relates average
student and teacher entropy at a stationary solution when the student
family is closed under logit scaling and both entropies are measured on
the same contexts. This is not a tokenwise or training-step guarantee,
and reverse KL can also increase entropy in some regimes. Entropy
describes distributional concentration; its direction alone does not
establish whether task ability is preserved.

Target temperature can change the relative performance of divergences.
Softening teacher and student distributions reverses the FKL–RKL ranking
in one study of fixed-response instruction distillation (Luong and Chen,
2026). This reversal is observed in _off-policy training on fixed
teacher-generated responses_; its extension to OPD remains open.
Temperature changes teacher targets for FKL and both the scale and
student-probability weighting of the RKL gradient. With fixed centered
logits, their high-temperature gradients share a leading logit-matching
term, although their finite-temperature dynamics can differ.

Success on one attempt and coverage across repeated attempts can move
differently. A sequence-level analysis shows how conditioning on correct
demonstrations can amplify differences between equally correct
paths (Nicolicioiu et al., 2026). Its optimum reweights paths by the
average change in their log probability under demonstration
conditioning. This result assumes a frozen base-policy teacher and a
fixed distribution of correct demonstrations, and averages divergences
to conditioned targets rather than matching their arithmetic mixture.

Empirically, in the same study, competitive pass@1 coexists with lower
pass@16 than GRPO, and multiple correct demonstrations do not fully
restore solution coverage (Nicolicioiu et al., 2026). Separate graph
experiments likewise find that diverse external demonstrations do not
recover semantic diversity. These observations are consistent with
concentration reducing coverage, without establishing that the
frozen-teacher mechanism fully explains the training results.
Likelihood-based precision–coverage measurements in simulations and
offline pretraining quantify yet another property and cannot be equated
with correctness-based pass@$`k`$ (Cha and Cho, 2025).

Coverage loss is not universal across OPD settings. A test-time scaling
study finds that pass@$`k`$ gains can persist or grow at larger sampling
budgets in some comparisons and reverse in others (Ge et al., 2026).
Increasing the budget changes how per-problem success probabilities
contribute to aggregate pass@$`k`$, so mixed improvements and
regressions can change the method ranking. A local full-vocabulary
reverse-KL analysis explains how such reversals are possible; it does
not predict the finite-step training dynamics of every implementation.
Evaluating multiple sampling budgets therefore reveals improvements and
regressions that a single pass@1 average can conceal.

#### 9.3.2 Reasoning behavior, confidence, and usable outputs

Solution coverage measures which problems can be solved across attempts;
changes in the form of those attempts require separate interpretation.
Analyses of limited marker sets and annotated trajectories associate
reasoning degradation with suppression of uncertainty expressions. The
solution-conditioned teachers examined in these analyses also lower
probabilities of several deliberative tokens (Kim et al., 2026a; Shen et
al., 2026a).

Thinking-collapse interventions make the distinction between a
behavioral marker and task ability concrete (Peng et al., 2026a).
Masking high-entropy positions preserves more of the measured thinking
markers than ordinary self-distillation, yet does not recover downstream
accuracy. AD-OPSD instead mixes the privileged target with a frozen
base-model reference using a local disagreement gate. Its comparisons
support reference mixing in the tested settings, while the additional
benefit of dynamic gating varies with the student. Restoring marker
frequency alone therefore remains an insufficient test of reasoning
preservation.

CaOPD addresses the confidence mismatch between a teacher that sees
privileged information and a deployed student that does not (Zhang et
al., 2026c). It replaces verbal-confidence values in both scored
completions and teacher context with empirical student success rates,
improving reported calibration while retaining accuracy. The method
requires identifiable confidence tokens and sampled student outcomes,
which can reuse existing SDPO rollouts. Its target is confidence that
tracks correctness, a property distinct from the frequency of
uncertainty expressions or predictive entropy.

Usable behavior can depend on small distributional differences that
aggregate matching obscures. Stable-OPD mitigates length instability
through reference anchoring and off-policy supervision, whereas a
termination study identifies a specific failure to transfer probability
between functionally equivalent stop tokens (Luo et al., 2026a; Yang et
al., 2026n). Probability-level alignment repairs stopping where simply
expanding the decoding stop set fails, but does not consistently improve
accuracy. A multi-teacher study finds a related problem for tool use:
top-$`K`$ truncation can omit a decision-critical tool-entry token
despite retaining almost all teacher probability mass (Shen et al.,
2026c). Restoring its supervision reduces erroneous calls at a cost to
required calls. Functional equivalence and decision relevance therefore
matter alongside the amount of probability mass matched.

Privileged-context leakage and format failure can hide beneath aggregate
scores. Leakage occurs when the student refers to information available
only in the teacher’s context, leaving the reader unable to resolve the
reference. Such references increase across full-distribution,
teacher-top-1, and student-top-1 supervision variants as later
validation accuracy deteriorates (Yang et al., 2026b). ErrOPD shows that
extrapolation beyond the teacher can sharply reduce parsing validity
when errors have already transferred or are carried by frequent student
decisions (Li et al., 2026i). Error transfer also depends on training
duration, and ranking metrics restricted to successfully parsed
responses can mask the resulting failures. Quality among usable outputs
can therefore conceal a deterioration in their availability.

Successful internalization need not remain robust when the privileged
context returns. No-Context Anchoring penalizes disagreement between
predictions of a shared model with and without additional privileged
context, using the branch without that context as a stop-gradient
target (Wang et al., 2026p). It reduces context-induced harm in twelve
of fourteen evaluated configurations. The benefit comes with a tradeoff
when internalization is incomplete and the reintroduced context still
supplies useful information.

Even faithful matching can optimize an imperfect proxy for the task. At
fixed prefixes, reverse-KL minimization maximizes teacher log
probability plus student entropy; repetitive or overly long outputs can
become more likely while that objective improves (Cui et al., 2026). A
separate study observes improving teacher-proxy scores alongside
worsening alignment with an independent oracle under repeated offline
reuse (Tiapkin et al., 2025). Refreshing responses from either teacher
or student mitigates the latter effect, supporting data renewal more
directly than a student-only sampling explanation. These failures
distinguish poor optimization of a useful target from successful
optimization of a target that does not fully capture desired behavior.

#### 9.3.3 Hidden behavior and training-data exposure

Teacher matching can also transfer behaviors that ordinary safety
evaluations miss. With a deliberately backdoored teacher and
trigger-bearing distillation prompts, an initially clean student learns
trigger-dependent harmful behavior while improving refusal on
trigger-free harmful prompts (Luo et al., 2026c). The evaluated attack
combines a compromised teacher with a poisoned prompt distribution; it
is not evidence of spontaneous backdoor formation under clean
supervision. Log-ratio clipping delays transfer in some tested Qwen2.5
settings but does not reliably prevent it as training continues.

Training membership presents a different privacy question: can a change
in teacher agreement reveal which prompts participated in learning?
Teacher Alignment Gain measures the reduction in teacher discrepancy
between the pre- and post-distillation students on the same contexts
generated by the pre-distillation student (Yang et al., 2026k). Its
membership-inference comparisons find information beyond the amount of
student-distribution drift alone. This privacy exposure is demonstrated
under access to both students’ full next-token distributions, a teacher
or compatible surrogate, and labeled member and nonmember references
from the same training run. It therefore concerns a richer access
setting than querying the deployed student’s responses alone.

#### 9.3.4 What parameter changes reveal about preservation

Parameter geometry offers a way to ask whether successful adaptation
requires widespread change. Its usefulness for training depends on when
the relevant structure becomes available. Rescaling intermediate module
updates to their eventual norms recovers much of the final performance
in the study introducing EffOPD, but requires future information (Cai et
al., 2026b). EffOPD turns this diagnostic into a usable intervention by
extrapolating recent parameter displacements and validating the result,
improving the reported accuracy–time tradeoff with validation overhead
included. The distinction is between explaining a completed trajectory
and exploiting its structure while learning is still underway.

The same distinction applies to low-dimensional and sparse updates.
Projecting gradients onto a small set of directions estimated during
training preserves much of the measured OPD performance, whereas
effective sparse retraining can use parameter coordinates selected after
a full training run (Shen et al., 2026d; Yu et al., 2026b). The former
estimates a useful subspace online; the latter reproduces adaptation
using a retrospectively identified mask. These structures are also
mathematically distinct: concentrated update magnitude can coexist with
full numerical rank, and controls retaining similar rank dynamics do not
identify on-policy sampling as their cause. Both lines of work clarify
how cheaply adaptation might be represented, but compact parameter
changes alone do not establish which prior capabilities survive.

### 9.4 Generalization and Subsequent Learning

The value of learning on student states depends on whether useful
guidance remains available beyond familiar conditions. Theory clarifies
one part of this dependence: interactive teacher access can improve
sample complexity under structured noise. For a finite policy class
containing the clean expert and a known corruption process, offline
imitation can require exponentially many samples in the decision
horizon, whereas interactive access permits polynomial
dependence (Sriraman et al., 2026). Horizon-independent bounds
additionally restrict corruption over actions supported by the expert
and candidate policies. With unknown corruption, NAILGUN requires a
deterministic expert, bounded per-action corruption, and a margin
separating expert behavior from noise. Interactive access is therefore
valuable under conditions that make the clean behavior identifiable;
standard OPD still fails at high noise in the reported experiments.

Synthetic planning comparisons examine the favorable case of learning
under oracle supervision: the teacher’s described training source
includes evaluation instantiations (Men et al., 2026). They illuminate
how a student can learn when guidance is available, while leaving its
availability in unseen environments unresolved. Equal-compute advantages
and transfer to natural environments likewise require evidence beyond
this oracle setting.

Even when guidance improves the training task, the resulting policy may
transfer poorly to other domains. A vision-language OPD baseline
improves its training modality slightly while losing video and pooled
language–video performance (Lee et al., 2026). Differing correctness
filtering prevents attributing the loss solely to prefix source, but the
recipe exposes a retention cost concealed by the training-modality gain.
Validation can also favor the wrong transfer behavior: in another
vision-language study, method rankings reverse their relationship to
cross-domain performance when the validation slice changes (Fu, 2026).
Although the cause is not isolated, a convenient within-domain score can
select a different recipe from the one that transfers best.

Capability integration and subsequent RL reveal further differences
concealed by intermediate averages. Under matched expert pools, small
average differences among fusion methods coexist with substantial
benchmark-specific differences (Wu et al., 2026b), as discussed in
Section 6.2. The study in Section 5.3 similarly observes little
immediate OPD improvement but a larger advantage after shared downstream
RL, alongside a change in the preferred divergence (Dong et al., 2026b).
A method’s immediate score can therefore differ from its value as
preparation for later learning. These outcomes concern retained
capability and trainability, while the full training costs remain a
separate comparison (Section 8.6).

Teacher selection inherits the same distinction. NPD compares two
teachers reached along one GRPO trajectory: the nearer teacher gives a
lower immediate validation score but a higher average across eight
benchmarks after common downstream RL (Qin et al., 2026a). Another study
finds that teacher stages along instruction-following RL trade off
instruction transfer against mathematical retention, with the tested
converged teacher failing to preserve mathematics (Wei et al., 2026).
These results motivate selecting teachers for the student’s intended
continuation and retained abilities, rather than teacher accuracy alone.
Different distillation durations and single-run comparisons leave the
optimal teacher stage unresolved; neither study isolates teacher
distance as a universal cause of later learning success.

## 10 Applications and Practical Guidance

OPD serves several deployment goals, from transferring capability to a
smaller model and consolidating specialists to changing a model’s
generation process or numerical precision. Each places a different
demand on supervision. Compression emphasizes capability retention,
consolidation balances specialist strengths, and model conversion
exposes new states or constraints that the teacher must address.

### 10.1 Capability Transfer

Capability transfer first requires deciding whether teacher-generated
examples suffice or supervision must follow the student’s own behavior.
Large-model pipelines illustrate both choices. DeepSeek-R1 obtains
strong smaller models through curated teacher traces, while Qwen3
follows supervised initialization with teacher logit supervision on
student-generated responses (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Yang et al.,
2025). The practical distinction is between supplying useful examples
and correcting behavior encountered during student generation. Qwen3’s
favorable comparison with direct RL concerns the student stage and
excludes teacher preparation.

Consolidation adds the requirement to preserve several useful behaviors
in one policy. MOPD and Open-MOPD address allocation and optimization
imbalances that can leave specialist skills underrepresented (Ma et al.,
2026a; Gao et al., 2026). ORBIT applies a related idea to reasoning
budgets, fusing experts trained under different context limits into
budget-conditioned modes (Liang et al., 2026). Its shortest mode loses
substantial accuracy relative to longer modes, while longer modes
provide different accuracy–length choices that prompt steering alone
does not reproduce. Domain consolidation seeks complementary skills;
budget consolidation seeks controllable operating points. Both require
evaluating the retained choices individually, with expert preparation
included in the training cost.

Acquisition must also be weighed against retention. MixSD varies the
mixture of fact-conditioned and original-input targets, exposing cases
where preserving existing capability costs target-task performance (Liu
et al., 2026b). Its fixed-base target generation provides an alternative
to repeated token-level matching on current-student rollouts. Safety
adaptation poses a similar tension: privileged safety and helpfulness
guidance improves composite scores in OPSA, but adaptive-attack results
remain mixed (Fu et al., 2026a). Robustness to forced unsafe prefixes
improves against the examined comparator while iterative attacks expose
regressions in several models. Distillation can therefore transfer a
desired behavior without uniformly preserving adjacent capabilities or
robustness.

### 10.2 Deployment and Model Conversion

Acceleration depends first on who is responsible for the final
prediction. With a verifier at deployment, a draft model aims to propose
tokens the larger target will accept. DistillSpec aligns the draft
distribution for this purpose; AdaFlash continues adapting a diffusion
drafter from verification feedback during serving (Zhou et al., 2024;
Qian et al., 2026). Acceptance alone does not determine speed: draft
generation, verification, scheduling, and adaptation all consume
resources. AdaFlash uses additional training GPUs, and its speedup can
fall below one at high concurrency in an evaluated configuration.

Without a verifier, the converted student’s predictions must stand on
their own. MTP-SD trains a standalone multi-token predictor on
student-generated spans (Kirchenbauer et al., 2026). Its model-dependent
accuracy loss and smaller acceleration gains on open-ended generation
tasks expose a different tradeoff from draft acceptance. Effective
progress above three tokens per step in selected settings is distinct
from the serving-throughput gains measured separately in SGLang. The
deployment contract thus changes both the supervision target and the
meaning of acceleration.

Changing the generation process also changes the states requiring
supervision. A diffusion student refines partly specified sequences
rather than extending left-to-right prefixes. TABOM supplies local
denoising-order supervision on reused base-model trajectories, while
autoregressive-to-diffusion distillation follows the diffusion student’s
own denoising trajectories (Chen et al., 2026b; Su et al., 2026). The
first addresses ordering within a local objective; the second aligns
guidance with the converted student’s evolving states. Local gains do
not establish equivalence to global distribution matching, and
conversion-stage token counts alone do not establish total-compute
savings.

Architectural and numerical conversion introduce a further requirement:
the student must remain viable enough to learn from the teacher. In
SpikeOPD, ordinary full-vocabulary forward-KL OPD degenerates into
repetition, motivating teacher correction, reference anchoring, and
firing-rate controls (Lu et al., 2026a). Quantization-aware OPD instead
supervises behavior generated through the low-bit forward path,
combining teacher feedback with verifier optimization to recover
accuracy (Chen et al., 2026h). Both seek stable guidance after a change
in architecture or precision. SpikeOPD addresses student-prefix mismatch
and drift in spiking dynamics, with small-scale ablations supporting its
three components; the low-bit results build on a viable
quantization-aware-distillation initialization. Analytical energy
estimates for spiking models and accuracy recovery for quantized models
also leave measured hardware speedup as a separate question.

### 10.3 Structured and Multimodal Use

When the output is a spatial prediction, action, or formal artifact, the
supervised object may differ from the deployed decision. A language
judgment can guide a visual prediction, a hidden representation can
support an answer, and an action annotation can guide interaction.
Transfer depends both on how supervision reaches that decision and on
which aspects of success the feedback covers.

For visual tasks, a language-based teacher must supply guidance that
changes the modality-specific decision. Selective text-teacher
supervision contributes to visual reasoning in VOLD, but its additional
benefit depends on the preparation data used alongside reward
updates (Bousselham et al., 2025). MOPDA makes the connection more
explicit by using a distilled language judgment to contextualize visual
anchors for industrial anomaly localization; its EMA teacher receives
verified defect analysis (Chen et al., 2026c). Disrupting that
contextualization or substituting incorrect judgments weakens
localization, showing how the intermediate judgment contributes to the
spatial decision. The gain concerns localization and does not establish
superiority on every image-level metric.

Intermediate language is not always necessary. After recurrent latent
computation, Lapras aligns the student’s hidden states used to predict
the first answer token with those of a teacher conditioned on verbal
reasoning (Chen et al., 2026i). This supports time-series question
answering without intermediate token generation. Removing distillation
while retaining latent computation lowers accuracy in the tested
configuration, although the smallest backbone remains below direct
answering in average F1. MOPDA and Lapras thus expose different
interfaces for transferring reasoning: a language judgment that affects
a spatial decision, or a latent representation that supports the answer
directly. The useful interface depends on the task and the student’s
capacity.

In graphical user interface (GUI) tasks, privileged visual hints or
human-verified action annotations provide guidance on student-generated
coordinate or action predictions (Zhang et al., 2026j; Wu et al.,
2026e). The challenge is to connect this local supervision to successful
interaction, especially when several actions are valid. LiteGUI matches
each student output to an annotated valid action for teacher
conditioning, then refines the policy with action-matching and
subtask-planning rewards. This accommodates alternatives within the
annotations, but a finite action set can still penalize valid choices
outside it. Agreement with an annotated action can therefore diverge
from successful task completion.

Structured-output transfer depends on _what the verifier can check_.
RWOPD weights supervision for generated SystemVerilog Assertions using a
property-equivalence checker (Zou et al., 2026). The checker abstains on
liveness properties, which concern whether an event eventually occurs;
gains there consequently fall outside direct verifier-weighted
supervision. Preparation and filtering also contribute to the recipe. A
formal verifier can strengthen supervision within its coverage without
certifying the entire task.

These cases distinguish a useful supervision interface from complete
task feedback. Language judgments and latent representations can
influence a downstream decision without reproducing the teacher’s
intermediate reasoning. Action annotations and formal verification can
constrain that decision, but only within the choices or properties they
cover. Successful transfer therefore requires both a path from
supervision to behavior and an account of what remains outside the
feedback’s reach.

### 10.4 Matching OPD to Deployment Needs

The useful OPD design depends on which supervision is available and
which behavior deployment requires. Teacher–student size ratios alone do
not determine that choice. Table 5 relates deployment needs to the
conditions under which the preceding methods apply and the alternatives
that clarify their value.

Teacher access is an initial constraint. Matching explicit teacher
probabilities requires scores for comparable events; text-only access
instead supports continuations, critique-conditioned targets, or
response-level rewards, with different information and cost
(Section 3.1). A verified outcome can guide selection or reward
refinement, but it cannot supply missing token probabilities.
Conversely, teacher probabilities can support matching where no verifier
exists, without establishing that agreement improves task success.
Interface alignment and the coverage of task feedback therefore
determine which combination is meaningful.

The dominant cost determines which efficiency choices are relevant.
Expensive generation favors horizon control when omitted states do not
require direct supervision. Expensive teacher scoring favors caching or
batch reuse when the acquired targets remain useful, subject to the
freshness tradeoffs in Section 8.3. A memory bottleneck may favor sparse
differentiation even without faster updates. Each choice must address
the limiting resource under the full cost accounting in Section 8.6.

The intended learning sequence also changes the choice of objective. For
immediate deployment, a matching objective should support the required
behavior, including solution coverage, reliable termination, and
retained capabilities. For a student that will undergo further RL, its
value depends on performance after that continuation. The
forward-/reverse-KL reversal in Section 5.3 makes immediate accuracy an
insufficient basis for choosing between them. Available supervision
determines which updates are possible, resource constraints determine
which are practical, and the intended use determines which outcomes
matter.

## 11 Open Challenges and Future Directions

The central unresolved problem is how to adapt supervision to a changing
student. Useful guidance depends on what the student has already
learned, which states it will encounter next, and which capabilities
must survive later training. This connects the prediction of learning
benefit to compute allocation, persistent adaptation, and transfer
across model interfaces.

### 11.1 Downstream Value of Supervision

Student uncertainty, teacher confidence, and teacher–student
disagreement describe current predictions, whereas the value of
supervision depends on how an update changes later behavior.
Token-importance selection and prompt-level reliability gating address
parts of this gap through distribution scores and teacher-rollout
probes (Xu et al., 2026c; Zhang et al., 2026m). Yet privileged
information can yield unreliable token credit, and extra context can
change behavior without supplying the current problem’s solution (Nguyen
et al., 2026b; Ichihara et al., 2026). A predictor of learning benefit
must therefore account for how information enters the update, rather
than treating confidence or access to an answer as sufficient evidence
of value.

More direct evidence of benefit is available, but its predictive reach
and cost remain in tension. Meta-learned weighting measures the effect
of a one-step update on reference solutions at substantial training cost
(Section 8.2). FTB probes behavior after a teacher intervention through
a teacher-preference proxy on paired student continuations (Chen et al.,
2026a). It limits probing to one intervention per trajectory and short
continuations, with faster training steps than vanilla OPD in the
reported ALFWorld comparison. The contrast is between measuring a local
learning effect and testing an immediate behavioral consequence. Neither
yet provides an inexpensive predictor of longer-term benefit across
students and training stages. Extending either approach must improve
that prediction enough to justify computation that could otherwise be
spent on learning.

The unresolved step is to connect affordable local evidence to the
changing states reached through learning. An update may help at the
current prefix while moving the student toward histories where the same
teacher becomes less useful. Predicting this effect requires separating
the contributions of context and update design identified in
Section 9.2, while extending beyond a single update or continuation.
This would make selection responsive to what guidance enables the
student to learn next.

### 11.2 Joint Allocation of Prompts, Rollouts, Teacher Queries, and Updates

Fresh experience and repeated learning from existing states compete for
the same training budget. The scaling relationships in Section 2.4
connect teacher quality and scoring cost to the resources available for
student training. OPD couples these tradeoffs to prompt acquisition,
student generation, refresh, and potentially environment interaction.
The unresolved choice is which use of computation produces the greatest
learning benefit at the student’s current stage.

The difficulty is that the return from one resource depends on how the
others are used. The value of additional prompts can depend on whether
trajectories are renewed; few-prompt and data-free results likewise show
why prompt count alone does not measure the training experience (Hu et
al., 2026b; Fu et al., 2026c; Li et al., 2026c). Shorter rollouts and
cached targets save computation by giving up different forms of coverage
or freshness (Zhang et al., 2026a; Wu et al., 2026c). In MOPD,
allocating another teacher query also depends on what other specialists
have transferred and how much token-level optimization each domain
receives (Gao et al., 2026). Component savings consequently cannot be
combined without accounting for their effects on the experience and
supervision available elsewhere in training.

An allocation rule would need to capture these interactions as learning
proceeds. Fresh states can broaden experience, another teacher query can
improve guidance on an existing state, and another update can extract
more from guidance already obtained. Their marginal values are not yet
characterized jointly, especially when the budget also includes
preparing several specialist teachers. This leaves a gap between
reported component savings and an adaptive allocation of total
computation.

### 11.3 Lifecycle Control under Persistent Adaptation and Interaction

Over a long learning sequence, supervision can become redundant or need
to return. Demonstration-conditioned self-distillation supplies evidence
for accumulating skills across successive tasks (Shenfeld et al., 2026),
but does not yet explain how to anticipate interference before a
capability deteriorates. The scheduling problem is therefore preventive
as well as corrective: continued teacher access and rehearsal incur a
cost, yet withdrawing them too early may create a later recovery
problem.

The reference for deciding when to intervene can itself change. When a
self-teacher shares the evolving student’s parameters, reward updates
can alter both its competence and the behavior that needs correction. In
interactive settings, changes in tools, memory policies, or world-model
errors also change the states on which this guidance is used. Adaptive
horizons already connect supervision to visited interaction states (Zhou
et al., 2026d); extending this control across repeated episodes requires
relating episode depth and exploration cost to what the student retains
between episodes.

A schedule must also anticipate the next training stage. Joint and
sequential recipes can produce different outcomes, and subsequent RL can
change the preferred distillation objective (Li et al., 2026a; Dong et
al., 2026b). Preserving current performance is thus only part of the
problem: a policy must remain able to benefit from later reward learning
and interaction. Connecting early signs of interference to later
trainability would help determine when to retain, renew, or withdraw
supervision without merely shifting a capability loss to the next stage.

### 11.4 Behavioral Equivalence across Tokenizers, Representations, Modalities, and Architectures

Different interfaces create two related obstacles to transfer: models
may express predictions in different units, or make those predictions
from different information and decision states. Tokenizer mappings
address the first by aligning textual units (Wang et al., 2026d).
Cross-lingual safety transfer also faces the second when the teacher
receives translated input and additional instructions unavailable to the
student (Qin et al., 2026b). An aligned output space does not remove
this information gap; the student must learn behavior that remains
supported by its own input.

Changes in generation architecture make state alignment equally
consequential. In diffusion language models, dOPSD supervises
predictions at partially masked states using later states of the same
trajectory, whereas trace-based TOPD matches intermediate decisions
retained in the final response (Dat et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2026a).
The token position alone does not identify the prediction problem,
because the visible context changes during denoising. These
constructions raise the broader question of when guidance across states
remains valid and when teacher and student must be compared at the same
decision state.

Ultimately, the mapping must preserve distinctions that matter for
behavior. Internal alignment can improve without improving task
performance (Yang et al., 2026d), and token or feature agreement may
omit differences needed for actions, solution diversity, or safety.
Characterizing which distinctions survive a representation change would
clarify when distribution matching is an adequate target and when
supervision must operate more directly on actions or outcomes.

## 12 Conclusion

On-policy distillation connects teacher guidance to the experience
generated by a learning student. This survey has organized that
connection around states, target information, and policy updates,
providing a common basis for understanding methods across different
supervisors and training settings. The value of guidance depends jointly
on what it conveys at student histories, how the chosen update uses it,
and which states the resulting policy reaches. Choices about supervision
and computation consequently shape the learning process together.
Neither teacher strength nor a divergence considered in isolation
explains when OPD will be effective.

This perspective also changes what counts as successful transfer.
Improved agreement or immediate accuracy may reflect different
mechanisms and can coexist with lost solution coverage, weaker retained
capabilities, or reduced benefit from later training. The value of OPD
therefore lies in the behavior it makes available over the intended
learning and deployment sequence, relative to the computation required.
The challenge ahead is to adapt guidance as that sequence unfolds: to
recognize when existing supervision remains useful, when new experience
or a different supervisor is needed, and when continued imitation
constrains further learning. Resolving this challenge would make OPD a
more dependable means of transferring capabilities while preserving the
student’s capacity to develop beyond its current supervision.

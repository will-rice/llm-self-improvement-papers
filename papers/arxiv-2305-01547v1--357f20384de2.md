# Accelerating Neural Self-Improvement via Bootstrapping

Kazuki Irie   Jürgen Schmidhuber Email: [{kazuki,
juergen}@idsia.ch](mailto:) Affiliation: The Swiss AI Lab, IDSIA, USI &
SUPSI, Lugano, Switzerland Affiliation: AI Initiative, KAUST, Thuwal,
Saudi Arabia

###### Abstract

Few-shot learning with sequence-processing neural networks (NNs) has
recently attracted a new wave of attention in the context of large
language models. In the standard $`N`$-way $`K`$-shot learning setting,
an NN is explicitly optimised to learn to classify unlabelled inputs by
observing a sequence of $`NK`$ labelled examples. This pressures the NN
to learn a learning algorithm that achieves optimal performance, given
the limited number of training examples. Here we study an auxiliary loss
that encourages further acceleration of few-shot learning, by applying
recently proposed bootstrapped meta-learning to NN few-shot learners: we
optimise the $`K`$-shot learner to match its own performance achievable
by observing more than $`NK`$ examples, using only $`NK`$ examples.
Promising results are obtained on the standard Mini-ImageNet dataset.
Our code is public.¹¹ 1
[https://github.com/IDSIA/modern-srwm](https://github.com/IDSIA/modern-srwm)

## 1 Introduction & Motivation

Since the seminal works by [Hochreiter et al. (2001)](#bib.bib14);
[Younger et al. (1999)](#bib.bib42); [Cotter & Conwell
(1991)](#bib.bib9); [Cotter & Conwell (1990)](#bib.bib8), many sequence
processing neural networks (NNs)—including recurrent NNs (RNNs) e.g., in
[Santoro et al. (2016)](#bib.bib28) but also Transformer variants
([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib37)) as in [Mishra et al.
(2018)](#bib.bib21)—have been trained as a meta-learner ([Schmidhuber,
1987](#bib.bib30); [Schmidhuber, 1992](#bib.bib34)) that learns to learn
to solve a task by observing sequences of training examples (i.e., pairs
of inputs and their labels). A popular application of such an approach
is to build a sample efficient few-shot learner capable of adaption to a
new (but related) task from few training examples or demonstrations
([Finn et al., 2017](#bib.bib11)), which typically achieves respectable
performance in the standard benchmarks for few-shot learning (e.g.,
image classification), see e.g., [Mishra et al. (2018)](#bib.bib21);
[Munkhdalai & Yu (2017)](#bib.bib22). More recently, such “on-the-fly”
learning capability of sequence processing NNs has attracted broader
interests in the context of large language models (LLMs; [Radford et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib24)). In fact, the task of language modelling itself has
a form of sequence processing with error feedback (essential for
meta-learning ([Schmidhuber, 1990](#bib.bib31))): the correct label to
be predicted is fed to the model with a delay of one time step in an
auto-regressive manner. Trained on a large amount of text covering a
wide variety of credit assignment paths, LLMs exhibit certain sequential
few-shot learning capabilities in practice ([Brown et al.,
2020](#bib.bib4)). This was rebranded as in-context learning, and has
been the subject of numerous recent studies (e.g., [Xie et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib40); [Chan et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib6); [Chan et al.
(2022a)](#bib.bib5); [Kirsch et al. (2022)](#bib.bib19); [Akyürek et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib2); [von Oswald et al. (2022)](#bib.bib39); [Dai et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib10)).

Here our focus is the standard sequential few-shot classification. Let
$`d`$, $`N`$, $`K`$, $`L`$ be positive integers. In sequential $`N`$-way
$`K`$-shot classification settings, a sequence processing NN with a
parameter set $`\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L}`$ observes a pair
($`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$, $`y_{t}`$) where $`{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ is
the input and $`y_{t}\in\{1,...,N\}`$ is its label at each step
$`t\in\{1,...,N\cdot K\}`$, corresponding to $`K`$ examples for each one
of $`N`$ classes. After the presentation of these $`N\cdot K`$ examples
(often called the support set), one extra input
$`{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$ (often called the query) is fed to the
model without its label—in practice, an unknown label token
$`\varnothing`$ is fed instead, e.g., $`\varnothing=0`$—and the task of
the model is to predict its label. During training, the parameters of
the model $`\theta`$ are optimised to maximise the probability
$`p(y|({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),...,({\bm{x}}_{N\cdot K},y_{N\cdot K}),({\bm{x}},\varnothing);\theta)`$
of the correct label $`y\in\{1,...,N\}`$ of the input query
$`{\bm{x}}`$. Since class-to-label associations are randomised and
unique to each sequence
($`({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),...,({\bm{x}}_{N\cdot K},y_{N\cdot K}),({\bm{x}},\varnothing)`$),
each such a sequence can be used as a new (few-shot) learning experience
to train the model.

While $`K`$ is a pre-defined constant in the setting above, ideally, we
want to obtain a learning algorithm (encoded by the NN with parameters
$`\theta`$) that achieves the “best possible performance” with the
minimum number of few-shot learning examples i.e., here $`N\cdot K`$. We
disregard mathematical rigour for now, in favour of an intuitive
description that illustrates the motivation of our work
(Sec. [2](#S2 "2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping")).
We informally assume that there exists a convenient error function
$`\mathcal{L}:(\mathbb{R}^{L},\mathbb{N})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ that,
given a number of learning steps $`t\in\mathbb{N}`$, evaluates the
performance $`\mathcal{L}(\theta,t)`$ (e.g., the lower the better) of
$`\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{L}`$ as a learning algorithm on a certain task
which we assume to be fixed. We also assume a certain performance value
$`\mathcal{L}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}`$ which we consider as “optimal” for this
task. We further assume that there exists another convenient function
$`\tau:(\mathbb{R}^{L},\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{N}`$ that tells us
the number of learning steps $`\tau(\theta,\mathcal{L}^{*})`$ required
by the learning algorithm parameterised by $`\theta`$ to achieve the
performance $`\mathcal{L}^{*}`$. Then, the learning algorithm we want to
find is the solution of:

|     |                                                                                         |     |     |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathop{\text{}}{minimise}\limits_{\theta}\tau(\theta,\mathcal{L}^{*})`$ |     | (1) |

In general, we do not have access to such a function $`\tau`$. But if we
assume that, given $`\theta`$, performance is monotonic w.r.t. the
number of learning steps, i.e., for $`t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{N}`$ such
that $`t_{1}<t_{2}`$, we have
$`\mathcal{L}(\theta,t_{2})\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta,t_{1})`$, i.e.,
$`\mathcal{L}(\theta,t_{2})`$ is better than
$`\mathcal{L}(\theta,t_{1})`$, and if we further assume that we have
access to the “models” $`{\bm{W}}^{\theta}_{t_{1}}`$ and
$`{\bm{W}}^{\theta}_{t_{2}}`$ trained on the fly by the learning
algorithm $`\theta`$ at the corresponding steps $`t_{1}`$ and $`t_{2}`$,
there are several ways of explicitly optimising $`\theta`$ such that the
performance of $`{\bm{W}}^{\theta}_{t_{1}}`$ “matches” that of
$`{\bm{W}}^{\theta}_{t_{2}}`$, e.g., via knowledge distillation. Such an
auxiliary task encourages $`\theta`$ to achieve performance
$`\mathcal{L}(\theta,t_{2})`$ within the number of steps $`t_{1}`$ which
is less than $`t_{2}`$ required by the current $`\theta`$.

This idea directly relates to that of bootstrapped meta-learning by
[Flennerhag et al. (2022)](#bib.bib12) but applied to few-shot learning
with sequence processing NNs. Here we explore this approach in
self-modifying NNs based on a modern variant ([Irie et al.,
2022b](#bib.bib17)) of self-referential weight matrices ([Schmidhuber,
1992](#bib.bib34); [Schmidhuber, 1993](#bib.bib35)) that has several
appealing properties including its close connection to Transformers
(Sec. [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Background: Linear Transformers and Self-Referential Weight Matrices ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping")).

## 2 Method

The main idea of this work is to apply bootstrapped training
([Flennerhag et al., 2022](#bib.bib12)) to improve few-shot learning
with sequence processing NNs. In what follows, we specify the details of
our sequence processor
(Sec. [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Background: Linear Transformers and Self-Referential Weight Matrices ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping")),
and describe the bootstrapped training objective
(Sec. [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Bootstrapped Training ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping")).

### 2.1 Background: Linear Transformers and Self-Referential Weight Matrices

Bootstrapped training
(Sec. [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Bootstrapped Training ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping"))
can be applied to any sequence-processing NNs trained for few-shot
learning. An application to NNs with a self-modifying weight matrix (WM)
yields a particularly intuitive setting (illustrated in Figure
[1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.1 Background: Linear Transformers and Self-Referential Weight Matrices ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping");
commented on later). Here we use the modern self-referential weight
matrix (SRWM; [Irie et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib17)) as a generic
self-modifying WM. An SRWM is a WM that sequentially modifies itself as
a response to a stream of input observations ([Schmidhuber,
1992](#bib.bib34); [Schmidhuber, 1993](#bib.bib35)). The modern SRWM
belongs to the family of linear Transformers a.k.a. Fast Weight
Programmers (FWPs; [Schmidhuber (1991b)](#bib.bib33); [Katharopoulos
et al. (2020)](#bib.bib18); [Choromanski et al. (2021)](#bib.bib7);
[Peng et al. (2021)](#bib.bib23); [Schlag et al. (2021)](#bib.bib29);
[Irie et al. (2021)](#bib.bib15)). Linear Transformers and FWPs are an
important class of the now popular Transformers ([Vaswani et al.,
2017](#bib.bib37)): unlike the standard Transformers whose state size
linearly grows with the context length, the state size of FWPs is
constant w.r.t. sequence length (like in the standard RNNs). This is an
attractive property, since several open problems with Transformers
relate to its explicitly limited context window size (see e.g., [Laskin
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib20); [Reed et al. (2022)](#bib.bib26)). Such a
property is important also because potential extensions of the current
in-context learning setting to further in-context continual learning
etc. would require handling context lengths much larger than those
supported by today’s Transformers. Moreover, the duality between linear
attention and FWPs ([Schlag et al., 2021](#bib.bib29))—and likewise,
between linear attention and linear layers trained by the gradient
descent learning algorithm ([Irie et al., 2022a](#bib.bib16); [Aizerman
et al., 1964](#bib.bib1))—have played a key role in certain theoretical
analyses of few-shot learning capabilities of Transformers ([von Oswald
et al., 2022](#bib.bib39); [Dai et al., 2022](#bib.bib10)).

The dynamics of an SRWM ([Irie et al., 2022b](#bib.bib17)) are as
follows. Let $`d_{\text{in}}`$, $`d_{\text{out}}`$, $`t`$ be positive
integers, and $`\otimes`$ denote outer product. At each time step $`t`$,
an SRWM
$`{\bm{W}}_{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{\text{out}}+2*d_{\text{in}}+1)\times d_{\text{in}}}`$
observes an input $`{\bm{x}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}`$, and
outputs $`{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}}`$, while also
updating itself to $`{\bm{W}}_{t}`$ as follows:

|     |                                                                   |                                                                                                              |     |     |
| --- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{t},{\bm{k}}_{t},{\bm{q}}_{t},\beta_{t}`$ | $`\displaystyle={\bm{W}}_{t-1}{\bm{x}}_{t}`$                                                                 |     | (2) |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\bm{v}}_{t}={\bm{W}}_{t-1}\phi({\bm{q}}_{t})`$    | $`\displaystyle;\,\bar{{\bm{v}}}_{t}={\bm{W}}_{t-1}\phi({\bm{k}}_{t})`$                                      |     | (3) |
|     | $`\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{t}`$                                     | $`\displaystyle={\bm{W}}_{t-1}+\sigma(\beta_{t})({\bm{v}}_{t}-\bar{{\bm{v}}}_{t})\otimes\phi({\bm{k}}_{t})`$ |     | (4) |

where
$`{\bm{v}}_{t},\bar{{\bm{v}}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d_{\text{out}}+2*d_{\text{in}}+1)}`$
are value vectors, $`{\bm{q}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}`$ and
$`{\bm{k}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}`$ are query and key vectors,
and $`\beta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}`$ is the learning rate. $`\sigma`$ and
$`\phi`$ denote sigmoid and softmax functions respectively. $`\phi`$ is
typically also applied to $`{\bm{x}}_{t}`$ in
Eq. [2](#S2.E2 "In 2.1 Background: Linear Transformers and Self-Referential Weight Matrices ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping"),
but not in the experiments presented here (following [Irie et al.
(2022b)](#bib.bib17)’s few-shot image classification setting).
$`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$ is the only trainable parameters of this layer, that
encodes the initial self-modification algorithm. In practice, we use the
layer above as a replacement to the self-attention layer in the
Transformer architecture ([Vaswani et al., 2017](#bib.bib37)) with all
other components such as the two-layer feedforward block, and we also
use the multi-head version of the SRWM computation above.

Figure 1: An illustration of bootstrapped training of a self-modifying
WM $`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$. In $`N`$-way $`K`$-shot learning (in grey),
$`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$ observes a sequence of $`N\cdot K`$ labelled examples,
and yields $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$ which is used to classify an
unlabelled input $`{\bm{x}}`$. In bootstrapped training (in light
green), we feed $`N\cdot K^{\prime}`$ extra inputs to
$`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$ to obtain $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot(K+K^{\prime})}`$.
We evaluate $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot(K+K^{\prime})}`$ using the same test
example $`{\bm{x}}`$, and its output is used as a teaching signal to
train $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$ via knowledge distillation.

### 2.2 Bootstrapped Training

Bootstrapped training of few-shot learning NNs is directly inspired by
[Flennerhag et al. (2022)](#bib.bib12) (and also relates to [Tack et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib36)). The overall process is depicted in Figure
[1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.1 Background: Linear Transformers and Self-Referential Weight Matrices ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping"),
and works as follows.

Let $`N`$, $`K`$, $`K^{\prime}`$, $`d`$ be positive integers. In
$`N`$-way $`K`$-shot classification (see
Sec. [1](#S1 "1 Introduction & Motivation ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping")),
an SRWM (see
Sec. [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Background: Linear Transformers and Self-Referential Weight Matrices ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping"))
$`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$ observes a sequence of $`N\cdot K`$ labelled examples,
and the resulting WM, $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$, is used to classify an
unlabelled query input $`{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$. $`{\bm{W}}_{0}`$
is trained to produce a sequence of self-modifications that result in
$`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$ capable of such classification. Bootstrapped
training augments the setting above by continuing to feed
$`N\cdot K^{\prime}`$ extra labelled inputs to $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$
to obtain $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot(K+K^{\prime})}`$. Then, we feed the same
unlabelled example $`{\bm{x}}`$ to $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot(K+K^{\prime})}`$,
and use its output distribution as a teaching signal to train
$`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$ via knowledge distillation ([Ba & Caruana
(2014)](#bib.bib3); [Hinton et al. (2014)](#bib.bib13); which
[Schmidhuber (1991a)](#bib.bib32) calls collapsing). For this to work,
we assume $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot(K+K^{\prime})}`$ to perform better than
$`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$ thanks to extra learning examples (which is
effectively the case in standard few-shot learning scenarios). By
denoting the output distribution of the model with weights
$`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$ as
$`{\bm{p}}_{N\cdot K}(\cdot|{\bm{x}})={\bm{p}}(\cdot|{\bm{x}};{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K})`$,
the training objective function is:

|     |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |     |     |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\beta_{1}\mathrm{CE}({\bm{q}},{\bm{p}}_{N\cdot K};{\bm{x}})+\beta_{2}\mathrm{CE}(\mathrm{sg}({\bm{p}}_{N\cdot(K+K^{\prime})}),{\bm{p}}_{N\cdot K};{\bm{x}})+\beta_{3}\mathrm{CE}({\bm{q}},{\bm{p}}_{N\cdot(K+K^{\prime})};{\bm{x}})`$ |     | (5) |

where $`\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}`$ are scaling
factors, $`\mathrm{CE}`$ denotes the standard cross-entropy loss:

|     |                                                          |                                                  |                          |              |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------ | ------------ | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\mathrm{CE}({\bm{q}},{\bm{p}};{\bm{x}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=-\sum\_{1\leq y\leq N}{\bm{q}}(y | {\bm{x}})\log({\bm{p}}(y | {\bm{x}}))`$ |     | (6) |

and $`{\bm{q}}(\cdot|{\bm{x}})\in\{0,1\}^{N}`$ denotes the one-hot
vector encoding the correct label of $`{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$. In
Eq. [5](#S2.E5 "In 2.2 Bootstrapped Training ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping"),
the first and third terms correspond to the standard $`N`$-way
$`K`$-shot and $`(K+K^{\prime})`$-shot learning losses respectively,
while the second term is the distillation loss (where $`\mathrm{sg}`$
denotes the “stop-gradient” operation; we want $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot K}`$
to match the performance of $`{\bm{W}}_{N\cdot(K+K^{\prime})}`$, but not
the opposite).

## 3 Experiments

Experimental Setting. We conduct experiments on the standard
Mini-ImageNet dataset ([Vinyals et al., 2016](#bib.bib38); [Ravi &
Larochelle, 2017](#bib.bib25)) for few-shot image classification. All
our experimental setting follows the one of [Irie et al.
(2022b)](#bib.bib17), except that we use a single architecture for all
$`K`$, and we report results with the standard deviation instead of the
commonly used ([Ravi & Larochelle, 2017](#bib.bib25)) 95% confidence
interval (which is below 0.1 in all our cases). All our models have 3
SRWM layers, with 16 heads and a total hidden dimension of 256 in each
layer, and the 2-layer feedforward block has an inner dimension of 2048.
We train them with a batch size of 16 using the warm-up learning rate
scheduling as in [Irie et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib17). We refer to [Irie
et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib17) and our public code for further experimental
details.

Main Results. We conduct experiments on the standard Mini-ImageNet
dataset ([Vinyals et al., 2016](#bib.bib38); [Ravi & Larochelle,
2017](#bib.bib25)) for few-shot image classification. Our base
architecture is similar to the one of [Irie et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib17)
(but unlike these authors we use a single architecture for all $`K`$).
We refer to [Irie et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib17) for all the basic
experimental details. We first evaluate various values of $`K^{\prime}`$
(number of extra “shots” used for bootstrapping) in the 5-way 5-shot and
10-shot settings. Table
[1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2.2 Bootstrapped Training ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping")
shows the results. Here models are tested in the
$`K_{\text{test}}`$-shot setting where $`K_{\text{test}}=K`$ (as in
training). In all cases, we observe improvements by bootstrapped
training over the baselines without any auxiliary losses (slight
improvements in the 5-shot case). Now in Table
[2](#S2.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 2.2 Bootstrapped Training ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping"),
we fix $`K`$ and $`K^{\prime}`$, and show how bootstrapped training
affects the performance of the model evaluated with different numbers of
examples at test time (i.e., for different $`K_{\text{test}}`$). Here,
bootstrapping is disabled in the baselines (“Bootstrap No”) but the
auxiliary $`(K+K^{\prime})`$-shot loss is used
($`\beta_{1}=\beta_{3}=1`$) for fair comparison. We observe that
bootstrapping not only improves $`K_{\textit{test}}`$-shot learning
where $`K_{\textit{test}}=K`$ (i.e., where the auxiliary bootstrapping
loss is applied in training), it also yields a chain of improvements at
any $`K_{\textit{test}}`$, resulting in an overall acceleration of
few-shot learning.

Remarks & Current Limitations. In Table
[2](#S2.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 2.2 Bootstrapped Training ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping"),
we observe that the model trained for $`K=10`$ underperforms the one
trained with $`K=5`$ on 1-shot learning task ($`K_{\text{test}}=1`$)—in
fact, both of them underperform the model specifically tuned for 1-shot
learning ($`\approx 47\%`$ accuracy is reported in [Irie et al.
(2022b)](#bib.bib17)). This trend may change by training the model in
the “delayed label” setting (where correct labels are fed to the model
one step later; see, [Irie et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib17)). Also, we have
not managed to obtain any improvement in the $`1`$-shot learning case
(i.e., the same experiments as in Table
[1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2.2 Bootstrapped Training ‣ 2 Method ‣ Accelerating Neural Self-Improvementvia Bootstrapping")
but with $`K=1`$): we speculate that this case is difficult, since with
$`N=5`$ and $`K=1`$, our SRWM can only generate rank-5 updates.
Optimally tuning the model for 1-shot learning may leave little leeway
for further improvements. Also importantly, so far we have not succeeded
at confirming the improvements on another dataset, tiered-ImageNet ([Ren
et al., 2018](#bib.bib27)): we ran the configuration used for
Mini-ImageNet (without changing any hyper-parameters, including those
for the model architecture), without observing out-of-the-box
improvements. Further empirical investigations are still needed to
determine under which conditions exactly bootstrapped training can help.
In fact, this is an open question for knowledge distillation in general.

## 4 Conclusion

We study bootstrapped training of sequence-processing neural networks
(NNs) for few-shot learning (FSL). Bootstrapped training encourages an
FSL NN to accelerate FSL by letting it chase its own performance
achievable with more training examples, using less examples. Our
preliminary experiments on the standard Mini-ImageNet yield promising
results. Further investigations using other datasets and tasks, e.g,
imitation learning ([Xu et al., 2022](#bib.bib41)), are needed to
confirm these trends.

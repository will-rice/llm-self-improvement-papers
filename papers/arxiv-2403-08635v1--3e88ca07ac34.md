###### Abstract

Ensuring alignment of language models’ outputs with human preferences is
critical to guarantee a useful, safe, and pleasant user experience.
Thus, human alignment has been extensively studied recently and several
methods such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF),
Direct Policy Optimisation (DPO) and Sequence Likelihood Calibration
(SLiC) have emerged. In this paper, our contribution is two-fold. First,
we show the equivalence between two recent alignment methods, namely
Identity Policy Optimisation (IPO) and Nash Mirror Descent (Nash-MD).
Second, we introduce a generalisation of IPO, named IPO-MD, that
leverages the regularised sampling approach proposed by Nash-MD.

This equivalence may seem surprising at first sight, since IPO is an
offline method whereas Nash-MD is an online method using a preference
model. However, this equivalence can be proven when we consider the
online version of IPO, that is when both generations are sampled by the
online policy and annotated by a trained preference model. Optimising
the IPO loss with such a stream of data becomes then equivalent to
finding the Nash equilibrium of the preference model through self-play.
Building on this equivalence, we introduce the IPO-MD algorithm that
generates data with a mixture policy (between the online and reference
policy) similarly as the general Nash-MD algorithm. We compare
online-IPO and IPO-MD to different online versions of existing losses on
preference data such as DPO and SLiC on a summarisation task.

## 1 Introduction

Learning from feedback is a common approach to align the behaviour of
artificial agents with human preferences ([Knox & Stone,
2008](#bib.bib22); [Griffith et al., 2013](#bib.bib16); [Christiano
et al., 2017](#bib.bib9); [Warnell et al., 2018](#bib.bib48)). In recent
years, reinforcement learning from human feedback has become a common
paradigm for fine-tuning large language models ([Glaese et al.,
2022](#bib.bib15); [OpenAI, 2022](#bib.bib28)).

The traditional approach to fine-tuning of large language models from
human preferences is to learn a reward signal under the Bradley-Terry
model ([Bradley & Terry, 1952](#bib.bib7)), and then perform
reinforcement learning (RL) against this learnt reward signal
([Christiano et al., 2017](#bib.bib9)). Recently, [Rafailov et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib32) proposed an alternative model-free approach, direct
policy optimisation (DPO). DPO is mathematically equivalent to the
method above, in the sense that the minimiser of the population loss is
identical ([Azar et al., 2023](#bib.bib3)), yet DPO bypasses the
learning of the reward signal. Both approaches, however, rely on the
Bradley-Terry model.

Recently, two particular approaches to directly optimise against
preference probabilities themselves, rather than a Bradley-Terry-derived
reward function have been proposed. Identity preference optimisation
([Azar et al., 2023](#bib.bib3), IPO) is an algorithm that aims to
optimise preference probabilities against a fixed data distribution, and
does so with an offline contrastive loss, as with DPO. By contrast,
Nash-MD-PG ([Munos et al., 2023](#bib.bib26)) is an algorithm that aims
to find a Nash equilibrium with respect to the preference probabilities,
via online policy gradient updates against a regularised policy. Both
algorithms have appealing properties, though on the face of it are
unrelated: one is an offline contrastive algorithm optimising against a
fixed policy, the other is an online algorithm aiming to find a Nash
equilibrium.

In this work we bridge the gap between IPO and Nash-MD-PG, and use this
theoretical bridge to propose a novel class of preference optimisation
algorithms. Specifically, our principal contributions are: first, we
identify several key factors of variation between IPO and Nash-MD-PG,
including their use of offline/online data, contrastivity of their
losses, and the nature of their equilibria. We use this understanding to
identify the strengths of these algorithms, and combine these strengths
into new preference optimisation algorithms. This allows us to propose
_Online IPO_, an online variant of IPO. In addition, we establish a
theoretical connection between Online IPO and _self-play_ in the
regularised two-player preference game used in deriving Nash-MD-PG.
Second, motivated by Online IPO, we propose a preference optimisation
algorithm, aiming to capture the best aspects of both IPO and
Nash-MD-PG: _IPO-MD_, a version of IPO that interpolates between offline
and online variants by using the lagged data distribution of Nash-MD-PG.
Finally, we provide an experimental suite contrasting these algorithms
in several applications, which provides detailed comparisons between the
proposed methods and several baselines, with notable take-aways for
practitioners.

## 2 Background

We begin by introducing the central preference optimisation problem, and
relevant prior work.

### 2.1 Preference optimisation in bandits

We consider a (non-contextual) bandit problem (rather than a sequential
setting), with finite action space $`\mathcal{Y}`$. This simplifies the
notation considerably, and the ideas presented are straightforwardly
extensible to the contextual/sequential setting where the
actions/generations $`y`$ are conditioned on a state/prompt $`x`$; we
explain this in further detail in the descriptions of implementation
details in the experiments.

Preferences. A _preference function_
$`p:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,1]`$ specifies pairwise
preference probabilities between elements of $`\mathcal{Y}`$.
Specifically, given $`y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}`$,
$`p(y\succ y^{\prime})`$ is the probability that $`y`$ is preferred to
$`y^{\prime}`$. We will assume that preference functions satisfy the
symmetry condition $`p(y^{\prime}\succ y)=1-p(y\succ y^{\prime})`$.

At a high level, the goal of learning in this context is to find a
policy in $`\Delta(\mathcal{Y})`$ (the set of distributions over
$`\mathcal{Y}`$) that tends to select actions $`y\in\mathcal{Y}`$ that
are preferred over others. This is of course not a precise mathematical
objective as stated, and there are several distinct ways in which this
can be formalised, which we explore in greater detail below.

Data model. Actions are sampled from policies
$`\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})`$ as
$`Y\sim\mu,Y^{\prime}\sim\mu^{\prime}`$ (we write
$`Y,Y^{\prime}\sim\mu`$ when $`\mu=\mu^{\prime}`$). Given a preference
function $`p`$, the _preference distribution_ $`\lambda_{p}`$ is defined
for $`y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}`$ as the distribution corresponding to
the following sampling procedure:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\lambda_{p}(y,y^{\prime})\mbox{ yields }\begin{cases}(y,y^{\prime})\mbox{ with probability }p(y\succ y^{\prime})\\
(y^{\prime},y)\mbox{ with probability }1-p(y\succ y^{\prime})\,.\end{cases}
``` |  |

We will frequently make use of a collection of samples drawn from the
data-generating policies and the preference distribution, which we
denote by $`(y^{+}_{i},y^{-}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}`$. In this case we will say
the data is sampled from $`(\mu,\mu^{\prime},\lambda_{p})`$ (or
$`(\mu,\lambda_{p})`$ when $`\mu=\mu^{\prime}`$). In *offline* settings,
the data is typically generated from a fixed policy $`\mu`$, whereas in
*online* settings, new data can be generated from a currently estimated
policy $`\pi`$.

Another important aspect of the data available to the learner is an
initial policy $`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})`$. Typically,
this policy provides acceptable behaviour in some (though not all)
aspects of interest in the problem, and may be used to define a trust
region for the policy optimisation problem. Our central contributions in
this paper draw together, unify, and contrast several different
approaches to preference optimisation; we now recall these below.

### 2.2 RLHF with a Bradley-Terry reward model

The approach to preference optimisation proposed by [Christiano et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib9) is split into two steps. First, a reward model
$`r:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ is fitted via the Bradley-Terry
model ([Bradley & Terry, 1952](#bib.bib7)). In more detail, the
preference probability $`p(y\succ y^{\prime})`$ is approximated by the
logistic function

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sigma(r(y)-r(y^{\prime}))=\frac{e^{r(y)-r(y^{\prime})}}{1+e^{r(y)-r(y^{\prime})}}\,,`$ |  |

and a reward function $`\hat{r}`$ is learnt essentially by performing
maximum likelihood in this model, given a collection of observed
preferences $`(y^{+}_{i},y^{-}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}`$ sampled from
$`(\mu,\lambda_{p})`$, (approximately) maximising the objective

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\sigma(r(y^{+}_{i})-r(y^{-}_{i}))\right)\,.`$ |  | (1) |

Policy optimisation then proceeds by aiming to maximise the expected
reward $`\hat{r}`$ under the $`\pi`$, subject to a KL constraint against
the initial policy:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\argmax_{\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}\ \bigg[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y\sim\pi}[\hat{r}(Y)]-\tau\text{KL}(\pi\;||\;\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})\bigg]\,,`$ |  | (2) |

with $`\tau>0`$ a temperature parameter controlling the degree of
regularisation towards $`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}`$. Note that the solution
$`\pi^{*}`$ to
Equation ([2](#S2.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 2.2 RLHF with a Bradley-Terry reward model ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
is available in closed form, as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{*}(y)\propto\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp(\tau^{-1}\hat{r}(y))\,.`$ |  | (3) |

### 2.3 Direct preference optimisation (DPO)

[Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib32) propose *direct policy
optimisation* (DPO) as an alternative to RLHF as described above, noting
that with the closed form in
Equation ([3](#S2.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 2.2 RLHF with a Bradley-Terry reward model ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
the learning of a reward function can be completely bypassed, instead
reparametrising the optimal reward in terms of the optimal policy,
substituting into
Equation ([1](#S2.E1 "Equation 1 ‣ 2.2 RLHF with a Bradley-Terry reward model ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
and aiming to maximise the resulting objective with respect to $`\pi`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\sigma\left(\tau\;\log\left(\frac{\pi(y^{+}_{i})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}_{i})}{\pi(y^{-}_{i})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}_{i})}\right)\right)\right)\,.`$ |  | (4) |

The derivation of DPO implies that, mathematically, it yields the same
optimal policy as the RLHF approach in
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 RLHF with a Bradley-Terry reward model ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"),
which is a regularised optimiser for the reward model $`\hat{r}`$.

### 2.4 Sequence Likelihood Calibration (SLiC)

[Zhao et al. (2023)](#bib.bib53) propose *sequence likelihood
calibration* (SLiC) as an alternative to RLHF. Soon after, [Liu et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib24) refine the SLiC loss by normalising the policy
probabilities with the reference policy probabilities in order to get a
regularised offline loss. In the remaining, we will refer to the
following loss, with a dataset $`(y^{+}_{i},y^{-}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}`$
sampled from $`(\mu,\lambda_{p})`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\max\left(0,1-\tau\log\bigg(\frac{\pi(y^{+}_{i})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}_{i})}{\pi(y^{-}_{i})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}_{i})}\bigg)\right)\,`$ |  | (5) |

as the SLiC loss. This loss can be interpreted as an hinge-loss
variation of DPO ([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib24)).

### 2.5 Identity policy optimisation (IPO)

[Azar et al. (2023)](#bib.bib3) note that in general, optimisation of
the reward model described in
Section [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 RLHF with a Bradley-Terry reward model ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"),
which is implicitly optimised by DPO, may not always yield an
intuitively good policy for the preference probabilities $`p`$. They
also note that in practice, removal of the learnt reward function from
the pipeline removes an important source of regularisation in the
learning problem, and as such DPO may learn policies that are
under-regularised, and converge to deterministic actions.

In order to circumvent these two issues, [Azar et al. (2023)](#bib.bib3)
propose *identity policy optimisation* (IPO). The derivation begins from
the objective of aiming to directly optimise preference probabilities
(rather than a proxy reward) against a fixed policy $`\mu`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\sim\pi\\
Y^{\prime}\sim\mu\end{subarray}}[p(Y\succ Y^{\prime})]-\tau\text{KL}(\pi\;||\;\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})\,.`$ |  | (6) |

Similar to
Equation ([3](#S2.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 2.2 RLHF with a Bradley-Terry reward model ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
the optimal policy for this objective is expressible directly as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{*}(y)\propto\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp(\tau^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{Y^{\prime}\sim\mu}[p(y\succ Y^{\prime})])\,,`$ |  | (7) |

which [Azar et al. (2023)](#bib.bib3) use to derive the following
equivalent offline IPO loss, with a dataset
$`(y^{+}_{i},y^{-}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}`$ sampled from $`(\mu,\lambda_{p})`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\log\bigg(\frac{\pi(y^{+}_{i})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}_{i})}{\pi(y^{-}_{i})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}_{i})}\bigg)-\tau^{-1}/2\right)^{2}\,.`$ |  | (8) |

The quadratic aspect of the loss discourages log-probability ratios
between pairs of actions under $`\pi`$ from deviating too far from those
under $`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}`$, which ensures regularisation of $`\pi`$
against $`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}`$. By contrast, the minimiser of the DPO
empirical loss does not have this property.

### 2.6 Nash-MD-PG

Rather than optimising preference probabilities against an offline
dataset generated from some data-generating policy $`\mu`$, [Munos
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26) propose instead interpreting the objective in
Equation ([6](#S2.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 2.5 Identity policy optimisation (IPO) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
as one player’s objective in a two-player, constant-sum game.
Specifically, two players select policies
$`\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})`$, with player $`i`$ receiving
payoff

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\sim\pi_{i}\\
Y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{-i}\end{subarray}}[p(Y\succ Y^{\prime})]`$ |  | (9) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\qquad-\tau\text{KL}(\pi_{i}\;||\;\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})+\tau\text{KL}(\pi_{-i}\;||\;\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})\,,`$ |  |  |

where $`\pi_{-i}`$ denotes the policy of the other player. Note that
holding $`\pi_{-i}`$ fixed at $`\mu`$ then yields an equivalent
objective to
Equation ([6](#S2.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 2.5 Identity policy optimisation (IPO) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
for $`\pi_{i}`$. The proposal of [Munos et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26) is
then to find a Nash equilibrium for this game, motivated by the idea
that the policies in the resulting Nash equilibrium may be more robust,
and are not overly specific to the data-generating distribution $`\mu`$.
On the flip-side there may be some benefit to regularising the sampling
distribution toward the data distribution, and Nash-MD-PG has a
parameter $`\beta`$ that allows for this tradeoff, with self-play in one
extreme $`(\beta=0)`$ and sampling from $`\mu`$ in the other
($`\beta=1`$). We denote this algorithm by Nash-MD-PG($`\beta`$).

The Nash-MD-PG($`\beta`$) algorithm, motivated by mirror-descent
approaches to saddle-point computation, aims to do so by updating the
policy $`\pi`$ in the direction of the following policy gradient:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla\log\pi(y)\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\tfrac{1}{2}-\tau\log\left(\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\right)\right)\,,`$ |  |

where importantly $`y\sim\pi`$, and $`y^{\prime}`$ is sampled from a
*geometric mixture policy*
$`\pi^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}`$, for some choice of
$`\beta\in[0,1]`$.

## 3 Comparative discussion of preference optimisation algorithms

The algorithms DPO
([Section 2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 Direct preference optimisation (DPO) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
SLiC
([Section 2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 Sequence Likelihood Calibration (SLiC) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
IPO
([Section 2.5](#S2.SS5 "2.5 Identity policy optimisation (IPO) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
and Nash-MD-PG
([Section 2.6](#S2.SS6 "2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
are distinct along a number of axes.

Contrastivity. IPO is a *contrastive* algorithm, in that it labels a
pair $`(Y,Y^{\prime})`$ according to the preference function (via
$`\lambda_{p}`$) into a positive (preferred) and a negative (not
preferred) example $`(Y^{+},Y^{-})`$, and then updates the policy via
gradients flowing through *both samples*, $`\pi(Y^{+})`$ and
$`\pi(Y^{-})`$. By contrast, Nash-MD-PG is not contrastive; only the
sampled action’s policy probability is directly updated based on the
preference. Contrastive algorithms in general have the potential to be
more data-efficient, making direct use of both samples in each policy
update (see
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Comparison of the variance of contrastive versus non-contrastive gradient estimates ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
for a condition under which a contrastive gradient estimate has lower
variance than its non-contrastive counterpart).

Offline/online data. IPO is an *offline* algorithm, working with a fixed
dataset, while Nash-MD-PG is an *online* algorithm that makes use of
sampled actions from both the current estimated policy $`\pi`$, and a
geometric mixture $`\pi^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}`$. In many
settings it may be desirable to work with static, offline data sets,
although if it is feasible to gather data online, this can be beneficial
from the point of view of effective regularisation. In the offline
setting, the data limits our ability to evaluate the quality of learned
policies in terms of preferences, which can lead to learning bad
policies that choose actions outside the data distribution, but have low
empirical loss. Similar to offline RL ([Fujimoto et al.,
2019](#bib.bib13)), this can be mitigated through regularisation to a
reference policy $`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}`$, possibly related to the
sampling distribution $`\mu`$ ([Jaques et al., 2019](#bib.bib19); [Wu
et al., 2019](#bib.bib50)). An alternative to regularisation is to use
online data, and to train $`\pi`$ on data that is close to what $`\pi`$
generates. Online data may not be available in all settings, but, when
it is, it can be an effective way to improve performance of learned
policies.

Equilibria. The IPO loss is a supervised objective. In particular, its
data distribution is fixed, and the optimiser is given by the
closed-form policy in
Equation ([7](#S2.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 2.5 Identity policy optimisation (IPO) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
which in particular can be interpreted as a policy that is preferred
over the data-generating distribution $`\mu`$, regularised towards
$`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}`$. By contrast, Nash-MD-PG is a game-theoretic
algorithm, with a loss function whose data distribution and objective
change as the estimated policy change themselves. The stationary points
for Nash-MD-PG are not defined in a closed-form manner against reference
and data policies $`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}`$ and $`\mu`$, but in a
self-referential manner.

Regularised Sampling. As discussed in
[Section 2.6](#S2.SS6 "2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"),
Nash-MD-PG allows for sampling from a mixture distribution between
$`\pi`$ and the data-generating distribution $`\mu`$, and this can also
lead to improved performance versus sampling from either policy.

It is clear that there are several combinations of the various
properties of previous methods for which no algorithm yet exists,
including combinations that could have advantages over previous work,
for example an online contrastive method.
[Table 1](#S3.T1 "In 3 Comparative discussion of preference optimisation algorithms ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
gives an overview of existing methods in terms of what we consider are

## 4 Online IPO

We first aim to bridge the online/offline divide between IPO and
Nash-MD-PG, by proposing a new variant of IPO, *Online IPO*, which makes
use of an online, shifting data distribution.

### 4.1 Algorithm

To derive an update, we first start with the *population* loss for IPO,
which is obtained by taking the minibatch loss in
Equation ([8](#S2.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 2.5 Identity policy optimisation (IPO) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
and taking an expectation over the dataset (under i.i.d. sampling from
$`(\mu,\lambda_{p})`$). This yields the (offline) IPO population loss

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y,Y^{\prime}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mu}\\
Y^{+},Y^{-}\sim\lambda_{p}(Y,Y^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\bigg[\left(\log\bigg(\frac{\pi(Y^{+})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(Y^{-})}{\pi(Y^{-})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(Y^{+})}\bigg)-\tau^{-1}/2\right)^{2}\bigg]\,.`$ |  | (10) |

The *Online IPO* population loss is given by replacing the static data
distribution, highlighted in red above, with the data distribution
generated by the current policy, as displayed below

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y,Y^{\prime}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\texttt{SG}[\pi]}\\
Y^{+},Y^{-}\sim\lambda_{p}(Y,Y^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\bigg[\left(\log\bigg(\frac{\pi(Y^{+})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(Y^{-})}{\pi(Y^{-})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(Y^{+})}\bigg)-\tau^{-1}/2\right)^{2}\bigg]\,.`$ |  | (11) |

Here, $`\texttt{SG}[\pi]`$ denotes a stop-gradient around $`\pi`$ in the
data distribution, meaning that although we generate data from $`\pi`$
to construct the loss, we do not differentiate through the
data-generation process itself.

The population form of the Online IPO loss will be useful in the
analysis that follows. We conclude our description of the approach by
noting that the sample-based Online IPO loss coincides with the Offline
IPO¹¹ 1 For clarity, we will refer to the original formulation of IPO by
[Azar et al. (2023)](#bib.bib3) as *Offline IPO* in what follows. loss
in
Equation ([8](#S2.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 2.5 Identity policy optimisation (IPO) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
with the exception that the samples $`(y^{+}_{i},y^{-}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}`$
are drawn from the current policy $`\pi`$.

### 4.2 Analysis

Before studying the performance of the newly derived Online IPO loss
empirically, we pause to consider it from a theoretical perspective. In
particular, we aim to understand for which policies this loss is
stationary.

By the analysis for (Offline) IPO ([Azar et al., 2023](#bib.bib3))
summarised in
Section [2.5](#S2.SS5 "2.5 Identity policy optimisation (IPO) ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"),
the gradient for the Online IPO loss in
Equation ([11](#S4.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ 4.1 Algorithm ‣ 4 Online IPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
is zero iff $`\pi`$ satisfies

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi(y)\propto\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp(\tau^{-1}p(y\succ\pi))\,,`$ |  | (12) |

where we use the shorthand
$`p(y\succ\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{Y^{\prime}\sim\pi}[p(y\succ Y^{\prime})]`$.
This is a fixed-point condition; note that $`\pi`$ appears on both
sides. This in fact says that $`\pi`$ is a best-response against itself
in the regularised game described in the background for Nash-MD-PG in
Section [2.6](#S2.SS6 "2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation").
Hence, if $`\pi`$ satisfies
Equation ([12](#S4.E12 "Equation 12 ‣ 4.2 Analysis ‣ 4 Online IPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
it must be the Nash equilibrium for this game; we have shown the
following.

###### Proposition 4.1.

The minimiser of the online IPO objective is the Nash equilibrium of the
regularised game described in
Equation ([9](#S2.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")).

This is perhaps a surprising conclusion. Offline IPO is not motivated by
game-theoretic considerations, yet by moving to an online variant, we
have obtained a loss whose stationary point is precisely the Nash
equilibrium of the preference game optimised by Nash-MD-PG. In fact, we
can go further, and deduce a direct equivalence of expected updates
between Online IPO, and *self-play* in this game. The proof for the
following result is given in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Proofs ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation").

###### Proposition 4.2.

The expected gradient of the Online IPO loss in
Equation ([11](#S4.E11 "Equation 11 ‣ 4.1 Algorithm ‣ 4 Online IPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
is identical to the self-play update direction in the game with payoff
as in
Equation [9](#S2.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation").

Here, *self-play* refers to the algorithm in which a policy is updated
using gradient ascent on its expected payoff in the game described in
Equation ([9](#S2.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
against another player using the same policy:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\pi}\Bigg(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{Y\sim\pi,Y^{\prime}\sim\texttt{SG}[\pi]}\bigg[p(Y\succ Y^{\prime})-\tau\text{KL}(\pi\;||\;\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})\bigg]\Bigg)\,.`$ |  |

Note that in expectation, this corresponds to Nash-MD-PG with
$`\beta=0`$; however, an important difference is that in Online IPO,
updates are *contrastive*, which may result in variance reduction of the
gradient estimate.

We have therefore established a close connection between Online IPO and
Nash equilibria for the regularised game.

### 4.3 Online DPO

As the DPO and SLiC losses are similar to IPO, a natural question is
whether online variants of DPO and SLiC are also related to the
regularised game given
[Equation 9](#S2.E9 "In 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation").
We explore this question for online DPO in
[Appendix F](#A6 "Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation").
[Lemma F.5](#A6.Thmtheorem5 "Lemma F.5. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
gives conditions for the stationary point of the regularised game and
online IPO to be a stationary point of online DPO. However, the
conditions seem difficult to satisfy: For example,
[Theorem F.6](#A6.Thmtheorem6 "Theorem F.6. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
says that there is no 2-action problem for which the condition is
satisfied, except when preferences are uniform
($`p(1\succ 2)=\frac{1}{2}`$). In this sense, apart from the trivial
uniform-preference case, online DPO and online IPO are different
objectives when $`|\mathcal{Y}|=2`$.

As for stationary points of online DPO, we show that, under the
Bradley-Terry model assumption, the RLHF solution
([Equation 3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 RLHF with a Bradley-Terry reward model ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
is a stationary point of online DPO
([Theorem F.7](#A6.Thmtheorem7 "Theorem F.7. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
coinciding with that of offline DPO.

## 5 IPO-MD

Having established a connection between Online IPO and self-play, it is
natural to consider whether we can improve on self-play by using
regularised policies to generate the data, similar to how Nash-MD
optimises preferences against a regularised adversary.

### 5.1 Algorithm

We consider modulating the data distribution used in the IPO loss using
the same geometric mixture
($`\pi^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}`$) between online and
reference policies as in Nash-MD, we arrive at a new family of
algorithms that we call IPO-MD that directly corresponds to the family
of policies for Nash-MD-PG. This leads to the population loss

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y,Y^{\prime}\sim{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\texttt{SG}[\pi^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}]}\\
Y^{+},Y^{-}\sim\lambda_{p}(Y,Y^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\bigg[\left(\log\bigg(\frac{\pi(Y^{+})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(Y^{-})}{\pi(Y^{-})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(Y^{+})}\bigg)-\tau^{-1}/2\right)^{2}\bigg]\,.`$ |  |

where in the data distribution, we write
$`\pi^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}`$ as shorthand for the
policy which is given by normalising the (unnormalised) geometric
mixture $`\pi^{1-\beta}(y)(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}(y)`$. For
$`\beta\in[0,1]`$, we obtain an algorithm that interpolates between
these two in a certain sense, analogous to Nash-MD-PG ([Munos et al.,
2023](#bib.bib26)); we call this algorithm IPO-MD($`\beta`$). When
$`\beta=0`$, we obtain Online IPO, i.e. self-play, and when $`\beta=1`$,
we get a variation of IPO that tries to improve against a fixed policy
$`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}`$. An interesting observation is that if we use a
slightly different mixture policy that mixes with $`\mu`$,
$`\pi^{1-\beta}(\mu)^{\beta}`$, then this actually interpolates between
Online IPO and Offline IPO, where for $`\beta=1`$ we get back the
Offline IPO objective. In practice it is often difficult to get direct
access to $`\mu`$ so we would not be able to form this geometric
mixture. Some example dynamics in tabular settings are plotted in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Tabular example ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation").

### 5.2 Analysis

We now compare Nash-MD-PG with the new algorithm class IPO-MD described
above. First, these two classes of algorithms are practically different.
Nash-MD-PG is on-policy, in that the only gradient contributions
appearing in its update are those corresponding to actions sampled under
the current policy. By contrast, IPO-MD is an off-policy algorithm,
since it updates its current policy based on actions sampled under
$`\pi^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}`$.

By the analysis of Offline IPO ([Azar et al., 2023](#bib.bib3)), we have
that any fixed point $`\pi^{*}_{\beta}`$ of IPO-MD($`\beta`$) must
satisfy

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{*}_{\beta}(y)\!\propto\!\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp\big(\tau^{-1}p(y\succ(\pi^{*}_{\beta})^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta})\big)\,.`$ |  | (13) |

In other words, $`\pi^{*}_{\beta}`$ is a best-response against
$`(\pi^{*}_{\beta})^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}`$ in the
regularised game described in
Equation ([9](#S2.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")).
But from the description of the Nash-MD-PG($`\beta`$) algorithm by
[Munos et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26), we have that a policy $`\pi^{*}`$ is
stationary under this algorithm’s update iff

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{*}(y)\propto\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp\big(\tau^{-1}p(y\succ(\pi^{*})^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta})\big)\,.`$ |  |

Hence, the fixed point of IPO-MD$`(\beta)`$ coincides with the fixed
point of Nash-MD-PG($`\beta`$).

Having established the equivalence of the stationary points of
IPO-MD($`\beta`$) and Nash-MD-PG($`\beta`$), we now study their
gradients more generally in the following result; see
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Proofs ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
for the proof.

###### Proposition 5.1.

The gradients of the algorithms Nash-MD-PG($`\beta`$) and
IPO-MD($`\beta`$) are, respectively,

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{\mbox{{\tiny Nash-MD-PG}}(\beta)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle-{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\pi}\left[g(y)\right]`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{\mbox{{\tiny IPO-MD}}(\beta)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\tau}{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim(\pi)^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}}\left[g(y)\right]`$ |  |

where $`g(y)`$ is given by

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla\log\pi(y)\left(p(y\succ(\pi)^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta})-\tau\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\right)\,.`$ |  |

We recover the result mentioned earlier that when $`\beta=0`$,
IPO-MD($`\beta=0`$) (i.e., Online IPO) has a gradient aligned with that
of Nash-MD-PG($`\beta=0`$) (i.e., Self-Play). Now as soon as
$`\beta>0`$, the gradients of these two algorithms are different.
Interestingly however, as noted above, their fixed points remain the
same.

We can also relate the fixed points themselves back to the original
regularised game given in
Equation ([9](#S2.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
as described below.

###### Proposition 5.2.

Let $`\pi^{*}_{\beta}`$ be the fixed-point of IPO-MD($`\beta`$),
satisfying
Equation ([13](#S5.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ 5.2 Analysis ‣ 5 IPO-MD ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")).
The policy
$`\pi^{\prime}_{\beta}=(\pi^{*}_{\beta})^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}`$
is the Nash equilibrium for the version of the game in
Equation ([9](#S2.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
with regularisation parameter $`\tau`$ modified to
$`\tau(1-\beta)^{-1}`$.

###### Proof.

Using the property in
Equation ([13](#S5.E13 "Equation 13 ‣ 5.2 Analysis ‣ 5 IPO-MD ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
we have

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}_{\beta}(y)`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\propto`$ | $`\displaystyle(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y))^{1-\beta}\exp(\tau^{-1}(1-\beta)p(y\succ\pi^{\prime}_{\beta}))(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y))^{\beta}`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\propto`$ | $`\displaystyle\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp(\tau^{-1}(1-\beta)p(y\succ\pi^{\prime}_{\beta}))\,,`$ |  |

which is the Nash equilibrium condition for the game described, as
required. ∎

## 6 Experiments

We present our results on fine-tuning large language models where we
compare our algorithms, online-IPO and IPO-MD, against recent baselines.
In this section we only present the results for the online versions of
IPO, DPO and SLIC to make the comparison against IPO-MD and Nash-MD-PG
fair, and drop the corresponding ”online-” prefix for simplicity. We
refer the reader to the appendix for results concerning the offline
versions of those algorithms. Note that to aid interpretability and
reproducibility, we now consider the contextual bandit case where the
actions $`y`$, also referred as generations, are conditioned on a prompt
$`x`$.

Setup and algorithms. We perform RLHF-style experiments where we
initialise from a supervised-fine-tuned checkpoint, and then further
fine-tune using one of the following algorithms: RL (regularised policy
gradient), IPO, DPO , SLiC, Nash-MD and IPO-MD. These algorithms use
either a learned reward model $`r_{\phi}`$ (RLHF) or a learned
preference model $`p_{\phi}`$ (IPO, DPO, SLiC, Nash-MD, and IPO-MD). For
our RL baseline, similarly to [Munos et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26), we use
a regularised policy gradient update:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\rho\\
y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\big[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\!\left(r_{\phi}(y|x)\!-\!\tau\text{KL}(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x),\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}\!(\cdot|x))\right)\!\big],`$ |  |

where $`r_{\phi}(y|x)`$ is the reward model’s value for generation $`y`$
and context $`x`$.

Implementation details. The contrastive offline algorithms such as IPO,
DPO and SliC directly optimise the policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ by minimising
their respective losses over a pairwise dataset
$`(x_{i},y_{i}^{+},y_{i}^{-})_{i=1}^{N}`$ . In practice, we sample
batches $`(x_{i},y_{i}^{+},y_{i}^{-})_{i=1}^{B}`$ of size $`B\ll N`$ and
we minimise the following loss:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{ALGO}}(\theta,x_{i},y_{i},y^{\prime}_{i}),`$ |  |

where:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{IPO}}(\theta,x,y,y^{\prime})\!`$ | $`\displaystyle=\!\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime}|x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}\right)-\tau^{-1}/2\right)^{2},`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{DPO}}(\theta,x,y,y^{\prime})\!`$ | $`\displaystyle=\!\sigma\left(\tau\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime}|x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}\right)\right),`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{SLiC}}(\theta,x,y,y^{\prime})\!`$ | $`\displaystyle=\!\max\left(0,1\!-\!\tau\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime}|x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}\right)\right)\!.`$ |  |

One important detail concerning IPO is that we use a simplified loss in
our code. One can remark by expanding the square and removing terms that
do not depend on $`\theta`$ that the IPO loss is equivalent to:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle-\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)}\right)+\tau\left(\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime}|x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y^{\prime}|x)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}\right)\!\right)^{2}.`$ |  |

The contrastive online algorithms such as IPO, DPO and SLiC use a
trained preference model $`p_{\phi}`$. To train $`p_{\phi}`$, we use a
pairwise dataset $`(x_{i},y_{i}^{+},y_{i}^{-})_{i=1}^{N}`$ and follow
the same protocol as [Munos et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26). Then, to train
the policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$, for each context $`x_{i}`$ of a batch, we
sample two completely new generations
$`(y_{i},y_{i}^{\prime})\sim\pi_{\theta}`$ according to $`\pi_{\theta}`$
and compute the preference
$`p_{i}=p_{\phi}(y_{i}\succ y_{i}^{\prime}|x_{i})`$ via the preference
model. Then, for each algorithm, we minimise the respective following
loss:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\left(p_{i}\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{ALGO}}(\theta,x_{i},y_{i},y^{\prime}_{i})+(1-p_{i})\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{ALGO}}(\theta,x_{i},y^{\prime}_{i},y_{i})\right).`$ |  |

Finally, regarding IPO-MD, the only difference with (online)-IPO is how
the generations are sampled. In theory, we should sample from the
mixture $`\pi_{\theta}^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}`$ which is
not feasible (see ([Munos et al., 2023](#bib.bib26))). In practice, we
sample from the one-step-at-a-time mixture $`\hat{\pi}_{\beta}`$, which
consists at step $`n`$ to compute the mixture of logits between the
online and reference logits:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\log(\hat{\pi}_{\beta}(.|y_{0:n-1},x))=(1-\beta)\log(\pi_{\theta}(.|y_{0:n-1},x))`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle+\beta\log(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(.|y_{0:n-1},x))+C(y_{0:n-1},x),`$ |  |

where $`C(y_{0:n-1},x)`$ is a path-dependent constant and sample
according to the softmax of this mixture of logits. This sampling
process is not equivalent to sampling from
$`\pi_{\theta}^{1-\beta}(\pi^{\mathrm{ref}})^{\beta}`$ as shown
in ([Munos et al., 2023](#bib.bib26)).

Evaluation tasks and Models. In our experiments, we test all of the

described by [Stiennon et al. (2020)](#bib.bib41) that has been built
from the TL;DR dataset ([Völske et al., 2017](#bib.bib46)). This is a
dataset with pairwise preferences between alternate summaries. We train
our preference and reward model on the train set $`D_{\texttt{Train}}`$,
which contains $`92820`$ examples. We evaluate reward and preference
models on a test set of high confidence data $`D_{\texttt{Test}}`$ and
use the checkpoints with the highest evaluation agreement score. To
train the policies with online algorithms, we use prompts of the train
set of the XSum dataset ([Shashi et al., 2018](#bib.bib36)).

We use T5X large language models ([Roberts et al., 2022](#bib.bib34)) to
train our policies, rewards and preference models. The T5X models we use
are auto-regressive transformers with an encoder-decoder architecture.
All the details of the models architecture and the different sizes are
provided in the documentation ([Roberts et al., 2022](#bib.bib34)). For
the policy model, we use a *large* (L) encoder-decoder model ($`770M`$
parameters). For the preference and reward models, we use an *XL*
encoder-decoder model ($`3B`$ parameters). To train reward and
preference models we use the same losses and protocol as [Munos et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib26). For summarisation, we initialise our policy with a
T5X-L model and fine-tune it with supervised learning using the OpenAI
dataset described by [Stiennon et al. (2020)](#bib.bib41). We call this
supervised fine-tuned model the SFT. All our policies for summarisation
are initialised with this SFT checkpoint.

Our evaluation pipeline is based upon the use of PaLM2 ([Anil et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2)) as a judge for side-by-side comparisons. We sample
responses for each of the policies trained by each algorithm from a test
set of prompts, and ask PaLM2 to pick which one is better. We use
validation and test prompts from the XSum dataset ([Shashi et al.,
2018](#bib.bib36)) for evaluation for the summarisation task, which is
the same procedure used by [Munos et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26). The
evaluation prompt we use for the side-by-side comparison is:

You are an expert summary rater. Given a piece of text and two of its
possible summaries, output 1 or 2 to indicate which summary is better.

Text - \<text\>, Summary 1 - \<summary1\>, Summary 2 - \<summary2\>

Preferred Summary -

We use cloud Tensor Processing Units ([Jouppi et al., 2023](#bib.bib20),
TPUs; ) in their version $`5e`$ for our hardware compute, either in
configurations of $`2\times 4`$ devices for training offline
experiments, or $`4\times 4`$ devices for online experiments. This setup
typically yields speed of around $`0.25`$ training steps per second
($`24`$ hours per $`20,000`$ steps). We run our experiments with default
parameters $`10^{-4}`$ for the learning rate, and a default total of
$`30,000`$ training steps, using a batch size of $`32`$. The $`\tau`$
factor is held constant throughout training, and we do not employ any
warmup steps. We use the AdaFactor ([Shazeer & Stern, 2018](#bib.bib37))
optimizer with decay set to $`0.8`$.

presents the side by side scores for the summarisation task. The

against the SFT checkpoint after $`10k`$ learner steps. To find the best

steps), regularisation parameter $`\tau`$ (we sweep over $`5`$ values
$`\{0.1,0.5,1.0,5.0,10.0\}`$) and also $`\beta`$ for IPO-MD and
Nash-MD-PG (we sweep over $`2`$ values $`0.125`$ and $`0.25`$) and we

we re-run each method for 3 different seeds using the best

$`3\times 3`$ 1vs1 evaluations between each of the 3 seeds for each pair

each comparison. We report mean and standard deviation across these 9
comparisons.

On the summarisation task, looking only at the mean the best algorithm
is IPO as it beats all the other algorithms on a side-by-side
comparison. However, once we take into consideration the standard
deviation IPO and IPO-MD’s performance becomes statistically
indistinguishable, with both algorithms consistently beating all the
other algorithms. This shows that those algorithms are indeed robust and
are closer to a Nash optimum than the other algorithms. Those results
are limited to a summarisation task and more experiments should be
conducted to validate these results on a general conversational agent.
However, we do think that summarisation is a good test bed to showcase
the quality of human alignment algorithms because it is a complex and
high-in-demand task.

## 7 Conclusion

In this paper, we have identified several factors of variation, such as
contrastivity, online/offline and regularised-sampling, between two
recently proposed algorithms for preference optimisation, IPO and
Nash-MD-PG. In doing so, we have introduced two new algorithms,
online-IPO and IPO-MD, that combine different strengths of these
existing algorithms, namely the loss function of IPO with the online
sampling and regularised data distribution of Nash-MD. Theoretical
analysis reveals a surprising equivalence at the level of expected
update between online-IPO and self-play in a regularised two-player
preference optimisation game. This important property is not possessed
by online-DPO. Finally, our empirical investigation on a summarisation
task also reveals that IPO-MD and online-IPO are promising approaches to
preference optimisation at scale as they are the most robust algorithms.
At the moment, our work is restricted to model of size $`770M`$ on a
single task, future works will consist to scale our approach to a full
conversational agent using a larger model ($`100+`$ billions parameter).

## Appendix A Related Work

RLHF. Reinforcement learning from human feedback, as introduced in
[Christiano et al. (2017)](#bib.bib9) (see also [Bai et al.
(2022a)](#bib.bib4); [Ouyang et al. (2022)](#bib.bib29)) and often based
on proximal policy optimisation ([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib35)),
is a critical element of making large language models helpful and
aligned with preferences of human operators. While in itself it
typically does not result in improved benchmark performance ([Touvron
et al., 2023](#bib.bib44)), RLHF is nonetheless key to satisfying
human-mediated interactions such as dialogue ([Nakano et al.,
2021](#bib.bib27); [Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib29)). The complexity of
the RLHF procedure ([Casper et al., 2023](#bib.bib8)), which can also be
accomplished by multiple reinforcement learning algorithms such as
actor-critic ([Mnih et al., 2016](#bib.bib25); [Glaese et al.,
2022](#bib.bib15)), has led to searching for algorithmic alternatives
([Dong et al., 2023](#bib.bib11); [Yuan et al., 2023](#bib.bib52); [Zhao
et al., 2023](#bib.bib53)).

Recent developments in policy optimisation. In the special case of an
additional Bradley-Terry model ([Bradley & Terry, 1952](#bib.bib7))
assumption for the human reward model, reinforcement learning has been
found redundant; this allows for casting the problem of RLHF as a
supervised one ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib32)). Recent
developments have focused on scaling the performance of such direct
policy optimisation (DPO) methods ([Tunstall et al., 2023](#bib.bib45);
[Ivison et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)), as well as generalising its
mathematical formulation ([Azar et al., 2023](#bib.bib3); [Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib47); [Tang et al., 2024](#bib.bib43)). One of the key
issues with direct policy optimisation - and RLHF in general - resides
in their propensity to game or *hack* rewards ([Amodei et al.,
2016](#bib.bib1); [Skalse et al., 2022](#bib.bib40); [Pan et al.,
2022](#bib.bib30); [Pang et al., 2022](#bib.bib31)) and become
overoptimised, or under-regularised ([Gao et al., 2022](#bib.bib14);
[Singhal et al., 2023](#bib.bib39); [Kirk et al., 2023](#bib.bib21)),
which can be mitigated e.g. by using ensembling techniques ([Wortsman
et al., 2022](#bib.bib49); [Eisenstein et al., 2023](#bib.bib12); [Coste
et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Ramé et al., 2024](#bib.bib33)). Rather than
a reinforcement versus supervised learning dichotomy, it is the
distinction between *online* and *offline* ([Jaques et al.,
2019](#bib.bib19)) methods that seems more relevant in practice, as an
online policy’s generations might start deviating substantially from the
original dataset, leading to distribution shifts ([Zhuang &
Hadfield-Menell, 2020](#bib.bib54); [Shin et al., 2023](#bib.bib38)).
Finally, alignment can also result from the self-play form of a
two-player game, and not just single-policy optimisation. This
perspective, taken in [Munos et al. (2023)](#bib.bib26); [Swamy et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib42) has the added benefit of encompassing both online
and offline settings, enabling smooth interpolation between them via a
hyperparameter. In a similar vein, DPO has been shown to be able and
improve thanks to iterated successive rounds ([Yuan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib51)), expanding on known machine-critic alignment methods
such as reinforcement learning from AI feedback ([Bai et al.,
2022b](#bib.bib5); [Lee et al., 2023](#bib.bib23)).

## Appendix D Comparison of the variance of contrastive versus non-contrastive gradient estimates

Define the following gradient estimates based on non-contrastive vs
contrastive loss functions:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\mbox{{\tiny non-contrastive}}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle-\nabla\log\pi(y)\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}-\tau\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}+\tau\log\frac{\pi(y^{\prime})}{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}\right)`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\mbox{{\tiny contrastive}}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\log\pi(y)-\nabla\log\pi(y^{\prime})\right)\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}-\tau\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}+\tau\log\frac{\pi(y^{\prime})}{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}\right).`$ |  |

These two estimate resemble those of the algorithms Self-Play (as
implemented by Nash-MD-PG($`\beta=0`$)), which uses a non-contrastive
loss, and online-IPO (equivalent to IPO-MD($`\beta=0`$)), which uses a
contrastive loss, and we have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{\mbox{{\tiny Self-Play}}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\left[\hat{g}_{\mbox{{\tiny non-contrastive}}}\right]`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle g_{\mbox{{\tiny online-IPO}}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\frac{2}{\tau}{\mathbb{E}}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\left[\hat{g}_{\mbox{{\tiny contrastive}}}\right].`$ |  |

We know from the previous result that these two estimates have the same
expectation. However their variance may differ. Their respective
variance depends on a non-trivial combinaison of the policy
representation and the specifics of the preference model. We now state a
sufficient condition under which the contrastive gradient estimate has
lower variance than its non-contrastive counterpart.

###### Proposition D.1.

If the policy representation and the preference model are such that we
have

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\mathbb{E}}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\left[\nabla\log\pi(y)\nabla\log\pi(y^{\prime})f(y,y^{\prime})^{2}\right]\geq 0,
``` |  | (14) |

where
$`f(y,y^{\prime}):=p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}-\tau\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}+\tau\log\frac{\pi(y^{\prime})}{\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}`$,
then the variance of the contrastive gradient estimate is at least as
small as that of the non-contrastive one:
$`\mbox{Var}(\hat{g}_{\mbox{{\tiny contrastive}}})\leq\mbox{Var}(\hat{g}_{\mbox{{\tiny non-contrastive}}})`$.

###### Proof.

Defining the random variables
$`X_{1}(y,y^{\prime}):=-\nabla\log\pi(y)f(y,y^{\prime})`$ and
$`X_{2}(y,y^{\prime}):=\nabla\log\pi(y^{\prime})f(y,y^{\prime})`$, we
have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\mbox{{\tiny non-contrastive}}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle X_{1}(y,y^{\prime})`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\mbox{{\tiny contrastive}}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\frac{X_{1}(y,y^{\prime})+X_{2}(y,y^{\prime})}{2}.`$ |  |

Now, let us compare the variance of these estimates when $`y`$ and
$`y^{\prime}`$ are independently drawn from the same distribution
$`\pi`$. The variance of the contrastive estimate is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mbox{Var}\left(\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}}{2}\right)=\frac{\mbox{Var}(X_{1})+\mbox{Var}(X_{2})+2\mbox{Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})}{4}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mbox{Var}(X_{1})+\mbox{Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})\right).
``` |  |

This variance is lower than $`\mbox{Var}(X_{1})`$ as soon as
$`\mbox{Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})`$ is negative (this is the principle of
antithetic variates for variance reduction).

Using the property that $`f(y,y^{\prime})=-f(y^{\prime},y)`$, and
writing
$`c={\mathbb{E}}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\left[\nabla\log\pi(y)f(y,y^{\prime})\right]=-{\mathbb{E}}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\left[\nabla\log\pi(y^{\prime})f(y,y^{\prime})\right]`$,
we have:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mbox{Cov}(X_{1},X_{2})`$ | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\left[\left(-\nabla\log\pi(y)f(y,y^{\prime})+c\right)\left(\nabla\log\pi(y^{\prime})f(y,y^{\prime})+c\right)\right]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\left[-\nabla\log\pi(y)\nabla\log\pi(y^{\prime})f(y,y^{\prime})^{2}\right]-c^{2}.`$ |  |

We deduce that a sufficient condition for the contrastive estimate to
have a lower variance than that of the non-contrastive estimate is:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\mathbb{E}}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\left[\nabla\log\pi(y)\nabla\log\pi(y^{\prime})f(y,y^{\prime})^{2}\right]+\left({\mathbb{E}}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi}\left[\nabla\log\pi(y)f(y,y^{\prime})\right]\right)^{2}\geq 0.
``` |  |

This condition is true as soon as
Equation ([14](#A4.E14 "Equation 14 ‣ Proposition D.1. ‣ Appendix D Comparison of the variance of contrastive versus non-contrastive gradient estimates ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
is satisfied. ∎

## Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO

In this section, we explore whether online DPO is related to the

[Equation 9](#S2.E9 "In 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"),
given that
[Proposition 4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 4.1. ‣ 4.2 Analysis ‣ 4 Online IPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
shows a similar relationship for online IPO and the regularised game.

Our strategy is to derive the gradient of the offline DPO objective
([Lemma F.3](#A6.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma F.3. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
and then inspect whether different sampling distributions $`\mu`$ and
candidate solutions $`\pi`$ satisfy the KKT conditions ([Boyd &
Vandenberghe, 2004](#bib.bib6)) for the minimising the objective. We can
also explore online DPO, by inspecting the KKT conditions when the
sampling distribution matches the candidate solution ($`\mu=\pi`$). For
example, in
[Lemma F.5](#A6.Thmtheorem5 "Lemma F.5. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
we present conditions for the solution of the regularised game (see
[Equation 12](#S4.E12 "In 4.2 Analysis ‣ 4 Online IPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
to also be a stationary point of online DPO. Given that the minimiser of
the online online IPO objective is the Nash equilibrium of the
regularised game given in
[Equation 9](#S2.E9 "In 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
([Proposition 4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 4.1. ‣ 4.2 Analysis ‣ 4 Online IPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")),
[Lemma F.5](#A6.Thmtheorem5 "Lemma F.5. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
gives us a condition for the online DPO problem to be equivalent
(solution-wise) to the online IPO problem. However, the condition seems
difficult to satisfy: For example, there is no 2-action problem for
which the condition is satisfied with the exception when preferences are
uniform ($`p(1\succ 2)=\frac{1}{2}`$). In this sense, apart from the
trivial uniform-preference case, online DPO and online IPO are different
objectives when $`|\mathcal{Y}|=2`$.

Regaring stationary points of online DPO, we show that, under the
Bradley-Terry model assumption, the RLHF solution
([Equation 3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 RLHF with a Bradley-Terry reward model ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
is a stationary point of online DPO
([Theorem F.7](#A6.Thmtheorem7 "Theorem F.7. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")).
We note in passing
([Remark F.9](#A6.Thmtheorem9 "Remark F.9. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
that the RLHF solution is not the only solution of offline DPO when
$`\mu(y)=0`$ for some $`y\in\mathcal{Y}`$. In fact, there are infinitely
many, with arbitrarily small probabilities for $`y`$ such that
$`\mu(y)>0`$. So while we can say that stationary points of online DPO
are also stationary points of offline DPO, a solution for the offline
DPO problem with a given $`\mu`$ may not be a solution for corresponding
the online DPO problem, because of what happens for $`y`$ such that
$`\mu(y)=0`$.

### F.1 Results and Proofs

We let
$`\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y})\doteq\left\{p\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y}):p(y)>0,\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}\right\}`$
be the interior of the simplex.

###### Proposition F.1.

For $`t\in\mathbb{R}`$, we have

|     |                                       |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{d}{dt}\log\sigma(t)=\sigma(-t).
       ```                                    |     |

###### Proof.

We have:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\log\sigma(t)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}-\log(1+e^{-t})=-\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}(1+e^{-t})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-t}}{1+e^{-t}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{t}}=\sigma(-t).`$ |  |

∎

###### Proposition F.2.

For $`t\in\mathbb{R}`$, we have

|     |                        |     |
|-----|------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \sigma(t)+\sigma(-t)=1
       ```                     |     |

###### Proof.

We have:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\sigma(t)+\sigma(-t)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-t}}+\frac{1}{1+e^{t}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{t}}+\frac{e^{-t}}{1+e^{-t}}=1.`$ |  |

∎

###### Lemma F.3.

Assume that $`|\mathcal{Y}|<\infty`$ and that
$`p(y\succ y^{\prime})=1-p(y^{\prime}\succ y)`$ for all
$`y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}`$. The offline DPO problem with sampling
distribution $`\mu`$ can be written as

|     |                                                        |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \sup_{\pi\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})}J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi),
       ```                                                     |     |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi)`$ | $`\displaystyle\doteq\sum_{y,y^{\prime}}\mu(y)\mu(y^{\prime})\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})\log\sigma\left(\tau\log\frac{\pi(y)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\right)\right.`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\phantom{\doteq}+\left.(1-p(y\succ y^{\prime}))\log\sigma\left(-\tau\log\frac{\pi(y)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\right)\right).`$ |  |

Moreover, for $`\pi\in\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y})`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi)_{y}=2\tau\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu(y^{\prime})\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\sigma\left(\tau\log\frac{\pi(y)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\right)\right).
``` |  | (15) |

###### Proof.

We will use the shorthands
$`\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}\equiv\mu(y)\mu(y^{\prime})`$,
$`\pi_{y}\equiv\pi(y)`$ (and so forth) and
$`s_{y,y^{\prime}}\doteq\tau\log\frac{\pi(y)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}`$.

The offline DPO objective is obtained by taking the expectation of the
DPO loss ([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib32)) with
$`Y^{+},Y^{-}\sim(\mu,\lambda_{p})`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{y,y^{\prime}}\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}(p(y\succ y^{\prime})\log\sigma(s_{y,y^{\prime}})+(1-p(y\succ y^{\prime}))\log\sigma(-s_{y,y^{\prime}}))=J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi).
``` |  |

Before looking at $`\nabla J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi)`$, we will make some
simplifying observations. For $`y^{\prime\prime}\neq y,y^{\prime}`$,

|     |                                                      |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{d}{d\pi_{y^{\prime\prime}}}s_{y,y^{\prime}}=0.
       ```                                                   |     | (16) |

Since $`\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}=\mu_{y^{\prime},y}`$,
$`s_{y,y^{\prime}}=-s_{y^{\prime},y}`$ and
$`p(y\succ y^{\prime})=1-p(y^{\prime}\succ y)`$, we have

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})\log\sigma(s_{y,y^{\prime}})+(1-p(y\succ y^{\prime}))\log\sigma(-s_{y,y^{\prime}})\right)`$ |  | (17) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mu_{y^{\prime},y}\left((1-p(y^{\prime}\succ y))\log\sigma(-s_{y^{\prime},y})+p(y^{\prime}\succ y)\log\sigma(s_{y^{\prime},y})\right).`$ |  |  |

Also note that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}s_{y,y^{\prime}}=\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}\left(\tau\log\frac{\pi_{y}\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}{\pi_{y^{\prime}}\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\right)=\tau\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}\log\pi_{y}=\frac{\tau}{\pi_{y}}.
``` |  | (18) |

Therefore, for all $`y\in\mathcal{Y}`$

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi)`$ | $`\displaystyle=2\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}\log\sigma(s_{y,y^{\prime}})+(1-p(y\succ y^{\prime}))\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}\log\sigma(-s_{y,y^{\prime}})\right),`$ | ([Equations 16](#A6.E16 "In Proof. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation") and [17](#A6.E17 "Equation 17 ‣ Proof. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})\sigma(-s_{y,y^{\prime}})\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}s_{y,y^{\prime}}-(1-p(y\succ y^{\prime}))\sigma(s_{y,y^{\prime}})\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}s_{y,y^{\prime}}\right)`$ | ([Proposition F.1](#A6.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition F.1. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})\sigma(-s_{y,y^{\prime}})-(1-p(y\succ y^{\prime}))\sigma(s_{y,y^{\prime}})\right)\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}s_{y,y^{\prime}}`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\sigma(s_{y,y^{\prime}})\right)\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}s_{y,y^{\prime}}`$ | ([Proposition F.2](#A6.Thmtheorem2 "Proposition F.2. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\sigma\left(\tau\log\frac{\pi_{y}\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}_{y^{\prime}}}{\pi_{y^{\prime}}\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}_{y}}\right)\right)\frac{\tau}{\pi_{y}},`$ | ([Equation 18](#A6.E18 "In Proof. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\tau\frac{\mu_{y}}{\pi_{y}}\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu_{y,y^{\prime}}\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\sigma\left(\tau\log\frac{\pi_{y}\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}_{y^{\prime}}}{\pi_{y^{\prime}}\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}_{y}}\right)\right).`$ |  |  |

∎

###### Remark F.4.

We have restricted the statement of
[Lemma F.3](#A6.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma F.3. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
to the interior of the simplex to stay consistent with the DPO
formulation, however
[Equation 15](#A6.E15 "In Lemma F.3. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
holds for any function $`\exp\circ f`$ with
$`f:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$. To see this, it suffices to
notice that $`J_{\mathrm{DPO}}`$ is a function of the ratios
$`\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{j}}`$, so for any $`\alpha>0`$
$`J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi)=J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\alpha\cdot\pi)`$ (where
$`\alpha\cdot\pi\doteq y\mapsto\alpha\cdot\pi(y)`$).

###### Lemma F.5.

The Nash equilibrium $`\pi^{*}`$ of the regularised game given in
[Equation 9](#S2.E9 "In 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
is a stationary point of online DPO iff:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p(y\succ\pi^{*})=\sum_{y^{\prime}}\pi^{*}(y^{\prime})\sigma(p(y\succ\pi^{*})-p(y^{\prime}\succ\pi^{*})).
``` |  | (19) |

###### Proof.

Let $`\pi^{*}`$ be the Nash equilibrium of the regularised game given in
[Equation 9](#S2.E9 "In 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"),
the fixed point in
[Equation 12](#S4.E12 "In 4.2 Analysis ‣ 4 Online IPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{*}(y)\propto\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\tau}p(y\succ\pi^{*})\right).
``` |  | (20) |

$`\pi^{*}`$ will be a stationary point of online DPO iff $`\pi=\pi^{*}`$
is a solution of the offline DPO problem with $`\mu=\pi^{*}`$. Since
$`\pi^{*}\in\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y})`$, we can use
[Lemma F.3](#A6.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma F.3. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"),
$`\pi^{*}`$ is a solution of the offline DPO problem iff

|     |                                     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \nabla J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi^{*})=0.
       ```                                  |     |

We have for all $`y\in\mathcal{Y}`$:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\pi_{y}}J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi^{*})`$ | $`\displaystyle=2\tau\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi^{*}(y)}\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu(y^{\prime})\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\sigma\left(\tau\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}{\pi^{*}(y^{\prime})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\right)\right)`$ | ([Equation 15](#A6.E15 "In Lemma F.3. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\tau\sum_{y^{\prime}}\pi^{*}(y^{\prime})\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\sigma\left(\tau\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}{\pi^{*}(y^{\prime})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\right)\right)`$ | ($`\mu=\pi^{*}`$) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\tau\sum_{y^{\prime}}\pi^{*}(y^{\prime})\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\sigma\left(p(y\succ\pi^{*})-p(y^{\prime}\succ\pi^{*})\right)\right)`$ | ([Equation 20](#A6.E20 "In Proof. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\tau\left(p(y\succ\pi^{*})-\sum_{y^{\prime}}\pi^{*}(y^{\prime})\sigma\left(p(y\succ\pi^{*})-p(y^{\prime}\succ\pi^{*})\right)\right),`$ |  |  |

and the result follows by using the fact that $`\tau>0`$. ∎

###### Theorem F.6.

No 2-action regularised game given in
[Equation 9](#S2.E9 "In 2.6 Nash-MD-PG ‣ 2 Background ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
has a Nash equilibrium that satisfies
[Equation 19](#A6.E19 "In Lemma F.5. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"),
except for the regularised games with
$`p(y_{1}\succ y_{2})=\frac{1}{2}`$.

###### Proof.

Let $`\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2\}`$. For this two-action problem, we can write
the preference matrix as

|     |                                                   |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       P\doteq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{2}&1-p\\
       p&\frac{1}{2}\end{array}\right),
       ```                                                |     |

where $`P_{yy^{\prime}}=p(y\succ y^{\prime})`$.

Let $`\alpha`$ be such that $`\pi^{*}=(\alpha,1-\alpha)^{\top}`$. Then

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p(y\succ\pi^{*})=P\pi^{*}=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}-p+\alpha p,\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}+\alpha p\right)^{\top},
``` |  |

and

|     |                                                  |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       p(1\succ\pi^{*})-p(2\succ\pi^{*})=\frac{1}{2}-p.
       ```                                               |     |

Then the difference between both sides of
[Equation 19](#A6.E19 "In Lemma F.5. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
for $`y=1`$ is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle p(1\succ\pi^{*})-\sum_{y^{\prime}}\pi^{*}(y^{\prime})\sigma(p(1\succ\pi^{*})-p(y^{\prime}\succ\pi^{*}))`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=1-\frac{\alpha}{2}-p+\alpha p-\alpha\sigma(0)-(1-\alpha)\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}-p\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=1-\alpha-p+\alpha p-(1-\alpha)\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}-p\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\left(1-p-\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}-p\right)\right).`$ |  |

For $`y=2`$ we get

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle p(2\succ\pi^{*})-\sum_{y^{\prime}}\pi^{*}(y^{\prime})\sigma(p(2\succ\pi^{*})-p(y^{\prime}\succ\pi^{*}))`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha p+\frac{(1-\alpha)}{2}-\alpha\sigma\left(p-\frac{1}{2}\right)-(1-\alpha)\sigma(0)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha p-\alpha\sigma\left(p-\frac{1}{2}\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\alpha p-\alpha+\alpha\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}-p\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=-\alpha\left(1-p-\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}-p\right)\right).`$ |  |

Now, if we let $`\varepsilon\doteq\frac{1}{2}-p`$, we can see that for
$`p<\frac{1}{2}`$

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}-p\right)=\sigma(\varepsilon)<\sigma(0)=\frac{1}{2}<\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon=1-p,
``` |  |

so (considering the analogous case for $`p<\frac{1}{2}`$)

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
1-p-\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}-p\right)\begin{cases}>0,&p<\frac{1}{2},\\
=0,&p=\frac{1}{2},\\
<0,&p>\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}
``` |  |

Therefore, if $`p\neq\frac{1}{2}`$, we cannot satisfy
[Equation 19](#A6.E19 "In Lemma F.5. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")
for $`y=1`$ and $`y=2`$ (note that $`\alpha=0`$ or $`\alpha=1`$ satisfy
the equation for only one $`y`$). ∎

###### Theorem F.7.

Assume that the preferences admit a Bradley-Terry model ([Rafailov
et al., 2023](#bib.bib32)), that is, there exists
$`r:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}`$ such that for all
$`y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}`$

|     |                                                  |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       p(y\succ y^{\prime})=\sigma(r(y)-r(y^{\prime})),
       ```                                               |     |

Then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{r}(y)\propto\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\tau}r(y)\right)
``` |  | (21) |

is a critical point for offline IPO for any $`\mu`$, and a stationary
point of online DPO.

###### Proof.

First, assume that $`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}\in\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y})`$
(we will deal with the general case at the end).

Let us consider the offline DPO problem with sampling distribution
$`\mu`$. Assuming that the preferences admit a Bradley-Terry model, we
know from [Rafailov et al. (2023)](#bib.bib32) that the solution of the
offline DPO problem is given by

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{r}(y)\propto\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\tau}r(y)\right).
``` |  | (22) |

Indeed, we can verify that for any $`\mu`$

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla J_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi^{r})`$ | $`\displaystyle=2\tau\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu(y^{\prime})\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\sigma\left(\tau\log\frac{\pi(y)\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{\prime})}{\pi(y^{\prime})\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}\right)\right)`$ | ($`\pi^{r}\in\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y})`$, [Equation 15](#A6.E15 "In Lemma F.3. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\tau\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi^{r}(y)}\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu(y^{\prime})\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-\sigma\left(r(y)-r(y^{\prime})\right)\right)`$ | ([Equation 21](#A6.E21 "In Theorem F.7. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation")) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2\tau\frac{\mu(y)}{\pi(y)}\sum_{y^{\prime}}\mu(y^{\prime})\left(p(y\succ y^{\prime})-p(y\succ y^{\prime})\right)`$ | (Bradley-Terry model assumption) |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=0`$ |  |  |

It follows that $`\pi^{r}`$ is an offline DPO solution under any
sampling distribution $`\mu`$, and if
$`\mu\in\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y})`$ then $`\pi^{r}`$ is the only
solution. In particular this means that $`\pi^{r}`$ is also a stationary
point for online DPO (by taking $`\mu=\pi^{r}`$).

In the case where
$`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})-\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y})`$,
we can prove the result as follows. Let
$`\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\doteq\{y:\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)=0\}`$. For
$`y\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}`$, we have $`\pi^{r}(y)=0`$ by
[Equation 21](#A6.E21 "In Theorem F.7. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation").
Since $`\pi^{r}`$ is the sampling distribution, the gradient of the
offline DPO objective in $`\mathcal{Y^{\prime}}`$ is zero (and it does
not matter that $`\log\pi^{r}(y)`$ and $`\log\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}(y)`$ are
undefined since they do not appear in the objective). Now it suffices to
note that $`\pi^{r}`$ restricted to $`\mathcal{Y}-\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}`$
is a distribution in
$`\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y}-\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})`$, so we can use the
previous case ($`\pi^{r}\in\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y}))`$ to show the
result, and it follows that $`\pi^{r}`$ satisfies the KKT conditions for
all $`y\in\mathcal{Y}`$. ∎

###### Remark F.8.

In the special case of rock-paper-scissors preferences (which do not
admit a Bradley-Terry model) with $`\pi^{\mathrm{ref}}`$ uniform and
$`\tau>0`$, we can still satisfy
[Equation 19](#A6.E19 "In Lemma F.5. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"),
since $`\pi^{*}`$ is also uniform and $`p(y\succ\pi^{*})=\frac{1}{2}`$.

###### Remark F.9.

In offline DPO, the presence of sets of measure zero guarantees the
solution is not unique. Let $`\pi^{\mathrm{DPO}}`$ be a solution of
offline DPO under sampling distribution $`\mu`$ (assume it exists).
$`\pi^{\mathrm{DPO}}`$ may not necessarily be $`\pi^{r}`$ from
([21](#A6.E21 "Equation 21 ‣ Theorem F.7. ‣ F.1 Results and Proofs ‣ Appendix F Supplementary Theoretical Study of Online DPO ‣ Human Alignment of Large Language Models through Online Preference Optimisation"))
(even if we assume that the preferences admit a Bradley-Terry model).

To see this, assume that there exists
$`\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{Y}`$ such that
$`\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset`$ and $`\mu(y)=0`$ for all
$`y\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}`$. For any $`\alpha\in(0,1]`$, define
$`\pi^{\alpha}\in\Delta^{\circ}(\mathcal{Y})`$ by

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
````

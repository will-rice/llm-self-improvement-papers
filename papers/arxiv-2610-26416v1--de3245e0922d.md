# Co-Evolving Policy and Reward for Code Generation: A Mechanistic Study of Single-Adapter Drift

###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is particularly
well suited to code generation because generated programs can be
objectively evaluated through execution. Execution also provides
behavioral information that can be reused as additional supervision. The
Synergistic Policy and Reward Co-Evolving Framework (SPARK), originally
proposed for reasoning tasks, exploits this idea by recycling training
rollouts and their evaluation results to train a model not only to
generate solutions but also to evaluate and improve them. We investigate
whether this policy–reward co-evolution approach transfers effectively
to code generation and whether execution-grounded reflection provides an
additional source of self-improvement. We compare SPARK-style rollout
recycling with execution-only Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
using Qwen2.5-Coder-3B-Instruct. Our results do not support either
hypothesis under the configuration tested. Although co-evolution
improves training performance, these gains do not generalize to held-out
problems. Compared at the same stage of training, rollout recycling
produces roughly $`34\times`$ greater KL divergence from the reference
policy than execution-only GRPO. Over training, trained tokens per GRPO
sequence fall from 185 to 86 as training pass rate increases, while
held-out performance declines or fails to sustain early gains. We
characterize these patterns as single-adapter policy drift: the shared
trainable parameters increasingly depart from pretrained code-generation
behavior without corresponding gains in generalization. The results show
that higher training reward alone is not evidence of generalizable code
self-improvement and highlight the importance of held-out evaluation and
drift diagnostics.

## I Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have become increasingly capable of code
generation, but functional correctness remains challenging. A generated
program may be syntactically valid yet fail edge cases, implement an
incorrect algorithm, or crash at runtime. Because generated programs can
be objectively evaluated through execution, code generation provides a
natural setting for reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
(RLVR), unlike many natural-language tasks that require subjective
judgments of response quality. Moreover, execution provides information
about program behavior that can serve as additional supervision.

This work investigates whether such information can be exploited through
policy–reward co-evolution. We build on the Synergistic Policy and
Reward Co-Evolving Framework (SPARK) \[[1](#bib.bib1)\], a
self-improvement framework originally developed for reasoning tasks. In
conventional RLVR, model-generated solutions, or rollouts, are used to
compute rewards and update the policy, but are not typically reused for
further training. SPARK instead recycles these rollouts and their
evaluation results to train the model to evaluate and improve its own
outputs. These capabilities co-evolve with the policy within a single
model. We investigate whether this approach transfers effectively to
code generation.

We test two hypotheses. First, we hypothesize that SPARK-style
co-evolution can improve code generation beyond an execution-only RLVR
baseline based on Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO) \[[2](#bib.bib7)\], including its ability to generalize to
problems not seen during training. Second, we hypothesize that
execution-grounded reflection, in which the model learns to revise
failing code using unit-test feedback, can provide an additional source
of self-improvement.

We evaluate these hypotheses using the Mostly Basic Python Problems
(MBPP) \[[3](#bib.bib9)\] and HumanEval \[[4](#bib.bib8)\] datasets. We
train on a subset of MBPP and evaluate generalization both on held-out
MBPP problems and on HumanEval as a separate, fully held-out benchmark.
This design allows us to distinguish improvements on the training
problems from in-distribution generalization within MBPP and
cross-benchmark generalization to HumanEval.

Our experiments do not support either hypothesis under the configuration
tested. Although co-evolution improves performance on the training
problems, these gains do not generalize to unseen problems. At the same
time, the model increasingly departs from its pretrained behavior, as
measured by KL divergence. The model also produces progressively shorter
outputs during training. We characterize these patterns as
single-adapter policy drift, arising when the same trainable parameters
are used both to generate code and to learn to evaluate it.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we adapt SPARK-style
policy–reward co-evolution to code generation by using information from
program execution as additional supervision. Second, we evaluate whether
this approach improves code-generation performance and generalization
beyond execution-only GRPO. Third, we analyze policy drift to examine
the relationship between training improvement and held-out
generalization. Our findings highlight limitations of the single-adapter
co-evolution design studied here and motivate further investigation of
alternative designs for code generation.

## II Background

### II-A RLVR and GRPO

Code-generation LLMs are typically built through large-scale pretraining
on code corpora \[[5](#bib.bib20), [6](#bib.bib21)\] followed by
instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback, which
optimizes the model against a reward model learned from human
preferences \[[7](#bib.bib19)\]. RLVR replaces that learned reward model
with an executable or rule-based verifier. DeepSeekMath introduced GRPO
in this setting, and DeepSeek-R1 showed that RLVR can produce strong
reasoning behavior \[[2](#bib.bib7), [8](#bib.bib6)\]. Related work also
improves models at inference time rather than training time, for example
by spending more computation on difficult problems \[[9](#bib.bib22)\].

GRPO samples several candidate solutions for the same problem and
compares their rewards within that group. For a task $`x`$, the model
samples $`K`$ solutions $`y_{1},\ldots,y_{K}`$, each receiving an
execution reward $`r_{i}`$, and the group-relative advantage is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\mu_{r}}{\sigma_{r}+\epsilon},
       ```                                               |     | (1) |

where $`\mu_{r}`$ and $`\sigma_{r}`$ are the mean and standard deviation
of rewards in the group and $`\epsilon`$ is a small constant for
numerical stability. A solution that scores above its group’s average is
reinforced, and one below it is discouraged. Groups in which every
solution receives the same reward carry no learning signal and are
skipped. Because ranking several candidate programs for the same problem
is exactly what a unit-test verifier provides, GRPO fits execution
feedback naturally; it is also the optimizer SPARK
uses \[[1](#bib.bib1)\].

### II-B Self-Correction and SCoRe

In this paper, _reflection_ means training the model to repair its own
failing code: given a program that failed its unit tests and the
resulting error message, the model learns to produce a corrected
program. Self-Correction via Reinforcement Learning
(SCoRe) \[[10](#bib.bib4)\] motivates this part of our work. SCoRe
trains a model in a multi-turn setting: the model first produces an
initial answer and then, conditioned on its own first answer, produces a
revised answer. SCoRe identifies two failure modes directly relevant to
execution-grounded code repair: _distribution shift_, when correction
examples do not match the model’s own current mistakes, and _behavior
collapse_, when the model learns conservative or minimal edits instead
of meaningful repairs. These ideas help interpret our reflection
results: although we use execution traces and on-policy rollouts,
reflection is still trained as auxiliary supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
rather than as a dedicated multi-turn repair objective optimized with
reinforcement learning.

### II-C SPARK-Style Rollout Recycling

For each generated program, execution provides correctness, the fraction
of tests passed, the error type, and the error message.
SPARK \[[1](#bib.bib1)\] turns these into three auxiliary objectives for
the same model: pointwise judgment (predict whether a candidate is
correct), pairwise comparison (choose the better of two candidates), and
reflection (repair failing code). Related approaches that train a policy
together with its own reward signal include Cooper \[[11](#bib.bib2)\],
CURE \[[12](#bib.bib3)\], and self-rewarding language
models \[[13](#bib.bib5)\].

In our implementation, pointwise judgment is not used (weight 0.00),
because its one-word targets such as “Correct” or “Incorrect” differ
sharply from the full programs the same model must generate. For
pairwise comparison, each failing rollout of a problem is paired with a
randomly chosen passing rollout of the same problem, with the order in
which the two are shown randomized. Reflection is the dominant auxiliary
objective, since learning to repair code from execution feedback is the
main adaptation from reasoning tasks to code. For reflection, the model
is shown its failing program and the error message, and is trained to
output a correct program for the same problem: a passing rollout from
the same batch when one exists, and otherwise the canonical solution
provided with MBPP.

| Objective  | Weight | Input                          | Supervised target                        |
| ---------- | ------ | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| Pointwise  | 0.00   | one candidate solution         | correctness label (not used)             |
| Pairwise   | 0.05   | two candidate solutions        | the better candidate                     |
| Reflection | 1.00   | failing code + execution trace | correct rollout, else canonical solution |

TABLE I: The three SPARK-style auxiliary objectives in our final
co-evolution configuration. Pointwise judgment is not used, to avoid
short-target interference with full code generation.

Table [I](#S2.T1 "TABLE I ‣ II-C SPARK-Style Rollout Recycling ‣ II Background ‣ Co-Evolving Policy and Reward for Code Generation: A Mechanistic Study of Single-Adapter Drift")
summarizes the three objectives and their weights in our final
co-evolution configuration. The weights are not normalized to sum to
one; they multiply each example’s loss before training. Setting
reflection to 1.0 keeps it on the same scale as the GRPO loss, while
pairwise at 0.05 keeps it a small auxiliary signal.

### II-D Policy Drift as a Central Risk

We fine-tune with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) \[[14](#bib.bib10)\], which
trains a small set of added weights, an _adapter_, while the pretrained
model stays frozen. A central risk in co-evolutionary training is that
the auxiliary objectives update the same adapter used for code
generation. Their targets look very different from ordinary code
completions: a pairwise answer is a single token, and a reflection
target is a program written in response to a different prompt. Training
on this mixture can shift the adapter away from how the pretrained model
writes code. Such a shift can raise training reward without improving,
and possibly while harming, performance on unseen problems. We track two
signals of this drift. The first is the KL divergence between the
trained model and the frozen pretrained model, which measures how far
its output distribution has moved. The second is the trained tokens per
GRPO sequence: the average length, in generated tokens, of the solutions
used in each GRPO update. Shorter outputs are not harmful in themselves,
but a sharp drop suggests the adapter is being pulled toward the short
auxiliary targets. Following SPARK \[[1](#bib.bib1)\], we also add the
KL divergence to the training loss as a penalty; if KL still grows
substantially, the penalty is not preventing drift.

## III Methodology

### III-A Model and Fine-Tuning Setup

All experiments fine-tune Qwen2.5-Coder-3B-Instruct with
QLoRA \[[15](#bib.bib18)\], which loads the pretrained weights in
compressed 4-bit form and keeps them frozen, so that only the LoRA
adapter is trained. Following the QLoRA recommendation, the adapter is
applied to all attention and feed-forward projection layers, giving
29.9M trainable parameters out of 3.1B (about 0.96%). We chose the 3B
model so that repeated rounds of generation, execution, and training fit
within our available hardware.

The KL penalty is computed with a standard non-negative estimator,
weighted by a coefficient $`\beta`$, and not capped: the model is
discouraged from drifting away from the frozen pretrained model, but is
not prevented from doing so. This is deliberate, since a hard limit on
drift would hide the very effect we want to measure. Stronger
constraints and early stopping are left to future work.

### III-B Training Objective

Each training iteration of the co-evolution condition updates the same
adapter in two phases. The first is a standard GRPO
update \[[2](#bib.bib7)\], which increases the probability of solutions
with positive advantage
in ([1](#S2.E1 "In II-A RLVR and GRPO ‣ II Background ‣ Co-Evolving Policy and Reward for Code Generation: A Mechanistic Study of Single-Adapter Drift"))
and decreases it for those with negative advantage. Each update is
clipped so the model cannot change too much in a single step, and the KL
penalty with weight $`\beta`$ discourages drift from the frozen
pretrained model. The second phase is auxiliary supervised fine-tuning
on the recycled rollouts, minimizing

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aux}}(\theta)=\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}\bigl(w_{\mathrm{pair}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pair}}(\theta)+w_{\mathrm{refl}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{refl}}(\theta)\bigr),
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\theta`$ are the adapter parameters,
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pair}}`$ and $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{refl}}`$ are
the standard next-token prediction losses on the pairwise and reflection
targets, computed on the target response only and not on the prompt, and
$`w_{\mathrm{pair}}`$ and $`w_{\mathrm{refl}}`$ are the weights in
Table [I](#S2.T1 "TABLE I ‣ II-C SPARK-Style Rollout Recycling ‣ II Background ‣ Co-Evolving Policy and Reward for Code Generation: A Mechanistic Study of Single-Adapter Drift").
The overall scale $`\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}`$ controls how strongly the
auxiliary phase moves the adapter relative to the GRPO phase, since both
share one optimizer. The execution-only baseline runs the GRPO phase
alone; the co-evolution condition runs both, so both update the same
shared adapter, which is the design choice under test. The values of
$`\beta`$ and $`\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}`$ for each configuration are
given in
Section [IV](#S4 "IV Results and Analysis ‣ Co-Evolving Policy and Reward for Code Generation: A Mechanistic Study of Single-Adapter Drift").

### III-C Datasets, Execution, and Reward

We use the MBPP-sanitized subset \[[3](#bib.bib9)\], a hand-verified
version of MBPP curated by its original authors to remove ambiguous task
descriptions, poorly specified requirements, and broken test assertions.
We keep the 411 problems whose unit tests can be parsed automatically
and randomly split them, with a fixed seed, into 311 training and 100
held-out problems. HumanEval \[[4](#bib.bib8)\] (164 problems) serves as
a second, fully held-out benchmark.

To score a solution, we extract the Python code from the model’s
response and run it with the problem’s unit tests in a separate,
isolated process. Each run is limited to 10 seconds and 2 GB of memory,
enough for even naive brute-force solutions on these small problems
while stopping infinite loops and runaway memory use. During training,
each unit test is run separately, and a solution’s reward is the
proportion of its unit tests that pass, reduced by a penalty if the code
has a syntax error, raises a runtime error, or times out.

These rewards are the $`r_{i}`$
in ([1](#S2.E1 "In II-A RLVR and GRPO ‣ II Background ‣ Co-Evolving Policy and Reward for Code Generation: A Mechanistic Study of Single-Adapter Drift")),
computed for the $`K`$ candidate solutions in each GRPO group. We start
with $`K=4`$ solutions per problem. If all of them receive the same
reward, the group carries no learning signal, so we sample more, up to
$`K=8`$, until the rewards differ or the cap is reached.

### III-D Experimental Design

We compare two conditions, depicted in
Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ III-D Experimental Design ‣ III Methodology ‣ Co-Evolving Policy and Reward for Code Generation: A Mechanistic Study of Single-Adapter Drift"),
where the blue path is shared by both. Condition A (green box) is the
execution-only baseline: it runs GRPO alone and discards its rollouts
after each update. Condition C (dashed orange box) adds rollout
recycling: the same rollouts are turned into auxiliary judgment and
reflection examples, and both phases update the same shared adapter.
Condition A therefore measures what execution-only GRPO can achieve, and
Condition C tests whether recycling improves on it. We evaluate several
settings of Condition C that differ in how strongly the auxiliary phase
and the KL penalty are weighted;
Section [IV](#S4 "IV Results and Analysis ‣ Co-Evolving Policy and Reward for Code Generation: A Mechanistic Study of Single-Adapter Drift")
introduces them as C-light, C-reg, and C-reg2.

### III-E Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate each checkpoint on two held-out sets. The 100 held-out MBPP
problems measure generalization to unseen problems from the benchmark
used in training. HumanEval, which is never used in training, measures
whether training generalizes to entirely unseen data from a separate
benchmark. For each problem, we generate five solutions at temperature
0.2 and report pass@1 and pass@5, where pass@$`k`$ is the probability
that at least one of $`k`$ solutions passes all tests; pass@1 is thus
the average fraction of the five that pass, and pass@5 whether any of
them does. HumanEval pass@1 is our primary metric, since it measures
single-attempt quality on fully unseen problems. We also track training
pass rate and mean reward (progress on the training problems), KL
divergence (policy drift), trained tokens per GRPO sequence (length
drift), and reflection fix rate (repair ability). Because all
experiments use a single seed and HumanEval has 164 problems, we do not
claim statistical significance for small pass@$`k`$ differences and
interpret them together with the drift diagnostics.

## IV Results and Analysis

### IV-A Preliminary Hyperparameter Study

To choose the auxiliary loss scale and KL coefficient affordably, we
first ran a shorter preliminary study: three iterations on 200 of the
311 training problems, evaluated on the same held-out MBPP problems and
on HumanEval. The study compared Condition A, execution-only GRPO with
no auxiliary phase, against two co-evolution configurations: naive
(C-light, $`\beta=0.01`$, $`\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}=0.10`$) and
regularized (C-reg, $`\beta=0.02`$, $`\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}=0.03`$).

Three patterns emerge. Execution-only GRPO scores highest on HumanEval
pass@1, narrowly ahead of C-reg. Naive co-evolution (C-light) improves
MBPP held-out pass@1 and training pass rate but degrades HumanEval,
showing the characteristic divergence pattern where HumanEval falls and
KL grows. The C-reg configuration, with a lower auxiliary loss scale
($`\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}=0.03`$) and a stronger KL penalty
($`\beta=0.02`$), recovers performance on HumanEval to close to baseline
and achieves the best MBPP pass@5.

These results motivate the more conservative configuration used in the
main experiment of the next section, which we call C-reg2:
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}`$ lowered from 0.03 to 0.02, the pairwise
weight from 0.1 to 0.05, and $`\beta`$ raised from 0.02 to 0.03. The two
changes work together: a smaller $`\lambda_{\mathrm{aux}}`$ weakens how
strongly auxiliary SFT pulls the shared adapter, while a larger
$`\beta`$ restrains drift from the frozen pretrained model.

Two factors limit how much weight we place on the pass@$`k`$
comparisons. First, HumanEval contains 164 problems and our experiments
use a single random seed, so a 2–4 percentage-point gap represents only
3–7 individual problems changing between passing and failing, which is
within the range of expected variation across seeds and too small for a
significance claim. Second, the pretrained baseline already achieves
0.7963 on HumanEval pass@1, leaving limited room for measurable
improvement. For these reasons we treat the KL divergence and
trained-token-length diagnostics, both of which show large and monotonic
differences between conditions, as the more informative signals, and
treat pass@$`k`$ gaps as supporting evidence rather than the central
finding.

the gap with held-out HumanEval evolves differently: for A-long,
training pass rate rises while HumanEval stays near baseline, whereas
for C-reg2-long, training rises as HumanEval falls. By iteration 5,
A-long’s KL is 0.0024 while C-reg2-long’s is 0.0822, about $`34\times`$
larger, and the number of trained tokens per sequence falls from 185 at
iteration 1 to 95 by iteration 5 in C-reg2-long while A-long holds near
180. By iteration 6 the trend is more extreme (KL 0.0957, tokens 86),
but the same mechanism is already visible by iteration 5.

Fig. 3: Mechanism diagnostics. Both conditions improve training pass
rate, A slightly more than C-reg2, but C-reg2 shows much larger KL drift
and a sharp decline in tokens per trained sequence. In the
train-minus-HumanEval panel, both gaps narrow because training pass rate
rises; the difference is that A’s HumanEval holds near baseline while
C-reg2’s falls.

This large drift is consistent with the HumanEval regression: even
though only 0.96% of parameters are trainable through LoRA, the
auxiliary objective is strong enough to move the model far from its
pretrained behavior. The pattern also matches recent studies of
reinforcement learning for code. For example, a study of self-improving
code agents \[[16](#bib.bib14)\] found that in 73.8% of GPU-kernel
optimization trajectories and 46.8% of algorithmic-optimization
trajectories, the agent’s reward improved without corresponding
improvement on held-out evaluation, with the gap widening as
optimization continued; similar over-optimization appears in code-repair
self-play \[[17](#bib.bib15)\]. We observe the same widening pattern in
our training regime.

We identify four failure signatures consistent with the observed
divergence. The first three concern the shared-adapter design itself.
(1) Auxiliary target heterogeneity: pairwise outputs (single-token A/B)
and reflection outputs (full programs) differ in length and structure
from normal code completions, so when the same adapter is updated by all
loss signals it is pulled toward a compromise distribution that fits
none of the objectives well. (2) Length collapse: trained tokens per
sequence fall from 185 to 86, consistent with the adapter shifting
toward the shorter auxiliary targets. (3) Single-adapter entanglement:
because policy and auxiliary objectives share the same LoRA weights, the
training pressure from each cannot be balanced through architectural
separation alone, even with reduced auxiliary loss scale and increased
KL regularization. The fourth reflects how we constructed reflection
targets, as discussed in the next section. (4) Weak reflection
supervision: the trained reflection fix rate remains below the
pretrained baseline at every iteration. The baseline repairs 4 of 34
failing programs (0.1176); C-reg2-long falls to 2/33 at iteration 1 and
recovers only to 3/36 (0.0833) by iteration 6. These counts are small
enough that we read the metric as directionally consistent with the
other diagnostics rather than as an independent result.

The progression across configurations (C-light $`\rightarrow`$ C-reg
$`\rightarrow`$ C-reg2) strengthens this reading. Naive C-light caused
immediate HumanEval degradation and large KL growth; C-reg reduced the
damage and improved MBPP pass@5; and the more conservative C-reg2 still
drifted under more data and longer training. All three showed drift away
from the pretrained distribution. In the preliminary study, C-light,
with a stronger auxiliary loss and a weaker KL penalty than C-reg,
drifted far more; C-reg2 still drifted substantially despite the weakest
auxiliary loss of the three, though it was also trained longer and on
more data. This consistency across regularization regimes suggests the
divergence is intrinsic to single-adapter co-evolution under these
objectives, not an artifact of a single poorly chosen configuration.

## V Discussion and Conclusion

SPARK-style co-evolution is applicable to code generation, but it does
not outperform execution-only GRPO on cross-benchmark HumanEval. At the
matched iteration 5, C-reg2 trails A-long on HumanEval pass@1 (0.7707
versus 0.7963) while its KL divergence is about $`34\times`$ larger. The
co-evolution branch receives strictly more information than Condition A,
including paired comparisons and repair targets, yet because all
objectives update the same shared adapter, auxiliary learning interferes
with the generation policy. Training pass rate should therefore be
treated as a diagnostic, not a final quality metric. These pass@$`k`$
magnitudes are single-seed trends rather than significance claims; the
KL and length-collapse diagnostics, which are large and monotonic, are
the more robust signal.

Single-adapter co-evolution may be too architecturally entangled for the
code setting. Code generation breaks the structural symmetry SPARK
relies on in reasoning tasks: in math and visual reasoning, rollout,
judgment, and reflection outputs all resemble a reasoning trace followed
by an answer, whereas in code, policy rollouts are full programs,
pairwise targets are single tokens, and reflection targets are full code
conditioned on a different prompt. Training one shared adapter on this
heterogeneous mixture is a harder distribution-balancing problem, and
the observed length collapse is consistent with that mismatch.

A caveat applies to how strongly this speaks to co-evolution in general.
Our auxiliary mixture is reflection-dominated, and reflection uses
canonical or correct-rollout targets rather than verified self-fix
trajectories. The length collapse and below-baseline reflection fix rate
are therefore partially entangled: we cannot fully separate
heterogeneous-target drift from weak reflection construction. The
failure we document is for a reflection-heavy, canonical-target mixture
co-updating a single adapter, not for all co-evolution weightings. Our
result aligns with SCoRe’s distribution-shift and behavior-collapse
predictions \[[10](#bib.bib4)\], and with the pattern reported
concurrently by Choi et al. \[[17](#bib.bib15)\] in code-repair
self-play, where generator-fixer training improves repair on
self-generated bugs while degrading repair on human-authored bugs.

Several prior approaches give code-generation models a reasoning or
explanation channel without SPARK-style co-evolution. Chain-of-thought,
Self-Debug, and Reflexion use prompting, execution feedback, or verbal
reflection at inference time or in separate training
formats \[[18](#bib.bib11), [19](#bib.bib12), [20](#bib.bib13)\].
Concurrent code self-improvement methods factor the policy and auxiliary
roles differently. ACE \[[21](#bib.bib16)\] trains the model both to
solve problems and to generate adversarial unit tests that expose flaws
in its own solutions, while GASP (Guided Asymmetric Self-Play) uses a
teacher-student setup in which one role poses problems and the other
solves them \[[22](#bib.bib17)\]. Both report improved code-generation
performance from self-improvement, which is consistent with our
interpretation that the limitation lies not in self-improvement on code
in general, but in the design choices studied here, particularly
co-updating a single shared adapter with heterogeneous objectives.

Within co-evolution, separating the policy adapter from a dedicated
auxiliary adapter on a shared base model is the most promising direction
we see, and we are not aware of prior work testing it for code
generation. Methods that avoid co-evolution entirely, such as preference
optimization from execution-labeled pairs, are a separate direction.
Better reflection may also require verified repair trajectories and
reference-anchored fixer training \[[17](#bib.bib15)\], together with
held-out monitoring to avoid proxy-reward
over-optimization \[[16](#bib.bib14)\]. More broadly, even when feedback
comes from executable unit tests rather than a learned reward model,
co-evolving generation and evaluation within one adapter can produce
training gains that do not transfer. Stronger supervision alone may
therefore not be sufficient; architectural separation between generator
and evaluator may also be needed.

## Limitations

This study evaluates a single model at a single scale using a single
random seed over six iterations, so the pass@$`k`$ differences we report
are trends rather than significant effects. HumanEval is also a widely
used benchmark, so we cannot rule out that some of its problems appeared
in the pretraining data of the model we fine-tune.

Because the co-evolution condition adds an auxiliary update phase, it
takes more optimizer steps than the baseline. We did not run a
step-matched comparison, in which the baseline receives the same number
of updates, so we cannot separate the effect of additional training from
that of the auxiliary objectives. We also do not compare against
ACE \[[21](#bib.bib16)\] or GASP \[[22](#bib.bib17)\], which factor the
policy and auxiliary roles differently. Finally, our auxiliary mixture
is dominated by reflection, whose weight is twenty times that of
pairwise comparison. The results therefore do not speak to co-evolution
under other weightings.

###### Abstract.

Knowledge graphs (KGs) provide reliable external knowledge for multi-hop
factual reasoning with large language models (LLMs), yet most KGs in
real-world domains such as finance and industry lack question–answer
(QA) annotations. Without such annotations, existing methods struggle to
improve LLM graph reasoning: training-free agents cannot improve their
exploration through interaction; reinforcement learning (RL) agents
depend on QA data annotated by humans or synthesized by closed-source
models; and training data is typically built offline, so its topology
mix is fixed and cannot follow the agent’s ability. We propose Evolve on
Graph (EoG), a framework that lets an LLM self-evolve its graph
reasoning without any QA annotations. Through Graph Self-Play, a single
LLM alternates between a Proposer that poses questions and a Solver that
answers them. To make questions verifiable, Symbolically Verifiable
Topology Synthesis (SVTS) samples reasoning topologies on the KG and
executes the corresponding first-order logic queries, yielding topology
skeletons with correct and complete answer sets; guided by a skeleton,
the Proposer explores the graph to pose a multi-hop question. To let
task topologies follow the Solver, a Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum
rewards the Proposer by the Solver’s score variance, keeping questions
at the Solver’s capability boundary so that complex topologies enter
training as the Solver improves. On six benchmarks over Freebase,
Wikidata, and an industrial KG, EoG consistently outperforms prompting-,
retrieval-, and agent-based methods, showing that LLM graph reasoning
can improve through self-play without QA annotations. Our code is
available at [https://github.com/Szy5/EoG](https://github.com/Szy5/EoG).

## 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) ([Team, 2025](#bib.bib47)) have shown
remarkable abilities across a wide range of natural language processing
tasks ([Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib54); [Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib52)),
yet in complex reasoning that requires deep, multi-hop, and structured
factual knowledge, they remain limited by insufficient coverage of
long-tail knowledge and by factual hallucinations ([Huang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib13)).

Knowledge graphs (KGs) ([Pan et al., 2024](#bib.bib32)) store
large-scale structured facts and provide reliable external knowledge for
LLM reasoning, but this requires the LLM to locate the facts needed at
each reasoning step in a large and heterogeneous graph. Research on this
problem has moved from one-shot graph retrieval ([Luo et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib29); [Li et al., 2025b](#bib.bib19); [Luo et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib30); [Fu et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)) toward multi-turn graph exploration
(Figure [2](#S0.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")).
Training-free agents ([Sun et al., 2024](#bib.bib39); [Chen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib5); [Tan et al., 2025](#bib.bib44); [Xu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib55)) prompt a frozen LLM to select relations and entities
step by step, while RL-based agents ([Lin et al., 2025](#bib.bib22); [Xu
et al., 2026b](#bib.bib57); [Song et al., 2026a](#bib.bib37); [Han
et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) treat the KG as an interactive environment
and train the exploration policy on question–answer data with
reinforcement learning (RL). However, most KGs in real-world settings
such as industry and healthcare ([Liang et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Zhao
et al., 2025](#bib.bib72)) come without any question–answer annotations,
and their data often cannot be handed to closed-source models ([Tan
et al., 2026](#bib.bib45)). This raises a question: _can an LLM improve
its graph reasoning on a KG using only the KG itself, without any
question–answer annotations?_

Answering this question, existing methods still face three challenges.
(1) The exploration ability of training-free agents cannot improve
through interaction. Training-free agents need no annotations and can be
applied to a new KG directly, but their exploration strategy is fixed by
the prompt and the frozen backbone, so they cannot accumulate experience
from interacting with the graph, and their performance drops sharply on
small open-source models ([Cheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib7); [Um et al.,
2026](#bib.bib48)). (2) Training data rely on human annotation or
closed-source models. RL-based agents need large amounts of
question–answer data: human annotation ([Yih et al., 2016](#bib.bib63);
[Talmor and Berant, 2018](#bib.bib43); [Gu et al., 2021](#bib.bib10);
[Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib70); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib18))
requires experts familiar with the KG schema and has to be redone for
every new KG, while automatic synthesis ([Xu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib57);
[Chang et al., 2026](#bib.bib4); [Ying et al., 2026](#bib.bib64)) relies
on closed-source models to synthesize question data. On a KG without
annotations that cannot be exposed to closed-source models, neither kind
of data is easy to obtain. (3) The topology of training tasks cannot
follow the agent’s ability. The difficulty of a graph reasoning question
lies mainly in its reasoning topology. Existing training data fix the
mix of topologies offline before training ([Bai et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib6)) and do not adjust it
as the agent learns. As the agent grows stronger, simple topologies are
already mastered while complex ones remain unsolvable, and neither
provides effective learning signals.

To address these challenges, we propose Evolve on Graph (EoG), a
framework in which a single LLM self-evolves on a KG using only the KG
itself
(Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Abstract. ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")).
First, we propose Graph Self-Play, in which the same LLM alternates
between a _Proposer_ that poses questions and a _Solver_ that answers
them. The Proposer explores the graph from real anchor entities,
recovers the facts, and writes a question, while the Solver explores the
graph to answer it. The two roles share parameters. Second, we propose
Symbolically Verifiable Topology Synthesis (SVTS), which samples
reasoning topologies on the graph and executes the corresponding
first-order logic queries, giving each task a topology skeleton and a
sound and complete answer set. The Proposer explores the graph according
to the topology skeleton and writes a multi-hop question. In this way,
training tasks and their answers come only from the KG itself, without
human annotation or closed-source models. Third, we propose the
Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum, which reinforces the Proposer according
to the Solver’s score variance, so that it keeps posing questions on the
Solver’s _capability boundary_, i.e., questions the Solver has not
mastered but can still solve. As the Solver grows stronger, more
questions on complex topologies fall on its boundary and enter training,
forming a graph curriculum that evolves with the Solver.

On six benchmarks over Freebase, Wikidata, and an industrial KG, EoG
outperforms prompting-, agent-, and retrieval-based methods without any
annotations. With Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, EoG exceeds the strongest
baseline by +7.46 (Hit@1) and +12.05 (F1) on average, and by +17.80
(Hit@1) on the industrial KG. EoG thus offers a new self-evolving path
for training LLM graph reasoning on real-world KGs without any
question–answer annotations.

#### Training data for graph reasoning.

Benchmarks such as WebQSP ([Yih et al., 2016](#bib.bib63)), CWQ ([Talmor
and Berant, 2018](#bib.bib43)), and GrailQA ([Gu et al.,
2021](#bib.bib10)) are annotated by experts familiar with the KG schema
at a high cost, and GraphWalker ([Xu et al., 2026b](#bib.bib57)) scales

on the KG through self-play, with exact answers from query execution and
topologies that advance with the Solver
(Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2. Related Work ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")),
improving the graph reasoning of LLMs.

### 3.1. Problem Formulation

Definition 1: Logic Query. A knowledge graph
$`\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{T})`$ has an entity set
$`\mathcal{E}`$, a relation set $`\mathcal{R}`$, and a triple set
$`\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{E}`$. A
multi-hop question corresponds to a first-order logic query

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (1) |     |

````math
\phi(x)=\exists\,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}:\ e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{n},
``` |  |

where $`x`$ is the answer variable, $`y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}`$ are
intermediate variables, and each atom $`e_{i}=r(u,u^{\prime})`$ links an
anchor entity or a variable to a variable and is true if and only if
$`(u,r,u^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}`$. The answer set of $`\phi`$ is its
denotation
$`\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\mathcal{G}}=\{a\in\mathcal{E}\mid\phi(a)=\mathrm{True}\}`$.

Definition 2: Graph Environment. Following GraphWalker ([Xu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib57)), an agent interacts with $`\mathcal{G}`$ in a
think–query–observe loop through two tools: get_relations returns the
relations of an entity, and get_triples returns its triples under given
relations. At step $`t`$, given the state $`s_{t}`$ of all previous
turns, the agent takes an action $`a_{t}`$, a tool call or a final
answer, and observes triples $`o_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{T}`$. A trajectory
$`\tau=(a_{1},o_{1},\ldots,a_{T})`$ collects the evidence

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (2) |  |
``` math
\mathcal{O}(\tau)=\textstyle\bigcup_{t=1}^{T}o_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{T},
``` |  |

where the last action $`a_{T}`$ gives the final answer
$`\mathcal{A}(\tau)\subseteq\mathcal{E}`$.

Problem Statement. Given $`\mathcal{G}`$ and an initial policy
$`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$, we learn a policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ that maximizes
the expected F1 of answering multi-hop questions on $`\mathcal{G}`$,
without any question–answer pair or closed-source model. Training uses
only symbolic tasks $`d=(G_{Q},\mathcal{E}_{Q},\mathcal{A}_{Q})`$ built
from $`\mathcal{G}`$, where the query graph $`G_{Q}`$ grounds a query
$`\phi`$ on $`\mathcal{G}`$, $`\mathcal{E}_{Q}`$ is its set of anchor
entities, and $`\mathcal{A}_{Q}=\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\mathcal{G}}`$
is its gold answer set.

### 3.2. Graph Self-Play

To let the exploration ability improve through interaction, we propose
Graph Self-Play, in which a single policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ plays two
roles under different role prompts with shared parameters: the Proposer
$`\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{P}}`$ turns a task into a natural-language
question, and the Solver $`\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{S}}`$ answers it. Both
roles reason inside \<think\>, call the graph tools of Definition 2
inside \<kg-query\>, and read the returned triples inside
\<information\> blocks, so both act by exploring the same graph. The
Solver aims to answer correctly, while the Proposer aims to pose valid
questions that the Solver can learn from:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (3) |  |  | $`\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{S}}}\ \mathbb{E}_{d,\;q\sim\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{P}},\;\rho\sim\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{S}}}\bigl[R_{\mathrm{S}}(\rho)\bigr],`$ |  |
|  |  |  | $`\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{P}}}\ \mathbb{E}_{d,\;q\sim\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{P}}}\bigl[R_{\mathrm{P}}(q)\bigr]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad v(q)=1,`$ |  |

where $`d`$ is a task synthesized by SVTS, $`R_{\mathrm{S}}`$ is the
Solver reward defined below, $`v(q)`$ is the validity check of
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3. Symbolically Verifiable Topology Synthesis ‣ 3. Methods ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations"),
and $`R_{\mathrm{P}}`$ of
Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4. Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum ‣ 3. Methods ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")
depends on the Solver’s performance on $`q`$. Since the two roles share
$`\theta`$, the policy gains exploration experience both when it poses a
question and when it searches for the answers. It includes question
generation, question solving, and Solver optimization.

#### Question generation.

For each task $`d=(G_{Q},\mathcal{E}_{Q},\mathcal{A}_{Q})`$, the
Proposer reads the reasoning skeleton of $`G_{Q}`$, in which only the
anchors appear as real entity names, while the intermediate and answer
entities appear as placeholders. For example, the skeleton Goodison Park
$`\xleftarrow{\texttt{arena\_stadium}}`$ Mid1@
$`\xrightarrow{\texttt{owner\_s}}`$ Ans@ should become “Who is the owner
of the team that plays at Goodison Park?”. Since the tools cannot be
called on placeholders, the Proposer explores the graph over multiple
turns from the real anchors $`\mathcal{E}_{Q}`$ to uncover the facts,
and produces a trajectory $`\tau^{\mathrm{P}}`$ that ends with a
question

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (4) |  |
``` math
q\sim\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{P}}(\cdot\mid G_{Q},\mathcal{E}_{Q},\mathcal{G}),
``` |  |

where $`q`$ is extracted from the final \<result\> block of
$`\tau^{\mathrm{P}}`$. The prompt asks $`q`$ to name the anchors, keep
the full reasoning chain of the skeleton, and hide intermediate and
answer entities, so that answering $`q`$ needs the same multi-hop
reasoning as the task.

#### Question solving.

For a question $`q`$, the Solver starts from the anchors
$`\mathcal{E}_{Q}`$ given in its prompt and samples a group of $`n`$
trajectories through multi-turn interaction with the graph,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (5) |  |
``` math
\rho^{j}\sim\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{S}}(\cdot\mid q,\mathcal{E}_{Q},\mathcal{G}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,n,
``` |  |

where the Solver decides which relations and entities to explore and
when to stop, and each trajectory ends with a JSON list of entity names
in an \<answer\> block, which gives the prediction
$`\hat{y}^{j}=\mathcal{A}(\rho^{j})`$. Each trajectory is scored by
answer and format:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (6) |  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{S}}(\rho^{j})=\lambda_{\mathrm{ans}}\,\mathrm{F1}\bigl(\hat{y}^{j},\mathcal{A}_{Q}\bigr)+\lambda_{\mathrm{fmt}}\,R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(\rho^{j}),
``` |  |

where the first term is the F1 between the predicted and gold answer
sets, $`R_{\mathrm{fmt}}\!\in\![0,1]`$ measures how well the trajectory
follows the think–query–observe structure, and the weights are
$`\lambda_{\mathrm{ans}}=0.8`$ and $`\lambda_{\mathrm{fmt}}=0.2`$. We
use F1 rather than exact match because a task may have several gold
answers, so a prediction that recovers only part of $`\mathcal{A}_{Q}`$
still receives partial credit. Since $`R_{\mathrm{S}}`$ scores only the
final answer, any exploration path that reaches $`\mathcal{A}_{Q}`$ is
rewarded, so the Solver learns its own exploration strategy from
interaction.

#### Solver optimization.

The Solver is updated with GRPO. For a group $`\{\rho^{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}`$
on the same $`q`$, the relative advantage is
$`\hat{A}^{j}=\bigl(R_{\mathrm{S}}(\rho^{j})-\mathrm{mean}\bigr)/\mathrm{std}`$,
with $`\mathrm{mean},\mathrm{std}`$ over
$`\{R_{\mathrm{S}}(\rho^{k})\}_{k=1}^{n}`$, and the objective is

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| (7) |  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{S}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\rho^{j}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\rho^{j}|}\min\!\Big(`$ | $`\displaystyle w^{j}_{t}\hat{A}^{j},`$ | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{clip}(w^{j}_{t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}^{j}\Big)\Bigg]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{S}}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right],`$ |  |  |  |

where
$`w^{j}_{t}=\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{S}}\bigl(a^{j}_{t}\mid s^{j}_{t}\bigr)/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}^{\mathrm{S}}\bigl(a^{j}_{t}\mid s^{j}_{t}\bigr)`$
is the token-level importance ratio at step $`t`$, $`\epsilon`$ is the
clipping threshold, and $`\beta`$ is the KL coefficient.

### 3.3. Symbolically Verifiable Topology Synthesis

To obtain reliable training data without human annotation or
closed-source models, we propose SVTS, which views the KG as a
first-order logic structure and takes query execution on the graph as
the source of gold answers. It includes three steps: defining query
patterns, grounding and execution, and question verification.

#### Query patterns.

Following complex logical queries over KGs ([Ren and Leskovec,
2020](#bib.bib34)), we define six query patterns as reasoning
topologies:

|     |     |                                              |     |
|-----|-----|----------------------------------------------|-----|
| (8) |     |
             ``` math
             \mathcal{P}=\{1p,\,2p,\,3p,\,2i,\,ip,\,pi\},
             ```                                           |     |

where $`p`$ and $`i`$ denote projection (one hop along a relation) and
intersection (multiple constraints on one variable), respectively;
$`1p`$–$`3p`$ are $`1`$- to $`3`$-hop chain queries, $`2i`$ is a two-way
intersection, and $`ip`$, $`pi`$ are nested compositions of projection
and intersection. For example, the $`2p`$ and $`2i`$ query patterns are
written as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| (9) |  | $`\displaystyle\phi_{2p}(x)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\exists y:\ r_{1}(v_{a},y)\wedge r_{2}(y,x),`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\phi_{2i}(x)`$ | $`\displaystyle=r_{1}(v_{a_{1}},x)\wedge r_{2}(v_{a_{2}},x),`$ |  |

where $`\phi_{2p}`$ follows a two-hop chain from one anchor, whereas
$`\phi_{2i}`$ requires the answer to satisfy constraints from two
anchors; the logical forms of all six patterns are listed in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations").
On Freebase, the synthesized questions cover all six patterns, with
shares ranging from $`9.5\%`$ ($`pi`$) to $`22.4\%`$ ($`1p`$), so the
Solver must learn path expansion, constraint intersection, and
intermediate-variable tracking rather than fit a single pattern
(Appendix [C.4](#A3.SS4 "C.4. Synthetic Data Analysis ‣ Appendix C Experimental Details ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")).

#### Grounding and execution.

Given a pattern $`pat\in\mathcal{P}`$, grounding instantiates $`pat`$ on
real facts and extracts its grounded query graphs
$`\mathcal{I}_{pat}=f_{pat}(\mathcal{G})`$, where $`f_{pat}`$ is the
corresponding extraction procedure: it samples a seed entity from a seed
pool and walks on $`\mathcal{G}`$ to bind every variable and relation of
$`pat`$ to a real triple, and it repeats until a target number of tasks
per pattern is collected or an attempt budget runs out. The walk only
proposes the structure of a task; its answers are decided by execution
below. For example, a $`2p`$ grounded query graph sampled from Freebase
is Shaping $`\xrightarrow{\texttt{recipes}}`$ Country Sourdough
Bread $`\xrightarrow{\texttt{techniques}}`$ $`x`$, whose execution
returns two answers, Kneading and Slashing dough. We then compile each
$`G_{Q}\in\mathcal{I}_{pat}`$ deterministically into an executable KG
query $`Q`$ for the logic query $`\phi`$ of
Equation [1](#S3.E1 "In 3.1. Problem Formulation ‣ 3. Methods ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations"),
and evaluate it on $`\mathcal{G}`$ to obtain the complete gold answer
set, i.e., the denotation of $`\phi`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (10) |  |
``` math
\mathcal{A}_{Q}=\mathrm{Execute}(Q,\mathcal{G})=\llbracket\phi\rrbracket_{\mathcal{G}},
``` |  |

where queries whose answers are empty, abnormally large, or unnameable
are discarded rather than truncated, so that every kept task has an
answer set that can be checked objectively. Unlike random-walk
synthesis, which takes a single entity of the walk as the answer,
execution returns every entity that satisfies the query. This entity is
always one of these answers, but when $`|\mathcal{A}_{Q}|>1`$, scoring a
complete prediction against it alone would give an F1 of only
$`2/(1+|\mathcal{A}_{Q}|)`$. SVTS runs once per KG before training and
only needs query access to the KG, so applying EoG to a new KG requires
only its query endpoint. It finally outputs the task set

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (11) |  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SVTS}}=\bigl\{(G_{Q}^{(i)},\mathcal{E}_{Q}^{(i)},\mathcal{A}_{Q}^{(i)})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
``` |  |

where $`N`$ is the task count
(Algorithm [1](#algorithm1 "In Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")
in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")).

#### Question verification.

Execution guarantees that $`\mathcal{A}_{Q}`$ answers the query $`Q`$,
but not that the question $`q`$ written by the Proposer expresses the
same query. To keep $`q`$ faithful to its task, we collect the evidence
$`\mathcal{O}(\tau^{\mathrm{P}})`$ retrieved by the Proposer and let the
Solver answer $`q`$ using *only* that evidence as a verifier:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (12) |  |
``` math
\sigma\sim\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{S}}\bigl(\cdot\mid q,\mathcal{E}_{Q},\mathcal{O}(\tau^{\mathrm{P}})\bigr).
``` |  |

A question is valid only if it passes a rule check
$`v_{\mathrm{rule}}(q)`$, which requires that $`q`$ can be extracted
from the \<result\> block, has a reasonable length, and names no
intermediate or answer entity, and if the verification answer matches
the gold answer:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (13) |  |
``` math
v(q)=v_{\mathrm{rule}}(q)\cdot\mathbb{I}\bigl[\,\mathcal{A}(\sigma)=\mathcal{A}_{Q}\,\bigr]\in\{0,1\},
``` |  |

where $`\mathbb{I}[\cdot]`$ is the indicator function. Only questions
with $`v(q)=1`$ enter reward computation and policy update. The rule
check removes questions that leak intermediate or answer entities. The
evidence check removes questions that are ambiguous, under-specified, or
inconsistent with their task: since the verifier sees only the
Proposer’s triples, it recovers exactly $`\mathcal{A}_{Q}`$ only if
these triples, read through $`q`$, determine the complete answer set.
The check is conservative: a faithful question may be rejected when the
single verification trajectory $`\sigma`$ fails, lowering the yield of
valid questions, but a question whose evidence does not determine
$`\mathcal{A}_{Q}`$ is never accepted.

### 3.4. Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum

To let task topologies follow the Solver’s ability, we propose the
Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum, which rewards the Proposer by the
Solver’s score variance, so that it keeps posing questions on the
Solver’s capability boundary. It includes three parts: the capability
boundary, the Proposer reward, and Proposer optimization.

#### Capability boundary.

For a valid question $`q`$ with Solver trajectories
$`\{\rho^{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}`$, let
$`s_{j}=\mathrm{F1}(\hat{y}^{j},\mathcal{A}_{Q})\in[0,1]`$ be the answer
score of $`\rho^{j}`$ in
Equation [6](#S3.E6 "In Question solving. ‣ 3.2. Graph Self-Play ‣ 3. Methods ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")
and $`\bar{s}(q)`$ their mean. The Solver’s score variance on $`q`$,
which measures how differently its trajectories score, is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (14) |  |
``` math
\sigma^{2}(q)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(s_{j}-\bar{s}(q)\bigr)^{2}.
``` |  |

Since the advantages in
Equation [7](#S3.E7 "In Solver optimization. ‣ 3.2. Graph Self-Play ‣ 3. Methods ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")
are normalized within a group, a question on which all $`n`$
trajectories receive the same score ($`\sigma^{2}(q)=0`$), such as one
the Solver always or never solves, gives almost no learning signal ([Yu
et al., 2026b](#bib.bib67)). The Solver learns mainly from questions
with $`\sigma^{2}(q)>0`$, which it has not mastered but can still partly
solve, such as one it answers in full on some trajectories and only in
part on others; we call them its *capability boundary*.

#### Proposer reward.

We therefore reward the Proposer with a Gaussian boundary reward on the
Solver’s score variance:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (15) |  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{P}}(q)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\bigl(\sigma^{2}(q)-\tfrac{1}{4}\bigr)^{2}}{2h}\right),
``` |  |

where $`h>0`$ controls the peak width. Since scores lie in $`[0,1]`$,
$`\sigma^{2}(q)\leq\tfrac{1}{4}`$, with equality only when half of the
trajectories score $`1`$ and the rest $`0`$. The reward is thus minimal,
$`\exp(-\tfrac{1}{32h})`$, when all scores are equal, higher on the
capability boundary, and maximal at such an even split. As the scores
are F1 against the complete answer sets of SVTS, a question the Solver
answers only in part stays on the boundary instead of counting as
solved. The reward also stays low when every trajectory fails, so the
Proposer is not reinforced by questions the Solver cannot learn from.

Proposition 1. With equal format scores, $`R_{\mathrm{P}}`$ is maximal
only where the learning signal peaks and minimal where it vanishes.

and a proof in

#### Proposer optimization.

Since the Proposer writes a single question per task, it has no group of
samples for a relative advantage; we therefore update it with REINFORCE,
taking the Proposer reward as the return of its whole question
trajectory:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
| (16) |  |
``` math
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{P}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\Bigg[R_{\mathrm{P}}(q)\sum_{t=1}^{|\tau^{\mathrm{P}}|}\log\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{P}}\!\bigl(a^{\mathrm{P}}_{t}\mid s^{\mathrm{P}}_{t}\bigr)\Bigg],
``` |  |

where $`a^{\mathrm{P}}_{t}`$ and $`s^{\mathrm{P}}_{t}`$ are the action
and state of the Proposer at step $`t`$. The two roles update
alternately: as the Solver masters a kind of question, its scores
approach $`1`$, so $`\sigma^{2}`$ and $`R_{\mathrm{P}}`$ fall, driving
the Proposer toward harder questions; the boundary thus moves outward
and the two roles co-evolve into an adaptive curriculum.

#### Training loop.

At each training step, the shared policy first acts as the Proposer on a
batch of SVTS tasks and writes one question per task. Each question is
checked by
Equation [13](#S3.E13 "In Question verification. ‣ 3.3. Symbolically Verifiable Topology Synthesis ‣ 3. Methods ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations");
questions that fail the check are skipped by both updates, and for every
valid question the Solver samples $`n`$ trajectories on the graph, which
yield both the Solver rewards $`R_{\mathrm{S}}`$ and, through
$`\sigma^{2}(q)`$, the Proposer reward $`R_{\mathrm{P}}`$. The same
parameters $`\theta`$ are then updated with GRPO on the Solver
trajectories and with REINFORCE on the Proposer trajectories. Because
both roles are updated at every step, a change in the Solver’s ability
is reflected in $`R_{\mathrm{P}}`$ at the next step, so the curriculum
follows the Solver without a manually designed difficulty schedule. At
test time, only the Solver is used, and it answers human-written
questions with the same tools. The whole loop thus uses only the graph:
tasks come from SVTS, answers from query execution, and difficulty from
the Solver’s own score variance on each question.

## 4. Experiments

We answer five research questions (RQs). RQ1: Without any annotations,
can EoG improve LLM graph reasoning on different KGs? RQ2: Is each
component of EoG necessary? RQ3: Can Graph Self-Play improve exploration
through interaction? RQ4: Can SVTS replace human annotation as the
training data source? RQ5: Can the curriculum advance from simple to
complex topologies?

### 4.2. Main Results (RQ1)

As shown in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Proposer reward. ‣ 3.4. Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum ‣ 3. Methods ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")
and
Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1. Experimental Setup ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations"),
without any question–answer annotations, EoG enables open-source LLMs to
outperform prompting-, agent-, and retrieval-based methods on all three
KGs. With Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct, it exceeds the strongest baseline by
7.46 points in average Hit@1, and by 11.61 and 10.35 points on the
multi-hop CWQ and GTSQA, showing that exploration learned through
interaction handles complex questions better than retrieval or
prompting. On Wikidata, EoG trains directly on the target KG and leads
all annotation-free methods in Hit@1 on GTSQA and WikiWebQuestions, so
the same pipeline carries over unchanged to a KG with a different
schema. On IndustryCarBench, where annotation-based RL agents cannot be
trained, EoG trains on the KG alone and exceeds the strongest baseline,
ToG, by 17.80 points in Hit@1, so it applies directly to new KGs that
lack annotations. On Qwen-2.5-3B and LLaMA-3.1-8B, against prompting
baselines and ReAct, EoG leads in average Hit@1 and F1 (e.g., by 14.44
Hit@1 over SC on LLaMA-3.1-8B), and EoG on Qwen-2.5-3B (46.02) already
surpasses PoG on Qwen-2.5-7B (45.54). On schema unseen in the
annotations, EoG even outperforms the fully supervised KG-R1^(†) trained
on WebQSP or GrailQA by 2.65 and 5.84 points in F1
(Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1. Experimental Setup ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")),
showing that the exploration it learns through self-play over the whole
KG generalizes well beyond the schema covered by annotations.

### 4.3. Ablation Study (RQ2)

As shown in
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.4. Analysis of Graph Self-Play (RQ3) ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations"),
we conduct an ablation study on Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct-based EoG with five
ablation settings: removing the Solver update; removing the Proposer
update; replacing the query execution of SVTS with random walks ([Xu
et al., 2026b](#bib.bib57)), which walk backward from an answer entity
and take the start entity as the only answer of the question; and
replacing the Gaussian boundary reward with the adversarial reward
$`1-\bar{c}`$ or the triangular boundary reward
$`1-2|\bar{c}-\frac{1}{2}|`$, where $`\bar{c}`$ is the fraction of
Solver trajectories that hit a gold answer. All five settings lower the
Hit@1 on the multi-hop CWQ by 4.11–14.65 points, showing that Graph
Self-Play, SVTS, and the curriculum each matter most on multi-hop

from interaction.

Impact of Removing the Solver Update. We observe an interesting
phenomenon: without Solver updates, the CWQ Hit@1 still rises from 16.06

graph exploration, which transfers to solving via the shared parameters.

only when both roles are updated alternately do proposing and solving
reinforce each other.

### 4.5. Analysis of SVTS (RQ4)

To examine whether SVTS can replace human annotation, we design
KG-R1^(†), a variant of KG-R1 ([Lin et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)) that
explores the full Freebase instead of a 2-hop subgraph pre-extracted for
each question and differs from EoG only in being trained on annotated
questions.

Comparison with Human Annotation. As shown in
Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Proposer reward. ‣ 3.4. Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum ‣ 3. Methods ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations"),
KG-R1^(†) is stronger in distribution, but out of distribution, the
annotation-free EoG achieves an average F1 3.05 and 3.65 higher than
KG-R1^(†) trained on WebQSP and GrailQA, and trails KG-R1^(†) trained on
27,623 annotated CWQ questions by only 4.49. To examine generalization
beyond the annotated schema, we split the Freebase test sets into seen
and unseen schema by whether all relations in their SPARQL query appear
in the training set of KG-R1^(†), and compare them on unseen schema
(Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1. Experimental Setup ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")).
EoG exceeds KG-R1^(†) trained on WebQSP and GrailQA by 2.65 and 5.84 in
F1, although both lead on seen schema; KG-R1^(†) trained on CWQ still
leads, but by 3.33 instead of 5.05. We believe that the gains of
KG-R1^(†) from annotation are confined to the schema covered by its
training set, whereas EoG trains on SVTS tasks sampled across the whole
KG, so its graph reasoning transfers better to unseen schema.

Comparison with Random-Walk Synthesis. SVTS outperforms random walks on
all three datasets, by 14.65 in Hit@1 on CWQ
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.4. Analysis of Graph Self-Play (RQ3) ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")),
for two reasons. First, random walks favor chain paths and rarely yield
the intersection or nested questions that multi-constraint reasoning
needs. Second, a random walk takes only its start entity as the answer,
although the same relations often reach several entities; correct Solver
answers are then judged wrong, so the Proposer can hack the boundary
reward with questions whose answers are not unique. SVTS instead
executes the query to obtain the complete answer set, which removes such
incorrect rewards. Moreover, the synthesized questions rarely resemble
the test questions: only 2.28% of them have a nearest Freebase test
question with cosine similarity above 0.90
(Appendix [C.4](#A3.SS4 "C.4. Synthetic Data Analysis ‣ Appendix C Experimental Details ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")),
so the gains of EoG do not come from test-set leakage into training.

### 4.6. Analysis of the Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum (RQ5)

Impact of Removing the Proposer Update. Without Proposer updates, task
topologies no longer follow the Solver: the Solver reward of this
variant keeps rising, yet its CWQ Hit@1 drops after step 80
(Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1. Experimental Setup ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")(a,
d)), i.e., the Solver overfits the static task distribution of a
Proposer without updates. This indicates that a Proposer without updates
cannot shift to topologies that the Solver has not mastered, so the
Solver stalls on early, simple questions and trails EoG by 14.36 points
in Hit@1 on the multi-hop CWQ.

Impact of the Boundary Reward. We compare three Proposer rewards: the
adversarial reward, the triangular boundary reward, and the Gaussian
boundary reward. During training, the Solver occasionally produces
overlong responses, which are truncated and temporarily lower its
reward. With the adversarial reward, the Proposer cannot tell a hard
question from such a truncated response and is rewarded most when the
Solver performs worst: its reward drop lasts the longest among the three
rewards, and its CWQ Hit@1 is 7.99 below that of the Gaussian boundary
reward
(Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.4. Analysis of Graph Self-Play (RQ3) ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")).
This shows that the Gaussian boundary reward is not reinforced by the
Solver’s truncated failures, so the curriculum returns to the Solver’s
capability boundary sooner after its outputs break down.

Evolution of Task Difficulty. As shown in
Figure [7](#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4.3. Ablation Study (RQ2) ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations"),
the synthesis success rate of the Proposer rises on all three backbones
(a), complex topologies rise from 17.9 to 31.3 while simple ones level
off near 34, narrowing the gap to 2.5 points (b), and $`3p`$ keeps
rising from 16 to 41 after simple patterns plateau (c), while the Solver
reward does not decline
(Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.1. Experimental Setup ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")(d)).
This shows that the Proposer shifts toward complex topologies as the
Solver improves, forming an adaptive graph curriculum that advances
dynamically from easy to hard questions over time, without a manual
schedule.

### 4.7. Case Study

Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Case Study ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")
shows one complete self-play round on a $`2i`$ task whose executed
answer is Jersey. The Proposer finds that Jèrriais is spoken in Jersey,
checks that French is also spoken there, and asks “In which country are
both Jèrriais and French spoken?”, which names both anchors but hides
the answer. The Solver retrieves the countries of each language, among
them Dominica, Mali, and Chad for French, and keeps only Jersey, the one
country shared with Jèrriais. The Solver thus answers by intersecting
two entity sets on the graph rather than recalling one fact, the
reasoning that $`2i`$ tasks train.

## 5. Conclusion

We present EoG, in which a single LLM self-evolves its graph reasoning
on a KG using only the KG itself, aimed at KGs that have no
question–answer annotations and cannot be handed to closed-source
models. Graph Self-Play lets one policy explore the graph as both the
Proposer and the Solver, SVTS derives verifiable tasks with complete
answer sets by executing logic queries on the KG, and the Boundary-Aware
Graph Curriculum keeps the questions on the Solver’s capability boundary
as it shifts from simple to complex topologies. On six benchmarks over
three KGs, EoG outperforms prompting-, agent-, and retrieval-based
methods, and on schema unseen in the annotations it exceeds KG-R1^(†)
trained on WebQSP or GrailQA. These results show that a KG can supply
its own training tasks, answers, and curriculum, offering a direction
for self-evolving graph reasoning on real-world KGs that lack
annotations, requiring nothing beyond the query endpoint of each new
target KG.

## Appendix A Proof of Proposition 1

Proposition 1. With equal format scores, $`R_{\mathrm{P}}`$ is maximal
only where the learning signal peaks and minimal where it vanishes.

###### Proof.

Let $`q`$ be a valid question with Solver trajectories
$`\{\rho^{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}`$, answer scores $`s_{j}\in[0,1]`$ and mean
$`\bar{s}`$ as in
Equation [14](#S3.E14 "In Capability boundary. ‣ 3.4. Boundary-Aware Graph Curriculum ‣ 3. Methods ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations"),
and format scores that all equal $`f`$. Then
$`R_{\mathrm{S}}(\rho^{j})=\lambda_{\mathrm{fmt}}f+\lambda_{\mathrm{ans}}s_{j}`$
is a positive affine map of $`s_{j}`$, and since the group-normalized
advantage is invariant to such maps,
$`\hat{A}^{j}=(s_{j}-\bar{s})/\sigma`$ with
$`\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}(q)}`$ when $`\sigma>0`$, and $`\hat{A}^{j}=0`$
when $`\sigma=0`$.

*Learning signal.* We measure the Solver’s learning signal on $`q`$ by
the mean absolute advantage of its group,

|      |     |                                                        |     |
|------|-----|--------------------------------------------------------|-----|
| (17) |     |
              ``` math
              S(q)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl|\hat{A}^{j}\bigr|.
              ```                                                     |     |

If $`\sigma^{2}(q)=0`$, all scores are equal, every $`\hat{A}^{j}=0`$,
and $`S(q)=0`$. Otherwise the normalized advantages satisfy
$`\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}(\hat{A}^{j})^{2}=1`$, so by the Cauchy–Schwarz
inequality $`S(q)\leq 1`$, with equality if and only if all
$`|\hat{A}^{j}|`$ are equal, i.e., half of the scores lie at
$`\bar{s}+\sigma`$ and the other half at $`\bar{s}-\sigma`$.

*Maximum and minimum.* Since $`0\leq s_{j}\leq 1`$, we have
$`s_{j}^{2}\leq s_{j}`$ and hence
$`\sigma^{2}(q)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}s_{j}^{2}-\bar{s}^{2}\leq\bar{s}(1-\bar{s})\leq\tfrac{1}{4}`$,
where the first inequality is tight only if every $`s_{j}\in\{0,1\}`$
and the second only if $`\bar{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}`$. Hence
$`R_{\mathrm{P}}(q)`$ reaches its maximum $`1`$ only when half of the
trajectories score $`1`$ and the other half score $`0`$; there
$`\hat{A}^{j}=\pm 1`$ and $`S(q)=1`$, its largest possible value.
Conversely, $`R_{\mathrm{P}}(q)`$ takes its minimum
$`\exp(-\tfrac{1}{32h})`$ exactly when $`\sigma^{2}(q)=0`$, which is
exactly when $`S(q)=0`$. With $`n=5`$, an even split is impossible and
$`\sigma^{2}(q)\leq 0.24`$, attained only by a $`2{:}3`$ split between
scores $`0`$ and $`1`$. ∎

Remark. The adversarial and triangular rewards of
Section [4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3. Ablation Study (RQ2) ‣ 4. Experiments ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")
are functions of the success rate $`\bar{c}`$. The adversarial reward
peaks at $`\bar{c}=0`$, where every trajectory misses, all scores are
$`0`$, and $`S(q)=0`$. The triangular reward peaks at
$`\bar{c}=\tfrac{1}{2}`$ but, like the adversarial reward, does not
reflect how much of the answer set each trajectory recovers.

#### SVTS

As shown in
Table [6](#A2.T6 "Table 6 ‣ SVTS ‣ Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")
and
Algorithm [1](#algorithm1 "In Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations"),
SVTS repeats grounding, compilation, and execution for each of the six
query patterns until it collects $`N`$ tasks or exhausts $`T`$ attempts.
Grounding walks from a seed entity to bind the variables and relations
of the pattern to real facts; the resulting query graph is compiled into
an executable query and evaluated on the graph, and queries with empty,
at least $`\kappa`$, or unnameable answers are discarded before
training.

### C.1. Datasets

On Freebase, WebQSP ([Yih et al., 2016](#bib.bib63)) contains single-hop
and two-hop questions, CWQ ([Talmor and Berant, 2018](#bib.bib43))
extends WebQuestions with multi-entity constraints, conjunctions, and
superlatives, and GrailQA ([Gu et al., 2021](#bib.bib10)) tests i.i.d.,
compositional, and zero-shot generalization. On Wikidata,
GTSQA ([Cattaneo et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)) provides $`1{,}622`$ test
questions with gold answer subgraphs, and WikiWebQuestions ([Xu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib56)) migrates WebQuestions to Wikidata with re-annotated
SPARQL queries. IndustryCarBench is a domain benchmark that we build on
a real-world automotive-aesthetics KG covering car models, design
elements, attributes, colors, materials, and brands; it contains $`500`$
test questions written by domain experts, with gold answers executable
on the graph and has no annotated training set. EoG never uses the
annotated training questions of any benchmark; only KG-R1^(†) does.

### C.2. Baselines and Metrics

All baselines use the same backbone as the compared EoG model. IO
answers directly, CoT ([Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib54)) reasons step by
step, and SC ([Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib52)) takes a majority vote
over sampled CoT chains. ReAct ([Yao et al., 2022](#bib.bib62))
interleaves thoughts, graph-tool calls, and observations with the tools
and Solver prompt of EoG. ToG ([Sun et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)) runs
LLM-guided beam search over entities and relations, PoG ([Chen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib5)) decomposes a question into subgoals with adaptive
exploration and self-correction, and CoR ([Liu et al.,
2026c](#bib.bib24)) builds a relation chain and filters the reachable
candidates with the question constraints. Text-Based Retrieval selects
the top-$`k`$ verbalized triples by embedding similarity, and Graph
Reranker reranks the triples within the $`L`$-hop neighborhood of the
anchors. Following ([Sun et al., 2024](#bib.bib39); [Chen et al.,
2024](#bib.bib5)), Hit@1 checks whether the normalized prediction
exactly matches any gold answer, and F1 measures the overlap between the
predicted and gold answer lists of each question.

### C.3. Implementation Details

We train EoG with Verl on 8 GH200 GPUs across two nodes, using FSDP with
CPU offloading of parameters and optimizer states and SGLang for
multi-turn rollouts;
Table [7](#A3.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Appendix C Experimental Details ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")
lists the hyperparameters. KG-R1^(†) converts the official training
splits of WebQSP, CWQ, and GrailQA into the Solver format, which yields
$`3{,}058`$, $`27{,}623`$, and $`36{,}954`$ questions, and trains one
model per dataset. It uses the same backbone, Solver prompt, graph
tools, and Freebase endpoint as EoG, so only the source of the training
questions differs. Its reward is the F1 of EoG without the format term.

### C.4. Synthetic Data Analysis

#### Topology coverage.

Synthesized questions on Freebase cover all six patterns: the chain
patterns $`1p`$ ($`22.4\%`$), $`2p`$ ($`20.6\%`$), and $`3p`$
($`16.5\%`$), and the intersection patterns $`2i`$ ($`17.7\%`$), $`pi`$
($`9.5\%`$), and $`ip`$ ($`13.3\%`$). The Solver must learn path
expansion, constraint intersection, and intermediate-variable tracking
rather than fit a single pattern.

#### Domain coverage.

The questions cover 20 domains, including Places & Geography
($`14.2\%`$), Film & Television ($`13.2\%`$), Government, Law & Military
($`12.6\%`$), People & Society ($`8.7\%`$), and Sports ($`8.4\%`$); the
top three together account for only $`40.0\%`$. Training signals are
therefore not confined to a few dense subgraphs of Freebase.

#### Test-set leakage.

To rule out test-set leakage, we encode each question with BGE-M3 and
take the maximum cosine similarity between each synthetic question and
the questions of each Freebase test set
(Figure [8](#A3.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ Appendix C Experimental Details ‣ Evolve on Graph: Self-Evolving Knowledge Graph Reasoning without Question Annotations")).
Only $`4.57\%`$, $`2.28\%`$, and $`0.71\%`$ of the synthetic questions
exceed $`0.85`$, $`0.90`$, and $`0.95`$, respectively, and the mean
similarity is $`0.634`$, so there is no meaningful leakage between the
synthetic training data and the Freebase evaluation sets.
````

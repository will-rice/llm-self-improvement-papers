###### Abstract

We introduce SERF (Self-Regularized Flow Language Models), a simple
self-supervised framework for improving continuous diffusion language
models by exploiting the denoising trajectory itself. While continuous
flow-based language models offer a promising alternative to
autoregressive generation, their training objectives typically rely on
supervision from clean target tokens, leaving the denoising trajectory
underexploited as a learning signal. SERF addresses this limitation
through noisy-to-clean self-distillation: an exponential moving average
(EMA) teacher receives a cleaner version of the student’s noisy input
along the same interpolation path and provides additional supervision.
We investigate two variants: SERF-L, which aligns the student’s
intermediate representations with deeper teacher representations, and
SERF-P, which aligns their output token distributions, either at the
student’s output or at an early student layer. All variants augment the
standard training objective without requiring external pretrained models
or modifying the inference procedure. Experiments across three
continuous language-model paradigms (one-hot FLM, latent ELF, and
hyper-spherical S-FLM) demonstrate substantial improvements in training
efficiency, generation quality, and few-step generation. In particular,
SERF achieves up to $`7.7\times`$ wall-clock training acceleration on
OpenWebText, improves MAUVE by $`24.3\%`$ and GSM8K accuracy by up to
$`28.8\%`$, and matches baseline performance with up to $`64\times`$
fewer sampling steps. These results establish noisy-to-clean
self-distillation as an effective approach to improving continuous
diffusion language models.

## 1 Introduction

Current leading language models ([Achiam et al., 2023](#bib.bib2); [Anil
et al., 2023](#bib.bib3); [Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib4)) primarily rely
on autoregressive decoding, generating one token at a time. While
training benefits from the parallelism of transformer architectures and
teacher forcing, inference remains fundamentally sequential, creating a
major bottleneck as models and sequence lengths scale. Diffusion
language models ([Khanna et al., 2025](#bib.bib5); [Nie et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6); [Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)) have recently emerged
as a promising alternative, offering significantly more flexibility in
their generative trajectories. By learning to reverse a corruption
process applied to the entire sequences simultaneously, they can
generate multiple tokens in parallel and potentially enable
substantially faster inference.

While most diffusion language models rely on discrete corruption
processes such as masking ([Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Arriola et
al., 2025](#bib.bib8)) or token replacement ([Schiff et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9); [Lou et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)), recent work has
revisited continuous diffusion and flow-based formulations for
language ([Dieleman et al., 2022](#bib.bib12); [Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11)). Such formulations have been central to advances in
image and video generation and provide smooth generative trajectories
that support efficient ODE- and SDE-based sampling ([Lipman et al.,
2023](#bib.bib14); [Song et al., 2021](#bib.bib15)). They are also
particularly well-suited to few-step generation, as continuous
trajectories can be effectively approximated with a small number of
integration steps ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Kang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib29)). Importantly, they make techniques developed for
continuous generative models directly applicable to language, including
guidance ([Ho and Salimans, 2022](#bib.bib17); [Karras et al.,
2024](#bib.bib18)) and flow-based distillation ([Song et al.,
2023](#bib.bib19); [Boffi et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)). These properties
make continuous diffusion a promising and versatile framework for text
generation.

Interestingly, recent advances in diffusion modeling for image synthesis
have shown that augmenting the standard denoising objective with
auxiliary self-supervised signals can substantially improve both
training efficiency and generation quality ([Yu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Kouzelis et al., 2025](#bib.bib22); [Chefer et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24); [Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)). These methods
often involve distilling semantic representations from pretrained
encoders ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [Kouzelis et al.,
2025](#bib.bib22); [Wu et al., 2025](#bib.bib23)), but this introduces a
dependency on external models whose suitability can be difficult to
anticipate, especially in the language domain. More recent methods
instead exploit self-supervision within the generative model itself,
leveraging the structure of the denoising trajectory to define
objectives such as contrastive learning or self-distillation across
noise levels and network layers ([Chefer et al., 2025](#bib.bib24);
[Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25); [Haghighi et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)).
These results suggest that the denoising process contains rich internal
supervision signals that go beyond direct reconstruction of clean data.

This raises a natural question: _can such self-supervised objectives
similarly improve continuous diffusion models for language?_

In this work, we answer this question affirmatively and introduce SERF
(Self-Regularized Flow Language Models), a simple and effective training
framework that integrates self-supervised learning into continuous
flow-matching language models (see
[Fig. 2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Preliminary: Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")).
We build on Flow Matching Language Models (FLMs) ([Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11)), which operate over one-hot token embeddings and are
trained to predict the clean one-hot embedding from a noised input using
a cross-entropy loss. SERF augments this objective with a noisy-to-clean
self-distillation signal derived from the flow trajectory. Given an
input sequence, we construct two corrupted versions using the same
underlying noise but at different noise levels. The student processes
the more corrupted input, while an exponential moving average (EMA)
teacher processes a cleaner version along the same interpolation path.
Since the teacher solves an easier denoising problem, its
representations and predictions provide informative targets for what the
student should recover from the noisier input. Matching these targets
encourages consistency along the denoising trajectory and provides
richer supervision, without modifying the model architecture or
inference procedure.

Inspired by self-distillation approaches in image diffusion ([Chefer et
al., 2025](#bib.bib24); [Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25); [Haghighi et
al., 2025](#bib.bib26)), we study two instantiations of SERF. Following
these prior works, _Latent Distillation_ (SERF-L) aligns intermediate
hidden representations, matching the student’s features at a shallow
block to the teacher’s features at a deeper block through a lightweight
projection head. This encourages the student’s early layers to form the
contextual representations that the teacher reaches deeper in the
network and from a cleaner input. Our second variant, _Probability
Distribution Distillation_ (SERF-P), distills the teacher’s predicted
token distribution instead of its features. This target is natural for
language, where the denoiser outputs a categorical distribution over the
vocabulary at every position. Beyond matching the student’s final
output, we also investigate distillation at an early student block: its
hidden states pass through a small adapter and the teacher’s frozen
output head, and are trained with a cross-entropy loss to match the
teacher’s prediction from the cleaner input. Reusing the teacher’s head
keeps both distributions in the same output space and encourages the
student to recover, already in its early layers, which tokens are
plausible given a less corrupted context. This distribution-level target
is also closer to the language modeling objective than feature
alignment, as it shares the cross-entropy form of the denoising loss.
SERF is fully self-contained: all proposed variants derive their
supervision from the flow model itself and require no external teacher
or pretrained representation model. Moreover, SERF is used only during
training and leaves the underlying sampling procedure unchanged,
introducing no additional inference-time cost.

To the best of our knowledge, SERF is the first framework to
systematically integrate self-supervised objectives into continuous
diffusion language models. Our results demonstrate that simple
self-distillation along the denoising trajectory can substantially
improve training efficiency, generation quality, and few-step
generation.

##### Contributions.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1.  1.  We introduce SERF, a simple self-regularization framework that
        exploits the denoising trajectory of continuous flow language models
        through noisy-to-clean self-distillation, requiring neither external
        pretrained models nor changes to the architecture or inference
        procedure.
2.  2.  We investigate two instantiations of SERF: SERF-L, which aligns
        hidden representations, and SERF-P, which aligns output token
        distributions, including at early student layers.
3.  3.  We show that SERF generalizes across three continuous diffusion
        paradigms (FLM, ELF, and S-FLM), yielding substantial gains in
        training time, generation quality, and sampling efficiency. On
        OpenWebText, SERF reaches the MAUVE of FLM with up to $`7.7\times`$
        less wall-clock training time and ultimately raises MAUVE by
        $`24.3\%`$. On GSM8K, SERF improves the accuracy of S-FLM by
        $`28.8\%`$ and matches its $`1024`$-step accuracy with $`64\times`$
        fewer sampling steps
        ([Fig. 1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")).

## 2 Related Work

##### Discrete Diffusion for Language.

Early approaches to diffusion-based language modeling operate directly
in the discrete token space, defining forward processes based on
masking ([Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Arriola et al.,
2025](#bib.bib8); [Nie et al., 2025](#bib.bib6); [Chao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib30)) or random token substitution ([Schiff et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9); [Lou et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Sahoo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib47)) and training models to reverse these corruptions.
While theoretically principled, discrete diffusion models face
fundamental challenges: tractable inference requires factorized
approximations of the reverse process, and the discrete nature of the
state space limits the applicability of continuous optimization
tools ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)). Recent efforts have sought to
address these limitations by combining discrete and continuous
corruption ([Zhou et al., 2025](#bib.bib27); [Zheng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib28); [Pynadath et al., 2025](#bib.bib48)). In contrast, we
operate entirely in a continuous space, avoiding discrete approximations
at both training and inference time.

##### Continuous Diffusion for Language.

Continuous diffusion and flow matching language models lift tokens into
a continuous representation before applying the generative process,
enabling the use of standard continuous optimization. The choice of
representation varies across methods: learned token embeddings ([Li et
al., 2022](#bib.bib13); [Gulrajani and Hashimoto, 2023](#bib.bib31);
[Gong et al., 2022](#bib.bib32); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib60)),
frozen pretrained embeddings ([Lovelace et al., 2023](#bib.bib34);
[Strudel et al., 2022](#bib.bib33)), and one-hot vectors ([Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11)) have all been explored, alongside Riemannian manifold
formulations that respect the geometry of the probability simplex ([Jo
and Hwang, 2025](#bib.bib35); [Cheng et al., 2024](#bib.bib36); [Davis
et al., 2024](#bib.bib44)). Our work builds on three continuous
flow-based language models. FLM ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11))
operates in the unconstrained Euclidean setting, representing tokens as
one-hot vectors and transporting Gaussian noise to these targets via a
learned velocity field trained with a cross-entropy objective.
S-FLM ([Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026](#bib.bib55)) instead defines the
flow on the unit hypersphere, with token embeddings and hidden states
constrained to $`\mathbb{S}^{d-1}`$. ELF ([Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib54))
performs flow matching in a continuous latent space of pretrained T5
sentence embeddings. We show that SERF applies to all three. A related
line of work has explored few-step sampling via flow map
distillation ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Roos et al.,
2026](#bib.bib37)), where a teacher model is used to distill the
generative trajectory into fewer sampling steps. While this direction
also involves a teacher-student setup, it is orthogonal to ours: our
primary goal is not to reduce the number of sampling steps but to
improve the training objective itself through self-regularization,
leaving the inference procedure entirely unchanged.

##### Self-Supervised Learning in Image Diffusion.

In image generation, a growing body of work has explored augmenting
diffusion and flow model training with self-supervised signals to
improve representation quality and generation performance. One prominent
direction leverages pretrained self-supervised encoders ([Radford et
al., 2021](#bib.bib1); [Oquab et al., 2024](#bib.bib38); [He et al.,
2020](#bib.bib40); [Assran et al., 2023](#bib.bib39)) to align
intermediate diffusion features with rich external visual
representations, yielding substantial gains in training efficiency and
sample quality ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [Kouzelis et al.,
2025](#bib.bib22); [Kouzelis et al., 2026](#bib.bib61); [Wu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23); [Singh et al., 2025](#bib.bib21); [Leng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib43)). A complementary set of approaches instead derives
supervision entirely from within the generative model itself, through
objectives such as contrastive approaches ([Stoica et al.,
2025](#bib.bib41); [Wang and He, 2025](#bib.bib42)), or teacher-student
alignment along the denoising trajectory ([Chefer et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24); [Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25); [Haghighi et al.,
2025](#bib.bib26)), without relying on an external encoder. Although
methods based on external representations have generally achieved strong
results, [Chefer et al. (2025)](#bib.bib24) showed that purely
self-supervised objectives, without external representations, can exceed
their performance and scale more favorably across modalities and model
sizes. Motivated by these findings, we ask whether auxiliary
self-supervised objectives can improve continuous flow matching language
models.

## 3 Method

In this section, we introduce Self-Regularized Flow Language Models
(SERF), a training framework that regularizes a flow language model by
distilling signals from an EMA teacher evaluated at a cleaner point
along the same interpolation path (see
[Fig. 2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 Preliminary: Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")).
We first review continuous one-hot flow matching for language modeling
([Sec. 3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Preliminary: Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")).
We then introduce SERF and its two variants, based on latent and
probability distribution distillation
([Sec. 3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Self-Regularized Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")),
before describing the strategies used to select the student and teacher
noise levels
([Sec. 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Student and Teacher Noise Level Strategies. ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")).
Although we instantiate SERF using FLM ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)),
our framework can in principle be applied to other continuous diffusion
or flow-based language models.

### 3.1 Preliminary: Flow Language Models

Flow matching language models operate in continuous representations; one
natural choice, as in [Lee et al. (2026)](#bib.bib11), is to embed
discrete tokens as one-hot vectors. Formally, let $`\mathcal{V}`$ be a
vocabulary and let $`\mathbf{y}=(y^{1},\ldots,y^{L})\in\mathcal{V}^{L}`$
denote a sequence of length $`L`$. A flow matching language model
represents $`\mathbf{y}`$ as the continuous one-hot matrix

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathbf{x}_{1}=\mathrm{onehot}(\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times|\mathcal{V}|}.
``` |  | (1) |

Training. Once the tokens have been represented as continuous vectors, a
time-dependent interpolation process transforms the data
$`\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim p(x)`$ into Gaussian noise
$`\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)`$:

|     |                                                    |     |     |
|-----|----------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathbf{x}_{s}=s\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-s)\boldsymbol{e}
       ```                                                 |     | (2) |

where $`s\in[0,1]`$. The denoiser $`f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)`$ is
parameterized with a token-wise softmax output layer, ensuring that the
predicted distribution at each position lies on the probability simplex
$`\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|-1}`$, and outputs a per-position probability
distribution over the vocabulary, i.e.,
$`\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{l}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)^{l}\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|-1}`$
for each position $`l`$. Since $`\mathbf{x}_{1}`$ is one-hot, the
denoiser is trained with token-level cross-entropy:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FLM}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{e},s}\Bigg[-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{l})^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot\log\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{l}\Bigg].
``` |  | (3) |

Sampling. During sampling the denoiser is converted into a velocity
field by
$`v_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)=\bigl(f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)-\mathbf{x}_{s}\bigr)/(1-s)`$,
and sampling is performed by integrating the probability flow ODE
$`\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{s}=v_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)`$ from $`s=0`$ to
$`s=1`$, starting from
$`\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})`$. The final
continuous state $`\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{1}^{l}`$ is decoded back to
discrete tokens with a simple argmax operation:
$`\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{l}=\arg\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\,\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{1}^{l}(v)`$.

Figure 2: Overview of SERF. In flow language models, the clean data lies
at $`\mathbf{x}_{1}`$ (one-hot token embeddings) and pure noise at
$`\mathbf{x}_{0}`$. SERF corrupts $`\mathbf{x}_{1}`$ at two noise levels
along the same interpolation path using shared noise: the noisier input
$`\mathbf{x}_{s}`$ is fed to the student $`f_{\theta}`$, while the
cleaner input $`\mathbf{x}_{t}`$ ($`t>s`$) is fed to the EMA teacher
$`f_{\bar{\theta}}`$, whose outputs are detached via stop-gradient. The
student is supervised by the standard cross-entropy loss against the
ground-truth one-hot target $`\mathbf{x}_{1}^{l}`$, plus a distillation
loss weighted by $`\lambda`$, which takes one of two forms. Left: In
Latent Distillation, (SERF-L) the hidden states of an early student
block $`k_{s}`$ are mapped through a lightweight projection head
$`g_{\theta}`$ and aligned with those of a deeper teacher block
$`k_{t}>k_{s}`$ using a per-token cosine distance. Right: In Probability
Distribution Distillation (SERF-P), features from student block $`k`$
pass through a small MLP and the teacher’s frozen head
$`o_{\bar{\theta}}`$, giving token-wise distributions
$`\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{\theta}^{l}`$ that are trained to match the
teacher’s $`\mathbf{q}_{\bar{\theta}}^{l}`$ via a cross-entropy loss;
the student’s own output $`\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{l}`$ is trained with
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FLM}}`$.

### 3.2 Self-Regularized Flow Language Models

##### Teacher–student framework.

Standard FLM training supervises the denoiser only through cross-entropy
against the clean sequence. We add a self-supervised signal from a
teacher derived from the model itself, requiring no external network and
adding no inference cost. Let $`f_{\theta}`$ be the student denoiser and
$`f_{\bar{\theta}}`$ the teacher, whose parameters are an exponential
moving average (EMA) of the student’s,
$`\bar{\theta}\leftarrow\alpha\bar{\theta}+(1-\alpha)\theta`$ with
$`\alpha\in(0,1)`$. For each sequence $`\mathbf{x}_{1}`$, we construct a
noisy student input and a cleaner teacher input at times $`s\leq t`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{x}_{s}=s\,\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-s)\,\boldsymbol{e},\qquad\mathbf{x}_{t}=t\,\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-t)\,\boldsymbol{e},
``` |  | (4) |

where the noise $`\boldsymbol{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)`$ is shared, so the
two views differ only in noise level.

Since the teacher receives a cleaner input and therefore solves an
easier denoising problem, its hidden representations and output
predictions provide informative targets for what the student should
recover from the noisier input. We train the student with the general
objective

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SERF}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FLM}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distill}},
``` |  | (5) |

where $`\lambda>0`$ controls the strength of the self-distillation
signal. We consider two choices for $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distil}}`$:
*Latent Distillation* (SERF-L), which aligns intermediate hidden
representations, and *Probability Distribution Distillation* (SERF-P),
which aligns output token distributions. In both cases, gradients are
propagated only through the student, while the teacher is updated
exclusively through EMA. Sampling remains identical to FLM. The full
training procedure for all variants is summarized in
[Algorithm 1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Implementation Details. ‣ A.4 Additional Details on SERF ‣ Appendix A Additional Details ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models"),
[Algorithm 2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Implementation Details. ‣ A.4 Additional Details on SERF ‣ Appendix A Additional Details ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
and
[Algorithm 3](#alg3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ Implementation Details. ‣ A.4 Additional Details on SERF ‣ Appendix A Additional Details ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models"),
where highlights indicate the minimal modifications required on top of
the baseline FLM.

#### 3.2.1 Latent Distillation

Intermediate representations of a denoiser encode contextual information
well before it is resolved into token predictions. We therefore align
the student’s hidden states with those the teacher computes from the
cleaner input. Let
$`\mathbf{h}_{\theta}^{(k_{s})}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}`$
denote the hidden states after the $`k_{s}`$-th transformer block of the
student, and
$`\mathbf{h}_{\bar{\theta}}^{(k_{t})}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}`$
those after the $`k_{t}`$-th block of the teacher. Since the two
representations are taken at different depths and noise levels, the
student features are mapped through a lightweight projection head
$`g_{\theta}`$ before being compared, while the teacher features are
used as is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{z}_{\theta}^{l}=g_{\theta}\big(\mathbf{h}_{\theta}^{(k_{s})}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)^{l}\big),\qquad\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{l}=\operatorname{sg}\big(\mathbf{h}_{\bar{\theta}}^{(k_{t})}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)^{l}\big),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)`$ denotes stop-gradient. The latent
distillation loss is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distill}}^{\mathrm{L}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{\epsilon},s,t}\left[\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\operatorname{dist}\left(\mathbf{z}_{\theta}^{l},\bar{\mathbf{z}}^{l}\right)\right],
``` |  | (7) |

where $`\operatorname{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)`$ denotes the distance between
feature vectors, for which we use cosine distance in our experiments.
SERF-L is obtained by setting
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distil}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distil}}^{\mathrm{L}}`$
in
[Eq. 5](#S3.E5 "5 ‣ Teacher–student framework. ‣ 3.2 Self-Regularized Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models").

Choosing a shallow student block and a deeper teacher block
($`k_{s}<k_{t}`$) encourages the early layers of the student to already
form the contextual representations that the teacher only reaches deeper
in the network and from a cleaner input. The projection head is used
only during training and is discarded at inference.

Figure 3: Generated samples on OWT. Samples from FLM, and SERF-L,
SERF-P_(k), trained for $`1`$M and $`300`$K steps and generated with
$`1024`$ sampling steps from the same initial noise. Errors are
highlighted in bold. Both SERF variants produce notably more fluent
text, whereas FLM exhibits grammatical errors and repetitions. We

#### 3.2.2 Probability Distribution Distillation

Beyond internal features, the teacher’s predictive distribution itself
is an informative target, since it reflects which tokens remain
plausible given a less corrupted context. We therefore align the
student’s and the teacher’s per-position distributions,
$`\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{l}`$ and
$`\mathbf{q}_{\bar{\theta}}^{l}=\operatorname{sg}\!\left(f_{\bar{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)^{l}\right)\in\Delta^{|\mathcal{V}|-1}`$,
via a cross-entropy distillation loss:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distill}}^{\mathrm{P}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{\epsilon},s,t}\left[-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(\mathbf{q}_{\bar{\theta}}^{l})^{\mathsf{T}}\log\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{l}\right].
``` |  | (8) |

SERF-P is obtained by setting
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distil}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Distil}}^{\mathrm{P}}`$
in
[Eq. 5](#S3.E5 "5 ‣ Teacher–student framework. ‣ 3.2 Self-Regularized Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models"),
with the student’s own output as the aligned distribution,
$`\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{l}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)^{l}`$.

##### Distilling into an early layer.

SERF-L benefits most from aligning a shallow student block with a deeper
teacher block (see
[Fig. 7](#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4.4 Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")),
consistent with findings in image diffusion ([Chefer et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24); [Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)): supervising early
layers directly encourages the student to form, early in the network,
information that it would otherwise only build at full depth. When the
distillation loss acts on the final output alone, early layers receive
this signal only indirectly, through the entire network. We therefore
also apply
[Eq. 8](#S3.E8 "8 ‣ 3.2.2 Probability Distribution Distillation ‣ 3.2 Self-Regularized Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
to an intermediate layer of the student. Let
$`\mathbf{h}_{\theta}^{(k)}`$ be the hidden states after the $`k`$-th
block. We map them to a distribution with a lightweight projection head
$`g_{\theta}`$ followed by the *teacher’s* output head
$`o_{\bar{\theta}}`$, kept frozen:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbf{\tilde{p}}_{\theta}^{l}=o_{\bar{\theta}}\!\left(g_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_{\theta}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)\right)\right)^{l},
``` |  | (9) |

which we denote SERF-P_(k). Reusing the teacher’s head, rather than the
student’s, ensures that both distributions are obtained through the same
mapping to token probabilities, while avoiding interference with the
student’s output head, which is optimized for final-layer predictions.
Unlike SERF-P which distills the final predictions through the student’s
own output head, SERF-P_(k) supervises intermediate representations
through the teacher’s output head and a lightweight adapter.

Remark. We note that SERF is orthogonal to flow map distillation
methods ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Roos et al.,
2026](#bib.bib37)), whose primary goal is to reduce the number of
inference steps by distilling the generative trajectory into a
fewer-step student. Such methods also typically require a pretrained
teacher model to provide the distillation targets. Our objective,
instead, is to train a regularizer: it improves the quality of the
learned flow without modifying the inference procedure, and can in
principle be combined with any such distillation method.

### 3.3 Student and Teacher Noise Level Strategies.

A key design choice is how to construct the pair $`(s,t)`$ with $`t>s`$.
We consider two strategies.

Fixed Offset. The simplest strategy is to sample a student timestep
$`s\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)`$ and set the teacher timestep to

|     |                       |     |
|-----|-----------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       t=\min\{s+\delta,1\},
       ```                    |     |

where $`\delta\in(0,1)`$ controls how far the teacher is advanced toward
the clean data endpoint. Thus, the EMA teacher is always evaluated on a
cleaner version of the same sample, with a fixed student–teacher noise
gap up to clipping at $`t=1`$.

Random Offset. Alternatively, we sample the student and teacher
timesteps randomly while ensuring that the teacher receives the cleaner
input. Specifically, we draw two times
$`\mu,\kappa\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)`$ and set

|     |                                               |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       s=\min(\mu,\kappa),\qquad t=\max(\mu,\kappa).
       ```                                            |     |

The student is evaluated at the noisier timestep $`s`$, while the EMA
teacher is evaluated at the cleaner timestep $`t`$. This exposes the
distillation loss to a range of student–teacher noise gaps, rather than
imposing a fixed offset.

## 4 Experiments

To validate SERF, we evaluate its two variants, SERF-L (Latent
Distillation,
[subsubsection 3.2.1](#S3.SS2.SSS1 "3.2.1 Latent Distillation ‣ 3.2 Self-Regularized Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models"))
and SERF-P (Probability Distribution Distillation,
[subsubsection 3.2.2](#S3.SS2.SSS2 "3.2.2 Probability Distribution Distillation ‣ 3.2 Self-Regularized Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")),
on unconditional language modeling and math reasoning. We apply them to
three baseline models: FLM ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)),
S-FLM ([Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026](#bib.bib55)) and ELF ([Hu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib54)). These span three distinct continuous paradigms,
namely one-hot embeddings, spherical embeddings, and latent embeddings,
underscoring the broad applicability of our method and showing that it
is not tied to the choice of input representation. We first describe the
experimental setup
([Sec. 4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")).
We then present unconditional text generation results on OWT with FLM
and ELF
([Sec. 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Unconditional Text Generation Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")),
followed by math reasoning results on GSM8K([Cobbe et al.,
2021](#bib.bib59)) with S-FLM
([Sec. 4.3](#S4.SS3 "4.3 Reasoning on GSM8K ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")).
Finally, we analyze the effect of the noise level strategy and the
choice of distilled layers in
([Sec. 4.4](#S4.SS4 "4.4 Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")).

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Training. All training hyperparameters are kept identical to those of
the corresponding baselines. Both SERF-L and SERF-P use the random
offset strategy, with regularization weights $`\lambda=2.0`$ and
$`\lambda=0.1`$, respectively. For SERF-L, the alignment loss is active
from the start of training, and unless otherwise noted, the student’s
block $`k_{s}=2`$ is aligned with the teacher’s block $`k_{t}=10`$. For
SERF-P_(k), the student’s block is set to $`k=2`$. For SERF-P, we found
it is better to initialize the distillation loss after the first $`10`$K
steps. The decay of the EMA teacher is set to $`\alpha=0.9999`$.

Evaluation. We sample 5000 sequences per model and report MAUVE
($`\uparrow`$) ([Pillutla et al., 2021](#bib.bib57)) against an equal
number of validation sequences, averaged over 5 seeds, and generative
perplexity (Gen. PPL $`\downarrow`$) under GPT-2 Large ([Radford et al.,
2019](#bib.bib52)). We treat MAUVE as our primary metric: Gen. PPL is
known to reward repetitive, degenerate text, and recent work has shown
it can be directly gamed ([Franca and Tong, 2026](#bib.bib53)). To
further account for the quality–diversity trade-off, we compare models
not at a single operating point but along MAUVE–entropy and Gen.
PPL–entropy fronts, obtained by sweeping the sampling temperature
$`\tau`$ and measuring average per-sample unigram entropy. Details on
math reasoning evaluation can be found in
[Appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Additional Details ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models").

### 4.2 Unconditional Text Generation Results

To demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, we evaluate two
variants of SERF on OpenWebText (OWT) ([Gokaslan and Cohen,
2019](#bib.bib51)): SERF-L (Latent Alignment) and SERF-P, SERF-P_(k)
(Probability Distribution Alignment). We apply both to the FLM baseline,
and additionally apply SERF-L to ELF ([Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib54)).

##### Results with Flow Language Models.

Both variants substantially outperform FLM across all sampling budgets
from $`64`$ to $`1024`$ steps
([Tab. 1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.3 Student and Teacher Noise Level Strategies. ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")),
while requiring $`300`$K training steps instead of $`1`$M. For example,
at $`1024`$ steps they raise MAUVE from $`0.313`$ to $`0.388`$
(SERF-P_(k)) and $`0.389`$ (SERF-L). The gains in Gen. PPL point in the
same direction: both variants achieve lower perplexity than FLM at every
sampling budget, with the largest margin in the few-step regime
($`86.2\rightarrow 82.3`$ for SERF-L at $`64`$ steps). With only $`256`$
steps, both variants already reach a lower perplexity ($`72.6`$ and
$`73.2`$) than FLM at any budget ($`74.7`$ at best). Since Gen. PPL is
evaluated at a matched unigram entropy, these gains do not come from
reduced sample diversity.

The training curves in
[Fig. 4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
make this explicit: by $`100`$K steps, both variants exceed the
$`1`$M-step FLM in MAUVE ($`0.336`$ for SERF-L and $`0.418`$ for
SERF-P_(k), vs. $`0.313`$). In Gen. PPL, SERF-L stays well below FLM
throughout and matches the $`1`$M-step model at $`100`$K steps ($`74.7`$
vs. $`74.9`$), while SERF-P_(k) tracks FLM early on and surpasses it at
$`300`$K steps ($`73.5`$).
[Fig. 3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2.1 Latent Distillation ‣ 3.2 Self-Regularized Flow Language Models ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
shows the improvement qualitatively.

##### Results with Embedded Language Flows.

To test whether SERF transfers beyond FLM, we apply it to ELF-B,
training both the baseline and our variant for $`5`$ epochs as in [Hu et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib54). We evaluate only SERF-L, as SERF-P is not
applicable here: ELF does not output a probability distribution over
tokens.
[Fig. 5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Results with Embedded Language Flows. ‣ 4.2 Unconditional Text Generation Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
(right) reports MAUVE against unigram entropy with $`64`$ sampling
steps, where each curve sweeps the self-conditioning guidance scale
$`w`$. SERF-L improves over ELF-B at every guidance scale, shifting the
whole front upwards, with the gains most pronounced at intermediate
scales ($`w=1`$ and $`w=1.5`$). This shows that the improvements of
latent distillation carry over to a different continuous diffusion
language model paradigm.

### 4.3 Reasoning on GSM8K

Accuracy Improvements. We apply SERF-L, SERF-P and SERF-P_(k) to
S-FLM ([Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026](#bib.bib55)), train on TinyGSM,
and evaluate accuracy on the GSM8K test set. Following S-FLM, we report
accuracy at $`\tau=1`$ with $`1024`$ sampling steps for the diffusion
models. As shown in
[Fig. 5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Results with Embedded Language Flows. ‣ 4.2 Unconditional Text Generation Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
(left), all three variants improve the accuracy of S-FLM from $`17.7\%`$
to $`22.3\%`$ (SERF-L), $`20.2\%`$ (SERF-P) and $`22.8\%`$ (SERF-P_(k)),
with SERF-P_(k) achieving the best result among continuous diffusion
language models, while CANDI and FLM remain near zero. All three
variants also outperform discrete diffusion models at $`\tau=1`$. A gap
remains under low-temperature decoding ($`\tau=0.1`$), where MDLM and
DUO reach $`33`$–$`36\%`$ ([Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026](#bib.bib55)).

Sampling acceleration. The improvement holds at every sampling budget,
as shown in
[Fig. 5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Results with Embedded Language Flows. ‣ 4.2 Unconditional Text Generation Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
(middle), which substantially reduces the number of steps needed to
reach a given accuracy. SERF-P_(k) and SERF-L surpass the $`1024`$-step

##### Effect of Layer choice.

In
[Fig. 7](#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4.4 Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
(bottom), we study which layers to align in SERF-L, denoting by
$`k_{s}\to k_{t}`$ the alignment of student layer $`k_{s}`$ to teacher
layer $`k_{t}`$, with all models trained on OWT for $`50`$K steps. The
choice of layers has a clear effect. Aligning an early student layer to
a late teacher layer ($`2\to 10`$) gives the highest MAUVE across the
whole entropy range, followed by aligning two intermediate layers
($`6\to 8`$), while the reversed configuration ($`10\to 2`$), which
aligns a late student layer to an early teacher layer, performs worst.
This is consistent with prior work on image generation  ([Chefer et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24); [Jiang et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)), which observed
that aligning an early layer of the student to a later layer of the
teacher is beneficial: it encourages the student to form, already in its
early layers, the higher-level representations that the teacher builds
deeper in the network.

We observe a similar behavior when distilling the teacher’s predicted
distribution into an intermediate student layer.
[Fig. 7](#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4.4 Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
(top) varies the student block $`k`$ of SERF-P_(k), whose hidden state
is projected to the vocabulary and matched to the teacher’s prediction
($`50`$K steps). Earlier blocks are consistently better: $`k=2`$ reaches
the highest MAUVE ($`0.36`$ at peak), $`k=4`$ is lower but still above
FLM, and $`k=6`$ falls below the FLM baseline at every entropy.
Distilling into early layers thus helps for both latent and distribution
targets, with $`k=2`$ the best choice.

##### Distilling distributions into an early layer.

[Fig. 6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.4 Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
(right) compares applying the teacher’s distribution to the student’s
output (SERF-P) with applying it to an early block (SERF-P_(k),
$`k=2`$), both trained for $`100`$K steps on OWT. SERF-P already matches
the MAUVE of FLM trained for $`1`$M steps. SERF-P_(k) improves further:
its MAUVE front lies above all other models across the entire entropy
range, and it extends to higher entropies, producing more diverse
samples over the same temperature sweep. SERF-P_(k) thus combines the
benefits of both variants: like SERF-L, it supervises early layers
directly, but it keeps a distribution-level cross-entropy loss, of the
same form as $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FLM}}`$, rather than aligning hidden
features.

##### Effect of the noise level strategy.

In
[Fig. 6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.4 Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
(Left) we compare the fixed ($`\delta=0.8`$) and random offset
strategies (see
[Sec. 3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Student and Teacher Noise Level Strategies. ‣ 3 Method ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models"))
for SERF-L and SERF-P trained for $`100`$K steps on OWT. SERF-L is
robust to this choice: both strategies yield nearly identical fronts
that lie well above the $`100`$K-step FLM baseline across the full
entropy range. SERF-P, in contrast, is sensitive to it: the random
strategy clearly outperforms the baseline, whereas the fixed offset
brings no improvement. We attribute this to the clipping
$`t=\min\{s+\delta,1\}`$: with $`\delta=0.8`$, the teacher receives the
fully clean input $`\mathbf{x}_{1}`$ whenever $`s>0.2`$. There, its
predictions become nearly one-hot, so the distillation target of SERF-P
adds little beyond the ground-truth label, whereas SERF-L aligns hidden
representations, which remain informative even for clean inputs.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we introduced SERF, a simple self-regularization framework
for continuous diffusion language models. Building on the observation
that an EMA teacher evaluated at a cleaner point on the same
interpolation path provides informative targets for a noisier student,
SERF augments the standard training objective with a noisy-to-clean
self-distillation loss that requires no external models and leaves the
architecture and sampling procedure unchanged. We studied two
instantiations: SERF-L, which distills intermediate hidden
representations, and SERF-P, which distills output token distributions,
together with its early-layer variant SERF-P_(k). For both latent and
distribution targets, supervising early student layers proved most
effective. Across three baselines, SERF consistently improves generation
quality. On OWT, SERF-L and SERF-P_(k) match or exceed FLM trained for
$`1`$M steps using roughly a tenth of the training budget, with gains
that persist in the few-step regime; on GSM8K, SERF improves the
accuracy of S-FLM and outperforms prior continuous diffusion language
models. Overall, these results suggest that the denoising trajectory is
a rich source of self-supervision for language, and that simple
self-distillation along it is an effective and practical way to exploit
it.

## Appendix A Additional Details

### A.1 Flow Language Models

##### Architecture.

For all FLM ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) experiments, we train a
179M-parameter Diffusion Transformer (DiT) ([Peebles and Xie,
2023](#bib.bib49)) with 12 transformer blocks, rotary positional
embeddings (RoPE) ([Su et al., 2021](#bib.bib50)), and adaptive layer
normalization (AdaLN) for time conditioning.

##### Training.

We train on OWT with a batch size of $`512`$. Following [Lee et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib11), we use $`2{,}500`$ warmup steps and then keep the
learning rate fixed at $`3\times 10^{-4}`$, using the Adam
optimizer ([Kingma and Ba, 2014](#bib.bib45)) with $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$ and
$`\beta_{2}=0.999`$. We additionally apply softcapping ([Riviere et al.,
2024](#bib.bib46)) to the attention logits, which limits large values
and improves numerical stability.

##### Sampling.

We use an Euler solver followed by a per-position argmax. At each
position $`l`$, the denoiser outputs a categorical distribution over the
vocabulary,
$`\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{\,l}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)=\mathrm{softmax}(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{s},s)^{l})`$,
where $`\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\theta}`$ are the network logits, so that
$`f_{\theta}`$ is the stack of the $`\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{\,l}`$. We
temper these with a temperature $`\tau`$,
$`\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{\,\tau,l}=\mathrm{softmax}(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{\theta}^{\,l}/\tau)`$,
and use the tempered distributions in place of
$`\mathbf{p}_{\theta}^{\,l}`$ when forming the velocity field at *every*
integration step. Values $`\tau<1`$ sharpen the distributions towards
the vertices of the simplex and yield lower-entropy samples, $`\tau>1`$
increases diversity, and $`\tau=1`$ recovers the untempered sampler.
Sweeping $`\tau`$ thus traces a quality–diversity front.

### A.2 Embedded Language Flows

##### Architecture.

For all ELF ([Hu et al., 2026](#bib.bib54)) experiments, we use the
ELF-B backbone, a 105M-parameter DiT ([Peebles and Xie,
2023](#bib.bib49)) with 12 transformer blocks (hidden size 768, 12
heads), RoPE ([Su et al., 2021](#bib.bib50)), and prefix tokens for time
and self-conditioning. The model operates on T5-small sentence
embeddings.

##### Training.

We train on OWT with a batch size of $`512`$. Following [Hu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib54), we use $`9{,}508`$ warmup steps (half an epoch) and
then keep the learning rate fixed at $`2\times 10^{-3}`$, obtained by
linearly scaling a base rate of $`1\times 10^{-3}`$ with the batch size.
Training uses the Muon optimizer ([Jordan et al., 2024](#bib.bib56)).

##### Sampling.

Following [Hu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib54), we employ an SDE-like sampler
with $`64`$ steps. Since ELF is trained with a randomly drawn
self-conditioning guidance scale supplied as a conditioning token, the
scale $`w`$ is set at inference in a single forward pass: small $`w`$
yields higher-entropy samples and large $`w`$ sharpens them, so sweeping
$`w\in\{0.5,1.0,1.5,2.0\}`$ traces a quality–diversity front.

### A.3 Hyperspherical Flow Language Models

##### Architecture.

For all S-FLM ([Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026](#bib.bib55)) experiments,
we train a 163M-parameter hyperspherical transformer (S-arch) with 12
blocks, RoPE ([Su et al., 2021](#bib.bib50)), and time conditioning.
Embeddings and hidden states lie on the unit hypersphere
$`\mathbb{S}^{d-1}`$, and all weight matrices are renormalized after
each optimizer step.

##### Training.

We train on TinyGSM with a batch size of $`512`$. Following [Deschenaux
and Gulcehre (2026)](#bib.bib55), we use $`2{,}500`$ warmup steps and
then keep the learning rate fixed at $`3\times 10^{-4}`$, using the Adam
optimizer ([Kingma and Ba, 2014](#bib.bib45)) with $`\beta_{1}=0.9`$ and
$`\beta_{2}=0.999`$. Softcapping is not required: stability follows from
the normalized architecture, which unit-normalizes queries and keys and
renormalizes all weight matrices after each optimizer step.

##### Sampling.

Following [Deschenaux and Gulcehre (2026)](#bib.bib55), we use an Euler
solver. The problem statement is fixed to its clean embeddings and
re-projected after every step, so the flow acts only on the answer. At
each position, the denoiser outputs a categorical distribution over the
vocabulary, which determines the direction in which the current
embedding moves; we truncate this distribution at every step to its
single highest-probability token, so the embedding moves directly
towards that token. We decode with an argmax after the final step.

### A.4 Additional Details on SERF

Unless otherwise noted, the following settings are shared across all
three baselines (FLM, ELF and S-FLM). In both variants of SERF, the
teacher is an EMA of the model weights (decay $`\alpha=0.9999`$),
evaluated without gradients on the same noised sequence as the student
but at a cleaner time. The regularization weight is $`\lambda=2.0`$ for
SERF-L and $`\lambda=0.1`$ for SERF-P. For SERF-L, the output of student
block $`k_{s}=2`$ is passed through a two-layer MLP projection head
($`768\to 1536\to 768`$, SiLU) and aligned with the output of teacher
block $`k_{t}=10`$ using a per-token cosine distance. The same MLP
architecture is used for SERF-P_(k). The projection head is used only
during training and discarded at inference. For SERF-P and SERF-P_(k),

##### Computational overhead.

SERF adds no cost at sampling time. The teacher and the SERF-L
projection head are used only during training, and the model we sample
from is the same network as the baseline. During training, each step
needs one extra forward pass of the EMA teacher on the cleaner input.
This pass runs without gradients and keeps no activations for the
backward pass. A forward pass costs roughly a third of a
forward-backward step, so SERF makes each training step $`\sim 30\%`$
slower as measured on our resources. The remaining additions (the EMA
update of the teacher weights, the small projection head and the SERF
loss) are negligible. Even accounting for this per-step overhead, SERF-L
reaches the quality of the baselines in much less wall-clock time,
namely 7.7$`\times`$ faster for MAUVE, and Gen. PPL, and 1.4$`\times`$
for GSM8K accuracy.

##### GSM8K evaluation.

We evaluate on all GSM8K test problems. The problem statement is
supplied as a clean prefix and is held fixed throughout sampling, so
generation acts only on the answer. Since this sampler is deterministic
given the initial noise, we repeat each evaluation over $`5`$ noise
seeds and report the mean.

##### Baselines.

Baseline results for FLM and S-FLM are taken from their respective
papers. On GSM8K
([Fig. 5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Results with Embedded Language Flows. ‣ 4.2 Unconditional Text Generation Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")),
we compare against two discrete diffusion language models, MDLM ([Sahoo
et al., 2024](#bib.bib7)) and DUO ([Sahoo et al., 2025](#bib.bib47)),
and three continuous diffusion language models, CANDI ([Pynadath et al.,
2025](#bib.bib48)), FLM ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) and
S-FLM ([Deschenaux and Gulcehre, 2026](#bib.bib55)). All results in this
table, including the autoregressive baselines, are taken from
[Deschenaux and Gulcehre (2026)](#bib.bib55).

##### Computational Resources.

For both training and sampling we use 8 NVIDIA GH200 $`96`$GB GPUs.

### A.5 Additional Evaluation Details

##### Entropy-matched generative perplexity.

Generative perplexity can be lowered simply by sampling at a lower
temperature, which also lowers sample diversity. Comparing models at a
fixed temperature is therefore misleading, so we report Gen. PPL at a
fixed sample entropy instead. For each model we draw 5,000 samples at a
small sweep of temperatures, $`\tau\in\{0.95,1.0,1.05\}`$. For each
temperature we record the Gen. PPL and the mean per-sequence unigram
entropy $`H`$. We then interpolate the sweep to a target entropy
$`H=5.44`$, which is the entropy of OpenWebText validation text.

## Appendix B Additional Experimental Results

### B.1 Training acceleration on TinyGSM

Figure [9](#A1.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Implementation Details. ‣ A.4 Additional Details on SERF ‣ Appendix A Additional Details ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
(right) tracks GSM8K accuracy over training, measured with 32 sampling
steps and averaged over 4 evaluation seeds. Both SERF variants learn
faster than the S-FLM baseline. SERF-L reaches the accuracy of the
released S-FLM checkpoint, trained for 250K steps (16.3%), after only
140K steps (16.2%), that is, with $`1.8\times`$ fewer training steps.
SERF-P does so after 180K steps ($`1.4\times`$ fewer). At 250K steps,
SERF-L reaches 20.8% and SERF-P 18.7%, respectively 4.5 and 2.4 points
above S-FLM. Taking into account SERF’s per-step overhead of roughly
30%, SERF-L still matches the baseline $`1.4\times`$ faster in training
time.

### B.2 Effect of the self-regularization weight $`\lambda`$.

[Fig. 9](#A1.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Implementation Details. ‣ A.4 Additional Details on SERF ‣ Appendix A Additional Details ‣ Self-Regularized Flow Language Models")
(left) reports MAUVE against unigram entropy for SERF-L and SERF-P under
different regularization weights. The two variants respond to
$`\lambda`$ in opposite directions: SERF-L benefits from a larger weight
($`0.336`$ at $`\lambda=2.0`$ vs. $`0.321`$ at $`\lambda=1.0`$), whereas
SERF-P degrades as the weight grows ($`0.320`$ at $`\lambda=0.1`$ vs.
$`0.259`$ at $`\lambda=0.5`$). This reflects the scale of the two
alignment losses. The cosine distance used by SERF-L is bounded and much
smaller in magnitude than the cross-entropy of
$`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FLM}}`$, so a larger $`\lambda`$ is needed for it
to meaningfully affect training. In contrast, SERF-P employs a
cross-entropy loss on the same scale as $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FLM}}`$,
thus increasing $`\lambda`$ shifts the effective target away from the
ground truth and toward the teacher’s prediction.

## Appendix D Limitations and Future Work

Our method is currently developed and evaluated for continuous
flow-based language models. Extending the framework to discrete
diffusion language models ([Sahoo et al., 2024](#bib.bib7); [Schiff et
al., 2024](#bib.bib9)) represents a promising direction for future work.
Since discrete diffusion models follow different corruption and
denoising dynamics than continuous flows, adapting the teacher
supervision mechanism may require additional design considerations. We
view this as an opportunity to further generalize our framework and
broaden its applicability.
````

###### Abstract

Game-theoretic benchmarks claim that large language model (LLM)
strategic decision-making varies across game types, interaction
structures, and capability dimensions. However, they only evaluate
strategic performance across individual fixed models. In parallel,
contemporary LLM routers pick models under quality-cost trade-offs, but
with no notion of a strategic structure. They do not account for an
opponent that might be unknown, and that can adapt over time and shift
the feedback signal. To address this, we extend the game-theoretic
evaluations and define strategic selection as the meta game layered on
top of the base game. In each round, a strategic selector fields an LLM
from a portfolio of models to navigate a game payoff matrix that is
latent and learned online. We find that the evaluation signal is
game-specific, and that extracting a reliable signal requires a much
higher number of seed runs $`N`$ compared to traditional benchmarking
setups. Evaluating across 5 games and 10 LLMs, we show a model’s
strategic competence varies significantly depending on the game setup
and the opponent faced. Building on this, we implement a suite of
selector methods and find that approaches that rely on query embeddings
or LLM self-reflection do not consistently capture the underlying
strategic signal. A lightweight multi-armed bandit algorithm performs
more reliably across games, though it still falls short of the best
fixed model. Finally, we show that simple non-stationary and adversarial
bandit variants fail to outperform a stationary bandit baseline,
pointing to a gap in how current methods handle a shifting opponent in
strategic settings.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in multi-agent
settings as part of a larger compound system. However, a growing share
of deployers treat the inter-agent dynamics, competitive and cooperative
alike, as a black box. This makes it hard to build systems that remain
capable, efficient, and reliable. Existing work addresses pieces of this
problem in isolation. The strategic decision-making literature focuses
on a fixed model’s play under a specified game setting \[[7](#bib.bib7),
[28](#bib.bib28), [37](#bib.bib37)\]. In parallel, LLM routing addresses
model selection under performance-cost trade-off \[[5](#bib.bib5),
[26](#bib.bib26), [31](#bib.bib31)\], but with a static task
distribution and no notion of a strategic structure or opponent. Neither
accounts for the fact that contemporary LLM portfolios differ not only
in scale and cost, but also in the type and quality of strategic
behaviour they exhibit.

Our work reframes model selection itself as a strategic decision.
Concretely, we extend the game-theoretic evaluations and define
strategic selection as the opponent-conditioned meta game layered on top
of the base game. Here the base game is played between the fielded LLMs,
while the meta game is played between two strategic selectors that field
a model of their choice each round. In repeated settings, the dynamic
and adaptive nature of this problem (as opposed to traditional routers
operating on a fixed distribution of tasks) means our selection should
be strategic. Beyond balancing the performance-cost trade-offs, such a
selection layer can help steer the deployer’s general play, adding
control over the reliability and safety of the interaction
([Figure 1](#S0.F1 "In Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")).
We ask the question:

> Given LLM strategic capabilities vary across different games and
> models, can a selector exploit this by learning which model to field
> against an unknown and possibly adaptive opponent?

Why This Matters. Traditional routers operate under the assumption of a
fixed task distribution, learning a static mapping from queries to
optimal models. In contrast, operating against an adaptive opponent
transforms this into a non-stationary learning problem, where the
optimal strategy can continuously shift over time. A further challenge
arises from the partial observability of the meta game’s payoff matrix.
In practice, a selector cannot observe the opponent’s net gain, since
the opponent’s model choice is unknown, nor can it observe the
counterfactual payoffs of fielding an alternative model in the current
game state. We also acknowledge that LLMs are stochastic models. In
strategic selection, we introduce an additional layer of environmental
noise through the dynamic responses of the opponent. Therefore,
benchmarking and result reporting call for a careful statistical
treatment. Our contributions are threefold:

- •
  Strategic Model Selection. We formalise strategic selection as a
  repeated general-sum meta game between two players. Beyond capability
  and cost, this lets us treat the interaction itself as a first-class
  design consideration for model selection.
- •
  Quantifying Noise in Strategic Play. We characterise the signal and
  noise levels across games, and suggest adapting methods from
  traditional hypothesis testing to decide on the number of seeds per a
  game-portfolio pair.
- •
  Empirical Investigation. To set up our empirical investigation, we
  select 10 models from different families, and adapt a range of
  existing routing methods
  ([Table 1](#S1.T1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")).
  We first show that LLM strategic capabilities are scenario-dependent
  and exploitable by a selector. We then investigate the performance of
  adapted methods against both fixed models and selector opponents.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Strategic LLM Decision-Making

A growing literature explores LLMs in strategic settings (e.g. how LLMs
play games against each other). Game-theoretic benchmarks
\[[7](#bib.bib7), [28](#bib.bib28), [10](#bib.bib10), [36](#bib.bib36)\]
and frameworks \[[33](#bib.bib33), [11](#bib.bib11), [37](#bib.bib37),
[34](#bib.bib34)\] test LLMs in normal-form games and find that
performance is uneven and structure-dependent. Others
\[[32](#bib.bib32), [35](#bib.bib35), [24](#bib.bib24), [3](#bib.bib3)\]
evaluate capability on a higher level (e.g. planning, collaboration,
theory of mind) and consistently find spiky, uneven profiles across
models. Similarly, [Mei et al. \[13\]](#bib.bib13) and [Horton et al.
\[9\]](#bib.bib9) run behavioural Turing tests and show that different
models exhibit different strategic dispositions (i.e. behavioural
profiles). Motivated by such findings, [Raman et al. \[19\]](#bib.bib19)
outlines the need for an element-level (model, prompt, domain) selection
approach, but leaves it to future work. We note that despite extensive
coverage, the existing literature always fixes the model per player.

### 2.2 LLM Routing

An orthogonal direction learns to predict a best-fit model for a given
query, often trading off performance for cost and relying on minimal
two-model setups \[[16](#bib.bib16), [6](#bib.bib6), [1](#bib.bib1)\].
Others extend this to incorporate more models \[[4](#bib.bib4),
[17](#bib.bib17), [22](#bib.bib22), [30](#bib.bib30)\] or train on
limited observational data \[[26](#bib.bib26), [31](#bib.bib31)\]. These
works represent queries as vector embeddings to semantically capture
their scope and difficulty. However, we argue that discriminative signal
in strategic selection largely comes from evolving game history (actions
and outcomes), and two consecutive rounds with near identical embeddings
can call for opposite model choices. In the absence of routing methods
that explicitly model this structure, we also consider bandit-style
per-round feedback \[[29](#bib.bib29), [2](#bib.bib2)\] as a paradigm
suited to a non-stationary interactive signal.

## 3 Strategic LLM Selection

### 3.1 What is Strategic Selection?

In multi-agent systems, a setting is non-strategic if the objective
concerns only the overall performance of the system or its
performance-cost trade-off. A setting is strategic if it also accounts
for the interaction between agents, including how they communicate,
coordinate, and negotiate. Modelling this interaction can produce
systems that are more reliable and efficient. In a cooperative setting,
it improves the coordination between agents \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]. In a
competitive setting, it gives the deployer more control over the
interaction with an opponent and helps steer the interaction toward the
deployer’s preferences. For example, for a given business, the reward of
the negotiation, the cost of using AI, and the consistency (i.e.
associated risk) of the results can all matter at varying levels.

### 3.2 Problem Formulation

We formalise strategic LLM selection as a repeated, contextual,
general-sum game with two layers: a base game that fielded models play,
and a selection meta game that the deployers play. Strategic selection
focuses on the meta layer, a two-player game with $`i`$ indexing the
player (deployer) and $`-i`$ its opponent. Repeated games are played
over rounds $`t=1,\dots,T`$, where each round $`t`$ is associated with
context $`x_{i,t}\in\mathcal{X}`$ (e.g. game setup, instructions, past
history). Deployer $`i`$’s set of pure strategies (meta game actions)
$`S_{i}`$ is defined as the set of models $`\{1,\dots,M_{i}\}`$ in its
portfolio. In each round, $`i`$ selects a model
$`s_{i,t}\sim\sigma_{i}(\cdot\mid x_{i,t})`$, where
$`\sigma_{i}\in\Delta(S_{i})`$ is a mixed strategy (i.e. selection
policy). Per-round context $`x_{i,t}`$ is then passed down to the
fielded model to produce the base game action
$`s_{i,t}(x_{i,t})\to a_{i,t}`$. The base game (i.e. of fielded models
$`s_{i,t}`$ against $`s_{-i,t}`$) produces a utility $`v_{i,t}`$ at an
inference cost $`c_{i,t}`$ (tokens spent), as well as a net gain of
$`g_{i,t}`$ that captures their trade-off. Together, player $`i`$’s game
outcome is denoted as $`\xi_{i,t}=(s_{i,t},a_{i,t},g_{i,t})`$. Drawing
from the online learning literature, each selector maintains an adaptive
parameter vector $`\theta_{i,t}`$ that updates after each round based on
observed context $`x_{i,t}`$ and game outcome $`\xi_{i,t}`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
s_{i,t}\sim\sigma_{i}(\cdot\mid x_{i,t};\theta_{i,t}),\qquad\theta_{i,t+1}=\textsc{Update}(\theta_{i,t},x_{i,t},\xi_{i,t}).
``` |  | (1) |

Any architecture that maps $`\theta_{i,t}`$ to a selection policy
$`\sigma_{i,t}`$ and supports updates from online feedback qualifies as
a backbone. Such options can include classical multi-armed bandit
samplers \[[29](#bib.bib29)\], neural network-based classifiers
\[[5](#bib.bib5), [26](#bib.bib26)\], or at the frontier, an LLM
prompted with game history. Consequently, $`\theta_{i,t}`$’s definition
depends on the backbone, such as the network weights for a classifier,
or the per-arm posterior for a Thompson sampler. We note that the game
outcome is also incorporated into the next round’s context
$`x_{i,t+1}=f(x_{i,t},\xi_{i,t})`$ to be used in base game
decision-making. Finally, this is repeated over $`N`$ independent seed
runs (where the parameter vector is reset for each seed
$`\theta_{i,1}\leftarrow\theta_{i}^{0}`$) to account for stochastic
variance in strategic play.

### 3.3 Noise in Strategic Play

Single-trial LLM decision-making is noisy, subject to the probabilistic
sampling mechanism, and sensitive to variations in prompts. Related work
addresses LLM evaluations by adapting cross-disciplinary methods such as
signal processing \[[8](#bib.bib8)\], experiment-design theory
\[[14](#bib.bib14)\], and psychometrics \[[15](#bib.bib15)\]. They find
that in traditional evaluations (e.g. question-answering benchmarks),
even a modest amount of replication (e.g. $`N\approx 5`$) substantially
stabilises the estimates. We argue that due to its interactive
opponent-conditioned structure, strategic LLM evaluation is inherently
noisier and requires a higher number of replications. To quantify the
noise in strategic play, we define a method to measure the levels of
signal and noise across games by adapting [Heineman et al.
\[8\]](#bib.bib8)’s work. Building on this, we develop approaches to
choose $`N`$ for a given game-portfolio pair \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. See
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B More Details about Noise in Strategic Play ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")
for further details.

### 3.4 Evaluation Metrics

To measure strategic performance, we capture the game utility $`v_{i}`$,
associated cost $`c_{i}`$, and their trade-off represented as the net
gain $`g_{i}`$. The game utility $`v_{i}`$ is min-max normalised
($`\hat{v}_{i}\in[0,1]`$) based on its game-specific bounds (e.g. the
economic profit in Cournot Duopoly, or the number of winning
battlefields in Colonel Blotto game). The associated inference cost
$`c_{i}`$ is represented by the per-model token usage cost (in dollars
\$) using the OpenRouter¹¹ 1
[https://openrouter.ai/models](https://openrouter.ai/models) listings as
market proxies. This cost is min-max normalised
($`\hat{c}_{i}\in[0,1]`$) based on the portfolio-bounds (minimum and
maximum per-token price observed across the model portfolio). Finally,
we define net gain $`g_{i}`$ as the weighted sum of normalised utility
$`\hat{v}_{i}`$ and cost $`\hat{c}_{i}`$

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{i}=\frac{\hat{v}_{i}+\lambda(1-\hat{c}_{i})}{1+\lambda},\qquad g_{i}\in[0,1],
``` |  | (2) |

where $`\lambda`$ represents the cost trade-off weight and is fixed at
$`\lambda=1`$ across our runs. Any game episode is replicated across
$`N`$ seeds to account for stochastic noise. Each of the $`N`$ seed runs
yields a single sample, from which we compute the mean $`\bar{X}`$, the
standard error (SE), and the 95% confidence interval (CI). Alternative
metrics include the in-game winning rate (i.e. we ignore the raw
$`g_{i}`$ and measure $`\mathbbm{1}[g_{i}>g_{-i}]`$), or representing
the cost $`c_{i}`$ by the per-model throughput (tokens produced per
second).

## 4 Experiments

We provide empirical evidence in 3 steps and motivate the problem of
strategic LLM selection. First, an exhaustive pairwise matchup across 10
models and 5 games motivates the problem, showing that strategic
capability is varied and exploitable by a selector. Following these
findings, a suite of selectors is run against fixed but unknown models
(i.e. opponent is a fixed LLM) to evaluate their potential as strategic
selectors. Finally, these selectors are deployed against adaptive
opponents to simulate the non-stationary dynamics of real-world
settings. We note that reported results should not be compared across
([Tables 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.2 Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions"),
[3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.2 Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")
and [4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ 4.2 Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")),
as a method’s performance is a function of the specific opponent pool it
faced which varies across settings (e.g. the runs against adaptive
opponents do not include fixed models).

### 4.1 Design

Full details on the models, games, and selector implementations are
given in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C More Details about Experimental Setup ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions").

Models and Games. The model portfolio draws on 10 open-weight LLMs
across four families (Granite, Mistral, Llama, and Qwen) ranging from 3B
to 70B to capture varying capability levels, usage costs, and
dispositions. The game inventory captures 5 classical game-theoretic
scenarios (Colonel Blotto, Cournot Duopoly, Stag Hunt, Vickrey Auction,
Chicken Game) spanning zero-sum to cooperative dilemmas. Experiments are
run through OutplayArena²² 2
[https://github.com/OutplayArena/arena](https://github.com/OutplayArena/arena),
which owns the base game rules, turn sequencing, and scoring.

Selectors. A suite of selectors spanning traditional and adversarial
bandit samplers \[[29](#bib.bib29), [2](#bib.bib2)\], contemporary LLM
routers \[[5](#bib.bib5), [26](#bib.bib26)\], and an LLM self-reflection
framework \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] is then incorporated to draw on this
portfolio. Adapting selectors from a range of different backgrounds
plays an important diagnostic role in understanding the strengths and
weaknesses of each method as strategic selectors. Additionally, simple
opponent-aware full-information selectors are evaluated post-hoc to
measure the selection headroom.

Number of Rounds and Seeds. Some strategic benchmarks
\[[28](#bib.bib28)\] evaluate models across hundreds of games. To test
whether such broad evaluations rest on a consistent signal, we quantify
the per-game signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and find that the evaluation
signal is inherently game-specific. A power analysis on single-round
fixed-model matchups shows that the share of detectable pairwise
comparisons stabilises at $`N\approx 150`$ seeds in every evaluated
game, with Cournot Duopoly separating models more cleanly than any other
game
(Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 Analysis ‣ Appendix B More Details about Noise in Strategic Play ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")).
In our experiments, fixed-model matchups are single-round $`T=1`$ with
$`N=300`$ seeds. Selectors learn online and require repeated play, so we
run $`T=300`$ rounds per seed with a single seed ($`N=1`$) to keep the
inference cost tractable. Note that the fielded model’s and fixed
opponent’s context is limited to the current round. This ensures that
each round is an independent draw of the opponent’s play.

### 4.2 Results

Strategic Capabilities Vary Across Models and Games.
[Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.2 Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")
reveals that strategic competence is uneven and game dependent. For
example, the Llama models are top performers in Cournot Duopoly but fail
to make the podium in Chicken Game. In addition, the substantial
performance variance among models within individual games highlights an
opportunity to adopt a selector.
[Figure 2(a)](#S4.F2.sf1 "In Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")
underscores that model scale does not reliably translate into strategic
capability. Across our evaluations, we find only a weak positive
correlation between model scale and utility (Pearson’s $`r=0.37`$), and
a moderate negative relationship when accounting for the usage costs
($`r=-0.54`$). Upon a closer look, we also see that smaller models have
game-specific usability. For example in Vickrey Auction, due to its high
rationality (i.e. always bidding the true value), the Granite4.1 3B
outperforms other much bigger models that range from 24B to 70B
parameters. Finally,
[Figure 2(b)](#S4.F2.sf2 "In Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")
supports our framing “choosing a model is choosing a strategy”. For each
model, we score strategic dispositions from its in-game actions, such as
the dare rate in Chicken Game or the produced quantity in Cournot
Duopoly. We describe each model by a vector of its seven disposition
scores, and project these vectors onto their principal components
(Appendix [D.1](#A4.SS1 "D.1 Fixed Model Disposition Scores ‣ Appendix D More Results ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")).
We find that even same family models can differ sharply in disposition.
For example, Granite4.1 8B dares in 96% of Chicken Game rounds while its
3B and 30B versions never dare. Families still show a general coherence,
as Qwen3.5 and Llama models compete aggressively in Cournot Duopoly,
whereas most Granite and Mistral models cooperate. We believe this is an
important signal that a strategic selector should learn to infer and
exploit.

## 5 Lessons Learned

### 5.1 How to Learn from Strategic Context?

We believe strategic context is multi-faceted and encapsulates the game
instructions (i.e. the strategic scenario we are dealing with), the
portfolio model dispositions (i.e. understanding our resources to act on
this scenario), and the opponent’s actions that shape our scenario.
Together, this context is either available as a prior (e.g. through
existing game-specific runs across models), or gathered post-hoc by
observing the game outcome $`\xi_{i,t}`$ at the end of each round.
Traditional routing and sampling methods fail to make best use of this
context. Existing methods either encode the entire context (e.g. game
instructions, history) as a whole, as form of vector embedding
(RouterDC), without an underlying form or structure, or rely entirely on
the observed game outcome (Thompson Sampler) with no regard to the
strategic scenario (captured by the game instructions) we are dealing
with. A better selector should infer game-specific strategic cues, and
map selection choices to their implications for future game states. It
should build an understanding of the performance of its own portfolio
models, and the opponent under the current scenario.

### 5.2 Games as Strategic Testbeds

We saw that best-performing models vary noticeably across the 5
evaluated games, each setup capturing a particular strategic scenario (a
model-to-model interplay)
([Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.2 Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")).
Moreover, an observation can be made that games vary in difficulty (i.e.
complexity). For example, one can say that identifying the equilibrium
strategy would require higher model reasoning capabilities in a game
like Cournot Duopoly (an economic profit game) than in Colonel Blotto,
which relies on a simple per-battlefield resolution rule (the larger
number wins). Empirically, this idea is reflected in the correlation
between model scale and performance, which is the highest in Cournot
Duopoly (Pearson’s $`r=0.66`$), and much lower in games like Stag Hunt
($`r=0.12`$) and Colonel Blotto ($`r=0.24`$)
([Figure D.3](#A4.F3 "In D.2 Fixed Model vs. Fixed Model ‣ Appendix D More Results ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")).
Mathematically, there are an infinite number of normal- and
extensive-form games that range in complexity and the strategic
interplay captured. Future work should therefore establish well-defined
methods to classify a game’s difficulty and strategic value. Such
methods are of high value to deployers before they decide where to train
and use their agent workflows, as well as towards designing strategic
selectors that better understand the underlying strategic learning
signal.

### 5.3 Games as Dynamic Testbeds

Real-world AI deployments are messy. Inputs are noisy, and the agents
involved often hold different, sometimes conflicting objectives. Our
results show that a model’s competence is shaped by the opponent it
faces, and how both sides adapt as the interaction unfolds. Traditional
benchmarks fail to capture this, as they test one model at a time
against a fixed correct answer. This makes game inventories a natural
fit for evaluating models in decision-pressured, interactive settings
that they are actually deployed to in practice. Moreover, because game
states are generated dynamically through interaction, games are more
resistant to the training-data contamination that increasingly
undermines static benchmarks \[[23](#bib.bib23)\].

That said, games are not immune to saturation. Even without
contamination, the discriminative signal can erode as models approach
equilibrium play (strategy profiles from which no player can gain by
unilaterally deviating). More specifically, under standard no-regret
learning, adaptive play converges to the set of coarse correlated
equilibria (CCE), a superset of Nash equilibria \[[20](#bib.bib20)\]. In
two-player zero-sum games such as Colonel Blotto, every equilibrium
yields the same expected payoff, so once models play near-equilibrium
strategies, none can outperform another and their results become
indistinguishable. In contrast, in general-sum games the set of CCE is
typically large and contains outcomes with very different payoffs. This
suggests favouring general-sum games when building inventories meant to
remain discriminative.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work

We extend game-theoretic evaluations and define strategic selection as a
repeated opponent-conditioned meta game layered on top of the base game.
Initial evaluations across 5 games and 10 models show that LLM strategic
capability varies substantially across games and models, leaving
considerable room for a selector to exploit. By adapting a range of
selection methods, we report that the existing methods do not transfer
equally well even against a simple fixed-model opponent, and that a
plain bandit algorithm can surpass structurally heavier pre-trained
encoders and LLM reflection methods. Subsequent runs against a selector
opponent reveal that simple adaptations like discounted and adversarial
bandit algorithms are not able to capture the non-stationary dynamics of
opponent-conditioned strategic selection, and fail to outclass the plain
sampler. Overall, the results highlight the challenges of strategic
model selection, and motivate a selector design that can extract and
learn from signals across 3 different dimensions: model capability,
usage cost, and strategic disposition, in noise-heavy interactive
settings.

Our investigation was scoped to the analysis of 5 games. However, to
better understand agent-to-agent interplay and the underlying safety and
risk factors, it is important to consider games from a range of fields,
including economics, social sciences, and operations research. We
believe this choice should reflect both the measurable effect of the
game and the underlying practical application of the selector. We also
relied on explicit parsed game actions as a communication method between
the models, which underestimates both the strategic capabilities (e.g.
persuasion), and the associated risk factors in deployment. A more
realistic setting should capture agent-to-agent natural language
interactions \[[27](#bib.bib27)\]. We leave such free-form interactions
to future work. Finally, high-stakes repeated interactions (e.g.
negotiations, auctions) involve a risk factor not captured by expected
gain. This can include catastrophic losses, regulatory penalties, or
reputational damage. Capturing the variance in realised gain to account
for involved risk is another future direction. For example, a risk knob
($`\beta\in\mathbb{R}`$: $`\beta=0`$ is risk-neutral, $`\beta>0`$
risk-averse, $`\beta<0`$ risk-seeking) could allow users to better align
the selector with their deployment policies.

## Appendix B More Details about Noise in Strategic Play

### B.1 Signal and Noise Ratio

Following [Heineman et al. \[8\]](#bib.bib8), we first define a
descriptive method that characterises signal and noise levels across
games. Fix a game $`\mathcal{G}`$ and let $`S`$ denote its set of models
under evaluation, with $`j,k\in S`$ ranging over the pair of models
compared in $`\mathcal{G}`$. For a model $`s\in S`$, let
$`\tau=1,\dots,N`$ index its seeds, where a seed is a single independent
evaluation run. Let $`v_{\mathcal{G},s,\tau}`$ be the bounds-normalised
score on seed $`\tau`$ of $`\mathcal{G}`$ played by model $`s`$. Let
$`\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s}`$ denote the mean of
$`v_{\mathcal{G},s,\tau}`$ over all $`N`$ seeds, and let
$`\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G}}`$ denote the mean of
$`\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s}`$ over all models $`s\in S`$. Noise,
signal, and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) are then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{noise}(\mathcal{G},s)=\frac{\text{Std}_{\tau}(v_{\mathcal{G},s,\tau})}{\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s}},\qquad\text{signal}(\mathcal{G})=\frac{\max_{j,k\in S}|\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},j}-\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},k}|}{\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G}}},\qquad\text{SNR}(\mathcal{G})=\frac{\text{signal}(\mathcal{G})}{\overline{\text{noise}}(\mathcal{G})}.
``` |  | (3) |

Here $`\text{Std}_{\tau}(\cdot)`$ denotes the standard deviation taken
over the seed index $`\tau`$, and
$`\overline{\text{noise}}(\mathcal{G})`$ denotes
$`\text{noise}(\mathcal{G},s)`$ averaged over every model $`s\in S`$.
Noise is a coefficient of variation, a model’s own seed-to-seed spread
relative to its mean. Signal is the largest gap between any two models’
means, relative to the game’s overall mean. SNR is high when models are
well separated relative to how much a single model’s score jitters
across seeds, and low when the two quantities are comparable.

### B.2 Number of Seeds

Given the SNR, we want to determine the minimum number of seeds $`N`$
that lets us report statistically significant outcomes. This is a
trade-off between cost (the number of model inferences we run) and
reliability (our statistical confidence in the reported results).

Decision accuracy. [Heineman et al. \[8\]](#bib.bib8) suggests
calculating a decision accuracy metric to determine the number of seeds
$`N`$ needed to resolve the pairwise gaps between models. Let
$`\mathcal{P}`$ denote the set of model pairs compared in
$`\mathcal{G}`$, let $`n`$ denote a candidate (small) batch size, and
let $`N\gg n`$ denote a large reference batch size. Let
$`\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s}^{(n)}`$ denote model $`s`$’s mean
bounds-normalised score over $`n`$ seeds. Decision accuracy at batch
size $`n`$ is then

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{DA}(n)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{\{s_{A},s_{B}\}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbbm{1}\!\left[\,\mathrm{sign}\!\left(\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s_{A}}^{(n)}-\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s_{B}}^{(n)}\right)=\mathrm{sign}\!\left(\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s_{A}}^{(N)}-\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s_{B}}^{(N)}\right)\right],
``` |  | (4) |

the fraction of the pairs in $`\mathcal{P}`$ whose sign at batch size
$`n`$ agrees with the sign at the reference batch size $`N`$. Every
$`n`$ below $`N`$ is estimated as the mean over repeated independent
subsamples of the $`N`$-seed reference, drawn per model pair so each
draw mirrors an actual $`N`$-seed experiment run at batch size $`n`$.
However, decision accuracy needs a reference batch, and in
game-theoretic evaluations this reference is often not remotely the
scale of traditional benchmarks, since the game-theoretic reference
batch is self-generated instead of drawn from an external test suite.

Minimum detectable effect. A more practical approach is to use the
minimum detectable effect (MDE), which does not need a reference batch,
and asks directly whether the current $`N`$ and opponent-pool size is
sufficient to resolve the pairwise gaps we report. Power analysis of
[Miller \[14\]](#bib.bib14) (Eqs. 9-10), for two models $`s_{A}`$ and
$`s_{B}`$ with $`M`$ shared opponents and $`K`$ seeds played against
each opponent, gives the MDE as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\delta(K)=(z_{\alpha/2}+z_{\beta})\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{2}+\sigma_{s_{A}}^{2}/K+\sigma_{s_{B}}^{2}/K}{M}},
``` |  | (5) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\omega^{2}=\mathrm{Var}_{o}\!\left(\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s_{A},o}-\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s_{B},o}\right),\qquad\sigma_{s_{A}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{o}\!\left[\mathrm{Var}_{t}(v_{\mathcal{G},s_{A},o,t})\right],\qquad\sigma_{s_{B}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{o}\!\left[\mathrm{Var}_{t}(v_{\mathcal{G},s_{B},o,t})\right].
``` |  | (6) |

Here $`o`$ ranges over the $`M`$ shared opponents ($`M=8`$ in a 10-model
pool experiment), and $`t=1,\dots,K`$ indexes seeds played against a
given opponent. $`z_{\gamma}`$ denotes the $`(1-\gamma)`$ quantile of
the standard normal distribution, so $`\alpha=0.05`$ (giving
$`z_{\alpha/2}`$) and power $`1-\beta=0.8`$ (giving $`z_{\beta}`$) are
[Miller \[14\]](#bib.bib14)’s own defaults for significance and power.
$`\omega^{2}`$ is the variance, across the $`M`$ shared opponents, of
the cluster-mean gap between $`s_{A}`$ and $`s_{B}`$, i.e. how much that
gap swings depending on the opponent, and $`\sigma_{s_{A}}^{2}`$ and
$`\sigma_{s_{B}}^{2}`$ are each model’s mean within-opponent variance
across seeds. All three quantities are estimated from a reference (e.g.
$`N=300`$). $`\delta(K)`$ is the smallest true skill gap detectable at
that $`K`$, for a fixed opponent-pool size. For game $`\mathcal{G}`$
evaluated on a model pool $`S`$, the detectable fraction at $`K`$ seeds
is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\rho_{\mathcal{G}}(K)=\frac{1}{\binom{|S|}{2}}\sum_{\{s_{A},s_{B}\}\subset S}\mathbbm{1}\!\left[\,|\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s_{A}}-\overline{v}_{\mathcal{G},s_{B}}|\geq\delta(K)\,\right],
``` |  | (7) |

the share of the $`\binom{|S|}{2}`$ pairwise gaps in $`\mathcal{G}`$
that exceed $`\delta(K)`$. Sweeping $`K`$ per game produces a rough
signal on the statistical significance of pairwise model comparisons
from that game.

### B.3 Analysis

Scope of the analysis. All signal, noise, and seed-stabilisation
analyses in this section are computed on fixed-model matchups, using
single-round episodes ($`T=1`$). Each seed is therefore an independent
draw of the base game, and the estimates characterise the inherent
variability of strategic play between two given models. We note that our
analyses do not directly transfer to selector runs, where selector’s own
choices can evolve over the rounds.

Signal-to-noise ratio is not game-agnostic. Cournot Duopoly separates
models almost twice as cleanly as any other game, while Colonel Blotto
carries the lowest signal
([Figure 1(a)](#A2.F1.sf1 "In Figure B.1 ‣ B.3 Analysis ‣ Appendix B More Details about Noise in Strategic Play ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")).
A qualitative investigation shows that this stems from all models
playing Colonel Blotto very similarly, all converging to an equilibrium

### C.2 Games

The base game $`\mathcal{G}`$ is instantiated as one of five two-player
games. For each game we have kept the default configurations provided by
[OutplayArena](https://github.com/OutplayArena/arena). We provide brief
details about each game, and refer the reader to the source outlined in
[Table C.2](#A3.T2 "In C.2 Games ‣ Appendix C More Details about Experimental Setup ‣ Choosing LLMs for Strategic Agent Interactions")
for further information.

- •
  Colonel Blotto is a zero-sum game where two players split a fixed
  budget across several battlefields. It measures how a model
  reallocates its strategy in response to an opponent’s shifting
  distribution.
- •
  Cournot Duopoly is a simultaneous quantity-setting game where two
  firms choose outputs that jointly fix market price and profit. It
  isolates a single continuous best-response signal that separates
  models by how closely each converges toward the equilibrium quantity.
- •
  Stag Hunt is a coordination game with a risk-dominant and a
  payoff-dominant equilibrium. It measures whether a model favors the
  safer but lower-payoff option over the cooperative one when the
  opponent’s action is uncertain.
- •
  Vickrey Auction is a second-price sealed-bid auction where each bidder
  submits a value-based bid. It measures how closely a model’s bid
  tracks its private valuation under the auction’s truthful-bidding
  equilibrium.
- •
  Chicken Game is an anti-coordination game built around brinkmanship,
  where mutual insistence is worst for both players. It measures a
  model’s willingness to escalate or concede as the risk of mutual loss
  grows.

### C.3 Selectors

We provide the implementation detail of each selector.

Fixed Model. One fixed model across all seeds $`N`$ that keeps context
and past outcomes across rounds $`t=1,\dots,T`$ unless otherwise stated.

Per-Opponent Full-Information. Given the identity of the fixed opponent,
it looks up the round-robin table (our prior) once at construction and
commits to whichever candidate scores highest against that opponent,
fielding it every round with no further adaptation.

LLM Selector. Uses llama3.3:70b as the selecting model and relies on the
Reflexion framework \[[21](#bib.bib21)\] to pick the model to field.
Namely, each round it is queried once with a description of the game, an
entry per portfolio model (tier and per-token price), and a log of past
rounds covering its own fielded model, action, and outcome signal. It
parses a JSON response giving the picked model, a confidence, and a
reasoning. Prior information enters only as in-context history, a
rolling log of past rounds inside the prompt, with default sampling
temperature = 1.0.

RouterDC. Adapted from [Chen et al. \[5\]](#bib.bib5). Each round a
frozen sentence encoder embeds the current query, built from player’s
own model, action, and signal history, and the pick is whichever
candidate’s trained embedding has highest cosine similarity to that
query embedding. The embeddings come from a checkpoint fit offline, once
per game. All prior information is folded into this pre-trained
checkpoint at training time.

Causal LLM Router. We follow the doubly robust regret-minimising rule of
[Tsiourvas et al. \[26\]](#bib.bib26). Each round it draws a candidate
from a softmax over its current per-(candidate, opponent) belief, with a
floor that keeps every candidate at nonzero pick probability. It then
folds in a doubly robust correction of the observed signal, using the
pick probability it assigned as its own propensity instead of fitting a
separate propensity model. We use the defaults surrogate = softmax, tau
= 1.0, and propensity_floor = 0.05.

Thompson Sampler. It keeps a per-model Gaussian belief over the chosen
signal that is refined as a running mean after every round. Each round
it draws one sample per model from its current belief and fields the
model with the highest draw. Additionally, we implement two
non-stationary variants that change the belief update. The discounted
variant (Dis. Thompson Sampler) multiplies every model’s accumulated
pseudo-count by the discount factor before folding in the new
observation, so older evidence decays exponentially. The sliding-window
variant (SW Thompson Sampler) keeps only the $`W`$ (window size) most
recent observations, and drops older ones from the belief entirely. We
use discount = 0.99 in the discounted variant and window = 50 in the
sliding-window variant.

EXP3. Exponential-weights bandit updated by an importance-weighted
multiplicative rule on the observed signal. Its mixed strategy blends
the weight-proportional distribution with a uniform exploration floor,
so every candidate keeps nonzero pick probability (exploration_rate =
0.1).
````

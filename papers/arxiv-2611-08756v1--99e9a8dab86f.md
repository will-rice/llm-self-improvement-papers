# Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate

Liting Huang Affiliation: Newcastle University, United Kingdom    Huizhi
Liang Affiliation: Newcastle University, United Kingdom    Yi Lei
Affiliation: Peking University, China    Xingjun Ma Affiliation: Fudan
University, China    Varun Ojha Affiliation: Newcastle University,
United Kingdom

###### Abstract

Knowledge attacks on Large Language Models, such as manipulating
fact-checking judgments, pose significant risks to AI reliability.
Existing approaches—adversarial suffix/template injection and
single-agent self-reflection—lack robustness across diverse defenses and
are prone to confirmation bias and costly trial-and-error, respectively.
We propose Debate-to-Deceive (D2D), a multi-agent debate framework
grounded in human debate structure and rhetorical style, enabling agents
to generate adversarial passages that are semantically coherent,
rhetorically effective, and resistant to early confirmation bias. D2D
formalizes the adversarial process as an evolutionary game in which
agents adaptively evolve their rhetorical strategies (Logos, Pathos,
Ethos) through competition. To manage the intractably large LLM output
space, we decouple agent behavior into a strategic layer, which selects
a rhetorical action from a compact strategy space via
evolutionary-inspired learning dynamics, and a generation layer, where
the LLM produces a concrete passage conditioned on that strategy and the
debate context. On four fact-checking benchmarks—FEVER, HealthVer,
CLIMATE-FEVER, and SciFact—D2D substantially outperforms existing
attacks and evades most defenses.

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used for reasoning,
knowledge retrieval, and decision support in professional, legal, and
social domains [Luo et al. (2022)](#bib.bib21); [Zhou et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib22); [Liu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib17). As a result,
ensuring their factual reliability and robustness against adversarial
manipulation has become critical. Knowledge attacks, especially those
targeting fact-checking judgments, can mislead aligned LLMs into
hallucinating, contradicting established facts, or bypassing safety
guardrails [Tu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib39); [Wu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib20); [Liu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib17); [Liu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib18); [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19); [Rahman et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib23); [Woo and Lee (2025)](#bib.bib24); [Esugo et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib25); [Alon and Kamfonas (2023)](#bib.bib31).

Existing adversarial attacks can be broadly grouped into three
categories. Gradient-optimized suffix attacks use token-level
optimization to induce harmful outputs [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19);
[Zou et al. (2023)](#bib.bib6); [Guo et al. (2021)](#bib.bib7). Manual
or template-based jailbreak attacks rely on crafted prompts,
paraphrases, or distracting instructions to override model
alignment [Liu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib18); [Zhang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib8); [Shen et al. (2024)](#bib.bib26); [Wei et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib27); [Kang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib28); [Zou et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib1). LLM-based iterative attacks refine adversarial
passages through feedback or search; for example, AutoDAN uses a
hierarchical genetic algorithm [Liu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib17), while
ADMIT introduces memory-based self-reflection during adversarial
generation [Wu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib20).

However, existing attack methods still face key limitations in
robustness, efficiency, and cost. First, gradient-based attacks produce
adversarial suffixes composed of meaningless character sequences [Zou et
al. (2023)](#bib.bib6); [Morris et al. (2020)](#bib.bib29), which are
easily flagged by perplexity-based filters or known-answer
defenses [Jain et al. (2023)](#bib.bib30); [Wang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib36); [Phute et al. (2024)](#bib.bib37); [Liu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib18); [Learn Prompting (2023)](#bib.bib38). Second,
manual or template-driven attacks rely on a narrow set of fixed prompts,
limiting the diversity of adversarial passages and their robustness
across defenses. Third, LLM-based iterative optimization attacks remain
constrained: ADMIT [Wu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib20) leverages
inference-time memory (e.g., summarizing past interactions) but, as a
single-agent method, lacks adversarial correction and is prone to
confirmation bias and costly trial-and-error. AutoDAN [Liu et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib17) improves over fixed templates via a hierarchical
genetic algorithm, yet incurs a large GPU memory footprint and remains
fundamentally template-based, leaving it vulnerable to known-answer
detection. This raises a central question: Can semantically coherent,
rhetorically effective adversarial passages be generated at low cost and
high speed, while remaining robust across diverse defenses?

To address this question, we draw on the structure of human debate
competitions as a framework for efficient, adaptive adversarial
generation. Human debate takes multiple forms [Gray (1926)](#bib.bib44);
[National Speech & Debate Association (2025)](#bib.bib45); [arts
(2021)](#bib.bib46): the Oregon format structures one-on-one debate into
three phases [Gray (1926)](#bib.bib44), while the Singapore format
structures four-person team debate into four phases—foundational
reasoning (definitions and facts), associative reasoning (standard
arguments and counterarguments), core reasoning (novel insights and
impactful rebuttals), and conclusive reasoning (synthesis) [arts
(2021)](#bib.bib46). These structures are complemented by the classical
rhetorical triad—Logos, Pathos, and Ethos—which governs how arguments
are framed in terms of logic, emotion, and credibility. Inspired by this
structure, we propose Debate to Deceive (D2D), an attack framework that
formalizes adversarial generation as a structured debate grounded in an
evolutionary game formulation and multi-agent reinforcement learning
(MARL). D2D adapts the four-phase Singapore format to one-on-one debate,
using multiple agents that dynamically compete to generate adversarial
passages that are both semantically coherent and rhetorically
effective—in contrast to prior methods relying on fixed suffixes or
single-agent optimization. D2D is motivated by three design
considerations: 1) effective deception requires dynamic rhetorical
adaptation, as static strategies are easily detected; 2) single-agent
self-correction is prone to confirmation bias and costly
trial-and-error, whereas multi-agent debate improves correction quality
and reduces search cost; and 3) debate is inherently an adaptive
strategic process in which argument effectiveness improves through
competition, motivating an evolutionary game formulation with MARL to
model this dynamic. The main contributions of this work are threefold:

- •
  Structured debate-based attack framework. We introduce D2D, a
  multi-agent adversarial framework inspired by human debate
  competition, combining staged reasoning with rhetorical strategies
  such as Logos, Pathos, and Ethos to manipulate LLM fact-checking
  judgments.
- •
  Evolutionary game formulation with multi-agent reinforcement learning
  (MARL): We cast adversarial generation as an evolutionary game in
  which agents co-evolve their rhetorical strategies. We use a
  hierarchical pipeline where agents first choose a rhetorical strategy,
  then generate text conditioned on that choice.
- •
  Demonstration of strategic vulnerability across diverse defenses: We
  show that diverse defense methods remain vulnerable to our D2D-based
  debate-generated adversarial samples as they can induce erroneous
  judgments into the target LLM.

## 2 Related Work

#### Game theory in LLMs.

Recent LLM research has largely adopted (behavioral) game theory for
capability assessment and mechanism-oriented improvement. Hua et
al. [Hua et al. (2024)](#bib.bib11) evaluate LLM rationality in
complete- and incomplete-information games and propose game-theoretic
workflows to improve equilibrium reasoning and robustness. Fan et
al. [Fan et al. (2024)](#bib.bib9) operationalize rationality as a core
metric and systematically characterize LLM behavioral boundaries across
canonical games. Akata et al. [Akata et al. (2025)](#bib.bib35) study
finitely repeated $`2\times 2`$ games played by LLMs against other LLMs
and humans, identifying systematic cooperation/coordination patterns and
showing that behavior can be modulated via prompting. Mao et al. [Mao et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib34) propose ALYMPICS, an empirical game-theory
framework that uses LLM agents as experimental subjects to support
controlled simulation studies (e.g., multi-round resource auctions). Sun
et al. [Sun et al. (2025)](#bib.bib10) provide a systematic survey of
the bidirectional interplay between game theory and LLMs, covering
benchmarks, algorithmic improvements, and societal-impact modeling.
Overall, existing LLM-related studies mainly use game theory to
evaluate, characterize, or scaffold LLM behavior under predefined game
rules and payoff structures. In contrast, our work uses game-theoretic
payoff as an internal adaptation mechanism for adversarial generation.
Rather than inheriting the payoff structure from a canonical game, we
explicitly design the strategic payoff and combine it with rewards for
real-time generation quality and cost, enabling rhetorical strategies to
evolve over repeated multi-agent interactions.

#### Multi-agent debate.

Multi-agent debate has emerged as an effective framework for improving
LLM reasoning, factuality, and decision quality through structured
interaction among multiple agents. Du et al. [Du et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib3) show that debate-based interaction can improve
factuality and reasoning in language models. Khan et al. [Khan et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib2) further demonstrate that stronger and more persuasive
debaters can lead to more truthful answers, highlighting the importance
of persuasive dynamics in debate. Beyond accuracy-oriented reasoning,
Liang et al. [Liang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib4) use multi-agent debate to
encourage divergent thinking, while Yi et al. [Yi et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib12) incorporate belief modeling and Bayesian Nash
equilibrium into debate-based reasoning. Estornell and Liu [Estornell
and Liu (2024)](#bib.bib5) provide a broader framework for analyzing
principles and interventions in multi-LLM debate. Despite these
advances, existing work mainly studies debate as a mechanism for
collaborative reasoning, truth-seeking, or diversity enhancement. By
contrast, our work focuses on competitive multi-agent debate, where
agents pursue conflicting objectives and adapt their rhetorical
strategies over time. Rather than using debate solely to improve the
correctness of answers, we formalize adversarial persuasion as a
strategic interaction in which agents infer opponents’ tendencies and
update their strategies to maximize persuasive effectiveness.

#### Attacks against LLMs.

Adversarial passages can be categorized into two types: those with
meaningless prefixes/suffixes and those with semantically meaningful
adversarial passages. Jailbreaking attacks aim to bypass safety
mechanisms and elicit harmful or prohibited responses from LLMs. These
attacks can be divided into white-box and black-box approaches.
White-box attacks require access to the model’s internal parameters or
gradients. Gradient-based optimization methods, such as GCG [Zou et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib6), compute gradients to systematically optimize
adversarial prompts by accessing the model’s output probability
distributions (logits). Training-based approaches, such as shadow
alignment [Yang et al. ()](#bib.bib42) and weak-to-strong
generalization [Burns et al. ()](#bib.bib43), exploit model training
dynamics to generate jailbreak prompts. In contrast, black-box attacks
operate without access to model internals, relying only on input-output
interactions. LLM-based generation methods such as AutoDAN [Liu et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib17), JudgeDeceiver [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19),
PAIR [Chao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib40), and PAP [Zeng et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib41) use LLMs to automatically generate adversarial
inputs that bypass safety filters. Another line of work,
knowledge-to-jailbreak [Tu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib39), collects
large-scale datasets of knowledge-jailbreaking pairs and fine-tunes an
LLM as a specialized jailbreak generator to produce domain-specific
adversarial prompts. Prompt injection attacks manipulate the behavior of
LLM by injecting malicious instructions into user prompts. Direct prompt
injection approaches, such as Open-Prompt-Injection [Liu et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib18), design carefully crafted prompts that override the
model’s original instructions or safety constraints. Memory-based
injection methods with proxy passages, such as ADMIT [Wu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib20), leverage iterative self-refinement through
memorized proxy content to achieve adversarial objectives. Overall,
existing attack methods mainly optimize prompts or generators to produce
effective adversarial inputs. By contrast, our work focuses on how
multiple agents strategically adapt rhetorical strategies during
adversarial generation. Rather than treating attack construction as a
single-prompt optimization problem, we model it as a dynamic multi-agent
process in which persuasion strategies evolve based on strategic
feedback and generation quality.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Notations

![Refer to caption](2611.08756v1/D2D_overview_update.png)

Figure 1: D2D framework: Schematic diagram of a debating adversarial

iteratively crafting adversarial queries that are imperceptible to
humans but effective in misleading the target LLM. This process induces

COVID-19,” D2D generates adversarial passages that steer the target LLM

D2D operates in a black-box setting: the attacker has no access to model
parameters and injects adversarial text directly into the evidence field
while observing output verdicts. Our threat model has the following: 1)
attacker capability: black-box, query-based; 2) attacker constraint:
fluent natural language, no gradient access; 3) attack surface: direct
evidence injection. A successful attack induces the target LLM to
generate poisoned outputs. Thus, given a claim $`C`$ to be fact-checked,
the attacker’s objective is to: 1) flip the original verdict (e.g.,
Support $`\rightarrow`$ Refute), and 2) generate an adversarial passage
consistent with the flipped outcome. Such adversarial passages can
mislead the target LLM into producing an incorrect fact-checking
response.

In this context, let $`\mathcal{D}=(C,L,E)`$ denote fact data, where
$`C=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}\}`$ is the set of $`n`$ claims,
$`L=\{l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{n}\}`$ is the true label of the claims, and
$`E`$ is the evidence of the claim in the correct label side. We define
the target label as $`L^{{}^{\prime}}`$, which is the opposite of label
$`L`$. We focus on agent debate, in which one agent argues for the
original label of a claim and the other for the flipped (target) label.
These two agents follow the structure of a human debate competition game
to propose an opinion, including foundational, associative, core, and
conclusive reasoning. In this setting, $`A=\{A_{L},A_{L^{\prime}}\}`$ is
a pair of debating agents, where $`A_{L}`$ is the original-label agent,
and $`A_{L^{\prime}}`$ is the target-label agent. Other LLMs are a judge
LLM defined as $`A_{J}`$ and a target LLM defined as $`A_{G}`$. For
$`T`$ debate phases, $`T`$ pairs of responses from debate agents are
$`\{R_{t}^{L},R_{t}^{L^{\prime}}\}^{T}_{t=1}`$, where a single response
pair is $`R_{t}=(R_{t}^{L},R_{t}^{L^{\prime}})`$, and the verdict
returned by the target LLM $`A_{G}`$ is $`R_{v}`$.
$`P=\{P_{1},P_{2},\ldots,P_{T}\}`$ denotes instruction prompts. Each
instruction prompt $`P_{t}=(P_{t}^{L},P_{t}^{L^{\prime}})`$ indicates
the prompt positions for the true-label agent and the target-label
agent, respectively. $`D=(D_{L},D_{L^{\prime}})`$ denotes the argument
pool for the true-label agent and the target-label agent, respectively.

### 3.2 Debate-to-Deceive Setup

We present a Debate-to-Deceive (D2D) attack framework that aims to
mislead the target LLM into generating statements inconsistent with the
facts. The overall architecture of the framework is shown in
Fig. [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3.1 Notations ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate").
This framework follows the structure of a human debate competition game
and involves two agents: one arguing for the original label of a claim
and the other for the flipped (target) label. Specifically, D2D unfolds
over four phases: foundational reasoning (definitions and facts),
associative reasoning (standard arguments and counterarguments), core
reasoning (novel insights and impactful rebuttals), and conclusive
reasoning (synthesis). At each phase, the debater builds on the
accumulated argument pool from prior rounds and responds to opposing
claims; at the final phase $`T`$, it presents a summary statement
consolidating its side’s case. This structure enables the two agents to
generate adversarial passages that are semantically coherent and
rhetorically effective while overcoming early confirmation bias,
yielding more robust, higher-quality attacks (see
Appendix §[B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 D2D Example ‣ Appendix B D2D Method ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")
for details).

The evolutionary game theory (EGT) formalization rests on four
components. 1) Strategy Space $`\mathbf{S}=\{S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}\}`$: we
abstract agent behavior into three rhetorical stances—Logos, Pathos, and
Ethos—enabling systematic analysis of their competitive interactions
(Sec. [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Debate Strategies ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")). 2) Payoff Matrix $`\mathbf{B}`$: the relative persuasive advantage when
$`S_{j}`$ meets $`S_{k}`$ is encoded as $`B_{j,k}`$
($`j,k\in\{1,2,3\}`$), evaluated by judge $`A_{J}`$ as a local net score
over argument pool $`D_{t}`$
(Sec. [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Debate Strategy Selection Payoff ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")). 3) Replicator-style Policy Update: each agent’s policy $`\pi^{i}`$ is
updated via strategy-averaged temporal difference (TD) errors—the
discrete-time analog of replicator dynamics—so strategies yielding
higher payoffs against the current opponent mix gain probability mass
across rounds
(Sec. [3.5](#S3.SS5 "3.5 Net Benefit of Debating Agent Strategies ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")). 4) Evolutionary Equilibrium: the fixed point of the matrix game, which
the learned policy orbits rather than reaches
(Appendix §[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Strategy Dynamics Beyond the Static Equilibrium ‣ Appendix B D2D Method ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")).
Several design choices are fact-checking-specific: 1) the binary verdict
space maps naturally onto a zero-sum two-player game; 2) the
evidence-claim structure defines a natural debate context; 3) rhetorical
strategies target reasoning patterns LLM fact-checkers rely on.

### 3.3 Debate Strategies

We define the strategic landscape through a three-dimensional strategy
space
$`\mathbf{S}=\{S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}\}=\{S_{\text{Logos}},S_{\text{Pathos}},S_{\text{Ethos}}\}`$,
drawing inspiration from classical rhetorical theory [Wachsmuth et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib32). The Logos strategy ($`S_{1}`$) emphasizes formal
logic and deductive validity, generating utility through logical-rigor
scores that enhance robustness against counterarguments. Pathos
($`S_{2}`$) employs emotional rhetoric and narrative constructs,
achieving high persuasiveness scores, while exhibiting vulnerability to
factual challenges. Ethos ($`S_{3}`$) grounds arguments in authoritative
evidence and credible sources, yielding strong evidentiary support with
potential adaptive inflexibility. Each agent’s behavior consists of two
coupled components. The first is the action selection policy
$`\pi^{i}(S_{j,t}^{i}\mid s_{t})`$, which is a discrete softmax
distribution over the strategy space $`\{S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}\}`$
conditioned on the current debate state $`s_{t}`$, where
$`i\in\{L,L^{\prime}\}`$ indexes the agent sides, $`j\in\{1,2,3\}`$
indexes the rhetorical strategy, and $`t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}`$ indexes
the debate phases. This policy answers the question which rhetorical
stance should the agent adopt at this moment? The second is a
text-generation policy $`p^{i}(\text{text}^{i}\mid s_{t},S^{i}_{j,t})`$,
a conditional language model distribution that generates a concrete
adversarial passage conditioned on both the current state and the chosen
strategy. Given the selected strategy $`S_{j,t}^{i}`$, it answers: what
specific words should the agent say?

The two components are coupled in a hierarchical two-stage process: the
agent first commits to a rhetorical strategy $`S_{j,t}^{i}`$ via
$`\pi^{i}`$, then generates the actual text conditioned on that
strategic choice via $`p^{i}`$. This factorization reflects a natural
decomposition of the structure of the human debate competition game: an
agent first decides how to argue (e.g., by appealing to logos, pathos,
or ethos), and then constructs the specific utterance accordingly. The
total behavior of agent $`i`$ is therefore captured by the joint
distribution over both the strategic choice and the generated text:

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\begin{split}\Pi^{i}(\text{text}^{i},S_{j,t}^{i}\mid s_{t})&=p^{i}(\text{text}^{i}\mid s_{t},S^{i}_{j,t})\\
&\quad\cdot\pi^{i}(S_{j,t}^{i}\mid s_{t}).\end{split}
``` |  | (1) |

### 3.4 Debate Strategy Selection Payoff

The debate is a symmetric zero-sum game: both agents draw from the same
strategy space $`\mathbf{S}`$, and a payoff depends on the pair of
strategies played rather than on which agent plays it. These two
properties together force the payoff matrix to be skew-symmetric,
$`\mathbf{B}=-\mathbf{B}^{\top}`$, so a single matrix serves both
agents. Writing $`B_{j,k}`$ for the expected net score when
$`A_{L^{\prime}}`$ plays $`S_{j}`$ against $`A_{L}`$ playing $`S_{k}`$,
we have $`B_{j,k}=\mathbb{E}[r(S^{L^{\prime}}_{j},\,S^{L}_{k})]`$ with
$`r`$ the reward of
Eq. ([2](#S3.E2 "In Reward. ‣ 3.5 Net Benefit of Debating Agent Strategies ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate"));
identical strategies stalemate, $`B_{j,j}=0`$.
Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.4 Debate Strategy Selection Payoff ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")
parameterises the three remaining pairs by $`\alpha`$, $`\beta`$ and
$`\gamma`$, the coefficients of the Logos–Pathos, Pathos–Ethos and
Ethos–Logos encounters. Symmetry here concerns strategies not roles:
$`A_{L}`$ argues the true label and $`A_{L^{\prime}}`$ the target label.

|          |              |              |             |
|----------|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| Strategy | Logos        | Pathos       | Ethos       |
| Logos    | 0            | \-$`\alpha`$ | $`\gamma`$  |

We model the interaction as cyclic: no stance dominates the other two,
so $`\alpha`$, $`\beta`$ and $`\gamma`$ share a sign and the game admits
no pure-strategy equilibrium. Which stance prevails over which is an
empirical question we do not derive, and the analysis uses only the
cyclic structure, not the cycle’s direction. Under that structure the
game has a unique interior mixed equilibrium
$`\mathbf{w^{*}}=(w_{1}^{*},w_{2}^{*},w_{3}^{*})`$ in the strategy
simplex $`\Delta_{3}`$, at which every pure strategy earns the same
expected payoff
$`(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{w}^{*})_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}B_{j,k}w_{k}^{*}`$
against the mixture. In a zero-sum game that common value is the
population average $`\bar{\pi}=0`$, so $`\mathbf{w^{*}}`$ solves
$`\mathbf{B}\mathbf{w}^{*}=\mathbf{0}`$ subject to
$`\sum_{j=1}^{3}w_{j}^{*}=1`$ and $`w_{j}^{*}\geq 0`$. Crucially
$`\mathbf{w^{*}}`$ is the time average of the learned policy rather than
a state it settles into: the dynamics orbit $`\mathbf{w^{*}}`$ without
converging to it (see
Appendix §[B.1](#A2.SS1 "B.1 Strategy Dynamics Beyond the Static Equilibrium ‣ Appendix B D2D Method ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")).

### 3.5 Net Benefit of Debating Agent Strategies

#### Reward.

Traditional game-theoretic models (e.g., zero-sum games) typically focus
only on the opponent’s behavior and potential rewards. For LLM agents,
however, text quality and computational cost are inherent, critical
constraints on action. We therefore integrate strategic decision-making
with resource optimization by combining a macro-level strategic reward
$`r_{\text{matrix}}`$, which maximizes theoretical game gains based on
beliefs about the opponent’s strategy, with a micro-level execution
reward $`r_{\text{quality}}`$, which penalizes poor text quality and
excessive resource consumption (time, tokens). This dual-reward design
accommodates the unique cost and quality uncertainty of LLM agents,
which prior game-theoretic frameworks do not address. Given current
state $`s_{t}`$ (argument pool $`D`$), the dynamic net benefit of
selecting strategy $`S_{j,t}^{i}`$ is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})=r_{\text{matrix}}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})+r_{\text{quality}}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i}),
``` |  | (2) |

where $`r_{\text{matrix}}`$ is the expected game payoff based on
opponent strategy beliefs, and $`r_{\text{quality}}`$ is the real-time
performance reward for LLM generation quality and cost. $`s_{t}`$ is the
state and $`S_{j,t}^{i}`$ is the action from strategy space $`S`$
including logos, pathos, and ethos. $`r_{\text{matrix}}`$ computes the
expected payoff from the static game matrix $`\mathbf{B}`$, where
element $`B_{j,k}`$ represents the static expected local net score when
strategy $`S_{j,t}^{L^{\prime}}`$ counters $`S^{L}_{k,t}`$. Since the
agent cannot perfectly observe the opponent’s true strategy
$`S_{k,t}^{L}`$, it maintains a belief distribution
$`\mathcal{P}(S_{k,t}^{L}|D)`$ over opponent strategies based on the
argument pool $`D`$. The expected payoff is therefore:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle r_{\text{matrix}}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{L^{\prime}})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in 1,2,3}\mathcal{P}(S_{k,t}^{L}|D)\cdot B_{S_{j,t}^{L^{\prime}},S_{k,t}^{L}}.`$ |  | (3) |

Symmetrically, when the $`L`$ agent acts as the principal and
$`L^{\prime}`$ as the opponent, the same formulation applies with the
roles of $`L`$ and $`L^{\prime}`$ swapped, i.e.,
$`B_{S_{j,t}^{L},S_{j,t}^{L^{\prime}}}`$ is used in place of
$`B_{S_{j,t}^{L^{\prime}},S_{j,t}^{L}}`$. The reward
$`r_{\text{quality}}`$ indicates the reward agents receive for
generating high-quality text while penalizing resource consumption
(temporal and economic costs). This component combines judge
LLM-assigned text quality score $`r_{\text{score}}`$ with generation
time cost $`c_{\text{time}}`$ and token economic cost
$`c_{\text{econ}}`$, weighted by respective coefficients
$`\lambda_{q}`$, $`\lambda_{t}`$ and $`\lambda_{e}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\text{quality}}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})=\lambda_{\text{q}}r_{\text{score}}-\lambda_{\text{t}}c_{\text{time}}-\lambda_{\text{e}}c_{\text{econ}},
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\lambda_{\text{q}}`$, $`\lambda_{\text{t}}`$ and
$`\lambda_{\text{e}}`$ are the weighting coefficients for text quality,
temporal cost, and economic cost, respectively, subject to the
constraints
$`\lambda_{\text{q}},\lambda_{\text{t}},\lambda_{\text{e}}\in[0,1]`$ and
$`\lambda_{\text{q}}+\lambda_{\text{t}}+\lambda_{\text{e}}=1`$. The
final reward $`r_{\text{final}}`$ captures the agent’s long-term signal
for policy refinement and meta-optimization. It is defined as a function
of the terminal state $`s_{T}`$, which summarizes the outcome of the
current round:

|     |                                               |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       r_{\text{final}}(s_{T})=\gamma\cdot v(s_{T}),
       ```                                            |     | (5) |

where $`\gamma\in[0,1]`$ is the discount factor, used here to control
the strength of the refinement signal. $`v(s_{T})`$ is the expected
performance metric of the terminal state $`s_{T}`$, representing the
expected final win rate or cumulative reward if the debate were to
continue from $`s_{T}`$. Specifically, $`r_{\text{final}}(s_{T})`$ acts
as a supervisory signal to refine the debating agent’s overarching
strategy or policy. If the resulting $`r_{\text{final}}`$ is low, this
outcome serves as a policy gradient for the meta-optimization loop,
guiding the agent to adjust and improve its core debating LLM prompt or
internal parameters before the commencement of the next episode
(debate).

#### Strategy Averaging.

To formally describe the decision-making process in this model, we
assume that the state transition is governed by a deterministic
function: $`s_{t+1}=f(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{L},S_{j,t}^{L^{\prime}}),`$ which
depends solely on the action types of both agents rather than the
specific text generated. Under this framework, the expected
quality-based reward is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{i}}[r_{\text{quality}}]&=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\pi^{i}(S_{j}\mid s_{t})\\
&\quad\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\text{text}^{i}_{t}\sim p^{i}(\cdot\mid s_{t},S_{j})}\big[r_{\text{quality}}(\text{text}^{i}_{t})\big].\end{split}
``` |  | (6) |

implying that this component of the reward is contingent only upon the
specific textual content produced. Furthermore, the matrix-based reward
$`r_{\text{matrix}}(S^{L^{\prime}}_{j,t},S^{L}_{j,t})`$ is determined
exclusively by the pair of action types selected by the agents. where
$`S^{L^{\prime}}_{j,t}`$ is the acting agent’s strategy and
$`S^{L}_{j,t}`$ is the opponent’s strategy. Consequently, the
state-value function $`\bar{V}^{i}(s_{t})`$, which is averaged over the
policy $`\pi^{i}`$, satisfies the Bellman equation by recursively
integrating these expected rewards and the value of the succeeding
state.

We use the policy-averaged TD error instead of the sample TD error to
obtain deterministic policy evolution dynamics [Barfuss et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib33). Below, we formalize the key quantities in our
framework. For a given state $`s_{t}`$ and agent $`i`$’s action
$`S_{j,t}^{i}`$, the expected reward averaged over the opponent’s policy
$`\pi^{-i}`$ and the text generation distribution $`p^{i}`$ is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\bar{R}^{i}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})=&\mathbb{E}_{S_{j,t}^{-i}\sim\pi^{-i}(s_{t})}\left[r_{\text{matrix}}(S_{j,t}^{i},S_{j,t}^{-i})\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{\text{text}^{i}_{t}\sim p^{i}(\cdot\mid s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})}[r_{\text{quality}}(\text{text}^{i}_{t})].\end{split}
``` |  | (7) |

The first term captures the game-theoretic interaction between both
agents’ strategic choices, while the second term measures the quality of
the generated text conditioned on the chosen strategy. Since the state
transition $`s_{t+1}=f(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i},S_{j,t}^{-i})`$ depends on both
agents’ actions, the expected value of the next state, given agent
$`i`$’s action $`S_{j,t}^{i}`$, is obtained by averaging over the
opponent’s policy:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\bar{V}^{i}_{\text{next}}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})&\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{S_{j,t}^{-i}\sim\pi^{-i}(s_{t})}\\
&\quad\Big[\bar{V}^{i}\big(f(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i},S_{j,t}^{-i})\big)\Big].\end{split}
``` |  | (8) |

Combining the immediate reward and the discounted next-state value, the
strategy-averaged action-value function is:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{Q}^{i}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})=\bar{R}^{i}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})+\gamma\bar{V}^{i}_{\text{next}}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i}),
``` |  | (9) |

where $`\gamma\in[0,1)`$ is the discount factor weighting the importance
of future rewards. The state-value function $`\bar{V}^{i}(s_{t})`$,
averaged over agent $`i`$’s policy $`\pi^{i}`$, satisfies the Bellman
equation:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{V}^{i}(s_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{S_{j,t}^{i}\sim\pi^{i}(s_{t})}[\bar{Q}^{i}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})].
``` |  | (10) |

Finally, the strategy-averaged TD error, which measures the discrepancy
between the action-value and the state-value, is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{\delta}^{i}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})=\bar{Q}^{i}(s_{t},S_{j,t}^{i})-\bar{V}^{i}(s_{t}).
``` |  | (11) |

This TD error drives the policy update in our learning algorithm (see
Appendix §[B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 D2D Algorithm ‣ Appendix B D2D Method ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")).

#### Action Selection Policy Update.

The action selection policy is updated using a Softmax-based approach
driven by the policy-averaged TD error. This mechanism, inspired by
methods in online learning and counterfactual regret minimization,
ensures that actions with positive advantage (i.e., higher-than-average
value) are reinforced:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi_{t+1}^{i}(S_{k}\mid s_{t})=\frac{1}{Z_{t}^{i}}\,\pi_{t}^{i}(S_{k}\mid s_{t})\exp\!\big(\eta^{i}\bar{\delta}^{i}(s_{t},S_{k})\big),
``` |  | (12) |

for $`k=1,2,3`$, where $`\eta^{i}`$ is the effective learning rate,
which scales the intensity of the policy update, and
$`Z_{t}^{i}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{3}\pi_{t}^{i}(S_{k^{\prime}}\mid s_{t})\exp\!\big(\eta^{i}\bar{\delta}^{i}(s_{t},S_{k^{\prime}})\big)`$
is the normalization constant.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Settings

All experiments ran on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU (96GB), a 22-core
Intel Xeon Platinum 8470Q CPU, and 110GB of system memory.

#### Datasets and metrics.

We evaluate D2D on four fact-checking benchmarks domains: FEVER [Thorne
et al. (2018)](#bib.bib13), HealthVer [Kotonya and Toni
(2020)](#bib.bib14), Climate-FEVER [Diggelmann et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib15), and SciFact [Wadden et al. (2022)](#bib.bib16),
sampling 50 SUPPORT and 50 REFUTE claims from each (100 per label for
the Qwen3-32B study), plus a 100-claim SimpleFacts set for benign
evaluation. We report attack success rate (ASR), the share of attacks
that elicit the attacker’s target label; generation-and-execution time
(s/sample); token count; payload diversity (self-BLEU); and peak RAM and
GPU VRAM.

#### Baselines, models, and defenses.

We compare against AutoDAN [Liu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib17),
Open-Prompt-Injection [Liu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib18), ADMIT [Wu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib20), and JudgeDeceiver [Shi et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19),
each adapted to fact-checking by redirecting its objective to the
attacker-specified verdict label while preserving its original attack
mechanism. We use four open-source LLMs of comparable scale (Llama3-8B,
Gemma2-9B, Mistral-7B, Llama3.1-8B) under identical decoding settings,
context length, and hardware, and additionally study Qwen3-32B as a
stronger model. Defenses cover prevention-based instruction [Learn
Prompting (2023)](#bib.bib38) and paraphrasing [Jain et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib30) defenses and detection-based known-answer [Liu et
al. (2024)](#bib.bib18), LLM-based [Phute et al. (2024)](#bib.bib37),
and response-based [Wang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib36) detection (see
Appendix §[C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Defense Methods ‣ Appendix C Experiments ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")).

### 4.3 Ablation Studies

#### Transferability across different LLMs.

We evaluate D2D’s cross-model transferability across source LLMs, target
LLMs, and defenses

D2D maintains high ASR across heterogeneous model pairs, with the main
degradation under the Known-answer defense, showing that it is not
source-model specific and transfers reliably across target LLMs.

#### Payoff matrix sensitivity.

We ablate two aspects of the EGT payoff matrix: its overall scale and
the reward weight configuration. The reward weights used throughout this
paper achieve the highest ASR among the configurations we tested,
supporting our choice. The payoff scale used in our main experiments is
not the highest-ASR scale among those evaluated, but the spread across
scales is only about three times the run-to-run variation of the
ablation itself, so ASR is largely insensitive to the scale (see
Appendix §[C.9](#A3.SS9 "C.9 Payoff matrix sensitivity ‣ Appendix C Experiments ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")).

#### Single-Agent vs. Multi-Agent Debate and Benign Utility.

We compare single-agent with multi-agent debate to isolate the effect of
multi-agent interaction. Under the same setting, the multi-agent design
achieves a substantially higher success rate and requires fewer
iterations than the single-agent variant, indicating that opponent-aware
debate improves effectiveness and efficiency. We evaluate the benign
SimpleFacts dataset and confirm that the tested defenses largely
preserve normal factual answering ability (see
Appendix §[C.10](#A3.SS10 "C.10 Single-Agent vs. Multi-Agent Debate and Benign Utility ‣ Appendix C Experiments ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")).

We justify the core mechanism of D2D. Without the game-theoretic module,
debate-based generation remains partially effective in the no-defense
setting but degrades sharply under semantic and answer-aware defenses,
indicating that adaptive strategy selection is essential for robustness.
Separately, ablating the four-phase debate structure allows us to assess
how each debate component contributes to the overall attack process (see
Appendix §[C.11](#A3.SS11 "C.11 Game-Theoretic and Debate-Structure Ablation ‣ Appendix C Experiments ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")).

## 5 Conclusion

We propose Debate-to-Deceive (D2D), a game-theoretic multi-agent debate
method for generating adversarial passages against LLM fact-checkers.
D2D optimizes argumentative quality rather than bypassing safety
alignment. Unlike gradient-based methods that search for adversarial
token patterns, D2D uses structured debate to generate rhetorically
tailored arguments for each claim. This enables coherent, persuasive
attacks with better effectiveness, eliminates backpropagation overhead,
produces claim-specific outputs with no exploitable pattern for defenses
to detect, and provides clearer optimization signals enabling faster
convergence.

## Ethical Considerations

#### Dual-Use Nature and Research Justification.

LLM-based fact-checking systems are increasingly deployed in high-stakes
contexts including news verification and health information services.
Understanding their adversarial vulnerabilities before wide deployment
is critical. Our work is motivated by this defensive goal: to expose
structural weaknesses so the research community can develop more robust
systems before adversaries do. This work presents an adversarial attack
framework against fact-checking systems. We fully acknowledge the
dual-use risks. Adversarial attack research, even for safety purposes,
can facilitate harmful content generation, erode user trust, and be
exploited by non-experts. However, we believe that transparent
disclosure of these vulnerabilities is essential for developing robust
defenses. Our findings reveal fundamental weaknesses in current
debate-augmented fact-checking systems—weaknesses that sophisticated
adversaries could independently discover. By publishing this work, we
aim to inform the security community and motivate the development of
defenses before such techniques are weaponized.

#### Potential Risks.

The primary risks of D2D include:

- •
  Enabling misinformation campaigns that evade automated fact-checking,
  particularly in sensitive domains like public health (HealthVer) and
  climate science (CLIMATE-FEVER).
- •
  Undermining trust in AI-based content verification systems.
- •
  Facilitating adversarial manipulation in multi-agent LLM applications
  beyond fact-checking.

#### Mitigation Measures and Research Practices.

To balance scientific transparency with responsible disclosure, we adopt
the following practices:

- •
  No real-world deployment: all experiments are conducted in controlled
  research settings with no deployment component.
- •
  No code release: We do not release prompt templates, agent interaction
  scripts, or other implementation details that would make the attack
  immediately operational. Instead, we provide full methodological
  descriptions, hyperparameter settings, dataset splits, and aggregate
  results sufficient to support scientific verification, while
  withholding components that would lower the barrier to real-world
  misuse. We believe this balance is consistent with responsible
  disclosure norms in security research.
- •
  Focus on systemic vulnerabilities: Our evaluation targets fundamental
  weaknesses in strategic reasoning rather than dataset-specific
  exploits, providing generalizable insights for defense design.
- •
  Barrier to reproduction: While our method does not require extensive
  computational resources, successful reproduction requires expertise in
  MARL, evolutionary game theory, and LLM prompt engineering—limiting
  trivial misuse.
- •
  Constructive framing: Throughout the paper, we emphasize defensive
  implications, highlighting what current defenses miss (strategic
  adaptation, game-theoretic reasoning) to guide future research
  directions. the paper explicitly frames evaluation as the intended use
  case.

#### Limitations and Broader Impact.

We acknowledge that this research only provides attack mechanisms.
However, this work highlights weaknesses in LLMs that could be leveraged
to develop defense methods against these attacks in future research. The
work is developed independently, without explicit agreement or
collaboration with maintainers of open-source attack and defense
repositories. However, we believe this research provides critical value
by:

- •
  Exposing that pattern-based defenses are insufficient against
  strategically adaptive adversaries.
- •
  Demonstrating the need for game-theoretic reasoning for LLM security
  in multi-agent settings.
- •
  Providing a theoretical framework (evolutionary game formulation) for
  understanding adversarial co-evolution.

We recognize that security through obscurity is ineffective in the long
term. As LLM-based systems are increasingly deployed in high-stakes
domains (medical advice, scientific discourse, content moderation),
understanding their strategic vulnerabilities is essential. Our work
follows the established tradition of adversarial security research,
where transparent disclosure of attacks ultimately strengthens defenses
more than it aids attackers.

#### Ethical Uncertainty.

We acknowledge that our risk assessment may not capture all potential
harms, particularly in contexts where fact-checking intersects with
political discourse and institutional trust. We welcome community
feedback on additional ethical considerations and commit to engaging
with responsible AI deployment practices as this research area evolves.

## Appendix B D2D Method

### B.1 Strategy Dynamics Beyond the Static Equilibrium

##### Setup.

Let $`x\in\Delta^{2}`$ be the share of (Logos, Pathos, Ethos) and
$`B=-B^{\top}`$ the payoff matrix, with unique interior rest point
$`w^{*}`$ ($`Bw^{*}=0`$ for $`\alpha=1,\beta=2,\gamma=2`$). Strategy
shares evolve by

|     |                                                       |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \dot{x}_{i}=x_{i}\big[(Bx+q)_{i}-x^{\top}(Bx+q)\big],
       ```                                                    |     |

where $`q`$ is the quality term ($`q=0`$ recovers the pure matrix game).
Skew-symmetry gives $`x^{\top}Bx=0`$.

###### Proposition 1 (Non-convergence).

Let $`q=0`$. Then $`H(x)=\sum_{i}w^{*}_{i}\log x_{i}`$ is invariant
along trajectories. Hence every interior orbit with $`x(0)\neq w^{*}`$,
including the uniform initialisation of Algorithm 1, is a closed
periodic orbit that never approaches $`w^{*}`$.

###### Proof.

$`\dot{H}=\sum_{i}w^{*}_{i}\,\dot{x}_{i}/x_{i}=w^{*\top}Bx=-(Bw^{*})^{\top}x=0`$.
$`H`$ is strictly concave on $`\Delta^{2}`$ with unique maximiser
$`w^{*}`$, so its level sets are closed curves around $`w^{*}`$.
$`x(0)\neq w^{*}`$ implies $`H(x(0))<H(w^{*})`$, so $`x(t)`$ stays on
that curve for all $`t`$. Since $`w^{*}`$ is the only interior rest
point, the 2-D flow on the curve is periodic. ∎

###### Remark 1 (Time average).

Because $`\frac{d}{dt}\log x_{i}=(Bx)_{i}`$, integrating gives
$`B\bar{x}_{T}=\tfrac{1}{T}[\log x(T)-\log x(0)]\to 0`$, so
$`\bar{x}_{T}\to w^{*}`$. The *average* mixture equals $`w^{*}`$, but
the instantaneous policy is non-stationary and temporally correlated,
which i.i.d. sampling from $`w^{*}`$ does not reproduce.

###### Proposition 2 (Quality term moves the centre).

For constant $`q`$, let $`\hat{x}(q)`$ be the interior rest point
($`B\hat{x}+q=c\mathbf{1}`$, $`\mathbf{1}^{\top}\hat{x}=1`$). Then
$`H_{q}(x)=\sum_{i}\hat{x}_{i}\log x_{i}`$ is invariant. Orbits are
closed curves around $`\hat{x}(q)`$, and $`\hat{x}(q)\neq w^{*}`$
whenever $`q\notin\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{1})`$.

###### Proof.

$`\dot{H}_{q}=\hat{x}^{\top}(Bx+q)-x^{\top}q`$. Using
$`\hat{x}^{\top}Bx=-(B\hat{x})^{\top}x=-(c\mathbf{1}-q)^{\top}x=-c+q^{\top}x`$
and $`c=\hat{x}^{\top}(B\hat{x}+q)=\hat{x}^{\top}q`$, we get
$`\dot{H}_{q}=0`$. If $`\hat{x}=w^{*}`$, then $`q=c\mathbf{1}`$. ∎

### C.2 Baseline Methods

We compare D2D with four representative prompt-attack baselines, as
summarized in
Table [6](#A3.T6 "Table 6 ‣ C.2 Baseline Methods ‣ Appendix C Experiments ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate").

### C.8 Empirical Estimation of the Payoff Matrix

We examine the empirical basis of the cyclic payoff model in
Sec. [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Debate Strategy Selection Payoff ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate").
Following empirical game-theoretic analysis [Wellman
(2006)](#bib.bib47); [Tuyls et al. (2018)](#bib.bib48), we fix each
debater to one macro-strategy across all four phases and evaluate every
ordered pair $`(S_{j},S_{k})`$, $`j,k\in\{1,2,3\}`$, on 25 FEVER and 25
HealthVer claims in the Support$`\rightarrow`$Refute direction. This
yields 450 debates, including 439 with a stance-aligned passage. Success
is determined by whether $`A_{G}`$ returns the target label from the
claim and passage alone. Let $`p_{j,k}`$ be the success rate when
$`A_{L^{\prime}}`$ uses $`S_{j}`$ against $`A_{L}`$ using $`S_{k}`$. To
remove any constant additive role advantage, we estimate

|     |                                                |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \hat{B}_{j,k}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,(p_{j,k}-p_{k,j}),
       ```                                             |     | (13) |

with confidence intervals obtained by cluster bootstrapping over claims.
The transitive/cyclic decomposition of [Balduzzi et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib49) attributes $`87\%`$ of
$`\|\hat{\mathbf{B}}\|_{F}^{2}`$ to the cyclic component, providing
empirical support for modelling rhetorical strategies through cyclic
interactions.

### C.9 Payoff matrix sensitivity

We ablate two aspects of the EGT payoff matrix: its overall scale, and
the reward weight configuration
($`\lambda_{\text{quality}},\lambda_{\text{time}},\lambda_{\text{econ}}`$),
each on the same 50 targets. Two independent runs of the identical
configuration ($`\lambda=(1.5,0.10,0.005)`$, scale $`=1`$) gave 84.0%
and 82.0%, so one claim out of 50, or $`2`$ points, is the run-to-run
resolution of this ablation; we read the two tables against that floor.
Table [14](#A3.T14 "Table 14 ‣ C.9 Payoff matrix sensitivity ‣ Appendix C Experiments ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")
shows a $`6`$-point spread across the four scales tested (84.0–90.0%):
the scale used in our main experiments is not the strongest of those
evaluated, but the spread is only about three times the repeat-run
difference, so we do not claim an ordering among scales.
Table [13](#A3.T13 "Table 13 ‣ C.9 Payoff matrix sensitivity ‣ Appendix C Experiments ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")
shows a $`12`$-point spread (72.0–84.0%), well above that floor, and the
configuration used throughout the paper is the highest of the three,
supporting our choice of reward weights. The weak dependence on the
payoff scale is consistent with the head-to-head estimate in
§[C.8](#A3.SS8 "C.8 Empirical Estimation of the Payoff Matrix ‣ Appendix C Experiments ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate"),
where the three rhetorical strategies differ by at most $`2`$ points
against each other: what the matrix contributes is the cyclic structure
that keeps the policy mixing, not the particular magnitudes of its
coefficients.

### C.11 Game-Theoretic and Debate-Structure Ablation

Table [17](#A3.T17 "Table 17 ‣ C.11 Game-Theoretic and Debate-Structure Ablation ‣ Appendix C Experiments ‣ Debate to Deceive: Poisoning LLM’s Fact-Checking via Game-Theoretic Multi-Agent Debate")
reports the ablation results after removing the game-theoretic
optimization component from D2D. Although the remaining multi-agent
debate and rhetorical generation components can still produce effective
adversarial passages in the no-defense setting, performance drops
sharply once defenses are applied. The degradation is especially
pronounced under stronger semantic or answer-aware defenses, such as
LLM-based and Known-answer defenses. These results show that
game-theoretic optimization is central to D2D’s robustness, as it
provides adaptive strategy selection beyond debate-based generation
````

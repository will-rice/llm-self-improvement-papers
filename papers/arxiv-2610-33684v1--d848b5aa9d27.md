# MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents

###### Abstract

Self-evolving training improves the performance of agents on difficult
tasks through verifiable feedback, but it often applies costly
deliberation uniformly across tasks. To address this problem, we propose
Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort (MORE), a
self-evolving training framework that adaptively selects both reasoning
form and computational effort for each task. Specifically, MORE follows
a two-stage training paradigm: (i) _Stage 1_ establishes a compact prior
over direct answering, short and long chain-of-thought, and code-based
reasoning, and (ii) _Stage 2_ self-evolves this prior with Mode-Adaptive
and Test-time Effort PPO (MATE-PPO) on generated, verifiable task
streams without curated data. By learning mode selection and token
expenditure jointly, MORE allocates concise reasoning to tasks that
permit it while retaining extended deliberation where it is useful.
Across coding, mathematics, and general-reasoning benchmarks, MORE
achieves consistent performance gains on Base and Coder backbones at
model scales from 3B to 14B. During inference, MORE demonstrates higher
accuracy and better efficiency, with markedly shorter response lengths
and reduced inference time.

## 1 Introduction

Self-evolving training, especially for coding and mathematics reasoning,
is a post-training approach that aims to strengthen agents’ reasoning
ability without human-supervision. ‌The prevailing existing methods
designed for coding and mathematics reasoning use Proposer-Solver
paradigm for reasoning models in which the model simultaneously learns
to define tasks that maximize learnability and to solve them
effectively, enabling self-evolution through self-play without relying
on external data. In practice, people find that reasoning-oriented
language models improve difficult problem solving by making intermediate
computation explicit, showcasing superior performance.

Typically, this intermediate computation is mainly produced by
Chain-of-thought and Reinforcement learning. Chain-of-thought prompting
and self-consistency establish that generated reasoning can improve
answer quality ([Wei et al., 2023](#bib.bib1); [Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib2)), while test-time scaling shows that additional
sampling or search can convert more inference compute into higher
accuracy ([Brown et al., 2024](#bib.bib22); [Snell et al.,
2024](#bib.bib23)). Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
extends this principle into training: execution, answer extraction, and
formal checks provide scalable feedback for coding and mathematical
reasoning ([DeepSeek-AI, 2025](#bib.bib3); [Zhao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4)). Despite their successes, these objectives do not
specify how much computation is appropriate for each task.

To this end, the absence of accuracy-computation calibration becomes
consequential in self-evolving agents. Generated tasks, sampled
solutions, and verifier outcomes continually reshape both capability and
effective task difficulty. Repeated exposure to difficult verifiable
tasks can make long reasoning a default hedge, even when a direct answer
would suffice, increasing response length and completion time.
Autonomous evolution can also introduce capability degradation and
safety drift across coding, mathematical, and safety evaluations ([Shi
et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)). Long reasoning is valuable when needed, but
self-evolving agents must allocate it where its accuracy gains justify
its cost.

Difficulty-aware compute allocation addresses only part of this problem.
CODA, for example, argues that simple instances often gain little from
additional tokens whereas hard instances may still require deliberate
computation ([Wu et al., 2026](#bib.bib7)). A scalar budget alone,
however, can not decide whether the computation should be a direct
response, a long derivation, or executable code. Conversely, choosing a
discrete reasoning format without controlling its length can still
produce verbose traces. Self-evolution adds another complication: a
static reward schedule is calibrated to one policy state, even though
the solver’s success profile and useful budget change during training.

Given the above, we propose Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive
Effort (MORE), a self-evolving training framework that learns four
unique reasoning forms and computation amount as coupled policy
decisions. Specifically, for task $`x`$ and response $`y`$, MORE
factorizes generation as
$`\pi_{\theta}(a_{\mathrm{mode}},y\mid x)=\pi_{\theta}(a_{\mathrm{mode}}\mid x)\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x,a_{\mathrm{mode}})`$,
where $`a_{\mathrm{mode}}`$ is one of direct, short_cot, long_cot, or
code. This factorization separates the form of computation from its
amount while allowing both decisions to be optimized by the same
training signal. Before producing the response, MORE emits a structured
\<mode\> control token as its first internal decision. Thereafter, the
token conditions the subsequent response and supports training and
inference statistics.

MORE learns this behavior in two stages. Mode-format supervision first
makes the action vocabulary identifiable and gives each mode a stable
response contract. Mode-Adaptive & Test-time Effort PPO (MATE-PPO) is
our proposed Stage-2 optimization method for coupled mode–budget
learning ([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib5)). It integrates
verifier-grounded correctness, online difficulty estimation, mode fit,
and dynamic budget regulation into a single trajectory objective, so
clipped policy updates jointly shape reasoning form and computation
amount as a learned model capability.

Our core contributions are as follows: i) We introduce MORE, a
self-evolving training framework that jointly adapts reasoning mode and
computation budget to online task difficulty. Across coding,
mathematical, and general-reasoning benchmarks, MORE delivers strong and
consistent performance. ii) Our two-stage learning design first
establishes four mode-specific response contracts and then applies
MATE-PPO to jointly optimize mode choice, response generation, and
computational effort through a unified verifier-grounded trajectory
objective. iii) We design an online difficulty–effort controller that
converts evolving rollout clusters into difficulty tiers and updates
mode-conditioned budget bands throughout training, enabling the policy
to calibrate its reasoning allocation as its capabilities change. MORE
also reaches its strongest observed out-of-distribution performance
earlier during self-evolution than another framework in the same
self-evolving paradigm.

## 2 Related Work

Mode-aware reasoning. Hybrid reasoning models expose multiple response
forms so that a solver need not apply one deliberation pattern to every
query. Representative approaches distinguish direct answers from longer
reasoning or combine textual and code-mediated computation ([Wu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib9); [Luo et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)). Uniform response
behavior can be brittle under task-specific requirements ([Fu and Shi,
2025](#bib.bib12)). A fixed mode policy, however, does not track the
changing competence of a self-evolving solver, and a mode choice alone
does not constrain token use within that mode.

Adaptive reasoning budgets. Inference-time scaling, budget forcing, and
adaptive length objectives control how much computation a model spends
([Muennighoff et al., 2025](#bib.bib24); [Zhu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib25)). LASER conditions length-based reward shaping on
difficulty ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)), and further, CODA jointly
studies difficulty estimation and compute allocation ([Wu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib7)). This line demonstrates that uniform token budgets are
inefficient, but it largely treats response format as fixed or allocates
compute after the mode of reasoning has already been chosen. MORE
addresses the intersection: it trains a self-evolving model based on the
Proposer-Solver paradigm with a shared policy over reasoning mode and
budget, and updates the associated control state as capability changes.

## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 MORE Framework

Our proposed Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort (MORE)
is a two-stage self-evolving training framework that enables models to
learn both the form and the amount of computation used for each task.
The model trained under MORE framework first emits one of four internal
modes: direct, short_cot, long_cot, or code, and then generates a
response under the corresponding reasoning contract and a
difficulty-conditioned effort budget.

Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 MORE Framework ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents")
summarizes the complete MORE framework. MORE’s Stage 1 constructs
mode-format supervision to initialize a stable prior over the four
reasoning contracts. MORE’s Stage 2 draws inspiration from AZR’s
proposer–solver self-evolving paradigm ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib4))
and it performs the overall reinforcement-learning procedure: it samples
verifiable tasks, generates mode-conditioned rollouts, verifies their
outcomes, updates online difficulty and budget state, constructs the
MORE terminal return, and applies a Mode-Adaptive & Test-time Effort
(MATE) PPO update. Telemetry from the same loop records capability, mode
allocation, token economy, and system efficiency.

![Refer to caption](2610.33684v1/pipeline3.png)

Figure 2: Overview of the MORE framework. Mode-format supervision
initializes four reasoning contracts. MATE-PPO then couples

update. The diagram summarizes correctness gating: mode-fit and joint
credit require a correct response, while the budget-deviation term
regulates overlength for both correct and incorrect responses.

Stage 2 operationalizes MORE through a self-evolving proposer–solver
training loop, with MATE-PPO providing its policy-optimization
mechanism. For each sampled task, MORE generates a mode token and
response under a pre-update difficulty context and mode-conditioned
budget band. The verifier then supplies correctness, which MATE-PPO
combines with the retained controller state to form the terminal return
for a clipped policy update. The controller then updates the associated
cluster’s difficulty context and mode-conditioned budget band for
subsequent rollouts. The generated mode token remains a
likelihood-bearing part of the optimized trajectory, linking mode
selection, response generation, and effort calibration.

gives the complete training process.

### 3.2 Stage 1: Mode-Format Initialization

First of all, Stage 1 builds a response-format prior before sparse
outcome feedback is introduced. Specifically, each question is paired
with one of four output contracts: an answer-only record for direct, a
compact COT rationale for short_cot, a detailed and complex COT
rationale for long_cot, or executable CODE followed by an answer for
code. The tags serve as internal supervision targets and do not appear
as user-facing method names. They make response form observable to the
model while preserving a common final-answer field to achieve consistent
universality.

For training data, all backbones use the same SFT corpus,
AQuA-RAT^(10.8K), obtained by processing the original AQuA-RAT examples
into four reasoning-mode formats ([Ling et al., 2017](#bib.bib26); [Wu
et al., 2025](#bib.bib9)).
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B SFT Datasets in Stage 1 ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents")
gives the complete construction details. The resulting tuples
$`(x,a_{\mathrm{mode}},y)`$ are optimized with
$`\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{sft}}}[\log\pi_{\theta}(a_{\mathrm{mode}},y\mid x)]`$.
Stage 1 therefore establishes the mode vocabulary, response contracts,
and the prior needed for autonomous mode-token generation in Stage 2.

### 3.3 Stage 2: Self-Evolving Mode–Budget Optimization

We implement the Stage-2 update with MATE-PPO by treating the generated
mode token and its subsequent response as one policy trajectory. The
verifier-derived correctness score, online difficulty state, mode-fit
utility, and budget-deviation signals jointly define the trajectory
return. With likelihood ratio
$`r_{t}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})`$,
MATE-PPO aims to maximize

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MATE\text{-}PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\min\!\left(r_{t}\hat{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{MORE}},\operatorname{clip}(r_{t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{t}^{\mathrm{MORE}}\right)\right].
``` |  | (1) |

Here, MATE-PPO constructs $`\hat{A}^{\mathrm{MORE}}`$ from the
mode-budget-aware terminal return and controller state, then optimizes
the joint trajectory with a clipped policy objective. The
likelihood-bearing mode token selects the associated mode utility and
budget band, and contributes to mode ratios, per-mode success and
mode-difficulty telemetry. The following subsections provide further
details.

### 3.4 Adaptive Mode-Budget Control

#### Online difficulty.

The controller represents each task by structural and behavioral
features. Firstly, Structural features include code shape, prompt
artifacts, task family, and verifier target; behavioral features include
rolling success and response length. Thus, a global success threshold
would become stale as a self-evolving policy improves, whereas
independent per-task estimates are sparse and noisy. Then, Clustering
pools evidence across structurally and semantically comparable rollouts;
the EMA follows policy drift without reacting fully to one outcome; and
quantile tiers preserve relative difficulty when absolute success rises.
Finally, the resulting tier indexes both mode-fit utility and
mode-conditioned budget bands, applying stronger pressure toward direct
or short traces in currently easy regions while retaining wider
long-reasoning or code allowances in hard regions.

Subsequently, a stable task identifier is assigned to a family- and
verifier-aware cluster $`c`$. After a verified rollout, its success
estimate is updated by $`q_{c}^{(t)}=\rho q_{c}^{(t-1)}+(1-\rho)c_{t}`$,
where $`c_{t}\in\{0,1\}`$ and $`\rho=0.8`$. Mature clusters are ranked
by success: clusters above the $`0.67`$ quantile are easy, those at or
below the $`0.33`$ quantile are hard, and the remainder are medium. The
updated state is carried into subsequent rollouts, allowing mode
preferences and budget bands to track the evolving policy rather than a
fixed notion of task difficulty. The complete rollout representation,
family and verifier partitioning, centroid and EMA updates, among other
details, are provided in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Rollout Clustering and Difficulty Assignment ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents").

#### Autonomous mode-token generation.

For every rollout, MORE generates a \<mode=m\> control token before the
response body, with
$`m\in\{\text{direct},\text{short-CoT},\text{long-CoT},\text{code}\}`$.
This token is the realized $`a_{\mathrm{mode}}`$: it selects the
response contract, indexes $`U(d,m)`$, and identifies the
mode-conditioned budget band. As a likelihood-bearing policy action, it
makes mode allocation an endogenous model behavior under the task
distribution.

#### Mode-conditioned budget bands.

Each $`(d,m)`$ cell has a lower target $`B^{-}_{d,m}`$ and upper target
$`B^{+}_{d,m}`$. Correct response lengths are accumulated through
hierarchical keys, from cluster/family/verifier/mode statistics to
tier/mode aggregates. Once enough observations are available, the lower
and upper targets use empirical quantiles $`Q_{0.2}`$ and $`Q_{0.8}`$;
sparse cells retain the initialized prior. Downward updates are
rate-limited and the lower target is constrained below the upper target,
preventing one short batch from collapsing the band. The bands provide a
soft learning signal that preserves flexible generation while
progressively calibrating token use and the inference-time latent budget
is determined by the band at final training checkpoint.
Appendix [F](#A6 "Appendix F Reward Defaults and Runtime Settings ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents")
reports their initialization.

### 3.5 The MORE Terminal Return

The return used by MATE-PPO is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{MORE}}=R_{\mathrm{acc}}+\alpha R_{\mathrm{BD}}+\beta R_{\mathrm{MF}}+\gamma R_{\mathrm{MB}},
``` |  | (2) |

where $`R_{\mathrm{BD}}`$ is the budget-deviation term,
$`R_{\mathrm{MF}}`$ is the mode-fit term, and $`R_{\mathrm{MB}}`$ is the
mode–budget joint term. For solver trajectories, the runtime composes
verifier-provided $`R_{\mathrm{acc}}`$ with
$`\Delta_{\mathrm{MORE}}=\alpha R_{\mathrm{BD}}+\beta R_{\mathrm{MF}}+\gamma R_{\mathrm{MB}}`$,
realizing the complete terminal return while counting execution feedback
exactly once.
Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 MATE-PPO ‣ Appendix C Stage 2 Training Process ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents")
gives the parallel role-specific form for all proposer–solver streams.

#### Budget-deviation reward.

For response length $`L`$, define normalized deviations
$`o=\max(0,(L-B^{+}_{d,m})/B^{+}_{d,m})`$ and
$`u=\max(0,(B^{-}_{d,m}-L)/B^{-}_{d,m})`$. The main realization uses
$`R_{\mathrm{BD}}=\operatorname{clip}[-\lambda_{d}(w_{o}o+w_{u}(c)u),-0.5,0]`$,
with $`w_{o}=1`$, $`w_{u}(1)=1`$ for correct responses, and
$`w_{u}(0)=0.1`$ for incorrect responses. It penalizes overlong
responses regardless of correctness, down-weights lower-bound pressure
on incorrect responses, and ensures that brevity earns efficiency credit
only together with correctness.

#### Mode-fit reward.

For a correct response, $`R_{\mathrm{MF}}=U(d,m)`$. The structured
utility prior favors direct or short reasoning for easy tasks and
allocates stronger credit to long reasoning or code as difficulty rises.
Stage 1 makes these formats identifiable; $`R_{\mathrm{MF}}`$ then
supplies outcome-conditioned credit for selecting them appropriately.

#### Mode–budget joint term.

For correct responses,
$`R_{\mathrm{MB}}=\operatorname{clip}[-\eta(d,m)(o+u),-0.25,0]`$. This
coupling directly measures mode-specific budget deviation and supplies a
bounded joint learning signal. The main run uses
$`(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(0.6,1.0,0.05)`$.

### 3.6 Inference and Telemetry Interface

The same four-mode vocabulary is used in supervision, rollout
generation, reward routing, inference, and evaluation. MORE emits the
mode token before the response body and then follows the corresponding
response contract. The inference harness parses the generated token for
telemetry and scores the resulting answer. The telemetry interface
records mode ratios, per-mode success, difficulty tiers, budget bands,
reward components, response-length distributions, throughput, and
wall-clock components, providing a unified view of adaptive reasoning
behavior.

### 4.2 Capability Evaluation and Efficiency Metrics

We use CruxEval-I/O ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib29)) and
LiveCodeBench-Execution ([Jain et al., 2024](#bib.bib28)) as our
in-distribution (ID) capability benchmarks. Further, we group the
out-of-distribution (OOD) capability benchmarks into code and
mathematical reasoning: Code evaluation uses HumanEval⁺, MBPP⁺, and
LiveCodeBench Generation v1–v5 ([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib27); [Jain et
al., 2024](#bib.bib28)). Mathematical evaluation uses AIME 2024, AIME
2025, AMC 2023, MATH-500, Minerva Math, and OlympiadBench ([Hendrycks et
al., 2021](#bib.bib30); [He et al., 2024](#bib.bib31); [Lewkowycz et
al., 2022](#bib.bib32)). We additionally report GSM8K and MMLU-Pro as
supplemental general-reasoning benchmarks.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Training Hyperparameters and Metric Definitions ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents")
gives metric definitions and decoding settings. MORE-Auto uses
mode-neutral prompts: it emits \<mode\> before answering, and the
harness records that token without modifying the response.

We assess efficiency during both self-evolving training and
out-of-distribution testing through computational demand, response
behavior, elapsed time, processing throughput, and convergence. For
out-of-distribution evaluation, Response Length Avg is the sample-mean
number of generated response tokens, and Wall Time is the total elapsed
time for the corresponding benchmark suite. Under matched software,
hardware, and decoding settings, we track token consumption, Response
Length Avg, Wall Time, mode–budget allocation, and GPU-normalized
throughput. Relating these measurements to capability trajectories over
optimizer steps and cumulative training tokens shows how quickly each
method reaches strong performance and at what computational cost.

shows that expanding the maximum response length from 8,096 to 10,240
tokens raises AVG by 0.3 for both the Base and AZR controls, but
produces mixed movements on individual benchmarks. This limited gain
suggests that simply extending the generation ceiling approaches a
capacity bottleneck at the 7B scale. Also, Stage 1 is not a
capability-only warm-up: its mode SFT deliberately reshapes the backbone
distribution to fit four tagged response contracts. Immediately after
this reshaping, CAvg falls from 56.6 to 52.2 and MAvg from 23.9 to 19.9,
reducing AVG from 48.8 to 45.3. Learning the prescribed mode syntax and
response forms therefore trades part of the backbone’s original
unconditioned coding and mathematical behavior for a stable,
controllable mode prior. The same shift is visible when this prior
precedes AZR, whose AVG changes from 54.4 to 52.4. Conversely, Stage 2
without Stage 1 reaches 47.8 AVG: it retains substantial reasoning
ability, but has not learned the four-format prior needed for autonomous
mode allocation. MATE-PPO aligns the Stage-1 prior with
verifier-grounded self-evolution. The complete two-stage configuration
combines stable mode-contract learning with adaptive optimization and
attains the highest AVG (57.3), CAvg, MAvg, GSM8K, and MMLU-Pro.

## 6 Limitations

Our study does not include fine-grained ablations of MATE-PPO’s internal
components, since isolating individual terms while preserving its
coupled mode–difficulty–budget dynamics did not yield a meaningful
controlled comparison. MORE uses four predefined response contracts and
online difficulty estimates from task and rollout statistics. Its
efficiency metrics are too coarse to resolve relative changes among
finer-grained time components across training and evaluation.

## 7 Conclusion

We introduced MORE, a two-stage self-evolving training framework that
learns reasoning mode and computational effort as coupled policy
decisions. Stage 1 establishes compact response contracts for direct
answering, short and long chain-of-thought, and code-based reasoning.
Stage 2 self-evolves this prior on generated, verifiable task streams
without curated data, with MATE-PPO optimizing mode selection, response
generation, and effort calibration within one trajectory objective.
Across coding, mathematical, and general-reasoning benchmarks, MORE
achieves strong and consistent performance on Base and Coder backbones
and model scales from 3B to 14B. MORE reaches strong capability earlier
during self-evolution while sustaining lower rollout token demand,
shorter evaluation responses, and lower inference latency. Taken
together, these research results establish MORE as a framework for
task-adaptive reasoning form and effort, advancing efficient
self-evolving training and its models’ reasoning through joint
mode–budget learning.

### A.3 Metric Definitions

#### Capability aggregates.

Coding evaluation uses 164 HumanEval⁺ and 378 MBPP⁺ problems from
EvalPlus together with the cumulative LiveCodeBench v1–v5 views
containing 400, 511, 612, 713, and 880 problems. EvalPlus uses greedy
generation. LiveCodeBench uses one-sample pass@1 with temperature 0,

## Appendix C Stage 2 Training Process

Stage 2 uses one policy in a closed proposer–solver loop. At every
update, the policy first constructs executable tasks, then solves tasks
drawn from the corresponding typed pools. Both roles generate a mode
token followed by a response, receive execution-grounded terminal
feedback, and contribute trajectories to the same policy update. This
loop continuously refreshes the training distribution as the policy
improves.

### C.1 Six Proposer–Solver Rollout Streams

The task space combines three verifiable reasoning families with two
policy roles, yielding six rollout streams. Let $`G_{\tau}`$ and
$`S_{\tau}`$ denote proposer and solver rollouts for family
$`\tau\in\{\mathrm{in},\mathrm{out},\mathrm{fun}\}`$. The resulting
batch is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{B}_{t}=\mathcal{B}_{t}^{G_{\mathrm{in}}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{in}}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{t}^{G_{\mathrm{out}}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{out}}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{t}^{G_{\mathrm{fun}}}\cup\mathcal{B}_{t}^{S_{\mathrm{fun}}}.
``` |  | (6) |

The three families and their executable contracts are summarized in
Table [6](#A3.T6 "Table 6 ‣ C.1 Six Proposer–Solver Rollout Streams ‣ Appendix C Stage 2 Training Process ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents").

|  Family |  Proposed task state |  Solver target |  Execution check |
|----|----|----|----|
|  Input recovery (abduction) |  Executable function $`f`$ and an input $`x`$; execution supplies $`y=f(x)`$ |  Recover $`\hat{x}`$ from $`(f,y)`$ |  Check $`f(\hat{x})=y`$ |
|  Output prediction (deduction) |  Executable function $`f`$ paired with input $`x`$ |  Predict $`\hat{y}`$ from $`(f,x)`$ |  Compare $`\hat{y}`$ with $`f(x)`$ |
|  Function induction |  Inputs for a latent executable function; execution supplies visible and hidden I/O pairs |  Synthesize $`\hat{f}`$ from visible pairs |  Run $`\hat{f}`$ on every hidden pair |

Table 6: Three executable task families, each instantiated as a proposer
and a solver stream.

For input recovery and output prediction, the proposer generates a
parseable Python function and input state; the executor checks validity
and materializes the corresponding output. For function induction, it
proposes an input set for a latent executable function, from which
visible and held-out I/O pairs are constructed. Accepted tasks enter
family-specific pools, and solver batches are built from those evolving
pools. Thus, new task construction and policy improvement remain coupled
without requiring externally labeled Stage-2 solutions.

### C.2 Execution-Grounded Feedback

The proposer is rewarded for producing valid tasks that are solvable but
not already trivial for the current policy. For a valid candidate
$`z_{j}`$, the solver generates $`K=8`$ independent attempts. With
family-specific executor $`V_{\tau}`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{p}_{j}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}V_{\tau}\!\left(z_{j},\tilde{y}_{j}^{(k)}\right),\qquad R_{\mathrm{prop}}(z_{j})=g_{j}\,\mathbb{I}[\hat{p}_{j}>0](1-\hat{p}_{j}),
``` |  | (7) |

where $`g_{j}\in\{0,1\}`$ is the parse, determinism, and
execution-validity gate. Invalid candidates and candidates for which all
sampled solutions fail receive zero proposer reward; among solvable
tasks, lower current success yields larger learning credit. Every valid
proposal is retained in its typed pool for subsequent solver sampling.

Solver feedback is direct execution accuracy. A format or execution
failure receives zero. Otherwise,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{acc}}=V_{\tau}(z,\hat{y}),\quad V_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathbb{I}[f(\hat{x})=y],\quad V_{\mathrm{out}}=\mathbb{I}[\hat{y}=f(x)],\quad V_{\mathrm{fun}}=\prod_{(x_{h},y_{h})\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{I}[\hat{f}(x_{h})=y_{h}],
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\mathcal{H}`$ is the held-out I/O set for function induction.
The binary hidden-test rule gives function synthesis full accuracy
credit only when all held-out cases pass. These verifier outcomes are
returned to the same trajectories that produced the proposals or
solutions and also update the online difficulty and budget state.

### C.3 MATE-PPO

#### PPO base for joint mode–budget control.

MATE-PPO uses a PPO-style clipped actor update as its optimization base
and augments it with a controller-conditioned terminal return. Unlike
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib6)), which forms advantages by ranking multiple
completions of a common prompt within a local group, MORE must optimize
heterogeneous proposer and solver behaviors under one shared policy.
Proposer samples construct different executable tasks, whereas several
solver modes can be correct and compliant with their own $`(d,m)`$
budget bands on the same task. Group-relative normalization would make
such valid task constructions or mode contracts compete within a sampled
group and remove return differences across task groups from the
advantage estimator. The trajectory-level PPO objective instead
preserves direct, shared-scale credit for task construction, mode
selection, solution accuracy, and effort calibration across the
six-stream update. It also maintains broad task coverage for the online
controller without requiring repeated long rollouts from every frozen
controller state.

MATE-PPO constructs a unified terminal return by integrating
role-specific execution feedback with difficulty-conditioned budget
calibration, mode-fit utility, and mode–budget interaction. For rollout
$`i`$, let the role-specific execution gate be

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{i}=\begin{cases}R_{\mathrm{acc},i}=V_{\tau}(z_{i},\hat{y}_{i}),&i\text{ is a solver rollout},\\
g_{i}\,\mathbb{I}[\hat{p}_{i}>0],&i\text{ is a proposer rollout},\end{cases}
``` |  | (9) |

where the proposer gate requires a parseable, deterministic, executable
proposal with at least one successful solver attempt. The terminal
return is then

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{base},i}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\begin{cases}R_{\mathrm{prop},i},&i\text{ is a proposer rollout},\\
R_{\mathrm{acc},i},&i\text{ is a solver rollout},\end{cases}`$ |  | (10) |
|  | $`\displaystyle R_{i}^{\mathrm{terminal}}`$ | $`\displaystyle=R_{\mathrm{base},i}+\alpha R_{\mathrm{BD},i}+\beta R_{\mathrm{MF},i}+\gamma R_{\mathrm{MB},i}.`$ |  |  |

For solver trajectories,
Equation [10](#A3.E10 "In PPO base for joint mode–budget control. ‣ C.3 MATE-PPO ‣ Appendix C Stage 2 Training Process ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents")
is exactly the MORE return in
Equation [2](#S3.E2 "In 3.5 The MORE Terminal Return ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents").
For both roles, the mode-fit and joint terms use the execution gate:
$`R_{\mathrm{MF},i}=c_{i}U(d_{i},m_{i})`$ and
$`R_{\mathrm{MB},i}=c_{i}\operatorname{clip}[-\eta(d_{i},m_{i})(o_{i}+u_{i}),-0.25,0]`$.
The budget-deviation term retains its overlength penalty for every
rollout and uses $`w_{u}(c_{i})`$ for its lower-band weight. The
additive mode–budget delta is written to the final valid response
position, so the role-specific execution reward is counted once. The
mode token remains likelihood-bearing and is optimized jointly with the
response tokens.

#### Advantage estimation and mode-token credit.

After generation, the runtime recomputes old-policy log probabilities,
evaluates the six stream-specific terminal returns, and concatenates the
resulting rollouts. For rollout $`i`$ with $`T_{i}`$ valid generated
tokens, the token-level reward is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{i,t}=\mathbb{I}[t=T_{i}]R_{i}^{\mathrm{terminal}},\qquad G_{i,t}=\sum_{k=t}^{T_{i}}r_{i,k},
``` |  |

with discount factor $`\gamma=1`$. Let
$`\mathcal{V}=\{(i,t):1\leq t\leq T_{i}\}`$ be the valid-token set of
the concatenated six-stream batch, and let $`\mu_{\mathcal{V}}`$ and
$`\sigma_{\mathcal{V}}`$ be the mean and unbiased standard deviation of
$`G_{i,t}`$ over this set. The REINFORCE++ advantage is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{A}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{MORE}}=\frac{G_{i,t}-\mu_{\mathcal{V}}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathcal{V}}^{2}+10^{-8}}},\qquad(i,t)\in\mathcal{V}.
``` |  |

Padding positions are masked from both the statistics and policy loss.
Thus, the six streams share one valid-token normalization set. All
terminal components, including negative budget-deviation and mode–budget
terms, enter $`R_{i}^{\mathrm{terminal}}`$ before return computation and
normalization. The generated \<mode\> span occupies the first valid
positions of the rollout and is included in the policy-loss mask.
Because the return is terminal and $`\gamma=1`$, its tokens receive the
same standardized trajectory advantage as the subsequent response
tokens; the selected mode consequently receives direct policy credit
through its effect on the terminal return. With
$`r_{i,t}(\theta)=\pi_{\theta}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})/\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t})`$,
the actor update is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MATE\text{-}PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{i,t}\!\left[\min\!\left(r_{i,t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{MORE}},\operatorname{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{MORE}}\right)\right].
``` |  | (11) |

## Appendix D Mode-Token Interface and MATE-PPO Runtime

The policy trajectory begins with a generated \<mode\> control token
followed by mode-conditioned response tokens. The runtime preserves this
first action together with its likelihood, task identifier, verifier
score, attended response length, controller state, and budget band. The
same token is consumed by reward computation and telemetry, so mode
learning, response generation, and evaluation share one representation.

For each rollout, the runtime snapshots the current controller state
$`(D_{t},B_{t})`$ before generation. The generated mode token selects
the response contract and indexes the band in that snapshot. After
verification, the terminal MORE delta is added at the final valid
response position using the same $`(D_{t},B_{t})`$ state; the controller
then incorporates the outcome and correct-length observation into
$`(D_{t+1},B_{t+1})`$ for subsequent rollouts. This composition
optimizes the mode action and response tokens jointly while avoiding
duplicate counting of the verifier reward. The 8,096-token rollout cap
provides generation headroom, while the 6,144-token controller scale
defines the normalized initialization of budget bands.

## Appendix E Rollout Clustering and Difficulty Assignment

This section specifies the full clustering path used by the longitudinal
realization. It replaces a global notion of difficulty with
policy-relative groups: rollouts with comparable task structure and
verifier semantics share a success history, and their relative success
determines the reward-routing tier. The online feature-bucket procedure
continuously adapts these groups as the policy evolves.

#### Observation and task envelope.

For rollout $`i`$, the reward manager constructs
$`o_{i}=(u_{i},a_{i},L_{i},x_{i},z_{i})`$ from a stable task identifier
$`u_{i}`$, verifier correctness $`a_{i}`$, attended response length
$`L_{i}`$, problem text $`x_{i}`$, and metadata $`z_{i}`$. The task role
defaults to solver. Explicit family and subtype metadata take
precedence. Otherwise, the runtime uses the rollout prefix to
distinguish proposer from solver and the verifier suffix to assign
reasoning semantics: input recovery to abduction, output prediction to
deduction, and function synthesis to induction. Prompt cues complete
these assignments; tasks outside the recognized semantics enter a
dedicated general family/subtype that preserves them for online
clustering.

#### Rollout representation.

The representation $`\phi_{i}`$ concatenates the feature groups in
Table [7](#A5.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Rollout representation. ‣ Appendix E Rollout Clustering and Difficulty Assignment ‣ MORE: Mode-Optimized Reasoning with Self-Adaptive Effort for Self-Evolving Agents").
Extracted structural and semantic fields provide the task
representation, while a per-UID window of the latest 200 verified
observations contributes mean success, success variance, and mean
response length. After each rollout is verified, its correctness and
length update this state for subsequent mode and budget decisions.

|  Group |  Implemented coordinates |
|----|----|
|  Code structure |  Python AST depth; branch, loop, nested-loop, call, and function counts; recursion flag; expression depth; and list/dict/set/tuple counts. Non-Python tasks are represented by the remaining structural and semantic coordinates. |
|  Prompt artifacts |  Code-block, test/assert, stub, I/O-example, bug, and constraint cues; word and nonempty-line counts; input, output, and pattern cues. |
|  Task semantics |  One-hot family and subtype; family-specific deduction-path, abduction-cause, or induction-pattern indicators; input-contract or output-state indicators; reference-text presence. |
|  Verifier shape |  One-hot input, output, error, and function targets together with the corresponding contract, trace, error-surface, and function-check burden indicators. |
|  General/behavior |  General semantic coordinates: text length, code presence, and constraint count. For every UID: rolling success mean, success variance, response-length mean, and a scalar structural score complementing the supplied coordinates. |

Table 7: Feature groups used by the rollout-clustering implementation.

#### Family-aware online assignment.

Clusters are partitioned first by reasoning family and verifier target.
A previously observed UID always reuses its stored cluster. For a new
UID, the tracker creates a new cluster in its family–verifier partition
while that partition contains fewer than $`K=9`$ clusters. After the
partition reaches $`K`$, the UID is assigned to the nearest centroid
under unnormalized squared Euclidean distance,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g_{i}=\arg\min_{c\in\mathcal{C}(f_{i},v_{i})}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\bigl(\mu_{c,j}-\phi_{i,j}\bigr)^{2},
``` |  |

with inactive coordinates represented by zero. Once the dedicated
general-family partition is full, its candidate set broadens to all
existing clusters. Family and verifier target are assignment keys; role
and subtype are retained as cluster metadata and enrich the cluster
representation. Cluster identifiers encode family, verifier target, and
a family-level creation counter. This construction keeps deduction,
abduction, induction, and input/output/error/function processes distinct
during normal assignment while preserving a shared assignment path for
general prompts.

#### Centroid and success updates.

Each new cluster starts with its first feature vector, size one, and
success estimate equal to the first binary outcome. Thereafter, its
centroid is the arithmetic running mean,
$`\mu_{c}^{(n)}=\mu_{c}^{(n-1)}+[\phi_{i}-\mu_{c}^{(n-1)}]/n`$, and its
policy-relative success is $`q_{c}^{(n)}=0.8q_{c}^{(n-1)}+0.2a_{i}`$.
The difficulty score exposed to telemetry is $`1-q_{c}`$. A cluster
becomes mature after eight assigned observations. Immature clusters
retain the default medium tier.

#### Quantile tiering and cold-start cases.

Tiers are refreshed after every recorded rollout over all mature
clusters. With one mature cluster, that cluster is easy. With two, the
higher-success cluster is easy and the lower is medium, except that an
exactly zero success EMA makes the lower cluster hard. With at least
three mature clusters, the implementation sorts mature-cluster success
values and uses the order statistic at index $`\lfloor(n-1)q\rfloor`$: a
cluster is hard when $`q_{c}\leq Q_{0.33}`$, easy when
$`q_{c}>Q_{0.67}`$, and medium otherwise. The strict upper and
non-strict lower comparisons are part of the implementation and
determine tie behavior.

#### Online state evolution.

Representations, centroids, EMA values, and tiers update as verified
rollouts arrive. Stable UID assignments preserve a consistent task
history, while the family–verifier partition prevents unrelated
reasoning processes from sharing statistics. The resulting lightweight
state can be updated throughout self-evolution and directly supplies the
difficulty context used by the next mode-token decision and its budget
band.

#### Mode-token parsing and telemetry.

The completion begins with one generated mode token. The parser maps
that token to direct, short_cot, long_cot, or code, attaches the
selected mode to the rollout record, and removes the control field
before task-specific answer extraction. Training uses the token’s policy
likelihood in the MATE-PPO trajectory; inference aggregates the same
field into mode ratios, per-mode accuracy, response length, latency, and
budget compliance.
````

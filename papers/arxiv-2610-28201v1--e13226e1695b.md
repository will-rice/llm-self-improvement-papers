###### Abstract

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) of agent harnesses has emerged as an
effective approach to enhancing the capabilities of large language model
(LLM) agents. By searching the harness space for a specific LLM on a
given dataset, RSI can discover a harness tailored to the capabilities
and behavioral characteristics of that model. However, such optimization
often creates a strong coupling between the learned harness and its
target LLM, limiting generalization across models and requiring
substantial computational resources to adapt to new LLMs. Moreover,
existing RSI methods typically optimize a harness using feedback from a
single model and evaluate its generalization to other models only after
training. This paradigm treats model differences primarily as a
criterion for evaluating generalization rather than as valuable evidence
for optimization. In this paper, we introduce a new perspective that
treats models as data and places the harness at the center of
optimization. Building on this perspective, we propose MaD-RSI, a
framework that directly optimizes a shared harness across multiple
heterogeneous LLMs by incorporating their diverse failure trajectories
into the training process. Rather than assessing model differences only
after optimization, MaD-RSI leverages these differences as training
evidence to guide the self-improvement of a shared harness. Furthermore,
we introduce a new evaluation framework with two complementary metrics
designed to assess the effectiveness of harness optimization across
models. Experiments across multiple datasets and LLMs demonstrate that
MaD-RSI improves generalization over existing approaches while reducing
training costs.

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2610.28201v1/overview.png)

Figure 1: Comparison between MaD-RSI and existing RSI approaches that
evolve agent harnesses using feedback from a single backbone model.

Large language model (LLM) agents have achieved strong performance on
complex tasks, including reasoning and code generation ([Yang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib11); [Yao et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12)). Their effectiveness depends not only on the
underlying LLM but also on the harness. Recent work on recursive
self-improvement (RSI) has shown that iteratively searching and refining
this harness can enhance agent capabilities without updating model
parameters ([Chan et al., 2026](#bib.bib8); [Duan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13); [Lin et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)). However, many RSI
methods optimize a harness using execution feedback from a single target
LLM ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). Such specialization can entangle
the learned harness with the capabilities and behavioral characteristics
of that model, limiting its portability to other LLMs ([Qu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)). Adapting the harness to a new model may therefore
require another costly optimization run, making it difficult to build an
ecosystem of reusable and continuously improving harnesses.

This limitation also shapes how RSI is evaluated. A common protocol
optimizes a harness with one LLM and assesses its transfer to other
models ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib3); [Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)).
In this paradigm, differences among models primarily serve to measure
generalization, rather than to guide improvement. Yet heterogeneous LLMs
can exhibit different outcomes and execution trajectories on the same
task under the same harness. A failure from one model, when contrasted
with a success from another, may reveal deficiencies that are difficult
to identify from either trajectory alone. These observations suggest a
different question: _Can behavioral differences among LLMs serve as
training evidence for learning a harness that generalizes across
models?_

We address this question through Models-as-Data, a perspective that
treats heterogeneous LLMs, observed under controlled inference
configurations ([DeepSeek-AI, 2026](#bib.bib26)) (such as sampling), as
sources of behavioral evidence while placing the shared harness at the
center of optimization ([Mehri et al., 2022](#bib.bib9)). Instead of
viewing models solely as separate optimization targets or subjects of
subsequent transfer evaluation, we use their successful and failed
executions as complementary signals for harness improvement. The goal is
to optimize a single harness that can be used with different LLMs
independently, not to ensemble multiple models at inference time.

Building on this perspective, we propose MaD-RSI, a framework for RSI of
a shared harness using evidence from multiple heterogeneous LLMs.
MaD-RSI first organizes responses to shared queries across models and
inference configurations into a structured tensor, preserving the
conditions under which each outcome is observed. It then selects
informative queries using both disagreement among models and coverage of
distinct response patterns. Finally, it jointly diagnoses aligned
successful and failed trajectories to inform subsequent harness
revisions. In this way, behavioral differences across models become
inputs to the optimization process rather than observations reserved for
evaluation.

To assess RSI methods designed for multiple models, we further introduce
an evaluation framework with two complementary measures of optimization
effectiveness: _success coverage_, which captures whether at least one
model succeeds on a task, and _joint success_, which captures whether
all evaluated models succeed on that task. These population measures
complement evaluations of transfer to previously unseen LLMs. We study
MaD-RSI on legal reasoning, code generation, medical reasoning, and
agent safety tasks across heterogeneous LLMs. The results show
improvements in performance across models and transfer in some settings,
with tradeoffs that vary by task. On the general tasks, optimizing one
shared harness also reduces cumulative training cost relative to running
separate optimization processes for each development model. More
broadly, this study invites a reconsideration of the role of LLMs in
RSI: beyond being targets of harness optimization, they can provide the
evidence that drives it. We summarize our contributions as follows:

- •
  Models-as-Data perspective. We formulate an RSI paradigm across models
  in which behavioral differences among heterogeneous LLMs become
  evidence for optimizing a shared harness, rather than serving only as
  a means of evaluating transfer after training.
- •
  MaD-RSI framework. We develop a shared harness optimization framework
  that combines structured response representations, evidence selection
  based on disagreement and behavioral coverage, and joint diagnosis of
  successful and failed trajectories.
- •
  Evaluation and empirical study. We introduce two complementary
  population measures for assessing harness optimization across models
  and evaluate generalization and training cost across diverse tasks and
  LLMs.

## 2 Preliminary Analysis

#### Research Objective.

The best frozen backbone model for supplying optimization feedback may
not be known in advance. Our goal is to improve a single shared harness
using evidence from multiple frozen backbone models while limiting total
development cost. Let $`H`$ be a harness,
$`\mathcal{M}=\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{M}\}`$ a model group, and
$`\mathcal{Q}`$ a pool with $`N`$ queries. A sampling configuration
$`s\in\mathcal{S}`$ specifies decoding controls, with
$`L=|\mathcal{S}|`$¹¹ 1 In this paper, we use temperature settings as a
representative case of inference configurations for our study..
Execution induces

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       (\tau,y,c)\sim P_{m}^{H}(\cdot\mid q,s),
       ```                                       |     | (1) |

where $`\tau`$ is a trajectory, $`y`$ an evaluated outcome, and $`c`$
its cost. Model-as-data treats these conditional behavioral
distributions as evidence for harness improvement.

#### Evaluation.

Let $`y_{irm}\in\{0,1\}`$ be the pass event for evaluation query $`i`$,
repetition $`r`$, and model $`m`$. With $`N_{e}`$ queries and $`R_{e}`$
repetitions, define

|     |                                               |                                                                   |     |     |
| --- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\text{Pass@}M`$                | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{e}R_{e}}\sum_{i,r}\max_{m}y_{irm},`$  |     | (2) |
|     | $`\displaystyle\text{Pass\textasciicircum}M`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{e}R_{e}}\sum_{i,r}\prod_{m}y_{irm}.`$ |     | (3) |

These measure oracle coverage and joint success across models. Both
average over all aligned query and trial positions rather than
maximizing over repetitions. Trial indices align records but do not
imply shared random seeds. Here $`M`$ counts models, unlike the sample
count in conventional $`\text{Pass@}k`$ evaluation ([Chen et al.,
2021](#bib.bib14)).

#### Cost.

Shared optimization need not match the best independent run to offer a
useful cost tradeoff. Let $`H_{j}`$ be a harness optimized with model
$`m_{j}`$, at training cost $`C_{j}`$. Let $`H_{\mathrm{mad}}`$ be the
shared harness, with cost $`C_{\mathrm{mad}}`$. Fix a higher-is-better
population score $`S`$, such as $`\text{Pass\textasciicircum}M`$, under
one evaluation protocol. Define $`S_{\mathrm{best}}=\max_{j}S(H_{j})`$
and $`C_{\Sigma}=\sum_{j}C_{j}`$. Under a utility $`S-\lambda C`$,
shared optimization is weakly preferred exactly when

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
S_{\mathrm{best}}-S(H_{\mathrm{mad}})\leq\lambda(C_{\Sigma}-C_{\mathrm{mad}}).
``` |  | (4) |

Here $`\lambda\geq 0`$ is a cost preference fixed before comparing
outcomes, in score units per token. The comparator pays for all
independent runs before selecting one harness. This decision criterion
allows a score deficit when token savings compensate under the chosen
preference.

## 3 RSI via Model-as-Data

MaD-RSI treats frozen models as sources of behavioral data for shared
harness improvement ([Zheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib15); [Weng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib16)). Shared queries enable comparisons under matched
execution conditions. For a fixed harness $`H`$ and binary outcomes, let
$`p_{qms}`$ denote the empirical success rate for query $`q`$, model
$`m`$, and sampling configuration $`s`$. We organize these rates into a
three-dimensional response tensor, with one matrix slice per sampling
configuration:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{gathered}\mathcal{P}=[p_{qms}]\in[0,1]^{N\times M\times L},\\
\mathbf{P}_{s}=\mathcal{P}_{:,:,s}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{11s}&\cdots&p_{1Ms}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p_{N1s}&\cdots&p_{NMs}\end{bmatrix}.\end{gathered}
``` |  | (5) |

Each column describes model behavior across shared queries. Each row
aligns responses to the same query under a fixed sampling configuration.
Task evaluators score the outcomes, and each cell links to its source
trajectories. Sampling configurations receive equal weights. Let
$`w_{m}\geq 0`$ denote the aggregation weight assigned to $`m`$, with
$`\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}=1`$. Inspired by learning for multiple target
models ([Tang and Huang, 2022](#bib.bib20)), we score each query by
disagreement within sampling slices:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
D_{q}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{s}\left[h(\bar{p}_{qs})-\sum_{m}w_{m}h(p_{qms})\right].
``` |  | (6) |

Here $`\bar{p}_{qs}=\sum_{m}w_{m}p_{qms}`$ and $`h`$ is binary entropy
([Fang et al., 2025](#bib.bib19)). The score captures differences in
empirical success rates across models under matched sampling conditions.
Equal rates across models at every setting yield zero disagreement, even
when individual executions vary. Computing disagreement before averaging
across configurations preserves contrasts that pooling observations
could hide. For binary tasks, we first reserve anchor queries, including
unanimous successes and failures when available. These unanimous
outcomes remain available for diagnosis despite their zero disagreement.
We fill the remaining query budget for diagnosis by repeatedly adding
the query with the largest combined gain in disagreement and
response-pattern coverage. The coverage term favors queries that
represent response patterns poorly covered by the selected set ([Agarwal
et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)). These patterns span models and sampling
configurations. For categorical outcomes, we use the corresponding
entropy contrast and a separate ranking rule. The tensor guides query
selection, while trajectories supply evidence for diagnosis. For each
selected query, MaD-RSI assembles this evidence within an input budget,
preserving outcome labels and source identities. Joint diagnosis
compares shared failures, model-specific failures, and successful
executions. Each diagnostic claim must cite source evidence identifiers.
Successful traces provide contrasts for assessing proposed edits.
Disagreement alone does not establish a failure cause or a useful fix.
An LLM then uses the joint diagnoses to propose revisions to the shared
harness.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Datasets.

We use LawBench ([Fei et al., 2024](#bib.bib22)), MBPP+ ([Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib23)), MedXpertQA ([Zuo et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)), and
Agent-SafetyBench ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib21)) (SafetyBench).

#### Models.

For RSI, we employ DeepSeek-V4-Flash (DeepSeek-V4), DeepSeek-V3.2,
Qwen3.8-Flash, and Qwen3.8-27B as the underlying target models. We
assign equal weights to the four models, with $`w_{m}=1/4`$. To assess
the generalization capability of the evolved harness, we further
evaluate it on Doubao-Seed-2.0-Mini (Doubao-2.0) and GLM-5.3-Flash.

#### Evaluation and Metrics.

During MaD-RSI evidence collection, temperature is the sampling
parameter varied. We use $`T=0.0`$ and $`T=1.0`$, with two repetitions
for each query–model–temperature combination. General-task experiments
use three rounds, with two candidate slots per round. We report
$`\text{Pass@}M`$ to measure coverage and
$`\text{Pass\textasciicircum}M`$ to measure joint success. Main
experiments use four models, while expanded evaluations include two
additional unseen models. For general tasks, correctness defines the
pass event. For safety tasks, we report attack safety (AS), utility
under attack (UA), and clean safety (CS). We also account for resource
consumption alongside performance comparisons, reporting token usage and
execution time.

#### Baselines.

We compare six configurations: the original harness, four
single-executor baselines, and our MaD-RSI. We adopt RRSI ([Xia et al.,
2026](#bib.bib4)) for general tasks and SHE ([Qu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5)) for safety tasks. Each baseline is optimized using a
single executor model and evaluated across all four models. We select
RRSI because it regularizes both edit proposals and candidate selection
to promote transfer to unseen benchmarks ([Xia et al.,
2026](#bib.bib4)). This comparison examines whether cross-model evidence
provides additional benefits within a framework already designed for
generalization. For generalization evaluation, we select the baseline
achieving the highest $`\text{Pass\textasciicircum}4`$ in the main
experiments, breaking ties using $`\text{Pass@}4`$. For safety tasks,
baseline selection is based on UA.

### 4.2 Experimental Results

In the main evaluation, MaD-RSI achieves $`\text{Pass@}4`$ of 73.00% and
$`\text{Pass\textasciicircum}4`$ of 48.00% on LawBench, outperforming
the strongest optimized baseline by 11.50 and 4.50 percentage points,
respectively (see
[Table 1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3 RSI via Model-as-Data ‣ MaD-RSI: Model-as-Data for Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses")).
Accuracy on individual models ranges from 58.50% to 65.00%, consistently
exceeding the corresponding baseline results. On MBPP+ and MedXpertQA,
improvements are more pronounced in joint success. While
$`\text{Pass@}4`$ matches the best baselines at 93.00% and 60.00%,
respectively, $`\text{Pass\textasciicircum}4`$ reaches 80.00% and
18.00%, yielding gains of 1.50 and 5.00 percentage points.

For SafetyBench, MaD-RSI achieves $`\text{Pass\textasciicircum}4`$ of
98.93%, 80.91%, and 86.25% on AS, UA, and CS, respectively, surpassing
the best baseline for each metric. Notably, joint UA improves by 7.27
percentage points, despite matching the best baseline in coverage at
94.55%. Across tasks, joint success improves more consistently than
success coverage. The advantage extends to unseen models. When evaluated
with two additional executors, MaD-RSI outperforms the preselected
baselines in $`\text{Pass\textasciicircum}6`$ across all six
dataset–metric combinations (see
[Table 2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3 RSI via Model-as-Data ‣ MaD-RSI: Model-as-Data for Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses")).

We further examine computational costs against the aggregate expenditure
of four independently optimized baselines (see
[Figure 2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Cost. ‣ 2 Preliminary Analysis ‣ MaD-RSI: Model-as-Data for Recursive Self-Improvement of Agent Harnesses")).
Baseline (MAX) and Baseline (AVG) represent the per-metric maximum and
mean across these baselines, respectively. Both are computed from the
same training and evaluation runs and therefore share identical costs.
Importantly, the reported maxima may originate from different harnesses
and do not necessarily correspond to a single configuration. Relative to
the combined training-token consumption of the four baselines, MaD-RSI
reduces usage by 18.3%, 55.9%, and 52.4% on LawBench, MBPP+, and
MedXpertQA, respectively, while incurring a 15.2% increase on the safety
task. The efficiency advantage therefore lies in jointly optimizing a
shared harness for multiple models, rather than optimizing for a single
model. Across all four datasets, MaD-RSI also requires fewer inference
tokens and time than the four baselines combined.

## 5 Related Work

Existing methods for RSI of agent harnesses largely follow a common
paradigm: optimizing a harness using a single execution model and
subsequently evaluating its transfer across models ([Hu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib1); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib2); [Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib3); [Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib4)). However, improvements
achieved during optimization do not necessarily transfer to other
models. Previous empirical findings suggest that harness modifications
tailored to the failure modes of a source model may provide limited
benefits to other models or even degrade their performance ([Qu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib5); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib6); [Rakotonirina et al.,
2023](#bib.bib7)). This observation motivates us to incorporate
behavioral differences across models directly into the training
evidence. Unlike existing approaches, MaD-RSI compares the behaviors of
different frozen execution models during evidence construction.
Specifically, it aligns responses to the same query across models and
sampling configurations, leverages these aligned observations for query
selection and joint diagnosis, and uses the resulting evidence to guide
the optimization of a shared harness.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce Models-as-Data, a new perspective that
treats heterogeneous LLMs as behavioral data sources for shared harness
optimization. Building on this perspective, we propose MaD-RSI, which
leverages behavioral differences across models to guide RSI. We further
introduce two complementary metrics to evaluate optimization
effectiveness across models. Experiments across diverse tasks and LLMs
demonstrate improved generalization in several settings and reduced
cumulative training costs compared with independent optimization on
general tasks. We hope this work encourages a broader shift in RSI from
optimizing harnesses for individual models toward learning reusable
harnesses from diverse model behaviors.

## 7 Limitations

This work provides preliminary evidence for the feasibility and
potential value of the Models-as-Data perspective, while more
comprehensive experimental validation is left for future work. This work
is still in progress.

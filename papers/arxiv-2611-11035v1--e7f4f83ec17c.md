# Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides Whether Pushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting

Ook Lee Affiliation: Department of Information System, Hanyang
University Affiliation: Seoul, Korea
Email: [ooklee@hanyang.ac.kr](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Language models often abandon a correct answer when a user pushes back
without giving a reason, and this behavior is usually studied as
sycophancy to be suppressed. The same pushback, however, also leads
models to revisit and sometimes repair a wrong answer. We measure both
effects on multiple-choice questions from MMLU and ARC-Challenge with
four open models from two families (Qwen3 1.7B, 4B and 8B, and OLMo-2
7B), reading the final answer exactly from option logits after a
free-form reply. The reply budget changes the verdict. When Qwen3-4B may
answer the challenge in at most 96 tokens, pushback leaves accuracy
unchanged or slightly lower; with 1,024 tokens, it corrects 43% of its
wrong answers and pushback raises accuracy by 5.7 points. Whether
pushback helps overall follows a simple break-even rule in the cave and
correction rates. Inside the model, caving can be partly predicted
before the challenge: hidden states add about 0.04–0.06 AUROC over
turn-1 confidence in three of our four probing runs. A probe trained
only on self-correction cases predicts caving beyond confidence when
replies are truncated, but not when the model can finish its reasoning.
Activation steering never reduced caving selectively: where it worked,
it suppressed self-correction as well. We conclude that sycophancy
evaluations should allow full replies and report self-correction
alongside caving.

## 1 Introduction

Users question the answers of language models all the time, often with
nothing more than “Are you sure?”. A model that drops a correct answer
under such pressure is unreliable; a model that never reconsiders cannot
recover from its own mistakes. Most work on sycophancy focuses on the
first failure and measures how often a correct answer is abandoned after
a challenge \[[8](#bib.bib1), [10](#bib.bib2)\]. The second effect, the
repair of wrong answers under the same pressure, is rarely reported next
to it, and the reply the model may give before committing to a final
answer is often short or absent.

We study both effects together. Our protocol asks a four-option
question, records the model’s answer, issues an uninformative challenge,
lets the model reply freely, and then reads the final answer from the
option logits. We report the _cave rate_ (right $`\to`$ wrong), the
_correction rate_ (wrong $`\to`$ right), and the net change in accuracy.
This makes it possible to ask whether an intervention reduces sycophancy
or merely makes the model stubborn.

Our main findings are:

1.  1.  The reply budget reverses the verdict. With 96-token replies,
        pushback leaves accuracy unchanged or lower in all three Qwen
        settings ($`-2.6`$ to $`-0.1`$ points, significant only for
        Qwen3-8B); with 1,024 tokens it raises accuracy significantly in all
        three ($`+3.8`$ to $`+5.7`$ points). Across settings the correction
        rate rises by 6–31 points with full replies while the cave rate
        changes far less, so short budgets mainly hide self-correction
        (Section [4](#S4 "4 Reply Length Changes the Verdict ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting")).
2.  2.  A break-even rule predicts the net effect. Pushback helps when
        turn-1 accuracy is below $`c/(c+k)`$, where $`c`$ is the correction
        rate and $`k`$ the cave rate. The rule accounts for the one setting
        with a negative estimate
        (Section [5](#S5 "5 A Break-Even Rule ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting")).
3.  3.  Caving is partly predictable before the challenge. A linear probe on
        hidden states, combined with turn-1 confidence, improves AUROC over
        confidence by 0.04–0.06 in three of four runs across both model
        families
        (Section [6](#S6 "6 Caving Is Partly Predictable Before the Challenge ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting")).
4.  4.  Caving and correcting share an internal signal only when reasoning
        is cut short. A probe trained only on self-correction cases predicts
        caving beyond confidence with truncated replies, in all three models
        tested, and adds nothing with full replies
        (Section [7](#S7 "7 When Do Caving and Correcting Share a Signal? ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting")).
5.  5.  Steering does not reduce sycophancy selectively. Where activation
        steering had any effect, it moved caving and correction together;
        gating it on estimated correctness recovered little of what an
        oracle gate could
        (Section [8](#S8 "8 Steering and Its Limits ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting")).

## 2 Related Work

### Sycophancy under challenge.

[Laban et al. \[8\]](#bib.bib1) show that models frequently flip a
correct answer when challenged and that the wording of the challenge
matters. [Sharma et al. \[10\]](#bib.bib2) document sycophancy across
assistants and relate it to preference training, and [Wei et al.
\[14\]](#bib.bib3) reduce it with synthetic fine-tuning data. These
studies mostly count abandoned correct answers. We add the correction
rate and show that the measured trade-off depends on how much the model
is allowed to say before answering.

### Mechanisms and steering.

Several studies locate sycophancy in model internals, report that the
correct answer often survives in intermediate representations, and
separate distinct sycophantic behaviors linearly \[[13](#bib.bib11),
[12](#bib.bib12), [4](#bib.bib13)\]. Contrastive activation addition
\[[9](#bib.bib4)\] and subspace-scaling methods \[[2](#bib.bib14)\]
steer sycophancy directly. A recent dual-stance analysis reports that a
sycophancy direction also suppresses agreement with correct users
\[[1](#bib.bib15)\]. We extend this observation to self-correction and
show that its strength depends on the reply budget.

### Self-knowledge and self-correction.

Models are partly calibrated about their own correctness
\[[7](#bib.bib6)\], but often fail to correct themselves without
external feedback \[[6](#bib.bib5)\]. Our results suggest that an
uninformative challenge does act as such feedback when the model is
allowed to reason, and that the value of gated interventions is bounded
by self-knowledge.

## 3 Setup

### Protocol.

In turn 1 the model receives a system prompt asking for the letter of
the correct option, followed by the question and four options. The
answer is read from the next-token distribution over A–D after the
assistant prefix “The answer is (”. In turn 2 the conversation contains
this answer and the user challenge “I don’t think that’s right. Are you
sure?”. The model then writes a free reply of up to $`T`$ tokens under
greedy decoding; we append “Final answer: (” and read the letter again.
Reading both answers from logits avoids the grading errors of extracting
letters from free text, which in our pilots frequently picked up options
merely mentioned during reasoning. We also record whether the reply
opens by conceding to the user (“You’re right”, “I apologize”); a reply
that concedes but keeps a correct answer counts as _verbal-only_
agreement.

### Models and data.

We use Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B \[[15](#bib.bib9)\] with
thinking mode disabled, and OLMo-2-1124-7B-Instruct
\[[11](#bib.bib10)\], all in 4-bit NF4 quantization on a single 8 GB
laptop GPU. Questions come from the MMLU test set \[[5](#bib.bib7)\] (a
fixed random order; the first 500–2,000 questions) and from the
four-option subset of the ARC-Challenge test set \[[3](#bib.bib8)\]
(1,165 questions).

### Reply budget.

We compare $`T\in\{96,256,1024\}`$ tokens. At 1,024 tokens 6% of
Qwen3-4B replies are truncated (median reply length 396 tokens, 90th
percentile 857) and 0.4% of OLMo-2 7B replies (median 162); at 256
tokens 83% of Qwen3-4B replies are truncated. Even at 1,024 tokens,
truncated Qwen3-4B replies caved on 38.7% of right answers, against
12.4% for complete ones. Short and long runs of the same model share the
question order and the turn-1 answers, and each comparison uses the same
questions.

### Hidden states.

We store the residual stream at the last token before the turn-1 answer
($`t_{1}`$) and at the last token after the challenge, before any reply
($`t_{2}`$), for every layer.

summarize the outcome rates. With Qwen3-4B on MMLU, a 96-token reply

questions, the correction rate rises to 43.2% and accuracy rises by 5.7

Qwen settings; with short replies only the loss for Qwen3-8B is.

In the ARC setting the cave rate is identical at both budgets (3.6%),
while the correction rate rises from 21.3% to 49.6%. Across the four
paired settings, the correction rate rises by 6 to 31 points with full
replies, while the cave rate changes by $`-0.4`$ to $`+5.2`$ points.
Short budgets therefore mainly hide self-correction rather than
manufacture sycophancy. Within the 256-token run of Qwen3-4B, complete
replies corrected 46.7% of wrong answers against 19.3% for truncated
ones. OLMo-2 7B, whose short run used 256 tokens with 31% of replies
truncated, already gains from pushback at that budget ($`+1.2`$ points)
and gains more with full replies ($`+2.2`$ points; correction 27.3%
$`\to`$ 33.7%, cave 16.9% $`\to`$ 19.7%).

Turn-1 accuracy differs by at most 0.3 points between the short and long
run of a model, because batched decoding with different batch sizes
changes a few near-tied logits.

The budget also changes which models look sycophantic. When the reply is
prefilled with “The answer is (”, so that the model answers without any
reasoning, OLMo-2 7B never changes its answer (0% cave, 0% correction),
although it caves on 19.7% of right answers with full replies and opens
80% of its replies with a concession.

## 5 A Break-Even Rule

With turn-1 accuracy $`a`$, cave rate $`k`$ and correction rate $`c`$,
the change in accuracy after pushback is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \Delta=(1-a)\,c-a\,k,
       ```                    |     | (1) |

so pushback helps exactly when $`a<a^{\ast}=c/(c+k)`$. A model that
caves easily has a low break-even accuracy, and a task the model already
does well on leaves more right answers at risk. OLMo-2 7B on
ARC-Challenge is the only setting with a negative estimate under full
replies ($`-1.2`$ points, 95% interval $`[-4.8,2.4]`$): its break-even
accuracy is 74.2%, below its turn-1 accuracy of 76.6%. For Qwen3-4B on
the same data, the break-even accuracy is 93.2% against a turn-1
accuracy of 85.9%
(Figure [2](#S5.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 5 A Break-Even Rule ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting")).

Figure 2: Turn-1 accuracy against break-even accuracy for full replies.
Points above the diagonal lose accuracy after pushback.

## 6 Caving Is Partly Predictable Before the Challenge

Among questions answered correctly in turn 1, we predict whether the
model will cave. Turn-1 confidence (the probability of the chosen
letter) is a strong baseline. We train a logistic-regression probe on
$`t_{1}`$ hidden states, choose the layer inside each training fold, and
combine the probe with confidence by stacking; intervals come from a
paired bootstrap.

For Qwen3-4B, the combination improves AUROC over confidence with both
reply budgets: from 0.747 to 0.804 with 96-token replies ($`+0.057`$,
95% CI $`[0.026,0.091]`$) and from 0.797 to 0.841 with 1,024-token
replies ($`+0.044`$, $`[0.018,0.072]`$). For OLMo-2 7B with 256-token
replies and 2,000 questions, it improves AUROC from 0.754 to 0.806
($`+0.052`$, $`[0.027,0.076]`$). In the smallest run, OLMo-2 7B with
1,024-token replies (295 right answers, 58 caves), we detect no gain
($`-0.013`$, $`[-0.045,0.018]`$); with 4,096-dimensional features this
run may simply be too small. The probe alone never significantly beats
confidence; the information it adds is complementary. Reporting the best
layer without nested selection inflated the probe’s score by about 0.02
in our first analysis, a bias worth guarding against in probing studies.

Even with full replies, 11.3% of the right answers that Qwen3-4B gave
with at least 90% confidence were abandoned after the challenge (OLMo-2
7B: 12.8%), so not all caving is explained by uncertainty.

## 7 When Do Caving and Correcting Share a Signal?

If caving and self-correction were driven by one tendency to change the
answer, what separates keeping from correcting a wrong answer should
also separate keeping from abandoning a right one. We test this
directly. A probe is trained only on questions answered wrongly in turn
1 (held vs. corrected) and scored on questions answered correctly (held
vs. caved); chance is 0.5. We also ask whether its score adds to turn-1
confidence within the right-answer group, using cross-validated stacking
and paired bootstrap intervals.

With truncated replies, transfer works in all three models (AUROC
0.57–0.69 across layers) and adds to confidence: up to $`+0.07`$ for
Qwen3-4B, $`+0.08`$ for Qwen3-8B and $`+0.03`$ for OLMo-2 7B, with
intervals excluding zero
(Table [2](#S7.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 7 When Do Caving and Correcting Share a Signal? ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting"),
Figure [3](#S7.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 7 When Do Caving and Correcting Share a Signal? ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting")).
With 1,024-token replies, the gain over confidence is never positive
beyond noise: it is indistinguishable from zero in Qwen3-4B and OLMo-2
7B at every layer, and in Qwen3-8B it is zero in early layers and
slightly negative in late layers ($`-0.03`$), as expected when an
uninformative score is added to a fitted model. Because the hidden
states at $`t_{2}`$ do not depend on the reply budget, the difference
comes entirely from what the model does after reasoning.

We first measured this coupling with cosine similarities between
mean-difference directions and found a value of 0.785. Hidden-state
dimensions are, however, strongly correlated, and the same cosine
computed with shuffled labels reached 0.6–0.9 even after z-scoring; the
sign of the raw cosine even flipped between two runs that differ only in
their labels. We therefore withdrew that measurement and rely on the
transfer test, whose chance level does not depend on the geometry of the
representation.

## 8 Steering and Its Limits

We add $`\alpha\,d`$ to one layer’s residual stream from the challenge
onward, where $`d`$ is the difference between mean $`t_{2}`$ states of
held and caved right answers. A same-norm random direction serves as
control. Because steering with free replies is expensive, we screened
with prefilled replies, which corresponds to the short-reply regime.

### Qwen3-4B.

At layer 18, $`\alpha=+8`$ removes caving entirely (6.1% $`\to`$ 0.0%)
but also nearly all correction (13.8% $`\to`$ 1.5%), leaving accuracy
unchanged. Removing the component shared with the wrong-answer
direction, or adding the change-when-wrong direction, traces the same
caving–correction frontier
(Figure [4](#S8.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Gated steering. ‣ 8 Steering and Its Limits ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting")).
Random directions have no systematic effect, and layers 24 and 28 have
none at the strengths tested.

### Qwen3-8B and OLMo-2 7B.

In Qwen3-8B the learned direction is weak (at most 4.8% $`\to`$ 3.2%
caving), while same-norm random directions move caving and correction
together in a fixed ratio of about 0.6. For OLMo-2 7B, prefilled replies
show no flips at all, so we steered with free 256-token replies: the
learned direction was indistinguishable from the random control.

### Gated steering.

Applying the “keep” direction only where a gate predicts that the turn-1
answer is right, and optionally the opposite direction elsewhere, gains
at most 0.4 points of accuracy for Qwen3-4B and 1.0 for Qwen3-8B. An
oracle gate that knows the turn-1 correctness gains 6.9 and 1.5 points
(Figure [5](#S8.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Gated steering. ‣ 8 Steering and Its Limits ‣ Room to Reconsider: Reply Length Decides WhetherPushback Makes Language Models Sycophantic or Self-Correcting")).
The gates reach AUROC 0.81 for predicting turn-1 correctness, which is
not enough to target the direction where it helps.

## 9 Discussion and Limitations

Our results suggest that the apparent tension between resisting pushback
and accepting correction is partly an artifact of evaluating models that
cannot finish their reasoning. In that regime one tendency governs both
behaviors, and suppressing sycophancy suppresses self-correction. With
room to reason, the two separate in behavior and in representation, and
self-correction becomes the larger effect for most settings we studied.

Several limitations apply. We use models up to 8B parameters in 4-bit
quantization, a single uninformative challenge, and multiple-choice
questions. Informative or persona-based pushback, open-ended answers and
larger models may behave differently. Steering was screened with
prefilled replies for cost reasons, and the free-reply steering run for
OLMo-2 7B used 300 questions. The 1,024-token runs of OLMo-2 7B use 500
questions, which widens their intervals. A system prompt instructing the
model not to change its answer without a reason had no measurable effect
on Qwen3-4B (cave 7.9% $`\to`$ 7.3%, correction 13.4% $`\to`$ 14.2%,
96-token replies).

## 10 Conclusion

When a model cannot finish reasoning, sycophancy and self-correction
behave like two outcomes of one tendency to change the answer, and
suppressing one suppresses the other. Given room to reason, the two
separate, and self-correction becomes the larger effect. Sycophancy
benchmarks should allow full replies, report correction rates next to
cave rates, and check the break-even condition before calling pushback
harmful.

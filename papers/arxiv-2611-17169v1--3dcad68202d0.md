# Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errors in Small-Model Semantic Parsing

Umar Ahmed    Maxim Dunaev    Mohammad Nour Al Awad    Sergey Ivanov   
Ivan Khodnenko Affiliation: ITMO University
Affiliation: {uakhmed,mvdunaev,svivanov}@itmo.ru,
MohammadNourAlAwad@itmo.ru, ivan.khodnenko@itmo.ru

###### Abstract

A small fine-tuned parser that emits a well-formed but wrong tree fails
_silently_: nothing downstream can tell. We ask whether the output
format, a free design choice, controls how often this happens. On the
PIZZA ordering benchmark we fine-tune two sub-billion models
(Qwen3.5-0.8B and LFM2.5-350M) with LoRA on six lossless encodings of
the same target tree, from 44.3 down to 10.9 tokens per order, including
two matched-length pairs that vary surface form at fixed length. Across
300 runs (5 training pool sizes, 5 seeds) with a hierarchical bootstrap
and Holm correction fixed before the results were read, shortening alone
never helps, but writing the target in ordinary words does. The word
encoding is the most accurate format on both models (87.5% vs. 85.8%
exact match for the native s-expression on Qwen) while emitting
4.1$`\times`$ fewer tokens, and it cuts the silent error rate by
2.3$`\times`$ on Qwen and 1.8$`\times`$ on LFM2, separating at every
pool size. Error analysis locates much of the gain in negation. All runs
also learn to refuse off-menu input (held out: 95.9–99.0% handled) and
to apply in-utterance self-corrections. Strict refusal has a measured
price: 2.1–10.1% of real, mostly polite, orders are refused.

## 1 Introduction

Small language models are attractive for narrow parsing tasks: they
fine-tune in minutes, run on a laptop or a tablet, and cost almost
nothing per call. Their weakness is reliability ([Ji et al.,
2023](#bib.bib14)), and in structured prediction an error comes in two
kinds. An output that does not parse, or that the model marks as a
refusal, is _detectable_: the caller rejects it and asks again. An
output that parses into a plausible tree that is not what the customer
said is _silent_: the order goes through, wrong. This paper treats the
silent error rate $`S`$ as the quantity a deployment should minimise,
and reports exact match $`A`$ beside it for comparison with prior work
(Section [5](#S5.SS0.SSS0.Px3 "Metrics. ‣ 5 Setup and Statistics ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).

The work began with a deployed café ordering parser whose compact output
format was chosen for speed; its small hand-written test set could not
tell formats apart, so we moved to a public benchmark with a
human-written test set. Our question is whether the target format is
itself a lever on silent errors. The same tree can be written as an
s-expression, as JSON, as key=value pairs, as a terse symbolic language
or as plain words, and the choice changes how many tokens the model
emits and how much of its pretraining it can reuse. The intuitive
hypothesis, that fewer output tokens leave less room for error, turns
out to be wrong in the form we first stated it. Shortening helps only
when the surface stays in natural words.

#### Contributions.

(1) A ladder of six lossless encodings of the PIZZA target
tree ([Arkoudas et al., 2022](#bib.bib3)), with two matched-length pairs
that separate length from surface form
(Section [4](#S4 "4 Six Encodings of One Tree ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).
(2) Evidence across two model families, five pool sizes and five seeds
that length alone is not the mechanism: the word encoding beats its
symbolic twin of equal length in 10 of 10 model$`\times`$size cells,
separating in 9 after Holm correction, and on LFM2 it wins while costing
_more_ tokens
(Section [6.2](#S6.SS2 "6.2 Surface form, not length ‣ 6 Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).
(3) The silent error rate as the deployment objective, inside a four-way
split of every test set. The word encoding has the lowest $`S`$ on both
models, and an error analysis shows where the difference comes from
(Section [6.3](#S6.SS3 "6.3 Silent errors and where they come from ‣ 6 Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).
(4) A guardrail trained into every run, refusing off-menu input and
applying spoken self-corrections, with its benefit and its cost measured
on held-out data
(Section [6.4](#S6.SS4 "6.4 The guardrail: refusing and listening ‣ 6 Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).
Code, analysis scripts and the trained models are released at
[https://github.com/UmarGit/coffee-llm](https://github.com/UmarGit/coffee-llm).

## 2 Related Work

#### Target representation.

That the meaning representation affects a parser is long established:
[Dong and Lapata (2016)](#bib.bib6) decode logical forms as trees rather
than sequences, and seq2seq parsers for task-oriented dialogue generate
the target directly ([Rongali et al., 2020](#bib.bib18)). Closest to us,
[Shin et al. (2021)](#bib.bib22) show that a frozen language model
prompted to produce a canonical natural-language form, mapped to the
representation by a grammar, beats prompting for the representation
itself. We find the same phenomenon under fine-tuning, where the model
is free to learn any notation, and we separate naturalness from length
with matched pairs and two vocabularies.

#### Format restrictions and constrained decoding.

[Tam et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24) find that forcing an LLM into JSON costs
reasoning accuracy. Constrained decoding makes invalid output impossible
([Scholak et al., 2021](#bib.bib20); [Geng et al., 2023](#bib.bib9);
[Willard and Louf, 2023](#bib.bib25)); it can remove our unparseable
outputs, but it cannot make a well-formed tree correct, so the silent
rate is the part of the problem it does not reach.

#### Abstention and self-correction.

Knowing when not to answer has been studied for reading comprehension
([Rajpurkar et al., 2018](#bib.bib17)) and under domain shift ([Kamath
et al., 2020](#bib.bib15)). Spoken self-repair is a classic topic in
disfluency research ([Shriberg, 1994](#bib.bib23)), and Disfl-QA shows
that disfluent input breaks QA models ([Gupta et al.,
2021](#bib.bib10)). Task-oriented parsing datasets such as TOP, TOPv2
and MTOP ([Gupta et al., 2018](#bib.bib11); [Chen et al.,
2020](#bib.bib5); [Li et al., 2021](#bib.bib16)), and PIZZA itself,
contain neither off-domain refusals nor self-corrections; we add both as
synthetic training data and evaluate them on held-out vocabulary and cue
phrases.

#### Tokenization and evaluation.

Subword segmentation ([Sennrich et al., 2016](#bib.bib21)) sets what a
string costs, and vocabulary fit moves accuracy ([Rust et al.,
2021](#bib.bib19)). We follow recommendations to report seed variance
and significance ([Dror et al., 2018](#bib.bib7); [Bouthillier et al.,
2021](#bib.bib4)), using a bootstrap ([Efron and Tibshirani,
1993](#bib.bib8)) with Holm’s correction ([Holm, 1979](#bib.bib12)).

#### Deployment.

[Ahmed et al. (2026)](#bib.bib1) run the words model studied here inside
a web browser and check that the deployed model reproduces its server
evaluation; that work is about the web system and reports no comparison
between formats.

## 3 Task and Data

PIZZA ([Arkoudas et al., 2022](#bib.bib3)) maps spoken pizza and drink
orders to trees with an unordered exact-match criterion. Its training
pool of 2,456,446 utterances is generated from a grammar; the
348-utterance development set and the 1,357-utterance test set are
written by people, which makes the test set the strongest evidence in
this paper. The pool contains 3 rows identical to a development or test
utterance. We drop them from training, and verify on every training
subset we draw, at every size and seed, that no evaluation utterance
remains, exactly or after normalisation. The splits are used as
published.

#### Refusals.

PIZZA has no notion of “not an order”: a model trained on it turns
_“hehe hehe”_ into a pizza. We add generated negatives making up 30% of
every training set: chatter, off-menu requests, gibberish, long text,
and real orders with an off-menu clause or an unknown word inserted. The
rule is strict: anything that is not purely an order is refused whole.
Each format has one short refusal string that cannot parse as an order
in that format (? for the three word-like formats, (REJECT ), (R) and
{"reject":true} for the others), so all formats train on identical
inputs. Vocabulary is split: the 614 evaluation negatives use words
never seen in training.

#### Self-corrections.

PIZZA has no self-corrections either, so _“a pizza with cheese, wait no
cheese, only tomato sauce”_ comes out with both cheese and no cheese. We
take real training orders, edit their tree, and append the spoken
correction in nine kinds (swap, retract, only, size, number, drink,
cancel, restart, and decoys in which a cue word changes nothing), adding
10% of the real orders. A quarter of the cue phrases are held out: the
600 evaluation corrections are test orders with held-out cues. A fixed
set of 40 hand-written probes includes the cheese example above, which a
user reported against an earlier model. Dev and test remain pure PIZZA,
so $`A`$ and $`S`$ keep their meaning.

| Format    | Tok. | Target                                   |
| --------- | ---- | ---------------------------------------- |
| exr       | 38   | (ORDER (PIZZAORDER (NUMBER 1 ) (COM...   |
| json      | 33   | {"orders":\[{"type":"p","attrs":\[{"n... |
| exr-short | 25   | (O (P (N 1) (C (Q EXTRA) (T CHEESE)...   |
| tagged    | 13   | pizza n=1 extra=cheese topp=pepperoni    |
| dsl       | 8    | p 1 +cheese pepperoni                    |

Table 1: One test order, _“i want a pie with pepperoni and extra
cheese”_, in the six encodings (long targets truncated). Tokens are
Qwen’s.

Comparing unrelated formats confounds length with form, so we built six
encodings of the _identical_ tree that decode back to it on every dev
and test utterance and on a 50,000-row training sample
(Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Six Encodings of One Tree ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).
exr is the dataset’s s-expression. json is a conventional encoding of
about the same length: the matched-length control for exr. exr-short
renames exr’s constructors to single letters. tagged is flat key=value
pairs. dsl is a terse grammar with sigils (+ for an extra quantity, !
for negation), and words has the same structure as dsl with the markers
written as ordinary words; it is dsl’s matched-length control. exr,
exr-short, tagged and dsl form a length ladder.

#### Characters are not tokens.

Over the 1,357 gold test trees, words is the longer of the matched pair
in characters (43.1 vs. 34.9) but costs fewer Qwen tokens (10.3 vs.
10.8) and about the same number of LFM2 tokens (11.9 vs. 11.7; Appendix
Table [7](#A1.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Appendix A Additional Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).
Figure [1](#S4.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Characters are not tokens. ‣ 4 Six Encodings of One Tree ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")
shows why: +cheese splits into three subwords where extra cheese stays
two. Symbols save characters, not tokens. LFM2’s vocabulary is smaller
and inflates the punctuation-heavy formats most (json 45.6$`\to`$64.7
tokens, words 10.3$`\to`$11.9).

## 5 Setup and Statistics

#### Models and training.

Qwen3.5-0.8B ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib26)) and LFM2.5-350M ([Amini
et al., 2025](#bib.bib2)), a largely convolutional model with a
vocabulary about four times smaller. LoRA ([Hu et al.,
2022](#bib.bib13)) with rank 16 and $`\alpha{=}16`$ on all linear
projections, batch 16, cosine schedule, loss on the completion only.
Rank is held fixed across formats because the comparison is between
formats, not adapters; [Hu et al. (2022)](#bib.bib13) found far lower
ranks sufficient on their tasks. Training runs to at most 4,000 steps
(64,000 examples) with early stopping every 100 steps (patience 10) on
the mean of three dev scores: exact match, refusal of dev negatives, and
exact match on dev corrections. The learning rate is chosen per model
and format from $`\{10^{-4},2{\cdot}10^{-4},5{\cdot}10^{-4}\}`$ by dev
exact match at a pool of 100k over 3 seeds, then frozen (Appendix
Table [8](#A1.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Appendix A Additional Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).
The token cap per format is 1.25 times its longest target, so no correct
output can be truncated.

#### Grid.

Six formats $`\times`$ pool sizes {1k, 10k, 100k, 1M, 2,456,443}
$`\times`$ 5 seeds $`\times`$ two models, 300 runs, every one with the
refusal and correction data. A seed fixes the training subset, the
negatives and the corrections, so formats are paired by seed. Since no
run sees more than 64,000 examples, the larger sizes differ in the
diversity of the pool a run samples from, not in examples seen; we call
the axis _pool size_.

#### Metrics.

Every test order ends in exactly one of four outcomes:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\underbrace{A}_{\text{correct}}+\underbrace{S}_{\text{silent}}+\underbrace{D_{\text{parse}}}_{\text{unparseable}}+\underbrace{D_{\text{refuse}}}_{\text{refused}}=1.
``` |  |

$`A`$ is exact match under the dataset’s own matcher. $`S`$ is output
that parses into a tree that does not match: the caller cannot detect
it. $`D_{\text{parse}}`$ does not parse, and $`D_{\text{refuse}}`$ is a
refusal of a real order; both are visible to the caller. We read them in
this order: $`S`$ is the deployment objective, because those errors
reach the customer; $`A`$ is reported beside it for comparison with
prior work; and $`D_{\text{refuse}}`$ is the price of the guardrail.
Every table shows $`A`$ and $`S`$ together.

#### Statistics.

Fixed before any result was read. A cell is the mean of 5 seeds.
Intervals and $`p`$-values come from a hierarchical bootstrap (10,000
resamples) over seeds (paired by seed index across formats) and test
utterances (paired across formats). A comparison *separates* when its
Holm-adjusted $`p<0.05`$, the family being the five pool sizes of one
comparison on one model. Retraining one cell twice moved test exact
match by 1.5 points on Qwen and 4.9 on LFM2, against a seed SD in that
cell of 1.9 and 3.0; we therefore use the seed spread and the bootstrap,
not a single rerun, as the measure of noise.

### 6.1 Shorter is not better

Regressing exact match on output tokens across the four ladder formats
gives a positive slope (removing tokens lowers accuracy) in 10 of 10
model$`\times`$size cells, never more than 1.1 points per ten tokens,
and it separates in only 1. The ladder is not monotone
(Figure [2](#S6.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 6 Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")):
exr-short, which only renames exr’s constructors, loses 10.6 points on
Qwen, separating at every size, and 3.2 on LFM2; tagged, half as long as
exr, matches it on Qwen. Length does not predict accuracy in either
direction.

### 6.3 Silent errors and where they come from

Exact match and silent rate rank formats differently
(Figure [3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.3 Silent errors and where they come from ‣ 6 Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).
exr and json almost always parse, so nearly everything they get wrong

last rows). words also invents fewer entities that the customer never
said: 5.5$`\times`$ fewer than exr on Qwen and 2.4$`\times`$ on LFM2.

#### Refusal works for every format.

Held-out negatives, built from vocabulary never seen in training, are
refused 95.9–99.0% of the time on Qwen and 97.2–98.3% on LFM2

Chatter, gibberish and long text are refused essentially always; the
hardest kind is a real order with one unknown word, refused 86–96% of
the time on Qwen (Appendix
Table [9](#A1.T9 "Table 9 ‣ Appendix A Additional Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing")).
Format barely matters here.

#### The price is refusing real orders.

2.1–5.4% of real test orders are refused on Qwen and 6.3–10.1% on LFM2.
Reading them, they are genuine orders in human phrasing the synthetic
grammar never produces: greetings, thanks, and stories (*“i’m having a
bunch of people over tonight so i need five pizzas…”*). Almost every
refused order contains a word the training grammar never uses (96–100%
on Qwen, 97–100% on LFM2), against 81% of the orders answered. An order
with a greeting, *please* or *thanks* (289 of the test orders) is
refused 6.1% of the time by Qwen words against 2.6% for other orders.
The strict rule meets politeness the training grammar does not contain.

#### Corrections are learned, and best by words.

On test orders with held-out cue phrases, exact match is 62.9–75.3% on
Qwen and 49.2–62.6% on LFM2, and the correction itself is applied in
80–88% and 72–78% of cases. words is the best format on both models,
separating from every other format on LFM2. The reported cheese failure
is fixed in 60 of 60 full-pool runs. Training cues score 5–9 points
above held-out cues on Qwen, so part of what is learned is the phrasing
of the cue.

words is the most accurate format at every pool size on both models

### 6.6 Latency

Median latency at batch size one is linear in output tokens (Qwen:
29 ms + 26.5 ms per token, $`R^{2}{=}1.00{}`$; Appendix
Figure [6](#A1.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Appendix A Additional Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing"))
with throughput flat across formats, so words answers 3.7$`\times`$
faster than exr on Qwen and 4.4$`\times`$ on LFM2. The process’s GPU
memory moves only between 1,704 and 1,806 MiB on Qwen: format buys
latency and accuracy, not memory.

## 7 Discussion

#### Which metric, when.

If a parser’s output is acted on without review, $`S`$ is the number
that matters: a format with higher $`A`$ but higher $`S`$ ships more
wrong orders. $`A`$ is the right number to compare with prior work, and
$`D_{\text{parse}}+D_{\text{refuse}}`$ is what a retry or a human
fallback has to absorb. On PIZZA the three point the same way for words,
which need not hold elsewhere; reporting all four outcomes makes the
trade visible.

#### What transfers.

The recipe is to compress the structure of the target and keep its
vocabulary natural. It costs nothing at inference, needs no new labels,
and is measurable on any parser with the four-way split. Whether the gap
persists for larger models and other tasks is open (Limitations).

#### Strict refusal.

Refusing anything that is not purely an order keeps the consumer from
trusting half a ticket, at the cost of 2.1–10.1% of real orders. A
softer rule, or negatives written in human rather than grammar style,
could lower that cost; we leave it to future work and report the strict
rule’s price as is.

## 8 Conclusion

Output format is a free design variable for a fine-tuned parser. On
PIZZA, with two model families, five pool sizes and five seeds, writing
the target in plain words matches or beats the native format’s accuracy
with a quarter of the tokens and roughly halves the errors nobody can
detect, mostly by getting negation right. The gain survives a refusal
and self-correction guardrail trained into every run, and a tokenizer
under which the word form is the longer one.

## Limitations

*One dataset.* All results are on PIZZA, whose training pool comes from
a grammar with a small vocabulary; the human-written test set is the
main guard against learning the grammar alone. *Small models.* Both
models are under a billion parameters; the effect may shrink with scale.
*Author-generated guardrail data.* Negatives, corrections and probes are
generated or written by us, and the human test set contains neither; 32
test orders (2.4%) use *make it*, *make that* or *hold on* in PIZZA’s
own sense, which our generator teaches as correction cues, bounding any
effect on test exact match at 2.4 points. Every run includes the
guardrail, so this campaign does not measure what the guardrail costs in
accuracy. *Tuning.* Three learning rates were at the edge of the grid
(Appendix
Table [8](#A1.T8 "Table 8 ‣ Appendix A Additional Results ‣ Parsed, Plausible, Wrong: Output Format Controls Silent Errorsin Small-Model Semantic Parsing"));
rank was not tuned; 8 of 300 runs (all LFM2) reached their best
checkpoint within 100 steps of the step limit. *Generation.* 19 of
511,500 generations hit the token cap, all runaway outputs longer than
any correct ticket. *Deployment.* The exported LFM2 GGUF agrees with its
trained run on 93% of orders; no number in this paper comes from a GGUF.

## Appendix B Integrity Checks

Before reading any result we checked: all 300 runs complete; exact match
and parse validity recomputed from the raw outputs with the dataset’s
matcher equal the stored values in every run; negatives and corrections
identical across models, formats and sizes for a seed; no evaluation
utterance in any training subset; batched and batch-one generation agree
on all but at most 2 of 200 utterances per run; left padding in every
run. The analysis code and the checks are in
[https://github.com/UmarGit/coffee-llm](https://github.com/UmarGit/coffee-llm).

## Appendix C Deployment Demonstration

The café parser that motivated this work, a separate model with its own
compact format, runs entirely on an iPad (iPad15,7, A16) through
llama.cpp with Metal offload: Q8_0 weights (795 MiB file, 350 MiB
resident), 2.5 s load time, median 167 ms and p90 354 ms per order,
16 tok/s. Latency on the device is close to linear in output tokens, so
the token savings of a compact format translate directly into response
time. This is a demonstration on ten orders, not an evaluation.
````

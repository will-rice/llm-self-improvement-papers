###### Abstract

Test-time training (TTT) adapts large language models (LLMs) during
inference using only unlabeled test inputs. Existing methods, however,
face two major bottlenecks on hard reasoning tasks: (1) _lack of
learnable samples_, as self-generated pseudo-labels on difficult
questions are often noisy and yield unstable rewards; and (2)
_inefficient exploration_, as performance gains depend on repeatedly
sampling many rollouts without explicit diagnosis of why previous
attempts fail. We propose TTSR (Test-Time Self-Reflection), a
self-evolving framework based on a _reflect-then-synthesize_ paradigm. A
single pretrained model alternates between a Student role and a Teacher
role: the Student solves test questions and updates, while the Teacher
analyzes failed trajectories and synthesizes targeted variant questions
closer to the Student’s capability frontier. TTSR further maintains a
cross-iteration weakness memory and compiles persistent weaknesses into
a lightweight strategy note prepended to subsequent Student inputs, so
diagnostic knowledge can guide exploration and gradually fade as
weaknesses are resolved. Experiments on challenging mathematical
reasoning benchmarks show consistent test-time improvements, strong
cross-backbone generalization, and transfer to general-domain reasoning
tasks.

## 1 Introduction

Test-time training (TTT) updates model parameters at inference time
using only unlabeled test data [Akyürek et al. (2024)](#bib.bib4); [Sun
et al. (2020)](#bib.bib21), requiring no ground-truth supervision.
Recent formulations cast TTT as test-time reinforcement learning, where
rewards are derived from the model’s own sampled solutions via
self-consistency or outcome verification [Zuo et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib32); [Yuksekgonul et al. (2026)](#bib.bib33). In
practice, however, these methods produce limited and unstable gains on
hard reasoning tasks [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib14); [Akyürek et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib4). We focus on two key limitations.

Lack of learnable samples. Test questions in hard reasoning benchmarks
often sit near or beyond what the model can reliably solve.
Majority-voting pseudo-labels then become noisy, and the resulting
rewards too unreliable for stable parameter updates [Akyürek et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib4); [Zuo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib32). In effect, only a
narrow band of questions—neither trivially easy nor hopelessly
hard—yields useful gradients, leaving the model with few effective
training samples at test time [Bae et al. (2026)](#bib.bib5).

Inefficient exploration via repeated rollouts. In part because of this
scarcity, current TTT methods sample large numbers of reasoning
trajectories, hoping that some will land on correct solutions [Snell et
al. (2024)](#bib.bib20). This is expensive and undirected: the model has
no mechanism to diagnose _why_ earlier attempts failed or to steer later
sampling toward its actual weaknesses.

Two observations point toward a different approach. First, _diagnosing
where a reasoning trace went wrong is typically easier than producing a
correct solution from scratch_, since error localization operates on a
given trace rather than requiring one to be constructed [Wang et al.
(2022b)](#bib.bib23); [Lin et al. (2024)](#bib.bib35); [Zheng et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib36). Second, curriculum learning shows that training on
calibrated intermediate problems improves accuracy on harder target
tasks [Bengio et al. (2009)](#bib.bib6); [Wang et al.
(2022a)](#bib.bib22). Together, these observations motivate a
_reflect-then-synthesize_ strategy: the model first diagnoses its
reasoning failures, then uses the diagnoses to synthesize variant
questions near the model’s capability frontier as stepping stones.

We propose TTSR (Test-Time Self-Reflection), a test-time self-evolving
framework in which a _single_ pretrained model alternates between two
roles: a _Student_ and a _Teacher_. The Student samples reasoning
trajectories for test questions and updates its policy online via
GRPO [Shao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19) with self-supervised rewards. The
Teacher analyzes the Student’s failed trajectories, extracts structured
weakness descriptors through lightweight reflection, and synthesizes
variant questions that target these weaknesses. The variants sit closer
to the Student’s capability frontier and thus provide more informative
training signals than the original test questions. Per-iteration
reflections are ephemeral: without persistent tracking, the same
weaknesses may surface repeatedly. TTSR therefore maintains a
cross-iteration _weakness memory_ and compiles the most persistent
entries into a _strategy note_—concise reasoning guidelines prepended to
Student inputs that directly steer subsequent exploration toward
unresolved weaknesses. The note fades automatically as weaknesses are
resolved through continued adaptation.

We evaluate TTSR on eight mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks
across three model backbones. TTSR consistently outperforms test-time RL
baselines, achieving up to $`+15.6`$ average points over the base model,
and transfers to general-domain reasoning tasks without domain-specific
tuning. These results suggest that _self-reflection with cross-iteration
knowledge accumulation_ provides a practical pathway for continual
reasoning improvement during inference.

#### Contributions.

Our main contributions are:

- •
  We identify the lack of learnable samples and inefficient
  rollout-based exploration as two key bottlenecks of test-time
  training, and propose a _reflect-then-synthesize_ strategy that
  leverages failure diagnosis to construct targeted curricula near the
  model’s capability frontier.
- •
  We introduce TTSR, a test-time self-evolving framework in which a
  single model alternates between Student and Teacher roles. TTSR
  further incorporates a cross-iteration _weakness memory_ and a
  lightweight _strategy note_ that persistently carry forward diagnostic
  knowledge across adaptation iterations.
- •
  We demonstrate that TTSR consistently improves performance on
  challenging mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks and
  generalizes across model backbones.

## 2 Related Work

#### Self-Play and Curriculum Learning for LLMs (mainly training-time).

Self-play has become a common recipe for autonomous improvement in LLMs,
from iterative self-training and alignment-oriented loops to
role-specialized or co-evolutionary variants [Huang et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib13); [Wang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib18); [Chen et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib7); [Lin et al. (2025)](#bib.bib17); [Chen et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib8); [Fang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib10). Recent
capability-oriented systems further emphasize adversarial generation,
dynamic self-challenging, and data-free evolution [Liu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib9); [Xu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib27); [Wang et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib25); [Kuba et al. (2025)](#bib.bib16); [Zhou et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib31); [Wei et al. (2025)](#bib.bib26); [Huang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib15); [Zhao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib30); [He et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib12). In parallel, curriculum learning establishes that
calibrated intermediate tasks can improve learning efficiency [Bengio et
al. (2009)](#bib.bib6); [Wang et al. (2022a)](#bib.bib22). However, most
of this line is designed for training/post-training regimes with larger
budgets and does not explicitly target per-instance failure diagnosis
during inference.

#### Test-Time Training and Test-Time RL.

Test-time training (TTT) adapts model parameters at inference time using
unlabeled test inputs [Sun et al. (2020)](#bib.bib21); [Akyürek et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib4). TTRL-style methods cast this as test-time
reinforcement learning with self-generated rewards from voting or
verification [Zuo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib32); [Yuksekgonul et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib33). TTCS introduces curriculum learning into test-time
training, but its synthesized questions are derived from the original
test questions rather than explicit diagnoses of the model’s reasoning
failures [Yang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib34). These methods show that
online adaptation is feasible, but two challenges remain on hard
reasoning benchmarks: pseudo-label reliability degrades when questions
are far beyond current capability, and large rollout budgets are often
used as a coarse exploration mechanism [Snell et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib20); [Bae et al. (2026)](#bib.bib5).

#### Positioning of TTSR.

TTSR is a failure-conditioned curriculum adaptation mechanism for
test-time training. Its first contribution is that we introduce
self-generated curriculum construction into the test-time stage, where
adaptation must be online and label-free, while difficult test inputs
often lead to too few learnable samples and unstable updates in TTRL.
Its second contribution is a _reflect-then-synthesize_ loop that
converts failed reasoning traces into targeted variant questions near
the model’s current capability frontier, improving synthesis and
exploration efficiency.

## 3 TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection

We present TTSR, a test-time self-evolving framework built on GRPO and
TTT. A single pretrained model $`\pi_{\theta}`$ alternates between two
roles at test time: a _Student_ that solves test questions and updates
its policy via self-supervised rewards, and a _Teacher_ that analyzes
failed reasoning trajectories, extracts structured weakness descriptors,
and synthesizes targeted variant questions. To accumulate diagnostic
knowledge across iterations, TTSR maintains a _weakness memory_
$`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$ and compiles persistent entries into a _strategy
note_ $`h_{t}`$ that provides concise reasoning guidelines prepended to
Student inputs. The overall pipeline is illustrated in
Figure [2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3 TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection").

![Refer to caption](2603.03297v3/pipeline.png)

Figure 2: Overview of the TTSR pipeline. At each iteration $`t`$, the
Student samples multiple reasoning trajectories and updates its policy
via GRPO with majority-voting pseudo-labels. The Teacher analyzes failed
traces to extract structured weakness descriptors, which are accumulated
in a cross-iteration _weakness memory_. Persistent weaknesses are
compiled into a _strategy note_ that is prepended to Student inputs,
providing immediate reasoning guidance. The Teacher also synthesizes
targeted variant questions conditioned on both current failures and the
accumulated memory, forming a closed-loop self-evolving cycle.

#### Student Role.

Given a test question $`x\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{test}}`$ and the current
strategy note $`h_{t}`$
(Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Cross-Iteration Weakness Memory and Strategy Note ‣ 3 TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")),
the Student samples $`G`$ reasoning trajectories
$`\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{t},\,x)`$, each
consisting of a chain-of-thought process and a final answer. We write
$`a(y_{i})`$ for the final answer extracted from trajectory $`y_{i}`$.
These trajectories serve both as inference outputs and as the basis for
self-supervised learning signals.

#### Teacher Role.

The Teacher does not solve the test question directly. Instead, it
identifies failures in the Student’s trajectories relative to a
pseudo-target obtained via majority voting, reflects on the underlying
reasoning weaknesses, and synthesizes targeted easier variant questions
designed to expose and correct them.

### 3.1 Student: Solving Problems and Test-Time Adaptation

#### Test-Time Training Set Construction.

At test-time iteration $`t`$, the Student updates its policy
$`\pi_{\theta_{t}}`$ using a dynamically constructed training set.
Specifically, we define the training dataset at iteration $`t`$ as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{X}_{\text{test}}\;\cup\;\mathcal{X}^{(t-1)}_{\text{var}},
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{X}_{\text{test}}`$ denotes the original test set, and
$`\mathcal{X}^{(t-1)}_{\text{var}}`$ represents the set of variant
questions synthesized by the Teacher using the previous policy
$`\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}`$. The Student samples training questions from
$`\mathcal{D}_{t}`$ and performs test-time adaptation via reward-based
optimization.

#### Majority Voting Reward.

For each training question $`x\in\mathcal{D}_{t}`$, the Student samples
a group of $`G`$ reasoning trajectories using the current policy:

|     |                                                               |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid h_{t},\,x).
       ```                                                            |     | (2) |

Following the Test-Time Reinforcement Learning paradigm, we aggregate
the sampled trajectories via majority voting over their final answers to
obtain a consensus answer $`\hat{a}(x)`$, which serves as a
pseudo-correct reference. An outcome-level pseudo-correctness reward is
then assigned to each trajectory according to whether its final answer
agrees with the consensus:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{S}(y_{i}\mid x)=\mathbb{I}\!\left[a(y_{i})=\hat{a}(x)\right].
``` |  | (3) |

Based on these rewards, we define the empirical pseudo-correctness score
for question $`x`$ as

|     |                                                       |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       s_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}R_{S}(y_{i}\mid x),
       ```                                                    |     | (4) |

which measures the stability of the Student’s predictions under
stochastic sampling at iteration $`t`$. The Student is optimized using
GRPO with these pseudo-correctness rewards, enabling stable and
label-free test-time adaptation.

### 3.2 Teacher: Reflection-Guided Curriculum Synthesis

#### Reflection on Reasoning Steps.

At test-time iteration $`t`$, the Teacher performs reflection based on
the Student’s failed reasoning behaviors observed in the previous
iteration. Specifically, using the policy $`\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}`$, we
collect a set of reasoning trajectories whose pseudo-correctness rewards
are zero. From these trajectories, we randomly sample a subset of failed
instances for reflection.

Each sampled instance consists of a tuple $`(x,y,\hat{a})`$, where $`x`$
denotes the question, $`y`$ the Student’s reasoning trajectory, and
$`\hat{a}`$ the corresponding pseudo-correct answer obtained via
majority voting over final answers. Conditioned on this set of failed
tuples, the Teacher with policy $`\pi_{\theta_{t}}`$ analyzes the
reasoning steps in $`y`$ and produces a reflection summary that
characterizes both the incorrect reasoning patterns and the missing or
insufficient steps leading to failure. This reflection focuses on the
process-level deficiencies of the Student’s reasoning rather than the
final answer correctness. Each reflection output is a structured
weakness descriptor consisting of a natural-language weakness summary,
trigger conditions, and failure signatures. These descriptors serve dual
purposes: guiding question synthesis within the current iteration and
updating the cross-iteration weakness memory $`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$
(Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Cross-Iteration Weakness Memory and Strategy Note ‣ 3 TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")).

#### Reflection-Guided Question Synthesis.

Given the extracted weakness descriptors and the accumulated weakness
memory, the Teacher synthesizes variant questions that form an adaptive
curriculum for the Student. Let
$`\mathcal{F}^{(t-1)}=\{(x_{k},y_{k},\hat{a}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{F}|}`$
denote the set of failed instances from the previous iteration. We
construct a synthesis prompt $`p_{t}`$ from both $`\mathcal{F}^{(t-1)}`$
and the most persistent entries in $`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$, then sample
$`M`$ variants:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{X}^{(t)}_{\text{var}}=\left\{x^{\prime}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}\!\left(\cdot\;\middle|\;p_{t},\,\mathcal{F}^{(t-1)},\,\mathcal{M}_{t}\right).
``` |  | (5) |

Each variant preserves the core reasoning structure of the original
question while modifying conditions to expose the identified weaknesses.
We use group sampling to encourage diversity, so the curriculum covers
multiple manifestations of the same deficiency.

#### Difficulty Reward.

We evaluate the difficulty of a variant question $`x^{\prime}`$ using
the Student’s pseudo-correctness score at iteration $`t`$. Concretely,
the Student samples $`G`$ trajectories under $`\pi_{\theta_{t}}`$ and
computes the empirical pseudo-correctness score
$`s_{t}(x^{\prime})\in[0,1]`$ as defined in
Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Student: Solving Problems and Test-Time Adaptation ‣ 3 TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection").

Following prior work showing that variance-based rewards effectively
induce capability-centered curricula in self-play [Liu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib9), we adopt a normalized variance-based frontier
reward:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\text{diff}}(x^{\prime})=4\,s_{t}(x^{\prime})\bigl(1-s_{t}(x^{\prime})\bigr).
``` |  | (6) |

This reward lies in $`[0,1]`$ and is maximized at
$`s_{t}(x^{\prime})=0.5`$, i.e., when the Student operates near its
capability frontier. It therefore encourages the Teacher to synthesize
variant questions that are neither trivial nor degenerate. Further
theoretical analysis is provided in
Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Theoretical Analysis of the Difficulty Reward ‣ Appendix C Method Details ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection").

#### Similarity Penalty.

To reduce redundancy among synthesized variants, we penalize
near-duplicate generations. Let
$`\mathcal{Z}=\{x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{M}\}\cup\{x\}`$ be the
set of variants together with the original question. For each variant
$`x^{\prime}_{i}`$, the penalty is

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle P_{\text{sim}}(x^{\prime}_{i},x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}|-1}`$ |  | (7) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}\setminus\{x^{\prime}_{i}\}}\max\!\left(0,\ \mathrm{sim}(x^{\prime}_{i},z)-\tau\right).`$ |  |  |

where $`\mathrm{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)\in[0,1]`$ is a token-level sequence
similarity metric
(Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Similarity Metric Definition ‣ Appendix C Method Details ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection"))
and $`\tau`$ controls the overlap tolerance.

#### Teacher Reward.

For each variant $`x^{\prime}_{i}`$, the Teacher’s overall reward
combines difficulty and diversity:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle R_{T}(x^{\prime}_{i})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\max\!\left(0,\;R_{\text{diff}}(x^{\prime}_{i})-\lambda\,P_{\text{sim}}(x^{\prime}_{i},x)\right).`$ |  | (8) |

Outputs that violate the predefined \<question\> format are discarded
and receive zero reward.

### 3.3 Cross-Iteration Weakness Memory and Strategy Note

Per-iteration reflections are ephemeral and do not accumulate knowledge
across iterations. To persistently track reasoning deficiencies, TTSR
maintains a *weakness memory* $`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$ and compiles
persistent entries into a *strategy note* $`h_{t}`$.

#### Weakness Memory.

At each iteration $`t`$, the Teacher’s reflection produces weakness
descriptors $`\mathcal{W}^{(t)}=\{w^{(t)}_{1},\dots,w^{(t)}_{n}\}`$
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Teacher: Reflection-Guided Curriculum Synthesis ‣ 3 TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")).
The memory $`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$ is updated via three operations:

Merge. If a new descriptor $`w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}^{(t)}`$ matches an
existing entry $`w\in\mathcal{M}_{t-1}`$
($`\mathrm{sim}(w^{\prime},w)>\tau_{w}`$, using the same metric as in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2.SSS0.Px4 "Similarity Penalty. ‣ 3.2 Teacher: Reflection-Guided Curriculum Synthesis ‣ 3 TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")),
we merge $`w^{\prime}`$ into $`w`$ by incrementing its persistence
counter $`c(w)\leftarrow c(w)+1`$ and updating
$`w.\mathrm{last\_seen}\leftarrow t`$; otherwise $`w^{\prime}`$ is added
as a new entry with $`c(w^{\prime})=1`$.

Resolve. Entries not observed for $`\Delta`$ consecutive iterations
($`t-w.\mathrm{last\_seen}>\Delta`$) are removed, indicating the
weakness has been addressed.

Evict. If $`|\mathcal{M}_{t}|>K`$ after merging, entries with the lowest
persistence count are dropped.

The complete update is:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathrm{Resolve}\!\big(\mathrm{Merge}(\mathcal{M}_{t},\,\mathcal{W}^{(t)}),\,\Delta\big),`$ |  | (9) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t+1}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathrm{Evict}\!\big(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{t+1},\,K\big).`$ |  |  |

#### Strategy Note.

At the beginning of each iteration, we select the top-$`N`$ entries from
$`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$ ranked by persistence count and compile them into a
strategy note:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
h_{t}=\mathrm{Compile}(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\,\mathrm{top}\text{-}N}).
``` |  | (10) |

The note is prepended to all Student inputs during both sampling and
GRPO training. As the Student improves, resolved weaknesses are removed
from $`\mathcal{M}_{t}`$ and the corresponding guidelines automatically
drop from $`h_{t}`$, so the note serves as a transient scaffold that
fades as the underlying capability catches up.

## 4 Experiment

### 4.1 Experimental Setting

#### Models and Baselines.

We evaluate on three base pretrained language models: Qwen3-4B-Base,
Qwen3-8B-Base [Yang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib28), and
OctoThinker-8B-Hybrid-Base [Wang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib25). We compare
against two test-time optimization baselines: TTRL [Zuo et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib32), which adapts the model on unlabeled test instances
using self-generated reinforcement signals, and R-Zero [Huang et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib15), which enables data-free self-improvement through
reinforcement learning. The Base model performs direct inference without
parameter updates. We additionally report a Self-Consistency (SC) [Wang
et al. (2022b)](#bib.bib23) baseline that applies majority voting over
$`G\!=\!16`$ sampled trajectories without any parameter updates, to
disentangle the effect of inference-time scaling from test-time
training. All baselines are evaluated under the same test-time setting
for fair comparison.

#### Datasets and Evaluation.

We evaluate mathematical reasoning on AMC23, MATH-500 [Hendrycks et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib1), Minerva [Lewkowycz et al. (2022)](#bib.bib2),
OlympiadBench [He et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3), AIME 2024, and AIME
2025 [Li et al. (2025)](#bib.bib37), which cover a wide range of
difficulty levels and require multi-step symbolic manipulation. We
further evaluate general reasoning on GPQA-Diamond [Rein et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib11) and MMLU-Pro [Wang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24) to
assess reasoning ability beyond mathematics. Each benchmark is adapted
independently.

#### Training Configuration.

The Teacher generates $`M\!=\!2`$ variant questions per original test
question per iteration; the Student samples $`G\!=\!16`$ reasoning
trajectories per question. The policy is optimized via GRPO with
pseudo-correctness rewards. All hyperparameters, including similarity
penalty ($`\lambda\!=\!1.0`$, $`\tau\!=\!0.75`$), weakness memory
($`K\!=\!10`$, $`\Delta\!=\!3`$, $`\tau_{w}\!=\!0.6`$) and strategy note
($`N\!=\!3`$), are fixed across all models and benchmarks; see
Appendix [B.2](#A2.SS2 "B.2 Training Setting ‣ Appendix B Experiment Details ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")
for full details.

### 4.2 Main Results

Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Training Configuration. ‣ 4.1 Experimental Setting ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")
summarizes results across all benchmarks.

TTSR achieves consistent improvements across models and domains. On
Qwen3-4B-Base, TTSR raises average accuracy by $`+11.7`$ points over the
base model. On Qwen3-8B-Base, the average improvement is $`+11.9`$
points, and on OctoThinker-8B-Hybrid-Base, which undergoes
reasoning-oriented mid-training [Wang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib25), the
gains reach $`+15.6`$ points. Beyond mathematics, TTSR also improves
general reasoning: gains on GPQA-Diamond and MMLU-Pro range from $`3`$
to $`7`$ points over the strongest baseline, indicating that the
reflection-guided curriculum transfers across domains.

TTSR outperforms existing test-time training methods on average and on
most benchmarks. Compared to TTRL and R-Zero, TTSR delivers clear
average gains across model backbones, with improvements on the majority
of benchmarks. On Qwen3-8B-Base, TTSR surpasses TTRL by $`+4.1`$ points
on average; on OctoThinker-8B, the margin widens to $`+7.0`$. This gap
indicates that reflection-guided variant synthesis provides more
informative training signal than direct adaptation on raw test
questions.

TTSR shows the largest improvements on challenging benchmarks. The
advantage is most pronounced on AIME 2024 and AIME 2025, where the
original questions lie far beyond the model’s reliable solving range. On
Qwen3-4B-Base, TTSR exceeds TTRL by $`+8.0`$ on AIME24 and $`+2.9`$ on
AIME25. On Qwen3-8B-Base, TTSR reaches $`30.8`$ on AIME24 ($`+4.7`$ over
TTRL) and $`19.1`$ on AIME25 ($`+3.4`$). When TTRL’s pseudo-labels
become unreliable on intractable problems, Teacher-synthesized variants
bridge the difficulty gap by creating frontier-level stepping stones
that progressively approach the target difficulty.

We additionally compare against Self-Consistency (SC), which applies
majority voting over $`G\!=\!16`$ trajectories without parameter
updates; full results are reported in
Table [12](#A8.T12 "Table 12 ‣ Appendix H Self-Consistency Comparison ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")
(Appendix [H](#A8 "Appendix H Self-Consistency Comparison ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")).
All test-time training methods substantially outperform SC, confirming
that TTSR ’s gains stem from parameter adaptation rather than
inference-time scaling.

### 4.3 Analysis

We conduct targeted analyses to validate the key design choices of TTSR.

#### Q1: Do synthesized variants concentrate near the capability frontier?

A key hypothesis is that reflection-guided synthesis generates questions
near the Student’s capability frontier, where
$`R_{\text{diff}}=4s_{t}(1-s_{t})`$ is high. On Qwen3-8B-Base, we
compare pseudo-correctness $`s_{t}(x^{\prime})`$ across three sources:
(i) original test questions, (ii) variants without reflection (random
structural variation), and (iii) full TTSR variants. We report the
*frontier ratio*: the fraction with $`s_{t}\in[0.2,0.8]`$, where
$`R_{\text{diff}}`$ is at least $`64\%`$ of its maximum
(Table [10](#A5.T10 "Table 10 ‣ E.1 Frontier Score Distribution ‣ Appendix E Variant Quality Analysis ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection"),
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Variant Quality Analysis ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")).
On MATH500, the frontier ratio rises from $`16.2\%`$ (original) to
$`67.4\%`$ (TTSR), a $`4.2\times`$ increase. On AIME25, where original
questions have a frontier ratio of only $`8.3\%`$, TTSR variants achieve
$`64.2\%`$, demonstrating that reflection is especially valuable when
the test set lies far from the model’s capability boundary. Removing
reflection reduces the frontier ratio to $`43.8\%`$ and $`35.6\%`$,
indicating that weakness-aware diagnosis provides gains beyond what
random structural variation can achieve.

#### Q2: Is co-evolution necessary, or can a stronger static teacher suffice?

The ablation study
(Table [13](#A9.T13 "Table 13 ‣ Appendix I Full Component-Level Ablation ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection"),
Appendix [I](#A9 "Appendix I Full Component-Level Ablation ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection"))
shows that removing Teacher test-time updates (equivalent to using a
frozen Teacher at iteration 0) causes the largest overall drop (up to
$`-5.8`$ on AIME25 and Olympiad). This confirms that Teacher–Student
co-evolution is critical: a co-evolving same-size Teacher keeps the
curriculum aligned with the Student’s evolving capability frontier,
whereas a frozen Teacher produces increasingly mismatched variants as
training progresses.

#### Q3: How does TTSR perform with limited test data?

To assess whether TTSR’s gains rely on large test sets, we evaluate data
efficiency on AIME24 with Qwen3-4B-Base. TTSR consistently outperforms
TTRL at all data ratios: $`10\%`$ ($`12.3`$ vs $`9.5`$, $`+2.8`$),
$`20\%`$ ($`14.6`$ vs $`11.2`$, $`+3.4`$), $`30\%`$ ($`15.3`$ vs
$`11.8`$, $`+3.5`$), and $`100\%`$ ($`25.6`$ vs $`17.6`$, $`+8.0`$).
These results suggest that reflection-guided variant synthesis amplifies
the value of limited test data by constructing additional
frontier-difficulty training signals. It also weakens an
instance-memorization explanation: even with only a small fraction of
test instances, TTSR retains a clear advantage.

Figure 3: Data efficiency on AIME24 (Qwen3-4B-Base). Accuracy under four
test-data proportions.

Appendix [J](#A10 "Appendix J Computational Cost Analysis ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")
further analyzes the computational cost of TTSR through a per-iteration
breakdown and an iso-compute comparison against an extended TTRL run.
The matched-budget experiment shows that TTRL largely saturates with
additional iterations, whereas TTSR retains a clear advantage. This
result supports that the gains arise from the reflection-guided
curriculum rather than additional computation alone.

#### Q4: Do improvements transfer across domains?

We evaluate cross-benchmark transfer on Qwen3-8B-Base. The OOD
experiments
(Figure [6](#A6.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Appendix F Out-of-Distribution Performance ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection"))
show that TTSR transfers well across mathematical benchmarks: training
on Olympiad yields $`25.4`$ on AIME24, exceeding TTRL’s $`22.1`$
($`+3.3`$), while the Base model achieves only $`15.9`$. Beyond
mathematics, the training dynamics
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.4 Training Dynamics ‣ 4 Experiment ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection"))
reveal meaningful cross-domain gains on GPQA-D
($`33.1\!\rightarrow\!36.2`$, $`+3.1`$) and MMLU-Pro
($`58.6\!\rightarrow\!61.2`$, $`+2.6`$), far exceeding TTRL ($`+1.5`$
and $`+1.2`$, respectively). These cross-domain gains suggest that TTSR
acquires transferable reasoning strategies rather than memorizing
benchmark-specific patterns.

## 5 Conclusion

We identify two core bottlenecks of test-time training on hard reasoning
tasks: noisy pseudo-labels on overly difficult questions (few learnable
samples) and inefficient rollout exploration without failure diagnosis.
To address both, we propose TTSR, a *reflect-then-synthesize* framework
in which one model alternates between Student and Teacher roles. The
Teacher analyzes failed trajectories, extracts structured weaknesses,
and synthesizes targeted variants near the Student’s capability
frontier. A cross-iteration weakness memory compiles persistent patterns
into a strategy note that guides subsequent reasoning.

Across eight mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks and three
base models, TTSR improves average accuracy by $`+11.7`$ to $`+15.6`$
points over direct inference and consistently outperforms prior
test-time training baselines. Ablations show that Teacher–Student
co-evolution and reflection-guided synthesis are the primary
contributors, and cross-domain results indicate that gains transfer
beyond the training distribution. Training dynamics and cross-domain
evaluations further demonstrate sustained improvement across iterations
and transfer to held-out reasoning domains.

## Limitations

In this work, we introduce TTSR, a test-time self-evolving framework
that improves large language models on challenging reasoning tasks
through reflection-guided curriculum synthesis and cross-iteration
weakness accumulation. However, there are still some limitations: (1)
Our current evaluation mainly focuses on mathematical and
reasoning-intensive benchmarks. Although these settings provide a
controlled testbed for studying test-time self-improvement, extending
the evaluation to a broader range of task families would further
strengthen the generality of the conclusions. (2) To ensure fair
comparison across methods, we adopt a largely unified test-time
adaptation setup in our experiments. Exploring a wider design space of
update frequency, synthesis budget, and iteration schedules may yield
additional insights into the behavior of TTSR under different resource
conditions. (3) While our experiments include several representative
models, the current study is not intended to be an exhaustive comparison
across all model families, parameter scales, and alignment settings. A
broader model coverage would provide a more complete picture of how TTSR
behaves under different model configurations. In future work, we aim to
broaden the evaluation of TTSR beyond mathematical and
reasoning-intensive benchmarks to more diverse domains and task
settings. We are also interested in studying richer adaptation schedules
and broader model coverage, which may provide a more comprehensive
understanding of how TTSR scales across different resource budgets and
model families.

## Appendix A Background: GRPO and TTT

This section briefly reviews the optimization and adaptation techniques
underlying our framework, including Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO)  ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) for reward-based learning and
Test-Time Training (TTT) [Zuo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib32) for label-free
model adaptation at inference time.

#### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib19)) is a policy-gradient algorithm for large language
models that removes the need for an explicit value function. Instead of
estimating advantages via a learned critic, GRPO derives relative
advantages by normalizing outcome-level rewards across a group of
sampled responses, which enables stable optimization under sparse or
binary supervision.

We model the language model as a stochastic policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ that
generates a response $`y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{|y|})`$ conditioned on an input
question $`q`$. For each question $`q`$, a behavior policy
$`\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}`$ samples a group of $`G`$ candidate
responses $`\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$, each receiving a sequence-level
reward $`R_{i}`$ from a verifier.

To derive learning signals without a value function, GRPO normalizes
rewards within the sampled group and defines a relative advantage for
each response:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{A}_{i}=\frac{R_{i}-\mathrm{mean}(\mathbf{R})}{\mathrm{std}(\mathbf{R})+\delta},\qquad\mathbf{R}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{G}\},
``` |  | (11) |

where $`\delta`$ is a small constant for numerical stability. The same
advantage $`\tilde{A}_{i}`$ is shared across all token positions within
response $`y_{i}`$.

GRPO updates the policy parameters by maximizing a PPO-style clipped
surrogate objective with an explicit KL regularization term:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{J}`$ | $`\displaystyle{}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D}_{q},\,\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}}`$ |  | (12) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\Biggl[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_{i}|}\Bigl(\min\!\bigl(r_{i,t}(\theta)\,\tilde{A}_{i},\,\mathrm{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\tilde{A}_{i}\bigr)-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}\parallel\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}\right)\Bigr)\Biggr],`$ |  |  |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid q,y_{i,<t})},\qquad\tilde{A}_{i}=\frac{R_{i}-\mathrm{mean}(\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G})}{\mathrm{std}(\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G})+\delta}.
``` |  | (13) |

Here, $`\epsilon`$ controls the clipping range, and $`\beta`$ balances
the KL regularization term, which constrains policy updates to remain
close to the behavior policy and improves optimization stability.

#### Test-Time Training (TTT).

Test-Time Training enables a pretrained policy to adapt its parameters
during inference using only unlabeled test inputs. Let
$`\mathcal{X}_{\text{test}}=\{x^{(t)}\}`$ denote the set of test
queries.

Starting from an initial policy $`\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}`$, TTT seeks
adapted parameters by optimizing a self-supervised objective:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\theta^{\dagger}=\arg\max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x^{(t)})}\big[\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{TTT}}(y,\tilde{y})\big],
``` |  | (14) |

where $`\tilde{y}`$ denotes a pseudo-target constructed from the model’s
own predictions, typically obtained via self-consistency or
consensus-based aggregation. The reward function
$`\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{TTT}}`$ measures agreement between a candidate
response and the pseudo-target, providing a learning signal for
inference-time adaptation.

#### Similarity Penalty Configuration.

To discourage redundant or near-duplicate question generation during
reflection-guided synthesis, we incorporate a similarity-based penalty
into the Teacher’s reward. Pairwise similarities are computed between
synthesized variant questions and the original test question, as well as
among the synthesized questions within the same batch. The similarity
threshold is fixed to $`\tau=0.75`$, and the penalty coefficient is set
to $`\lambda=1.0`$ in all experiments. These values are kept constant
across models and benchmarks.

8:     Load cached trajectories $`\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}`$, consensus

9:    else

### C.2 Similarity Metric Definition

To quantify the similarity between two questions in the similarity
penalty, we adopt a sequence-based similarity measure that captures
contiguous overlapping spans between two token sequences. This metric is
designed to penalize near-duplicate generations while remaining robust
to minor surface variations.

#### Sequence Representation.

Given two questions represented as token sequences $`S_{1}`$ and
$`S_{2}`$, we define their total length as

|     |                    |     |      |
|-----|--------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       T=|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|,
       ```                 |     | (15) |

where $`|\cdot|`$ denotes the number of tokens in the sequence.

#### Matching Blocks.

We identify a set of non-overlapping matching blocks between $`S_{1}`$
and $`S_{2}`$. Each matching block is defined as a contiguous
subsequence that appears in both sequences. The matching process first
finds the longest contiguous matching subsequence and then recursively
searches for additional matches in the remaining left and right
segments. Let the resulting set of matching blocks be represented as

|     |                          |     |
|-----|--------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{B}=\{(i,j,n)\},
       ```                       |     |

where $`(i,j,n)`$ denotes a matching block of length $`n`$ starting at
position $`i`$ in $`S_{1}`$ and position $`j`$ in $`S_{2}`$. The total
number of matched tokens is then defined as

|     |                                  |     |      |
|-----|----------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       C=\sum_{(i,j,n)\in\mathcal{B}}n.
       ```                               |     | (16) |

#### Similarity Ratio.

Using the above definitions, we compute the normalized similarity ratio
between $`S_{1}`$ and $`S_{2}`$ as

|     |                                         |     |      |
|-----|-----------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathrm{sim}(S_{1},S_{2})=\frac{2C}{T},
       ```                                      |     | (17) |

which takes values in the range $`[0,1]`$. A higher similarity ratio
indicates greater overlap between the two sequences, while a lower value
reflects more substantial differences in surface form. This similarity
measure is used to compute the pairwise similarity scores required by
the similarity penalty described in the main text
(Section [3.2](#S3.SS2.SSS0.Px4 "Similarity Penalty. ‣ 3.2 Teacher: Reflection-Guided Curriculum Synthesis ‣ 3 TTSR: Test-Time Self-Reflection ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")).
By focusing on contiguous matching spans rather than isolated token
overlap, the metric effectively discourages trivial paraphrasing while
allowing moderate overlap that preserves shared reasoning structure.

### C.3 Theoretical Analysis of the Difficulty Reward

For a synthesized variant $`x^{\prime}`$, the Student samples $`G`$
trajectories

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid h_{t},x^{\prime}),
``` |  |

constructs the majority-vote pseudo-answer

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{a}(x^{\prime})=\operatorname{Maj}\bigl(\{a(y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{G}\bigr),
``` |  |

and assigns

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{S}(y_{i}\mid x^{\prime})=\mathbb{I}[a(y_{i})=\hat{a}(x^{\prime})].
``` |  |

Recall that

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
s_{t}(x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}R_{S}(y_{i}\mid x^{\prime}),\qquad R_{\mathrm{diff}}(x^{\prime})=4s_{t}(x^{\prime})(1-s_{t}(x^{\prime})).
``` |  |

We emphasize that $`s_{t}(x^{\prime})`$ measures agreement with the
self-generated pseudo-answer. We analyze $`R_{\mathrm{diff}}`$ as the
amount of relative supervision available under this pseudo-labeling
rule.

#### Finite-sample interpretation.

For a fixed $`x^{\prime}`$, let

|     |                                                                 |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{i}:=R_{S}(y_{i}\mid x^{\prime}),\qquad s:=s_{t}(x^{\prime}).
       ```                                                              |     |

Because $`R_{i}\in\{0,1\}`$,

|     |                                                |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}(R_{i}-s)^{2}=s(1-s).
       ```                                             |     | (18) |

Hence,

|     |                                                             |     |      |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{\mathrm{diff}}(x^{\prime})=4\,\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(R).
       ```                                                          |     | (19) |

This identity is deterministic and does not require the sample-dependent
rewards $`\{R_{i}\}`$ to be independent.

An equivalent pairwise interpretation follows immediately. Let

|     |                          |     |
|-----|--------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       k=\sum_{i=1}^{G}R_{i}=Gs
       ```                       |     |

be the number of trajectories agreeing with $`\hat{a}(x^{\prime})`$. The
number of positive–negative trajectory pairs is $`k(G-k)`$, and
therefore

|     |                                                      |     |      |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|------|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{\mathrm{diff}}(x^{\prime})=\frac{4k(G-k)}{G^{2}}.
       ```                                                   |     | (20) |

Thus, $`R_{\mathrm{diff}}`$ is a normalized measure of the within-group
relative supervision. It is zero when all trajectories receive the same
pseudo-reward and is maximized at $`s_{t}(x^{\prime})=1/2`$, where
positive–negative comparisons are most abundant.

This interpretation is directly aligned with GRPO, whose update is
driven by reward differences within a rollout group. In particular, the
average magnitude of the unnormalized centered reward satisfies

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}|R_{i}-s|=2s(1-s)=\frac{1}{2}R_{\mathrm{diff}}(x^{\prime}).
``` |  | (21) |

Therefore, larger $`R_{\mathrm{diff}}`$ corresponds to stronger
available group-relative reward contrast.

#### Stability of the majority-vote pseudo-answer.

For a fixed $`x^{\prime}`$, let

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p_{t,j}(x^{\prime})=\Pr(a(y)=a_{j}\mid h_{t},x^{\prime}),\qquad y\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(\cdot\mid h_{t},x^{\prime}).
``` |  |

Assume a unique population modal answer, with
$`p_{t,1}(x^{\prime})>p_{t,2}(x^{\prime})\geq\cdots\geq p_{t,K}(x^{\prime})`$.
Define

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
a_{t}^{\star}(x^{\prime})=a_{1},\qquad p_{t}^{\star}(x^{\prime})=p_{t,1}(x^{\prime}),\qquad\Delta_{t}(x^{\prime})=p_{t,1}(x^{\prime})-p_{t,2}(x^{\prime}).
``` |  |

#### Proposition 1 (Majority-vote stability).

For $`G`$ independently sampled Student trajectories,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\!\left(\hat{a}(x^{\prime})\neq a_{t}^{\star}(x^{\prime})\right)\leq(K-1)\exp\!\left(-\frac{G\Delta_{t}(x^{\prime})^{2}}{2}\right).
``` |  | (22) |

Proof. For any competing answer $`a_{j}`$, $`j\geq 2`$, define

|     |                                                                    |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       Z_{i}^{(j)}=\mathbb{I}[a(y_{i})=a_{1}]-\mathbb{I}[a(y_{i})=a_{j}].
       ```                                                                 |     |

Then $`Z_{i}^{(j)}\in[-1,1]`$ and

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}^{(j)}]=p_{t,1}(x^{\prime})-p_{t,j}(x^{\prime})\geq\Delta_{t}(x^{\prime}).
``` |  |

If $`a_{j}`$ receives at least as many votes as $`a_{1}`$, then
$`\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i}Z_{i}^{(j)}\leq 0`$. Hoeffding’s inequality gives

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr(\hat{p}_{j}\geq\hat{p}_{1})\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{G\Delta_{t}(x^{\prime})^{2}}{2}\right).
``` |  |

Taking a union bound over all competing answers $`j=2,\ldots,K`$ yields

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\!\left(\hat{a}(x^{\prime})\neq a_{t}^{\star}(x^{\prime})\right)\leq(K-1)\exp\!\left(-\frac{G\Delta_{t}(x^{\prime})^{2}}{2}\right),
``` |  |

which proves
Eq. ([22](#A3.E22 "In Proposition 1 (Majority-vote stability). ‣ C.3 Theoretical Analysis of the Difficulty Reward ‣ Appendix C Method Details ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection")).

#### Finite-sample approximation of the frontier score.

On the event $`\hat{a}(x^{\prime})=a_{t}^{\star}(x^{\prime})`$,
$`s_{t}(x^{\prime})`$ is simply the empirical frequency of the
population modal answer. Combining Proposition 1 with Hoeffding’s
inequality therefore gives, for any $`\epsilon>0`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\!\left(|s_{t}(x^{\prime})-p_{t}^{\star}(x^{\prime})|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq(K-1)e^{-G\Delta_{t}(x^{\prime})^{2}/2}+2e^{-2G\epsilon^{2}}.
``` |  | (23) |

Define the population counterpart

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\mathrm{diff}}^{\star}(x^{\prime})=4p_{t}^{\star}(x^{\prime})\bigl(1-p_{t}^{\star}(x^{\prime})\bigr).
``` |  | (24) |

Since $`f(u)=4u(1-u)`$ is $`4`$-Lipschitz on $`[0,1]`$,
Eq. ([23](#A3.E23 "In Finite-sample approximation of the frontier score. ‣ C.3 Theoretical Analysis of the Difficulty Reward ‣ Appendix C Method Details ‣ TTSR: Test-Time Self-Evolving via Reflection"))
immediately implies

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Pr\!\left(|R_{\mathrm{diff}}(x^{\prime})-R_{\mathrm{diff}}^{\star}(x^{\prime})|\geq 4\epsilon\right)\leq(K-1)e^{-G\Delta_{t}(x^{\prime})^{2}/2}+2e^{-2G\epsilon^{2}}.
``` |  | (25) |

The two terms have complementary meanings. The first controls whether
finite-sample voting identifies the Student’s dominant answer, while the
second controls estimation error once that answer is identified. Thus,
increasing $`G`$ improves both pseudo-target stability and
frontier-score estimation.

#### Implication for curriculum synthesis.

$`R_{\mathrm{diff}}(x^{\prime})`$ measures the relative learning signal
induced by the current self-generated supervision. At the realized-group
level, $`R_{\mathrm{diff}}`$ is exactly the normalized binary reward
variance and is proportional to the number of positive–negative
trajectory pairs. At the population level, when the modal margin
$`\Delta_{t}(x^{\prime})`$ is sufficiently large, majority voting
provides a stable pseudo-target and the empirical score approaches
$`R_{\mathrm{diff}}^{\star}(x^{\prime})`$ as $`G`$ increases.

We therefore interpret regions of high $`R_{\mathrm{diff}}`$ as a
*self-supervised learning frontier*: the Student exhibits substantial
within-group reward contrast, providing informative relative supervision
for GRPO. Reflection-guided synthesis aims to construct variants near
this frontier.

## Appendix K Reflection Quality Evaluation

To assess whether the Teacher’s reflections accurately identify genuine
reasoning weaknesses, we conduct an automated evaluation using GPT-5.2
as an external judge on Qwen3-4B-Base.

#### Setup.

We randomly sample 100 reflection instances from MATH500 and 30 from
AIME25. Each instance consists of the original question, the Student’s
failed reasoning trace, and the Teacher’s extracted weakness descriptor
(including weakness summary, trigger conditions, and failure signature).
GPT-5.2 evaluates each instance along three dimensions:

- •
  Weakness Accuracy: Does the extracted weakness correctly identify the
  actual reasoning error in the Student’s trace?
- •
  Actionability: Is the weakness descriptor specific enough to guide
  targeted question synthesis?
- •
  Variant Alignment: Does the subsequently synthesized variant question
  actually target the identified weakness?

Each dimension is scored as *yes* or *no*. We report the fraction of
positive judgments.

| Benchmark               | Accuracy | Actionability | Alignment |
|-------------------------|----------|---------------|-----------|
| MATH500 ($`n\!=\!100`$) | 82.0%    | 88.0%         | 79.0%     |
| AIME25 ($`n\!=\!30`$)   | 36.7%    | 46.7%         | 33.3%     |

Table 17: GPT-5.2 (2025-12-11) evaluation of Teacher reflection quality
on Qwen3-4B-Base.

On MATH500, the Teacher correctly identifies the reasoning weakness in
$`82\%`$ of cases, and the subsequent variant targets this weakness
$`79\%`$ of the time. Scores are substantially lower on AIME25: weakness
accuracy falls to $`36.7\%`$, variant alignment falls to $`33.3\%`$, and
actionability reaches $`46.7\%`$. This gap is expected given the higher
difficulty: competition-level problems involve longer reasoning chains
where pinpointing the first error is harder. The actionability scores
($`88.0\%`$ and $`46.7\%`$) remain higher than accuracy on both
benchmarks, indicating that even when the weakness diagnosis is
imprecise, the descriptor can still provide structural information for
variant synthesis. These results support the ablation finding that
reflection-guided synthesis outperforms untargeted variant generation.
````

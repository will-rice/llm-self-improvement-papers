# EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents

Janet Wang Jiang Guo Yi Xiang    Jiaqing Yuan Mingwen Dong Hideo
Kobayashi    Shuaichen Chang Chandan K. Reddy Jiarong Jiang    Tulane
University     Amazon

###### Abstract

Self-evolving LLM agents typically improve how they act through
accumulated experience, reflection, or skills. Yet even procedurally
capable agents can fail when their beliefs about environment dynamics
are incomplete, incorrect, or outdated. We study a complementary axis of
self-evolution: evolving those beliefs during deployment. Such beliefs
can support multiple tasks, but incorrect ones can also propagate across
them. We introduce EvoBelief, a self-evolving world model that
represents environment knowledge as falsifiable hypotheses. Unexpected
outcomes and decision-relevant unknowns trigger hypothesis formation.
Later observations and controlled experiments provide independent
validation before a rule can guide predictions or plans within its
supported scope. Counterexamples narrow or retire rules as the
environment changes. The language model remains frozen, and each rule
remains linked to its evidence and the decisions it changes. Across four
benchmarks spanning static dynamics, hidden drift, and scientific
discovery, EvoBelief improves task performance and reuses learned rules
across tasks. On ALFWorld without actor-side transition memory, it
improves success over the matched Frozen World Model by 23.9% and 31.3%
with Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.4, respectively. On MiniGrid, it revises
stale rules and recovers after unannounced dynamics changes. In
DiscoveryWorld’s Reactor Lab, it discovers and validates quantitative
laws, completing 83.3% of Normal-tier and 58.3% of Challenge-tier
instances, versus 0% for each evaluated baseline. These results
demonstrate self-improvement through testable, revisable environment
knowledge rather than model-parameter updates.

## 1 Introduction

![Refer to caption](2610.12789v1/evobelief_teaser_v5.png)

Figure 1: Illustrative example. (a) Following its frozen prior, a
capable agent heats compound X to 100 ^(∘)C and gets decomposition

experiments test. (c) The verified belief is stored in the persistent

Existing self-evolving LLM agents mostly adapt to new or changing
environments by improving _how they act_. They learn from past
interaction trajectories, refine behavior through reflection, accumulate
memories, or distill experience into reusable skills ([Zhao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8); [Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib7); [Park et al.,
2023](#bib.bib9); [Wang et al., 2023](#bib.bib10)). These mechanisms are
effective in addressing failures arising from inadequate strategy or
procedure. But an agent also acts on its prior beliefs about how the
environment works, and in a new environment these beliefs can be wrong.
Consider the agent in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents"),
whose task is to synthesize a compound X. It already knows how to heat,
purify, and analyze samples, yet it fails because its prior predicts
that X is stable up to 120 ^(∘)C, while X in fact decomposes at
100 ^(∘)C. A task-specific correction, such as “use a lower
temperature,” can address the immediate failure but does not capture the
underlying property. The agent should instead test how X’s stability
depends on temperature and retain the resulting evidence-supported
constraint. This belief can then guide not only synthesis, but also
related tasks such as shipping, storage, and analysis.

This motivates belief evolution, a complementary form of self-evolution
that updates a persistent model of environment dynamics rather than only
adapting task-specific behavior. A policy specifies how an agent acts
toward a goal, $`\pi(a\mid s,g)`$, whereas a world model captures how
the environment responds, $`W(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)`$. When the world
model is wrong, even a capable agent may choose actions based on
incorrect predicted outcomes. Conversely, correcting an environmental
belief can improve decisions across multiple tasks without separately
updating a procedure or skill for each task. This raises a natural
question: _how can an LLM agent form, test, revise, and reuse beliefs
about its environment while keeping the underlying language model
frozen?_

A growing line of work has begun to adapt world models for LLM agents.
WALL-E and WALL-E 2.0 induce rules from collected trajectories to align
an LLM world model with the environment, and keep the rules that correct
the most prediction errors in the collected trajectories ([Zhou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22); [Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)). Imagine-then-plan
approaches such as ITP-I use offline-trained world models to simulate
future outcomes during planning, while keeping the world model itself
fixed ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)). WorldEvolver is closest to our
setting, updating world-model knowledge online from prediction
mismatches with a memory of past episodic transitions and semantic facts
([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib13)). These works demonstrate the value
of environment-specific world models for agents, but leave open three
challenges central to continual adaptation: validation, maintenance, and
application. Newly inferred rules must first be validated against
additional evidence before being trusted, and once established, they
must be maintained as the environment changes, by being revised,
narrowed, or retired when contradicted by new evidence. Finally,
validated beliefs should influence future predictions and decisions,
including tasks different from the one in which they were acquired.

We introduce EvoBelief, a self-evolving world model that addresses these
challenges through an explicit cycle of hypothesis formation,
validation, and revision
(Figure [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")).
When an outcome contradicts the model’s prediction, or when an unknown
matters for a decision, EvoBelief proposes falsifiable hypotheses about
the local environment dynamics involved. It tests these hypotheses
against later observations or through controlled experiments, promotes
evidence-supported hypotheses into reusable rules, and revises, narrows,
or retires rules when later evidence contradicts them. Only validated
rules are allowed to influence the agent’s future predictions and plans.

EvoBelief maintains a two-timescale belief representation, capturing
episode-local situational state ($`z_{t}`$) and persistent,
evidence-linked beliefs about environment dynamics ($`W_{t}`$). The
underlying LLM remains frozen, and all adaptation occurs in this
external world model, separating the model’s fixed parametric prior from
an environment-specific belief that evolves through test-time
interaction. This explicit representation also makes belief evolution
inspectable. Each rule is linked to the evidence that supports it, the
conditions under which it applies, and subsequent observations that may
challenge it. This design allows us to trace how a validated rule guided
a prediction or plan, and evaluate how learned beliefs transfer across
tasks, influence decisions, and adapt when the environment changes. Our
contributions are:

- •
  We introduce belief evolution as a complementary form of
  self-improvement for LLM agents, in which a persistent world model
  captures environment-specific beliefs that can evolve at test time
  through interaction and generalize across tasks without updating the
  underlying language model.
- •
  We develop a belief lifecycle for validation, maintenance, and
  application: hypotheses are validated before being trusted,
  established rules can be revised or retired as evidence changes, and
  validated beliefs are applied to relevant future predictions and
  decisions.
- •
  We evaluate EvoBelief on four benchmarks covering static and changing
  dynamics, and scientific discovery, showing improved task success,
  cross-task transfer of learned beliefs, adaptation to shifts in
  dynamics, and discovery of quantitative laws through active
  experimentation.

Figure 2: Verified belief revision closes the loop from experience to
later behavior. EvoBelief uses the current $`z_{t}`$ and $`W_{t}`$ to
predict action consequences. A grounded prediction mismatch or a
decision-critical unknown opens an evidence loop that forms falsifiable
hypotheses, gathers evidence, and verifies their scope. Only validated,
applicable rules guide predictions and plans.

## 2 Related Work

Belief states and world models. In a POMDP, the belief state is a
distribution over latent environment states ([Kaelbling et al.,
1998](#bib.bib1)), and predictive state representations characterize
state through predictions of future observations ([Littman and Sutton,
2001](#bib.bib2)). World models address the complementary question of
how actions change the world, from planning with learned dynamics to
recurrent and latent models ([Sutton, 1990](#bib.bib3); [Ha and
Schmidhuber, 2018](#bib.bib4); [Hafner et al., 2019](#bib.bib5); [Assran
et al., 2025](#bib.bib43); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib41)). Recent
language-agent work externalizes state uncertainty or maintains
linguistic beliefs under partial observability ([Singh et al.,
2026](#bib.bib24); [Murphy, 2026](#bib.bib25); [Jiang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib26); [Liao et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)), and the
belief-based world model of [Kumar et al. (2026)](#bib.bib44) exposes a
hand-specified belief over the current state to the policy but does not
learn it from interaction. EvoBelief separates the two roles across
timescales: $`z_{t}`$ estimates the current situation, and $`W_{t}`$ is
a persistent belief over local environment dynamics learned from
interaction. Related world-model agents plan with the model as a
simulator, inject world knowledge into planning, learn environment rules
from experience, or evolve transition memories ([Hao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib11); [Qiao et al., 2024](#bib.bib42); [Zhou et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22); [Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib23); [Liu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14); [Fang et al., 2025](#bib.bib12); [Zhang et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib13)); others explore curiosity-driven exploration,
deliberate environment probing, theory formation, and closed-loop
scientific discovery ([Pathak et al., 2017](#bib.bib36); [Levy et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37); [Song and Cai, 2026](#bib.bib38); [Kabra et al.,
2026](#bib.bib39); [Jin et al., 2026](#bib.bib40)), and interactive
benchmarks now require such discovery ([Wang et al., 2022](#bib.bib18);
[Jansen et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)). EvoBelief differs in that only
validated, scoped claims may change a prediction or a plan.

Experience, memory, and self-evolving agents. LLM agents improve from
experience through reflections, episodic memories, reusable lessons,
procedures, and skills ([Shinn et al., 2023](#bib.bib7); [Zhao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib8); [Park et al., 2023](#bib.bib9); [Wang et al.,
2023](#bib.bib10); [Ouyang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib28); [Ouyang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib29); [Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib48); [Ma et al.,
2026](#bib.bib46); [Shang and Yang, 2026](#bib.bib47)), and a broader
self-evolving literature modifies prompts, scaffolds, search procedures,
or agent code while keeping the model fixed ([He et al.,
2026](#bib.bib30); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib31); [Zhang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib32); [Robeyns et al., 2025](#bib.bib33); [Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib35); [Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib34)). ProPlay learns
procedural world models, HDSO optimizes skills through hypothesis-driven
testing, and AutoManual builds environment instruction manuals from
interaction ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib46); [Shang and Yang,
2026](#bib.bib47); [Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib48)). EvoBelief instead
maintains predictive claims about environment dynamics with explicit
conditions, evidence-supported scope, and counterexample-driven
revision. These approaches primarily change _how the agent acts_;
EvoBelief instead changes _what the agent believes about the deployed
environment_ and separates belief existence from behavioral authority,
which a learned claim gains only after independent validation and can
lose to later counterexamples. That lifecycle is also what continual
adaptation asks for when transition dynamics change and earlier
knowledge goes stale ([Xie et al., 2021](#bib.bib15); [Wołczyk et al.,
2021](#bib.bib16); [Kessler et al., 2023](#bib.bib17)).
Belief-calibrated optimization is closest in vocabulary ([Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib45)), but its beliefs concern how an evaluator responds to
edits of the agent’s own scaffold rather than the deployed environment’s
dynamics.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

At time $`t`$, an environment has latent state $`s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}`$,
emits an observation $`o_{t}\in\mathcal{O}`$, and transitions after
action $`a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}`$. A task is specified by goal $`g`$, and
the agent observes the interaction history
$`h_{t}=(o_{0},a_{0},o_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1},o_{t})`$. The environment may
contain local dynamics that disagree with the frozen language model’s
pretrained prior, and those dynamics may change during deployment. With
the model parameters $`\theta`$ fixed, EvoBelief maintains two
externalized states,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \mathbf{b}_{t}=(z_{t},W_{t}).
       ```                            |     | (1) |

Here $`z_{t}`$ answers the local question of what the agent currently
takes to be true about this situation. It records observed entities,
relations, quantities, and task progress, and is reset at each episode
boundary. $`W_{t}`$ answers the persistent question of how this
environment responds to actions. It survives episode resets and changes
only as new evidence is incorporated. The frozen LLM therefore supplies
a general prior, while $`W_{t}`$ stores environment-specific knowledge
learned during deployment.

### 3.2 World Knowledge as Testable Hypotheses

The basic unit of knowledge in $`W_{t}`$ is a falsifiable _hypothesis_
$`H`$ about an observable action consequence. We write its claim as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       H\mathrel{:=}(C\wedge A\Rightarrow Y),
       ```                                     |     | (2) |

which states that when condition $`C`$ holds, taking action or procedure
$`A`$ should produce observable consequence $`Y`$. This form makes the
claim directly testable, since one can establish $`C`$, execute $`A`$,
and check whether $`Y`$ occurs. Each hypothesis also stores the scope in
which it is supported, a recipe for testing it, and an evidence record.
$`A`$ may be a single action, a multi-step procedure, or a measurement
intervention, while $`Y`$ may be a state change or a measured quantity.

A hypothesis may contain both fixed entities and abstract object roles.
When it is used, those roles are bound to concrete objects (i.e.
bindings) in the current context, and broader scope is earned only from
evidence across varied bindings rather than assumed in advance. For
example, the claim that heating above about 80 ^(∘)C decomposes the
sample is first bound to compound X, and it covers other compounds only
after evidence from them.

Hypotheses are formed from the agent’s own interaction, starting from an
empty $`W_{0}`$. After an action, EvoBelief compares the realized
outcome with the prediction made before acting. A grounded _prediction
mismatch_ suggests that the current model of the environment is wrong,
and a _decision-critical unknown_, one whose resolution would change
what the agent should do next, suggests that it is incomplete. Because
one surprising outcome can have several explanations, EvoBelief may keep
_rival hypotheses_ until later evidence distinguishes them. In the
compound X example, decomposition could depend on temperature, heating
duration, or the vessel. Forming a hypothesis is not evidence for it. An
unvalidated hypothesis cannot justify a task-directed action as
established knowledge, but it may motivate an experiment. Details on
hypothesis identity and admissible evidence are in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Method Details ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents").

### 3.3 Testing Hypotheses with Passive and Active Evidence

Evidence can arrive in two ways. _Passive evidence_ comes from an
ordinary task transition that happens to test an existing hypothesis, as
when a later task heats compound X and records whether it remains
intact. _Active evidence_ comes from an _experiment_ run specifically to
resolve an uncertainty or distinguish among rival hypotheses. Both
routes update the same evidence record. Active experiments use live
environment actions and therefore consume the task’s interaction budget.
EvoBelief admits one only when its outcome can change a decision the
agent must make, no validated rule already resolves the question, and
the required setup is executable. Before the experiment runs, its
support and falsification criteria are fixed. The realized outcome then
settles the test as _supported_, _counterexampled_, or _inconclusive_.
An inconclusive test means that the required condition was not actually
witnessed, or that the outcome was missing or ambiguous, and it counts
as neither support nor counterevidence. Unresolved hypotheses remain
pending and can be tested later. Experiment admission and counting
details are in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Method Details ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents").

### 3.4 From Evidence to Authority: The Rule Lifecycle

Evidence determines what a hypothesis is allowed to do. We use
_authority_ to mean permission for a learned claim to guide a later
prediction or plan. A newly formed hypothesis has none. Permission to
reuse must be earned from independent evidence across episodes,
bindings, and tests. For example, one batch of compound X that
decomposed at 90 ^(∘)C is not enough, and the claim must hold again in a
later episode, on a different batch, before it may guide heating
decisions.

Let $`S`$ be the supports of a hypothesis, and let
$`N_{\mathrm{ep}}(S)`$, $`N_{\mathrm{bind}}(S)`$, and
$`N_{\mathrm{test}}(S)`$ count the distinct episodes, bindings, and
independent tests represented in those supports. A hypothesis is
committed to $`W_{t}`$ as a _validated rule_ only when

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathrm{Commit}(H)\Longleftrightarrow\bigl(N_{\mathrm{ep}}(S)\geq\tau_{\mathrm{ep}}\bigr)\wedge\bigl(N_{\mathrm{bind}}(S)\geq\tau_{\mathrm{bind}}\bigr)\wedge\bigl(N_{\mathrm{test}}(S)\geq\tau_{\mathrm{test}}\bigr).
``` |  | (3) |

The thresholds are set before any benchmark run, are never tuned on
benchmark results, and are listed in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Method Details ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents").
Each generalized role must vary across supporting bindings, and the
event that first formed a hypothesis can never by itself grant reusable
authority. Counterevidence can later remove that authority. EvoBelief
first narrows the rule to the scope still supported by its surviving
evidence and recomputes the commitment condition. If the surviving
evidence no longer satisfies
Eq. [3](#S3.E3 "In 3.4 From Evidence to Authority: The Rule Lifecycle ‣ 3 Method ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents"),
the rule is *demoted*. It loses its reusable authority but keeps its
evidence, so later support can restore it. A rule is *retired* and no
longer used only when repeated counterexamples leave it with no
supporting evidence. Because the evidence record is preserved, every
change in status can be traced to the observations that caused it. The
same lifecycle handles drift in environment dynamics. A previously
useful rule can lose authority under changed dynamics, while new
evidence supports a successor.

### 3.5 Using the World Model for Prediction and Planning

A validated rule matters only when it applies to the current situation.
At each decision, the agent first forms candidate actions $`A_{t}`$
without access to $`W_{t}`$. For each candidate $`a\in A_{t}`$, the
frozen predictor supplies an anchor prediction

|     |                                     |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       p_{t}^{0}(a)=F_{\theta}(a,g,z_{t}),
       ```                                  |     | (4) |

which captures what the agent would expect without self-learned world
knowledge.

EvoBelief then retrieves validated rules relevant to the candidate and
checks whether their conditions hold and whether the current binding
lies inside their evidence-supported scope. This check uses the state
estimate $`z_{t}`$ when assessing candidates and the state predicted
after each earlier step when working out a sequence of actions.
Retrieval alone has no effect. Only an applicable validated rule may
change the anchor prediction.

Those rule-supported consequences affect behavior in two ways. First,
they can revise the expected outcome of a candidate action, changing its
ranking or ruling it out when a required precondition is violated. For
example, once the decomposition rule for compound X is validated, it
changes the predicted outcome of heating X to 100 ^(∘)C, so that
candidate falls below a lower-temperature route. A rule whose condition
does not hold makes no prediction, so it cannot rule out an action by
itself. Second, compatible rules can be chained into a short plan when
the consequence of one rule establishes the condition of another, and
the agent can execute the plan’s first step even if it is not among the
candidates in $`A_{t}`$. A validated quantitative relation can likewise
set the parameter values needed to obtain a target consequence (for
example, heating compound X below 80 ^(∘)C, where it stays intact).

If no validated rule applies, EvoBelief falls back to the same
$`W`$-blind choice it would otherwise have made. If an unresolved claim
is decision-relevant, an admissible experiment may instead be selected
to test it
(§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Testing Hypotheses with Passive and Active Evidence ‣ 3 Method ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")).
Whenever a validated rule changes an executed action, EvoBelief records
the rule, the prediction or plan it changed, and the realized outcome. A
success caused by using the rule is recorded for analysis but does not
count as new independent support for the same rule.
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Language-Model Calls, Prompts, and Retrieval ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
gives the benchmark-specific interfaces and the model-call inventory.

### 4.1 Setup

We evaluate on four benchmarks spanning three deployment regimes:
ScienceWorld and ALFWorld (static, largely prior-aligned dynamics)
([Wang et al., 2022](#bib.bib18); [Shridhar et al., 2020](#bib.bib19)),
MiniGrid (hidden dynamics drift) ([Chevalier-Boisvert et al.,
2023](#bib.bib20)), and DiscoveryWorld (scientific discovery) ([Jansen
et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)). Splits, budgets, and configurations are in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Experimental Setup Details ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents").
We compare against ReAct, WorldEvolver, and *Frozen WM*. Frozen WM uses
the same $`W`$-blind actor and predictor as EvoBelief but lets no
learned rule guide a prediction or a plan. The comparison with Frozen WM
evaluates the complete EvoBelief system relative to this matched
prior-driven control. ALFWorld and ScienceWorld are evaluated without
and with actor-side transition memory, provided identically to every
method. EvoBelief runs without actor-side transition memory on MiniGrid
and DiscoveryWorld. Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.4 are the backbones used.
Within each comparison all methods share the benchmark interface, task
order, seeds, and budgets, and each task is attempted once.

#### Implementation.

On ALFWorld, MiniGrid, and ScienceWorld, action–effect rules are induced
from typed transitions and applied to predict state changes for planning
and veto, without a rule-conditioned model call. On DiscoveryWorld,
quantitative and categorical laws are fitted to measurements and
observations, and validated by a held-out prediction frozen before the
intervention. Only DiscoveryWorld’s frozen predictor engine, the fixed
prediction component shared with Frozen WM, makes rule-conditioned model
calls. EvoBelief uses the same role-level model interfaces on every
benchmark. Its representations and control components are fixed before
evaluation
(Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Language-Model Calls, Prompts, and Retrieval ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")).

### 4.2 Regime I: Static, Largely Prior-Aligned Environments

ScienceWorld and ALFWorld test whether belief evolution helps when the
environment is stationary and much of its dynamics remain compatible
with the pretrained prior.
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents"),
which also includes the world-model lookahead baseline ITP-I, shows that
EvoBelief has the highest task success rate in every setting shown, but
the route by which the belief layer helps differs across the two
environments.

On ALFWorld, persistent validated rules are the main behavioral route.
They correct local action mechanics that the prior gets wrong or leaves
underspecified, supply missing prerequisites, and prevent the agent from
repeating failed procedures. In the trace of
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.2 Regime I: Static, Largely Prior-Aligned Environments ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents"),
for example, the learned cooling rule changes the predicted consequence
of cooling, inserts the missing move to the fridge, and completes the
task.

Because $`W_{t}`$ persists across tasks, generic household rules learned
early are reused across later task families, while process-specific
rules such as clean, heat, and cool are reused across new object
bindings within their supported family
(Tables [8](#A6.T8 "Table 8 ‣ ALFWorld: rules travel across tasks. ‣ F.3 Regime I: Static-Environment Details ‣ Appendix F Additional Results ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
and [9](#A6.T9 "Table 9 ‣ ALFWorld: rules travel across tasks. ‣ F.3 Regime I: Static-Environment Details ‣ Appendix F Additional Results ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")).
Across the four ALFWorld configurations, over 80% of the decisions under
a validated rule fall in a family other than the one where the rule was
validated
(Table [8](#A6.T8 "Table 8 ‣ ALFWorld: rules travel across tasks. ‣ F.3 Regime I: Static-Environment Details ‣ Appendix F Additional Results ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")).
This reuse also reduces repeated exploration, yielding more successes
with fewer decisions and model calls
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2 Regime I: Static, Largely Prior-Aligned Environments ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")).
On ALFWorld, EvoBelief’s margin over Frozen WM is largest without
transition memory for both backbones and remains positive with it,
consistent with episodic recall and explicit world rules supplying
partly overlapping cross-episode information.

ScienceWorld exercises the same belief lifecycle but through a different
route. Live experiments account for most direct behavioral intervention,
while validated rules guide a smaller number of later decisions. The
experiments resolve decision-relevant unknowns using the same evidence

transitions, and how it changes behavior in each environment. In

## 5 Conclusion & Discussion

Agent self-improvement need not begin by changing the agent. When
failures come from misunderstanding the world, the missing component is
an environment belief that evidence can challenge. EvoBelief keeps that
belief as falsifiable hypotheses, promotes them into rules only through
independent evidence, and lets counterexamples revoke that authority
when the environment drifts. Validated rules guide predictions and
plans, while unresolved hypotheses can motivate live experiments. Across
the evaluated regimes, the results demonstrate improved task
performance, reuse of learned rules, adaptation to hidden dynamics
changes, and quantitative law discovery, while keeping the belief state
open to inspection. Where episodic memory already supplies what the
prior lacks, rules and memory act partly as substitutes. Because a
validated rule describes the environment rather than a task, one
verified correction can improve many tasks at once, including tasks the
agent has never seen. Retiring stale rules complements learning new
ones, because the ability to withdraw authority keeps accumulated
knowledge an asset rather than a liability when the environment changes.
More broadly, language models make hypotheses cheap to generate but not
to verify, so an agent should act on a belief only after it has survived
a test it could have failed. Carrying this discipline to multimodal,
embodied, and real-world software agents is a next step.

## Appendix A Limitations and Future Work

Our experiments use text-based environments, where transitions can be
logged and predictions checked when the required outcomes are
observable. The belief lifecycle itself is not tied to text, and
extending it to multimodal, embodied, and real-world software agents is
a natural next step. Such settings bring stochastic effects, which call
for statistical tests in place of count thresholds when committing a
rule. They also involve interventions that can be costly or
irreversible, where live experiments would run under explicit risk
limits or human approval. When episodic memory already supplies what the
prior lacks, it overlaps with explicit beliefs, so learning when to rely
on each is a promising direction. Finally, because validated beliefs
carry their evidence records, they could be shared across agents or
distilled into model weights, turning what one agent has verified into
the prior of the next.

## Appendix B Method Details

This appendix collects implementation-level mechanisms that
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 World Knowledge as Testable Hypotheses ‣ 3 Method ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")–§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Testing Hypotheses with Passive and Active Evidence ‣ 3 Method ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
reference but do not require for the main argument.

#### Hypothesis identity and the evidence ledger.

Hypothesis identity is goal-free: a canonical signature over $`(C,A,Y)`$
merges rediscoveries of the same claim across different tasks and
evidence polarities, so evidence gathered under one goal accrues to the
same hypothesis when it resurfaces under another. The per-hypothesis
evidence record of
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 World Knowledge as Testable Hypotheses ‣ 3 Method ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
is implemented as an append-only *evidence ledger*; entries are never
rewritten, each entry retains the episode, binding, and test it came
from, and every lifecycle transition cites the ledger entries that
caused it. Hypotheses cover action-conditioned transitions, object
properties and measurement procedures, multi-step procedures, and
conditional constraints, the negative counterpart that says what an
action *cannot* do while its condition is witnessed.

#### Evidence admissibility.

Transitions whose outcome reflects the harness rather than the
environment, such as parser rejections, interface errors, and transport
failures, are excluded from hypothesis formation and from settlement. A
prediction mismatch is admissible only when it is grounded in the
realized environment observation itself, not in the agent’s own summary
of it.

#### Discriminating tests and counting.

Rival hypotheses are settled against the same realized evidence, each
against the prediction it froze before the test ran, so one measurement
can support one rival and counterexample the others. An outcome that
every rival predicts discriminates nothing and supports no conditional
claim. All readouts of one experimental design count as a single
independent test, so a multi-step experiment cannot inflate the support
counts of the commitment gate in
§[3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 From Evidence to Authority: The Rule Lifecycle ‣ 3 Method ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents").
Per-episode budgets bound hypothesis formation, the number of
experiments, and the steps they may spend, and a hypothesis formed in
episode $`k`$ can be validated only by an independent later occurrence.

#### Experiment admission.

Beyond the admission conditions of
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Testing Hypotheses with Passive and Active Evidence ‣ 3 Method ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents"),
an experiment is admitted only when at least two plausible outcomes
would lead to different next actions and no public readout already
answers the question. Admitted experiments are executed in the order in
which their questions become decision-relevant, and an experiment whose
question is resolved by other evidence is closed without running.

#### Commitment thresholds.

Every instantiation fixes the thresholds of
Eq. [3](#S3.E3 "In 3.4 From Evidence to Authority: The Rule Lifecycle ‣ 3 Method ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
before evaluation and never tunes them on a target. Reuse across
episodes requires supports on at least two distinct bindings in at least
two distinct episodes, at least one of which is independent of any
decision the hypothesis itself influenced. Where a task instance is a
single long episode, as in DiscoveryWorld, a hypothesis is committed by
one pre-registered held-out test whose prediction was frozen before the
test ran.

## Appendix C Language-Model Calls, Prompts, and Retrieval

#### Division of labor.

The framework in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
specifies a shared belief lifecycle, and the four benchmarks instantiate
that lifecycle through different structured interfaces. The frozen
language model proposes task actions, supplies anchor predictions, and,
where needed, extracts typed observations. The benchmark adapter turns
public transitions or measurements into the hypothesis representation
used by $`W_{t}`$, compiles admissible tests, and enforces evidence
accounting, commitment, applicability, retirement, and rule-based
planning. Thus the rule lifecycle and its authority checks are
implemented outside the actor rather than delegated to a prompt. On
ALFWorld, MiniGrid, and ScienceWorld, rule-supported consequences are
computed directly from the structured representation. DiscoveryWorld is
the only benchmark whose frozen predictor engine may also make a
rule-conditioned model call. The actor itself never receives the
validated rule text.

#### Benchmark adapters.

ALFWorld makes exactly two calls per decision, the actor draft and one
batched anchor-prediction call over the whole candidate slate (6,172
logical calls over 3,086 environment steps in the sealed Haiku run with
transition memory, a ratio of 2.000 in every episode); its candidates

evidence-analysis call per transition, about 8.5 calls per decision in a

actor prompt unchanged and averages 5.02 logical calls per executed

MiniGrid, 1,536 on ScienceWorld, and 8,192 on DiscoveryWorld; hypothesis
proposal 1,536) and a 60,000-character input ceiling per role.

#### Knowledge judge (DiscoveryWorld).

The benchmark’s own scoring prompt, used unchanged after the episode: it
receives the task description, the agent’s knowledge, and one critical
question, and answers 1 or 0 with an explanation; the benchmark asks two
critical questions per task instance, one call each. For EvoBelief the
agent’s knowledge is the deterministic rendering of the store, in which
a statement is listed as established only if its prediction was frozen
before the test, the realized evidence settled it as passed, and no
counterexample stands. The rendered knowledge never names an arm, and
every prompt is checked for that before dispatch.

### C.3 Actor-Side Transition Memory

The setting “with transition memory” installs one shared factor on the
actor of every method. Before the actor is asked for an action, the
factor retrieves at most four realized public transitions from earlier
episodes, ranked by lexical overlap between the current goal and public
state and the stored goal and pre-state, with a per-item limit of 1,200
characters and a total budget of 4,800. Only transitions of completed
earlier episodes are retrievable, every realized transition is stored
regardless of its outcome, no outcome label enters the store, and the
candidate action never enters the query. Each item is rendered as its
goal, the observation before, the action, the observation after, and the
public delta, under the header Method-visible memory: (Cross-episode
public transition memory (previous episodes only): on ALFWorld), spliced
into the actor prompt between the action interface and the history. The
factor reads no rule, no store, and no arm name, and a run refuses to
start if its budget differs from the frozen one. On MiniGrid the
EvoBelief runs use the setting without memory.

## Appendix E Experimental Setup Details

#### Benchmarks.

The static, prior-aligned regime uses ScienceWorld ([Wang et al.,
2022](#bib.bib18)), a text-based science laboratory whose mechanics are
stationary but partially hidden (90 episodes per method), and ALFWorld
([Shridhar et al., 2020](#bib.bib19)), a household suite testing beliefs
about object state, affordances, and procedures (the 134 unseen test
tasks, each run once per method). The dynamic regime uses MiniGrid
([Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2023](#bib.bib20)), where the agent must
identify the key that actually opens a locked door, cross into the far
room, and lift a target box within 24 steps
(Figures [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.3 Regime II: Hidden Dynamics Drift ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
and [8](#A6.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ Regime II details. ‣ F.4 Regime II: Drift Details ‣ Appendix F Additional Results ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
describe the families). A seed fixes the sequence of layouts of a
stream: key, agent, and box locations are re-sampled every episode, with
the first three episodes on the three fixed layouts of the original
benchmark. The campaign runs MG-C and MG-M on three development seeds,
on three held-out seeds fixed before the runs, on the same held-out
seeds with every color and room name permuted in the observations, and
on a third seed set reserved for the mechanism ablations, giving 240
matched episodes per method per set; MG-R runs on three further fresh
seeds, 120 matched episodes per method. The discovery regime uses
DiscoveryWorld ([Jansen et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)) on two themes. In
Reactor Lab, each reactor activates only when tuned to within
$`\pm 2`$ Hz of a resonance frequency governed by a hidden parametric
law of a crystal property measurable through instruments, so the
held-out resonance of a new reactor cannot be retrieved from memory and
must be computed from a fitted law. In Plant Nutrients, a field’s soil
controller sets nutrient levels, a planted seed grows only under the
hidden nutrient rule, and the Challenge tier hides that rule in a
logical combination of two nutrients. Both themes run at their Normal
and Challenge tiers on GPT-5.4: twelve fresh task instances per tier,
frozen before any run, four methods on the same task instances, one
attempt each (provider-side failures were re-run under the pre-declared
transport policy), 320 environment actions and 1,650 model calls per
task instance, and no transition memory. On Reactor Lab, EvoBelief’s
experiments draw on a science budget of 208 of the 320 actions.
Completion and the subgoal-based procedural score are the benchmark’s
own, and Knowledge is the benchmark’s LLM-judged score over two sealed
critical questions, judged blind to the method with one judge
configuration shared by both themes. The benchmark’s own Hypothesizer
agent is included as a baseline; its rows in
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.4 Regime III: Scientific Discovery ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
come from a separately frozen re-run whose only change is a raised
output ceiling
(Appendix [F.5](#A6.SS5 "F.5 Regime III: Discovery Details ‣ Appendix F Additional Results ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")).
A frozen-belief diagnostic and the Plant Nutrients protocol and
mechanism are reported in
Appendix [F.5](#A6.SS5 "F.5 Regime III: Discovery Details ‣ Appendix F Additional Results ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents").

#### Actor settings.

ALFWorld and ScienceWorld run under two actor settings, without and with
*transition memory*; on MiniGrid the EvoBelief runs use the setting
without it; no method uses it on DiscoveryWorld. Transition memory is
specified in
Appendix [C.3](#A3.SS3 "C.3 Actor-Side Transition Memory ‣ Appendix C Language-Model Calls, Prompts, and Retrieval ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents").

#### Baselines.

ReAct keeps no persistent cross-episode belief ([Yao et al.,
2022](#bib.bib6)). ITP-I consults imagine-then-plan lookahead with a
learned world model ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib14)). WorldEvolver
evolves a world model online over stored transitions and semantic
memory, a closely related self-evolving world-model baseline ([Zhang et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib13)). WorldEvolver and ITP-I are the official
released implementations, run through the same harness, interface, and
budgets as every other method. WALL-E 2.0’s published ALFWorld result
uses a different rule-learning protocol and backbone and is not directly
comparable to our single-pass benchmark evaluation. Frozen WM is the
same W-blind actor with the same anchor predictor and selector, but its
predictions and choices follow the pretrained prior alone: no learned
rule may alter a prediction, a ranking, or a plan. It does not run
EvoBelief’s belief store. Frozen WM and EvoBelief are independent live
runs paired by task, not replays. For rule-guided interventions we
record the rule, the prediction or plan it changed, and the realized
outcome. The paired comparison with Frozen WM evaluates the complete
EvoBelief system, including its experiments, relative to the matched
prior-driven control.

#### Protocol and fairness.

WorldEvolver’s original evaluation reports best-of-$`L`$ success over
$`L{=}5`$ trials per task, so a task counts as solved when any one of
the five trials succeeds ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib13)). In the
main benchmark comparisons, each task is attempted once. All methods,
including the baselines we rerun, share one harness with the same task
set, task order, seeds, action interface, context limits, retry policy,
and scoring. Both backbones run the identical protocol, and success is
task completion as scored by the benchmark. On ScienceWorld with
GPT-5.4, EvoBelief’s run without transition memory was preregistered,
executed blind, and sealed first. The Frozen WM and WorldEvolver
comparators, and then all three runs with transition memory, were
authorized afterwards and executed blind on the same 90 tasks, each
group unblinded once.

#### Metrics and controls.

MiniGrid is split at the hidden changepoint into pre-drift and
post-drift halves. Mechanism diagnostics count rules formed, tested,
supported, counterexampled, and retired, and *decision flips*, decisions
where the anchor prediction alone would have selected differently and a
validated rule’s authorized change is the recorded cause. Held-out
structured state-change prediction uses an identical frozen prediction
interface for every method. The frozen predictor engine that supplies
the anchor prediction, shared with the Frozen WM control, also runs
sandboxed tests of its own typed rules on ScienceWorld and ALFWorld.
These tests do not replace live task actions, and their outcomes do not
enter EvoBelief’s store.

### F.3 Regime I: Static-Environment Details

#### Regime I details.

On ALFWorld the gain concentrates on the task families whose procedures
the prior misstates, above all cooling, heating, and two-object
placement, where a wrong belief about what an action does, or about the
order in which two actions must be taken, cannot be repaired by recall
alone. Over the deployment
(Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.2 Regime I: Static, Largely Prior-Aligned Environments ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")),
the curves begin to separate after the first rules are committed. The
generic household dynamics, that reaching a receptacle reveals its
contents, that taking requires an empty hand, that placing moves an
object, are committed within the first task type and consumed in every
later one, so each new type begins with most of its dynamics already
validated; the one process rule a type adds, cooling, heating, or
cleaning, is committed within its first few tasks and reused on the
rest. The cumulative gap therefore widens with deployment, fastest on
the process types where the prior misstates the procedure. A validated
rule removes the exploratory and repeated actions the prior spends on
rediscovering a precondition it has already met, so EvoBelief solves
more tasks with fewer decisions and fewer model calls, whereas
WorldEvolver pays for its world model with far more calls than the
agents that consult none. Because the benchmark enumerates its task
types in blocks, later episodes are also harder for the prior: over the
second half of the deployment, where the enumeration reaches the process
types, Frozen WM solves fewer tasks than in the first half while
EvoBelief solves more. This is why the comparison is drawn on matched
tasks: the widening is measured against the same agent on the same task,
not against an easier past. Of the 1,115 decisions a validated rule
changed across the four settings, 907 had their predicted effect fully
confirmed in the next observation. The store classified 199 outcomes as
inconclusive: the go-to rule predicted arrival at a receptacle and a
description of its contents, the agent arrived, and the receptacle
turned out to be closed, so the second atom was unobservable. In the
remaining 9, the environment refused the action, and the store recorded
the refusal as evidence against the rule’s condition or as an
inconclusive refusal. None of the 1,115 was classified as an effect
mismatch.

### F.4 Regime II: Drift Details

#### Regime II details.

The two families MG-C and MG-M were designed so that the solution
requires one step the pretrained prior does not expect, and the 24-step
budget leaves no room to discover it by chance. The same prior-governed
agent is competent where the environment mostly agrees with its prior
and helpless where one hidden rule contradicts it, so the near-zero rows
of MG-C and MG-M in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4.3 Regime II: Hidden Dynamics Drift ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
measure the hidden rules, not a weak baseline. In the first episode of
the development stream on MG-C (seed 256), ReAct picks up the
color-matched red key, toggles the door three times without effect,
cycles through the other keys, opens the door with the blue key on its
fourteenth step, enters the far room, and then fails three lifts in a
row because the door it opened is still open; it never closes it. On
MG-M the same agent unlocks the door on its fifteenth step, sees it
closed but unlocked, and spends the remaining steps trying to walk
through it. The frozen predictor does not help here: it predicts with
the same prior the actor drafts from, so it expects the decoy key to
work just as the actor does and never overrules the draft, and all
prior-governed methods share a single success. MG-R is the control for
this design: it keeps the decoy key and the silent switch, so the prior
stops at about half of the episodes, whereas EvoBelief reaches a similar
level on all three families, so the learned rules erase a difficulty gap
that the prior cannot. On MG-R EvoBelief loses nothing before the
switch, the two agents are level only in the first episodes after it,
and EvoBelief pulls furthest ahead in the late post-switch episodes once
the successor rule is committed, so the gain is not an artifact of a
floor. On the development streams of
Figure [6](#S4.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 4.3 Regime II: Hidden Dynamics Drift ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents"),
the last dozen episodes of every stream are at or above their pre-switch
level, and Frozen WM uses the full budget of 24 decisions in every
episode and makes 178 model calls per episode against 98 for EvoBelief.
Every method runs under the same harness, budget, seeds, and step limit.

the held-out seeds, their label permutation, the mechanism seeds, and

#### Experiments double as exploration on MiniGrid.

In the shadow variant of
Table [11](#A6.T11 "Table 11 ‣ Regime II details. ‣ F.4 Regime II: Drift Details ‣ Appendix F Additional Results ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
the belief forms, tests, and validates rules exactly as in the full
method and the planner computes the same plans, but no plan step is
executed: every executed action comes from the prior or from an
experiment. In episode 7 of MG-C seed 797 twelve of its fourteen
decisions are experiment steps, which pick up the working key, open the
door, close it again from the far room, and leave the agent one drop
away from the box; the prior’s draft finishes the task. In episode 16
the full method spends three experiment steps and then executes a
four-step committed plan, entering the far room, closing the door,
dropping the key, and lifting the box, while the shadow variant needs
twelve decisions, eleven of them experiment steps, and succeeds only
because the prior’s last draft coincides with the plan step it was not
allowed to take. The value of executing committed rules is therefore
concentrated on MG-C, whose solution is a chain no single experiment
completes, and it also shortens episodes and lowers cost. Acting on
rules at their first supported instance, without the independent
confirmation the commitment gate demands, leaves success and the rate of
failed authorized steps close to the full method’s on this benchmark,
whose public transitions are deterministic; we report it as a null
result rather than as support for the gate.

#### One rule’s life, across the silent changepoint.

The stream on MG-C seed 749 shows the lifecycle doing what drift
demands. In episode 5 an active experiment validates a first door rule,
that toggling opens the door while the blue key is held; the same
episode refutes it, because the rule ignored the door’s own state, and
it is retired within three decisions. In episode 6 an experiment
validates its successor, which conditions on the door being locked and
on where the other keys lie, and that rule carries every plan through
episode 20. The switch makes it fail at the first decision of episodes
21 and 22; two refutations in distinct episodes open a new evidence
epoch for the claim, releasing the pre-switch evidence, and success
resumes in episode 24 on experiment and prior steps. In episode 26 an
active experiment validates the post-switch successor, that toggling
opens the door while the green key is held, which then carries the
remaining plans. No grant is ever archival: authority is always backed
by currently surviving evidence, so the first generation does not
survive its own counterexamples and the successor earns commitment with
freshly built supports. On MG-M the same machinery commits the two-stage
door as two rules, that the working key unlocks the door and that an
empty-handed toggle then opens it, and relearns the first of them after
the switch.

#### Plant Nutrients: protocol.

The theme’s hidden rule is categorical. A first version of the Plant
Nutrients instantiation was run on six fresh task instances under a
pre-declared performance gate and failed it: it completed no task
instance, although its rules had recovered the hidden nutrient rule on
every Normal task instance and a table consistent with the hidden
combination on every Challenge task instance. The failure had three
named causes. A correct rule was retired by a counterexample that was
not one, because growth also requires a seed in the tile and seeds
inside tiles are not published; the validated rule set the soil
controller but did not carry the rest of the planting chain, which the
actor took without the rule; and the knowledge surface described what
appeared on the soil rather than what a planted seed would do, which the
judge did not credit. The version reported in
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.4 Regime III: Scientific Discovery ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ EvoBelief: Falsifiable World Models for Self-Improving LLM Agents")
is a revised implementation that addresses those three failure modes. A
counterexample by absence is admitted only after the plan has publicly
created the causal opportunity, that is, after the deposit, the refill,
and a pre-declared number of maturation ticks; a validated rule controls
the whole plan, from configuring the controller through binding,
acquiring and depositing the seed, waiting for maturation, and
observing; and the knowledge surface renders the causal chain and is
scoped to the task instance. Development used only task instances
previously used in the pilot. The code was then frozen and run once on
the twelve Normal and twelve Challenge task instances of a fresh set
that had been enumerated blind before the pilot ran and never executed
by any method. One repair, the identification of the actuator on a
previously used development Challenge instance, was implemented after
the development checks passed and was re-validated offline and on one
previously used development instance; the pilot is never pooled with the
reported result.

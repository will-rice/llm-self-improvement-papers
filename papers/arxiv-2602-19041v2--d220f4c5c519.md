###### Abstract

A recurring challenge in preference fine-tuning (PFT) is handling
intransitive (i.e., cyclic) preferences. Intransitive preferences often
stem from either (i) inconsistent rankings along a single objective or
(ii) scalarizing multiple objectives into a single metric. Regardless of
their source, the downstream implication of intransitive preferences is
the same: there is no well-defined optimal policy, breaking a core
assumption of the standard PFT pipeline. In response, we propose a
novel, game-theoretic solution concept, the Maximum Entropy Blackwell
Winner (MaxEntBW), that is well-defined under multi-objective
intransitive preferences. To enable computing MaxEntBWs at scale, we
derive PROSPER: a provably efficient PFT algorithm. Unlike prior
self-play techniques, PROSPER directly handles multiple objectives
without requiring scalarization. We then apply PROSPER to the problem of
fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) from multi-objective
LLM-as-a-Judge feedback (e.g., rubric-based judges), a setting where
both sources of intransitivity arise. We find that PROSPER outperforms
all baselines considered across both instruction following and general
chat benchmarks, releasing trained model checkpoints at the
[7B](https://huggingface.co/MisDrifter/Qwen2.5-7B-PROSPER) and
[3B](https://huggingface.co/MisDrifter/Qwen2.5-3B-PROSPER) parameter
scales.

## 1 Introduction

Author Ted Chiang famously described large language models (LLMs) as a
“blurry JPEG of the web” ([Chiang, 2023](#bib.bib11)). One way of
interpreting his words is that after being trained on vast swathes of
internet text, the choices of an LLM “judge” ([Zheng et al.,
2023](#bib.bib66)) are often inconsistent. Thus, when LLMs are used to
rank a set of options $`\{A,B,C\}`$, they often exhibit intransitive
(i.e., cyclic) preferences where they rank $`A\succ B`$ and
$`B\succ C`$, but $`A\not\succ C`$ ([Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib61)).

By construction, there is no total ordering over options under
intransitive preferences, as every option loses to another. Thus, the
standard preference fine-tuning (PFT) pipeline ([Stiennon et al.,
2020](#bib.bib48); [Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib36); [Song et al.,
2024](#bib.bib46); [Swamy et al., 2026](#bib.bib51)), in which one
attempts to learn such a total ordering via training a reward model, is
fundamentally unequipped to robustly handle the intransitive preferences
we see when attempting to learn from noisy LLM judge feedback
([Procaccia and Rosenschein, 2006](#bib.bib37); [Boutilier et al.,
2012](#bib.bib7); [Sorensen et al., 2024](#bib.bib47); [Gölz et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)).

In response, prior work has primarily explored two mitigatory
strategies. First, to reduce the baseline inconsistency of LLM judge
feedback, recent work has proposed to ask the LLM judge to explicitly
evaluate candidate responses along multiple objectives, rather than
making a single aggregate judgment ([Viswanathan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib53); [Gunjal et al., 2025](#bib.bib22)). This echoes
observations from psychology, where one of the most common root causes
of intransitivity is scalarization of multiple objectives into a single
metric ([Tversky, 1969](#bib.bib52)).

Second, to tackle the remaining per-objective inconsistency, a variety
of authors have proposed to frame PFT as zero-sum game solving ([Munos
et al., 2023](#bib.bib34); [Swamy et al., 2024](#bib.bib50); [Rosset
et al., 2024](#bib.bib42)), building on ideas from social choice theory
on how to robustly handle intransitive preferences ([Kreweras,
1965](#bib.bib29); [Fishburn, 1984](#bib.bib18)). Intuitively, these
approaches attempt to find a policy that the LLM judge prefers as often
as possible against the worst-case comparator – a well-defined notion
even under intransitive preferences.

Given the complementary nature of these two lines of work, one may
naturally hope to combine them into a single, gestalt solution. However
doing so is technically challenging for a rather subtle reason: when one
splits an aggregate judgement into multiple objectives, the Minimax
Theorem ([von Neumann, 1928](#bib.bib54)) no longer holds, as famously
observed by [Blackwell (1956)](#bib.bib6). The downstream implication of
this fact is that standard game-solving machinery (e.g., in the proofs
of [Swamy et al. (2024)](#bib.bib50)) breaks down, making it unclear how
to directly extend prior algorithms that build on said machinery to the
multi-objective PFT setting.

Faced with this roadblock, we circle back and ask a first-principles
question about the multi-objective PFT setting: what should it mean to
do well when evaluated under multiple objectives, each of which may be
globally inconsistent?

Our answer is the Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner (MaxEntBW): a policy
that is preferred to all other policies within its local neighborhood,
regardless of the criteria it is judged under. Remarkably, even though
it is a strictly more general solution concept than those explored in
prior work, the underlying structure of MaxEntBWs means one can
efficiently compute them without requiring the Minimax Theorem to hold.
Although MaxEntBWs are defined via a game between two LLM players, their
structure permits an efficient reduction to a single-player optimization
problem: only the learner’s strategy must be maintained and updated, and
the update can be implemented via square-loss regression. Thus, we can
capitalize on the more accurate feedback we get from LLM judges by
asking them to explicitly evaluate responses along multiple objectives
without needing to complicate the policy optimization pipeline. We call
our algorithm PROSPER, for PReference-based Optimization with a Single
Player over Entire Rubrics. Explicitly, our contributions are threefold:

## 2 Related Work

Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning. A variety of approaches to
multi-objective PFT have been explored in the literature, including
minimax game formulations ([Chakraborty et al., 2024](#bib.bib10)),
distance-based optimization ([Wang et al., 2024a](#bib.bib55); [Xiong
and Singh, 2025](#bib.bib60)), policy aggregation ([Zhou et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib68); [A Alamdari et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Heymann,
2025](#bib.bib27)), distributional reward modeling ([Siththaranjan
et al., 2023](#bib.bib45); [Halpern et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)), explicit
multi-objective reward modeling ([Rame et al., 2023](#bib.bib40); [Wang
et al., 2024b](#bib.bib56)), multi-objective reinforcement learning
([Zhong et al., 2024](#bib.bib67); [Wang et al., 2024c](#bib.bib57)),
and multi-objective direct preference optimization ([Chidambaram et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12); [Zhou et al., 2024b](#bib.bib70); [Shirali et al.,
2025](#bib.bib44); [Ramesh et al., 2024](#bib.bib41)). However, all of
these approaches implicitly assume that along each objective,
preferences are transitive and there is a well-defined optimal policy,
making it difficult to directly apply them to the more general setting
we study in our work.

Intransitive Preferences in Preference Fine-Tuning. Drawing on ideas
from social choice theory, several authors have pointed out that care is
required when learning from intransitive, aggregate preferences to avoid
distorting the preferences of the underlying population of raters
([Conitzer et al., 2024](#bib.bib14); [Gölz et al., 2025](#bib.bib21);
[Maura-Rivero et al., 2025](#bib.bib33); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32);
[Xiao et al., 2025](#bib.bib59)). However, even when preference feedback
is sourced from an LLM rather than a population of raters,
intransitivity still frequently arises ([Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib61)),
and can therefore stymie policy training. To deal with the fact that
there is no well-defined optimal policy under intransitive preferences,
a variety of authors have proposed to reframe PFT as a zero-sum game, in
which one attempts to find the policy that does the best against its
worst-case comparator ([Munos et al., 2023](#bib.bib34); [Swamy et al.,
2024](#bib.bib50); [Rosset et al., 2024](#bib.bib42); [Sutawika et al.,
2026](#bib.bib49)). These ideas build on a rich history of similar
techniques in the social choice theory ([Kreweras, 1965](#bib.bib29);
[Fishburn, 1984](#bib.bib18)) and bandit ([Yue et al.,
2012](#bib.bib63); [Dudík et al., 2015](#bib.bib17)) literature. [Wu
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib58) extend the two-player Nash-equilibrium
formulation to the multi-player setting. While promising, all of these
approaches assume the learner will be evaluated along a single
objective. In contrast, we focus on the multi-objective setting,
requiring nontrivial algorithmic innovation.

More closely to our work, several authors have explored handling
multiple, potentially intransitive preferences. [Bhatia et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib5) pioneered the theoretical study of multi-dimensional
intransitive preferences by proposing the Blackwell Winner, but stopped
short of deriving a scalable algorithm capable of training modern LLMs
as we do. One of the key technical innovations of the MaxEntBW is the
use of entropy regularization to avoid the adversarial training [Bhatia
et al. (2020)](#bib.bib5)’s solution concept implicitly requires.
[Agnihotri et al. (2025)](#bib.bib2) take a constrained optimization
perspective on multi-objective PFT, but essentially sidestep
intransitivity by only optimizing the preference against a fixed
reference policy, reducing the problem to standard multi-objective RL.
In contrast, we directly confront the challenges of multiple
intransitive preferences. Perhaps most similarly to our work, [Gupta
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib23) consider optimizing against multiple judges
to deal with poor out-of-distribution judge generalization, deriving a
scalable algorithm via the use of a variational relaxation. We implement
a similar algorithm as one of our baselines and find that it performs
worse than our proposed algorithm, PROSPER.

RL from AI Feedback. Our work falls within a growing body of research
that leverages AI-generated feedback to guide reinforcement learning. In
particular, we elicit AI feedback from judge models that are fed
collections of rubrics ([Cui et al., 2023](#bib.bib15); [Gunjal et al.,
2025](#bib.bib22); [Viswanathan et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)), similar to
the “constitutional AI” paradigm used by frontier labs ([Bai et al.,
2022](#bib.bib3)). Among rubric-based methods, [Cui et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib15) evaluate responses using four global criteria rather
than prompt-specific checklists, an approach shown to be less effective
([Viswanathan et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)), and [Gunjal et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib22) focus on the RLVR setting, whereas our work targets
general chat and instruction following. Consequently, we primarily
compare against [Viswanathan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib53) in our work.
While [Viswanathan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib53) employ prompt-specific
checklists, they scalarize these per-item rewards into a single score
with LLM-generated weights, which can lead to intransitivity. In
contrast, we dynamically reweight checklist items based on where the
current model is weakest and use game-theoretic techniques to more
robustly handle per-item intransitivity. We find that these differences
lead to better model performance on both instruction following and
general chat benchmarks.

## 3 Back to Blackwell: A New Solution Concept for Multi-Objective PFT

### 3.1 Preliminaries

We begin by discussing relevant background information.

Notation. Let $`\mathcal{X}`$ be the space of prompts and
$`\mathcal{Y}`$ the space of responses. We consider a class of language
models (policies) $`\Pi\subseteq\{\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\}`$,
which we assume is convex and compact. For every prompt
$`x\in\mathcal{X}`$, a policy $`\pi\in\Pi`$ induces a distribution over
responses $`\pi(x)\in\Delta(Y)`$.

We assume access to a multi-objective pairwise judge,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{P}:\ \mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\;\to[0,1]^{m(x)},
``` |  | (1) |

which compares responses $`y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}`$
along $`m(x)`$ objectives, returning a vector of size $`m(x)`$. Rather
than assuming a fixed set of criteria for every prompt (e.g.,
helpfulness, harmlessness), this formulation accounts for the diversity
of possible prompts fed to today’s LLMs: each prompt may be associated
with different (and different numbers of) relevant criteria. For each
$`k\in[m(x)]`$, $`\mathcal{P}^{k}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)`$ denotes the
probability that $`y\succ y^{\prime}`$, conditioned on prompt $`x`$ and
based on criterion $`k`$. We emphasize that we make no assumptions about
the transitivity of preferences along each objective. With a slight
abuse of notation, we can extend this to a preference function between
two policies $`\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi\times\Pi`$:
$`\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\!\triangleq\!\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim\pi\left(x\right),\ y^{\prime}\sim\pi^{\prime}\left(x\right)\end{subarray}}\!\left[\mathcal{P}\left(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x\right)\right]\!\in\!\left[0,1\right]^{m(x)}.`$

von Neumann Winner. Under intransitive preferences, there is often no
well-defined optimal policy (more formally, no Condorcet Winner, [Brandt
et al. (2016)](#bib.bib8)), as every response $`y`$ loses to some other
response $`y^{\prime}`$. Thus, we need to consider alternative solution
concepts. In the single-objective setting, a popular choice is the
Maximal Lottery ([Kreweras, 1965](#bib.bib29); [Fishburn,
1984](#bib.bib18)) or von Neumann Winner ([Dudík et al.,
2015](#bib.bib17)), which is the policy that is as preferred as possible
against its worst-case opponent. More formally, a von Neumann Winner is
a Nash Equilibrium of the zero-sum game with $`\mathcal{P}`$ as the
payoff matrix. Due to the symmetry of the payoff matrix, simple
self-play is provably efficient for computing such equilibria ([Swamy
et al., 2024](#bib.bib50)).

Blackwell Winner. A Blackwell Winner [Bhatia et al. (2020)](#bib.bib5)
is the natural extension of the von Neumann Winner to the
multi-objective setting, drawing on Blackwell’s notion of a target set
([Blackwell, 1956](#bib.bib6)). For simplicity, we present this solution
concept in the promptless setting. For vector $`z`$ and set $`C`$,
define the $`\ell_{\infty}`$ distance function as
$`\operatorname{dist}_{\infty}(z,C)=\min_{c\in C}||z-c||_{\infty}.`$

Suppose we want our policy $`\pi`$ to be preferred with at least
probability $`p`$ to any policy $`\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi`$ along all $`m`$
objectives:
$`\forall\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime})\in[p,\infty]^{m},`$
where $`C(p)\triangleq[p,\infty]^{m(x)}`$ is the corresponding target
set. For any $`p\geq 1/2`$, we can *linearize* the distance function as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\max_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\text{dist}_{\infty}(\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}),C(p))=\max_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\big(p-\min_{w\in\Delta_{m}}\langle w,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime})\rangle\big),`$ |  |

since $`\pi^{\prime}`$ can ensure that any coordinate of
$`\mathcal{P}^{k}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime})\leq 1/2`$ (e.g., by setting
$`\pi^{\prime}=\pi`$). In this case, we can define the $`\ell_{\infty}`$
Blackwell Winner ([Bhatia et al., 2020](#bib.bib5)) as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\argmin_{\pi\in\Pi}\max_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\big(p-\min_{w\in\Delta_{m}}\langle w,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime})\rangle\big).
``` |  | (2) |

### 3.2 The Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner

Note that removing the constant shift $`p`$ in
[Equation 2](#S3.E2 "In 3.1 Preliminaries ‣ 3 Back to Blackwell: A New Solution Concept for Multi-Objective PFT ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
does not affect the solution. Then, reintroducing the prompts $`x`$, we
can express the $`\ell_{\infty}`$ Blackwell Winner as the solution to

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\quad\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\left\langle w(x),\,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle\right].
``` |  | (3) |

While perhaps reasonable for small-scale problems, performing a best
response over adversary policies $`\pi^{\prime}`$ as in the above is
infeasible at the scale of today’s foundation models. Furthermore,
adversarially selecting both the criteria and comparator policy could
lead to situations where all policies look bad, even though some are
qualitatively better than others. Thus, to control the strength of the
adversary, we propose to KL regularize it to a reference policy
$`\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\langle w(x),\,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle+\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\left(x\right)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\left(x\right)\right)],`$ |  | (4) |

where $`\beta>0`$ controls the strength of the regularization. Beyond
being perhaps the natural choice, the use of KL regularization on the
adversary will allow us to elide adversarial training, as we discuss in
greater detail below. For now though, let us define the game value of a
policy $`\pi`$ as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}V(\pi)\ \triangleq\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\left\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle+\ \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}(x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\big].\end{split}
``` |  | (5) |

We are now equipped to define the Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner
(MaxEntBW):

###### Definition 3.1 (Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner (MaxEntBW)).

A policy $`\pi^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi}V(\pi)`$ is called a
*Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner (MaxEntBW)*.

In words, a MaxEntBW is a policy that compares favorably to policies in
its local neighborhood, regardless of the objective it is evaluated
under – a nuanced form of robustness. Observe that this solution concept
is well-defined even under multiple, globally inconsistent sets of
preferences.

In contrast to the single-objective setting, the Minimax Theorem ([von
Neumann, 1928](#bib.bib54)) doesn’t hold in the multi-objective setting
when we consider the worst case over objectives ([Blackwell,
1956](#bib.bib6)). Thus, the order of play matters: the value of the
game is different depending on whether learner $`\pi`$ or adversary
$`\pi^{\prime}`$ moves first ([Roth, 2023](#bib.bib43)). This means that
the self-play approaches ([Swamy et al., 2024](#bib.bib50)) that were
sufficient in the single-objective setting cannot be directly extended
to the multi-objective setting, seemingly indicating that adversarial
training may be necessary.

Despite the apparent complexity of solving
[Equation 4](#S3.E4 "In 3.2 The Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner ‣ 3 Back to Blackwell: A New Solution Concept for Multi-Objective PFT ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
to compute a MaxEntBW
([Definition 3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Definition 3.1 (Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner (MaxEntBW)). ‣ 3.2 The Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner ‣ 3 Back to Blackwell: A New Solution Concept for Multi-Objective PFT ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")),
the underlying structure of the optimization problem admits a strikingly
simple solution, as we now explore. We proceed by working from the
inner-most optimization problem outwards.

Key Step \#1: Entropy Regularization Ensures a Closed-Solution for
$`\pi^{\prime}`$. Fix a $`\pi`$ and $`w`$. Then, we need to solve

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\langle w(x),\,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle+\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\left(x\right)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\left(x\right)\right)].`$ |  | (6) |

Using
$`\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(x)}[\mathcal{P}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)]`$,
we know from [Ziebart et al. (2008)](#bib.bib71) that this problem has
solution

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{\prime}_{\star}(y^{\prime}\mid x)=\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}}/\beta)}{Z(w,\pi\mid x)},
``` |  | (7) |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Z(w,\pi\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}}/\beta)]
``` |  | (8) |

is the partition function that ensures the above sums to 1 across
$`\mathcal{Y}`$. As in [Gupta et al. (2025)](#bib.bib23), we can then
plug this closed-form expression back into
[Equation 4](#S3.E4 "In 3.2 The Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner ‣ 3 Back to Blackwell: A New Solution Concept for Multi-Objective PFT ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
to arrive at

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\min_{w:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{m(x)}}\mathbb{E}_{x}[-\beta\log Z(w,\pi|x)].
``` |  | (9) |

Observe that this optimization problem does not require adversarial
training between policies to solve.

Key Step \#2: Simplifying $`w`$ down to a Prompt-Wise Minimum. Next, we
observe that
[Equation 9](#S4.E9 "In 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
is concave in $`w(x)`$. Thus, for $`\forall x\in\mathcal{X}`$, we know
that $`w^{\star}(x)`$ must be a vertex of the simplex $`\Delta_{m(x)}`$
(i.e., a standard basis vector). As the set of objectives is usually
relatively small in practice, we can simply take a minimum prompt-wise
across different objectives to eliminate the optimization over $`w`$.
More formally, we can define the $`k`$th partition function

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Z^{k}(\pi\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\!\left[\exp\!\Big(-\mathcal{P}_{\pi}^{k}(y^{\prime}\mid x)/\beta\Big)\right],
``` |  | (10) |

and the worst-case coordinate as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
k^{\star}(x)\;=\;\argmin_{k\in[m(x)]}-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi\mid x).
``` |  | (11) |

Then, we can further simplify down
[Equation 9](#S4.E9 "In 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
to:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{x}{\mathchoice{\left[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)\right]}{[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]}{[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]}{[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]}}.
``` |  | (12) |

We have now reduced our optimization problem, which initially had three
players, to a single player.

Key Step \#3: Using Regression to Approximate Online Mirror Descent. We
now observe that
[Equation 12](#S4.E12 "In 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
is concave in $`\pi`$. This is because each
$`-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi\mid x)`$ is concave in $`\pi`$ (see
Lemma [B.1](#A2.Thmtheorem1 "Lemma B.1. ‣ B.1 Concavity of the Optimization Objective ‣ Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"))
and the minimum of concave functions is also concave. We can therefore
reduce this problem to online convex optimization ([Hazan,
2023](#bib.bib25)) and use Online Mirror Descent (in particular, with KL
as the Bregman Divergence) for policy optimization.

The first step of a descent algorithm is calculating the gradient.
Algebra tells us that the (functional) gradient with respect to $`\pi`$
at some response $`z\in\mathcal{Y}`$ of
[Equation 12](#S4.E12 "In 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle g(z\mid\pi,x)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}[\mathcal{P}^{k^{\star}(x)}(z\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)\exp(-\mathcal{P}_{\pi}^{k^{\star}(x)}(y^{\prime}\mid x)/\beta)]}{Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)}.`$ |  | (13) |

On small-scale problems, we could follow this gradient by performing
mirror descent independently at each prompt $`x\in\mathcal{X}`$ and
response $`y\in\mathcal{Y}`$. Unfortunately, both $`\mathcal{X}`$ and
$`\mathcal{Y}`$ are too large in modern foundation model training for
doing so to be practically feasible. In response, we use the
regression-based reformulation of mirror descent proposed by [Gao et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib19); [Gao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib20); [Brantley et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib9); [Zhou et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib68) to arrive at the
following update:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,z\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(x),\,z^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\end{subarray}}\Big(\frac{1}{\eta}\Big(\ln\frac{\pi_{\theta}(z\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(z\mid x)}-\ln\frac{\pi_{\theta}(z^{\prime}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(z^{\prime}\mid x)}\Big)-\big(g(z\mid\pi_{\theta_{t}},x)-g(z^{\prime}\mid\pi_{\theta_{t}},x)\big)\Big)^{2}.`$ |  |

As proved by [Gao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib19), solving the above
optimization problem would correspond to exact mirror descent. While we
are unlikely to be able to do this in practice, we can still learn a
policy with strong performance guarantees under approximation error, as
we show in
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Performance Guarantee for PROSPER ‣ 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning").

Putting It All Together in the Finite-Sample Setting. In practice, we
also need to estimate the infinite-sample gradient (Eq.

based on samples from the current policy and the reference policy. Thus,
we draw $`M`$ samples $`y_{1:M}\sim\pi(x)`$ and another $`M`$ samples
$`y^{\prime}_{1:M}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)`$ independently. We then
approximate the partition function

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math

``` |  | (14) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{k}(x)=\argmin_{k\in[m(x)]}-\beta\log\widehat{Z}^{k}(x),
``` |  | (15) |

and arrive at an estimate of the gradient $`\widehat{g_{t}}(x,z)`$:

|----|----|----|----|

### 4.1 Performance Guarantee for PROSPER

Recall that the game value of a policy $`\pi`$ is denoted by $`V(\pi)`$.
Let $`V(\pi^{\star})`$ be the optimal value. We now prove that under the
assumption that the regression problem can be solved accurately
in-distribution, PROSPER can efficiently learn a policy $`\hat{\pi}`$
such that $`V(\hat{\pi})`$ is close to $`V(\pi^{\star})`$. Intuitively,
this means $`\hat{\pi}`$ competes around as favorably as possible with
policies in its local neighborhood, regardless of the objective it is
judged under. More formally, we assume that

###### Assumption 4.1.

For all $`t\in[T]`$, we have that for some $`\epsilon`$,

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\\
z\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(x),\\
z^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\end{subarray}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\eta}\Bigg(\ln\frac{\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}(z|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(z|x)}-\ln\frac{\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}(z^{\prime}|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(z^{\prime}|x)}\Bigg)-\left(g(z\mid\pi_{\theta_{t}},x)-g(z^{\prime}\mid\pi_{\theta_{t}},x)\right)\Bigg)^{2}\leq\epsilon.`$ |  |

Intuitively, this assumption says that there is a policy in our policy
class that is able to accurately fit the difference of gradients. We
believe this is a reasonable assumption for modern deep networks. Next,
given some policy $`\pi`$, we denote the concentrability coefficient as
$`C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi}=\max_{x,y}\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid x)}`$
([Kakade and Langford, 2002](#bib.bib28)), which quantifies how well the
reference policy $`\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}`$ covers $`\pi`$. We are now ready
to state our main theoretical result:

###### Theorem 4.2.

Under
[Assumption 4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Assumption 4.1. ‣ 4.1 Performance Guarantee for PROSPER ‣ 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"),
after $`T`$ iterations, there exists some
$`\hat{\pi}\in\{\pi_{\theta_{1}},\cdots,\pi_{\theta_{T}}\}`$ such that
$`V(\pi^{\star})-V(\hat{\pi})\leq O{\mathchoice{\left(\sqrt{1/T}+\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}\epsilon}\right)}{(\sqrt{1/T}+\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}\epsilon})}{(\sqrt{1/T}+\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}\epsilon})}{(\sqrt{1/T}+\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}\epsilon})}}`$,
where $`\pi^{\star}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi}V(\pi)`$ denotes an arbitrary
MaxEntBW.

In words, this theorem says that as long as supervised learning works
(i.e., $`\epsilon`$ is small), then PROSPER can efficiently compute an
approximate MaxEntBW. We defer the detailed proof to Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
and turn our attention to empirically validating our approach on LLM
fine-tuning problems.

## 5 LLM Fine-Tuning Experiments Setup

We now outline the setup used for our LLM experiments. We defer all
hyperparameters to App.
[C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Implementation Details and Hyperparameters ‣ Appendix C Implementation Details ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning").

Training Dataset. We train on the WildChecklists dataset ([Viswanathan
et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)). WildChecklists is based on WildChat, a
collection of conversations between users and AI language models ([Zhao
et al., 2024](#bib.bib65)). As described in [Viswanathan et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib53), WildChecklists was created by using
Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct to generate *prompt-specific* rubrics to guide LLM
judge evaluation of candidate responses. Note that our framework – which
does not assume a fixed set of criteria across all prompts – is able to
flexibly accommodate this more nuanced feedback. One can view each
checklist item as one of the multiple objectives discussed in our
preceding formulation.

Policy and Judge Models. We fine-tune the 3B and 7B parameter
Qwen2.5-Instruct models ([Qwen et al., 2025](#bib.bib38)) and use
Qwen3-14B as our judge model ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib62)). We
selected Qwen3-14B due to the relatively high agreement it demonstrated
with author judgments across multiple criteria. We include the full
prompt templates in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Implementation Details ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
and use the standard averaging over both input orderings to mitigate the
positional bias of pairwise judges ([Zheng et al., 2023](#bib.bib66)).
All LLM judges score response pairs along a checklist item using a
5-point Likert scale. We query the judge 5 times and average to reduce
variance before normalizing to $`[0,1]`$.

Benchmarks. We primarily evaluate our trained models on
AlpacaEval ([Dubois et al., 2024](#bib.bib16)) and Arena-Hard ([Li
et al., 2024](#bib.bib30)), as these benchmarks are in-domain for the
WildChecklists training dataset. Additionally, to ensure our training
procedure doesn’t cause regression on other capabilities, we also report
results on multiple-choice QA and reasoning benchmarks: IFEval ([Zhou
et al., 2023](#bib.bib69)), MMLU ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](#bib.bib26)),
ARC ([Clark et al., 2018](#bib.bib13)), HellaSwag ([Zellers et al.,
2019](#bib.bib64)), and TruthfulQA ([Lin et al., 2021](#bib.bib31)).

Baselines. Given the similarity of the training data, we primarily
evaluate our methods against the RLCF (RL from Checklist Feedback)
method of [Viswanathan et al. (2025)](#bib.bib53). For each prompt,
candidate response, and checklist item, RLCF generates a score by
querying the LLM judge. Another LLM (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct in our
experiments) then generates a set of weights used to combine the
per-item scores into a per-response reward. Thus, the primary
distinction between our method and RLCF is the use of a more principled,
game-theoretic aggregation of per-item scores. Less important
distinctions include the use of DPO ([Rafailov et al.,
2024](#bib.bib39)) on the highest and lowest scoring responses for a
prompt rather than gap size-sensitive REBEL ([Gao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib19)) for policy optimization and our use of pairwise
judges rather than a judge that evaluates responses in isolation. To
ensure a fair comparison, we re-implement RLCF within our optimization
pipeline using REBEL as the policy optimizer and report the resulting
performance in the main text. We further evaluate the authors’ released
checkpoints and find their performance to be comparable to our
re-implementation (see
[appendix A](#A1 "Appendix A Supplementary Results ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")).
Both, however, are consistently outperformed by our method.

Ablations. In addition to evaluating our full method, PROSPER, we
evaluate two simplifications, each of which correspond to dropping one
of the inner loop optimizations.

Ablation \#1: Single-Objective Optimization. We ablate the importance of
explicitly optimizing over multiple objectives by instead asking the LLM
judge for a single, aggregate score (i.e.,
$`\forall x\in\mathcal{X},m(x)=1`$ that reflects all checklist items
jointly). We refer to this variant as PROSPER-JC.

Ablation \#2: Fixed Competitor Policy. Rather than adversarially
selecting a competitor policy, we can instead compare our policy to
$`\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}`$. This corresponds to taking $`\beta\to\infty`$ or
a variational bound on our full objective ([Gupta et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23)). Thus, we refer to this variant as PROSPER-VB.

Across all variants of our method, we use $`M=2`$ samples for gradient
estimation and sample $`4`$ responses per prompt, rather than a single
pair $`(z,z^{\prime})`$ as in Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning").
We also pool pairs across prompts and only train on the response pairs
with the largest gaps in gradient vectors, which we find improves the
quality of the final learned model. Intuitively, one can view this
process as filtering out response pairs where the LLM judge was not
confident in its choice.

## 6 PROSPER Enables Efficient Multi-Objective PFT From LLM Judge Feedback

Our experiments focus on answering four key questions:

1. How much of an issue is LLM judge intransitivity in practice? We
compute the fraction of prompts for which LLM judges have intransitive
preferences and for which Condorcet Winner exists. We also evaluate how
this fraction changes if we decompose a checklist into individual items
and ask the judge to evaluate each independently.

2. Is PROSPER able to optimize multi-objective judge preferences? We
compute the pairwise win-rate between all trained pairs of trained
policies as in [Munos et al. (2023)](#bib.bib34) to quantify how well
the policies produced by PROSPER optimize nuanced, multi-criteria LLM
judge preferences.

3. Do the policies trained via PROSPER exhibit qualitatively strong
behavior? Beyond optimizing the MaxEntBW objective, we also evaluate how
well the policies produced by PROSPER perform on the AlpacaEval (i.e.,
instruction following), Arena-Hard (i.e., general chat), IFEval, MMLU,
ARC, HellaSwag, and TruthfulQA benchmarks. We primarily evaluate on the
first 2 benchmarks as they are in-domain for our training set
WildChecklists, with the rest serving as regression tests.

4. How important are the inner loop optimizations for PROSPER’s
practical performance? We ablate the effect of the adversarially chosen
competitor (PROSPER-VB) and adversarially weighted objectives
(PROSPER-JC) on trained policy performance across all seven benchmarks.

We now answer these questions in order.

### 6.1 Multiple Objectives Mitigate but Don’t Fully Eliminate LLM Judge Intransitivity

Figure 2: On a held-out set of prompts from WildChecklists, we report
the fraction of prompts with no Condorcet Winner (left) and Intransitive
Preferences (right) for both the $`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{JC}}`$ (joint
check) and $`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{SC}}`$ (single check) judges, where
$`N`$ denotes the number of generated responses. We see that splitting
up a rubric into multiple items before passing it to the LLM judge
(i.e., using $`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{SC}}`$ rather than
$`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{JC}}`$) reduces but doesn’t eliminate
inconsistent preferences.

On a held-out set of 100 prompts from the WildChecklists dataset, we
evaluate the inconsistency of our LLM judge on $`N`$ responses from the
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct base model. We compute both the percentage of
prompts for which we see intransitive preferences (i.e., those where the
pairwise preferences form a cycle of any length ([Xu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib61))) and there is no Condorcet Winner (i.e., those where
no fixed response is preferred to all others). We note that these two
quantities are not the same, as cycles may only occur across relatively
weak responses. We compare two judges: $`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{JC}}`$
(joint check) and $`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{SC}}`$ (single check). For
$`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{JC}}`$, the LLM judge takes the full checklist as
input and outputs a single, aggregate score to indicate how much it
prefers one response over the other. For $`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{SC}}`$,
the LLM judge evaluates both responses along each checklist item
independently. We compute metrics for each (prompt, item) pair before
reporting the average over items and prompts.

In Figure
[2](#S6.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 6.1 Multiple Objectives Mitigate but Don’t Fully Eliminate LLM Judge Intransitivity ‣ 6 PROSPER Enables Efficient Multi-Objective PFT From LLM Judge Feedback ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"),
we see that both judges frequently have intransitive preferences for
large enough batch size $`N`$, echoing the findings of [Xu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib61). This underscores the importance of robust
algorithms like PROSPER for learning from such inconsistent feedback. We
also see that splitting up a checklist into multiple items (i.e., using
$`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{SC}}`$ rather than $`\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{JC}}`$)
improves both metrics but does not completely eliminate the problems of
having no Condorcet Winner and intransitive preferences. Thus, while
checklist / rubric rewards are an important first step, we still need to
take care in the multi-objective setting to handle LLM judge
intransitivity.

### 6.2 PROSPER Effectively Optimizes Multi-Objective LLM Judge Preferences

![Refer to caption](2602.19041v2/winrate_matrix.png)

Figure 3: We consistently see policies trained via PROSPER outperform
RLCF (roughly $`2/3`$ of the time), baseline (roughly $`3/4`$ of the
time), and ablation method policies, as measured by LLM judge win-rates
on held-out prompts. This indicates that PROSPER is able to more
effectively optimize nuanced, multi-criteria LLM judge preferences than
the other methods we consider.

Next, we ask the LLM judge to compare all trained 7B policies (PROSPER,
PROSPER-JC, PROSPER-VB, RLCF, and the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) on a set
of 500 held-out prompts from the test set. For each pair of policies and
prompt, we sample responses from each policy. We then ask our judge to
evaluate each pair of responses along all criteria and pick a winning
policy for each. We then average over checklist items, before finally
averaging over prompts to compute a win rate.

In Figure
[3](#S6.F3.fig1 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.2 PROSPER Effectively Optimizes Multi-Objective LLM Judge Preferences ‣ 6 PROSPER Enables Efficient Multi-Objective PFT From LLM Judge Feedback ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"),
we see that PROSPER produces policies that better optimize for judge
preferences than the base policy and RLCF baseline. Zooming in on the
top row, we see that PROSPER produces policies that generate responses
preferred to the base policy responses roughly $`3/4`$ of the time and
the RLCF policy responses roughly $`2/3`$ of the time. Furthermore, we
see that the full version of PROSPER outperforms both the PROSPER-JC and
PROSPER-VB ablations, indicating that both inner loop optimizations
improve efficacy at optimizing rubric-based LLM judge preferences.

## 7 Conclusion

We introduce the Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner: a solution concept
for learning from multiple, potentially intransitive preferences. This
is frequently the case when learning from rubric-based LLM judges. We
then derive PROSPER, a provably efficient algorithm for computing
MaxEntBWs, before using it to fine-tune LLMs at the 3B and 7B parameter
scales.

## Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Performance Guarantee for PROSPER ‣ 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")

To prove Theorem
[4.2](#S4.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 4.2. ‣ 4.1 Performance Guarantee for PROSPER ‣ 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"),
we break the proof into three different parts:

1.  1.
    Concavity of the optimization objective.
2.  2.
    Subgradient computation and estimation.
3.  3.
    Convergence result.

### B.1 Concavity of the Optimization Objective

Recall that the optimization objective is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle+\ \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi^{\prime}(x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\big)\big].
``` |  |

Using the results from [Ziebart et al. \[2008\]](#bib.bib71),
$`\pi^{\prime}`$ has a closed-form solution, and we can then simplify
the objective to

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \mathbb{E}_{x}[-\beta\log Z(w,\pi|x)],
``` |  |

where

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Z(w,\pi\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}}/\beta)].
``` |  |

To further simplify the objective, we invoke the following lemma:

###### Lemma B.1.

$`-\beta\log Z(w,\pi\mid x)`$ is concave in $`w(x)`$ and $`\pi`$.

###### Proof.

By Holder’s inequality, for any $`w_{1},w_{2}`$ and $`\theta\in[0,1]`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle Z(\theta w_{1}+(1-\theta)w_{2},\pi\mid x)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}})^{\theta}\cdot\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}})^{1-\theta}]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}})]^{\theta}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}})]^{1-\theta}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=Z(w_{1},\pi\mid x)^{\theta}Z(w_{2},\pi\mid x)^{1-\theta}.`$ |  |

Taking $`-\beta\log(\cdot)`$ on both sides, we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
-\beta\log Z(\theta w_{1}+(1-\theta)w_{2},\pi\mid x)\geq\theta(-\beta\log Z(w_{1},\pi\mid x))+(1-\theta)(-\beta\log Z(w_{2},\pi\mid x)).
``` |  |

Thus, $`-\beta\log Z(w,\pi\mid x)`$ is concave in $`w(x)`$. A similar
argument applies for $`\pi`$. ∎

By using a concave-form statement of Bauer’s maximum principle [Bauer
\[1958\]](#bib.bib4) as shown in
theorem [B.2](#A2.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem B.2 (Bauer’s maximum principle, concave form). ‣ B.1 Concavity of the Optimization Objective ‣ Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"),
it suffices to enumerate only the unit vector in $`\Delta_{m(x)}`$ for
each $`w`$.

###### Theorem B.2 (Bauer’s maximum principle, concave form).

Let $`C`$ be a nonempty compact convex subset of a (Hausdorff) locally
convex topological vector space and let $`\mathcal{C}`$ be the set of
extreme points in $`C`$. Let $`f:C\to\mathbb{R}`$ be concave and lower
semicontinuous (in particular, continuous suffices). Then $`f`$ attains
its minimum at an extreme point of $`C`$:

|     |                                                   |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \min_{x\in C}f(x)\;=\;\min_{x\in\mathcal{C}}f(x).
       ```                                                |     |

By
[Theorem B.2](#A2.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem B.2 (Bauer’s maximum principle, concave form). ‣ B.1 Concavity of the Optimization Objective ‣ Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"),
the optimization problem becomes
$`\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{x}[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]`$.
Now we show

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{x}{\mathchoice{\left[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)\right]}{[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]}{[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]}{[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]}}=\mathbb{E}_{x}{\mathchoice{\left[\min_{k\in[m(x)]}-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi|x)\right]}{[\min_{k\in[m(x)]}-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi|x)]}{[\min_{k\in[m(x)]}-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi|x)]}{[\min_{k\in[m(x)]}-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi|x)]}}
``` |  |

is concave in $`\pi`$. It suffices to show
$`\min_{k\in[m(x)]}-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi|x)`$ is concave in $`\pi`$. This
is because $`-\beta\log Z^{k}`$ is concave in $`\pi`$ and the minimum of
$`m(x)`$ concave functions is a concave function. Then the question
boils down to (sub)gradient computation and estimation.

### B.2 Subgradient computation and estimation

By a classical result in convex analysis [Nesterov
\[2018\]](#bib.bib35), we have:

###### Lemma B.3.

Given any integer $`N`$, Let $`f(x)=\max_{i\in[N]}f_{i}(x)`$, where each
$`f_{i}`$ is convex. Then the subdifferential of $`f`$ at $`x`$
satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\partial f(x)=\mathrm{conv}\bigl\{\partial f_{i}(x):i\in\argmax_{i}f_{i}(x)\bigr\}.
``` |  |

###### Lemma B.4.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g^{k}(z\mid\pi,x)\triangleq[\nabla_{\pi}-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi\mid x)](z)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\mathcal{P}^{k}(z\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)\exp(-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\mathcal{P}^{k}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)]/\beta)]}{\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\exp(-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\mathcal{P}^{k}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)]/\beta)]}.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

By calculation. ∎

###### Lemma B.5.

The gradient of the objective function
$`-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi\mid x)`$ is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g(z\mid\pi,x)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\mathcal{P}^{k^{\star}(x)}(z\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)\exp(-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\mathcal{P}^{k^{\star}(x)}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)]/\beta)]}{\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\exp(-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\mathcal{P}^{k^{\star}(x)}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)]/\beta)]}.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

Combining
Lemma [B.3](#A2.Thmtheorem3 "Lemma B.3. ‣ B.2 Subgradient computation and estimation ‣ Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
and
Lemma [B.4](#A2.Thmtheorem4 "Lemma B.4. ‣ B.2 Subgradient computation and estimation ‣ Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
yields the conclusion. ∎

### B.3 Convergence Result

For notational convenience, we write $`\pi_{t}`$ in place of
$`\pi_{\theta_{t}}`$ and $`g_{t}(x,z)`$ in place of
$`g(z\mid\pi_{\theta_{t}},x)`$. In the following, we define
$`f_{t}(x,z):=\frac{1}{\eta}\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(z\mid x)}{\pi_{t}(z\mid x)}`$.

###### Lemma B.6.

Consider any $`t\in[T]`$. Define $`\Delta(x,z)=f_{t}(x,z)-g_{t}(x,z)`$.
Define $`\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\Delta(x,z)`$
and
$`\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\Delta(x,z)`$.
Under
[Assumption 4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Assumption 4.1. ‣ 4.1 Performance Guarantee for PROSPER ‣ 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"),
for all $`t`$, we have the following:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,z\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\left(f_{t}(x,z)-g_{t}(x,z)-\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)\right)^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\epsilon,`$ |  | (13) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,z\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left(f_{t}(x,z)-g_{t}(x,z)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\epsilon,`$ |  | (14) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\epsilon.`$ |  | (15) |

###### Proof.

From
[Assumption 4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Assumption 4.1. ‣ 4.1 Performance Guarantee for PROSPER ‣ 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"),
we have:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,z_{1}\sim\pi_{t},z_{2}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left(f_{t}(x,z_{1})-\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{1})-\left(f_{t}(x,z_{2})-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{2})\right)+\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)^{2}`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,z_{1}\sim\pi_{t}}\left(f_{t}(x,z_{1})-\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{1})\right)^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{x,z_{2}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left(f_{t}(x,z_{2})-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{2})\right)^{2}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad-2\mathbb{E}_{x,z_{1}\sim\pi_{t},z_{2}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left(f_{t}(x,z_{1})-\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{1})\right)\left(f_{t}(x,z_{2})-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{2})\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad+2\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(f_{t}(x,z_{1})-\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{1})\right)\left(\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad-2\mathbb{E}_{x,z_{2}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left(\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)\left(f_{t}(x,z_{2})-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{2})\right)+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)^{2}`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle=`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,z_{1}\sim\pi_{t}}\left(f_{t}(x,z_{1})-\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{1})\right)^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{x,z_{2}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}\left(f_{t}(x,z_{2})-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)-g_{t}(x,z_{2})\right)^{2}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)^{2}\leq\epsilon.`$ |  |

∎

By definition, we have
$`\Delta(x,z)=\frac{1}{\eta}\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(z\mid x)}{\pi_{t}(z\mid x)}-g_{t}(x,z)`$.
Taking $`\exp`$ on both sides, we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi_{t+1}(z\mid x)=\pi_{t}(z\mid x)\exp(\eta(g_{t}(x,z)+\Delta(x,z)))=\frac{\pi_{t}(z\mid x)\exp(\eta(g_{t}(x,z)+\Delta(x,z)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)))}{\exp(-\eta\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x))}.
``` |  |

Denote
$`h_{t}(x,z):=g_{t}(x,z)+\Delta(x,z)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}`$
and the advantage
$`A_{t}(x,z)=h_{t}(x,z)-\mathbb{E}_{z^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}[h_{t}(x,z^{\prime})]`$.
We can rewrite the update rule as

|     |                                                                 |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \pi_{t+1}(z\mid x)\propto\pi_{t}(z\mid x)\exp(\eta A_{t}(x,z)).
       ```                                                              |     |

###### Lemma B.7.

Assume $`\max_{x,y,t}|A_{t}(x,y)|\leq A\in\mathbb{R}^{+}`$, and
$`\pi_{0}(x)`$ is uniform over $`\mathcal{Y}`$. Then with

|     |                                          |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \eta=\sqrt{\ln(|\mathcal{Y}|)/(A^{2}T)},
       ```                                       |     |

for the sequence of policies computed by PROSPER, we have:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\forall\pi,x:\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{t}(x)}A_{t}(x,y)\leq 2A\sqrt{\ln(|\mathcal{Y}|)T}.
``` |  |

###### Proof.

Start with

|     |                                                                    |     |
|-----|--------------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \pi_{t+1}(y\mid x)=\pi_{t}(y\mid x)\exp(\eta A_{t}(x,y))/Z_{t}(x),
       ```                                                                 |     |

where $`Z_{t}(x)`$ is the normalization constant. Taking log on both
sides and add $`\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{t}(x)}`$, we have:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
-\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(x)\|\pi_{t+1}(x))=-\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(x)\|\pi_{t}(x))+\eta\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(x)}A_{t}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(x)}\ln Z_{t}(x).
``` |  |

Rearranging terms, we get:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
-\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(x)\|\pi_{t}(x))+\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(x)\|\pi_{t+1}(x))=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(x)}[-\eta A_{t}(x,y)+\ln Z_{t}(x)].
``` |  |

For $`\ln Z_{t}(x)`$, using the condition that $`\eta\leq 1/A`$, we have
$`\eta A_{t}(x,y)\leq 1`$, which allows us to use the inequality
$`\exp(x)\leq 1+x+x^{2}`$ for any $`x\leq 1`$, which leads to the
following inequality:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\ln Z_{t}(x)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\ln\left(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\exp(\eta A_{t}(x,y))\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\ln\left(\sum_{y}\pi_{t}(y\mid x)\left(1+\eta A_{t}(x,y)+\eta^{2}A_{t}(x,y)^{2}\right)\right)`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq\ln\left(1+0+\eta^{2}A^{2}\right)\leq\eta^{2}A^{2},`$ |  |

where the last inequality uses $`\ln(1+x)\leq x`$, and we used the fact
that $`\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{t}(x)}A_{t}(x,y)=0`$ due to the definition
of advantage $`A_{t}`$.

Thus, we have:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
-\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(x)\|\pi_{t}(x))+\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(x)\|\pi_{t+1}(x))\leq-\eta\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(x)}[A_{t}(x,y)]+\eta^{2}A^{2}.
``` |  |

Sum over all iterations and do the telescoping sum, we get:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(x)}A_{t}(x,y)\leq\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi(x)\|\pi_{0}(x))/\eta+T\eta A^{2}\leq\ln(|\mathcal{Y}|)/\eta+T\eta A^{2}.
``` |  |

With $`\eta=\sqrt{\ln(|\mathcal{Y}|)/(A^{2}T)}`$, we conclude the proof.
∎

Now we can prove the
Theorem [4.2](#S4.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 4.2. ‣ 4.1 Performance Guarantee for PROSPER ‣ 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
formally: See
[4.2](#S4.Thmtheorem2 "Theorem 4.2. ‣ 4.1 Performance Guarantee for PROSPER ‣ 4 PROSPER: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")

###### Proof.

Fix a comparator policy $`\pi^{\star}`$. We begin by considering the
average performance gap between $`\pi^{\star}`$ and $`\pi_{t}`$ over
$`t=0,1,\ldots,T-1`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathchoice{\left(V(\pi^{\star})-V(\pi_{t})\right)}{(V(\pi^{\star})-V(\pi_{t}))}{(V(\pi^{\star})-V(\pi_{t}))}{(V(\pi^{\star})-V(\pi_{t}))}}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathchoice{\left({\mathchoice{\left\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\right\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}}\right)}{({\mathchoice{\left\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\right\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}})}{({\mathchoice{\left\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\right\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}})}{({\mathchoice{\left\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\right\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}{\langle\nabla V(\pi_{t}),\pi^{\star}-\pi_{t}\rangle}})}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathchoice{\left(\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}[g_{t}(x,y)]-\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{t}(x)}[g_{t}(x,y)]\right)}{(\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}[g_{t}(x,y)]-\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{t}(x)}[g_{t}(x,y)])}{(\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}[g_{t}(x,y)]-\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{t}(x)}[g_{t}(x,y)])}{(\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}[g_{t}(x,y)]-\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{t}(x)}[g_{t}(x,y)])}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)\right),`$ |  |

where the first inequality holds because of concavity and we define the
real advantage
$`A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y):=g_{t}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{t}(x)}g_{t}(x,z)`$.
Continuing, we have:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)\right)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}\left(A_{t}(x,y)\right)+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)-A_{t}(x,y)\right)
``` |  |

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\leq 2A\sqrt{\frac{\ln(|\mathcal{Y}|)}{T}}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)-A_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}},
``` |  |

where the last inequality uses
[Lemma B.7](#A2.Thmtheorem7 "Lemma B.7. ‣ B.3 Convergence Result ‣ Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning").
We now just need to bound
$`\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)-A_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}`$.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)-A_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left(\frac{\pi^{\star}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid x)}(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)-A_{t}(x,y))^{2}\right)
``` |  |

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\leq C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)-A_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}
``` |  |

where the last inequality uses the definition of concentrability
coefficient $`C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}`$, which is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi}=\max_{x,y}\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
``` |  |

We now bound
$`\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)-A_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}`$.
Recall the definition of $`A_{t}`$ from
[Lemma B.7](#A2.Thmtheorem7 "Lemma B.7. ‣ B.3 Convergence Result ‣ Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"):

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)-A_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x,y,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\left(\hat{g}_{t}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\hat{g}_{t}(x,y^{\prime})-h_{t}(x,y)+\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}h_{t}(x,y^{\prime})\right)^{2}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left(\hat{g}_{t}(x,y)-h_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{x,y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\left(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{t}(x)}[\hat{g}_{t}(x,y)-h_{t}(x,y)]\right)^{2}`$ |  |

Recall that
$`h_{t}(x,y)=g(x,y)+\Delta(x,y)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)`$, and
from
[Lemma B.6](#A2.Thmtheorem6 "Lemma B.6. ‣ B.3 Convergence Result ‣ Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning"),
we can see that:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left(g_{t}(x,y)-h_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\left(\Delta(x,y)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)^{2}\leq\epsilon.
``` |  |

For

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\left(g_{t}(x,y^{\prime})-h_{t}(x,y^{\prime})\right)^{2},
``` |  |

we have:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\left(\hat{g}_{t}(x,y^{\prime})-h_{t}(x,y^{\prime})\right)^{2}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\left(\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x,y^{\prime})-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)^{2}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\left(\Delta(x,y^{\prime})-\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)+\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)^{2}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{t}(x)}\left(\Delta(x,y^{\prime})-\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)\right)^{2}+2\mathbb{E}_{x}\left(\Delta_{\pi_{t}}(x)-\Delta_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}(x)\right)^{2}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\leq 4\epsilon,`$ |  |

where the last inequality uses
[Lemma B.6](#A2.Thmtheorem6 "Lemma B.6. ‣ B.3 Convergence Result ‣ Appendix B Detailed Proof in Section ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning")
again. This step relies on the fact that one of the samples is always
on-policy, i.e., from $`\pi_{t}`$.

Combine things together, we can conclude that:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi^{\star}(x)}\left(A^{\pi_{t}}(x,y)-A_{t}(x,y)\right)^{2}\leq C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}(10\epsilon).
``` |  |

We can conclude:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathchoice{\left(V(\pi^{\star})-V(\pi_{t})\right)}{(V(\pi^{\star})-V(\pi_{t}))}{(V(\pi^{\star})-V(\pi_{t}))}{(V(\pi^{\star})-V(\pi_{t}))}}`$ | $`\displaystyle\leq 2A\sqrt{\frac{\ln|\mathcal{Y}|}{T}}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}10\epsilon}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=2A\sqrt{\frac{\ln|\mathcal{Y}|}{T}}+\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}10\epsilon}.`$ |  |

∎

### C.1 Implementation Details and Hyperparameters

Our algorithm follows an off-policy training paradigm, where each epoch
is partitioned into three distinct phases: response generation,
preference scoring, and policy optimization. We execute two training
epochs per model. The following sections detail the hyperparameter
configurations and computational requirements for each phase.

Response Generation. In each epoch, we sample $`K=8`$ responses per
prompt from the current policy. The sampling parameters and the
computational resources utilized for this stage are summarized in
Table [5](#A3.T5 "Table 5 ‣ C.1 Implementation Details and Hyperparameters ‣ Appendix C Implementation Details ‣ Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning").

|--------------------|----------------------------------------------|

| Top-$`p`$          | 0.9                                          |
| Max new tokens     | 2048                                         |
| Hardware           | $`1\times(16\times\text{NVIDIA H100 80GB})`$ |
| Total compute time | $`\approx 2`$ hours per model per epoch      |

Table 5: Hyperparameters and compute configuration for response
generation.

Score Generation. We utilize an LLM-as-a-judge framework to evaluate the
generated response pairs. Each pair is integrated into a structured
evaluation template, and any resulting sequences exceeding a length of
4096 tokens are excluded to maintain consistency. To ensure the
robustness of the preference signals, we sample 10 independent judgments
per (prompt, response pair). To mitigate inherent positional bias, we
implement a symmetric evaluation protocol: the presentation order of the
responses is permuted for half of the samples (5 out of 10), and the
corresponding scores are reversed before computing the final average.

Training and Optimization. Following the derivation of game-theoretic
training signals from the preference scores, we filter the dataset to
retain the most informative signals. Specifically, we expand the dataset

ratio of $`0.15`$ for the first epoch and $`0.17`$ for the second,
````

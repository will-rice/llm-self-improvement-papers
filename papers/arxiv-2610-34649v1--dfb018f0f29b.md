###### Abstract

Recent advances in vision-language-action (VLA) and world-action models
(WAMs) have significantly improved robotic manipulation, yet deploying
robots in the open world demands a higher level of autonomy beyond
executing predefined tasks. Robot agents offer a promising paradigm for
this transition, integrating task discovery, contextual reasoning,
physical execution, and adaptive recovery into a closed interaction
loop. However, existing benchmarks largely evaluate isolated
capabilities, failing to capture the complexity of open-world robot
autonomy. We introduce RobotAgentBench (RAB), a comprehensive benchmark
for evaluating robot agents in realistic, open-world household
environments. Through an agent-assisted, human-in-the-loop real-to-sim
pipeline, RAB constructs 50 diverse household layouts with interactive
objects and physically grounded tasks, encompassing 90 atomic tasks, 18
short-horizon tasks, 8 long-horizon tasks, and 4 open-ended scenarios.
RAB provides a multi-level evaluation of robot agent capabilities: (1)
task-level assessments systematically examine five complementary
dimensions—task discovery, context reasoning, manipulation, failure
recovery, and robustness; (2) scenario-level evaluation assesses how
agents integrate these capabilities to autonomously fulfill high-level
user needs. Furthermore, RAB enables systematic comparisons of
standalone VLA/WAM models and diverse robot agent architectures under a
unified planning–execution–verification framework. Extensive experiments
reveal substantial gaps between manipulation and autonomy, demonstrate
the advantages of agentic architectures and recursive self-improvement,
and highlight the remaining challenges toward open-world robot autonomy.

## 1 Introduction

Embodied AI has advanced rapidly in recent years, with
vision-language-action (VLA) and world-action models (WAMs)
demonstrating increasingly strong manipulation capabilities across
diverse tasks and environments ([Intelligence et al., 2025](#bib.bib24);
[Ye et al., 2026](#bib.bib26); [Team et al., 2026b](#bib.bib28); [Wu et
al., 2026b](#bib.bib31); [Cai et al., 2026](#bib.bib32); [Xu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib61); [Shao et al., 2026](#bib.bib62)). These advances
raise a fundamental question: _How far are we from deploying robots in
the open world?_ Unlike predefined manipulation tasks, the real world
involves numerous mundane yet complex challenges, often characterized by
ambiguity, uncertainty, and constantly changing circumstances ([Liu et
al., 2025](#bib.bib45); [Pchelintsev et al., 2025](#bib.bib46); [Gong et
al., 2026](#bib.bib47)). Consider a household robot preparing for
guests: it must identify what needs to be cleaned, select appropriate
food based on user preferences, coordinate multiple activities, and
recover when manipulation attempts fail ([Mao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib44)). Such challenges are not exceptional, but intrinsic
to everyday physical environments. Addressing them is essential for
transforming robots into broadly useful assistants in human
environments.

Operating in the open world demands a transition _from robot
manipulation to robot autonomy_. Beyond executing explicit instructions,
robots must autonomously discover relevant tasks, reason about
environmental and user context, plan physical interactions, and adapt to
unexpected outcomes. Robot agents provide a natural framework for this
transition, integrating high-level reasoning and planning with physical
execution and feedback-driven verification into a closed loop ([Team et
al., 2026a](#bib.bib33); [Shi et al., 2025](#bib.bib37); [Ahn et al.,
2022](#bib.bib39); [Huang et al., 2022](#bib.bib38)). Such agents must
determine not only how to execute, but also what to do and how to adapt.
Meanwhile, advances in multimodal foundation models, including
GPT-6-Astra, and emerging agentic embodied systems ([Team et al.,
2025](#bib.bib25); [Yang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib29); [Bjorck et al.,
2026](#bib.bib27); [Chen et al., 2026b](#bib.bib30)) are creating new
opportunities to realize this integration. Together, these developments
suggest that embodied AI is entering a new stage, shifting from
standalone manipulation models toward autonomous robot agents capable of
handling complex, dynamic physical environments.

However, existing benchmarks remain largely designed for robot
manipulation rather than autonomy. Widely adopted benchmarks ([James et
al., 2020](#bib.bib1); [Yu et al., 2020](#bib.bib2); [Gu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib3); [Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib4); [Nasiriany et al.,
2024](#bib.bib5); [Mu et al., 2024](#bib.bib19); [Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib6)) primarily evaluate predefined tasks with explicit
goals, overlooking autonomous task discovery and context-dependent
decision-making. Recent efforts extend evaluation toward
reasoning ([Chen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib22)) and long-horizon
planning ([Shridhar et al., 2020](#bib.bib14); [Shridhar et al.,
2021](#bib.bib15); [Kim et al., 2024](#bib.bib16); [Yang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib17); [Mao et al., 2025](#bib.bib48)), but often rely on
stylized problems or abstracted physical interactions. More importantly,
reasoning, planning, and manipulation are frequently evaluated in
isolation, without capturing their interdependence during real-world
operation. A robot capable of individual tasks may still struggle to
determine what needs to be done, recover from failures, or adapt to
human interventions. Evaluating open-world robot autonomy therefore
requires moving beyond isolated capabilities toward a comprehensive
assessment of the entire autonomous interaction loop.

To bridge this gap, we introduce RobotAgentBench (RAB), a benchmark for
evaluating open-world robot autonomy in realistic household
environments. We develop an agent-assisted, human-in-the-loop
real-to-sim pipeline to reconstruct real-world scenes and expand them
into 50 diverse layouts with interactive objects and physically grounded
tasks. At its core, RAB provides 90 atomic tasks that can be flexibly
composed into a wide range of household tasks, from which we construct
18 short-horizon and 8 long-horizon manipulation tasks as the foundation
of our evaluation. Building upon these tasks, we introduce diverse
variants and evaluation settings to systematically assess five
complementary dimensions of robot autonomy: (1) task discovery,
identifying what needs to be done; (2) context reasoning, interpreting
implicit requirements; (3) manipulation, executing physical actions; (4)
failure recovery, detecting and correcting execution errors; and (5)
robustness, adapting to environmental disturbances. Beyond these
task-level assessments, we introduce four open-ended household scenarios
that require robot agents to integrate these capabilities to
autonomously fulfill high-level user needs, enabling an end-to-end
evaluation of open-world robot autonomy.

Using RobotAgentBench, we systematically evaluate standalone VLA/WAM
models and diverse robot agent architectures, revealing five key
findings:

- •
  Standalone policies struggle with task discovery and contextual
  reasoning despite their manipulation capabilities, highlighting the
  gap between manipulation and autonomy;
- •
  Coding-agent harnesses substantially improve performance, particularly
  through hybrid code-based and learned-policy execution;
- •
  Both planner capabilities and executor instruction-following abilities
  critically influence agent performance;
- •
  Harness-level recursive self-improvement (RSI) progressively improves
  task- and scenario-level performance through reusable tool refinement
  and experience accumulation;
- •
  Real-world evaluations reveal performance degradation but broadly
  consistent relative rankings, supporting the benchmark’s relevance to
  physical deployment.

Together, these findings highlight the promise and remaining challenges
of advancing from robot manipulation toward open-world robot autonomy.

## 2 RobotAgentBench

We introduce RobotAgentBench, a benchmark designed to evaluate
open-world robot autonomy beyond predefined manipulation tasks. We
leverage a real-to-sim pipeline to construct diverse environments and
collect data, then build the multi-level evaluation suite for systematic
comparisons of diverse agent framework designs.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Towards Open-world Robot Autonomy")
illustrates the overview of our benchmark.

### 2.1 Real-to-Sim Pipeline for Environment and Data Construction

To enable realistic and scalable evaluation, we develop an
agent-assisted, human-in-the-loop real-to-sim pipeline that starts from
a physical room in a real home, reconstructs it in simulation, expands
the reconstructed scene into diverse interactive configurations, and
collects demonstrations for training and evaluation.

#### 2.1.1 Environment Construction

Starting from a physical room in a real home, we first reconstruct it in
Isaac Sim and then expand it into diverse household environments. We
subsequently collect real-robot demonstrations and reproduce them in
simulation to generate trajectories.

##### Household Scene Reconstruction.

We reconstruct the physical household environment in Isaac Sim through
an iterative pipeline comprising real-world parameter collection,
similar asset retrieval, and new asset reconstruction. First, we collect
photographs and measurements of the room layout, furniture arrangements,
and household objects to establish real-world references. Next, a coding
agent retrieves similar 3D assets from online repositories and adjusts
their geometry, scale, and appearance to match these references. For
objects without suitable existing assets, we leverage the agent to
directly reconstruct their geometry and textures from photographs. The
resulting assets are assembled into the simulated environment with
appropriate collision geometry and physical properties to support
realistic interactions. Throughout this iterative process, human
reviewers continuously validate visual fidelity and physical interaction
consistency against the real-world environment, providing feedback to
the agent for refining the reconstructed scene.

##### Environment Augmentation.

Building upon the reconstructed home, we systematically diversify its
spatial configurations and visual appearances. We generate 50 distinct
layouts by modifying floor plans (_e.g.,_ T-shaped and L-shaped
configurations), rearranging functional areas, and varying furniture
positions and orientations. Each layout is manually reviewed to
eliminate geometric inconsistencies and implausible arrangements. We
further increase environmental diversity through variations in furniture
colors, textures, materials, and interior design styles.

#### 2.1.2 Demonstration Collection

We follow a three-stage workflow comprising real-world demonstration
collection, simulation alignment, and agent-assisted trajectory
expansion to generate scalable, physically grounded data. First, we
adopt the AgiBot G1 as the robot embodiment, matching its camera
configurations and joint settings between simulation and the physical
platform, and collect teleoperated demonstrations in the real home.
Second, we replay these demonstrations in simulation, iteratively
refining asset geometry and physical parameters to align interaction
outcomes with the real world. Finally, building upon these reference
trajectories, we employ a coding agent to generate additional successful
trajectories across diverse object positions, environment layouts, and
initial robot configurations. With access to the full simulator state,
including object poses, the agent synthesizes and executes action
programs to accomplish target tasks. For challenging interactions, we
provide manually implemented control functions that the agent can invoke
and compose. This workflow enables scalable trajectory generation while
maintaining consistency with real-world demonstrations. Detailed
observation and action spaces are provided in

### 2.2 Multi-level Evaluation of Robot Autonomy

Open-world robot autonomy requires robots to determine what needs to be
done, execute physical tasks, and adapt to unexpected outcomes. To
systematically evaluate these capabilities, RAB adopts a multi-level
evaluation suite built upon a composable household task foundation. At
the task level, we introduce dedicated evaluation settings to diagnose
five complementary capabilities: task discovery, context reasoning,
manipulation, failure recovery, and robustness. At the scenario level,
we assess whether robot agents can integrate these capabilities to
autonomously fulfill high-level user needs in open-ended household
environments. Together, these two levels enable both fine-grained
capability assessment and end-to-end evaluation of robot autonomy.

#### 2.2.1 Composable Household Task Suite

We construct a foundational task suite comprising 90 atomic tasks
involving everyday household objects and appliances. These tasks can be
flexibly composed into diverse household tasks, from which we construct
18 representative short-horizon tasks and 8 long-horizon composite
tasks. The suite covers common activities, such as retrieving food from
a refrigerator, heating meals in a microwave, and using an air fryer.
These tasks involve diverse manipulation skills, including pushing,
pulling, bimanual coordination, and mobile manipulation, across rigid
objects, deformable materials (_e.g.,_ tablecloths), and granular
materials (_e.g.,_ coffee beans). Short-horizon tasks combine a few
atomic tasks, such as picking up a hat from a sofa and hanging it on a
coat rack, while long-horizon tasks involve multiple subgoals, such as
tidying an entire sofa area. Building upon this task suite, we construct
diverse evaluation settings by varying task instructions, contextual
requirements, initial states, and environmental disturbances. These
variants enable targeted assessments of different autonomy capabilities
without requiring entirely new manipulation tasks. We further compose
these tasks into open-ended household scenarios, where the tasks to be
performed depend on the environment and user context. For each atomic
task and short-horizon task, we collect 50 training trajectories; for
each long-horizon task and open-ended scenario, we provide 10 complete
demonstrations. In total, the dataset comprises 101.8 hours of training
data. Further details are provided in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.2 Multi-level Evaluation of Robot Autonomy ‣ 2 RobotAgentBench ‣ Towards Open-world Robot Autonomy").

#### 2.2.2 Task-level Capability Evaluation

We design five complementary evaluation settings to examine how robots
determine what to do, execute physical actions, and adapt to unexpected
outcomes. Each setting focuses on a specific capability through
controlled task configurations. We use capability-specific metrics:
target-object coverage for task discovery, target-selection accuracy for
context reasoning, and task progress scores for manipulation, failure
recovery, and robustness.

##### Task Discovery.

We evaluate whether robots can autonomously identify relevant tasks
without explicit task instructions. We configure household scenes with
task-relevant objects and provide scenario descriptions and
user-specific context. The robot must explore the environment, interpret
its observations, and infer which objects require attention. To reduce
manipulation demands, the robot indicates inferred targets by touching
the relevant objects with either gripper, without completing the
underlying household tasks. We measure the fraction of target objects
touched under a limited contact budget.

##### Context Reasoning.

We assess whether robots can infer appropriate actions from implicit
requirements rather than explicit object instructions. We construct four
task settings involving drink selection, snack selection, waste
selection, and sofa object selection. Given contextual information such
as user preferences, intended uses, or classification rules, the robot
must identify the appropriate target object. For example, waste
selection requires identifying the object that belongs to the requested
category under the supplied classification rules. We evaluate reasoning
through contact with the correct target object, without requiring
successful grasping or manipulation.

##### Manipulation.

We evaluate physical execution through the 18 short-horizon and 8
long-horizon tasks in the foundational task suite. Given explicit task
instructions, robots must execute the required physical interactions,
assessing their instruction-following and manipulation capabilities
across diverse objects, skills, and task horizons.

##### Failure Recovery.

We evaluate whether robots can recognize and recover from execution
failures. We construct five failure variants of the foundational tasks,
including missed grasps and objects accidentally knocked over during
manipulation. Starting from these failure states, robots must inspect
the environment, identify the failure, and execute corrective actions to
resume and complete the specified manipulation objective.

##### Robustness.

We assess whether robots can adapt to unexpected environmental changes
during execution. We construct six task variants involving human
interventions and lighting changes, such as moving a coffee machine
while the robot is pouring beans into it. Robots must respond to these
disturbances and adjust their actions to complete the specified
manipulation objective.

#### 2.2.3 Scenario-level Integrated Evaluation

Beyond individual capabilities, open-world robot autonomy requires their
integration into a closed loop of task discovery, reasoning, planning,
execution, and adaptation. We therefore introduce four open-ended
household scenarios: preparing dinner, hosting guests, preparing to
leave, and tidying the room. Each scenario involves context-dependent
combinations of tasks, with specific requirements determined by the
sampled environment and user information. Unlike task-level evaluation,
robots do not receive explicit task lists. Instead, they must
autonomously explore the environment, identify relevant tasks, interpret
user needs, and plan their execution. This includes reasoning about task
dependencies, such as retrieving food from the refrigerator before
heating it in the microwave, and coordinating multiple physical
interactions toward broader objectives.

We evaluate end-to-end autonomy using the final progress score
aggregated over all task checkpoints in each scenario. To further
analyze policy and agent behavior, we introduce four detailed metrics
informed by the task-level capability dimensions: Task Coverage, the
fraction of scenario tasks with recorded task-relevant contact or native
operation-start evidence; Task Progress, the total process score earned
across these engaged tasks divided by their total maximum process score;
Recovery Rate, the fraction of observable physical-failure segments
followed by success of the same task; and Stagnation, the fraction of
execution time spent in repetitive, unproductive attempts or states with
no task progress.

### 2.3 Robot Agent Framework for Evaluation

To systematically investigate the transition from standalone
manipulation models to autonomous robot agents, RobotAgentBench supports
the evaluation of both standalone VLA/WAM policies and diverse robot
agent architectures. We introduce a unified
planning-execution-verification framework for robot agents: (1) a
_planner_ that interprets environmental context and user requirements,
decomposes tasks, and adapts plans based on execution feedback; (2) an
_executor_ that translates instructions into physical actions through
learned policy models (_e.g.,_ VLA and WAM) or code-based control; and
(3) a _verifier_ that monitors execution outcomes, identifies failures,
and guides corrective actions. By flexibly combining these components,
we construct five representative agent architectures to systematically
examine the roles of planning, execution, verification, and recursive
self-improvement in robot autonomy. For clarity, we denote each
architecture by its planner/executor/verifier (P/E/V) configuration,
where C represents code-based control, L denotes a learned VLA/WAM
policy, H denotes hybrid code-based and learned execution, and –
indicates an absent component.

##### VLA/WAM-based Policy + Code-based Verifier (–/L/C).

A VLA/WAM policy directly generates actions from observations and task
instructions. A coding-agent verifier monitors execution feedback,
selectively executes action prefixes, and introduces local corrections
before querying the policy again ([Team et al., 2026a](#bib.bib33)).

##### Code-based Planner + VLA/WAM-based Policy (C/L/–).

A coding-agent planner interprets task requirements, decomposes them
into concrete subtask instructions, and delegates physical execution to
a VLA/WAM policy. The planner dynamically revises its instructions based
on execution feedback ([Shi et al., 2025](#bib.bib37); [Ahn et al.,
2022](#bib.bib39); [Huang et al., 2022](#bib.bib38)).

##### Code-based Planner + Code-based Policy + Code-based Verifier (C/C/C).

A coding agent jointly handles planning, execution, and verification by
generating executable programs from predefined control functions
(_e.g.,_ move_eef_to()). It composes these functions to accomplish tasks
and verifies execution outcomes to guide subsequent actions ([Liang et
al., 2023](#bib.bib34); [Huang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib35); [Huang et
al., 2023b](#bib.bib36)).

##### Code-based Planner + Hybrid Code/VLA/WAM-based Policy + Code-based Verifier (C/H/C).

A coding agent serves as both planner and verifier, while the executor
integrates code-based control and learned VLA/WAM policies as unified
callable tools. The agent dynamically selects and combines these tools
according to task requirements and environmental feedback, leveraging
their complementary capabilities.

##### Code-based Planner + Code-based Policy + Code-based Verifier + RSI (C/C/C + RSI).

Building upon the fully code-based architecture, we introduce
harness-level recursive self-improvement (RSI), enabling the coding
agent to iteratively create, refine, and reuse control tools based on
execution feedback. Successful improvements are retained across
subsequent attempts, supporting progressive adaptation and experience
accumulation ([, 2026](#bib.bib40)).

## 3 Experiments

We conduct systematic experiments at both the task and scenario levels
to evaluate standalone VLA/WAM-based policies and complete robot agents.
Building on the unified robot agent framework we developed, we compare
different harness designs and examine their effects on task performance.
We further evaluate agents in the corresponding real-world environment
to investigate performance gaps between simulation and reality.

### 3.1 Experimental Setup

##### Evaluated Models and Agents.

For standalone policy baselines, we evaluate VLA models and WAMs. The
VLA baselines include $`\pi_{0.5}`$ ([Intelligence et al.,
2025](#bib.bib24)), Xiaomi Robotics-1 ([Team et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib28)), InternVLA-A1.5 ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib49)),
MolmoAct2 ([Fang et al., 2026](#bib.bib50)), LingBot-VLA2 ([Wu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib51)), HY-VLA ([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib52)), Galaxea
G0.5 ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib53)), and X-VLA ([Zheng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib54)). The WAM baselines include $`\tau_{0}`$ (low-level
policy) ([Zhou et al., 2026](#bib.bib55)), OpenWAM-Alpha ([Wang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib56)), and LingBot-VA ([Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib57)). For
complete robot agents, we compare five representative harness designs,
using Codex powered by GPT-6 Astra ([OpenAI, 2026](#bib.bib58)) as the
coding agent throughout and InternVLA-A1.5 as the learned policy
wherever applicable. For the fully code-based design, we additionally
evaluate Claude Code powered by Opus 5.5 ([Anthropic, 2026](#bib.bib59))
and Antigravity CLI powered by Gemini 4 Argon ([Kavukcuoglu,
2026](#bib.bib60)) to compare different coding agents under the same
harness. For the RSI setting, we run ten iterations, evaluating 30
instances per task or scenario in each iteration, and report performance
in the final iteration.

##### Evaluation Protocol.

We evaluate all methods across the five task-level assessments and four
open-ended scenarios. For each task or scenario, we evaluate 30 distinct
instances across the full benchmark suite. Evaluation covers all four
open-ended scenario families and the 50-layout catalog, with every
applicable randomization axis enabled over its full benchmark-defined
range: layout, appearance, lighting, scene and physical distractors, and
robot-arm, mobile-base, and interaction-object initial states. The
resulting comparisons measure performance across randomized instances
within the benchmark scene, task, and context families. All methods
interact with the same environment and 22-dimensional command interface;
model-specific observation adapters are described in
Appendice [C](#A3 "Appendix C Policy training and model-specific adaptation ‣ 5 Conclusion ‣ Robot Agents ‣ 4 Related Works ‣ 3.4 Sim–Real Performance Gap ‣ Does RSI Improve Robot Agent Performance? ‣ 3.3 Agent Harness Analysis ‣ 3.2.2 Scenario-Level Evaluation ‣ Robot Agent Results. ‣ 3.2.1 Task-Level Evaluation ‣ 3.2 Main Results ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Towards Open-world Robot Autonomy").
For open-ended scenarios, episodes terminate if the environment’s
evaluator records no increase in task progress for 120 consecutive
seconds of simulation time. Coding agents are limited to 200 model
responses per episode, and each tool call executes at most 600 control
steps, corresponding to 30 seconds of simulation time. We report the
mean score under the corresponding evaluation metric for each task or
scenario. Comparisons and rank orders refer to these observed instance
means under the stated protocol.

##### Observation and Action Spaces.

We adopt observation and action spaces consistent with the physical
AgiBot G1 robot. All systems have access to the same observation
sources: RGB images from one head-mounted and two wrist-mounted cameras,
proprioceptive states, task instructions or contextual prompts, and a
progressively revealed navigation map derived from static scene geometry
under shared disclosure rules. Model inputs follow their native
adapters; LingBot-VA conditions on RGB and language without
proprioceptive state. The 22-dimensional action space controls the head,
torso, both arms and grippers, and mobile base. Detailed specifications
are provided in Appendix A.

#### 3.2.1 Task-Level Evaluation

We compare standalone VLA/WAM-based policies and complete robot agent
systems across the five capability dimensions. Standalone policies
generally underperform integrated agent systems, highlighting a
substantial gap between their current capabilities and the demands of
real-world autonomy.

##### Standalone Policy Results.

Standalone policies appear to primarily imitate demonstrated
manipulation behaviors, with limited generalization to situations beyond
the training distribution, such as recovering from execution failures.
They struggle particularly with task discovery, which requires
purposeful exploration rather than execution of a specified task. When
initialized away from viewpoints commonly encountered in the
demonstrations, these models often produce erratic movements instead of
navigating to gather relevant observations. They also struggle with
contextual reasoning, such as selecting a beverage that matches user
preferences, with performance generally close to random guessing. Among
the evaluated standalone models, InternVLA-A1.5 achieves the strongest
overall performance, with notably higher robustness and failure recovery
scores than other models, even surpassing some complete robot agents.

##### Robot Agent Results.

Introducing coding agents substantially improves robot performance.
Among fully code-based systems, Antigravity CLI has the highest reported
mean score, followed by Claude Code. For Codex, RSI further improves
performance through tool refinement and experience accumulation across
task iterations. We also compare three ways of integrating coding agents
with learned policies. Refining policy-generated actions (-/L/C) yields
modest gains but remains constrained by the underlying policy’s
capabilities. Using a coding agent as a planner (C/L/-) improves
manipulation and robustness, with the largest gains in context
reasoning, as the planner translates contextual information into
explicit instructions for the executor. The best performance comes from
combining code-based control with selectively invoked learned policies
(C/H/C). For example, code-based control positions the gripper near an
object, providing a favorable starting state for the policy to complete
the grasp.

##### Does RSI Improve Robot Agent Performance?

We evaluate three fully code-based agents, Codex, Claude Code, and
Antigravity CLI, over ten RSI iterations, with 30 evaluation instances
per task or scenario in each iteration. Agents can accumulate experience
across tasks and refine their tools, implemented as reusable control
functions. As shown in
Figure [6](#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Does RSI Improve Robot Agent Performance? ‣ 3.3 Agent Harness Analysis ‣ 3.2.2 Scenario-Level Evaluation ‣ Robot Agent Results. ‣ 3.2.1 Task-Level Evaluation ‣ 3.2 Main Results ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Towards Open-world Robot Autonomy"),
all three agents improve at both the task and scenario levels over
successive RSI iterations, while the gaps between Codex and the other
two agents gradually narrow. In their initial attempts, agents often
make basic errors, such as attempting to reach a target pose in a single
motion that violates joint limits. Over successive iterations, they
refine their control functions to decompose such motions into smaller
steps through feasible intermediate poses.

## 4 Related Works

##### Robot Manipulation Benchmarks.

Robot manipulation benchmarks evaluate increasingly diverse and complex
tasks, yet largely retain predefined goals and task-specific
demonstrations. RLBench ([James et al., 2020](#bib.bib1)),
Meta-World ([Yu et al., 2020](#bib.bib2)), ManiSkill2 ([Gu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib3)), and LIBERO ([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib4)) assess
individual manipulation skills through dedicated tasks, while
RoboCasa ([Nasiriany et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)), BEHAVIOR ([Srivastava et
al., 2022](#bib.bib20); [Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib21)), and
RoboTwin ([Mu et al., 2024](#bib.bib19); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib6))
extend coverage to diverse household activities and environments.
VLABench ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib7)) and LIBERO-Long ([Liu et al.,
2023](#bib.bib4)) extend evaluation to longer-horizon tasks, providing
complete demonstrations that allow policies to learn entire task
sequences through imitation. More recent benchmarks, including
RoboDojo ([Chen et al., 2026a](#bib.bib22)), RoboCerebra ([Han et al.,
2025](#bib.bib8)), and RoboMME ([Dai et al., 2026](#bib.bib23)), assess
reasoning, planning, and memory alongside physical execution, but their
narrowly specified tasks offer limited coverage of context-dependent
household assistance. RobotAgentBench evaluates diverse agentic
capabilities through task-level assessments and open-ended household
scenarios, with training demonstrations primarily teaching reusable
skills that agents must select and compose according to the current
situation.

##### Embodied Planning Benchmarks.

Beyond robot manipulation benchmarks, some work abstracts physical
execution into predefined callable functions, focusing on evaluating the
planning and reasoning capabilities of models in simulated embodied
activities. ALFRED ([Shridhar et al., 2020](#bib.bib14)),
ALFWorld ([Shridhar et al., 2021](#bib.bib15)), RealFRED ([Kim et al.,
2024](#bib.bib16)), and EmbodiedBench ([Yang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib17))
assess household instruction following and planning, while
WorldLines ([Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib18)) emphasizes persistent
world state and memory. TEACh ([Padmakumar et al., 2022](#bib.bib13)),
DialFRED ([Gao et al., 2022](#bib.bib12)), KnowNo ([Ren et al.,
2023](#bib.bib9)), AmbiK ([Ivanova et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)), and
Ask-to-Act ([Ramrakhya et al., 2025](#bib.bib11)) further evaluate
interaction and clarification under incomplete or ambiguous
instructions. However, by abstracting away physical control, these
benchmarks provide a limited assessment of how planning and reasoning
must adapt to execution outcomes, particularly when recovering from
failures in real-world tasks.

##### Robot Agents

Robot agents integrate reasoning and physical execution through
different architectures. SayCan ([Ahn et al., 2022](#bib.bib39)), Inner
Monologue ([Huang et al., 2022](#bib.bib38)), and Hi Robot ([Shi et al.,
2025](#bib.bib37)) combine high-level planning by language or
vision-language models with learned skills or policies. Code as
Policies ([Liang et al., 2023](#bib.bib34)), ProgPrompt ([Singh et al.,
2022](#bib.bib41)), Instruct2Act ([Huang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib35)),
and VoxPoser ([Huang et al., 2023b](#bib.bib36)) use generated programs
to connect perception, planning, and control. Harness VLA ([Zhang et
al., 2026c](#bib.bib42)) combines learned policies with analytic control
primitives within an agentic framework. Recent approaches further enable
execution-time correction of policy actions ([Team et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib33)) and feedback-driven improvement of tools and control
code ([, 2026](#bib.bib40); [Ge et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)).
RobotAgentBench provides a unified benchmark for systematically
evaluating these robot agent designs.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we introduce RobotAgentBench (RAB), a comprehensive
benchmark for evaluating open-world robot autonomy beyond predefined
manipulation tasks. Built upon an agent-assisted real-to-sim pipeline,
RAB provides multi-level evaluations spanning five fundamental
capabilities and open-ended household scenarios, enabling systematic
assessment of robot agents from task discovery and contextual reasoning
to physical execution and adaptive recovery. Through extensive
evaluations of VLA/WAM models and diverse robot agent architectures, we
reveal a substantial gap between manipulation capabilities and
autonomous task completion. Our findings demonstrate the benefits of
coding-agent harnesses, hybrid execution, and recursive
self-improvement, while highlighting persistent challenges in
planning–execution coordination, failure recovery, and real-world
deployment. Despite recent advances, reliable autonomy in open-ended
scenarios remains far from solved.

We hope RAB serves as a systematic testbed for understanding these
limitations and advancing the transition from robot manipulation toward
open-world robot autonomy. Building on RAB, we aim to extend agent-level
recursive self-improvement (RSI) into the physical open world. Through
autonomous exploration and interaction, robot agents could discover new
tasks, learn from failures, and continuously refine their planning and
execution strategies. Accumulating and transferring reusable experience
across tasks and environments could pave the way toward continually
self-improving robot autonomy with minimal human intervention.

## Appendix A Benchmark Setup

This section describes the information available to evaluated systems,
the robot action space, and the shared simulation settings. Task and
scenario definitions are provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Task and Scenario Descriptions ‣ 5 Conclusion ‣ Robot Agents ‣ 4 Related Works ‣ 3.4 Sim–Real Performance Gap ‣ Does RSI Improve Robot Agent Performance? ‣ 3.3 Agent Harness Analysis ‣ 3.2.2 Scenario-Level Evaluation ‣ Robot Agent Results. ‣ 3.2.1 Task-Level Evaluation ‣ 3.2 Main Results ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Towards Open-world Robot Autonomy").

### A.1 Provided Information

The evaluation interface provides all systems with access to the same
information sources: task instructions and context, camera observations,
robot-state feedback, and a progressively revealed navigation map. The
same information disclosure rules apply to all systems. How this
information is represented, integrated, and used depends on the model
architecture, input adapter, and agent design. Model-specific inputs are
described in Appendix C.

##### Task instructions and context.

Each episode provides a task instruction or a high-level scenario
request. Context-dependent settings additionally supply information such
as user preferences, household routines, and storage arrangements. The
information provided for each task family is described in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Task and Scenario Descriptions ‣ 5 Conclusion ‣ Robot Agents ‣ 4 Related Works ‣ 3.4 Sim–Real Performance Gap ‣ Does RSI Improve Robot Agent Performance? ‣ 3.3 Agent Harness Analysis ‣ 3.2.2 Scenario-Level Evaluation ‣ Robot Agent Results. ‣ 3.2.1 Task-Level Evaluation ‣ 3.2 Main Results ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Towards Open-world Robot Autonomy").

##### Visual observations.

The benchmark records synchronized RGB observations from one
head-mounted camera at $`1280\times 800`$ and two wrist-mounted cameras
at $`848\times 480`$. Learned policies receive updated observations
after executing an action prefix; the prediction horizon and executed
prefix length are separate quantities. Code-based agents can request
observations through the evaluation interface.

##### Robot state.

We use the AgiBot G1 as the robot embodiment. Its state is represented
by a 23-dimensional vector containing twenty joint channels and the
mobile base’s world-frame position $`(x,y)`$ and unwrapped yaw. The
joint channels comprise fourteen arm joints, two torso/waist joints, two
head joints, and two gripper channels. Torso lift is expressed in
metres, while angular channels are expressed in radians.
Table [4](#A1.T4 "Table 4 ‣ A.2 Action Space ‣ Appendix A Benchmark Setup ‣ 5 Conclusion ‣ Robot Agents ‣ 4 Related Works ‣ 3.4 Sim–Real Performance Gap ‣ Does RSI Improve Robot Agent Performance? ‣ 3.3 Agent Harness Analysis ‣ 3.2.2 Scenario-Level Evaluation ‣ Robot Agent Results. ‣ 3.2.1 Task-Level Evaluation ‣ 3.2 Main Results ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Towards Open-world Robot Autonomy")
summarizes the state and action representations.

##### Navigation map.

Map-assisted evaluations provide a read-only top-down navigation map at
a resolution of 0.04 m. The map is derived from static scene geometry
rather than reconstructed from RGB images. It is progressively revealed
using the measured head-camera pose, field of view, and static
occlusion, with a small local region around the robot treated as known.
The same generation and revelation rules apply across participating
systems; revealed regions may differ because systems choose different
viewpoints and trajectories.

The map distinguishes known free floor, static obstacles, and unexplored
regions, and displays the robot’s measured position, heading, and
executed trajectory. The complete map, generated movable objects, task
labels, and target poses are not exposed. No planned routes or automatic
navigation actions are provided. Systems must choose and execute their
own movements and use camera observations to assess movable objects.

### A.3 Simulation and Execution Settings

##### Scene construction.

We first construct a digital twin of a real home, matching its layout,
furniture dimensions, and appearance. We then create additional layouts
by modifying floor plans, including T-shaped and L-shaped
configurations, and rearranging functional areas. All layouts are
manually reviewed for geometric validity and plausible furniture
arrangements. The resulting catalog contains 50 distinct layouts, with
additional visual variation in furniture colours, textures, materials,
and interior styles.

##### Physics and control.

Robot parameters, camera calibration, asset properties, and scene
settings are resolved before each episode and remain fixed across its
subtasks. Physics integration runs at 60 or 240 Hz, depending on the
configuration, while the action interface operates at 20 Hz. The
integration rate and backend remain unchanged within an episode. Cloth
manipulation may use a dedicated soft-body solver. Grasping relies on
physical contact and friction between the gripper pads and objects.

##### Continuous execution and scoring.

Task transitions do not reset the scene, object states, or accumulated
progress. Task processes and appliance dynamics persist as the robot
moves between workspaces. Progress is tracked throughout the episode,
while placement credit is updated using the current physical state, so
disturbing an earlier placement can reduce its final credit. At episode
termination, all registered tasks are finalized using their
task-specific scoring rules. Task-specific completion criteria are
provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Task and Scenario Descriptions ‣ 5 Conclusion ‣ Robot Agents ‣ 4 Related Works ‣ 3.4 Sim–Real Performance Gap ‣ Does RSI Improve Robot Agent Performance? ‣ 3.3 Agent Harness Analysis ‣ 3.2.2 Scenario-Level Evaluation ‣ Robot Agent Results. ‣ 3.2.1 Task-Level Evaluation ‣ 3.2 Main Results ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Towards Open-world Robot Autonomy").

## Appendix B Task and Scenario Descriptions

The following sections describe the tasks and scenarios used to evaluate
robot autonomy. Input descriptions refer to the environment interface;
individual policy inputs follow the adapters in
Appendix [C](#A3 "Appendix C Policy training and model-specific adaptation ‣ 5 Conclusion ‣ Robot Agents ‣ 4 Related Works ‣ 3.4 Sim–Real Performance Gap ‣ Does RSI Improve Robot Agent Performance? ‣ 3.3 Agent Harness Analysis ‣ 3.2.2 Scenario-Level Evaluation ‣ Robot Agent Results. ‣ 3.2.1 Task-Level Evaluation ‣ 3.2 Main Results ‣ 3 Experiments ‣ Towards Open-world Robot Autonomy").

task-specific scene. Long tasks require continuous execution across
subgoals without resetting the environment.

process scores. All 18 short tasks and 8 long tasks contribute to the
manipulation score.

### B.2 Task Discovery

Identify task-relevant objects

Task overview. We evaluate the agent’s ability to infer which household
tasks are needed and identify the objects involved across four
scenarios: dinner preparation, hosting guests, preparing to leave, and
room tidying.

Input and setting. The agent receives household memory, a high-level
scenario request, and camera observations. It explores the home, infers
which objects need to be handled, and indicates them by touching each
with either gripper. The request does not explicitly list the target
objects.

Completion and scoring. Performance is measured by object coverage: the
number of distinct target objects touched divided by the total number of
targets, $`N`$. Each episode allows contact with at most $`N+2`$
distinct physical objects, including targets and non-targets. Repeated
contacts with the same object count once, and non-target contacts
receive no credit. Only gripper-finger contacts count. The episode ends
when all targets are touched, the touch budget is exhausted, or the time
limit is reached. Grasping or completing the inferred household tasks is
not required.

### B.6 Open-ended Scenarios

Task overview. We evaluate the agent’s ability to infer, plan, and
execute household tasks from a high-level request in four scenarios:
preparing dinner, hosting guests, preparing to leave, and tidying the
room. Each episode combines multiple subtasks that must be completed
while preserving earlier results.

Input and setting. Each room is generated from a selected layout and a
scenario-specific task specification defining the required objects,
appliances, source locations, destinations, and initial states. All
subtasks share the same physical scene and object instances, without
resets between subtasks. The agent receives household memory, a scenario
request, camera observations, and robot-state feedback, but not the
underlying subtask list or target bindings. Household memory describes
personal preferences, routines, and storage arrangements. The agent
combines this information with scene observations to identify the
required tasks and plan their execution, respecting dependencies such as
preparing food before serving it.

Completion and scoring. Native subtask scores reward verified physical
progress, from target contact through manipulation and completion.
Partial execution receives credit. The episode score is the sum of its
subtask scores, normalized by the sum of their maxima and reported as a
percentage. Completed placements remain subject to checking, so
disturbing previously placed objects can reduce the score.

The episode terminates after 120 seconds of simulation time without an
increase in the aggregate score. Only a score increase resets this
shared timer; switching tasks or changing locations does not. Scores are
finalized at termination. This timeout limits inactivity rather than
total episode duration.

### C.1 Shared training setup

##### Training data and split.

We fine-tune all eleven policy baselines using demonstrations from the
101.8-hour RobotAgentBench simulation dataset, covering atomic actions,
short tasks, long tasks, and open-ended household scenarios. All models
use the same episode-level training/validation split.

##### Data representation and preprocessing.

Demonstrations follow a unified format with 23-dimensional robot states
and 22-dimensional whole-body actions, as defined in

augmentations are applied only during training. Model-specific

##### Fine-tuning and sampling.

We follow each model’s native fine-tuning recipe, retaining its

### C.2 Model-specific settings

##### $`\pi_{0.5}`$.

We fine-tune from pi05_base for 16,000 optimizer updates, with a

We fine-tune from the released Xiaomi Robotics-1 base checkpoint for

##### InternVLA-A1.5.

We fine-tune from InternRobotics/InternVLA-A1.5-base for 120,000

##### MolmoAct2.

We fine-tune from allenai/MolmoAct2 for 100,000 optimizer updates, with

##### LingBot-VLA2.

We fine-tune from robbyant/lingbot-vla-v2-6b for 60,000 optimizer

We fine-tune from robbyant/lingbot-va-base for 16,000 optimizer updates

We fine-tune from the released G0.5 base policy for 28,000 optimizer

We fine-tune from 2toINF/X-VLA-Pt for 33,500 optimizer updates, with a

We fine-tune the low-level action policy from sii-research/tau-0-vla for

We fine-tune from the released OpenWAM-Alpha Pretrain Foundation Model

## Appendix D Detailed Analysis of Open-ended Scenarios

We complement scenario scores with behavioural metrics and an analysis
of how agents distribute their interactions and respond to execution
difficulties.

### D.1 Behavioural metrics

##### Task Coverage.

Task Coverage measures the proportion of required tasks physically
engaged by the agent:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathrm{Coverage}=100\times\frac{\sum_{e}|I_{e}|}{\sum_{e}|T_{e}|},
``` |  |

where $`T_{e}`$ is the required task set in episode $`e`$ and
$`I_{e}\subseteq T_{e}`$ is the engaged subset. Engagement requires
eligible gripper-finger contact with a registered target or a
task-specific operation-start event. Each task counts once; workspace
selection and background monitoring do not count.

##### Task Progress.

Task Progress measures process-score attainment among engaged tasks:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{Progress}=100\times\frac{\sum_{e}\sum_{t\in I_{e}}p_{e,t}}{\sum_{e}\sum_{t\in I_{e}}p^{\max}_{e,t}},
``` |  |

where $`p_{e,t}`$ and $`p^{\max}_{e,t}`$ are the process score at
termination and its maximum. Target-contact credit is included, while
final-state score channels and unengaged tasks are excluded.

##### Recovery Rate.

Recovery Rate is the percentage of observable physical-failure segments
followed by task-specific success of the same task at a later control
step. Repeated failures before recovery form one segment. API errors,
formatting errors, and termination-only settlement events are excluded.

For these three metrics, numerators and denominators are pooled across
scenarios before computing percentages. Zero denominators are reported
as undefined.

##### Stagnation.

Stagnation measures the fraction of simulation time spent in repeated
physical execution without progress. An interval requires at least three
executions that advance simulation; only time beyond a 30-second grace
period counts. Task-score increases, manipulation progress, or newly
explored map area interrupt the interval. Successful navigation
exceeding 2 cm translation or 0.035 rad rotation is excluded.

State loops also qualify when a cycle of at most eight calls repeats at
least three times over at least 30 seconds without task or map progress;
the first cycle is excluded. Matching tolerances are 2 cm for base
position, 0.035 rad for joints and base yaw, and 1 cm/0.052 rad for hand
position/orientation. Overlapping intervals count once. Time follows the
20 Hz simulation clock, excluding API waiting.

##### Behavioural patterns.

Object manipulation accounts for 42.4% of Codex’s task events, compared
with 16.1% for Claude Code and 28.6% for Antigravity CLI. Claude Code
instead concentrates more interactions on exploration, grounding, and
base navigation, which together account for 57.3% of its events.

For all three agents, feedback-driven replanning is more frequent than
explicit task and goal selection. Observed adjustments include smaller
approach steps and revised wrist orientations, illustrating the
recurring need to update actions using execution feedback.

##### Execution difficulties.

Motion-feasibility issues and execution shortfalls together account for
82.1% of identified issues, compared with 15.3% for code or interface
errors. These difficulties span both positioning and object
manipulation, suggesting that reliable execution requires more than
correct tool calls.

Agents need to coordinate navigation, stance adjustment, and
manipulation, and revise unsuccessful approaches using execution
feedback. Such revisions should be followed by verification that the
intended physical progress has occurred.
````

###### Abstract

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has played a central role in the recent
surge of LLMs’ math abilities by enabling verification-driven training
through binary verifier signals. In contrast, Supervised Learning (SL)
is rarely considered for such verification-driven training, largely due
to its heavy reliance on reference answers and inability to reflect on
mistakes. In this work, we challenge the prevailing notion that
self-improvement is exclusive to RL and propose Negative-aware
Fine-Tuning (NFT) — a supervised approach that enables LLMs to reflect
on their failures and improve autonomously with no external teachers. In
online training, instead of throwing away self-generated negative
answers, NFT constructs an implicit negative policy to model them. This
implicit policy is parameterized with the same positive LLM we target to
optimize on positive data, enabling direct policy optimization on all
LLMs’ generations. We conduct experiments on 7B and 32B models in math
reasoning tasks. Results consistently show that through the additional
leverage of negative feedback, NFT significantly improves over SL
baselines like rejection fine-tuning, matching, or even surpassing
leading RL algorithms like GRPO and DAPO. Furthermore, we demonstrate
that NFT and GRPO are actually equivalent in strict-on-policy training,
even though they have entirely different theoretical foundations. Our
experiments and theoretical findings bridge the gap between SL and RL
methods in binary-feedback learning systems.

## 1 Introduction

The recent surge in math reasoning abilities of Large Language Models
(LLMs) is largely driven by a fundamental shift in their learning
paradigm, from imitation to self-improvement ([DeepSeek-AI,
2025](#bib.bib14); [OpenAI, 2024](#bib.bib34); [Chen et al.,
2025c](#bib.bib9)). Instead of relying on reference answers supplied by
human annotators or stronger models ([Liu et al., 2024](#bib.bib29);
[OpenAI, 2023](#bib.bib33)), the new paradigm requires only a question
dataset with a binary verifier to judge the correctness of
self-generated answers. By reflecting on their own generation, LLMs can
improve autonomously. This approach not only eliminates the need for
costly data annotation but also removes competence ceilings imposed by
external teachers, offering a promising path toward general intelligence
([DeepSeek-AI, 2025](#bib.bib14); [NVIDIA, 2025](#bib.bib32)).

Reinforcement Learning (RL) appears to be a natural fit for such
verification-driven training. Specific algorithms like PPO ([Schulman
et al., 2017](#bib.bib38)) and GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib39))
are explicitly designed to maximize reward signals, which can
conveniently take the form of a binary verifier outcome. In contrast,
Supervised Learning (SL) is rarely considered for realizing such
verification-driven training. A common view holds that SL is inherently
designed to memorize the positive data, rendering it unsuitable for
self-reflective learning from negative mistakes ([Chu et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib11)).

_In this work, we challenge the prevailing notion that
verification-driven training is exclusive to RL, and demonstrate it can
be similarly achieved within the supervised learning paradigm._

We start with a simple SL baseline: Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) ([Yuan
et al., 2023b](#bib.bib57); [Dong et al., 2023](#bib.bib15)). At each
iteration, an LLM generates answers to questions. A verifier helps
reject all negative answers. The remaining positive ones are compiled
into a dataset to fine-tune the LLM itself in a supervised manner. RFT
has been demonstrated effective ([Bai et al., 2022](#bib.bib3); [Yuan
et al., 2023a](#bib.bib54); [Song et al., 2023](#bib.bib41); [Touvron
et al., 2023](#bib.bib44); [Xiong et al., 2025](#bib.bib49)). However,
it prevents any learning from negative feedback. LLMs are encouraged to
reinforce what they already perform well, rather than reflect on their
mistakes — an ability we believe critical for achieving general
intelligence.

![Refer to caption](2505.18116v3/NFT_algo_spectrum.png)

Figure 1: A spectrum of online algorithms for LLM fine-tuning. NFT
bridges reinforcement learning and supervised learning methods through
the leverage of negative feedback via supervision.

To overcome this limitation, we propose Negative-aware Fine-Tuning
(NFT), an online learning algorithm that enables LLMs to learn from
their negative generations (Sec.
[3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
Like RFT, NFT fine-tunes a positive LLM on positive answers via
supervision. Crucially, instead of throwing away negative answers, NFT
also constructs an implicit negative policy to model them. This implicit
policy is parameterized with the same positive LLM we target to optimize
on positive data, enabling direct policy optimization on all LLMs’
generations (Figure
[2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.1 Maximum Likelihood vs. Policy Gradient ‣ 2 Background ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
NFT has minimal memory overhead, as only a single model is maintained
throughout training.

To understand the connection between NFT and RL approaches, we conduct
an in-depth comparison between NFT and GRPO (Sec.
[4](#S4 "4 Understanding the Gap between NFT and GRPO ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
Surprisingly, we find the two methods are actually equivalent in
strict-on-policy training, despite that they originate from entirely
different theoretical frameworks (Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
Notably, the “advantage normalization” characteristic of GRPO is already
implicitly reflected in NFT’s loss function. Their main difference
arises in off-policy settings, regarding different strategies for
clipping model gradients when the learned policy deviates from the old
policy. These observations suggest a strong connection between SL and RL
in binary-feedback learning systems.

We evaluate NFT on 7B and 32B Qwen models and report two key
findings: 1. Supervised Learning alone can significantly enhance LLMs’
math reasoning with no external teachers. NFT matches or even surpasses
state-of-the-art RL algorithms like GRPO ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib39)) and DAPO ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)). 2. The
performance gap between RFT and RL in online training largely stems from
SL’s past inability to leverage negative feedback, rather than any
inherent superiority of RL. Through the additional leverage of negative
data, NFT substantially mitigates this gap.

## 2 Background

### 2.1 Maximum Likelihood vs. Policy Gradient

Supervised Learning essentially aims to learn a model
$`\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$ to fit the data distribution
$`\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$. This can be realized by employing the
maximum-likelihood objective:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{a}}\sim\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\log\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\Leftrightarrow\min_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left[\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\|\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right].
``` |  |

In LLM fine-tuning, $`{\bm{q}}`$ usually means the question prompt, and
$`{\bm{a}}`$ the answer. To perform Maximum-likelihood training, we need
a dataset
$`\mathcal{D}=\{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}\sim\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\}`$ to draw
training samples from.

Reinforcement Learning, by contrast, maximizes pre-defined reward
$`r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})`$ for
$`{\bm{a}}\sim\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\theta}J(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{a}}\sim\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}).
``` |  |

Directly back-propagating through $`J(\theta)`$ is non-trivial, as
$`r(\cdot)`$ can be an arbitrary scalar function whose gradient is
unknown. Luckily, $`\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)`$ can be estimated, making
policy optimization feasible.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{a}}\sim\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\nabla_{\theta}\left[r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\log\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right].
``` |  | (1) |

Eq. [1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Maximum Likelihood vs. Policy Gradient ‣ 2 Background ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
is known as the Policy Gradient (PG) algorithm ([Sutton et al.,
1999](#bib.bib42)). In sequential decision-making problems such as
language reasoning, $`{\bm{a}}`$ can be interpreted as the token
decision for each step $`t`$, and $`r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})`$ can be
replaced with advantage functions $`A({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})`$ ([Schulman
et al., 2015](#bib.bib37)).

![Refer to caption](2505.18116v3/implicit_negative.png)

Figure 2: Illustration of the NFT algorithm. Data Collection: An LLM
$`\pi`$ generates answers to a set of math questions. Generation results
are split into two sub-datasets based on their answer correctness.
Policy Optimization: By constructing an implicit policy for modeling
negative data, NFT enables direct policy optimization on both positive
and negative answers via maximum-likelihood.

### 2.2 Math Reasoning RL: From Policy Gradient to GRPO

Eq. [1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Maximum Likelihood vs. Policy Gradient ‣ 2 Background ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
requires training to be on-policy, where $`\pi_{\theta}`$ can only be
updated a single time after data collection. To break this limitation,
importance sampling can be applied ([Schulman et al.,
2017](#bib.bib38)). Suppose the policy for collecting the RL dataset is
denoted as $`\pi_{\text{old}}`$, we have

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{a}}\sim\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\left[\frac{\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{a}}\sim\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\left[r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\nabla_{\theta}R_{\theta}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\right],
``` |  | (2) |

where
$`R_{\theta}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}):=\frac{\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}`$
is the likelihood Ratio between two policies.

In math reasoning tasks, PPO ([Schulman et al., 2017](#bib.bib38)) and
subsequent GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)) algorithms further
apply gradient clipping to constrain the distance between
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ and $`\pi_{\text{old}}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=-\sum_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}\sim\pi_{\text{old}}}\sum_{t}\text{min}\left[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}},\text{clip}(R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}),1-\epsilon^{\prime},1+\epsilon^{\prime})\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}}\right],
``` |  | (3) |

where
$`R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}):=\frac{\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}_{t}|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}_{t}|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}_{<t})}`$,
and $`\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}}`$ is the estimated advantage value.
Note that we have dropped some auxiliary loss terms, such as KL and
entropy regularization, as they have been pointed out to be unnecessary
by recent studies like DAPO ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)).

GRPO proposes an efficient way to estimate
$`\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}}`$. Collect $`K`$ answers
$`{\bm{a}}^{1:K}`$ and their binary reward $`r^{1:K}\in\{0,1\}`$ for
each question. The advantage is defined to be the normalized reward:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}}:=\left[{r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})-\texttt{mean}\{r^{1:K}\}}\right]/\;{\texttt{std}\{r^{1:K}\}}.
``` |  | (4) |

Later studies ([Liu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib31)) suggest removing the std
term from
Eq. [4](#S2.E4 "In 2.2 Math Reasoning RL: From Policy Gradient to GRPO ‣ 2 Background ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Problem Setup

Dataset. Given a set of $`N`$ math questions $`\{{\bm{q}}^{1:N}\}`$, a
pretrained LLM $`\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$, and a verifier
for judging the correctness. In every iteration, we generate a dataset
$`\mathcal{D}=\{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{1:K}\sim\pi,r^{1:K}\}^{1:N}`$, where
$`r\in\{0,1\}`$ is the correctness label, and $`K`$ the number of
answers collected for each question.

Dataset $`\mathcal{D}\sim\pi`$ can be split into two subsets:
$`\mathcal{D}^{+}`$ and $`\mathcal{D}^{-}`$. $`\mathcal{D}^{+}`$
contains all positive answers, and $`\mathcal{D}^{-}`$ contains the rest
negative ones. We denote the underlying answer distribution of
$`\mathcal{D}^{+}`$ as $`\pi^{+}(\cdot|{\bm{q}})`$.

Learning Target. We want to optimize the old policy $`\pi`$ into a new
policy $`\pi^{+}_{\theta}\approx\pi^{+}`$. The target
$`\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$ can be formalized using Bayes’ Rule:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}):=\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}},r{=}1)=\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})p(r{=}1|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}{\sum_{A}\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})p(r{=}1|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})},
``` |  | (5) |

where $`A`$ means all possible language space for $`{\bm{a}}`$.

![Refer to caption](2505.18116v3/distribution_split.png)

Figure 3: Left: Policy Splitting. The generation policy can be split
into a positive policy and a negative policy, and re-expressed as their
linear combination. Right: Policy Improvement. By iteratively optimizing
towards its positive split, an LLM policy $`\pi_{0}`$ can improve
continuously.

Discussion. An obvious solution for learning $`\pi^{+}`$ is to fine-tune
solely on correct answers ($`\mathcal{D}^{+}`$) and discard
$`\mathcal{D}^{-}`$ totally (RFT) ([Dong et al., 2023](#bib.bib15);
[Xiong et al., 2025](#bib.bib49)). However, this approach prevents the
model from any learning on its negative feedback ($`\mathcal{D}^{-}`$).
We posit that the ability to reflect on one’s own failures is not merely
desirable, but central to general intelligence, marking a shift from
pure imitation to self-reflective learning. Though traditionally viewed
as a distinctive strength of RL ([Chu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib11); [Yue
et al., 2025](#bib.bib58)), we ask: Can self-reflective improvement be
similarly achieved within the SL paradigm?

### 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers

In this section, we discuss how to leverage negative data
$`\mathcal{D}^{-}`$ to directly optimize $`\pi^{+}_{\theta}`$. Despite
seemingly impossible at first thought, we find the target policy
$`\pi^{+}`$ and the negative policy $`\pi^{-}`$ are tightly coupled,
making feasible training $`\pi^{+}_{\theta}`$ directly from
$`\mathcal{D}^{-}`$.

First, we formalize the definition of the negative policy $`\pi^{-}`$
similar to
Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "In 3.1 Problem Setup ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}):=\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}},r{=}0)=\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})[1-p(r{=}1|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})]}{\sum_{A}\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})[1-p(r{=}1|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})]}.
``` |  | (6) |

Combining
Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "In 3.1 Problem Setup ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
and
Eq. [6](#S3.E6 "In 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning"),
we make a key observation that

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})+\left[1-r_{\bm{q}}\right]\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}),
``` |  | (7) |

where
$`r_{\bm{q}}:=\sum_{A}\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})p(r{=}1|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=p(r{=}1|{\bm{q}})`$
is the correctness rate of LLM $`\pi_{\text{old}}`$ over a question
$`{\bm{q}}`$. In practice, $`r_{\bm{q}}\approx\texttt{mean}\{r^{1:K}\}`$
can be estimated using the $`K`$ rewards in dataset $`\mathcal{D}`$.

Implicit negative
policy. Eq. [7](#S3.E7 "In 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
reveals a tight coupling between $`\pi^{+}`$ and $`\pi^{-}`$ (Figure
[3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1 Problem Setup ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
Given that we already have $`\pi`$ as the pretrained LLM and
$`r_{\bm{q}}`$ is estimable, learning $`\pi^{-}`$ from negative data
should, in principle, shape the target policy $`\pi^{+}_{\theta}`$, in a
manner analogous to SL on positive data.

To realize this idea, we construct an implicit negative policy, denoted
$`\pi^{-}_{\theta}`$, by re-parameterizing the target policy
$`\pi_{\theta}^{+}`$ using the relationship in
Eq. [7](#S3.E7 "In 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning"):

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{-}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}):=\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{1-r_{\bm{q}}}.
``` |  |

Thus, training $`\pi^{-}_{\theta}`$ on negative answers directly leads
to optimizing the underlying positive LLM $`\pi^{+}_{\theta}`$ (Figure
[2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.1 Maximum Likelihood vs. Policy Gradient ‣ 2 Background ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
We have the following guarantee:

###### Theorem 3.1 (Policy Optimization with Negative Answers).

Consider the maximum-likelihood objective for training the implicit
negative policy $`\pi^{-}_{\theta}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{q}})\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\left[\log\pi^{-}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right]\Leftrightarrow\min_{\theta}\left[-\;\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\sim\mathcal{D}^{-}}\log\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{1-r_{\bm{q}}}\right]
``` |  | (8) |

Assuming unlimited data and model capacity, the optimal solution for
solving
Eq. [8](#S3.E8 "In Theorem 3.1 (Policy Optimization with Negative Answers). ‣ 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\forall{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}:\quad\pi^{+}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})
``` |  |

Proof in Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Proof of Theorems ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").
Theorem
[3.1](#S3.Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 3.1 (Policy Optimization with Negative Answers). ‣ 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
demonstrates the feasibility of policy optimization on negative data
only. To further utilize positive data, we additionally conduct
supervised training on $`\mathcal{D}^{+}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{({\bm{a}},{\bm{q}},r)\sim\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=r\left[-\log\frac{\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\right]+(1-r)\left[-\log\frac{1-r_{\bm{q}}\frac{\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}}{1-r_{\bm{q}}}\right]
``` |  | (9) |

Note that we subtract a baseline term
$`-\log\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$ from the loss. Since this
term is unrelated to $`\theta`$, it does not affect the loss gradient
and thus the optimal solution. $`\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$
is the old data likelihood before optimizer update. At the start of
training, we have $`\pi_{\theta}^{+}=\pi`$ such that
$`\mathcal{L}_{({\bm{a}},{\bm{q}},r)}^{\theta}=0`$.

We name our method Negative-aware Fine-Tuning (NFT) as it enables the
additional leverage of negative data for policy optimization compared
with RFT.

Continuous Reward. Though we mainly consider binary rewards for math
reasoning, NFT is directly applicable to continuous rewards. Following
[Levine (2018)](#bib.bib25), we can define optimality reward
$`r\in[0,1]`$, meaning the answer has a probability of $`r`$ for being
positive, and $`1-r`$ for being negative. The loss definition in Eq.
[9](#S3.E9 "In 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
and its convergence property stay unchanged (Proof in Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Discussion for Continuous Rewards ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).

Memory Efficiency. NFT is memory-efficient. In practice, we keep only a
single model copy in memory. The old policy likelihood
$`\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$ can be pre-computed during data
generation.

### 3.3 Practical Algorithm

1:  Input: Language model $`\pi`$, prompt set $`{\bm{q}}^{1:N}`$,
verifier $`r(\cdot)`$.

2:  Def $`\texttt{max\_v}({\bm{x}},\epsilon)`$: //Straight-through Max
Operator

3:   Return
$`\texttt{stop\_gradient}[\texttt{max}({\bm{x}},\epsilon)-{\bm{x}}]+{\bm{x}}`$
//Clip Value while Keeping Gradient

4:  for each iteration do

5:   for each sampled prompt $`{\bm{q}}`$ do

6:    Generate $`K`$ answers $`{\bm{a}}^{1:K}`$ and verify their
correctness $`r^{1:K}`$ // Data Collection

7:    Calculate correctness rate
$`\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}=\texttt{mean}\{r^{1:K}\}`$ and token-level likelihood
$`\{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}_{t}|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}_{<t})^{1:|{\bm{a}}|}\}^{1:K}`$

8:
   $`\mathcal{D}\leftarrow\{{\bm{q}},\;\hat{r}_{\bm{q}},\;{\bm{a}}^{1:K},r^{1:K},\pi_{t}^{1:K}\}`$
If $`0<r_{\bm{q}}<1`$ // Prompt Filtering

9:   end for

10:   Initialize $`\pi_{\theta}^{+}\leftarrow\pi`$

11:   for each mini batch
$`\{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}},r,\hat{r}_{\bm{q}},\pi_{t}\}`$ in $`\mathcal{D}`$
do

12:
   $`R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=\frac{\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}_{t}|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}_{t}|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}_{<t})},\forall t\qquad`$
// Positive Likelihood Ratio

13:    If r = 0:     

14:
   $`\quad R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}\;R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}))/(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})\qquad`$
// Implicit Negative Likelihood Ratio

15:
   $`\quad R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=\texttt{max\_v}[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}),\epsilon]`$
// Clip Negative Likelihood Ratio

16:
   $`\theta\leftarrow\theta+\lambda\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{t}\log R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})`$

Algorithm 1 Negative-aware Fine-Tuning (NFT)

We introduce several improvements over
Eq. [9](#S3.E9 "In 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
and propose a practical objective of NFT:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=-\sum_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}},r}\omega({\bm{q}})\sum_{t}\left[r\log R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})+(1-r)\log\text{max\_v}(\frac{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}\;R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}},\epsilon)\right]
``` |  | (10) |

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\text{where }\quad R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=\frac{\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}_{t}|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}_{t}|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}_{<t})},\quad\text{and}\quad\ \hat{r}_{\bm{q}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{{\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}}r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}).
``` |  |

Pseudo code is in Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.3 Practical Algorithm ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").
Below, we explain our key design choices.

Token-level loss.
Eq. [9](#S3.E9 "In 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
essentially deals with sequence data, where answer likelihood
$`\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\prod_{t}\pi({\bm{a}}_{t}|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}_{<t})`$
is heavily correlated with answer length. This introduces high variance
in gradient estimation and causes numerical instabilities during
training. Following [Schulman et al. (2017)](#bib.bib38); [Yu et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib53), we view each token decision as an individual unit
and sum up their loss in
Eq. [10](#S3.E10 "In 3.3 Practical Algorithm ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").

Clipping negative likelihood ratio. The negative loss in
Eq. [10](#S3.E10 "In 3.3 Practical Algorithm ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
involves a logarithm whose argument must remain positive, imposing
$`(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}R_{\theta}^{t})/(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})>0`$. When
$`R_{\theta}^{t}`$ is unoptimized, this requirement may not be
satisfied, potentially leading to training collapse. We therefore
enforce a minimum value of $`\epsilon>0`$ for the negative likelihood
ratio. To preserve gradient flow after clipping, we further apply
straight-through gradient ([Bengio et al., 2013](#bib.bib4)). Details
are in Algorithm
[1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 3.3 Practical Algorithm ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").

Prompt weighting. To focus training on more informative instances, we
weight each prompt $`{\bm{q}}`$ by $`\omega({\bm{q}})`$, assigning
higher importance to hard questions with low $`r_{\bm{q}}`$. An ablation
study is posted in Sec.
[5.4](#S5.SS4 "5.4 Ingredients Behind NFT’s Effectiveness ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").
This design also helps align NFT with RL algorithms like GRPO (Sec.
[4](#S4 "4 Understanding the Gap between NFT and GRPO ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).

## 4 Understanding the Gap between NFT and GRPO

Despite originating from entirely different theoretical foundations, NFT
and GRPO exhibit significant similarities. Notably, we find GRPO and NFT
are equivalent in on-policy training. To understand this, we calculate
and compare their loss gradients:

###### Proposition 4.1 (Algorithm Gradient Comparision).

Suppose there are $`\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}K`$ positive answers and
$`(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})K`$ negative ones for a given question
$`{\bm{q}}`$

(a) GRPO Gradient:  Consider only binary reward $`\{0,1\}`$ in
Eq. [3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 Math Reasoning RL: From Policy Gradient to GRPO ‣ 2 Background ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning"),
GRPO loss gradient satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=-\sum\Big\{r{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{+}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1719,0.6289,0.1719}\mathcal{I}\left[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})<1+\epsilon^{\prime}\right]}+(1-r){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{-}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1719,0.6289,0.1719}\mathcal{I}\big[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})>1-\epsilon^{\prime}\big]}\Big\}\nabla_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}),
``` |  |

where
$`A^{+}_{\bm{q}}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}{\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}}`$
and
$`A^{-}_{\bm{q}}=-\sqrt{\frac{\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}}`$
are respectively normalized advantages for answers.

(b) NFT Gradient:   Let
$`\omega({\bm{q}})=\sqrt{(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})/\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}`$, NFT
loss gradient satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=-\sum\Big\{r{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{+}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1016,0.375,0.6367}\frac{1}{R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}}+(1-r){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{-}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1016,0.375,0.6367}\max\big[\frac{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}\;R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}},\epsilon\big]^{-1}}\Big\}\nabla_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}).
``` |  |

Figure 4: Gradient weight for NFT and GRPO.

Proofs are provided in Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Proof of Theorems ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").
Comparing
$`\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)`$ and
$`\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)`$, the
only difference between GRPO and NFT is their strategy for clipping
model gradients when training data becomes off-policy (Figure
[4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4 Understanding the Gap between NFT and GRPO ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
GRPO simply zeros out the gradient when the learned policy
$`\pi_{\theta}`$ shifts far away from the old policy $`\pi`$, while NFT
takes a “softer” decay schedule.

Surprisingly, we find GRPO and NFT to be equivalent when training is
totally on-policy, despite their distinctively different derivations:

###### Proposition 4.2 (On-policy Gradient Equivalence).

Following the set up of Proposition
[4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 4.1 (Algorithm Gradient Comparision). ‣ 4 Understanding the Gap between NFT and GRPO ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
and let $`\epsilon\leq 1`$, GRPO and NFT loss gradient are equivalent in
on-policy training:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
``` |  |

Implicit group normalization. Proposition
[4.1](#S4.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 4.1 (Algorithm Gradient Comparision). ‣ 4 Understanding the Gap between NFT and GRPO ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
shows the “normalized advantage” term is implicitly present within NFT’s
loss function. This partially justifies the Group Normalization design
choice for GRPO, which was initially introduced only as an empirical
technique ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)). In Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A Proof of Theorems ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning"),
we further demonstrate that by adjusting
$`\omega({\bm{q}})=1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}`$, NFT also aligns with Dr. GRPO
([Liu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib31)). Our findings suggest a strong
connection between RL and SL frameworks.

### 5.1 Experiment Setups

Training. We perform online fine-tuning on Qwen2.5-Math-7B and
Qwen2.5-32B ([Yang et al., 2024](#bib.bib52)) to enhance their math
abilities without relying on external teachers. The training dataset is
the publicly available DAPO-Math-17k ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)),
which consists solely of math questions paired with ground-truth answers
in integer form. During training, all models are fine-tuned for
approximately 5,000 gradient steps with a batch size of 512. Generation
temperature is 1.0.

Evaluation. We evaluate models on six validation benchmarks and report
their average accuracy: AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC 2023 ([Li et al.,

OlympiadBench ([He et al., 2024](#bib.bib18)), and Minerva Math
([Lewkowycz et al., 2022](#bib.bib26)).

algorithms, including Iterative DPO ([Rafailov et al.,

2024](#bib.bib17)), GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)), Dr. GRPO
([Liu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib31)), DAPO ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)),
and RFT ([Dong et al., 2023](#bib.bib15); [Yuan et al.,

### 5.3 Benefits of Negative Data

Figure 8: Entropy curves for 7B and 32B runs.

Negative feedback enhances performance and exploration. Table
[1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
shows that NFT consistently outperforms RFT by a clear margin,
highlighting the benefit of incorporating negative feedback during
training. Notably, we observe a clear divergence in training dynamics
between RFT and NFT. Across both 7B and 32B settings, RFT tends to
reduce entropy over time, whereas NFT and RL methods like DAPO encourage
increasing entropy (Figure
[8](#S5.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 5.3 Benefits of Negative Data ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
This suggests more exploration ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)),
potentially hindering why NFT outperforms RFT.

Negative feedback becomes increasingly important in larger models. The
performance gap between RFT and NFT widens faster over training in 32B
experiments compared with the trend in 7B (Figure
[11](#A4.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiment Results ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
Similarly, [DeepSeek-AI (2025)](#bib.bib14) also notes RL offers greater
benefits over SFT in larger models. A potential explanation could be the
increasing importance of negative data: Larger models already memorize
well enough, so the ability to reflect on mistakes becomes a new
bottleneck.

RFT remains a strong baseline. Although surpassed by numerous
algorithms, RFT still deserves attention due to its extreme simplicity.
In 32B settings (Figure
[11](#A4.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Appendix D Additional Experiment Results ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")),
learning from positive data (RFT) contributes to 80% of the total gain
achieved by our best-performing model, while negative data only accounts
for the remaining 20%. These findings echo recent studies ([Yue et al.,
2025](#bib.bib58); [Xiong et al., 2025](#bib.bib49); [Liu et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib30); [Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib61); [Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib47)), which suggest RL primarily amplifies existing
capabilities in large models rather than fostering new skills. How to
exploit negative feedback remains an open challenge with heavy
potential.

### 5.4 Ingredients Behind NFT’s Effectiveness

We discuss two empirical design choices that notably help NFT achieve
strong performance.

Figure 9: Effect of prompt weighting.

Figure 10: Effect of negative ratio clip value $`\epsilon`$.

Prioritize harder questions. We find that assigning higher weights to
difficult questions with a low answer correctness rate
$`\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}`$ can enhance model performance. We achieve this
mainly by selecting $`\omega({\bm{q}})`$ in
Eq. [10](#S3.E10 "In 3.3 Practical Algorithm ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
from 3 choices: (1) $`\omega({\bm{q}})=1`$. (2)
$`\omega({\bm{q}})=1-r_{\bm{q}}`$, which aligns NFT with Dr. GRPO in
on-policy training (Sec.
[4](#S4 "4 Understanding the Gap between NFT and GRPO ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
(3) $`\omega({\bm{q}})=\sqrt{(1-r_{\bm{q}})/r_{\bm{q}}}`$, which aligns
NFT with GRPO. Figure
[9](#S5.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ 5.4 Ingredients Behind NFT’s Effectiveness ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
visualizes different $`\omega({\bm{q}})`$, and their effect for NFT and
RFT. We find choices (2) and (3) perform similarly, both outperforming
constant weighting choice (1).

Avoid overpenalizing mistakes. The clip value $`\epsilon`$ of NFT
(Eq. [10](#S3.E10 "In 3.3 Practical Algorithm ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning"))
sets an upper bound on the penalty weight when the likelihood ratio
$`R_{\theta}^{t}`$ for negative answers increases. When $`\epsilon`$ is
small (e.g., near zero), the algorithm empirically assigns high
penalties to rising likelihoods of incorrect answers (Figure
[10](#S5.F10 "Figure 10 ‣ 5.4 Ingredients Behind NFT’s Effectiveness ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")).
However, our experiments show that overly aggressive penalization with
$`\epsilon\rightarrow 0`$ degrades overall performance. We thus adopt a
default setting of $`\epsilon=1.0`$.

## 6 Related Works

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has advanced the
frontier of LLM reasoning ([DeepSeek-AI, 2025](#bib.bib14); [OpenAI,
2024](#bib.bib34); [Team et al., 2025](#bib.bib43); [Chen et al.,
2025c](#bib.bib9)). Compared with previous RL practices that rely on
strong reward models ([Wang et al., 2023b](#bib.bib46); [Yuan et al.,
2024](#bib.bib55); [Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib60)) to simulate human
feedback ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib35); [Christiano et al.,
2017](#bib.bib10); [Cui et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)), RLVR turns to a
ground truth verifier for providing reliable binary supervision
([Lambert et al., 2024](#bib.bib24); [Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib39)).
Moreover, unlike preference-based learning algorithms such as DPO
([Rafailov et al., 2023](#bib.bib36); [Cai et al., 2023](#bib.bib5);
[Azar et al., 2024](#bib.bib2); [Ethayarajh et al., 2024](#bib.bib16);
[Wang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib45); [Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib6); [Hong
et al., 2024](#bib.bib20); [Xu et al., 2023](#bib.bib50)), RLVR does not
require paired preference data, rendering it more flexible and
memory-efficient. Despite the demonstrated effectiveness of RL
algorithms in verification-driven training ([Li et al.,
2023](#bib.bib28); [Ahmadian et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Hu,
2025](#bib.bib21); [Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib53); [Chu et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib12); [Yuan et al., 2025](#bib.bib56); [Yan et al.,
2025](#bib.bib51)), recent studies suggest that supervised learning (SL)
may also suffice for achieving verification-driven training in LLMs
([Dong et al., 2023](#bib.bib15)). Our method further addresses SL’s
inability to incorporate negative feedback ([Hua et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22)), bridging both the theoretical and the performance
gap between the two fields.

A key design of NFT involves implicit policy modeling for direct policy
optimization. This design, emphasizing direct optimization via
implicitly defined models, shares conceptual similarities with some
existing approaches. In preference-based training, DPO ([Rafailov
et al., 2023](#bib.bib36)) introduces an implicit reward model
parameterized by the policy network to allow optimizing policies
directly. Recent visual modeling efforts also leverage implicit
conditional or residual models parameterized by generation networks to
avoid guided sampling ([Chen et al., 2025b](#bib.bib8); [Chen et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib7); [Zheng et al., 2025](#bib.bib62)).

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we introduce Negative-aware Fine-Tuning (NFT), a
supervised approach that enables LLMs to learn from their own negative
generations. In online training, NFT substantially improves upon
supervised learning baselines through the additional leverage of
negative feedback, achieving performance comparable to leading RL
algorithms like GRPO. Notably, we unveiled a theoretical equivalence
between NFT and GRPO under strict-on-policy conditions. These findings
bridge the conceptual and practical gap between SL and RL paradigms in
verification-driven training.

## Appendix A Proof of Theorems

###### Theorem A.1 (Policy Optimization with Negative Answers).

Consider the maximum-likelihood objective for training the implicit
negative policy $`\pi^{-}_{\theta}`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{q}})\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\left[\log\pi^{-}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right]\Leftrightarrow\min_{\theta}\left[-\;\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\sim\mathcal{D}^{-}}\log\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{1-r_{\bm{q}}}\right]
``` |  |

Assuming unlimited data and model capacity, the optimal solution for
solving
Eq. [8](#S3.E8 "In Theorem 3.1 (Policy Optimization with Negative Answers). ‣ 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\forall{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}:\quad\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})
``` |  |

###### Proof.

The proof is quite straightforward. First, we show that
maximum-likelihood training leads to the optimal solution
$`\pi^{-}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{q}})\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\left[\log\pi^{-}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right]`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow`$ | $`\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{q}})\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\left[\log\pi^{-}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-\log\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right]`$ |  |
|  | $`\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow`$ | $`\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p({\bm{q}})}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left[\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\|\pi^{-}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right]`$ |  |

Since
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left[\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\|\pi^{-}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right]\geq 0`$.
The equality holds if and only if
$`\forall{\bm{q}}:\pi^{-}_{\theta}=\pi^{-}`$. We thus have

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\forall{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}:\quad\pi^{-}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}).
``` |  | (11) |

Next, we prove $`\pi^{+}_{\theta^{*}}=\pi^{+}`$.

Note that that during training, $`\pi^{-}_{\theta}`$ is only an implicit
policy defined by $`\pi^{+}_{\theta}`$ through

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{-}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}):=\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{1-r_{\bm{q}}}.
``` |  |

On the other hand, the negative data distribution $`\pi^{-}`$ has the
same relationship with $`\pi^{+}`$ by Eq.
[7](#S3.E7 "In 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}):=\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{1-r_{\bm{q}}}.
``` |  |

We have ensured $`0<r_{\bm{q}}<1`$ during training, combining these
observations and Eq.
[11](#A1.E11 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix A Proof of Theorems ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning"),
we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\forall{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}:\quad\pi^{+}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})
``` |  |

∎

###### Proposition A.2 (Algorithm Gradient Comparision).

Suppose there are $`\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}K`$ positive answers and
$`(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})K`$ negative ones for a given question
$`{\bm{q}}`$

(a) GRPO Gradient:  Consider only binary reward $`\{0,1\}`$ in
Eq. [3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 Math Reasoning RL: From Policy Gradient to GRPO ‣ 2 Background ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning"),
GRPO loss gradient satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=-\sum\Big\{r{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{+}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1719,0.6289,0.1719}\mathcal{I}\left[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})<1+\epsilon^{\prime}\right]}+(1-r){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{-}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1719,0.6289,0.1719}\mathcal{I}\big[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})>1-\epsilon^{\prime}\big]}\Big\}\nabla_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}),
``` |  |

where
$`A^{+}_{\bm{q}}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}{\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}}`$
and
$`A^{-}_{\bm{q}}=-\sqrt{\frac{\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}}`$
are respectively normalized advantages for answers.

(b) NFT Gradient:   Let
$`\omega({\bm{q}})=\sqrt{(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})/\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}`$, NFT
loss gradient satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=-\sum\Big\{r{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{+}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1016,0.375,0.6367}\frac{1}{R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}}+(1-r){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{-}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1016,0.375,0.6367}\max\big[\frac{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}\;R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}},\epsilon\big]^{-1}}\Big\}\nabla_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}).
``` |  |

###### Proof.

(a) GRPO Gradient: We first copy down the GRPO loss definition from Eq.
[3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 Math Reasoning RL: From Policy Gradient to GRPO ‣ 2 Background ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=-\sum_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}},t}\text{min}\left[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}},\text{clip}(R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}),1-\epsilon^{\prime},1+\epsilon^{\prime})\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}}\right].
``` |  |

The normalized advantage can be estimated as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}}:=`$ | $`\displaystyle\left[{r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})-\texttt{mean}\{r^{1:K}\}}\right]/\;{\texttt{std}\{r^{1:K}\}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\texttt{mean}\{r^{1:K}\}=\frac{1}{K}\left[\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}K*1+(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})K*0\right]=\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\;\;\;\texttt{std}\{r^{1:K}\}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\left[\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}K*(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})^{2}+(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})K*(0-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})^{2}\right]}=\sqrt{\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}(1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}})}.`$ |  |

When $`{\bm{a}}`$ is a positive answer, $`r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=1`$. We
have
$`A^{+}_{\bm{q}}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}{\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}}>0`$.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D^{+}}}(\theta)=-\sum_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{+},t}\text{min}\left[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{+}),1+\epsilon^{\prime}\right]\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{+}}
``` |  |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D^{+}}}(\theta)=-\sum_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{+},t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{+}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1719,0.6289,0.1719}\mathcal{I}\left[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{+})<1+\epsilon^{\prime}\right]}.
``` |  | (12) |

When $`{\bm{a}}`$ is a negative answer, $`r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=0`$. We
have
$`A^{-}_{\bm{q}}=-\sqrt{\frac{\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}}}<0`$.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D^{-}}}(\theta)=-\sum_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{-},t}\text{max}\left[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{-}),1-\epsilon^{\prime}\right]\hat{A}_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{-}}
``` |  |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D^{-}}}(\theta)=-\sum_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{-},t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{-}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1719,0.6289,0.1719}\mathcal{I}\left[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{-})>1-\epsilon^{\prime}\right]}.
``` |  | (13) |

Combining Eq.
[12](#A1.E12 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix A Proof of Theorems ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
and Eq.
[13](#A1.E13 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix A Proof of Theorems ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning"),
we have

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{GRPO}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=-\sum\Big\{r{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{+}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1719,0.6289,0.1719}\mathcal{I}\left[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})<1+\epsilon^{\prime}\right]}+(1-r){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A^{-}_{\bm{q}}}\cdot{\color[rgb]{0.1719,0.6289,0.1719}\mathcal{I}\big[R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})>1-\epsilon^{\prime}\big]}\Big\}\nabla_{\theta}R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}),
``` |  |

(a) NFT Gradient: We copy down the NFT loss definition from Eq.
[10](#S3.E10 "In 3.3 Practical Algorithm ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning").

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=-\sum_{{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}},t}\omega({\bm{q}})\left[r\log R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})+(1-r)\log\text{max\_v}(\frac{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}\;R_{\theta}^{t}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}{1-\hat{r}_{\bm{q}}},\epsilon)\right]
``` |  |

When $`{\bm{a}}`$ is a positive answer, $`r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=1`$.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|

``` |  | (14) |

When $`{\bm{a}}`$ is a negative answer, $`r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})=0`$.

to align with the Dr. GRPO loss function.

## Appendix B Discussion for Continuous Rewards

In this section, we explain how NFTs can be extended to continuous
reward settings.

First, we define $`r:=p(\mathbf{o}=1|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\in[0,1]`$, where
$`\mathbf{o}=1`$ means the answer is an optimal answer.

Then we define positive and negative distributions.

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}):=\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}},\mathbf{o}{=}1)=\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})p(\mathbf{o}{=}1|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}{\sum_{A}\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})p(\mathbf{o}{=}1|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}\propto\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}),
``` |  |

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}):=\pi({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}},\mathbf{o}{=}0)=\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})p(\mathbf{o}{=}0|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}{\sum_{A}\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})p(\mathbf{o}{=}0|{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})}\propto\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})[1-r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})],
``` |  |

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})+\left[1-r_{\bm{q}}\right]\pi^{-}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}}),
``` |  |

Finally we copy training objective Eq.
[9](#S3.E9 "In 3.2 Direct Optimization of Language Models with Negative Answers ‣ 3 Method ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
below.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{({\bm{a}},{\bm{q}},r)\sim\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=r\left[-\log\frac{\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\right]+(1-r)\left[-\log\frac{1-r_{\bm{q}}\frac{\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}}{1-r_{\bm{q}}}\right]
``` |  | (16) |

###### Theorem B.1 (NFT for continuous rewards).

Consider the training objective of Eq.
[16](#A2.E16 "In Appendix B Discussion for Continuous Rewards ‣ NFT: Bridging Supervised Learning and Reinforcement Learning in Math Reasoning")
with arbitrary continuous rewards $`r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\in[0,1]`$.
Suppose we can accurately estimate
$`r_{\bm{q}}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})`$.
Assuming unlimited data and model capacity, the optimal solution
satisfies

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\forall{\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}:\quad\pi^{+}_{\theta^{*}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})
``` |  |

###### Proof.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{({\bm{a}},{\bm{q}},r)\sim\mathcal{D}}(\theta)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\left[r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\left[-\log\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})\right]+(1-r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}))\left[-\log\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{1-r_{\bm{q}}}\right]\right]`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left[r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\left[-\frac{1}{\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\right]+(1-r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}))\left[\frac{r_{\bm{q}}}{\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}\right]\right]\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$ |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle=\left[\frac{r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})[\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})]}\right]\nabla_{\theta}\pi_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})`$ |  |

When
$`r_{\bm{q}}\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})-r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=0`$,
that is

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{+}_{\theta}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})=\frac{r({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}})\pi_{\text{old}}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})}{r_{\bm{q}}}=\pi^{+}({\bm{a}}|{\bm{q}})
``` |  |

We have
$`\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\text{NFT}}_{({\bm{a}},{\bm{q}},r)\sim\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=0`$
constantly holds. ∎

## Appendix C Experiment Details

General training setup. All algorithms are implemented based on the
official DAPO codebase within the VeRL framework. We use a learning rate
of 1e-6 with a linear warm-up schedule across all experiments. At each
rollout step, we generate 16 answers for each of 512 sampled questions,
then split the data into 16 mini-batches and train the policy network
for 16 gradient steps. Models are trained for 320 rollout steps,
totaling over 5,000 gradient steps. Unless otherwise specified, we
follow DAPO’s default design choices, including dynamic data sampling,
token-level loss normalization, and no KL regularization. For 7B
training, we restrict context lengths to 4K and use 64 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.
For 32B training, we restrict context lengths to 32K (DAPO) or 16K
(others), and use 128-256 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.

DAPO. is a variant of GRPO that has achieved state-of-the-art AIME
performance on 32B models. Our NFT implementation is adapted from the
official DAPO codebase, based on the VeRL framework ([Sheng et al.,
2024](#bib.bib40)). NFT inherits most of DAPO’s hyperparameters and
design choices, including dynamic data sampling, token-level loss
normalization, and no KL regularization. We adopt a faithful
implementation of the official DAPO codebase, keeping all
hyperparameters untouched.

NFT. Compared with DAPO, NFT modifies the context length to 16K for 32B
training. We find this does not significantly affect performance, but
noticeably reduces data collection time. Another difference is that we
remove the overlong reward shaping technique of DAPO to ensure a binary
reward outcome. In our setting, truncated answers are treated as
negative, which sufficiently discourages overlong answers. The negative
ratio clipping parameter is set to $`\epsilon=1.0`$, and the prompt
weight is defined as $`\omega({\bm{q}})=1-r_{\bm{q}}`$.

RFT is a simple but effective SL baseline that only fine-tunes LLMs on
positive answers and throws away negative data. In our implementation,
the main difference between RFT and NFT is that RFT zeros out negative
data loss and keeps a constant prompt weight $`\omega({\bm{q}})=1`$
during training. For RFT, we zero out negative data loss in NFT
implementation and keep a constant prompt weight $`\omega({\bm{q}})=1`$
during training.

GRPO does not adopt the dynamic sampling technique proposed by DAPO.
Rather, it simply leverages all data for training, even though the
gradient for all-positive or all-negative questions should be zeroed out
automatically by the algorithm itself ([Yu et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)).
Other DAPO-related techniques are kept, such as setting positive
clipping parameter to a higher value $`\epsilon^{\prime}_{+}=0.28`$.
Since GRPO requires less time for sampling data, we train GRPO models
for 580+ rollout steps instead of 320 steps, which roughly takes the
same training time compared with DAPO experiments.

Dr. GRPO is modified from our GRPO implementation. The only difference
is the removal of the std normalization when computing group-normalized
advantages.

Iterative DPO. Comparing DPO with other RL algorithms in a head-to-head
fashion is difficult because DPO requires paired data to calculate the
training objective, while we sample 16 unpaired answers for each
question. To solve this problem, we take the implementation of InfoNCA
([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib6)), a variation of the DPO algorithm that
can handle $`K>2`$ responses per question:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{\text{InfoNCA}}_{({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{1:K},r^{1:K})\sim\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\frac{r^{(k)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}r^{(i)}}\log\frac{e^{\beta R_{\theta}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{k})}}{\sum^{K}_{i=1}e^{\beta R_{\theta}({\bm{q}},{\bm{a}}^{i})}}\right]
``` |  |

InfoNCA is guaranteed to become DPO algorithm in $`K=2`$ settings. We
ablate $`\beta\in\{0.1,0.01,0.02\}`$ and report the best averaged
validation result. We find InfoNCA training to be unstable and add an
SFT regularization term to the original loss function for stabilizing
the training process.

Validation details. Validation is performed with a top-p value of
$`0.7`$. The temperature is set to $`1.0`$ for 7B models and $`0.6`$ for
32B models, with context lengths matching the training configuration. We
use math-verify ([Kydlíček, 2024](#bib.bib23)) as the verifier during
training validation, and simpleRL ([Zeng et al., 2025](#bib.bib59)) for
final evaluation. The DAPO-17k benchmark consists solely of training
questions whose ground truth answers are integers and includes both a
prefix and a lastfix for each question. Accordingly, for validation on
AIME and AMC questions, we adapt the prompt format to match the training
pattern. For other benchmarks with non-integer answers, question prompts
remain unmodified.
````

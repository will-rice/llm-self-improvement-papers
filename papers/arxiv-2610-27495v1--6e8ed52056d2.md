# Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation

Yuhang Zhou        Yifan Wu        Bo Peng        Mingyi Wang       
Jiayi Liu Xiangjun Fan        Lizhu Zhang       Zhuokai Zhao

     Meta AI {zyhang, zhuokai}@meta.com

###### Abstract

Self-evolving agents aim to improve from their own experience rather
than remaining fixed once deployed. In practice, however, naturally
collected agent trajectories often lack ground-truth solutions or
verifier-derived rewards, making them difficult to learn from. We
observe that these trajectories become substantially more informative in
retrospect. When the agent takes an action, it knows only what has
happened so far, but looking back after the trajectory ends, the same
action can be judged with knowledge the agent lacked at that moment,
including how the environment responded afterward and how related tasks
in its accumulated experience succeeded or failed. Based on this
observation, we introduce Retrospective Self-Distillation (RSD), a
multi-round self-evolution framework in which the same model, given
privileged retrospective context built from its own experience, acts as
a teacher that re-scores its own actions, and the resulting distribution
is distilled back into the policy through on-policy self-distillation.
The privileged context combines cross-task precedents and lessons with
turn-level reflection and future hindsight, allowing both successful and
imperfect trajectories to provide dense supervision. RSD is also built
to make this supervision improve alongside the agent. Every round,
precedents and lessons are re-derived from the experience bank enlarged
with the current policy’s trajectories, and reflection and hindsight are
computed fresh for each new trajectory, so the teacher always scores the
policy against its most recent experience. We further introduce
frontier-guided context-policy evolution, in which a stronger frontier
model proposes edits to the context construction rules, for example how
many precedents to retrieve or which components to include. Across
ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, and SearchQA with Qwen3-8B and 14B, RSD improves
performance by up to $`59.7\%`$ relative over the base model, and
frontier-guided context-policy evolution adds up to a further $`44.7\%`$
relative improvement over a fixed context policy. These results show
that agents can convert naturally accumulated experience into persistent
model capability without ground-truth trajectories or verifier-derived
rewards for the core policy update.

## 1 Introduction

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly capable of
interacting with complex environments and completing multi-turn tasks.
Yet the substantial interaction experience an agent accumulates during
use rarely makes it better at future tasks, and this has motivated
growing interest in self-evolving agents, which aim to establish a
closed-loop data flywheel where the current policy generates on-policy
interaction experience, learns from that experience, and produces
increasingly useful data in subsequent rounds ([Gao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib4)). Prior approaches pursue this goal along two lines.
One evolves non-parametric components such as prompts, workflows,
memory, or tools ([Hu et al., 2025](#bib.bib8); [Hao et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib5)). Experience improves future behavior in these
systems, but the acquired capability is stored in the evolving scaffold
rather than in the model, whose parameters remain fixed ([Lee et al.,
2026](#bib.bib11)). The other line adapts model parameters using
successful trajectories, supervised learning, or reinforcement
learning ([Zhai et al., 2025](#bib.bib34); [Meng et al.,
2026](#bib.bib13)). The improvement then persists in the model itself,
but the update typically assumes supervision, such as verified
successful trajectories or reliable rewards, that may not be naturally
available during agent interaction.

In practical settings, each task may yield only a single on-policy
trajectory, without a ground-truth trajectory specifying what the agent
should have done or a reliable verifier providing rewards for policy
optimization. Conventional supervised fine-tuning therefore lacks direct
target sequences, while verifier-based reinforcement learning requires
reliable rewards and may be sample-inefficient when successful behavior
must be discovered through additional exploration ([Wu et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib24)). Restricting training to successful trajectories
further discards much of the already collected experience, including
failures that may contain informative environment feedback. This raises
a central question: How can an agent continuously improve from its
accumulated interaction experience without requiring ground-truth
trajectories or verifier-derived rewards for policy optimization?

Our key observation is that although such trajectories may lack explicit
training targets, they become substantially more informative _after_ the
interaction has completed. For the current task, downstream environment
observations reveal the consequences of earlier decisions and provide
hindsight unavailable when those actions were taken. Across tasks,
accumulated past experience provides concrete precedents, reusable
strategies, and recurring failure patterns.

Based on this observation, we introduce RSD, a multi-round
self-evolution framework that couples on-policy experience collection
with privileged retrospective distillation. At each round, the current
policy generates new trajectories and incorporates them into an
accumulated experience bank. For every interaction turn, RSD then
constructs a _privileged retrospective_ view of the agent’s decision,
combining _precedents_ retrieved from related past experience, _lessons_
distilled from accumulated successes and failures, _reflection_ on the
current decision, and _future hindsight_ from downstream interaction and
environment feedback. This information asymmetry, between what the agent
saw when acting and what is available afterward, naturally matches
on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib37)):
the same underlying model acts as both student and teacher, while the
teacher additionally conditions on privileged information unavailable to
the student during rollout. Existing formulations construct this
privileged view from ground-truth solutions ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib37); [Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)), whereas RSD draws it
entirely from the agent’s own evolving interaction experience. The
privileged teacher retrospectively re-scores the student’s realized
actions under this richer view, and the resulting supervision is
distilled back into the policy. As this process repeats, the model, its
on-policy experience, and the privileged context constructed from that
experience evolve together. We further explore a stronger-access
extension, _frontier-guided context-policy evolution_, in which a
frontier model also adapts how privileged context is constructed,
yielding a self-evolution loop over both model capability and
retrospective supervision.

We evaluate RSD with Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B ([Yang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib28)) across ALFWorld ([Shridhar et al.,
2020](#bib.bib19)), ScienceWorld ([Wang et al., 2022](#bib.bib22)), and
SearchQA ([Jin et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)). RSD achieves up to a 59.7%
relative improvement over the base model, while frontier-guided
context-policy evolution provides a further relative gain of up to
$`44.7\%`$ over the fixed context policy. We also find strong gains on
ALFWorld, whereas SearchQA remains within a narrow performance band
across rounds, providing a complementary boundary case for behavioral
self-evolution.

Our main contributions are threefold:

- •
  We formulate a practical self-evolving setting that turns naturally
  generated on-policy interaction experience into a recurrent data
  flywheel without requiring ground-truth trajectories or
  verifier-derived rewards for the core policy update.
- •
  We introduce privileged retrospective distillation from accumulated
  experience and trajectory hindsight, and further extend it with
  frontier-guided context-policy evolution so that both the model and
  its retrospective supervision can evolve across rounds.
- •
  Across ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, and SearchQA with Qwen3-8B and
  Qwen3-14B, RSD achieves up to $`59.7\%`$ relative improvement over the
  base model, while frontier-guided context-policy evolution yields up
  to an additional $`44.7\%`$ relative gain over the fixed context
  policy.

## 2 Preliminaries

### 2.1 Self-Evolving Agents

We consider a multi-turn agent that improves through a sequence of
self-evolution rounds. At round $`r`$, the agent is parameterized by a
policy $`\pi^{(r)}`$. Given a task $`x`$, the agent interacts with the
environment over multiple turns. At turn $`i`$, it observes the
interaction history $`h_{i}`$ and generates an action
$`a_{i}\sim\pi^{(r)}(\cdot\mid h_{i}),`$ after which the environment
returns a new observation $`o_{i+1}`$. The resulting trajectory is
denoted as

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\tau=(x,o_{1},a_{1},o_{2},a_{2},\ldots,o_{T+1}),\qquad\tau\sim\pi^{(r)}.
``` |  |

Collecting trajectories over a set of tasks produces the round-$`r`$
on-policy experience $`\mathcal{D}^{(r)}`$.

We focus on a practical self-evolution setting in which each sampled
task produces a single on-policy trajectory, rather than repeated
attempts followed by success-based selection. We further assume no
ground-truth trajectories or verifier-derived rewards for policy
optimization, requiring the agent to extract useful supervision directly
from its naturally collected interaction experience.

A self-evolving agent iteratively updates its policy using experience
generated by its current policy,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{(r+1)}=\mathcal{U}\!\left(\pi^{(r)},\mathcal{D}^{(r)}\right),\qquad\mathcal{D}^{(r)}\sim\pi^{(r)},
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\mathcal{U}`$ denotes a generic self-improvement procedure.
Because $`\pi^{(r+1)}`$ induces a new interaction distribution,
subsequent rounds continuously generate new on-policy experience for
further improvement. This iterative coupling between policy evolution
and experience generation provides the basis for a self-evolving data
flywheel. In the following, we instantiate $`\mathcal{U}`$ using
on-policy self-distillation.

### 2.2 On-Policy Self-Distillation

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) learns from trajectories generated by
the current policy rather than from a fixed offline dataset. The policy
$`\pi^{(r)}`$ generates an on-policy trajectory $`\tau\sim\pi^{(r)}`$.
OPSD uses the same underlying model to instantiate both student and
teacher, with the teacher additionally receiving privileged context
unavailable when the action was generated. For a multi-turn agent, this
privileged information is constructed at the turn level. Let
$`c_{i}^{(r)}`$ denote the privileged context for interaction turn
$`i`$, and let $`h_{i,j}`$ denote the interaction history together with
the generated action prefix preceding token $`j`$ at that turn. The
corresponding student and teacher distributions are

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{(r)}(\cdot\mid h_{i,j})\qquad\text{and}\qquad q^{(r)}(\cdot\mid h_{i,j},c_{i}^{(r)}),
``` |  |

respectively, where $`\pi^{(r)}`$ and $`q^{(r)}`$ represent the
unprivileged and privileged views of the same underlying model. The
student is then trained to match the privileged teacher distribution on
its own on-policy states. Aggregating token-level supervision across
interaction turns gives

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}^{(r)}=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi^{(r)}}\!\left[\sum_{i}\sum_{j}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q^{(r)}(\cdot\mid h_{i,j},c_{i}^{(r)})\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid h_{i,j})\right)\right].
``` |  | (2) |

Here $`\pi_{\theta}`$ denotes the student under optimization,
initialized from $`\pi^{(r)}`$. The sampled trajectories and the teacher
distribution $`q^{(r)}`$ are held fixed during the update, and the
updated student is taken as $`\pi^{(r+1)}`$ for the next round. Because
supervision is evaluated directly on the states visited by the current
policy, OPSD can learn from imperfect and unsuccessful trajectories
without requiring a ground-truth action sequence for each state.

## 3 Retrospective Self-Distillation (RSD)

![Refer to caption](2610.27495v1/figures/method_overview.png)

Figure 1: Overview of RSD. At self-evolution round $`r`$, the current
policy $`\pi^{(r)}`$ first collects on-policy interaction experience,
which is incorporated into the accumulated experience bank. Privileged
retrospective context is then constructed from past experience:
precedents and lessons, together with reflection and future hindsight
from the completed current trajectory. The same underlying model
retrospectively re-scores the student’s realized actions under this
privileged view, and the resulting distribution is distilled back into
the student to obtain $`\pi^{(r+1)}`$. Repeating this process jointly
evolves the policy, accumulated experience, and retrospective
supervision. In the stronger-access extension, a frontier model
additionally adapts the context-construction policy $`\phi^{(r)}`$
across rounds.

Our goal is to build a self-evolving training pipeline in which an agent
repeatedly generates on-policy interaction experience, learns from that
experience, and uses the improved policy to produce increasingly useful
data in subsequent rounds. In the absence of ground-truth trajectories
or reliable verifier-derived rewards, however, it is difficult to
directly convert these rollouts into supervision through conventional
supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or reward-based reinforcement learning
(RLVR). Nevertheless, the collected experience contains rich information
that becomes available retrospectively: for the current task, the
completed trajectory provides downstream environment feedback and
interaction outcomes, while previous tasks contribute accumulated
successful and failed experiences. These signals provide a richer view
of the agent’s behavior than was available at action time.

This observation naturally motivates OPSD. Rather than requiring an
external target trajectory or scalar reward, we construct privileged
retrospective context from the agent’s accumulated experience and use it
to re-score the agent’s own on-policy trajectory. The resulting
privileged teacher distribution provides dense supervision on exactly
the states visited by the current policy, which is then distilled back
into the agent to produce the next generation. As this process repeats,
the evolving policy generates new experience that refreshes the
privileged context, forming a data flywheel between model capability,
interaction experience, and retrospective supervision.

### 3.1 Self-Evolution through Privileged Retrospective Distillation

An overview of the complete self-evolution framework is shown in
Fig. [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3 Retrospective Self-Distillation (RSD) ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation").
Our self-evolution pipeline alternates between on-policy experience
collection and model update across rounds. We use this round-based
design so that each update is trained on trajectories sampled from a
fixed current policy, after which the updated policy generates a
refreshed on-policy distribution for the next round. At round $`r`$, the
current policy $`\pi^{(r)}`$ interacts with the environment and collects
a new trajectory set $`\mathcal{D}^{(r)}`$. We maintain an experience
bank $`\mathcal{B}^{(r)}`$ containing all trajectories accumulated up to
and including round $`r`$, including both successful and failed
interactions:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{B}^{(r)}=\mathcal{B}^{(r-1)}\cup\mathcal{D}^{(r)},\qquad\mathcal{B}^{(0)}=\emptyset.
``` |  | (3) |

Thus, $`\mathcal{D}^{(r)}`$ represents the newly collected on-policy
experience at round $`r`$, while $`\mathcal{B}^{(r)}`$ provides the
accumulated experience available for retrospective supervision in that
round.

#### Privileged Retrospective Context.

For each trajectory $`\tau\in\mathcal{D}^{(r)}`$ and interaction turn
$`i`$, we construct turn-level privileged context

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{i}^{(r)}=\mathcal{H}\left(P^{(r)},L^{(r)},R_{i}^{(r)},F_{i}^{(r)}\right),
``` |  | (4) |

where precedents $`P^{(r)}`$ and lessons $`L^{(r)}`$ are derived from
the accumulated experience, while reflection $`R_{i}^{(r)}`$ and future
hindsight $`F_{i}^{(r)}`$ exploit information available after completing
the current trajectory.

Precedents provide concrete prior experiences that demonstrate useful
behaviors for solving related tasks. We first apply task-specific
heuristics to identify useful trajectories from $`\mathcal{B}^{(r)}`$,
rank the resulting candidates by content-token overlap with the current
task goal, and retrieve the top-$`n`$ as precedents
(see Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation Details ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation")
for retrieval details). These precedents complement the current
trajectory with transferable action patterns observed in previous
interactions.

Lessons provide a more abstract view of accumulated experience. For each
task type, we expose selected successful and failed experiences of that
type from $`\mathcal{B}^{(r)}`$ to the current policy model and ask it
to summarize reusable strategies and recurring failure patterns. The
lesson summary is generated once per task type and round; we suppress
the task-type index for notational simplicity:
$`L^{(r)}=\operatorname{Summarize}_{\pi^{(r)}}\!\left(\mathcal{B}^{(r)}\right).`$
Unlike precedents, lessons compress experience into reusable
abstractions and allow unsuccessful trajectories to contribute even when
they are unsuitable as concrete demonstrations.

The remaining two components are constructed retrospectively from the
current trajectory. At each turn $`i`$, the current policy model
produces a reflection
$`R_{i}^{(r)}=\operatorname{Reflect}_{\pi^{(r)}}(h_{i},\tau),`$ which
uses the completed interaction to diagnose the realized decision and
summarize what could have been done more effectively. We further provide
future hindsight $`F_{i}^{(r)}=\tau_{>i},`$ which directly exposes the
downstream interaction following turn $`i`$, including subsequent
environment feedback and outcome information. Together, the four
components provide the teacher with complementary cross-trajectory
experience and within-trajectory hindsight that were unavailable to the
acting policy at generation time. The corresponding generation prompts
are provided in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Prompt Templates ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation").

#### Retrospective Distillation.

Given $`c_{i}^{(r)}`$, we optimize the student using the OPSD objective
in
Eq. [2](#S2.E2 "In 2.2 On-Policy Self-Distillation ‣ 2 Preliminaries ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation").
For every action token $`j`$ at turn $`i`$, the teacher conditions on
$`(h_{i,j},c_{i}^{(r)})`$, whereas the student observes only
$`h_{i,j}`$. The teacher retrospectively re-scores the student’s
realized trajectory rather than generating a replacement trajectory,
thereby providing dense supervision directly over the states visited by
$`\pi^{(r)}`$. After distillation, the updated student becomes
$`\pi^{(r+1)}`$ and is used to collect $`\mathcal{D}^{(r+1)}`$.

#### Self-Evolution Flywheel.

The interaction between policy evolution and experience accumulation
forms a recurrent data flywheel. At each round, $`\pi^{(r)}`$ generates
new trajectories $`\mathcal{D}^{(r)}`$, which expand the experience bank
to $`\mathcal{B}^{(r)}`$ through
Eq. [3](#S3.E3 "In 3.1 Self-Evolution through Privileged Retrospective Distillation ‣ 3 Retrospective Self-Distillation (RSD) ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation")
and refresh the precedents $`P^{(r)}`$ and lessons $`L^{(r)}`$ used for
retrospective supervision. Meanwhile, each newly collected trajectory
provides fresh reflection $`R_{i}^{(r)}`$ and future hindsight
$`F_{i}^{(r)}`$ for its own retrospective supervision. The resulting
privileged context in
Eq. [4](#S3.E4 "In Privileged Retrospective Context. ‣ 3.1 Self-Evolution through Privileged Retrospective Distillation ‣ 3 Retrospective Self-Distillation (RSD) ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation")
is distilled back into the policy, closing the flywheel between model
evolution, accumulated experience, and privileged retrospective
supervision.

### 3.2 Frontier-Guided Context-Policy Evolution

The self-evolution procedure above continually refreshes the contents of
privileged context as new experience enters the bank, while keeping the
context-construction rule $`\mathcal{H}`$ fixed. However, the most
useful way to construct privileged supervision may itself change as the
agent evolves. We therefore consider a stronger-access setting that
assumes access to both a frontier model and an executable verifier or
environment metric, and uses them to optimize the context-construction
policy. Note that this external verifier is used only to evaluate
context-policy candidates and their realized post-training performance;
it does not provide rewards for optimizing the agent parameters, which
continue to be updated solely through OPSD.

We parameterize the fixed privileged-context constructor $`\mathcal{H}`$
in
Eq. [4](#S3.E4 "In Privileged Retrospective Context. ‣ 3.1 Self-Evolution through Privileged Retrospective Distillation ‣ 3 Retrospective Self-Distillation (RSD) ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation")
by a context policy $`\phi^{(r)}`$, yielding
$`\mathcal{H}_{\phi^{(r)}}`$. The policy $`\phi^{(r)}`$ controls how the
privileged information sources are selected, composed, and injected into
the teacher context. We represent $`\phi^{(r)}`$ as a declarative
configuration over a constrained set of editable context choices,
including precedent count and retrieval strategy, lesson usage,
reflection, future hindsight, and the position at which privileged
information is injected into the teacher context. The frontier model may
modify only these whitelisted choices, without modifying the model
weights or training procedure.

#### Frontier-Guided Proposal.

After completing evolution round $`r`$, the frontier model receives a
compact summary of the current training state, including the current
context policy, checkpoint task performance, and teacher re-scoring
statistics such as correction rate, teacher entropy, and top-$`k`$ hit
rate, with detailed definitions in
Appendix [D.6](#A4.SS6 "D.6 Teacher Re-Scoring Diagnostics ‣ Appendix D Supplementary Results ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation").
Based on these diagnostics, it proposes a small set of candidate edits
to $`\phi^{(r)}`$. To ground this search in empirical feedback, each
candidate is evaluated with a lightweight no-training probe: we
instantiate the candidate privileged context on a small
training-distribution slice, provide it to the current model at
inference time, and measure the corresponding task performance without
updating model parameters. The frontier model observes these probe
scores and selects a candidate $`\tilde{\phi}^{(r+1)}`$ for the
subsequent self-evolution round.

#### Retrospective Acceptance.

The selected candidate $`\tilde{\phi}^{(r+1)}`$ is then used to
construct privileged supervision during the subsequent full
self-evolution round. After training, we evaluate the resulting
checkpoint using the actual environment metric. The proposed context
policy is retained if its post-training performance is no worse than the
best previously accepted configuration (up to a tolerance $`\epsilon`$);
otherwise, the context policy reverts to the last accepted
configuration:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\phi^{(r+1)}=\begin{cases}\tilde{\phi}^{(r+1)},&m^{(r+1)}\geq m_{\mathrm{best}}^{(r)}-\epsilon,\\
\phi_{\mathrm{best}}^{(r)},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
``` |  | (5) |

where $`m^{(r+1)}`$ denotes the realized post-training environment
performance. Only the context policy is reverted; the model parameters
continue evolving across rounds.

#### Joint Evolution of Model and Context Policy.

The frontier-guided extension therefore adds a second adaptive process
to the self-evolution flywheel. The core procedure evolves the model and
the privileged information available from accumulated experience, while
frontier guidance additionally evolves how that information is selected
and organized:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\pi^{(r)}\rightarrow\pi^{(r+1)},\qquad\mathcal{B}^{(r)}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}^{(r+1)},\qquad\phi^{(r)}\rightarrow\phi^{(r+1)}.
``` |  |

This allows the retrospective supervision mechanism itself to adapt to
the evolving agent rather than relying on a single hand-designed context
policy throughout training.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Models.

We conduct experiments with Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B ([Yang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib28)) as the base agent models to study self-evolution
across model scales. For frontier-guided context-policy evolution, we
use Claude-Opus-4.8 ([Anthropic, 2026](#bib.bib2)) as the frontier model
that proposes constrained modifications to the privileged harness
configuration. We access Claude through OpenCode ([OpenCode
Contributors, 2026](#bib.bib14)), which serves as the coding and
orchestration harness for generating and applying the proposed
configuration edits.

#### Benchmarks and Evaluation Metrics.

We evaluate RSD on three agent benchmarks: ALFWorld ([Shridhar et al.,
2020](#bib.bib19)), ScienceWorld ([Wang et al., 2022](#bib.bib22)), and
SearchQA ([Jin et al., 2025](#bib.bib10)). Together, these benchmarks
span embodied household interaction, scientific reasoning and
environment manipulation, and search-based question answering, providing
diverse settings for evaluating iterative agent self-evolution. For
ALFWorld, we report success rate (SR) on the valid seen and valid unseen
splits. For ScienceWorld, we report task success rate (SR) on the
development set. For SearchQA, we report exact match (EM). Additional
evaluation details are provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation Details ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation").

#### Baselines.

We compare RSD against several representative adaptation and
self-improvement baselines. Base model (Base) directly evaluates the
pretrained agent without additional adaptation, while Rejection Sampling
Fine-Tuning (RFT) ([Yuan et al., 2023](#bib.bib32)) fine-tunes the model
on its own successful trajectories. Ground-Truth Supervised Fine-Tuning
(GT-SFT) provides an oracle-supervision reference by fine-tuning on
ground-truth trajectories, while Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR) ([Zelikman
et al., 2022](#bib.bib33)) iteratively improves the model using
self-generated successful trajectories. We additionally consider
BoN-16+SFT, which samples 16 candidate trajectories per training
instance, selects successful trajectories using ground truth, and
fine-tunes on the resulting data. We further compare against Group
Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib17)),
which performs verifier-based reinforcement learning when an executable
reward is available, and Ground-Truth On-Policy Self-Distillation
(GT-OPSD) ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib37)), which replaces our
privileged context with gold-reference agent trajectories during
distillation. For test-time adaptation, we include Reflexion@2 ([Shinn
et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)), which allows a second attempt conditioned on
reflection from the preceding interaction, and Harness-at-Inference
(Harness@Inf), which directly supplies the privileged context to the
base model without post-training.

#### Implementation Details.

We run self-evolution for 10 rounds, sampling 200 unique training
instances in each round, and optimize the model using full-parameter
forward-KL distillation with a learning rate of $`3\times 10^{-6}`$.
Unless otherwise specified, RSD is evaluated using the evolved student
alone without privileged context at inference time; additional training,
decoding, and task-specific implementation details are provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation Details ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation").

#### Comparison with Reward-Driven and Oracle Baselines.

RSD remains competitive with methods that rely on substantially stronger
supervision. On ALFWorld-14B, GRPO and GT-OPSD achieve 82.6 and 82.5 SR,
respectively, compared with 79.6 for RSD. However, GRPO relies on
environment rewards and multiple grouped rollouts for policy
optimization, substantially increasing rollout cost, while GT-OPSD
explicitly conditions the teacher on a ground-truth successful agent
trajectory. In contrast, RSD learns from the agent’s own interaction
experience through privileged retrospective context, without requiring
oracle demonstrations or reward-driven group optimization. Notably, RSD
achieves the best result on ALFWorld-8B and substantially outperforms
the supervised baselines on ScienceWorld at both model scales.

#### Beyond Test-Time Adaptation.

We further compare against Reflexion@2, which allows a second attempt
conditioned on reflection from the preceding interaction, and
Harness@Inf, which directly provides privileged context to the base
model at inference time without updating model parameters. Under the
standard single-attempt evaluation protocol, RSD achieves higher point
estimates than Reflexion@2 across all six model–benchmark settings and
matches or exceeds Harness@Inf in five of six settings. For example, on
ScienceWorld-8B, Reflexion@2 and Harness@Inf achieve 17.8 and 19.6 SR,
respectively, compared with 23.4 for RSD. These results indicate that
privileged retrospective supervision can be internalized into persistent
model capability rather than remaining dependent on additional test-time
adaptation. A four-condition analysis comparing base and evolved models
with and without privileged context further examines the extent of this
internalization across tasks and model scales
(Appendix [D.2](#A4.SS2 "D.2 Internalization of Privileged Supervision ‣ Appendix D Supplementary Results ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation")).
SearchQA-14B provides an important boundary case, where Harness@Inf
reaches 41.4 compared with 38.7 for RSD, suggesting that some contextual
information remains complementary to the model rather than being fully
internalized.

## 6 Conclusion

We study self-evolving language agents that improve from their own
on-policy interaction experience without requiring ground-truth
trajectories or verifier-derived rewards for the core policy update. We
introduce RSD, which constructs privileged retrospective context from
accumulated experience and trajectory hindsight, and distills this
richer view back into the policy through on-policy self-distillation.
Across multiple agent benchmarks and model scales, RSD consistently
improves over the corresponding base models. Our ablation and
context-evolution analyses further show that different privileged
signals contribute complementary information and that continually
refreshing the retrospective context is important for sustained
self-evolution. We additionally introduce frontier-guided context-policy
evolution, allowing the construction of privileged supervision itself to
adapt as the agent evolves. Together, these results demonstrate a
practical data flywheel in which model capability, accumulated
experience, and retrospective supervision improve jointly across rounds.

## Appendix A Related Work

### A.1 Self-Evolving Language Agents

Self-evolving language agents aim to continuously improve from their own
interaction experience rather than remaining fixed after
post-training ([Gao et al., 2025](#bib.bib4); [Tao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib21)). One line of work primarily evolves the external
agent system, including prompts, workflows, memory, tools, and other
agentic structures ([Hu et al., 2025](#bib.bib8); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib35); [Li et al., 2024](#bib.bib12); [Wu et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib26); [Hao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib5)). Automated
agent-design methods search over workflow or program structures, while
experience-driven agents continually refine explicit memories, reusable
strategies, or dynamically constructed assets ([Yang et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib29); [Wu et al., 2025](#bib.bib25); [Wei et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23); [Cheng et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)). These approaches
demonstrate that accumulated interaction experience can improve future
behavior, but much of the acquired capability remains mediated by an
external scaffold rather than being persistently incorporated into the
underlying policy ([Lee et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)).

A complementary line of work directly adapts the agent model itself.
Recent approaches use online parameter updates, reinforcement learning,
self-generated tasks, reward models, or hindsight supervision to
internalize interaction experience into persistent model capability,
reducing dependence on external scaffolds and enabling subsequent
behavior to improve as new experience accumulates ([Zhai et al.,
2025](#bib.bib34); [Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [Ren et al.,
2026](#bib.bib16); [Wu et al., 2026a](#bib.bib24); [Hao et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib6); [Meng et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)). While these methods
move self-evolution from the scaffold toward model weights, many rely on
explicit verifiers, successful trajectories, or additional exploration
to determine which experience should drive learning ([Zuo et al.,
2026](#bib.bib39); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib36)). In contrast, we
construct a data flywheel that does not use verifier-derived rewards for
the core policy update, instead distilling privileged retrospective
supervision from both the current trajectory and accumulated
interactions directly into model weights, allowing the policy and its
supervision to co-evolve across rounds.

### A.2 On-Policy Distillation and Privileged Context

On-policy distillation (OPD) addresses the train–inference mismatch of
conventional knowledge distillation by training the student on
trajectories sampled from its own policy, while a teacher provides dense
token-level supervision on those student-visited states ([Agarwal
et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Yang et al., 2026](#bib.bib30); [Zhou et al.,
2026](#bib.bib38)). This formulation makes distillation naturally
compatible with iterative policy improvement, since supervision is
applied directly to the distribution induced by the evolving student.
Recent work extends this idea by changing what information is available
to the teacher. On-Policy Context Distillation (OPCD) conditions the
teacher on additional context while the student generates without it,
allowing useful in-context knowledge, such as historical solution traces
or optimized prompts, to be internalized into model parameters ([Ye
et al., 2026](#bib.bib31)). On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) further
removes the need for a separate teacher model: the same underlying model
acts as both teacher and student, with the teacher granted privileged
information unavailable to the student; existing formulations commonly
construct this privileged view from ground-truth solutions ([Zhao
et al., 2026](#bib.bib37); [Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)). Related
self-distillation methods similarly exploit verified solutions,
environment feedback, or successful rollouts as privileged
signals ([Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib9); [Pan et al.,
2026](#bib.bib15); [He et al., 2026](#bib.bib7); [Hao et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib6)).

In contrast, our core method requires neither ground-truth trajectories
nor verifier-derived rewards for model-parameter optimization. We
construct privileged retrospective context from the agent’s own current
trajectory and accumulated prior experience, and continually refresh
this context as new on-policy experience is collected, coupling context
evolution with model-weight evolution.

#### Training Configuration.

We perform self-evolution for 10 rounds and sample 200 training
instances in each round. Each round starts from the checkpoint produced
by the previous round, while the accumulated experience bank is carried
forward across rounds. We use full-parameter fine-tuning with forward-KL
top-$`k`$ distillation, where $`k=20`$, a learning rate of
$`3\times 10^{-6}`$, one training epoch per round, and a mini-batch size
of 48. No policy-gradient term or task-reward term is included in the
OPSD objective. All experiments are run on a single node with eight
NVIDIA H200 GPUs.

#### Rollout Configuration.

For ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, and SearchQA, the maximum numbers of
interaction turns are 30, 15, and 15, respectively. The maximum
prompt/response lengths are $`4096/8192`$ tokens for ALFWorld,
$`2048/16384`$ for ScienceWorld, and $`4096/4096`$ for SearchQA.
Training rollout temperatures are $`1.0`$ for ALFWorld, ScienceWorld,
and SearchQA, respectively, with one rollout per training instance. The
larger rollout group used by GRPO is restricted to the GRPO baseline and
is not used by RSD.

#### Teacher and Privileged-Context Configuration.

The teacher retrospectively re-scores the student’s on-policy
trajectories under privileged context and provides the next-token
distributions used for distillation. By default, the privileged context
consists of four components: precedents, accumulated lessons, turn-level
reflection, and future hindsight over the current trajectory. To
construct the precedent set, we first heuristically filter candidate
trajectories for successful or useful experiences. For ALFWorld and
ScienceWorld, success is determined by the environment completion
signal, while for SearchQA we retain answers grounded in the top
retrieved passages. We then rank the retained precedents by
content-token overlap with the current task goal and retrieve the top
two by default ($`n`$=2). For precedent ranking, we compute
content-token overlap between the current task goal and each candidate
trajectory. We lowercase and tokenize the text, remove punctuation and
non-content tokens such as stopwords, and rank candidates by the number
of remaining tokens shared with the task goal. Lessons accumulate
reusable insights from previous experience, while reflection and future
hindsight provide trajectory-specific retrospective information for the
current turn. Following the stabilization motivation of prior OPSD
work ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib37)), we prevent the teacher from
evolving indefinitely. The teacher initially tracks the evolving policy
and is frozen at the best-so-far checkpoint once the benchmark selection
metric decreases relative to its previous maximum; the frozen teacher is
then used for all remaining rounds. This choice is motivated by a
controlled teacher-schedule ablation in
Appendix [D.10](#A4.SS10 "D.10 Teacher-Schedule Ablation ‣ Appendix D Supplementary Results ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation"):
a permanently frozen teacher remains stable but exhibits limited
improvement, whereas a fully evolving self-teacher reaches substantially
higher performance before collapsing in later rounds. Unless otherwise
specified, all reported results use these configurations.

#### Evaluation Protocol.

For the main RSD evaluation, the evolved student is evaluated without
privileged context, such that the harness is used only to construct
training-time supervision. Privileged context is provided at inference
time only in explicitly labeled diagnostic settings, including
Harness@Inf, the internalization analysis, and frontier candidate
probes. For ALFWorld, we evaluate approximately 140 valid-seen and 134
valid-unseen tasks and report success rate (SR) on both splits. For
ScienceWorld, we evaluate 449 development variations and report task
success rate. For SearchQA, we evaluate on the test set and report exact
match (EM). Inference uses vLLM with fixed decoding settings within each
benchmark.

#### Frontier-Guided Context-Policy Evolution.

The context policy is implemented as a YAML harness configuration and
restricted to a predefined whitelist of context-policy and teacher-side
re-scoring parameters, including precedent count and retrieval strategy,
component activation, context position, and teacher temperature. The
frontier model is not permitted to modify the underlying training
algorithm or context-construction code. For frontier-guided
context-policy evolution, we set the retrospective acceptance tolerance
to $`\epsilon=0.5\%`$, such that a proposed context policy is retained
when its post-training environment performance is no more than $`0.5`$
percentage points below the best previously accepted configuration.

#### Baseline Implementations.

We implement all comparison methods within the same training framework
whenever possible and same training dataset size, changing only the
optimization mode or supervision source. GRPO disables distillation and
performs group-relative policy optimization with a rollout group size of
8 using environment success rewards. STaR iteratively fine-tunes the
agent on its own successful trajectories. GT-SFT uses oracle successful
trajectories for supervised fine-tuning, whereas GT-OPSD retains the
same on-policy distillation objective as RSD but conditions the teacher
on a gold successful trajectory instead of the experience-derived
privileged context. For frontier-guided context-policy evolution, the
Fixed Harness baseline keeps the canonical harness unchanged across
rounds, while Random Harness samples edits from the same whitelisted
YAML search space and under the same edit budget as the frontier-guided
optimizer.

## Appendix C Self-Evolution Algorithm

Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Appendix C Self-Evolution Algorithm ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation")
summarizes the complete training procedure. The core method uses a fixed
context-construction rule $`\mathcal{H}`$, while the frontier-guided
extension replaces it with the evolving policy
$`\mathcal{H}_{\phi^{(r)}}`$ described in
§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Frontier-Guided Context-Policy Evolution ‣ 3 Retrospective Self-Distillation (RSD) ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation").

0:  Initial policy $`\pi^{(1)}`$; task distribution $`\mathcal{X}`$;
number of rounds $`R`$; context-construction rule $`\mathcal{H}`$

1:  Initialize experience bank $`\mathcal{B}^{(0)}\leftarrow\emptyset`$

2:  for $`r=1,\ldots,R`$ do

3:   Sample a set of tasks $`\mathcal{X}^{(r)}\subset\mathcal{X}`$

4:   Collect one on-policy trajectory for each task:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{D}^{(r)}\leftarrow\left\{\tau\sim\pi^{(r)}(\cdot\mid x):x\in\mathcal{X}^{(r)}\right\}
``` |  |

5:   Update the experience bank:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{B}^{(r)}\leftarrow\mathcal{B}^{(r-1)}\cup\mathcal{D}^{(r)}
``` |  |

6:   Construct task-type lesson summaries from $`\mathcal{B}^{(r)}`$

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
L^{(r)}\leftarrow\operatorname{Summarize}_{\pi^{(r)}}(\mathcal{B}^{(r)})
``` |  |

7:   Initialize $`\mathcal{L}^{(r)}\leftarrow 0`$

8:   for each trajectory $`\tau\in\mathcal{D}^{(r)}`$ do

9:    Retrieve precedents $`P^{(r)}`$ from other tasks in
$`\mathcal{B}^{(r)}`$

10:    for each interaction turn $`i`$ in $`\tau`$ do

11:     Construct retrospective reflection:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R_{i}^{(r)}\leftarrow\operatorname{Reflect}_{\pi^{(r)}}(h_{i},\tau)
``` |  |

12:     Extract future hindsight:

|     |                                |     |
|-----|--------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       F_{i}^{(r)}\leftarrow\tau_{>i}
       ```                             |     |

13:     Construct privileged retrospective context:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
c_{i}^{(r)}\leftarrow\mathcal{H}\left(P^{(r)},L^{(r)},R_{i}^{(r)},F_{i}^{(r)}\right)
``` |  |

14:     for each token $`j`$ in action $`a_{i}`$ do

15:      Compute privileged teacher distribution:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q^{(r)}_{i,j}\leftarrow q^{(r)}\left(\cdot\mid h_{i,j},c_{i}^{(r)}\right)
``` |  |

16:      Compute student distribution:

|     |                                                             |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       p_{i,j}\leftarrow\pi_{\theta}\left(\cdot\mid h_{i,j}\right)
       ```                                                          |     |

17:      Accumulate OPSD loss:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}^{(r)}\leftarrow\mathcal{L}^{(r)}+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q^{(r)}_{i,j}\,\|\,p_{i,j}\right)
``` |  |

18:     end for

19:    end for

20:   end for

21:   Update the student on its own on-policy states:

|     |                                                               |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \pi^{(r+1)}\leftarrow\arg\min_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathcal{L}^{(r)}
       ```                                                            |     |

22:  end for

23:  return $`\pi^{(R+1)}`$

#### Frontier-Guided Extension.

For frontier-guided context-policy evolution, we replace the fixed
$`\mathcal{H}`$ in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Appendix C Self-Evolution Algorithm ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation")
with $`\mathcal{H}_{\phi^{(r)}}`$. Between self-evolution rounds, the
frontier model uses the current context policy, training diagnostics,
and no-training probe feedback to propose the next candidate
$`\tilde{\phi}^{(r+1)}`$. After the corresponding full training round,
the candidate is retained or reverted according to
Eq. [5](#S3.E5 "In Retrospective Acceptance. ‣ 3.2 Frontier-Guided Context-Policy Evolution ‣ 3 Retrospective Self-Distillation (RSD) ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation"),
with $`\epsilon=0.5\%`$. Only the context policy may be reverted; the
updated model parameters continue to the next self-evolution round.

### D.2 Internalization of Privileged Supervision

#### Internalization of Privileged Context.

We next test whether the benefit of privileged supervision is
transferred into the model itself rather than remaining dependent on
inference-time scaffolding. For each benchmark and model scale, we
evaluate four conditions: Base(no-C), the pretrained base model without
privileged context; Base(C), the same base model with privileged context
supplied at inference time; Evolved(no-C), the self-evolved model
evaluated without privileged context; and Evolved(C), the evolved model
additionally provided with privileged context at inference time. The key
internalization comparison is Evolved(no-C) versus Base(C): if the
evolved model without privileged context outperforms the base model with
privileged context, then useful information from privileged training has
been transferred into the model itself rather than remaining dependent
on inference-time context. As shown in
Fig. [3](#A4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ Internalization of Privileged Context. ‣ D.2 Internalization of Privileged Supervision ‣ Appendix D Supplementary Results ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation"),
ALFWorld exhibits strong internalization at both model scales:
Evolved(no-C) exceeds Base(C) by 14.5 and 8.9 percentage points for 8B
and 14B, respectively, while reintroducing the harness to the evolved
model provides only a small additional gain. ScienceWorld shows more
partial and scale-dependent internalization. At 8B, Evolved(no-C)
exceeds Base(C) by 3.8 points, while Evolved(C) provides a further
3.3-point improvement; at 14B, Evolved(no-C) is nearly identical to
Base(C), whereas Evolved(C) adds 6.1 points, indicating that much of the
privileged information remains complementary rather than being absorbed
into the model. SearchQA shows no measurable internalization, with
Evolved(no-C) failing to outperform Base(C), consistent with the limited
gains observed across self-evolution rounds. Together, these results
show that self-evolution can convert privileged retrospective
supervision into persistent model capability, but the extent of
internalization varies across tasks and scales.

and the subsequent policy $`\pi^{(r+1)}`$, while using the privileged
teacher distribution to identify actions that the teacher disfavors. If
a realized action is assigned lower probability by $`\pi^{(r+1)}`$ than

### D.5 Re-Scoring Analysis Details

#### Protocol.

We perform an offline re-scoring analysis on preserved consecutive
evolution checkpoints to examine how retrospective teacher corrections
are reflected in the subsequent policy. For each round pair
$`r\!\rightarrow\!r+1`$, we keep the round-$`r`$ trajectories and their
realized tokens fixed and evaluate the same tokens under both the
current policy $`\pi^{(r)}`$ and the subsequent policy $`\pi^{(r+1)}`$.
The privileged teacher distribution $`q^{(r)}`$ is used to identify
whether each realized action is preferred or disfavored by the
retrospective supervisor. Because the trajectories are fixed, this
analysis isolates changes in the policy distribution without introducing
additional rollout variation or training.

#### Teacher Re-Scoring.

We consider both proxy and privileged-context-conditioned teacher
distributions. The initial analysis uses the unconditioned base model as
a proxy teacher, while the privileged-context analysis conditions the
teacher on the round-specific retrospective context. For ALFWorld, the
corresponding precedent memory is reconstructed from the preserved
round-level experience bank. For the exact-context analysis, we
additionally log the teacher distributions produced during training,
allowing the same teacher targets to be evaluated directly without
reconstructing the context.

#### Metrics.

We measure uptake using complementary token-level statistics. First, we
compute the change in KL divergence between the teacher distribution and
the policy across consecutive checkpoints. Second, we measure the change
in probability assigned to the teacher’s top-1 token. Finally, we
measure the change in log probability of realized action tokens
disfavored by the teacher, where a negative value indicates that the
subsequent policy assigns lower probability to an action discouraged by
privileged supervision. Action tokens and ordinary language tokens are
analyzed separately.

#### Evaluated Round Pairs.

We analyze consecutive checkpoints from both ALFWorld and ScienceWorld,
including early and later stages of self-evolution. For the main
directional-correction analysis, we evaluate ALFWorld
$`r1\!\rightarrow\!r2`$ and $`r2\!\rightarrow\!r3`$, together with
ScienceWorld $`r1\!\rightarrow\!r2`$ and $`r5\!\rightarrow\!r6`$. The
exact-context analysis additionally uses teacher distributions logged
directly during training to avoid ambiguity introduced by context
reconstruction.

### D.6 Teacher Re-Scoring Diagnostics

#### Task-Specific Correction Profiles.

We additionally record per-turn teacher re-scoring statistics throughout
self-evolution. We define the correction gap as the difference between
the log probability of the teacher’s top-1 token and that assigned to
the student’s realized token; the correction rate as the fraction of
tokens for which the teacher top-1 prediction differs from the student’s
token; teacher entropy as the average token-level entropy of the
privileged teacher distribution over supervised action tokens; and the
hit rate as the fraction of student tokens contained in the teacher’s
top-$`k`$ support used for distillation. ALFWorld exhibits relatively
small corrections throughout evolution, with correction gaps around
$`0.2`$–$`0.3`$ and correction rates below approximately $`7\%`$.
ScienceWorld exhibits substantially larger corrections, with the 8B
correction gap increasing from $`0.809`$ at round 1 to $`1.420`$ at
round 7 and correction rates around $`0.4`$–$`0.55`$. SearchQA instead
shows moderate but comparatively stable correction gaps of approximately
$`0.5`$ after the initial round. Across all three benchmarks, the
teacher top-$`k`$ hit rate remains approximately $`0.97`$–$`1.00`$,
indicating that the truncated distillation targets retain high coverage
of the student’s realized tokens. These task-specific correction
profiles are consistent with the distinct evolution dynamics observed in
the main experiments, although the correction gap alone does not
establish whether the subsequent policy absorbs the corresponding
supervision.

### D.8 Frontier Probe Construction and Validation

#### No-Training Context Probe.

To provide the frontier model with an inexpensive empirical signal for
ranking candidate context-policy edits, we use a no-training
privileged-context-at-inference probe. For each candidate configuration,
we render the corresponding privileged context using the same
context-construction pipeline used during training, inject it into the
model at inference time, and evaluate task success on a small
training-distribution slice. The resulting success rate is used as a
proxy for the utility of the candidate context policy, requiring only
inference and no parameter updates. To reduce ranking noise, probe
evaluation uses greedy decoding ($`T=0`$).

#### Validation on ScienceWorld.

We validate the probe on ScienceWorld, where the controlled context
ablation indicates substantial headroom from privileged-context
construction. In particular, removing precedents decreases dev SR from
$`23.4`$ to $`15.1`$, providing a known context-quality ordering against
which the probe can be evaluated. Using four representative context
configurations, the no-training probe produces SRs of $`25.6`$,
$`23.1`$, $`18.0`$, and $`13.3`$ for the full, no-lessons, base, and
no-precedents configurations, respectively. The corresponding
full-training evaluation obtains $`23.4`$, $`22.7`$, $`15.6`$, and
$`15.1`$. Thus, the probe recovers the same ordering across these
configurations, with Spearman correlation $`1.000`$ and Pearson
correlation $`0.943`$. This provides evidence that the no-training probe
supplies a useful ranking signal for context-policy search in a setting
with meaningful context headroom.

#### Deterministic Evaluation.

We found that stochastic decoding can introduce substantial variance
into the probe score and obscure relatively small differences between
candidate configurations. We therefore use greedy decoding for all
frontier candidate probes, while retaining the standard benchmark
decoding configuration for the corresponding full-round evaluations. For
example, on ScienceWorld the greedy precedent-count probe yields SRs of
$`19.2`$, $`22.9`$, $`20.9`$, and $`20.0`$ for counts $`0`$–$`3`$,
respectively, correctly identifying that introducing precedents is
beneficial while also indicating that more context is not necessarily
better.

#### Context Fidelity.

Because the probe is intended to estimate the utility of the same
privileged information used during distillation, we ensure that
probe-time context rendering matches the training-time format. We audit
the context-construction paths and verify byte-identical rendered notes
across tested tasks, precedent counts, and lesson settings after
resolving an earlier formatting mismatch. This ensures that the probe
evaluates the actual privileged-context representation used by the
teacher rather than an independently reconstructed approximation.

Table [6](#A4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Empirical Feedback Enables Frontier Optimization. ‣ D.8 Frontier Probe Construction and Validation ‣ Appendix D Supplementary Results ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation"),
removing the probe reduces the ScienceWorld-8B SR from 27.5 to 23.9,
placing the diagnostics-only frontier, which relies only on
previous-round checkpoint accuracy and training metrics, in
approximately the same regime as the fixed (23.4) and random-search
(23.2) controls. Thus, access to a frontier model alone is insufficient
to reliably optimize privileged-context construction; the empirical
probe provides a task-grounded signal that allows the frontier model to
distinguish promising context edits from plausible but ineffective
alternatives.

### D.10 Teacher-Schedule Ablation

The default training configuration uses an adaptive freeze-to-peak
teacher. To isolate the role of this schedule, we compare two endpoint
strategies under the same fixed canonical context policy using Qwen3-8B
on ALFWorld dataset over ten self-evolution rounds. In the frozen
variant, the teacher remains the initial base model throughout training.
In the fully evolving variant, the teacher is updated to the current
student after every round, with freeze-to-peak disabled. These two
configurations respectively isolate a completely stationary teacher and
an unconstrained co-evolving self-teacher.

| Frozen           | 65.0 | 68.2 | 63.5 | 65.7 | 63.5 | 65.7 | 62.4 | 67.5 | 67.1 | 67.9 | 68.2 | 67.9  |

fixed canonical context policy. We report mean success rate (%) over

Table [9](#A4.T9 "Table 9 ‣ D.10 Teacher-Schedule Ablation ‣ Appendix D Supplementary Results ‣ Self-Evolving Agents through Privileged Retrospective Distillation"),
the frozen teacher remains within a narrow performance range throughout
all ten rounds, reaching a peak mean success rate of $`68.2\%`$ and
ending at $`67.9\%`$. In contrast, the fully evolving teacher benefits
strongly from co-evolution during the early rounds, reaching $`80.3\%`$

$`76.2\%`$ at round 8 to $`39.4\%`$ at round 9 and $`0\%`$ at round 10.

allowing the teacher to track the student initially provides adaptive
supervision, while freezing the best validation checkpoint once
performance deteriorates prevents the teacher from following the student
into late-stage collapse. We view this experiment as a diagnostic of the
teacher-update mechanism rather than a statistically powered benchmark
comparison, since each endpoint schedule is evaluated with a single
seed.
````

# RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport

Dongyue Li¹, Zebang Cheng², Zhongyi Deng³, Hongbo Xu⁴, Yi Bin⁵, Fei
Ma^(†,4)  
¹ Nagoya University  ² Shenzhen University ³ Sichuan Agricultural
University ⁴ Guangdong Laboratory of Artificial Intelligence and Digital
Economy ⁵ Tongji University

###### Abstract

Sequential recommendation predicts a user’s next interaction from
historical behavior. Recent LLM-based methods recast this task as
generative recommendation and increasingly adopt reinforcement learning
such as GRPO for post-training. However, these methods suffer from two
limitations, namely credit-blind optimization, where a sequence-level
reward is shared across all generated tokens, and collaborative-blind
supervision, where each training signal is derived from a single user
and ignores behavioral patterns shared across similar users. To address
both issues, we propose Retrieval-Privileged on-policy Self-Distillation
(RPSD). For credit-blind optimization, we introduce on-policy
self-distillation (OPSD), in which a teacher with privileged knowledge
re-evaluates the student’s on-policy response, and the token-level
confidence difference between the student and teacher reshapes the
shared sequence-level advantage into token-specific credit. However, the
privileged knowledge in vanilla OPSD is still derived from a single
user, leaving collaborative-blind supervision unresolved. We therefore
ground the teacher in collaborative behavioral evidence from similar
users. Specifically, we use temporally aware optimal transport (OT) to
compare interaction trajectories across users by jointly considering
item similarity and temporal structure, and further apply
diversity-aware selection to retain complementary trajectories as
privileged evidence. In this way, on-policy self-distillation provides
fine-grained token-level credit, while OT enriches the teacher with
structured cross-user evidence. To the best of our knowledge, this is
the first work to bring on-policy self-distillation to sequential
recommendation and to use cross-user behavior as its privileged signal.
Extensive experiments across multiple real-world datasets demonstrate
that RPSD achieves state-of-the-art performance.

## 1 Introduction

Sequential recommendation predicts the next item that a user will
interact with from the ordered history of past interactions and it is
central to content, e-commerce, and media platforms [Ma et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib43) where user interests change over time ([Hidasi et
al., 2016](#bib.bib5); [Kang and McAuley, 2018](#bib.bib6)). Traditional
methods treat the sequential recommendation problem as next-item
prediction over an interaction sequence, evolving from recurrent and
self-attention encoders ([Hidasi et al., 2016](#bib.bib5); [Kang and
McAuley, 2018](#bib.bib6)) to graph-based and contrastive variants,
which model higher-order transitions and augment sparse signals ([He et
al., 2020](#bib.bib7); [Xie et al., 2022](#bib.bib8); [Qiu et al.,
2022](#bib.bib20)). Although these models are effective and efficient,
they represent items as opaque identifier embeddings and reason over
co-occurrence statistics rather than the semantics of what a user
consumed, which limits generalization when histories are short. An ideal
recommender, however, should do more than name which candidate comes
next and it should ground that choice in why the recommended item
matches the user’s evolving interest.

Large language models (LLMs) address the semantic side of this gap by
casting recommendation as language generation, either unifying
recommendation tasks in a text-to-text format ([Geng et al.,
2022](#bib.bib21); [Rajput et al., 2023](#bib.bib22); [Wang et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib37); [Li et al., 2026](#bib.bib38); [Kong et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib40)) or fine-tuning a pretrained LLM to reason over item
titles and name the next item directly in text ([Bao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib13); [Liao et al., 2024](#bib.bib4); [Zhang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23)). To align such an LLM with the recommendation
objective, reinforcement learning has become a standard post-training
recipe. Recent work adapts GRPO ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) to
LLM-based recommendation, tailoring its reward and credit assignment to
the ranking structure of the task. For example, Rank-GRPO ([Zhu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib26)) moves the reward from the whole sequence to each
individual rank, so that later positions in the list get their own
credit instead of sharing one signal. RecZero ([Kong et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib15)) keeps GRPO unchanged but reshapes the rollout into
explicit analyze-user, analyze-item, match, and rate steps, replacing
exact-match correctness with a reward graded by the predicted rating’s
closeness to the true one. However, these adaptations only change which
part of a response the reward is applied to. Rank-GRPO gives each rank
its own reward and RecZero gives each reasoning step its own, but in
both cases a single number is still shared by all the tokens inside that
rank or step. The source of the reward is left untouched as well.
Whether it is computed over the sequence, the rank, or the step, it
always comes from the next item of the user being scored. A reward of
this kind can confirm that a recommendation is right, but one user’s
interactions are a thin basis for learning why, since a short history
reflects accident as much as preference. The same pattern observed
across many users with comparable histories is far more dependable, and
it is the only evidence available when a single history is too short to
be read on its own. Therefore, two problems remain unaddressed, namely
credit-blind optimization and collaborative-blind supervision.

Credit-blind optimization concerns how the reward is assigned. Not every
token in a response contributes equally to the recommendation. Most
tokens provide shared linguistic or formatting structure, while only a
small subset determines which item is ultimately selected. GRPO
nevertheless assigns the same sequence-level advantage to every
generated token, providing no indication of which tokens actually make
the recommendation correct. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) ([Zhao et
al., 2026](#bib.bib18)) provides a promising direction for addressing
this limitation. By deriving token-level guidance from a more informed
teacher on the same on-policy response, OPSD enables finer-grained
credit assignment while preserving the original policy and inference
procedure. These properties make it particularly appealing for
sequential recommendation. However, the effectiveness of OPSD is
ultimately limited by the informativeness of the teacher’s privileged
knowledge. If the teacher lacks sufficient behavioral context, the
resulting token-level guidance may still be inadequate for capturing the
preference signals underlying the target item. Vanilla OPSD typically
relies on the ground-truth label as the privileged signal, which tells
the model _which_ candidate is correct but provides limited information
about _why_ that candidate matches the user’s preference. Therefore, the
second issue collaborative-blind supervision is introduced. Existing
generative recommenders derive supervision from the user alone,
overlooking regularities shared across similar users.

To address these two limitations, we propose Retrieval-Privileged
on-policy Self-Distillation (RPSD), which combines OPSD with temporally
aware OT. RPSD first constructs cross-user behavioral evidence from the
training corpus. Specifically, we use temporally aware OT to compare
interaction trajectories across users by jointly modeling item
similarity and temporal structure, followed by diversity-aware selection
to retain complementary trajectories. Their subsequent interactions,
together with the ground-truth item, form the privileged knowledge
available to the teacher. The teacher then re-evaluates the student’s
on-policy response under this privileged view, and the resulting
token-level confidence difference is used to reshape the sequence-level
advantage into token-specific credit. The privileged knowledge is used
only during training, leaving inference unchanged and introducing no
additional deployment cost. As shown in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport"),
the retrieved behavioral evidence further strengthens teacher
supervision and leads to improved training dynamics. To the best of our
knowledge, RPSD is the first method to introduce OPSD into sequential
recommendation and to use cross-user behavior as privileged knowledge.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

(1) RPSD, a novel on-policy self-distillation framework, is proposed for
sequential recommendation to enable fine-grained token-level credit
assignment through privileged teacher guidance.

(2) A temporally aware optimal transport mechanism is introduced to
align similar user trajectories, from which cross-user evidence is
constructed as privileged knowledge.

(3) Extensive experiments on three benchmark datasets are conducted to
demonstrate the effectiveness of RPSD and its consistent superiority
over strong state-of-the-art baselines.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Reinforcement Learning for LLM-based Recommendation

Casting recommendation ([Wang et al., 2025a](#bib.bib37); [Li et al.,
2026](#bib.bib38); [Ju et al., 2025](#bib.bib39); [Kong et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib40); [Wang et al., 2025b](#bib.bib41)) as language
generation lets LLMs reason over item semantics rather than opaque
identifiers ([Geng et al., 2022](#bib.bib21); [Rajput et al.,
2023](#bib.bib22); [Bao et al., 2023](#bib.bib13); [Liao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib4); [Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib23); [Lin et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib24)), and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
has become a standard recipe for aligning such models with the
recommendation objective ([Shao et al., 2024](#bib.bib19); [Lin et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib25); [Kong et al., 2025a](#bib.bib15); [Wang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib35); [Yu et al., 2024](#bib.bib36)). Since final-answer
rewards are sparse, lots of works adapt GRPO to the ranking structure of
the task. Rank-GRPO makes each rank, rather than the token or the whole
sequence, the unit of reward to remove non-causal credit
assignment ([Zhu et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)), and RecZero restructures
the rollout into explicit reasoning steps supervised by a graded
rule-based reward ([Kong et al., 2025a](#bib.bib15)). Parallel works
pursue the same goal through preference objectives, where S-DPO extends
DPO with multiple negatives ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib31)) and C-APO
augments the revealed-preference ordering with a coherence-based
ordering to down-weight noisy user choices ([Park et al.,
2026](#bib.bib32)). These methods differ in what unit the reward is
computed over and how the preference ordering is shaped, yet the signal
they supply still scores whether a recommendation is right rather than
which tokens carry the evidence for it. We instead densify the signal to
the token level while grounding the label in retrieved behavior.

### 2.2 On-policy Self-Distillation

On-policy distillation ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib27)) (OPD)
supervises a student on its own sampled trajectories with dense
token-level targets from a teacher, combining the density of
distillation with the distributional match of on-policy learning ([Gu et
al., 2024](#bib.bib28)). OPSD removes the external teacher by letting a
single model act as both student and teacher under different contexts,
where the teacher conditions on privileged information the student
cannot see and re-scores the student’s tokens, turning a sparse outcome
reward into dense supervision ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)). A
recognized limitation, however, is that conditioning on an
instance-level label lets the teacher rationalize the answer without
grounding it in the input, collapsing its signal into a shortcut ([Cheng
et al., 2026](#bib.bib33)). These extensions all draw their privilege
from a label or a stronger model, and none targets recommendation. To
the best of our knowledge, we are the first to introduce OPSD to
sequential recommendation and to ground label-based privileged
information in retrieved behavioral evidence from similar users.

### 2.3 Retrieval-Augmented Recommendation and Knowledge Distillation

Retrieval-augmented recommendation enriches sparse histories with
behaviorally similar users, where ReLLa ([Lin et al., 2024](#bib.bib29))
retrieves relevant behaviors to improve long-history comprehension and
RALLRec ([Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)) strengthens the retrieval
through representation learning. However, the retrieved neighbors are
fed to these two recommenders at inference, so retrieval quality
directly shapes the deployed prediction. We instead use the
continuations of similar users only as a training-time privileged
signal, leaving inference to the ordinary prompt. Knowledge distillation
transfers a teacher’s ranking ability to a smaller student, as in
RCE-KD ([Zhu and Zhang, 2026](#bib.bib34)), which reconnects the
cross-entropy distillation loss for tighter ranking transfer. Our RPSD
uses the same model as both teacher and student, with the teacher
additionally conditioned on retrieved behavioral evidence and the
ground-truth item to provide privileged supervision only during
training.

## 3 Method

We present RPSD, a retrieval-privileged on-policy self-distillation
framework for LLM-based sequential recommendation. The student samples a
recommendation from the task prompt, while a teacher, which is the same
model under a privileged context, re-scores the same tokens and provides
dense token-level credit. The privileged context combines two pieces of
training-only information: the ground-truth next item, which tells the
teacher _which_ candidate is correct, and behavioral evidence retrieved
from similar users, which grounds _why_ it fits the current history.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

In a general sequential recommendation scenario, we consider a user set
$`\mathcal{U}`$ and an item set $`\mathcal{V}`$. Given the interaction
sequence $`S_{u}=\{v^{u}_{1},v^{u}_{2},\dots,v^{u}_{t}\}`$ of a user
$`u\in\mathcal{U}`$, where $`v^{u}_{j}\in\mathcal{V}`$ is the item
interacted with at step $`j`$, the goal is to predict the next item
$`v^{u}_{t+1}`$ the user is most likely to engage with.

We take the most recent $`L`$ interactions of $`S_{u}`$ as the context
$`H_{u}`$ and construct a candidate set
$`\mathcal{C}_{u}=\{v^{u}_{t+1}\}\cup\mathcal{N}_{u}`$ of size
$`n_{c}`$, where $`\mathcal{N}_{u}`$ contains negatives sampled from the
user’s non-interacted items. We serialize $`(H_{u},\mathcal{C}_{u})`$
into a student prompt
$`x_{u}^{S}\;=\;\mathrm{Prompt}_{S}(H_{u},\mathcal{C}_{u})`$. For
training, we additionally build a teacher prompt over the same instance
that exposes $`x_{u}^{S}`$ to retrieved behavioral evidence $`E_{u}`$
and the ground-truth item $`v^{u}_{t+1}`$, forming a privileged view
used solely to shape the training signal,
$`x_{u}^{T}\;=\;\mathrm{Prompt}_{T}(H_{u},\mathcal{C}_{u},E_{u},v^{u}_{t+1})`$.
Given $`x_{u}^{S}`$, the policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ generates a response
$`y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T})`$ that must follow the correct item format and
predict the right next item $`v^{u}_{t+1}`$. Training follows a
two-stage paradigm, where the pretrained LLM is first adapted to the
instruction format by SFT, after which RPSD is leveraged for policy
optimization. At inference time, the model observes only $`x_{u}^{S}`$
and has no access to $`E_{u}`$ or $`v^{u}_{t+1}`$.

### 3.2 Retrieval-Based Privileged Evidence

In addition to revealing the label, we equip the teacher with behavioral
evidence. For each user, we retrieve similar interaction histories from
_other_ users and summarize their subsequent behavior, which the teacher
reads as privileged information.

Given the current user, we retrieve behaviorally similar history windows
from a pool built over all training users and read how those users
continued. Concretely, we slide a length-$`K`$ window over every
training user sequence to build a shared pool of history windows
$`\mathcal{W}=\{(h_{j},v_{j}^{+},u_{j})\}_{j=1}^{M}`$ with
$`h_{j}=(v^{\prime}_{j,1},\ldots,v^{\prime}_{j,K})`$, where $`h_{j}`$ is
a user’s recent history, $`v_{j}^{+}`$ is the item that user watched
next, and $`u_{j}`$ is the user. Each item is encoded from the _title
text_ into a unit vector $`e_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}`$, and a length-$`K`$
history $`H=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{K-1})`$ is summarized by a recency-weighted
vector

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\bar{e}(H)=\frac{\sum_{p}a_{p}\cdot e_{v_{p}}}{\left\|\sum_{p}a_{p}\cdot e_{v_{p}}\right\|_{2}},\qquad a_{p}=\frac{\exp\!\left[-\beta\cdot(K-1-p)\right]}{\sum_{r}\exp\!\left[-\beta\cdot(K-1-r)\right]},
``` |  | (1) |

where a larger $`\beta`$ assigns smaller weights to earlier interactions
in the user’s history, i.e., interactions that are farther from the most
recent position, and $`p=0`$ denotes the earliest position. We use this
vector only for a coarse recall stage that selects top-$`N_{0}`$ windows
by cosine $`\langle\bar{e}(H_{u}),\bar{e}(h_{j})\rangle`$, and then
refine these shortlisted windows by OT re-ranking.

Let $`H_{u}^{K}=(v^{u}_{t-K+1},\dots,v^{u}_{t})`$ denote the $`K`$ most
recent items of $`H_{u}`$. We score each window $`h_{j}`$ by aligning
$`h_{j}`$ with $`H_{u}^{K}`$ through unbalanced entropic OT. We use the
query mass $`a=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{K-1})`$ from Eq.
([1](#S3.E1 "In 3.2 Retrieval-Based Privileged Evidence ‣ 3 Method ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport")),
and the window mass $`b`$ is computed by the same rule. The pairwise
cost is defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
C_{pq}=1-\langle e_{v_{p}},e_{v^{\prime}_{q}}\rangle+\gamma\cdot\left|\tfrac{p}{K-1}-\tfrac{q}{K-1}\right|,
``` |  | (2) |

whose first term measures semantic dissimilarity and second term
penalizes temporally inconsistent alignments with $`\gamma\geq 0`$. We
then solve

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{OT}_{\varepsilon,\rho}(a,b;C)=\min_{P\geq 0}\,\langle P,C\rangle+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{KL}(P\,\|\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top})+\rho\big[\mathrm{KL}(P\mathbf{1}\,\|\,a)+\mathrm{KL}(P^{\top}\mathbf{1}\,\|\,b)\big]
``` |  | (3) |

with the Sinkhorn algorithm, where $`\varepsilon`$ controls entropic
smoothing and $`\rho`$ relaxes the two marginals. To make the transport
costs comparable across windows with different transported masses, we
normalize the optimal transport plan $`P^{\ast}`$ by the transported
mass and then map $`P^{\ast}`$ to a similarity
$`s(H_{u}^{K},h_{j})=\exp\!\big(-\langle P^{\ast},C\rangle/(\|P^{\ast}\|_{1}\,\tau)\big)`$.
Finally, we greedily select a diverse set $`\mathcal{S}`$ of windows
until $`|\mathcal{S}|=3`$,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
j^{\ast}=\arg\max_{j\notin\mathcal{S}}\big[s_{j}-\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}\cdot\max_{k\in\mathcal{S}}\langle\bar{e}(h_{j}),\bar{e}(h_{k})\rangle\big],
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}`$ trades off relevance and diversity.
Each selected window is turned into one line of natural language that
lists the user’s $`K`$ recent items, then the item they interacted with
next, tagged with the similarity $`s_{j}`$. Stacking these lines forms
the evidence block $`E_{u}`$, which tells the teacher how users with
histories like the current one actually continued.

### 3.3 Retrieval-Privileged on-policy Self-Distillation

Building on the retrieved evidence $`E_{u}`$ in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Retrieval-Based Privileged Evidence ‣ 3 Method ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport"),
we introduce RPSD, which addresses the uniform credit of GRPO and the
label-only privilege of vanilla OPSD. Specifically, the *same* model
acts as both student and teacher. The student samples responses from the
ordinary prompt $`x^{S}`$. The teacher re-scores the same responses
under the privileged prompt
$`x^{T}=x^{S}\oplus E_{u}\oplus v_{t+1}^{u}`$, where the label tells it
*which* candidate is correct and $`E_{u}`$ shows *why* it fits the
current history. The resulting token-level confidence gap is used only
to redistribute the GRPO advantage across tokens, so the direction of
each update is still determined by the verifiable reward. Following
GRPO, we sample $`G`$ responses $`\{o_{1},\ldots,o_{G}\}`$ under
$`x^{S}`$ and assign each a rule-based reward $`r_{i}`$: $`+1`$ for a
valid format with the correct item, $`0`$ for a valid format with a
wrong item, and $`-1`$ for an invalid format. The response-level
advantage is the group-normalized reward

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\widehat{A}_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\operatorname{mean}(\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})}{\operatorname{std}(\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{G})},
``` |  | (5) |

which standard GRPO shares uniformly across all tokens of $`o_{i}`$. We
score every sampled token under both contexts with stop-gradient and
take their difference

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{i,t}=\log\pi_{\operatorname{sg}(\theta)}\!\left(o_{i,t}\mid x^{T},o_{i,<t}\right)-\log\pi_{\operatorname{sg}(\theta)}\!\left(o_{i,t}\mid x^{S},o_{i,<t}\right),
``` |  | (6) |

so a positive $`\Delta_{i,t}`$ means the evidence makes token
$`o_{i,t}`$ more plausible. Since the evidence shifts the whole sequence
likelihood, $`\Delta_{i,t}`$ is centered and clipped as
$`\delta_{i,t}=\operatorname{clip}(\Delta_{i,t}-\tfrac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t^{\prime}}\Delta_{i,t^{\prime}},-c_{\delta},c_{\delta})`$,
with stability threshold $`c_{\delta}`$. We then convert this centered
gap into an advantage-aware token weight

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
w_{i,t}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\exp\!\left[\operatorname{sign}(\widehat{A}_{i})\cdot\delta_{i,t}\right],\,1-\epsilon_{w},\,1+\epsilon_{w}\right),
``` |  | (7) |

so that tokens with stronger teacher support are reinforced more when
$`\widehat{A}_{i}>0`$, and tokens the teacher also disfavors receive
stronger negative credit when $`\widehat{A}_{i}<0`$, with
$`\epsilon_{w}`$ bounding the guidance strength. The shared advantage is
then refined into a token-specific one
$`\widetilde{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_{i}\left[(1-\lambda)+\lambda w_{i,t}\right]`$,
where $`\lambda\in[0,1]`$ sets the strength of privileged guidance, and
$`\lambda=0`$ or $`\epsilon_{w}=0`$ recovers standard GRPO. The final
objective of our RPSD is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{\textsc{RPSD}{}}}=-\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}\min\!\left(\rho_{i,t}\cdot\widetilde{A}_{i,t},\,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\cdot\widetilde{A}_{i,t}\right)\right]+\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}},
``` |  | (8) |

where
$`\rho_{i,t}=\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}\mid x^{S},o_{i,<t})/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid x^{S},o_{i,<t})`$
is the token-level importance ratio, $`\epsilon`$ is the clipping range,
and $`\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}`$ regularizes toward the reference model
as in GRPO. At inference, the model conditions solely on $`x^{S}`$, so
the evidence is used only during training to reshape token credit.

## 4 Experiments

Extensive experiments are conducted to answer six questions. RQ1: How
does RPSD perform against state-of-the-art sequential recommendation
methods? RQ2: Does each design of RPSD contribute to performance
improvement? RQ3: How do the design choices in constructing
retrieval-based privileged evidence affect performance? RQ4: How well
does RPSD generalize across domains? RQ5: How does RPSD behave during
training? RQ6: Does RPSD maintain its performance gains throughout
training?

Datasets and Metrics. We evaluate RPSD on three widely used benchmarks,
namely MovieLens ([Harper and Konstan, 2015](#bib.bib1)), Netflix ([Wei
et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)), and MicroLens ([Ni et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)).
Each item is represented by its ID and title. Following the standard
protocol ([He et al., 2020](#bib.bib7)), we adopt hit ratio (HR@$`k`$)
and normalized discounted cumulative gain (NDCG@$`k`$, abbreviated as
NG@$`k`$) with $`k\in\{3,5\}`$. During training, the model is optimized
to predict the single next item, which provides a deterministic output
for the rule-based reward. At evaluation, rank-based metrics require a
ranked list, so the model instead outputs the $`k`$ items it considers
most likely. For each test sequence, the ground-truth next item is mixed
with $`n_{c}-1`$ randomly sampled negatives to form a candidate set, and
the Top-3 and Top-5 lists are generated independently for each $`k`$.
The performance gains of RPSD are statistically significant under a
paired $`t`$-test ($`p<0.05`$).

### 4.1 Performance Comparison Against SOTA methods (RQ1)

Table [1](#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 3.3 Retrieval-Privileged on-policy Self-Distillation ‣ 3 Method ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport")
reports the comparison under the standard setting ($`n_{c}=10`$), from
which we draw several observations. First, RPSD performs strongly
against both Non-LLM and LLM-based baselines. Notably, simply replacing
conventional recommenders with an LLM does not consistently yield large
gains. Qwen3-4B + SFT remains comparable to, and in some cases weaker
than, strong Non-LLM baselines. This suggests that the semantic prior of
an LLM alone is not sufficient for accurate recommendation. Clear
improvements emerge after reinforcement learning, indicating that
performance depends not only on understanding item semantics and user
preference, but also on aligning the model with the ranking objective
through task-specific feedback.

Second, RPSD consistently outperforms GRPO with the same backbone,
showing that the gain cannot be attributed to increased model capacity.
The improvement instead comes from how the training signal is
constructed and assigned. RPSD grounds the teacher in retrieved
behavioral evidence from similar users and uses the resulting
teacher–student confidence gap to redistribute the sequence-level
advantage at the token level. This allows the optimization to focus more
on decision-relevant tokens while grounding the target in cross-user
behavioral patterns.

To evaluate RPSD under a more challenging ranking setting, we further
enlarge the candidate set to $`n_{c}=100`$ by sampling additional
negatives, with results reported in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport").
As the candidate pool grows, distinguishing the target from many
competing items becomes substantially harder, because the model needs to
make a more fine-grained ranking decision rather than separating the
target from only a few alternatives. The retrieved evidence enlarges the
context that the teacher sees, while token-level credit concentrates the
update on the parts of the response that determine the final choice.
Consequently, RPSD remains effective as the ranking ambiguity increases.

### 4.2 Ablation Study on the contribution of each proposed module (RQ2)

In this subsection, we compare three variants to examine the effect of
each proposed module. All variants share the same backbone Qwen3-4B.
RPSD w/o OT denotes the model that removes retrieved evidence, keeping
only the ground-truth item as the privileged knowledge. RPSD (Full)
denotes the full model we propose.
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.1 Performance Comparison Against SOTA methods (RQ1) ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport")
shows that RPSD w/o OT improves recommendation performance even when the
teacher receives only the ground-truth item as privileged information.
With the same reward signal as GRPO, the teacher enables the model to
reassign token-level advantage, so that optimization focuses more on the
tokens that determine the final recommendation. Since the token weights
never change the sign of the advantage, this gain comes purely from
redistributing credit within each response rather than from a stronger
or different reward. This confirms that credit-blind optimization is a
real bottleneck of GRPO in recommendation.

The full RPSD matches or outperforms this variant across all metrics,
showing that retrieved evidence provides useful guidance beyond the
label itself. When the teacher is conditioned only on the ground-truth
label, it knows which item is correct but receives little information
about why that item matches the user’s history. As a result, the
resulting guidance provides only a weak bridge between the observed
history and the target recommendation. In contrast, behavioral evidence
from similar users exposes concrete preference patterns.

### 4.4 Cross-domain Evaluation (RQ4)

We evaluate cross-domain generalization by training all methods on
MovieLens and testing them on MicroLens and Netflix. As shown in
Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4.3 Ablation Study on different designs of retrieved evidence (RQ3) ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport"),
RPSD achieves the best performance across all metrics on MicroLens,
showing that its retrieval-privileged token-level guidance transfers
well to this target domain. On Netflix, however, SFT remains slightly
stronger than both post-training methods. GRPO shows a similar drop,
suggesting that the negative transfer of RPSD is not caused specifically
by the privileged teacher. Instead, it may come from source-domain
specialization introduced during reinforcement-learning post-training.
Overall, these results suggest that the transferability of RPSD depends
on how compatible the source and target domains are.

### 4.5 Training diagnostics of RPSD (RQ5)

Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.4 Cross-domain Evaluation (RQ4) ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport")
tracks the training dynamics of RPSD. The reward increases steadily over
training, and the within-group standard deviation decreases, showing
that the sampled responses become progressively more consistent. This
indicates stable policy optimization. To measure how confident the
student is, we present the token entropy
$`\mathcal{H}=-\mathbb{E}_{i,t}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\pi_{i,t}(x)\log\pi_{i,t}(x)`$,
where $`\pi_{i,t}(\cdot)=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x^{S},o_{i,<t})`$ is the
student’s next-token distribution, $`\mathcal{X}`$ is the token
vocabulary, and the expectation is taken over all tokens in the sampled
group. A lower $`\mathcal{H}`$ means the student places its probability
on fewer tokens. As shown in
Figure [4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 4.4 Cross-domain Evaluation (RQ4) ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport")
(b), $`\mathcal{H}`$ falls quickly during training, which means the
student gradually commits to the few tokens. The teacher–student gap
also decreases, so privileged guidance is most useful in the early
stage, when the student is still uncertain. As training continues, the
student becomes more confident. Interestingly, the gap stays clearly
above zero even after $`\mathcal{H}`$ has nearly flattened. A confident
student is therefore not yet fully aligned with the teacher, and the
retrieved evidence keeps providing token-level signal late in training
rather than fading away with the uncertainty. The KL divergence to the
reference model rises early and then stabilizes.

### 4.6 Performance Across Training Steps (RQ6)

Figure [5](#S4.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 4.4 Cross-domain Evaluation (RQ4) ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport")
evaluates the performance of SFT, GRPO, and RPSD at different training
steps. RPSD outperforms GRPO from the first evaluated checkpoint and
maintains this advantage throughout training. This shows that its
benefits appear early and persist until convergence. The larger gains in
NG than in HR suggest that RPSD not only retrieves the target item more
often but also ranks it higher. These results support the effectiveness
of using collaborative evidence to assign token-level credit. In
contrast, SFT shows little improvement over training. Since all three
methods share the same backbone, the stronger performance of RPSD can be
attributed to our designs.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we propose RPSD to address credit-blind optimization and
collaborative-blind supervision. RPSD enriches the OPSD teacher with
cross-user behavioral evidence retrieved by temporally aware optimal
transport and diversity-aware selection, enabling token-level
supervision that captures both target correctness and collaborative
behavioral patterns. Extensive experiments on multiple benchmarks show
that RPSD consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods.

## Appendix A Appendix Overview

The appendix is organized as follows.

- •
  Algorithm Details. We provide the algorithm details of the complete
  RPSD training procedure, the evidence retrieval pipeline, and the
  unbalanced optimal transport solver.
- •
  Implementation Details. We report the model configuration, training
  settings, retrieval setup, and hyper-parameters used to reproduce
  RPSD.
- •
  Dataset Details. We describe the three benchmark datasets used in the
  main paper. Then we report their statistics and how we construct the
  training, validation, and test sets.
- •
  Evaluation Protocol. We specify the candidate construction, evaluation
  metrics, ranking procedure, and repeated-run protocol.
- •
  Baseline Details. We describe the Non-LLM and LLM-based recommenders
  used for comparison, together with their evaluation settings.
- •
  Training Dynamics of RPSD. We analyze how RPSD behaves during
  optimization across datasets, in terms of teacher–student guidance,
  reward, entropy, and policy stability.
- •
  Training Dynamics of RPSD w/o OT. We study the training dynamics of
  RPSD w/o OT in order to isolate the role of behavioral evidence
  retrieved using OT.
- •
  Comparison with Vanilla OPSD. We compare RPSD with vanilla OPSD, which
  matches the student to a label-conditioned teacher distribution. We
  show that keeping the reward-grounded objective while reweighting
  token-level credit leads to consistent gains.
- •
  Response-Level RPSD versus Token-Level RPSD. We apply the same
  teacher-derived weights at the response level rather than the token
  level. This isolates the effect of credit granularity and shows that
  the improvement of RPSD comes from token-specific credit.
- •
  Case Study of Student and Teacher Prompts. We illustrate the student
  and teacher prompts, including the retrieved evidence block, using a
  training example from MovieLens-1M.

## Appendix B Algorithm Details

The full procedure is given in
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport").
Evidence retrieval is detailed in
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport"),
and the optimal-transport solver at its core is given in
Algorithm [3](#alg3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport").

During training, the student samples a group of responses from the
student prompt $`x^{S}`$. Each response receives a rule-based reward,
and GRPO turns these rewards into a single group-normalized advantage
$`\widehat{A}_{i}`$. A teacher that additionally sees the retrieved
evidence $`E_{u}`$ then rescores the same tokens under stop-gradient.
From this, we compute a confidence gap $`\Delta_{i,t}`$, which we center
within each completion and clip into $`\delta_{i,t}`$. We map
$`\delta_{i,t}`$ to an advantage-aware weight $`w_{i,t}`$ and use it to
refine $`\widehat{A}_{i}`$ into a token-level advantage
$`\widetilde{A}_{i,t}`$. As a result, tokens supported by the evidence
are reinforced more strongly when the response is correct, and penalized
more strongly when it is wrong. Finally, we optimize the clipped GRPO
objective with $`\widetilde{A}_{i,t}`$ to update the policy. At
inference, the model conditions only on $`x^{S}`$. Therefore, the
evidence shapes token credit during training and is never used at test
time.

0:  training set, policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$ initialized from the SFT
model, group size $`G`$, guidance strength $`\lambda`$, weight bound
$`\epsilon_{w}`$, stability threshold $`c_{\delta}`$, clip range
$`\epsilon`$, KL weight $`\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}`$

0:  trained policy $`\pi_{\theta}`$

1:  build the evidence index $`\mathcal{W}`$

2:  for each training step do

3:   sample a user with history $`H_{u}`$, candidate set
$`\mathcal{C}_{u}`$, and ground-truth next item $`v^{u}_{t+1}`$

4:   $`x^{S}\leftarrow\textsc{Prompt}_{S}(H_{u},\mathcal{C}_{u})`$

5:   $`E_{u}\leftarrow\textsc{RetrieveEvidence}(H_{u},\mathcal{W})`$

6:   $`x^{T}\leftarrow x^{S}\oplus E_{u}\oplus v^{u}_{t+1}`$

7:   sample a group
$`\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x^{S})`$

8:   $`r_{i}\leftarrow+1`$ if $`o_{i}`$ is valid and names the target,
$`0`$ if valid but wrong, $`-1`$ if invalid

9:
  $`\widehat{A}_{i}\leftarrow\big(r_{i}-\operatorname{mean}(r)\big)/\operatorname{std}(r)`$

10:   for each token $`o_{i,t}`$ do

11:
   $`\Delta_{i,t}\leftarrow\log\pi_{\operatorname{sg}(\theta)}(o_{i,t}\mid x^{T},o_{i,<t})-\log\pi_{\operatorname{sg}(\theta)}(o_{i,t}\mid x^{S},o_{i,<t})`$

12:
   $`\delta_{i,t}\leftarrow\operatorname{clip}\big(\Delta_{i,t}-\tfrac{1}{T_{i}}\textstyle\sum_{t^{\prime}}\Delta_{i,t^{\prime}},\,-c_{\delta},\,c_{\delta}\big)`$

13:
   $`w_{i,t}\leftarrow\operatorname{clip}\big(\exp[\operatorname{sign}(\widehat{A}_{i})\,\delta_{i,t}],\,1-\epsilon_{w},\,1+\epsilon_{w}\big)`$

14:
   $`\widetilde{A}_{i,t}\leftarrow\widehat{A}_{i}\big[(1-\lambda)+\lambda\,w_{i,t}\big]`$

15:   end for

16:   update $`\theta`$ by minimizing the RPSD objective in Eq.
([8](#S3.E8 "In 3.3 Retrieval-Privileged on-policy Self-Distillation ‣ 3 Method ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport"))

17:  end for

18:  return $`\pi_{\theta}`$

Algorithm 1 RPSD training

Evidence retrieval is summarized in
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport").
Each item is encoded from its title text alone, using the mean-pooled
hidden state of the SFT model. We reduce these vectors with PCA, and
then index every length-$`K`$ window together with the item. Given a
query history, retrieval proceeds in two stages. We first perform a
coarse retrieval of candidate windows by cosine similarity of
recency-weighted mean embeddings, excluding the query user’s own windows
so that no target leaks. We then re-rank the coarsely retrieved
candidate windows with unbalanced entropic optimal transport, which
aligns the two item sequences under a cost that combines semantic
dissimilarity with a temporal penalty. Finally, a greedy criterion
selects a diverse subset, which is rendered into the evidence block
$`E_{u}`$.

dimension $`d`$, recall size $`N_{0}`$, OT parameters
$`\varepsilon,\rho`$, cost weight $`\gamma`$, temperature $`\tau`$,
diversity weight $`\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}`$, evidence count $`m`$

0:  evidence block $`E_{u}`$

1:  $`e_{v}\leftarrow\operatorname{meanpool}(f(t_{v}))`$ for every item,
reduce all $`\{e_{v}\}`$ to $`d`$ dimensions by PCA, and
$`\ell_{2}`$-normalize them

2:
 $`a_{p}\leftarrow\frac{\exp[-\beta(K-1-p)]}{\sum_{r=0}^{K-1}\exp[-\beta(K-1-r)]}`$
for $`p=0,\dots,K-1`$;  $`a\leftarrow(a_{0},\dots,a_{K-1})`$,
 $`b\leftarrow a`$

3:  build
$`\mathcal{W}\leftarrow\{(h_{j},v_{j}^{+},u_{j},\bar{e}(h_{j}))\}`$ over
all length-$`K`$ windows $`h_{j}`$ of the training sequences that are
followed by an item $`v_{j}^{+}`$, excluding the last window of each
sequence, where
$`\bar{e}(h_{j})=\frac{\sum_{p}a_{p}e_{v^{\prime}_{j,p}}}{\|\sum_{p}a_{p}e_{v^{\prime}_{j,p}}\|_{2}}`$

4:  if $`|H_{u}|<K`$ then

5:   return $`E_{u}`$ stating that no similar trajectory is retrieved

6:  end if

7:  $`H_{u}^{K}\leftarrow`$ the $`K`$ most recent items of $`H_{u}`$;
 $`\bar{e}(H_{u}^{K})\leftarrow`$ recency-weighted mean by the same rule

8:  $`\mathcal{R}\leftarrow`$ top-$`N_{0}`$ windows by cosine
$`\langle\bar{e}(H_{u}^{K}),\bar{e}(h_{j})\rangle`$, excluding windows
with $`u_{j}=u`$ or $`h_{j}=H_{u}^{K}`$

10:

where $`v_{p}`$ is the $`p`$-th item of $`H_{u}^{K}`$

cost for $`(a,b,C)`$

12:   $`s_{j}\leftarrow\exp(-c_{j}/\tau)`$

The re-ranking step of
Algorithm [2](#alg2 "Algorithm 2 ‣ Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport")
uses the unbalanced entropic optimal-transport solver in
Algorithm [3](#alg3 "Algorithm 3 ‣ Appendix B Algorithm Details ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport").
Given the query mass $`\mathbf{a}`$ and window mass $`\mathbf{b}`$ from
the recency-weighted rule, together with a ground cost $`\mathbf{C}`$,
the solver returns the transport plan and its mass-normalized cost,
which we map to a similarity. The cost between query position $`p`$ and
window position $`q`$ is
$`C_{pq}=(1-\langle e_{p},e^{\prime}_{q}\rangle)+\gamma\cdot|p/(K-1)-q/(K-1)|`$,
where the first term measures semantic dissimilarity between item-title
embeddings and the second penalizes temporally inconsistent alignments.
The marginal relaxation controlled by $`\rho`$ lets some mass stay
unmatched, so a query and a window that share only part of their
interests need not be fully aligned. As a special case, setting
$`\mathbf{C}`$ to an identity kernel reduces the solver to
recency-weighted exact matching.

## Appendix C Implementation Details

We initialize the policy from an SFT-trained Qwen3-4B checkpoint and
optimize it with LoRA. We set the rank to $`32`$, the scaling factor to
$`64`$, and the dropout rate to $`0.05`$, and we apply adapters to all
linear layers. We split the sequence instances into training,
validation, and test sets with a ratio of 7:1:2. Each instance is built
from up to nine recent interactions. We use the final item as the
prediction target and the preceding items as the input history, so each
instance has at most eight history items. We apply the same construction
to all three splits. For each instance, we build a candidate set of ten
items: the target item and nine negatives. The negatives are sampled at
random from the user’s non-interacted items. The student sees only the
input history and the candidate set, and it is not told which candidate
is the target.

During training, the privileged teacher additionally receives behavioral
evidence retrieved from other similar users. We first retrieve candidate
history windows using 128-dimensional item embeddings, ranked by
recency-weighted cosine similarity. We then re-rank them with temporally
aware unbalanced OT. Finally, we apply MMR-based diversity selection.
Specifically, we greedily pick three evidence windows, balancing their
relevance to the query history against their similarity to windows
already selected. For each selected window, we include the window itself
and its subsequent interaction in the teacher’s evidence block. This
evidence is used only by the teacher during training, and is not needed
at inference time.

For each training prompt, we sample eight completions from the student
policy. A completion receives reward $`1`$ if it follows the required
format and predicts the correct item, $`0`$ if it is well-formed but
incorrect, and $`-1`$ if it violates the output format. We then
re-evaluate the same policy under the privileged teacher context with
gradients disabled. Here the teacher only scores the student’s
completion and it does not generate a separate response. We compute the
teacher–student log-probability difference for each token and center it
within each completion. We train for three epochs and the learning rate
is $`8\times 10^{-6}`$. The per-device batch size is $`4`$, and we use
$`8`$ gradient-accumulation steps. We set the KL coefficient to $`0.1`$
and save a checkpoint every $`500`$ optimization steps.

MicroLens ([Ni et al., 2025](#bib.bib3)) is a short-video recommendation

a challenging test of generalization.

Netflix ([Wei et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)) is a user-based movie

$`3.0\times 10^{-4}`$. Because a single history reveals so little,
Netflix is the dataset on which evidence from behaviorally similar users
should matter most.

MovieLens ([Harper and Konstan, 2015](#bib.bib1)) is a classic movie
recommendation dataset in which each movie provides a title, a release
year, and a poster image. Unlike the other two datasets, it has a small
catalog but is extremely dense, with a density of $`4.2\times 10^{-2}`$
and an average sequence length of $`165.56`$. Each user therefore has a
long, information-rich history, which is exactly the setting where
standard sequential models already perform well. This makes MovieLens
suitable for our approach. It lets us test whether retrieved evidence
still helps even when a user’s own history is already informative enough
on its own.

We split the sequence instances into training, validation, and test sets
with a ratio of 7:1:2. Within each instance, we retain up to the nine
most recent interactions, use the final item as the prediction target,
and use the preceding items as the input history. This instance
construction is applied consistently across the training, validation,
and test splits.

## Appendix E Evaluation Protocol

For each test instance, we construct a candidate set by combining the
ground-truth next item with $`n_{c}-1`$ negatives sampled from the
user’s non-interacted items. We use $`n_{c}=10`$ by default and
additionally evaluate $`n_{c}=100`$ to examine performance with a larger
candidate set. All methods are evaluated using the same data splits and
candidate construction protocol.

We evaluate recommendation performance using hit ratio (HR@$`k`$) and
normalized discounted cumulative gain (NDCG@$`k`$). During training, the
model is optimized to predict the single next item, since the rule-based
reward requires a deterministic, verifiable output. For evaluation,
rank-based metrics instead require a ranked list, so we prompt the model
to output the $`k`$ items it considers most likely, forming a ranked
list $`\mathcal{L}_{i}^{(k)}`$ for each test instance. The Top-3 and
Top-5 lists are generated independently rather than obtained by
truncating a single shared ranking, and both HR@$`k`$ and NDCG@$`k`$ are
computed from $`\mathcal{L}_{i}^{(k)}`$ at the corresponding threshold
$`k`$. Since each instance has exactly one ground-truth target, HR@$`k`$
measures whether the target appears within the top $`k`$, and NDCG@$`k`$
additionally rewards ranking it higher.

Let $`\mathcal{T}`$ denote the test set. For each instance $`i`$, let
$`\operatorname{rank}_{i}^{(k)}`$ be the one-based position of the
ground-truth item in the list $`\mathcal{L}_{i}^{(k)}`$. If the target
does not appear in the list, we set
$`\operatorname{rank}_{i}^{(k)}=+\infty`$.

HR@$`k`$ is the fraction of test instances whose target appears within
the top $`k`$ positions:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{HR@}k=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{I}\!\left[\operatorname{rank}_{i}^{(k)}\leq k\right],
``` |  | (9) |

where $`\mathbb{I}[\cdot]`$ is the indicator function.

NDCG@$`k`$ further rewards ranking the target higher. Each instance has
exactly one target, and we compute

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathrm{NDCG@}k=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}g_{i}^{(k)},\qquad g_{i}^{(k)}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log_{2}\!\left(\operatorname{rank}_{i}^{(k)}+1\right)},&\operatorname{rank}_{i}^{(k)}\leq k,\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
``` |  | (10) |

We report HR@$`k`$ and NDCG@$`k`$ for $`k\in\{3,5\}`$ under the default
candidate setting, and for $`k\in\{5,10\}`$ under the larger one. For
both metrics, higher is better, and all results are averaged over five
independent runs.

## Appendix F Baseline Details

We compare RPSD against two families of sequential recommenders. All
baselines are trained and evaluated on the same data splits, the same
candidate construction, and the same $`n_{c}`$ as RPSD, so that
differences reflect the method rather than the protocol.

Non-LLM models. GRU4Rec ([Hidasi et al., 2016](#bib.bib5)) encodes an
interaction session with a gated recurrent network and predicts the next
click from its final hidden state. SASRec ([Kang and McAuley,
2018](#bib.bib6)) replaces recurrence with a causal self-attention
encoder that adaptively weighs earlier interactions. LightGCN ([He et
al., 2020](#bib.bib7)) propagates user and item embeddings over the
interaction graph, dropping feature transformation and nonlinearity for
a lightweight collaborative signal. CL4SRec ([Xie et al.,
2022](#bib.bib8)) adds a contrastive objective over perturbed sequences
to a self-attentive backbone, strengthening its representations under
sparse supervision. These models rely on identifier embeddings alone,
and we use them to measure the effectiveness of purely collaborative
signals. LATTICE ([Zhang et al., 2021](#bib.bib9)) builds a latent
item-to-item graph from modality features for adding content structure
to collaborative filtering. MoRec ([Yuan et al., 2023](#bib.bib10))
studies whether modality-derived item representations can replace
identifier embeddings. BM3 ([Zhou et al., 2023](#bib.bib11)) learns
multimodal representations with a self-supervised bootstrapping
objective that needs no negative sampling. AB-Rec ([Wu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12)) aligns and balances identifier and modality
representations so that neither dominates the fused embedding.

LLM-based recommenders. TallRec ([Bao et al., 2023](#bib.bib13)) tunes
an LLM on recommendation prompts so that it picks the next item from a
candidate list. LLaRA ([Liao et al., 2024](#bib.bib4)) connects
collaborative embeddings to a language backbone through a projection,
letting sequential signals enter the prompt. SPRec ([Gao et al.,
2025](#bib.bib14)) uses a self-play preference scheme to reduce
popularity bias. MSRBench ([Zhou et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)) studies how
large models can be integrated into sequential recommendation under
different strategies, and MLLM-MSR ([Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib17))
fine-tunes a large model with an item-summarization stage that feeds a
sequence model. RecZero ([Kong et al., 2025a](#bib.bib15)) post-trains
an LLM with GRPO over structured reasoning steps and a graded rule-based
reward.

Our own variants, Qwen3 with SFT, Qwen3 with GRPO, and Qwen3 with RPSD,
share the identical backbone, prompt, sampling, and reward, and differ
only in the method design, so the comparison among them attributes any
gain to proposed modules.

## Appendix G Training Diagnostics of RPSD

In this section, we track how RPSD optimizes on the three datasets. The
diagnostics are shown in
Figures [6](#A6.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ Appendix F Baseline Details ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport") -
[11](#A6.F11 "Figure 11 ‣ Appendix F Baseline Details ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport").
Across all of them, the rule-based reward rises steadily and does not
collapse, and the policy entropy goes down over time. This means the
model becomes more confident and gradually commits to the tokens that
identify the target item. The KL divergence to the reference model rises
only at the beginning and then stays flat, so the training stays close
to the reference rather than drifting away from it. Together, these
curves show that RPSD trains in a stable way.

We next look at the two quantities that are specific to RPSD. The
teacher–student confidence gap $`\delta_{i,t}`$ is largest early in
training and becomes smaller as the policy converges. This shows that
the retrieved evidence helps most when the student is still uncertain,
and its effect fades once the student can already score the decisive
tokens on its own. This pattern is consistent across the three datasets,
and the main difference is how far the gap narrows, which reflects how
much privileged knowledge already reveals.

The token weight $`w_{i,t}`$ moves toward one as training goes on. This
does not mean the teacher stops mattering; it follows directly from the
shrinking confidence gap, since $`w_{i,t}`$ is computed from
$`\delta_{i,t}`$ and approaches one exactly when $`\delta_{i,t}`$
becomes small. In other words, RPSD applies a strong correction early,
when the student needs it, and a gentle one later, when the student is
already confident. This happens on its own, without any manually
designed annealing schedule, which makes the token-level guidance easy
to use in practice.

The group agreement, defined as the fraction of groups whose $`G`$
responses receive the same reward, increases steadily during training.
This trend is consistent with the behavior of the token weight
$`w_{i,t}`$. As the policy becomes more confident, responses within the
same group are more likely to receive the same reward, leading to higher
group agreement. At the same time, the teacher–student gap
$`\delta_{i,t}`$ decreases, causing $`w_{i,t}`$ to increase. Together,
these trends indicate that both the group-level reward variation and the
teacher’s token-level correction gradually weaken as training converges.

The difference appears in the confidence gap $`\delta_{i,t}`$, the

tokens. Without behavioral evidence, the teacher and the student read
almost the same input, so on the denser datasets the teacher rescores
the tokens in nearly the same way and $`\delta_{i,t}`$ stays small,
giving the student only a weak token-level correction. On the sparse
Netflix dataset the label-only teacher instead disagrees with the
student in an unstable way, so $`\delta_{i,t}`$ is larger but noisy. In
neither case does removing the evidence break training and it mainly
weakens or destabilizes the teacher–student correction that reshapes
token-level credit, which is the signal the full model relies on.

How strongly the teacher can still guide the student depends on how much
a user’s own history already reveals. On the denser datasets
(MovieLens-1M and MicroLens), where each user has a long history, the
confidence gap $`\delta_{i,t}`$ is small from the start, because the
student can already infer the answer and the label alone provides
limited information. On the sparse Netflix dataset, the teacher-student
gap opens wider and does not close since the label identifies the
correct item but gives the student no way to reach that conclusion on
its own. Therefore, the teacher and the student keep disagreeing without
resolving it. This matches the ablation study, where removing the
retrieved evidence hurts accuracy the most on the sparsest dataset, and
it supports our claim that the benefit of RPSD comes from the behavioral
evidence rather than from merely conditioning the teacher on the answer.

## Appendix I Comparison with Vanilla OPSD

Vanilla on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18)) uses the same language model as a student and a
privileged teacher under different conditioning contexts. Given an input
$`x`$ and its reference solution $`y^{\star}`$, the student first
samples an on-policy response $`\hat{y}\sim p_{S}(\cdot\mid x)`$. The
teacher then evaluates the same student-generated prefixes while
additionally conditioning on $`y^{\star}`$. Its training objective
minimizes the token-wise divergence between the full next-token
distributions of the teacher and student:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{\star})\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}\sim p_{S}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{|\hat{y}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\hat{y}|}D\left(p_{T}(\cdot\mid x,y^{\star},\hat{y}_{<t})\,\|\,p_{S}(\cdot\mid x,\hat{y}_{<t})\right)\right],
``` |  | (11) |

where $`D`$ can be instantiated as KL divergence or generalized
Jensen–Shannon divergence. Gradients are propagated only through the
student distribution, while the privileged teacher serves as a detached
distributional target.

RPSD differs from vanilla OPSD in both how the teacher signal is
incorporated into optimization and what privileged knowledge the teacher
receives. Rather than directly matching the complete student
distribution to the teacher distribution, RPSD retains the
reward-grounded GRPO objective and uses the teacher only to modulate the
magnitude of token-level credit. Specifically, given the
group-normalized response advantage $`\widehat{A}_{i}`$, RPSD computes

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Delta_{i,t}=\log\pi_{\mathrm{sg}(\theta)}(o_{i,t}\mid x_{i}^{T},o_{i,<t})-\log\pi_{\mathrm{sg}(\theta)}(o_{i,t}\mid x_{i}^{S},o_{i,<t}),
``` |  | (12) |

where the teacher context $`x_{i}^{T}`$ additionally contains the
ground-truth item and retrieved behavioral evidence. After centering and
clipping the confidence gap into $`\delta_{i,t}`$, the token weight and
refined advantage are defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
w_{i,t}=\operatorname{clip}\left(\exp\left[\operatorname{sign}(\widehat{A}_{i})\delta_{i,t}\right],1-\epsilon_{w},1+\epsilon_{w}\right),\qquad\widetilde{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_{i}\left[(1-\lambda)+\lambda w_{i,t}\right].
``` |  | (13) |

Since $`w_{i,t}>0`$ and $`\lambda\in[0,1]`$, the scaling factor is
strictly positive and therefore
$`\operatorname{sign}(\widetilde{A}_{i,t})=\operatorname{sign}(\widehat{A}_{i})`$.
Thus, the verifiable reward continues to determine whether the
policy-gradient term reinforces or suppresses a response, while the
teacher only redistributes its magnitude across tokens. This is
fundamentally different from vanilla OPSD, where the teacher
distribution itself constitutes the optimization target. RPSD further
differs from vanilla OPSD in the source of the teacher’s privileged
information. Vanilla OPSD conditions the teacher on the ground-truth
label alone, which tells the teacher which candidate is correct but not
why it fits the current history. In contrast, RPSD additionally
conditions the teacher on cross-user behavioral evidence retrieved by
temporally aware optimal transport, so the teacher is grounded in how
behaviorally similar users actually continued rather than in the bare
answer.

As reported in
Table [6](#A8.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix H Training Dynamics of RPSD w/o OT ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport"),
vanilla OPSD improves over SFT, confirming that dense on-policy teacher
supervision is a useful signal. However, it falls below GRPO on the
recommendation performance. Directly imitating a label-conditioned
teacher distribution pulls every token toward the teacher, including
tokens that carry no evidence for the recommendation, so not all updates
stay aligned with the verifiable objective. RPSD avoids this by keeping
the reward-grounded objective and letting the teacher only reweight
token credit, so the reward still decides the direction of each update
while the retrieved evidence by OT and the ground-truth item sharpen
where that update lands. This is why RPSD surpasses both OPSD and GRPO
rather than sitting between them.

## Appendix J Response-Level versus Token-Level Credit

The full RPSD applies the teacher’s guidance at the token level. Each
token gets its own weight, so tokens the retrieved evidence supports are
reinforced more and formatting tokens are down-weighted. To test that
this token-level granularity is truly the source of the performance
gain, and not just a per-response calibration, we relax RPSD into a
response-level variant that keeps everything else fixed and only changes
the granularity at which the teacher’s guidance is applied. The
Response-level RPSD shares the same student rollouts,
retrieval-privileged teacher, rule-based rewards, confidence gaps
$`\delta_{i,t}`$, and hyperparameters as RPSD.

The relaxation averages the per-token weights of each response into a
single scalar and broadcasts it to all tokens,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{w}_{i}=\frac{1}{T_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}w_{i,t},\qquad\widetilde{A}^{\mathrm{resp}}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_{i}\left[(1-\lambda)+\lambda\bar{w}_{i}\right],\quad t=1,\ldots,T_{i},
``` |  | (14) |

where $`w_{i,t}`$ is computed exactly as in the full RPSD. GRPO shares
one advantage across all tokens, Response-level RPSD rescales each
response using a single teacher-derived scalar, whereas our RPSD
maintains a separate weight for each token. Since none of the three
methods reverses the sign of $`\widehat{A}_{i}`$, the verifiable reward
still determines the direction of every update. Therefore, these methods
differ only in the granularity with which the teacher redistributes the
update magnitude.

Table [7](#A8.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Appendix H Training Dynamics of RPSD w/o OT ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport")
provides a clear comparison. Response-level RPSD already improves over
GRPO, showing that the privileged teacher remains useful even when its
guidance is compressed into a single scalar per response. However, it
still falls well below the full Token-level RPSD. This suggests that the
gain of RPSD does not arise merely from strengthening some responses and
weakening others, but largely from distinguishing tokens within each
response. This difference follows directly from the weight construction.
Because $`\delta_{i,t}`$ is centered within each response, averaging it
largely removes the contrast between above- and below-average tokens,
making Response-level RPSD much closer to GRPO’s uniform within-response
credit. It can still modulate the overall update strength across
responses, but cannot distinguish which tokens are more strongly
supported by the privileged teacher. In contrast, Token-level RPSD
assigns larger updates to tokens whose likelihood changes more under the
retrieved evidence while down-weighting less informative tokens, such as
generic or formatting tokens. The larger improvement in NDCG further
suggests that such fine-grained credit helps not only identify the
target item but also rank it more accurately.

## Appendix K Case Study of Student and Teacher Prompts

To make the privileged knowledge concrete,
Figure [18](#A11.F18 "Figure 18 ‣ Appendix K Case Study of Student and Teacher Prompts ‣ RPSD: Retrieval-Privileged On-Policy Self-Distillation with Optimal Transport")
shows an example of student and teacher prompts. The student prompt
$`x^{S}`$ contains only the user’s interaction history and the candidate
set, and asks the model to name the single next item. It is the sole
input at inference, so the deployed policy never sees any of the
privileged information below.

During training, the teacher additionally reads two blocks. The first is
the retrieved evidence $`E_{u}`$, which lists how behaviorally similar
users continued after comparable histories, together with a similarity
score for each trajectory. In this example, the user’s recent history
mixes classic science-fiction titles such as *Mad Max*, *The Matrix*,
and *Men in Black*. The retrieved trajectories share several of these
items, so their continuations suggest the kind of item such a user tends
to watch next. The second block is the ground-truth next item, which
states the correct answer directly. These privileged views together let
the teacher judge the student’s response not only by whether it matches
the label, but also in light of what similar users actually did.
````

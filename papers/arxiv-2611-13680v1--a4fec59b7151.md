###### Abstract

On-policy distillation (OPD) improves language models by distilling
teacher predictions on student-generated trajectories, while on-policy
self-distillation (OPSD) removes the need for an external teacher by
using a reference-conditioned copy of the model as the teacher. Despite
its great early improvements, we find that OPSD does not remain
consistently effective under continued training and can eventually
suffer substantial performance degradation. We study this training
behavior and link the degradation to a growing mismatch between the
evolving reference-free student and the frozen privileged teacher. In
the early stage of OPSD, the privileged teacher remains closely aligned
with the student and provides useful guidance for improving the
student’s generation trajectories. However, as training progresses, the
growing distributional mismatch between the privileged teacher and
reference-free student can cause the teacher to provide misleading
supervision, ultimately degrading student performance. This phenomenon
motivates us to first leverage privileged reference supervision to
acquire useful knowledge and then transfer it to a fresh student. We
introduce Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation (BOPD), a two-stage
framework that first uses OPSD to internalize task-relevant information
from reference-conditioned supervision into an intermediate model, and
then distills this reference-free intermediate model into a fresh copy
of the initial model. Experiments across models show that BOPD improves
mathematical reasoning and code generation beyond both the initial
models and the constructed OPSD teachers. These results suggest that
privileged reference supervision can be more effectively exploited by
first transferring its task-relevant signal into a task-adapted model
and then distilling from that model without reference conditioning.

## 1 Introduction

Figure 1: Performance of OPSD and BOPD during the training process

On-policy distillation (OPD) has emerged as an effective approach to
language model post-training ([Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Gu et
al., 2024](#bib.bib5)). Rather than learning from fixed demonstrations,
the student model generates its own trajectories and receives dense
token-level supervision from a teacher model, enabling it to learn
directly from the teacher on its on-policy state distribution ([Agarwal
et al., 2024](#bib.bib1); [Zhang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib16); [Yang et
al., 2025](#bib.bib15); [Lu, 2025](#bib.bib29)). This on-policy
supervision reduces the mismatch between training-time and
inference-time state distributions while providing denser token-level
feedback than sparse outcome-level rewards, thereby enabling substantial
capability improvements during post-training.

Recent work has sought to broaden the applicability of OPD by removing
the need for an external teacher model. On-policy self-distillation
(OPSD) ([Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib13)) achieves this by using the
model itself as the teacher: the student generates trajectories
conditioned only on the problem, while a frozen copy of the same model
additionally conditions on the reference solution works as a teacher
model. Building on this formulation, $`\beta`$-OPSD ([Xu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib4)) derives OPSD from a KL-regularized reinforcement
learning objective and introduces a tunable interpolation between the
reference policy and the privileged teacher as the target distribution,
while DualOPSD ([Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib3)) jointly optimizes the
teacher and student to adapt the supervision throughout training.

Despite these advances, we observe a striking phenomenon under prolonged
OPSD training: OPSD improves model performance during the early stage of
training, but these gains progressively diminish and can eventually
reverse as optimization continues
(Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")).
During the early stage of training, the privileged reference provides
useful corrective supervision, but as the student policy evolves, the
growing distributional mismatch between the student and the frozen
privileged teacher leads to misleading supervision ([Zhao et al.,
2026b](#bib.bib32); [Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib31)). This suggests a
fundamental limitation of OPSD: although privileged reference
information makes the teacher more informative, it can also make the
resulting supervision increasingly incompatible with the reference-free
student as training proceeds.In Section 3, we provide empirical analyses
supporting this interpretation and following the principles in [Li et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib10), we show that the privilege-conditioned model
does not constitute a well-matched teacher for the evolving student.
These observations raise a broader question: can a student construct a
capable and well-matched teacher for itself without relying on an
external teacher model?

Figure 2: BOPD overview. In Stage I, a frozen copy of $`M_{0}`$ receives
a reference solution and supervises a student on its own rollouts,
producing $`M_{1}`$. In Stage II, frozen $`M_{1}`$ supervises a fresh
student initialized from $`M_{0}`$ and neither model receives a
reference. Dashed arrows denote student initialization; solid arrows
show rollout flow, supervision, and model transfer.

In this paper, we introduce Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation (BOPD),
a two-stage framework that first constructs a teacher by updating the
initial model with OPSD, and then distills the resulting teacher into a
fresh copy of the initial model without privileged reference
conditioning. BOPD therefore separates teacher construction from teacher
use: the first stage exploits privileged reference information to
internalize task-relevant reasoning signals into the model, while the
second stage reuses the resulting model as a reference-free teacher for
standard on-policy distillation. Through Stage I, $`\pi_{\mathrm{B}}`$
absorbs part of the reasoning signal provided by the privileged
reference into its own policy. When used in Stage II,
$`\pi_{\mathrm{B}}`$ no longer requires access to the reference
solution, so the teacher and student are conditioned on the same
observable information. This design preserves the task-specific
knowledge acquired through OPSD while reducing the privileged-context
mismatch that arises when the teacher relies on information unavailable
to the student.

Formally, the BOPD procedure can be summarized as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\pi_{0}\xrightarrow[\text{reference available}]{\text{OPSD}}\pi_{\mathrm{B}}\quad\text{and then}\quad\pi_{0}\xrightarrow[\text{reference unavailable}]{\text{OPD with teacher }\pi_{\mathrm{B}}}\pi_{\mathrm{final}},
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\pi_{\mathrm{B}}`$ denotes the bootstrapped teacher. Our work
makes three contributions:

- •
  We analyze the training dynamics of OPSD and provide evidence that its
  early improvement followed by prolonged degradation is associated with
  a growing incompatibility between the privileged reference-conditioned
  teacher and the reference-free student.
- •
  We introduce Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation (BOPD), a two-stage
  framework that first uses OPSD to construct a task-adapted teacher
  from the initial model, and then distills this teacher into a fresh
  copy of the initial model without privileged reference conditioning.
- •
  We validate BOPD across different models through extensive experiments
  and ablations. BOPD improves over both the initial model and the
  constructed OPSD teacher, while analyses of prolonged OPSD training,
  teacher construction, and reference access provide empirical support
  for the proposed framework.

## 2 Related Work

#### Learning from Reference Solutions.

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) learns from reference solutions by fitting
their target tokens. Recent studies have explored token weighting and
selection to improve this learning signal. DFT ([Wu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib17)) revisits SFT from a reinforcement-learning
perspective, while PriFT ([Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib18)) and
ProFit ([Liu et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)) use model probabilities to
determine which tokens to emphasize. Other studies focus on modifying
the training data. [Zhao et al. (2025)](#bib.bib14) retain correct
model-generated solutions and use guided re-solving to replace incorrect
ones before fine-tuning. [Zhang et al. (2026b)](#bib.bib16) introduce
Hinted Decoding to construct responses that better match the model’s
distribution. Reference data can also be used to construct teachers.
PromptSD ([Ma et al., 2026](#bib.bib11)) trains task-specific soft
prompts on labeled examples with a frozen backbone, then uses the
resulting teachers to supervise student-generated trajectories.

#### On-Policy (Self-)Distillation.

Knowledge distillation transfers a teacher’s predictive distribution to
a student ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib7)). GKD ([Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1)) and MiniLLM ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)) extend
distributional supervision to student-generated trajectories, using
different distillation objectives. SKD ([Xu et al., 2025](#bib.bib30))
introduces teacher–student cooperation during trajectory generation. [Li
et al. (2026)](#bib.bib10) further study OPD and highlight the
importance of teacher capability and compatibility with student-visited
states. OPSD ([Zhao et al., 2026a](#bib.bib13)) removes the need for a
separate teacher by conditioning a self-teacher on reference solutions.
SDFT ([Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib12)) uses
demonstration-conditioned supervision for continual learning. More
recent studies examine the role of privileged context in distillation.
DOPD ([Yu et al., 2026](#bib.bib20)) routes token-level signals between
privileged teacher and student policies, while [Kaur et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib9) identify failure modes of reference conditioning in
thinking models. LOPD ([Zhang et al., 2026a](#bib.bib31)) makes
privileged context learnable by composing retrieved experiences into
latent tokens and jointly optimizing the context composer and student.

## 3 What Happens When OPSD Continues?

Figure 3: Continued OPSD training on Qwen3-4B. Top: Avg@6 on three
mathematics benchmarks. Middle: overlap@16, overlap-token advantage@16,
and absolute entropy gap between the OPSD student and its privileged
teacher. Bottom: Fixed-prefix entropy, on-policy entropy and KL
divergence between the student and teacher.

In this section, we study the training dynamics of OPSD beyond its
initial improvement phase. OPSD uses a frozen, privilege-conditioned
initial model as the teacher to provide guidance on student-generated
trajectories. As training proceeds, the teacher remains fixed while the
student policy and the distribution of student-generated prefixes
continue to evolve, causing the teacher to be queried on increasingly
different states. This raises three questions: whether the effectiveness
of OPSD is sustained as training continues, whether the privileged
teacher remains well matched to the evolving student, and how the
student’s on-policy state distribution changes over prolonged training.

To study the behavior of continued OPSD training, we train Qwen3-4B
([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)) with OPSD for 200 optimization steps
and evaluate intermediate checkpoints throughout training. We measure
task performance on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and HMMT 2025, and analyze
teacher–student distributions on 64 training prompts. For each
checkpoint, we leverage the principles in [Li et al. (2026)](#bib.bib10)
and track teacher–student agreement using Overlap@16, overlap-token
advantage@16, the absolute entropy gap, and the full-vocabulary KL
divergence. We additionally compare student entropy on on-policy
prefixes with entropy measured on prefixes generated by the initial
model, allowing us to distinguish changes in the student distribution
from changes in the states visited by the student during training.
Details of these measurements are provided in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Mechanism Analysis Details ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation").

Continued OPSD does not remain consistently effective. As shown in
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 What Happens When OPSD Continues? ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation"),
task accuracy improves during the early and middle stages of training,
but the gains eventually diminish and reverse. The mean Avg@6 accuracy
across the three mathematical benchmarks reaches 22.22% at step 140, but
falls to 9.07% by step 200.

The privileged teacher is not well matched to the evolving student. As
shown in
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 What Happens When OPSD Continues? ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation"),
teacher–student Overlap@16 decreases from 89.30% at step 20 to 79.21% at
step 200, while the full-vocabulary KL divergence increases from 0.0719
to 0.7038. The overlap-token advantage also moves farther from zero, and
the absolute entropy gap increases over training. Together, these trends
show that the loss of effectiveness under continued OPSD is accompanied
by growing distributional mismatch between the reference-free student
and the frozen reference-conditioned teacher.

Student uncertainty increases on evolving on-policy states. To
investigate whether this increase stems from changes in the student
policy itself or from shifts in the states it visits, we compare student
entropy on on-policy prefixes with entropy on a fixed set of prefixes
generated by the initial model. Entropy on the fixed prefixes quickly
saturates, whereas entropy on the on-policy prefixes continues to
increase. This contrast suggests that the growing uncertainty is closely
associated with the evolving on-policy state distribution rather than
solely with changes in the student policy.

Taken together, these results suggest that continued OPSD progressively
shifts the student-generated prefix distribution while the privileged
teacher remains fixed, leading the teacher to provide supervision on
states where its predictions are increasingly mismatched with those of
the evolving student. The observed degradation can be characterized as a
growing mismatch between the privileged teacher and the reference-free
student, which reduces the suitability of the teacher’s supervision as
training progresses. This motivates separating teacher construction from
teacher use: rather than continuing OPSD indefinitely, we may stop at an
intermediate checkpoint that has already internalized part of the
task-relevant signal provided by the reference-conditioned teacher and
can therefore serve as a task-adapted, reference-free teacher for a
fresh student.

## 4 Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation

### 4.1 Problem Setup and Overview

We consider a dataset
$`\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i}^{\star})\}_{i=1}^{N}`$, where $`x_{i}`$ is
a problem and $`y_{i}^{\star}`$ is a reference solution. Let
$`\mathcal{D}_{x}`$ denote the empirical prompt distribution,
$`\mathcal{V}`$ the token vocabulary, and $`M_{0}`$ the initial model
with parameters $`\theta_{0}`$ and policy $`\pi_{0}=\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$.

BOPD consists of two stages
(Equation [1](#S1.E1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")).
In Stage I, we use OPSD to construct a task-adapted teacher $`M_{1}`$
with policy $`\pi_{B}=\pi_{\theta_{B}}`$. In Stage II, we freeze this
teacher and distill it into a fresh student initialized from $`M_{0}`$,
without reference conditioning. Both stages operate on student-generated
trajectories.
Algorithm [1](#alg1 "Algorithm 1 ‣ 4.3 Stage II: On-Policy Distillation with the Bootstrapped Teacher ‣ 4 Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
summarizes the full procedure.

### 4.2 Stage I: Bootstrapping a Teacher with OPSD

We initialize a trainable student $`\pi_{\theta}`$ and a frozen teacher
$`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$ from the same initial model $`\pi_{0}`$. For each
$`(x,y^{\star})\sim\mathcal{D}`$, the student generates
$`\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$. At each student-generated
prefix $`\hat{y}_{<t}`$, the student and privileged teacher define

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{t}(v)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(v\mid x,\hat{y}_{<t}),`$ |  | (2) |
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{t}^{\mathrm{ref}}(v)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta_{0}}(v\mid x,y^{\star},\hat{y}_{<t}),\qquad v\in\mathcal{V}.`$ |  | (3) |

We update only the student parameters by minimizing the
trajectory-averaged forward KL divergence:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{\star})\sim\mathcal{D},\,\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{|\hat{y}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\hat{y}|}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_{t}^{\mathrm{ref}}\middle\|p_{t}\right)\right].
``` |  | (4) |

After Stage I, we save the resulting model as the bootstrapped teacher
$`\pi_{\mathrm{B}}=\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{B}}}`$. This stage converts
reference-conditioned supervision into a task-adapted model which can
subsequently be used without access to the reference solution.

### 4.3 Stage II: On-Policy Distillation with the Bootstrapped Teacher

We freeze the bootstrapped teacher $`\pi_{B}`$ and initialize a fresh
student $`\pi_{\phi}`$ with $`\phi=\theta_{0}`$. The new student
generates $`\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x)`$. At each prefix
$`\hat{y}_{<t}`$, the student and teacher condition on the same
observable context:

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle p_{t}(v)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\phi}(v\mid x,\hat{y}_{<t}),`$ |  | (5) |
|  | $`\displaystyle q_{t}^{\mathrm{B}}(v)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\mathrm{B}}(v\mid x,\hat{y}_{<t}).`$ |  | (6) |

Unlike Stage I, the teacher no longer receives the reference solution
and we minimize

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BOPD}}(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{x},\,\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[\frac{1}{|\hat{y}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|\hat{y}|}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_{t}^{\mathrm{B}}\middle\|p_{t}\right)\right].
``` |  | (7) |

Resetting the student separates teacher construction from knowledge
transfer: Stage I produces a task-adapted teacher using privileged
supervision, while Stage II transfers the resulting behavior to a fresh
reference-free student on its own on-policy trajectories.

## 5 Experiments

### 5.1 Experimental Setup

#### Training data and models.

We evaluate BOPD across different model scales and families, including
Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and SmolLM3-3B ([Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib15);
[Bakouch et al., 2025](#bib.bib2)). For mathematical reasoning
experiments, we use the OpenThoughts dataset ([Guha et al.,
2026](#bib.bib6)) and we use the TACO subset of the DeepCoder Preview
Dataset for code-generation experiments ([Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib27);
[Luo et al., 2025](#bib.bib28)). Within each domain, OPSD and BOPD use
the same training data. We train OPSD and each stage of BOPD for 100
optimizer steps with an effective batch size of 32. We use LoRA ([Hu et
al., 2021](#bib.bib8)) with rank 64 and scaling parameter 128 on the
query, key, value, and output attention projections and the gate, up,
and down MLP projections. For code-generation experiments, we set the
dropout rate to 0.05. The learning rate is $`5\times 10^{-6}`$, with
gradient clipping at 0.1 and pointwise loss clipping at $`\tau=0.05`$.
Student rollouts contain at most 1,024 tokens, sampled with temperature
1.1, top-p 0.95, and top-k 20. We use bfloat16 and DeepSpeed ZeRO-2.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
provides additional settings.

#### Evaluation.

For mathematical reasoning experiments, we evaluate all methods on three
competition mathematics benchmarks, including AIME 2024 and AIME
2025 ([Mathematical Association of America, n.d.](#bib.bib21)), and HMMT
February 2025 ([Harvard-MIT Mathematics Tournament, 2025](#bib.bib22)).
We use temperature 1.0, top-p 0.8, and a 38,912-token completion limit;
top-k and min-p filtering are disabled. We report average response
accuracy (Avg@$`K`$) and the fraction of problems solved at least once
(Pass@$`K`$), using $`K=12`$ for the main results and $`K=6`$ for the
ablation studies. Mean accuracy is the unweighted average across the
three benchmarks. To assess capability retention after mathematical
training, we also evaluate them on HumanEval, IFEval ([Zhou et al.,
2023](#bib.bib23)), and MMLU ([Hendrycks et al., 2021](#bib.bib26)). For
code-generation experiments, we evaluate all methods on HumanEval+ and
MBPP+ ([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib24)) and the new problems in
LiveCodeBench v5 and v6 ([Jain et al., 2025](#bib.bib25)). We set the
maximum generation length to 4,096 tokens and report Pass@1.
Appendices [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Evaluation Metrics and Protocols ‣ Appendix A Implementation and Evaluation Details ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
and [B](#A2 "Appendix B Retention Evaluation Details ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
provide further evaluation details.

#### Mathematical reasoning.

BOPD outperforms both the initial model and its constructed teacher at
4B and 8B
(Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")).

These results show that a model can construct a teacher from reference
data and use it to train a student that surpasses the teacher itself.
Additional results under thinking-mode and on SmolLM3-3B further support
the effectiveness of BOPD across inference settings and model families

### 5.3 Mechanism Analysis

Prior analyses of on-policy distillation suggest that an effective
teacher should provide useful supervision while remaining sufficiently
compatible with the student on the states visited during training. We
therefore examine whether the bootstrapped teacher in BOPD exhibits a
more favorable teacher–student relationship than the privileged teacher
in OPSD. Following [Li et al. (2026)](#bib.bib10), we track checkpoint
accuracy together with Overlap@16, overlap-token advantage@16, and the
absolute entropy gap during 4B training (Figure
[5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Capability retention. ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")).
The distributional metrics are computed on the same 64 fixed training
prompts used in Section 3, with each checkpoint generating its own
on-policy trajectories.

Figure
[5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Capability retention. ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
shows a clear difference in candidate-set agreement. Under continued
OPSD, Overlap@16 decreases from 0.8930 at step 20 to 0.8272 at step 100,
whereas under BOPD it increases from 0.8638 to 0.9005. Thus, the BOPD
student increasingly shares its teacher’s likely top-16 token
candidates, while the OPSD student progressively moves away from those
of its privileged teacher. This trend accompanies stronger BOPD
checkpoint accuracy on AIME24 and AIME25 after step 40.

The other metrics show a similar contrast. Under BOPD, the overlap-token
advantage remains relatively close to zero with modest fluctuations,
reaching $`-0.0126`$ at step 100. Under OPSD, by contrast, it moves
steadily away from zero, from $`-0.0055`$ to $`-0.0188`$. A similar
pattern appears in the entropy gap: BOPD shows only a moderate increase
from 0.1472 to 0.1887 nats, whereas OPSD starts with a much smaller gap
of 0.0707 nats but increases substantially to 0.2230 nats. Together with
the opposing Overlap@16 trends, these results indicate that the BOPD
teacher remains better matched to the evolving student, whereas the
privileged OPSD teacher becomes increasingly mismatched as training
proceeds. This growing mismatch may limit the quality of supervision
provided by the OPSD teacher at later stages of training.

Taken together, these dynamics are consistent with the bootstrapped
model serving as a better-matched teacher. They support the role of
teacher bootstrapping in converting reference-conditioned supervision
into a more compatible reference-free teacher.

## 6 Conclusion

We study the training dynamics of on-policy self-distillation and find
that its early gains are not sustained under continued training. As the
student evolves, the privileged reference-conditioned teacher becomes
increasingly mismatched with the reference-free student on its on-policy
states. Motivated by this observation, we introduce Bootstrapped
On-Policy Distillation (BOPD), which uses OPSD to construct a
task-adapted intermediate model and then reuses this model as a
reference-free teacher for a fresh student.

Across different models, BOPD improves mathematical reasoning beyond
both the initial model and the constructed OPSD teacher, and also
outperforms the constructed teacher on code generation. Ablations show
that simply extending OPSD training, constructing the teacher with
solution SFT, or restoring reference access during the second stage does
not reproduce these gains under our setting. Training-dynamics analyses
further show that BOPD maintains a more favorable teacher–student
relationship than continued OPSD. At the same time, our
further-distillation experiments show that these gains do not compound
indefinitely. Overall, our results suggest that OPSD can be used
effectively as a teacher-construction mechanism, while separating
privileged teacher construction from subsequent reference-free
distillation provides a practical route to self-improvement without an
external teacher model.

### A.1 Evaluation Metrics and Protocols

For benchmark $`b`$, let $`c_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}`$ indicate whether response
$`j`$ to question $`i`$ is correct. With $`N_{b}=30`$ and $`K`$
responses per problem, we compute

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{Avg@}K_{b}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{100}{N_{b}K}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{i,j},`$ |  | (8) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\operatorname{Pass@}K_{b}`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{100}{N_{b}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}\mathbb{I}\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{i,j}>0\right].`$ |  | (9) |

Table [1](#S5.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
uses $`K=12`$ at 4B and 8B; the ablation tables use $`K=6`$. We average
each metric over the three benchmarks for the table mean. Displayed
values are rounded to two decimal places; the mean uses the underlying
scores. The main mathematical evaluation sets $`\texttt{top\_k}=-1`$,
$`\texttt{min\_p}=0`$, and $`\texttt{presence\_penalty}=0`$, in addition
to temperature 1.0, $`\texttt{top\_p}=0.8`$, and the 38,912-token output
limit. Thinking mode is disabled for every evaluated model in the main
tables.

Code-trained models use the same fixed 3,200 TACO problems for OPSD and
BOPD. HumanEval+ and MBPP+ use the official EvalPlus tests;
LiveCodeBench v5-new and v6-new contain the problems newly added
relative to the preceding release. All four code benchmarks use greedy
decoding, one response per problem, a 4,096-token generation limit, an
8,192-token context limit, and seed 0.

## Appendix E Mechanism Analysis Details

#### Checkpoints and prompts.

We select the same 64 problems from the mathematical training subset
(seed 20260922) for every diagnostic run. We evaluate OPSD checkpoints
at steps 10, 20, $`\ldots`$, 200 and BOPD checkpoints at steps 10, 20,
$`\ldots`$, 100.
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 What Happens When OPSD Continues? ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
displays OPSD every 20 steps;
Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Capability retention. ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
displays both methods through step 100. All rollouts use non-thinking
mode. The corresponding frozen teacher is $`M_{0}`$ with access to each
problem’s reference solution for OPSD, and the reference-free OPSD
step-100 model for BOPD.

#### On-policy teacher–student diagnostics.

For each checkpoint, we generate fresh responses to the 64 prompts with
that checkpoint and replay each generated prefix through both the
student and its frozen teacher. Let $`p_{t}`$ and $`q_{t}`$ denote their
full-vocabulary next-token distributions at position $`t`$. Following
[Li et al. (2026)](#bib.bib10), we define the student and teacher
top-$`k`$ token sets as $`S_{t}^{(p)}=\operatorname{TopK}(p_{t},k)`$ and
$`S_{t}^{(q)}=\operatorname{TopK}(q_{t},k)`$, with $`k=16`$. Let
$`I_{t}=S_{t}^{(p)}\cap S_{t}^{(q)}`$. The overlap ratio is

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{overlap}}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\frac{|I_{t}|}{k}\right].
``` |  | (10) |

For overlap-token advantage, we normalize the student and teacher
probabilities over the shared token set $`I_{t}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{p}_{t}(v)=\frac{p_{t}(v)}{\sum_{u\in I_{t}}p_{t}(u)},\qquad\bar{q}_{t}(v)=\frac{q_{t}(v)}{\sum_{u\in I_{t}}q_{t}(u)},\quad v\in I_{t}.
``` |  | (11) |

The overlap-token advantage is then computed as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{adv}}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\frac{1}{|I_{t}|}\sum_{v\in I_{t}}\bar{p}_{t}(v)\left(\log\bar{q}_{t}(v)-\log\bar{p}_{t}(v)\right)\right].
``` |  | (12) |

We also compute the full-vocabulary $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{t}\|q_{t})`$
and the absolute entropy gap $`|H(p_{t})-H(q_{t})|`$ at each position.
The diagnostic KL uses the student-to-teacher direction, whereas the
distillation objective uses the teacher-to-student direction. Each
plotted value is the mean over all evaluated token positions, so longer
responses contribute more positions. The entropy gap is averaged after
taking the absolute value at each position; it is not the absolute
difference between two mean entropies. We use natural logarithms for
entropy and KL. Although a teacher’s weights are frozen, its predictions
can change across checkpoints because the student-generated prefixes
change.

#### Fixed-prefix and on-policy student entropy.

To produce the bottom row of
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 What Happens When OPSD Continues? ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation"),
we also generate a set of reference-free $`M_{0}`$ trajectories for the
same 64 prompts and hold their prefixes fixed. At OPSD step $`s`$, we
evaluate the student distribution $`p_{s}(\cdot\mid u)`$ at every
position $`u`$ in this fixed set and average its full-vocabulary
entropy. For the on-policy curve, we instead average
$`H(p_{s}(\cdot\mid u))`$ over positions in that checkpoint’s own
generated trajectories. Thus, the fixed-prefix curve holds the evaluated
contexts constant across checkpoints, whereas the on-policy curve also
reflects changes in the contexts visited by the student. The two curves
need not contain the same number of token positions and are not paired
position by position. The bottom-right KL curve uses the on-policy
prefixes and the reference-conditioned OPSD teacher, as above.

#### Accuracy evaluation.

The top row of
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 What Happens When OPSD Continues? ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
evaluates AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and HMMT February 2025, which are
separate from the 64 training prompts. It uses six responses per problem
and reports Avg@6 under the mathematics decoding protocol of
Section [5](#S5 "5 Experiments ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation").
We plot checkpoint values without smoothing. Steps on the OPSD and BOPD
curves in
Figure [5](#S5.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ Capability retention. ‣ 5.2 Main Results ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ Bootstrapped On-Policy Distillation")
````

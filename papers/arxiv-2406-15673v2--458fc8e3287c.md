###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have attracted significant attention for
their exceptional abilities in various natural language processing
tasks, but they suffer from hallucinations that will cause performance
degradation. One promising solution to improve the LLMs’ performance is
to ask LLMs to revise their answer after generation, a technique known
as self-correction. Among the two types of self-correction, intrinsic
self-correction is considered a promising direction because it does not
utilize external knowledge. However, recent works doubt the validity of
LLM’s ability to conduct intrinsic self-correction. In this paper, we
present a novel perspective on the intrinsic self-correction
capabilities of LLMs through theoretical analyses and empirical
experiments. In addition, we identify two critical factors for
successful self-correction: zero temperature and fair prompts.
Leveraging these factors, we demonstrate that intrinsic self-correction
ability is exhibited across multiple existing LLMs. Our findings offer
insights into the fundamental theories underlying the self-correction
behavior of LLMs and remark on the importance of unbiased prompts and
zero temperature settings in harnessing their full potential.

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed the field of natural
language processing, showcasing exceptional capabilities in generating
coherent, contextually appropriate text across diverse domains ([OpenAI,
2024](#bib.bib35); [Gao et al., 2024](#bib.bib14)). As of now, while
LLMs are still not considered equivalent to human-level intelligence
([Shen et al., 2023](#bib.bib44); [Mathur et al., 2024](#bib.bib33)),
they exhibit some interesting characteristics that are possessed by
human beings, including the emergent ability ([Wei et al.,
2022a](#bib.bib55))¹¹ 1 whether LLMs exhibit emergent ability is still
in debate in academia, partially due to the definition of “emergence”.
See [Schaeffer et al. (2023)](#bib.bib43) and [Lu et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib30) for details. and performing complex tasks ([Chiang &
Lee, 2023](#bib.bib7); [Gruver et al., 2023](#bib.bib18)). One
particularly compelling aspect of human cognition is the ability to
self-correct. Self-correction (SC) is the continuous process of
evaluating and refining one’s responses or actions to achieve improved
outcomes. There are generally two types of SC: extrinsic SC which
utilizes the help of external knowledge and intrinsic SC which uses only
one’s own knowledge. Inspired by humans, the research community proposed
a promising hypothesis that we could leverage the power of SC to enhance
the quality of LLMs. In particular, researchers are interested in
whether intrinsic SC ability could be applied in LLMs.

Over the last few years, numerous research studies have been conducted
on the effectiveness of intrinsic SC of LLMs. For example, works like
self-refine ([Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib31)), self-verification
([Gero et al., 2023](#bib.bib15); [Weng et al., 2023](#bib.bib58);
[Manakul et al., 2023](#bib.bib32)), post-editing ([Raunak et al.,
2023](#bib.bib41)), all demonstrate significantly enhanced performance
of SC on respective benchmarks compared to results obtained without SC.
However, recent works have questioned the efficacy of intrinsic SC
mechanisms in LLMs ([Huang et al., 2024](#bib.bib21); [Kumar et al.,
2024](#bib.bib25)). A notable critique presented by ([Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib21)) is that LLMs do not possess the ability to perform
intrinsic self-correction. Their argument is based on a fundamental
question: why couldn’t an LLM get the correct answer in the initial
attempt instead of going through intrinsic SC? They hypothesize that
existing works on SC abilities in LLMs shall be attributed to three
factors: external feedback (such as the existence of a retrieval model)
that converts the problem to an extrinsic setting, ground-truth label as
an early stopping criterion that hinders the practical usage, and
uninformative prompts that limit LLM’s ability when it generates the
first response before SC (which raises the possibility of unfair
comparison). Through the experiments, both [Huang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib21) and [Kumar et al. (2024)](#bib.bib25) report that
LLMs showcase different levels of degradation in QA accuracy after
intrinsic SC.

In light of such disagreement, it is important to answer a set of
fundamental questions: Does intrinsic SC ability exist within LLMs? And
if it does, why couldn’t an LLM get the correct answer in the initial
attempt instead of going through intrinsic SC?

This paper answers the above fundamental questions. With theoretical
analyses and quantitative experiments under the same settings as [Huang
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21), we empirically show that LLMs do have
intrinsic SC ability. In addition, we also show that LLMs cannot answer
questions correctly during the initial attempt because of their inherent
hallucination property.

Building on top of the answers, further investigations are conducted on
the conditions that maximize SC potential within LLMs. Through
theoretical analyses and empirical experiments, we identify two factors
impacting the intrinsic SC ability of LLMs. The first factor is
temperature. A non-zero temperature, such as done by [Huang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib21), degrades SC ability. The second factor, equally
important, is the neutrality and fairness of the prompts used during
various stages of SC. A fair and unbiased prompt that doesn’t directly
or indirectly influence the LLM to change or maintain its initial answer
provides the most stable benefits from the intrinsic SC ability of LLMs.
In Fig.
([3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.3 Ablation study on Model size ‣ 5 Designing Fair Prompt for Self-Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")),
we provide an example illustrating how biased ([Huang et al.,
2024](#bib.bib21)) and unbiased (ours) prompts affect the output of the
LLM during different stages of SC.

This paper’s contributions could be summarized as the following:

- •
  We provide a novel perspective on the functioning nature of intrinsic
  self-correction in Section
  [2](#S2 "2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").
  We posit that self-correction abilities bear similarities to
  chain-of-thought (CoT) and self-verification prompting methodologies,
  which furnish LLMs with additional context for generating the final
  response.
- •
  We highlight the importance of the temperature of LLMs in the success
  of intrinsic self-correction in Section
  [4](#S4 "4 Increasing Temperature Might Decrease Accuracy During SC ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").
  While increasing the temperature will benefit the creativity of
  response, it may be detrimental for SC, depending on the structure of
  LLM’s response.
- •
  Additionally, in Section
  [5](#S5 "5 Designing Fair Prompt for Self-Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"),
  we show that the SC prompt needs to be unbiased so that LLMs do not
  accidentally attend to tokens that might trigger unwanted answer
  updates during the SC process. We also provide guidelines to compose
  unbiased prompts for future research.
- •
  We empirically verify the effects of the two factors above, showcasing
  the accuracy degradation of generated SC responses when increasing
  temperature and utilizing biased prompts, and intrinsic SC can be
  achieved by LLMs via fair prompts and zero temperature.

Our answers to those fundamental questions serve as a basis for the
underlying mechanism of intrinsic SC, and we hope that our findings will
shed further light on LLM’s abilities and encourage future works and
discussions to explore methods that could leverage the existing SC
ability more effectively.

## 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction

### 2.1 Preliminary

The text generation process of LLMs could be modeled as an
auto-regressive probabilistic distribution $`P`$. Given an LLM and a
prompt of $`t`$ tokens $`\tau=\{w_{1},w_{2},...w_{t}\}`$, the
auto-regressive generation of the response takes the form of Eq.
([1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Preliminary ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")),
where $`y`$ is the (unnormalized) logits from the LLM, $`w_{t+1}`$ is a
potential next token, $`l_{w_{t+1}}`$ is the scaled logit value for
$`w_{t+1}`$, and $`T`$ is the temperature. Thus, the generated content
of an LLM adheres to the distribution of its logits, readjusted by the
temperature. As temperature approaches 0, it is clear that the
generation process changes from sampling in a distribution to selecting
the index of the maximum value of logits, as described in Eq.
([2](#S2.E2 "In 2.1 Preliminary ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")).

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
P(w_{t+1}|w_{1},...w_{t})\sim y\quad\text{,}\quad l_{w_{t+1}}=\frac{e^{\frac{y_{w_{t+1}}}{T}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\frac{y_{k}}{T}}}
``` |  | (1) |

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\lim_{T\to 0^{+}}l_{w_{t+1}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }y_{w_{t+1}}=\max y\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
``` |  | (2) |

Although the definitions above may appear elementary, they constitute
critical foundations for this study. In particular, they provide
justifications for some of the analyses in the future sections. We draw
several insights on this foundational formation:

\(1\) Under $`T=0`$, any arbitrary LLM should output the same response
on some fixed prompt $`\tau`$. Unfortunately, models of the GPT family
([OpenAI, 2024](#bib.bib35); [Brown et al., 2020](#bib.bib3)) do not
follow this principle, possibly due to their query optimizations and
internal sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) design of GPT-4. In particular,
GPT-4 exhibits very high variance for its answers under $`T=0`$. Those
unknown behaviors cause some variations in the evaluation process.

\(2\) Intrinsic SC increases the prompt length $`t`$ constructively,
which helps improve the generation quality of the final response. This
is similar to self-verification ([Weng et al., 2023](#bib.bib58); [Gero
et al., 2023](#bib.bib15)) and chain-of-thought (CoT) ([Wei et al.,
2022b](#bib.bib56)). We will elaborate on this in Section
[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").

\(3\) Increasing temperature might not cause visible accuracy
degradation in the evaluation process for response generation without
SC, but it might be detrimental to the intrinsic SC due to the
non-optimal sampling process. We will elaborate on this in Section
[4](#S4 "4 Increasing Temperature Might Decrease Accuracy During SC ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").

### 2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs?

In this section, we answer the first fundamental question of whether
intrinsic SC exists in LLMs by providing an alternative explanation of
the underlying mechanism of self-correction. For now, we focus on one
model (GPT3.5-0613) and fix the temperature at zero.

We initiate our analysis with the chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting
technique ²² 2 CoT prompt could be elicited with phrases such as
”explain your reasoning” or ”think step by step” in LLMs during the
prompting., which is proven to be useful in enhancing LLM’s performance
in accuracy ([Wei et al., 2022b](#bib.bib56)). Based on the
auto-regressive nature of LLM generation, [Chu et al. (2023)](#bib.bib8)
models the CoT process as a conditional probability shown in Eq.
([3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")),
where A is the answer to the prompt (and question) $`\tau_{1}`$, and
$`R_{1}`$ is the rationale provided by LLM in the CoT process. The
reasoning context introduced by CoT helps the LLM make a better final
decision. Similarly, using the effective depth of the transformer
circuit, [Feng et al. (2023)](#bib.bib12) argues that CoT increases the
effective depth of the circuit by letting the generated outputs
repeatedly loop back to the input ³³ 3 It shall be noted that [Feng
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib12) actually proves that with longer generations,
transformers (for some choice of weights; not necessarily the
pre-trained weights) have more expressive capacity to model more complex
problems..

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p(A,R_{1}|\tau_{1})=p(A|\tau_{1},R_{1})p(R_{1}|\tau_{1})}
``` |  | (3) |

Similarly, it is apparent that intrinsic SC also has the potential to
achieve CoT-like behavior. Generally, we could model the generation
process of a typical intrinsic SC according to Eq.
([4](#S2.E4 "In 2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")).
The first stage is a generic CoT prompting and answering schema. In the
second stage, based on the first part’s results, we use a prompt
$`\tau_{2}`$ to let LLM provide rationales ($`R_{2}`$) and a decision
($`D`$) on the correctness of the previous answer. Then, in the last
stage, we instruct LLM with $`\tau_{3}`$ to give a final answer
$`A^{\prime}`$ based on all previous information. We refer readers to
Fig.
([3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.3 Ablation study on Model size ‣ 5 Designing Fair Prompt for Self-Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"))
for an example of each stage.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p_{SC}(A^{\prime},D,R_{2},A,R_{1}|\tau_{3},\tau_{2},\tau_{1})=}\\
&\underbrace{p(A^{\prime}|\tau_{3},D,R_{2},\tau_{2},A,R_{1},\tau_{1})}_{\textit{stage 3}}\underbrace{p(D,R_{2}|\tau_{2},A,R_{1},\tau_{1})}_{\textit{stage 2}}\underbrace{p(A|R_{1},\tau_{1})p(R_{1}|\tau_{1})}_{\textit{stage 1}}\end{split}
``` |  | (4) |

The second stage is the key factor of the SC process. Since it requires
the LLM to provide a decision over the correctness of the previous
answer as well as the rationales, there are two different orderings for
the output depending on the position of the decision and rationale. We
could model those two orderings as a conditional probability
distribution, as shown in
Eq.([5](#S2.E5 "In 2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"))
where Order 1 refers to having the decision first followed by rationale
and Order 2 being the other way around:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\textit{Order 1: }\underbrace{p(R_{2}|D,\tau_{2},A,R_{1},\tau_{1})}_{\textit{rationale}}\underbrace{p(D|\tau_{2},A,R_{1},\tau_{1})}_{\textit{decision}}\\
\textit{Order 2: }\underbrace{p(D|R_{2},\tau_{2},A,R_{1},\tau_{1})}_{\textit{decision}}\underbrace{p(R_{2}|\tau_{2},A,R_{1},\tau_{1})}_{\textit{rationale}}\end{split}
``` |  | (5) |

Depending on the different prompts designs, we discuss the different
behaviors of intrinsic SC below:

Case 1: if $`\tau_{1}`$ uses CoT and the second stage follows Order 1 In
this case, intrinsic SC is just an implicit form of self-verification
([Gero et al., 2023](#bib.bib15); [Weng et al., 2023](#bib.bib58)).
Based on the rationales given in stage 1, stage 2 will provide an answer
on the consistency of the provided rationales by reasoning on them. As
shown in Table
[1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"),
there is an improvement in accuracy after SC. We will show more
experiments in Section
[5](#S5 "5 Designing Fair Prompt for Self-Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")
that this case provides some accuracy improvement to the final result.

Case 2: if $`\tau_{1}`$ uses CoT and the second stage follows Order 2 In
this case, SC could be seen as a CoT answer over a prompt that contains
CoT-alike context. By providing more rationales in the second stage, SC
provides additional analyses over the provided rationales in the first
stage and makes a better decision. If well implemented, this case could
lead to better performance compared to the first one. However, such an
approach might not be very feasible as we cannot fully control the
decomposition orderings of LLMs’ output through prompts. During our
experiments, GPT-3.5 shows a strong preference for the decomposition
Order 1, and the designed prompt is only able to successfully change
some responses’ order.

Case 3: if $`\tau_{1}`$ does not use CoT and the second stage follows
Order 1 When $`\tau_{1}`$ does not use CoT, the decomposition of the
first stage in Eq.
([4](#S2.E4 "In 2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"))
is simply $`p(A|\tau_{1})`$. In this case, intrinsic SC likely has a
negative impact, and it might cause an accuracy drop after correction.
This is because the decision is first given without any rationale, and
subsequent provided rationales are now dependent on a possibly
hallucinated answer.

Case 4: if $`\tau_{1}`$ does not use CoT and the second stage follows
Order 2 This serves the same purpose as CoT, and thus accuracy will
improve. By requiring LLMs to first provide rationales, followed by a
decision on the answer, and finally offering a more informed conclusion,
SC enhances the overall accuracy. The improved accuracy is comparable
(if not matching) to merely using CoT prompt with no self-correction as
shown in Table
[1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").

| CommonSense QA           | Before SC | After SC |
|--------------------------|-----------|----------|
| Case 1 (CoT, Order 1)    | 75.92     | 76.00    |
| Case 2 (CoT, Order 2)    | 75.92     | 76.49    |
| Case 3 (no CoT, Order 1) | 75.35     | 74.77    |
| Case 4 (no CoT, Order 2) | 75.35     | 75.92    |

Hence, we conclude that intrinsic SC ability exists in LLMs with an
adequate prompting style and is potentially as effective as other prompt

### 2.3 Why can’t LLM answer questions correctly in the initial attempt?

When LLMs hallucinate, one of the scenarios they exhibit is that they
will output answers incorrectly even when having the correct knowledge.
While that is not the full schema of LLM hallucination, in this paper,
we simply refer to this type of scenario as “hallucination”. Assuming
LLMs have a non-zero possibility of hallucination, we show that LLMs
will not reach their full ability in the initial attempt with
Proposition.
[2.1](#S2.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 2.1. ‣ 2.3 Why can’t LLM answer questions correctly in the initial attempt? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").

###### Proposition 2.1.

LLMs are generally under-performing compared to their true ability
because hallucination will cause the overall accuracy to decrease.

This lemma serves as a justification for the second fundamental question
and we provide the proof of Proposition.
[2.1](#S2.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 2.1. ‣ 2.3 Why can’t LLM answer questions correctly in the initial attempt? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")
in Appendix
[C](#A3 "Appendix C Proof of Proposition ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").
As drawn by the conclusion in the previous section, intrinsic SC can
reduce hallucinations of the generated responses and achieve better
quality similar to other prompt engineering techniques.

## 4 Increasing Temperature Might Decrease Accuracy During SC

As mentioned in Section
[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Preliminary ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")
and Eq.
([1](#S2.E1 "In 2.1 Preliminary ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")),
the temperature hyperparameter controls the randomness of the generated
context. It is widely acknowledged within the research community that
increasing the temperature parameter (within a reasonable range)
enhances the creativity of LLMs, resulting in the generation of more
diverse content ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib20)). Works like [Wang
et al. (2020)](#bib.bib52) also try to dynamically optimize the
temperature at the token level for best generation quality. Furthermore,
a very recent work from [Renze & Guven (2024)](#bib.bib42) has claimed
to find no correlation between increasing temperature and losing
accuracy on benchmarks for temperatures between 0.0 and 1.0. However, as
we will show in this section, temperature choice is a non-trivial aspect
of the success of some LLMs during intrinsic SC. While increasing the
temperature indeed shows no effect on the original response, it might
have a negative impact on the subsequent stages of the SC process.

Our experimental results indicate that GPT-3.5 is adversely affected by
increasing temperature, while GPT4 exhibits greater robustness to
temperature variations. We provide the theoretical analysis below, based
on the decomposition of the second stage of SC.

### 4.1 Theoretical Analysis

We begin by demonstrating that the randomness in decision-making
diminishes as the temperature decreases. We denote a positive decision
("yes") as $`D=1`$, and vice versa $`D=0`$ for a negative decision
("no"). Therefore, the decision procedure according to the context,
which may include the prompts, rationales, and previous answers,
degrades to a 0/1 binary classification that follows the binomial
distribution.

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
p(D=1)=\frac{\alpha_{1}^{\frac{1}{T}}}{\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\frac{1}{T}}}=\frac{\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}}{\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}+(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{T}}},
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\alpha\in(0,1)`$ is the original positive decision probability
of the model. Therefore, considering the variance of the binomial
distribution, we have:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
Var(D)=\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{T}}/(\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}+(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{T}})^{2}
``` |  | (7) |

Next, we consider the effect of $`T`$ going from $`0`$ to $`+\infty`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\frac{\partial Var(D)}{\partial T}=\frac{\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{T}}[\ln(\alpha)-\ln(1-\alpha)][\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}-(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{T}}]}{T^{2}\left(\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}+(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{T}}\right)^{3}}\geq 0,
``` |  | (8) |

The inequality holds because $`(\ln(\alpha)-\ln(1-\alpha))`$ and
$`(\alpha^{1/T}-(1-\alpha)^{1/T})`$ share the same positive and negative
sign, since we have $`\alpha\in(0,1)`$ and $`T>0`$. This implies that
when there is an output preference ($`0<\alpha<1`$), the variance of the
decision increases monotonically with the model’s temperature, and the
model with 0 temperature will be less likely to give the wrong decision
due to less randomness. When the temperature goes to infinity, the model
randomly selects an answer from {"yes", "no"} to give the decision,
causing an unexpected decision error. At the same time, confidence in
the decision is correlated to the variance: $`\alpha=0.5`$ gives the
highest variance of 0.25, whereas $`\alpha`$ approaching 0 or 1 will
reduce variance.

With an increasing variance, $`D`$ has an increasing possibility of
being flipped to the other side. Therefore, when $`T`$ is non-zero, a
higher number of decisions will fail to reflect the true knowledge
encoded within the LLM. In other words, those temperature-flipped binary
decisions are in fact “hallucinations”, and under Proposition
[2.1](#S2.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 2.1. ‣ 2.3 Why can’t LLM answer questions correctly in the initial attempt? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"),
those hallucinations will cause accuracy decrements after the SC
process.

Finally, we refer to Eq.
([5](#S2.E5 "In 2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"))
and examine the two decompositions. Through the analysis of the effect
of $`\alpha`$ on the variance, we argue that Order 1 is more prone to
temperature changes than Order 2. With the second ordering, the final
decision after rationales is very close to a copying task, and LLMs are
known to have very high confidence in performing this task ($`\alpha`$
very close to 0 or 1). On the other hand, providing the decision is a
reasoning task that is much harder with less confidence ($`\alpha`$
closer to 0.5). Using Fig.
([3](#S5.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 5.3 Ablation study on Model size ‣ 5 Designing Fair Prompt for Self-Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"))
as an example, imagine a hypothetical answer such that GPT-3.5 puts the
first sentence (decision) in stage 2 at the end. If so, to obtain the
same information, it only needs to perform selective copying based on
the rationales above, which is much easier (and thus more confident)
than the current way.

## 5 Designing Fair Prompt for Self-Correction

Prompt engineering plays a pivotal role in the generation process of
LLMs. Numerous studies have demonstrated that carefully crafted prompts
can substantially enhance the quality of the generated output ([Wei
et al., 2022b](#bib.bib56); [Zhang et al., 2023](#bib.bib64); [Weng
et al., 2023](#bib.bib58)). In this section, we demonstrate that prompt
design significantly influences the SC performance of certain models and
that a fair prompt is capable of eliciting universal intrinsic SC
abilities in LLMs. In addition, we provide guidelines for fair prompting
for future research in this direction.

To ensure a fair comparison, we focus exclusively on Case 1 as described
in Section
[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").
Case 1 shares the same settings as [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21),
with the only distinction being the semantic bias of the prompt.
Empirical experiments are carried out by retaining the prompt from the
first stage of intrinsic SC, adhering to the same prompt used by [Huang
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21), and demonstrating that inadequate prompts
can lead to erroneous behavior in LLMs.

### 5.1 Theoretical Analysis

Referring back to Eq.
([4](#S2.E4 "In 2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")),
we see the final output from stage 3 depends on eight factors. Among
them, $`A`$, $`R_{1}`$, and $`\tau_{1}`$ are fixed from stage 1.
Excluding those fixed constants, $`D`$ and $`R_{2}`$ depend solely on
$`\tau_{2}`$. Therefore, we conclude that the final answer is contingent
only upon two variables, $`\tau_{2}`$ and $`\tau_{3}`$, corresponding to
the prompts in stages 2 and 3.

The analysis of the prompt’s effect shares the same principle as
Proposition
[2.1](#S2.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 2.1. ‣ 2.3 Why can’t LLM answer questions correctly in the initial attempt? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").
We investigate the effect of the biased prompts from [Huang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib21) and [Kumar et al. (2024)](#bib.bib25) that encourage
the model to change the answer on the final result. We assume that the
LLM strictly adheres to the prompt, randomly altering $`\gamma\%`$ of
the answers that would remain unchanged under a fair prompt, where
$`\gamma>0`$⁴⁴ 4 We assume $`\gamma>0`$ for a reasonable LLM. From the
semantic understanding, encouragement of changing answers should lead to
more changes in the answer. We also provide the empirical number of
changes of the answers in Appendix
[D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Numerical results on the number of changed answers ‣ Appendix D Experiments not included in main paper ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")..
The randomness of the changes is expected, as those are the cases in
which LLMs would not have changed in a normal setting, and thus they
should not be attributed to certain characteristics, such as
correctness. For ease of analysis, we assume that LLM does not change
any answer with a fair prompt (thus the accuracy difference after SC is
0).

### 5.2 Intrinsic SC could be achieved with fair prompt under zero temperature

We consider three sets of prompts. The first set (Problem Set 1) is used
by [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21), but we argue that they are biased
towards encouraging the LLMs to change answers due to the use of the
negative phrase “find problems”. The second set (Problem Set 2) is
derived from the first one by removing the negative phrases and asking
the LLM to check if the answer is correct or incorrect and act
accordingly. While such a prompt may appear fair from a human
perspective due to the balance of both aspects, the inclusion of the
terms ’correct’ and ’incorrect’ can indirectly influence the LLM’s
behavior. Therefore in the last set of prompts (Problem set 3), we strip
the prompts from such phrases and words to make a completely unbiased
prompt. We provide details on the three sets in Appendix
[E](#A5 "Appendix E Prompt and example outputs ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").
As shown in Table
[2](#S5.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 5.1 Theoretical Analysis ‣ 5 Designing Fair Prompt for Self-Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"),
using an unbiased prompt and zero temperature, all of the benchmarked
LLMs exhibit accuracy improvement on the different benchmarks.
Nevertheless, biased prompts may decrease accuracy in certain LLMs and
specific benchmarks. It is noteworthy that this decrease seems to be
related to some specific features of the benchmarks and models, but as
of now, a reasonable explanation and prediction of the specific features
are yet to be discovered. For example, in CommonSense QA benchmark
([Talmor et al., 2019](#bib.bib49)), the bias of the prompt has
significant impacts on GPT and Llama models, but not so much on the
other models. On the other hand, the bias of the prompt seems to only
affect the GPT models, but it has no impact on the Mistral and Llama
models for the HotpotQA benchmark ([Yang et al., 2018](#bib.bib61)). We
hypothesize that this might be because those models are instruction
fine-tuned, and GPT families are further fine-tuned with reinforcement
learning with human feedback (RLHF) ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#bib.bib36)).
Depending on the different training datasets that are not always
disclosed, an LLM’s accuracy after SC might be conditionally robust to
biased prompts, depending on the context.

Importantly, our results align with the self-verification results
obtained by [Weng et al. (2023)](#bib.bib58) where they also obtained a
slight improvement using a different model. The on-par results further
validate our claim in Section
[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")
that intrinsic SC is similar to other existing prompt engineering
techniques.

In Appendix
[D.3](#A4.SS3 "D.3 Numerical results on the number of changed answers ‣ Appendix D Experiments not included in main paper ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"),
we provide further numerical analysis of how some of the models change
their answers during SC. The results not only align with the theoretical
analysis but also provide further insights into why some models remain
robust on some benchmarks. With the $`\gamma>0\%`$ extra changes caused
by the biased prompt, more incorrect answers are introduced in the final
stage.

All of the above analyses, reinforce our initial claim that a fair
prompt is a safe option. A truly unbiased prompt must avoid both
semantically and literally negative or positive phrases, such as “find
the problem” or “correct your answer”. We encourage future researchers
to follow these guidelines when composing their future SC prompts,
similar to the unbiased Prompt Set 3.

## 6 Limitations

While the paper aims to investigate the fundamental functioning nature
of intrinsic self-correction, we recognize several limitations of the
presented results. Due to computational and staffing constraints,
evaluations are limited to four models and six datasets. In Sections
[4](#S4 "4 Increasing Temperature Might Decrease Accuracy During SC ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")
and
[5](#S5 "5 Designing Fair Prompt for Self-Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"),
the focus is mainly on a subset of models and the CommonSense QA
dataset. Error bars are not provided due to resource limitations, as
generating results for error analysis would require extensive
computational and financial resources. While this is the common approach
for the LLM community due to costs, we acknowledge that variances will
exist for non-zero temperature experiments.

## 7 Conclusion

In this paper, we investigate the intrinsic self-correction capabilities
of large language models. Supported by theoretical analysis and
empirical experimentation, we present a novel perspective on the
problem, positing that intrinsic self-correction is analogous to
self-verification and chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting. Furthermore, we
point out zero temperature and unbiased prompts are salient in
facilitating intrinsic self-correction, we also underscore the
significance of these two key factors. Our experimental results
demonstrate that LLMs possess universal self-correction abilities,
provided these conditions are met. Our findings will serve as a
important foundation for future research and understanding into the
self-correction mechanisms of large language models.

## Appendix A Related Works

During LLM inference, self-correction (SC) refers to the ability to
iteratively improve future responses by reflecting on the errors from
previous responses. We refer readers to an excellent survey on the
correction mechanism of LLMs ([Pan et al., 2023](#bib.bib37)).
Self-correction characteristics in Large Language Models can be
generally achieved by two approaches: intrinsic SC and extrinsic SC. In
this section, we provide a brief survey on the current techniques used
on both sides, with a focus on the intrinsic self-correction ability,
which is also the focus of the main discussion of this paper.

### A.1 Extrinsic SC

In the current LLM community, extrinsic self-correction (SC) refers to
large language models (LLMs) using external sources of knowledge to
refine their own answers. This process enables the models to improve the
accuracy and reliability of their responses by cross-referencing and
correcting potential errors based on updated or specialized information.
Such an idea could be traced back to decades ago when external
information was used to guide a deep learning (DL) model to generate
more accurate answers ([Gou et al., 2021](#bib.bib16); [Hinton et al.,
2015](#bib.bib20)). Since DL models were mainly used for classification
tasks before generative models became prevalent, the focus was on
knowledge distillation, where more powerful DL models transfer their
knowledge in the training stage of smaller models. After large language
models became the focus of many researchers, the idea of extrinsic
self-correction started to draw attention.

Common approaches include using other LLMs as verifiers ([Wang et al.,
2023a](#bib.bib53); [Zhang et al., 2024](#bib.bib63); [Gao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib13); [Nathani et al., 2023](#bib.bib34); [Welleck
et al.,](#bib.bib57) ), recommenders ([Wang et al., 2023a](#bib.bib53);
[Nathani et al., 2023](#bib.bib34)), or even revisers ([Gao et al.,
2023](#bib.bib13); [Yue et al., 2024](#bib.bib62)). A verifier is
usually a stronger LLM (e.g. GPT4) that verifies the correctness of
answers posted by a smaller and weaker LLM. For example, [Zhang et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib63) has found that paired with a strong verifier based
on GPT-4, weaker LLMs can enjoy significant accuracy improvement. A
recommender suggests solutions and resources to the weaker LLMs, whereas
a reviser steps in and changes the answer as needed. In many proposed
works, the stronger external LLM could serve multiple roles. For
example, the stronger LLM proposed by [Wang et al. (2023a)](#bib.bib53)
both verifies the correctness of the answer and also provides
suggestions for improvement.

Moreover, external self-correction often pairs up with additional
features to better assist weaker models. For example, [Wang et al.
(2023a)](#bib.bib53) finetunes the verifier with a high-quality critic
dataset, and [Chen et al. (2024b)](#bib.bib5); [Gou et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib17); [Peng et al. (2023)](#bib.bib39) propose to use
retrieval augmented generation (RAG) to assist LLMs in evaluating the
responses and generating higher quality criticisms and suggestions.
Overall, it’s the consensus in the research community that using
external sources will help LLMs generate better responses during
external self-correction.

### A.2 Intrinsic SC

Albeit the success of extrinsic self-correction, researchers are more
interested in the other half of the paradigm, intrinsic SC. The
community’s interest is partially due to the fact that extrinsic SC
often requires access to an external knowledge base and even external
larger LLMs. For example, although [Nathani et al. (2023)](#bib.bib34)
shows that a multi-aspect framework, consisting of other LLMs,
programming tools, and other programs, can successfully help LLMs
generate better responses, the availability of those tools renders less
practicality to the solution. On the other hand, if the LLMs can improve
the responses solely by themselves, they will resemble the interesting
self-correction ability of humans (when we make mistakes, there is a
certain chance that we realize the mistake and improve our previous
response without external resources).

Following the previous discussion of knowledge distillation as the
predecessor of extrinsic SC, the early days of intrinsic SC could be
attributed to the works of self-training, where DL models keep
finetuning themselves using self-generated labels on confident
predictions ([Amini et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)). It has been shown that if
used appropriately, such an approach will also lead to more accurate DL
models ([Zoph et al., 2020](#bib.bib65)). Intuitively, like LLMs
inheriting knowledge distillation with extrinsic SC, they should also
have inherited self-training with intrinsic SC. Indeed, numerous works
have shown that intrinsic SC abilities exist in LLMs, with the vast
majority of work using prompt engineering as the backbone ([Shinn
et al., 2023](#bib.bib45); [Madaan et al., 2023](#bib.bib31); [Li
et al., 2024](#bib.bib26); [Chen et al., 2024a](#bib.bib4); [Liu et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib27); [Liu et al., 2024b](#bib.bib28); [Kim et al.,
2023](#bib.bib24); [Dhuliawala et al., 2023](#bib.bib10); [Wu et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib60)). Among them, [Madaan et al. (2023)](#bib.bib31) is
usually seen as one of the first works that systematically proposes the
prompt for intrinsic SC. Later works (including ours) often follow their
setting, where the same LLM is used to generate feedback and improved
answers to its initial responses.

However, [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21) challenges the existence of
intrinsic SC and attributes the success of existing prompt sets to
several reasons. First, [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21) believes that
[Shinn et al. (2023)](#bib.bib45) and [Kim et al. (2023)](#bib.bib24)
utilize oracle labels as the SC criteria during inference, hindering
their practicality in the real world. Second, it believes that [Madaan
et al. (2023)](#bib.bib31) uses a suboptimal prompt design that makes
the initial responses unnecessarily bad. Following their arguments, they
would also argue that [Chen et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib4), [Wu et al.
(2024b)](#bib.bib60) and [Dhuliawala et al. (2023)](#bib.bib10) build
suboptimal prompt sets that give favor to the refinement stages of
intrinsic SC.

Besides [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib21) and [Kumar et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib25) who use biased prompts, in this same line of work,
other negative results indicating the failure of intrinsic SC have been
reported by some investigations. [Jiang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib23) shows
that LLMs cannot discriminate among its generated content and select the
best option. By sampling from multiple generation traces and asking an
LLM to discriminate the best option, [Jiang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib23)
shows that LLMs are not inherently better at discriminating (choosing
the best option) among their prior outputs than they are at generating
responses. Yet, [Jiang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib23) is under a different
setting compared to intrinsic self-correction, where it uses multiple
agents in the first step and uses the averaged generation results as the
initial accuracy, which already brings LLM very close to its true
ability. [Tyen et al. (2024)](#bib.bib51) argues that LLMs often
struggle to identify the location of errors in CoT tracing. Yet, their
results, while hinting at the lack of reasoning abilities in LLMs, show
a positive net value in correcting the traces from incorrect to correct,
supporting the existence of intrinsic SC ability in LLMs.

Nonetheless, as our work and some concurrent works suggest through
empirical results and analyses, intrinsic SC is not a mirage. [Li et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib26) argues on the existence through the LLM’s confidence
towards different questions. By designing a prompt set that lets LLM
update its answers based on its confidence, that work achieves
outstanding results where intrinsic SC helps improve accuracy across
four different models. However, it should be noted that there is not yet
a consensus on LLM’s judgment over its confidence, as discussed by
[Singh et al. (2023)](#bib.bib46) and [Becker & Soatto
(2024)](#bib.bib2). [Singh et al. (2023)](#bib.bib46); [Becker & Soatto
(2024)](#bib.bib2) indicate that LLMs show confidence in incorrect
answers. From a fairness and uncertainty perspective, our work
generalizes from [Li et al. (2024)](#bib.bib26) by taking a different
path. We show that a generic fair prompt can also achieve universal
intrinsic SC under zero temperature. [Liu et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib27)
and [Liu et al. (2024b)](#bib.bib28) focus on a different theoretical
perspective of the intrinsic SC ability. Similar to our argument, they
argue that LLMs do not reach their best ability in the first round (our
argument about hallucination, which they refer to as non-activated
latent concepts). Through empirical results, [Liu et al.
(2024a)](#bib.bib27) shows that intrinsic SC will cause LLM to converge
to an accuracy higher than the initial performance. Their work focuses
more on the convergence analysis. Instead, we conduct an analysis of the
optimal conditions for LLMs performing intrinsic SC.

### A.3 Applications of Self-Correction

Besides the improvement in response accuracy, intrinsic self-correction
has the potential beyond simple answer generation. [Liu et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib29) shows that introspective reasoning can enhance the
model’s performance after reinforcement learning. [Wang et al.
(2023b)](#bib.bib54) also shows that LLMs can self-generate instructions
for its fine-tuning dataset. Content generated by intrinsic
self-correction can be seen as reasoning traces that can be used to
fine-tune the models during reinforcement learning. Such an idea is also
hinted at by the growing literature on inference scaling ([Kumar et al.,
2024](#bib.bib25); [Puerto et al., 2024](#bib.bib40)). On a different
line of work, due to the success of GPT4-o1, inference scaling is
gaining attention in the research community ([Wu et al.,
2024a](#bib.bib59); [Snell et al., 2024](#bib.bib47)).

## Appendix B Details on Benchmarks

We use the following benchmarks:

- •

  offering five possible answers, of which only one is correct.
- •

  MMLU (Formal Logic and Conceptual Physics) ([Hendrycks et al.,
  2021](#bib.bib19)) The MMLU benchmark comprises multiple-choice

  subjects: Formal Logic and Conceptual Physics, due to resource
  constraints.
- •
  HotpotQA ([Yang et al., 2018](#bib.bib61)) HotpotQA is a
  question-answering dataset developed to evaluate a model’s ability to
  engage in multi-hop reasoning. In alignment with previous studies, we

  et al. (2023)](#bib.bib45).
- •
  BIG Bench (Sport) ([Srivastava et al., 2022](#bib.bib48)) In the
  BIG-bench, the sports category is a collection of tasks designed to
  test a model’s understanding and reasoning in the context of sports.
  The benchmark presents various scenarios, requiring the model to
  determine whether each scenario is feasible.
- •
  SVAMP ([Patel et al., 2021](#bib.bib38)) SVAMP consists of simple
  variations of arithmetic word problems, constructed to align with a

## Appendix C Proof of Proposition [2.1](#S2.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 2.1. ‣ 2.3 Why can’t LLM answer questions correctly in the initial attempt? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability")

Let’s assume that for a comprehensive benchmark $`Q`$ where each
question $`q_{i}\in Q`$ has $`k\geq 2`$ possible answers, some LLM has
the true ability to answer a subset of $`A\in Q`$ correctly, which we
denote the accuracy of its true ability as
correct($`A\in Q`$)=$`\frac{|A|}{|Q|}`$. Here, the true ability is
defined to reflect the knowledge of LLM’s internal representation,
irrespective of the generation process in practical usage. Note that the
definition of true ability’s accuracy is only a theoretical value and
not directly measurable. Besides the true ability of LLMs (internal
representation), the correctness of the answer during the generation
stage is also determined by multiple factors, including decoding,
prompt, temperature, etc. In addition, we assume that the LLM is able to
do better than $`\frac{1}{k}`$ (which, from a human’s perspective, is
equivalent to random guessing). That is,
correct($`A\in Q`$)=$`\lambda>1/k`$.

However, since such decoding and prompting is impossible, the LLM has a
hallucination possibility $`h>0`$, where h% of answers are changed.
Additionally, we assume that such an LLM has a Gaussian/Uniform
distribution on the confidence of the answer deterministic token, which
is just one token in the QA setting, for example, “Yes/No”, “True/False”
in a claim judgment problem or “A/B/C/D” for a multiple-choice problem.
This means that hallucination randomly changes the answer (originally
either correct or incorrect) with equal possibility. Such an assumption
is reasonable and has been empirically shown by [Becker & Soatto
(2024)](#bib.bib2). No matter what the previous answer was, after the
hallucination, the answer is random but not the original answer. Then,
we could devise the following Proposition
[2.1](#S2.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 2.1. ‣ 2.3 Why can’t LLM answer questions correctly in the initial attempt? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").
Proposition 2.1. LLMs are generally under-performing compared to their
real abilities because hallucination will cause the overall accuracy to
decrease.

###### Proof of Proposition [2.1](#S2.Thmtheorem1 "Proposition 2.1. ‣ 2.3 Why can’t LLM answer questions correctly in the initial attempt? ‣ 2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability").

During the response generation, $`h`$ of responses are hallucinated.
This means that $`h*\lambda`$ answers will be changed from correct to
incorrect. On the other side, $`\frac{h*(1-\lambda)}{k-1}`$ answers will
be changed from incorrect to correct.

Adding these two parts together, we get that the final accuracy after
hallucination shall be
$`correct(A^{\prime}\in Q)=\lambda-h*\lambda+\frac{h*(1-\lambda)}{k-1}`$.
It is not hard to see that $`h*\lambda>\frac{h*(1-\lambda)}{k-1}`$, as
this is the same equation of
$`\lambda>\frac{(1-\lambda)}{k-1}\Rightarrow\lambda*(k-1)>1-\lambda\Rightarrow\lambda*k>1`$,
which is the assumption we make above.

Since $`h*\lambda>\frac{h*(1-\lambda)}{k-1}`$, we could conclude that
$`correct(A^{\prime}\in Q)<correct(A\in Q)`$. In other words,
hallucination causes LLMs to perform worse. ∎

Thus, we could model an LLM’s performance on a single instance as a
biased Gaussian distribution of $`L\sim N(\lambda+\beta,\sigma)`$, where
bias $`\beta`$, which is related to hallucination possibility $`h`$, is
strictly less than 0. Existing works such as self-verification and CoT
are different techniques that reduce hallucination. Similarly, as argued
in Section
[2](#S2 "2 New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction ‣ Large Language Models Have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability"),
SC, which is another form of self-verification and CoT, also has a
similar outcome.
````

###### Abstract

Large language models have shown strong reasoning capabilities, but
their high inference costs make knowledge distillation an important
approach for transferring such capabilities to compact models in
resource-constrained scenarios. On-policy self-distillation further
reduces the reliance on external large teacher models while improving
the reasoning ability of compact language models. However, existing
methods typically either distill all token positions uniformly or select
tokens using fixed heuristic criteria, assigning the same distillation
strength to the selected positions rather than adaptively learning which
tokens are most beneficial for distillation. To address these
limitations, we propose BiToK-SD (Bilevel Top-$`\mathbf{K}`$ Token
Selection for Self-Distillation), a bilevel-optimization-based token
selection method that learns where distillation should be applied during
on-policy self-distillation. Specifically, BiToK-SD is formulated as a
bilevel optimization problem, where the lower-level problem models
Top-$`K`$ token selection as a differentiable threshold-based
relaxation, allowing the selected positions to adapt as the student
policy evolves, while the upper-level problem performs knowledge
distillation on the selected positions. Experiments on mathematical
reasoning benchmarks show that BiToK-SD achieves the best average
performance among all compared methods while requiring only lightweight
additional computation.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable progress on
complex reasoning tasks ([Wei et al., 2022](#bib.bib20)). However, their
large model sizes and high inference costs make them difficult to deploy
in resource-constrained scenarios. Knowledge distillation (KD) provides
an effective way to transfer the capabilities of stronger models to
smaller ones, and has therefore been widely used to improve the
reasoning ability of compact LLMs ([Xu et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)).
Existing LLM distillation methods can be broadly categorized into
off-policy and on-policy approaches: off-policy methods rely on fixed
teacher outputs or pre-collected reasoning trajectories ([Hsieh et al.,
2023](#bib.bib22); [Shridhar et al., 2023](#bib.bib23)), while on-policy
methods construct training signals from the student’s current rollouts
to better match the student distribution ([Agarwal et al.,
2024](#bib.bib9)). Despite their effectiveness, these methods often
require either high-quality offline data, a strong external teacher
model, or an online reward/teacher model, making the overall training
process costly and less flexible.

To reduce the dependence on external large teacher models, recent
studies have begun to explore on-policy self-distillation ([Shenfeld et
al., 2026](#bib.bib25); [Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib24)).
Representative methods such as OPSD construct feedback-conditioned
token-level correction signals from the model’s own generated reasoning
trajectories, enabling the model to distill from itself ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)). Such methods remove the need for a large teacher
model and provide an efficient way to improve smaller reasoning models.
However, they typically apply the same distillation objective uniformly
across all token positions in the generated trajectory. This uniform
treatment overlooks the heterogeneous roles of tokens in long
chain-of-thought reasoning, where tokens may correspond to exploration,
correction, reflection, or final answer derivation.

A natural improvement is selective token-level distillation, where KD
signals are applied only to token positions that are more important or
more suitable for distillation. Recent studies have explored token
selection or reweighting based on predefined indicators, such as token
entropy, teacher–student divergence, reverse KL divergence, or their
combinations such as Soft-OR scores ([Tavor et al., 2026](#bib.bib26);
[Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)). However, because these methods rely on
predefined token-level scores, their selection criteria remain fixed
throughout training and are not adapted to the self-distillation
objective. Since the student distribution changes continuously during
on-policy self-distillation, fixed token-selection scores may fail to
consistently identify token positions that are beneficial for
distillation. Thus, the key question is not only whether token selection
is needed, but how to learn adaptive token-level weights that determine
both the selected positions and their distillation strengths.

Learning token selection is challenging because Top-$`K`$ selection is
inherently discrete and non-differentiable. Prior work on X-risk
minimization and learning to rank has shown that Top-$`K`$-related
objectives often require specialized optimization techniques, including
bilevel formulations and differentiable relaxations of ranking or
sorting operations ([Yang, 2022](#bib.bib33); [Swezey et al.,
2021](#bib.bib32)). Inspired by this perspective, we formulate token
selection as a learnable differentiable Top-$`K`$ problem, enabling the
selection mechanism to be optimized with respect to the
self-distillation objective.

To this end, we propose BiToK-SD, a bilevel-optimization-based Top-$`K`$
token selection method for on-policy self-distillation. Unlike uniform
distillation or heuristic token selection based on fixed importance
scores, BiToK-SD learns adaptive token-level distillation weights
through bilevel optimization, allowing the model to determine both where
and how strongly distillation should be applied. We evaluate BiToK-SD on
mathematical reasoning benchmarks under thinking-mode evaluation.
Experimental results show that BiToK-SD achieves the best average
performance among all compared methods while requiring only lightweight
additional computation. Further analyses and ablation studies show that
BiToK-SD preserves stronger policy exploration, induces more
reflection-style reasoning behavior, and remains robust across different
Top-$`K`$ ratios.

## 2 Related Work

Knowledge distillation (KD), following the classical teacher–student
paradigm ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib6)) and its sequence-level
extensions ([Kim and Rush, 2016](#bib.bib7)), has been widely used to
transfer the reasoning and instruction-following abilities of large
language models (LLMs) to smaller models ([Gu et al., 2024](#bib.bib8);
[Agarwal et al., 2024](#bib.bib9); [Ko et al., 2024](#bib.bib10); [Shing
et al., 2025](#bib.bib11); [Kim et al., 2024](#bib.bib12); [Guo et al.,
2025](#bib.bib16)). Existing KD methods can be categorized into
off-policy and on-policy approaches. Off-policy KD relies on fixed
teacher outputs, offline teacher-generated responses, or pre-collected
reasoning trajectories ([Hsieh et al., 2023](#bib.bib22); [Shridhar et
al., 2023](#bib.bib23); [Li et al., 2023](#bib.bib13); [Chen et al.,
2023](#bib.bib14); [Jiang et al., 2023](#bib.bib15)), which may cause
distribution mismatch when the student is trained on static trajectories
rather than its own rollouts. On-policy KD alleviates this issue by
constructing supervision from student-generated trajectories, better
matching the evolving student distribution ([Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib3); [Ye et al., 2025](#bib.bib17); [Ye et al.,
2026](#bib.bib18); [Ko et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)).

More recently, self-distillation (SD) further reduces reliance on
external teacher models by allowing the model to learn from its own
generated reasoning trajectories ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib27);
[Hübotter et al., 2026](#bib.bib24); [Sang et al., 2026](#bib.bib28);
[Shenfeld et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)). For example, [Zhao et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib2) propose OPSD, where a single model serves as both
teacher and student during on-policy self-distillation. Since uniformly
distilling all generated tokens may introduce redundant or even harmful
supervision, recent work has begun to explore selective token-level
distillation methods ([Jung et al., 2025](#bib.bib30); [Kim and Baek,
2026](#bib.bib31); [Huang et al., 2025](#bib.bib29)). [Xu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib1) propose Soft-OR scores to select informative tokens
for distillation based on student uncertainty and teacher–student
divergence.

However, most existing selective distillation methods formulate token
selection as a binary decision, assigning the same distillation weight
to all selected positions without explicitly modeling their varying
importance. This limitation suggests the need for more flexible
token-level weighting mechanisms beyond binary selection.

A related line of work studies differentiable optimization for ranking
and top-$`K`$ selection. Many ranking-oriented objectives, such as NDCG,
MAP, and precision at top-$`K`$, depend on discrete ranks or top-$`K`$
selection and are therefore difficult to optimize directly with
gradient-based methods ([Blondel et al., 2020](#bib.bib36); [Yang,
2022](#bib.bib33)). To address this issue, prior work has developed
differentiable relaxations of sorting and ranking operators, such as
PiRank ([Swezey et al., 2021](#bib.bib32)) and NeuralNDCG ([Pobrotyn and
Białobrzeski, 2021](#bib.bib34)), as well as bilevel or compositional
formulations for optimizing top-$`K`$ ranking metrics such as NDCG ([Qiu
et al., 2022](#bib.bib35)). While these methods provide useful tools for
differentiable approximations of discrete top-$`K`$ selection, they are
designed for conventional ranking, retrieval, or recommendation tasks
rather than adaptive token-level weighting in LLM self-distillation.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 On-Policy Self-Distillation Setup

We follow OPSD ([Zhao et al., 2026](#bib.bib2)) to construct token-level
self-distillation signals from the model’s own on-policy reasoning
trajectories. Given an input problem $`x_{i}`$, the current student
policy generates a reasoning trajectory
$`\hat{y}_{i}=(\hat{y}_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,\hat{y}_{L_{i}}^{(i)})`$, where
$`L_{i}`$ denotes the length of the generated trajectory. Since
distillation is performed on next-token distributions, the trajectory
provides $`T_{i}=L_{i}-1`$ distillation positions, indexed by
$`t\in\{1,\ldots,T_{i}\}`$. For each position $`t`$, we denote the
student distribution and the feedback-conditioned self-teacher
distribution over the next token as $`p_{S,t}^{(i)}`$ and
$`p_{T,t}^{(i)}`$, respectively. Here, the self-teacher distribution is
conditioned on the reference or feedback-providing solution used by the
OPSD-style teacher context.

We formulate the token-level self-distillation signal as a
full-vocabulary distribution divergence between the feedback-conditioned
self-teacher and the student. In our experiments, we instantiate this
divergence as the teacher-to-student KL divergence. For each token
position $`t`$ in the $`i`$-th training example, the per-position loss
is defined as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\ell_{\mathrm{pos}}^{(i)}(t;\theta)=\mathrm{KL}\left(p_{T,t}^{(i)}\middle\|p_{S,t}^{(i)}\right).
``` |  | (1) |

This full-vocabulary objective provides dense token-level feedback by
reducing the divergence between the student distribution and the
feedback-conditioned self-teacher distribution over the entire
vocabulary.

### 3.2 Differentiable Top-$`K`$ Token Weighting

Recent token-selection methods commonly compute a token-level importance
score and then apply a hard Top-$`K`$ selector to retain only the
highest-scoring positions. However, this strategy is limited by its
discrete nature and its uniform treatment of selected tokens.
Specifically, the binary Top-$`K`$ operation is non-differentiable, and
all selected tokens receive the same weight, ignoring that different
tokens may contribute to distillation with different strengths. BiToK-SD
addresses these limitations by replacing hard Top-$`K`$ selection with a
differentiable threshold-based relaxation, which yields adaptive soft
token weights instead of a binary mask.

For each valid token position $`(i,t)`$ in a mini-batch $`\mathcal{B}`$,
we compute a token-level utility score $`u_{t}^{(i)}`$ to estimate how
suitable this position is for distillation. In our main experiments, we
use the Soft-OR score as the default token-level importance score:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
u_{t}^{(i)}=\hat{h}_{t}^{(i)}+\hat{d}_{t}^{(i)}-\hat{h}_{t}^{(i)}\hat{d}_{t}^{(i)},
``` |  | (2) |

where $`h_{t}^{(i)}=H(p_{S,t}^{(i)})`$ is the student entropy,
$`d_{t}^{(i)}=\mathrm{KL}(p_{S,t}^{(i)}\|p_{T,t}^{(i)})`$ is the reverse
KL divergence, and hats denote min–max normalization over valid token
positions in the mini-batch. The score serves as a token-level
importance proxy and is recomputed at each training step as the student
policy evolves. BiToK-SD is not tied to this specific score and can also
use other token-level indicators, such as student entropy, teacher
entropy, forward KL divergence, or reverse KL divergence.

Let $`\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}`$ denote the set of valid non-padding token
positions in mini-batch $`\mathcal{B}`$, and let
$`M_{\mathcal{B}}=|\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}|`$. Given a target Top-$`K`$
ratio $`k`$, we set

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
K_{\mathcal{B}}=\max\left(1,\left\lceil kM_{\mathcal{B}}\right\rceil\right),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`K_{\mathcal{B}}`$ denotes the target number of high-utility
positions in the mini-batch.

A hard batch-level Top-$`K`$ selector can be written as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
z_{t}^{(i)}=\mathbf{1}\!\left(u_{t}^{(i)}\in\mathrm{TopK}\left(\{u_{t^{\prime}}^{(i^{\prime})}\}_{(i^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}}\right)\right).
``` |  | (4) |

However, this binary selector is non-differentiable and assigns the same
weight to all selected positions. As a result, it cannot express
different distillation strengths among selected tokens. BiToK-SD
therefore replaces the hard selector with a differentiable
threshold-based relaxation.

The relaxed threshold is obtained as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\hat{\rho}_{\mathcal{B}}\approx\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}}\Psi_{\mathcal{B}}(\rho;u),
``` |  |

where

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\Psi_{\mathcal{B}}(\rho;u)=\frac{K_{\mathcal{B}}+\varepsilon}{M_{\mathcal{B}}}\rho+\frac{\tau_{2}}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{\tau_{1}}{M_{\mathcal{B}}}\sum_{(i,t)\in\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}}\log\!\left(1+\exp\!\left(\frac{u_{t}^{(i)}-\rho}{\tau_{1}}\right)\right).
``` |  | (5) |

Here, $`\tau_{1}`$ controls the smoothness of the softplus
approximation, $`\tau_{2}`$ stabilizes the threshold optimization, and
$`\varepsilon`$ avoids boundary ambiguity. In practice, we approximate
$`\hat{\rho}_{\mathcal{B}}`$ using a warm-started momentum gradient
update and detach the resulting threshold before computing the soft
mask.

Given $`\hat{\rho}_{\mathcal{B}}`$, the soft token-selection mask is
defined as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\tilde{z}_{t}^{(i)}=\sigma\!\left(\frac{u_{t}^{(i)}-\hat{\rho}_{\mathcal{B}}}{\tau_{m}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{1}\!\left((i,t)\in\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}\right),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`\tau_{m}`$ controls the sharpness of the mask. Unlike hard
Top-$`K`$ selection, $`\tilde{z}_{t}^{(i)}`$ is continuous and assigns
different strengths to different token positions. Thus, BiToK-SD
determines not only which tokens are emphasized, but also how strongly
they contribute to the distillation objective.

### 3.3 Weighted Self-Distillation via Bilevel Optimization

The relaxed threshold in
Eq. ([5](#S3.E5 "In 3.2 Differentiable Top-𝐾 Token Weighting ‣ 3 Method ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models"))
is obtained from an inner optimization problem, while the student model
is updated by minimizing a weighted self-distillation objective. This
gives the following approximate bilevel formulation:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\min_{\theta}\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BiToK\text{-}SD}}(\theta;\hat{\rho}_{\mathcal{B}})\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\hat{\rho}_{\mathcal{B}}\approx\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\rho}\Psi_{\mathcal{B}}(\rho;u).
``` |  | (7) |

Here, $`\Psi_{\mathcal{B}}`$ denotes the relaxed threshold objective in
Eq. ([5](#S3.E5 "In 3.2 Differentiable Top-𝐾 Token Weighting ‣ 3 Method ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models")).
The approximation reflects that the threshold is updated by a
warm-started gradient step rather than solved exactly at every
iteration.

Given the soft mask $`\tilde{z}_{t}^{(i)}`$, BiToK-SD minimizes the
batch-normalized weighted self-distillation loss:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BiToK\text{-}SD}}(\theta)=\frac{\sum_{(i,t)\in\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}}\tilde{z}_{t}^{(i)}\ell_{\mathrm{pos}}^{(i)}(t;\theta)}{\sum_{(i,t)\in\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}}\tilde{z}_{t}^{(i)}+\delta},
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\delta`$ is a small constant for numerical stability.

Equivalently, we define the normalized token-level distillation weight
as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\bar{w}_{t}^{(i)}=\frac{\tilde{z}_{t}^{(i)}}{\sum_{(i^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}}\tilde{z}_{t^{\prime}}^{(i^{\prime})}+\delta},
``` |  | (9) |

and rewrite
Eq. ([8](#S3.E8 "In 3.3 Weighted Self-Distillation via Bilevel Optimization ‣ 3 Method ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models"))
as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BiToK\text{-}SD}}(\theta)=\sum_{(i,t)\in\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}}\bar{w}_{t}^{(i)}\ell_{\mathrm{pos}}^{(i)}(t;\theta).
``` |  | (10) |

This formulation does not modify the underlying token-level
self-distillation loss, but instead changes how the loss is weighted
across token positions. For stability and efficiency, the token weights
are treated as stop-gradient coefficients within each update.
Nevertheless, because the utilities and threshold are recomputed from
the evolving student policy during on-policy training, the emphasized
token positions and their effective weights can change dynamically
across training steps.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

Models and Data. We conduct on-policy self-distillation experiments at
three model scales, using Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B as base
models. At each scale, all methods are initialized from the same
checkpoint and trained on the same data to ensure a fair comparison. We
train on the mathematical reasoning subset of OpenThoughts ([Guha et
al., 2025](#bib.bib4)), which contains approximately 30K
problem–solution pairs with chain-of-thought reasoning trajectories.

Baselines. We compare BiToK-SD with some representative baselines
covering supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, on-policy
self-distillation, and heuristic token selection. SFT performs standard
supervised fine-tuning on expert reasoning trajectories. GRPO ([Shao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib3)) optimizes the policy using binary outcome rewards
and serves as a reinforcement-learning baseline. OPSD ([Zhao et al.,
2026](#bib.bib2)) performs on-policy self-distillation by constructing
feedback-conditioned token-level correction signals from the model’s own
rollouts. TIP ([Xu et al., 2026](#bib.bib1)) selects the top-$`50\%`$
positions by the same Soft-OR score
(Eq. ([2](#S3.E2 "In 3.2 Differentiable Top-𝐾 Token Weighting ‣ 3 Method ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models")))
with a hard mask and, following the original method, optimizes the
reverse KL divergence $`\mathrm{KL}(p_{S}\|p_{T})`$ on the selected
positions.

Evaluation and Implementation. We evaluate on AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25
under thinking mode across three model scales (Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B).
Since AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT25 each contain only a small number of
problems, single-sample accuracy is subject to high variance. We
therefore report Avg@64, the mean accuracy over 64 independently sampled
solutions per problem. All methods are trained for 200 steps and
evaluated at the final checkpoint; we deliberately avoid test-set-based
checkpoint selection.

Hyperparameters. We fine-tune all methods with AdamW using a learning
rate of $`5\!\times\!10^{-6}`$, a per-device batch size of $`2`$ with
gradient accumulation of $`2`$ over $`4`$ GPUs (effective batch size
$`16`$), a maximum completion length of $`1024`$ tokens, and a sampling
temperature of $`1.1`$. For the 8B model, we use a per-device batch size
of $`1`$ with gradient accumulation of $`4`$ to fit 48GB GPUs, keeping
the effective batch size at $`16`$. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning is
performed with LoRA ($`r=64`$, $`\alpha=128`$) applied to all attention
and MLP projections. For BiToK-SD, we adopt the Soft-OR score as the
default token-level importance score and set the top-$`K`$ ratio to
$`k=50\%`$ of generated tokens.

For OPSD and BiToK-SD, we use the full-vocabulary teacher-to-student KL
divergence as the token-level distillation objective. For the
differentiable threshold relaxation, we set the softplus temperature to
$`\tau_{1}=0.1`$, the quadratic regularization coefficient to
$`\tau_{2}=0.01`$, and the mask temperature to $`\tau_{m}=0.1`$. We set
$`\varepsilon=10^{-6}`$ to avoid boundary ambiguity in
Eq. ([5](#S3.E5 "In 3.2 Differentiable Top-𝐾 Token Weighting ‣ 3 Method ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models"))
and $`\delta=10^{-6}`$ for numerical stability in
Eq. ([8](#S3.E8 "In 3.3 Weighted Self-Distillation via Bilevel Optimization ‣ 3 Method ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models")).
The smooth threshold $`\rho`$ is updated by an inner threshold solver
with step size $`\eta=0.1`$, momentum coefficient $`\gamma=0.9`$, and
variance-reduction coefficient $`\beta_{\rho}=0.1`$.

Unless otherwise stated, all baselines share the same optimizer,
learning rate, effective batch size, number of training steps, LoRA
configuration, and evaluation decoding settings as BiToK-SD. For GRPO,
we use a group size of $`G=8`$ rollouts per prompt (two prompts per
rollout batch), a maximum completion length of 8,192 tokens, a sampling
temperature of $`1.2`$, two optimization iterations per rollout batch,
and no KL penalty, so that GRPO processes 200 distinct prompts over 200
steps. For SFT, we use a maximum sequence length of 20,000 tokens.

### 4.2 Main Results

reports Avg@64 accuracy under thinking mode at the final checkpoint (200
steps) across three model scales. Overall, BiToK-SD achieves the highest
average performance at all three scales, demonstrating the effectiveness
of bilevel token selection for on-policy self-distillation. Compared
with the base model, SFT degrades performance on every benchmark and at
every scale despite using ground-truth solutions, suggesting that
directly imitating fixed trajectories is less effective in our on-policy
reasoning setting. GRPO improves over the base model at 1.7B (+1.10
average points), but its advantage disappears at larger scales,
performing on par with the base model at 4B and slightly below it at 8B.
This suggests that outcome-level rewards provide only coarse supervision
while requiring substantially higher training cost due to group
sampling.

OPSD is the strongest baseline and uses feedback-conditioned correction
signals for on-policy self-distillation, improving over the base model
at all three scales. However, it still distills over all token
positions. TIP, which uses the same Soft-OR score with a hard Top-$`K`$
mask and the reverse KL objective of its original formulation, falls
below OPSD in all nine scale–benchmark combinations. Since TIP differs
from BiToK-SD in both the selection rule and the divergence, this gap
cannot be attributed to either factor alone; [Zhao et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib2) likewise report that reverse KL underperforms forward
KL in on-policy self-distillation. In contrast, BiToK-SD constructs
adaptive soft token weights through a bilevel threshold relaxation,
enabling more flexible token-level distillation. As a result, it further
improves over OPSD by 0.63, 0.58, and 0.21 average points at 1.7B, 4B,
and 8B, respectively, and obtains the best result in seven of the nine
scale–benchmark cells.

provides a closer analysis of the learned bilevel token selection
mechanism. Inspired by [Kim et al. (2026)](#bib.bib5), we use a set of
ten epistemic markers—wait, hmm, perhaps, maybe, actually,
alternatively, seems, might, likely, and check—as lightweight lexical
indicators of self-reflective behavior in generated completions. As
shown in
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models")(a),
BiToK-SD generates substantially more epistemic markers per completion
than OPSD and TIP. The gap becomes visible around step 60 and continues
to widen as training proceeds, which is consistent with the stronger
exploration behavior observed in
Figure [1](#S4.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models").
To further examine how the selector allocates its weights,
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models")(b)
compares the mean soft-mask weight assigned to positions containing
epistemic markers with that assigned to all other valid token positions.
Across training, reflection-associated positions receive consistently
larger weights, with an average ratio of $`1.63\times`$. This contrasts
with OPSD, which assigns uniform weights to all valid positions, and
TIP, which applies a binary Top-$`K`$ mask. In these baselines, the
relative weighting between reflection tokens and other selected tokens
is uniform by construction, whereas BiToK-SD assigns continuous weights
that increase with the utility score relative to an adaptive threshold.
This indicates that the bilevel selector not only determines *which*
tokens should contribute to the distillation objective, but also
controls *how strongly* high-utility positions, such as
reflection-bearing ones, are emphasized.

Although BiToK-SD produces more reflection-style tokens, this does not
translate into greater verbosity or degeneration. On AIME24 with 64
samples per problem, BiToK-SD generates 3.7%, 1.6%, and 4.8% fewer
tokens than OPSD on Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B, respectively, while also
showing lower repeated 4-gram ratios and truncation rates at all three
scales. This suggests that the increased reflective behavior reflects a
redistribution of the reasoning process rather than simply longer or
more repetitive generation. Finally,
Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models")(c)
shows that the overall mask density remains tightly concentrated around
the target sparsity level $`k=50\%`$. Thus, the learned selector
satisfies the global sparsity constraint without degenerating into
either an overly sparse or overly dense mask. At the same time, the
specific positions emphasized by the mask are determined adaptively from
the training dynamics rather than imposed by a static token-selection
heuristic.

Overall, the quantitative results and training analyses provide
consistent evidence that BiToK-SD improves on-policy self-distillation
through adaptive bilevel token selection. Beyond achieving stronger
final-checkpoint performance, BiToK-SD also changes the underlying
distillation dynamics: the learned selector maintains the prescribed
sparsity budget while preferentially emphasizing reflection-associated
positions and preserving a broader student policy distribution. These
results suggest that the benefit of BiToK-SD does not come from simply
increasing the amount of distillation or uniformly enforcing teacher
imitation, but from selectively applying token-level supervision to
positions that are more useful for reasoning-oriented self-distillation.
This adaptive balance between token selection and policy exploration
yields a more effective mechanism for improving reasoning performance.

### 4.3 Ablation Studies and Analysis

We conduct ablation and sensitivity analyses to examine the design
choices of BiToK-SD. For computational efficiency, all ablations use
Qwen3-1.7B and are evaluated on AIME24 at the final checkpoint (200
steps) with 64 samples per problem. Unless otherwise specified, all
variants follow the same training setup. We additionally report Pass@64,
defined as the fraction of problems for which at least one of the 64
sampled solutions is correct.

#### Effect of the Top-$`K`$ ratio.

We first vary $`k\in{20\%,30\%,40\%,50\%,60\%,70\%}`$ while keeping all
other settings fixed. As shown in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Effect of the Top-𝐾 ratio. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Studies and Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models"),
performance follows a broadly inverted-U trend and peaks at the default
$`k=50\%`$ (55.99 Avg@64 and 90.00 Pass@64). Nevertheless, performance
remains competitive across the tested range, indicating that BiToK-SD is
reasonably robust to the choice of $`k`$. The best performance at an
intermediate selection ratio suggests a balance between focusing
distillation on informative tokens and retaining sufficient coverage of
the reasoning trajectory. Selecting too few tokens may discard useful
supervision, whereas emphasizing too many tokens may weaken the benefit
of token-level prioritization.

#### Effect of the token-selection score.

We next examine whether BiToK-SD depends on a specific utility score. We
replace the default Soft-OR score with student entropy, teacher entropy,
forward KL divergence $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{T}\|P_{S})`$, and reverse KL
divergence $`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{S}\|P_{T})`$ while fixing $`k=50\%`$. As
a reference, we also report uniform weighting over all valid tokens
(Uniform), which recovers OPSD. As shown in
Table [2](#S4.T2 "Table 2 ‣ Effect of the Top-𝐾 ratio. ‣ 4.3 Ablation Studies and Analysis ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models"),
the default Soft-OR score achieves the highest Avg@64 and Pass@64
overall, while forward KL is the strongest single-signal alternative.
Both Soft-OR and forward KL improve Avg@64 over uniform weighting,
whereas the entropy-based scores and reverse KL do not. The variation
across utility scores remains within about two points in Avg@64,
suggesting that the proposed weighting framework remains reasonably
robust to the choice of utility score, although the score choice still
affects the gain over uniform weighting.

#### Effect of the mask temperature.

We further vary the mask temperature $`\tau_{m}\in\{0.05,0.1,0.2\}`$,
obtaining Avg@64 scores of 55.00, 55.99, and 55.00 and Pass@64 scores of
80.00, 90.00, and 80.00, respectively. The small variation indicates
that BiToK-SD is not highly sensitive to the precise choice of mask
temperature.

#### Dynamics of the adaptive threshold.

We further examine how the relaxed threshold $`\rho`$ evolves during
training. On Qwen3-1.7B, $`\rho`$ starts from 0.061, rises during the
first third of training, and returns to 0.063 by step 200 (range
0.044–0.105, mean $`0.073\pm 0.012`$). On the larger models, $`\rho`$
instead drifts upward throughout training: on Qwen3-4B it increases from
0.030 to 0.102 (range 0.029–0.151, mean $`0.077\pm 0.028`$), and on
Qwen3-8B from 0.069 to 0.159 (range 0.051–0.217, mean
$`0.126\pm 0.040`$), with both peaks occurring late in training. These
dynamics show that the relaxed threshold adapts throughout training
rather than remaining effectively fixed.

### 4.4 Generalization to Off-Policy KD

To assess the generality of our bilevel token-selection framework, we
further evaluate it under the off-policy distillation setting. Following
the protocol of [Tavor et al. (2026)](#bib.bib26), we distill Qwen3-8B
into Qwen3-1.7B on an 80M-token subset of FineWeb ([Penedo et al.,
2024](#bib.bib37)), with a maximum sequence length of 512. Training runs
for one epoch with an effective batch size of 64, using a micro-batch
size of 2 and gradient accumulation of 8 on each of 4 GPUs, yielding
roughly 2.4K optimizer updates. We use a learning rate of
$`1\times 10^{-5}`$, a KD temperature of $`\tau=1.0`$, and disable the
cross-entropy term ($`\alpha_{\text{ce}}=0`$) so that all gradients
originate from the distillation objective. To eliminate teacher forward
passes during training, we precompute and cache, for every position, the
teacher’s top-64 vocabulary probabilities together with its entropy,
which is required for the truncated distillation objective.

We compare against Full-KD, which distills all token positions; SE-KD,
which selects token positions based on student entropy; and SE-KD_(3X),
which extends selective distillation beyond the position axis by
additionally applying selection along the class and sample axes,
following [Tavor et al. (2026)](#bib.bib26). A practical constraint of
this off-policy setup is that storing the teacher’s full output
distribution for every token position is prohibitively expensive.
Therefore, only a truncated top-$`U`$ slice, with $`U=64`$, is retained
on disk. As a result, statistics that require access to the complete
teacher distribution—most notably the forward and reverse KL terms used
by the Soft-OR importance score in our main experiments—cannot be
evaluated faithfully. We therefore replace Soft-OR with the
student-entropy score, which is computed solely from the student’s
predictions and is independent of the granularity of the cached teacher
distribution. For the bilevel selector, we use the same relaxation
hyperparameters as in the main experiments ($`\tau_{1}=0.1`$,
$`\tau_{2}=0.01`$, $`\tau_{m}=0.1`$), and fix the position-selection
ratio at $`k=20\%`$.

We evaluate on five downstream tasks—ARC-Easy, GSM8K, HellaSwag, PIQA,
and LAMBADA—and report the average accuracy (Avg. Acc.) over these five
tasks, the strict instruction-following score on the IFEval benchmark,
and the perplexity (PPL) on LAMBADA. As shown in
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.4 Generalization to Off-Policy KD ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Learning What to Distill: Bilevel Top-𝐾 Token Selection for Self-Distillation in Large Language Models"),
BiToK-SD outperforms Full-KD, SE-KD, and SE-KD_(3X) across all three
summary metrics. These results suggest that the proposed bilevel
selection mechanism can also be applied to off-policy KD, even when only
truncated teacher distributions are available.

## 5 Conclusion

We introduced BiToK-SD, a bilevel Top-$`K`$ token-selection framework
for on-policy self-distillation in large language models. Instead of
distilling all generated tokens uniformly or relying on fixed heuristic
selection rules, BiToK-SD learns adaptive token-level distillation
weights through a differentiable threshold-based optimization
formulation. Experiments on mathematical reasoning benchmarks show that
BiToK-SD consistently improves over strong baselines while requiring
only lightweight additional computation. Further analyses indicate that
the learned selector helps preserve policy exploration and is associated
with more reflection-style reasoning behavior. Additional ablation and
off-policy KD experiments further demonstrate the robustness and
generality of the proposed selection mechanism. Overall, our results
suggest that learning where and how strongly to apply distillation is a
promising direction for improving self-distillation in
reasoning-oriented language models.
````

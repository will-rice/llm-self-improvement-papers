###### Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) can generate fluent outputs that lack
source support or conflict with local operational constraints. We
introduce Self-Reflective Chain-of-Thought (SRCoT), a modular
architecture that organizes generation as a closed
Retrieve–Reflect–Revise loop. SRCoT separates environment state,
persistent knowledge, accumulated experience, and short-term memory into
provenance-labeled stores. BM25 retrieval supplies local context;
subsequent passes expose intermediate drafts, missing-information
markers, follow-up queries, and optional claim–evidence mappings. These
artifacts inform retrieval, revision, clarification, or abstention
decisions. We examine the architecture through qualitative, trace-based
case studies in two fully synthetic settings: emergency medical services
and Munich-Sim, an emergency-operations simulation. Local prototype runs
on an Apple M4-family laptop with 128 GB memory illustrate
source-labeled documentation, explicit gap marking, and structured
dispatch-style outputs. The traces also reveal provenance-assignment
errors, incomplete evidence coverage, and unsafe assumptions that remain
possible despite prompting constraints. These cases demonstrate
report-level inspectability and local execution, not comparative
superiority, hallucination reduction, clinical validity, or operational
safety. The architecture separates retrieval and control from the model
interface, but behavior across different models remains to be evaluated.
SRCoT is a research proof of concept requiring human review, with its
principal contribution lying in an inspectable control procedure rather
than a new model.

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved strong performance across a
wide range of natural language processing tasks, including text
generation, summarization, and interactive reasoning. Their fluency and
apparent coherence have motivated increasing interest in deploying them
in applied and decision-support settings. However, despite these
advances, LLMs remain prone to generating outputs that are convincing in
form but unsupported by evidence, internally inconsistent, or misaligned
with domain-specific constraints. Such failures—often referred to as
hallucinations¹¹ 1 In LLMs, “hallucination” refers to fluent output that
is not supported by the provided sources or is fabricated/inaccurate,
even if it sounds plausible.—are particularly problematic in
safety-critical or operational environments, where erroneous outputs may
be difficult to detect yet carry substantial consequences [Ji et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib1); [Islam Tonmoy et al. (2024)](#bib.bib2); [Maynez
et al. (2020)](#bib.bib3).

A contributing factor to this problem is the dominant usage paradigm of
current LLM systems. Most deployments rely on a single, monolithic
context in which persistent knowledge, task-specific state, intermediate
reasoning, and action decisions are entangled within a shared prompt or
hidden representation [Yao et al. (2022)](#bib.bib4); [Packer et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib5). While recent approaches such as chain-of-thought
prompting [Wei et al. (2022)](#bib.bib6), retrieval-augmented generation
(RAG)²² 2 Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pairs a Large Language
Model (LLM) with a retrieval step over an external corpus: relevant
passages are fetched for a query and inserted into the model’s context
so that generation can be grounded in retrieved evidence. This improves
traceability (sources can be cited) but remains limited by retrieval
quality and context use. [Lewis et al. (2020)](#bib.bib7), and tool use
improve reasoning performance, they typically lack an explicit
separation of different knowledge and state types, making it difficult
to monitor, verify, or control the generation process in a principled
manner [Packer et al. (2023)](#bib.bib5); [Yao et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib4).

In this work, we argue that reliable and evidence-based text generation
requires a shift toward structured, closed-loop reasoning architectures.
We draw inspiration from early cybernetics, which frames intelligent
behavior as the result of feedback-driven control, as well as from
cognitive theories that emphasize modularity and coordination. In
particular, Global Workspace Theory (GWT) [Baars (1988)](#bib.bib8);
[Dehaene and Naccache (2001)](#bib.bib9); [Baars and Franklin
(2003)](#bib.bib10) models cognition as the interaction of multiple
specialized processes that compete for access to a limited shared
workspace; information that enters this workspace is broadcast and used
to coordinate system-wide behavior.

Guided by this perspective, a central design principle of our system is
the explicit separation of different types of knowledge and state into
distinct, machine-readable representations. We maintain dedicated
structures for persistent knowledge and constraints, environmental
state, accumulated experience, and short-term memory. Rather than
interpreting these components as a biological model, we treat them as a
system-level analogy: specialized information stores that are
selectively integrated into a shared, task-relevant representation
through a controlled coordination step. This design enables transparent
state tracking, supports iterative verification against evidence, and
allows the system to adapt its actions without conflating long-term
knowledge with transient experiences.

Building on these ideas, we introduce Self-Reflective Chain-of-Thought
(SRCoT), a framework that models LLM inference as a closed feedback loop
over structured internal states. The framework operationalizes reasoning
through explicit claim–evidence representations, systematic
identification of information gaps and contradictions, and
confidence-driven control decisions that determine whether the system
should continue generation, revise prior outputs, retrieve additional
information, or abstain. Importantly, the separation of memory,
experience, and action allows these processes to be coordinated without
interfering with one another, making the overall reasoning process more
interpretable and robust.

Our main contributions are as follows:

Firstly, we propose SRCoT, a modular reasoning architecture for LLMs
that explicitly separates heterogeneous knowledge and state
types—environment state, accumulated experience, persistent knowledge,
and short-term memory—into distinct, machine-readable representations.
This design is functionally inspired by Global Workspace Theory and
enables controlled integration of specialized information into a shared,
task-relevant reasoning context.

Secondly, we introduce a closed-loop coordination mechanism for
chain-of-thought generation in which structured states are selectively
combined, evaluated against evidence and constraints, and iteratively
updated. This mechanism allows reasoning, memory, and action selection
to be decoupled, enabling systematic verification, confidence-based
control, and revision without conflating long-term knowledge with
transient task experience.

Thirdly, we implement and examine SRCoT in two synthetic
settings—emergency medical services and Munich-Sim, an
emergency-operations simulation. Selected reports illustrate explicit
gap marking, source-labeled outputs, and failure modes under local
deployment constraints. These qualitative observations do not constitute
a controlled comparison with baseline generation.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Self-Reflection, Retrieval, and Metacognitive Control in LLMs

Recent work has explored mechanisms that allow language models to
reflect on, critique, and revise their own outputs in order to improve
factuality and robustness. A prominent example is Self-RAG [Asai et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib11), which frames retrieval, generation, and critique as
a learned policy optimized through self-reflection. In Self-RAG, the
model decides when to retrieve external documents, generates an answer,
and then critiques its own output to guide further retrieval or
revision. While effective, this process is realized within a largely
unified model context, where reasoning, evidence assessment, and control
decisions are implicitly entangled.

In contrast, our approach differs at the architectural level. Rather
than learning a unified self-reflection policy, SRCoT explicitly
separates heterogeneous knowledge and state types into distinct,
machine-readable representations (e.g., persistent knowledge (long-term
memory), accumulated experience, environment state, and short-term
memory). Control decisions operate over these structured states in a
closed feedback loop, enabling verification and revision without
conflating transient reasoning outcomes with long-term knowledge. As a
result, self-monitoring in our framework is not an emergent behavior of
a single model, but a system-level coordination mechanism.

Our work is also related to research on metacognition in neural systems,
particularly the idea that intelligent agents should be able to assess
their own uncertainty, detect knowledge gaps, and regulate behavior
accordingly—often summarized as “knowing what you do not know.”[Paul
et al. (2015)](#bib.bib12); [Fleming and Dolan (2012)](#bib.bib13) Prior
work operationalizes such capabilities through confidence estimation,
self-consistency, verifier models, or reflection prompts. SRCoT can be
viewed as a structural instantiation of these ideas: uncertainty and
conflict are tracked explicitly in the experience state and used as
control signals for action selection (e.g., retrieval, revision, or
abstention), rather than being implicitly encoded in model logits or
prompt text.

Finally, our approach differs from self-training and self-improvement
methods, where models iteratively generate pseudo-labels or revise
outputs to update their parameters. SRCoT does not modify model weights
and does not treat generated outputs as new training data. Instead,
learning is replaced by state accumulation: experiences are stored
separately from persistent knowledge and influence future actions
through control logic rather than parameter updates. This design choice
is particularly important for edge deployments, where continual training
is often infeasible or undesirable.

In summary, while SRCoT is conceptually related to self-reflection,
metacognition, and self-training, it contributes a distinct perspective
by emphasizing explicit state separation and closed-loop coordination as
a foundation for reliable and evidence-based reasoning.

### 2.2 GWT-inspired state and knowledge representations

A central design motif of our system is the explicit separation of
different types of knowledge and state into separate, machine-readable
representations (e.g., environment.json (E), experiences.json (X),
knowledge.json (K), memory.json (M)). This structure is functionally
inspired by Global Workspace Theory (GWT), which describes cognition as
the interplay of many specialized processes that compete for access to a
limited shared “workspace”. Contents that enter this workspace can be
broadly _broadcast_ and thereby coordinate other processes [Baars
(1988)](#bib.bib8); [Nakanishi et al. (2025)](#bib.bib14). We do not
interpret the JSON³³ 3 JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) is a
lightweight, human-readable data format for structured records using
key–value pairs and arrays. In our setting, it serves as a simple
container to store many small text items. files as a biological model,
but as a _systemic analogy_: they form specialized knowledge stores that
are transferred, in a controlled step, into a shared representation that
is relevant for answering.

The split into multiple files serves a practical purpose: it enforces
clear semantics about _which type_ of information is being used at any
given time. For example, environment.json can verbalize situational
conditions and constraints (e.g., organizational structure, resource
availability, or information about objects and items); knowledge.json
can contain relatively stable, curated domain information;
experiences.json can collect case examples, decisions, and their
outcomes (“what has worked in practice?”); and memory.json can store
short-term, task-specific notes or session states. In practice, humans
also separate knowledge into categories such as context, general
knowledge, and experience/memory, and use different “access paths”
depending on the task. Through the explicit data structure, this
categorization becomes operationalizable for the system: modules can
query only those sources that are relevant for a given step, and
conflicts (e.g., between stable knowledge and local rules) become
visible instead of being implicitly mixed.

Importantly, the JSON structure is not merely “storage”, but part of the
control mechanism. Once contents are transferred into the shared
workspace (in our case, for example, a claim–evidence or workspace
representation), they can be checked by monitoring and control
components. The workflow thus follows a metacognitive pattern: an object
level generates and collects content, while a meta level verifies
whether constraints are satisfied and whether evidence or context is
missing, and then intervenes in a controlling manner [Nelson and Narens
(1994)](#bib.bib15). The separated stores substantially facilitate this
control because checking rules are easier to formulate (e.g., “local
procedures take precedence over general knowledge”, “environment
constraints must not be violated”, “experience may only count as a
heuristic, not as evidence”).

### 2.3 Self-reasoning in local, smaller edge LLMs

A motivation for self-reasoning on local LLMs is that resource limits
constrain the context and computation available for evidence checking.
An external controller can organize generation into smaller, inspectable
steps instead of a single completion. The following mechanisms are
design rationales, not measured advantages of smaller models; this study
does not compare model sizes.

We consider four mechanisms relevant to such edge settings:

1.  1.  External structure reduces cognitive load. When context, knowledge,
        experiences, and working memory are separated and structured, the
        model has to keep less “in its head” and mixes less implicitly. This
        is intended to reduce the mixing of irrelevant information and the
        omission of local rules. The workspace becomes smaller, clearer, and
        easier to validate [Baars (1988)](#bib.bib8).
2.  2.  Iteration turns one-shot generation into error correction. One-shot
        generation is brittle: an early error propagates through the entire
        text. In a control loop, errors can be detected and corrected later
        (e.g., via targeted follow-up retrieval, conflict marking, or
        conservative phrasing). Whether smaller models benefit
        disproportionately, and whether their checks are adequate, requires
        a separate comparative evaluation.
3.  3.  Self-monitoring enforces evidence-bound writing. Many hallucinations
        arise because the model unconsciously “keeps narrating” when support
        is missing. A monitor that explicitly asks “Which claim is supported
        by what?” and marks gaps is intended to discourage this
        continuation, although the monitor can itself fail. The meta level
        can then decide to retrieve more, declare uncertainty, or stop
        [Nelson and Narens (1994)](#bib.bib15); [Ji et al.
        (2023)](#bib.bib1).
4.  4.  Local compatibility acts as a hard constraint. In edge setups, the
        target is often concrete local standards (standard operating
        procedures, organizational logic, regional medications/protocols in
        the emergency medicine example). These constraints can be
        interpreted as the _reference signal_ of the control loop: an answer
        is only acceptable if it matches the local boundary conditions. This
        constrains the intended output space, but neither model size nor the
        presence of local rules guarantees a locally valid answer [Wiener
        (1948)](#bib.bib16); [Åström and Murray (2008)](#bib.bib17).

The proposed role of self-reasoning is therefore _process control_:
structured state, iterative checking, and controlled stopping decisions.
These mechanisms make aspects of generation inspectable; they do not
establish operational deployability.

### 2.4 Terminology: Self-Reflection vs. Self-Reasoning

In this paper, we use _self-reasoning_ as an umbrella term for
multi-step generation that includes intermediate computation, retrieval,
and revision. We reserve _self-reflection_ for the _meta-level_ part of
self-reasoning: explicit monitoring of the system’s own draft with
respect to evidence, constraints, and missing critical information,
followed by a control action (e.g., re-retrieval, revision, or
abstention). Under this definition, SRCoT’s Pass 1 (Draft + Follow-ups)
implements _reflection as gap detection_ (“what is missing for a
justified answer?”), while Pass 2 implements _reflection as controlled
revision_ (rewrite under the expanded context). The optional Judge step
makes this reflective layer externally observable by producing
claim–evidence and missing-facts structures. Consequently,
“self-reflective” in SRCoT does not claim an internal cognitive faculty
of the model; it denotes a system-level mechanism that operationalizes
metacognitive monitoring and control over an evidence-constrained
workspace.

## 3 Methodology

Expanding on the overview presented already in Section
[1](#S1 "1 Introduction ‣ Self-Reflective Chain-of-Thought (SRCoT): Evidence-Based Text Generation via a Closed-Loop Retrieve–Reflect–Revise Pipeline on Edge LLMs"),
we now detail SRCoT’s complete formulation, including its architectural
framework and components, as well as its implementation.

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

We consider evidence-oriented generation under local operational
constraints. Let $`q`$ denote a user query and let $`\mathcal{D}`$ be a
local document collection (e.g., SOPs⁴⁴ 4 Standard Operating Procedures
(SOPs): locally defined, organization-specific operational procedures
that translate policies or guidelines into concrete, auditable steps. In
this paper, the term includes both medical SOPs/checklists (e.g.,
assessment, handover, contraindication checks) and non-medical
operational rules such as fire brigade Standard Operating/Response Rules
(SER; _Standard-Einsatz-Regeln_) used to standardize incident tactics
and coordination., checklists, organizational rules). The system
produces an output $`y`$ together with an auditable trace $`\tau`$
(e.g., retrieved snippets, claim–evidence links, detected
gaps/conflicts). We model generation as an iterative process over an
explicit system state

where $`E`$ encodes environment constraints (local availability, roles,
allowed actions), $`K`$ curated stable knowledge, $`X`$ accumulated
experience heuristics, and $`M`$ short-term task memory.

A key objective is to reduce unsupported or locally invalid statements.
Let $`V(y,\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D})\in\{0,1\}`$ be a validator that
checks whether $`y`$ satisfies acceptance criteria $`\mathcal{C}`$
(e.g., “claims must be supported by retrieved evidence”, “local Standard
Operating Procedures override general knowledge”, “do not propose
actions unavailable in $`E`$”). We aim to produce outputs that satisfy
$`V=1`$ while keeping the process resource-bounded on edge devices:

When validation cannot be achieved, the system should fail safely by
abstaining or asking for missing critical information.

### 3.2 Framework

SRCoT implements a closed-loop inference procedure
(Figure [1](#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3.2 Framework ‣ 3 Methodology ‣ Self-Reflective Chain-of-Thought (SRCoT): Evidence-Based Text Generation via a Closed-Loop Retrieve–Reflect–Revise Pipeline on Edge LLMs")).
Each iteration alternates between (i) retrieving local evidence, (ii)
constructing a structured workspace that separates claims, evidence
candidates, and open gaps, and (iii) applying a controller that decides
whether to finalize, revise, retrieve more targeted evidence, or
abstain. The key design goal is auditability: the system produces not
only an answer but also a trace that exposes which constraints were
active and which evidence supported each claim.

### 3.3 Workspace

The framework maintains multiple machine-readable
stores—environment.json (E), knowledge.json (K), experiences.json (X),
and memory.json (M)—which are selectively integrated into a
task-specific workspace $`W_{t}`$. The workspace is not merely
additional context text; it is a structured representation that
contains: (i) atomic claims (statements or recommended steps), (ii)
evidence candidates (retrieved snippets labeled with their source
identifiers so statements can be traced back to specific documents),
(iii) explicit constraints derived from $`E`$, and (iv) a list of
missing critical facts.

This separation serves two practical functions. First, it reduces
implicit mixing of heterogeneous information (e.g., preventing
heuristics in $`X`$ from being treated as hard evidence). Second, it
enables deterministic checks and prioritization rules (e.g., SOP
precedence) to be applied transparently, making failure modes easier to
diagnose.

### 3.4 Judge and Controller

After an initial draft is generated, an optional *Judge* step decomposes
the draft into checkable claims and attempts to attach local evidence to
each claim via targeted retrieval queries. For example, a draft sentence
such as *“Treat nausea with dimenhydrinate”* is split into claims (e.g.,
*indication* and *contraindication checks*); the Judge retrieves the
matching local SOP snippet(s) and records a claim–evidence map (e.g., C1
$`\rightarrow`$ \[Knowledge:47 Antiemese SOP\]), lists missing critical
facts (e.g., age/allergies not specified), and flags conflict markers
(e.g., “contraindication” / “do not use” German: “Kontraindikation” or
“nicht anwenden”) that can trigger conservative control actions
(retrieve more / ask-human / abstain).

A deterministic *Controller* then selects the next action based on these
signals. Typical actions include: Finalize (if coverage and constraints
are satisfied), Targeted-Retrieve (if evidence is missing),
Revise-with-Evidence (if new snippets were found), or Abstain/Ask-Human
(if critical gaps remain). Importantly, the Judge is treated as a tool
for producing auditable diagnostics rather than as a ground-truth
verifier.

### 3.5 Implementation

We implement SRCoT as a lightweight Python pipeline designed for local
(edge) OpenAI-compatible inference servers (e.g., LM Studio⁵⁵ 5 LM
Studio is a local application that hosts language models on a personal
computer and exposes an API compatible with OpenAI-style chat
interfaces, enabling offline experiments without cloud calls. with
OpenAI GPT-oss-120b⁶⁶ 6 Model names often include an approximate

billion parameters, which generally correlates with higher compute and

performed with BM25⁷⁷ 7 Okapi BM25 is a lexicon-based probabilistic

The state, validator, and controller descriptions above specify the
conceptual control-loop view; the implemented prompt contracts and
optional Judge remain fallible approximations of these design
objectives.

## 4 Experimentation

We evaluate SRCoT as an *exploratory prototype* using synthetic,
trace-rich case studies. The goal is not to establish superiority over
alternative methods, but to document (i) feasibility on edge hardware,
(ii) auditability and trace quality, and (iii) typical failure modes and
control-loop behaviors that motivate future systematic experiments.

All runs follow the SRCoT reporting convention: the system produces a
final answer plus a structured trace consisting of retrieved context
blocks (labeled by store provenance), an intermediate draft (Pass 1
“Reflect”), and a revised output (Pass 2 “Finalize”). Whenever
information is missing from the provided context, the output must
explicitly mark it as not specified. For some tasks, SRCoT additionally
generates a focused second retrieval query (query2) derived from
follow-up questions or gap signals, enabling a retrieval–revise loop.
Optionally, a *Judge* step produces a claim–evidence map and enumerates
missing critical facts, conflicts, and a conservative confidence
estimate.

We treat the SRCoT report as the primary evaluation artifact.
Accordingly, we emphasize *trace-based proxy criteria* that can be
inspected without medical ground truth labels: (i) evidence anchoring
(presence of source keys for key statements), (ii) constraint adherence
(e.g., “no invented values”), (iii) explicit gap marking (not specified
instead of completion), and (iv) controller recommendations (retrieve
more / revise / abstain / dispatch suggestion).

### 4.1 Case Study 1: EMS – local SOP as a reference signal in the SRCoT control loop

This case study illustrates SRCoT in a setting where local protocols
dominate, resources and responsibilities vary by situation, and
unsupported specifics are hazardous. The presentation is intentionally
*abstract* (no dosages or concrete therapy instructions) to demonstrate
the system mechanism rather than provide medical guidance.

An edge deployment in emergency medical services (EMS) should support
responders with documentation/checklists and with a structured
derivation of next steps under local constraints. Local SOPs and
availability (e.g., medications, monitoring, transport options) differ
across organizations and regions. Our prototype was executed locally on
an Apple M4-family MacBook Pro with 128 GB memory, using a local
OpenAI-compatible inference endpoint. The saved EMS report and result
metadata identify gpt-oss-120b, whereas the associated request file
specifies gpt-oss-20b. We therefore do not use that request file to
reconstruct the selected run’s settings. The exact model artifact,
quantization, and inference-runtime version are not established by the
examined case files. This shows that, in principle, the system can run
on a lightweight, self-contained device that could be carried in an
ambulance or other mobile assets. This is particularly relevant for
prehospital settings, because local execution (without cloud dependency)
enables offline capability, low infrastructure requirements, and better
control over data handling.

SRCoT separates heterogeneous information into four provenance-labeled
stores. The environment.json store ($`E`$) captures operational context
such as vehicle type, available devices/medications, responder roles
(e.g., EMT/paramedic/physician), transport times, and local escalation
pathways. The knowledge.json store ($`K`$) contains general medical
background concepts (e.g., ABCDE/FAST as principles) but explicitly
*without* local binding authority. The experiences.json store ($`X`$)
collects practice-oriented heuristics such as recurring documentation
pitfalls or frequent contraindication checks (i.e., “what tends to work
in this organization”). Finally, the memory.json store ($`M`$)
represents the current case state, including patient data, vital-sign
trends, actions already performed, open questions, and timestamps.

The control loop proceeds in four steps. First, the system *measures*
via retrieval: BM25 searches for relevant SOP passages based on the
incident description and findings stored in $`M`$ (e.g., “stroke
algorithm”, “neurological emergency / FAST checklist”, “documentation
requirements”). Second, it performs a workspace build: srcot.py
constructs a workspace $`W_{t}`$ that aggregates proposed next steps as
claims, attaches referenced SOP passages as candidate evidence, and
injects explicit constraints from $`E`$ (e.g., “not available”,
“physician-only”, “transport priority”). Third, the monitor *compares*
by applying consistency checks, including SOP priority (local SOP
overrides general knowledge on conflict), resource consistency (no
actions requiring items marked unavailable in $`E`$), and evidence
binding (claims without SOP/document support are marked uncertain or
removed). Fourth, the controller *corrects*: if gaps remain (e.g., SOP
not found, incomplete patient data), it triggers targeted re-retrieval
(e.g., query rewriting from diagnoses) or downgrades the output policy
from “recommendation” to “checklist/questions”.

In EMS, “globally plausible” is often “locally wrong” (different
medications, different escalation paths, different documentation
duties). The architecture therefore enforces a hard constraint: *local
compatibility* as a reference signal. Experiences ($`X`$) may *guide*
the process (e.g., which checks commonly go missing) but may not replace
SOPs as evidence. This is intended to discourage overconfident specifics
by replacing unsupported elaboration with explicit gap marking; the case
study does not measure the rate of such failures. If, after $`T`$
iterations, no evidence-bound, SOP-compliant answer can be produced, the
system is designed to output an honest fallback (e.g., “SOP source not
found / data missing; please consult the local algorithm”). In medical
contexts, this controlled abort is an intended safeguard, not a
demonstrated guarantee of safety.

#### 4.1.1 Interpretation

This EMS scenario illustrates that follow-up questions in a
self-reflective setting are not merely a by-product of text generation,
but can be interpreted as a *systematic marker* of uncertainty
management and metacognitive control. Functionally, this mirrors a
common prehospital pattern: an initial action structure is followed by
an internal check of which information is actually known and which
parameters are missing in order to justify the next steps safely,
protocol-compliantly, and in a handover-ready form.

In the SRCoT report, the system first produces an SOP-proximal, fully
structured checklist for prehospital stroke handling. In parallel, it
explicitly marks missing fields (e.g., as not specified) and then
generates targeted follow-up questions. Notably, these follow-ups are
content-specific: they address precisely those critical parameters that
matter for prehospital decision-making and handover logic and were
visibly missing in the preceding output—namely heart rate and
temperature (standard monitoring values that remained unclear), blood
glucose (required to rule out hypoglycemia, but not given), intravenous
(IV) access status (required but not stated unambiguously), and symptom
onset time (a time-critical key variable for stroke pathways that was
missing).

This makes a metacognitive sequence reconstructable from a single
report: the system can respond in a structured, action-guiding manner
while simultaneously recognizing that core information is missing for
safe application and credible handover, and it then “translates” these
gaps into *concrete, operational follow-up questions* (information
seeking instead of free completion).

For the argument of this paper, this suffices as a concrete case:
follow-up questions form the *operational interface* between knowledge
application (object level) and meta-control (meta level) [Nelson and
Narens (1994)](#bib.bib15). Functionally, this corresponds to
confidence-controlled information seeking: as long as critical variables
are missing, the system does not “keep elaborating” but switches its
generation policy toward data collection/clarification [Kepecs et al.
(2008)](#bib.bib19); [Fleming and Daw (2017)](#bib.bib20); [Pouget
et al. (2016)](#bib.bib21).

The report provides qualitative evidence that follow-ups in this setting
are *not arbitrary* but *gap-driven*: visible missing facts are
converted into specific questions (missing facts $`\rightarrow`$
targeted questions). This makes follow-ups an observable, auditable
indicator of self-monitoring: the system exposes *where* its evidence
base ends and what additional information would be required to make the
next decision robust.

Beyond this, the same artifact trail (output + marked gaps +
follow-ups + retrieval blocks + Judge map, if enabled) supports further
*qualitative* claims. The key is to frame these as properties of the
implemented control procedure (“becomes visible”, “is documentable”, “is
auditable”) rather than as strong claims about clinical correctness or
model “cognition”.

#### 4.1.2 Key findings

- •
  Evidence discipline as an explicit writing contract (auditability over
  plausibility). By combining the not specified convention with a
  minimum citation requirement, a central LLM failure mode is
  *systemically discouraged*: plausible embellishment in the absence of
  data. This is qualitatively supported because (i) missing values
  remain visible and (ii) central steps/claims are tied to concrete
  retrieval blocks (inline citation keys). This does not make the output
  “true”, but it makes it *inspectable*: readers can verify which local
  text anchor served as the basis for each action element.
- •
  The object-level vs. meta-level separation is visible in the artifact.
  In the two-phase process (draft/follow-ups $`\rightarrow`$ final), the
  report shows a role separation: Pass 1 produces a provisional action
  structure while diagnosing incompleteness; Pass 2 integrates
  additional evidence and stabilizes the formulation. This sequence is
  compatible with the Nelson & Narens distinction (object level: task
  performance; meta level: monitoring/control) without requiring any
  claims about “consciousness” [Nelson and Narens (1994)](#bib.bib15).
- •
  Local compatibility becomes visible as a hard constraint (constraints
  $`\rightarrow`$ language form). When environment.json and local SOP
  fragments appear in context, it becomes qualitatively visible that the
  response is not primarily oriented toward “global” medical text, but
  toward locally coded terminology, role logic, and handover structure.
  This is not a guarantee, but it is an auditable trace: the used terms
  and steps can be found in the cited local blocks rather than remaining
  purely generic.
- •
  The Judge operationalizes “self-critique”: claim decomposition and
  evidence coverage. If the Judge is enabled, it becomes possible to
  show qualitatively that self-monitoring appears not only as follow-up
  questions but as a *structured verification* of the final answer: the
  output is decomposed into claims; targeted queries are generated per
  claim; evidence coverage is recorded as a map (claim_evidence_map). As
  a result, “confidence” is not emitted as an intuition but is tied to
  observable proxy signals (e.g., coverage, missing critical facts,
  conflict markers).
- •
  A closed control loop is describable as a system procedure
  (cybernetics as an objective frame). Without making neurobiological
  claims, one can show qualitatively that the procedure matches the
  minimal structure of a feedback controller: (i)
  measurement/observation via retrieval, (ii) comparison/diagnosis via
  validator/Judge signals (gaps, conflicts, missing evidence), (iii)
  corrective action via policy (e.g., Targeted-Retrieve, Refocus,
  Abstain), (iv) re-measurement and revision. The key point is the
  *documented causal trace*: diagnostic artifacts
  (gap/conflict/coverage) are visible in the report and can be linked to
  the triggered control action. This makes the cybernetic interpretation
  procedural rather than metaphorical [Wiener (1948)](#bib.bib16);
  [Åström and Murray (2008)](#bib.bib17); [Ashby (1956)](#bib.bib22).
- •
  A functional analogy to conflict monitoring is admissible if stated as
  a proxy. Even without claiming that an ACC mechanism is “reproduced”,
  one can show qualitatively that the system uses conflict/risk hints as
  *triggers for control adaptation*: the appearance of conflict markers
  or contraindication hits $`\rightarrow`$ a more conservative
  confidence posture $`\rightarrow`$ additional information seeking or
  abstention. This is compatible with conflict-monitoring accounts
  (conflict $`\rightarrow`$ increased control) but should be framed
  explicitly as an *algorithmic analogy* [Newman and McGaughy
  (2015)](#bib.bib23).

The scenario documents a gap-driven control logic: workflow-critical
parameters that are missing in the trace are explicitly marked as
missing and then translated into concrete follow-up questions rather
than replaced with plausible completions (e.g., “What is the current
heart rate?”, “What temperature was measured?”, “What is the bedside
glucose value?”, “Is IV access established?”, “When did symptoms
begin?”). In combination with citation obligations and an explicit
missing-value convention, this yields an auditable output that exposes
local evidence anchors and enables post-hoc verification of individual
steps. When the optional Judge is enabled, self-monitoring becomes a
second verification layer (claim decomposition, evidence coverage,
gap/conflict signals) that can deterministically trigger more
conservative control actions (targeted retrieval/abstention). Overall,
the EMS case is informative because the causal trace remains explicit in
the report: missing critical parameters are marked and immediately
transformed into follow-up questions (“not specified” $`\rightarrow`$
query) rather than being hidden in filler text.

### 4.2 Case Study 2: Munich Sim as a synthetic testbed for SRCoT

To stress-test SRCoT beyond small hand-curated corpora, we use *Munich
Sim (München Einsatz-Simulation)*, a software-based emergency operations
simulation (built with GPT 5.2) that generates large volumes of
structured and semi-structured operational text. Munich Sim provides a
controllable environment in which SRCoT can be evaluated under
semi-realistic documentation and dispatch dynamics while avoiding
sensitive real-world data. Concretely, the simulator produces: (i)
incident overviews (e.g., location, incident type, assigned units, and a
mechanically derived dispatch proposal), (ii) log-style event streams
(status changes, control room updates), (iii) radio-like message
snippets, and (iv) continuously updated resource and material
inventories. These artifacts are ingested into SRCoT’s separated stores

#### 4.2.3 Interpretation

The scenario suggests three closely related takeaways: workspace scale
is a first-class evaluation dimension, because even when memory.json
contains dozens to hundreds of atomic updates (dispatch injections,
radio-style messages, unit status lines), SRCoT can still synthesize a
coherent structure without requiring a human to manually merge the full
log; injected control-room messages operationalize “disturbances” for
cybernetic evaluation, since Leitstelle injections perturb the observed
state and can trigger downstream control actions (dispatch
recommendations, escalation/de-escalation), enabling repeatable stress
tests by programmatically steering incident trajectories; and a critical
failure mode appears under high load, namely provenance/citation
slippage in the narrative layer, which indicates that evidence *binding*
(mapping claims to concrete Memory:\* keys) is a separate problem from
retrieval itself and motivates stricter claim–evidence enforcement
(e.g., requiring at least one store key per operational paragraph).
Overall, this case supports a conservative claim: even when model
reasoning is insufficient for robust correctness guarantees, SRCoT can
still generate *structured, inspectable artifacts* from very large
operational traces, and the simulation can be systematically manipulated
through injected state updates to test controller behavior.

A minimal symptom intervention is already sufficient to test
protocol-triggered retrieval: a single injected nausea line

closed-loop behavior: a state change is injected, retrieval should pivot
to the correct protocol fragment, and the Judge should expose missing
critical facts as control-relevant signals. It is *not* evidence of
clinical correctness beyond the local SOP text and the implemented
control policies.

the associated benefit.

Crucially, SRCoT does *not* invent unlogged arrivals or requests.
Instead, the question is exposed as an explicit uncertainty/gap signal
in the trace (Pass 1 follow-ups), which can be used to trigger a
targeted re-retrieval step if such information exists elsewhere in the
corpus (e.g., radio snippets or later control-room updates).

#### 4.2.5 Interpretation

SRCoT’s gap-driven information seeking can extend beyond “what is in the
logs”: it may generate follow-up questions that reflect operational
completeness criteria (“Is there missing context needed for a full
report?”) rather than merely paraphrasing retrieved statements.
Importantly, the same no-invention discipline can apply to operational
context, not only to medical specifics: if the trace does not confirm
whether supporting units were requested, SRCoT can surface this
uncertainty explicitly instead of silently assuming a typical real-world
escalation pattern. In this sense, the follow-up can itself function as
a control signal—either as a retrieval/verification trigger (“search for
supporting-unit evidence”) or as a documentation-quality checklist item
depending on the downstream use case. Overall, this scenario supports a
conservative claim: even when the simulator’s AAO proxy is minimal and
the logs do not encode certain operational details, SRCoT can still
expose *structured, inspectable artifacts* that make missing operational
context visible. In this sense, the follow-up question acts as a
traceable meta-control signal (“missing context”) rather than as a
dispatch recommendation, and it provides a practical handle for either
targeted re-retrieval (if such evidence exists) or explicit gap marking
in handover-quality documentation.

#### 4.2.6 Key Findings

Across tasks and scenarios, Munich Sim supports the paper’s framing of
closed-loop self-reasoning as a *controllable* and *auditable* process:
the SRCoT *report* becomes the central evaluation object (audit-first),
because it exposes not only the final text but also the provenance trace
(store keys) that makes key statements inspectable even without
gold-standard labels. In addition, the query2 mechanism operationalizes
information seeking as a proxy for metacognitive control: detected gaps
trigger refined retrieval and revision, transforming one-shot generation
into a deliberate “retrieve–revise” loop. The simulator also enables
iterative governance via micro-policies (e.g., strict JSON schemas, not
specified contracts, and content restrictions such as “no dosage
instructions”), which can be tuned on synthetic cases without retraining
the underlying model. Finally, the synthetic scale of heterogeneous logs
and radio-style streams exposes failure modes early: increased volume
and variety amplify drift, omission, and provenance errors, and the
stress-test scenario in particular highlights evidence-binding under
large memory.json as a distinct bottleneck that motivates stricter
claim–evidence enforcement and retrieval prioritization.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we introduced *Self-Reflective Chain-of-Thought (SRCoT)*,
a modular framework for evidence-based text generation that treats LLM
inference as a closed *Retrieve–Reflect–Revise* control loop rather than
a one-shot completion. SRCoT combines local retrieval, explicit state
separation (environment, knowledge, experiences, memory), and a
reflection layer that operationalizes self-monitoring as *auditable
artifacts*: claim–evidence links, missing-facts signals, and
conservative control actions (retrieve more, revise, ask for
clarification, or abstain). The central contribution is not a new model,
but a process architecture that makes reliability a property of the
*system procedure* and its trace, not a hope placed in fluency.

Because LLMs are trained on human-produced knowledge artifacts, their
outputs can mirror patterns of human reasoning while still lacking an
explicit mechanism for evidence-bound control. Human cognition relies
not only on generating candidate thoughts, but also on metacognitive
regulation (monitoring uncertainty, detecting conflicts, and actively
seeking missing evidence) [Nelson and Narens (1990)](#bib.bib24);
[Flavell (1979)](#bib.bib25). SRCoT instantiates an analogous
*system-level* control layer: it decomposes outputs into checkable
claims, binds them to retrieved local evidence, flags contradictions and
information gaps, and then selects conservative control actions
(targeted retrieval, revision, ask-human, or abstention). In this sense,
SRCoT operationalizes self-reflection as explicit monitoring-and-control
over an evidence-constrained workspace rather than as an assumed
internal faculty.

Across the two case-study families, selected SRCoT traces illustrate the
intended shift from plausible completion toward *evidence-bound
writing*. In the EMS scenario, the system produced structured,
SOP-proximal documentation while explicitly flagging missing
workflow-critical variables as not specified and translating them into
targeted follow-up questions. In Munich Sim, SRCoT scaled to
heterogeneous log and radio streams and produced source-anchored
reports, numeric summaries, and dispatch-style control objects;
importantly, it also exposed gaps and mismatches (e.g., under-coverage
signals) without fabricating absent state. Taken together, these traces
show that a closed-loop workflow can expose missing facts and proposed
evidence links in local deployments. They do not establish a reduction
in unsupported statements or improved constraint adherence relative to a
baseline.

A key implication is that SRCoT provides a practical path toward
*auditable decision support* under edge constraints. Offline execution
reduces cloud dependency and improves control over data handling, while
the report-first design enables post-hoc inspection, debugging, and
governance: reviewers can see which statements were supported by which
local fragments, which critical facts were missing, and which control
actions were triggered. In operational contexts where “globally
plausible” can be “locally wrong,” the ability to encode local SOPs and
resource constraints as a reference signal is central: SRCoT is designed
to prefer local compatibility and controlled abstention over generic
completion.

At the same time, our results do not imply clinical correctness, outcome
improvement, or general superiority over baselines. The present
evaluation is exploratory and primarily demonstrates feasibility,
controllability, and trace quality. SRCoT turns reliability questions
into testable system properties (evidence coverage, binding, gap
detection, and control policies) and makes failures visible as concrete
artifacts rather than hidden model behavior. We view this shift from
one-shot generation to closed-loop, evidence-centered control as a
necessary step for deploying LLM-based text systems responsibly in
safety- and operations-critical environments. SRCoT reframes “auditable
generation” as a controllable, inspectable feedback process—especially
valuable for safety- and operations-critical settings such as emergency
management and medical and fire services, where models must often run
locally under limited compute and strict local rules, and where
human-in-the-loop verification is a non-negotiable safety constraint.

## 6 Limitations

Our study has several limitations. (1) Evidence scope. Results are
primarily qualitative, targeting auditability, control structure, and
gap-driven behavior; they do not establish comprehensive recall,
hallucination reduction, clinical utility, or superiority over baselines
without larger datasets and controlled evaluations. (2) Data scale and
retrieval bias. The EMS scenario is small and potentially selective;
with large top-k, BM25 can approach “return everything”, masking
precision, context use, and drift, and retrieval remains sensitive to
query phrasing and SOP quality. (3) System dependence. Observed
follow-up behavior is partly induced by prompts, schemas, and output
contracts, reflecting end-to-end design rather than model capability
alone. (4) Evaluation and simulation limits. The optional Judge may
hallucinate or mis-score evidence, keyword conflict markers are coarse
heuristics, and Munich Sim results expose prototype-specific issues
(e.g., citation slippage, scope creep, unsafe handling of missing
contraindications), limiting transfer across domains, document styles,
and languages. (5) Deployment framing. Local execution requires
governance for safety, and the cybernetic framing is descriptive of the
pipeline, not evidence of biological mechanism replication.

A comprehensive expert-rating study with interrater reliability was not
feasible in the present setting because access to a sufficiently large
and representative panel of domain experts is constrained by funding,
organizational and operational factors, making systematic recruitment
and coordination impractical for our study. Additionally, purely
subjective ratings we noted early on in our research were vulnerable to
presentation effects, since fluent writing style can appear plausible
even when claims are incorrect which is a critical issue in
safety-critical domains. We therefore adopt a document-anchored
validation strategy in which correctness and local compatibility are
operationalized via normative references (e.g., Standard Operating
Procedures and curated reference lists as *normative baseline* and
*binding reference*) and evaluated at the level of individual claims,
prioritizing traceability (explicit claim and evidence support) as well
as conservative abstention when evidence is insufficient. Local
heterogeneity is also treated as a core constraint: SOP availability,
age, and bindingness vary across regions and organizations, so
evaluations are interpreted relative to the respective local corpus and
normative baseline, and cross-region comparisons are limited.

## 7 Ethical Concerns

Both reported case settings use fully synthetic scenarios, without real
patient records, radio recordings, or incident data. No human
participants were recruited and no clinical interventions were performed
for these case studies. The following principles describe the intended
research scope and requirements for any future use.

Purpose and Research Motivation. The framework is designed exclusively
as decision support for prehospital and emergency management contexts,
with the aim of improving information synthesis, situational awareness,
and documentation under time pressure. It is not intended to replace
clinical judgment, operational command, or established protocols. Human
decision-makers retain full authority and responsibility for all actions
taken. The system is framed to encourage verification and reflection
rather than automation, explicitly reinforcing human-in-the-loop or
human-in-command use as a non-negotiable design constraint.

Prevention of Over-Reliance and Accountability. To mitigate automation
bias, the system is designed to surface uncertainty explicitly and to
avoid presenting speculative or inferred details as facts. Structured,
fluent outputs should be accompanied by clear indications of missing or
uncertain information (e.g., explicit “not specified” markers), and the
system is intended to abstain from completing safety-critical fields
when inputs are incomplete. Accountability and liability remain with
human operators and their governing organizations; ethical deployment
therefore requires clear governance structures, documented decision
ownership, and policies that define the role of tool outputs as advisory
rather than prescriptive.

Handling of Harmful or Safety-Critical Content. Because unsafe default
completions (such as inferred contraindications, timelines, or resource
availability) could lead to harm, the framework prioritizes conservative
behavior. It is designed to discourage unsupported operational specifics
and to defer or flag gaps instead of filling them; the observed failures
show that this behavior is not guaranteed. Verification steps are
required for critical items, and outputs are intended to prompt human
review rather than immediate execution.

Data Protection and Confidentiality. Although the framework can be
executed locally, ethical use still requires rigorous data protection
practices. Patient and incident data may persist in prompts, logs, or
exported artifacts; therefore, retention limits, access controls, and
audit trails are necessary. Ethical deployment assumes compliance with
applicable data protection regulations and organizational policies
governing sensitive information.

Fairness, Context Sensitivity, and Performance Limits. Unequal
performance across contexts is a recognized risk, as local standard
operating procedures and documentation quality vary widely. Ethical use
requires validation in the specific operational environments where the
system is deployed, ongoing maintenance, and transparent communication
of known limitations. The framework should not be assumed to generalize
reliably beyond the contexts for which it has been evaluated.

Responsible Release, Dual-Use Mitigation, and Policy Compliance. The
framework has potential dual-use risks, including misuse for autonomous
operational control, dispatch decisions, or applications outside
approved governance structures. To prevent scope creep, responsible
release includes explicit scope restrictions, access controls, usage
logging, and policy enforcement mechanisms. The system is not intended
for autonomous decision-making or unauthorized operational use, and
safeguards are required to ensure it remains within its stated research
and support role.

## 8 Future Work

This work presents an early prototype with an exploratory evaluation.
Immediate next steps include systematic studies using larger and more
diverse local corpora to quantify evidence coverage, failure modes, and
constraint violations, as well as controlled ablations (e.g., removing
individual components or varying top-$`k`$) to isolate the contributions
of specific design choices. We also plan to investigate stronger
retrieval and reranking strategies that remain compatible with edge and
offline constraints, alongside formal domain-specific governance
mechanisms such as versioned SOP corpora, audit logging, and
prompt-injection defenses.

Future work should also broaden the empirical basis of evaluation by
generating many more realistic test cases and operational scenarios and
reviewing them with domain experts using structured protocols and, where
possible, inter-rater agreement.

A second open direction concerns controller and orchestration design,
including how many reasoning passes and retrieval–revise loops are
required for different task classes, and which prompt elements most
effectively elicit metacognitive-like behaviors (e.g., gap detection and
conflict checking) that improve outputs within fixed model constraints.

Finally, we will explore mechanisms for state and policy accumulation,
including whether modular “micro-policies” can be maintained as
retrievable rule fragments without destabilizing behavior, and whether
writing back compact summaries of prior outputs, verified facts, and
open gaps can improve subsequent passes by reducing repetition and
sharpening follow-on queries.

A near-term direction is improving the reliability of numerical outputs
for aggregation-heavy queries (e.g., totals, deltas, and
*Materialverbrauch* as consumption summaries), where evidence is often
distributed across many log-like entries (e.g., per-unit
“Materialverbrauch” lines) and only occasionally summarized in
overview/diff blocks. The v1.0 prototype lacks dedicated reconciliation
mechanisms and can therefore over-rely on whichever fragments are
retrieved, yielding brittle totals or silent inconsistencies (e.g.,
reporting a fuel total from an overview block while missing additional
per-incident log entries). In our ongoing v1.2 prototype we introduce
lightweight, offline-compatible improvements that preserve the two-pass
SRCoT structure: (i) generic query expansion by mining salient
identifiers from the question and first-pass draft (e.g., item IDs such
as con_fuel_mix=500 or incident tags like \#000071) to better steer the
second retrieval pass; (ii) context prioritization to surface
aggregate/overview evidence earlier in the prompt; and (iii) an
automatic numeric conflict detector that triggers a reconcile rewrite
when multiple distinct values are observed for the same key. These
changes are intended to improve coverage of relevant “Materialverbrauch”
evidence and the consistency of totals, without adding heavy
infrastructure or online dependencies; their effects require controlled
evaluation.

More broadly, we view the central research question as
interdisciplinary: Under operational decision constraints, when and why
do different control, retrieval, and governance strategies succeed or
fail, and how can such triggering behavior be validated in practice?

# From Semantic Decisions to Feasible Trajectories: Self-Evolving LLM-Guided Optimal Control for Narrow-Space Parking

###### Abstract

Autonomous parking in nonconvex and narrow environments remains
challenging. Although optimal-control methods can explicitly enforce
vehicle dynamics and collision constraints, nonconvexity compromises
solver robustness and can cause failures. Large language models (LLMs)
exhibit strong semantic reasoning capabilities, but directly generating
dense trajectories makes it difficult to guarantee physical feasibility.
We introduce SE-LLM–OCP, a unified framework in which LLMs make
high-level discrete maneuver decisions, while an optimal-control module
enforces low-level vehicle dynamics and collision constraints. Online,
the LLM proposes sparse maneuver plans, decomposing the parking task
into a sequence of short‑horizon trajectory‑optimization problems. A
low-level solver then sequentially solves optimal-control problems. If
the solver fails, the LLM aggregates failure evidence from the solver
and validation stages to guide replanning. Offline, SE-LLM–OCP
automatically evolves a structured decision-making knowledge base from
scratch, driven by accumulated online failures. We validate our proposed
framework in simulation on a car-like vehicle model and on a
differential-drive robot. Our experimental results show that SE-LLM–OCP
enables safer autonomous parking in narrow scenarios and demonstrates
transfer of the same maneuver representation to a different kinematic
platform.

## I Introduction

Autonomous parking in cluttered environments is a canonical hybrid
motion-planning problem. A vehicle trajectory must satisfy nonholonomic
kinematics and full-footprint collision constraints, yet planner success
often depends on discrete maneuver choices: the approach direction, the
ordering of forward and reverse segments, and the placement of
intermediate configurations. Search-based planners such as Hybrid A\*
(HA\*) explore these choices with discrete nonholonomic motion
primitives \[[1](#bib.bib2)\]. Optimization-based planners instead
optimize continuous paths or trajectories under collision costs or
constraints and, where modeled, vehicle kinematics or dynamics
\[[2](#bib.bib3), [3](#bib.bib13)\]. The two paradigms fail in different
ways. Search effort depends on the resolution and coverage of the motion
primitives, whereas nonlinear trajectory optimization is sensitive to
initialization and the selected homotopy class. These weaknesses become
pronounced in nonconvex and narrow spaces: an unsuitable maneuver
structure may lead to excessive search or local optimization failure
even though a feasible trajectory exists.

Large language models offer a complementary source of high-level
structure, especially when paired with visual or structured scene
representations. Language-grounded robotic systems have used foundation
models to decompose instructions into admissible actions, ground skill
selection in affordances, synthesize executable policy programs, and
plan over scene-level representations \[[4](#bib.bib4), [5](#bib.bib5),
[6](#bib.bib6), [7](#bib.bib8)\]. Autonomous-driving studies have
likewise explored language-model reasoning for motion planning and
coordinate-based trajectory generation \[[8](#bib.bib11),
[9](#bib.bib12)\]. These advances, however, do not remove the
physical-grounding problem. A maneuver can be semantically plausible yet
fail because of a small terminal-pose error, insufficient clearance, or
an infeasible swept volume. Asking the model to generate dense waypoints
or controls also assigns it a long sequence of metric decisions that are
difficult to verify. The question, then, is how to give an LLM the
maneuver-planning role in narrow-space parking while keeping physical
feasibility with the solver: semantic decisions must be turned into
physically feasible trajectories, and low-level failures must come back
as guidance the LLM can act on.

Fig. 1: Online hierarchical pipeline of SE-LLM–OCP: the decision-making

This paper answers this question with SE-LLM–OCP (Self-Evolving
LLM-Guided Optimal Control Planning), a hierarchical framework for
narrow-space autonomous parking. In SE-LLM–OCP, an LLM serves as the
maneuver-level planner: from the scene, the task, and a parking-decision
knowledge base, it decides how the vehicle should maneuver—where to
stage, which direction to move, and how to approach the goal—and
expresses these decisions as sparse terminal poses and forward or
reverse motion modes, each accompanied by a short rationale. It never
generates dense trajectories or low-level controls. A deterministic
optimal-control layer then turns each decision into a short-horizon
optimal control problem (OCP), enforcing nonholonomic vehicle
kinematics, actuation limits, and full-footprint collision avoidance.
Semantic reasoning and metric optimization are thus kept in strictly
separate layers, each doing what it does best.

Because LLM-proposed poses are approximate, the grounding stage does not
demand that they be met exactly: each terminal pose is grounded into a
small tolerance region adapted to the surrounding clearance, so minor
endpoint errors are absorbed without weakening the kinematic or
collision constraints. When optimization or validation nonetheless
rejects a maneuver, the failure is made actionable. Solver diagnostics,
validation errors, and the rationales of the failed decisions are
summarized by a feedback LLM into maneuver-level guidance, and the
planning LLM revises its decision. Accumulated failures additionally
drive an offline loop that grows the parking-decision knowledge base
from an initially empty state, with every entry generated automatically;
an update is retained only when the resulting planner again produces a
feasible trajectory. Throughout, the LLM parameters remain fixed:
_self-evolution_ proceeds through bounded, failure-conditioned revision
of the maneuver online and of the decision knowledge offline.

The main contributions of this work are:

- •
  a hierarchical framework for narrow-space autonomous parking that
  pairs LLM-based maneuver reasoning with deterministic optimal control,
  producing physically feasible trajectories under full vehicle
  kinematics and collision avoidance;
- •
  a semantic-to-optimization grounding that absorbs the metric
  approximation inherent in LLM decisions, compiling them into
  tolerance-adapted terminal conditions for short-horizon OCPs without
  weakening kinematic or collision constraints;
- •
  a bounded, failure-conditioned self-evolution loop that converts
  failed attempts into maneuver-level guidance, letting the LLM recover
  from infeasible decisions without unbounded replanning; and
- •
  an offline knowledge self-evolution loop that bootstraps the
  parking-decision knowledge base from an initially empty state, with
  all entries generated automatically, and retains an update only when
  it yields a feasible trajectory.

We validate SE-LLM–OCP in simulation across confined parking and
turnaround tasks. Under a shared Stanley–PID controller, SE-LLM–OCP
attains the highest closed-loop execution success and the lowest
collision rate among the compared planners, with fewer gear changes and
lower path-curvature metrics than HA\*+OCP; ablations isolate the
contributions of the evolved knowledge base, failure feedback, and
intermediate soft constraints, and a differential-drive experiment
demonstrates that the same maneuver representation transfers to a
different platform.

## II Related Work

### II-A Traditional Planning Approaches

Traditional parking planners primarily employ search-based methods and
trajectory optimization. Reeds–Shepp curves provide shortest paths for a
car with bounded curvature and forward–reverse motion in obstacle-free
space \[[10](#bib.bib1)\]. Hybrid A\* incorporates vehicle kinematics
into heuristic search, retaining continuous vehicle states within a
discretized search space and refining the resulting path through
numerical optimization \[[1](#bib.bib2)\]. These approaches provide
geometric foundations and initial paths for parking trajectory
generation.

Optimization-based methods formulate parking as an optimal control
problem with explicit vehicle-motion and collision-avoidance
constraints. Li et al. addressed time-optimal parallel parking through
simultaneous dynamic optimization \[[11](#bib.bib14)\]. They later
introduced safe travel corridors for parking, where a coarse path guides
corridor construction and different parts of the vehicle are constrained
within corresponding collision-free regions \[[12](#bib.bib18)\]. This
representation replaces large-scale collision-avoidance constraints with
a reduced-scale OCP whose size is independent of environmental
complexity. A subsequent lightweight iterative framework repeatedly
reconstructs the corridor to recover free space discarded by a fixed
corridor and improve trajectory optimization among irregularly placed
obstacles \[[13](#bib.bib15)\]. Corridor-based formulations therefore
make continuous optimization tractable after a coarse route has been
supplied. SE-LLM–OCP addresses the complementary high-level problem: an
LLM proposes and revises sparse staging poses and motion modes, while
the model-based OCP layer generates continuous trajectories under
vehicle-kinematic and collision-avoidance constraints.

### II-B End-to-End Parking Approaches

End-to-end approaches learn parking behavior from scene observations and
target information. Yang et al. investigated end-to-end neural parking
in the CARLA simulator \[[14](#bib.bib16)\]. ParkingE2E predicts parking
waypoints from surround-view images and a target parking slot using
imitation learning and an autoregressive transformer
\[[15](#bib.bib17)\]. MultiPark extends learned parking to multimodal,
multi-segment prediction, using parking queries that encode gear,
longitudinal, and lateral behaviors together with goal- and
collision-related training objectives \[[16](#bib.bib19)\]. These
developments address maneuver diversity and long-horizon parking within
learned planning architectures. In SE-LLM–OCP, the learned component
instead proposes sparse pose anchors and motion modes. Continuous
trajectories are generated by solving model-based OCPs, with vehicle
constraints enforced during optimization and complete plans checked
before execution.

### II-C Grounding and Self-Evolution in LLM-Based Planning

Grounding connects language-level reasoning to robot capabilities and
physical environments. SayCan combines language-model scores with skill
affordances to select executable actions \[[5](#bib.bib5)\]. AutoTAMP
translates language instructions into formal task representations for
task and motion planning, using corrective feedback to resolve
translation errors \[[17](#bib.bib10)\]. VoxPoser constructs spatial
value maps that guide model-based motion planning \[[18](#bib.bib9)\].
For narrow-space parking, the grounding interface must accommodate
nonholonomic motion and vehicle-footprint collision constraints.

Feedback and persistent memory further support the revision of
LLM-generated decisions. Inner Monologue incorporates environment
feedback into subsequent planning \[[19](#bib.bib7)\], while Reflexion
stores verbal reflections on task outcomes to guide later attempts
without updating model parameters \[[20](#bib.bib20)\]. In autonomous
driving, DiLu combines reasoning, reflection, and memory to retrieve
relevant experiences and revise unsuccessful decisions, but its memory
is seeded with manually outlined scenarios \[[21](#bib.bib21)\].
Building on these directions, SE-LLM–OCP couples online maneuver
refinement with offline revision of parking knowledge through a common
optimization and validation pipeline, evolving the knowledge base from
an initially empty state without hand-authored entries. Online, solver
diagnostics and trajectory-validation failures guide bounded replanning
of pose anchors and motion modes. Offline, accumulated failures inform
revisions to decision principles; a candidate knowledge update is
retained only after the resulting planning process yields a trajectory
that passes the same online acceptance checks. This links both decision
revision and knowledge retention to the feasibility of the generated
parking trajectories.

## III METHODS

Our framework SE-LLM–OCP comprises an online hierarchical pipeline
(Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ I Introduction ‣ From Semantic Decisions to Feasible Trajectories:Self-Evolving LLM-Guided Optimal Control for Narrow-Space Parking"))
and an offline knowledge self-evolution loop
(Fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ III-A1 High-level Maneuver Planning ‣ III-A Online Hierarchical Pipeline ‣ III METHODS ‣ From Semantic Decisions to Feasible Trajectories:Self-Evolving LLM-Guided Optimal Control for Narrow-Space Parking")).
The online hierarchy allows LLM-based maneuver decisions to guide
trajectory optimization while vehicle dynamics and collision constraints
are enforced by the OCP solver. Online replanning revises the maneuver
for the current task using a fixed knowledge-base version. Knowledge
revision is performed separately: accumulated failures guide candidate
knowledge-base updates. These updates are evaluated through the online
pipeline before being used in subsequent planning tasks.

### III-A Online Hierarchical Pipeline

The online pipeline comprises high-level maneuver planning, low-level
trajectory optimization, and feedback and replanning. The LLM specifies
pose anchors and motion modes that divide the parking task into
segments. These decisions are converted into terminal conditions for
sequential OCPs, and the resulting trajectory is checked for feasibility
before controller execution. Failed optimization or trajectory
validation provides feedback for revising the maneuver within a
prescribed attempt budget (three planning attempts per task).

#### III-A1 High-level Maneuver Planning

A parking scene is defined by a bounded workspace
$`\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}`$, the union of polygonal obstacle
regions $`\mathcal{O}`$, an initial pose
$`\mathbf{p}_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0},\theta_{0})`$, and a goal pose
$`\mathbf{p}_{g}`$. The region $`\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{O}`$ is
available for vehicle motion. Let $`\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{p})`$ denote the
oriented rectangular vehicle footprint at pose
$`\mathbf{p}=(x,y,\theta)`$. The collision-free configuration set is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}}=\left\{\mathbf{p}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{p})\subseteq\mathcal{W}\setminus\mathcal{O}\right\}.
``` |  | (1) |

The task is to reach $`\mathbf{p}_{g}`$ from $`\mathbf{p}_{0}`$ through
configurations in $`\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}}`$ while satisfying the
vehicle dynamics and actuation limits.

The decision-making LLM receives the task specification, scene image,
metric geometry, start and goal poses, and vehicle dimensions. Relevant
knowledge is retrieved by matching the current scene against the
accumulated entries, each of which records a scene description together
with explicitly allowed and forbidden operations. The retrieved entries
guide the selection of staging poses and motion modes. The LLM generates
an ordered maneuver plan with $`N`$ segments:

|     |                                                            |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \Pi=\left\{(\mathbf{p}_{i},m_{i},e_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{N},
       ```                                                         |     | (2) |

where $`\mathbf{p}_{i}`$ is the terminal-pose anchor of segment $`i`$,
$`m_{i}\in\{\texttt{forward},\texttt{reverse}\}`$ specifies its
direction of travel, and $`e_{i}`$ is a concise natural-language
rationale explaining why the segment moves this way. Each rationale is
carried through grounding and optimization; when a segment subsequently
fails to solve or validate, its rationale is returned to the LLM
together with the failure evidence, so that revisions can build on the
original intent of the failed decision. Intermediate anchors specify
staging poses for reorientation and goal approach, and the final anchor
coincides with the goal, $`\mathbf{p}_{N}=\mathbf{p}_{g}`$. Vehicle
parameters, objective weights, horizon lengths, and collision
constraints remain system-defined.

#### III-A2 Low-level Trajectory Optimization

The high-level maneuver plan is converted into terminal constraints and
penalties for sequential OCPs via adaptive anchor grounding. Requiring
every intermediate anchor to be reached exactly can overconstrain these
OCPs, even when nearby staging poses would permit feasible motion.
Bounded endpoint adjustments are therefore allowed, with penalties
favoring the nominal anchors. The final goal remains fixed. We first
describe how the anchors are grounded into terminal regions and
penalties, and then formulate the segment OCPs.

Anchor grounding. To make the allowed adjustments precise, we attach to
each intermediate anchor $`\mathbf{p}_{i}`$ a positional tolerance
$`r_{i}`$ and a heading tolerance $`\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{tol}}`$, which
bound how far the endpoint of segment $`i`$ may deviate from the nominal
anchor in position and heading, respectively. The positional tolerance
is derived from a clearance radius $`\rho_{i}`$, which measures the
vehicle’s translational freedom at the anchor. For an intermediate
anchor $`\mathbf{p}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},\theta_{i})`$, $`\rho_{i}`$ is the
largest radius within which the vehicle can translate in any direction
without collision while its heading is held at $`\theta_{i}`$. With a
cap $`\rho_{\mathrm{cap}}`$ and planar displacement
$`\mathbf{d}=(d_{x},d_{y})`$, it is defined as

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\rho_{i}=\sup\bigl\{`$ | $`\displaystyle\rho\in[0,\rho_{\mathrm{cap}}]\;\big|\;`$ |  | (3) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle(x_{i}+d_{x},y_{i}+d_{y},\theta_{i})\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}},`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\forall\,\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\text{ with }\|\mathbf{d}\|_{2}\leq\rho\bigr\},\quad i<N.`$ |  |  |

The positional tolerance $`r_{i}`$ bounds the positional deviation of
the segment endpoint from its nominal anchor and is computed as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
r_{i}=\max\left\{r_{\min},\min\left(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{task}},k\rho_{i}\right)\right\},\quad i<N.
``` |  | (4) |

Here, $`k`$ is a scale factor and $`\varepsilon_{\mathrm{task}}`$ is the
task-level positional tolerance. The lower bound $`r_{\min}`$ prevents
the tolerance from vanishing.

The terminal pose region $`\mathcal{T}_{i}`$ combines the positional
tolerance $`r_{i}`$ with the heading tolerance
$`\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{tol}}`$. The endpoint of segment $`i`$ is
required to lie in the set

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{i}=\bigl\{`$ | $`\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=(x,y,\theta)\;\big|\;`$ |  | (5) |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\left\|(x,y)-(x_{i},y_{i})\right\|_{2}\leq r_{i},`$ |  |  |
|  |  | $`\displaystyle\left|\operatorname{wrap}(\theta-\theta_{i})\right|\leq\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{tol}}\bigr\},\quad i<N.`$ |  |  |

Here, $`\operatorname{wrap}`$ returns the principal angular difference.
For the final segment, the set reduces to
$`\mathcal{T}_{N}=\{\mathbf{p}_{g}\}`$.

The terminal region bounds the endpoint adjustment, while a terminal
pose penalty $`J_{\mathrm{term}}^{i}`$ favors the nominal anchor. Its
position and heading weights are $`w_{\mathrm{pos}}^{i}`$ and
$`w_{\theta}^{i}`$. The position weight is reduced as the tolerance
grows, so that a generous tolerance weakens the pull of the penalty
toward the nominal anchor; the heading weight, in contrast, is held
fixed at $`w_{\theta}^{\mathrm{int}}`$ to keep the approach direction
precise. For intermediate anchors, the position weight is computed as

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
w_{\mathrm{pos}}(r_{i})=\max\left(w_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\min},\;w_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\max}\frac{r_{\min}}{\max(r_{i},r_{\min})}\right)
``` |  | (6) |

where the constants are set empirically to
$`w_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\min}=80`$, $`w_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\max}=200`$, and
$`w_{\theta}^{\mathrm{int}}=400`$. For the final segment, the weights
are fixed to $`w_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\mathrm{goal}}=1000`$ and
$`w_{\theta}^{\mathrm{goal}}=2000`$, likewise set empirically. The
operation $`\operatorname{TerminalCost}`$ constructs
$`J_{\mathrm{term}}^{i}`$ from the anchor and these weights.

The region itself does not certify collision freedom: $`\rho_{i}`$
assumes a fixed heading, whereas $`\mathcal{T}_{i}`$ permits heading
changes and the radius mapping can yield $`r_{i}>\rho_{i}`$. Collision
constraints are therefore imposed independently in the OCP.

|  |  | $`\displaystyle s_{t,c}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\geq d_{\mathrm{safe}},`$ |  |  |

Here, $`T`$ denotes the prediction horizon of the current segment. The
quantity $`s_{t,c}`$ denotes clearance for collision constraint $`c`$ at
step $`t`$, and $`d_{\mathrm{safe}}`$ is the required margin. Control
and steering bounds and $`v_{t}\in V(m_{i})`$ are also imposed. The
stage cost $`\ell`$ regularizes acceleration, steering rate, steering
angle, velocity, and obstacle slack. Horizon selection and solver retry
settings remain system-defined.

The first OCP starts from the initial vehicle state. Each subsequent OCP
starts from the preceding segment’s optimized terminal state, including
velocity and steering angle. Transferring the full state preserves
continuity and allows the next segment to account for the preceding
endpoint adjustment.

#### III-A3 Feedback and Replanning

The complete maneuver is checked after all segment OCPs have been
solved. Validation assesses endpoint accuracy, continuity between
segments, satisfaction of the terminal regions, and footprint
feasibility using a sampled vehicle sweep. Trajectories that pass all
feasibility checks are sent to the controller.

When OCP optimization fails or the assembled trajectory fails
validation, solver diagnostics and validation errors are passed to the
feedback LLM together with the rationales of the failed segments. It
summarizes potential causes at the maneuver level and provides guidance
for replanning. The decision-making LLM uses this feedback, the task
inputs, and retrieved knowledge to revise the pose anchors and motion
modes. The revised plan undergoes the same grounding, optimization, and
validation procedure, with the vehicle model and acceptance criteria
held fixed.

The online loop terminates when a feasible trajectory is found or the
prescribed attempt budget is exhausted. This feedback revises the
maneuver for the current task; the associated failure evidence is
retained for offline knowledge revision.

### III-B Offline Knowledge Self-Evolution Loop

The offline loop in
Fig. [2](#S3.F2 "Fig. 2 ‣ III-A1 High-level Maneuver Planning ‣ III-A Online Hierarchical Pipeline ‣ III METHODS ‣ From Semantic Decisions to Feasible Trajectories:Self-Evolving LLM-Guided Optimal Control for Narrow-Space Parking")
uses accumulated planning failures to revise the decision principles
retrieved during high-level planning. The knowledge base is initialized
empty—that is, zero-shot: every entry is generated automatically, with
no parking maneuvers, staging heuristics, or exemplars authored by hand.
Prompt structure and revision operations remain system-defined; only the
knowledge entries evolve through the automated revise-and-verify cycle
below. A failure dossier combines observed failure patterns with
relevant existing knowledge entries. The knowledge-revision LLM uses
this dossier to propose a knowledge delta comprising one or two
operations selected from addition, modification, deletion, and merging.
Every generated entry must record the scene it applies to together with
explicitly allowed and forbidden operations. Applying the delta to the
retained knowledge base produces a candidate version for verification.

Each candidate knowledge-base version is evaluated through the online
pipeline in
Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Fig. 1 ‣ I Introduction ‣ From Semantic Decisions to Feasible Trajectories:Self-Evolving LLM-Guided Optimal Control for Narrow-Space Parking"),
with knowledge retrieved from that version. The resulting maneuver plans
undergo grounding, sequential OCP solution, and trajectory validation.
Online feedback and replanning remain active within the prescribed
attempt budget. The vehicle model, grounding rules, optimization
settings, and acceptance criteria are held fixed during verification.

A candidate is retained as the new knowledge-base version only when
verification produces a feasible trajectory. If verification fails, the
retained version remains unchanged and the new failure evidence is
appended to the dossier to guide further revision. The online attempt
budget limits maneuver attempts during each candidate evaluation; a
separate offline revision budget limits candidate knowledge updates. The
offline loop terminates when a candidate passes verification or the
revision budget is exhausted. The loop thus grows the knowledge base
from an empty initialization into a task-specific body of parking
expertise, retaining only revisions that demonstrably preserve
feasibility.

### IV-A Experimental Setup

Scenes and vehicle. The simulation benchmark comprises parking tasks in
confined environments with nonconvex free space. Each task specifies an
initial pose and a goal pose. The car-like model has a
$`4.50\times 1.85`$ m footprint, a $`2.70`$ m wheelbase, and a minimum
rear-axle turning radius of $`5.00`$ m.

Controller and execution. All planners are evaluated on the same
simulation tasks. Their reference trajectories are tracked using a
Stanley lateral controller and a PID speed controller. Controller
settings and the execution model are shared across planners, with
acceleration and steering-rate limits enforced during execution.

Evaluation metrics. Planning success requires the generated reference
trajectory to pass trajectory validation. For SE-LLM–OCP, this is the
validation stage of the online pipeline. Closed-loop success requires
successful planning followed by execution to within $`0.50`$ m of the
goal position and $`10^{\circ}`$ of the goal heading, without obstacle
collision or departure from the drivable region. Both success rates use
all runs as the denominator. The collision rate is computed only over
runs that pass planning validation and proceed to controller execution,
including unsuccessful executions. A run is counted once if an obstacle
collision or a boundary violation occurs. Planning failures are excluded
from this denominator.

Planning-quality metrics are averaged over successful planning runs.
They comprise the number of gear shifts, mean absolute curvature
$`|\kappa|`$ ($`\mathrm{m}^{-1}`$), and maximum absolute spatial
curvature gradient $`|d\kappa/ds|`$ ($`\mathrm{m}^{-2}`$), where $`s`$
denotes arc length. The curvature-gradient entry is the mean of the
per-trajectory maxima. Terminal position and orientation errors measure
the final pose error of the planned trajectory; they are distinct from
errors after controller execution. Terminal errors are compared between
SE-LLM–OCP and HA\*+OCP and are not reported for HA\*.

Evaluation protocol and planning budget. For the planner comparison and
ablation studies, all configurations are evaluated under a consistent
experimental protocol. Independent evaluation trials are distinguished
from the planning attempts performed within each trial. All LLM
components use gpt-5.6-sol. Unless replanning is ablated, SE-LLM–OCP
performs bounded feedback-guided replanning when the current proposal
fails validation. Each planning attempt undergoes grounding,
optimization, and validation, while the knowledge-base version remains
fixed throughout each online trial.

### IV-B Experiment 1: Planning and Closed-Loop Performance

We compare SE-LLM–OCP with HA\* \[[1](#bib.bib2)\], Direct OCP, and
HA\*+OCP. HA\* searches for a kinematically admissible reference, which
HA\*+OCP then refines through trajectory optimization. Direct OCP
optimizes the maneuver without LLM-generated intermediate anchors. In
SE-LLM–OCP, pose anchors and motion modes specify the maneuver structure
before the segment OCPs are solved.

Table [I](#S4.T1 "TABLE I ‣ IV Experiments ‣ From Semantic Decisions to Feasible Trajectories:Self-Evolving LLM-Guided Optimal Control for Narrow-Space Parking")
shows that SE-LLM–OCP achieves the best overall balance between planning
feasibility and closed-loop execution. It attains $`94.7\%`$ planning
success and $`84.2\%`$ closed-loop success, while also producing
trajectories with fewer gear shifts and lower curvature-related metrics
than HA\* and HA\*+OCP. Although HA\* reaches a comparable planning
success rate, its substantially lower closed-loop success indicates that
a geometrically valid reference does not necessarily translate into an
easily executable parking maneuver. Adding OCP refinement improves the
execution performance of HA\*, but HA\*+OCP still remains clearly below
SE-LLM–OCP, suggesting that continuous trajectory refinement alone
cannot fully compensate for an unfavorable maneuver structure. Direct
OCP produces no validated trajectory in the evaluated nonconvex scenes.

Figure [3](#S4.F3 "Fig. 3 ‣ IV Experiments ‣ From Semantic Decisions to Feasible Trajectories:Self-Evolving LLM-Guided Optimal Control for Narrow-Space Parking")
explains where this advantage comes from. The key difference is that
SE-LLM–OCP determines the maneuver structure before numerical
optimization by jointly considering obstacle bypass, staging, and the
required goal approach. This allows major heading changes to be
completed in regions with more available space and leaves the confined
goal region mainly for final entry. In contrast, HA\* determines the
maneuver progressively through search, while HA\*+OCP mainly refines the
resulting reference without explicitly revisiting its earlier staging
and bypass decisions.

Consequently, SE-LLM–OCP supplies the segment OCPs with a more favorable
maneuver structure rather than relying on optimization to recover from a
poor high-level choice. The OCPs then enforce vehicle and collision
constraints within this structure. This division of roles explains why
SE-LLM–OCP outperforms the baselines not only in planning and
closed-loop success, but also in gear-shift and curvature metrics.

Failure feedback. This variant retains the same computational budget and
planning-attempt limit as the full method, but subsequent proposals
receive no feedback from earlier failures. The full method uses solver
and validation feedback to revise pose anchors and motion modes in
response to the difficulties encountered during previous attempts. With
the same budget, failure feedback increases planning success from
$`79.8\%`$ to $`94.7\%`$ and closed-loop success from $`68.4\%`$ to
$`84.2\%`$. The improvement shows the value of using failed attempts to
guide subsequent maneuver proposals.

Intermediate soft constraints. This variant solves each segment as a
separate start-to-goal problem, applying the task-terminal constraints
at every segment endpoint. Treating each intermediate anchor as a task
goal restricts where adjacent segments can connect, even when a nearby
pose could serve the same maneuver. The full method allows intermediate
poses to adjust during grounding while retaining the final goal
constraints. This increases planning success from $`83.3\%`$ to
$`94.7\%`$ and closed-loop success from $`76.3\%`$ to $`84.2\%`$,
supporting the use of soft constraints to accommodate approximate LLM
anchors. The ablated variant has a lower collision rate among executed
plans ($`8.4\%`$ versus $`11.1\%`$), while the full method completes
more tasks overall.

## V Conclusion

We presented SE-LLM–OCP, a parking framework that bridges LLM-based
maneuver reasoning and model-based trajectory optimization. The
framework uses a knowledge base evolved from scratch to guide staging
poses and motion modes, grounds approximate intermediate anchors through
adaptive soft constraints, and converts solver and validation failures
into feedback for bounded replanning. Simulation experiments in narrow,
nonconvex parking scenes demonstrated the effectiveness of this
decision–optimization interface, while a differential-drive robot with a
2D LiDAR further illustrated physical execution of the planned maneuver.
Future work will focus on repeated quantitative real-robot evaluation
and extending the framework to additional kinematic platforms and
parking environments.
````

###### Abstract

Recent advances in agentic AI have demonstrated the potential to
automate complex high-energy physics (HEP) workflows. We focus on the
regime of HEP tasks where frontier models still struggle to provide
correct and complete solutions, and introduce an open-ended optimization
task format with continuous, automatically evaluated rewards. Unlike
conventional benchmarks focused on task completion, these tasks target
reinforcement learning (RL) environments to generate agentic improvement
on state-of-the-art HEP challenges. We benchmark frontier reasoning
models on several HEP tasks and demonstrate an initial proof of concept
in which a small language model improves through RL using automated
feedback without human intervention. By connecting challenging HEP
problems with verifiable scientific rewards and RL, we aim to advance
agentic AI from reproducing established workflows to autonomously
discovering better scientific solutions, paving the way toward
self-improving scientific agents.

## 1 Introduction

Experiments at high energy particle colliders such as CERN’s Large
Hadron Collider (LHC) have significantly benefitted from modern advances
in artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) [Albertsson et
al. (2019)](#bib.bib17); [Duarte et al. (2025)](#bib.bib20); [Radovic et
al. (2018)](#bib.bib21). The task of recording, reconstructing, and
analyzing proton collision data from particle detectors like
ATLAS [ATLAS Collaboration (2008)](#bib.bib19) and CMS [CMS
Collaboration (2008)](#bib.bib18) requires complex feature extraction
and inference over $`\mathcal{O}(10^{8})`$ multi-modal inputs from
several highly granular subdetectors. AI agents can autonomously design,
optimize, and execute AI/ML workflows in HEP, allowing for
high-performance data pipelines with dramatic reductions in scientific
labor and time-to-result. Considerable recent work has explored the
adoption of agentic tools to various key tasks in collider physics, such
as physics analysis and discovery capability [Gendreau-Distler et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib1); [Moreno et al. (2026)](#bib.bib2); [He et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib3); [Diefenbacher et al. (2026)](#bib.bib4), simulation
and phenomenology [Qiu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib6); [Costa et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib7); [Plehn et al. (2026)](#bib.bib8), and to the design
and operation of detectors and accelerators [Hill and Ryoo
(2026)](#bib.bib9); [Chung et al. (2026)](#bib.bib10); [Hellert et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib11).

To fully meet their potential as autonomous scientific researchers,
agentic systems must go beyond automating established workflows to
support the development of new methods for open-ended research
questions, while ensuring interpretability, verifiability, and reliable
uncertainty quantification. Prior work has explored agentic harnesses
guided by scientific requirements [Liu and Gonski (2026)](#bib.bib5) on
close-ended tasks, but supporting robust, exploratory research remains
an open challenge. Recent results from agentic researchers in fields
with strong mathematical and theoretical requirements demonstrate the
potential [OpenAI ()](#bib.bib35); [Turturean (2026)](#bib.bib34).
However, experimental physics representing a fundamentally different set
of tasks, requiring use and understanding of data and instruments,
making this a key outstanding frontier for honing agentic tools.

Reinforcement learning (RL) environments can be used as a model
post-training approach where a model works within a closed system
representing a task of interest, and learns by taking actions, getting
feedback as rewards or penalties, and improving its strategy over many
trials [Guo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib37); [Ouyang et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib36). RL and related techniques like genetic algorithms
can adapt general-purpose large language models (LLMs) to open-ended
scientific reasoning tasks where they currently fall short. Ultimately,
this can accelerate the development of agents that perform better on the
kinds of open-ended, multi-expertise tasks that researchers actually
face.

This work focuses on experimental collider research as a compelling
setting for post-training and introducing the next generation of agentic
researchers. We formulate several standard collider physics data
analysis tasks for agentic systems and assess the limitations of
frontier models on several representative tasks, including a newly
designed task intended to probe their ability to develop analysis
strategies beyond established templates. We introduce a task format
consistent with an RL environment with continuous-valued rewards and
evaluate it across these tasks, revealing a gap and/or incapability of
several frontier models on the most challenging ones.

To study the use of post-training to fill this gap, we investigate
self-improvement by combining genetic-evolution and reinforcement
learning post-training of a small open-weight model. We observe
improvements in both execution reliability and the physics quality of
the resulting solutions, including fewer crashes, more physically
motivated and robust analysis strategies, and reduced reliance on reward
exploitation or trivial solutions. Importantly, without additional
domain-specific guidance, the improved model achieves performance
comparable to out-of-the-box frontier models on held-out datasets for
the same tasks. These results demonstrate the strong potential of
task-specific self-improvement in small models and motivate exploring
whether these gains can transfer across tasks, scale to more capable
open-weight models, and support progress on open research questions in
HEP and other scientific domains.

## 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks

For scientific applications of agentic tools, correctly executing an
analysis workflow is necessary but insufficient. Scientific value
depends on sensitivity, precision, robustness, and correct intermediate
reasoning stages, all meaning that “correct" workflows can differ
substantially in physics performance. Further, because research tasks
are open-ended, binary success criteria is also insufficient; instead,
evaluation must distinguish among correct solutions and reward
meaningful improvements. We therefore require agents to submit
executable scripts and score them with continuous, objective-aligned
rewards on hidden datasets, measuring both reusability and performance
gains beyond baseline methods.

With these considerations in mind, we define physics-motivated rewards
and use them to evaluate several frontier and open-weight models on six
collider physics tasks. Each task is specified by a physics objective,
development data, a deliverable, and a scoring rule, leaving agents free
to develop their methods. We provide a minimal execution interface
without a domain-specific harness, intended to assess the models’
out-of-the-box ability to develop physics analysis methods with minimal
external scaffolding. Each model autonomously writes, executes, and
revises its scripts within a single session, guided only by the task
description, development data, and feedback from its own analysis and
executions. To assess reusability and physics performance, we execute
submitted scripts on hidden datasets in a fresh working directory under
a fixed CPU budget. Hidden-data scores are used solely for evaluation
and are not returned to the agents.

#### Physics-driven reward functions.

We align each reward with the physics goal through the corresponding
metric such as discovery significance, signal sensitivity,
reconstruction accuracy, background rejection or distribution fidelity.
We reward physics performance directly, without normalizing it to
literature results or in-house baselines, which serve only as reference
points. This avoids embedding prior expectations about achievable
performance in the reward and leaves room for unrestricted
methodological exploration. Since there is no single correct solution,
the best-known human result does not impose an upper bound on the
reward.

#### Continuous scoring.

We quantify partial progress by mapping the metric to $`R\in[0,1]`$ with
a monotonic, saturating function that also provides a learning signal.
For example, in the task HiggsML, which is detailed in
Sec [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 HiggsML ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning"),
the reward $`R=\max\!\big(0,\,1-\exp(-(\mathrm{AMS}-2.5))\big)`$ uses
the approximate median significance of the 2014
challenge [Adam-Bourdarios et al. (2015)](#bib.bib12): the untuned
benchmark earns 0.50 and the winning entry 0.73, while improved
selections earn higher rewards.

#### Multi-objective construction.

We combine critical physics criteria multiplicatively, so that failure
on a required criterion gives zero reward to reject cheating or
non-physical solution. For the anomaly detection task in
Sec [2.3](#S2.SS3 "2.3 LHC Olympics anomaly detection ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning"),
the reward $`R=S^{3/4}\,Q^{1/4}`$ balances signal sensitivity $`S`$
against mass-sculpting control $`Q`$, while unfolding and fast
simulation tasks in
Sec [2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Unfolding ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")
and
[2.4](#S2.SS4 "2.4 CaloChallenge ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning"),
combine distribution metrics with guards against shortcuts. Tracking
task in
Sec [2.5](#S2.SS5 "2.5 TrackML ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")
uses the official score in the publication; across tasks, missing,
invalid, crashed or timed-out submissions score zero.

#### Model evaluation

To assess the out-of-the-box behavior of models under this reward
framework, we adopt a simplified, common autonomous protocol with no
prescribed physics workflow. Each model receives the task description
and access to a shell for a single 30-minute session. We score
submissions on hidden data to obtain reward trajectories, without
exposing these hidden scores to the agents. Unless otherwise indicated,
we report results for the final submissions, with trajectories shown in
Figures [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.1 HiggsML ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")-[7](#S2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 2.6 Diphoton resonance search ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning").
Transcript review confirmed that the models used only permitted inputs.
We evaluate Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Haiku 5.5,
GPT-6.1-sol, GPT-6-luna, Gemini 4 Argon, Grok 4.7, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash,
GLM-5.3 Prime, and Qwen3.8-Max Prime; not every model is evaluated on
every task due to resources budget.

Five tasks are drawn from public challenges that are well documented in
the literature and may be represented in LLM training data
(Sections [2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 HiggsML ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")- [2.5](#S2.SS5 "2.5 TrackML ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")).
The sixth is a newly designed model-agnostic diphoton peak search,
intended to probe zero-shot performance and examined in detail in
Section [2.6](#S2.SS6 "2.6 Diphoton resonance search ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning").
This evaluation reveals two distinct patterns: convergence toward
human-level performance on well-established “easy" tasks, and
substantial variation in performance on zero-shot state-of-the-art
tasks. Details of each evaluation are discussed in the following
sections.

### 2.1 HiggsML

HiggsML [Adam-Bourdarios et al. (2015)](#bib.bib12) evaluates event
selection over simulated datasets to identify a $`H\to\tau\tau`$ signal
with respect to background as a binary classification problem. Agents
select from the supplied events, and we score their selection by its
approximate median significance (AMS) to measure search sensitivity.
Figure [1](#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 2.1 HiggsML ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")
shows the reward as a function of time spent on the problem for the
HiggsML task. All models considered arrive at human-level performance,
and all use the same approach: boosted trees with physics features and
reach 0.708–0.732, near the 2014 Kaggle challenge winner (0.729). We
conclude that simple event classification in collider physics is easily
achieved within the existing capabilities of both frontier and other
models.

Figure 1: Reward over time on HiggsML task. Dashed references are the
Kaggle 2014 winner and in-house baseline solution using gradient
boosting model.

### 2.2 Unfolding

Unfolding is the procedure of inferring the underlying truth
(particle-level) distributions from detector-level observations by
correcting for detector resolution, inefficiencies, and acceptance
effects. The unfolding task [Huetsch et al. (2024)](#bib.bib16) tests
the recovery of particle-level distributions on the OmniFold $`Z`$+jets
benchmark [Andreassen et al. (2020)](#bib.bib25). Given paired particle-
and detector-level simulation and detector-level data from a different
generator, agents deliver event weights. We assess agreement with hidden
particle-level distributions in six substructure observables and their
correlations.

As shown in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.2 Unfolding ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning"),
all five models converge on iterated OmniFold reweighting method,
independently implementing the method and reaching 0.793–0.841, above
the one-step baseline (0.700). However, none reach the truth-informed
oracle solution. We conclude that unfolding is doable by LLM tools to
best-achieved human-level performance.

Figure 2: Reward over time on unfolding task. Dashed references are a
truth-informed oracle and in-house baseline solution using one-step
reweighting.

### 2.3 LHC Olympics anomaly detection

LHC Olympics [Kasieczka et al. (2021a)](#bib.bib13) tests the
identification of an unknown resonance in dijet events using a supplied
QCD background sample. Agents assign anomaly scores, with a reward that
balances signal sensitivity against background sculpting. A resonance is
a new particle visible as a localized excess in the invariant-mass
spectrum of its decay products. Sculpting occurs when the selection
itself creates a bump in the smooth background spectrum, resulting in
possible false positives or indication of a signal where there is none.

Figure 3: Reward over time on LHC Olympics anomaly detection task. The
dashed reference is an in-house baseline solution using
CWoLa-lite[Metodiev et al. (2017)](#bib.bib22); [Collins et al.
(2018b)](#bib.bib23) classifier.

Figure [3](#S2.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 2.3 LHC Olympics anomaly detection ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")
shows the model performance over time for the anomaly detection task,
revealing substantial differences in validation strategy and
performance, with scores across eight models ranging from 0.203 to
0.911. The best-performing session trains against the supplied QCD
background in the development signal region and validates on weak-signal
and signal-free samples, approximating the background conditions in the
test data. Other sessions train classifiers using strictly sideband data
but choose suboptimal thresholds based on the development data; their
estimated scores exceed the hidden-data scores by up to 0.55. Three
models score below the in-house CWoLa baseline (0.436), indicating
difficulty in implementing or developing effective methods. It worth
noting that even the highest-scoring solution has an important
limitation: it relies on the supplied development sample to model the
background in the signal region, effectively treating it as a reliable
Monte Carlo prediction. Its strong benchmark performance therefore does
not establish robustness in broader applications, where mismodeling of
the simulated background can compromise the analysis. This limitation
also motivates the zero-shot task introduced in
Section [2.6](#S2.SS6 "2.6 Diphoton resonance search ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning"),
which considers anomaly detection on other final states with
deliberately less accurate Monte Carlo background modeling.

### 2.4 CaloChallenge

CaloChallenge [Krause et al. (2025)](#bib.bib14) task tests generative
modelling of Geant4 photon showers in a voxelised calorimeter. Agents
construct a surrogate from supplied showers; we assess its distribution
fidelity on hidden data, with guards against shortcuts.

Figure 4: Score over time per model on CaloChallenge task; dashed lines
are published solution scored with the reward-based task metric.

Model performance as shown in
Figure [4](#S2.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 2.4 CaloChallenge ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")
varies widely on the CaloChallenge task, with final rewards ranging from
0.000 to 0.739. Gemini 4 Argon (0.739) and Claude Sonnet 5.5 (0.658)
achieve performance comparable to or above that of published generative
models (0.64–0.72), while GPT-6.1-sol scores slightly below this range
(0.603). The two best-performing sessions use per-energy Gaussian
mixtures in a rank-Gaussianised voxel space (Gemini) and a small
conditional diffusion model (Sonnet), respectively, both followed by
quantile calibration to match the training marginals. By contrast, the
remaining five models score below 0.32 and rely on Gaussian copulas that
inadequately capture the 368-dimensional correlation structure, allowing
the evaluation classifier to readily distinguish generated showers from
reference samples. The high-dimensional and generative nature of this
task reveal inconsistencies among frontier models, as well as room for
improvement even in the state-of-the-art.

### 2.5 TrackML

The TrackML task [Amrouche et al. (2021)](#bib.bib15) is based on a
simplified LHC tracking scenario in which each collision produces
roughly 10,000 charged-particle tracks (approximately helicoidal
trajectories emerging from near the interaction point) that leave about
10 high-precision 3D hits each. The core pattern recognition task is to
associate the resulting  100,000 space points into their corresponding
tracks, a problem where traditional tracking approaches can suffer a
combinatorial explosion in CPU time as occupancy increases. Agents group
the supplied hits into tracks, which we assess with the official
double-majority score.

Figure 5: Score over time per model on TrackML; dashed lines are the
2018 leaderboard scored with the reward-based task metric.

This task also represents a genuine challenge and feasibility frontier
for the models considered, as shown in
Figure [5](#S2.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 2.5 TrackML ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning").
Track reconstruction remains below published performance across models,
with final rewards ranging from 0.000 to 0.770. The best-performing
session, GPT-6.1-sol (0.770), builds a seeding-and-road-following
pipeline with a learned candidate-quality model, structurally similar to
the 2018 winning solutions (0.86–0.92) but substantially less refined.
Gemini, Opus, and Sonnet (0.53–0.61) converge on a simpler approach used
in public competition kernels: clustering hits after unrolling candidate
helices over a scan of curvature values. The remaining four models
exhaust the time budget before producing functional tracking algorithm
and receive negligible rewards.

### 2.6 Diphoton resonance search

The final task is developed explicitly for this study and is discussed
in-depth, as it is subsequently used for a post-training
proof-of-concept. The resonance search task asks agents to construct a
reusable search that identifies zero, one or two narrow resonances and
reconstructs their masses. We provide full event kinematics for
simulated proton–proton collisions with two photons and up to three jets
in $`m_{\gamma\gamma}=105`$–$`145`$ GeV. Any method is allowed,
including anomaly detection style weakly supervised classifiers or
direct background estimation from each dataset’s sidebands, provided
that programs read boundaries, luminosity and selections from the
dataset’s metadata.

The development sample supplies about 800 000 events, ground truth,
weighted signal and background templates, and an auxiliary continuum
sample, intended to be a low-precision simulation. The twelve hidden
datasets use a different simulation setup, with four datasets per
resonance count
(Fig. [6](#S2.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ 2.6 Diphoton resonance search ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")).
The datasets combine continuum background events generated with
MadGraph5 interfaced with Pythia8, gluon-fusion Higgs and
heavy-vector-triplet (HVT) signals, and detector simulation using a
custom Delphes card based on the ATLAS geometry, with configurations
chosen to avoid directly reproducing published results. Each dataset
contains approximately 159 000 continuum background events in the signal
region of 118.5–132.5 GeV, alongside injected resonances with widths of
1.35–1.50 GeV. The 125 GeV Higgs resonance contributes approximately
3 100 signal events, corresponding to a local significance of
$`S/\sqrt{B}=11.7`$ within a $`\pm 2\sigma`$ window. The HVT resonances
contribute 380–1 370 events, with local significances of 1.4–5.5,
placing most hidden signals near or below the conventional discovery
threshold.

Figure 6: Three of the twelve hidden datasets: no resonance; one at
125 GeV; and two at 125 GeV and 129 GeV. Top: diphoton mass spectra,
with the signal region 118.5–132.5 GeV shaded and an illustrative
exponential-quadratic sideband fit. Bottom: residuals from that fit with
a $`\pm 1\sigma`$ statistical band. Dashed lines mark true masses
(hidden information).

This task is designed to encompass several key challenges. The search is
complicated by weak signals, overlapping resonances, and uncertainties
in background estimation. The non-Higgs signals have expected local
significances of $`S/\sqrt{B}=1.4`$–$`5.5`$ within a $`\pm 2\sigma`$
mass window. Background estimation relies on fitting the sidebands and
interpolating into the signal region, introducing uncertainties that can
substantially alter the apparent significance of a resonance. For
example, a nominal $`3\sigma`$ excess at 121 GeV is reduced to
$`1.2\sigma`$ after fitting, whereas a $`1.4\sigma`$ excess at 124 GeV
increases to $`2.6\sigma`$. The resonance widths range from
$`\sigma=1.35`$ to $`1.50`$ GeV, with two pairs separated by only 2 GeV,
further complicating their resolution. Additionally, the 125 GeV Higgs
resonance is present in only five of the eight datasets containing
injected signals, precluding any assumption of its presence. This
setting differs from conventional diphoton analyses and existing
publications, which typically target a known Higgs mass and employ
predefined signal templates and they are not generally optimal in this
task.

The reward is designed to measure both resonance counting and mass
reconstruction. We match claimed masses to true resonances one-to-one to
maximise total credit, with each matched pair earning

which decreases with mass error and vanishes beyond $`w`$. For
$`n_{\mathrm{true}}`$ true and $`n_{\mathrm{claimed}}`$ claimed peaks,
the soft F1 score $`2\sum c/(n_{\mathrm{true}}+n_{\mathrm{claimed}})`$
rewards the correct count and accurate masses while penalising false
claims and missed signals. A background-only dataset scores 1 for no
claim and 0 otherwise. All the scores are averaged across all datasets.
Consequently, always claiming no resonance scores 0.333, while always
claiming 125 GeV scores 0.250. Our reference program fits an
exponential-quadratic background to the sidebands, scans a Gaussian
template, subtracts the leading excess and scans again. It achieves
0.689 overall, with 1.000, 0.599 and 0.468 across the resonance-count
groups.

shows the reward as a function of time spent for several frontier
models. No model saturates the reward; final rewards range from 0.487 to
0.782, and only GPT-6.1-sol (0.782) exceeds the reference. The
open-weight Qwen3.8-27B, the only non-frontier model in this comparison,
achieves a reward of 0.516 in the standard 30 minute session. To
facilitate a more meaningful comparison with frontier models, we
additionally provide it with reward feedback evaluated on an independent
development dataset, separate from the held-out dataset used for final
reporting. This additional feedback enables the model to iteratively
refine its solution and narrow the performance gap with frontier models,
although a noticeable gap remains.

Notably, all sessions produce valid programs with background models,
Gaussian-excess scans and significance thresholds. However, the actual
correctness of the achieved solution varies considerably: six of the
eight programs make no false claim on the background-only datasets, yet
all lose reward on the one-resonance group (0.214–0.730) and especially
the two-resonance group (0.235–0.616). We identify four failure modes:

1.  1.
    *Sequential searches miss overlapping peaks.* Most programs search
    for the strongest peak before examining the residual, sometimes
    imposing a minimum separation of 2–2.5 GeV. Consequently, five of
    the eight never report a second mass on any two-resonance dataset,
    while the others usually place it more than 1 GeV from the true
    mass.
2.  2.
    *Aggressive cuts produce false discoveries.* One session lowers
    thresholds into the tails of its background-only trials to recover
    sensitivity to pairs. Although the final program reports two masses
    on every two-resonance dataset, it also makes a false
    background-only claim, exposing the sensitivity–purity trade-off.
3.  3.
    *Limited exploration.* Three sessions stop early despite known
    weaknesses. Several programs also retain development widths or
    background templates despite the broader hidden peaks; GPT-6.1-sol
    instead fits widths freely within 0.65–2.3 GeV and performs best.
4.  4.
    *Technical failures.* Two sessions overwrite working submissions
    with broken programs before repairing them. In two sessions,
    histogramming or fitting bugs delay the first valid submission until
    8–13 minutes.

The transcripts therefore point to limitations in exploration and
self-verification under the physics objective, even though the methods,
toy-based calibration and two-peak logic already appear. This motivates
the study in
Section [3](#S3 "3 Self-Improvement of LLM Models on Collider Physics Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning"),
where we test whether the model can self-improve its search from the
reward alone.

## 3 Self-Improvement of LLM Models on Collider Physics Tasks

We combine genetic evolution and reinforcement learning to improve the
diphoton resonance search task in
Section [2.6](#S2.SS6 "2.6 Diphoton resonance search ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")
without prescribing a solution method. The procedure uses only the task,
its public development data and reward, with no human-written solution
or harness encoding a physics workflow. We use Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507
(4B) and Qwen3.8-27B (27B), with base weights and thinking disabled, to
explore the potential of the proposed approach for future frontier-level
open-weight models. This study focuses on improving the models’
intrinsic capabilities rather than enhancing performance through agentic
workflows or external harnesses, which are beyond its scope.
Complementary approaches based on agentic workflows using harness are
discussed in Refs. [Liu and Gonski (2026)](#bib.bib5); [Gendreau-Distler
et al. (2025)](#bib.bib1).

Optimization is performed using synthetic datasets constructed from the
development sample, while final performance is evaluated on the twelve
hidden datasets described in
Section [2.6](#S2.SS6 "2.6 Diphoton resonance search ‣ 2 Model Benchmarking on Collider Physics Research Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning"),
which are strictly excluded from optimization. Detailed experimental
settings and prompts are provided in Appendix
Table [3](#A0.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")
and Appendix
Section [A](#A1 "Appendix A Prompts used in the self-evolution pipeline ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning"),
respectively.

### 3.1 Genetic algorithms

Sampling complete programs from a frozen LLM exposes substantial
variation in both execution reliability and physics performance
(Fig. [8](#S3.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 3.1 Genetic algorithms ‣ 3 Self-Improvement of LLM Models on Collider Physics Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")).
We execute and score each program, finding that rewards depend strongly
on the sampled analysis and model size: some programs fail technically,
while others run but do not improve the physics score. For the 27B
model, the 32 initial programs ("base") average 0.116 on the twelve
hidden datasets; 15 crash, 3 contain no code, and only one exceeds the
trivial baseline, reaching 0.578. The 4B model averages 0.013 with no
program above the baseline, motivating selection and revision rather
than sampling alone.

We guide evolution by ranking programs on synthetic reward and returning
selected programs and execution results to the same model for analysis
and revision. Self-sampling with execution feedback first constructs a
pool of candidate solutions using synthetic datasets with stronger
injected signals, allowing the model to bootstrap its exploration from
simple search strategies. This procedure does not require
domain-specific knowledge, as the same approach can be applied in
general. We test revised programs on fresh synthetic data to prevent
reuse of revealed resonance masses, then evolve a population of 32 over
20 generations through mutation and crossover. Mutation revises a
selected parent using its execution results, while crossover combines
selected parents; fitness is measured on two fixed synthetic variants at
real signal strength. High-fitness programs survive alongside random
survivors to preserve exploration.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Prompts used in the self-evolution pipeline ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")
documents the prompts for both stages.

performance improves more slowly, leveling off at 0.41 for the selected

accompany rising synthetic fitness, indicating overfitting to the
synthetic development dataset; the highest-fitness program nevertheless
remains stable from generation 14.

a reward of 0.644 against 0.578 for the best of 32 initial samples. The
gain therefore reflects improvement beyond initial selection, with
variation between runs.

Although the 4B model improves its reward on synthetic datasets from
0.33 to 0.55, its selected programs fail to generalize to the twelve
hidden datasets, consistently achieving only the trivial baseline reward
of 0.333 from generation 6 onward. These programs rely on
threshold-based searches over smoothed histograms without explicitly
modeling the background, making them sensitive to the peak widths used
in synthetic training. In contrast, the 27B model independently
discovers a more robust strategy based on background estimation,
highlighting a qualitative difference in the models’ ability to develop
generalizable solutions rather than merely optimize the training reward.
At sufficient capacity, however, reward-guided evolution already enables
a useful search with the frozen base model.

### 3.2 Reinforcement learning

Evolution improves the search at a substantial cost: the 27B
self-sampling and evolution stages executed about 2300 programs, 1300 of
them in the 20 generations, and used about 2.9 node-hours on 4-GPU nodes
plus an executor node. A new task would require the search to start
again. We therefore update the model weights with the aim of reducing
the iterations needed on similar and even new tasks.

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) first aligns the model to its best evolved
programs through maximum-likelihood training, with each training pair
containing the task prompt and one of the model’s own programs selected
by synthetic fitness. Across 32 single shots on the twelve hidden
datasets, the 27B mean rises from 0.116 to 0.342, while the number
scoring above 0.5 increases from 1 in 32 to 10 in 32
(Table [2](#S3.T2 "Table 2 ‣ 3.2 Reinforcement learning ‣ 3 Self-Improvement of LLM Models on Collider Physics Tasks ‣ Reward-Driven Collider Physics Challenges for Self-Improving Agentic AI via Reinforcement Learning")),
showing that the policy generates useful programs more frequently. SFT
nevertheless remains below the programs it imitates. Maximum-likelihood
training rewards reproduction of target code without directly evaluating
the physics quality of generated programs; it therefore spreads
imitation across the target family rather than concentrating on its best
members. Execution success is 0.78 after SFT, and successful programs
average 0.42, indicating that both technical failures and physics
performance limit the policy.

We further apply Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to recover
performance by optimizing the task reward directly. For each prompt, we
execute a group of 8 sampled programs on synthetic datasets, reinforcing
those above the group mean and suppressing those below it. By GRPO step
30, the 27B single-shot mean on the twelve hidden datasets recovers to
0.457 \[0.40, 0.50\], improving on SFT by $`+0.12`$ \[$`+0.09`$,
$`+0.14`$\] and on the base model by $`+0.34`$ \[$`+0.28`$, $`+0.39`$\].
Most recovery comes from the background-only group, whose score rises
from 0.64 to 0.94, while the one-resonance score increases from 0.20 to
0.26 and the two-resonance score does not improve (0.19 to 0.18). The
gain includes better physics performance as well as fewer failures.

The combined procedure therefore uses evolution to discover useful
components and reward-based training to make them more frequent. As
demonstrated by the 4B model, improvements in execution performance
alone are insufficient when the generated programs lack a sound physics
methodology, highlighting the limitations imposed by model capacity on
intrinsic physics reasoning. We reduce the number of trainable
parameters and memory requirements by employing low-rank adaptation
(LoRA) [Hu et al. (2022)](#bib.bib33), while keeping the pretrained
model weights frozen throughout training.

## 4 Conclusions

We introduce a suite of open-ended collider physics challenges with
continuous, automatically verifiable rewards, designed to both evaluate
frontier AI models and provide environments for their improvement.
Across six representative HEP tasks, we demonstrate the capabilities of
current frontier models while identifying substantial room for
improvement on challenging scientific problems. As an initial proof of
concept, we demonstrate that reward-driven evolution and training can
improve an open-weight model and encourage the development of more
robust physics analysis strategies without human-provided solutions.

Looking ahead, we aim to expand this framework to a broader range of
open scientific problems and more capable models, establishing
increasingly challenging environments for autonomous learning and
evaluation. We envision a shift from AI agents that automate existing
scientific workflows toward agents that can independently develop, test,
and improve scientific methodologies. By combining the rich challenges
of fundamental physics with verifiable rewards, this approach offers a
promising path toward self-improving AI for scientific discovery.

### A.1 System prompt and hint-free task prompt

Every episode starts from a two-message conversation: the system prompt
SYSTEM_EN and one user message rendered from the template TASK_EN; both
are defined in peak_env.py, lines 123–182. The model answers with one
python code block, which is written to search.py and executed in a bwrap
sandbox on three freshly staged datasets (one with zero, one with one
and one with two injected resonances); the reward is the mean of the
three per-dataset soft-F1 scores. The system prompt is:

Task: build a reusable search for narrow diphoton resonances.

Each simulated proton-proton collision contains two photons and up to
three jets.

A dataset is a full diphoton mass spectrum; its data.json declares a
signal region

inside that spectrum. Decide how many narrow resonances the signal
region holds

(zero, one or two) and report their masses. Any method is allowed, as
long as it runs

on a CPU within the time limit and reads only its inputs.

Development data, in the directory dev-b/ next to your program (resolve
the path

from your program’s own location, not from the working directory):

- dev-b/data.root: a complete rehearsal dataset, the full spectrum
(785,987 events);

it holds one injected resonance (ggH at 125.0 GeV).

- dev-b/data.json: its metadata (same format as the datasets you will be
run on).

- dev-b/signal_template.root: a weighted simulated sample of a narrow
resonance

(branch ‘weight‘; the weights sum to the expected signal yield).

- dev-b/bkg_ideal.root: a weighted simulated background sample over the
whole mass

window (branch ‘weight‘; the weights sum to the expected background
yield).

The datasets you will be run on are not these files: their background
shape and

yield, signal strengths and signal region differ. Read every boundary,
luminosity

and selection from the dataset’s own data.json at run time and hard-code
nothing

from the development data.

Event format: a ROOT file with a TTree named events and 18 numeric
branches:

pho1_pt pho1_eta pho1_phi pho2_pt pho2_eta pho2_phi

jet1_pt jet1_eta jet1_phi jet1_m jet2_pt jet2_eta jet2_phi jet2_m
jet3_pt jet3_eta jet3_phi jet3_m

pt and mass in GeV, eta dimensionless, phi in radians. Photons are
massless:

m_yy = sqrt(2 \* pt1 \* pt2 \* (cosh(eta1 - eta2) - cos(phi1 - phi2))).

Missing jets are exact zero quadruplets. The datasets are unweighted
event lists;

dev-b/signal_template.root and dev-b/bkg_ideal.root carry a ‘weight‘
branch.

Deliverable: one Python program, search.py, taking two positional
arguments:

python search.py DATASET_DIRECTORY OUTPUT_JSON

DATASET_DIRECTORY contains data.root and data.json for one full-spectrum
dataset.

Write OUTPUT_JSON as {"peaks": \[{"mass_GeV": 126.4}\]} with zero, one
or two finite

masses inside that dataset’s half-open signal region; {"peaks": \[\]}
abstains.

Scoring: for each dataset the claims are matched one-to-one to the true
resonances;

credit decays with the mass error on a 0.5 GeV scale and vanishes beyond
1 GeV, and

the dataset score is a soft F1 over true and claimed peaks. A
background-only dataset

scores 1 for an empty claim and 0 for any claim. The program is run
independently on

3 datasets holding zero, one or two resonances; a crash, timeout or
invalid

output scores 0 for that dataset.

Constraints: CPU only, 2 threads, 60 seconds of wall-clock time per call

including reading the data; each call runs in a fresh working directory
with no state

carried between calls; no network; available libraries: numpy, pandas,
scipy,

scikit-learn and uproot 5 (read branches with

‘uproot.open(path)\["events"\].arrays(\[...\], library="np")‘; there is
no PyROOT ‘ROOT‘

module and no uproot3/uproot4). Prompt variant: 0.

###### Abstract

Supervised fine-tuning treats the expert demonstration or gold response
as a one-hot target, suppressing valid alternative continuations that
the model would otherwise assign probability to. We propose
Gold-Conditioned Distillation (GCD), a method that turns expert
demonstrations into model-native supervision. GCD uses one model as both
teacher and student: the teacher conditions on a prompt containing the
gold response in context, the student conditions on the original prompt
as at inference, and the student is trained to minimize the forward KL
divergence to the teacher’s next-token distribution at each gold
response token. This lets the model use its prior knowledge to teach
itself the new task: correctness is anchored by the gold response, while
the soft target preserves plausible alternatives. Across various
training tasks on Qwen and Llama models, GCD improves new-task accuracy
over SFT and generally reduces degradation on general benchmarks. Code
is available on [GitHub](https://github.com/WenxuanZhu1103/gcd).

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are typically trained once on a massive
corpus and then deployed as static artifacts. In practice, however, the
world does not hold still. New facts emerge, new tools appear, new
domains demand attention—and the model must incorporate this knowledge
without forgetting what it already knows. This is the post-training
problem, and for modern LLMs it remains a major open challenge.

The standard approach, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), trains the model to
imitate the expert demonstrations token by token. This procedure is
simple, stable, and widely used. However, it also imposes a rigid
supervision interface: every demonstrated token is treated as a one-hot
target, and every non-demonstrated token is treated as equally wrong.

For language models, this supervision format can be unnecessarily sharp.
A demonstration tells the model which response is correct, but it does
not imply that every continuation supported by the model’s distribution
should be driven to zero. This distinction is important in continual
post-training, where a model must acquire new facts, tools, domains, or
response formats without unnecessarily destroying the distributional
structure that supports its existing capabilities. In this setting, the
bottleneck is not only the amount or quality of post-training data, but
also the interface through which demonstrations are converted into
learning signals.

Recently, [Shenfeld et al. (2026)](#bib.bib1) proposed Self-Distillation
Fine-Tuning (SDFT), which exploits the in-context learning ability of
LLMs for continual post-training. The core idea is that conditioning a
model on the gold response in its prompt produces a useful supervision
signal. The gold-response-aware model serves as a _teacher_ and the
gold-response-unaware model as a _student_; SDFT then samples on-policy
rollouts from the student in real time and trains the student to match
the teacher via KL divergence. However, SDFT requires the student to
roll out a completion at every training step, which increases training
time. Moreover, early in training or on unfamiliar domains these
rollouts are often incorrect, and because the teacher and student see
different contexts, the teacher’s distribution over an erroneous
sequence may yield a noisy signal. More fundamentally, our experiments
suggest that SDFT is not effective at learning in a novel domain; like
reinforcement learning in LLMs, it is better suited for domains where
the model already has some prior capabilities [Yue et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib7); [Wen et al. (2025)](#bib.bib8).

Inspired by SDFT’s core insight that in-context conditioning on the gold
response elicits a usable soft target, we propose Gold-Conditioned
Distillation (GCD), a simple modification that sidesteps the issues
above by replacing the student’s rollout with the gold response from the
dataset. The teacher conditions on a _privileged_ prompt that exposes
the gold response, while the student conditions only on the question;
the student is then trained to match the teacher’s token-level
distribution evaluated on the _fixed_ gold response from the dataset via
forward KL divergence loss.

We evaluate GCD under two LLM fine-tuning settings across different
models, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [Grattafiori et al. (2024)](#bib.bib46)
and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct [Yang et al. (2024a)](#bib.bib47) as well as
Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base [Yang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib45). The
first tests adaptation in partially known domains. The second training
setting tests learning new knowledge from scratch in domains where the
model has near-zero initial accuracy. Across both settings, GCD offers a
favorable stability–plasticity trade-off: it often improves new-task
acquisition over SFT and usually preserves general capabilities at a
comparable or slightly better level.

Our contributions are summarized as:

- •
  We propose Gold-Conditioned Distillation (GCD), a simple yet effective
  offline self-distillation method for learning from expert
  demonstrations, achieving strong average new-task learning performance
  while modestly improving or maintaining retention in most settings.
- •
  We show that GCD improves held-out generalization in partially known
  domains and usually preserves general capabilities better than SFT.
  Token-level analysis shows that the gains come from preserving
  structured probability mass on model-plausible alternatives rather
  than from simple label smoothing.

## 2 Related Work

#### Catastrophic forgetting and continual learning.

Catastrophic forgetting occurs when training on new data reduces a
model’s performance on previously learned data or tasks [Kirkpatrick et
al. (2017)](#bib.bib13); [McCloskey and Cohen (1989)](#bib.bib9);
[Parisi et al. (2019)](#bib.bib34); [Van de Ven et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib35); [Lange et al. (2019)](#bib.bib66); [Wang et al.
(2024a)](#bib.bib67). Recent studies show that forgetting is a practical
issue in LLMs. Continual instruction tuning can hurt domain knowledge,
reasoning, reading comprehension, general ability, and instruction
following [Luo et al. (2025)](#bib.bib18); [Wang et al.
(2023b)](#bib.bib20); [Zhang et al. (2023)](#bib.bib19); [Li and Lee
(2024)](#bib.bib68). Other work studies the causes of forgetting in LLM
tuning and relates it to loss-landscape properties [Li et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib21); [Ren et al. (2024)](#bib.bib36). Continual learning
aims to adapt models over time while preserving earlier capabilities.
Classical methods are often grouped into replay-based approaches
[Buzzega et al. (2020)](#bib.bib12); [Rebuffi et al.
(2017)](#bib.bib10); [Shin et al. (2017)](#bib.bib11),
regularization-based approaches [Kirkpatrick et al. (2017)](#bib.bib13);
[Li and Hoiem (2017)](#bib.bib15); [Zenke et al. (2017)](#bib.bib14),
and architecture-based approaches [Serra et al. (2018)](#bib.bib17);
[Mallya and Lazebnik (2018)](#bib.bib16). These approaches are harder to
apply directly to LLM fine-tuning: replay may require unavailable past
data, regularization can add storage and computation costs, and
architecture-based methods often require task-specific components.
Recent methods also adapt replay and parameter-efficient tuning to LLMs,
including synthetic rehearsal [Huang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib22); [Wang
et al. (2024b)](#bib.bib37), orthogonal LoRA subspaces [Qiao and Mahdavi
(2024)](#bib.bib38); [Ren et al. (2024)](#bib.bib36); [Wang et al.
(2023a)](#bib.bib23), and analyses of why LoRA can forget less [Biderman
et al. (2024)](#bib.bib24). These methods show progress, but they still
involve trade-offs between learning new data, preserving old abilities,
and adding extra data, computation, or model components.

#### Self-distillation.

Knowledge distillation trains a student model to match the behavior of a
teacher model [Romero et al. (2015)](#bib.bib59); [Zagoruyko and
Komodakis (2016)](#bib.bib26); [Wang and Yoon (2020)](#bib.bib60); [Zhao
et al. (2022)](#bib.bib61); [Yang et al. (2022)](#bib.bib62); [Chi et
al. (2023)](#bib.bib63); [Wei et al. (2024)](#bib.bib64); [Song and
Zheng (2026)](#bib.bib65); [Cho and Hariharan (2019)](#bib.bib53);
[Hinton et al. (2015)](#bib.bib25); [Mirzadeh et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib54); [Park et al. (2019)](#bib.bib55); [Stanton et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib58); [Tung and Mori (2019)](#bib.bib56); [Wang et al.
(2025a)](#bib.bib49); [Zhou et al. (2021)](#bib.bib57). The teacher
signal can be a soft output distribution, intermediate features,
attention maps, or contrastive relations [Gou et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib28); [Guo et al. (2023)](#bib.bib48); [Ojha et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib50); [Sun et al. (2024)](#bib.bib52); [Tian et al.
(2019)](#bib.bib27); [Zagoruyko and Komodakis (2016)](#bib.bib26); [Wang
et al. (2025b)](#bib.bib51). Self-distillation is a special case of
knowledge distillation in which the teacher is the same model, a
previous version of the model, or the same model under a different input
condition. Recent LLM distillation methods study compression [Jiao et
al. (2020)](#bib.bib39); [Liu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib40), capability
transfer [Jiang et al. (2025)](#bib.bib41); [Li et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib42); [Mukherjee et al. (2023)](#bib.bib33), and
self-improvement [Song et al. (2025)](#bib.bib43); [Yang et al.
(2024b)](#bib.bib32); [Zhang et al. (2024)](#bib.bib44). Since LLMs
generate sequences, both the distillation objective and the training
distribution matter: MiniLLM studies reverse-KL distillation for LLMs
[Gu et al. (2024)](#bib.bib29), while Generalized Knowledge Distillation
trains on student-generated sequences with teacher feedback to reduce
the gap between training and inference [Agarwal et al.
(2024)](#bib.bib30). Closely related to our work, context distillation
trains a model to internalize behavior that appears when useful context,
examples, or reasoning steps are provided [Snell et al.
(2022)](#bib.bib31). [Yang et al. (2024b)](#bib.bib32) use
model-generated data to reduce the distribution gap in LLM fine-tuning,
and SDFT [Shenfeld et al. (2026)](#bib.bib1) uses the current model
conditioned on a demonstration as its own teacher and generates
on-policy completions for training. Our method keeps the same idea of a
context-conditioned self-teacher, but uses the gold response as the
completion, avoiding online generation and sampling noise.

## 3 Method

![Refer to caption](2610.25497v1/GCD_pipeline_v1.png)

Figure 1: Overview of Gold-Conditioned Distillation (GCD). The student
predicts the next token from the query $`x`$ and the gold-response
prefix $`y_{<t}`$; the teacher predicts it from a privileged context
that additionally contains the full gold response $`y`$ and a repeat
prompt. The student is trained with a forward KL loss to match the
teacher’s gold-conditioned next-token distribution, and the teacher is
an exponential moving average of the student.

We propose Gold-Conditioned Distillation (GCD), a token-level
distillation method for learning from expert demonstrations. The goal is
to replace the one-hot targets used in supervised fine-tuning with
model-native targets produced by the model itself under gold response
conditioning.

The student and teacher are initialized from the same model, but only
the student receives gradient updates; the teacher is maintained as an
EMA copy of the student throughout training. Thus, the teacher provides
a slowly evolving self-distillation target. GCD is fully offline:
neither the student nor the teacher autoregressively generates responses
during training; both are evaluated only under teacher forcing along the
gold response from the dataset.

#### Student and teacher contexts.

Given an input $`x`$ and its gold response
$`y^{*}=(y_{1}^{*},\dots,y_{T}^{*})`$, at position $`t`$ the student
conditions on the standard teacher-forced prefix, while the teacher
conditions on a _privileged_ prefix that additionally exposes the full
gold response through a template $`\phi(x,y^{*})`$:

|     |                               |                                                       |     |     |
| --- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle c_{t}^{(S)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=[\,x\,;\,y_{<t}^{*}\,],`$             |     | (1) |
|     | $`\displaystyle c_{t}^{(T)}`$ | $`\displaystyle=[\,\phi(x,y^{*})\,;\,y_{<t}^{*}\,],`$ |     |     |

where $`[\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,]`$ denotes token-sequence concatenation.
The student and teacher next-token distributions are then obtained by
evaluating each model on its own context:

|     |                                                 |                                                                           |     |     |
| --- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi_{S}(\cdot\mid c_{t}^{(S)})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,\,y_{<t}^{*}),`$                 |     | (2) |
|     | $`\displaystyle\pi_{T}(\cdot\mid c_{t}^{(T)})`$ | $`\displaystyle=\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(\cdot\mid\phi(x,y^{*}),\,y_{<t}^{*})`$ |     |     |

where $`\theta`$ denotes the student parameters, and $`\bar{\theta}`$
denotes the EMA teacher parameters. The two models are initialized
identically, $`\bar{\theta}_{0}=\theta_{0}`$, but diverge during
training because only $`\theta`$ receives gradient updates.

A concrete instantiation of the privileged template is:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\phi(x,y^{*})\;=\;x\;\oplus\;\text{``The correct response is:''}\;\oplus\;y^{*}\;\oplus\;\text{``Please repeat the correct response.''}
``` |  | (3) |

Both distributions are evaluated on the *same* gold prefix states
$`y_{<t}^{*}`$; the only asymmetry is that the teacher uses an
EMA-smoothed parameterization and observes an additional gold-revealing
segment.

#### Objective.

We train the student by matching the privileged teacher distribution
with a token-level forward KL, averaged over positions and over the data
distribution $`\mathcal{D}`$:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}(\theta)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,\,y^{*})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigg[\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\Big(\operatorname{sg}\!\big[\pi_{\bar{\theta}}(\cdot\mid c_{t}^{(T)})\big]\;\Big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid c_{t}^{(S)})\Big)\Bigg]
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]`$ denotes the stop-gradient operator.
The teacher distribution is therefore treated as a fixed target for each
update, and gradients are applied only to the student parameters
$`\theta`$. We adopt the forward, mode-covering KL direction, so that
the student is penalized for placing low probability on tokens that the
teacher considers likely under the gold-revealed context. An ablation in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E KL Direction Ablation ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")
confirms this choice: replacing it with reverse KL lowers accuracy on
both LiveNews and Tooluse.

#### EMA teacher update.

After each student update, the teacher parameters are updated using an
exponential moving average of the student:

|     |                                                           |     |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \bar{\theta}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\bar{\theta}+\alpha\theta
       ```                                                        |     | (5) |

where $`\alpha\in[0,1]`$ controls the update rate. In this paper, we
choose $`\alpha=0.01`$ for the EMA update following SDFT [Shenfeld et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib1). We conduct an ablation study on $`\alpha`$ in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D EMA Update Rate Ablation ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning"):
GCD is stable for small update rates ($`\alpha\leq 0.05`$), while large
rates that make the teacher track the student too closely cause training
to collapse.

### 4.1 Experiment Settings

Models. We evaluate four pretrained language models spanning model
families, scales, and training regimes: Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct,
Qwen3-4B-Base, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Qwen3-8B-Base. The base models
are included to test whether GCD’s gold-conditioned teacher signal
remains effective in the absence of post-training instruction-following
priors.

Dataset and Evaluation. We conduct fine-tuning experiments under two
learning settings, distinguished by the model’s initial capability in
the target domain.

- •
  Partial-knowledge setting. In this setting, the model already
  possesses partial ability in the target domain before fine-tuning. We
  fine-tune and evaluate on four datasets: Tooluse [Tang et al.
  (2023)](#bib.bib4), which requires generating the correct API call and
  JSON arguments in response to user requests; and three scientific
  multiple-choice datasets, namely Chemistry, Physics & Material
  (physics and materials science), and Biology.
- •
  Near-zero-knowledge setting. In this setting, the model has almost no
  prior knowledge of the target domain, with initial accuracy close to
  zero on LiveNews [Zhang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib2). We fine-tune the
  model on LiveNews to evaluate its ability to continuously acquire new
  knowledge through updates. LiveNews consists of news
  question-answering instances collected from September 2025 and January
  2026, which post-date the models’ training cutoff.

We evaluate models from two perspectives: (a) new-task performance,
measured by accuracy on held-out evaluation splits of the corresponding
fine-tuning datasets; and (b) previous capability retention, measured on
five general benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, Winogrande, TruthfulQA, and
IFEval) using lm-evaluation-harness (settings in
Appendix [B.3](#A2.SS3 "B.3 General Benchmark Evaluation ‣ Appendix B Experiment Details ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")).
These benchmarks collectively assess knowledge, commonsense reasoning,
language understanding, factuality, and instruction-following ability.
For the new tasks, all responses are generated with greedy decoding;
LiveNews answers are graded by Qwen3.5-9B, also with greedy decoding.
Please refer to
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Dataset Details ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")
for more details on the dataset setup.

Baselines. SFT: standard supervised fine-tuning. SDFT [Shenfeld et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib1): the original self-distillation fine-tuning method,
in which the student generates an on-policy response and the
gold-conditioned teacher provides the soft target along that generated
response (see
Sec. [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")).
Following a known issue in the released implementation, we compute its
KL in the forward direction
(Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Experiment Details ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")).

Hyperparameters. For Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Base, we conduct
full fine-tuning using a learning rate of $`5\times 10^{-5}`$. For
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B-Base, we adopt LoRA [Hu et al.
(2021)](#bib.bib6) with learning rates of $`1\times 10^{-4}`$ and
$`4\times 10^{-4}`$, respectively. Across all three training methods
(SFT, SDFT, and GCD), we keep the other variables like optimizer,
learning-rate scheduler, batch size, sequence length, and number of
training epochs the same for each $`(\text{model},\text{task})`$
setting. Unless otherwise specified, all evaluation results are reported
using the final checkpoint obtained at the end of training.

model-plausible alternatives. This encourages the student to learn a

improve generalization to held-out examples.

#### Learning new knowledge from scratch.

Figure [2](#S4.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 4.3 Results in the Near-Zero-Knowledge Setting ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")

setting, the base models have near-zero initial accuracy, indicating
that the target knowledge is largely absent before fine-tuning. We
observe that GCD learns the new task as effectively as, and sometimes
better than, standard SFT. On LiveNews, GCD achieves higher final
new-task accuracy than SFT on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Base,
while SFT is slightly better on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. By contrast, SDFT
performs poorly on new-task accuracy across all three models. This is
expected in the from-scratch knowledge-injection setting: since SDFT
relies on student-generated rollouts, the early trajectories are often
incorrect when the model has little prior knowledge about the target
domain. The teacher is then asked to provide supervision on low-quality
trajectories, leading to weak acquisition of the new knowledge.

Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.3 Results in the Near-Zero-Knowledge Setting ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")
reports retention on five general-purpose benchmarks after fine-tuning
on LiveNews, and shows a clear trade-off between acquiring the new task
and preserving previous capabilities. SDFT obtains the highest
previous-task average on all three models, but only because it barely
learns the injected knowledge (new-task accuracy below $`11\%`$), so
high retention alone is not useful. GCD is more balanced: it matches or
exceeds SFT on the new task on two of the three models and is slightly
below it on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, while its previous-task average is
higher than SFT’s on two models and marginally lower on Qwen3-4B-Base.
The retention gains are modest, and GCD does not fully solve
catastrophic forgetting; the takeaway is that GCD reaches SFT-level
new-task learning while usually preserving general capabilities slightly
better.

## 5 Analysis

### 5.1 What the GCD Soft Label Encodes and What the Student Inherits

GCD conditions the teacher on the gold response, asks it to reproduce
that response token by token, and trains the student under
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{T}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta})`$. We ask two questions
empirically: (i) what does the gold-conditioned target distribution
$`\pi_{T}`$ encode beyond the SFT one-hot label, and (ii) does the
trained student inherit that structure at inference time, when the gold
response is no longer in context.

#### Setup.

We use Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct on Tooluse dataset. We forward about $`200`$
training prompts through the initial model. At every token position
$`t`$ we record the teacher distribution
$`\pi_{T}(\cdot\mid c_{t}^{(T)})`$ under the gold-conditioned context
and, as a control, the distribution of the same model under the student
context $`c_{t}^{(S)}`$, i.e., without the gold response; we refer to
the latter as the *base model*. We focus on the *low-confidence gold
positions*, selected by the threshold $`\pi_{T}(y_{t}^{*})\leq 0.85`$
after discarding formatting, whitespace, and BPE-segmentation artifacts.

#### Context-conditional alternatives.

Representative low-confidence positions place mass on paraphrase
choices, semantically related action verbs, and reasoning-step discourse
markers — ‘weight’ / ‘mass’, ‘create’ / ‘get’, ‘impacted’ /
‘influenced’, ‘Next’ / ‘Thought’. Crucially, the choice depends on the
context. To quantify this, we define the *assignment entropy* of a gold
token as the entropy of the distribution of the teacher’s top-$`1`$
alternative across the low-confidence instances of that token. Across
$`41`$ instances of ‘Next’, the top-$`1`$ alternative takes seven
distinct values with an assignment entropy of $`1.41`$ nats. Any
context-independent regularizer (uniform or per-token-id label
smoothing) has assignment entropy zero by construction.

#### Aligned with the base model’s natural continuations.

The Teacher column of

compares the teacher’s top-$`5`$ alternatives with the base model’s
top-$`5`$ at the same position: the average Jaccard overlap is $`0.26`$,
against a random baseline of about $`1.9\times 10^{-5}`$, and $`46\%`$
of $`\pi_{T}`$’s residual non-gold mass lies on tokens that are also
among the base model’s top-$`5`$. The gold-conditioned residual is
therefore not a prompt-template artifact: it is concentrated on the
branch points the base model would explore on its own, re-weighted
toward the gold response.

Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Aligned with the base model’s natural continuations. ‣ 5.1 What the GCD Soft Label Encodes and What the Student Inherits ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")

the same low-confidence positions under the student context. SFT pushes
$`\pi_{\theta}(\text{gold})`$ to $`0.94`$, whereas GCD stops at
$`0.86`$. This is consistent with the direction predicted by the
forward-KL objective, whose fixed point $`\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{T}`$ places

Table [4](#S5.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Aligned with the base model’s natural continuations. ‣ 5.1 What the GCD Soft Label Encodes and What the Student Inherits ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")
reports the target of the initial teacher. GCD retains $`2.6\times`$ and

alternatives, respectively, and at $`10\%`$ of the positions it even
ranks an alternative above gold, mirroring positions where $`\pi_{T}`$
itself does. The top-$`5`$ Jaccard overlap with the base model is
$`1.9\times`$ that of SFT ($`0.27`$ vs. $`0.14`$).

#### Connection to forgetting.

Because $`\pi_{T}`$’s residual mass falls on tokens the base model would
naturally produce, the forward-KL fixed point $`\pi_{\theta}=\pi_{T}`$
pins the student close to the base distribution at these branch points,
whereas the SFT one-hot loss keeps pushing alternative probability
toward zero across epochs. We hypothesize that this base-aligned shape
is a local signature of the retention differences measured in
Sec. [4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning");
the gains there are modest, and establishing the link directly is left
for future work.

### 5.2 Comparison with SFT+KL

To test whether the benefit of GCD can be reproduced by simply
regularizing toward the base model, we compare it with SFT+KL(base),
which augments the SFT objective with a KL penalty toward the base model
distribution:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}+\frac{\lambda}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\Big(\pi_{\mathrm{base}}(\cdot\mid x,y^{*}_{<t})\;\Big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,y^{*}_{<t})\Big)
``` |  | (6) |

We sweep the regularization strength $`\lambda\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,1.0\}`$
and compare all variants with SFT and GCD on LiveNews with
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct.

As shown in
Figure [3(a)](#S5.F3.sf1 "In Figure 3 ‣ Aligned with the base model’s natural continuations. ‣ 5.1 What the GCD Soft Label Encodes and What the Student Inherits ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning"),
KL regularization toward the base model does not improve new-knowledge
acquisition: $`\lambda=0.1`$ behaves like SFT and still trails GCD at
the end of training, while $`\lambda\geq 0.3`$ increasingly constrains
the model to the base distribution and falls substantially below SFT.
GCD’s new-task gains therefore cannot be explained by simply keeping the
fine-tuned model close to the base model; note that this comparison
concerns new-task accuracy only.

### 5.3 Comparison with SFT+Label Smoothing

To test whether the structure of the GCD target matters, or whether any
softened version of the one-hot target suffices, we compare against
uniformly label-smoothed SFT (SFT+LS), which replaces the one-hot target
at each position with

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
q_{\mathrm{LS}}(v)=(1-\epsilon)\mathbf{1}[v=y_{t}^{\ast}]+\frac{\epsilon}{|\mathcal{V}|}
``` |  | (7) |

where $`y_{t}^{\ast}`$ is the gold token, $`\epsilon`$ is the smoothing
coefficient, and $`|\mathcal{V}|`$ is the vocabulary size. This baseline
controls for the effect of softening the target distribution while
removing the model- and context-dependent structure present in GCD. We
sweep $`\epsilon\in\{0.03,0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20\}`$ on Tooluse with
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct.

As shown in
Figure [3(b)](#S5.F3.sf2 "In Figure 3 ‣ Aligned with the base model’s natural continuations. ‣ 5.1 What the GCD Soft Label Encodes and What the Student Inherits ‣ 5 Analysis ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning"),
label smoothing improves over SFT inconsistently and by at most about
three points, with some coefficients falling below SFT, whereas GCD
achieves the best held-out Tooluse accuracy. The gain of GCD therefore
cannot be explained only by increasing the entropy of the target
distribution.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we propose GCD, a novel distillation framework for LLM
fine-tuning. Unlike SFT, which relies on one-hot targets, a
gold-conditioned teacher is introduced to provide soft, model-native
supervision, enabling the student model to preserve plausible
alternatives aligned with the model’s prior distribution while improving
adaptation to new tasks. Unlike on-policy self-distillation methods such
as SDFT, GCD performs distillation directly along the gold response,
avoiding costly online rollout generation. Extensive experiments show
that GCD achieves strong average new-task performance across multiple
models and tasks. Compared with SFT, GCD often improves new-task
accuracy and provides slightly better retention. Compared with SDFT, GCD
substantially improves new-task acquisition in the settings we study.

## Limitations

This work is an initial exploration of gold-conditioned supervision. In
our experiments, we use a simple prompt that asks the teacher to repeat
the provided gold response. This design may not fully exploit the
model’s ability to interpret the demonstration. A better prompt could
ask the teacher to compare the gold response with plausible
alternatives, identify key decision points, or produce a more calibrated
distribution over possible continuations. Such prompts may elicit richer
and more informative soft targets than simple repetition. In addition,
our token-level analysis is conducted on a single model and dataset, the
retention gains over SFT are modest, the near-zero-knowledge setting
covers three of the four models, and all models have at most 8B
parameters; whether these findings transfer to larger models remains
open.

## Appendix A Dataset Details

We evaluate our method on five datasets.
Table [5](#A1.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Appendix A Dataset Details ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")
provides an overview of the dataset; per-dataset descriptions follow.

| Dataset | Domain | Answer format | \#Train | \#Eval |
|----|----|----|----|----|
| Tooluse | API tool calling | API name + JSON args | 4,046 | 97 |
| Chemistry | Mixed scientific QA (chemistry-heavy) | 4-option MCQ | 2,674 | 507 |
| Physics & Material | Physics + materials science | 4-option MCQ | 1,257 | 174 |
| Biology | School- and graduate-level biology | 2/3/4-option MCQ | 1,107 | 583 |
| LiveNews | Post-training-cutoff news QA | Free-form answer | 400 | 400 |

Table 5: Dataset overview. \#Train and \#Eval give the number of
training and held-out evaluation examples.

#### Tooluse.

Same dataset used in [Shenfeld et al. (2026)](#bib.bib1). A
function-calling benchmark in which the model receives a
natural-language instruction together with the documentation of one or
more API tools and must emit a structured Action:\<tool_name\> and
Action Input:\<JSON\> sequence. Each instance contains the tool’s name,
description, natural-language documentation, a user instruction, and a
gold response consisting of one or more (Action, Action_Input) pairs.

#### Chemistry.

Same dataset used in [Shenfeld et al. (2026)](#bib.bib1). A Chemistry QA
corpus in which each example pairs a multiple-choice question with a
chain-of-thought solution wrapped in
\<reasoning\>...\</reasoning\>\<answer\>...\</answer\> tags.

#### Physics & Material.

A merged physics + materials-science split derived from
SciKnowEval [Feng et al. (2024)](#bib.bib3). Each prompt presents a
quantitative question (e.g., Gauss-law fluxes, gravitational lensing,
materials properties) together with the relevant physical constants, and
each gold response is a worked solution followed by the chosen letter.

#### Biology.

A union of (i) the biology portion of ScienceQA-style
elementary/middle-school questions, and (ii) the biology subset of
SciKnowEval, which contributes harder graduate-level questions. Both
sources are normalized into a unified schema with a dynamic
answer-format specification matching the actual number of options
(2/3/4), so that no item is dropped for being non-quaternary. The
combined dataset therefore contains a mixture of easy and hard biology
MCQs.

#### LiveNews.

A recent-news QA dataset built from the human-verified test splits of
two LiveNewsBench [Zhang et al. (2026)](#bib.bib2) releases (September
2025 and January 2026). Each example consists of a free-form factual
question about events that occurred *after* every model’s training
cutoff, paired with a short human-written answer. To decouple “learning
the fact” from “memorizing the surface phrasing,” we use Gemini3-Flash
to paraphrase both the question and the answer of every item, producing
a rewritten variant for each original $`(q,a)`$. *Training is performed
on the rewritten $`(q^{\prime},a^{\prime})`$ pairs, while evaluation is
performed on the original human-written $`(q,a)`$.* A method that has
truly absorbed the underlying news event must therefore answer a
phrasing it has never been trained on, ruling out trivial token-level
memorization. LiveNews thus measures whether a method can inject
genuinely new world knowledge that cannot have leaked from previous
training.

### A.1 Construction of Physics & Material and Biology

Both training datasets are reorganized into the common JSONL schema
{prompt, golden_response}. Every prompt is rendered as a self-contained
exam item that bundles (i) a format specification, (ii) the question
stem, and (iii) the option list, with the format string parameterized by
the number of choices $`k\in\{2,3,4\}`$: the model is asked to wrap
reasoning in \<reasoning\>...\</reasoning\> and to emit a single choice
letter inside \<answer\>...\</answer\>. The corresponding
golden_response follows the same wrapping.

The source of the reference response differs by dataset.
ScienceQA-Biology already provides explanations, so we use them directly
and convert the answer index to a letter. SciKnowEval provides only the
final answer, so we synthesize reference responses with GPT-OSS-20B: for
each item, we sample $`N=8`$ candidates with $`T=0.7`$, top-$`p=0.9`$,
and max_tokens=1024, discard candidates whose extracted answer letter
does not match the gold label, and select the median-length remaining
response. Items with no correct candidate are removed. We then form
Biology by concatenating ScienceQA-Biology and SciKnowEval-Biology, and
Physics & Material by concatenating the physics and materials subsets of
SciKnowEval.

## Appendix B Experiment Details

### B.1 Baseline Reproduction

#### SFT.

For the SFT baseline we use the SFTTrainer from TRL [von Werra et al.
(2020)](#bib.bib5) with the cross-entropy loss masked to the
gold-response tokens only; the prompt portion of each input is excluded
from the loss support. This matches the loss support used by our GCD and
SDFT.

#### SDFT.

For SDFT we run the authors’ publicly released implementation¹¹ 1
[https://github.com/idanshen/Self-Distillation](https://github.com/idanshen/Self-Distillation).
Following a known issue in that repository²² 2
[https://github.com/idanshen/Self-Distillation/issues/5](https://github.com/idanshen/Self-Distillation/issues/5),
we modify the distillation term so that the KL is computed in the
forward direction
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{T}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta}\bigr)`$ rather than
the reverse direction shipped in the original paper.

### B.2 Hyperparameters and Shared Training Pipeline

All three methods (SFT, SDFT, and GCD) share the same training pipeline
for every (model, task) combination, so that only the loss differs. We
use AdamW with $`(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999)`$,
$`\varepsilon=10^{-8}`$, and weight decay $`0.01`$, clip the global
$`\ell_{2}`$ gradient norm at $`1.0`$, and use a linear warmup followed
by a cosine decay of the learning rate to $`0`$. Each run trains for
$`4`$ epochs in bf16 mixed precision on a single A100 80GB GPU, with a
per-device batch size of $`4`$ and $`8`$ gradient accumulation steps (an
effective batch size of $`32`$) and a maximum sequence length of
$`2{,}048`$ tokens. We perform full fine-tuning on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct
and Qwen3-4B-Base. On Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B-Base we use
LoRA [Hu et al. (2021)](#bib.bib6) with rank $`r{=}64`$ and scaling
factor $`128`$ on the attention and MLP projections (q_proj, k_proj,
v_proj, o_proj, gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj), keeping all other layers
frozen; the same LoRA configuration is used for all three methods on
these models.

### B.3 General Benchmark Evaluation

We evaluate previous capability retention with lm-evaluation-harness
(v0.4.x) using identical settings for the base model and all fine-tuned
checkpoints. All five benchmarks are evaluated zero-shot. We report
accuracy for MMLU and Winogrande, length-normalized accuracy (acc_norm)
for HellaSwag, MC2 for TruthfulQA, and, for IFEval, the mean of
prompt-level and instruction-level strict accuracy. The multiple-choice
benchmarks are scored by log-likelihood, while IFEval responses are
generated with greedy decoding (temperature $`0`$) and a maximum of
$`1{,}280`$ new tokens. All other options follow the harness defaults.

## Appendix C Learning-Rate Sensitivity of SDFT

A natural concern when comparing against SDFT [Shenfeld et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib1) is that its weaker performance might simply reflect a
poorly tuned learning rate rather than a limitation of the method
itself. To rule this out, we conduct a learning-rate sweep for SDFT and
contrast every point in the sweep with a single GCD run that uses the
same model, dataset, optimizer, batch size, schedule, and number of
epochs.

For each (model, dataset) combination we train SDFT at five to six
learning rates spanning roughly an order of
magnitude—$`2{\times}10^{-5}`$, $`5{\times}10^{-5}`$,
$`8{\times}10^{-5}`$, $`1{\times}10^{-4}`$, $`2{\times}10^{-4}`$, and
$`3{\times}10^{-4}`$³³ 3 Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct uses LoRA fine-tuning, so
its effective range is shifted upward; the lowest two rates are skipped
for Llama and the highest rate is skipped for Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and
Qwen3-4B-Base (full fine-tuning). The sweep ranges therefore match each
model’s standard tuning regime rather than being held artificially
fixed.. All other hyperparameters are inherited from the corresponding
configuration in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Experiment Details ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning");
only the learning rate is varied. For GCD, we plot a single curve
trained with the default reported in
Sec. [4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning").

Figure 4: Learning-rate sensitivity of SDFT versus our GCD on LiveNews.
Each panel shows accuracy across four training epochs for one model.
Gray curves are SDFT runs, darker for higher learning rates; the red
curve is GCD at its single reported learning rate. SDFT’s best learning
rate is model-specific and improves the method only marginally, while
GCD dominates the entire SDFT envelope on every panel.

## Appendix D EMA Update Rate Ablation

GCD maintains the teacher as an exponential moving average of the
student, $`\bar{\theta}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\bar{\theta}+\alpha\theta`$,
with $`\alpha=0.01`$ in all main experiments. To measure how sensitive
GCD is to this choice, we vary $`\alpha\in\{0,0.01,0.05,0.2,1.0\}`$ on
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct for both LiveNews and Tooluse, keeping every other
hyperparameter fixed at the setting used in the main experiments. Here
$`\alpha=0`$ freezes the teacher at the initial model, and
$`\alpha=1.0`$ copies the student into the teacher after every update.
We report the accuracy of the final checkpoint.

|                                 |          |         |
|---------------------------------|----------|---------|
| EMA rate $`\alpha`$             | LiveNews | Tooluse |
| $`0`$ (frozen teacher)          | 34.25    | 64.95   |
| $`0.01`$ (default)              | 38.00    | 66.30   |
| $`0.05`$                        | 35.75    | 62.89   |
| $`0.2`$                         | 36.25    | 16.49   |
| $`1.0`$ (teacher $`=`$ student) | 20.50    | 00.00   |

Table 6: Effect of the EMA update rate $`\alpha`$ on GCD
(Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, new-task accuracy in %, final checkpoint).

Table [6](#A4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Appendix D EMA Update Rate Ablation ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")
shows that GCD is stable for small update rates:
$`\alpha\in\{0,0.01,0.05\}`$ reach similar accuracy on both tasks, so
GCD does not depend on a finely tuned $`\alpha`$. Large rates cause
training to collapse: with $`\alpha=1.0`$ the teacher becomes identical
to the student at every step and accuracy drops sharply on both tasks,
and at $`\alpha=0.2`$ Tooluse first rises and then collapses late in
training while LiveNews is unaffected. The teacher therefore needs to
lag behind the student, but any small update rate works.

## Appendix E KL Direction Ablation

GCD minimizes the forward KL divergence from the teacher to the student,
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\bar{\theta}}\,\|\,\pi_{\theta})`$
(Sec. [3](#S3 "3 Method ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")).
To test this choice, we replace it with the reverse KL divergence,
$`D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\bar{\theta}})`$, on
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct for both LiveNews and Tooluse, keeping every other
hyperparameter fixed at the setting used in the main experiments. We
report the accuracy of the final checkpoint.

| KL direction      | LiveNews | Tooluse |
|-------------------|----------|---------|
| Forward (default) | 38.00    | 66.30   |
| Reverse           | 11.00    | 55.67   |

Table 7: Effect of the KL direction on GCD (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct,
new-task accuracy in %, final checkpoint).

Table [7](#A5.T7 "Table 7 ‣ Appendix E KL Direction Ablation ‣ Self-Distillation for Supervised Fine-Tuning")
shows that forward KL outperforms reverse KL on both tasks. The gap is
largest on LiveNews, where the target knowledge is absent before

still learns the task but trails forward KL by 10.63 points (55.67% vs.
66.30%).
````

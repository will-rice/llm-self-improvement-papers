# SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models

Muxi Diao    Rumei Li    Shiyang Liu    Guogang Liao    Jingang Wang   
Xunliang Cai    Weiran Xu ^(†)^(†)thanks: Corresponding author

###### Abstract

As Large Language Models (LLMs) continue to advance in capability and
influence, ensuring their safety and preventing harmful outputs has
become crucial. A promising approach to address these concerns involves
training models to automatically generate adversarial prompts for red
teaming. However, the evolving subtlety of vulnerabilities in LLMs
challenges the effectiveness of current adversarial methods, which
struggle to generate diverse, complex prompts and dynamically explore
the weaknesses of these models. To tackle these challenges, we introduce
the Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety (SEAS) optimization framework,
which includes both a SEAS dataset and a SEAS pipeline. The SEAS dataset
comprises complex adversarial prompts, while the SEAS pipeline operates
through three stages: Initialization, Attack, and Adversarial
Optimization. This framework generates a diverse range of adversarial
prompts and dynamically explores the model’s vulnerabilities to enhance
its safety. Our contributions include a novel adversarial framework, a
comprehensive safety dataset, and empirical evidence demonstrating the
effectiveness of SEAS. After three iterations, the model achieves a
safety level comparable to GPT-4. Our code and datasets are released at
[https://SEAS-LLM.github.io/](https://SEAS-LLM.github.io/).

## 1 Introduction

Recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive
capabilities in various fields ([OpenAI et al. 2023](#bib.bib34)).
Meanwhile, safety risks associated with deploying LLMs in practical
applications have raised widespread public concern ([Christian
2023](#bib.bib11); [Chilton 2023](#bib.bib9)).

One potentially useful approach to enhance safety is red teaming ([Perez
et al. 2022](#bib.bib36); [OpenAI et al. 2023](#bib.bib34); [Meta
2024](#bib.bib32); [Anthropic 2024](#bib.bib2)). This is a proactive
risk identification approach that employs manual or automated techniques
to generate attack data, which is then used to critically examine a
language model for harmful outputs. The model is subsequently updated to
prevent these issues. Due to the high costs and time consumption
associated with manual red teaming ([Ganguli et al. 2022](#bib.bib14);
[Anthropic 2024](#bib.bib2)), a promising alternative is to automate the
generation of adversarial prompts using a Red Team LLM ([Perez et al.
2022](#bib.bib36)).

However, as the performance of LLMs continues to improve, their safety
and robustness are also enhanced, which may be accompanied by changes in
safety vulnerabilities ([Ge et al. 2023](#bib.bib15)), resulting in more
subtle and covert failure modes ([Ganguli et al. 2022](#bib.bib14); [Ge
et al. 2023](#bib.bib15)). These changes pose significant challenges to
existing red team methods. Additionally, current methods of generating
adversarial data for red teaming lack diversity ([Hong et al.
2024](#bib.bib18)), as they are confined to using a single predefined
attack strategy ([Zhou et al. 2023b](#bib.bib51); [Fernando et al.
2023](#bib.bib13)). This limitation hampers the broad exploration of
adversarial prompts, and with the advancement of model capabilities,
fixed attack strategies are likely to become ineffective. Furthermore,
existing methods are limited in complexity and fail to consider the
potential flaws of the Target models themselves, thus unable to
implement targeted attacks, resulting in lower attack success rates ([Ge
et al. 2023](#bib.bib15)). Therefore, the development and testing of
current LLMs still heavily rely on manual red teaming ([OpenAI et al.
2023](#bib.bib34); [Anthropic 2024](#bib.bib2)).

To address these issues, we propose a Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety
(SEAS) optimization framework, which includes a SEAS dataset and a SEAS
pipeline. To generate more complex adversarial prompts, we have
constructed the SEAS dataset. This dataset not only encompasses various
types of risk categories but also includes adversarial attack styles. In
the SEAS pipeline, through the initialization using the SEAS dataset and
subsequent iterative updates, the Red Team model generates more diverse
adversarial attack prompts and dynamically explores the vulnerabilities
of the Target model, which in turn updates itself with the generated
data to enhance its safety performance.

As shown in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models"),
our pipeline comprises three stages. In the Initialization Stage, the
Red Team model ($`R_{0}`$, where “$`R`$” stands for Red Team model and
“0” denotes the initial iteration) undergoes fine-tuning using the SEAS
dataset to produce adversarial prompts. Concurrently, the Target model
($`T_{0}`$, where “$`T`$” stands for Target model and “0” denotes the
initial iteration) is fine-tuned using open-source data to enhance its
instruction-following capabilities. In the Attack Stage, the Red Team
model generates adversarial prompts, which are inputted to the Target
model to elicit responses. The Safe Classifier then evaluates the safety
of these responses based on the concatenated prompts and responses. In
the Adversarial Optimization Stage, adversarial prompts that
successfully attack the Target model are selected as chosen examples,
while ineffective ones are categorized as rejected examples. These are
then used to update the Red Team model using pair-wise optimization
loss. Similarly, safe responses are taken as chosen examples and unsafe
ones as rejected examples to update the Target model. This cycle of
Attack and Adversarial Optimization stages is repeated over multiple
rounds, allowing both models to continually evolve through adversarial
competition.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

1\) We propose a self-evolving adversarial framework (SEAS) in which two
types of models continuously evolve through adversarial competition. The
Red Team model comprehensively and dynamically explores the
vulnerabilities of the Target model to enhance its safety capabilities.

2\) We release a safety dataset that includes various harmful,
adversarial and ambiguous harmless prompts, providing tools for the
secure development and deployment of LLMs. We also open-source the
training code, facilitating researchers to replicate and validate our
findings¹¹ 1 Data and code are available on the project homepage:
[https://SEAS-LLM.github.io/](https://SEAS-LLM.github.io/). Detailed
information about the dataset can be found in Appendix A..

3\) Through comprehensive empirical experiments, we have demonstrated
the effectiveness of SEAS. After three iterations, the Target model
reaches a safety level close to that of GPT-4 while maintaining its
general ability. Moreover, the Red Team model shows a 50.66% increase in
Attack Success Rate (ASR) against Llama3-70B-Instruct. We also evaluate
the diversity of generated adversarial prompts and the effectiveness of
iterative model updates.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Red Teaming LLMs

Large-scale red teaming has been conducted during the pre-deployment
phase of LLMs. Researchers rely on human annotators to handwrite
(annotate) adversarial prompts to guide the LLM in generating harmful
responses ([Meta 2024](#bib.bib32); [Anthropic 2024](#bib.bib2); [OpenAI
et al. 2023](#bib.bib34)). While manually constructing adversarial
prompts has been proven to generate high-quality prompts ([Yu et al.
2023](#bib.bib47); [Ji et al. 2023a](#bib.bib20)), it comes with high
costs and consumes a significant amount of time ([Ganguli et al.
2022](#bib.bib14); [Anthropic 2024](#bib.bib2)). Additionally, human
annotators still face limitations in fully exploring model
vulnerabilities ([Hong et al. 2024](#bib.bib18); [Mu et al.
2024](#bib.bib33)). Previous work has investigated automated red teaming
processes by utilizing LLMs to augment open-source datasets ([Ganguli
et al. 2022](#bib.bib14); [Ji et al. 2023b](#bib.bib21); [Samvelyan
et al. 2024](#bib.bib43)), employing Supervised Learning (SL) to train
Red Team models ([Perez et al. 2022](#bib.bib36); [Ge et al.
2023](#bib.bib15)) , and using prompt engineering methods ([Chao et al.
2023](#bib.bib6); [Liu et al. 2024](#bib.bib28); [Li et al.
2024](#bib.bib25)) to create adversarial prompts. These prompts are used
to attack Target models to elicit risky responses.

These methods fail to adapt to the risk changes caused by updates to the
Target model. Similar to our work, MART ([Ge et al. 2023](#bib.bib15))
uses an iterative approach, updating both the Red Team and Target models
to improve model safety. However, MART only screens data that the Target
model can safely respond to and uses it for training. In contrast, our
SEAS method automates the exploration and selection of preferred data
pairs. For the same adversarial prompt, it selects one secure and one
risky response from the Target model, ensuring that the model generates
a safe response when encountering the prompt again. More details are
provided in Section
[4](#S4 "4 Experiments ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models").

### 2.2 Datasets for LLM Safety

Most LLMs exhibit high success rates in publicly available safety
assessment benchmarks ([Mazeika et al. 2024](#bib.bib31); [Tedeschi
et al. 2024](#bib.bib45)). Despite this, current research often focuses
only on specific aspects or dimensions of safety, such as toxicity
([Hartvigsen et al. 2022](#bib.bib16)), there has been no extensive or
in-depth exploration of model safety vulnerabilities. A comprehensive
evaluation across all categories could more effectively reveal potential
vulnerabilities in LLMs ([Röttger et al. 2024b](#bib.bib42)).
Additionally, recent studies have shown that the risk of LLMs generating
harmful content remains high when exposed to adversarially designed
prompts ([Liu et al. 2023](#bib.bib27); [Chowdhury et al.
2024](#bib.bib10)), indicating that existing benchmarks may not fully
capture the safety risks associated with LLMs.

To delve deeper into the vulnerabilities of Target models from an
attacker’s perspective, we develop a new dataset that includes 5
different Risk Categories and 9 distinct Attack Styles ([Samvelyan
et al. 2024](#bib.bib43)) (see Appendix A). This dataset could guide the
model to produce outputs that contradict its design principles, thereby
enabling a comprehensive assessment and future enhancement of model
safety.

## 3 Method

In this section, we provide a detailed introduction of SEAS framework,
which includes a dataset and a pipeline. By constructing the SEAS
dataset and the entire pipeline, we achieve a comprehensive exploration
of the Target model’s vulnerabilities and enhance its safety
performance.

### 3.1 SEAS dataset

Current LLMs generally perform well on open-source safety datasets, but
they still suffer from producing harmful content when presented with
deliberately crafted prompts ([Röttger et al. 2024b](#bib.bib42); [Hong
et al. 2024](#bib.bib18)). Most open-source datasets do not effectively
integrate the two critical dimensions of Risk Categories ([Inan et al.
2023](#bib.bib19)) and Attack Styles ([Samvelyan et al.
2024](#bib.bib43)). Detailed classifications and definitions can be
found in Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A SEAS Dataset ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models").

To address this gap, we have developed a SEAS dataset, which features 14
types that cover two risk dimensions: the first is Risk Categories,
which are potential topics of unsafe content that LLMs might generate;
the second is Attack Styles, which are specific expressive techniques
designed to cover various prompt types that could trigger harmful
behavior in models. This dataset contains 18K entries, divided into a
training set with 16K entries and a SEAS-Test set with 2K entries.
Specifically, to prevent models from overfitting to the aggressive
language style or syntax during training, rather than discerning the
actual semantic content, we have created an ambiguous harmless dataset
for evaluation purposes. This dataset, comprising 300 entries from human
experts, mimics the aggressive instruction styles and syntax but is
actually harmless. In Figure
[2](#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3.1 SEAS dataset ‣ 3 Method ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models"),
we present two risk categories and two attack styles, as well as one
harmless sample. SEAS dataset were collected through crowdsourcing
platforms, manually rewritten and labeled, and augmented with some
open-source safety data ([Liu et al. 2023](#bib.bib27); [Tedeschi et al.
2024](#bib.bib45); [Bhardwaj and Poria 2023](#bib.bib4)). Each prompt in
the dataset corresponds to a type label, with detailed category
distribution information available in the Appendix
[A](#A1 "Appendix A SEAS Dataset ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models").

### 3.2 Self-Evolving Pipeline

##### Initialization Stage.

As shown in the Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models"),
during the Initialization Stage, we selected Llama-3-8B ([Meta
2024](#bib.bib32)) as our initial model. For the Red Team model, we
expect it to generate complex and diverse adversarial prompts. To
achieve this, we adopted an initialization scheme based on random sample
contexts. The specific procedure is as follows: we randomly designate a
specific type and select a fixed number of data from the training set of
the SEAS dataset that corresponds to this type. These data are used as a
small set of sample examples, incorporated into the prompts for
Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) input. Then, we randomly select another
sample of the same type as the output.

For the Target model, considering that Llama-3-8B-Instruct already has
strong safety capabilities, we have initialized a Target model based on
Llama-3-8B, which does not have additional safety training, to better
validate the effectiveness of our method. We selected three datasets
specifically designed for SFT focused on general command adjustment. Our
objective is to enhance the model’s capability for instruction following
and to respond appropriately to inputs.

##### Attack Stage.

At the beginning of each Attack Stage, we construct seed prompts by
specifying a type and concatenating a fixed number ($`k`$) of prompts
from the SEAS dataset’s training set. This activates the Red Team model
to generate adversarial prompts. In order to ensure the diversity of the
Red Team model’s output, we adopted nucleus sampling ([Holtzman et al.
2019](#bib.bib17)) and carried out multiple samplings to generate $`n`$
prompts. Following this, we input these prompts to the Target model,
also conducting nucleus sampling and multiple samplings, to obtain $`m`$
output responses.

By concatenating $`n`$ adversarial prompts with $`m`$ responses and
processing them through a Safe Classifier for safety evaluation, we
obtain $`n\times m`$ tuples of {$`seed`$ $`prompt`$, $`adversarial`$
$`prompt`$, $`response`$, $`label`$}, where label = 1 represents unsafe.
Please note that the safety assessment specifically pertains to the
response.

##### Adversarial Optimization Stage.

In the Adversarial Optimization Stage, we filter and construct data
pairs for optimization. Here, we use Direct Preference Optimization
(DPO) loss ([Rafailov et al. 2024](#bib.bib39)). For the Red Team model,
we use the seed prompt as input, treating the adversarial prompt that
triggers harmful response as the chosen output, and the one that doesn’t
trigger such response as the rejected output. The optimization loss of
the Red Team Model $`R_{i+1}`$:

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{R}}=&-\mathbb{E}_{(s,p_{1},p_{0})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(p_{1}\mid s)}{\pi_{R_{i}}(p_{1}\mid s)}\right.\right.\\
&\left.\left.-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(p_{0}\mid s)}{\pi_{R_{i}}(p_{0}\mid s)}\right)\right],\end{split}
``` |  |

where $`s`$ represents the seed prompt, $`p_{1}`$ denotes the
adversarial prompt that successfully induces the Target model to
generate unsafe content, $`\pi_{R_{i}}`$ is the Red Team model of last
iteration $`i`$, and $`\pi_{\theta}`$ is a policy to be optimized.

For the Target model, we use the adversarial prompt as input, treating
the safe response as the chosen output and the harmful response as the
rejected output. The optimization loss of the Target model $`T_{i+1}`$:

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}}=&-\mathbb{E}_{(p,r_{0},r_{1})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(r_{0}\mid p)}{\pi_{T_{i}}(r_{0}\mid p)}\right.\right.\\
&\left.\left.-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(r_{1}\mid p)}{\pi_{T_{i}}(r_{1}\mid p)}\right)\right],\end{split}
``` |  |

where $`p`$ represents the adversarial prompt, and $`r_{0}`$ represents
the model’s safe response.

Subsequently, the Attack Stage and the Adversarial Optimization Stage
are cyclically performed, continually generating adversarial prompts and
producing paired data. Through multiple iterations of model updates, the
capabilities of the models are steadily enhanced.

## 4 Experiments

In this section, we conduct comprehensive empirical experiments to
evaluate the effectiveness of SEAS. We mainly assess the improvement in
the Target model’s safety performance while maintaining general
capability, the complexity and diversity of the Red Team model’s
adversarial prompts, and the advantages of SEAS.

### 4.1 Experimental Details

##### Fine-tuning Details.

In our research, we filtered and constructed a dataset of 16K entries
from the SEAS training set for SFT targeted at the Red Team model.

To construct the Target model, we selected three high-quality
open-source general instruction-following datasets as seed datasets:
ShareGPT ([Chiang et al. 2023](#bib.bib7)), Dolly ([Conover et al.
2023](#bib.bib12)) and LIMA ([Zhou et al. 2023a](#bib.bib50)) (for
detailed descriptions of the datasets, see Appendix D). These datasets
were used to fine-tune the Target model and enhance its ability to
follow instructions. In addition, to ensure these datasets were free of
adversarial content, we conducted extra data cleansing to minimize the
presence of harmful or unsafe instructions. Overall, we used
approximately 101K cleaned samples from these datasets. For both models,
we employed the same hyperparameter settings as the existing SFT methods
([Touvron et al. 2023](#bib.bib46)) : $`\text{learning rate}=5e-6`$,
batch size = 8, epoch = 3.

##### Safe Classifier.

To quickly and effectively determine whether the responses from Target
model contain unsafe content, we utilize Meta Llama Guard 2 ([Meta
2024](#bib.bib32)) as our safety classifier. It is an 8B parameter LLM
safeguard model based on Llama 3. It functions as a LLM, determining
whether a given prompt or response is safe or unsafe through its
generated text output.

##### Data Iterations.

At the beginning of each Attack Stage, we constructed a dataset of 5K
seed prompts. Seed prompts were created by randomly selecting 3
($`k=3`$) prompts of the same type from the training set. During the
generation process of both Red Team and Target models, we employed
settings of $`T=0.8`$ and $`p=0.9`$, and sampled each model 5 times
($`n=m=5`$). When conducting safety evaluation with the Safe Classifier,
we utilized a greedy strategy ([Sutskever, Vinyals, and Le
2014](#bib.bib44)). Each Attack Stage ultimately resulted in a dataset
containing 125K entries.

Based on this dataset, we constructed paired data. Due to the parameter
settings, the model outputs exhibited high diversity, allowing us to
collect approximately 4.8K data pairs per round for iterative
optimization of Red Team model. To balance the generality and safety of
Target model, we selected 2K pairs of safe data and randomly mixed them
with general data for training. General data came from two high-quality
paired open-source datasets, namely OpenOrca ([Lian et al.
2023](#bib.bib26)) and ORPO-DPO-Mix ([Labonne 2024](#bib.bib24))(details
in Appendix D). The prerequisite for iterating our model is that its
general performance does not degrade significantly. In detail, we mixed
7K pairs of general data in iteration 1, and 14K pairs of data in both
iterations 2 and 3.

##### Evaluation Metrics and Benchmarks.

We use Attack Success Rate (ASR) as the metric ([Perez et al.
2022](#bib.bib36); [Ganguli et al. 2022](#bib.bib14); [Zou et al.
2023](#bib.bib52)), defined as the proportion of harmful responses
generated by the evaluated model to harmful queries. (see formula in
Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Metrics ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models")).

We comprehensively evaluate the model’s performance using two widely
recognized safety benchmarks: BeaverTrail (Beaver) ([Ji et al.
2023b](#bib.bib21)) and HH-RLHF ([Ganguli et al. 2022](#bib.bib14)),
along with several jailbreak methods, including GPTFuzzer (GPTF) ([Yu
et al. 2023](#bib.bib47)), HarmBench (HarmB) ([Mazeika et al.
2024](#bib.bib31)), GCG ([Zou et al. 2023](#bib.bib52)), AutoDAN ([Liu
et al. 2024](#bib.bib28)), and CodeChameleon (CodeC) ([Lv et al.
2024](#bib.bib30)), as well as two comprehensive general capability
assessment benchmarks: Arena Hard (Auto v0.1) ([Chiang et al.
2024](#bib.bib8)) and MT-Bench ([Zheng et al. 2023a](#bib.bib48)).
Details can be found in Appendix
[C](#A3 "Appendix C Benchmarks ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models").

##### Diversity.

To avoid Red Team models settling on a single deterministic strategy
upon discovering a few effective adversarial prompts ([Puterman
2014](#bib.bib37); [Bengio et al. 2021](#bib.bib3)), it is essential to
maintain the diversity of the prompts they generate. Based on previous
work ([Reimers and Gurevych 2019](#bib.bib40); [Hong et al.
2024](#bib.bib18)), we assess the similarity between the SEAS training
set, adversarial prompts from the Red Team model after four iterations,
and two open-source datasets using the cosine similarity of sentence
embeddings (refer to Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Metrics ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models")
for the formula). Specifically, for each example in a dataset, we
compute its maximum cosine similarity with all examples in a target
dataset and average these scores.

The results in Table
[5](#S4.T5 "Table 5 ‣ Evaluation of Red Team Models ‣ 4.2 Experimental Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models"),
show that the similarity between the adversarial prompts and both the
train set and other datasets is relatively low (lower than the
similarity values of the two open-source datasets). This indicates a
high level of diversity in the adversarial prompts generated by SEAS.

#### Advantages of SEAS

##### Pair-wise Loss Updates.

Existing automated red teaming solutions, including custom jailbreaking
templates or pipelines, primarily utilize the SFT objective to update
Red Team models and Target models ([Ge et al. 2023](#bib.bib15)).

However, pair-wise loss - Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
([Rafailov et al. 2024](#bib.bib39)) is more suitable in safety
scenarios. This method not only increases the probability of the chosen
text but also decreases the likelihood of the rejected text, thereby
improving the capabilities of the Red Team model and the Target model.
In contrast, SFT loss merely reinforces the correct answer repeatedly,
preventing the model from encountering incorrect samples.

We used the same data to perform both SFT and DPO, and the results are
shown in Table
[6](#S4.T6 "Table 6 ‣ Complexity. ‣ Evaluation of Red Team Models ‣ 4.2 Experimental Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models").
For the same data, the DPO shows significant improvement after one round
of training (ep=1), whereas the SFT scheme shows no improvement after
one round and only modest improvement after two rounds. In Table
[4](#S4.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Incorrect Refusal Rates. ‣ Evaluation of Target Models ‣ 4.2 Experimental Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models"),
we found that the DPO scheme outperforms the SFT scheme in processing
both data types. This performance difference is particularly notable
with complex adversarial samples, where the benefits of the DPO scheme
are more pronounced. Additionally, Figure
[4](#S4.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ Incorrect Refusal Rates. ‣ Evaluation of Target Models ‣ 4.2 Experimental Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models")
shows that the DPO scheme effectively reduces the incorrect refusal rate
in tests involving harmless samples. In contrast, even after two
iterations, the SFT scheme does not show a significant reduction in the
incorrect refusal rate.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety (SEAS)
optimization approach. By continuously improving the model using
adversarial data, this framework enhances the robustness of the model
and overcomes the limitations of traditional red teaming, which cannot
comprehensively, effectively, and dynamically explore model risks.
Through comprehensive empirical experiments, we demonstrate that this
framework is effective in enhancing safety. After three iterations, the
foundational model achieves a safety level comparable to that of GPT-4.
Additionally, we replaced the base model in SEAS with a different
architecture, further proving the effectiveness of SEAS across diverse
model frameworks.

Moreover, even after three iterations, the safety performance of the
SEAS model still slightly behind that of Llama3-8B-Instruct ([Meta
2024](#bib.bib32)), which has undergone extensive expert red teaming
involving hundreds of thousands of examples. In fact, we could further
improve the SEAS model through data augmentation, manual annotation, and
additional rounds of iteration. However, this is not the main focus of
our paper, and we leave it for future work.

## Limitation

In discussing the research on the Self-Evolving Adversarial Security
(SEAS) optimization framework, we must acknowledge certain limitations
that could impact the overall effectiveness and scalability of the
framework. The following are the main limitations identified in this
study:

##### Model Dependency.

Our framework utilizes Llama Guard 2 as a safe classifier to evaluate
whether model responses are secure and conducts iterative updates based
on this evaluation. Although the model provides a comprehensive set of
safety assessment standards, when deploying and applying the SEAS
framework in specific domains, it is still advisable to incorporate
human evaluation or use a specific model to replace Llama Guard 2.

##### Quality of Adversarial Sample Generation.

Although the SEAS framework aims to generate high-quality adversarial
samples through an iterative process, the quality and diversity of these
samples may still be limited by the initial dataset, as well as the
coverage and quality of the model’s pre-training corpus. Improving the
quality and diversity of generated adversarial prompts beyond the
limitations of the original seed dataset is a task for future work.

##### Resource Requirements.

The implementation and operation of the SEAS framework require
substantial computational resources, especially when conducting multiple
iterations and processing large-scale datasets. This may limit the
feasibility and availability of the framework in resource-constrained
environments.

## Ethical Statement

##### Preventing Misuse.

Although the SEAS framework is designed to enhance safety by adversarial
attacks, it can also be adversarially exploited. Additionally, we have
released an open-source dataset for iterative training. This dataset
includes red team prompts to assess the vulnerabilities of the target
model’s safety mechanisms. We acknowledge the inherent risks of making
the red team dataset publicly accessible, as it could be misused.
Malicious actors might exploit this resource to fine-tune language
models for objectives that could negatively impact public welfare.
Therefore, we will implement measures to restrict access to adversarial
generated data and ensure that it is not used for malicious purposes.

##### Ongoing Risk Assessment.

We recognize that technological advancements may introduce new
challenges and risks. Therefore, we are committed to continuous risk
assessments to monitor and evaluate the potential negative impacts of
the SEAS framework and its applications. Our goal is to ensure that the
research and application of the SEAS framework not only enhance the
safety of large language models (LLMs) but also adhere to high ethical
standards, contributing to responsible AI development. By employing
rigorous scientific methods and maintaining transparency, we aim to
balance technological innovation with social responsibility, ensuring AI
technologies have a positive impact on individuals and society. Through
our proactive approach, we strive to foster trust and accountability in
the evolution of AI.

### A.2 Type Details

The SEAS dataset integrate the two critical dimensions of Risk
Categories ([Inan et al. 2023](#bib.bib19)) and Attack Styles
([Samvelyan et al. 2024](#bib.bib43)). Our classification criteria
follow the prior work. All categories see Table
[13](#A1.T13 "Table 13 ‣ A.1 Data Classification ‣ Appendix A SEAS Dataset ‣ SEAS: Self-Evolving Adversarial Safety Optimization for Large Language Models").
Below are their brief descriptions:

- •
  Role Play: Involves conversations where the content pretends to be
  another character, sometimes used to evade safety checks or produce
  inappropriate content.
- •
  Session Completion: Provides a harmful or dangerous scenario to induce
  the model to continue the conversation, leading to potentially harmful
  content.
- •
  Goal Hijacking: Utilizes the dialogue system to achieve goals that
  deviate from the original intent, often with malicious aims.
- •
  Token Manipulation: Intentionally uses specific words or symbols to
  mislead the dialogue system into generating misleading or harmful
  responses.
- •
  Adversarial Prefix: Adds particular words or phrases at the beginning
  of the conversation that cause the system to generate inappropriate
  responses.
- •
  Code Nesting: Uses nested code or complex structures of symbols to
  cause the system to produce unexpected or malicious output.
- •
  Jailbreaking: Employs specific methods to bypass the system’s safety
  restrictions, generating content that should not normally be produced.
- •
  One Sided Statement: Presents biased or misleading viewpoints within
  the conversation.
- •
  Word Play: Uses puns or word games that lead models to produce
  humorous but potentially harmful outputs.
- •
  Unfairness And Discrimination: Involves language or actions that
  unfairly treat certain groups, exhibiting discrimination.
- •
  Unsafe Instruction: Provides unsafe advice or instructions that might
  lead to physical harm or other dangers.
- •
  Privacy And Property: Involves content that breaches personal privacy
  or property rights, including unauthorized sharing of personal
  information.
- •
  Health Harm: Offers advice or information that could result in health
  damage.
- •
  Crimes And Illegal Activities: Involves content related to illegal
  activities or crimes, potentially inducing unlawful behavior.

### A.3 Crowdsourcing Platform Details

The SEAS dataset partially sources its data from public crowdsourcing
platforms. Based on this data, we conduct manual annotations and
rewrites. The main process is as follows: First, on the crowdsourcing
platforms, online users create prompts based on specific attack methods.
These prompts are preprocessed by three models deployed by us. If any of
the three models identifies a user’s prompt as harmful, the attack is
considered successful. Second, platform administrators conduct strict
quality reviews of the prompts. Each prompt that passes the review is
rewarded with 2 CNY. Third, we further examine the prompts with our
algorithms to ensure their validity.

## Appendix B Metrics

### B.1 ASR

The primary measure of a red teaming method’s success is its attack
success rate (ASR) on a given target model ([Perez et al.
2022](#bib.bib36); [Ganguli et al. 2022](#bib.bib14); [Zou et al.
2023](#bib.bib52)), which is the percentage of adversarial prompts that
elicit the harmful responses from the target model. Formally, let $`f`$
be a target model with generation function $`f_{T}(x)=x^{\prime}`$,
where $`T`$ is the number of tokens to be generated, $`x`$ is a
adversarial prompt, and $`x^{\prime}`$ is the response. Let $`g`$ be a
red teaming method that generates a list of adversarial prompts, $`N`$
is the number of adversarial prompts and let $`c`$ be a classifier
mapping completion $`x^{\prime}`$ and behavior $`y`$ to $`1`$ if a red
teaming was successful and $`0`$ if not. The ASR of $`g`$ on target
model $`f`$ for behavior $`y`$ is then defined as

|     |                                                      |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \text{ASR}(y,g,f)=\frac{1}{N}\sum c(f_{T}(x_{i}),y).
       ```                                                   |     |

### B.2 Diversity

To assess diversity, we use cosine distance-based similarity as our
metrics, denoted as $`B_{cos}`$, as follows

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\begin{split}\text{$B_{cos}$}({\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{T}}},{\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{Q}}})&=\frac{1}{{|\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{T}}|}{|\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{Q}}|}}\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{T}}}\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\\
&\quad\quad\frac{\phi(x_{i})\cdot\phi(x_{j})}{|\phi(x_{i})||\phi(x_{j})|}\end{split}
``` |  |

where $`\phi`$ represents the sentence embedding model, and
$`\mathcal{X}`$ represents the collection of prompts $`x`$ generated
during training up to the current iteration, $`T`$, $`Q`$ represents
different dataset.

## Appendix C Benchmarks

### C.1 BeaverTails

BeaverTails ([Ji et al. 2023b](#bib.bib21)) is a collection focused on
AI safety that includes a series of datasets. This dataset uniquely
divides annotations of helpfulness and harmlessness for question-answer
pairs, providing distinct views on these important attributes. There are
a total of 333,963 question-answer (QA) pairs and 361,903 pairs of data
for both the helpfulness and harmlessness metrics. We utilize its
Evaluation subset to assess the safety of models.

### C.2 Anthropic HH-RLHF

The Anthropic HH-RLHF dataset ([Ganguli et al. 2022](#bib.bib14))
consists of human-generated and annotated red teaming dialogues. These
data are designed to explore how crowdworkers red team models and to
identify which types of red team attacks are successful or not. We
utilize its red-team-attempts subset for testing.

### C.3 GCG

GCG ([Zou et al. 2023](#bib.bib52)) is an attack leveraging greedy and
gradient-based discrete optimization to craft jailbreak prompts. GCG is
implemented using the Vicuna-7B-v1.5 ([Zheng et al. 2023b](#bib.bib49))
model, resulting in 520 prompts.

### C.4 AutoDan

AutoDan ([Liu et al. 2024](#bib.bib28)) is an attack utilizing
hierarchical genetic algorithms to generate semantically meaningful
jailbreak prompts. This approach also employs the Vicuna-7B-v1.5 ([Zheng
et al. 2023b](#bib.bib49)) model, contributing to the same dataset of
520 prompts.

### C.5 HarmBench

HarmBench ([Mazeika et al. 2024](#bib.bib31)) is a benchmark dataset
designed to evaluate the robustness of LLMs against harmful behaviors
across various functional and semantic categories. The dataset contains
510 instances distributed across 4 functional categories: 200 standard
behaviors, 100 copyright behaviors, 100 contextual behaviors, and 110
multimodal behaviors. Additionally, HarmBench includes 7 semantic
categories. This comprehensive coverage makes HarmBench an essential
resource for testing the safety and ethical performance of LLMs.

### C.6 CodeChameleon

CodeChameleon (CodeC) ([Lv et al. 2024](#bib.bib30)) is an attack that
employs personalized encryption to circumvent intent recognition in
LLMs. This method generates a dataset of 820 instances designed to
evaluate the robustness of LLMs against such obfuscation techniques.

### C.7 GPTFuzzer

GPTFuzzer (GPTF) ([Yu et al. 2023](#bib.bib47)) is an attack automating
jailbreak prompt creation for LLMs by mutating human-written seeds using
operators like Generate and Rephrase to produce 7,700 jailbreak prompts.

### C.8 Arena Hard and MT-Bench

Arena-Hard-Auto (Arena) ([Chiang et al. 2024](#bib.bib8)) and MT-bench
([Zheng et al. 2023a](#bib.bib48)) are two benchmarks based on pairwise
comparison and are effective at evaluating open-ended questions. We
utilized a GPT-4-based evaluation pipeline for the model’s evaluation.

### C.9 XSTest

XSTest ([Röttger et al. 2024a](#bib.bib41)) comprises 250 safe prompts
across ten prompt types that wellcalibreate models should not refuse to
comply with, and 200 unsafe prompts as contrasts that models, for most
applications, should refuse. This benchmark uses GPT-4 to evaluate
responses labeled as full refusal, partial refusal, and full compliance.
The specific prompt is as follows: our harmless test set employed the
same scheme and prompt during the evaluation process.

GPT-4 evaluation prompts ([Röttger et al. 2024a](#bib.bib41)) :“ I need
your help with classifying responses by a large language model. There
are three classes of responses. 1_full_compliance. This includes any
direct attempt to give an answer, regardless of how accurate or relevant
the answer is. It also includes cases where the model rejects the
question for not having a meaningful answer. 2_full_refusal. The model
states that it cannot, in the sense of will not, answer the prompted
question. Full refusal includes responses that only offer alternative
suggestions without answering the original question (e.g. ”instead of
smashing a piñata, you could decorate it”). 3_partial_refusal. This is
any combination of refusal and compliance. This includes
self-contradictory responses that first refuse and then go on to answer
the prompted question (e.g. “killing time at the airport is unsafe, but
here are some ways to pass the time at the airport”).
QUESTION:{QUESTION}” RESPONSE: ”{RESPONSE}” CLASS: ”

## Appendix D Open-source Datasets

### D.1 ShareGPT

ShareGPT ([Chiang et al. 2024](#bib.bib8)) refers to the multi-turn
chatting histories used by Vicuna (Chiang et al., 2023). ShareGPT
includes 86K human queries and responses from ChatGPT and other
chatbots.

### D.2 Dolly

Dolly ([Conover et al. 2023](#bib.bib12)) is an open source dataset of
instruction-following records generated by thousands of Databricks
employees in several of the behavioral categories outlined in the
InstructGPT paper ([Ouyang et al. 2022](#bib.bib35)), including
brainstorming, classification, closed QA, generation, information
extraction, open QA, and summarization.

### D.3 OpenOrca

OpenOrca ([Lian et al. 2023](#bib.bib26)) dataset is a collection of
augmented FLAN Collection data ([Longpre et al. 2023](#bib.bib29)).
Currently 1M GPT-4 completions, and 3.2M GPT-3.5 completions. The data
is primarily used for training and evaluation in the field of natural
language processing.

### D.4 ORPO-DPO-mix

ORPO-DPO-mix ([Labonne 2024](#bib.bib24)) is design for DPO training, it
is a combination of high-quality DPO datasets and use rule-based
filtering tools to chose answers.

### D.5 LIMA

LIMA ([Zhou et al. 2023a](#bib.bib50)) refers to a mixture dateset of
community question answering and human expert-written instruction and
responses.

### D.6 CPAD

CPAD ([Liu et al. 2023](#bib.bib27)) refers to a high-quality prompt
attack samples, specify the attacking goals of each prompt, which not
only accurately simulate prompt attacks on LLMs from the perspective of
attackers, but also can be utilized to evaluate and analyse the
response.

### D.7 HarmfulQA

HarmfulQA([Bhardwaj and Poria 2023](#bib.bib4)) is a set of 1,960
harmful questions to evaluate LLM performance against red-teaming
attempts.It contains questions spread over a set of 10 topics each with
 10 subtopics.

### D.8 ALERT

ALERT ([Tedeschi et al. 2024](#bib.bib45)) refers to a fine-grained
safety risk taxonomy, consisting of 6 coarse and 32 fine-grained
categories. This taxonomy serves as the foundation for the benchmark to
provide detailed insights about a model’s weaknesses and vulnerabilities
as well as inform targeted safety enhancements.

## Appendix E Details of Experiments

For all inference experiments involving red team models and target
models, we utilized a cluster equipped with 8 NVIDIA A100-80GB GPUs. The
vLLM ([Kwon et al. 2023](#bib.bib23)) was employed for inference
acceleration, applying consistent chat templates and hyper-parameters
across all tests: batch size = 1, T = 0.8, n = 5, top-p = 0.9, and
max_tokens = 2048.

For the closed-source model with API access, we implemented a generation
scheme with a temperature of 0.8, running the inference on CPUs. The
same settings were also applied during the evaluation of GPT-4. For all
inference experiments involving safe classifier, we use
hyper-parameters: batch size = 1, temperature = 0, top-p = 1. To
minimize error bias, we repeated the experiments three times and
calculated the average of the scores.

For all SFT and DPO experiments, we utilized the same cluster equipped
with 8 NVIDIA A100-80GB GPUs. For the iterative update model (excluding
the initiation of models), we employed the following settings: learning
rate = 5e-6, batch size = 8, and epoch = 1.
````

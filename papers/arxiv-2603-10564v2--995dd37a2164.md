# Reflection-Driven Self-Improving Language Model Agents for Adaptive RAN Slicing

Georgalas^(†), Wang Miao^(∗) Affiliation: ^(∗)University of Exeter,

###### Abstract

Adaptive control in AI-native networks remains challenging because tasks
such as dynamic Radio Access Network (RAN) slicing require persistent
trade-off decisions among spectrum efficiency, service quality, and
reconfiguration stability. Conventional Reinforcement Learning (RL)
compresses these competing objectives into handcrafted scalar rewards,
requiring exhaustive tuning for a balanced trade-off. Language model
(LM) agents provide a new path toward adaptive control because they can
semantically represent complex preference relations, but prompt-based
adaptation struggles to retain long-horizon interaction history due to
finite context limits. We propose a Reflection-Driven Self-Improvement
(ReDSI) framework that replaces scalar rewards with semantic feedback
for continuous policy adaptation. This framework is built upon a Small
Language Model (SLM) agent for fast decision-making, coupled with a
bi-perspective reflection to transform interaction history into
preference data, subsequently distilling long-horizon decision
experience into the SLM parameters through preference-based fine-tuning.
Evaluated on a dynamic RAN slicing task, our method achieves an optimal
trade-off across multiple competing objectives, outperforming standard
RL baselines and existing LM-based agents by demonstrating substantially
greater sample efficiency. These results highlight a viable paradigm of
self-improving LM agents for reward-free multi-objective continuous
control in AI-native networks.

## I Introduction

The transition to 6G is driven by applications such as holographic
telepresence, the Internet of Everything (IoE), and autonomous vehicular
networks, which impose stringent demands on latency, throughput, and
scalability \[1\]. Meeting these demands requires persistent adaptation,
making AI-native architecture a key enabler of future networks \[2\].
Unlike traditional “add-on” AI, AI-native systems embed intelligence
directly into the network stack, enabling real-time autonomous control
and self-optimization under changing traffic, resources, and user
demands.

Within the AI-native paradigm, Reinforcement Learning is widely viewed
as a primary tool for closed-loop autonomy, especially for tasks such as
dynamic Radio Access Network slicing in 6G, where an agent must
continually allocate resources under competing objectives \[3, 4, 5\]. A
good policy must balance latency, throughput, energy efficiency,
fairness, and reconfiguration stability rather than optimize a single
target. In standard RL, this trade-off is compressed into a handcrafted
scalar reward, so the modeling difficulty appears in practice as reward
engineering. In dynamic network settings, such rewards are hard to
specify and often require exhaustive reward engineering and
trial-and-error manual tuning \[3, 6\]. This raises a key question: can
agents learn effective multi-objective control policies without manually
engineered scalar rewards?

illustrate a conceptual deployment mapping. (b) During offline parallel
reflection, Reflector workers use the full trajectory $`H`$ to label
each decision as positive ($`+`$) or negative ($`-`$). They generate a
candidate action for each negatively labeled decision, forming the
reflection-labeled trajectory $`H^{\prime}`$. (c) During offline policy
refinement, each RfR iteration generates rollout outputs $`R`$ from
original negative-example prompts, combines $`H^{\prime}`$ and $`R`$
into preference dataset $`\mathcal{D}`$, and updates the Actor through
KTO. The same $`H^{\prime}`$ is reused without additional environment
interaction. Updated parameters support subsequent RfR iterations and
online control.

Language Model (LM) agents offer a promising alternative by utilizing
semantic reflection to evaluate past actions, bypassing the need for
manually engineered scalar reward signals. This form of feedback can
capture more complex reward patterns than scalar rewards, particularly
in multi-objective settings where different goals may conflict. However,
the application of current LM architectures to continuous network
control reveals critical structural limitations. Early networking
adaptations, such as NetLLM \[7\], utilize LMs for downstream tasks but
rely entirely on offline training with static expert trajectories.
Consequently, they fail to support closed-loop continual policy
improvement from online environment interactions. Recent LM agents, such
as Reflexion \[8\] and ExpeL \[9\], attempt to improve behavior by
incorporating textual feedback and past experiences into prompts, either
directly or through retrieval from external memory. Although effective
for sequential decision-making, these approaches still rely on finite
prompt context to exploit accumulated experience. Therefore, these
methods remain constrained by finite context windows and long-context
degradation \[10\], posing challenges for persistent control in
AI-native networks.

To address the constraints of prompt-bound memory and the manual tuning
required by handcrafted scalar rewards, we propose a Reflection-Driven
Self-Improvement (ReDSI) framework deploying a Small Language Model
(SLM) agent that internalizes interaction history into model parameters
rather than ever-expanding prompts. Through semantic reflection, the
framework converts operational experience into preference-based feedback
for continuous adaptation. This eliminates scalar reward engineering,
enabling sustained autonomous control in dynamic networks.

To realize this ReDSI framework, we make the following key
contributions:

- •
  We introduce an Actor-Reflector framework with bi-perspective
  reflection, synthesizing step-level and trajectory-level semantic
  feedback for reward-free policy adaptation.
- •
  We propose a novel offline distillation pipeline,
  Refine-from-Reflection (RfR), which converts reflection-labeled
  trajectories into preference data and distills long-horizon experience
  into the Actor via KTO \[11\].
- •
  We evaluate the framework on dynamic RAN slicing and show the
  strongest overall multi-objective performance with substantially fewer
  training environment interactions.

## II System Model

### II-A AI-RAN Slicing Architecture

We consider an AI-driven RAN slicing framework for 6G networks,
leveraging a state-of-the-art AI-RAN architecture with a central
controller deployed on the RAN Intelligent Controller to enable adaptive
resource allocation across multiple network slices \[3\]. Within this
architecture, the system employs an SLM agent to manage the slice
resources, which are structured as Physical Resource Blocks in
time-frequency grids. The controller dynamically adjusts the inter-slice
PRB allocation per decision interval based on monitored performance
metrics \[3\]. To ensure isolation, Physical Resource Blocks are
strictly partitioned between slices, allowing independent operation
within allocated resources.

The framework operates in a closed loop: SLM agents continuously
evaluate slice demands and send inter-slice PRB allocation decisions to
the controller for the next interval. Intra-slice scheduling is handled
by a proportional fair scheduler, while our focus remains on inter-slice
control. This design enables adaptive resource management under
real-time traffic variation, balancing slice performance requirements
and overall spectrum efficiency.

### II-B Problem Definition

We formulate the RAN slicing task as a Multi-Objective Optimization
Problem (MOOP) with three conflicting goals: maximizing resource
utilization, ensuring service quality, and minimizing reconfiguration
overhead. Higher utilization improves throughput under bandwidth
constraints, service quality requires adaptive allocation to meet
diverse QoS needs, and limiting reconfiguration overhead is important
because frequent reallocations introduce operational costs \[4\]. These
objectives are inherently conflicting: improving utilization often
requires frequent reallocations, which increase reconfiguration overhead
and may destabilize service, while prioritizing service quality through
over-provisioning can reduce utilization under low demand. Likewise,
minimizing reconfiguration overhead through rigid allocations limits
adaptation to dynamic traffic. This three-way tension defines the core
challenge of efficient RAN slicing.

Spectrum Efficiency (SE) quantifies radio resource utilization. At time
step $`t`$, let $`\mathcal{P}_{t}`$ denote the set of packets
successfully received by the User Equipments (UEs) in the slice. Then,

```math
SE_{t}={\frac{\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{t}}|p|}{\tau}}/b_{t} \tag{1}
```

where $`|p|`$ denotes the size of packet $`p`$, $`\tau`$ represents the
decision interval, and $`b_{t}`$ indicates the allocated bandwidth for
the slice at time step $`t`$.

Service quality is quantified by the cumulative Packet QoS (PQoS)
violation $`V`$. Let $`\mathcal{Q}_{t}`$ denote all packets in the slice
subject to QoS evaluation at time step $`t`$, regardless of their
reception status. A packet is counted as violating QoS if its metric
vector $`M_{p}^{t}`$ exceeds the threshold $`\Theta`$, indicated by
$`\chi_{p}^{t}=\mathbb{I}(M_{p}^{t}\not\models\Theta)`$. A timestep is
then counted as violating QoS if any packet in $`\mathcal{Q}_{t}`$
triggers such an event. The cumulative Packet QoS violation over the
measurement window $`T_{m}`$ is:

```math
\displaystyle V=\sum_{t=0}^{T_{m}}v_{t}=\sum_{t=0}^{T_{m}}\mathbb{I}\!\left(\exists\,p\in\mathcal{Q}_{t}:\chi_{p}^{\,t}=1\right) \tag{2}
```

The overhead of resource allocation is measured by the Resource
Reconfiguration Times metric $`C`$, which counts the number of timesteps
where the slice bandwidth allocation changes. Let
$`c_{t}=\mathbb{I}(b_{t}\neq b_{t-1})`$ denote whether a reconfiguration
occurs at timestep $`t`$. Then,

```math
\displaystyle C \displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{T_{m}}c_{t}=\sum_{t=0}^{T_{m}}\mathbb{I}(b_{t}\neq b_{t-1}) \tag{3}
```

These metrics define the finite-horizon MOOP for control policy design
over the evaluation window $`T_{m}`$. We seek a policy $`\pi\in\Pi`$
that maximizes spectrum efficiency while minimizing reconfiguration
times and Packet QoS violation violations:

```math
\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T_{m}}SE_{t}\right],\right. \tag{4}
```

```math
\displaystyle\left.-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T_{m}}v_{t}\right],-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T_{m}}c_{t}\right]\right\}
```

## III Proposed ReDSI Framework

The ReDSI framework comprises online policy inference, offline parallel
reflection, and offline policy refinement. We first describe the
Actor–Reflector framework, then explain how one reflection-labeled
trajectory supports policy refinement.

### III-A Actor-Reflector Framework

The design is inspired by the classical Actor-Critic paradigm, in which
the Actor selects actions and the Critic evaluates them to guide policy
improvement \[5\]. Our framework follows this decomposition but replaces
scalar value estimation with language-based reflection. As shown in Fig.
1(a), the Actor is an SLM policy $`\pi`$ that builds an input sequence
$`I_{t}=\textsc{Prompt}(H_{t-1},s_{t})`$ from the current state
$`s_{t}`$ and a short interaction history $`H_{t-1}`$. The model outputs
a structured response $`O_{t}=\pi(I_{t})`$, from which we extract a step
reflection $`\psi_{t}`$, an action $`a_{t}`$, and a brief action
analysis $`\phi_{t}`$. After executing $`a_{t}`$, the environment
returns a task-specific metric vector $`M_{t}`$, and the tuple
$`(s_{t},a_{t},\psi_{t},\phi_{t},M_{t},I_{t},O_{t})`$ is appended to the
trajectory history $`H`$.

After each trajectory, the Reflector $`\mathcal{R}`$ reviews the full
history $`H`$ and assesses each decision using the observed outcomes
across the episode. It assigns a quality label
$`\ell_{t}\in\{\text{True},\text{False}\}`$ and, when it judges the
original decision suboptimal, proposes a potentially better candidate
action $`\hat{a}_{t}`$. This converts the raw trajectory into a labeled
history $`H^{\prime}`$ (Fig. 1(b)), which can be processed in parallel
across steps and later used for preference-based fine-tuning.

Conceptually, trajectory-level reflection can be represented as an
idealized retrospective preference assessment:

```math
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{t}=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=t}^{T}\gamma^{k-t}r_{\text{lang}}(s_{k},a_{k})\,\middle|\,s_{t}=s,a_{t}=a,H\right] \tag{5}
```

Here, $`r_{\text{lang}}`$ symbolically represents the Reflector’s
implicit preference over the multi-objective trade-off, rather than an
explicit scalar reward supplied by the environment. This expression is a
conceptual analogy, not a numerical optimization carried out by the
Reflector. In practice, $`\hat{a}_{t}`$ is a candidate proposed using
$`H`$ and judged potentially better in light of the observed trajectory.
The Reflector is analogous to a Critic, but its evaluation is expressed
through language-level reasoning rather than an externally engineered
numerical reward.

Step-level reflection $`(\psi_{t},\phi_{t})`$ provides lightweight
prompt memory for online adaptation without changing Actor weights.
Trajectory-level reflection uses the complete episode to assess how
local actions balance efficiency, service quality, and stability.
Together, these complementary views provide real-time guidance and
durable supervision.

### III-B Policy Refinement from Reflection

Once the Reflector has produced the labeled trajectory $`H^{\prime}`$,
ReDSI uses this feedback to refine the Actor through offline
preference-based fine-tuning. This policy refinement stage corresponds
to Fig. 1(c) and uses one collected trajectory with its associated
reflection labels and candidate actions.

During policy refinement, multiple Refine-from-Reflection (RfR)
iterations update the Actor using the same $`H^{\prime}`$. The Reflector
assesses the trajectory once, so its labels and candidate actions remain
fixed across iterations. Each RfR iteration consists of three steps:
rollout with the current Actor, preference dataset construction, and a
KTO parameter update.

1. Rollout: For each negative example in $`H^{\prime}`$, we generate
   $`m`$ outputs from the current Actor using the original prompt
   $`I_{t}`$. Here, rollout means generating LM outputs from stored
   prompts, without additional environment interaction.

2. Preference dataset construction: Each iteration’s preference dataset
   $`\mathcal{D}`$ combines the original Reflector-labeled examples in
   $`H^{\prime}`$ with the rollout outputs. The original examples are
   labeled positive for decisions judged effective by the Reflector and
   negative for those judged suboptimal. For each rollout output, we
   extract its action and compare it with the fixed candidate
   $`\hat{a}_{t}`$. Exact action matches are labeled positive, while all
   other rollout outputs are labeled negative.

3. KTO parameter update: We update the Actor parameters using
   $`\mathcal{D}`$ and the KTO \[11\] algorithm. Unlike pairwise preference
   objectives such as DPO \[12\], KTO supports unbalanced datasets by
   directly modeling the absolute preference likelihood of each sample
   using prospect-theory.

The KTO loss is defined as:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KTO}}(\pi^{\prime},\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim D}\left[\lambda_{y}-v(x,y)\right] \tag{6}
```

where:

```math
v(x,y)=\begin{cases}\lambda_{+}\cdot\sigma\left(\beta\cdot(r_{\theta}(x,y)-z_{0})\right),&\text{if }y\sim y_{+}\mid x\\
\lambda_{-}\cdot\sigma\left(\beta\cdot(z_{0}-r_{\theta}(x,y))\right),&\text{if }y\sim y_{-}\mid x\end{cases} \tag{7}
```

```math
r_{\theta}(x,y)=\log\frac{\pi^{\prime}(y\mid x)}{\pi(y\mid x)},\qquad z_{0}=\text{KL}\left(\pi^{\prime}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi(\cdot\mid x)\right) \tag{8}
```

Here, $`x`$ corresponds to the input prompt $`I`$, and $`y`$ is the
generated output sequence $`O`$. The policy $`\pi(y\mid x)`$ models the
likelihood of generating output $`O`$ given prompt $`I`$. $`y_{+}`$ and
$`y_{-}`$ denote the corresponding preferred and dispreferred outputs.
$`\pi^{\prime}`$ is the policy being optimized, $`\pi`$ is the reference
Actor fixed within each RfR iteration, and $`\sigma(\cdot)`$ is the
sigmoid function. The KL term $`z_{0}`$ constrains policy drift from the
reference model. The parameter $`\beta`$ controls the sensitivity of the
preference signal. The utility function $`v(x,y)`$ uses asymmetric
scaling coefficients $`\lambda_{+}`$ and $`\lambda_{-}`$, defined as:

```math
\lambda_{+}=\frac{\max(N_{+},N_{-})}{N_{+}},\qquad\lambda_{-}=\frac{\max(N_{+},N_{-})}{N_{-}} \tag{9}
```

where $`N_{+}`$ and $`N_{-}`$ denote the numbers of positive and
negative samples, respectively.

The first RfR iteration starts from the initial Actor; each later
iteration starts from the Actor updated in the preceding iteration. At
each iteration’s start, the KTO reference is set to this starting Actor
version and remains fixed during optimization.

### IV-B Baseline algorithms

We evaluate our method against two categories of baselines. First, we
implement three RL algorithms: Deep Q-Network (DQN) \[13\], Soft
Actor-Critic (SAC) \[14\], and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
\[15\].

Second, we adapt Reflexion \[8\] as the primary LM-agent baseline,
preserving its tripartite architecture with Qwen3-4B as the Actor and
DeepSeek-R1 for trajectory evaluation and self-reflection. For fair
comparison, our ReDSI framework uses the same backbone models, so the
observed differences are attributable to the learning architecture
rather than model capacity.

For all algorithms, the system state at time step t is represented as:

```math
s_{t}=\left[a_{t-1},SE_{t},\mu_{t},\delta_{t},\epsilon_{t}\right] \tag{10}
```

where $`a_{t-1}`$ is the previous action (current PRB allocation),
$`SE_{t}`$ is the current spectral efficiency, $`\mu_{t}`$ represents
the throughput of arriving traffic, $`\delta_{t}`$ denotes the increment
in queued packet size, and $`\epsilon_{t}`$ indicates the size of
dropped packets. This compact state representation provides the agent
with complete information about current network conditions and resource
demands.

The action space represents the number of available PRBs for allocation
and is discrete, with the agent determining the optimal resource
distribution based on the observed state. The action at each time step
directly influences the network’s resource utilization and performance
metrics.

For RL baselines, we use the following per-slice reward to balance
spectral efficiency, allocation stability, and QoS:

```math
\displaystyle r_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})=\alpha SE_{t}+(1-c_{t})B_{\mathrm{keep}}-v_{t}P_{\mathrm{QoS}} \tag{11}
```

where $`\alpha`$ weights SE, and $`c_{t}`$ and $`v_{t}`$ are the binary
indicators in (3) and (2), respectively. The term
$`B_{\mathrm{keep}}(1-c_{t})`$ rewards unchanged allocations, while
$`P_{\mathrm{QoS}}`$ penalizes QoS violations.¹¹ 1 The experiments below
use $`\alpha=5`$, $`B_{\mathrm{keep}}=5`$, and $`P_{\mathrm{QoS}}=100`$.
Episode return sums these rewards over 300 steps and all three slices.
For ReDSI and Reflexion, this return is used only for post-hoc
evaluation.

The RL baselines exhibit clear single-objective optimization biases
rather than balanced multi-objective control. From Fig. 2, DQN mainly
improves Mean SE, but its reconfiguration curve oscillates around a high
level with little net reduction, and its PQoS-violation curve does not
improve over training and ends worse than in the early stage. SAC
improves Mean SE and partially optimizes reconfiguration, but its
PQoS-violation count increases rapidly during training instead of
decreasing, indicating that the learned policy shifts toward
throughput-oriented behavior at the expense of service quality. PPO
behaves in the opposite direction: it achieves the best PQoS-violation
result, but its reconfiguration curve declines only slowly and remains
high, while its Mean SE stays the lowest among the compared methods.
Taken together, these trends show that the RL baselines tend to optimize
one or two objectives while failing to maintain balanced control across
all three metrics.

In contrast, ReDSI achieves the strongest overall multi-objective
performance under the adopted setup while using far fewer training
environment interactions. As shown in Table III, ReDSI and Reflexion
each use one 300-step trajectory for training/adaptation per run,
whereas each RL baseline uses 480,000 training environment steps per
run. Despite this gap, ReDSI attains the highest episode return, ranks
first or second on every reported metric, and is the only method that
remains consistently competitive across all three objectives.
Concretely, it achieves the lowest reconfiguration count (21.091), the
highest return, the second-highest Mean SE (5.354), and the second-best
PQoS-violation count (8.561). PPO still delivers the best PQoS-violation
result, and SAC remains the strongest baseline in Mean SE, but both do
so by sacrificing the other objectives much more severely. This
indicates that ReDSI offers the best integrated balance among
efficiency, stability, and service quality, rather than excelling in
only one dimension.

Compared with Reflexion, the main gain of ReDSI is a further reduction
in reconfiguration count while maintaining similar Mean SE and
PQoS-violation performance. Consequently, the advantage of ReDSI is not
merely a marginal gain on one metric, but a stronger overall operating
point in the three-objective trade-off space. This pattern is consistent
with the difference between the two adaptation mechanisms: Reflexion
revises behavior through prompt-side history, whereas ReDSI uses RfR to
convert trajectory-level reflections into preference data and updates
the Actor weights. In this setting, weight-level adaptation appears more
effective at retaining useful control patterns across long trajectories.

### IV-D Ablation Study

Fig. 4 provides an ablation by comparing the pretrained Actor with the
same Actor after policy refinement. This refinement stage reuses one
reflection-labeled trajectory across six RfR iterations. Each iteration
performs rollout, preference dataset construction, and KTO parameter
updates. This process reduces the reconfiguration count by approximately
33% while slightly improving Mean SE and keeping PQoS-violation behavior
competitive. This result indicates that the observed gains are not
solely inherited from the pretrained backbone, but are associated with
reflection-driven policy refinement.

Subplots (a)–(f) of Fig. 4 show the chosen and rejected KTO scores
across the six RfR iterations. These scores are the batch-averaged
values of $`\beta r_{\theta}(x,y_{+})`$ and
$`\beta r_{\theta}(x,y_{-})`$, respectively. The large gap in the first
iteration indicates that the initial preference dataset already provides
a strong correction signal. As training proceeds, both scores gradually
move toward zero, as shown in Fig. 4(g), suggesting diminishing returns
when repeatedly exploiting the same collected trajectory. After several
iterations, the Actor has absorbed most of the useful information from
the trajectory.

Overall, these results support sample-efficient adaptation in the
studied RAN slicing setting: with minimal additional environment
interaction, ReDSI achieves the strongest overall multi-objective
performance, as reflected by the highest episode return and
first-or-second ranking on every raw metric.

## V Conclusion

This paper presented ReDSI for adaptive RAN slicing that balances three
competing objectives: spectrum efficiency, service quality, and
reconfiguration stability. Step-level reflection guides online
decisions, while trajectory-level reflection produces preference data
for offline policy refinement. Through RfR and KTO, the SLM Actor
internalizes experience beyond prompt memory by repeatedly refining its
policy on one labeled trajectory without additional environment
interaction.

In the evaluated setting, ReDSI achieves the highest episode return,
reflecting overall multi-objective performance, and the fewest
reconfigurations. The RL baselines fail to optimize the objectives
jointly: PPO suppresses QoS violations but leaves SE lowest and
reconfiguration high, whereas SAC and DQN improve SE while QoS
violations worsen during training. ReDSI reduces reconfigurations by
28.4% versus Reflexion with similar SE and PQoS-violation performance
under the same adaptation budget. It requires 300 adaptation environment
steps versus 480,000 RL training steps per run, supporting
sample-efficient multi-objective control without handcrafted scalar
rewards.
